## Foreword: The Conception of Nameless Heroes

The years around the War of Resistance were years of long upheaval.

In the annals of our country's secret service work there have been many deeds, heroic and tragic, deeds that ask to be sung and wept over, and all of them, for the sake of secrecy, were buried and lost. More than forty years have now passed; what has drifted loose and been handed on from mouth to mouth has in some cases been distorted, in others deliberately wrapped in mystery, until most of it has lost all truth. Now, while I am still here, a living witness, I mean to set these things down—the ones I lived through myself and cannot erase from memory—choosing the cases of gravest consequence and most harrowing peril, and recording them one by one in the order they occurred. By rights this is a thing worth doing, and many old friends have urged me to get it written quickly.

This is a truthful record. From first page to last it is all real people and real events, with nothing of the storyteller's embroidery. As for the times, the places, the persons, and the course of what happened—unless my memory is hazy—every part of it can be checked against the evidence. And as for the substance of these episodes, there is no need to invent anything: they were stirring to begin with, not merely stirring but moving, and some of them frankly astonishing. Whenever I look back over the whole course of the work I took part in or directed, I cannot reckon how many comrades and colleagues were killed or wounded; how many families and friends were dragged down with them; how many innocent countrymen were caught in the blast. Whether or not they have been enrolled among the loyal dead and honored with commendation, or are past all tracing and have sunk into obscurity, in my own heart they are every one of them nameless heroes deserving of reverence.

These men, to defend their country and uphold justice, gave the whole strength of their lives, and in the end gave up their lives as well. Set aside the question of success or a martyr's death: their names appear in no history that came after, and their brave and terrible deeds are unknown to the world outside. Righteous and fierce in life, silent and forgotten in death—it is a grief to think of. Now, as I set these old events down, disclosing them with all solemnity and writing with a careful, trembling hand, I have given the book the respectful title *Nameless Heroes*.

As to the spirit in which it is written: for the sake of preserving the truth, and of rendering an account to history, I say what a thing is and say it as it ought to be said, without ornament or gloss; still less would I dare, out of private feeling, to belittle anyone or to puff anyone up. Right and wrong, merit and fault, have a justice of their own and need no veiled insinuation. Should the facts anywhere touch on a person's private life or good name, I simply leave them unmentioned.

I did not want to write a memoir. My chief reason is that I have nothing worth trumpeting about myself—those who know me will know this for the plain truth. And so, in the facts I set down here, there is no intention whatever of covering over the errors and failings of the past. That I tell it in the first person is only for ease of writing; if I must put it in an image, I am no more than the thread that stitches the pages together.

What the book records all took place in the nineteen-twenties and thirties. Some of it was never known to outsiders at all and has faded quietly away; some caused a great stir at the time and was talked of on every side. But the real facts, then and for some years after, were classified secret—so far-reaching were their effects, so wide the web of people they involved. Even where rumor got out, it was a scale and half a claw, never the whole beast. Now that the people concerned are gone and the times have changed, to make these things public can do no harm—not to the state, not to the nation, not to any body of men, not to any single person. And should there still be anything that must be held back, I will of course put the national interest first, keep to a strict measure, and not slip by so much as a hair.

*Nameless Heroes* is planned in five parts. The outlines are ready, and writing is under way, for three of them; what follows is a brief account of the headings of those first three.
## Introduction to Rooting Out Traitors in the North

Part One: *Rooting Out Traitors in the North*

The opening section of Part One offers a general introduction to the era that gave rise to our country's secret service work, its early scale, and the basic tasks of the time, with the emphasis on the part of it I experienced myself. It also describes the actual circumstances under which Mr. Dai Li (courtesy name Yunong) was charged with leading the work, and how I came to know Mr. Dai, to enter his confidence, and at last to join the service. There are here not a few hidden matters that others have never told, or told only by halves. My reason for writing this section first is to give the reader a basic conception of what "secret service work" was, and at the same time to lift the veil on much that has been kept mysterious and to clear up a number of doubtful points, so that as I write on and the reader reads on, everything will follow in its natural course.

The main substance of *Rooting Out Traitors in the North* is a recounting of the best of the direct-action work carried out in the Beiping and Tianjin region in the years around the War of Resistance, from 1932 to 1938, with some slight touching as well on intelligence activity and on the doings of the Anti-Japanese Traitor-Killing Corps and the Luan-Yu guerrillas.

During this period the direct-action work I directed, in my capacity as chief of the Beiping Station or of the Tianjin Station, had its successes and its failures, and its consequences reached further than the two words "success" and "failure" can measure. These few operations may be called the first cry of our country's action men, the maiden effort in the whole history of Chinese secret service work; and what most compels one's admiration lies in the spirit of our comrades in the work—their willingness to brave danger and to sacrifice themselves, their shared hatred of the enemy, their valor in rooting out traitors—all of it carried to the utmost, a thunderclap that startled the world and made China and foreign lands alike look on with new eyes.

The first operation of all, the one that opened the record, took as its target Zhang Jingyao, former military governor of Hunan in the age of the Beiyang warlords. He had slipped from Tianjin into the foreign Legation Quarter of Beiping, and there, at the instigation of the Japanese military, was inciting serving officers on our side to treasonous designs. Through the wit and daring of our comrades, and a run of extraordinary strokes of luck, the blow succeeded where the way had seemed darkest. And by that single blow the great plot the Japanese warlords had schemed over so long, meaning to dismember our North China, was shattered utterly. Only afterward did we learn that another "suspected accomplice," Sun Chuanfang, former commander-in-chief of the Five Provinces Allied Forces, was lodging at the same hotel; when the shots rang out, the old rascal was frightened off without our meaning it, and from then on he vanished from sight and dared do no more mischief—until, later, he was stabbed to death by Miss Shi Jianqiao, which brought his life of crime to its close.

Because this was a great event in the history of the Republic, people have gone on describing the case for decades, right down into the 1970s, on the mainland, overseas, and in Taiwan alike. But for one who took part in it himself to lay out the whole course of the facts—this is the first time.

The second operation took place in the French Concession of Tianjin. Its target was Ji Hongchang, who under the false banner of resisting Japan had joined with Feng Yuxiang, Fang Zhenwu, and others to organize the "People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army" in Chahar, meaning to enlarge his anti-government armed strength. Ji had risen through the Northwest Army and was a wanted man for having broken with the central authorities. This time he took the post of Second Army commander in the "Anti-Japanese Allied Army" and styled himself "Front Commander" for the recovery of the lost lands of eastern Chahar. But his labor was in vain: the so-called "Anti-Japanese Allied Army" would not catch fire, and Feng Yuxiang, seeing the thing was hopeless, pulled out halfway and went back to Mount Tai. Ji Hongchang only grew more reckless, and finally renamed his force the "Anti-Japanese Anti-Chiang Army"; far from fighting the Japanese, he turned his guns around and openly made war on the government forces for more than fifty days. He was subdued and captured in the end, but the people of the country around Beiping were cruelly ravaged by the fighting and suffered all the misery of flight and homelessness. More regrettable still, while Ji Hongchang was being escorted back under guard, he most unexpectedly slipped away by a ruse.

Here one thing must be stressed in particular: Ji Hongchang's outrages, all that he did, were by no means his own private doing; what drove him from behind was the utterly evil Communist Party.

The strange thing happened in the course of carrying out this operation. Once the action men were in position, and Ji Hongchang's place in the room had been fixed beyond doubt, in the blink of an eye it was the Southwest delegate Liu Shaorang who was struck down in his stead—the plum tree withered in the peach tree's place. For a time the riddle could not be answered; only forty years later was the answer found, in documents the Communists themselves had published. This story has been written of before, and is recorded in the official papers too, yet not merely in outline, and with wide discrepancies. Even I, who planned the work and served as its commander on the ground, took nearly a year, and much cross-checking of evidence, to write this account.

In the third operation our work met with utter failure. The place set for it was the Japanese Concession of Tianjin, and the target of sanction was Shi Yousan, a fallen and fickle soldier who blew hot and cold and shifted with every wind. Shi had come out of the Northwest Army; a habitual opportunist, forever tacking this way and that, he held a whole string of "commander" titles. After taking part in the failed revolt of Yan and Feng in 1930, he shuttled constantly between Dairen and Tianjin, biding his time. Just then, with the situation in North China murky and unsettled, Shi seized his chance to recruit scattered stragglers into a plainclothes corps, meaning to disturb the local peace and to manufacture for the Japanese a pretext for aggression against our country. The man was crazed with malice, a tiger's accomplice, deserving of death many times over. But of the men who took part in this operation, brave and loyal though they were, none had experience enough; and being overwrought at the critical moment, they gave themselves away. In the end—"the campaign not yet won, the soldier falls first"—they became, one and all, the sacrifices of this action.

Because in this case no report had been made to our superiors beforehand, and because it afterward involved the death of an operative with whom no formal working relationship had yet been established, difficulties arose in setting matters right. Here too I acted on the impulse of the moment, and so committed a grave blunder that all but ruined my name and standing.

The fourth was the case of Yin Rugeng. Yin Rugeng had been the administrative inspector for the East Hebei district of our Hebei provincial government; sheltered under the wing of the Japanese army, brazen and lawless, he actually set up a puppet regime of his own, usurping the title of "East Hebei Anti-Communist Autonomous Committee." In truth it neither opposed communism nor governed itself: it harbored evil designs and did nothing but foment disorder. Our plan at the time was to work turncoat inducement and sanction in tandem—on the one hand to bring the security corps of the East Hebei puppet regime back to loyalty, and on the other to be rid of that scoundrel Yin Rugeng.

As the case went forward there were twists and reversals, and troubles sprang up on every side. In the end the work of sanction was checked halfway, while the work of inducement was crowned with complete success.

At Yin Rugeng's side were two remarkable women, both closely bound up with this case, in ties subtle and richly dramatic. The secret of it was kept for decades; now it has become yesterday's faded flower, and there is no harm in telling it, no injury in setting it down.

The fifth operation, both planned and carried out in the year after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident and the outbreak of the War of Resistance, when Beiping had already fallen, took as its target of sanction Wang Kemin, the number-one figure of the North China puppet regime. First we tried to take him by guile through an inside man, and failing that, resolved on an armed assault by main force, bent on slaying the ringleader outright. This too was our comrades' bravest and most heroic showing.

I remember still how foul the weather was that day—stones and sand flying, the sky black as dusk. Under the enemy's dense guard, in a hail of gunfire, we fought a sniping action of sheer frontal assault. But as ill luck would have it we ran up against a Japanese adviser, Yamamoto Eiji, who was willing to die in another's place and so saved the life of "Blind Wang," who by every right should have died and did not. This Yamamoto, a Japanese rōnin, had done much wrong in China and deserved to die well enough; yet by throwing his own body down in sacrifice he brought the whole plan we had built with our heart's blood to ruin on the very edge of success.

Setting aside the enmity between us and looking at it from another angle: that Yamamoto Eiji could rush into danger at the crisis and "give up his life for righteousness"—did he not, after all, act out one scene of the so-called spirit of bushidō?

Such, then, is the outline of what is in Part One, *Rooting Out Traitors in the North*. Do not be put off that the telling is all fighting and killing; the air of slaughter is not so heavy after all—the less so since "rebellious ministers and villainous sons, anyone may put them to death." For I was carrying out the duties of the work. In private I never cared to take up knife or gun.
## Introduction to Disgrace at Hanoi

Part Two: *Disgrace at Hanoi*

The Hanoi affair—the case of Wang Jingwei—was a great matter that shook the world and stirred men's hearts, one that bore upon the whole future of the War of Resistance and the fate of the nation.

I was the man who, under orders, took charge of carrying it out on the ground, in Hanoi, in Vietnam.

For forty years those who have discussed this business have been many indeed, and every kind of account has been given; now let us hear what the man who was there confesses to.

Strictly speaking, this work fell short of complete success, and the responsibility is mine and cannot be shifted. Yet the comrades who took part in the action—eighteen men, among them Yu Jiansheng and Wang Luqiao—truly gave all the effort they were bound to give; only one of them, Tang Yingjie, a man who could pass through rooms and vault the rooftops and was skilled in combat, filed a reconnaissance report that was somewhat off the mark, and this became one of the chief causes of the failure of the attempt on Wang.

*Disgrace at Hanoi* runs from the reshuffling of personnel all the way to the final assault; the span of time it covers is not long, but the twists and entanglements within it are, to tell the truth, a myriad tangled threads.

The book will lift, so far as it can, some of the baffling secrets that history holds. Such as: was Wang Jingwei loyal or a traitor? To put it plainly, once he had issued his peace telegram and openly called for accommodation with the enemy, had he beforehand an understanding with the government authorities—playing out a two-man act in concert with the Chongqing high command, which was for resistance to the end? Or again: was there from the very outset no choice but to put him to death? Or was it some other special cause that provoked the whole upheaval? To all of this the book can offer the best-grounded answers, and once the reader has read it, all will be clear.

After the Hanoi affair, Wang Jingwei published a long essay titled "Let Me Give One Example," a piece of literary fencing that came close to sophistry and quite lost the bearing of a statesman of his generation.

In 1944, as Wang lay dying in Japan, no longer able to take up a brush, he dictated from his sickbed to his wife, Chen Bijun, an essay called "My Final Frame of Mind," in which, between the lines, there was a good deal of remorse. Is it truly so that when a man is about to die, his words turn to good? Whether the piece be genuine or false, it was well able to win him a measure of sympathy.

The book cites a number of relevant documents in corroboration; with the passage of years and the change of circumstances, these papers, though of no great rarity, were nonetheless not easily come by.

The tensest moment of the action itself ran from ten in the morning of 20 March 1939 to one o'clock in the small hours of the 21st; in those dozen-odd hours my action men struck three times running—first pursuing and attacking, then storming the front gate, and last scaling the wall, breaking down the door, mounting the stairs, and pouring in fire. Because I myself was on the scene throughout, the whole course of it is graven on me to this day, plain before my eyes.

And what I shall never in my life forget, what I shall regret to the end of my days, are the grave consequences brought on by the missed openings, the mistimings, the failures of those three assaults.

Rear a tiger and you leave yourself a calamity: the puppet regime that Wang Jingwei went on to raise up at Nanjing, a scourge to family and country, was precisely the greatest evil fruit left by the failure of this action. Had we been able then to sanction Wang Jingwei and make an example of him before the law, would not the whole thing have been settled at a stroke? There would have been no "opening of the government at Nanjing," no such disgraceful farce of a sham court set up; the entire recorded history of the War of Resistance would have had to be rewritten.

After Wang set up his puppet regime, our operations against him still went on, and unhappily there were four more failed attempts. Every comrade taken was put heroically to death. The cruelty of these deaths is more than words can bear; and each time I think of it, my shame is past all bounds.
## Introduction to Renown Won in a Hundred Battles

Part Three: *Renown Won in a Hundred Battles*

In July 1939 a grave disaster befell the Shanghai working unit, and Mr. Dai Li took me up once again, sending me from Chongqing by back roads to Shanghai to clear away the wreckage. No sooner had I arrived than I was appointed chief of the Shanghai District. Because the times were extraordinary, and it was moreover a moment of changeover between old and new, the new crop not yet ripe while the old was spent, the only people I could make contact with were Zheng Xiuyuan, secretary of the District headquarters, and a bare three or four inside staff. Through this transitional stage the whole heavy burden may be said to have rested on Brother Xiuyuan's shoulders alone. Only after more than two months of reorganizing and refitting did things gradually return to normal, and the work step by step get under way.

The substance of this part is chiefly a recounting of the two years and more—from August 1939 until my own capture in October 1941—in which the comrades of the Shanghai District, every man doing his duty, showed the mightiest enemy-slaying power in their greatest deeds.

These two years were truly the glory days of the Shanghai District, which was the sharpest main force among all the behind-the-lines units under the Juntong. With its large organization, its gathering of talent, and ample material resources of funds and equipment, it grew correspondingly active in intelligence-gathering and in destruction-work, scoring one fine success after another. Against the bloody rule of the Japanese occupation army in the Shanghai area, against the lawless doings of the great traitors and archvillains of the puppet regime, and against those opportunists in society who had hidden strength and played both sides, it dealt severe blows each in turn. For a time the strong enemy quailed and the pack of traitors lost their nerve; it truly wielded an unprecedented power to overawe. Unseen, it heartened the people of the occupied zone, and strengthened too the conviction that the resistance must be won; and in the harvest of spiritual force, this was beyond all reckoning.

The showing of the Shanghai District—that is, the effect of the work during the War of Resistance—so far as it entered the record: setting aside the fruits of intelligence, turncoat inducement, and underground propaganda, the rough figures for direct action and sabotage alone are these:

Sanctions by direct action: over one hundred and fifty in all. Of these the greater part were traitors; a few were the high-ranking detectives of the various Concession police stations in Shanghai, who willingly served as the enemy's hawks and hounds and set themselves against our side. In addition, some forty-odd regular soldiers in Japanese uniform were killed by various means, the higher ranks among them running to major general and colonel.

Sabotage of military installations: over fifty in all. All of them airfields, warehouses, provisions, and munitions in the districts around Shanghai.

Among the sanctions by direct action, several were more notable than the rest—such as Fu Xiao'an, the puppet mayor of Shanghai, and Zhang Xiaolin, one of Shanghai's Three Tycoons, a boss of the underworld who was in secret communication with the enemy and the puppets.

Here one point needs particular explanation: the cases of killing and burning are nothing to boast of. Their great significance lies rather in this—that they displayed our national spirit of integrity and the might of the state, and served notice on the Japanese warlords that the Chinese nation would never, ever submit.

### The Other Two Books

Besides the three books spoken of above, there is much material that could be written; the fourth and fifth books, their titles not yet fixed, are meant to carry on the story of the grievous blow the Shanghai District of the Juntong suffered, and of how I myself came to be captured. One scene among them is the awkward moment when Wang Jingwei's wife, Chen Bijun, presided over my interrogation in person—to paint it out brings not only a blush to the face but an ache to the heart. Such a scene is more than ordinary feeling could ever imagine.

My capture, after several strange and tortuous turns before I was at last identified, became on the instant the hot news of all Shanghai and Nanjing and the whole occupied zone, and its ripples went on spreading for many days. I have kept to this day a copy of the *Xin Shen Bao* and half a copy of the *Zhonghua Ribao*, both published in Shanghai in 1941, and these too will be reproduced in the book for the reader to see.

Why did the Japanese military and the Wang puppet regime not kill me? This is a riddle many would like the answer to, and the book will give a full and exact account of it. Here I need only let slip one word: after my capture, the truth was not so simple a matter as merely "asking only to die," still less did I go "on my knees begging for mercy," base and witless as that would be.

Not long after, while still in confinement, I made a friend of Li Shiqun, boss of the "Special Operations Headquarters" of the Wang puppet regime. Did he win me over, or did I win him? An outsider may say what he likes; but I myself know that it was I who made use of his political ambition, and, working on his wavering unease and his cornered helplessness, lured him into accepting my grand design. After many secret talks, Li Shiqun and I reached a secret agreement and had already drawn up a plan: in the region of northern Jiangsu, on the pretext of reviewing the troops, we would seize the chance to abduct Wang Jingwei and "do one great deed." But the secret leaked—a man named Wan betrayed us. As a result the Japanese Kempeitai in Shanghai moved first, and through the Tokkō section chief, Lieutenant Colonel Okamura, had Li Shiqun poisoned to death; while Captain Nakajima Shin'ichi of the Japanese "Ume Kikan" contrived to shield me. Put this way it may seem I am spinning mysteries, hard to credit, and much at odds with the usual reports; but in truth every joint of it is buckled tight, with its own chain of cause and effect and of interest and harm. What actually happened, seen whether from a "political" or a "secret-service" point of view, was charged through and through with a fierce quality of "struggle."

Decades later, in December 1980, when I met in Taipei with Mr. Ōtsuka Kiyoshi, former Tokkō section chief of the Kempeitai command of the Japanese expeditionary army at Nanjing, I raised these old questions again, and Mr. Ōtsuka, from what he knew, supplied truthful and reliable answers. Their content will all be disclosed within the bounds of what Mr. Ōtsuka charged me to keep.

One more great and fortunate thing: when a gap opened in the watch and guard that the enemy and the puppets, both sides, kept over me, I restored my former working connection. My superiors sent the wireless comrade Zhang Yamin to Shanghai to set up a secret radio for my exclusive use, and to establish contact with Brother Mao Wanli, stationed at Yanshan in Jiangxi, the nearest point to Shanghai. But no sooner was this station set up than it was detected by the Japanese military, and once again there was a mishap. By good luck, this time, under the cover of the puppet forces, danger was turned aside.

The personal experiences set down in this book are complex and winding in the extreme—battles of wits and of strength, true and false, real and feigned, changing a thousand ways—and they run through many layers at once; yet as I set brush to paper I have held throughout to a mood of caution and dread, seeking only to be truthful, and would never dare to write loose nonsense at the run of the pen.

The foregoing sections have earnestly set out my motive in bringing out the book and the spirit in which it is written, and have presented in brief the main content of each volume.

### My View of Secret Service Work

Below, circling round the "meaning of secret service work," a thing seldom discussed in the open, I offer my own understanding, grasp, and experience of it, so as to lead the reader more easily toward the reality and into the picture.

"Secret service work"—*tewu gongzuo*—is called for short *tegong*; and the man who does secret service work is called a *tewu*, a "secret agent." The word *tewu* has left people with no good impression. The image commonly built up of him has a certain imported flavor, or is the sort one is used to seeing on the screen: a figure in a trench coat and a flat cap, a cigarette dangling from his lips or a pipe clenched in his teeth, furtive in movement and cold in expression. Or else a "cold-blooded killer," gun in hand and quick to gamble his life, whom everyone keeps at a wary distance. At the least, in the common notion, he is taken to be a man of crooked byways and ill omen.

In fact the basic nature of "secret service work" is purely political. To put it another way: any and all such activity that is divorced from politics is not the "secret service work" we are speaking of here. For example, when some private company or manufacturer, to reap commercial profit, goes about collecting the intelligence it needs—whatever the manner or means of its collecting—this does not count as "secret service work," the main reason being that it carries no "political" character. But if a state-run production unit or trading body, in order to compete in foreign trade against the Chinese mainland under Communist rule, goes about collecting the relevant intelligence, then this falls within the scope of "secret service work"—because, over and above safeguarding the national economic interest, it has a "political function."

The greatest use of "secret service work"—on the side of the party: it is the "vanguard" for gaining political power and making that power secure; on the side of the government: it is the "secret weapon" for defending the state and warding off foreign insult.

The distinguishing mark of "secret service work": it cannot exist on its own, nor will it exist in isolation; it is attached to a form of political life. Plainly put, "secret service work" is only a political instrument, and its gains and losses, its success and failure, are never a matter of the individual. Its spirit is therefore "for others," not "for self."

In the Second World War, Germany's "Gestapo," because of Hitler's dictatorship, was corrupted, and so sank into being the instrument of one man.

"Secret service work," mysterious though it is, will in the trend of the future grow more and more institutionalized. Today, in nearly every nation on earth, there exists the establishment and activity of "secret service work," listed too as a chief department of government. Hereafter its mystery is bound to fade by degrees.

In the past, and down to the present, our country has known many troubles, beset within and without, never a day at peace. A true "secret service worker" is, without exception, a man of virtue and high resolve who casts aside personal fame and gain and, with a breast full of ardent blood, pledges his very body to the country! He must have not only good cultivation, strict training, firm faith, and a spirit of sacrifice; more important still, he must possess that high and pure integrity which riches and honor cannot corrupt, poverty and low estate cannot swerve, power and force cannot bend. Only so can he be staunch beyond compare, brave danger and hardship, and, where great righteousness lies, feel no regret though he die. Well did Mr. Dai Li say: "Our glorious history is a thing wrought from the blood, the sweat, and the tears of our comrades!" This is indeed the plain truth, not one word false.

The word *tewu*—"special service"—evolved from "special mission," "special task," "special-category task," "special-category duty." When our government first formally founded an intelligence-training institution, it was called the "Special Service Police Training Class." Later there was also the "Special Police Training Class," and there was nothing improper in it; still less would anyone belittle himself on that account.

At bottom, it was rather the united-front activity of the Communists that deliberately twisted and blackened the word *tewu*. In the past, in our struggles against the Chinese Communists, whoever the Communists wished to strike at, no matter who, they branded a "Kuomintang secret agent"; and whenever they came off worse, they were sure to say they had been taken in by "Kuomintang secret agents." Even when the "Gang of Four" were struggling against Liu Shaoqi, Tao Zhu, and a whole run of other Communist chieftains, they likewise clapped on them the same cap of "Kuomintang secret agent." In the Kaohsiung violence of 1979, that pack of worthless, ignorant petty scoundrels also took over the Communists' shameless trick and called the intelligence and security men "Kuomintang secret agents" too—which only shows both that they are not up to the mark and that they have no common sense at all.

In recent years we ourselves have not often used the term "secret service work," but have changed it to "intelligence work." Work with a "protective" character is also called "intelligence and security work." Strictly speaking, to use "intelligence work" to cover intelligence, action, demolition, turncoat inducement, counter-espionage, psychological warfare, political warfare, commando raids, guerrilla operations, and all the other special duties is not comprehensive; and it is easily confused, too, with the plain intelligence business of merely "collecting," "processing," and "employing" intelligence. It is likely that no fitter name could be put forward, and so this one was adopted for want of a better. The United States, for instance, has a "Central Intelligence Agency," much the same in character, and it too uses this kind of name—though in fact the scope of its business is far more than the collecting of intelligence.

"Secret service work" or "intelligence work," it makes no difference: this is only a difference of name, and does not touch the great import of such work. One distinctive point needs noting: our country's intelligence work is not entirely the same as that of other countries; and in its division of duties it differs too from other lines. For the training and selection of a secret-service man must meet four great requirements, namely:

a sense of nation; political conviction; a spirit of sacrifice; hatred of the enemy.

As for ability and skill, it is not that they do not matter, but they are not the precondition. Of the four great requirements listed above, however, not one may be lacking.

This is no high-flown talk, still less preaching. A secret-service man who lacks the four requirements listed above may very likely sink into an "intelligence peddler" or a "professional thug"; or else, having entered this mysterious narrow gate, he will regret it his whole life long.

### Why "Sanction"?

Someone asks: "We have always spoken of rule by law; when a citizen breaks the law, there are statutes for it—why, without going through judicial process, must you use special means to sanction him?" This argument has of course its own firm and unshakable reason; but where, by reason of the local setting, judicial power cannot be exercised, or where, bound by the unequal treaties between nations, we hold no extraterritorial jurisdiction—there, to safeguard the national interest and protect the nation's security, we can by no means sit and watch a vermin of the state roam beyond the law's reach, leaning on foreign force, or even sheltering under the enemy's wing, to carry on his crimes of ruining the country and blighting the people; and so we have no choice but to meet the reality and resort to this.

For example, the Legation Quarter of Beiping was a foreign diplomatic district; the Japanese, French, and British Concessions of Tianjin; the French Concession and the International Settlement of Shanghai—in all of these we could exercise no judicial power. As for Hanoi, in French-ruled Vietnam of those earlier years, it was of course foreign soil, and needs no saying. Again, in wartime places like Beiping and Shanghai, fallen into enemy hands and controlled by the Japanese occupation army, many such districts lay beyond the reach of our government's writ; and just for that reason, those traitors to the country and dregs of the nation all made of these places their hotbed and their bolt-hole. So we were bound to take extraordinary means and root them out. In all the districts named above we did real work, and for this very purpose: to stop the source of the scourge and to uphold the law of the land.

For all that, we had our side of mercy beyond the law as well—in the working term, this is the pairing of "action" with "turncoat inducement." Unless a man was an archvillain and arch-scourge, guilty past all pardon, then so long as he could thoroughly awaken and bitterly mend his former wrongs of harming society and selling out the country for glory, there was of course room left him to "turn back." Of cases in the book of men who atoned by merit, who "turned resistance into help," there is no lack at all.

Friends chatting in private have also raised a delicate question: "Since it was for the country's sake to rid it of harm, why not do away with that demon Mao Zedong a little sooner?" It is a long story to tell, and this too is an old matter of thirty and more years ago.

From the end of 1948 into the beginning of 1949, when the two Communist armies of Lin Biao and Nie Rongzhen were closing in on Beiping, I myself was serving as commander of a certain special-operations unit of the national forces, waging "political warfare" against the Communist army across the three provinces of Hebei, Chahar, and Suiyuan, and also handling the "pacification work" of the recovered areas; at the same time I was charged with the responsibility of a secret intelligence unit as well. As the general situation turned against us and Beiping was plainly in peril, I chose in advance, by volunteer, from among the troops under my command, several dozen brave men, all of them fit for lying low, and "planted" them singly in Beiping, Tianjin, Chahar, and Suiyuan, to bear the hard mission of the time after the fall.

This work of "planting" won at the time the encouragement and support of Mr. Zheng Jiemin of the Second Department of the Ministry of National Defense and Mr. Mao Renfeng of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation. Thus we could obtain the issue of large quantities of wireless equipment of every kind and light arms, along with flour, rice, working funds, and the like—all meant for the needs of a long and bitter struggle to come.

The meaning of the "planting work" was not only to gather intelligence; its greatest aim was to meet a sudden turn, to make opportunities, to assassinate the bandit chief Mao and the other important leaders. Could it have produced the looked-for effect according to plan, it might well have been a surprise force able to achieve an exploit without peer. But this "planting" work, its foundations frail and its roots not deep, unhappily came to grief one unit after another in the two or three years after Beiping fell. From what the Chinese Communists let out in their open broadcasts and secret communications, all those planted men of high resolve met deaths so heroic and so cruel that, set beside the years of resistance against Japan, they wring the heart and sting the eyes the more. Of these many deeds the fifth book will go further into the how and why.

The "sanction by direct action" spoken of in the book naturally takes men for its target, and what it records are the true accounts of men of high resolve who braved death to cut down traitors. And so there arises, bound up with it, a question of the "power of life and death."

An old gentleman once put the doubt to me: "How can you kill people as you please?" The question came abruptly, and may be one that outsiders in general share. In fact, how should we dare do it "as we please"? Even within the set bounds and procedures, we might not be off by a hair.

Take first the man in charge of the work in a given district: in every case of sanction, it was either carried out on orders, or the item was submitted for instruction and sanctioned by approval; there was absolutely no power to kill whom one wished to kill.

The commander on the scene, too, could only reconnoiter the target designated by his superiors and act as the moment allowed. If on the spot there arose some obstacle or hindrance blocking the work, then in principle it must be cleared at once—yet one had also to weigh the actual case and make the decision. Say it was plainly one of the other side's guards, a bodyguard or escort: that you must deal with him, since if you did not deal with him he would deal with you, goes without saying. But if it was some innocent passerby who by chance blocked the way, or shielded the line of fire, and hindered the carrying-out of the mission, then better to miss the opening—whatever else, one could not just up and shoot!

Many too misunderstand and suppose that Mr. Dai Li must have held the power of life and death. To this question the answer can be given for certain: "He held none, absolutely none!" He was the leader of this part of our work, and the executor of matters bearing on state policy; his every measure had its rule to follow, and was purely rational. That he had rather more chance to advise those above him is a fact; but it was by no means the "high-handedness" that outsiders rumor and guess at.

Let me give one more example. During the war we had a senior comrade—he was the former Shanghai District chief whom I succeeded—who, for some reason I do not know, killed a man in Shaanxi; and though it too was on public business, it had not been sanctioned by approval beforehand, and in the end he was found guilty of breaking the law and sentenced to death. This is one of the plainest instances. So strict was the discipline—who would dare kill at will!

Again, action work was mostly carried out in haste, under emergency conditions. Mistakes could of course hardly be avoided, and so there came about the cases of "wronged ghosts under the gun" and of "striking the wrong carriage." In the work I took part in and led, this happened several times over. For instance, in 1934 in Tianjin, we had our sights clearly on Ji Hongchang, yet it was Liu Shaorang who died; in 1938 in Beiping, the target of sanction was Wang Kemin, yet the one who died in his stead was the Japanese adviser Yamamoto Eiji; in 1939 in Hanoi, the target was Wang Jingwei alone, yet the one mortally struck was Zeng Zhongming. In such cases, some were sheer accident; some, strange turns of circumstance; some, perhaps, errors of reconnaissance. In all these situations, though no direct responsibility attached, the man in charge could not escape an administrative penalty—a warning, a demerit, and the like. From this one may know that in any case not sanctioned by approval beforehand, one was of course not permitted to act on one's own judgment; and even outside a fixed target, to get it wrong was not allowed either. The work of sanction by direct action was never a thing of "acting as one pleased" on one's own authority.

### The Chinese Model of the Secret Agent

One point especially to be stressed is this: in order to honor our country's inherent moral tradition and to accord with the spirit on which our state was founded, the Chinese model of "secret service work" has a set of moral norms of its own, suited to the national character.

In many writings I have seen, and from the talk of some "old hands" I have heard, that in the secret-service activity of every country in the world, democratic or totalitarian alike, there are forever cases—one shudders to raise them—of using any means whatever: borrowing the enemy's knife to kill one's own; forcing one's own men to take their lives to manufacture a pretext; killing one's own to silence them, in hope that the dead may leave no witness; shutting a healthy man in a madhouse and insisting he has lost his mind; for the sake of dispelling a suspicion, with no regard for human life, striking first oneself to make an end of it; or, because a man's usefulness is spent, cooking the hound once the hare is caught—all of these cruel deeds that violate moral norms, and a reflection, too, of imbecile shamelessness. Such things we have never been willing to do, and would scorn to do!

We are an ancient and civilized country, a land of benevolence and righteousness, and what shows in our "secret-service" work bears the same supremely high moral standard. Therefore, even where it would profit the work at hand, we would never take such inhuman and base means! In the past, too, there have been instances of men willing to sacrifice themselves to gain a working result; their resolve was admirable, yet it was gently declined by their superiors.

Our work was carried on under the lead of political faith and patriotic feeling, winning through by wit, courage, technique, and ability. In the drawing-up of an action plan, "accomplishing the mission" and "withdrawing in safety" were of equal weight; and though at times one might have to gamble all in a desperate fight, there was still the chance of survival, of "you die" and "I live."

Writing to this point, I am reminded of an affair of thirty years ago. A certain great Western power, making much of human rights, professing cooperation and a wish jointly to oppose communism, insisted—its preparations beforehand insufficient, the ground on the spot ill understood, the conditions for survival mostly wanting, its staff work built on guesswork—on air-dropping our working comrades one by one into the mainland. Our comrades showed courage beyond compare, yet in the end nothing was gained, and many lives were thrown away for nothing! To treat human life as so much straw, and squander for no return the bright futures of these promising young men, was an outrage past forgiving! Several of these comrades were my brothers-in-arms on one front in the North China fighting against the Communists; and what grieves one the more, after their deaths even their pensions were left hanging, with no kin to claim them.

Next I must speak of the questions of "living conditions" and "social standing."

Outsiders mostly suppose that secret-service men not only draw generous pay but have found the "Zhongnan shortcut" to officialdom besides. Why else would some people be forever trying to "worm" their way in? In fact, only after they have truly got in do they come to taste the bitterness within the sweet.

As for living conditions, from the early days down to now—that is, over the whole long stretch from 1932 to 1980—whether at the "head" or the "tail," a secret-service man drew never more than an ordinary bank clerk. Even when ordered to carry out a raid at the risk of his life, all the funds he received did not come to as much as government officials spend on a single trip abroad to "observe conditions." The facts are all there; I do not talk wildly.

As for promotion in rank: before the government departments had firmly established a personnel system, few set store by seniority papers—the more so since men working in the occupied zone or behind the lines took no photographs, printed no name cards, cut no private seals, and did not even use their real names, and so of course could not possibly hold or keep those documents of "proof of identity." When later, without such things, no appraisal of rank could be processed, they could produce no papers at all; and to complete the formalities of appointment they had even to go about kowtowing and bowing to beg on the spot for proof. Often, swallowing much to make things do, they ended with name and reality out of joint.

To go back further still: from the founding of the work down to now, of the whole system belonging to the "Juntong," inside staff and field staff together, the officers came at most to a little over three hundred thousand. Of these, only one man—Mr. Zheng Jiemin—rose in his lifetime to full general. Next, those able to reach the rank of lieutenant general numbered but a handful.

Even so, over all these years one has never seen anyone haggle over it. One may see that a man who has given himself to secret-service work counts this a small thing, having long since held fame and gain lightly. What can truly bring him satisfaction should be a sacrifice, an offering, made for country and nation, and the accomplishing of a mission.

### A Historical Record for the Nameless Heroes

Last of all, to speak of myself. I have lived the greater part of my life amid raging waves and startling billows. Though for years on end, day and night, I was constantly in a state of tension, I never once managed the composure of keeping calm through change. Even to this day, whenever I hear a rap-rap at the door or the ring-ring of the telephone—knowing full well that no one will come to arrest me again, nor any ill tidings of the work reach me—I still cannot help a start, as if it had become a habit that no amount of correcting will correct.

Over these decades of the work's course, there is truly nothing to speak of by way of achievement—at most I made some news material that clamored for a moment and soon sank into silence. And if one is to weigh merit and fault, right and wrong, that depends on the angle from which one measures.

Most fortunate of all, as by a miracle, I have actually lived to seventy, and can still go on living, hale and hearty and at ease, in a free world—this is truly Heaven's grace! Yet in the fortunes of a life there is luck and there is ill luck; and though I was singled out for "Heaven's favor," when I look round on the comrades-in-arms who once shared hardship and life and death with me, some met a martyr's death; some died with their aims unfulfilled; some are gone none knows where; and of the few who remain, the sole surviving fruit, I watch them "wither with age," one by one.

For just this reason I should cherish the years that are left, reckoning neither honor nor disgrace, heeding neither praise nor blame, and, doing all I can and taking pains for the truth, write down this book, *Nameless Heroes*—to vent a breath for those who gave themselves to secret-service work, and to console the spirits in heaven of the nameless heroes who died for their country. More than that, I would wish it to offer corroboration for the recorded history of the resistance and of the suppression of the rebellion.
## Prefatory Note

Born of the needs of the age, under the two great shocks of the September 18th Incident and the January 28th affair, in March 1932 there came into being, as the times demanded, the "Society for Vigorous Practice of the Three Principles of the People," with graduates of the Whampoa Military Academy for its backbone.

One of the important departments under the Lixingshe was the "Special Services Department," directed by Mr. Dai Li (courtesy name Yunong).

The Special Services Department opened a training class to cultivate working cadres—the earliest institution in our country to take even rough shape as a school for secret-service men.

In September 1932 the Military Affairs Commission of the National Government set up a "Bureau of Investigation and Statistics," and appointed Dai Li chief of its "Second Department."

In its nature, the "Special Services Department" under the Lixingshe should be reckoned a political secret organization; the "Second Department" under the Juntong was a formal organ of government. On the surface they were two units, each with its own line of subordination; but in reality they were two faces of one body leading the secret service work.

I myself was a member of the "Revolutionary Soldiers' Comrades Association," a second-tier body under the leadership of the Lixingshe, and at the same time the officer stationed at Beiping for both the "Special Services Department" and the "Second Department."

The working unit in Beiping, called the "Beiping Station," began at the end of 1932, and was built up—through thorns and brambles—by a few of us green striplings newly come into the world.

This section, besides a simple account in outline of the founding of the Juntong, holds many secret tales as well—things I falter to tell, yet that are precious all the same.
