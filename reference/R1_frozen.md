## Section 1. A Heavy Charge, Pressing Onward

### 1. A Small Gathering of Classmates: First Acquaintance with Dai Yunong

All this happened fifty years ago.

In the spring of 1931 the Commandant, Mr. Chiang, at his official residence at the Xianglin Temple in Nanjing, received us in batches—more than a hundred academy classmates newly returned from posts all over the country—for individual talks.

Since finishing enlisted training, graduating, and receiving my first posting, apart from the mass exhortations and the roll-calls in ranks, this was the first time the Commandant had spoken with me face to face and alone in his office. On that occasion it was Zeng Kuoqing, of the first class, who made the introduction.

The Commandant wore a Zhongshan tunic and sat serenely in the chair behind his desk, on which lay a roster copied out on ten-column paper; in his hand he held a thick two-colored pencil, red and blue. As I marched up to the desk and came to a halt, bowed, and raised my eyes to look at him, what struck me most deeply was that the Commandant's eyes were bright and piercing, his bearing awe-inspiring and stern, so that no one dared look him full in the face.

The Commandant first asked after such family matters as my native province and county, whether my parents were living or dead, how many brothers I had; asking and listening as he went, and checking the roster against my answers. Then he asked what work interested me, and I answered boldly and from the heart: "I have grown rusty in the military work of the troops; I should be glad to learn work of any other kind." I thought these words would anger him, but I saw the Commandant make a mark on the roster, with no sign of displeasure.

This audience lasted, from first to last, about three minutes, and he put no more than three or four questions to me; the process being so simple, I felt nothing special at the time. Thinking it over afterward, I guessed that the Commandant was perhaps looking me over and hearing me out, making a selection through observation; as to his standard, that only the old gentleman himself would know.

Three or four days later word came that, out of more than a hundred of us, fourteen had been marked out to open a class and undergo training. On the list my name stood last.

This small training unit was named the "Central Military Academy Special Research Class," with Feng Ti, director of the academy's political department, serving concurrently as its director, and Shen Zunhui as its secretary. Zhang Zhizhong, then the education chief of the Central Military Academy, seemed to take no part in the matter.

This training class was styled "Special," and in fact it truly was different from the ordinary run of training bodies. First, it had no premises of its own: making do as best we could, we set a few single-drawer long tables in one office of the academy's political department, two men to a wooden bench, and so we managed. Second, no uniform was prescribed; a Zhongshan tunic or a Western suit would do, so long as you did not wear a long gown and short jacket. Because we wore no uniform, there was no military discipline either. Beyond that, there was no fixed timetable of work and rest, no need to come and go by the clock; coming late or leaving early was never interfered with, so that the academy authorities, you could say, granted us a high degree of self-government—and just because of that, we too knew well enough to conduct ourselves with self-respect. As for the courses, apart from specially engaged lectures, there was ordinarily no instructor to teach us.

The courses we studied were several books the Commandant himself had prescribed, such as the *Complete Works of Lu Xiangshan*, the *Complete Works of Wang Yangming*, the *Complete Works of Zeng Wenzheng*, Qi Jiguang's *Records on the Governance of Troops*, and *The Theoretical System of the Three Principles of the People*. After reading them on our own, we had to write up what we had gained into notes; these, once gathered, were submitted once a week to the Commandant for his review, and came back marked, often with comments in the margins.

Of these vast and profound works of Confucian learning, not even a third of us fourteen could truly read them "through," and I was not among them.

During the training there was, however, one thing laid down as a hard rule: that, except on Sundays, we must at noon gather for a common meal in the academy's "Gong-Character Mess Hall." The Gong-Character Mess Hall was in those days a new-style building that could hold several hundred; its arrangement was neat, its cleaning spotless, the rows of dining tables making a straight line whether you looked across them or along them, each in its proper place. The tables were spread with white cloth, and the tableware too was uniformly white porcelain; if you wanted to find fault, perhaps the one thing wanting was a vase of bright fresh flowers. Because the building was shaped like the character *gong* (工), it was called the Gong-Character Mess Hall.

The Commandant would arrive at about five minutes past twelve; first the duty officer would call the commands—all rise, be seated—and we would answer, "Begin," and everyone fell to together. Though every man was careful to make no sound, there was still, now and then, the unavoidable clink of tableware knocking together.

The meal took about ten minutes; one by one everyone laid down his bowl and chopsticks and tidied things up, sat straight in his place, drew up his chest, and listened respectfully to the Commandant's exhortation on matters of the spirit.

Before the Commandant stood a seating chart bearing our names, and whenever he had finished speaking he would now and then call on someone by name to answer a question. Those who could speak freely and reply without a stumble were not often seen. Everyone was quietly anxious, dreading to be called on and made a fool of on the spot; yet there were also those itching to try.

In the second month after the class opened, at the urging of the senior elder brothers, we fourteen put our names to a report requesting the Commandant to permit the restoration of our party membership and academy enrollment—for at that time all fourteen of us had, each for a different reason, had our party membership and academy enrollment struck off. The Commandant wrote his ruling with a brush: "The restoration of party membership and academy enrollment is permitted," and signed it "Zhongzheng." The date was around June 1931. The original was at first kept by Han Jun (Zhongjin) of the first class. Before long the Central Military Academy Special Party Branch issued a party card of the "junyu" series; and separately, senior brother Tian Zailong, in charge of the "Investigation Section for Military Academy Graduates," notified us that the restoration would be "annotated" in the graduate registers of our several classes.

Of this ruling of the Commandant's, all fourteen of us kept a photographic copy. The one I held has, over these several decades of running about north and south, east and west, ended up who knows where. Thinking it over carefully, I believe that, for reasons of secrecy, it was most likely destroyed by my own hand.

Of us fourteen I was the youngest in years and the lowest in class; among us were seven senior elder brothers of the first class—Han Jun, Chen Lie, Huang Yong, Yu Yong, Wu Naixian, Xu Huizhi, and Liu Jiwen; two of the second class, Zhang Yanyuan and Cao Xu; none of the third class; one only of the fourth, Zheng Sikang; and four in all of the fifth class: Han Jiwen, Xia Dakang, Xie Juecheng, and the present writer.

The monthly living allowance the fourteen of us drew was not the same, but was granted according to class in descending order: the first class eighty yuan, the second seventy, and so on down to the fifth at only forty. In truth, forty yuan in those days, for a single man with no family in tow, ought to have been quite enough.

Those with families all lived outside; we few bachelors lived in the academy staff dormitory, two men assigned to a room, and I roomed with Xie Juecheng. Xie was gifted at song and recitation, with an unusually strong literary streak; he would not read the prescribed books, but sprawled all day over his desk writing new-style verse, and when the mood took him would declaim a few lines aloud—so that at the time he was reckoned an odd fellow.

By the fourth month of training, two of our classmates suddenly vanished. At first we supposed they had taken personal leave and would be back in a few days, but, as it turned out, two whole months passed, we all graduated and left, and we never saw them again.—Both were Cantonese: one was Wu Naixian, the first-class elder brother; the other was Han Jiwen, a fifth-class classmate, also called Shangying, who had shared a table with me during the training.

At the time no one paid the matter any special heed, nor did the classmates discuss it. Only years afterward did we learn that they had gone to Shanghai and carried through a great and secret affair. Since it is called "secret," though the years are long gone by and things have changed, it is still best to keep it so. Yet this much may be said: the affair was one of grave moment, and it holds a page too in the history of political-party activity; only it is a pity that the few who knew the most of it have all passed away.

To speak now of what befell the twelve of us:

When the six-month training was up, just after the September 18th Incident, the Commandant, Mr. Chiang, by his own hand directed that the twelve of us report to the Party Central. Having reported, we waited a long while, but nothing came of it, and not one of us was assigned any work. For what reason, I do not know to this day. There was nothing for us to do but part there, each to go his own way.

I had no social connections to speak of, and just when I stood at a loss, no way forward and no way back, the two senior elder brothers Huang Yong (Jianqiu) and Zhang Yanyuan (Binghua) took me kindly under their wing and had me move in with them. Neither had brought family; they had sublet a room on an upper floor of a house in Zhongyi Lane in Huapailou, already furnished. At the time the two elder brothers slept in a great iron bed hung with a mosquito-net, while I, alone, laid a pallet on the floor at night and rolled up the bedding again in the morning.

The three of us were seldom together; but sometimes we would buy a little fish and meat, cook it on the pump-stove, and the three of us would dig in around the table—and how good it tasted. Years later, whenever we met and spoke of those days, we could still taste it.

One afternoon a guest came calling. He first shook hands and exchanged greetings with Jianqiu and Binghua, and then, with a smile, held out the hand of friendship to me as well. Brother Jianqiu hurried to introduce us to each other: "This little brother is Chen Gongshu, of the fifth class; this elder brother is Dai Li, Dai Yunong, of the sixth class." That the fifth class should be called "little brother" and the sixth "elder brother"—there was something odd in this form of introduction.

To look at, he was a good deal older than I: heavy brows and large eyes, a high-bridged nose and prominent cheekbones; though not tall, he seemed very solid and firmly built, a man you would call uncommon in feature and extraordinary in bearing. This first impression was a strong one; as to what he made of me, there was no telling.

When classmates gather, the talk flows without ceremony. Though all four of us spoke ordinary Mandarin, brother Huang Jianqiu was from Pingjiang in Hunan, brother Zhang Binghua from Meixian in Guangdong; and the guest, Dai Yunong, from Jiangshan in Zhejiang—we all spoke with rather thick regional burrs, yet the talk went so well that, if the light had not begun to fail, we would never have noticed the sun already sinking in the west.

Brother Jianqiu proposed that we all go out for a bite. Not far beyond Zhongyi Lane there was a small local eating-house, by the name of the Yingfeng Tavern, modest in its scale, only a single storey over a ground floor. We ordered a few dishes and called for a pot of wine, and the whole came to less than two yuan; everyone thought it not bad at all.

From then on Dai Yunong often dropped in to sit with us, and now and then we would go together to see a film. One day, as we were playing mahjong with the landlady, Dai Yunong came again; seeing that I was losing, with all the air of a knight drawing his sword to the rescue, he told me to stand up, and played a few hands in my stead—but from the way he drew and discarded tiles, he seemed no old hand at the game.

I once asked brothers Jianqiu and Binghua what work this classmate Dai did. They told me they knew only that he was at the Commandant's residence, but were not clear what post he held. I pressed on, puzzled: if he served at the residence, why, when the Commandant had just now stepped down and returned to Fenghua, had he not gone along? The two elder brothers gave me no straight answer, only mumbled a vague "hm" and let it go.

As we came to know one another better, and our acquaintance deepened not a little, Dai Yunong once asked me out of the blue: "Brother Gongshu, do you know a man named Cao Xiaoqing?" I answered: "Know him! We did our enlisted training in the same company, were in the same platoon and the same squad, and slept the one over the other in a double bunk. He'd graduated from Meiji University in Japan and I never got past middle school, yet somehow the two of us became fast friends; he could not even wind his puttees properly, and I used to tease him for it."

I paused a moment and then added: "Since he went up into the political science course of this academy with the fourth class, and I, falling behind through illness, was put into the infantry course of the fifth—we parted, and I have never seen him since."

Dai Yunong said, in dead earnest: "And if Cao Xiaoqing were in Nanjing at this moment?" I said, "Then I'll go and see him at once." And then I asked back: "Has he really come to Nanjing?" Dai Yunong shook his head and said, "I only hope he might come to Nanjing."

Dai Yunong's question quite bewildered me; only later did he tell me that Cao Xiaoqing was then in Shanghai, attached to Hu Hanmin and working up a "New Kuomintang," engaged in divisive activity against the government.

Later still I came to see that Dai Yunong's sudden mention of Cao Xiaoqing had in it something of a sounding-out, or a test.

Before long the Commandant returned to the capital to take up his heavy charge, and the times Dai Yunong met with us grew fewer, and each visit shorter.

Brother Jianqiu and I went on as before, but Zhang Binghua showed himself uncommonly busy, sometimes not coming home the whole night, and even when he did come back, his rest was cut short, and in no time he was off again. I guessed that brother Binghua was very likely taking part in some important and secret work.

This was just the time of the "January 28th," when the Japanese invaders pushed into Shanghai and our army fought back with valor.

Around this time, whenever Dai Yunong met with us, he would remark: "If you have a report for the Commandant, hand it to me and I'll pass it up—that's the quicker way." Each time I answered: "I have no request to make, nor any memorial to offer; I dare not trouble the Commandant." And saying it, I felt I had struck just the right note. In truth I was too thick-witted: the "report" he meant was not so narrow a thing as I imagined, and it is a pity that at the time I could not take his meaning.

When the "January 28th" affair drew to a close, the National Government moved to Luoyang, the Commandant, Mr. Chiang, took up quarters at Xuzhou, and Dai Yunong stayed on in Nanjing.

One day Dai Yunong came looking for me, a large, heavy, bulging envelope in his hand, sealed with wax on the back. He said he was "entrusting" me to make a special trip to Xuzhou without delay and deliver it in person to the aide-de-camp, Dai Songyi.

The Commandant, I had heard, was quartered at Xuzhou; if Dai Yunong wanted me to go there and hand it in person to the aide Dai, its importance was plain to see. Without a second thought I agreed. He gave me two travel warrants and a little for the road, and before I set out told me further: "There is a special train standing at Xuzhou station, with armed guards near it, and plainclothes guards too—you are sure to spot them, I think; ask anyone, and you will find this aide Dai. Once the letter is delivered, just have him sign for it."

That very night I reached Xuzhou, and before I had even left the platform I found the aide Dai Songyi. I handed the wax-sealed envelope to him in person; he turned it over and over, took out a name card and wrote a few words on it by way of a receipt. He gave me no spoken instructions, and simply sent me back.

Looking at the card, I learned that his rank was lieutenant colonel and that he was from Yunnan; on inquiring later, I found he too was an academy classmate.

Back in Nanjing, I went for the first time to the Dai residence at No. 53 Ji'e Lane. It was a not-very-tidy one-storey house; once inside the door, you turned this way and that through several crooked bends before reaching the parlor. In a well-appointed household the floor would be spread with carpet; his was spread with straw matting. The floor was damp, and there was nothing to be done about it; he was making do on very little.

I gave Dai Yunong the card that served as a receipt, and so completed his "commission." Seeing that he was busy, I was about to take my leave when he suddenly asked me: "Have you any close friend among the classmates from Fujian province?" I thought a moment and hit on Lian Mou, a fourth-class classmate and a good friend, also known as Lian Liangshun. I answered that there was such a man, first-rate in both ability and appearance; but I did not know what he wanted a Fujian man for. Dai Yunong gave no straight answer, only asked that I introduce them as soon as I could.

I introduced them, and they met.

Dai Yunong sent Lian Mou to Fuzhou to set up a working station—of which at the time I knew nothing, nor did brother Liangshun say goodbye to me before he left.

Judging from these two matters, where lay Dai Yunong's mission? One might guess well over half of it.

In March I moved from Zhongyi Lane to Mingwalang, living alone in a small inn. Brother Binghua had already been sent to Guangdong—or, by another account, to Hong Kong—and with him went Wu Jinfu (Naixian). When we parted, though we did not say aloud what we were going to do, we already understood each other in our hearts.

Brother Jianqiu, too, brought his good lady over and rented another house. He had made it clear to me that he did not mean to join the work Dai Yunong led, but would rather stand in a supporting role; and so indeed it proved.

Mingwalang is very near Sandaogaojing, and the Military Academy Alumni Association stood on that very street of Sandaogaojing. With this for a hub, the small inns of the surrounding quarter housed a good many academy classmates—some waiting for work, some seeing to their registration—all mixed together in a great bustle. My own moving to Mingwalang was neither to seek work nor to register, but entirely at Dai Yunong's wish: he wanted me to keep a closer eye on the classmates' doings, and above all on any activity out of the ordinary run, beyond the bounds. Seen simply, it was a small matter he had entrusted to me; put another way, it was as good as assigning me a mission. I did not quite grasp that this was what is called "intelligence work." But the one thing I remember most clearly is that he never paid me any wage. In other words, I was still in the walk-on stage.

By the common accounts, at the end of February 1931 Dai Yunong had already been marked out as head of the Special Services Department. This Special Services Department was no organ of government, but came under the newly formed secret revolutionary organization, the Society for Vigorous Practice of the Three Principles of the People. After a period of preparation, it was formally established in Nanjing on April 1; and so the Juntong afterward set "April First" as its founding anniversary.

Dai Yunong perished in an air crash on March 17, 1946. And so the "April First commemoration" was moved and merged into "March 17th," known in short as the "March 17th Commemorative Assembly." The March 17th Assembly was held year after year, and after the move to Taiwan it went on without a break; while the late President lived, he came in person to speak each year. It is indeed a day worth commemorating.

Looking back, in that season of our first acquaintance Dai Yunong already bore a mission of "liaison."

Whether there was at that time a unit called the "Liaison Group," I cannot say. Only in the *Chronicle of Mr. Dai Yunong* is it recorded: "In December 1931, in order to bring about unity and meet the national peril together, Mr. Chiang resigned his post as chairman of the National Government and other offices and left the capital for his native place. Before setting out he ordered Mr. Dai Li to take charge of liaison, the chief task being to unite the revolutionary forces centered on the Whampoa students, to reconnoiter the enemy's movements, and to suppress the reactionaries. Thereupon organizations were set up and active work carried on in Nanjing, Shanghai, Hangzhou, Beiping, Tianjin, Hankou, Hong Kong, Guangzhou, Jiangxi, and other places."

From this it is clear that Dai Yunong then bore a mission of "liaison"; that is beyond doubt. But the so-called "Liaison Group" was probably no formal establishment, only a name used for the time being to stand for this line of work.

As for the statement that, in December 1931, organizations were already "set up and active work carried on in Nanjing, Shanghai, Hangzhou, Beiping, Tianjin, Hankou, Hong Kong, Guangzhou, Jiangxi, and other places"—this too is not wholly so. I cannot speak for other places; but I was myself the founder of the work in Beiping, and I was not sent there until November 1932.

This is one proof of how very hard it is to write history.

Of Dai Yunong's Special Services Department there are many tales; for his class-standing and position among the senior cadres of the Lixingshe were both below those of others, so how did this heavy charge come to be laid on him? By Dai Yunong's own account:

"At eight in the evening on 26 February 1932, in the Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum park at Nanjing, I received from the Leader the order to establish the Special Services Department. The Leader supported me, and I of course bore absolute responsibility; but on the morning of the next day a certain comrade—now gone abroad, whose name I had better not speak—came to pull me down. He said, 'As for the personnel and funds of the Special Services Department, the Commandant has told him to take charge of them.' The next day I reported to the Leader and asked to resign; in the end it was not allowed, and a full year later the Leader told me that there were still people coming to attack me. …"

From this passage it is plain that he owed the Special Services Department post entirely to the support and favor of the Commandant, Mr. Chiang. And that was because he already had behind him a record of distinguished work.

Setting aside the deeds already told in the *Complete Works of Mr. Dai Yunong*, the *Chronicle of Mr. Dai Yunong*, the *Life of Dai Li*, and other writings—which I shall not repeat—an old man more than ten years my senior, who would not let me give his name, told me a great deal about Mr. Dai Yunong's conduct. Since this is hearsay, I set it down for what it is worth, as anecdotes of Mr. Dai's unrecorded history—or perhaps as some corroboration of his earlier working career.

Early on, between 1928 and 1930, Mr. Dai Yunong, Mr. Tian Zailong, Mr. Wang Tianmu, Mr. Hu Baoyi, and others had organized in Kaifeng, Henan, a "Three People's Principles Great Chivalry Corps," which on the one hand recruited patriots of high resolve, and on the other served as a feeler for intelligence.

Again, one year, the black-hearted Feng Yuxiang paid out money to hire a band of desperadoes—Middle Easterners who called themselves Syrians—and slipped them into Nanjing to carry out an assassination; Dai Yunong got word of it beforehand and broke up the plot.

Again, in Shanghai, a set of secret-society men, meaning to pick a quarrel on some pretext, put terms to the authorities and talked loudly of "the cut," threatening this and that if they were refused; and it was Dai Yunong and his men who came forward and smoothed it over.

Mr. Dai Yunong never made a show of himself; of such matters as those set out above there must be many more, and I never once heard him bring them up. What I know, or rather what I understand, is that Dai Yunong's combative spirit was strong: he meant to overcome every adversity; yet he was not stubborn, and while holding fast to principle he had a superhuman knack of adapting to any circumstance. As for his knowing men and using them well, everyone with any knowledge of him knew it; and—here is what set him beyond the common run—many a famous "iron man" of the day, once under his hand, would turn soft enough to wind round a finger.

### 2. Drawn into the "Military Association" and Called Up for "Special Training"

I was in the little inn at Mingwalang, head down and alone over a manuscript, when a man I had never met, who gave his surname as Pan, came to talk with me—what an abrupt thing that was. Without asking my leave, he struck up a conversation, ranging from my family circumstances to my ideological schooling, and from the state of the nation to the international situation; and he said, in fact, more than I answered. Turn it over as I might, I truly could not make out what he was driving at.

As this uninvited guest was leaving, he left me word that in a few days someone else would come to talk with me again.

After he had gone, the more I thought about it, the less it sat right with me: since I did not understand where he came from, why had I answered his questions so readily and without demur? Had I simply been cowed by that irresistible momentum of his? Or had I sensed all along that he was one of our own, sent on orders?

Two days later, sure enough, someone came again to talk with me—this time it was fellow student Yi, of my own class, whom I had met before but had little to do with. He beat about the bush no longer, but said outright: "Now a political organization full of new life has been born, one may say a product of a great age: whole-heartedly upholding our Commandant as Leader, with the academy classmates for its backbone, practicing vigorously the Three Principles of the People, reviving the revolutionary spirit, restoring Chinese culture, sacrificing personal interest, with an iron will and strict discipline, obeying the organization's decisions, and, under the Commandant's brilliant leadership, opening up a new order together."

He asked me: "Would you be willing to join such an organization?" In my heart I thought: "I had meant all along to gather comrades and get up just such an organization; but I am of small account and my word carries no weight—I am simply not qualified, and in any case I should not dare to propose it openly." As I was thinking so, he pressed me again: "Have you any further considerations?" And so I put my own question back to him: "That comrade Pan who talked with me a few days ago—was he sent by this organization?" Fellow student Yi told me: "Yes; this is one of the procedures by which the organization takes in comrades, and may be explained as a necessary step. But a visit so sudden as that easily leaves the other party at a loss, and may even bring on needless misunderstanding; it seems it ought to be improved."

Toward the end I asked him, naively: "Why was I singled out?" He answered with a smile: "Someone recommended you, I suppose." I asked again: "Can you tell me who?" But he only smiled and did not answer, and before he left he hinted that I had passed this test too.

What forms I filled out and what procedures I went through—or whether the record of their talks with me took their place—I can no longer remember. Within a few days word came, and I knew I had indeed been "taken in" as a comrade of the Revolutionary Soldiers' Comrades Association. At the time, to become a member of a secret revolutionary organization naturally brought a certain secret pride, for I was now a mainstay of the revolutionary camp.

At that time, and even for some while after, I had no notion at all that above the Revolutionary Soldiers' Comrades Association there was a core organization—the Society for Vigorous Practice of the Three Principles of the People; I understood only that, besides the Revolutionary Soldiers' Comrades Association (the "Military Association" for short), there was a sister organization parallel to it, the Revolutionary Youth Comrades Association (the "Youth Association" for short). In plain terms, I had been taken in only at the second tier, still on the "periphery" of the core.

Not long after I joined—within perhaps three or five days—Dai Yunong "called me up" to undergo secret-service training, so that I never did take part in the organizational activity of the Military Association—such as its small-group meetings.

Most puzzling of all was that the secret-service training ran a full six months, and yet the Military Association never once made contact with me. I did not understand: could it be that this "training class" had no connection at all with that "revolutionary body"? By rights, though their natures differed, there should surely have been some link. Who would have guessed there was a deep mystery in it—a point I shall account for later in the course of events.

In 1979 I read in a magazine this passage: "Among the various training bodies run by the Lixingshe was an 'Intelligence Personnel Training Class,' with Dai Li as director, established at the end of March 1932; the trainees were all selected by examination from graduates of the various classes of the Military Academy, and from persons of every walk with an aptitude for intelligence. To meet the Lixingshe's needs, it gathered and drew on choice teaching materials, instructors, and cadre officers from China, Japan, Germany, Russia, Britain, and America, and gave a strict, secret, short-term training in intelligence business."

This piece, written by a senior cadre of the Lixingshe, proves one point: that between the Lixingshe, its peripheral organization the Revolutionary Soldiers' Comrades Association, and the "secret-service training body" there was a definite relation of subordination, of principal and follower. In other words, this "training class" was run by the Lixingshe.

But the several points about the intelligence training class raised in that piece still differ greatly from the actual state of things as I lived them. And that is no surprise, for even a hair's difference of standpoint can breed a great divergence. I shall set out the differences below, point by point.

At that time, in 1932, though I was a comrade of the Military Association, what I knew was no more than this much. Beyond it, half was rumor and half surmise, and none of that can count. The greatest surprise of all was that I never dreamed Dai Yunong held so high a position in this organization.

The long passage below sets down in detail how I answered the call-up and underwent the training:

In May 1932 Dai Yunong sought me out; without first asking my consent, he seemed already certain that I was bound to agree to his request. Neither explaining nor accounting for it, he asked me to seek out, among the academy classmates of the fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh classes, thirty men to undergo a special training of no very long term. He added one further point: it would be best if, within a week, I furnished him a list with brief personal particulars attached, for his use.

In only three days, in the small inns around the "Investigation Section for Military Academy Graduates" at Sandaogaojing and Mingwalang, I gained the consent of thirty classmates. Some pressed me for every last detail; some did not care to look far into it; some were half believing and half in doubt, for they could not fathom that I should have this sort of connection, or rather did not wholly believe there was any such thing in it. Yet most felt this was a rather novel affair.

Mr. Dai Yunong accepted the whole list on the spot, without a second thought. The total was thirty men, myself included.

The address of the training class was in the rear court of the Investigation Section for Military Academy Graduates at Sandaogaojing, everyone entering and leaving by the main gate of Sandaogaojing. The site was not large, with some old-style buildings of no set plan. A rough, uneven courtyard was opened up for a drill ground. In one side wall facing south a small narrow door had newly been cut. Call it a door—it was really more like a hole, admitting only a short man, bent low, one at a time. Beyond the narrow door was a little alley only a few feet wide, down which not even a rickshaw could pass.

On the far side of that little alley rose another high wall, and within that wall was the "Honggongci." This was the origin of the "Honggongci Training Class" of the Juntong's records.

The formal, full title of the training class was the "Special Service Police Training Class of the General Staff Headquarters." The post of director was held concurrently by Shen Tingchan, then chief of the Second Bureau of the General Staff Headquarters, and that of deputy director by Wang Gupan, chief of the Capital Police Department. Plainly the director and deputy director alike held their posts in name only, and in fact seldom showed their faces. At the opening, at graduation, or when the Leader came, they would put in an appearance, and answer the roll, and that was all.

Under the class headquarters it was divided into sections, or by other tellings into courses—the accounts differ; three sections, as I recall. Mr. Zheng Jiemin was in charge of instruction; Mr. Dai Yunong presided over the class's affairs under the title of "administration"; Mr. Li Shizhen was in charge of moral discipline and served concurrently as team leader.

This was the early form of secret-service training in our country; being newly begun, it had only taken its first rough shape. Yet, high and low, inside and out, the whole class was charged with an air of mystery.

I really could not understand it: since this training class was in fact Mr. Dai Yunong's responsibility, why did he go under a title of "administration"? Was it that his standing was not weighty enough? That his academy class was too low? Or for fear of exposing his identity? There must surely have been some reason in it.

There was another thing past understanding, and it was this: though the Special Service Police Training Class was run by the Lixingshe, from the opening to the graduation, over half a year, the Commandant came in person many times—while not one of the senior cadres of the Lixingshe was ever seen to come. What was this for? It is truly a thing to ponder. These matters of personnel were too tangled; I was not yet up to analyzing them.

As for the training class's furnishings, to call them merely "crude" hardly seems enough; add "shabby," and it comes near the mark. In saying so I mean no mockery, but only to show how hard the founding was—truly a road of thorns and brambles.

The class headquarters office was set in two low rooms, and the several office desks of unequal height were each spread with a piece of blue cloth. The blue cloth was new, and though it covered the mottled desktops, it could not hide the worn desk legs. On the desks lay a few articles of stationery, and besides them a jangling call-bell.

The rooms had no doors left; over each doorway hung a white curtain that swayed without rest whenever the wind blew.

The row of rooms diagonally across from the office was the trainees' dormitory. It was dim, with doors but no windows. The air within did not circulate well; add the damp of the mildew season, and there was a foul reek fit to sting the nose. The beds were in upper and lower bunks, knocked together out of thin planks; they creaked the moment you lay down, and even turning over would rouse the whole row. Bedding and sheets were our own, of every color and kind; and had strict tidiness of quarters been demanded, it would have been a hard thing to achieve.

The classroom for lessons was the great assembly hall, and all our gatherings were held there too. The platform was built of planks, standing a foot or more off the ground. On it was an oiled, glossy black table, reckoned the most eye-catching new object of all. Behind it was mounted an old blackboard; call it black—it was really dark gray, and would fade paler still in time. The platform was small in area, holding at most a dozen men standing; set out chairs and sit down, and it held only six or seven. To the left and right of the platform stood two small plank-built side rooms, used to store books, lecture notes, and materials for experiments, and to serve too as a place for the instructors to rest a little.

In the classroom fifteen long tables were set out in neat order, ranged in three rows, two men to a long bench, holding just thirty. Classroom, assembly hall, and meeting-place in one, the great all-purpose room was bare of any adornment; only in the first few days of the term were some gaudy slogans pasted on the surrounding walls. The wording of the slogans held nothing striking; as for that couplet later composed in Mr. Dai Yunong's own hand—"the eyes and ears of the Leader; hands and feet to the brethren"—it was not yet there at this time.

The mess was only a wall away from the classroom, seven or eight square tables with long benches set round them. Of the three daily meals, by rule it was steamed bread and rice gruel in the morning, and rice at noon and evening. Six to a table was the rule, with four dishes and a soup served; teaching staff and trainees ate all together, the fare exactly the same. As for whether the food was good or bad—it filled the belly, that was all; but no one ever complained of it.

The place for washing and bathing was an inconspicuous corner beside the mess. In the open, with no cover at all, a few planks front and back served for a screen. With an enamel basin and a great wooden tub, you could draw water at will and rinse off. The murmur of the drain running off—was that not a scene in itself?

The hardest days to bear were the sweltering ones of high summer; you longed for a fall of rain, and then the roof would leak.

The team leader, Mr. Li Shizhen, enforced a strict military discipline. Not only did he take charge, he gave himself heart and soul to it, throwing himself into his duty twenty-four hours a day, almost without sleep or rest.

During the training all Sundays were canceled outright; not only were we not allowed out, we could not so much as make a telephone call to the outside. In other words, we were cut off from every tie with the outer world. And so, in those six months, except when we came and went in ranks for course practice, we never once, any one of us alone, walked out through that hole-like narrow door.

At the two musters, morning and evening, team leader Li Shizhen took the roll in person, six months on end without a day's slackening, and never careless with a single tick of the roll. Above all, that dose of exhortation after the evening roll was long and tedious, and hard on our patience.

At our lessons, team leader Li came into the classroom along with us at almost every session, and did not leave until the instructor had begun to speak; yet in no time you would find him standing there again. The classmates found him something of a nag, but on the whole respected him, and in fact he was a good team leader.

Mr. Li Shizhen was also an academy classmate, and had studied "higher police" in Japan; in the class he too took charge of one course, called simply "Higher Police," and seemed the type who, without great natural gifts, strove all the harder to get ahead.

Mr. Li Shizhen and I, after parting then, have never met again in the more than forty years since. It is said Mr. Li made quite a name in police affairs; and there is a rumor that at one time he even contended with Mr. Dai Yunong for control of the police—I do not know whether it is so. And if it were, I think that "age" is long since past.

The date of the opening, according to the *Complete Works of Mr. Dai Yunong*, was June 1932; by my own memory it was May—perhaps I misremember.

The opening was a simple, solemn ceremony; the chairman of the Military Affairs Commission and commandant of the Military Academy, our Leader, Mr. Chiang, came in person to administer the oath. The man who attended the Leader as he stepped into the hall was neither the class director, Shen Tingchan, nor the deputy director, Wang Gupan, nor any secretary or standing cadre of the Society for Vigorous Practice of the Three Principles of the People: it was Dai Li, head of the administration section of the training class.

Into the hall came only the Leader and Dai Li; all the others, some in military uniform and some in plain clothes, halted outside the door—a somewhat uncommon scene.

Beforehand, each trainee had a mimeographed copy of the oath; the Leader raised his left hand and led the reading, and Dai Li and the trainees read it after him once more. The reading done, Dai Li at once gathered up all the oaths and burnt them then and there. The whole hall was solemn and silent.

The Leader gave his exhortation, urging each of us to sacrifice the lesser self, to guard the nation, and to be a nameless hero.

The oath taken, we began our lessons in earnest. As to the courses, Mr. Zheng Jiemin, in charge of instruction, was indeed at great pains; his greatest difficulty was finding teachers. For a training of this kind, being the first of its sort with no precedent, what subjects were to be set out? What teaching materials were needed? Where were instructors to be found and engaged, and who was equal to the task? For a time no satisfying answer could be had to any of it; beyond feeling the way forward, no one could offer a better plan.

As for what a certain magazine said—"it gathered and drew on choice teaching materials and instructors from China, Japan, Germany, Russia, Britain, and America, and gave a training in intelligence business"—those are fine words, and no more.

In fact, in whatever age, down to the world of today, materials of this kind are for the most part compiled by each country for itself. Think of it: what country would honestly hand this whole set of things over to be taught to foreigners? Would that not truly be handing others a weapon against oneself? One can only say that the foreign goods we had translated could, at the very most, be ranked as "reference material."

Now see the course of study we received:

The most basic remained the political course, which took in party doctrine, political theory, international questions, and the like. To my own mind, this course ought to have been presided over by the senior cadres of the Lixingshe; but they did not come either. Why, I have never been able to fathom.

Shorthand—the "Zhang Cai shorthand system" was chosen, taught by an heir of Zhang Cai's line; well taught, and hardest to learn.

Quick sketching—those who came to teach were the Liang brothers. I recall that Mr. Liang Dingming once took us to his "Xianggu Temple studio" to view his large oil paintings. The unfinished picture seemed to be "The Battle of Mianhu."

Photography—this took in the use of the camera and development in the darkroom, and the teacher was likewise a master, though who he was I cannot recall.

Driving—divided into motorcar and motorcycle driving, both practiced; but, with time short, most of us—all but a few—never mastered it, and the instructor was Zhou Qibang, an elder brother of the first class.

Demolition—we all had some grounding, so this time it was as good as revision, with more variety besides; what everyone enjoyed most was making our own blasting cans. A bang, and how happy we were—great fun, too.

Shooting—this took in the stripping and assembling of firearms of every kind. Live firing, though, was not taken so seriously as target practice at the academy had been; everyone seemed to treat it half as play, and no one guessed that one day it would really be put to use.

Carrier pigeons—we called this course "raising pigeons"; it was taught by an instructor named Huang, who lectured and also demonstrated such things as message-carrying. This instructor Huang was said to be an expert too.

Biochemistry—the main thing was expounding the properties of drugs, such as anesthetics, stimulants, and asphyxiants. Trials were made on a rabbit too, but who could have known this rabbit bore a charmed life: one injection, and it hopped about for the longest while and would not die, to the instructor's great embarrassment.

The academic subjects and their instructors were roughly as follows:

Military Intelligence Science—lectured by Mr. Zheng Jiemin; the noted book published later, *Military Intelligence Science*, is this very one.

Reconnaissance Method—taught by an officer in charge from the Second Bureau, its content mostly compiled from cases published during the First World War. Even then it was felt to be rather out of date.

Communications Method—this took in wireless telegraphy, ciphers, secret writing, secret speech, and the like. We had a few practice sessions, no more than a touch of each; most of it was specialized technique, and a short course could do no more.

Intelligence Collection—this was a very practical course, with practice too; the whole operation from getting word to submitting a report had to be mastered. But the early exercises, set beside those of today, were amateur work, a thing neither fish nor fowl.

Action and Demolition—weighted toward theory. The instructor who taught this course did not necessarily have practical experience either.

Armed Struggle and Mass Insurrection—this course was aimed at the two sides, attack and defense, of the fight against the bandits; Mr. Ye Daoxin compiled the lecture notes himself, then expounded it all in fine detail. It was so well taught that it won the class's unanimous praise.

Secret Societies—expounding the origins of the Sandianhui, the Gelaohui, the Green Gang, and the Red (Hong) Gang. In truth, secret-society activity was fading day by day, and a little understanding was enough.

The Work of the Cheka—there was a booklet, translated out of Russian, on the secret-service work of Soviet Russia. It sounded rather new to hear in those days.

Higher Police—lectured by our team leader, Mr. Li Shizhen; his lectures were clear and well-ordered, and much hard work had gone into them. If that set of methods he expounded could really have been applied to public-security work, the effect would surely have been great.

The above are what I can recall; there must be quite a few I have left out. And it is a great fault of mine that I can no longer supply the honored names of many of the instructors.

What made a deep impression was that every instructor was full of zeal; lateness or absence was all but unheard of, and they answered questions in untiring detail—a rare thing indeed in a training body. And so, by and large, the classmates listened even to what they did not wish to hear, and labored on at what they could not master.

It is a pity that many of the courses were too specialized: never mind learning so many arts in six months—even one or two alone would not necessarily carry a man past the threshold to real "mastery." Still, the good of it was that every trainee could form a notion of secret-service work, and that may be reckoned as reaching the aim of the training.

In sum, most classmates' minds were not on learning skills; everyone longed to try his hand at the first chance.

### 3. A Class Monitor with a Secret Mission

Having answered the call-up to join the training, and been entrusted with seeking out the classmates, I became the "class monitor" as a matter of course. By "class monitor" I do not mean the head of the whole class; apart from leading all the classmates into the classroom, or standing at the head of the ranks, I was, as at any other school, also the class leader the whole body of classmates had elected. At the two roll-calls, morning and evening, it was I who reported the numbers to the team leader; and at each lesson it was I who called the commands—rise, be seated—as the instructor came and went. My place was at the table nearest the platform, the first thing you saw on entering the classroom, so that even shirking was out of the question.

This was only the surface; underneath, in secret, I had yet another mission. A few days before the training began, Mr. Dai Yunong charged me most earnestly with one task: in the first three months of the training I must set down in writing every classmate's thoughts, words, and every matter worth noting, and secretly furnish them to him for his reference—and it must not be known to the other classmates. It was then agreed that he would appoint the adjutant Jia Jinnan, whom I knew, to come and collect them once each Monday, Wednesday, and Friday.

Mr. Dai Yunong added: "Should any grave incident arise in the class, you may go to the instruction section and get in touch with the two comrades Zheng Jiemin or Cen Jiazhuo, and telephone me at once." What sort of incident Mr. Dai meant by "grave incident" was not clear to me. I found it very strange: if one day such a thing really happened, would it not be enough to report it to Mr. Zheng Jiemin for him to pass on? Why, then, must it go through Mr. Zheng's channel—that is, get Mr. Zheng's leave for me to telephone out—before I reported to him directly? Of all this I understood little; perhaps Mr. Dai had his own reasons, for he never liked people to question him much, and even when questioned would sometimes answer with silence.

Once the training began, I made the investigation reports as he wished. But at the very start I met a small difficulty: the notebooks the class issued us were barely enough to begin with, and tearing a page or two from each would not show, while tearing more might, if discovered, bring on trouble. Apart from drawing on the notebooks, blank paper fit for use was not easy to find. In the end I reported it to Mr. Dai, and it was settled when the man who came, Jia Jinnan, brought in two pads of airmail paper. Small as this matter was, it shows that in anything at all, a lack of forethought will not do.

Our round of life and work was tightly packed from the first, with little slack in between. And I, being class leader besides, had many routine forms to fill in each day; to find, on top of that, a little more time to write things was truly no easy matter. However much I racked my brains, I could only write a little at a time, by stealth and in scraps.

Think of it: apart from myself, the class was twenty-nine men. By the items Mr. Dai had laid down, even writing plainly and to the point, each man came to two or three hundred characters at least and as much as six or seven hundred at most; all told, on an average reckoning, it came to some 15,000 characters—and this, moreover, was no essay to be spun out at will and handed in. In writing this sort of report I could neither be slipshod nor harbor the least private bias; for I understood keenly that a single word of praise or blame could bear on the whole of a man's future, and so I set brush to paper with care, striving to be strictly fair.

A trainee's daily studies were already heavy enough; being class leader loaded far more on me than the ordinary classmate carried. And writing this investigation report on top of it all fairly ran me off my feet. Yet, for a full understanding of the classmates under training, I had of necessity to draw closer to every one of them, win their goodwill, and leave no trace of it—no simple thing at all. And so, three months of this wore me half to death; had it not been for a young man's high spirits and my keenness to do well, I could scarcely have borne up.

The business of writing the investigation reports did not go wholly as I would have wished, and there was an episode in it besides: at the hours when I should have been resting I often stayed alone in the classroom, head down and writing, instead of going back to the dormitory—and since it looked like neither note-tidying nor schoolwork, it drew team leader Li's notice. Though he never questioned me to my face, he kept a close watch on me and would not let go. At this I was at a loss. In the end I wrote a little slip of paper and reported the truth to Mr. Dai, and only then was the watch on me lifted. From the standpoint of intelligence work, a case like this counts as having one's identity exposed.

To speak, again, of getting on with the classmates during the training: on the whole it might be called a general harmony, the chief reason being that no one had any clash of interest, and, added to that, there was no spare time at all; even small private frictions, hemmed in by circumstance, had no room to rub themselves into anything. There is one more layer, and I may as well blow my own horn a little: being the class leader the men had elected, then, without claiming to be a model to the whole body, I had at least, even for appearance's sake, to keep myself straight. Happily my regular marks were graded A, and in the ordinary practice tests I seldom made a fool of myself, so that the classmates thought well enough of me; and so, seen and unseen, I too worked some harmonizing effect.

Most important, and most to the point: besides having an equal chance with the other classmates in the training, in living conditions I was exactly as they were. Though I was class monitor and had a personal connection with Mr. Dai Yunong who presided over the training, I had not the least privilege. Whatever others might think, and whatever they might say behind my back, my heart was quite at ease—which is the point on which I feel my ground firmest.

The six months were fast slipping by. Before graduation, each classmate filled in, as required, a work-preference form, one column of which was for the place of work—that is, where one hoped to be posted in future. Reaching this column, I wrote without hesitation the two words "Hangzhou"; this was no last-minute decision; I had set my heart on it long before.

On this point Mr. Dai had specially questioned me; what he valued was whether I had some special working line in Hangzhou, or some plan already in view. At the time it flustered me, and for a moment I could not answer. The true reason was of the subconscious kind: my ancestral home being Hangzhou, a man—and above all one whose thinking is half new and half old—can hardly help harboring the traditional clan feeling; and then I was drawn a little by the saying "Above there is heaven; below, Suzhou and Hangzhou"—which, in truth, had nothing to do with the work.

On graduation day a simple ceremony was held, and for the third time the Leader came in person to address us, with many words of encouragement. The whole teaching staff was present, from the director and deputy director on down; and this time there were also several men of high rank, fully armed and immaculately turned out, whom I did not know and had not seen on the two previous occasions. It was said these senior officers had a duty connection with the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics newly set up under the Military Affairs Commission in September of that year.

The Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission mentioned here is what people later called the "Juntong." But the Juntong as it really was, the writer would presume to divide into two stages, early and late; the Juntong of this moment ought to be called the "early Juntong," for at that time the Bureau was divided into three departments, and Mr. Dai Yunong was responsible for only one of them—the Second Department. Not until 1938, after the outbreak of the War of Resistance, was the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics reorganized, with He Yaozu as nominal chief and Dai Li presiding over the whole bureau's work as deputy chief—and this is the formal Juntong. On this foundation, through several changes of name, though the "system" is no more, its working spirit has carried on to this day: it survives as the Intelligence Bureau of the Ministry of National Defense.

That evening the whole class dined together; the director and deputy director both stayed away, and Mr. Dai Yunong presided over all as their representative. The dinner over, individual talks were held in the class headquarters office, Mr. Dai Yunong and Mr. Zheng Jiemin summoning us in turn. My turn to speak with Mr. Dai came last of all; he gave me some special words of encouragement, and asked me to come to No. 53 Ji'e Lane the next morning for a talk.

By this time I had known Dai Yunong exactly one year. From then on we were partners in the work; in the relation of superior to subordinate, he was my chief and I his man.

In the assignment of work, Mr. Dai did not consider my wish to go to Hangzhou; he had already decided to send me to Beiping to found the work. As for Beiping, it was my second home, where I was born and where I grew up, and should have been dearer to me than Hangzhou; so of course I was glad of it in my heart. Going with me—sent out at the same time—were Yang Ying and Qi Nanpu, the two who had trained alongside me. Though I was appointed to take charge, at that time there was as yet no fixed establishment, so it was not clearly laid down that I was the "group leader" among the three.

I remember very clearly: there was nothing whatever in writing; what work I was assigned, what mission I bore, rested on Mr. Dai's word alone. If he did write out a slip, it went only to the personnel department for registration, and the man concerned might not even see what was written on that slip. It was thus, "without a scrap of proof," that I joined the secret-service work.

There was a question I had long bottled up, and one day I at last put it to Mr. Dai. I said: "I have already joined the 'Revolutionary Soldiers' Comrades Association'—you've long known that, Mr. Dai, haven't you?" Mr. Dai answered straight: "It was brother Huang Jianqiu who put your name forward—he has already told me." Only then did I grasp that it was brother Jianqiu who had introduced me—though he had never once mentioned the matter to me.

I asked Mr. Dai again: "How does our work stand in relation to the 'Military Association'? Are they one thing, or two?" He was silent a long while and said nothing, and after another moment said only, "You will come to understand it in time"—just that one vague, noncommittal sentence. At the time I was thoroughly put out: I held that where there is a question there should be an answer, and that he ought in reason to explain it to me plainly. Only later did I come to see that, because there was this layer of the Lixingshe in it, and I was only a "member" beneath the Lixingshe, the organization was bound to keep it from me, so that he simply could not tell me.

Before I set out, Mr. Dai also held a farewell gathering for me alone, chiefly to make me acquainted with several comrades in charge of inside work; present were brothers Xu Weibin, Lin Huan, Zhang Gunfu, and others. The feast over, Mr. Dai made me a gift of a Browning pistol. That pistol was short and thick, of large bore, its make impossible to tell; some said it was Italian-made. As he handed me the pistol, he stressed once more that this was a personal gift for a keepsake, and hoped I would keep it long. Who would have guessed that this pistol would truly "startle all with its first cry" and "sound out beyond the common," and, in its maiden trial, be put to great use?

Checking dates and times, I make it that training ended and we were sent out in November or December 1932.

Writing to this point, I may as well repeat what was said before and speak of the truth of the Juntong and the Lixingshe. In the past there were many rumors, and not only outsiders but even some of those in the organization could not necessarily tell the one clearly from the other; below I first give a general explanation. As for the detailed inside story, I shall show it as it truly happened across the several parts of *Nameless Heroes*.

In March 1932 the Lixingshe was founded at Nanjing. On April 1 of the same year the Special Services Department was formed under Dai Li. For a time it was also called the "Sixth Department"; afterward the name "Sixth Department" no longer appears in the documents.

In March 1932 the National Government set up the Military Affairs Commission, and in September of that year, under the Military Affairs Commission, established the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics, the "Juntong" for short. This body was not made public, so few outside knew of it; Dai Li was appointed head of the bureau's Second Department.

On the surface, though the Special Services Department belonging to the Lixingshe and the Second Department belonging to the Juntong were under different lines of subordination, in fact they were two faces of one body. For example, the two shared a single office, and the Intelligence Section under the Special Services Department was the very same as the Intelligence Section under the Second Department, and so on.

From 1932 down to Mr. Dai Yunong's death in 1946, and even for some years after Mr. Dai's death, the secret-service work he led and founded took shape, it cannot be denied, into a powerful "system." This system, on the Chinese political scene, at times went beyond its own working sphere and exerted a certain political influence. This would take a special volume to set out thoroughly; here I touch on it only in brief, and shall treat it fully in its place.

To speak fairly and judge by the facts, the doings of the Juntong did indeed make a great contribution to the nation; though, of course, no one would dare say there was not one small blemish.

To go on further: the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission set up in September 1932 was a non-established unit of the earlier period, and in the several decades since, no one has ever said who the bureau's chief was; there are of course some rumors, mostly without any proof, and now there is no one left to verify it. So far as I personally know, the bureau, besides the Second Department under Department Head Dai Li, of course also had a "First Department," which, I have heard, was headed by a man sent from the several central ministries, and may well be what later became the "Zhongtong." Nor was it only the First and Second Departments; there was also a "Third Department," whose head was Chen Zhuo, a former chief of the Capital Police Department. Whether this is exactly so I dare not warrant, and I respectfully await the guidance of those in the know.

By 1938 the reorganized Bureau of Investigation and Statistics had been formally brought into the establishment, still under the Military Affairs Commission. He Yaozu took the post of bureau chief, and Dai Li bore the real responsibility as deputy chief. Why He Yaozu should hold an empty title there must be some reason; we simply do not know it. If I were to guess, it may be that Dai Yunong was not yet senior enough.

As for the name "Special Services Department," it was probably abolished in 1938, at the same time as the Lixingshe and the "Renaissance Society" were transformed and reorganized into the "Three People's Principles Youth Corps"—or perhaps a little earlier. In other words, by 1938 the secret-service work Mr. Dai presided over was no longer that two-faces-of-one-body state of things. Yet, as need required, many new bodies were established one after another outside the Juntong, and all of them too were Mr. Dai's responsibility.

For fear of straying on too long and wandering ever further from the point, on this matter of the relation between the Juntong and the Lixingshe, I shall leave it there.

To pick up the earlier thread, let me return to matters bearing on the Honggongci Special Service Police Training Class.

The first term of the Honggongci Special Service Police Class had, at its opening, thirty trainees in all. Midway, a classmate named Zeng dropped out for some reason; and afterward two more withdrew through illness, so that by the time of graduation and assignment there were only twenty-seven.

Where all twenty-seven classmates were assigned, I never wholly knew; and in work of our kind it does not do to ask too much. Yet, our friendships being deep, there were those who privately passed word to one another. For instance, Liao Zongze of Hangzhou and I often corresponded in friendship. So far as I know, besides Yang Ying and Qi Nanpu who went to Beiping with me, four classmates—Weng Yikui, Zhuang Junlie, Wang Yishi, and Tang Huanwen—were later added to the work of the Beiping Station.

Two years later, that is in 1934, when I attended meetings and was received in audience at Nanjing and Mount Lu, I chanced to meet classmates Liu Yiguang at Nanjing, Liao Zongze at Hangzhou, Tian Dongyun in Sichuan, and Zheng Xingzhou at Zhengzhou. As to what work they did in those places, I understood little.

Through several upheavals and changes, of those who came to Taiwan after 1949 there were eight in all; the rest, who stayed on the mainland, are, I must suppose, no longer living. Of those I have word of: Qi Nanpu was struggled to death by the Chinese Communists long ago in his native Quanjiao County in Anhui; Weng Yikui fell in battle against the Communist army at Laohekou—but of the dates of both I know nothing. As for the others, the Intelligence Bureau may now have records and know something; I have not gone to inquire, but I can only hope some are still alive and well.

A few years ago classmate Weng Guanghui, after long torment from a stroke, died in Taiwan.

As of the end of May 1981, seven were still living in Taiwan:

Yu Yaoli, of Jiangxi, fifth class of the Military Academy, delegate to the National Assembly, in poor health, once operated on for emphysema.

Chen Zhijing, of Henan, fifth class of the Military Academy. Once a Taipei city councillor, suffering from arthritis.

Liu Yiguang, of Hunan, fourth class of the Military Academy. For several decades he was in attendance on Zhang Xueliang, rising step by step from major to major general. Now retired, close on eighty, he no longer moves about easily.

Qiu Yaoxun, of Fujian, sixth class of the Military Academy. To this day hale in body, past seventy yet like a man of fifty; still, he is kept busy making a living.

Yang Ying, of Hunan, sixth class of the Military Academy. Once education chief of the Infantry School, long since retired; now he copies calligraphy models and tends flowers, dandling his grandchildren—the most fortunate of men.

Zhuang Junlie, of Fujian, fifth class of the Military Academy. Bedridden with a stroke for years now, still struggling on with life.

Chen Gongshu, of Hebei, fifth class of the Military Academy. A whole life of folly—the good-for-nothing is the writer himself. By Heaven's care, when I should have died I did not, and this life was left me.

Now I do nothing at all, but with a single mind write out this book, *Nameless Heroes*.

Of the seven men above, the youngest is seventy-two, the eldest seventy-eight or nine; the circumstances of each are all different, and could they be written out, each would make a fine story rich in the stuff of legend.

### 4. All Unknowing, I Meet a Great International Spy

When a body of men have trained together in one class, at graduation and parting they would normally leave an address, or take a group photograph for a keepsake; we did none of this, no one telling another where he was now bound, not even saying, "Till we meet again." Could it be that this was the parting of the ways, each to go his own road? The managing staff instructed us all: "This is the nature of our work."

The higher-ups decided to send Yang Ying, Qi Nanpu, and me, three men, to Beiping to found the work. To fit ourselves out with clothes we were held up a few more days. The weather was already turning cool, and we had thought to dress in a way that fitted our station, but the clothing allowance was truly pitiful; if we saw to the head we could not see to the feet—truly, as the saying goes, straighten the lapel and the elbow shows—and we ended up looking a sorry, ill-assorted lot instead.

Our travel money, too, ran only to third-class carriages, and on ordinary passenger-and-freight trains the journey took three days and three nights; ill-fed and ill-slept, and every one of us ended up disheveled and grimy, in a sorry state.

On reaching Tianjin, Yang Ying and Qi Nanpu changed trains and went straight on to Beiping, while I was to stop and make contact with one man and see to two matters.

I had once stayed in Tianjin at the Liyuan Inn on Hebei Avenue, and, knowing no other quarters, this time found my way back to it. As for the grand hotels in the Concessions—even with money enough, I doubt I should have dared go in.

The man I was to contact was the person in charge of the Tianjin area; the higher-ups told me his name was Zheng Shisong—surely a cover name. The address was in a quiet residential quarter of the British Concession, a good long way from Hebei Avenue, and I changed cars two or three times before I found it.

He came to the door in person, and, before I could open my mouth, seemed already to know who I was. I gave my name, and he too asked no more, but very warmly asked me in to sit. And so we made contact quite naturally, without in fact any of that business of passwords and hand-signs.

The parlor was quite splendidly furnished, and even the small ornaments had all been picked with taste and ingenuity; but they looked to carry, more or less, a Japanese flavor.

This Zheng Shisong was a fine figure of a man, of middling build and regular features, without a single distinguishing mark to be found on him from head to foot. He had a pair of large eyes, full of spirit, though his gaze never rested—the mark, perhaps, of a man with his plans already laid. He wore the newest of narrow-legged Western suits, square-toed leather shoes with cloth gaiter-covers, a high-collared white shirt, a silk figured necktie—the air of a most fastidious gentleman. Set beside him, my own turnout, though nothing to be ashamed of, looked distinctly threadbare.

We had scarcely begun to talk—not three sentences in—before it was plain how green I was. He was sixteen years my senior; I was a fledgling barely out of the nest, while he was already seasoned in the ways of the world.

Mr. Zheng Shisong's true surname was Wang, his original name Renqiang; after entering the work he changed his name to Wang Tianmu. His schooling and career took in the Baoding officers' academy, a Japanese military academy, service as an adviser in the Northwest Army, and the enlisting of bandit bands about Henan, where he once styled himself a commander—and so on. In talent and learning he was truly deep, and even a few casual strokes of his brush were fit to hang on a plaque; a remarkable man indeed.

He was an old friend of Mr. Dai Yunong's; they had a deep friendship, and later all but became kin by the marriage of their children. Before Mr. Dai had yet taken up the Special Services Department, they had long worked together. He had been sent to found the work in Tianjin half a year before we went to Beiping, and at this time the work in Tianjin was already on quite a scale.

What I stopped in Tianjin to ask his help with was the matter of how to send telegrams once I reached Beiping. In those days the work under Mr. Dai, whether the Special Services Department or the Second Department, had no wireless equipment of its own, and a field unit's telegrams to Nanjing had to be sent by the good offices of the Party Central's Investigation Section (?). So it was in Tianjin, and so too in Beiping. Because Tianjin had a link with them, I asked Mr. Zheng Shisong to build me a bridge.

In the ordinary way, field units were not allowed to form lateral ties; this time, for once, it was the higher-ups themselves who wanted our two units at Beiping and Tianjin in close touch. That was the second matter I had to see to.

Once I had made my errand plain, these two matters were easily concluded and smoothly settled. Mr. Zheng Shisong warmly insisted on taking me out to a meal, and, since I could not well refuse, I went. The place we went was so grand that I tasted nothing of the whole meal, minding only that I must on no account make a fool of myself. After the meal Mr. Zheng Shisong sent me back to the inn in his own motorcar. This was our first contact, and he left a deep impression on me, and I liked him well besides.

Beiping is my birthplace, and in my mind, so long as a thing belonged to Beiping, all of it was good, without exception good—good even to the point of doting.

Once my two comrades Yang Ying and Qi Nanpu and I had gathered together, we did as we had planned on the road and took our first step at once—finding a good place to lodge, to settle in, to work from, and to make our start.

Within a day or two we actually found a house of a kind you might scour all four quarters of the city and hardly light on; nothing could have suited our needs better.

The rent was very cheap: pay in advance and move in, with not even a deposit asked.

This house stood on a great through-street, most convenient for coming and going.

There were only two and a half usable rooms; but the good of it was that the house stood apart with its own gate and court, and, with the gate shut, no one could see what deviltry we were up to inside.

Unexpectedly, the landlord had a telephone installed and did not mean to move it away; we had only to return him the telephone deposit, and it was ours to use.

Of course there is no perfect thing under heaven: this house had no bath, and the privy was too filthy besides.

Neither Yang Ying nor I was superstitious; never minding whether the day was lucky or ill, we moved in first and let the rest follow. Qi Nanpu was willing to take the field work, and settled himself separately outside.

None of us had a cover occupation; too clever by half, we hung a wooden signboard at the gate reading "Beiping Branch of the Military Journal Press," fully thinking it could pass for a front, and that, should anyone come to inquire, we could meet the case—little dreaming it would later land us in great trouble.

The substation police came to check the household registration too. Whatever you said, that was what he wrote in the register—easy to deal with; but if he had walked into the rooms for a look, then even without our giving ourselves away, suspicion would likely have been roused. Speaking of the Beiping police substation, it is a most interesting thing, and truly worth saying a few words more about.

For many years the Beiping natives all called the police substation the "gezi"; why it is called "gezi," I have never asked the learned, and dare not talk wildly. But the gezi I have seen were, for years, small huts built of planks on an open lot, the outside painted liver-red, with two little tables set inside, the household registers strung on cords and hung in rows on the wall; the police gentlemen sitting up on a ladder, winter cold or summer heat alike, would carry on their business just so.

For service to the people, the Beiping substations may be reckoned first in the whole country: to look up a name, go to the gezi; for a brawl or a fight, up to the gezi; to report a birth or a death, to the gezi; a quarrel between mother- and daughter-in-law or a squabble among sisters-in-law also troubles the gezi—anything you cannot settle yourself, there is nothing you may not take to the gezi. Even on matters beyond him, he would answer you with a kind face and untiring patience, never lording it over you or holding you at arm's length.

Just because the Beiping police were close to the residents, they knew, eight or nine parts in ten, the state of every household in their district. If a weak point must be found, it was the lack of political sense.

Once we had a fixed working address, besides reporting it to the higher-ups for the record, we also gave word to Zheng Shisong in Tianjin, so that the two units could link up.

We began to learn the use of the cipher book brought from Nanjing, and sent out our first telegram; Yang Ying served as code clerk, and I doubled as "courier," carrying this draft telegram to Suzhou Hutong in the east city and handing it to "Mr. Zhang" of "Zhang Pei"—"Zhang Pei," I suppose, being a cover name, and being also the wireless station of the Party Central's Investigation Section at Beiping. The time we relied on them to send our telegrams was very brief; by the beginning of 1933 the higher-ups sent the wireless specialist comrade Cheng Jun to Beiping, and set up a station of our own for our exclusive use.

In the early days of pushing the work forward, short of hands, pinched for funds, and, on top of that, with no working experience at all, the four words "nothing to boast of" fit us most aptly.

By February 1933, with our ranks a little strengthened, we gradually pressed forward a small step.

In the matter of personnel, those who joined the inside work were Bai Shiwei, a seventh-class academy classmate introduced by Qi Nanpu, and Wang Yunsun, recommended by Wang Zhaohuai, a veteran comrade of the bureau headquarters. Neither of the two went through any ceremony or formality in joining the work; being introduced by comrades of our own, they were treated from the outset as basic comrades.

The intelligence contacts taken in on the spot (whom we called internally "handled personnel") were three: Hou Zichuan, Zhang Bowu, and Fan Xing.

Zhang Bowu was recommended by Mr. Zheng Shisong of Tianjin, and Hou Zichuan in turn brought in by Zhang Bowu. The two of them were originally of the Youth Party, though at the time I did not know it. Hou Zichuan gradually admitted it; Zhang Bowu never would, to the end. What Zhang and Hou supplied was all local intelligence, of limited working value; now and then there was some data on the movements of the Northeast Army too, but that was of no account either.

Fan Xing, courtesy name Jiman, of Sichuan, claimed to be of the sixth class of the Central Military Academy; at this time he was also reading at the "Art Academy" in Beiping and studying painting. This gentleman could truly be called a figure of legend.

Our meeting too was quite by chance. One day I went to see Jiang Tian (Lisheng), a classmate of my own enlisted company, newly come to Beiping from his native Yutian County in East Hebei and lodging for the time at the home of his elder cousin Jiang Yunqing. Jiang Yunqing's father, named Jiang Hao, was an old-line Communist ranked with Li Dazhao and Chen Duxiu, who had died years before at Vladivostok. Jiang Yunqing had two younger sisters besides, the second Yiqing, the third Taiqing, both of somewhat leftist leaning. Fan Xing was the boyfriend of the third sister, Jiang Taiqing.

The Jiang house was small, and we all crowded together to chat. Fan Xing was not only a good talker, but everything he said was to the point; we were all held by his voice and manner. I thought: here is a capable hand; whatever it takes, I must draw him in. Once the notion was born, a few visits back and forth made him our intelligence man.

At the start it was nothing much, but afterward it gained ground more and more, and the intelligence he supplied grew ever more important, such as: Japan's military dispositions in China, and the Japanese military's political intrigues; the exchange of understandings among the powers over Japan's aggression against China, and other high-grade intelligence. The source, by Fan's report, was an interpreter in the military attaché's office of a certain country's embassy in China—but we never once saw this man. At the time I personally had not yet the ability to judge intelligence of this kind, and so passed it all on as it came; and the reaction of the higher-ups was that it was "of considerable reference value" too.

This was how our gathering of important intelligence began, and may be called, too, a strange encounter in the course of intelligence work; but the affair did not unfold so simply, and it held many doubtful points we could not crack.

Besides this, the two the higher-ups assigned me merely to keep in touch with (only to keep contact, with no power to direct) were Liao Huaping and a certain Yu (whose name I have forgotten).

Mr. Liao Huaping had, in his academy days, been one of our political instructors—I recall he taught "History of Social Evolution"—and I could never have thought we should meet again in such circumstances. Mr. Liao had been a delegate to the First National Congress of the Chinese Kuomintang, and also a member of the First Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party—a man of very old standing. In the party purge of 1927 Liao Huaping fled from Guangzhou, slipped into Beiping to carry on activity, and was arrested by the Beiping Gendarmerie Command. Because he had repented his former errors and was willing to work for us, he was handed over for liaison to the unit Mr. Dai had stationed at Beiping. Mr. Liao later held such posts as director of the inspectorate of the "Bureau of Confidential Investigation," and died in Taiwan in 1952.

This Yu's history was much like Mr. Liao Huaping's, and he too was a man of long standing. By 1935 and 1936, when this man surnamed Yu held a post in the "Bandit-Suppression Headquarters for the three provinces of Henan, Hubei, and Anhui," he was found stealing secret documents, arrested a second time, and executed.

In March 1933 the higher-ups ordered us to draw up a budget, in which were such items as living expenses, operating expenses, office expenses, and special expenses. The sum approved was about three thousand-odd yuan. My own "living and operating expenses" came to some three hundred-odd yuan, not reckoned separately, with a further "special expense" of one hundred, coming to four hundred-odd in all—a considerable sum indeed. I can still remember clearly that fine foreign white flour then sold at only two yuan eight jiao a sack; converted at today's rate in Taiwan, the four hundred-odd yuan of 1933 comes to about 50,000-odd yuan of 1981—and if the figure is off, it is not off by much.

The name "Beiping Station" was probably fixed from about this time. Yet there was no commission, no assignment, let alone any express notice; but the name stuck all the same.

The work of the Beiping Station had no definite prescription; at that time, apart from gathering intelligence, there was nothing else we could really do.

The unrest of the times and the shifts of the political scene affected us too. On March 11, 1933, the topmost man in Beiping, Zhang Xueliang, announced his retirement by open telegram. On the 12th, General He Yingqin took the post of acting chairman of the Beiping Branch of the Military Affairs Commission. The North China situation, pressed by Japan, grew tenser still.

Mr. Zheng Jiemin, too, was posted to Beiping at this time.

Mr. Zheng came bearing a grave mission, and wearing more than one identity. In the way of secret-service work, he was the immediate superior of our Beiping Station, being the "special commissioner" the Military Affairs Commission's Bureau of Investigation and Statistics had stationed in the North China region, his jurisdiction taking in Chahar, Suiyuan, and the Northeast; at the same time he was an assistant in the Special Services Department belonging to the Lixingshe, in position second only to Mr. Dai Yunong.

Beyond this double identity, Mr. Zheng held yet another post, in the Third Bureau of the General Staff Headquarters—and that was the formal military office, the one with uniform, sidearm, and rank.

By what I understood, on the side of the secret organization, the Society for Vigorous Practice of the Three Principles of the People, Mr. Zheng was also the highest man in charge stationed in North China. Under what title, I cannot say; but I had private dealings with fellow student Jia Yi, secretary of the Beiping branch of the Lixingshe, and Mr. Zheng's position must have stood above the branch secretary's, from which I judge he may have been the special commissioner, or the secretary of the "North China Branch." Under Mr. Zheng Jiemin's charge were two assistant secretaries, one Wang Renyuan, the other Qi Qingbin. Qi Qingbin will appear many times in this book, *Nameless Heroes*, and in the end he too became one of our "nameless heroes."

Though I was a member of the Revolutionary Soldiers' Comrades Association, the second-tier body under the leadership of the Lixingshe, I had never once taken part in its organizational activity. I did not know that the Revolutionary Soldiers' Comrades Association had already been dissolved and reorganized and merged into the Revolutionary Youth Comrades Association, nor did I receive any notice transferring my membership from the Military Association to the Youth Association.

Mr. Zheng Jiemin worked out of two places. One was on Fuyou Street near Zhongnanhai—this was the "special commissioner's office" of the Juntong's Second Department, which had a direct bearing on our work; helping Mr. Zheng handle his daily affairs there was a comrade Xing Shan (Senzhou). The other was the Lixingshe organization's, and I went there too.

Ever since Mr. Zheng came, the link between the Beiping Station and the Nanjing bureau headquarters was markedly reduced; apart from personnel and funds, in all such matters as work guidance and the handling of intelligence we took our orders straight from Mr. Zheng—that is, in the working system, one more layer had been added above the Beiping Station.

As for the Beiping Station's working position being lowered a notch, we took it as a necessity of the working arrangements and thought nothing more of it; whatever the higher-ups decided, we followed—the more so as everyone rested easy under Mr. Zheng's enlightened leadership.

Mr. Zheng was generous by nature and treated his subordinates as his own sons and younger brothers; the longer you were with him, the more you came to feel that genuine warmth. He was from Wenchang in Guangdong, of the second class of the Whampoa Academy, had studied in Russia, and had a deep grounding in modern military science; later he was especially versed in international affairs. At that time he was also the only man who had studied the theoretical system of secret-service work.

Mr. Zheng took a special interest in the intelligence data gathered through Fan Xing, and summoned Fan Xing many times to exchange views on that source of intelligence, though he never got a satisfying answer. On this matter Mr. Zheng took me aside and instructed me: "Fan Xing's intelligence is high in quality, but its reliability is uneven; if we cannot get thoroughly to the truth of the source, there will still be misgivings in putting the intelligence to use hereafter."

Mr. Zheng pointed out further: "Judging by Fan Xing's past intelligence, it is very unlikely that so much could be got from one and the same source."

Mr. Zheng's meaning was that, though Fan Xing's intelligence was not poor in quality, if we did not know just how it was got—where on earth it came from—then, because we had our doubts, we dared not rashly put it to actual use. This was my responsibility, and I had, by Mr. Zheng's instruction, to make a fuller, many-sided inquiry into Fan Xing.

Fan Xing lived alone near the Art Academy in Beiping, in a small room with only a table and a bed. Such houses were let expressly to students, at a rent of only some three or four yuan a month. One day I paid him a special call, meaning to chat more with him and improve my understanding. To my surprise there was a girl in the room as well—call her a girl, but she was in fact over twenty. Fan Xing introduced her as his fellow student Peng Yaluo, also a fellow provincial from Sichuan; and by the look of things the two were somewhat abashed, as though they were more than merely fellow students and fellow provincials. I thought to myself: if this Peng Yaluo too is Fan Xing's sweetheart, then was there not still that Jiang Taiqing at the Jiang house?

This was only a single doubtful point, and it settled nothing; whether it had any bearing on what I wanted to know was not yet certain.

Some days later, I went alone to stroll in the Dong'an Market and, wandering to the bookstalls of the Dangui Bazaar, turned over this and looked at that, meaning to buy a few books too. Speaking of the Dangui Bazaar, it too was a relic of the Beiping of old: the Dangui Bazaar was a part of the Dong'an Market, on the side near Wangfujing Avenue, several hundred feet in length, just like a street. Roofed over, so that wind and rain did not matter, it had on both sides nothing but bookshops and stationers, and down the middle of the way too were spread bookstalls long and short, leaving only two narrow passages, left and right, for people to walk. On the stalls and in the shops, books and journals of every character were all to be had; among them some sold thread-bound books only, some only new literary works, and it was no rarity for one stall to sell several kinds of journal of differing political stripe side by side. If a man wanted to buy some banned book of the far left, the stallkeeper would fetch it out, from a crack between the piled books or from a wooden cabinet, to make the sale; and there were a few shops more improper still that sold even lewd books and pictures just the same, and their keepers seemed very skilled at reading faces, telling at a glance who was a real buyer and who a detective "looking for trouble."

Finding at the stalls no book I cared to buy, and just as I was picking over this and that, I raised my head and saw Fan Xing, courier directly under the Beiping Station, just then coming out of a bookshop diagonally opposite. In his hand he held a small roll of something—I could not make out clearly what it was—and when he in turn caught sight of me, he hurriedly stuffed the roll into the pocket of his short overcoat, and, while walking toward me, kept glancing back over his shoulder; he made small talk as he put out his hand to shake mine, but the look on his face, flushing red then white, was anything but natural. The thought came to me: "This fellow surely has something to hide."

I very much wanted to snatch the roll from his pocket and see just what it was; but had I done so, would it not have made things awkward, and what then of the second step? Better to pretend I had seen nothing, pay out a long line, and deal with it in due course.

Fan Xing perhaps had his own reckoning; from then on what he showed seemed more active than before, more fitted to the needs of the work. By any reasonable measure, this was not normal.

Over his case the Beiping Station put in a good deal of effort too—frontal, flanking, and covert alike; but the result was still that what could be affirmed was little, and what stayed in doubt was much, and it was very hard to reach a judgment.

What we did come to understand of him was this: he knew the languages of several countries—English and Russian—and could both write and speak them. In his everyday talk, what hung on his lips were all words to do with literature and art, and the books on his table and by his bed were all of that same kind, only he never once talked politics.

He said he studied painting at the Art Academy, yet his work was never seen; and when we inquired at the Art Academy, it was true enough—there was such a man, who had registered and often went in, but seldom attended lessons.

He often went to such foreign-flavored places as the Beijing Hotel and the German Hotel, seeming to be looking for someone more than sitting down to eat, and he was never once found in contact with anyone—which always struck us as very strange.

He insisted that the intelligence he reported came from the "military attaché's office" of a certain country in the legation district; yet he was never seen going in or out of the Legation Quarter.

Then how did the stuff of this "military attaché's office" come to hand? Was it "made up" by himself, and then fitted out with a borrowed source? That could not be either, for a part of the intelligence he reported was afterward confirmed.

Time and again we asked to meet, just once, this man who supplied the intelligence and called himself "Huang Runsheng"; Fan Xing always said the man would not. We asked again that Fan Xing bring the man somewhere, anywhere, and point him out to us, promising on no account to speak with him; and Fan Xing, though he agreed by word of mouth to try to arrange it, always put us off with "there has been no chance," and dragged it out again and again.

Fan Xing's two sweethearts—Jiang Taiqing and Peng Yaluo—were neither of them simple; whether the courting there was real, or served some other purpose, could not for the moment be decided; but it seemed to have no connection with our work.

What on earth was Fan Xing after? Could it be only for that paltry sum of a few hundred yuan? If the true aim lay in money, we reckoned this a very good bargain—it likely could not be so simple.

I laid all this data before Mr. Zheng Jiemin and discussed it with him; he thought the matter not hard to handle, and at the same time laid down several principles for dealing with it:

"If it is for money, we can satisfy him accordingly; what should be spent, spare not;

"Supposing he has some political background or international connection, that is just the working lead we could ask for; there is no harm in playing along with his game and carrying on a testing 'intelligence war' and 'political struggle';

"As for certain doubtful points, do not press to solve them at once, for so far we have suffered no loss whatever. From now on we should observe coolly, and on no account provoke him in word or act; best to build up a personal feeling with him, which will work a steadying effect;

"With the intelligence he passes on, we must from now on handle it carefully, keeping the originals, for concentrated review and comparison of the earlier with the later." We did as Mr. Zheng directed; what came of it will appear later.

My relations with Fan Xing thus began, and were kept up off and on, in "friendship," for a full twenty-one years. Not until the spring of 1949, just before I was to leave Shanghai, was this sealed gourd of a riddle at last cracked open. Yet even that was not the end of it; there is a sequel, and that belongs to 1953, in Hong Kong. I shall set out these incomparably strange turns one by one in what follows.

Do not make light of him: he was a man with quite a "background." Guess if you can—what was his true identity?

The foregoing account looks somewhat trifling; but in fact, at the founding of the work, it was indeed a jumble without order.

### 5. The Political Use of Intelligence Work

At the beginning of April 1933, Mr. Dai Yunong came to Beiping to inspect the work, with his confidential secretary Mao Wanli and a party of several including a code clerk. In fact he had other, higher-level activity besides.

After Mr. Dai reached Beiping, at first he lodged alone at the Beijing Hotel, the rest of his party at the Garden Hotel in the west city. Before long he rented, in a lane off Qifenglou in the east city, a two-storey dwelling with a court, and engaged a cook besides, and so settled in—meaning, I suppose, to make a rather long stay.

As for the true state of the Beiping Station's work, there was no need for me to lay it out for him; he knew it already like the back of his hand. He had no mind to blame us; what he looked for from us was that we push the work forward vigorously and make a showing.

To speak truly, we few were held back by our social connections not being broad enough and our social standing being too low; in fact there were many upper-level channels we simply could not reach, and, wish as we might to do well, that was easier said than done—it was a thing not to be forced.

How discerning Mr. Dai was; of course he could appreciate this, and in settling in for a long stay at Beiping he intended, in large part, to take the field himself and show what he could do.

At this juncture, because the North China situation was pressing, the declining old capital of Beiping suddenly became again a center of military and political weight; for a time the great ones gathered thick, and it was very lively. As to what the military and political chiefs were conferring over, none of it reached so humble a little unit as ours.

On the day after Mr. Dai reached Beiping, the man in charge of the Tianjin Station, Zheng Shisong (Wang Tianmu), came at his summons. It is said that he and Mr. Dai were old friends, and that even before the Special Services Department and the Second Department were set up, when Mr. Dai worked in the Personal Staff Office, they had already been partners in secret-service work. The time may be traced back to 1928. Now that there was a fixed working post, they were all the more bound to join forces and make something great of it.

The range of Mr. Dai and his men's activity was quite wide, reaching into every layer of society. As to its content, apart from the matters I had a part in, there was much I did not know. We had an unwritten rule: what one need not know, one should not ask or hear much of. Yet a man is a man after all; setting aside the thirst for knowledge and curiosity, still, seen and unseen, meant and unmeant, one could not help lending an ear here and casting an eye there.

One tale of much interest is how Mr. Dai Yunong came to "strike up ties" with Wu Taixun, one of the "Four Young Masters":

Wu Taixun, courtesy name Youquan, called "the Fifth Master" and not by his name, was the son of Wu Junsheng (Xingquan), the former military governor of Heilongjiang. Wu Junsheng was one of the leading figures "murdered" by the ambitious Japanese soldiery in the "Huanggutun train-bombing plot," dying at the same time as Zhang Zuolin. This "Fifth Master Wu" had never seriously studied a day in his life, and was a typical young man of fashion; only in the way he dealt with people and affairs, he fully earned the old phrase "open-handed and devoted to righteousness." Though he had inherited the vast fortune his old father left, he was by no means one of those rich men without a conscience.

The tale is that even he himself could not reckon how much property his house held. Since Mr. Dai and he had long had dealings, I too grew familiar with Wu Youquan. One day, after two cups of wine, in high spirits and half in jest, I asked him: "Your family has all that money—do you know where it came from?" He caught my meaning, and, taking no offense, told me: "My father didn't scrape this money off the backs of the people! In the old days the country folk of Heilongjiang all burned as firewood the soybeans they could not eat, thinking it no waste; later, when someone put them wise, they learned they could sell them to foreigners for good money. We bought up the surplus beans from the country folk and sold them on, and that is how the money was laid by." How much truth there is in this account I dare not rashly judge, and can only let it pass. Perhaps this noble young man was so guileless that in his own mind he believed it truly so.

Wu Youquan never did join our work, nor was his name ever formally entered in our internal personnel records. So far as I know, he gave the work under Mr. Dai's leadership real help of many kinds—besides manpower, financial support as well. From April 1932 right down to after the victory in the War of Resistance, whether at Beiping, Shanghai, or Hong Kong, he kept up close comings and goings with Mr. Dai.

Mr. Dai treated this noble young man with much courtesy, and had a real mind to bring him along and temper him, hoping he might do something worthwhile. So during the War of Resistance a plan was once made to send Wu Youquan and our working comrades Li Guochen and Wu Anzhi, following General Li Du, into the Northeast by a roundabout route, to organize a Volunteer Army together and attempt something great. But the plan was blocked at every turn, and they never set out.

Unhappily, when Mr. Dai perished in the air crash of 1946, the Juntong no longer kept close ties with Wu Youquan. Though Mr. Zheng Jiemin and Mr. Mao Renfeng both had occasional contact with Wu Youquan, it was never again the closeness of Mr. Dai's lifetime.

For Wu Youquan personally, a still unhappier thing befell: in 1948, Wu Youquan's wife, Miss Zhu the Ninth (by name Yuan, ninth sister of Miss Zhu the Fifth, and daughter of Zhu Qiqian, finance minister of the Beiyang government), on a flight to Hong Kong, was killed when the plane struck a mountain. The many precious jewels she carried were all scorched; certain passbooks, receipts, and securities were burnt up too—a truly fatal blow. In matters of money it had always been Miss Zhu the Ninth who kept charge, while Wu Youquan himself understood little; once the accident came, the loss was hard to mend. By Wu Youquan's account, the deposits recovered afterward came to a very limited sum too. From then the family declined, and fell past all recovery.

Wu Youquan lived in reduced straits in Hong Kong a long while, and in 1957 died of liver cancer, aged only forty-odd. At the end he suffered terribly, so poor that he could not even pay his mutual-aid society's one hundred Hong Kong dollars a month. To think of it brings a great bitterness. What may console him in the world below is that his two sons are grown to manhood, each with his own attainments.

To speak fairly, Wu Youquan did indeed give the Juntong a great deal of help; only it is a pity it was never set down on the Juntong's account. Now, with the writer for witness, in my *Nameless Heroes* I count him as a "nameless hero" and pay him my respects.

Of course, Mr. Dai's activity in Beiping was not all built up through Wu Youquan's connection. But following this line of Wu Youquan's, he did indeed come into contact with many worthies and talents of Northeastern origin. Among those I knew were Li Tiancai, Guan Jiyu, Wang Zhuoran, and others. There were besides young officers, serving or retired, such as Wang Yizhe, Yuan Chonggu, and Feng Yong.

Of these men, some later formed working ties, some got no further than a first meeting or the building of a certain measure of tacit understanding. I recall that Mr. Dai appointed me to keep in touch with two of them, Li Tiancai and Guan Jiyu. The "keeping in touch" here meant passing letters for them and fixing a time of meeting; as to what business they were specifically about, I did not know.

After a while I came gradually to see that Mr. Dai's doings were not confined to intelligence work, but reached beyond it into a higher form of political operation. In that period, such things as reassuring the serving generals of the Northeast Army and winning over the eminent men of Northeastern origin may all be reckoned within the scope of that political operation. Without solid social standing and a powerful political background, none of it could have been done well.

Having written this far, I am drawn on to a matter of later date—call it a consequence of the political operation just described. I take the chance to mention it here.

In 1934 Mr. Zhang Xueliang returned from a tour of study in Europe and took the post of deputy commander-in-chief of the Bandit-Suppression Headquarters for the three provinces of Henan, Hubei, and Anhui, quartered at Hankou. Perhaps having caught in Europe some political current, he seemed to have changed a little in thought, and so proposed to the concurrent commander-in-chief, Chairman Chiang, that the secret political organization already existing in the Northeast and Beiping—the "Society to Protect the East"—be dissolved; and asked that Commander-in-Chief Chiang appoint cadres to join with the cadres of the former "Society to Protect the East," after its dissolution, in forming together a new political body.

Mr. Zhang Xueliang's idea was to form a political organization inclined toward "Fascism"; Chairman Chiang consented only to appoint cadres to form a body upholding the Chinese tradition—propriety, righteousness, integrity, and shame—and named it the "Siwei Society." Thus, plainly, the original "Fascist" flavor and its evident dictatorial leaning were corrected.

Those appointed to join the Siwei Society, on the central side, were all cadres of the Society for Vigorous Practice of the Three Principles of the People, among them Liu Jianqun, Deng Wenyi, Dai Li, Zheng Jiemin, Gan Guoxun, Ding Bingquan, Zhao Longwen, Qiu Kaiji, Han Wenhuan, and others. Except for Liu Jianqun, all were academy classmates. On the Northeastern side there were military and civil cadres both, among them Mao Zhuoran, Li Tiancai, Guan Jiyu, Yan Baohang, Gao Chongmin, Wang Yizhe, He Zhuguo, Liu Duoquan, and others. Whether the Lixingshe organization had by then been disclosed to the Siwei Society is not known; by my judgment, it was very likely kept secret.

Mr. Dai's position in the Siwei Society was quite important; indeed its very forming may fairly be called a fruit of his political operation.

The Siwei Society's seat was at Hankou; Mr. Dai had many other tasks and could not attend to it as well, so he appointed Qiu Kaiji, the Special Services Department's man in charge at Hankou, to take part in its affairs regularly.

The Li Tiancai and Guan Jiyu spoken of before had old ties with Mr. Dai Yunong, and had had dealings with him in intelligence work besides, so that Mr. Dai's relations with Li Tiancai and Guan Jiyu were quite delicate. My meaning is that they may have leaned rather more toward Dai Yunong.

Because the Siwei Society was a "composite" political organization, lacking, in faith and in thought alike, the spirit of being "of one heart and one virtue," it ceased activity not long after its forming. Or, as another telling has it, it was dissolved because a minority in the Siwei Society who had belonged to Zhang Xueliang's cadres were too dishonest, and had designs of egging Zhang Xueliang on to this and that. Whether so or not, I cannot tell. As for whether the breaking-up of the Siwei Society had any bearing on the later "Xi'an Incident"—that is very hard to say, and here too I will not presume to guess.

To speak again of Mr. Dai's time at Beiping: his range of contact was very broad, and, first and last, he gathered in a good many able men. These men did not all necessarily join in the intelligence work proper; in his far-seeing view, they were mostly a store of talent kept for use. In the ten years and more that followed, if the work Mr. Dai led could grow in many directions beyond the bounds of secret-service work, it owed much, of course, to this strong point of his—knowing men and using them well.

When he was fairly free, he did not rest either, but loved to gather a few of us—Wang Tianmu, Wu Youquan, Mao Wanli, Wang Yunsun (secretary of the Beiping Station, called the "Beiping know-it-all"), and the writer—and go out for a walk, most often to Beihai Park and Zhongshan Park. Sometimes too we would sit down at the Five-Dragon Pavilion in Beihai or the Laijinyuxuan in Zhongshan Park and drink a pot of tea, and now and then go to one small eating-house or another, each with its own distinct flavor. This was no idle pastime; to him it was a part of the work too—to observe a man and understand him, it is in the small and trifling details that you see most truly.

Brother Mao Wanli, who came with Mr. Dai, had duties much like an ordinary confidential secretary's, though at that time there was as yet no formal title. When Mr. Dai had other business, he, too, often lingered with the writer; we had many points of temper in common, and, standing on no ceremony with each other, slipped imperceptibly into fast friendship. Our friendship was very pure, and may be called a natural growth, without a trace even of any thought of "convenience to the work." From then we have been friends fifty years, and are good friends still. People call us wine-and-meat friends; I hold that in making friends what harm is there in "wine and meat"—but there must be a bond of true hearts as well.

The friendship between brother Wanli and me grew close beyond what our working rules allowed; it will figure many times in the perilous years to come.

Mr. Dai was about to leave Beiping for the capital; before setting out, accompanied by Mr. Zheng Jiemin, he called on acting Chairman He, and went in turn to the garrison front to pay respects to Huang Jie, commander of the Second Division, and Guan Linzheng, commander of the Twenty-Fifth Division. I think this was not merely a courtesy call or a leave-taking. What lay behind it, I had best not guess wildly.

The day before he left Beiping, Mr. Dai gathered the Beiping and Tianjin comrades for a talk at his lodging at Qifenglou—in fact, to listen to him speak.

The time appointed was ten in the morning; present were Zheng Jiemin, Xing Shan, Mao Wanli, Wang Tianmu, Yang Ying, Qi Nanpu, Bai Shiwei, Wang Yunsun, the writer, and others. Mr. Dai had done a little arranging in the parlor on the second floor of his lodging, just enough to hold ten men.

There was no ceremony at the time; before speaking, Mr. Dai addressed a few especially courteous words to Mr. Zheng Jiemin, who was present, and then began to speak. He said a great deal, which, summed up, may be put in four points:

First, he analyzed the present situation, at home and abroad, and stressed the gravity of the North China situation.

Next, he directed that the Beiping Station and the Tianjin Station strengthen their working disposition—that is, open up intelligence lines on many sides.

Third, he set out the intelligence needs of the higher command: not only to gather every kind of concrete intelligence bearing on Japan's military moves and its support of traitors' work of sabotage, but to note especially the Communist Party's political intrigues and its intent to fish in troubled waters.

Last, he asked that each of us spur and school himself, enrich himself, and strengthen his working ability, that he might bear the heavy burden.

Mr. Dai, having finished speaking, again very respectfully asked "Mr. Jiemin" to give some views. Mr. Zheng could hardly decline, and drew out a few points along the lines Mr. Dai had laid down.

Mr. Dai asked Wang Tianmu and the writer too; brother Tianmu said nothing, and I had all the less to say. The speaking done, all were silent, nor did we exchange any views among ourselves. This too was where we differed from the common run. Then we took a plain meal there at Mr. Dai's, which was as good as a farewell dinner.

Mr. Dai went by train; in those days there was no air service between Beiping and Nanjing, and the handiest way down was the "Blue Steel express," the special fast train of the Tianjin–Pukou Railway. The "Blue Steel express," so called, was simply a train painted blue, built rather more solidly, and fast, punctual, and smooth in the running—nothing more. When we went to the station to see him off, I said a special "keep well" to brother Mao Wanli; we clasped hands at parting and could hardly bear to let go. Mr. Dai knew too that we were fond of each other, and said: "I hope the two of you will work closely together."

A moment before the train started, Mr. Dai suddenly recalled a matter and charged me to see to it. I thought it something specially urgent, and listened closely for his instructions, when he said: "That car I bought for someone—look after it for now; it will be shipped off before long. And don't take it out for fun—if it's damaged there'll be trouble." So that was all it was; hearing it, I felt distinctly flat.

It was a 1931 "Buick," eight cylinders, open-topped, bought secondhand to begin with, but well kept and still a good eight-tenths new; taken out for a spin, it would of course cut a fine figure. This was a small matter, not worth a line here; but there just are such coincidences under heaven, and who would have thought that, in the near future, this car would win first honors in our work.

On May 4, 1933, Mr. Zheng Jiemin, "special commissioner of the Juntong at Beiping," relayed through the night the urgent sanction order handed down by the highest military and political authorities in Beiping, setting a term of one week to report back.

Three days later, on May 7 of the same year, the Beiping Station and the Tianjin Station of the Juntong, working in concert, put to death Zhang Jingyao—that former military governor of Hunan, that leftover creature of a spent warlord, blinded by greed and lost to all national feeling, who was willing to be a claw and fang of the Japanese invaders—in the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits in the Legation Quarter of Beiping, a special district where our government held no extraterritorial jurisdiction.

This forceful blow not only quelled the frenzied schemer's design to stir up disorder, but also shattered the plot drawn up by Itagaki Seishirō on behalf of the Japanese military. The influence of the case was such that it at once eased the extremely tense North China situation and steadied the frightened hearts of the Beiping and Tianjin people. It also played its part in allowing the preparations for a long war of resistance to be laid, unhurried, over the four years that followed.

It was learned afterward that the importance of this case far exceeded the common imagining; for Zhang Jingyao's treasonous activity in Beiping was no affair of a day, and, working on our highest military and political organs and our fighting units with every means of incitement, seduction, bribery, and deception, he had won over a good many senior officials, among them even military commanders holding troops, and was on the point of launching a "mutiny" or a "coup." Had he not been cut off in good time, and a disaster dissolved before it took shape, the consequences would have been fearful to contemplate.

The working unit at Beiping and Tianjin then, short of hands, crude in equipment, and having to report back within a set term on a matter come up all at once, yet did not fail its mission; we counted it, with some pride, a flawless and masterly stroke. And the man of the first merit in carrying through this case was Mr. Bai Shiwei, who is happily still living today.
