## Chapter 2. Secret-Service Organization

### Section 1. Principles of Organization

Whatever kind of undertaking we set our hand to, we must have a settled plan and a tight organization behind it; and secret-service work, being a most complex and difficult kind of work, must of course attend all the more to the question of organization. In speaking of organization, the first thing to attend to is the principles of organization, for it is only by building on sound organizational principles that any real organizational result can be obtained.

1. The secret-service organization takes absolute secrecy as its principle. If secret-service work is to win, it must proceed by secret methods; and for the work to be secret, secrecy must begin with the organization itself, since the secrecy of the work is sheltered beneath the secrecy of the organization. When necessity requires, an open, public organ may also be set up — as Soviet Russia has its State Political Directorate — but such a public organ is a mere outward form: the very marrow of its organization, and the center of gravity and manner of its work, remain absolutely secret.

2. The organizational system has only vertical relations, never lateral ones. The highest organ of the secret-service organization should be directly subordinate to the Party's supreme central organ, and unknown to people at large. Only so can direction be smooth, and action swift and secret.

3. The lower ranks must obey the direction of the higher and may not defy orders. But note well: when the higher directs the lower, it must proceed absolutely from the standpoint of the interests of the work.

4. Every cell under the secret-service organization should be both widespread and deep-reaching, so that the effectiveness of secret-service work may extend into every minute corner of society.

5. The organization must be sound and tightly knit, without the least gap or slackness, and must conform to the principle of being scientific.

6. The persons in charge at the very top of the secret-service organization must have passed through long and bitter struggle in the Party's history, possess a deep understanding of the Party and its doctrine, be resolute in will and superior in ability, and have both a deep grasp of secret-service work and rich experience of it.

7. The core cadres of the organization are like the framework of a machine; the personnel among them must have a deep understanding of the Party and its doctrine, be faithful in conduct and superior in ability, and have ample study and experience of secret-service work.

8. The lower cadres are the base layer of the organization; the working personnel among them must have a basic understanding of, and faith in, the Party and its doctrine, and must be given continual political training, and training in every kind of technique, by the responsible comrades.

9. The organization should be divided into three layers. The innermost elements are those longest in the Party's history, deepest in understanding, and richest in the knowledge and experience of secret-service work. Next come the cadre elements — the personnel who do the actual lower-level work — who must have ample understanding and experience of the Party and of secret-service work. Last come the bought-over and utilized elements, the so-called outer ring of the organization, who must not be let into any of the organization's secrets.

10. At every level of the organization there must be one principal and one deputy responsible officer, so that they may rest in turn and stand in for each other during sick leave or leave of absence, and so avoid excessive fatigue or any stoppage of the work.

### Section 2. The Detective Network

In secret-service work, the detective personnel are charged specifically with keeping watch on the movements of every hostile party and hostile faction outside the Party, and of the small groupings and suspect persons within it, reporting up through the ranks to the responsible officers above, so that suitable measures may be chosen to crush or forestall their activity, and so safeguard the Party's security and the stability of the power already won. They are thus the most sensitive eyes and ears of Party and government; the more of them, naturally, the better, for the more there are, the greater the effect they produce. Such personnel should be spread throughout the country, forming one entire, finely meshed detective network. The essential points to observe in laying out a detective network are these:

1. In point of organization, tight secrecy must be especially maintained; it is best to take the individual as the unit.

2. The personnel who take up this work must keep its secrets absolutely; otherwise it loses all effect, and the whole enterprise fails.

3. Those charged with counter-surveillance above all must be able to keep secrets; otherwise not only does the work fail, but their own lives are put in danger.

### Section 3. The Communications Network

The communications network in secret-service work is likewise of the first importance. Of all the parts of the work, it is intelligence that bears the closest relation to the communications network, for the two prime requisites of intelligence work are speed and secrecy. If intelligence is not swift, it loses its timeliness altogether and the whole of it turns to so much waste; and if intelligence loses its secrecy — passing into the enemy's eyes and ears before we even know it — that is as good as gathering intelligence for the enemy. We cannot rely on the ordinary, open channels of communication to carry our secret messages; we must therefore set up many secret channels of our own, forming one entire, finely meshed, and swift communications network across the whole country to carry our own special, secret messages. The communications network is inseparable above all from intelligence work; and under the principles of speed, secrecy, and close liaison with intelligence work, its organization and disposition should observe the following points:

1. Wherever there is an intelligence organization, a communications organ must be established.

2. Communications work comprises several branches: wireless stations, long-distance communications, and local communications within a given port or city. Personnel of this kind must undergo specialized training before being assigned to duty, and the wireless stations too must be installed by our own people.

3. Each communications station has one station chief and a number of assistants.

4. The wireless station must be set up near the communications station, but not inside it, lest the two be destroyed together at a single stroke, or a secret be leaked. The location of the station is known only to the responsible officer.

5. The radio operators, cipher clerks, and other personnel at a station must complete training and a term of practice at a secret wireless training class run specially by the head station before being employed. Where special circumstances require, a locality may also run a wireless training class of its own.

### Section 4. Selection of Personnel

The one great obstacle in secret-service work is the question of talent. Given sound people, no difficulty in the work is too great to overcome. And it is small wonder that the question of talent is not easily solved, for secret-service work is truly no easy matter: in it, everything without exception must have its solution wrung out of contradiction. Only with exceptional people — and with the ability to use them well — can the results of secret-service work improve day by day.

What special conditions, then, should secret-service personnel possess?

1. A firm political standpoint. The political standpoint of a secret-service agent must be firm — that is, he must hold an unshakable faith in the Party and the doctrine he upholds. Otherwise he serves Qin in the morning and Chu by evening, and the resulting abuses are past counting.

2. Fidelity. Fidelity means never, under any circumstances, surrendering to the enemy: the enemy's gold cannot shake our will, the enemy's blade cannot bend our integrity. But the foundation of fidelity should be built on a political understanding of serving the Party and the doctrine, not on personal feeling; for fidelity built on personal feeling will dissolve when that feeling breaks, whereas only fidelity built on a thorough political understanding and an unshakable political faith can hold fast forever, unchanged from first to last.

3. Alertness. A secret-service agent must have a genius for alertness, for secret-service work is of all work the most complex and the most fluid, and only by adapting to circumstance as it shifts can he meet every demand with ease.

4. Versatility. A secret-service agent must be versed in every kind of secret-service technique, for the work is many-sided, not one-sided, and at any moment must draw on this or that different technique to attain its particular end.

5. Seasoning, and the capacity to blend fully into society. As the common saying has it, a hundred temperings make steel and a thousand make gold; and so it is with a secret-service agent. He must have rolled through every sort of society and through every kind of hard and bitter circumstance before he can thoroughly grasp the real conditions of society and fully master the tricks of every trade and calling. Only after deep tempering can he become a seasoned hand, able in actual work to blend fully into society; and once he can do that, good results come of themselves.

6. The endurance of hardship. Because secret-service work is down-to-earth, low-level, and long-drawn-out, none but those inured to hardship and toil can undertake it. The showy and insubstantial sons of wealthy houses are all excluded.

7. A creative spirit. A secret-service agent must have a creative spirit, for the work is active and ever-changing, not rigid and static; he must at all times and places hold to a creative spirit and wring the solution out of contradiction. And since the work is very hard, and most apt to fail midway, we must not lose heart at failure, but strive on with the same great creative spirit — failing once, coming back a second time; failing the second, a third; and so on to ten times, a hundred times, never stopping until the aim is reached.

8. Courage, composure, and the spirit of self-sacrifice. Secret-service work is, in eight or nine cases out of ten, a matter of hazard. A secret-service agent must be bold in heart and careful in mind, and must have force of character and judgment; on no account may he be timid, fearing the head and fearing the tail, or given to half-belief and half-doubt. But the careless and rashly bold, who act on impulse, are likewise not wanted.

9. Zeal and a sense of responsibility. A secret-service agent should, to be sure, govern his feelings absolutely by reason; but the exercise of reason must take the collective as its center and starting point, for only then can it reach genuine zeal and genuine responsibility. If he takes himself as the center, then in all that does not serve his own advantage he will be cold and irresponsible — which ill suits secret-service work indeed.

10. Soundness of body and mind. A secret-service agent must have a strong and powerful physique; the furtive and shifty-looking are all to be rejected.

At the same time, on the negative side, persons who show any of the following points cannot be entrusted with secret-service work:

1. Those without a thorough political understanding and a firm political faith.

2. Those who cannot keep secrets and are fond of vanity and of showing off.

3. Those with an especially heavy preoccupation with money.

4. Those who have contracted a bureaucratic air, or corrupt habits.

5. Those who go entirely by personal feeling and subjective bias, blotting out the objective realities.

A word of explanation is needed here. To require of every worker that he possess in full all the positive conditions above and none of the negative points would, of course, be very hard to achieve; one must always go by the principle of "discarding the short and taking the long," making use of a man's strong points and avoiding his weak ones. But whatever else, the two basic conditions — a firm political standpoint and fidelity — simply cannot be dispensed with. Even in a time of dearth of talent one must not recruit at random, but must hold to the principle of "better a vacancy than a poor fit"; for secret-service work is unlike other work — as the saying goes, a general is easy to recruit and hard to dismiss. At the same time, in choosing people one must take the person himself as the standard, and not take a man because he is a relative, a fellow-townsman, a friend, or bound by some other tie of feeling — nor reject one for the lack of such a tie. To do so would be most unsuitable in secret-service work; caution here is essential.

Furthermore, no one is born with knowledge; knowledge comes by learning. The responsible officers above should stand in the spirit of education toward the personnel below, guiding them continually through every kind of concrete problem; and the personnel below should receive this with an open mind, bringing to the work, in every particular, the spirit of a learner. Only after long cultivation can a man be tempered into an all-round hand at secret-service work.

### Section 5. Discipline

#### I. Why Must Secret-Service Work Have a Discipline of Steel?

Discipline is the body of rules that the members of a group mutually observe; its function is to maintain the order of the whole group and the peace of its members. In general, the more complex and the more secret an organization, the sterner its discipline — witness a state's national law, an army's military law, and the gang rules of the Green and Red Gangs. Since secret-service work has an organization of a special kind, it must of course be furnished with a special discipline of steel; of this there can be no doubt. For the sake of clarity, let us set out the reasons at greater length:

1. Secret-service personnel are the finest elements picked out from among the Party members. A Party member who breaks the law is punished more heavily than an ordinary person; a secret-service agent who breaks the law, more heavily again than an ordinary Party member — punished on the one hand from the standpoint of the Party, and on the other from the standpoint of secret-service work.

2. Secret-service personnel are treated more generously than ordinary officials, and they stand besides in a position of setting an example by their own conduct; should they then knowingly break a law, they ought all the more to be dealt with severely.

3. Secret-service personnel have received special training, possess special abilities, and hold special authority; and since their work is largely secret in nature, they may all too easily fall into such offenses as fraud, malpractice, taking bribes, deceit, and using public position for private ends. Without a discipline of steel to hold them within bounds, the prospect does not bear thinking of.

4. The secret-service organization is exceedingly broad and complex; without a discipline of steel to bind it together, it cannot be kept in good order, every part answering to the pull of the main rope.

#### II. Points to Observe in Fixing Discipline

1. In principle, apart from personnel hired on a temporary basis, there should be no penalty of expulsion or suspension within the secret-service organization. If someone's character is unsuited to secret-service work, he ought never to have been let in to begin with; and once in, he has come to know something of its secrets — so how can he be allowed to carry those secrets out again? If he is judged past all remedy, he should be put to death or placed in confinement. If his offense is judged to have been unintentional, or due to a want of learning, he should be given the penalty of admonition, warning, demotion, or reassignment, and the work of educating him should be intensified so as to make good his deficiency.

2. There should be no penalty of docking pay within the secret-service organization. Since an agent's remuneration is fixed according to his personal needs, to dock the pay due him would leave him in dread for his livelihood and, far from improving his work, would set it back — which surely runs clean against the very intent of laying down discipline.

3. What acts are violations of discipline? By way of example: (1) slacking at work; (2) disobedience; (3) dereliction of duty; (4) leaking secrets; (5) colluding with the enemy; (6) selling information; (7) absconding with funds; (8) deceit and concealment; (9) leaving the organization of one's own accord; (10) taking bribes, and disloyalty.

For personnel who violate discipline, the following punishments are to be applied according to the gravity of the case: (1) oral admonition, warning, or reprimand; (2) written admonition, warning, or reprimand; (3) reassignment; (4) demotion; (5) confinement; (6) the death penalty.

4. Secret-service discipline should be set down in explicit written regulations, handed to a worker to read the moment he arrives and explained to him in full; he is asked whether he can abide by them, and if he agrees he must sign in his own hand. Once a worker knows the regulations, he can watch himself at all times as to what conduct violates discipline and must not be done, and so avoid punishment. Were secret-service discipline not laid down in explicit written regulations — were a man not told beforehand what counts as an offense, and were the questions of whether he had violated discipline, and whether his punishment should be light or heavy, left wholly to one person's passing moods — his joys and angers, his griefs and delights — it would be no better than a benighted despot of the old autocratic age "putting his subjects to death without having taught them," with everyone in fear for himself; and what discipline could then be spoken of at all?

5. The secret-service organization should have a secret confinement room.

6. In punishing a worker one must stand on the standpoint of the interests of the organization and the work, not go by personal feeling, and must explain the grounds of the punishment in full, so that the person disciplined submits with a willing heart and, though punished for his offense, gains withal a measure of knowledge and experience. At the same time, the handling of a given person's discipline is made public within the organization, and this serves two purposes: first, to deepen the whole body of workers' impression of discipline; second, to punish one and so warn a hundred, making of the occasion a chance to educate the comrades.

### Section 6. Treatment and Remuneration

The mission of secret-service personnel — its weight and its solemnity — has already been described in the previous section. They hand over their lives and their freedom wholly to the organization; one may therefore say that a man has become the organization's own. To put it another way, in joining the organization to serve it, he must live for the organization and die for it; and so the organization's treatment of him should be especially generous, more so than that of ordinary officials, and must safeguard his safety. We must be clear that the meaning of treating secret-service personnel with special generosity and of guarding their safety lies not in the personnel themselves, but in their work — and, indirectly, in the organization itself; for once the organization has entirely resolved the difficulties of their livelihood, they can naturally give themselves to its work single-mindedly and without a care. Otherwise, serving on an empty stomach, they may wish to work hard, but with body and mind unsettled they can never produce good results!

#### I. Material Treatment

1. Types.

a. Living allowance. This provision is meant solely to maintain the personal livelihood of the working comrade. As for the rent of the place he lives in, that should still be paid separately by the organization, since his private dwelling is also his place of work.

b. Relief allowance. The living allowance is for the expenses of daily life; but when he meets with some extraordinary event — a wedding or a funeral, a birth, flood or fire, the destruction of his house, whether his own or his family's — the organization should give him a separate subsidy, so that his mind is not divided and his work not affected.

c. Family subsidy. Most people, once grown, carry the burden of a family; and since a secret-service agent has already given his life and freedom to the organization and cannot take up any separate livelihood to provide for his household, the support of his family should of course be the organization's charge.

d. Children's education fee. A child's parents have given their lives, their freedom, and all else to the secret-service organization, and naturally have no spare energy or time to educate him; the organization should therefore take full responsibility, providing schools of its own for the personnel's children to attend — much as the schools run by the Shanghai Municipal Council are set aside for the children of its Settlement police. Failing that, it must at least furnish the funds for their education.

e. Medical fee. Even a machine of steel, after long use, will develop faults; how much more a creature of flesh — however healthy, he will at times fall ill, from overwork, from the changes of hot and cold weather, or from the assault of infectious germs. The gravity and duration of his illness bear at every point on the scale of the work's effectiveness within the organization; so, if the secret-service organization is a sound one, it will at once send him to a hospital of its own to be treated, and give him besides an allowance for convalescence. Failing that, it must at least furnish his medicine fees and have him treated at an ordinary hospital.

2. The standards of treatment.

a. The cost of living of the place where he is stationed.

b. Actual need.

#### II. Rewards

a. Oral commendation. b. Written commendation. c. Promotion in grade.

#### III. Safety Guarantees

The life and freedom of a secret-service agent are not his own but belong to the secret-service organization; and every sacrifice he makes is likewise for his political faith and the needs of the organization. He is like a first-rate bomb: when it is thrown — and only as a last resort — it must be made to fetch ten times its price, while in ordinary times it must be stored away with care, to avoid any pointless sacrifice. And so, in ordinary times, the organization should give an agent's life a guarantee of safety; such a guarantee is nothing other than the husbanding of the organization's own strength.

From the foregoing it will be seen that the specially generous treatment a secret-service agent enjoys is in truth no windfall, but is given for the sake of the smooth progress of the organization's work. The generous treatment the organization gives him is neither a bounty of favor nor a pointless extravagance, but has its own weighty meaning and its own price.

But we should take care not to transplant into the secret-service organization the mistaken ways in which ordinary employees are treated in society at large — for instance:

1. Raising a man's salary because he has done a comparatively successful piece of work.

2. Handing him a lavish bonus because he has broken up one of the enemy's organs.

We know that rewarding secret-service personnel is a proper matter under the principle of reward and punishment; it is not meant to raise their appetite for the good life, nor to stir up in them a vain and arrogant frame of mind. What a secret-service agent must most avoid is precisely too high an appetite for living and too heavy a vanity and arrogance; these are the chief causes that drive a man to corruption and ruin, until his future is such that without large sums of money he can neither work nor live. Moreover, in the principles of secret-service organization it was said that the division into core, cadre, and bought-over-and-utilized elements is precisely our way of turning the profit-loving mind of people at large to the advantage of secret-service work; and if a secret-service agent should himself have this same weakness, then the enemy can turn the very same method back against us, and our secret-service work will fail utterly — a danger too dreadful to contemplate!

### Section 7. Training

Secret-service work is a most special kind of work, which none but people of special qualities can undertake — as we have said plainly again and again. But society sets up no public school for secret-service work, and there is thus no ready supply of trained agents to be recruited at will; we can only choose the abler elements from among the Party members (or from others in society who think as we do), put them through rigorous training, and then assign them to duty. Let us discuss the question of training point by point:

#### I. How to Select Personnel for Training

We know that not just anyone can do secret-service work; selection must be made before any training is given. The standard of selection, however, must be absolutely objective, not overly subjective or ruled by preconception, lest the suitable talent go unchosen and those chosen prove people not easily made into anything. The points to observe in selecting personnel are as follows:

1. Note whether the candidate's mind is clear and whether he has any other political background: if his thinking is muddled or he has other political attachments, he must not be enrolled.

2. Note his family circumstances and his social history, so as to examine his origins and his conduct. The sons of officials or of the wealthy are, for the most part, unable to bear hardship and toil and are ill-fitted for secret-service work; the rule is not to take them. And if his past conduct has been base, and he lacks a faithful character, it is likewise best not to take him.

3. Note whether the man has mettle, courage, and force of character. One who would let another spit in his face and wait for it to dry of itself — a dull, slow-reacting sort — must not be enrolled.

4. Note the man's intelligence and his physique. A dolt whose stupidity is laughable, or a sickly wretch too frail to stand up to a breeze, is in neither case to be taken.

5. Note whether the man has it in him to keep a secret. A careless, slapdash, dissolute, and unbridled person is best not taken.

#### II. How to Train the Selected Personnel

Training talent in our secret-service organization is a very different thing from training students in an ordinary school. In an ordinary school the teacher's aim is mostly to draw his pay once the lesson is done, and the student is finished once he has learned enough to take his diploma. With us it is otherwise: standing on the ground of the Party and the doctrine, and in a spirit of affection, we give our workers continual, unbroken training in every kind of secret-service knowledge and technique. Besides setting up a training class specially to carry on the work of training secret-service personnel, we must, in ordinary times, still guide and correct our workers at every turn amid all their concrete work and concrete problems. Let us set this out under two heads:

1. The establishment of the training class and the essential points of its training.

a. The responsibility for training must be borne by personnel firm in political standpoint and possessed of deep study and rich experience of secret-service work.

b. The training class must be set up in secret, and its front made to look commonplace — dressed up as a tutoring school, say, or as some sort of "preparatory office" — adapting to local conditions and fixed to no single pattern, the one requisite being that it not easily be detected.

c. The training class should be set up in several places, with no more than twenty persons to a place in any one term, and these kept from mixing with one another, lest too many come to know each other and secrets be readily leaked.

d. The site and premises of the training class must not stand isolated, yet must form a self-contained compound, so that no sound made within carries to the outside.

e. The equipment must be thorough and complete; there can be no making do with crude, makeshift means. The trainees' daily necessities and their materials for study and practice — the various weapons used in secret-service work, and the apparatus for secret communications, photography, disguise, and the rest — must all be amply and fully provided.

f. The trainees' own living needs, and those of their families, must be provided for, so that they may study single-mindedly and without a care.

g. The training materials must fit closely with secret-service work, and attention must be paid to practical exercise.

h. The methods of training should stress the heuristic form and the discussion form, and avoid the rigid, mechanical, cram-it-in form; they should dwell on concrete problems and avoid vague theorizing.

i. Discussion meetings and criticism meetings should be held often, so that through open debate and mutual criticism a common conclusion may be reached in thought, in outlook, and in the methods of handling the work.

j. Hold individual talks often, for only through individual talks can one thoroughly understand what a man is, and train him individually according to his character.

k. Awaken in him the spirit of self-criticism, so that each day he can reflect on all that he has done — mending his faults where he has them, and where he has none, spurring himself on all the same.

l. According to the depth of attainment, training is divided into three grades: advanced, intermediate, and elementary.

m. During the period of training, trainees should be kept from mixing much with the outside world, lest in their rawness they leak secrets, and so that they may study with an undivided mind.

2. Training in ordinary times.

a. Personnel already at work in the field after being assigned must still be called back in turn to the training class for further training.

b. The training of secret-service personnel is not confined to the training class; in ordinary times, at every moment and in every place, they must be given continual, unbroken guidance and correction in light of every kind of concrete problem.

c. Keep constant watch on the daily life of secret-service personnel, examining their thought and conduct for any corruption or other unwholesome tendency, and correct it at once wherever found.

d. Keep constant watch for any error in the methods by which secret-service personnel handle their work, and correct it at once wherever found.

e. For those at a distance use secret correspondence, and for those near at hand use individual talks, so as to resolve the workers' doubts and difficulties, or to correct their errors.
