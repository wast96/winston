## Chapter 3. Methods of Secret-Service Work

### Section 1. Principles of the Work

1. **Proactiveness.** Viewed within politics as a whole, secret-service work is naturally a negative, defensive thing; but viewed in itself it is the most positive of work, never a matter of meeting each crisis as it comes or of merely keeping up appearances. Suppose there is some reactionary group which for the moment has done no harm to the Party and the state: we must still contrive to destroy it, and not neglect it merely because it has not yet shown its fearful hand. Our first step is to send people deep into its organization to reconnoiter its movements and its aims; our second, to contrive to keep those movements under watch; our third, to wreck its organization and secretly seize its leaders. To wipe out a reactionary force in this way, before it has taken visible shape, is the most positive and the best method of all. Were it otherwise — were we to wait until the reactionary force had already grown and struck, and only then move to crush it — we should not only stir up endless trouble and squander much of our strength, but by such negative action lose the very meaning of secret-service work. In itself, then, secret-service work is a most positive thing. To put it in a plainer figure that all can grasp: in the sense of guarding the Party's safety, the secret-service worker is not only the best of physicians but the best of hygienists — when the disease has broken out he has, to be sure, good methods of treating it, but before ever it appears he has already laid in his preventive medicines and carried out his preventive procedures.

2. **Secrecy.** Secret-service work possesses a kind of mysterious power that rests wholly upon secret working. To surveil a reactionary group, for instance, we must employ secret techniques so that it never becomes aware of us; to arrest a reactionary, we must likewise go about it by secret methods. Were it not so, our quarry would certainly not sit waiting for us to come and surveil or arrest them. Secrecy is therefore of the utmost importance in secret-service work. To work without secrecy is to report one's own aims and movements to the enemy and let him prepare in good time — it is to wreck one's own work, and may even cost one one's life: a policy of suicide. As to how secrecy is to be kept, and all the rest, this is reserved for detailed treatment under the head of secrecy.

3. **Agility.** In any undertaking, to be without a method is fatal; but for the method to come too late is fatal also. If we mean to wreck some reactionary organ, we must have everything ready in an instant, reach the objective at the highest speed, and carry out the work of destruction by the most ingenious means. Were we to prepare at leisure and proceed at a crawl, the other side would catch wind of it and be long since prepared — and could we then still attain our end? Hence the saying in the art of war: *Still as a virgin, then breaking out like a bolting hare.* To be able to be still as a virgin demands absolute secrecy; to break out like a bolting hare is to be supremely agile and to lose no moment's advantage. Secret on the one hand and agile on the other, we complete our task in the space where neither god nor ghost perceives it. Agility, then, is likewise of special importance.

4. **Precision.** We may have been positive, secret, and agile, and yet if our reconnaissance reports are imprecise or inaccurate, all is still in vain. Take an example. We may know that a certain reactionary organ stands on such-and-such a road and street, yet not know the house number or the very room; or we may have failed to seal off entirely the lines by which that organ communicates with the outside — which is truly to fish with a torn net, so that by the time we come in to make the arrest they have already slipped away to a man. Is that not toil for nothing? Or the description may be unclear, so that we arrest the wrong man in the end — and again to no purpose. Precision, then, can no more be dispensed with than the rest in the methods of secret-service work.

5. **Universality.** Work that is not universal must end by grasping one thing and missing ten thousand. Take intelligence: if it is not gathered universally, what is obtained will be vague, incomplete, or inaccurate. For knowing the situation in only a few places, we cannot be sure the many other places are the same; and observing from a single vantage, the conclusion we draw may be wholly wrong. In its methods, therefore, secret-service work should be thoroughly universal and thoroughly penetrating — like quicksilver spilt on the ground, entering at every pore — so that its effectiveness may reach into every least corner of society; only then can the results attained be truly outstanding.

6. **Practicality.** Secret-service work is not empty talk, nor is it conjuring; it is rich in the scientific spirit, applies scientific method at every turn, demands evidence for everything, and must be done with both feet on the ground. A novelist can write a most beautiful novel with no need for any of it to have truly happened; a conjurer may perform before his audience the trick of turning stone into gold, yet nowhere in the world is there a single nugget that a conjurer has made out of stone. A secret-service worker may himself write novels or perform conjuring tricks, but he never carries these things over, mold and pattern alike, into secret-service work — precisely because secret-service work is neither a novel nor a set of conjuring tricks, but a matter of attending to the practical and of settling upon methods of solution on the basis of objective fact. Not one of the various plans in secret-service work is without foundation, and not one is left in two minds. In handling the work one may neither fire off big talk nor deliberately blow the scope of things up, nor yet make light of it as easy; one must weigh it out precisely on the basis of fact.

From all the above it is plain that in the methods of secret-service work — proactiveness, secrecy, agility, precision, universality, practicality, and the rest — every one is important and none may be lacking; and more than that, they are interlocked, links of a single chain, so that here too not one can be spared.

### Section 2. Things Absolutely Forbidden in the Work

1. **Bureaucratization.** Bureaucratization is the doing of everything according to a fixed, mechanical procedure, in the manner of official red tape. Its ills are many; here I speak only of those that bear most heavily on secret-service work.

a. *It destroys agility.* Secret-service work must, at many turns, adapt to the moment, suit its measures to the time, and act on the instant, brooking not the least delay. If one goes by official procedure, reporting up through rank after rank, then by the time the approving order comes back the moment has passed, the scene has changed, and there is no longer any place to begin. To take a common instance: suppose the Communists are at this hour holding an important secret meeting somewhere. Were we to follow the usual procedure, we should have to report it up — we draft the report and send it over; the superior deliberates, minutes his decision, has it copied out, and sends it back — and these several rounds of paperwork take at the least a full day; on a holiday or outside office hours, slower still. Even a verbal report may run up against constraints of timing, since it too must be approved before it is acted on, and the work will come to nothing. Bureaucratization, then, will not do.

b. *It destroys secrecy.* Official paperwork ordinarily passes through many hands, and is thus already hard to keep secret; the more so with ordinary functionaries who lack any training in secrecy, through whom secret information leaks out the more readily, since they count it the highest glory and satisfaction to be able to pass a secret on to others. Should secret-service work be conducted by the paperwork routines of bureaucracy, it must fail utterly and achieve nothing at all. If a secret-service organ is fitted out in the style of a government office, everyone will come to know of it — the household-registration police who check the census, at the very least, will have it clear. If comrades are received in the manner of an ordinary office receiving callers, then once the visiting-card is handed in the doorman knows your name; and even an urgent matter needing immediate attention must wait its turn like any common caller, which both wastes time and makes known to many people a comrade who ought to have been known to none. From these two points alone it is clear that secret-service work can on no account allow any bureaucratic mode to exist within it; as for its other ills, they are too many to enumerate, and I will say no more of them here.

2. **Empty formalism.** Empty formalism is the absolute contrary of precision and practicality. Secret-service work is work that does much and says little, and what it does must be practical and useful; otherwise it merely squanders great sums of money, and time, and energy, and gains not a particle of result. A plan in secret-service work must be such that one can take it up and carry it out at once, without a single word that wanders from the point — for it is no armchair strategy on paper. When the responsible officer says a thing shall be done to a certain point, to that point it must be done; his orders should not be such that his subordinates cannot fully grasp them, or cannot tell where to lay hold. The working personnel, for their part, must say little and do much! Again, if secret-service work has no foundation in the lower ranks, it cannot weather wind and rain: for a day that political power is in hand, for that day the service can go forward, but should that power be lost, the secret-service work collapses along with it at the same moment. Such secret-service work is a mere joke, and not what we mean by secret-service work at all. And it comes to such a pass precisely because its past work was all of the upper layer — surface work, hollow work, not done with both feet on the ground, not done in a practical, truth-seeking spirit and built up from a foundation in the lower ranks.

3. **The mercenary spirit.** If secret-service work is turned into hired labor, it can possess neither proactiveness nor universality. For proactiveness requires men who make secret-service work the calling of their whole lives, who bear its sole charge, who frame a plan entire and carry it out on schedule; and universality requires that in every part of the country, within every kind of association and small organization, we have comrades at secret work. The hired hand can only meet each occasion as it comes, confined to his one corner. Moreover, the foundation among the lower ranks can never be built by money and force; it takes political faith and a spirit of sacrifice, a working on regardless of all cost, before it can gradually be made firm. The upshot of hiring is this: with money the work gets done, without money it stops; the man's aim is to take the pay and not to do the work — money present, he comes; money gone, he goes; money plentiful, he comes; money scant, he goes. And should some reactionary group give him more money and buy him over, all our secrets pass at the same stroke into that group's hands — and what sort of organization is that then, what discipline is there left to speak of? Secret-service work brought to such a pass has lost its very nature altogether and become a marketplace bargain, or a bare policy of purchase.

The above are but a few main points. Broadly, whatever runs counter to the principles of secret-service work is harmful: display these various weaknesses to the enemy, and the enemy will use them to fasten a fatal grip upon us — and can we then still talk of wiping the enemy out? The making use of, and buying over of, individuals does indeed have its place among the means of our secret-service work; but such people are neither the center nor the cadre, to be used only now and then as need requires — it was never a method to be applied across the board.

### Section 3. Carrying Out the Work

1. **A creative spirit is required.** When the secret-service worker sets out upon a task, he must press forward in a creative spirit. For secret-service work suits its measures to the matter, to the time, and to the place, deciding its methods according to the needs of the circumstances; it has no fixed formula ready for application, and no ready-made precedent to serve as a model. He who does not throw himself into everything in a creative spirit will come to nothing. To attain this creative end, two points must be observed:

a. Fear neither hardship nor toil; go deep into the lower strata of society, do all that lies within your power, make friends with people of every sort, and study the concrete conditions of the local society — and then decide upon the methods by which the work is to proceed, and how the organs are to be set up and arranged, and the like.

b. Struggle forward with a will that a hundred setbacks cannot turn back! Fail once — study the cause of the failure — and come again a second time; fail the second time, and press on to a third, and so on to the tenth and the hundredth attempt, never stopping until the aim is achieved. Men of weak will, at the least reverse, commonly lose heart and go no farther, and in the end accomplish nothing at all — and the reason lies precisely in their want of a creative spirit.

2. **A plan entire is required.** Whatever the undertaking, one must first settle upon a plan and act by it, for only so can one advance step by step, entrenching at each, and keep all in good order. The more is this true of secret-service work: before the work begins, a plan entire must be laid down, and the plan for secret-service work must be especially thorough, fine-grained, penetrating, and practical — for secret-service work is complex, its parts interlinked, and charged with a grave mission, so that the least carelessness, one wrong move, will often bring the whole into failure. In framing a plan, therefore, on no account may one fob it off or scamp it or do it by halves, nor rest it wholly on the hollow fancies of one man; for a plan of that unpractical sort does the work harm and no good.

3. **The steps of execution must be fixed.** Whatever the undertaking, there must be a settled sequence of steps: too fast will not do, and too slow will not do. The more is this true of secret-service work. One must study the circumstances of the moment, and, according to the real need, lay down a rigorous order of steps, then carry them out step by step without the least confusion — and the work will naturally come by good results with ease. Otherwise, lacking the cultivation of composure, a man will panic when things arise and blunder ahead at random, and is bound to fail, with no hope whatever of success.

4. **One must be able to blend into society.** If, in his actual work, the secret-service worker would accomplish the greatest mission with the most economical use of time and the most sparing use of money, he must be able to blend into society; for one who can so blend gains, at the least, the following several conveniences:

a. He grows familiar with the ways of society and so is not caught out.

b. He becomes one with the society around him and so draws no one's notice; carrying on his work in secret, he is spared many an obstacle.

c. The more friends he makes, the more hands there are to help; with many hands all lifting together, things are easily brought off.

As to how one is to blend into society, this awaits detailed treatment under the head of blending in.

5. **Seek only the practical, not the empty.** The one thing most vital in secret-service work is to seek only what is practical and to set no store by the empty.

a. Measure your own powers: ask of yourself only so much work as you can do, and do not overstate the case or boast beyond your strength — else you will fail to accomplish the mission that is yours to bear.

b. Report just so much work as you have done; you may not deceive or dissemble in the hope of currying credit, and so lead the responsible officers, on the strength of an untruthful report, to frame an unworkable plan, whereby the action miscarries and the whole is brought to ruin.

Here the responsible officers of the higher ranks must take heed as well: they must not exact of the lower-level comrades results beyond what can be achieved, and so drive them to falsify their work in order to make a show of discharging their duty. To give an example: a worker is sent into a bandit area, and for a month or two, or even half a year or a year, shows no marked result. In such a case one cannot straightway blame the man for want of effort and withhold one's trust from him, for there may in fact be special difficulties of which one knows nothing.
