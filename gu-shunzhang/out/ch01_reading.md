## Chapter 1. Introduction

### Section 1. The Nature of Secret-Service Work

#### 1. What Is Secret-Service Work?

People are apt to liken secret-service work to the detective squads of a police headquarters, the informers of the concession police stations, the military spies sent out by a general staff, or the international and political detectives dispatched by a foreign ministry. It is true that secret-service work has much in common with all of these, and stands in close relation to them; but to explain it by those terms alone would make its meaning far too simple.

What, then, is secret-service work, at bottom? Its proper definition should run as follows.

Secret-service work is a secret and extraordinary kind of work set against a political background. To describe it with an existing term, one might call it political policing; to liken it to an existing example, one might say it is Soviet Russia's GPU.

Whatever the party, and whether it has already seized power or has not yet done so, it must have plans and arrangements of every kind — on the negative side, to consolidate the party's foundations and guard against the enemy's attacks; on the positive side, to work toward seizing power and to hold the power already won. Among all these plans and arrangements there is much that, pursued by open and ordinary means, can never or only with difficulty attain its object, and that can be accomplished only by secret and extraordinary methods. Work that accomplishes its political tasks by such secret and extraordinary methods is what we call secret-service work.

If the definition above is granted, then it will be seen that secret-service work contains two indelible characteristics, and that these two are bound up with each other, so that neither can be missing. What are these two characteristics?

First, secret-service work has politics for its background. The secret-service work we speak of is not the "special-duty section" of an ordinary office, nor the special-duty regiments, battalions, and companies of an army; it is an organization of an absolutely political character, charged with a specific political mission. Those who take up secret-service work must have a sound political mind and a fixed, unshakable political faith.

Second, secret and extraordinary methods are its very essence. In bringing secret-service work to full effect, one must employ secret and extraordinary techniques, contriving to shift from moment to moment and to baffle gods and devils alike; for without this, no absolute victory can be won. Those who take up this work must meet many special conditions: they must be both capable and composed, or they will surely fail and never succeed.

#### 2. The Mission It Bears

Every country has its own secret-service work, and every party its own; since each stands on different ground, the mission each bears naturally differs too. What, then, is the mission our own secret-service work bears? Let us say it plainly. We stand on the ground of the Three Principles of the People and on the battle-line of the National Revolution; under the direction of the supreme central authority of the Chinese Nationalist Party, we consolidate the party's foundations by secret and extraordinary methods, and we take charge of maintaining the power the party has already won.

Here a word of explanation is needed. Will the Chinese Nationalist Party win the lasting support of the Chinese people? Will the power it has won collapse midway? First, that depends on the party's guiding principles, on whether its members are loyal to the party and strive for those principles. Second, it depends on whether the party's administration can satisfy the wishes of the people. Only third does the turn come to secret-service work. For secret-service work is for the most part an effort on the negative side: it is no more than the eyes and ears of the party and the government, or, to put it further, the party and government's bureau of protection. If the party's principles fail to win the people's support, and its administration fails to satisfy the people's wishes, then the foundations it has laid are already shaken at the root, and no matter how well secret-service work is conducted, it cannot long preserve the power in being.

Does secret-service work, then, lose its importance on this account? No — no. When two parties, or more than two, are locked in struggle, and their forces are evenly matched with nothing to choose between them, then victory or defeat turns entirely on how the secret-service work is conducted. And in the case of "guarding against trouble before it arises," secret-service work is likewise of the first necessity: conducted well, it can often destroy a reactionary force before that force has ripened, and so, imperceptibly, keep power secure for a long time to come.

The heads under which our secret-service work proceeds are as follows.

1. To employ reconnaissance, assassination, secret sabotage, secret arrests, and other extraordinary methods to wipe out every reactionary group, so that the one-party rule of the Chinese Nationalist Party may proceed unobstructed.

2. To detect and sweep out every corrupt, opportunist, and party-straddling element within the party, so as to preserve the party's purity and let it flourish and grow, renewing itself with each passing day.

3. To use extraordinary methods to root out corrupt officials, venal clerks, local bullies, and evil gentry, in the hope of cleansing the administration and advancing the rights of the people.

Finally, we should recognize clearly that secret-service work is one part of the work of the National Revolution — indeed the vanguard and the special-operations corps on its battle-line. In the advance of the National Revolution, what sets secret-service work apart from the other departments is simply this: that it fulfills the mission laid upon it by secret and extraordinary methods.

#### 3. How It Differs from Ordinary Work

Secret-service work differs from ordinary work in many respects; the essentials are set out below.

1. Secret-service work takes the secret and the extraordinary as its principle; ordinary work is the reverse.

2. Secret-service work has, for the most part, no fixed form, no fixed time, and no fixed place; ordinary work is the reverse.

3. Secret-service work requires a tightly knit organization and iron discipline; ordinary work does not make these its sole conditions.

4. Secret-service work is exceptional: none but a person of exceptional qualifications can take it on. Ordinary work is commonplace, and anyone of ordinary ability can fill the post.

5. Secret-service work is pure, high-minded, and self-sacrificing, a struggle for the cause; ordinary work does not necessarily possess these qualities.

#### 4. Its Connection with Politics

It was said above that secret-service work has politics for its background; the meaning is that it must be built upon the standpoint of the cause and of the party. Should secret-service work depart from the party and the cause, it would not only lose its original character but give rise to grave abuses.

First, if secret-service work as a whole has no politics for its foundation and no cause for its soul, it will surely degenerate into a private secret-service apparatus, and those who take part in it will become the tools of private men.

Second, if the individual who takes up secret-service work has no grasp of politics and no faith in the cause, then as to why he sacrifices and struggles — for whom he toils, for whom he suffers — he will be utterly at a loss. He came only in blind pursuit of a living, and he will leave in the same blind pursuit; he is easily used by this side and just as easily by that. To lay secret-service work on a man so perpetually apt to waver, and to hope that he will stay loyal to the end and never change — is that not the same as trying to raise a solid Western-style house upon a patch of sand?

And so the very first article of secret-service work — its opening theme — is the question of political understanding. Apart from those hired for a passing need, every secret-service worker must be clear on this: Why do we uphold the Chinese Nationalist Party? Why do we believe in the Three Principles of the People, and carry them out? Why do we sacrifice everything to struggle for the cause? Why do we join secret-service work to destroy the reactionary forces? Only when these questions are clear can a man truly govern his feelings by reason and stir his courage by will, be loyal to the work, and strive to the very end.

### Section 2. The Importance of Secret-Service Work

Every institution, of whatever kind, arises out of the needs of its age, and secret-service work is of course no exception. To judge whether secret-service work is important, we have only to see how far the age requires it.

First, as regards the domestic situation. Under the impact of the currents of the wider world, doctrines and ideas of every kind are reflected across a China in turmoil, and reactionary groups of every kind are swelling within it. By now the sum of these reactionary forces is enough to shake the central government. If the center does not care to consolidate the foundations of the Chinese Nationalist Party, and does not care to preserve the power it has won, then let it be; but if it does, it had better make haste to find sound methods of wiping them out.

The facts tell us that, whether one meets reaction with military force or resolves it by political means, in a situation so tangled as this only the aid of secret-service work can win an absolute victory.

No reactionary group is ever formed all at once: as the frost underfoot foretells the hard ice, so such things come on by degrees. If we have a thorough secret-service organization and bring the full efficacy of secret work to bear, then, unseen and without a trace, we can keep constant watch on the first sprouting of a reactionary force and cut it away early, before it has ripened, so that no later trouble springs from it. And once a reactionary group has grown up and its force become great, we must all the more bring the power of secret-service work to bear — resolving the matter either by direct action such as assassination and sabotage, or by penetrating deep into the reactionary camp itself, reconnoitering the true and the false of its inner state and serving as the inside contact and the fuse for a military and political solution. Only then can a victory that never misses its mark be kept wholly in our own hands. In short, when it comes to crushing a reactionary group, no satisfactory result can be had without the aid of secret-service work.

I will say it with conviction: a party that already holds power, if it means to keep that power and not let it collapse, must first have a great cause and must truly strive to carry it out, so as to win the people's true and lasting support; and it must, second, have an invisible force that, working in secret, destroys reactionary forces of every kind as they arise, and so guards the safety of the party and its power. That invisible force is secret-service work.

Second, as regards the international situation. As society advances by the day, the rivalry among nations grows by the day more complex. It is not only when two sides have already come to blows that they count as at war, nor is the outcome decided by the troops each has under arms at the moment of battle; in peacetime too there must be active preparation. In diplomacy, in the economy, in commerce, in finance, and the rest, there is not a moment when nations are not scheming against one another in the struggle for advantage. Amid this dazzling and ferocious contest there is one matter to which every nation alike gives heed: how to attain the way of "knowing oneself and knowing the enemy" — for only by "knowing oneself and knowing the enemy" can one "win a hundred battles out of a hundred."

How are we to guard our own secrets so that the enemy cannot spy them out? And, at the same time, how are we to spy out the enemy's secrets so as to meet him as occasion serves? Such questions all fall within the province that secret-service work exists to resolve.

The British treasury spends some five million gold pounds a year on "secret" outlays; Japan's military budget takes up half of its total expenditure, and the "secret" fund in turn takes up a third of that military budget; while the rigor of the Soviet Union's secret-service organization and the vastness of its "secret" expenditure need no telling. Other countries too — Britain, America, Germany, France, Italy, and the rest — one and all give secret-service work the closest attention; from which one may see how important it is.

In our war with Japan we have known only retreat and never victory. The causes are of course many, but no one can deny that our secret-service work falling short of Japan's is one important factor. Many of our secrets have long since been spied out by Japan, while of all Japan's plans we can spy out very little; there are even many affairs of our own country that we cannot learn for ourselves and must instead glean from the Japanese side. Such being the facts, how can we not burn with shame?

That the Second World War will one day break out in the Pacific is granted by many students of the international scene, Chinese and foreign alike. If we hope in that coming world war to gain a little more victory and suffer a little less defeat, then of course we must brace ourselves and prepare with all speed on every side; and to secret-service work, too, we ought to give due attention.

Third, China's secret-service work — past, present, and future. That China's secret-service work is in its infancy no one can deny, and the reason is simply that its history is so shallow. Offices such as the sili and siji of the Zhou, or the xiangting and youjiao of the Qin and Han, were no more than the embryo of today's administrative police, judicial police, and social detectives; they cannot be said to carry any sense of secret-service work at all. After the reforms of the late Qing, the detective section within the law-enforcement branch of the provincial police-intendant's office, and, since the founding of the Republic, the detective sections, detective squads, investigation squads, and detective teams within the police headquarters and public-security bureaus — these too were only a kind of hired ordinary police, or social detectives; in no way were they secret-service work of a political character.

China's secret-service work proper was born only after the Nationalist–Communist split of 1927, and it began with the C.P. — and a formidable thing it was, thoroughly versed in the military, political, and social conditions of the whole country. The Nationalist Party's own Central Headquarters, by contrast, paid secret-service work no attention until lately, when the needs of the times drove it at last to set formally to work. But in these founding days everything is still crude, and trained secret-service personnel are felt to be especially wanting. For secret-service work is the most practical of undertakings, one that embraces all things: the man who does it must, besides holding a sound understanding of and faith in politics, have a fair grounding in every branch of knowledge — above all a complete understanding of the actual conditions of society, and a command of every practical, ordinary skill current in it. He must not merely know a little of each but be expert in each; only then, once he sets to secret-service work, can he reach a pitch of consummate mastery and handle whatever comes with ease. Yet talent meeting these conditions is very hard to come by; nor is there any suitable book by which to study the art, and what books there are were written by foreigners and cannot be made to fit China's actual conditions.

We see how harsh is the environment in which the Chinese Nationalist Party finds itself, so that closer attention to secret-service work is imperative; and we see too how shallow and immature China's secret-service work still is. From this we arrive, as a matter of course, at a fixed and settled conclusion: that we must resolve to labor at secret-service work. In carrying it forward we must lay out a plan as a whole; its funds must have a comprehensive budget and a reliable source; and in matters of organization, remuneration, discipline, training, and the rest we must proceed with our feet on the ground, step by orderly step. We must govern feeling by reason, and never let feeling cloud reason. Only if we go about it so can the progress of secret-service work advance with each new day and a thousand li at a stride; and only so can it truly help the party to hold power, sweep away reaction, and let the party carry out unhindered the cause it cherishes.

### Section 3. The Scope of Secret-Service Work

The nature of secret-service work and its importance we have now made clear. The question now is: what is its scope? That is, what exactly does secret-service work do? This section sets it out plainly.

#### 1. Reconnaissance

Reconnaissance means seeking out every last particular of the other side's circumstances while he remains wholly unaware, as preparation for studying how to deal with him.

a. Reconnaissance directed inward:

(1) To detect all corrupt, opportunist, party-straddling, and other hidden elements within the party, and to report them to the center for punishment, so that the party itself may remain forever pure and sound.

(2) To detect the corrupt officials and venal clerks of the government, together with the local bullies and evil gentry who band with them in wrongdoing, so that means may be found to root them out — letting the party's cause be carried through smoothly, the administration grow cleaner by the day, and the people of their own accord uphold the party with zeal.

b. Reconnaissance directed outward:

(1) To reconnoiter the inner workings of hostile parties and factions — their organization, their plans, their actions, and their strengths and weaknesses, and where these lie — searching it all out to the last detail, so that it may be reported to the party; and then, according to the other side's circumstances, to study how to deal with them: whether by a military solution, by a political solution, or by resort to direct and secret action — sabotage, assassination, arrest, and the like — to destroy them, or else to guard against their attacks.

(2) To reconnoiter every condition of a hostile state — political, military, diplomatic, fiscal, economic, financial, social, and the rest — so that it may be reported to the government, and the government, on the strength of the actual situation, may decide upon its policy of response.

#### 2. Counter-Surveillance

Counter-surveillance means that, once the enemy's agents have been identified, not only can nothing of ours be spied away by them, but their very movements can be turned to account to reconnoiter everything about the enemy.

#### 3. Intelligence

Intelligence means working the material gained from reconnaissance and counter-surveillance into reports and sending them up to the center, so that the center may frame its policy of response upon them. In compiling intelligence, three points must be observed: first, that it be accurate; second, that it be prompt; and third, that it be systematic.

#### 4. Communications

a. The communications of secret-service work are absolutely secret in character. They are set up for the convenience of reconnaissance, counter-surveillance, intelligence, and the other branches of the work, and are wholly unlike the Ministry of Communications that a government sets up in the open.

b. If the communications of some open organ are borrowed — the telegraph, the telephone, and so on — there must still be ciphers and secret signs.

c. The particulars are explained in the section on secret communications.

#### 5. Sabotage

a. The destruction of organs — wiping out the enemy's organs so that he has no means of operating.

b. The destruction of communications — severing the enemy's railways, watercourses, hills, bridges, telegraph lines, and telephones, so that he loses all contact.

c. Destroying the enemy's powder magazines, arsenals, ordnance works, shipyards, and airfields, together with his other large-scale factories, so as to reduce his fighting power.

d. Spreading rumors everywhere, in writing or by word of mouth, to set the enemy's opinions at odds, so that he is divided within and his strength dispersed.

e. In peacetime, sending counter-surveillance agents into the enemy's sphere of power to contrive the wrecking of his finances, provoking runs on the banks and like disorders, and a panic in his economy and treasury.

f. In wartime, sending counter-surveillance agents into the enemy's sphere of power to put out false news of defeats at the front, so that his rear is thrown into panic, the people flee their homes, and order breaks down.

#### 6. Protection

a. A party working in secret must of course attend to protective work, to guard against the enemy's reconnaissance, arrests, sabotage, assassination, and the like.

b. A party in the open must attend to the protective side of secret-service work as well, for the open army and police cannot make their protection thorough enough.

In sum, whatever the party, in all the work of purging itself and destroying its enemies, whatever cannot be brought to a result by open and ordinary means, and can attain its object only by secret and extraordinary methods, falls within the scope that secret-service work exists to resolve.
