## Chapter 6. Secret-Service Tradecraft

### Section 1. Disguise

When the effectiveness of secret-service work is to be brought fully into play, the secret techniques must be pressed into service; of these much has already been said in the earlier chapters, and there is no need to explain them again here. Disguise, in truth, may be reckoned one of the secret techniques. The reason the secret-service worker must learn the art of disguise is simply this: to hide his true face — his real face of Mount Lu — and so, for the ends of secrecy, carry his work forward. The art of disguise may be studied under the following heads:

#### I. Disguise of Dress

1. The cut and color of one's clothing must be changed often; but let them be commonplace, never out of the ordinary.

2. Bearing, movement, and manner must match the clothes one wears. Bearing, movement, and manner should be practiced constantly in ordinary times until they become second nature; do not wait until you are thirsty to dig the well, or the result will be that, "setting out to draw a tiger, you get a dog instead."

3. On the head one may wear a felt "basin" hat got up for disguise, which at any moment can be turned into a little melon-rind skullcap.

4. The sleeves of the long gown can be made double-lined, so that at any moment they may be turned inside out to show a different color.

#### II. Disguise of the Face

1. In ordinary times one must fully practice the various facial expressions — joy, anger, grief, delight, and the rest — until they become habitual, so that when a given expression is called for it can be summoned on the instant, perfectly natural and without the least trace of effort.

2. A kind of yellow wax sold in Chinese apothecaries can be smeared on the face to change its natural color — but not too much of it.

3. False hair and false whiskers, a nose-piece of thin rubber, false tooth-caps, and the like can alter the face's original shape. A certain medicinal drop, put in the eye, can make the pupil dilate or contract.

4. To change one's looks with dark glasses is the most childish device of all.

#### III. Disguise of Speech

1. In ordinary times one must study the various dialects, and study them to perfection, so that when a given speech is needed one can speak it, and no one can guess what part of the country one really comes from.

2. The spoken idiom of every class of society must likewise be thoroughly understood and practiced: meet a given sort of man, and speak in his sort of speech.

3. The expression that goes with the speech must also be studied with care, to lend one's words their full effect.

#### IV. Disguise of Identity

1. Disguise of identity is of many kinds: a man got up as a woman, a woman as a man, or one made up as a laborer, a student, a young master, an old master, a mistress of the house, a young lady, a married couple, a maidservant, and so on.

2. Once in disguise, one must humbly enter into whatever person one is playing — what expression such a person should wear — and rehearse it all beforehand, until it is entirely natural; only then may it be put to use.

### Section 2. Shadowing / Tailing

#### I. What Shadowing Means, and Its Value

In ordinary detective science, shadowing is called "tailing"; it is a most important task, and by no means an easy one to perform. The best way to picture what shadowing is like is to take the most common, most readily grasped thing for a comparison — and that is the picking-up of a girl on the street. Everyone knows that picking up a girl is work for clever men, not for fools: no blockhead ever brought it off. In the same way, a man who has had no training must never be set to shadowing work; force him to it, and not only will he fail to fasten onto his tail, but he may well be tailed in turn by the very man he was set to follow — and is that not a case of "losing the lady and the soldiers both"?

"But once political power is in our hands," it may be said, "we can arrest any suspicious man on sight and put him to the question — what need of shadowing then?" No, no; the matter is not so simple. First: is there evidence on his person? To seize a man at will, with no evidence, is that a thing the law of the land allows? Second: the aim of shadowing is not to arrest a single man and have done, but to wreck the reactionary apparatus, to obtain the reactionaries' evidence and documents, and to net their partisans in a mass. To reap such a harvest from one man is, as the common saying goes, "one unit of capital and ten-thousandfold profit." It is for this reason that shadowing is called a most important task, and no easy one to perform.

#### II. The Organization of a Shadowing Party

A shadowing party is ordinarily made up of three men. Why three? Because one man, or two, is somewhat too few and will not answer the need, while four or more is too many: let their order once fall into confusion and they are the more easily marked and spotted by the quarry, and they rouse the suspicion of bystanders besides. A party of three is therefore reckoned the fittest.

To give a concrete instance: suppose a reactionary is being shadowed. The first man is spotted by him — he can signal the second to take up the tail. The second is spotted in turn — he can signal the third to carry on. Again, when they come into a crowd, an alley, or aboard a tram or train, the three can spread out and take care that the tail is not shaken. Again, in shadowing an enemy one must guard against the enemy's shadowing us back, or doing us harm; with three men they can look after one another and not be caught off guard. From all these instances it is plain that a shadowing party is best made up of three: fewer will not do, and more is needless.

#### III. Preparations Before Shadowing

1. From the officer in charge, learn exactly where the man to be shadowed lives — what street and number — the cast of his face, any distinguishing marks, his height, and the cut and color of his clothes; fix it all firmly in mind and do not forget.

2. The three who set out together must agree beforehand and fix on a set of signs to stand for words: what it means, say, to wipe the face or the nose with a handkerchief; what it means to wipe the hands with it; what it means to take off the hat and give it a pinch. All this being settled, they must then practice it until it is second nature, so that in the act of shadowing they use it naturally and let no horse's hoof show.

3. If the situation is foreseen to be complicated or highly dangerous, one must prepare beforehand the means of quick disguise, weapons, and the like.

#### IV. Points to Observe While Shadowing

1. Guarding against detection. — To shadow an enemy and be detected by him is the most dangerous thing of all. Should he discover that you are on his tail, he can turn a nimble hand to tailing you back, and may even contrive to take your life. The man charged with shadowing must therefore be quick, alert, and versed in psychology, able to read to the full what every movement of the other man means. His own movements must be natural, his manner composed; he must let no flaw be seen. Should the other by mischance begin to suspect, you must at once think of some way to do a second, very obvious thing, plainly in his sight, to show that you are not on his tail at all, and so dissolve his suspicion.

2. Guarding against the tail's being shaken. — If the man being shadowed discovers that someone is on his tail, he will assuredly try to throw it off; of that there is no doubt. The shadowing man must therefore take note of everything about the quarry — the distinguishing marks of his face, the cut and color of his clothes, his luggage, and the color and number of the rickshaw or motorcar he rides in — and remember it clearly, so as to prevent his shaking free, or to retrieve the matter should he do so.

3. Guarding against the destruction of evidence. — Once the quarry perceives that he is being shadowed, then, if there is incriminating material on his person or elsewhere, he will surely seek to destroy it, so that should trouble arise he may deny all. At such a moment the shadowing man must fix a keen eye upon him, and mark above all the movements of his hands.

4. Guarding against the counter-tail. — If the quarry is himself an old hand at shadowing work, he will often turn some clever method to counter-tailing: not only keeping you from learning anything of his affairs, but even detecting from what quarter you were sent and where your organization lies. For example: having discovered that you are on his tail, he walks, as if it were the most natural thing, into a bathhouse, a department store, or a friend's house, and swiftly makes a telephone call in code to tell his fellows how many men are on him, in what clothes and with what faces, and where — bidding them come quickly to counter-tail. Once they have arrived, the original quarry contrives to shake you off; and you, thinking the tail simply lost and the matter closed, go home, never suspecting that someone is now tailing you in turn. This is but a simple instance by way of illustration; in practice it will surely be more tangled still. The shadowing man must therefore be alert and quick: first, that he not be marked and spotted by the other; and second, that, the tail being lost, he still guard against being counter-tailed himself.

5. Guarding against harm. — To fail to fasten the tail and be counter-tailed instead is dangerous enough, and galling enough; but there are times when one not only fails at the tail but has one's very life injured — and that is more dangerous and more galling still. To take the case above: should those who come to counter-tail hold the upper hand, they can not only tail you back but may on the instant turn harsh means against you. Or again, a quarry who has discovered you on his tail may, as though it were the most natural thing, lure you deep into the sphere of his own power and there take you alive — and then is your life still to be insured? In sum, all of it, everything, rests upon the shadowing man's own gift of alertness, his nimble hand and his lively frame, in adapting to the moment, in observing the objective circumstances, and in framing his own way to meet them. On paper one can offer no more than hints such as these.

1. Shadowing on the street. — The quarry walks in front and the shadowers follow behind, four figures in all forming two joined triangles with three diagonal sightlines. See the diagram:

Once ◎, ②, and ③ have all gone past, ① takes up the position ③ held before. Should the quarry ◎ enter an alley, a shop, a hotel, or the like, the three of the party must spread out on the instant and conceal themselves — at the main gate, the back gate, a side lane, in shops or other places thereabouts — making a pretense of buying goods, changing silver dollars, or breaking coppers, and at the same time keeping a nimble watch to prevent the tail's being shaken; taking note, too, of any quick disguise the quarry may adopt — the "basin" hat exchanged for a skullcap, false teeth slipped in, the shape of the face altered on the instant, and so forth. Of all the quarry's marks, therefore — hair, eyes, nose, ears, teeth, build, and the rest — the more one remembers the better. Should all three be spotted by him, they must report at once to the officer in charge, and a fresh party be sent to carry on the work. As for the distance between shadower and quarry, and the distances among the three of the party, there is no fixed rule: it is settled by the circumstances of the moment, the standard always being what best serves the work.

2. Shadowing by vehicle. — Should the quarry hail a rickshaw or a motorcar, then you must certainly take a rickshaw or motorcar too and keep up the tail; but note that a rickshaw cannot tail a car, and that a car tailing a rickshaw must keep in front of it and, at a suitable spot, get down and hire a rickshaw to carry on the tail. Should there by chance be no car and no rickshaw to be had, there is nothing for it but to note his vehicle's number, color, the time and place of his ride, its direction, and the rest, and to inquire afterward at the garage. In Shanghai a car's number is, in the concessions, white characters on a black ground, and in the Chinese municipality blue characters on a white ground; the numbers of hire-cars and of private cars also differ, and all this must be got clear. When a tailing car comes to a crossroads the distance must not be too great, lest the change of the traffic light break the connection; and by night the distance must be less still, lest the quarry's car put out its number-plate lamp or let off a cloud of smoke, so that the car behind cannot make out the truth of things. In tailing by car one must also be ready against his firing a gun when hard pressed, and prepare for it beforehand.

In tailing aboard a tram there is this to note: before boarding, watch the quarry's manner, his movements, where he stands, whether he is changing money, and the like, and judge whether he means to board; the shadower then makes ready with him. Once aboard, they sit in different cars according to their several stations: if I am in ordinary clothes I may sit in the third-class car, while he, in a Western suit, may sit in the first — each keeps his own reckoning in his heart, and there is no need to hail one another. Once aboard, note the ticket the quarry buys; there is no need to see the ticket itself to learn where he will get down — one has only to watch how much money he counts out to know it near enough. Buy your own a long-distance ticket, and at every moment keep heed of the quarry's movements and manner; but do not stare at him woodenly, or not only will he take note of you and grow suspicious, but others too will take note of you and suspect you — take you for a pickpocket. In boarding and alighting be quick and do not hesitate, or once the iron gate shuts you can neither get on nor off. Board it jumping on facing forward, right foot first and left after; alight jumping with the motion, left foot first and right after.

3. Shadowing in a crowd. — The moment the quarry runs into a place thick with people, he readily shakes the tail; so when the three shadowers find the man running into a press of people — a crossroads, a place where men are brawling, a bend in the way, a spot where a show is being played — they must all take special heed, and guard against the tail's being shaken.

4. Special shadowing. — By this is meant long-haul shadowing — aboard a train, aboard a steamer, in a hotel, and the like. This kind of work is especially hard to do; if you can shadow a man for a year without being spotted by him, then you may be said to have graduated in the art of shadowing. It is in work of this kind that the measure of each man's skill is truly shown. On a train, sit behind your man; best of all, find some way to have his luggage set near where you sit, so that when he takes it up you are startled awake. Only when he is worn out and drowsing may you snatch a little sleep. Contrive to set a special mark on his luggage, but one the man himself will not notice, so that should the tail be shaken you can trace him by the mark; and be watchful at the same time lest he abandon his luggage altogether and shake the tail without your knowing it. Here the art of blending into society is needed: if you can turn a deft hand to getting in with the steward, and have the steward help you keep watch, then the man will have no way to shake you off.

Aboard a steamer, heed must be paid every time it draws in to a wharf. Once, a student returning home from Russia, coming by way of Vladivostok, boarded a ship one evening; the ship had already put out when he found that an international detective was on his tail. Judging it dangerous, he leapt at once into the sea and made his escape. Next morning the international detective, finding him gone, looked at the tea in his teapot — still quite hot — and supposed that he had truly just then leapt into the sea, and so must beyond doubt have drowned. What the detective did not know was that, soon after the ship put out, the man had slipped over the side alone, swum ashore, and that the tea was still hot only because the steward had seen to it for him. From this one may see that this student back from Russia had skill far outmatching the international detective's.

In sum, shadowing work is most important and most hard to do; the man charged with it must be uncommonly clever, lively, and alert, versed in psychology, and must practice long at the actual work, before he can attain to that mastery which passes into wizardry and use his art with perfect ease.

### Section 3. Counter-Surveillance

#### I. What Counter-Surveillance Means, and Its Function

The meaning of counter-surveillance, put broadly, is to place the enemy's detectives under surveillance. Put in detail: (1) to place under surveillance — and counter-surveillance — the detectives and counter-surveillance agents the enemy sends into our midst; and (2) to send our own detectives deep into the enemy's ranks to serve there as the enemy's detectives, and thereby counter-surveil the enemy's intelligence.

To carry the point a step further: when the enemy's detective penetrates the ranks of our detectives to serve as a detective and spy out our intelligence, that is the enemy's counter-surveillance; and when we in turn put the enemy's counter-surveillance agent under surveillance to get at the enemy's intelligence, that is our counter-counter-surveillance. When our detective penetrates the enemy's ranks to serve as the enemy's detective and spy out the enemy's intelligence, that is our counter-surveillance; and if the enemy, grown suspicious of our counter-surveillance agent, puts him under surveillance to get at our intelligence, that again is the enemy's counter-counter-surveillance.

Counter-surveillance is very hard work to do: on the surface one must be the enemy's detective, while in reality one is our own. Its danger is exceedingly great. An ordinary body of men is short on the knowledge of detective work, and to slip an agent of ours into it is easy enough; but the enemy's own detective organization is expert in that knowledge, and merely to get a foot inside it is already hard — to work there, further, without being suspected or detected is harder still — and once detected, there is not one chance of life in ten thousand, for the man has gone deep into the tiger's mouth, and to think then of escaping the tiger's mouth is truly harder than climbing to heaven!

The function of counter-surveillance is very great. When we send an agent into the enemy party to do ordinary work, what he brings back is no more than ordinary news; but if we can slip an agent into the enemy party's detective organization to do counter-surveillance, then we can obtain a great deal of special intelligence, and things no ordinary member of the enemy party could ever know lie clear before us as the lines of our own palm. Thereby we can turn special means against them. Since, then, we know counter-surveillance to have so great a function, we ought to press on with the training of a good number of the shrewdest and ablest workers and slip them into the detective organizations of every reactionary bloc — and of the Communist Party above all — to carry out the work of counter-surveillance.

#### II. How to Tell an Enemy Detective, and the Enemy's Counter-Surveillance Agent

That the reactionaries send detectives into our own Party, and send counter-surveillance agents into our own Party's special-service organization, is a common thing; and naturally, by such means they can carry their anti-Party work forward smoothly. Now, to tell an enemy detective within the Party at large is comparatively easy, for a detective bears the marks of his calling, and when he mixes among ordinary members who know nothing of detective work, the cover his surroundings afford him is rather poor.

But should he work his way into our special-service organization, then, first of all, we shall surely harbor a preconceived notion — namely, that the men in our special-service organization are of course all comrades loyal to the Party and rich in the spirit of self-sacrifice, so how could such surroundings ever admit a reactionary's foot? This, in truth, is a mistake. It is precisely because our special-service organization bears a heavy mission and is hard to get a foot into that the enemy is bent on getting a foot in, to spy out how the land lies.

The counter-surveillance agents they send are the shrewdest and most seasoned of detectives; even attending closely we can scarcely detect them and lay bare their dark schemes — how much less if we disregard them altogether! In that case our special-service organization is set up for their benefit, and our special-service work done for their sake; ours the loss in spirit and in substance, and theirs the solid gain — and how great a loss is that!

Therefore we must attend not only to detecting the enemy's detectives within the Party, but above all to detecting the enemy's counter-surveillance agents within the special-service organization. Among comrades, in our dealings as individuals, we take the attitude of affection and sincerity; yet in the work itself we must keep a constant watch upon one another — and only that is the true showing of a common care for the organization.

In the principles of special-service organization it was said before that among comrades there must absolutely be no lateral tie in the work, lest secrecy be broken. What this means is that the mission you now bear within the organization, and how you are to accomplish it, you ought not to let me know, nor ought I to ask to know; but whether there is in your conduct any point disloyal to the Party or to the organization — of that I ought to take heed. And in the same way, of my conduct you ought to take heed. Were it otherwise, we should have no way to press on with the wiping-out of reaction and so fulfill our mission of defending the Party — which is to say, we should be disloyal to Party and organization, and slack in our duty! In telling the enemy's counter-surveillance agent, the following points should be observed:

1. A special-service man who often goes to places unconnected with the work, or often keeps company with persons unconnected with the work — his movements can hardly be free of something suspect;

2. he is often in special and secret consultation with his household;

3. his personal movements are often furtive, such that even the officers in charge seem not quite to know them;

4. his spending seems unusually free compared with the run of comrades — for a man doing counter-surveillance draws, besides the living allowance he takes from our own Party, a salary from the enemy as well; a man drawing two salaries is of course far more flush than one drawing but a single one;

5. his talk in attacking the enemy party is fiercer than other men's, and at ordinary work of no importance he is more diligent than other men, yet he has no better or more valuable results to show than the rest — as when the enemy apparatus he wrecks yields no principal figures, those he takes being mostly people of no consequence, or the enemy apparatus has already moved on, leaving behind only papers of no importance;

6. he comes by the enemy's documents too easily, and they are mostly of murky provenance — their moment already past, or forged by the enemy on purpose — and do not square well with what other comrades have obtained;

7. he takes too keen an interest in others' work, work he has no business knowing;

8. his eyes and ears are ever on the alert, and he is unusually secretive in what he does and in the things he keeps by him, as though he had some secret of his own he feared to have found out, or feared that someone was watching him;

9. he is too eager to be in touch with his comrades, too anxious to be on good terms;

10. in his everyday speech and writing he lets slip, unawares and again and again, the phrases and terms the enemy commonly uses;

11. we may now and again leak a false work-plan of ours on purpose, and watch whether the enemy comes by that plan, whether there is among the comrades any who pays it particular heed, and whether his conduct at once turns furtive and tense;

12. and if we find among the comrades a man of suspicious bearing, we should report him at once to the officer in charge while ourselves surveilling him in secret; and when it is proved that he is indeed the enemy's counter-surveillance agent, we are not to deal with him on the spot, but may use him to spy out all the enemy's affairs — and this is what is called counter-counter-surveillance.

#### III. How to Exploit the Enemy's Counter-Surveillance Agent, and Deploy Our Own

1. How to exploit the enemy's counter-surveillance agent. — The counter-surveillance agent the enemy sends is sure to be one the enemy holds most loyal, most diligent, and most seasoned, and his reports they will of course hold most exact and most reliable. Having found him out, we may meet his scheme with a scheme of our own and turn him to use, with no need to alarm him. Suppose the strategy we have settled on for dealing with the enemy is a certain plan: we leak it on purpose to the enemy's counter-surveillance agent, and when he has reported it back, the enemy, acting on their agent's report, will surely answer us with a "scheme upon our scheme." Let us then be neither flurried nor hurried, but, all unbeknownst, answer them in turn with a "scheme upon the scheme upon the scheme," so that in the end the enemy falls into our trap and their agent carries home a great wooden dummy for his pains — their work is bound to be overwhelmed by ours and to suffer a heavy loss; and this is what is called "using your own spear to attack your own shield."

To make it plainer, let me give an example. Suppose we have found out that within our special-service organization there is a counter-surveillance agent of the enemy's, nominally working for us but in truth working for the enemy, whose whole business is to spy out our strategy for dealing with the enemy and report it, so that they may meet us with a scheme upon our scheme. Though we have found him out, we neither alarm him nor expose him, but seek instead to use him and send him home carrying a wooden dummy.

For instance: we deliberately lay a charge against one of our own loyal and able comrades and lock him up; and when he is let out, we deliberately charge him again and cut his allowance, so that his livelihood is thrown into alarm. This comrade, for his part, deliberately makes a show of gloom and disaffection, and little by little, in a manner seemingly natural, lets slip to the enemy's counter-surveillance agent — betraying an intention to go over to the enemy. The enemy's agent is sure to report it back.

On receiving the report they surveil him in secret, and in the end, believing that our comrade truly means to come over, they send someone to make contact and take him on as one of the enemy's own workers — and we can then seize the chance to carry our work a step further.

Or again, in time of battle between armies, we may deliberately put out word that it has now been decided to send a great force to attack by the right road, making a loud show of strength as if it were all real, so that the enemy's detective reports it back; the enemy, taking it for true, presses his preparations on his own left (that is, our right), while in fact we attack by the left — and the enemy, having failed in his defense, gives us a certain victory.

In sum, once the enemy's counter-surveillance agent has been found out, we are not to put him to death at once, but to keep him always under secret watch, letting no true news reach him and manufacturing false news for him alone; and having laid our snare well, we let him report back and so bring the enemy to throw himself into the net. Only when the enemy's agent grows aware do we then put him to death, to guard against his slipping away.

2. How to deploy our own counter-surveillance agent. — When we send a counter-surveillance agent into the enemy's detective organization, then, besides spying out accurate intelligence and reporting it back as the ground for deciding our countermeasures, he may further employ the stratagem of sowing discord, and manufacture rumors of every kind:

a. to set the enemy at mutual suspicion within, so that his forces split apart;

b. to make them frame mistaken plans upon inaccurate news, and so, in the end, walk into our cage.

#### IV. Points to Observe in Carrying Out Counter-Surveillance

The man charged with counter-surveillance stands amid the enemy's encirclement, ring within ring; however loyal, alert, and able he may be, that cannot after all lessen his danger. We must therefore go about it with care, and not lightly send some comrade of feeble powers to fob the task off in a trying-it-out spirit. Should we set clumsy heart and hand to using the enemy, the end must be that the enemy uses us instead; we mean to make him carry off a wooden dummy, and the end is that we carry off his — which is to overreach oneself and bungle it, the spring silkworm bound fast in its own thread. Here, then, are set out for reference a few points to observe in carrying out counter-surveillance:

1. Be exceedingly careful in the choice of the man; one not equal to it must not be taken to fill the gap.

2. In sending him the organization is preparing to have him seek life amid ten thousand deaths, and he too must so regard it; therefore the organization must take full responsibility for his family.

3. In deploying a counter-surveillance agent the organization must take the single individual absolutely as its unit; save for his direct connection with the officer in charge, the agent must be known to no one.

4. The Party must openly and of set purpose visit punishment on our counter-surveillance agent and strike him from the Party rolls, so that within the Party and without all shall know it; thus the enemy comes to trust him, and his great work is made the easier to begin.

5. Once our agent has been struck from the Party rolls, his name should no more be mentioned, so that the memory of him within the Party and without grows cold by degrees and at last is forgotten; then his work will have the fewer obstacle by far.

6. Have his family, too, deliberately keep out of the way, in token of a complete break with the Party.

7. From the time our agent begins his work, he is to have no further direct connection with the officers in charge, and should no longer be let know the organization's news.

8. Intelligence the agent obtains he should hand to a member of his family, who passes it on at once to a shop kept for special communications; from there another courier flies it to the officer in charge. But at the foot of his report he should set a special mark to stand for his name, which the officer in charge alone knows.

9. Such a line of communication, for all its many turnings, must still hold speed for its first condition: where intelligence received, once its urgency is weighed, will brook no delay, it must be reported and passed on at once, and the officer in charge must likewise decide his course with speed, or give the agent his instructions.

10. Both the agent's reports and the officer's instructions must be carried by word of mouth, in brief and plain phrases, that no trace be shown; and where it is truly needful, they may be set down in a cipher agreed on beforehand.

11. The agent should not keep much incriminating paper about him or in his boxes, lest it draw suspicion; his diary least of all should record matters touching the work, and if it must, then only in brief, in substitute words — for such a thing is what men most heed.

12. In the habits of his life and in all he says and does, on the surface he should assimilate wholly to the enemy, taking it for his rule to draw no attention whatever.

13. It is best that our agent work long within the enemy's detective corps: the longer the time, the more he wins the enemy's trust, and the more accurate the intelligence he gets.

14. The agent should mark where the enemy keeps his most important documents and, watching his chance, steal them back, hand them to the organization to be photographed, and swiftly return them to their place, letting nothing be discovered.

15. The enemy, too, will surely demand results of work from our agent. Our agent may report this to our officer in charge, who appoints a seasoned comrade to feign the part of a man greedy for gain who sells off important documents; forged documents are made up, or ordinary papers of no consequence are taken, and this comrade is sent to hand them to our agent and carry back the money to the officer in charge. But in carrying out the transaction our agent must by all means contrive to bring one enemy detective along to witness it with his own eyes, or to let the enemy's officer in charge know for certain that the papers were bought from the hand of our agent — so as to strengthen their trust in him.

16. Our agent should at all times take heed whether he is being watched or suspected; if the enemy grows suspicious, he should at once take leave on the pretext of illness or the like, to dodge the edge of it for a while; and when he truly can stay no longer, he should contrive to flee back and hide, lest the enemy find him out and danger befall. Our own officer in charge must moreover have a man ready beforehand to carry on the mission, and slip him in early, that the work be not broken off.

#### V. Special Qualifications Required of Counter-Surveillance Personnel

Counter-surveillance is the most hazardous and the hardest work of all, not to be borne by the run of special-service men. The special qualifications it requires are set out below:

1. His political standpoint must be absolutely firm, unshaken unto death, so that no fine-sounding doctrine or theory can move his will.

2. He must be absolutely loyal, so that even under the enemy's heavy and unending encirclement he in no way alters his integrity.

3. His general knowledge of special-service work must be most ample, his tradecraft most practiced, his experience especially great, his skill above that of all other men.

4. He must have a good hand at social dealings, adept at company and adept at speech.

5. His outward appearance must carry a look of entire honesty and good faith.

6. His temper must be gentle and self-possessed; he does not wrangle with men over the day's petty gains and losses, and can bear what other men cannot bear.

7. Not proud, not contentious, not vain, not covetous of fame and gain, not given to tobacco, drink, whoring, or gambling.

8. He must be bold of heart yet careful of mind, alert yet composed.

9. Body and mind alike must be sound, equal to the most toilsome work — and he must not so much as talk in his sleep at night.

10. His judgment, his understanding, and his memory must every one of them be especially strong.

### Section 4. Weapons Used in the Work

#### I. Why a Secret-Service Worker Must Understand Weapons

On the active side, a secret-service worker who is to carry out assassination or wrecking must understand weapons — of that there can be no doubt. On the passive side, to guard his own safety and forestall the enemy's snares he must understand weapons no less; and the more kinds of weapon he understands the better, so that whatever weapon he meets he can take up on the spot and put to use — can even, indeed, turn the enemy's own weapon against the enemy.

There was once a detective sent by the Shanghai Garrison Command to take a prisoner out into the countryside to recover stolen goods. Out there the detective thought he would seize the chance for a little target practice; but after he had fired one round, the second would not fire. He did not know that the spent case had jammed in the chamber and failed to eject, and in his fluster he flung the gun down.

The prisoner, however, knew where the trouble lay: he picked the gun up off the ground, worked the action once by hand, the spent case flew out, and with a single shot, taken at his aim, he killed the detective.

Then there was a certain Wang who set out to assassinate a man, carrying a Mauser pistol. The evening before, in high excitement, he drank a farewell meal, made himself ready to give his life, and asked a friend to see to all his affairs after his death.

Next day he came to the house of the man he was to kill, gave out that he had a letter to hand over in person, and asked to be admitted. As the servant went upstairs to announce him, Wang followed up with the gun on him. His target was not yet risen; hearing someone come, he pulled on his underclothes, got up and opened the door — and the moment Wang saw him he fired. But the man threw himself backward on purpose, so the shot did not find its mark, and the servant was frozen stiff with fright and dared not raise a cry. Wang made to fire a second, killing shot — and this time the gun would not fire at all. What he did not know was that inside was a round that had taken damp; had he known the trick, then in that hair's-breadth instant he need only have worked the action once to throw the dud clear and go on firing.

As it was, unable to fire the second shot and fearing that the longer he lingered the greater the danger, he fled — and so failed of his purpose altogether. This came of his not being expert with the gun. And the intended victim and his servant knew nothing of guns either; had they understood what ailed the weapon, they could have taken Wang's own gun and shot Wang with it, and never let him get away.

To be thoroughly familiar with the working of a gun, then, brings many advantages and is of great worth.

#### II. Which Weapons Are Best Suited to Secret-Service Work

The standard for the weapons a secret-service worker uses is that they be easy to carry, wide in effect, and easy to keep secret: (1) the handgun, (2) the gas-gun, and (3) the explosive charge.

#### III. The Handgun

1. Protecting and storing the handgun.

(a) When the gun is not in use, take the cartridges out of the magazine; otherwise the magazine spring easily loses its tension.

(b) After firing, clean the gun within six hours, for the powder is salty and the barrel rusts easily; let too long pass and the rust will not clean off. To get it off then, you must fire the gun again and again clean within six hours.

(c) The cleaning oil should be of the thinnest kind — that sold by the Kwong Sang firm is best. When the gun is to be laid by, coat it with a heavy grease such as tallow or vaseline, and it will keep years without rust; when it is wanted again, wash it clean and dry with kerosene or gasoline, wipe it over with thin oil, and it will not rust at all.

(d) If trouble arises and the gun must be hidden away, coat it with vaseline, wrap it well in waxed paper and sink it in water; so it will keep ten years unspoiled. Fish it out when the time comes, wash off the vaseline, wipe it with thin oil, and it is ready for use as before.

(e) Cartridges in store must be kept from damp, but also from any place too near a strong heat.

(f) When the gun is not in use, release the trigger sear, lest the spring of the hammer inside (which drives the firing pin) lose its force.

(g) Gun and cartridges must be kept apart, so that even if the enemy finds out or steals the one, it is of no use to him by itself.

2. How to take the handgun apart, put it together, and clean it.

*Disassembly.*

(1) First find the gun's first "gate" — the first catch to be released; if there is a maker's manual, consult it.

(2) The new-model pistols made within the last five years mostly come apart beginning at the catch of the barrel-spring, or at the safety; once you can open that first gate, the rest is very easy.

(3) Lay out the parts you remove in their order — (1), (2), (3), (4) and so on — and keep them from being jumbled, or you will go wrong in reassembling.

(4) In taking it apart, watch that a spring does not fly off and carry a small part away with it, hard then to find.

(5) Spread a sheet of stiff white paper on the table as you work, so the parts show up plainly wherever they lie.

*Assembly.*

(1) In fitting the parts, mind the order of first and last, and let nothing be jumbled, or an error will bring trouble.

(2) If a part will not seat, do not force it with a hard blow; ease it gently into place. Only in the last resort tap it with a wooden mallet against a board, and lay something soft over it first, for hard struck on hard is sure to damage one or the other.

(3) When it is assembled, carry out the test-check:

— With the gun empty, work the action once by hand and pull the trigger; listen whether the hammer sounds. A crisp, clear report proves the hammer is sound.

— Load the cartridges and keep working the action steadily by hand, watching whether the rounds chamber and eject one after another in good order; if so, the gun will give no trouble in firing.

— Test the safety for any fault.

— Inspect whether the cartridges are of the matching bore, and whether they have taken damp.

*Cleaning.*

(1) A new gun must be cleaned, for the heavy grease packed inside cakes into gummy scabs that hinder the working parts.

(2) After firing it must be cleaned, for it rusts easily then.

(3) Where the sweat of the hand has touched the gun it must be cleaned too; in summer, wear thin gloves to handle it.

(4) Where the nickel plating has rusted, use a chemically made rubber to clean it, not emery cloth.

(5) The blued parts must be cleaned with kerosene.

(6) For ordinary cleaning use a smooth, lint-free soft cloth and the thinnest oil, such as the gun-oil sold by the Kwong Sang firm.

3. Handling the gun in use.

(a) A novice, not yet practiced with the gun, should not keep a round in the chamber, for safety's sake; when he needs it, one pull of the action chambers a round.

(b) A practiced hand may chamber a round and then set the safety; when he needs it he has only to push the safety off.

(c) When there is no help for it and you must fire, put the left hand behind you and the right to the front, turn your body side-on to cut down the target you offer the enemy's eye, raise the arm, and aim for the enemy's head or chest. Be bold, do not fluster; the first to strike has the mastery.

(d) Whatever happens, do not fire off every round; keep a few back to guard yourself and to be ready should the enemy rush in to seize the gun.

4. What are the important parts of the handgun, and why must we understand its temper?

(a) The most important parts within the gun are the firing pin and the hammer; inspect them constantly for any damage.

(b) Every kind of gun has its own temper: some kick hard, some hardly at all. Before you use a gun you must first know its temper; only then can you hit the mark easily, and only then can you keep yourself out of danger.

(c) You should shoot at the target often in ordinary times — testing the temper of each kind of pistol, and at once training your aim, so that when it comes to the real thing you can hit the mark.

5. The dangers of the handgun, and the points we must observe.

(a) A gun is very dangerous; in ordinary times never let the muzzle point at anyone.

(b) In taking the gun apart, assembling it, loading or unloading, hold the muzzle down — or up — if you are in a single-story house; in a building of several floors, mind whether there is anyone above or below.

(c) When carrying it on you, the muzzle must point down, and see that it can be drawn out quickly and without snagging.

(d) To judge whether a gun is new or old, look whether the bluing is still bright.

(e) Note, when firing, whether the spent cases eject quickly and without hitch.

6. Loading the cartridges.

(a) Set the cartridges in the right hand, gripping a round with the thumb, forefinger and middle finger.

(b) Hold the magazine in the left hand, the thumb pressing down the follower plate over the spring.

(c) With the three fingers of the right hand press the rounds down one by one, the left thumb lending its help as you go.

(d) Load quickly, and when it is loaded tap the magazine on a flat surface, in case the rounds have not seated evenly.

7. How to tell a good handgun from a bad one.

(a) By date of make — the more recent the better; taking this year, for instance, one made in 1933 is best of all.

(b) By country of make — (1) Germany is best, (2) Spain next, (3) Switzerland, Austria and Portugal after that.

(c) By form — the fewer screws the better; none at all is of course best, save for the screws on the grip.

(d) By bore — if the gun is small yet the cartridge it fires is of large bore, that gun is the best.

8. The distance over which a handgun's shot stays effective.

(a) By sheer effect — the revolver is best.

(b) A six-inch pistol falls off in effect beyond thirty paces.

(c) A four-inch pistol falls off in effect beyond twenty paces.

9. The bore of the handgun.

(a) A common six-inch pistol runs to a bore of about 7.65.

(b) A common four-inch pistol runs to a bore of about 6.35.

#### IV. The Gas-Gun

Types and forms. The gas-gun is a recent invention, not yet in common use, and the general science of arms has as yet no name for it. It was first devised in Japan, and China too can now make it; it is called a gas-gun because the round it fires is a charge of poison gas.

There are two forms. One is large, much like the malacca baton the Shanghai constables carry stuck in a trouser pocket, about one chi two cun long and a little over one cun across. The other is small, of the size and black color of a fountain pen, and can likewise be clipped on the person, so that at a glance one takes it for a fountain pen and never suspects it is a weapon.

Its make and use. The large one is a steel tube, threaded at the middle so that it unscrews; the charge loads into the long end, while the short end holds a firing pin and an open-and-shut mechanism, and at need one trips the catch and it fires.

The small one is likewise made of steel tube, fashioned exactly to the pattern of a fountain pen; the poison gas is driven out by the force of the primer's ignition.

Its effect and function. The large one may possibly kill a man; the small one absolutely cannot.

A man struck by the large charge comes round after ten minutes; struck by the small, in only three to five minutes.

The range is ten to thirteen paces for the large, five to seven for the small; beyond that it has no effect.

The purpose of this kind of gun is to take a man alive rather than kill him: the large is used when a few must master many, the small when seizing someone on the road and outmatched in strength.

Points to observe in using the gas-gun: if a man struck by the gun does not revive, spray water on him and he will come round.

The charges must be kept in a dark place, out of the sunlight and out of the damp; once damp, the round will not fire.

### Section 5. Sabotage / Destruction

#### I. What Sabotage Means in Secret-Service Work

The foremost aim of secret-service work is to wipe out every enemy and to safeguard the Party's recovery and keeping of political power, that it may carry its cause through without a check. Of the ways to wipe out the enemy within this work there are many. Some take a direct form — reconnoitering the enemy's situation and, under close watch, bringing him to sanction. Others take an indirect form, such as the sabotage this section treats. Sabotage, then, we may call a technique for eliminating the enemy by indirect means, and its significance is very great. Whoever takes up this work must study it closely and go about it soberly; he must never be slack or careless, and least of all may he boast of the thing before it is done, and so let it leak out.

#### II. The Kinds of Sabotage

Political sabotage — turning the enemy's every political weakness into our best opening against him — is the most important kind of all. Under it:

1. Strike at the enemy's leaders, so that, their head gone, the rest scatter like birds and beasts.

2. Wreck their organizations and their organs, so that their work is left with nowhere to stand.

3. Break the enemy up from within, so that their unity will not hold and their strength is dispersed.

4. Set faction against faction and work division among them, aggravating the contradictions within their party and government, so that they lose the people's trust and their foundation is shaken at the root.

5. Send worker-comrades in numbers deep into the enemy's body, to carry out every sort of small-cell disruption.

6. Buy their lower ranks with money, to do sabotage of every kind.

7. Having assassinated an enemy leader, put out false word that the killing at the enemy's own center was murder from within — a case of their cutting one another down — and so stir up their internal strife.

*[Here the source passes to the physical and chemical means of destruction themselves — the making, charging and placing of the devices. That material is withheld from this edition; see the note above.]*

By way of summary, the techniques of sabotage divide into four kinds — the written, the verbal, the chemical, and the mechanical — though in practice a single piece of work needs several of them at once to reach its end, and they are set apart here only for convenience of explanation. And mark this above all: in carrying out sabotage one must use the subtlest and most hidden methods, moving the fewest pieces to win the greatest success.

#### III. The Organization for Sabotage

The number in each cell is fixed by the actual work; best is a cell of three to five. The cells do not know one another; only the person in charge knows the whole; and the more responsible members are kept apart — for otherwise, the moment something goes wrong, the work is easily brought into danger.

1. The choosing of personnel. Sabotage is very hard work, not to be handed to just anyone; a man must meet certain conditions. To take the chief of them:

(a) He must hold a firm political faith, for only out of such faith can a special resolve be born.

(b) Bold of heart yet careful of mind, brave and decisive in action.

(c) Of strong will, and firm in his resolve.

(d) Without kin and without ties, without a care or a worry, looking on death as a homecoming.

(e) Seasoned by experience, equal to any turn of events, deep in his knowledge of the world.

(f) Clever and quick, adapting to the moment, full of resource.

2. The training of personnel. On the conditions above one sets the standard of training: one seeks out the right men, has them learn every kind of specialized skill, and then puts them to solid practical test. Their living should be made easy, that they may keep a professional spirit. Shun above all the man who begins like a tiger and ends like a snake, and the soft temper of the bookish scholar.

3. The conduct of sabotage. The comrade who shoulders this work must mind the principles of action:

(a) Secrecy — the principle never to be forgotten. If, in doing a piece of sabotage, we fail to keep it close and let others come to know of it, the enemy is sure to guard against it closely; the work then fails of its aim, and the operator instead comes to harm — and is that not a great loss to the work?

(b) Speed — this work is wholly a race against time; it must be done nimbly, catching the enemy unprepared, so that swiftly and deftly the thing is brought off.

(c) Cleverness — to this add our own resourcefulness: turn the enemy's every circumstance to account, meet his scheme with a scheme of our own, and by the subtlest methods, in each differing setting, accomplish the task.

### Section 6. The Art of Conversation

#### I. The Function of the Art of Conversation in Secret-Service Work

As the proverb well says, "A hundred sicknesses enter by the mouth; a hundred calamities issue from the mouth." And again: "He who can talk can make men laugh; he who cannot can make them leap in rage." From this one may see how much the art of speech matters.

A Western philosopher once said, "I have often heard men regret that they spoke too much; seldom that they spoke too little"; and China too has the maxim of "the golden man with his mouth thrice sealed." But these look only to the passive side of the matter, not to the active.

In truth, in the dealings of man with man, silence may indeed spare one many faults; but when the occasion demands speech and will not suffer you to be silent, then you must have a good training in the art of speech before you can come by a good result.

The art of conversation holds an especially important place in secret-service work, for the work must reach into every smallest corner of society, and the worker must have dealings with every sort of person, must fall into talk with people of every walk of life. If you have no training in the art, then on the active side you will not win the other's sympathy, will not draw out his secrets, still less gain his help.

And on the passive side, a wrong word will let others see the flaw in you and leak your own secret away. So the secret-service worker must give heed to the art of talk.

The one governing principle of the art is to go by the objective facts — to suit your words to the man and act as the moment offers; but beyond that there are points common to all cases.

1. In talking, seize the central point; do not blaze away at random, leaving the other unable to make head or tail of you.

2. Be natural and orderly; do not talk without sequence, so that the hearer wearies of it.

3. Read the moment and take the hint; do not be tactless. If you see the other is busy, or tired, make an end quickly and do not run on; for as the proverb says, "The unlettered man may still get his rice, but the man who cannot read a face goes hungry."

4. Observe the other's condition, so that what you say holds his attention. If you see his spirits flagging, take your most telling sentence and raise your voice upon it.

#### II. Dealing With and Conversing With Ordinary People

1. Study everything about the person before you:

(a) Note his bearing.

(b) Note his time.

(c) Note his spirits.

(d) Note the matters that touch him closely.

(e) To each sort of man, the sort of talk that suits him.

2. Leave room in your words; do not blow your own trumpet without stint.

3. Say little yourself and make the other say much; set things going yourself, but draw out his opinions — for as they say, "The poor talker rushes to speak; the good talker thinks before he speaks."

4. Guard your secrets: do not, when the talk has warmed you and carried you away, blurt out plainly to the other all your own past secrets — which runs clean against the rule that you should know many men and be known by few.

5. In speaking with ordinary people, take an accommodating line: play to what they like and steer clear of what they dislike; let your manner be sincere and warm; neither talk too much nor be too dull. In all things make it your rule to fall in with the other's frame of mind.

6. Before a crowd — at meetings, weddings and the like, and above all when speaking for the first time — a great deal turns on it. Observe the following points:

(a) Let your sentences come out with decision; do not be so noncommittal as to weary the hearers.

(b) Let the voice be pitched with force, so that a man is stirred the moment he hears it.

(c) Let your manner be utterly sincere, so that men are easily moved to sympathy.

(d) Let beginning and end answer one another in good order, so that the audience carries away a whole impression.

(e) Fall in with the audience's frame of mind, so that they feel a comfort of spirit.

(f) The words that matter must be brought out in a weighty tone, so that the audience marks them particularly.

7. In talking with women, let the manner be peaceable and gentle, and offer comfort withal.

#### III. How to Converse With Political Prisoners and Suspects

1. On political prisoners. To interrogate a political prisoner by torture and beating can settle nothing at all; one should use the method of conversation to draw out his confession.

(a) Study everything about him:

(1) Ascertain his rank — a high leader, a middle cadre, or a low one.

(2) Learn his knowledge, his level of education, and the circumstances of his household.

(3) Learn his bearing and his character.

(b) In talking, let your manner be sincere, and be knowing in the work; ask after his thinking, his line, the organs he knows of, and so forth.

(c) Now comfort him, now try him, now deceive him — true by turns and false — and never let him know your real face. Herewith a few points, set down below:

(1) "You have nothing to fear; a man is not shot the moment he's taken."

(2) "Suppose you were given work to do — how would you go about it?"

(3) "I was once just as you are — did you not know?"

2. On suspects. In talking with a suspect, attend to his needs, to win his sympathy and so come by a confession; as, for example, asking:

(1) "Would you send word home? I will carry it for you!"

(2) "Do you want a bedsheet, a sleeping-mat, some clothes?"

(3) "Do you know all the rules inside the jail?"

(a) In talking with his family and friends: do not put on the standing of an official; speak from a sympathetic attitude and from the position of one lending a hand — offering, say, to act as his volunteer lawyer, to carry letters for him, and the like — and so you can investigate how things really stand.

(b) If even so you get no result, you may use the following methods:

(1) Put a sham prisoner in the same cell as the suspect, to draw everything out of him.

(2) Shut the co-suspects up together and listen from the next room.

#### IV. How to Converse With a Person Under Suspicion

Once we have found a man to be suspect, we mark him in our own minds as a suspect, and thereafter watch him at every turn. In conversation above all we should, by every means, fall in with his frame of mind so as to draw out his secret and reach our end.

Say a man is suspect, yet we cannot fix for certain how the case stands with him: then we set someone he does not distrust to fall into talk with him, showing him all possible sympathy — but taking care not to let him grow suspicious midway, and not to be hasty, but to probe his drift slowly, and only at the very last to show our hand openly.

#### V. How to Converse With Fellow Workers

1. Let the manner of the talk be utterly sincere and warm, never that of an official.

2. Let the substance lay its weight above all on encouragement; do not dwell only on the negative side, harshly blaming him and threatening him.

3. Stand in the spirit of a teacher, and talk with him in the way of drawing-out and of discussion; not wholly in the way of a lecture; and keep the work itself at the center.

4. On a first talk, take particular care to give him a good impression.

5. When he sets something out, listen closely and in quiet, that you may fully grasp his meaning.

6. In answering his questions, lay the matter out for him by reason, point by point, and do not be swayed by feeling.

7. If he holds fast to a contrary opinion, you cannot make him yield by command; you must think out some further way to bring him round.

8. A subordinate speaking to a superior must be wholly truthful — one is one and two is two — and practice no deceit or concealment.

In sum, in talking with a fellow worker one must proceed in a spirit of affection, overcoming his errors by reason — not compelling his submission by feeling or by force.
