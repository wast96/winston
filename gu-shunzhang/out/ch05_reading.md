## Chapter 5. Secrecy

Much has already been said in the earlier chapters about the meaning of secrecy and the part it plays. Secrecy bears intimately upon secret-service work; which is to say that the whole foundation of secret-service work is built upon the principle of secrecy, and that, cut off from secrecy, secret-service work becomes a mere castle in the air, stripped of all its real authority. For that reason I do not shrink from repeating myself, but set apart a chapter of its own to give a systematic account of the uses of secrecy, the techniques of secrecy, and the rest — so that the worker, once he has thoroughly grasped the meaning of secrecy and its importance, may bring to the employment of the various secret techniques a further and closer study, and so raise his effectiveness in the actual work.

### Section 1. The Meaning of Secrecy

#### I. What Is Called "Secrecy"?

The meaning of secrecy may be explained from two sides:

First, secrecy means keeping a thing hidden and unspoken: all that I have, I alone know; and to no one besides myself — not though he be dear to me as a parent, beloved as a wife — will I ever tell it. There is a set phrase that describes such a man: "close-mouthed as a stoppered bottle."

Second, secrecy means a tight, unrelenting vigilance: our organization, our apparatus, our plans, our operations, and everything else besides — whatever we do not wish made public — all this we can, by devising every kind of method and applying every kind of technique, so keep that no god knows it, no ghost perceives it, and no man is aware of it. There is a fine common saying that describes this state of things: "no wind gets through, and no rain leaks in."

The first sense of secrecy has to do broadly with the individual, the second broadly with the organization; the two are bound up with each other, and let either fail and the other loses its force as well. In sum, the meaning of secrecy is this: that nothing whatever of what we have shall ever be let known to anyone, and that an outsider, however much he may wish to know it, shall have no way of knowing it.

#### II. Why Must Secret-Service Work Be Secret?

This question, too, may be answered from two sides:

First, as regards secret-service work taken as a whole. The mission of secret-service work is to defend one's own Party and to wipe out the reactionaries. Were our organization, our plans, our operations, and all the rest of it known to the enemy, then the enemy would surely make ready beforehand — "deep moats and high ramparts, drawn up in full array to await us" — and when we went forward to the attack, not only should we win no victory, but we might well fall instead into some trap of the enemy's laying; and then what use would there be in secret-service work at all? Hence the saying: the foundation of secret-service work is built upon the principle of secrecy, and cut off from secrecy it becomes a castle in the air and loses all its real authority.

Second, as regards the individual working comrade. The secret-service agent is never for a single moment out from under the enemy's close reckoning; at every instant he stands in danger of assassination by the enemy, and the harder he works, the fiercer grows the enemy's hatred and the more urgent the plotting against him. His address, his movements, his dress, his speech — every last thing about him — must therefore be made absolutely secret.

The secrecy of secret-service work as a whole is enough to safeguard the individual, and the individual comrade's secrecy in turn adds to the victory of the work as a whole; the two are interlocked and cannot be pulled apart. If I separate them here, it is only for the convenience of exposition.

#### III. How Can Secrecy Be Achieved?

To keep a secret one must understand the techniques of secrecy, and be able to put them to use as well. This will be discussed in detail in the sections that follow; but let me first set the matter out in point of principle:

1. In secret-service work as a whole, in each and every thing and at every point, heed must be paid to the principle of secrecy.

2. The working comrade must at every moment understand and attend to the importance of secrecy.

3. For the needs of secret work, everything should be made commonplace, blended into society, tightly guarded, and kept alert.

4. Root out vanity; do more solid work and less showing off; and do not, before a thing is even done, fire off the big guns about it.

5. Speak little and listen much; know much of others, and let little of yourself be known.

6. Within the organization, do not seek to know more than you need.

7. Secret work must be made a matter of habit.

In sum, secrecy too is a technique, and only through long training can one come to wield it with mastery. Here I can set out no more than the principles; it remains for the working comrade, in a spirit of study, to feel his way into it little by little amid the real problems of the work, until, the effort accumulating over time, results come of their own accord.

### Section 2. The Secret Apparatus

#### I. The Importance of the Secret Apparatus

Within the secret-service organization the secret apparatus fills an important page. Whether an organization is tightly knit is to be judged by whether its secret apparatus is sound. Should the secret apparatus be unsound, two consequences are certain to follow. First, if the apparatus is frail, the spirit of the work finds no stable resting-place, and the progress of the work meets with obstacles accordingly — like a man weak in body, whose spirit too must droop, so that any great charge laid upon him is felt as so much difficulty. Second, an unsound apparatus is easily wrecked by the enemy; and once the apparatus is wrecked, the organization is left maimed, and its tight-knit secrecy vanishes along with it. And with no tightly knit organization, how is the work to be keyed up to its pitch? For this reason the secret-service agent must give close and careful heed to the establishment of the secret apparatus, to its arrangement, to its operation, and to all the rest.

#### II. Establishing an Apparatus

1. The principles on which an apparatus is established:

a. Establish the apparatus according to its function. When we set out to establish an apparatus, we must first be clear as to what its function is to be. If it is meant, say, to serve as a training class or as a secret office — places that in the ordinary way have little traffic with the outside — then there is no harm in setting it up in some quiet, out-of-the-way spot. But if it is for making contact or for communications, where people come and go and the comings and goings are much mixed, then such an apparatus had best be established where the transport is convenient.

b. Establish the apparatus according to the number and the kind of people who will come and go. When we first set up an apparatus, we must at the same time consider how many people, and what kind of people, will be passing in and out of it:

(1) If the numbers are large, the premises must be large as well — though it is best to arrange them in the form of sublet rooms.

(2) If the numbers are small, the premises too should be somewhat fewer.

(3) If those coming and going are all men in Western suits and leather shoes, splendidly turned out, then the premises must likewise be a fine tall building, neatly appointed.

(4) Conversely, if they are men of untidy dress and unkempt appearance, then the premises must be correspondingly shabby and old, if the two are to match.

2. Matters to attend to when establishing an apparatus:

a. Take note whether the head landlord or the sub-landlord is himself a plainclothes detective, or a hoodlum connected with such detectives, or the like.

b. Take note whether there is any other group's secret apparatus nearby, or any secret apparatus set up by our own people. All this must be cleared up beforehand; otherwise, to move in and then, a few days later, on discovering such a thing, to have to move house again — is that not a most vexing and troublesome business?

c. Take note of the escape routes in case of some sudden emergency:

(1) Is there a way out at the back? Can one open and shut the doors oneself, to serve as a line of escape?

(2) Can the neighboring houses be used for getting away?

(3) Where can one climb a wall or leap from a window — and there set a rope-ladder ready beforehand, for use at need.

#### III. Arranging the Apparatus

1. Arrange according to the function of the apparatus:

a. If it is a contact apparatus, where people will surely be coming and going in numbers, it may be got up as a lawyer's correspondence office, a Shanghai trading agency, a business office, or the like.

b. If it is an apparatus for secret office work, where the traffic is light, it may be got up like a private household — modern in style or old-fashioned, according to the people living there. But the parents, wife and children, brothers and sisters, the maidservant, and the rest must be arranged to look exactly as if they were real, so that no one can tell the thing is a sham; and once the parts are cast, all concerned had best rehearse them constantly.

c. If it is a document-writing apparatus, it may be got up to look like a family fallen on hard times, and those who come and go in it should likewise resemble the local sort of stale, old-fashioned folk.

d. If it is a communications apparatus, where letters pass to and fro in quantity, it is best to borrow some ordinary establishment out in society — a hotel, a native-place association, a law office, and so on; and if one must set up an apparatus of one's own, it may be got up as a books-and-periodicals distributing agency or the like.

2. Arrange according to the number and the kind of people coming and going.

3. Arrange according to the local circumstances.

4. The arrangement should be made commonplace, and nothing out of the ordinary.

5. In a place easily seen by outsiders, set up a warning signal that can readily be shifted, against the case of the apparatus being raided: then whoever comes afterward, seeing the signal moved, will at once find a way to go off elsewhere and so escape a further round of arrests. A towel hung at the window, for instance, may serve as the signal; and after the apparatus has been raided one may, calmly and without haste, contrive to take the towel away — making, say, as if to wipe one's hands — so that the detectives suspect nothing, while whoever comes afterward, seeing the towel gone, does not go in but turns aside at once.

6. When an electric bell is installed, a signal must be agreed on beforehand — one long ring and one short, say, or two long and one short — so as to tell whether the caller is one of our own or an outsider. If it is an outsider, one must be ready on the instant; and the great point is that people must not be gathered together in secret talk, but must scatter, each about his own business, with one man told off on purpose to deal with the stranger from without.

7. The electricity meter should be set close to the front door, not inside, so that the man who comes to read the gas meter need have no cause to come in.

8. The doorkeeper must at all times be ready to deal with whatever comes; and since one man is sometimes not enough — there may be both a front door and a back to watch — the post may need two men or more.

9. Where the traffic is heavy, several doors should be arranged for going in and out, so as not to arouse the neighbors' suspicions.

10. Best of all is a separate house with its own single door, the members living apart, to keep clear of prying eyes and ears and to avoid, should some emergency arise, being caught up together in it.

11. When one cannot help sharing a house with others, each party must still use its own door, and the two must not be mixed together.

12. Within the house one sets up a temporary hide of one's own, to hold documents and to store arms; and on moving out, one restores everything to its former state.

#### IV. Operating the Apparatus

1. Any comrade of ours who is on familiar terms with the shops, the dwellings, the police, or the concession patrolmen near the apparatus — and of whom those people, in turn, know something — must on no account be allowed to frequent the apparatus, for fear that his own person compromise it, so that if trouble comes they are the more readily dragged in together. Suppose you are going to an apparatus and have already reached the door, when all at once you run into a patrolman, a policeman, or some other person of the neighborhood, all of whom know you well: then you should not go on into the apparatus, but may relieve yourself, or buy something, or make a pretense of asking the way, and thereupon turn off elsewhere — only do not let any look of alarm show, nor turn on your heel and bolt. For if you press on regardless and go straight in, someone who knows you may well ask: "Mr. So-and-so, this family that has just moved in — are you acquainted with them too? What line of business are they in?" And so the apparatus is easily seen through; and then what secrecy is left to speak of?

2. As to those who come and go — what their business is, what their names are, where they are from, and so on — all must be settled in good order beforehand, and nothing left in confusion.

3. Once you find you are being shadowed, on no account go into the apparatus again, lest it be seen through and wrecked and many others affected thereby.

4. Everything must be made utterly commonplace, with nothing to set oneself apart: at the New Year and the festivals, for instance, one must keep up the same bustle and cheer as the local people.

5. In dealing with the head landlord, the rent-collecting agent, the lane-keeper, or the sub-landlord, our own people must all speak in one and the same tenor, and never contradict one another.

### Section 3. Secret Communications

The human body has its five senses and four limbs, its blood-vessels and its nervous system; and were the senses and limbs to have no blood-vessels and nerves to knit them together, they would be as good as not there at all, could never make up a whole and living body, and no intelligence or action could arise from them. So it is with an organization, and above all with a secret-service organization: were there no secret communications running through it to bind it together, inner and outer, upper and lower would be wholly cut off from one another; and though every single apparatus were built up sound and secure, and every comrade a man of many talents laboring hard, the sum would still come to nothing, and no advance be made as a whole. The bearing of secret communications upon secret-service work is therefore weighty indeed. Consider again: under conditions of secrecy every apparatus is secret, and should the secret communications spring but a single leak, the certain effect is that every secret must be given away — nay, that the whole of the work meets with a general ruin. The Communist Party's central organs have of late been broken up again and again, and its leading figures seized, and precisely because their communications men were the first to be caught: from these men's confessions, used as a thread to follow, the blow was dealt them, and a heavy blow it was.

#### I. Classification of Communications Work

1. Local — within the same city.

2. Long-distance.

a. By post and telegraph.

(1) Making use of the existing public post and telegraph.

(2) Setting up a wireless station of one's own.

b. By human carrier — sending by people drawn from society, or by dispatching one's own communications men.

Of the above, local traffic is for the most part carried by hand: the distances being short, and safety and speed being the aim, it is generally entrusted to communications men from within the organization. Long-distance traffic makes use of the existing public post and telegraph; and here, whether or not the machinery of government is in our hands, particular care must be given to the technique of secret correspondence. In getting people from society to carry things for us — the sailors, stokers, drivers, and stewards of steamships, trains, long-distance buses, commercial aircraft, and the like — one employs the technique of blending into society, strikes up a friendship with them, and entrusts them with an article to carry to a certain place and hand to a certain person, having meanwhile given notice beforehand to the other party to wait and take delivery of it. This method is the best of all: it saves expense, and it is safe besides.

#### II. The Qualifications of Communications Personnel

Communications personnel must possess special qualities, and not every ordinary agent is equal to the work. To slight this point — to reckon that a man who is no good for other kinds of work may, so long as he knows the roads well, be set to communications duty as one would set an ordinary servant to carry a letter — is a very great error indeed. The Communist Party once held just this notion, and put its most useless people to communications work; and in 1930 a Party communications man on the Shanghai side was spotted by the detectives, with the result that all the important organs across Shanghai's seven great districts were shadowed by the detectives day after day and were broken up in the end, at extraordinary cost, so that the whole country felt the effects — and only then did they wake to the importance of choosing the right people for communications work.

Communications men must therefore be given training in the technique of secret communications before they are put to use. To make use of the existing public post and telegraph, one must have comrades within the organization sit the examinations for the public post-and-telegraph training institutes and thereafter work their way into the postal and telegraph offices, so as to pass the organization's secret messages from within. To run a wireless station of one's own, one must set up a radio-operators' training class of one's own; secret correspondence too requires a training class in its techniques; and the long-distance communications man must besides give long practice to the general knowledge and the techniques of secret-service work.

The qualities required in one who takes on communications duty are these:

1. Loyalty. Even were he to be caught and put to death for it, he must never let out the organization's secrets to the enemy.

2. Resourcefulness. If soldiers or police should chance to search him on the way, he must keep his countenance unchanged and turn the moment to account, dodging the search by such means as buying something, changing money, relieving himself, or turning off down another road.

3. Seasoned steadiness. If the soldiers or police searching passers-by are already upon him, with no way to slip aside, then he must keep his look composed and show not the least fluster, letting them search him as though it were nothing at all, and on no account fall into a panic that would only draw suspicion upon him. This is hard for the ordinary man to manage; for the Shanghai stop-and-frisk patrols, say, will often level a gun at one on the sudden, the trigger cocked and ready, so that even an idling civilian will get no small fright of it — how much more a communications man charged with a secret errand and carrying something vital, whose heart already misgives him, and who, meeting all at once with so grave a pressure, is bound to change color on the instant and lose his nerve. Let him remember above all that at such a moment he must plant his feet, raise both hands above his head, keep his face natural, and let them search; and if he is questioned, he must answer in a voice neither hurried nor slow, even and ringing, in a firm tone and in plain, well-ordered sentences — and so he can turn danger into safety. But this takes steady practice in ordinary times.

4. Blending into society. He must be able to disguise himself as men of every station, must practice the local dialects of the various places until his changes of speech baffle all guessing, and must have friends out in society to help him.

5. A thorough knowledge of the roads. The communications man must know the roads through and through — not the main streets only, but the lanes and alleys as well; how far each way runs, where it turns, where it connects with another; whether there is a barrier-gate, and when it opens and shuts; where the sentry posts are; and when and where the stop-and-frisk patrols are about — all this he must know to perfection, that he may get through by the quickest and smoothest way. Otherwise his difficulties multiply, and his danger grows the greater.

#### III. Methods of Secret Transmission

The methods used in secret transmission are very many; here are set out a few of the most current:

1. Chemical.

a. The reagent-developing method. This is very widely used: one writes with a certain fluid on white paper — in the spaces between the lines of an ordinary letter — and lets it dry, whereupon no trace of it remains; and when a second fluid is then washed over it, the writing comes out. The common recipes are these: (1) write with starch paste, develop with tincture of iodine or dilute hydrochloric acid; (2) write with an infusion of gallnut, develop with green vitriol; (3) write with milk, develop with tincture of iodine; (4) write with essence of rose, develop with rose-water.

b. The heat-developing method. (1) Write with sugarcane water; heated over a flame it comes out yellow. (2) Write with orange juice; heated it comes out yellow. (3) Write with rice-water; heated it comes out a scorched yellow. (4) Write with cobalt chloride; heated it comes out blue. (5) Write with dilute nitric acid; heated it comes out blackish-brown. (6) Write with soda water; heated it comes out blackish-grey. (7) Write with alum water; heated it comes out blackish-grey. (8) Write with strong brine; heated it comes out blackish-grey.

c. The light-developing method. This is the most advanced and at the same time the most secret of modern methods of secret correspondence: one writes beforehand with a certain chemical such that no reagent whatever will bring the writing out; there must be a specially produced ray — ultraviolet light, say, or infrared, or cathode rays — shone upon the back of the paper, and only then can the words be read off. It does no damage to the notepaper, and the moment the writing leaves the ray it vanishes wholly away; so it can be kept for a good while and examined at any time without harm.

2. Physical. This is to hide a written document secretly within some ordinary object, or to write matter of importance, in the finest hand, upon the appurtenances of an ordinary object, so that no one can find it out; as the common saying has it, "one man hides a thing, and eighteen cannot find it." Secret-service work is at bottom a contest of wits and not of strength, and the man of the higher wit is bound to carry the day. The methods of concealment are as follows:

a. On the back of the label of any tinned goods, write with a hard pencil or with a fluid, then seal the label up again just as it was.

b. Melt open the soldered seam of a tin with a soldering iron, put the secret document inside, and solder the tin up again with the iron just as it was.

c. Gently draw out the stem of a piece of fruit, insert a small secret document wrapped in waxed paper, and press the stem back in as before.

d. Unpick the lining of a garment or the sole of a cloth shoe, put the secret document in, and sew it up again.

e. Draw the tobacco out of a cigarette, put a small document in, then pack the tobacco back from both ends and trim it even.

f. Melt open a biscuit tin with a soldering iron, take out the biscuits, make a false lining inside the tin of the same sheet-tin, put the secret document in, then set the biscuits back as they were and seal it up.

g. The handle of an umbrella or a walking-stick may be hollowed out to hold a secret document, with a decorative brass ring made over the outside to cover the traces.

h. A woman communications agent may wrap a small secret document in waxed paper or rubber and tuck it into her sanitary belt, or hide it in the bun of her hair.

i. On the envelope, in the part that the postage stamp will cover, write a brief secret message in fine pencil; the other party moistens the stamp off with water and can then read it.

j. A secret document may also be hidden in the vacuum jacket of a thermos flask, though it must be wrapped in thin waxed paper or the like.

For all the above, the paper used must be thin foreign paper, of good quality and small in bulk.

3. Mathematical. This method of secret correspondence needs only to be agreed on beforehand, and is very convenient in use.

a. The grille method. On a sheet agreed upon between the parties — beginning, say, at such a line and such a character, and skipping one space, or two, or three, or one, two, three, four, and five spaces by turns — cut holes according to the count; then write an ordinary letter, fitting the words of the secret message in order into the holes of the grille. The other party has only to lay the same grille over it to read off the contents of the secret message. But in such letters the writer must make the ordinary cover-text read smoothly and in good sense, so that nothing shows.

b. The telegraph-code method. In sending a wire one uses agreed-on code numbers, so that no one else can get at the secret. There are several such methods:

(1) The left-right substitution method — that is, it is agreed beforehand to use the code-group standing one to the left, or one to the right, of the true one in the code-book. For example, where the original code reads 4782, 5789, after substitution it becomes 2784, 9785.

(2) The up-down substitution method — that is, it is agreed beforehand that both parties will use the code-group standing one above, or one below, the true one. For example, where the plaintext codes read 2872, 4281, 5872, 8281, the groups standing directly below them being 5693, 8419, 2693, 4419, after substitution one sends 5693, 8419, 2693, 4419.

(3) The addition-and-subtraction method — that is, adding a certain figure to, or subtracting a certain figure from, the plaintext code. For addition, for example: the original code being 3782, 8734, adding 4 turns it into 3786, 8738. And for subtraction, for example: the original code being 9756, 3412, subtracting 2 turns it into 9754, 3410.

#### IV. The Provisions Secret Communications Should Have

1. Communications men should each be made responsible for a marked-off district, and not let know the addresses of too many of the organization's apparatuses, lest, should one of them meet with mishap, a number of the apparatuses be wrecked on his account.

2. Communications men, or the people from society used to pass messages, should not be let know one another, lest they fall to talking together and let secrets slip, or lest one man's mishap come to affect the other communications men.

3. Frequent traffic easily draws the eye; it is best to have the communications man disguised as a hawker of food or of daily necessities — a peddler of steamed cakes, steamed rice, fried bean-curd, sesame cakes and fried dough-sticks, spiced tea-eggs, sweets, fruit, needles and thread, embroidery patterns, soap, daily papers, and the like — who, though he passes by every day, gives no ground for suspicion at first. Better still if even the postman be a communications man of the organization.

4. If a communications man finds he is being shadowed or watched, he should at once be transferred to work elsewhere and another chosen in his place; for the danger is not to his own life alone — the secrecy of the work is affected too, and the loss the greater.

5. The objects used to carry secret documents should be chosen from those in the very commonest daily use — an umbrella, cigarettes, a tin of food, a market-basket, a lunch-box, and such thoroughly ordinary things — so as to arouse no suspicion.

6. For a larger batch of documents, one may use the printed, branded wrapping paper and cord of some well-known local store, doing up the parcel in that store's own manner, and have the communications man got up as a customer — or, better still, as one of the store's own delivery men — to carry it: in Shanghai, say, the Sincere Company, the Wing On Company, and the like.

7. Where the secret lies in the object that carries the documents, and the nature and contents of those documents, need not be made known to the communications man, lest at a stop-and-frisk search he lose his head and so give himself away. It is enough that the two parties concerned know the secret of it.

8. For the various techniques needed in communications work — opening tins, hollowing umbrella-handles, and rigging up the objects that carry documents — ample materials must be laid in beforehand, and a person of practiced skill appointed to take sole charge of it.

9. To set up one's own wireless station, one must, besides running a radio-operators' training class, set up a wireless-manufacturing works of one's own, so as to be able to make the sets oneself.

#### V. Matters Communications Personnel Must Attend To

1. His art of disguise must be practiced to perfection.

2. He must note whether there are stop-and-frisk patrols along the way, and must know the hours they keep.

3. He must note, when on the move, whether anyone is shadowing him.

4. On the roads he commonly travels, is anyone grown suspicious of him, or watching him?

5. The hours at which he daily comes and goes — is anyone taking note of them?

6. The clothes he daily wears, and his station and bearing — is anyone suspicious of them, or watching them?

### Section 4. Ordinary Secrets

#### I. Everyday Life

The secret-service worker must cultivate habits of secrecy in his everyday life; for without them, not only is the work as a whole easily wrecked, but his very life is put in danger. How, then, are such habits to be formed?

1. He must have no fixed place of lodging; and the fewer even of his own people who know it, the better.

2. He must take his regular meals at no fixed eating-house.

3. He must bathe at no fixed bath-house.

4. In hiring a vehicle he must not keep by rote to one and the same carriage and driver.

5. He must keep no fixed hours of coming and going.

6. He must follow no fixed route on foot.

7. In hiring a carriage he must not hire one the moment he steps out of doors, but must walk a good way first, looking well about him — is anyone waiting outside his gate?

8. In alighting he must not have the carriage stop only at the very door, but must get down a good way off, take a turn, and come to his destination by that round; and he must look about him on all sides — is anyone shadowing him? If someone is, then he must not go in, but make a pretense of relieving himself or of asking the way, or say pointedly, "Oh! Mr. Wang has already moved away."

In sum, the secret-service worker must in his every last act come and go like a spirit and vanish like a ghost, past all men's guessing, so that even one bent on plotting against him can find no place to lay hold.

#### II. Conduct

The secret agent's conduct, too, must be made secret:

1. He should have several gaits in walking, and should from time to time practice several manners of it.

2. He should have several sets of registration plates and permits for his motor-car.

3. His expression should not be all of one cast: he should be able to look genial, and also grave; able to seem refined, and also to play the rough; suiting himself to the man before him, and holding to no fixed mold.

4. In the face of danger his bearing must be composed, with no flurry, and his action quick rather than slow. Suppose he is riding in a rickshaw and meets the enemy, and is no match for them in force: if he loses his head, gets down, and bolts, he is sure to bring on disaster. Rather, naturally and very quickly, he should put a handkerchief to his face as if wiping his nose, or bend his head over a picture-magazine; and once he is past their line of sight, then find a way to slip off.

5. In arranging to meet someone, the time must be exact; and once the time is past he should not wait on, lest the man he was to meet have been arrested on the way and drag him in too. Notice of the time should be given the other party not too far in advance.

#### III. Speech

In speech, too, the secret-service worker must form habits of secrecy.

1. He must learn several dialects, and speak to the people of each place in the speech of that place.

2. He should say little and listen much.

3. His talk should chime with the other man to win his liking, so that he tells out his own secrets of his own accord.

4. In speaking he must keep a clear head, a composed manner, and a genial look, and must not let his edge show.

#### IV. Dress

In dress, too, the secret-service worker must form habits of secrecy.

1. He should have clothes of several styles.

2. The colors of his clothes should not be all of one rote pattern, but should be kept commonplace.

3. In each kind of setting he should wear clothes of the style to suit it.

4. His gait should match his dress.

5. He should keep a good stock of clothes for disguise.

#### V. Writing

1. What he sets down in his diary should be such that he alone understands it and no one else can make it out.

2. Places, house numbers, names, receipts and payments of money, and the like should all be recorded under substitute terms.

3. He should practice several styles of his own handwriting.
