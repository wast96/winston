## Chapter 8. The Self-Cultivation of Secret-Service Personnel

### Section 1. The Spirit of the Work

#### I. Recognizing the Purpose of Your Own Work

We who do secret-service work ought to be perfectly clear in our minds as to what the purpose of our work is. If a man has come to it for the sake of a livelihood, he has come in error; there are many places where a living may be had, and there is no need to come to this one in particular. We take up secret-service work, to put it in a word, for the sake of that one thing: revolution. We have seen China's circumstances clearly, and they still call for a National Revolution; they still call for the Chinese Nationalist Party to lead the struggle forward. Yet at the same time we must see clearly that the Chinese Nationalist Party, as things now stand, is beset from without by the encircling assault of every kind of reactionary force, and gnawed from within by the corrupt, the opportunist, the cross-party element and their like, until it has come to a moment of life and death, a moment hanging by a single hair. To rescue China from ruin, and to uphold the Chinese Nationalist Party in its continued leadership of the National Revolution, is not a thing to be accomplished by the striking of high-sounding attitudes; it calls for effort that is positive and planted firmly on the ground. First, on the basis of objective fact and revolutionary theory, we wage the struggle of principle against every reactionary faction, and so make firm the Chinese Nationalist Party's revolutionary leadership. Second, by secret and extraordinary means we wipe out every hostile party and faction, and sweep from within the Party every corrupt, opportunist, cross-party element and the rest, and so make firm the Party's foundation and preserve the authority it has won. This second kind of work is precisely the charge that falls to us, the secret-service personnel. That is why we say that we have come for the sake of revolution, and not for the sake of a livelihood; and that we are, moreover, the stout vanguard on the very front line of the revolution, and not the hired clerks of some ordinary office, still less the instruments of any private man's convenience. We must recognize how grave is the responsibility that rests upon us, and press forward in that great spirit; on no account may we throw ourselves away or willingly sink into ruin.

#### II. The Spirit of Initiative and Self-Motivation

Secret-service work is, in its very nature, wholly a matter of positive effort and of self-started action. The moment we are clear as to what it is we work for, we ought of our own accord, and with all our energy, to press forward. On no account may we be slipshod, drifting through our days in a drunken dream and seeking no advance; still less, on meeting the least reverse, may we lose heart and give over trying. We who do the work of revolution have only an advance, and no retreat. Failure, in the eyes of the vain and worldly sort of man, is a shameful thing; but in ours it is the very foretoken of success, for each failure a man passes through adds one more measure to his experience — and the more his failures, the more his experience, and the greater his hope of success. Let us only keep up the struggle, positive and self-started and unbroken, forever pressing forward, and the final victory must in the end be ours.

#### III. Taking Responsibility

We who do secret-service work must be faithful, and in the doing of a thing must be solid and must discharge our office to the full. Whatever it is our duty to do, we take the responsibility on ourselves and carry it through; and having succeeded, we are not to make a boast of it, nor, having failed, to shift the blame and put the fault on another. When we set our hand to a thing, we must see it through from first to last, and press it to success.

#### IV. Self-Examination, Self-Encouragement, and Self-Criticism

Self-examination means that a man should go back over what he has done, or what he has said to others, and consider whether there is anything in it that was mistaken. Self-encouragement means that, if a man has fallen into error, he should own it frankly, urge himself on, mend his ways and begin afresh, and seek to advance the further. Self-criticism means that a man should constantly bring his own conduct under review: where he has gone wrong, to find a way to change it; and where he has done right, to spur himself the harder, refining what is already fine, and never resting content. The charge that lies on the secret-service man is a great one, and to self-examination, self-encouragement, and self-criticism he must give heed and put them earnestly into practice; only then can his work show a daily advance. Every comrade in the work should set aside two moments of the day — on rising in the morning and before sleeping at night — to put into practice these few words.

#### V. Accepting Guidance

Secret-service work is work of great difficulty, and one man's learning and experience are very narrow; to go by the notions of a single man alone is certain to lead into error. We must therefore be open-minded, taking a learner's spirit to everything we do. The suggestions of our comrades, and the directions of the responsible officer above us, we must accept in all sincerity; and more than that, we must exert ourselves to think them over and weigh them, and carry them out on the instant — not merely give an empty word of assent and in fact do nothing.

#### VI. Points to Attend to in Handling the Work

Secret-service work is work of a peculiar kind: its danger is great, and its contradictions are many. It is like a game of chess, in which one may not make a single false move. In the doing of the work, attend to the following points.

1. Method. Whatever kind of work is being handled, and be it difficult or easy, one must go about it with care and caution: on a settled plan, in good order and good sequence, doing it step by step and with both feet on the ground, and taking heed all the while that it accord with the principles of secret-service work. The result will then of itself come out well. Do the contrary — go at it in a slipshod way, or in a hot temper blunder wildly through — and the result is sure to be very bad.

2. Quickness. The handling of secret-service work must be lively and quick, and must not let the moment slip; for once the moment is past, the thing has already altered, and it is like offering an umbrella after the rain — what use is there in it then? Not only is it of no use; it does harm besides.

3. Doing away with preconceptions. The worst fault a secret-service man can have is an excess of the subjective, for secret-service work is, one may say, ten parts in ten a matter of settling one's course of action on objective fact. Let this one point be neglected, and at every turn he will feel the vexation of things that will not fit together. This may be put under two heads.

a. Toward one's comrades in the work, one should hold no preconception.

b. In settling any particular question, one should hold no preconception.

4. Attending to secrecy. In his every move the secret-service man can never forget those two words, "keep secret"; for a secret-service man parted from secrecy is as a man who has lost his soul, and of him there is simply nothing more to be said. As for how secrecy is to be kept, that is set out in full in the chapter on Secrecy.

### Section 2. Cultivation of Body and Mind

#### I. Understanding Wine, Women, Wealth, and Temper

1. Wine. Wine is a stimulant, and a bad thing. Should the enemy learn that we are given to drink, he can go on and on plying us with it; and once a man is drunk, he will pour out all his own secrets to the last — and what a peril that is to the work! Wine, besides, is harmful to the brain, and once the brain is harmed, can a man still do his work? The secret-service man, therefore, should not drink much.

2. Women. Here is meant the charm of women. We who do secret-service work should make use of women's charm, and must not be seduced by it, or there will be great hindrance to the work. The desire of the sexes is, in the ordinary way, a matter of some moment; but there must be no loose intercourse, for loose intercourse injures the body, tells upon the work, and tells too upon one's offspring.

3. Wealth. Here is meant money. We are to make use of money, and not be made use of by money; that is, we are to be money's master, and not money's slave. In secret-service work above all, the thought of money must not run deep, for the enemy has money in plenty, and can use his money to buy a man over — a thing of what gravity for the work!

4. Temper. Here is meant the temper. The secret-service man must have a high cultivation, and must never fly into a rage on the least occasion; for when the temper runs high the reason is shut off, and when the reason is shut off the clear light of the mind is stopped up, so that in the handling and meeting of affairs he can no longer keep to the proper track.

We who do secret-service work must on no account let wine, women, wealth, or temper grow into an indulged habit, to the hindrance of the work; and yet we must understand every one of them, and be adept in their use, so that on the one hand we may free ourselves from the enemy's seductions, and on the other turn the same things to use in seducing the enemy, to the profit of our work.

#### II. Health and the Work

A man sound in body and mind will do a thing thoroughly, and will hold on from beginning to end alike, and not start off like a tiger's head only to trail away like a snake's tail. The secret-service man, therefore, must in his everyday life take care to harden and train both body and spirit, and must not himself be the ruin of himself. It is best, in the ordinary way, to sleep early and rise early, and to take plenty of exercise; and the brain must not be overworked, but kept within measure.

#### III. Temperament and the Work

1. The strong. A man of too strong a temperament, though he is not easily tempted over by the enemy, is not easily guided either.

2. The weak. A man of too weak a temperament wants both courage and decision.

3. The slow. A man of a slow temperament, when a thing comes upon him, is neither dead nor alive about it, and is of all men the least suited to secret-service work.

4. The rash. A man of a rash, hot temperament, when a thing comes upon him, is thrown into a fluster and takes no thought, and is apt to spoil the business past all mending.

These four types are all of them unfavorable to the work. In the ordinary way a man must harden and train himself the harder, and make his temper level and even, leaning neither to the one side nor the other; only then, in the meeting of the work, can he handle it aright.

#### IV. Bold in Spirit, Careful in Mind

We who do secret-service work must be bold in spirit and careful in mind. Once the plan is fixed, go boldly and do it. Once a man has been given a charge, boldly let him do it with a free hand; for a man who shrinks at the head and shrinks at the tail will never win a great result — as the saying has it, "If you do not enter the tiger's den, how shall you take the tiger's cub?" Yet a man may not be all boldness and give no heed to carefulness of mind: at every instant he must look before and behind him and to the left and the right, must not lose touch with his own comrades, and must give the enemy no opening to seize. But boldness and carefulness are not to be had by mere talk of the lips; they come of slow training. To be bold and careful in a great matter, a man begins by practicing boldness and care in small ones.

#### V. Training the Various Faculties

1. The faculty of activity. Secret-service work is a thing of movement, not of stillness; of advance, not of standing still. The secret-service man must therefore give his faculty of activity a full training. In the ordinary way let him mark and study those who are good at getting about, observe what methods they use, and use those same methods himself, practicing them little by little; and in the long course of time he will of himself come to succeed.

2. The faculties of observation and judgment. We secret-service men must train hard the faculties of observation and of judgment; for if the observation is unclear and the judgment untrue, the work is bound to go wrong. Observation comes first, judgment after. We must first give the whole of our object a detailed observation, and study it besides with care, and only then pass judgment; we must not pass judgment on a smattering half understood.

3. Self-confidence and force of will. Once we have passed our judgment on a matter, we must have the self-confidence and the force of will to carry it out with all our strength, and must not, because someone opposes us, fall into doubt and, shrinking at head and tail, not dare to act. Force of will comes after self-confidence: a man whose self-confidence is not strong will assuredly have no force of will.

4. Steadfast endurance and the power to meet events. To be steadfast is to hold firm; to endure is to bear patiently. Secret-service work is work most difficult and most complex, and we secret-service men must have the steadfast endurance to hold firm to the very end, unturned through a hundred rebuffs. Should difficulty arise midway, or humiliation be met with, that too must be borne with patience, and on no account may a man give way to temper or lose heart. And since secret-service work is of all work the likeliest to alter of a sudden, we must have the power to meet events: with a composed bearing and an alert, quick wit, we meet them according to the objective circumstances, so that the work may still go forward without a break, and not be brought to a standstill by the turn of events.
