## Chapter 4. The Secret-Service Mindset

### Section 1. Why the Question of Mindset Matters

The theory of mindset in itself is a most intricate one, and from the standpoint of secret-service work there is no need to go into it in detail. But we hold that the question of mindset is a very important one, and that it bears with especial closeness upon secret-service work. We hold, further, that mindset governs action: where the mindset is correct, the action too is bound to be correct; where the mindset is mistaken, the action goes astray along with it. This is precisely why the question of mindset matters. Secret-service work is a secret and extraordinary kind of work carried on against a political background, which is to say that its point of departure is the Party and the cause, and not the individual; and should the mindset be got wrong, no end of danger will arise from it in the days to come. This is why the question of mindset weighs with especial heaviness upon secret-service work. We hold, still further, that a correct mindset is a thing drawn out of the objective environment, the fruit of close and careful thought upon every kind of concrete circumstance. Turn it about, and the case is reversed: a man who goes not by the actual conditions of society but only by his own private fancies will have a mindset that is certainly incorrect, and an action that is certainly off the rails. Of this the instances are past all counting. Take the student fresh out of school, who at every turn incurs blame, and just because he is cut off from the actual state of society. The very reason the secret-service worker must give particular heed to the question of blending into society is that only so can he understand the actual state of society, and only then arrive at a correct mindset. And once he has that correct mindset, then let him come into whatever vile surroundings he may, he will never be softened by them. Which is to say that, once he has a central conviction, then however many seasons of wind and frost and rain and dew he passes through, the will he set out with will never be shaken.

### Section 2. The Struggle over Mindset

In a man's mind there will often be two or more different ideas present at once, and it is then that the problem of a struggle over mindset arises. Let us take an example. Suppose there is a comrade whose charge it is to keep a secret document, and the enemy comes to buy that document of him with a bribe of one hundred thousand yuan: two contrary ideas are certain to spring up in him at one and the same moment.

First, he reasons within himself: here he has toiled and slaved all this while, and his life is still far from full and comfortable; yet now, on the instant, he may become a rich man — live in a foreign-style house, ride in a motor-car, dine on Western fare, and taste every good thing this world affords. Set beside his former state, the gulf between wealth and poverty is as heaven is above the earth: how should the distance between them ever be reckoned in li? And so there forms in his mind a selfish, self-serving idea, and with that he sells the secret document to the enemy.

Second, at that same moment there must also be circling in his mind an idea directly opposed to the first. This business, he thinks, does harm to the organization; it is a thing disloyal and dishonorable. How can I injure the common good for my private gain, and, at the sight of profit, forget all righteousness? There is nothing for it but to refuse, and refuse sternly! And so there forms in his mind, on the other side, an idea of serving the common good, and with that he refuses to sell the secret document.

These two opposing ideas arise together, and a struggle over mindset breaks out in his mind whether he wills it or not. After a fierce engagement, if the selfish, self-serving idea wins the day, he is bound to sell the secret document to the enemy; and by that, though the ruin is his own — his character bankrupt, his good name swept into the dirt — the organization too will suffer great harm along with him. But if the idea of the common good wins the day, he is bound to refuse to sell the document; and by that, not only does he himself become a loyal and admirable comrade, but the organization also reaps a boundless benefit. As the saying has it, let the workings of Heaven turn but once and a man may rise to a sage; let a single thought go astray and he sinks to a Jie or a Zhou. How weighty, then, is all that hangs upon the struggle over mindset! If the secret-service worker would master the incorrect mindset by the correct one, he need only take constant heed of his own political standpoint and of the mission his work lays upon him. For so long as his thinking never departs from that political standpoint, and his conduct never forgets its own mission, he will not be softened by money, nor have his original resolve shaken by any reverse.

### Section 3. The Life-Outlook of Secret-Service Personnel

The point of departure of secret-service work is the Party and the cause, and its personnel are the ablest men picked out from among the Party's members — all of which has been said already. Upon the front line of the revolution the secret-service worker should be the most loyal and the most valiant of comrades. He must renounce utterly all the splendor and riches of the world, and with a single, undivided heart hold fast to a resolve of sacrifice, upholding the Party and the cause with his hot blood and his very head, so that the flower of the revolution may blaze in its full glory and the sufferings of the people be wholly done away with. His every last act sets out from the idea of the "public," not from the idea of the "private." That is to say: the life-outlook of the secret-service worker is built upon the standpoint of serving the Party and the cause, and not upon the standpoint of selfish self-interest. His spirit is sacrifice, his lifework is struggle, and his final aim is to guard the Party, so to bring its cause to realization, and thereby to lift the people out of their suffering.

Suppose a secret-service worker does not see this clearly, and has come to the work not to struggle and sacrifice but to make himself a name and turn a profit, not to carry the cause into effect but to rise in office, grow rich, and play the speculator's tricks: then his mindset is wrong through and through, and, thinking and acting so, he will not merely be "climbing a tree to look for fish" and get no result of it, but may well run into some far greater danger besides!

So those who have a mind to take up secret-service work must first get their ideas clear. Let them try the method of the struggle over mindset, and take a keen and searching look into themselves: which, in fact, has won the day — the idea of the "public," or the idea of the "private"? If a man can do the work, let him do it, and do it well; if he cannot, then let him not do it at all, and let him bring to it not the least particle of constraint, lest he blight the work in time to come.

In the actual conduct of the work the secret-service worker should give heed to the following several points:

1. Let your feelings be spent upon the organization and the cause, and not upon any individual.

2. Use money for the sake of the work; do not use the work for the sake of money.

3. Fall in with your surroundings in order to master them; do not, by falling in with them, come to be assimilated to them.

4. Turn the charms of women to the account of the work; do not let yourself be seduced by them.

5. Work for the sake of the work, not for the sake of what you may seize by it.

6. Work for the Party and the cause, not for yourself.

7. In the work be composed, be brave, be alert, and settle your tactics according to what the objective circumstances require. Do not be overly subjective, nor cling stubbornly to preconceived views.

8. Among comrades, stand upon a footing of affection and good faith: counsel and help one another, and on no account be jealous of one another or fall to mutual intrigue.

9. Senior comrades should guide their juniors in a spirit of education; they must not set themselves up as bureaucrats, nor treat their comrades as hired hands.

10. Be absolutely open-minded, bending every effort in a spirit of study and of creation; grow not proud in success, nor disheartened in failure.

In sum, the secret-service worker must at every moment keep in mind his own political standpoint and the mission his work lays upon him, and must arrive, by way of the struggle over mindset, at a correct mindset; only then will his actions fall into no error, and his work run into no danger.
