## Chapter 7. General Knowledge for Secret-Service Work

### Section 1. Blending Into Society

#### I. What "Blending Into Society" Means, and Its Value

What is meant by blending into society? Put simply, it means becoming one with society, and being able to work society, or turn society to use, so as to drive society forward. From birth to death, there is not an instant when we are not living and breathing within society, not an instant when we do not need society's help. Without society, no one, whoever he might be, would be anything but isolated and unaided, and the very meaning of a man's being a man would have to be explained in some other way. In society there is one kind of man who can only be society's appendage: however society shifts, he shifts along with it; he can only passively receive society's influence, and can never actively, energetically influence society in return. For such a man we have no name but this: society's imbecile. There is, on the other hand, another kind of man who occupies a guiding place in society. On the one hand he has become one with society, moving as it moves; and at the same time he is able to turn the forces already present in society to use, driving society forward and shaping it. For this kind of man too we have no name but this: society's finest.

We must understand that to become one with society is no easy thing, and that to use society and drive it forward is harder still. The learning of society is the broadest and most profound learning there is. A man returned from study abroad may have a special command of some specialized branch of knowledge, and yet of the learning of society have made no real study at all — indeed he may not so much as have found the door to it. To hope to become one with society without having properly studied the learning of society is nothing but a fool recounting his dreams; and to hope, without even being able to become one with society, to serve as its backbone — to use it, to guide it, to drive it forward — that is raving in a fever.

We must understand, further, that the learning of society is not to be had in a school, nor to be read out of books: whatever one may pick up that way is at most a smattering, no more. The learning of society must be studied from society itself and taken in through actual experience, learned in an open-minded, energetic, and fearless spirit amid society's many different circumstances and many different problems, until it is thoroughly grasped. The more times a man passes through hardship and adversity, the wider his chances to learn, and the deeper his attainment in the learning of society. Only so can he come to a true and deep understanding of each and every thing in society; and only when he understands society can he merge into it and become one with it. Only when he has become one with society, standing at its center and serving as its backbone, can he then guide society and drive it forward. Society's truly great leaders, then, and its truly central figures, are without exception men who have rolled and tumbled through every kind of society, stout fellows who have battled their way up out of every kind of hardship — not doctors or bachelors of this or that, and still less the pampered sons of great houses. However high the office and fat the salary of those pampered young gentlemen, however grand and untouchable they may appear, to our eyes they are nothing but society's great imbeciles; or, to put it more sharply, mere parasites and vermin upon society. What worth is there in them to speak of?

Let us explain, in plainer and more immediate terms, what blending into society is and what it is worth.

1. Blending into society means understanding every circumstance in society, becoming one with it, and turning the forces already present in society to the solving of every difficulty in our work. Put more plainly still: when in society we can strike up friendships with people of every walk of life, and when those friends can all be put to our use, that is blending into society.

2. The value of blending into society is beyond all reckoning. At the very least it keeps us from coming to grief in the ordinary business of daily life, for we already understand every circumstance of society, and in every walk of life we have friends who can come to our aid at any moment.

3. We who do secret-service work, for the sake of the work's own requirements, must make a deep study of the question of blending into society.

#### II. How Blending In Relates to Secret-Service Work

The power of secret-service work must reach into every least corner of society; of this much has already been said. The question now is: how can secret-service work penetrate, broadly and deeply, into society's every least and most minute corner? Our answer is this: the most important thing of all is to attend to the question of blending into society.

1. If our blending into society is well done, our secret-service work can be wholly concealed within society, beyond the enemy's detection; while at the same time the enemy concealed within society is the more easily detected by us.

2. If our blending into society is well done, the forces of society can all be made to serve our use, and so carry our work to its end.

3. If our blending into society is thorough, it can spare much manpower and money, yielding twice the result for half the effort.

4. Whether secret-service work rests on a sound footing is to be judged by how well the blending into society is done, for secret-service work cannot for a single moment part company with blending into society. Blending into society is to secret-service work what the foundation is to a house: build the foundation firm, and the house is bound to stand solid; build it weak, and the house is bound to be unsound. Just so, if the blending into society is done poorly, the secret-service work can never come to a satisfactory result.

Let us give a concrete example to show the relation between blending into society and secret-service work. Suppose there are a hundred inns in Nanjing, and we wish to find out whether any harbor reactionary elements. We must call on the help of the tea-boys of the inns: for one thing, they know the ins and outs of an inn far better than we do; for another, it spares us much time, men, and money. Here, then, we must put the craft of blending into society to work. We need only make a friend of one tea-boy at each inn, or give each of them a small monthly retainer, and they will report to us regularly whether any suspicious guests have come; and on their reports we can then make ready our next step.

#### III. How to Blend Into Society

1. One must understand the common psychology of people in society. The common psychology of society comes down, roughly, to the following points:

a. People are fond of a small advantage. In dealing with them, so far as we can, there is no harm at the outset in letting them get a little the better of us, to win their favor — but the money must be spent on the blade's edge, not on its back.

b. People are fond of vanity, fond of being fitted with a tall hat. In conversation there is no harm in praising the other man freely; only one must not let it show as flattery or fawning, which would rouse his distaste.

c. People wish to be sympathized with. It is the common psychology of society to want others to show one sympathy. Toward their temperament, their talk, and their doings, then, we must contrive to show sympathy; and when they meet with failure, we must offer comfort, and on no account take up a position against them.

d. People wish for work. It is the common psychology, in a spell of unemployment, to hope that someone will find work — for oneself or for a relation — and so settle the problem of a livelihood. If it lies within our power, there is no harm in exerting ourselves a little on their behalf, and they will be grateful for the rest of their lives.

2. One must be good at social intercourse and at getting about.

a. One must have a ready tongue: in conversation, adapt to the moment and answer without a hitch.

b. One must have a presentable bearing: in meeting people, be easy in manner and thorough in courtesy.

c. One must know how to read a situation, and not, by failing to grasp the moment, make oneself a tiresome nuisance.

3. When a friend is in difficulty — at a wedding or a funeral, say — to give him fitting help, in spirit and in substance, is the surest way to deepen a friendship.

4. When ordinary friends in society need a hand to fill out the scene, there is no harm in lending them one, so that they keep face; their feeling for you is sure thereby to deepen a degree.

5. Attend, in an open mind, to studying the various kinds of learning in society. This point is the most important of all. Suppose a friend meets with a bereavement, and you are all eagerness to help him, and he needs your help too — but you have not the least experience of such matters, and are wholly a novice; then it becomes a case of "the will is there but not the strength," and you cannot make a start. Whatever we come up against, therefore, we must set ourselves in humility to learn the knowledge that lies within it.

6. Constantly and without cease seek out teachers and friends. This point is more important still, for our knowledge is got from good teachers and helpful friends; and if in science, literature, philosophy, engineering, law, medicine — in each and every field — we have good teachers and helpful friends, then whatever we may run up against, there will always be someone to help and settle it at once. But note that the teacher one seeks need not be within any school: everywhere in society there are teachers to be found, which is just what the ancients meant by "where three walk together, one is sure to be my teacher." In sum, how one is to blend into society each man must attend to, take in, and learn amid society's many different circumstances; here we have done no more than sketch the essential points.

#### IV. Blending In and Secret Work

On the one hand we must blend into society; on the other we must never for a moment forget that we are secret-service workers, and must not let our open activity get in the way of our secret work. The name we use in open activity, for instance, must not collide with the name we use in secret work. Open visits and secret rendezvous must not be run together; and the rest — accommodation address, place of residence, and so on — must all be kept apart. In sum, our purpose in moving about in society as secret-service workers is to understand society and to use it; but of our own affairs, society must be allowed to know nothing, and must have nothing it can turn to its use.

### Section 2. CP [Communist Party] Secret-Service Work

#### I. The Historical Development of CP Secret-Service Work

Chinese secret-service work takes its origin from the CP; and so we ought to study the CP's secret-service work, take up what is strong in it and discard what is weak, and make of it a warning from the cart ahead. The CP's secret-service work is of no long standing either. In the winter of 1927, when the Nationalists and the Communists parted ways, the CP — after the failure of its Hai-Lu-Feng uprising in Guangdong — gathered in secret at Shanghai, mustered its forces afresh, and only then did secret-service work come into being; but these were its first raw beginnings, and it had as yet no results to show. By the first month of 1928 — that is, the seventeenth year of the Republic — they felt the situation they were in to be extremely dangerous and hostile; and so, to guard against the enemy's onslaught and to safeguard their own security, they had no choice but to model themselves on the organization of the Soviet GPU and set up secret-service work in formal shape. They had no experience worth the name, and yet, being well led, and working in a spirit of study and invention, they laid out a whole plan, followed a fixed set of steps, and pressed forward with their feet on solid ground. At first only one section was established; six months on, a second and a third were set up in turn; and after another six months a fourth followed. In under three years they came to an astonishing development and a singular record of success — and standing on objective ground, we cannot but admire the fighting spirit with which they struggled.

#### II. The Tasks and Mission of CP Secret-Service Work

The task of CP secret-service work is to safeguard the security of the Party's organization and the advance of its political line. Put briefly, they are the CP's strong-box: on the one hand they must help the Party wipe out its enemies, and on the other they must clear out of their own ranks the horse that harms the herd — the man who has turned himself in or turned informer. They also help the Party vet unsound elements in its organization; and when the Party runs into any special difficulty, it falls to secret-service work to resolve it. Thus they take their orders from the CP, and it is for the CP that they exert themselves.

#### IV. The Strategy of CP Secret-Service Work

The strategy of CP secret-service work is to drive deep into society. The secret-service worker must blend into society down to the ground; his life, his work, his every last thing must reach deep into society, taking effect within it, and must reach deep into the enemy's ranks as well, to listen out for the enemy's news.

#### V. The Spirit of CP Secret-Service Work

The CP's secret-service workers have a full grasp of politics and a full understanding of the Party and its doctrine, and the substance of their work is charged through with an energetic, nimble, ardent, and inventive spirit. Within the CP itself it is held that of the whole Party the secret-service workers have the finest spirit of all.

#### VI. The Present State of CP Secret-Service Work

From the twentieth year of the Republic (1931) to the present, their secret-service work has gone bankrupt. A most unfortunate incident befell them, and by it everything was smashed to pieces. In the past year many of their organs have been broken up and seized — the iron proof that they have lost the protection their secret-service work once gave. The bankruptcy of CP secret-service work told upon the CP's organization, which suffered great losses by it; for over the past several years they had lived wholly in reliance on secret-service work, until, without their noticing it, secret-service work had turned into a kind of insurance office on which everyone leaned, so that even comrades of some standing, before setting out on a road, would first ask the secret-service men about it. Once secret-service work went bankrupt, then, they at once lost their protection, and the whole position fell into visible disarray.

#### VII. What Work Does CP Secret-Service Work Do?

The CP's First Section deals wholly with general-affairs matters — setting up important establishments, arranging the halls for large meetings, and the like; this can be seen clearly enough in the diagrams, and needs no great explanation.

The Second Section deals wholly with counter-detection work, sending many of its people into societies of every kind and into the enemy's own ranks. Thus the news they gather on the country's politics, its military affairs, its economy, and international matters is exceedingly quick and of great value. Above all, when their organs are about to be broken up they know of it beforehand — for every day they put out an intelligence sheet they call the *Minjie Fei*, so that their news runs freely and they are hard to catch. At the same time they contrive to sap the enemy, and now and then put out contradictory reports of every sort, to set the other side's people against one another, breed mutual suspicion, and scatter their strength.

The Third Section deals wholly with protection work. If the CP means to hold some important meeting at a given place, this section must be told, and must go to the meeting-place to see whether there is any spot that is not secure. Their protection turns wholly on the use of electrical devices, armed men, and the like. Should danger arise, they divide on the instant into two parts, one to hold off the enemy and one to get the various principals away — such is protection indoors. As for protection out of doors, a bicycle squad bears the duty of patrol, and a good many petty tradesmen are charged with a protecting mission besides. If someone comes to break them up, or if it is learned that someone has already got wind of the premises where they meet and danger is about to break, then a prearranged special signal is pressed on the doorbell, and inside, alarm-bells of various kinds already installed warn everyone charged with protection to be especially on guard. When at times, to safeguard the Party's security, they must kill an enemy, the methods are many — on the road, or in the enemy's house, as the case demands, with nothing fixed. When one of their own comrades has turned himself in, they will contrive to take him alive and bring him back for questioning, to see whether there are further plots, small cells, or other parties involved, and only then kill him. The method of killing may be electricity, or poison, or something else again. Beyond this the Third Section has one further duty: when the CP's funds run short, they must by extraordinary means, or by other methods, raise money to furnish the Party's chest. But we should note that in all this their conduct is not for private gain, but for their Party.

The Fourth Section bears sole charge of secret, special communications: they pass news back and forth by wireless. Pressed by their circumstances, they did the research themselves and set up their own wireless workshop; their people are all specialists, and their technique moreover exceedingly fine, so that the wireless sets they make are of the greatest delicacy and hard to detect. The operators who send and receive are graduates of a crash course they run themselves. Besides posting transmitters about the country, they have set up transmitters for passing news to and from abroad. And they have social communications of every kind, laying the duty of courier upon the deckhands and tea-boys of the steamers, the drivers and hawkers of the trains, and so on.

The Fifth Section was in fact never set up; it was to have taken sole charge of hoodlum work, and to have borne the duty of sabotage.

All work that is important and of the utmost secrecy is given to the core comrades to carry out. There are, besides, a few principles in the CP's methods of secret-service work that we must know:

1. This work must be driven into the very center of the enemy.

2. The enemy's every resource must be turned to working for us.

3. One must blend into society completely, for blending in can resolve every difficulty in the work and smooth its way.

4. Every problem of ours is to be solved by turning to the enemy's side and to society.

5. The work is to be absolutely secret.

An example will make it plain. Suppose the CP now knows that the Nationalist Party too has taken up secret-service work; it will then contrive to drive deep into the Nationalists' secret-service organization, and contrive to drive into its headquarters. Thereby the policies headquarters decides and the steps it takes are all known to the CP, and they can shape their own countermeasures on the strength of our strategy. Their so-called core members are the keenest and most loyal comrades in the Party, men who have moreover passed a long probation and whose effort and record at the work are better than the common run. Take again the First Section's people charged with commercial duties: they can enter into commercial dealings, and now and then make use of some shop — one that serves better, indeed, than a shop they had spent their own money to open. Those charged with arrangements find premises for a meeting, and once the meeting is over give the premises up again, to avoid danger. And because they must so often be finding premises for meetings, and fear to draw suspicion, they set up a commission-agency called the *Xinyi*, which will handle any matter in society on one's behalf, so that no one takes notice or suspects — and this is putting the blending into society to use. As for the Third Section's methods: they will, for instance, send well-trained comrades to sit the entrance examination for the Shanghai concession police, so that while standing their own beat as constables they may cover a meeting and keep the work from danger. Another set of men, once trained, are told to run petty businesses, whereby they can rent premises as secret communication stations without anyone's suspicion; and they can moreover be taught to make explosives and to do sabotage and assassination.

#### IX. The Defects CP Secret-Service Work Has Found in Itself Over the Years

Over these several years the CP's secret-service workers have themselves found the following defects:

1. The men who do secret-service work feel themselves in great danger; all their various activities have cut them off from the influence of the revolutionary masses, and they drift gradually toward passivity.

2. The men who do secret-service work have been too long away from the life of the Party, living wholly the life of blending into society; and so, wanting study of politics, they have fallen into decline.

3. The CP's responsible men rely wholly on secret-service work to protect them, and grow careless even of their own movements and secrets, reckoning that they have an insurance office at their backs.

4. The CP would have the secret-service men do for them every trifling small chore, and in the end a bad impression grew up on both sides.

To sum up: the CP leaned at every moment on the secret-service men for its protection, and to lose them was as good as to lose the guarantee of its safety. The collapse of the CP's whole work in the last two years came just of this — that it had lost the protection of secret-service work, and that in ordinary times it had leaned upon that work too far.

### Section 3. Soviet Secret-Service Work

#### I. The Origins of the GPU

The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics under Communist rule has, from its seizure of power in 1917 down to now, been in being more than ten years. Through those ten-odd years it faced, without, the blockade and assault of the capitalist Powers, and within, the activity of the White Russians and other reactionary combinations, together with stirred-up peasant risings and the like; while inside the Communist Party itself there was such strife as that between Trotsky's Opposition and the cadre faction. Beset on all four sides in this way, the Party's grip on power did not for all that collapse; and though the reasons are many, the achievement of the GPU holds, in truth, an important place among them. Now "Gebowu" is a transliteration of G.P.U., that is, the State Political Directorate, and it is much of a piece with secret-service work in China.

Stalin once said: "…No one can deny that the State Political Directorate deals the enemies of the revolution a blow full on the head — this is one of its characteristics. From the first down to the present, the State Political Directorate has been the terror of the bourgeoisie, the talisman of the revolution, a drawn sword in the hand of the proletariat." From this one may see how important the GPU is.

In the days of the Tsar the Communist Party, to safeguard its organization, to guard against spies, to withstand arrest by the gendarmes and police, and to head off the activity of party turncoats, had what was called the Red Defense Corps. When the revolution broke out (1917) this body was enlarged, and its name changed to the Extraordinary Commission — the thing rendered in Chinese as the "Cheka." Its chief task was to make firm the power the Bolsheviks had seized and to put down all counter-revolutionary activity. When the civil war was over and the intervention of the Powers had ceased, the government abolished it by open decree, and organized in its stead the GPU, narrower in scope but more sharply defined in task; so that the GPU is the successor of the Extraordinary Commission.

#### II. The Organization of the GPU and Its System

1. Who makes up the GPU. The GPU's organization is tighter even than the Communist Party's. The cadre workers taken into it are not confined to ordinary card-holding Party members; to qualify, a man must further meet the following conditions:

a. He must be a Communist Party member, and one of fairly long standing in the Party;

b. He must be absolutely loyal to the Party;

c. He must be of the highest working ability.

Such people are for the most part distributed through military, governmental, and Party bodies, through production concerns, and through social organizations of every other kind, passing as clerks in some office or society while in reality using those bodies as their cover, to escape outside notice and so go about their reconnaissance and their work. These belong to the secret personnel, and their number must run into the tens of thousands.

There is besides what is called the open arm — the armed force, whose activity is much like the police apparatus of an ordinary state. Under the GPU's direct command stands a fully-armed force of one hundred thousand men, together forming a single army, most of them drafted over from the Red Army. Within this armed force Party members make up only the smallest fraction; yet the power of command rests wholly in the hands of that small number of Party members.

2. The GPU's organizational system. The State Political Directorate is one of the highest organs of the Soviet government. It is subordinate to the Council of People's Commissars of the USSR, and takes its guidance particularly from the Central Executive Committee. Its chief is appointed by the Central Executive Committee of the USSR, chosen from among the members of the Council of People's Commissars, the government's highest organ; and he must besides be one of the members of the Party's Central Politburo.

His organizational system, for ease of understanding, may be tabulated as follows: below the Council of People's Commissars of the USSR and the Party's Central Politburo stands the All-Union State Political Directorate; below it, in order, the branch directorates of the six republics, then the provincial and county bureaus, the city bureaus, and the local branches.

3. The reach of the GPU's power. The reach of the GPU's power is exceedingly broad and wide. Over the Soviet Union's politics, its economy, and its national defense — over all of it — the GPU bears a boundless responsibility of protection. It has jurisdiction over every act reckoned unlawful in politics, in the economy, and in national defense. Even the guarding of the railways and the censorship of publications fall within the reach of its power. On the bench of the courts at every level it has its representative; and it may, without any trial by a court, put a prisoner to any sentence it chooses.

On the political side — to speak of the Cheka era — from Central Committee members on down (Lenin alone excepted), through government officials, down to the ordinary citizen, it had in an emergency the power to arrest and summon anyone; but from the change to the GPU onward the reach of its power has been much narrowed, and where a Party member is concerned it must first notify the Party branch and act jointly with it. On the economic side it can call up great sums of money. To reach the same measure of results in another country would, of course, be impossible; for of the GPU's working staff more than half are Party members serving as a duty. It has its own independent armed force besides. And so its reach is such that scarcely any least thing escapes it: at home or abroad, everywhere their footprints are to be found.

4. The GPU's relation to the Union Communist Party. The GPU's organization is independent, and, like the other high government organs of the Soviet Union, is a part of the central Soviet structure. On the face of it, it is under the command of the central Soviet government. But the real command, held behind the scenes, is still the All-Union Communist Party's Central Politburo. Only, the Party does not command the GPU by direct order in form; it does so indirectly, working through its Party fractions inside the Soviet government. The Party hands its plans to the members working within the government; these, through the working of the Party fraction, lay them before the government; and then, in the government's name, they are passed on to the GPU.

In the general way, the whole body of ordinary Communist Party members, in their duty of protecting the revolutionary power, are each bound to help the GPU of their own accord in its spying and its reporting.

#### III. The Working Methods of the GPU

The general outline of the GPU's organization has been set out in the preceding subsection; now, for the sake of a further inquiry, we set forth the GPU's working methods point by point below:

1. The division of work within the GPU. As to the division of work within the GPU — whether it be the head directorate, a branch directorate, or a local branch — it comes down, in general, to no more than the following six heads:

a. General Affairs — the organ that manages ordinary business, and directs the garrisoning of troops and the activity of all subordinate organs.

b. Foreign Affairs — which manages counter-revolutionary activity at home and political espionage abroad, and all such matters.

c. Economic — which manages all matters bearing on the economy, such as smuggling, counterfeiting, go-slows, and economic espionage.

d. Communications and Transport — which manages all unlawful doings aboard trains and steamers, at stations and wharves, and so on, and the inspection of travelers' passports and the like.

e. Military — which has sole charge of all military matters, such as the suppression of mutinies and the like.

f. Secret Detection — which manages counter-revolutionary activity in the various localities and the work of secret investigation. Under each of these heads there are again several subsections, tending toward specialization, so that each subsection has its specialist to answer for it, and the work goes forward the more smoothly.

2. The distribution of GPU organs. The GPU's organs are distributed until there is scarcely a place without one. Within the Soviet Union, Moscow has the All-Union GPU head directorate; the other six republics each have their own republican branch directorate; below these, the provinces have their provincial bureaus, the counties and cities their county or city bureaus, and then the district bureaus and local branches, until they are found nigh everywhere — small wonder that anti-government plots within the Soviet Union are so often broken up. And in the great cities of other countries too there is none without its establishment; these foreign organs mostly take the consulates and legations and the commercial agencies for their cover or their base. The searches now and then made of Soviet consulates in various countries, and the arrests now and then made of their staff, are the proof of it. That the spying organs of every country are lodged within the embassies has become an open secret.

3. The passing of news and the disposition of couriers. How important the passing of news is to detective work everyone knows, and it needs no laboring. Where the GPU has an important organ there is mostly a transmitter of its own building, on which it relies to pass news; ordinarily, though, it makes use of the state postal and telegraph service to send it. Documents going to and fro are mostly in cipher or code, so that even if now and then one is lost, no great harm follows. Besides this, they use chemical means for secret correspondence. In sum, the GPU's work of passing news is done to the highest perfection, nimbly and at speed: no sooner does an event occur than, within moments, the GPU organs throughout the country may know of it; and so it is no less with the GPU in the various countries abroad.

The couriers are disposed for the most part aboard the steamers and trains, and at the wharves and stations too; their chief task is to carry news, and so knit the GPU workers of the several places into a closer connection. Beyond this they must bear a spying duty as well — to spy out the movements of travelers, for one; and should some person's manner strike them as suspicious, they follow him in secret, the better to spy out his movements and turn up further secrets. The meaning of this work is very great, and worth our study.

4. The censorship of post and wire. This work the GPU does with a special rigor and thoroughness, and above all the post and wire going to and from abroad it examines fiercely. Let the wording within a letter or wire but once be careless and touch ever so little on a political matter, and at the least the item is held back, at the worst the addressee is arrested and thrown into prison.

5. Watch over stations, wharves, and places of amusement. Stations, wharves, and places of amusement are the spots where secret gatherings are most easily held, and stations and wharves are moreover places the traveling public must all pass through; do the work well in such spots, and public order in society will meet with no trouble to speak of. Over these places the GPU keeps armed men regularly posted, patrolling to and fro, along with the watch of secret plants. But these plants of theirs are not so dull-witted as the detectives of our country, who are so easily spotted by people for the detectives they are: they have made a close study of the art of disguise. At a station, for instance, they will pass in plain clothes for an attendant, a hawker, a wharf-porter, and the like — matters no one takes notice of — and so are not easily found out either, and their work goes forward smoothly enough.

6. Secret work hidden within social organizations of every kind. Within the GPU there is besides a sort of resident plant that ordinary people take no notice of: certain staff of government offices and of social organizations of every kind who have joined the GPU. On the face of it they are no more than clerks of such-and-such an office; in reality each is a plant the GPU has posted within that office, charged solely with spying out the talk and the doings of the people there. There is, further, another set whom the GPU sends to enter into relations with them. Should anything be turned up, it is reported to the superior, and when the time is ripe armed GPU men are dispatched to make the arrest.

7. Buying and coercion. In gathering news the GPU, besides spying by its own devices, uses great sums to buy foreign agents, setting them to spy out the situation in their own countries against them; and to the ordinary informant abroad, too, it will often give money, luring him to spy out news and report for the GPU. Besides buying with money there is another method — coercion, whereby the people are pressed to report reactionary news. By the GPU's rule, every citizen bears, in political matters, the duty of reporting any criminal act or suspect activity that he knows of; and a man who fails in this duty, who knows and does not report, is dealt with as one sheltering a criminal. Thus of all that goes on within the Soviet Union there is scarcely anything the GPU does not know — small wonder that its news runs so free, and that it deals with disturbances so nimbly.

8. Cracking cases and making arrests. This work too the GPU does with a special finesse. When they have found that some man is truly guilty of reactionary conduct, or under suspicion of it, they do not arrest him at once — though there are, of course, special exceptions. This is not to let the man go free, but to use another and higher method: to make of the man already found out a line to be followed — without his ever knowing his true position is known — by assigning an able agent, quick to meet the moment, to pay out the line and watch where he goes and what he does, whereby more of the reactionary picture may be turned up: where the reactionary organs lie, and who their workers are. Then a further and deeper step is taken — a planned, close, large-scale search and arrest. To crack cases by methods such as these is common with the GPU, and its record at it is well worth remark.

When the GPU carries out the arrest of a reactionary offender, it is mostly done deep in the night before the dawn; thus the neighbors are not disturbed, and secrecy the better kept. And so the GPU is forever cracking a great many cases and taking great batches of offenders, while the general run of people neither notice nor know. When the GPU cracks a case, then, of offenders who slip the net one may fairly say there are none.

9. Activity abroad. The GPU's activity is found in nigh every great city of all the world. As was said above under the second head, they mostly take a consulate or a commercial agency for their cover or their base, and dispatch many an international spy to be posted about the important towns of the several countries — we may put it plainly thus: wherever there is a Soviet consulate, there is spying activity, by the same methods used in the other countries. As for the aims of that activity, they are, roughly, of the following kinds:

a. To spy out the general state of things within a given country — its politics, military affairs, economy, society, and so on.

b. To buy the agents of a foreign government, that they may spy back, on the GPU's behalf, all the news of that country.

c. To spy out that country's attitude toward the Soviet Union, and what preparations it is making by way of action.

d. To spy out the international relations among the several countries.

e. To make contact with the secret organs of the Communist parties of the several countries, and get in touch with them, and so through them spy out the general news of that country.

f. Those working abroad all operate under cover identities — as pastors, priests, engineers, industrialists, advisers, journalists, or merchants of one sort or another. The most important organs go further still, using people who hold foreign nationality, the better to cover themselves. Besides these there are also temporary intelligence outfits got up as travelling parties, survey missions, and the like.

### Section 4. The Study of Society's Lower Strata

#### I. What Organizations the Lower Stratum of Society Contains, and Why We Must Study Them

What we ordinarily call the organizations of society's lower stratum refers, broadly, to the Green and Red Gangs and to the itinerant trades of the nine streams and three teachings. In a Chinese society where the residue of feudalism has yet to be swept away, these lower-stratum forces still hold a place of real weight. They share a common faith and a secret organization; and though the men who make them up are riffraff, they have all the same, without quite knowing it, undergone a certain special training, and are bold enough to sacrifice everything for the protection of the fellowship. Positioned as these forces are along the front line between our side and the enemy's, if we can turn them to good use they can be of great help to us; whereas if we let them alone, they will surely be turned to use by the enemy, and then they can do us great harm. Secret-service personnel must therefore, first, understand the organizations of the lower stratum of society and their inner workings; and, second, make contact and strike up friendships with them, so as to have them ready to use when the need arises.

#### II. A Study of the Green and Red Gangs

1. The origin of the Green Gang. The Green and Red Gangs are a lower-stratum organization of a patriarchal society, and the men who compose them are commonly called riffraff. The Green and Red Gangs are, in fact, a single body: the Red Gang split off from the Green Gang after the fall of the Taiping. In the seventeenth year of the Ming Jiajing reign (A.D. 1538), at a place called Luo Family Village in Hongdong county, Shanxi, eighteen li from the county town, there lived a man given to the cultivation of the Way, surnamed Luo, whom later generations honored as Patriarch Luo, and who is held by tradition to have been the earliest and sole teacher of the Green Gang. Patriarch Luo's father was named Luo Jun, and his uncle Luo Yi. He lost his mother at three and his father at seven, and was raised to manhood by his uncle. At sixteen he sat and passed the examinations, but, having seen through the vanity of the world and being unwilling to hold office, he betook himself to the Purple Cloud Grotto on Mount Qixia to give himself wholly to religious cultivation. In that time there were three heroes — Weng (first), Qian (second), and Pan (third) — sworn brothers who robbed the rich to help the poor; and, drawn by Patriarch Luo's fame, they wished to take him as their master. He barred his gate and refused them, saying: "Only when red rain falls from heaven, or red flowers open from the stone, will I take you in." The three were fixed in their resolve; they knocked their heads on the ground below the mountain until blood dyed the stone, and when a great snow happened to fall they did not flinch from the cold. Seeing their sincerity, Luo at last opened his gate, took them as disciples, and taught them his arts. Afterward, the realm being much troubled, it happened more than once that the imperial grain shipments were plundered on the road, and the Emperor issued a proclamation summoning the stout fellows of the land to come and escort the convoys. The three heroes, Weng, Qian, and Pan, obeying Patriarch Luo's command, came down from the mountain to answer the call; the Emperor ordered each of them to recruit something over a hundred disciples, who bound themselves into a gang and were granted the Green Dragon staves, called the "staves of family law." Its original purpose was to guard the grain and defend the realm; from that time it was handed down generation to generation in an unbroken line. Such, roughly, is the origin of the Green Gang.

2. The splitting-off of the Red Gang. In the early Qing the Green Gang held to the aim of "destroy the Qing and restore the Ming," and so later took part in the Taiping rising against the Manchus. When the Taiping was routed, much of the Green Gang was softened and won over by the Manchus, and fell into a hopeless corruption; whereupon the men of purpose within it formed the Red Gang apart. The Red Gang has more educated men and a tightly-knit organization, but it runs on a chief-and-brothers system, without the wholly patriarchal cast of the Green Gang, where the members address one another as master and disciple.

3. The spread of the Green and Red Gangs, and their bearing on the Revolution. During the Premier's founding of the Republic and the National Army's Northern Expedition, the Green and Red Gangs did much to help the Revolution in both periods; but their leaders were driven by money, while the rank and file merely followed blindly, with no grasp of revolutionary theory. By now the Green Gang has fallen off by degrees, while the Red Gang has better kept its former state; yet in point of numbers the Green Gang makes up ninety-nine percent and the Red Gang a mere one. The Red Gang flourishes in Changsha and Liling, in Sichuan, Guangdong, Henan, and other places; and around North Sichuan Road and Hongkou in Shanghai, where the population is all Cantonese, the Red Gang too has strength, its bodies going by such names as the Three Harmonies Society, the Hongmen Society, and the New Society. The Green Gang is strongest in Shanghai, and next along the Yangtze wharves from Hankou downstream.

4. A general picture of the Shanghai Green Gang.

a. Its leaders. (1) Huang Jinrong — a pockmarked face, a man from Jiangbei. (2) Du Yuesheng — from Pudong. (3) Zhang Xiaolin — from Anhui. All three belong to the "Tong" generation and wield the greatest power; together they formed a great opium company that contracts to move opium throughout the country, and each is worth some two million or more. Huang Chujiu, on the other hand, does not belong to the gang at all.

b. Their learning. The great majority are riffraff, with no learning to speak of.

c. Their temperament, which is mostly frank and blunt. (1) Among their own: utterly straightforward, answerable for their word, a single word standing for the whole day. (2) Toward outsiders: they call the man who is not in the gang a "foreign platter," an "empty one," or a "big dish," and what they say to him runs to boasting and tall talk, little of it to be believed.

d. Their livelihood. (1) Contracting to transport opium and taking a cut of it. (2) The protection money levied on gambling dens and opium houses, known as "guarding the tables." (3) Extortion within teahouses. (4) Shakedowns, squeeze, "road-money" wrung from weddings and funerals, and "help-money" from newly-opened shops.

e. Their cant. (1) A uniformed constable — a "diaolin mazi" (by the sound). (2) A plainclothes detective — a "sanguang mazi" (by the sound). (3) The detective's right- and left-hand helpers — "handhold hands," "crab legs." (4) Money — "blood." (5) Poor — "cassia flower." (6) One who breaks faith — a "half-string." (7) Arrested — "fallen into the pen." (8) Squeezing money out of an outsider — "swinging the cleaver." (9) A hole worn in one's clothes — "opening the peach garden." (10) Pawning one's clothes — "lodging them at the maternal uncle's." (11) And of these lowest little underlings of the Green Gang there is a set jingle: run out in front, take the fists, share a scrap of the leavings, gnaw a few of the bones.

f. The rite of joining. (1) The sponsor is called the "introducing master." (2) A red pledge-slip is written out: first one's own name, then the father's name, then the grandfather's, and last the master's surname and given name (surname above, the two characters of the given name below). At the foot is written: "Handed down from one patriarch, through all the ages, the water flowing east and never turning back." (3) One kowtows forty-nine times before the spirit-tablets of Weng, Qian, and Pan, and three times before one's own "old man," laying the left hand on the back of the right as one bows.

g. The generations. The first twenty-four run: <i>qing, jing, dao, de; wen, cheng, fo, fa; ren, lun, zhi, hui; ben, lai, zi, xin; yuan, ming, yu, li; da, tong, wu, xue.</i> There is a further set of twenty-four generation-characters, such as <i>wan, xiang, gui, yi,</i> and so on.

h. The gang keeps a register of its lineage down the generations, called the "common record," the "rush-pith book," the "not-to-be-exchanged-for-gold," or the "sea-bottom." This book may not be shown to any outsider. When members first fall into talk with one another, that is called "opening dealings," and there are set forms for inquiry. One may ask, "Old fellow, how many censers of incense do you bear upon your head?" and if the answer is "Twenty-two on the head, twenty-three about the body, twenty-four wound about the hand" (or "twenty-four trodden underfoot"), one knows that the man himself is of the "Wu" generation, that his own master is of the "Tong" generation, and that the disciples he takes will be of the "Xue" generation. Or again: "Old fellow, may I ask the honored name of your predecessor?" — to which he must first return the polite formula, "At home a son does not speak his father's name, and abroad a disciple does not speak his master's," and only when pressed again will answer, "Very well — my master's surname is So-and-so, his given name such-and-such."

#### III. The Nine Streams and Three Teachings

The nine streams and three teachings mean all those who scrape a living by their wits on the road. They too have real power within the lower stratum of Chinese society, and a great many people live by these connections. Secret-service personnel ought to study the various dark dealings within this world as well, and for the sake of convenience in the work I set them out separately, in brief, below.

1. Fortune-tellers and physiognomists. A part of these too are organized. The head of the Shanghai trade is one Li Zegao, a Cantonese living in the British Concession, now fifty-eight years old, with a fortune run up to as much as a million yuan. Their methods of business generally involve a hidden apparatus: a man can be seated in the cabinet beneath either end of the writing-desk, or the contrivance can be set in the next room, connected through to the room where fortunes are read. When you enter, the master first opens with tea and tobacco and chats you up, asking your honored name, your birth-year, your trade, your address, and so on, making a deliberate show of courtesy; and meanwhile the clerk seated under the desk, or in the adjoining room, has already noted down everything about you and written it out on a prepared fate-sheet, which is passed by a secret means into the fortune-teller's drawer, together with a signal. The fortune-teller then pulls open the drawer, takes out the fate-sheet already made up for you, and hands it over, saying that yesterday he had already reckoned you would come today to have your fortune told, and so had done you up the day before. You are sure to be astonished, and to take him for a marvel past fathoming — when in truth the whole thing is a sham! And the fee he asks, likewise already written on the sheet, is fixed by a look at the caller's clothes and signaled in advance to the clerk to write down, so that the sum is never the same twice.

2. Itinerant quacks. These are the men who, as the saying goes, "know the true disease and sell the false cure": they understand medical lore well enough in the main, but the drugs they sell are mostly counterfeit, and swallowed they have not the least effect. Such a man cheats you only once and then never comes back. There are besides those who peddle nostrums to "dissolve a copper coin," to purge roundworms, to cure inflamed eyes, or to "dissolve broken crockery": on the face of it their money-cheating looks much like the ordinary quack's, but in truth they are a separate school. They claim that one of their golden pills will melt away a swallowed shard of crockery, a copper coin, or the like, and will even test it before your eyes — but the broken shard they swallow is not real, being made of cuttlefish bone or of some flour-paste and the like; and if you hand them a genuine shard of crockery or a genuine copper coin of your own to swallow, they are at a loss. The golden pills they sell are likewise all false, kneaded of bean-flour or wheat-flour and merely gilded over with clay-gold on the outside.

3. Gambling. In Shanghai the varieties are very many — the flower-club lottery, poker, roulette, fantan, and the rest.

a. The flower-club lottery. The Shanghai version runs to thirty-six "gates," the Chaozhou version to thirty-seven, each gate bearing the name of some sprite or goblin out of the myths — the first gate, for instance, named Wu Zhankui, being the White-Fish Spirit. If a bettor hits the gate, every copper cash staked pays out twenty-nine, thirty counting the stake. Ignorant folk will go so far as to murder a husband, a wife, or a child in hope of a prophetic dream, or will pass the night burning incense in a nunnery to beg a god's guidance; and so young women are often violated — the abuses of it are enormous. The keeper of the lottery sets up a "character-cabinet" and shuts a "grotto-master" inside to fix upon one gate, letting the players guess against it; nineteen in twenty who bet lose, ruining house and fortune and breeding every kind of evil. The reigning "flower-club king" of Shanghai today is Bao Fang, "Scabby-head Tendon." Those who ply this trade are for the most part riffraff, living by it or making a windfall of it.

b. Poker. In the gambling clubs the poker sharp — that is, the "card master," who lives by play — takes a pack and marks the backs with signs almost impossible to detect, which is called "starting the flowers," and passes it to a teahouse waiter. He then makes a show of paying the waiter to go and buy a pack of cards, and the waiter hands this marked pack to the gamblers to play with; and so a great many are cheated and lose, the card sharp sharing out a portion of his winnings to the waiter afterward.

c. Roulette. A wheel is made with numbers marked around the rim and an iron pointer set at the center that can be spun; whichever slot the point of the pointer stops at, that slot wins. But the sharper can carry a lodestone concealed and fit it inside the wheel, so that however you spin, the iron pointer always comes to rest at the slot nearest the magnet; and so nineteen in twenty of the outsiders lose.

d. Fantan. Here one wins on odd or even: the sharper grabs a handful of prepared dice or beans from his pocket and counts out whether the number is odd or even — but what he grabs is always the count he wants. This game is played by Cantonese.

4. Rigging the dice-society pool. A dice society has a "witness" in the middle to oversee the shaking; and a member who wants to win the pool must engage a man who contracts to rig it. This man first colludes with the witness, finds out the pattern of the dice in use, and has a false set made to match, with lead run into the "one" face; the "one" face being the heavier, it readily falls downward. The rigger also carries a large lodestone concealed, bound to his thigh, to draw the "one" side surely downward — and the face opposite the "one" being the "six," the result of his shaking is bound to come up a winner, so that he can collect his fee from you. As for the charms and spells he uses, they are nothing but a fraud.

5. Brothels. The brothels of Shanghai are commonly graded into the changsan, the yao'er, the tangpai, the "pheasants," the salt-pork houses (also called Han houses), the flower-smoke rooms, the nailed-shed cribs, and so on — the changsan the highest, the nailed-shed the lowest.

a. The changsan. In Shanghai there is a kind of "flower-book," also called the little telephone-book — in Hankou it is called the "flower-gazette" — which lists nothing but the professional names and addresses of the courtesans. Whenever a guest is feasting at a wine-house, he may, whether he knows her or not, send for one to keep the cups company, which is called "calling a house-attendance." The courtesan comes with two attendants, "one flower and one medicine," commonly styled the "master" and the "elder sister," and brings along a blind fiddler with his two-stringed huqin; once the strings are tuned, the one styled "master" sings a passage of opera to entertain the guest, while the "elder sister" merely accompanies the attendance without singing. After the song she sits a little, exchanges a few courtesies, and withdraws, asking on the spot no attendance-fee. If the guest fancies this courtesan, then after three or four such attendances he may visit her house to amuse himself, which is called "holding a tea-party"; and thereafter he may invite friends round to play cards, which is called "making a flower-do." Formerly the "head-money" taken on each card-table was twelve or twenty-four yuan, now anywhere from thirty-six to forty-eight, twelve yuan counting as one "flower-do"; and any invited guest who does not care to play may pay three silver dollars to the host, which is called "buying a ticket" (now six dollars, and sometimes ten, according to the standing and intimacy of guest and host). When the cards are done, the head-money is turned over together to the courtesan's house, and the host lays on a banquet for the company, at which the guests each write out a flower-note to summon courtesans to add to the merriment; and when the pleasure is spent they scatter. After several such rounds the guest may stay the night, and once he has tipped the maidservants, the house asks no separate charge for the night. But a smooth-handed courtesan will make endless demands of a simple-natured guest, or of one new to such outings — over and above the card-and-wine tributes she will have him fit her out with clothes and ornaments and the like, so that though he throw away a thousand in gold he still wins not a single glance; this is called "taking the axe to him." There are also those who feign a wish to marry the guest as a concubine and trick him into redeeming them, then, once redeemed, will not marry after all, or, having married, pick a quarrel on some pretext a few months on and end by extorting a huge "leaving-fee" before going off — what the common tongue calls "a good rinsing."

b. The yao'er, commonly called "six knocks you down," where six yuan buys a night.

c. The tangpai. These are mostly "pheasant" streetwalkers passing themselves off as women of respectable families. They go into the amusement halls and let men flirt with them freely, and to strike up with one of them is called "trawling a tangpai."

d. The Han house, properly called the salt-pork house. Its keepers are mostly women, the more easily to worm their way in and lure the young wives and unwed daughters of respectable homes to serve as the merchandise of the trade; and of late, with the economy in decline, many women of poor families have sacrificed their looks and thrown themselves upon the keeper of their own accord. The method of business is to rent a house, partition it into many rooms, and furnish each with bed, table, chairs and the like. When a client comes to the house and is seated, he is first asked what age and sort of person he wants; then, weighing his standing too, the keeper decides which girl to bring out to attend him. If, when she comes, she does not please him, the client gives her four jiao for cab-fare to send her off, and the keeper brings another. Should the client be satisfied and wish to stay to slake his lust, the keeper is called in to name a price; whereupon the girl feigns shyness and leaves the room, and the keeper loudly protests, in a string of self-contradicting talk, that she is from a respectable household and does not follow this trade at all. The price varies: it is set (1) by the rank of the Han house, and (2) by the looks of the girl brought out — broadly, some six to twenty yuan for the night, and for a "quick round" (that is, a brief visit) three to ten, of which the keeper takes four parts in ten and the girl six. The latest arrangements differ again from the above: they set out many chairs and tables in the house, where the girls all sit together — the number in each house is not fixed, the largest having upward of a hundred and the smallest some tens — and the client, on arriving, may choose for himself, then withdraw with his choice to another room to talk; and if not one is to his liking, the keeper can still fetch a girl over from some other house.

e. The "pheasants," who tout for custom on the streets by night — as everyone knows, along Fourth Avenue in Shanghai, or by Stolen-Chicken Bridge and Niuzhuang Road.

f. The flower-smoke rooms. Only the very lowest prostitutes enter the flower-smoke rooms, where the charge for a night and the rest is exceedingly cheap; it is coolies and their like who go there.

g. The nailed-shed cribs. These are the lowest prostitutes of all — old women, old opium-wrecks and the like — and the price is dirt cheap.

h. The Cantonese "salt-water girls" and the foreign prostitutes. The foreign prostitutes in Shanghai include Russians, Japanese, Koreans, and others, while the "salt-water girls" are Cantonese of our own country; and these are scattered about North Sichuan Road and Hongjiang Road (formerly around Sikaziqiao and Yalu Road). Their clientele is mostly the sailors, marines, and crewmen off the warships of the various nations stationed at Shanghai, very few of our own countrymen going to them; and the rickshaw men around the Bund and Hongkou will tout for such custom in pidgin English, since, over and above the fare, the destination they pull him to often brings an unlooked-for windfall.

Many a young man, lured by the charms of women, has strayed onto the wrong road, gone so far as to ruin house and fortune and take the pox into his body. Once we secret-service workers understand these snares, we are to use them against the enemy and to further our own work — not, knowing full well a thing is a trap, to fling ourselves into the net.

6. Swindler gangs and the "immortal's leap." The swindler gangs have both men and women — the men to seduce women, the women to seduce men — their aim being to cheat people of money, and their methods wonderfully quick and cunning; and so many a rich merchant's or noble house's womenfolk, or its concubines, or its pampered young gentlemen, sink into their toils and fall into the trap without ever knowing it, until at last they have suffered grievous loss in all three of name, money, and body, and repentance comes too late. There is also the so-called "insurance gang," made up of four or five beautiful young women, who set themselves to lure a young man home; once there, they forbid him to go out, take out a sum of life insurance on him with an insurance company, and then hem him in day and night with a "screen of flesh." The victim may fancy himself in the height of bliss, but in truth they mean to cut his life short and collect a large indemnity from the insurance company.

The "immortal's leap" is where a beautiful woman entices a rich, well-dressed man from some public place to a den the gang has laid ready in advance. She tells you her father and brothers are all out and she alone is at home; naturally you can no longer hold down the fire of your desire, and just as you are loosening your clothes, in the thick of the half-yielding, half-resisting — there, the men they have lain in wait spring out. The woman at once cries that it is her father come, or else her husband or her brother; and burly fellows, one after another, come on in a fury, ready to feast you with their fists, charging that you have debauched a daughter of their respectable house, while the beautiful woman stands by feigning tears and terror. Then another steps out to mediate; and the end of it is that they strip you of everything you have on, or even force you to write a check, take the money, and only then let you go.

7. Pickpockets. These too have their organization, their leaders, and their carved-out territories, each district having its own man in charge. Their touch is very quick: in a crowded place they will crush in on purpose the better to pick a pocket, and what they lift is passed off at once to a confederate, so that you cannot so much as find the evidence. They are thickest at Shanghai's transport wharves, on the trams and buses, at the clothing shops on Stone Road, and in Hongkou, North Sichuan Road, the City God Temple, and such places. On the trams they will sometimes be in league with the conductor, who cries out on purpose, "Mind yourselves, everyone — there are pickpockets aboard!" — whereupon the timid feel at once for their purses to see whether anything is gone, which is nothing less than announcing themselves to the pickpockets, letting them know who has money and where it is, so that by one means or another the money is lifted away. In a pinch what has been lifted can be dropped into the conductor's own money-bag to escape a search. If you know the ropes of the trade and can find their leader, you may recover the lost goods within three days — but the money is returned only by half, and articles only as pawn-tickets.

8. Confidence swindlers. Such a man will make a point of renting a large house and fitting it out grandly, then buy costly goods from the furniture shops, the silk shops, the jewelers and the like, bidding the shop's porter deliver them, and giving him a bank check in payment — but the check is a dud that draws no money, and by the time you go back to find him he has already moved out, and the house is bare and empty.

9. Sneak-thieves. Shanghai's sneak-thieves are organized as well, each band named after its leader — the Ma band, the Zhu band, and so on. A band runs to some twenty or thirty men, or fifty or sixty, the leader dividing the spoils; he does not go out himself, but sits at home and takes four parts in ten, though when a thief's luck runs bad the leader is bound to keep him fed, and instructs him besides in the methods of thieving. Their cant is abundant — a sneak-thief is a "civil errand," a robber a "military errand," a theft a "gate errand," an arrest a "reverse in the field," and so forth. They have all sorts of ingenious methods: by day one will first disguise himself as a beggar and go begging food at a rich house, spying out how many are in the household and the ways in and out, and then, going by night to steal, will first set obstacles along the routes and leave clear the way he means to take, so that once he strikes he is not easily run down by the master; and the stolen goods are pawned off by the chief. The secondhand stalls along Beijing Road by Zhengjia Wooden Bridge in Shanghai, and beside the little railway on Zhongzheng Street in Nanjing, are the markets where such goods are unloaded.

They also carry various tools of the trade to make the stealing easier — a flashlight, keys of all kinds, nails, and stupefying drugs such as "swoon-incense" or "ether." The "ether" can be bought at any pharmacy.

10. "Pig-skinners." The pig-skinners, once called "carrying the maternal uncle," are those who waylay and rob passersby. They are commonest in winter, since the clothes people wear then are worth the more.

11. Kidnapping for ransom. Kidnapping in China originated in Shengxian, Zhejiang — it is also called "welcoming the god of wealth" or "trussing a man" — and in the out-of-the-way parts of Guangdong. In Chaozhou, for one, it is likewise rampant, but Shengxian is where it flourishes most. Many of the people of Shengxian make kidnapping their main trade or a sideline, tilling the fields by day like honest folk, and by night splitting a stout length of bamboo and dragging it along the ground as a signal to muster for the business of a kidnapping. The kidnappers too are divided into bands, each of some tens of men — or as many as several hundred. 12. Character-divination. Divining by written characters turns wholly on the diviner's quick wit, sparked by the moment, with nothing fixed about it beforehand. Two men once went to have characters divined. A refined, scholarly candidate went to ask the outcome of his examination; he happened to be holding a string of copper cash, so he pointed to the character <i>chuan</i> ("string") on the cash-string, and the diviner said: "I warrant you will surely pass — for this is two <i>zhong</i> characters, one hitting the mark upon another; how could you fail to pass?" A second candidate, a repellent fellow of dull speech, hearing this, likewise pointed out a <i>chuan</i> for divining; but the diviner said: "No, no — I warrant you will surely fail, for this string of yours has got a 'heart' in it, and that makes the character <i>huan</i> ('calamity'); to come off without disaster would be well enough — how can you hope to pass?" And in the end it fell out just as he had said. But he was merely a good reader of a man's look and color; it had nothing to do with the character divined.

13. Selling antiques. Antiques can be forged. These men take the cheapest false antiques and set them out in the old mansion of some ruined family, giving out that such-and-such an old house has come down in the world and must sell off all its antiques to keep body and soul together; and the unwitting are fooled into laying out great sums, only to find, once they have seen through it, that the seller of the false antiques has long since moved away. At the far steamship wharves too there are often sellers of false antiques, by whom they cheat travelers of their money.

14. Scrying. Scrying began among the exorcist arts of Chenzhou in Hunan, where "mirror-scrying" is one branch; it too is a cheating trick. The diviner gauges the truth of a matter from watching the other man's manner and drawing out his drift, while the charms and incantations and the rest are nothing but a cover.

The dark dealings of society are many, and cannot all be set out here; I have taken only the commonest to speak of, leaving the rest for our comrades in secret-service work to go and investigate and learn at first hand, as the need arises.

#### IV. What Attitude Secret-Service Personnel Should Take Toward These Lower-Stratum Organizations, and How to Deal With Them

1. Secret-service personnel must take these lower-stratum organizations seriously, and not make light of them.

2. First understand what they are made of, then make contact with them, so as to have them ready to use when the need arises.

3. In dealing with them, put on airs neither too great nor too small.

4. In speaking with them keep your word, and do not let them look down on you.

5. At the outset be generous in spending money on them and not stingy — but not too generous either, lest it put the notion of a shakedown into their heads.

6. Never wholly believe what they tell you, for they are given to boasting.

7. In their company become wholly one of the riffraff, wholly a man of the road — but never forget that you yourself are not in fact a rogue or a drifter.

8. Know everything about them, and let them know nothing about us; use them, and do not be used by them.

9. Once we have the ropes of these trades clear, we shall not be cheated and put to loss, and can moreover turn them to use, when we must, to reach the ends of our work.

10. Secret-service personnel stand in the closest relation to all the lower-stratum organizations set out above, and should therefore study them in detail.
