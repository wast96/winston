# STYLE.md — the translation voice

This is the house style for the English translation, distilled from the
commissioner's line edits at the voice gate. It is the fuller, worked-example
companion to the compact checklist in `HANDOFF.md` ("Voice / house style").
Read this once; then keep the approved reference chapter open and match it.

---

## The north star

**An American reader who has never seen the original should not be able to
tell this was translated from another language.** Every sentence should read
as though an English-language novelist wrote it — chosen for a Penguin/NYRB
edition, not run through a crib. That is the whole test. When a choice is in
doubt, ask: *would a good English novelist have written this sentence?* If not,
it's wrong, however faithful it is to the source's words.

This is a higher bar than "accurate." Accuracy is assumed. The job is prose.

---

## Why the first drafts failed (and what the fix teaches)

The first pass rendered the source one sentence per line and close to
word-order. It was accurate and it was dead on the page. The commissioner's
edits, round by round, all push the same direction — away from the source's
grammar and toward native English prose. Five things recur:

1. **Source syntax leaking through.** "Zhang Fang and his men had no eye for
   it" is a literal calque of 没有欣赏的意思; no English writer builds that
   thought that way. The fix is not a better phrase for "had no eye for it" —
   it's to throw out the clause and rebuild the *idea* ("had no mind for the
   moon," folded into the next beat, or cut as redundant).

2. **Pleonasm.** The source repeats and pads (it was serialized fiction).
   "hot, right enough, and sweet, and fragrant" carries three qualities and
   three commas where English wants a clean "hot and sweet." Say it once, well.

3. **Comma trains.** Long strings of commas read like a speech, not prose.
   (Detailed below — it's the single most frequent note.)

4. **Flat register on vivid characters.** River-pirates who "only ever want
   your money, not your lives, so there's no cause for fear" sound like a
   textbook. Real outlaws don't talk like that. (See "Register" below.)

5. **Robotic name repetition.** Repeating the full name every line — partly an
   artifact of chasing a name-survival check — reads mechanical. Native prose
   leans on pronouns and re-anchors the name only when a beat turns.

## The principles, with the edits that prove them

### 1. Fluency over literalism — translate the idea, not the words

Render the *meaning and the image*, then build the most natural English
sentence that carries them. Recast, reorder, resubordinate, change what's the
main clause. A literal rendering that preserves the source's structure is a
failure even when every word is "right."

> zh: 夕阳西下，秋风吹着衰草，岸上渺无人迹…
> ✗ "The sun was going down in the west. An autumn wind worried at the withered
>   grass, and not a soul was left on the bank, only a lone crow…"
> ✓ "The sun was sinking in the west, and an autumn wind swept through the
>   withered grass. Not a soul remained on the riverbank, save a lone crow
>   that swooped in from afar…"

"worried at" → "swept through"; "was left on the bank, only" → "remained on
the riverbank, save." Same facts; the second reads like a novel.

### 2. Economy — cut everything that isn't earning its place

Trim padding, redundant qualifiers, and limp connective tissue. **Never cut
plot, a name, a number, or a real image** — only the fat. When genuinely
unsure whether a detail is fat, keep it.

> ✗ "The chestnuts were hot, right enough, and sweet, and fragrant; but Zhang
>   Fang ate only the one."
> ✓ "Just as the old woman had promised, the chestnuts were hot and sweet; but
>   Zhang Fang ate only one."

Note what economy is *not*: it is not cutting content you find inconvenient.
"hot and sweet" still carries the source; the callback "just as the old woman
had promised" is faithful (she said exactly that a line earlier) and does more
work than the dropped "fragrant."

### 3. Comma density and rhythm — the note that recurs most

The reader is **not anti-comma**. Serial lists and a single parenthetical
aside are fine. The enemy is the *pile-up*: one sentence dragging a train of
commas that reads breathless and clumsy. **Test: read it aloud. If you'd run
out of air, break it.**

Fixes, in order of preference:

- **Split into two sentences.** The most common fix.
  > ✗ "He only sat there, head bowed, looking ill at ease, and then all at
  >   once he sprang up and made for the prow, where a plank lay ready…"
  > ✓ "He only sat there, head bowed, looking ill at ease. Then all at once he
  >   sprang up and made for the prow, where a plank lay ready…"
- **Delete a needless comma** — especially before a coordinated verb sharing
  the subject:
  > ✗ "he stumbled, and pitched down at her feet."
  > ✓ "he stumbled and pitched down at her feet."
- **Recast to shed a clause:**
  > ✗ "a pair of gaudy red shoes, embroidered like a bride's."
  > ✓ "a pair of gaudy red shoes that had been embroidered like a bride's."

**Em dashes: sparingly.** Use one *only* when it genuinely beats a comma
cluster (e.g. setting off "three cun of solid wood," or a mid-word speech
cutoff, "pois—"). Most sentences want none. Never reach for an em dash as a
default connector; the reader flagged over-use explicitly.

And when the previous sentences were comma-heavy, drop the optional comma in
the next one to let the passage breathe:
> ✓ "The night mist thickened and the moon grew rounder still." (no comma
>   before "and")

### 4. Diction — concrete, exact, never purple

Prefer the specific, vivid word to the flat, general one — but exact, never
ornate. "a crooked shape," "a happy stupor," "a shape too wicked for words,"
"gone into the wind." One strong word beats three vague ones. Avoid the
translationese abstractions ("displayed an expression of," "a feeling of").

### 5. Rhythm — vary length and shape on purpose

Follow a long, flowing, subordinated sentence with a short flat one. Let a
fragment or a one-line paragraph carry a beat that should land. Give
punch-lines their own line; never bury them.

> ✓ "Then the froth turned red. *Blood*."   (own line, italicised)
> ✓ "Five words, one life."
> ✓ "He had come to kill, not to talk."

### 6. Paragraph by beat, not by sentence

Group narration into paragraphs that hold one moment or movement together;
break at a shift of subject, place, time, or beat. **Dialogue turns each get
their own line** — that is correct, not choppy. The failure mode is every
sentence sitting alone until the line breaks mean nothing.

### 7. Contractions — measured, not stuffed

Use them for a living voice in *both* narration and dialogue. But don't pile
the same contraction up ("they'd… they'd… they'd" was flagged). And keep the
occasional grave, uncontracted line where the weight wants it: "Because I am a
blind man."

### 8. Register — every character sounds like a real English speaker of their
type

This is where the "can't tell it's translated" test is won or lost. Do not
render every voice in the same neutral literary English. Pitch each to who the
speaker *is*, the way an English novelist would.

- **Low-life / outlaws** get earthy, colloquial, even regionally-flavored
  English — think a road-agent, a Jesse-James holdup, not a police report.
  > zh: "…一向只要钱，不要命…只要把…金银财宝全拿出来，就没事了。"
  > ✗ "We only ever want your money, not your lives, so there's no cause for
  >   fear. Just hand over every last piece of gold and silver you're carrying,
  >   and we'll trouble you no further."
  > ✓ "Easy, now — we're the Water Snake Gang, and it's your money we're after,
  >   not your lives. Do as you're told and nobody need get hurt. Hand over
  >   every last piece of gold and silver you're carrying, and we'll be gone
  >   before you know it."
  > ✓ "Leave it. This filthy monk doesn't look worth the trouble. Let's get
  >   outta here."   ("outta" is deliberate — these men are rough.)
- **The gentle, cultivated voice** (Hua Manlou) is warm, unhurried, plain;
  short serene declaratives, dry understatement for a weapon.
- **The cold killer** (Ximen Chuixue) is monosyllabic, absolute, no courtesy.
- **The folksy predator** (Granny Xiong) uses grandmotherly endearments over
  the knife — "Sugar-roasted chestnuts, dear."

A small idiomatic touch that fits a speaker is welcome even when it isn't
literally in the source ("dear," "outta here"). This is *flavor*, and it is
allowed. It is not license to invent plot or facts.

### 9. Names vs pronouns

Name a character on a new beat or when they are the object of the sentence;
use a pronoun within a run. Do not restate the name every line. Re-anchor the
full name once when a paragraph would otherwise be ambiguous, or when a new
beat starts. (Mechanically: the name-survival check wants the character's full
rendered name once per paragraph they appear in — satisfy it there, and let
pronouns carry the rest.)

### 10. No invented substance

Color comes from diction, rhythm, and what the reader knows that the
characters don't — **never** from adding facts, thoughts, motives, or events
the source lacks. Foreboding is *tone*, not a narrator announcing dread. Do
not, for instance, add "sickly-tinged steam" to poisoned food the source
presents as sweet and appealing — that both invents a detail and destroys the
point (the horror is that it's delicious). Atmosphere yes; fabrication no.

### 11. Mechanics

- Italics: `*word*` in the en.json → `<i>`. Use for the one word that should
  ring ("*Blood*") and for internal foreign terms glossed inline.
- Ellipsis `...` for a trailing/failing voice; em-dash cutoff (`pois—`) for
  speech chopped off mid-word.
- Keep the source culture's proper nouns and its units of money, weight, and
  distance (cash, catty, tael, li, cun, zhang for this book) — do not
  domesticate them into "bucks"/"inches." Let **footnotes** carry the
  background. (This was decided explicitly.)

## The apparatus (footnotes) — same spirit

Footnotes serve a reader with no background in the source culture. Gloss what
such a reader would miss and what the *original audience understood for free*.
Two calls the commissioner made, worth generalizing:

- **Footnote the invisible logic.** When a character acts on a belief the
  source audience shares silently — a gambler dreading a monk because a bald
  head omens being "cleaned out" (输光/光) — the English reader needs the note,
  or the scene reads as a non-sequitur. If you can explain *why* something
  makes sense to the original audience, that's a note.
- **Don't footnote the invented.** A made-up gang, sect, or place with no
  real-world referent gets a glossary line at most, not a footnote. Footnotes
  are for the real world behind the fiction (institutions, customs, money,
  history), not for the fiction's own furniture.

Density tapers as the furniture gets covered; never pad to a quota, never
invent a note.

## Working checklist (per paragraph)

1. Read the source; get the meaning and the image.
2. Write the most natural English sentence(s) that carry them — not the source
   word order.
3. Cut padding; keep every fact, name, number, image.
4. Read it aloud. Comma pile-up? Split or trim. Breathless? Break it.
5. Right register for the speaker? Would a novelist have written it?
6. Punch-line on its own line; name once, then pronouns.
7. Nothing invented. If unsure a detail is in the source, check.

When in doubt, reread the last approved chapter. The pages are the voice; this
document only names what they do.
