# STYLE.md — the prose contract for *China's Secret War*

This is the falsifiable standard the reading text of *China's Secret War*
(《中国秘密战》, Hao Zaijin) must meet. It is adapted from the collection's prose
contracts for the Japanese novels (*The Stealthy Ones*, *Owl's Castle*, *Burn,
O Sword!*), which were written from close comparison of rejected drafts against
the commissioner's rewrites. Their central lesson is structural, not lexical:
the diction is usually fine; the sentence architecture, rhythm, and
paragraphing are where translationese lives. Fixing the voice is therefore
mostly cutting, breaking, reordering, and compressing, and only occasionally
choosing a more vivid word. Do not reach for grander vocabulary. Reach for
cleaner structure.

**This book is not a novel, and the target register is different.** It is
报告文学 (reportage): partisan, popular, documentary history, built on the
author's own interviews. The proven machinery below is kept; the two big
adaptations are (a) every Japanese-specific rule is re-tuned for Chinese, and
(b) the "sound like a novelist" target becomes "sound like a first-rate writer
of narrative nonfiction," and a new doctrine governs the author's partisan,
buttonholing voice, which is the book's signature and must survive.

(This sheet obeys CLAUDE.md rule 6 in its own prose: no em dashes here, except
inside the quoted before/after examples, which necessarily show them.)

## The core principle

Write English that reads as if it were composed in English by a first-rate
writer of popular narrative history, not decoded from Chinese. Fidelity is to
the meaning and to the fact, never to the syntax or the information order of the
source. The failure mode to hunt down is audible machinery: prose in which a
reader can hear the Chinese grammar and the translator's own glosses showing
through. Good translation hides the machinery completely.

The test (from CLAUDE.md, sharpened): could a first-rate contemporary writer of
narrative nonfiction or literary journalism (think a good English-language
espionage history) have written this sentence, unprompted, as original English?
If it sounds like a careful, faithful decoding, it fails. If it sounds like a
book you would read for pleasure, it passes.

## The author's voice is the book's signature — preserve it, do not flatten it

Hao Zaijin narrates in a specific voice, and it is the point. He buttonholes the
reader with rhetorical questions (有谁知道…？ 是谁…？), lands facts with
exclamations, editorializes freely, admires his subjects, and reaches often for
Mao-era metaphor and set phrases. He is a sympathetic insider telling you an
astonishing story he has spent decades collecting. That energy survives into
English AS ITSELF. Do not neutralize it into flat expository history, and do not
academicize it. The commonest way to ruin this book is to iron the author flat.

Two consequences, developed in the doctrines below:
- The **understatement doctrine** disciplines the NARRATOR'S plain description,
  but it never cools the author's raised voice. Where he runs hot, English runs
  hot.
- The **interested-witness doctrine** governs the other side of that same voice:
  his partisanship is content to be rendered faithfully and then, where it
  matters, flagged in a note, never silently corrected and never silently
  amplified.

## What "stilted" means here — the failure modes to eliminate

The first six are the shelf-wide classes. The rest are specific to Chinese.

1. **The dashed-in appositive gloss.** The worst offender. A relative clause or
   identifying phrase jammed between dashes to interrupt the main clause and
   deliver a fact in passing. The single loudest tell of translationese. The
   information is real and must be kept, but give it its own clean clause or
   sentence, moving forward, not squeezed in as an aside.
       Avoid: "Gu Shunzhang, who had run the Special Branch's assassination
       squad, defected."
       Prefer: "Gu Shunzhang had run the Special Branch's assassination squad.
       Then he defected."
2. **The long periodic sentence as default.** Three or four ideas stacked with
   "and," "which," and subordinate clauses until the sentence sags. When every
   sentence is built this way, the prose has no pulse. The author's own Chinese
   is quick and clipped; a slack English period betrays it.
3. **Flat literalism.** Rendering a chengyu or a stative construction word for
   word into abstract, lifeless English. The meaning is correct and the life is
   gone.
4. **Doubled synonyms.** Chinese pairs near-synonyms constantly, and builds
   two-node compounds (情报保卫 "intelligence-and-security," 隐蔽斗争 "hidden
   struggle," 侦察反侦察). Carried into English as a matched pair it reads as
   padding. Usually English wants the one strongest word, or one node plus a
   verb. Keep both only when both carry distinct weight.
5. **The dense wall paragraph.** One paragraph carrying several distinct beats
   with no break, so eye and ear get no rest. (Parity is checked against the
   source line count, so a deliberate split or merge is a DECLARED parity
   exception with a written reason, not a silent one.)
6. **Adjective-linking-verb strings.** Chains of "was / were + adjective" that
   describe without anything acting.
7. **Flattened parallelism and antithesis (对仗).** The author loves balanced,
   mirrored phrases: 枪杆子…刀把子, 一明一暗, 化敌为友，化友为我, 打着红旗反红旗.
   Two failure modes here, in both directions. Do NOT trace the parallel
   mechanically into wooden English ("turn the enemy into a friend, turn the
   friend into oneself"); and do NOT dissolve it into flat prose when it is
   doing real rhetorical work. Find the English figure that lands the same
   punch, and let it breathe: "turn enemies into friends, and friends into our
   own." When the figure cannot survive, render the sense cleanly and, if the
   lost image matters, footnote it.
8. **The chengyu calque.** A four-character idiom rendered by its literal image
   when English has a live equivalent, or rendered by a dead English cliche when
   the Chinese image is vivid. Test each one: translate for EFFECT. Keep the
   vivid literal image where it lands in English ("kill the chicken to warn the
   monkey" can work); use the natural English idiom where the image would not
   ("臭不可闻" is "stank to high heaven," not "so smelly it could not be
   smelled"); footnote the ones whose flavor cannot survive either way.
9. **的-clause and coverb pile-up.** Long pre-nominal 的 modifier stacks and
   chains of 把/被/给 coverbs invite nested, back-loaded English. Unstack them:
   put the head noun first and let the modifiers follow as clauses, or split.
10. **The flattened rhetorical question and exclamation.** The author's 有谁
    知道？ / 是谁…？ / …！ are voice, not decoration. Keep the question a
    question and the exclamation an exclamation; do not convert them into calm
    declaratives ("It is not widely known that…"), which kills the voice.

## The rules

### Sentence architecture and rhythm
- **Vary sentence length deliberately, and use short sentences as instruments.**
  A short declarative lands a fact, resets the rhythm, and creates emphasis by
  isolation. Follow a long, flowing sentence with a short one. A fragment is
  allowed where it strikes a note.
- **One idea, one clause, moving forward.** Prefer a sequence of clean
  statements over one clause nested inside another. Let each fact arrive as its
  own act.
- **Reorder freely; end on the strongest element.** Source information order is
  never binding. Put the point first and let the specifics expand it; land the
  sentence on the concrete or vivid word, not on a trailing subordinate phrase.
  A colon is often the cleanest way to state a thing and then unpack it.
- **Prefer active, transitive verbs and real agents.** Let people and
  institutions act. Avoid strings of linking verbs and the reflexive passive
  that Chinese 被-constructions invite when they need not be passive in English.

### Paragraphing
- **Break at every shift of focus.** One paragraph holds one beat, one turn.
  When the focus moves (from an institution to a person, from a scene to the
  author's comment), start a new paragraph. Remember the parity check: a split
  or merge is a declared exception with a reason.
- **Short paragraphs are welcome**, and a single striking fact or a turn of
  thought may stand alone. White space is part of the rhythm.

### Diction and idiom
- **Idiomatic before literal, concrete before abstract.** Choose the physical
  verb and the specific noun. For a stative or abstract Chinese construction,
  find the living English equivalent rather than tracing its shape.
- **Compress where English compresses.** If English carries a whole clause in a
  word or phrase, do that. Terseness is a virtue.
- **Never send the reader to a dictionary.** Prefer the common word to the exact
  but arcane one, unless the precision is the whole point (then keep it and
  footnote). A rare term is a red flag: reach for the plain equivalent first.
- **Keep period and institutional vocabulary; gloss, do not modernize.**
  Intelligence and Party terms of art (中央特科, 中统, 军统, 社会部, 锄奸, 保卫)
  keep their specific sense and get a first-use gloss or footnote; do not
  flatten them into generic "secret police." Keep the author's own printed
  abbreviations and gloss them (中社部, 边保).
- **Enrich the physical scene, but never invent events.** See the enrichment
  doctrine below.
- **Understatement over intensifiers, in description only.** See below.
- **No archaism, no purple.** No mock-antique diction, no Victorian cadence, no
  ornamental adjectives. The register is clean, controlled, contemporary. Period
  flavor comes from precise nouns (the offices, ranks, and geography of the
  1930s and 40s), not from fake-old grammar.

### Punctuation
- **Use the full toolkit for rhythm.** The period chops and isolates. The colon
  states then expands. The semicolon yokes two balanced clauses. Use all three.
- **The em dash is for genuine interruption or a real dramatic break only.**
  Never for a parenthetical identifying gloss; if a dash carries a fact that
  could be a clause, rewrite it as a clause. Budget: at most ONE em dash per
  sentence, or one matched PAIR bracketing an interjection; never a pile-up.
  When a sentence would exceed the budget, swap one dash for a semicolon (a
  balanced second clause), an ellipsis (a trailing-off), a comma (a light
  aside), or a period (split it). Note the source's own ——: the author uses it
  for the chapter-subtitle dash and for emphatic breaks; render the sense, do
  not import a Chinese dash count into English. (Prose written TO the
  commissioner uses NO em dashes at all; the translation may, as English
  punctuation demands.)
- **Comma pile-up is the commonest tell — read it aloud.** Chinese strings
  clauses on commas (逗号) freely, including the enumerating 顿号. A serial list
  or one aside is fine; the enemy is a sentence dragging a train of commas until
  it reads breathless. Fixes in order: split into two sentences; delete a
  needless comma (especially before a coordinated verb sharing its subject);
  recast to shed a clause.
- **Give a real punch-line its own line** when it should land. A one-line
  paragraph is a legitimate instrument (and a declared parity exception).

## Names and pronouns

Native prose leans on pronouns and re-anchors the name when a beat turns;
repeating a full name every line reads mechanical (and is partly an artifact of
chasing the name-survival check). Name a person on a new beat, or when they are
the object of the sentence; use a pronoun within a run; re-anchor the full name
once when a paragraph would otherwise be ambiguous. The entity check
(`qc_entities.py`) wants the rendered name once per paragraph the person appears
in: satisfy it there and let pronouns carry the rest. In scenes with several men
of the same side (very common here), err toward naming to kill pronoun fog. One
rendering per referent, decided in `glossary.json` and checked against
`authority.json` before anything is romanized; pinyin except for settled English
forms (Chiang Kai-shek, Yangtze, Canton where period flavor wants it) and the
shelf's agreed renderings.

## What stays sacred (fidelity is absolute here)
- Every fact, name, place, date, number, unit, quantity, and unit designation
  (第115师, 617 名特务), exactly as in the source. Reordering and compression
  change the sentence, never the content. Numerals in unit designations are
  load-bearing; crop-verify them.
- The source's own errors and inconsistencies stay visible (rendered as printed,
  flagged in a note where it matters), never silently harmonized. This book
  contradicts itself and the other shelf books about contested people and
  events; that disagreement is content, not error.
- Footnote anchors are verbatim substrings of the reading text; when a sentence
  is re-voiced, update its anchor to a phrase that survives.
- Where the book's claims meet the documentary record, the note states the
  verdict: corroborated / uncorroborated / contradicted, with sources. Never
  source LLM-generated content (CLAUDE.md rule 5).

## Application across the book's modes
- **Narrative (the case stories):** the rules above, in full. Keep the pace.
- **Exposition (institutional and historical background):** the highest-risk
  zone for stiltedness, because the density invites long sentences and
  dash-glosses. Apply the rules hardest here. Break the information into short,
  confident statements. A run of exposition should read like a good writer
  telling you something, not like a decoded reference entry. Distinguish what is
  the author's own narration (translate it) from what the English reader needs
  supplied (footnote it); do not fold a translator's gloss into his sentence.
- **The author's editorial / fourth-wall voice:** keep its buttonholing,
  conversational, sometimes indignant energy. This is the book's signature, and
  flattening it into neutral history loses it. Rhetorical questions stay
  questions; exclamations stay exclamations.
- **Quoted material (documents, telegrams, slogans, and INTERVIEW quotes):**
  keep the register the source gives it. Official documents and telegrams keep
  their formality; political slogans (逼供信, 打着红旗反红旗, 砸烂公检法) are kept
  and glossed, not paraphrased away; quoted speech from the author's interviews
  and from historical figures is natural and contracted, differentiated by
  speaker (gangsters, generals, Mao, Kang Sheng, and provincial cadres do not
  all talk alike; keep the voice sheets in HANDOFF). Registers formal by design
  keep their formality, but even they must sound like real speech or real formal
  writing, never wooden.

## Enrichment doctrine — where rendering ends and inventing begins

Enriching the RENDERING of what the source already holds is craft. Adding to the
CONTENT is fabrication, and fabrication is the worst error in this work because
nothing downstream catches it. This is doubly true here: this is history, and an
invented "fact" in a footnoted documentary edition is a real falsehood, not just
a bad sentence.

Legitimate enrichment (make the implicit explicit; choose the exact word):
- ambient conditions the scene has already fixed;
- physical properties inherent in a thing already named;
- the physical completion of a stated action in its stated setting;
- the sensory quality the source has already asserted.
Test: would a reader of the source already picture this, unprompted, from what
the source built? If yes, enrich. If not, stop.

Invention (forbidden, absolutely):
- new events or actions;
- new facts about place, person, motive, date, or number;
- INVENTED PRECISION — the deadly one, dressed as enrichment: where the source
  is vague, English stays vague. 多时 ("a good while") never becomes "three
  weeks"; 上百位 ("more than a hundred") never becomes "a hundred and twenty";
  一批 ("a batch of") never becomes "forty." Definiteness the source withholds
  is withheld. This class greps better than it samples; watch for it.
- emotional or interpretive overlay the source keeps closed.
Second test: could a careful bilingual reader point at the source and say "that
is not there and is not entailed"? If yes, cut it.

Asymmetry: enrichment touches only atmosphere and word-choice. It NEVER touches
a number, name, date, unit, or factual specific; those are copied exactly, never
grounded, rounded, or sharpened.

The dial: a light touch, not a repaint. One exact noun and one grounding phrase
usually suffice to lift a line from decoded to composed. Piling on detail is its
own infidelity, overriding the source's economy.

## Understatement doctrine — trust the fact, without draining the author

Default: trust the fact to carry its charge; the reflexive intensifier weakens.
Put intensity in the verb and the noun, not in an adverb. This disciplines the
NARRATOR'S DESCRIPTIVE register: strip "very," "utterly," "terribly" from
straight description, physical violence, and the accounts of the purges and
executions, where the plain statement hits hardest.

But understatement has a direction. It never lowers the temperature of a source
that already runs hot, and this author runs hot often. Keep the heat where the
source has it:
- the author's own raised voice (admiring, sardonic, sometimes genuinely
  indignant in his political asides): vehement in the source, vehement in
  English;
- rhetorical set-pieces and slogans: full force;
- a quoted speaker's heightened moment: a speaker who rages, rages.

Governing test: is the heat in the source, or am I adding it, or removing it?
Match the source's temperature. Plain where it is plain, hot where it is hot.
Avoid both failure modes: purple adverb-pushed English, and a bloodless uniform
cool that drains the author's passion, humor, and anger.

## Interested-witness doctrine — the partisanship is content, not error

This is the section the novels do not need. *China's Secret War* is written from
inside its subject: it celebrates the CCP's hidden front, adopts its vocabulary
(特务 for the enemy's agents, 汉奸 for collaborators, 敌 / 我 for the two sides),
treats its own side's violence more gently than the enemy's, and is at its most
partisan on the contested episodes (the purges, the "Rescue Campaign," Kang
Sheng, Pan Hannian, Gao Gang / Xi Zhongxun, the Wu Shi case, Taiwan).

Rules:
- **Render the partisan voice faithfully.** Do not neutralize loaded terms into
  false balance in the text. 特务 stays "agent / secret agent" in the author's
  sense; 汉奸 stays "traitor / collaborator"; 我方 is "our side" when he speaks
  as a partisan. Keep the "我们" when he includes himself and the reader.
- **Do not soften or sharpen his verdicts.** If he excuses an atrocity, or
  passes over a victim, translate exactly what he wrote. Silently editing his
  judgment destroys the evidence of how the book was made.
- **Put the counter-record in the footnote, not the text.** Where the account
  is self-serving, one-sided, or contradicted by the documentary record, the
  note says so, with sources, and states the verdict (CORROBORATED / PARTLY /
  UNCORROBORATED / CONTRADICTED). The text stays faithful; the apparatus is
  where honesty lives. This is CLAUDE.md's "author as interested witness"
  footnote class, and it is central to this book, not occasional.
- **Loaded terms get a first-appearance note, once.** Explain that 特务 and
  汉奸 carry the author's side's charge, then let the rendering stand.

## Apparatus, in the same spirit (two heuristics; full policy in CLAUDE.md)
- **Footnote the invisible logic.** When the narrative assumes knowledge its
  original Chinese reader had silently (who Kang Sheng was, what the 中统 / 军统
  split meant, why the 八办 could exist openly, what 抢救运动 refers to), the
  English reader needs the note or the passage reads as a non-sequitur. If you
  can explain WHY something made sense to the author's reader for a line, that is
  a note.
- **Footnote the real world, not the book's own shorthand.** History, offices,
  customs, geography, money, and contested claims earn notes. The author's own
  coined labels and nicknames get a glossary line and a gloss at first use, not
  a research footnote.

## Register-drift discipline (the measured guard)

The shelf's hardest-won lesson: dialogue and voice drift formal over a long book
without any single chapter looking wrong. Measure every unit against the FROZEN
ch01 reference, never a running average: `check_register.py --ref
out/ch01_reading.md`. The primary signal is the dialogue contraction rate; if it
falls below roughly half the reference rate, the unit has gone formal, fix it
then. Do NOT blanket-contract: the exempt registers (quoted documents and
telegrams, slogans and set-piece phrases, a speaker formal by design, the
author's own formal passages) keep their form. Punctuation rates (em dash,
semicolon) vary legitimately with content; report the spread, do not fake
uniformity.

## How to add to this sheet

When the commissioner corrects a line, record the CLASS of error here, not just
the one fix. Use the format that travels with its reasoning:
- **RULE.** The general rule that prevents the whole class.
- **WHY IT HAPPENED.** The source-language or process cause, so the class is
  recognizable next time.
- **FIX.** The before and the after.
- **CHECK.** The script or the read that catches a recurrence.
A single correction is a data point; the rule that prevents the whole class is
the deliverable.

## The one-line summary
Cut the scaffolding. Break the walls. Trust the short sentence. Keep the
author's heat. Hide the machine.
