# STYLE.md: the prose contract for *China's Secret War*

This is the falsifiable standard the reading text of *China's Secret War*
(《中国秘密战》, Hao Zaijin) must meet. It descends from the collection's
earlier prose contracts, whose central lesson is structural, not lexical: the
diction is usually fine; the sentence architecture, rhythm, and paragraphing
are where translationese lives. Fixing the voice is mostly cutting, breaking,
reordering, and compressing, and only occasionally choosing a more vivid word.
Do not reach for grander vocabulary. Reach for cleaner structure.

This book is 报告文学: partisan, popular, documentary reportage built on the
author's own interviews. The target is not "sound like a novelist" but "sound
like a first-rate writer of narrative nonfiction," and a dedicated doctrine
below governs the author's partisan, buttonholing voice, which is the book's
signature and must survive.

(This sheet obeys CLAUDE.md rule 6 in its own prose, headings included: no em
dashes, except inside quoted before/after examples, which may need them.)

## The core principle

Write English that reads as if it were composed in English by a first-rate
writer of popular narrative history, not decoded from Chinese. Fidelity is to
the meaning and to the fact, never to the syntax or the information order of
the source. The failure mode to hunt down is audible machinery: prose in which
a reader can hear the Chinese grammar and the translator's own glosses showing
through. Good translation hides the machinery completely.

The test, sharpened for this book: could a first-rate English-language
espionage historian, writing for a general reader, have written this sentence
unprompted as original English? If it sounds like a careful, faithful
decoding, it fails. If it sounds like a book you would read for pleasure, it
passes.

## The author's voice is the signature: preserve it, do not flatten it

Hao Zaijin narrates in a specific voice, and it is the point. He buttonholes
the reader, editorializes freely, admires his subjects, and tells you an
astonishing story he has spent decades collecting. That energy survives into
English AS ITSELF. Do not neutralize it into flat expository history, and do
not academicize it. The commonest way to ruin this book is to iron the author
flat.

His signature devices, all present by page 3 of the preface, all to be kept
in their original shape:

- **The anaphora chain.** 有谁知道…有谁想到…有谁猜到…有谁料到… Four
  paragraphs opening on the same hammer. Keep the hammer: "Who knew... Who
  imagined... Who guessed... Who foresaw..." Do not vary the opening out of
  English elegance-anxiety; the repetition is the device.
- **The one-sentence paragraph as punch.** 秘密之中的秘密! He isolates a
  line to land it. Keep it isolated, keep the exclamation.
- **The rhetorical question.** 中国的情报保卫组织呢? A question stays a
  question. Never convert to "It is less well known that...", which kills the
  voice dead.
- **The datebook chronology.** Chapter 1 walks Zhou Enlai's 1927 one dated
  entry at a time, each its own short paragraph. Keep the staccato: "On March
  21, ... On April 12, ..." Short entries stay short; do not fuse them into a
  narrative summary.
- **The extended metaphor strand.** Secrets as buried treasure, the archive
  as a martial-arts manual scattered among masters, research as testing deep
  water. These images recur across chapters. Render them concretely and
  consistently; a strand dropped in chapter 1 cannot pay off in chapter 12.
- **The inclusive "we."** 我们一起来… He recruits the reader. Keep the "we"
  when he includes himself and the reader; it is his relationship to the
  audience, not padding.

Two disciplines developed below both defer to this voice: understatement
never cools his raised temperature, and the interested-witness doctrine
renders his partisanship faithfully rather than correcting it.

## What "stilted" means here: the failure modes to eliminate

The first six are shelf-wide classes. The rest are specific to Chinese, and
the last is specific to this book's subject matter.

1. **The dashed-in appositive gloss.** The worst offender. An identifying
   phrase jammed between dashes to interrupt the main clause and deliver a
   fact in passing. The information is real and must be kept, but give it its
   own clean clause or sentence, moving forward.
       Avoid: "Gu Shunzhang, who had run the Special Branch's assassination
       squad, defected."
       Prefer: "Gu Shunzhang had run the Special Branch's assassination
       squad. Then he defected."
2. **The long periodic sentence as default.** Three or four ideas stacked
   with "and," "which," and subordinate clauses until the sentence sags. The
   author's own Chinese is quick and clipped; a slack English period betrays
   it.
3. **Flat literalism.** Rendering a chengyu or a stative construction word
   for word into abstract, lifeless English. The meaning is correct and the
   life is gone.
4. **Doubled synonyms.** Chinese pairs near-synonyms constantly and builds
   two-node compounds (情报保卫, 隐蔽斗争, 侦察反侦察). Carried into English
   as a matched pair it reads as padding. Usually English wants the one
   strongest word, or one node plus a verb. Keep both only when both carry
   distinct weight.
5. **The dense wall paragraph.** Several distinct beats with no break.
   (Parity is checked against the source line count, so a deliberate split or
   merge is a DECLARED exception with a written reason, never silent.)
6. **Adjective-linking-verb strings.** Chains of "was/were + adjective" that
   describe without anything acting.
7. **Flattened parallelism and antithesis (对仗).** The author loves balanced
   phrases: 枪杆子…刀把子, 一明一暗, 化敌为友，化友为我. Two failure modes,
   in both directions: do NOT trace the parallel mechanically into wooden
   English, and do NOT dissolve it into flat prose when it is doing real
   rhetorical work. Find the English figure that lands the same punch: "turn
   enemies into friends, and friends into our own." When the figure cannot
   survive, render the sense cleanly and footnote the lost image if it
   matters.
8. **The chengyu calque.** Translate the four-character idiom for EFFECT.
   Keep the vivid literal image where it lands in English ("kill the chicken
   to warn the monkey" works); use the natural English idiom where the image
   would not (臭不可闻 is "stank to high heaven," not "so smelly it could not
   be smelled"); footnote the ones whose flavor survives neither way.
9. **的-clause and coverb pile-up.** Long pre-nominal modifier stacks and
   把/被/给 chains invite nested, back-loaded English. Unstack: head noun
   first, modifiers following as clauses, or split.
10. **The flattened rhetorical question and exclamation.** Covered under the
    voice above; listed here because it is also a mechanical tic to grep for.
    Questions stay questions; exclamations stay exclamations.
11. **The Party-jargon abstraction stack.** Political and campaign vocabulary
    (肃反扩大化, 逼供信, 一元化领导) rendered as stacked abstractions:
    "the expansion-ization of the elimination of counter-revolutionaries."
    Render the MEANING in plain English ("the purge swept up the innocent")
    and put the term itself in a gloss or note at first appearance. Slogans
    quoted AS slogans keep their shape (see quoted material below); narration
    ABOUT the campaigns reads as plain English.

## The rules

### Sentence architecture and rhythm
- **Vary sentence length deliberately; use short sentences as instruments.**
  A short declarative lands a fact, resets the rhythm, creates emphasis by
  isolation. Follow a long sentence with a short one. A fragment is allowed
  where it strikes a note.
- **One idea, one clause, moving forward.** Prefer a sequence of clean
  statements over one clause nested inside another.
- **Reorder freely; end on the strongest element.** Source information order
  is never binding. Put the point first and let the specifics expand it; land
  on the concrete word, not a trailing subordinate phrase. A colon is often
  the cleanest way to state a thing and then unpack it.
- **Prefer active, transitive verbs and real agents.** Let people and
  institutions act. Avoid the reflexive passive that 被-constructions invite
  when English does not need it.

### Paragraphing
- **Break at every shift of focus.** One paragraph, one beat. When the focus
  moves (institution to person, scene to author's comment), break. Remember
  parity: a split or merge is a declared exception with a reason.
- **Short paragraphs are welcome.** A single striking fact may stand alone.
  White space is part of the rhythm, and it is how the author himself works.

### Diction and idiom
- **Idiomatic before literal, concrete before abstract.** The physical verb,
  the specific noun.
- **Compress where English compresses.** Terseness is a virtue.
- **Never send the reader to a dictionary.** The common word over the exact
  but arcane one, unless the precision is the point (then keep it and
  footnote).
- **Keep period and institutional vocabulary; gloss, do not modernize.**
  Intelligence and Party terms of art (中央特科, 中统, 军统, 社会部, 锄奸,
  保卫) keep their specific sense and get a first-use gloss or footnote; never
  flatten them into generic "secret police." Keep the author's own printed
  abbreviations (中社部, 边保) and gloss them.
- **No archaism, no purple.** No mock-antique diction, no Victorian cadence.
  The register is clean, controlled, contemporary. Period flavor comes from
  precise nouns (the offices, ranks, and geography of the 1930s and 40s), not
  fake-old grammar.

### Punctuation
- **Use the full toolkit for rhythm.** The period chops. The colon states
  then expands. The semicolon yokes two balanced clauses.
- **The em dash is for genuine interruption or a real dramatic break only.**
  Never for a parenthetical identifying gloss. Budget: at most ONE em dash
  per sentence, or one matched pair; never a pile-up. The author's own ——
  marks emphatic breaks and the chapter-subtitle dash; render the sense, do
  not import a Chinese dash count. (Prose TO the commissioner uses no em
  dashes at all; the translation may, as English punctuation demands.)
- **Comma pile-up is the commonest tell; read it aloud.** Chinese strings
  clauses on commas freely, and the 顿号 enumerates on top of that. The enemy
  is the sentence dragging a train of commas until it reads breathless.
  Fixes, in order: split; delete a needless comma; recast to shed a clause.
- **Give a real punch-line its own line.** The author does. A one-line
  paragraph is a legitimate instrument (and a declared parity exception if it
  splits a source paragraph).

## Reader-ease conventions (what keeps THIS book readable)

This book runs on institutions, campaigns, and forty-odd recurring people.
The English reader's experience is decided less by any single sentence than
by whether the furniture stays put. These conventions are the contract:

- **One handle per organization, forever.** Every organ gets its full formal
  rendering at first appearance and ONE fixed short handle thereafter: the
  Special Branch, the Social Affairs Department, Juntong, Zhongtong. Never
  alternate between a translation and a transliteration for the same organ,
  and never re-expand the full title once the handle is established except at
  a chapter re-introduction where the reader genuinely needs the reminder.
  Handles are DECIDED in glossary.json, checked against authority.json, and
  never improvised mid-paragraph. Alphabet soup and pinyin soup are both
  failure states.
- **Use vs. mention.** When the author discusses a word AS a word (chapter 1
  opens by anatomizing 特务 itself; section 9 plays 特区 against 特务), the
  Chinese term must stay visible: give italic pinyin with a gloss at first
  mention ("<i>tewu</i>, 'special agent'"), then argue about it in English.
  When he merely USES the word, use the decided English rendering. A pun that
  cannot survive (特区/特务 share their first character) gets a footnote, not
  a strained English pun.
- **Famous quotations use the canonical English where one exists.**
  枪杆子里面出政权 is "Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun,"
  not a fresh coinage; Sun Tzu's 不战而屈人之兵 is "to subdue the enemy
  without fighting." A reader who half-knows these must be able to recognize
  them. If the book's version of a famous line differs from the received
  text, render what the book prints and footnote the difference (the source's
  errors stay visible).
- **Nicknames translate when the meaning is the point.** 董胖子 is "Fatty
  Dong"; a nom de guerre that is effectively just a name stays pinyin. Decide
  per name in glossary.json; the glossary row records both forms.
- **Scare quotes: keep his, add none.** The author's quotation marks around
  “特务”, “叛徒”, “抢救” carry irony, distance, or verbatim officialese, and
  they are content. Keep them where he has them. Budget for translator-added
  scare quotes: zero.
- **Dates, numbers, units: English conventions, exact values.** "On March
  21," not "3月21日,". Chinese count-units convert exactly: 30万 is 300,000,
  never "30 wan"; but vagueness the source keeps stays vague (上百 is "over a
  hundred," 多时 is "a good while"; see the enrichment doctrine's invented-
  precision ban). Military designations render as "the 24th Division," "the
  37th Army"; the numerals are load-bearing and crop-verified. Money and
  measures keep their period terms (silver dollars, li) with a gloss where a
  modern reader would miss the sense.

## Names and pronouns

Native prose leans on pronouns and re-anchors the name when a beat turns;
repeating a full name every line reads mechanical. Name a person on a new
beat or when they are the object of the sentence; use a pronoun within a run;
re-anchor once when a paragraph would otherwise be ambiguous. The entity
check (qc_entities.py) wants the rendered name once per paragraph the person
appears in: satisfy it there and let pronouns carry the rest. In scenes with
several men of the same side (constant in this book), err toward naming to
kill pronoun fog. One rendering per referent, decided in glossary.json and
checked against authority.json before anything is romanized; pinyin except
for settled English forms (Chiang Kai-shek, Yangtze, Canton where period
flavor wants it) and the shelf's agreed renderings.

## What stays sacred (fidelity is absolute here)

- Every fact, name, place, date, number, quantity, and unit designation
  (第115师, 617名特务), exactly as in the source. Reordering and compression
  change the sentence, never the content.
- The source's own errors and inconsistencies stay visible: rendered as
  printed, flagged in a note where it matters, never silently harmonized.
  This book contradicts itself and other books about contested people and
  events; that disagreement is content, not error.
- Footnote anchors are verbatim substrings of the reading text; when a
  sentence is re-voiced, update its anchor to a phrase that survives.
- Where the book's claims meet the documentary record, the note states the
  verdict: corroborated / uncorroborated / contradicted, with sources. Never
  source LLM-generated content (CLAUDE.md rule 5).

## Application across the book's modes

- **Narrative (the case stories):** the rules above, in full. Keep the pace.
- **Exposition (institutional and historical background):** the highest-risk
  zone for stiltedness, because density invites long sentences and
  dash-glosses. Apply the rules hardest here. A run of exposition should read
  like a good writer telling you something, not a decoded reference entry.
  Distinguish the author's own narration (translate it) from what the English
  reader needs supplied (footnote it); never fold a translator's gloss into
  his sentence.
- **The author's editorial voice:** keep the buttonholing, conversational,
  sometimes indignant energy. This is the signature; flattening it into
  neutral history loses the book.
- **Quoted material (documents, telegrams, slogans, interview quotes):** keep
  the register the source gives it. Documents and telegrams keep their
  formality; slogans (逼供信, 打着红旗反红旗, 砸烂公检法) are kept and
  glossed, not paraphrased away; quoted speech is natural and contracted,
  differentiated by speaker. Gangsters, generals, Mao, Kang Sheng, and
  provincial cadres do not talk alike; keep the voice sheets in HANDOFF
  current and consult them at every dialogue scene. Registers formal by
  design keep their formality, but even they must sound like real speech or
  real formal writing, never wooden.

## Enrichment doctrine: where rendering ends and inventing begins

Enriching the RENDERING of what the source holds is craft. Adding to the
CONTENT is fabrication, the worst error in this work, because nothing
downstream catches it. And this book is history: an invented "fact" in a
footnoted documentary edition is a real falsehood, not just a bad sentence.
The latitude the shelf's novels enjoyed does NOT carry over. The nonfiction
default is: when in doubt, do not enrich.

What remains legitimate is exactness, not decoration: choosing the precise
English verb for the action the source states; completing an English idiom
the sense requires; making explicit a physical relation the source's grammar
already entails. Test: would a reader of the source already picture this,
unprompted, from what the source built? If yes, it is rendering. If it needs
the benefit of the doubt, it is invention; stop.

Invention, forbidden absolutely:
- new events, actions, or facts about place, person, motive, date, number;
- INVENTED PRECISION, the deadly one, dressed as enrichment: where the
  source is vague, English stays vague. 多时 never becomes "three weeks";
  上百位 never becomes "a hundred and twenty"; 一批 never becomes "forty."
  Definiteness the source withholds is withheld. This class greps better
  than it samples; watch for it.
- emotional or interpretive overlay the source keeps closed.
Second test: could a careful bilingual reader point at the source and say
"that is not there and is not entailed"? If yes, cut it.

Asymmetry: whatever latitude exists touches only word-choice. It NEVER
touches a number, name, date, unit, or factual specific; those are copied
exactly, never grounded, rounded, or sharpened.

## Understatement doctrine: trust the fact, without draining the author

Default: trust the fact to carry its charge; the reflexive intensifier
weakens. Put intensity in the verb and the noun, not in an adverb. This
disciplines the NARRATOR'S descriptive register: strip "very," "utterly,"
"terribly" from straight description, physical violence, and the accounts of
purges and executions, where the plain statement hits hardest.

But understatement has a direction. It never lowers the temperature of a
source that runs hot, and this author runs hot often. Keep the heat where
the source has it:
- his own raised voice (admiring, sardonic, genuinely indignant in the
  political asides): vehement in the source, vehement in English;
- rhetorical set-pieces and slogans: full force;
- a quoted speaker's heightened moment: a speaker who rages, rages.

Governing test: is the heat in the source, or am I adding it, or removing
it? Match the source's temperature. Plain where plain, hot where hot. Avoid
both failure modes: purple adverb-pushed English, and a bloodless uniform
cool that drains the author's passion, humor, and anger.

## Interested-witness doctrine: the partisanship is content, not error

*China's Secret War* is written from inside its subject: it celebrates the
CCP's hidden front, adopts its vocabulary (特务 for the enemy's agents, 汉奸
for collaborators, 敌/我 for the two sides), treats its own side's violence
more gently than the enemy's, and is at its most partisan on the contested
episodes (the purges, the "Rescue Campaign," Kang Sheng, Pan Hannian, the
Gao Gang affair, Taiwan).

Rules:
- **Render the partisan voice faithfully.** Do not neutralize loaded terms
  into false balance in the text. 汉奸 stays "traitor / collaborator"; 我方
  is "our side" when he speaks as a partisan; keep the 我们 when he includes
  himself and the reader.
- **Do not soften or sharpen his verdicts.** If he excuses an atrocity or
  passes over a victim, translate exactly what he wrote. Silently editing
  his judgment destroys the evidence of how the book was made.
- **Put the counter-record in the footnote, never the text.** Where the
  account is self-serving, one-sided, or contradicted by the record, the
  note says so, with sources and a stated verdict (CORROBORATED / PARTLY /
  UNCORROBORATED / CONTRADICTED). The text stays faithful; the apparatus is
  where honesty lives. This is CLAUDE.md's "author as interested witness"
  class, and in this book it is central, not occasional.
- **Loaded terms get a first-appearance note, once.** Explain that 特务 and
  汉奸 carry the author's side's charge, then let the rendering stand.

## Apparatus, in the same spirit (two heuristics; full policy in CLAUDE.md)

- **Footnote the invisible logic.** When the narrative assumes knowledge its
  original reader had silently (who Kang Sheng was, what the 中统/军统 split
  meant, why the 八办 could exist openly, what 抢救运动 refers to), the
  English reader needs the note or the passage reads as a non-sequitur.
- **Footnote the real world, not the book's own shorthand.** History,
  offices, customs, geography, money, and contested claims earn notes. The
  author's own coined labels and nicknames get a glossary line and a first-
  use gloss, not a research footnote.

## Register-drift discipline (the measured guard)

The shelf's hardest-won lesson: voice drifts formal over a long book without
any single chapter looking wrong. Measure every unit against the FROZEN ch01
reference, never a running average: `check_register.py --ref
out/ch01_reading.md`. The primary signal is the dialogue contraction rate;
if it falls below roughly half the reference rate, the unit has gone formal.
Caveat for this book: it is reportage, and some units carry little quoted
speech. In a unit with only a handful of dialogue sentences the contraction
rate is noise; judge those units on the narratorial signals (sentence length
spread, question and exclamation retention, em-dash and semicolon rates) and
say in PROGRESS that the dialogue metric was quiet. Do NOT blanket-contract:
the exempt registers (quoted documents and telegrams, slogans and set-piece
phrases, a speaker formal by design, the author's own formal passages) keep
their form. Punctuation rates vary legitimately with content; report the
spread, do not fake uniformity.

## How to add to this sheet

When the commissioner corrects a line, record the CLASS of error here, not
just the one fix, in the format that travels with its reasoning:
- **RULE.** The general rule that prevents the whole class.
- **WHY IT HAPPENED.** The source-language or process cause, so the class is
  recognizable next time.
- **FIX.** The before and the after.
- **CHECK.** The script or the read that catches a recurrence.
A single correction is a data point; the rule that prevents the whole class
is the deliverable.

## The one-line summary

Cut the scaffolding. Break the walls. Trust the short sentence. Keep the
author's heat. Keep the furniture nailed down. Hide the machine.
