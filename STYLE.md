# STYLE.md — the prose contract for this translation

This is the falsifiable standard the reading text must meet. It is distilled from
the translation shelf's prose contracts and calibrated from commissioner feedback
at the first-chapter voice gate. Read it once, early, every batch. It governs the
translation itself; the apparatus policy at the end governs the footnotes.

The lesson behind it is structural, not lexical: the diction is usually fine; the
sentence architecture, rhythm, and paragraphing are where translationese lives.
Fixing the voice is mostly cutting, breaking, reordering, and compressing, and
only occasionally choosing a more vivid word. Do not reach for grander
vocabulary. Reach for cleaner structure.

## The core principle

Write English that reads as if it were composed in English by a first-rate
novelist, not decoded from another language. Fidelity is to the meaning and to
the image, never to the syntax or the information order of the source. The
failure mode to hunt down is audible machinery: prose in which a reader can hear
the source grammar and the translator's own glosses showing through. Good
translation hides the machinery completely.

The test: could a first-rate contemporary translator of serious literary fiction
have written this sentence, unprompted, as original English? If it sounds like a
careful, faithful decoding, it fails. If it sounds like a novel, it passes.

## Preserve the source's register; de-stiffen the machine, not the voice

Every book has a keynote register — cool and restrained, warm and expansive,
dry, ornate, comic. **That register is the thing to protect.** Stiffness in a
draft is almost never the register; it is the machinery (long sentences,
dash-glosses, comma trains, calques). Strip the machinery and leave the voice.

The trap to name explicitly: when a restrained, analytical voice reads a little
stiff, the cure is NOT to warm it up — not to make the narration chatty, lush, or
emotive. A cool voice that reads slightly austere is working as intended; loosen
its sentences, not its temperature. De-stiffen the *how*, never the *who*.

## What "stilted" means — the failure modes to eliminate

1. **The dashed-in appositive gloss.** The loudest tell of translationese: a
   relative clause or identifying phrase jammed between dashes to deliver a fact
   in passing. Keep the fact; give it its own clean clause, moving forward.
2. **The long periodic sentence as default.** Three or four ideas stacked with
   "and," "which," and subordinate clauses until the thing sags. When every
   sentence is built this way the prose has no pulse.
3. **Flat literalism, especially of set idioms.** Rendering an idiom or a stative
   construction word for word into abstract, lifeless English. The meaning is
   right and the life is gone. (For idioms the rule has a second half — natural
   in the text, literal in the note; see Diction.)
4. **Doubled synonyms.** Many languages pair near-synonyms freely. Carried across
   as a pair it reads as padding. English wants the one strongest word, or one
   verb doing the work of two.
5. **The dense wall paragraph.** Several beats with no break, so the eye and the
   ear get no rest.
6. **Adjective-linking-verb strings** ("was dutiful and professional and
   attentive"): describe by letting a thing act, not by chaining "was + adj."
7. **The body-part-as-agent calque.** Recast so the person acts, not the mouth or
   the hand. (Genuinely idiomatic English — "her eyes smiled" — is fine; the
   target is the calque where a body part performs a human act on its own.)
8. **Pronoun fog**, the opposite danger — see Names and pronouns.

## The rules

### Sentence architecture and rhythm
- **Vary sentence length deliberately; use the short declarative as an
  instrument.** It lands a fact, resets the rhythm, and creates emphasis by
  isolation. Follow a long sentence with a short one.
- **One idea, one clause, moving forward.** Prefer a run of clean statements over
  clauses nested inside clauses.
- **Reorder freely; end on the strongest element.** Source order is never
  binding. Put the point first and let specifics expand it; land on the concrete
  word, not a trailing subordinate phrase. A colon states a thing and then
  unpacks it.
- **Active, transitive verbs and real agents.** Avoid the reflexive passive.

### Paragraphing — respect the parity the pipeline enforces
Where the build pairs one English paragraph to one source paragraph and a check
enforces the count, **do not split or merge paragraphs to improve rhythm** — a
mismatch breaks the pairing and the build. Do all rhythm work *inside* the
paragraph: short sentences, breaks, a one-line beat that would elsewhere stand
alone stays a sentence here. The only merges allowed are the mechanical
extractor-splits the ingest leaves, which are artifacts of segmentation, not the
author's paragraphing.

### Dialogue
- **Normalize dialogue to English convention.** Where the source marks speech
  differently — or leaves it unmarked — set every line of dialogue in quotation
  marks. This is a floor, not a stylistic choice.
- **Natural and contracted, differentiated by character.** Contractions make a
  living voice; use them by default in speech, and keep each speaker's manner
  distinct (see the voice sheets in the handoff).
- **Keep the occasional grave, uncontracted line where the weight wants it** — a
  flat declaration, a formal or ceremonial register, an aphorism. Measured, not
  stuffed.
- **Name the speaker once per paragraph, not every line** — see below.

### Names and pronouns
Native prose leans on pronouns; repeating a full name every line reads
mechanical. But the entity/content checks pull the other way, and the resolution
is one rule:
- **Name each character once in every paragraph the source names them, then let
  pronouns carry the rest.** Name them on a new beat or as the object of a
  sentence; use the pronoun within the run.
- If one check accepts a short form (a surname) while a stricter one wants the
  full rendering, satisfy the stricter: where a paragraph would otherwise carry
  only a pronoun, an endearment, or an address-form, re-anchor the full name
  once.
- In an action scene with two figures of the same side, err toward naming to kill
  pronoun fog.
This once-per-paragraph naming is partly an artifact of the checks, accepted as
the cost; the aim is one clean anchor, not the three or four per paragraph that
read stiff.

### Diction and idiom
- **Idiomatic before literal, concrete before abstract.** Choose the physical
  verb and the specific noun.
- **Render a set idiom to its living English equivalent, not a calque.** The
  natural idiom goes in the text.
- **Then preserve the lost image or allusion in a footnote when the nuance is
  worth keeping.** Natural in the text, literal in the note: the prose reads
  cleanly, and the note rewards the reader who wants the depth. (See the
  apparatus policy.)
- **Never send the reader to a dictionary in the text.** Prefer the common word
  unless the precision is the whole point (then keep it and footnote).
- **Compress where English compresses; understatement over intensifiers** (see
  the doctrines below).
- **No archaism, no purple.** Period flavour comes from precise nouns — the
  offices, tools, and geography of the setting — not from fake-antique grammar.

### Punctuation
- **Use the full toolkit for rhythm:** the period chops and isolates, the colon
  states then expands, the semicolon yokes two balanced clauses.
- **The em dash is for a genuine interruption or a real dramatic break only,
  never for a parenthetical identifying gloss.** If a dash carries a fact that
  could be a clause, rewrite it as a clause. (Prose written TO the commissioner —
  handoffs, progress logs, chat — uses no em dashes at all; the translation may,
  as English punctuation demands.)
- **Comma pile-up is the commonest tell.** Read it aloud; if you would run out of
  air, split it.

## Names and romanization — one rendering per referent
- Decide every rendering ONCE, in the glossary, checked against the shelf
  authority before anything is romanized; use it consistently for the whole book.
- Give real people, places, and institutions their **attested** form; reserve a
  romanization of your own only where no attested form exists, and mark it as
  such.
- **Preserve the source's own transliterations where they are the author's**, and
  flag them in a note.
- **Keep an honorific or address-form when it carries meaning** — a marker of who
  is speaking, or of the relationship — rather than flattening it away.

## What stays sacred (fidelity is absolute)
- Every fact, name, place, date, number, unit, and quantity, exactly as in the
  source. Reordering and compression change the sentence, never the content.
- **Numbers:** a real quantity is fixed in the English, never dropped and never
  "noised." Only non-quantitative numerals — idioms and lexical compounds that
  merely contain a digit — belong in the number-check's noise list, each with a
  comment. Never noise a real number.
- **The source's own errors of fact stay visible** — rendered as printed, flagged
  in a note where it matters — never silently harmonized. (The lone exception is
  a mechanical typo in imprint/colophon matter, corrected with the discrepancy
  recorded.)
- **Footnote anchors are verbatim substrings of the reading text.** When a
  sentence is re-voiced, update its anchor to a phrase that survives, or move the
  note; an unmatched anchor is a build failure by design.

## Apparatus doctrine — footnotes

Be generous — the standing instruction is to footnote freely — but **never pad,
and never invent.** Density is a reader model, not a quota: the reader has no
background in the source's history, society, or custom, and anything such a
reader would miss earns a note.

**What earns a note:**
1. **Translation uncertainty** — state the choice; never smooth an ambiguity
   silently.
2. **A real-world reference a non-specialist would miss** — history, people,
   institutions, money, geography, custom, material culture. State the verdict.
3. **Texture and nuance lost in translation** — the class the translation, by
   choosing the natural English, necessarily drops. This is where footnotes are
   most wanted. It includes:
   - **Telling names,** where a name carries lexical or thematic meaning the
     original reader gets for free. Note them at first appearance.
   - **Title-words and loaded keywords,** where the choice of one term over a
     near-neighbour carries the theme.
   - **Idioms, set phrases, and allusions** rendered plainly in the text but
     opaque underneath: give the literal image or the source of the allusion in
     the note.
   - **Symbolism** a native reader reads without prompting.
   - **Honorifics and register** where they carry meaning.
4. **The author as an interested witness** — where the author's framing or
   sympathy shows.

**What does NOT earn a note:** the fiction's own furniture — invented characters,
cover names, made-up places — gets a glossary line at most. Footnotes are for the
real world behind the fiction, not the story's own props.

**Fact-checking (non-negotiable).** Fact-check every historical claim against
real scholarship — never an AI-written source. State the verdict *in the note*
(corroborated / uncorroborated / contradicted, or plainly "historical"). When
sources conflict, say so, and do not silently "correct" the source to match one
of them; render the source faithfully and let the note carry the discrepancy.
Repetition is not corroboration; trace a claim to its earliest source.

**Craft of the note:** first-appearance discipline, with a "not re-noted" ledger
kept in the progress log; the note says MORE than the glossary row; the anchor is
verbatim and verified at write time (through the merge tool, never a shell
heredoc); a chapter heading carries no note; numbering is the builder's; bodies
are XHTML with numeric character references only (a literal dash or curly quote
is fine, a named entity is not). Keep nuance notes tight but rich.

## Enrichment doctrine — where rendering ends and inventing begins
Enriching the RENDERING of what the source already holds is craft. Adding to the
CONTENT is fabrication, and fabrication is the worst error in this work because
nothing downstream catches it.
- **Legitimate enrichment:** make the implicit explicit; choose the exact word;
  name an ambient condition the scene has already fixed; give the sensory quality
  the source has already asserted. Test: would a reader of the source already
  picture this, unprompted? If yes, enrich. If not, stop.
- **Invention, forbidden:** new events, actions, or facts; emotional overlay the
  source keeps closed; and above all **INVENTED PRECISION** — where the source is
  vague, English stays vague ("a good while" never becomes "three weeks"; "a man"
  never becomes "a tall man"). Definiteness the source withholds is withheld.
- **Asymmetry:** enrichment touches only atmosphere and word-choice. It NEVER
  touches a number, name, date, unit, or factual specific; those are copied
  exactly, never grounded, rounded, or sharpened.
- **The dial:** a light touch, not a repaint. One exact noun and one grounding
  phrase lift a line from decoded to composed; piling on detail is its own
  infidelity, overriding the source's economy.

## Understatement doctrine — trust the fact, match the temperature
Default: trust the fact to carry its charge; the reflexive intensifier weakens.
Put intensity in the verb and the noun, not in an adverb; strip "very," "utterly,"
"terribly" from straight description, physical violence, and grief, where the
plain statement hits harder.
But understatement has a direction; it never lowers the temperature of a source
that already runs hot. Keep the heat where the source has it: a character's
heightened moment, a rhetorical set-piece, the author's own raised voice.
Governing test: is the heat in the source, or am I adding it, or removing it?
Plain where it is plain, hot where it is hot.

## The one-line summary
Cut the scaffolding. Break the walls within the paragraph. Trust the short
sentence. Preserve the source's own temperature. Hide the machine — and put
everything it hides into a footnote.
