A moonlit night, the moon at first quarter. The hour of zi was not yet come, and sunrise was three double-hours off at the least.
Lu Xiaofeng was back at the inn. He had called for a table of good wine and good food in his room, and he laughed. "Say what you will, at the least I can still eat and drink my fill, and merrily too."
"You ought to get some sleep," said Hua Manlou.
"If a man like Huo Tianqing had challenged you to a duel at sunrise," said Lu Xiaofeng, "could you sleep?"
"I could not," said Hua Manlou.
Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "The best thing about you is that you never tell a lie; the pity is that the plain truth, out of your mouth, sometimes sounds for all the world like one."
"I can't sleep," said Hua Manlou, "simply because I don't understand the man at all."
"He is a hard man to understand," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"How long have you known him?" said Hua Manlou.
"Getting on four years," said Lu Xiaofeng. "Four years ago Yan Tieshan went up Mount Tai to watch the sunrise, and Huo Tianqing went along; and that day I'd happened to fix a somersault-match with a certain thief, up on the very summit of Mount Tai."
"And how much do you understand of him?" said Hua Manlou.
"A little," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"You said he's young in years, but very high in his martial generation?" said Hua Manlou.
"Have you never heard of the Two Elders of Mount Shang, the Heaven-Pine and the Cloud-Crane?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"The Two Elders of Mount Shang have been honoured this age and more as the Mount Tai and Pole Star of the martial world," said Hua Manlou. "I'd have heard of them if I were deaf."
"They say he is the youngest martial-brother of the Two Elders of Mount Shang," said Lu Xiaofeng.
Hua Manlou looked moved. "If the Two Elders of Mount Shang are living yet, they must be seventy or eighty; Huo Tianqing is thirty at the most. How can the years between martial-brothers run so wide?"
Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "There are husbands and wives forty and fifty years apart. Why not martial-brothers?"
"So the great hero of Guanzhong, the Shanxi Wild Goose, whose name has stood near forty years, still ranks as no more than his martial-nephew," said Hua Manlou.
"Just so," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"In his day the Old Man of Heaven's Birds shook the eight quarters," said Hua Manlou, "yet in his whole life he took only these two, the Two Elders of Mount Shang, for pupils. How did a Huo Tianqing spring up all at once besides?"
Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "The Hua family plainly had only six sons; how did a seventh, a you, spring up all at once?"
That parents should get sons, and masters take pupils, is a thing, after all, that no one else has any say in.
But a shadow of worry had come over Hua Manlou's face. "I have never met the Shanxi Wild Goose, yet even I know his lightness-skill and his palm-work are called the Twin Perfections of Guanzhong. I wonder how Huo Tianqing stands against him."
"I've never seen Huo Tianqing lift a hand either," said Lu Xiaofeng. "But watching him gather up a man as heavy as Yan Tieshan and still work the swallow's-three-skimmings lightness-skill, on that one stroke alone there are few men on earth to match him."
"And you?" said Hua Manlou.
Lu Xiaofeng did not answer. He never would answer such a thing. The truth is that, saving himself, there was hardly a second soul on earth who knew just how good his own skill was.
But this time Hua Manlou seemed set on getting to the bottom of it. "Are you sure of beating him?" he asked again.
Lu Xiaofeng still did not answer. He only poured a cup of wine and drank it slowly down.
Hua Manlou sighed suddenly. "You aren't sure. That's why you daren't even drink too much."
Lu Xiaofeng did not, in truth, drink like this as a rule.
Since coming here, Princess Danfeng had turned surprisingly meek; she had sat quietly by all this while, listening, and now of a sudden she asked, "You said just now you'd fixed a somersault-match with a thief on the very summit of Mount Tai. Who was the thief?"
Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "A king of thieves, a thief above all thieves, who has robbed every hand under heaven and left no equal. And the men he's robbed, far from taking it ill, count it an honour."
"Why?" said Princess Danfeng.
"Because there aren't many yet worth his robbing," said Lu Xiaofeng, "and because he never steals anything of real value. He steals only ever for a wager."
He smiled again, and went on, "Once someone bet him he could never make off with the chamber-pot of the wife of Chen Fuzhou, the first miser under heaven."
Princess Danfeng could not help a lovely laugh. "And?"
"And he won," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"Why did you match somersaults with him?" said Princess Danfeng.
"Because I knew I could never out-steal him," said Lu Xiaofeng, "but I wanted to win off him the fifty jars of old wine he'd just won off somebody else."
"There's the way of it," said Princess Danfeng, with a lovely smile. "That's playing your own strength against his weakness. Why can't you deal with Huo Tianqing the same way? You needn't fight him to the death at all."
But Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "There's a kind of man in this world that no trick will serve you against. Ximen Chuixue is such a man. So is Huo Tianqing."
"You think he truly means to settle it with you, life against life?" said Princess Danfeng.
Lu Xiaofeng's mood was heavy. "Yan Tieshan treated him as a prince treats a knight of the realm. A kindness of that kind he must repay; he has long since held his own life cheap against it."
"But you needn't be the same!" said Princess Danfeng.
Lu Xiaofeng only smiled, as though he had no wish to speak of it further, and rose and walked slowly to the window.
The window had been propped open all along, and he found now that at some point an old man in a long gown and a little cap had carried a stool out into the courtyard and sat there smoking a long-stemmed pipe. The night was far gone, yet the old man showed not the least sign of wanting his bed; he sat there at his ease, as though he meant to sit until the sky grew light.
Lu Xiaofeng called out to him, laughing, "The night is cold and the dew chill, sir. If you've a mind to it, come in and take a cup or two with us to see the long night through."
But the old man paid him no more heed than if he were deaf, as though he had not heard a word. Lu Xiaofeng could only smile wryly.
Princess Danfeng, though, was cross. "When a body asks you kindly to drink, you'll drink whether you like it or no," she said with a cold laugh.
She darted to the window again and flicked her hand, and the cup of wine in it flew out at the old man, fast and true, without spilling so much as a drop.
The old man sneered of a sudden, hooked out a hand and caught the cup; then he flung the whole cupful on the ground, bit the empty cup to pieces and swallowed them down, crunch by crunch, as easily as a man eating broad beans.
Princess Danfeng stared, dumbfounded. "Is there something wrong with the old fellow? He won't drink the wine, and eats the cup instead."
Lu Xiaofeng's eyes glinted, and he smiled. "That will be because the wine is mine, and the cup is not."
Just then another man came in from beyond the courtyard, and this one was a pedlar selling meat buns. At such an hour of the night, could he truly be thinking of trade here?
Princess Danfeng blinked. "You there, are your meat buns for sale?"
"For sale, so long as there's money," said the pedlar.
"How much apiece?" said Princess Danfeng.
"Cheap enough," said the pedlar. "Ten thousand taels of silver the bun, and not a copper less."
Princess Danfeng's face changed a little; then she sneered. "Good. I'll buy two of your buns at ten thousand taels each. Bring them over!"
"Right," said the pedlar.
He had just picked up two buns when a yellow dog shot out from the corner of the wall, barking at him, wah, wah.
The pedlar glared. "Do you want my meat buns too, like the young lady there? Don't you know a meat bun is a thing for throwing at dogs?"
And he did throw the meat bun at the dog. The yellow dog stopped barking at once, seized the bun, took two bites, and then with one pitiful yelp rolled over on the ground; the live dog was a dead dog.
Princess Danfeng went pale. "There's poison in your buns?"
The pedlar smiled a little, and said easily, "Poison, and human flesh in the filling besides."
"You dare bring buns like that out to sell?" cried Princess Danfeng.
The pedlar rolled his eyes. "I sell what's mine; buy or don't, it's all one to me. I never forced you."
Princess Danfeng flushed red with anger, and could barely keep from rushing out to box the man's ears.
But Lu Xiaofeng quietly took her hand; and just then a voice came drawling out a chant: "Such stars, and such a night! For whom do I stand in the wind and dew at midnight?"
A threadbare scholar, reeking of sour poverty, came sauntering into the courtyard with his hands clasped behind him, and smiled at the bun-pedlar. "How many have you poisoned to death today?"
The pedlar rolled his eyes. "These buns of mine only poison dogs; they'll not kill a man. Don't believe me? Try one."
He tossed a bun over, and the poor scholar actually caught it and ate it down, then patted his belly and laughed. "It seems these buns of yours not only won't kill a man, they'll even cure what ails him."
From beyond the wall a voice said, "Cure what?"
"Hunger," said the poor scholar.
"I've got that ailment too, and badly," said the voice beyond the wall. "Be quick and fetch a bun to cure it."
"Right," said the pedlar.
He picked up another bun and pitched it at the top of the wall, and all at once a tousle-headed beggar was there, mouth open, catching the bun square; one snap of his jaws, and it went down whole. The pedlar's hands never stopped, flinging out seven or eight buns in a stream, and as fast as he threw them the beggar swallowed them, until all seven or eight were gone, down the gullet of that thin little scarecrow of a beggar.
The poor scholar laughed. "Well, that should have cured your hunger by now."
The beggar pulled a long face. "You've had me there. These buns won't poison a man, but they'll swell him up till he bursts."
From beyond the courtyard yet another voice laughed. "Bursting's no matter. Burst, starved, or nagged to death by a wife, I've a physic for the lot."
A travelling quack came limping in, a lame man, a medicine-chest on his back and a string of physic-bells in his hand. And that bleak, deserted courtyard filled up all at once, as though for a fair; and after him came, if you please, a pedlar of face-powder and a vegetable-seller with his shoulder-baskets too.
Princess Danfeng watched until her eyes near glazed over. Little as she had knocked about the jianghu, she could see now that these men had all come on their account. The strange thing was that they only crowded into the courtyard, with no sign of coming in to trouble them.
She could not help asking under her breath, "Do you think these people have come to avenge Yan Tieshan?"
Lu Xiaofeng shook his head, smiling. "How would a boss like Yan have friends of this sort?"
"But I don't think they're really quacks and pedlars," said Princess Danfeng. "They all seem to have some skill in them."
"The marketplace has always been a place where dragons hide and tigers crouch," said Lu Xiaofeng mildly. "So long as they don't come for us, we needn't meddle in their affairs."
Hua Manlou smiled suddenly. "And when did you turn into a man who won't meddle?"
Lu Xiaofeng smiled too. "Just this minute," he said.
The watch-drums sounded: the third watch was past.
The old man with the pipe stood up suddenly, stretched himself, and said, "The man who called us here, why hasn't he come himself?"
So he was neither deaf nor dumb after all.
But Princess Danfeng was more puzzled still: who had called these men here? And why?
"The long night is near its end," said the poor scholar. "He'll be coming soon, I dare say."
"I'll take a look," said the bun-pedlar.
His hands went to work again without a pause, flinging out all the buns in his basket, dozens of them, stacked one on another straight up, seven or eight chi high. Then the pedlar sprang up and stood on the pile of meat buns, poised on one leg like a golden cockerel, square and steady, without a quiver. His hands were not only quick and sure; his lightness-skill too might be reckoned first among the men of the jianghu.
Princess Danfeng sighed, and murmured, "It's no easy thing, this roving the jianghu; I see that now."
Hua Manlou smiled. "To see it is always something gained."
Then the pedlar gave a great shout: "He's here!"
That one cry, "He's here," seemed to brace every man on the spot; even Princess Danfeng's heart beat quicker, for she had long been longing to see who it would be.
But when she saw him, her heart fell a little. A young girl's fancies run always to the beautiful: in her imagining, if not some graceful young knight-errant, then at the very least a majestic hero of the jianghu, armed with rare and secret skills.
The man who came instead was a bald-headed old fellow with a sallow, sickly face, in a suit of dust-grey homespun that reached just to the knees; on his feet white cloth socks and grey cloth shoes. For all the world a country codger come in to market. But his eyes were bright, keen and blazing, and shot authority in every direction.
The strange thing was this: these men had plainly been waiting for him, yet now that he had come not one of them went over to greet him. They only made way for him in silence.
The bald old man cast his eyes about, and then of a sudden strode straight for the room where Lu Xiaofeng sat. He did not seem to walk fast; but in two or three steps he had crossed the courtyard and passed through the door.
The door had stood open all along. He neither knocked nor gave anyone a greeting, but sat himself down, bold as brass, across from Lu Xiaofeng, lifted the wine-jar from the floor and sniffed at it. "Good wine."
Lu Xiaofeng nodded. "Good wine indeed."
"Half each?" said the bald old man.
"Right," said Lu Xiaofeng.
The bald old man said not another word, but hoisted the wine-jar to his mouth and poured it down, glug, glug, glug.
In no time half the jar was inside him, and the sallow face turned all at once red and glowing, the whole man seeming to come alive; he wiped his mouth on his sleeve and said, "His mother's, but that's got body."
Lu Xiaofeng said nothing either, but took the jar and drank, no slower than the old man, no slower than any man alive.
When the jar was empty the bald old man burst out laughing. "Good. The wine's got body, and so has the man."
Lu Xiaofeng wiped his own mouth on his sleeve. "The man has body; that's why the wine has."
The bald old man looked at him. "Three years without a sight of you, and you still haven't drunk yourself dead."
"Good men die young; a plague lasts a thousand years," said Lu Xiaofeng. "It's only you I fear for. You're a good man."
The bald old man glared. "Who says I'm a good man?"
Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "Who in the jianghu doesn't say the Shanxi Wild Goose has grit and stands by his friends, that he's his mother's own first and finest good man?"
The bald old man roared. "You're a great plague, and I'm a great good man; his mother's, but that's a fine joke."
Princess Danfeng watched him, scarcely believing her eyes. Never could she have dreamed that this bald, boorish old codger with a mouthful of coarse talk was that famous man of thirty years' renown, the great hero who had cowed all Guanzhong with a pair of iron palms: the Shanxi Wild Goose.
For all that, to be called a great hero is no small thing. Yet this old man had not a particle of the great hero about him; could that, perhaps, be the very secret of his success? Princess Danfeng could not work it out. And she was beginning to find that the things she could not work out were somehow more and more.
The Shanxi Wild Goose's laughter died; his eyes, keen and bright, fixed on Lu Xiaofeng. "You'll not have looked to see me come after you."
Lu Xiaofeng owned it: "I did not."
"The truth is, I knew the moment you reached Taiyuan," said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "No wonder in that. It would be a wonder if you didn't know when I'd come."
"And yet I've only come to you now," said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
"You're a busy man," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"I'm not busy in the least. I didn't come, because you were my martial-uncle's guest; and since I couldn't very well fight him for the honour of hosting you, I did best to play the blind man," said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "And here I'd thought that, once I'd shaved my whiskers, even old friends would take me for a stranger."
The Shanxi Wild Goose roared again. "I always did think those two draggled whiskers of yours an eyesore."
"You may find them an eyesore," said Lu Xiaofeng. "Others don't."
The Shanxi Wild Goose's laughter stopped. "Huo Tianqing is my martial-uncle. Many in the jianghu won't credit it, but you at least must know it's so."
"I know it," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"The old freak smoking his pipe out there, his name's Fan E," said the Shanxi Wild Goose. "Do you know him?"
"Would that be the great Master Fan of old," said Lu Xiaofeng, "who single-handed stormed Flying-Fish Marsh and levelled the Eight Great Strongholds, whose one tobacco-pipe strikes the body's thirty-six great pressure-points and its seventy-two small?"
"The same," said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
"Of the Two Talents of the Northwest, Fan and Jian, then that sour scarecrow of a scholar must be the one and only heir of the Finger-Flicking art, the great Master Jian the Second," said Lu Xiaofeng.
The Shanxi Wild Goose nodded. "That beggar, the quack, the bun-pedlar and the vegetable-seller, the powder-pedlar, and the innkeeper here, and fat Wang still selling noodles at the gate: the seven of them are sworn brothers. Men call them the Seven Heroes of the Marketplace, or else the Seven Righteous of Shanxi."
"A high-spirited lot, these renowned knights and men of honour," said Lu Xiaofeng with a mild laugh, "to have crowded, every one, into this little courtyard tonight to take the cool air."
"You truly don't know what they've come for?" said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
"I don't," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"They're all of my own school too," said the Shanxi Wild Goose. "By generation, some of them are even Huo Tianqing's martial-grandsons."
Lu Xiaofeng laughed again. "The man's fortune's-favoured indeed!"
"Sixty years ago, when the founding patriarch set up the Heaven's Bird sect," said the Shanxi Wild Goose, "the very first great precept was that we honour our master and hold to the Way. Rank and rule are things that must never, never be got wrong."
"Wrong they must never be," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"The founding patriarch gave his whole life to the martial art, and thought of a household only in his last years," said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
"So the Old Man of Heaven's Birds took a wife, and got a son?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"It's a thing few in the jianghu know, true enough," said the Shanxi Wild Goose. "The patriarch got his heir only in his seventy-seventh year."
"And that heir is Huo Tianqing?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"The same," said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "Now at last I see why he's so young in years and so terribly high in generation."
"And why the burden on his shoulders is a terrible one," said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
"Oh?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
The Shanxi Wild Goose's face turned grave of a sudden. "He is not only the patriarch's own blood, the one lamp of his line; he is the one man who can carry on the traditions of the Heaven's Bird sect. We are steeped in the great kindness of our school; sooner than let the least mischance touch him, we would be ground to powder, every one. That much you surely understand."
"I understand it," said Lu Xiaofeng.
The Shanxi Wild Goose heaved a long sigh. "So if he should have the ill luck to die at sunrise tomorrow, of the several hundred disciples of our Heaven's Bird sect, high and low, not one could go on living either."
Lu Xiaofeng knit his brows. "How should he die?"
"If he loses to you, then even should you not kill him, he'll not go on living," said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
"I know he's a man of fierce and unbending temper," said Lu Xiaofeng. "But it's by no means certain he'll lose!"
"Certain, no," said the Shanxi Wild Goose.
"And if he beats me," said Lu Xiaofeng mildly, "won't all the several hundred disciples of your Heaven's Bird sect, high and low, have a deal of face by it?"
"You are my friend," said the Shanxi Wild Goose. "I've no wish to see you lose to him, and the goodwill between you spoiled."
Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "You're a good man, truly."
The Shanxi Wild Goose's face seemed to redden again. "Once the two of you cross hands," he said with a wry smile, "whoever wins and whoever loses, the end of it is not to be borne. Master Huo and you are friends in the Way besides. Why must it come to this?"
Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "Now at last I take your meaning. You want me gone from here before sunrise, so he'll not find me."
The Shanxi Wild Goose said nothing at all; and to say nothing was to say yes.
Princess Danfeng sneered suddenly. "Now I take your meaning too. You called all these men here to force him out, so Huo Tianqing might win without a fight; and if you couldn't, you'd deal with him yourselves. There's little of the night left now: even if he beat off the whole pack of you, by sunrise he'd have no strength left to face Huo Tianqing." Her face was iron-grey; she went on with a cold laugh, "A neat scheme, I must say. It would take a great hero like you to think of it!"
The Shanxi Wild Goose's face went now green, now white; then all at once he threw back his head and laughed wildly. "Good, well cursed! Only, worthless as I am, the Shanxi Wild Goose could never do a thing like that!"
"If you couldn't do that," said Princess Danfeng, "then if he won't go, what will you do?"
The Shanxi Wild Goose rose sharply to his feet and strode outside, where the whole courtyard-full of men held not a whisper. His bright eyes swept over their faces one by one, and of a sudden he said, "If he won't go, what will you do?"
The bun-pedlar rolled his eyes and said coldly, "Simple enough. If he won't go, I will."
The Shanxi Wild Goose laughed again, and in the laugh there seemed some grief past telling; slowly he nodded. "Good. You go, and I go, and we all go."
"In that case," said the bun-pedlar, "why shouldn't I go first?"
His hand turned over, and out came a keen wrist-cutting dagger; of a sudden he swung it backhand at his own throat. His stroke was steady, and it was fast, very fast. But there was one there faster still.
There came a clang, a scattering of sparks, and the knife in his hand had snapped in two; and a thing that fell with the broken point to the floor turned out to be a half of Lu Xiaofeng's chopstick. The other half was still in Lu Xiaofeng's hand: the knife was of steel, the chopstick of ivory. There cannot be many men under heaven who could break a steel blade with an ivory chopstick.
Of a sudden Princess Danfeng understood why the Shanxi Wild Goose had done as he did: Huo Tianqing was simply no match for Lu Xiaofeng. Others might not know it; the Shanxi Wild Goose knew it very well.
The bun-pedlar stared in astonishment at the broken half-blade in his hand, dumbstruck a long moment; then he stamped his foot in fury, glared up at Lu Xiaofeng and said harshly, "What do you mean by that?"
Lu Xiaofeng smiled a little, and said mildly, "I mean nothing by it, only I've one more thing to ask you!"
"What thing?" said the bun-pedlar.
"When did I ever say I wouldn't go?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
The bun-pedlar was struck dumb.
Lu Xiaofeng sighed lazily. "Fighting is a wearisome, wearing business. Far better I find myself somewhere to sleep. Why should I sit up waiting for other men to fight?"
The bun-pedlar stared at him, his face working as if between tears and laughter; then of a sudden he cried aloud, "Good. Lu Xiaofeng is Lu Xiaofeng after all. From this day, whatever you want of me, if I so much as knit my brow, I'll be your grandson."
Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "A grandson like you I can do without. Only knock a little off the price the next time I buy your buns, and you'll have done handsomely by me."
He caught up the great red cape from the bed-head as he went, tossed off another cup of wine, and said, "Who'll come with me out beyond the walls to Yet Another Village and eat a bowl of Big Pockmarks's dog-meat stew?"
Hua Manlou smiled. "I."
Master Fan the Elder tapped out his pipe of a sudden. "And I."
"Where he goes, I go," said Master Jian the Second. "We two have always been scale and weight."
"I'll come too," the bun-pedlar cried at once.
"You that hawk dog-clubbing buns," said Master Jian the Second, "and you dare go and eat dog-meat? Aren't you afraid all those big dogs' and little dogs' wronged ghosts will play the devil in your belly?"
The bun-pedlar's eyes flew wide. "I'm not afraid to die; what should I be afraid of?"
The Shanxi Wild Goose roared with laughter. "Good, you've grit, lad. Let's all go and eat his mother's dog-meat together; and any man who doesn't come is his mother's own turtle-spawn!"
Hua Manlou smiled, and said slowly, "It seems a man can be a good man after all."
"Once in a while it's no matter," said Lu Xiaofeng. "Make a habit of it, and it won't do."
Hua Manlou could not help asking, "Why?"
Lu Xiaofeng kept his face straight. "Good men die young. Have you never heard the saying?"
His face was straight, but there seemed the shine of hot tears in his eyes.
Princess Danfeng watched them, and of a sudden sighed softly, and softly said to herself, "Whoever says a good man can't be made, let him be his mother's own turtle-spawn."
The dog-meat was all sold; there was no dog-meat. But they did not care. What they had come to eat was never the dog-meat in the first place, but a warmth that heats a man through more than any dog-meat can; and to take one's wine with a warmth like that, there is nothing on earth to match it.
And at sunrise, moreover, a man on a fast horse overtook them and brought a letter.
Huo Tianqing's letter read:
Every day has its sunrise; why should today's appointment not be put off to the day after tomorrow?
You have not failed me; how then could I fail you?
The old debt of the Golden Roc may be settled at any time; the day the Princess comes again is the day I go wandering afar. The pearls and splendour that were the glory of an hour have become the chrysanthemums of yesterday; and so what shines down the ages is these two words alone: honour and duty.
Tianqing bows again.
On the strength of that letter alone a man might have washed down a hundred pecks of wine and lain drunk three days together, the more so with that warmth in it that no downpour could chill.
A downpour. The rain had begun only at noon; by noon they were drunk already, for none would go home sober; they would drink first and go after.
Lu Xiaofeng, half drunk and half not, seeming drunk and seeming sober, could scarcely tell himself whether he was drunk or awake; he sat staring blankly out at the pouring rain beyond the window.
Princess Danfeng watched him, and said of a sudden, "If you don't go, will all those men truly die there?"
Lu Xiaofeng was silent a long while before he said slowly, "Do you know the meaning of these two sayings: there are things a man will not do, and things he must?"
"Of course I do," said Princess Danfeng. "It means that a thing you hold you ought not to do, you will not do though men drive and tempt you to it, though they lay a knife to your neck; and a thing you hold you ought to do, you must do, though it cost you your head and your heart's blood."
Lu Xiaofeng nodded. "Just so; and that is why there have been men who tattooed and disfigured themselves and swallowed burning charcoal to keep faith to the death, and men who took up an iron mace of eighty-three catties to strike down a tyrant."
Princess Danfeng put in quickly, "And that is why Huo Tianqing will pay Yan Tieshan back with his death, and the Shanxi Wild Goose and those sellers of buns and dumplings will spend their lives for him without grudging."
"Whatever they may be," said Lu Xiaofeng, "a man who can live up to those two sayings has not failed those two words, chivalry and honour."
Princess Danfeng sighed softly. "But look the wide world over, and how many are there who can truly live up to those two words, chivalry and honour?"
Hua Manlou, a cup of wine in his hand, chanted low: "The pearls and splendour that were the glory of an hour have become the chrysanthemums of yesterday; and so what shines down the ages is these two words alone, honour and duty... A fine man, this Huo Tianqing; I came near to making too little of him. This calls for a great cup." And he truly raised his cup and drank it off, and seemed a little drunk himself, and murmured, "Only a pity about that Su Shaoying; he too was a fine young man. He should not have died, he should not have died..."
His voice sank lower and lower; he laid his head on the table, and seemed to have fallen asleep.
Princess Danfeng slipped quietly to the window, quietly drew Lu Xiaofeng up, and said softly, "Are you still angry with me?"
"When was I ever angry with you?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
Princess Danfeng gave a lovely smile, bent her head, and asked in a whisper, "And are you still afraid, today, of taking the wrong person?"
Her breath came soft, her fingertips seemed to tremble a little, and her hair carried a fragrance sweeter than any flower.
Lu Xiaofeng might be a gentleman, or he might not; but he was past doubt a man, a man some seven or eight parts gone in drink.
Beyond the window the rain came down in torrents, curtain on close-hung curtain of pearl, shutting off the traveller from the road and the road from the traveller. Within, the room was dim and still as dusk; and through an open door at the back could be seen a bed with fresh-changed sheets.
Lu Xiaofeng found of a sudden that his heart was pounding hard; found of a sudden that Shangguan Danfeng's heart was pounding hard too. "Your heart's racing?" he asked.
"Let's see whose beats the faster."
"How?"
"I'll feel your heart, and you feel mine..."
Then of a sudden, through the rush of rain as thick as ten thousand galloping horses, came the drumming of hoofs as thick as the rain itself: ten-odd fast horses driving through the downpour, sweeping past that lonely village inn.
The riders were all in blue rush-capes and white bamboo hats; and as they passed the window they flung out their hands as one, and there came a whistling, hiss, hiss, hiss, thicker than the raindrops and swifter than the hoofs, dozens of black glints, some driving in through the window, some striking the wall outside.
Lu Xiaofeng turned side-on and had already pulled Princess Danfeng down behind the window.
But Hua Manlou, face-down on the table, had shot to his feet. "Sulphur-and-saltpetre thunderbolts," he cried out.
The five words were not out before there came a roar; and where the black glints had struck, within the window and without, flames leapt up several chi high on the instant, red flames shot through with a ghastly green.
Lu Xiaofeng's face changed. "Get out first, all of you. I'll go and save Zhao the Pockmarked."
Zhao the Pockmarked had gone to sleep; a moment ago they had still heard him snoring.
But in the blink of an eye the flames had sealed the doors, and the very walls outside had caught, past the quenching of any downpour.
Hua Manlou pulled Shangguan Danfeng out with him; the ten-odd riders had galloped on and were far off now, and the men on horseback raised one wild shout of laughter together, and one of them cried aloud, "Lu Xiaofeng, this is but a small lesson for you. Cross us again, and we'll see you dead without a grave to lie in!"
The words were scarcely done before horses and men were curtained off by the pearl-hung rain, and slowly lost to sight.
Look back, and Zhao the Pockmarked's little inn too was swallowed whole in the flames; and where in all that was Lu Xiaofeng to be seen?
Shangguan Danfeng set her teeth. "You wait here. I'll go in and find him."
"Go back in," said Hua Manlou, "and you'll not come out again."
"But he..." said Shangguan Danfeng.
Hua Manlou smiled a little. "He can come out. A fire far bigger than this has never burned him to death yet."
He was drenched to the skin, but his face was calm still.
Just then, from far off, a scream burst out, a scream ghastly and shrill, like a pack of beasts trapped and dying, but very short; it broke off the instant it rose, and after it came the panicked neighing of horses.
Shangguan Danfeng looked struck. "Can those riders just now have fallen foul of somebody's hand as well?"
Then of a sudden came another roar; the burning house was smashed open in a great hole, and a man flew out of it like a ball of living flame, turned one somersault in the rain, dropped to the ground and rolled, rolling the fire out; his clothes and his hair were scorched through in seven or eight places, but he cared not a jot, and with another roll he was on his feet: Lu Xiaofeng.
Shangguan Danfeng let out her breath, and murmured, "It seems the man truly can't be burned to death!"
Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "To burn me to death is no easy thing." He was laughing still, but his face seemed smoked black.
Shangguan Danfeng looked at his face, and smiled of a sudden. "But you used to have four eyebrows; now you've hardly one left."
"Eyebrows may be burned off and grow again," said Lu Xiaofeng mildly. "It's the wine I mourn, those good jars of it..."
Hua Manlou broke in on him of a sudden. "And Zhao the Pockmarked?"
"I don't know," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"He wasn't inside?" said Hua Manlou.
"He was not," said Lu Xiaofeng.
Shangguan Danfeng's face changed. "Then can he be of the Blue-Robe Tower too? Can he have been in league with those men all along? How else should they have known you were here?" She went on with bitterness, "You risked your neck to save him, near burned off your eyebrows for him, and he turns out such a man."
"All I know is that he stews the best dog-meat," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"And nothing else?" said Shangguan Danfeng.
"Nothing else at all," said Lu Xiaofeng.
Shangguan Danfeng looked at him, and could not hold back a sigh, and murmured, "Why do they all say he has two heads, when as far as I can see he's simply..." Her voice stopped short, for she had seen another man come striding through the downpour.
A big, powerfully-built man, a bamboo hat on his head, a bamboo pole over his shoulder with a jumble of things dangling from it that she could not make out; but she could make out that the man was Zhao the Pockmarked.
Lu Xiaofeng laughed, and said easily, "You mustn't lose your faith in everyone. There aren't so many bad men in this world as you fancy; there's always, after all..."
His voice too stopped short, for he had seen clearly now what hung from Zhao the Pockmarked's pole: a string of hands, human hands! The blood on them had been washed clean by the downpour, but they had plainly been cut fresh from men's wrists just now, thirteen or fourteen of them, bound with a trouser-belt and slung from the pole.
And thrust into Zhao the Pockmarked's own belt, plain to see, was a knife: a dog-killing knife.
Lu Xiaofeng stared at him in astonishment. "So you don't only kill dogs; you kill men as well."
Zhao the Pockmarked grinned, mouth wide. "I can't kill dogs. I've only ever killed men."
Lu Xiaofeng looked at him a long while, then sighed. "You're not Zhao the Pockmarked!"
The man laughed. "Who said I was Zhao the Pockmarked?"
When he laughed, there was nothing in his face beyond the wide mouth stretching open; no other expression at all.
"Who are you?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
The man's eyes glinted. "If even you can't tell who I am, it seems my art of changing faces, if not quite the first under heaven, is near enough to it."
Lu Xiaofeng stared at him, then smiled of a sudden too. "But your somersaulting still won't do..."
He had not finished before Shangguan Danfeng cried out, "This is the thief you spoke of just now?"
The man sighed. "True enough. I'm the very Sikong Zhaixing who matched somersaults with him; only I'm no thief, I'm a Thief."
Shangguan Danfeng smiled sweetly. "I know. Not only a great thief, but the king of thieves, who's robbed every hand under heaven and left no match."
Sikong Zhaixing threw out his chest. "That much I'll not sell myself short on. For the art of thieving, not even Lu Xiaofeng dares match himself against me; who else could measure up?"
"Why must you dress as a pockmarked dog-butcher, of all things?" said Shangguan Danfeng.
Sikong Zhaixing laughed. "There you don't understand. Got up as a pockmarked man, one's the harder to see through."
"Why?" said Shangguan Danfeng.
"When did you ever see a man stare hard at a badly pockmarked face and study it?" said Sikong Zhaixing.
Shangguan Danfeng laughed too. "It seems there's no small learning to this face-changing art."
"No small learning indeed," said Sikong Zhaixing.
Lu Xiaofeng knit his brows. "When did you come to Guanzhong?"
"A day or two ago," said Sikong Zhaixing.
"To do what?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"To wait for you," said Sikong Zhaixing.
"Wait for me?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"Because you were bound for the Boss Yan's," said Sikong Zhaixing, "and this is the road you must pass; the more so since, once you were near Taiyuan, you could hardly miss a meal of Zhao the Pockmarked's dog-meat stew."
He sighed, and went on, "Even I can't but own that for stewed dog-meat there's not a man to touch him."
"And it was for fear I'd taste the difference and see through you," said Lu Xiaofeng, "that you told me the dog-meat was sold out?"
Sikong Zhaixing roared with laughter. "Whatever else, I did take you in this time, sharp as you are."
"What are you waiting here for me for?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"What else would a man like me be at?" said Sikong Zhaixing.
"You mean to steal something off me?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"Name a thing you can, and I'll steal it," said Sikong Zhaixing proudly.
"And what of mine would you steal?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"You truly want me to say?" said Sikong Zhaixing.
"If you daren't say, I'll not press you," said Lu Xiaofeng mildly.
Sikong Zhaixing glared. "And why shouldn't I dare say?"
Shangguan Danfeng could not help asking, "What is it you mean to steal?"
"You," said Sikong Zhaixing.
Shangguan Danfeng's eyes went wide, and she froze.
"Someone has offered two hundred thousand taels of silver," said Sikong Zhaixing, "to have me steal you away."
"I'd never have thought I was worth two hundred thousand taels of silver..." Shangguan Danfeng began, and before the words were out her own face had flushed crimson.
"Only, the one who wants you stolen away," said Sikong Zhaixing, "doesn't mean it in the way you're thinking."
Shangguan Danfeng flushed, and could not help crying, "How do you know what way I'm thinking?"
Sikong Zhaixing blinked, and held his tongue.
"What way does the man mean it, then? Who is he?" said Shangguan Danfeng.
Sikong Zhaixing still would not open his mouth.
Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "He won't tell. In his line, once a man lets slip a client's secret, who'd ever dare come to his door again?"
"A thief has clients who come to his door?" said Shangguan Danfeng.
"I told you long ago, this is no common thief," said Lu Xiaofeng. "He never steals what's worth money."
"But I have to eat too," said Sikong Zhaixing.
"Not only eat, but drink, and drink good wine," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"And so I steal only when a man will pay a great price to have me steal," said Sikong Zhaixing.
"Only there aren't many who can meet your price," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"Not many indeed," said Sikong Zhaixing.
"So say nothing though you will, I know all the same who has sent you this time," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"That you know is your affair; that I don't tell is mine," said Sikong Zhaixing.
"Know or not, you'll say nothing either way," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"Just so," said Sikong Zhaixing.
"But why have you changed your mind now, and told me the secret after all?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
Sikong Zhaixing sighed. "You went into the fire at the risk of your life to save me, and near burned off your eyebrows doing it. How could I have the face to go on and steal your friend?"
"It seems you're a case of honour even among thieves," said Lu Xiaofeng.
"Right again," said Sikong Zhaixing.
Shangguan Danfeng could not help crying, "And if you hadn't the face for it, could you truly have stolen me away?"
"Don't forget I'm the king of thieves," said Sikong Zhaixing proudly. "There's nothing yet under heaven I can't steal."
Shangguan Danfeng sneered. "I should like to hear just how you meant to steal me."
"Did you ever hear of a plaster-seller telling out his secret recipe to another soul?" said Sikong Zhaixing.
"No," said Shangguan Danfeng.
"This too is a secret recipe of mine," said Sikong Zhaixing easily, "and so I can't tell it you either."
Shangguan Danfeng stared at him, then said of a sudden, "Nine pockmarked men in ten are queer ones. I'll wager you're truly pockmarked underneath."
Sikong Zhaixing glared. "Who says so?"
"I say so," said Shangguan Danfeng. "Or else roll up that pockmarked face of yours and let me see what you really look like!"
"That won't do," said Sikong Zhaixing.
"Why won't it?" said Shangguan Danfeng.
"If by mischance you were to lose your heart to me," said Sikong Zhaixing, "wouldn't Lu Xiaofeng be for matching me at somersaults all over again? That last bout spun my head half off me; a second I dare not face."
Shangguan Danfeng reddened, and yet could not help bursting into a laugh.
"Whose hands are these?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"The men who fired the house," said Sikong Zhaixing.
"You caught up with them?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"Once I'd made myself Zhao the Pockmarked," said Sikong Zhaixing, "and someone came to burn his house, of course I had to see him righted."
"So you cut off their hands," said Shangguan Danfeng, "to keep them from ever burning anyone's house again."
"I mean to sell those ten-odd horses of theirs," said Sikong Zhaixing, "and make it up to Zhao the Pockmarked."
"And the men themselves?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"Still back there in the wood," said Sikong Zhaixing. "I kept them specially for you."
"Kept them for me to do what?" said Lu Xiaofeng.
"They meant to burn you to death," said Sikong Zhaixing. "Won't you want to ask them where they come from?"
