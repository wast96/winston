# Fact-checking a popular history

When the book intersects documented history, the annotation is often the most
valuable thing you produce — more valuable than the translation, because it is
the part no reader can do for themselves.

Method learned from checking a fifteen-chapter popular biography whose claims
turned out to be roughly half sound and half constructed.

## The cardinal rule

**Repetition is not corroboration.**

A claim appearing in a dozen outlets is very often **one source copied twelve
times**. On the real project, the central claim of several chapters appeared in
dozens of articles across many respectable-looking sites — and traced, on
inspection, to a **single family memoir published decades after the events**.

Ways to test whether you have twelve sources or one:

- **Look for identical phrasing.** Near-verbatim wording across outlets is the
  signature of propagation, not independent attestation.
- **Trace to the earliest appearance.** When does this claim first surface? If
  everything postdates one text, you have one source.
- **Check what a masthead actually is.** Pieces carrying major newspapers'
  names turned out to be popular-book excerpt columns — reprints of trade
  paperbacks, not reporting. A masthead is not a citation.
- **Ask who is conspicuously silent.** The strongest evidence on the real
  project was a *negative*: the memoir of the person best placed to know the
  subject's operations, written from the opposing side, **did not mention** the
  most famous one. That silence outweighed a hundred repetitions.

## Leave the source language

The decisive checks all came from outside the book's own literature:

- naval records in a third language, confirming an attack the book described
  and independently dating it
- national reference works in a fourth language, showing that an operation the
  book claimed for its subject was organized entirely by others
- a parliamentary record placing a man on another continent on the exact day
  the book put him in a hotel across the world

A self-contained literature cannot falsify itself. If every check stays inside
it, you will confirm everything and learn nothing.

## Look for the shape, not just the errors

Individual corrections are worth less than the pattern they form.

Sorting one book's claims chronologically and by type revealed that everything
before a certain year checked out and everything after collapsed — and that the
sound claims all shared one property the unsound ones lacked. That single
observation explained the book better than any list of corrections, and it
became the headline of the final report.

Look for:

- **Temporal boundaries.** Do failures cluster in a period?
- **Categorical boundaries.** Do they cluster by type of claim?
- **Directional bias.** Do errors all flatter the subject, or all diminish him?
- **The author's habits.** On the real book, three recurred:
  - **relocation** — a real event moved to a different place, victim or date
  - **retrojection** — later events and institutions pulled backward so the
    subject can be present
  - **annexation** — other people's operations attributed to the subject

Naming the habits lets a reader predict where to be sceptical, which is worth
more than a correction list.

## Verdicts

Record one per checkable claim:

- **CORROBORATED** — independent sources support it
- **PARTLY** — the outline holds, details differ or rest on memoir
- **UNCORROBORATED** — no support found; say where you looked
- **CONTRADICTED** — the record says otherwise; give the record

Say **which** it is in the note itself. A note that recites background without
telling the reader whether the book is right is doing half the job.

## Distinguish tiers of evidence

- **archival / contemporary** — records, contemporary press, official papers
- **scholarly** — academic treatment, ideally in more than one language
- **memoir** — first-hand but late, and often written under pressure. On the
  real project one memoirist's recollections, written in custody decades after
  the fact, underpinned most of the granular detail in the entire literature.
  Attribute to him by name rather than stating as record.
- **popular** — trade books, aggregator sites. Not evidence.

Say which tier a claim rests on. "According to the recollections of X" is
honest; stating the same thing flatly is not.

## Note-writing discipline

- State what the record says, then whether the book agrees.
- Give the sources.
- Where sources conflict, **say they conflict** and give both.
- Where you could not check something, **say so** — "I could not confirm this"
  is information.
- Never source from LLM-generated reference sites. Prefer encyclopedias in
  multiple languages, academic work, archives, contemporary press.
- Distinguish "not found" from "does not exist." Say where you looked.

## Also correct yourself

On the real project, notes written early contained errors found later —
a location stated too loosely, a confession described more confidently than the
evidence supported. When later research contradicts an earlier note, **cascade
the correction** through every affected note and record it in a changelog.
Notes are apparatus, and apparatus that contradicts itself is worse than none.
