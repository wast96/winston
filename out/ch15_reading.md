## Chapter Fifteen. The Last Effort

### 1. Chen Yun Arrives

The *Biography of Chen Yun* (2005) records that on May 31, 1935, the CCP Center, at Luding in Sichuan, "convened a meeting of the Standing Committee of the Politburo" and resolved to send Chen Yun "to Shanghai to rebuild the Party's organization in the White areas." Chen Yun thereupon "slipped away from the Long March column alone and in secret," reached Shanghai in early July, and, under "Li Jiesheng," the alias he had used in his Central Special Branch days, took a room at the Yong'an Hotel, at the corner of Cathedral Street (today Sichuan South Road) and New Yong'an Street (today Xin Yong'an Road) in the French Concession.

At the hotel Chen Yun looked up, in the telephone directory, the number of Zhang Naiqi, deputy general manager of the Zhejiang Industrial Bank on Kiangse Road (today Jiangxi Middle Road). Zhang Naiqi was the elder brother of Zhang Qiuyang, a close former colleague of Chen Yun's at the Commercial Press distribution office, and through that tie the two men knew each other fairly well. Chen Yun telephoned and asked him to set up a meeting with Zhang Qiuyang. Zhang Qiuyang was by then a broker on the Shanghai stock exchange, but "still did outer-ring cover work for the Party." He came at once and took Chen Yun to his own house, at No. 21 Shangxianfang, Lane 358, Avenue Joffre (today Huaihai Middle Road), in the French Concession. (CCP Central Party Literature Research Office, 2005)

Chen Yun could have stayed on in Zhang Qiuyang's house, but "no sooner had he settled in than He Xiaozhang, another old colleague from their Commercial Press days, happened to call at the house and saw him. He Xiaozhang had once been a Party member himself, but to be safe, the moment he left, Zhang Qiuyang moved Chen Yun to his father-in-law's house, at No. 111 Laotai'anli, Beishanxi Road, in the International Settlement."

Zhang Qiuyang, alias Naiqi, courtesy name Yu'an, was born in 1901, a native of Qingtian, Zhejiang. In 1922 he joined the Hongkou branch of the Commercial Press distribution office as a clerk, and it was Xu Meikun (then secretary of the Shanghai local-and-district executive committee) who brought him into the Chinese Communist Party. He went on to serve as a member of the executive committee of the Commercial Press trade union, a Youth League committee member, secretary of the CCP branch at the Commercial Press distribution office, chairman of the Shanghai Shop Clerks' General Union, a Youth League branch secretary, and a citizen delegate of the Shanghai Special Municipality.

After Chen Yun had settled in at No. 111 Laotai'anli, Beishanxi Road, Zhang Qiuyang sent for Sun Shipu to come and meet him.

Sun Shipu was born in 1911, a native of Xiaoshan, Zhejiang. Back in 1925 Chen Yun had been brought into the Chinese Communist Party by Dong Yixiang and Yun Yutang, and had then taken over as secretary of the CCP branch at the Commercial Press distribution office; he and Xue Zhaosheng in turn recruited their distribution-office colleague Sun Shipu into the Communist Youth League, and in 1926 Sun transferred to full Party membership. (Dong Yixiang, original name Yanbiao, also known as Zhuangshou, courtesy name Shutong, was born in 1896, a native of Wujin, Jiangsu, and joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1921; in January 1925, on the open ground between the Duoshou Pavilion and the Nine Elders' Chamber in the central park of Wuxi, he presided over a members' meeting that formally proclaimed the founding of the CCP's Wuxi branch. Yun Yutang was born in 1902, a native of Wujin, Jiangsu, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1923, and in 1931 was martyred, together with his wife Li Wen, at Longhua in Shanghai.)

After Sun Shipu joined the Party, he threw himself, under the leadership of Xu Meikun, Chen Yun, and others, into the three armed uprisings of the Shanghai workers. When the second uprising failed, he went with Chen Yun and the rest "by boat to Yuyao, in the Ningbo region of Zhejiang, and made contact with the local Party organization." Because "the Northern Expedition army had by then reached Yuyao county," the Yuyao Party organization asked Chen Yun to "help open up the work" and "lead the salt-workers' movement." (CCP Central Party Literature Research Office, 2005)

In the spring and summer of 1927 the Great Revolution collapsed. Sun Shipu was arrested and jailed, and suffered terribly, not to be released until 1932, when he resumed contact with Zhang Qiuyang. Seeing Sun Shipu back in the light of day with no way to earn his bread, Zhang Qiuyang went to Xing Yuantang, warden of the Wuxi New Prison (that is, Wuxi Prison, or Jiangsu No. 5 Prison), to find him some work. (Xing Yuantang, styled Songlu, also known as Xing Songling, was born in 1893, a native of Jiangyin, Jiangsu.) Xing was "on the closest of terms" with Zhang Qiuyang and never refused him anything. Once Zhang had asked, Xing could hardly turn him down, and he set Sun Shipu to work under him as a clerk, copying documents all day long. Luckily Sun Shipu could turn his hand to anything and bend without breaking, and before long Xing found him so useful he could scarcely do without him.

"When I got the Party's order, I set off for Shanghai at once and made contact with Zhang Qiuyang and the others. But how could we be sure of Chen Yun's safety? Zhang Qiuyang suggested we think about sending Chen Yun to Wuxi, since Xing Yuantang, who ran the Wuxi New Prison, was on very close terms with him, and that tie could be put to use. The Party organization approved the plan, so it sent me back to Wuxi to find Xing Yuantang, with the story that a friend of mine named Li had run up a debt of several thousand yuan at the exchange and, while he raised the money to cover it, meant to lie low in Wuxi a while and would come back to Shanghai once the debt was cleared. Xing Yuantang agreed to take him in and arranged his board and lodging. With that settled, I went back to Shanghai and stayed at the law office of three barristers, Zhou Bingyang, Sun Xinqi, and Yue Junying, at the corner of Sichuan Road and Beijing Road. The office had three telephones, so Zhang Qiuyang could reach me at any time; the idea was that if it came to it I'd take Chen Yun to Wuxi myself and hide him there." (Sun Shipu, 1995)

While Sun Shipu was shuttling back and forth between Wuxi and Shanghai, Zhang Qiuyang had already put Chen Yun in touch with Pan Weinian (that is, Pan Qizhi, Pan Hannian's cousin) and his wife Lü Jianying (also a cousin of Pan Hannian's), and through them had learned Pan Hannian's latest movements. Back in late February 1935, the Red Army had won battle after battle, crossed the Chishui four times, destroyed the enemy's effective strength in mobile warfare, and ranged freely across the Sichuan-Guizhou-Yunnan border country; Pan Hannian, meanwhile, had quietly left the column, put on a disguise, and made his way south through Guiyang, Liuzhou, Wuzhou, Guangzhou, and Hong Kong before slipping into Shanghai by water. There he sought out Pu Huaren (then a member of the CCP Shanghai Central Bureau's "Group of Three," running its daily affairs) and Dong Weijian (then acting head of the Shanghai Central Bureau's propaganda department) to learn how the Shanghai Central Bureau stood and how it was linked to the Comintern. Pan Hannian was acting on Zhang Wentian's orders. Zhang Wentian, speaking for the Center, wanted Pan Hannian to "lie low in Shanghai for the long term and keep the line between the CCP and the Comintern open and unbroken." But the White Terror in Shanghai was worse than ever: the successive betrayals by Li Zhusheng (the first secretary of the CCP Shanghai Central Bureau), Sheng Zhongliang (the second), and others kept doing their damage, and Pan Hannian simply couldn't find a footing. Before long he had to go back to Hong Kong to wait for word of Chen Yun.

The Pu Huaren and Dong Weijian whom Pan Hannian had met, Chen Yun too now arranged to see, through Pan Weinian and Lü Jianying. In *Lazily Seeking Old Dreams* (2006), Xia Yan says that the "Dong Weijian" whom Chen Yun and Pan Hannian met in turn was "Pastor Dong," who worked under "the cover of a Christian minister." This is a case of mistaken identity. In the Shanghai of those years our Party had only one "Pastor Dong": Dong Jianwu, brought into the Party by Pu Huaren.

Pu Huaren, who had used the names Wang Yangsan, Zhang Peiling, and Zhang Peilin, was born in 1887, a native of Wuxi, Jiangsu. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1927 and served as a member, then head, of the propaganda department of the CCP Shanghai Central Bureau.

Dong Jianwu, original name Shouqing (one account says Xuanqing), alias Zhou Jiwu, was born in 1891, a native of Qingpu, Shanghai. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1927, and in 1929 joined the Second Section of the Central Special Branch, using his standing as a pastor of St. Peter's Church to help Chen Geng and Pan Hannian gather intelligence and eliminate traitors.

In *My Life* (2015), Feng Yuxiang writes of Pu Huaren and Dong Jianwu more than once. Pu Huaren, he says, he had "known since my days as military governor of Shaanxi, a man full of warmth, firm and bold," with whom he had "spent a long time"; later Pu "actually joined the Communist Party as an Anglican pastor and became a faithful, active Communist." Dong Jianwu (Dong Xianwu), Feng adds, was a "friend" of his, and he had once put Dong and Pu Huaren together "in charge of welfare work for the railway workers."

In March 1930, at Pu Huaren's urging, Dong Jianwu founded the Datong Kindergarten at No. 441 Gordon Road (today Jiangning Road) in the Shanghai International Settlement, later moved to Route Vallon in the French Concession, today No. 48 Nanchang Road. It sheltered a great many orphans of the revolution's fallen and children of our Party's leaders, among them the sons of Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and Yun Daiying, the daughters of Cai Hesen, Xiang Jingyu, and Li Lisan, and Mao Zedong and Yang Kaihui's sons Mao Anying, Mao Anqing, and Mao Anlong. Mao Anqing later wrote to Dong Jianwu's son Dong Shouqi, saying from the heart: "When I think back on our time in Shanghai, it feels as if it were before my eyes. In that Shanghai of the White Terror, in our hardest days, Uncle Dong and your whole family looked after us, and Anying and I have never been able to forget it, and are deeply grateful."

In April 1931 Dong Jianwu went with Gu Shunzhang to Wuhan on a mission. Once it was done, Gu Shunzhang ignored Dong Jianwu's repeated warnings and insisted on staying in Wuhan to perform conjuring tricks in public; he also took up with a "fashionable young woman all in white, white dress, white hat, white high heels," and so was spotted and seized by the enemy, after which he shamelessly turned traitor. In that desperate hour the "Three Heroes of Longtan," Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di, acted at once, turned back the tide, and warned the CCP Center in time. Dong Jianwu, who had gotten away and hurried back to Shanghai, meanwhile gave Zhou Enlai still more that could be used to confirm the danger. With that, Zhou Enlai, helped by Chen Yun, Chen Geng, Li Qiang (then head of the Fourth Section of the Central Special Branch), and others, moved swiftly through a whole series of countermeasures, got ahead of the enemy, and put out a catastrophe that had directly threatened the safety of the CCP Center, the Jiangsu Provincial Committee, and the Comintern's Far Eastern Bureau.

The *Biography of Chen Yun* (2005) says that not long after Chen Yun had seen Pu Huaren and Dong Weijian, the two were themselves arrested, and then the Communist Youth League's Shanghai Central Bureau was broken up as well.

### 2. The "Group of Three"

On July 22 the CCP Shanghai Central Bureau and the Jiangsu Provincial Committee were smashed once again. The wave came on top of the arrest, on February 19, of thirty-six people, among them Shanghai Central Bureau secretary Huang Wenjie; now Pu Huaren, who headed the Shanghai Central Bureau, and Dong Weijian, its acting propaganda chief, were taken too. On the 24th the Youth League's Shanghai Central Bureau was broken up in turn: eight people arrested, its secretary Wen De among them, and Wen De turned traitor. With things gone so badly, Chen Yun's work of rebuilding the CCP organization in Shanghai could not go forward for the time being. (CCP Central Party Literature Research Office, 2005)

On the matter of that February 19, 1935 arrest of "thirty-six people, among them Shanghai Central Bureau secretary Huang Wenjie," one of those caught up in it, Chen Tongsheng, later recalled that in prison, "using a turncoat who had been let out to be 'examined,' perhaps while he was reporting his results to his superior," he had "a fairly detailed talk" with Zhu Jingwo. (Zhu Jingwo was born in 1901, a native of Yin county, Zhejiang, and joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1928; he served as propaganda chief of the CCP Jiangsu Provincial Committee, Party-group secretary of the China League of Social Scientists, Party-group secretary of the China League of Left-Wing Cultural Circles, a standing-committee member of the Shanghai Central Bureau, and its propaganda chief.) Zhu Jingwo told him, heartsick: "After last October's great wave of arrests, the Center put Comrade Huang Wenjie in charge of building a new leading body. Now that too has been smashed. It is plain that traitors have gotten inside our organization. This wave is without precedent; the Youth League and the left-wing groups have scarcely a survivor. Of all the secret organization, only a very small part may still be intact." (Chen Tongsheng, 1959)

That "very small part" that "may still be intact" was gathered up and knit back together by Liu Zhonghua, in March 1935.

Liu Zhonghua, who used the aliases Hua Zaicheng, Liu Zihua, and Qin Xuwu, was born in 1899, a native of Guo county, Shanxi, and joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1923.

In March 1935 Liu Zhonghua was introduced by Rao Shushi and met "the Old Man" in Shanghai. (Rao Shushi, alias A Shi, was born in 1903, a native of Fuzhou, Jiangxi, and joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1925; he had served as Party-group secretary of the strike committee of Shanghai's Huxi district, secretary of the Northeast Jiangxi Special Committee, secretary-general of the Communist Youth League Center, propaganda chief and concurrently secretary-general of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, Party-group secretary of the Shanghai Executive Bureau, director of the North China Office, and acting secretary of the CCP Hebei Provincial Committee.) "The Old Man" was Gebert's nickname. Gebert was not the Comintern's "resident plenipotentiary in Shanghai," only "a very experienced operative"; but at that moment there was "only one man" in Shanghai, and it was Gebert. Liu Zhonghua laid the Central Bureau's plight before him. Gebert held that the Central Bureau must be rebuilt as fast as possible and couldn't be left without anyone at the helm. So it was that at a meeting on March 17, attended by Liu Zhonghua himself, He Changzhi (alias Zhang Tao, who had joined the Central Special Branch in 1933 under the direct leadership of Wu Hujing), and Pu Huaren, Liu announced the founding of a "Group of Five," the three of them plus a "stand-in" for the ACFTU delegate and one member of the Youth League Central Working Committee, with Liu, He, and Pu, in effect a "Group of Three," jointly running the Central Bureau's daily affairs.

On March 22 the Shanghai Central Bureau wrote to the Executive Committee of the Comintern and to the CCP delegation attached to it, reporting the "founding" of "the new Central Bureau" and the "division of labor" among its leaders, and at the same time begging the "Comintern to send several comrades" to Shanghai to "strengthen the development of the soviet revolution and strengthen the leadership of the new Central Bureau."

This was the background to the Center's decision, at the Luding meeting, to send Chen Yun "to Shanghai to rebuild the Party's organization in the White areas." Pan Hannian's June 20 letter from Shanghai to the Comintern's representatives in China bears the point out: "The [CCP] Center, seeing that the CCP Shanghai Central Bureau was being broken up again and again and that the leadership of the mass struggle in the Kuomintang areas was weak, resolved to strengthen the leadership. At the same time the [CCP] Center's new responsible comrade Pokrylov (whose Chinese name is Zhang Wentian) and Chen Yun held that, since the [CCP] Center, moving with the army through a long march and a long civil war, could not lead the work of the whole country, it ought to change its way of leading."

In early July Chen Yun reached Shanghai and put up at the Yong'an Hotel.

On the night of July 9 Liu Zhonghua told He Changzhi that the Comintern had sent someone to organize a new, formal Central Bureau, and that the "Group of Three" was to hand over. The next day Liu Zhonghua had Pu Huaren and He Changzhi make ready, and then left for Moscow.

The trouble was, no one had foreseen that "on July 22 the CCP Shanghai Central Bureau and the Jiangsu Provincial Committee would be smashed once again," with forty people arrested and jailed, Pu Huaren among them.

### 3. Only in the Raging Sea Is the Hero's True Color Seen

By now Chen Yun was living above an iron-and-steel shop on the south side of Rubbish Bridge (today Zhejiang Road Bridge, named for the "Rubbish Wharf" beside it), on Beijing Road (today Beijing East Road) near Zhejiang Road (today Zhejiang Middle Road), a place the Central Special Branch had arranged. The *Biography of Chen Yun* (2005) quotes what Chen Yun himself wrote, in a statement drawn up for Zhu Jun on December 5, 1967: "Through Zhang Qiuyang I found one of the men then running the Party's special branch in Shanghai, the branch that did protection and intelligence work. He was from around Shantou in Guangdong; I can no longer recall his name." Of "this man," Chen Yun noted that he was the husband of Su Mei, younger sister of Kang Sheng's wife Cao Yi'ou. Chen Yun said: "Su Mei's husband arranged my lodgings. The first time it was above some rented rooms, whose location I cannot recall. The second time it was above the house that Zhu Jun occupied on his own in the French Concession."

Zhu Jun was born in 1908, a native of Jing county, Hebei. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1927, and after the founding of New China he served as political commissar of the Naval Torpedo-Boat School, deputy president of the Naval Military Academy, president of the Naval Engineering Academy, head of the Naval Political-and-Military Cadre School, and president of the Naval Academy.

Zhu Jun was doing the Party's underground work in Shanghai at the time. Living there with him were his wife Liu Jiping and a small child. "In those days of secret work it was not done to ask people's names, so I did not even know they were called Zhu Jun and Liu Jiping; only later, when both of them reached Yan'an after the war broke out, did I learn their names were Zhu Jun and Liu Jiping." Chen Yun also wrote: "I was very safe at Zhu Jun's. I moved in safely, and I left his house safely, going from Shanghai to the Soviet Union." (CCP Central Party Literature Research Office, 2005)

Chen Yun's memory was exact and unmistakable, yet the notes to the *Biography of Chen Yun* (2005) wrongly give "Su Mei's husband" as "Chuwen." In fact Su Mei's second husband was not called Chuwen but Qiu Wen, that is, Xiao Shouhuang, born in 1911, a native of Dabu, Guangdong, who joined the Central Special Branch in 1932. In *China's Secret War: A Documentary Record of CCP Intelligence and Security Work* (2015), Hao Zaijin writes: "In 1934, when Kirov, a member of the Politburo of the Soviet Communist Party, was assassinated, the Soviet Party began a purge of Trotskyists within its ranks. Wang Ming and Kang Sheng took an active part in the Soviet 'anti-Trotsky' drive, and at the same time went after the Chinese comrades studying in Moscow. Xiao Shouhuang, a Special Branch man well known to Kang Sheng, was arrested and killed; and Wu Hujing, who had taken charge of Special Branch work after the Center left Shanghai in 1933, and who was Kang Sheng's own townsman and colleague, was likewise arrested and killed on a trumped-up charge of being a traitor and a spy."

During Chen Yun's stay "above some rented rooms," arranged by Xiao Shouhuang, his three meals a day were all brought up by Yu Sanyuan, the young son of Zhang Qiuyang and Tang Wenyun (who at the time traded government bonds at the Chinese Stock Exchange).

In the newspapers Yu Sanyuan carried up with the meals, Chen Yun saw that among the turncoats who had publicly announced their break with the Party were more than seventy men who knew him by sight.

The Shanghai of those years was truly a place of jumping shadows, where every step held a fright.

The Shanghai of those years, in Chen Tongsheng's words, was quite simply "the blackest of times": "Not only did the Party's organization take an unprecedented battering, the left-wing mass groups suffered unprecedented losses too. From the Party branches, the district committees, and the provincial committee up to the Central Bureau, and among the red groups, the League of Left-Wing Writers, the League of Social Scientists, the Dramatists' League, the Artists' League, the Film League, and the rest, people were arrested everywhere. Every cell was crammed with political prisoners, jammed in tight: a cell for four held ten, a cell for ten held twenty-odd, packed tighter than a tin of sardines and still they could not all fit." (Chen Tongsheng, 1959)

Only when the seas run wild is the hero's true color seen. The valiant Chinese Communist Party had come through the counter-revolutionary betrayal of 1927, the "Left" errors of January 1931 to January 1935, and the Zunyi Conference of January 1935 and "the start of the new leadership of the Center headed by Comrade Mao Zedong," and was by now a Party highly "consolidated and united," a Party "on the verge of victory," a Party "that no force could any longer defeat." (Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party, 1945)

All the more so as the fast-shifting scene at home and abroad, with the Seventh Congress of the Comintern, brought the Chinese Communists new opportunities and new challenges. The opportunity was that Stalin was giving more attention to domestic affairs and had largely stopped interfering in the internal business of the various parties; the challenge was that "Japanese imperialism is pressing its attack on us, the treasonous Nanjing government is surrendering step by step, and our northern provinces, after the four northeastern ones, are in effect being lost as well."

With a great enemy at the gate and the nation in peril, the Chinese Soviet government and the CCP Center called on "all compatriots unwilling to be slaves of a conquered country," including "all officers and soldier-brothers with a patriotic conscience," including "all parties and groups willing to join the sacred cause of resisting Japan and saving the nation," including "all hot-blooded youth of national feeling within the Kuomintang and the Blue Shirts": that whatever "differences of political view or interest they had held in the past or held now," whatever "differences of opinion or of interest," whatever "hostile acts" lay between them, "all should come to the sincere understanding that 'brothers who quarrel within the walls still join to repel insult from without,'" and "all should halt the civil war, so as to concentrate every national strength, men, matériel, money, and arms, in the struggle for the sacred cause of resisting Japan and saving the nation." (Chinese Soviet Government and Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party, 1935) The newly installed general secretary of the Comintern Executive Committee, Dimitrov, was equally plain: "We favor the Chinese Communist Party's forming, with all organized forces truly ready to fight to save their own country and people, the broadest anti-imperialist united front against Japanese imperialism and its Chinese agents."

The *Letter to All Compatriots on Resisting Japan and Saving the Nation* (that is, the "August First Declaration," dated August 1, 1935), issued by the Chinese Soviet government and the CCP Center, was, in Wang Ming's words, "drafted together with the CCP delegation that attended the [Comintern's] Seventh Congress"; on July 19, 1935, it was passed to Mif (then president of the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow and part of the preparatory work for the Comintern's Seventh Congress) for review and revision, and then sent to the Comintern for "final approval." (Wang Ming, 1935)

The united front was something wholly new. It could not be reduced simply to "an abrupt change of slogans"; more fundamentally it "meant 'unilateral disarmament' and unilateral concessions to today's enemy," meant that from now on, "in propaganda and in organizing the masses for anti-imperialist action, the first slogan to raise should be the salvation of China, not the slogan of 'defending the Soviet Union,'" and meant "admitting that the policy of sovietizing China adopted in 1927 had been mistaken," a "mistake" that had, as it happened, been "put forward and argued by Stalin himself."

And so, "the Comintern, the CCP delegation to the ECCI, and the Soviet intelligence services had, well before the Comintern's Seventh Congress closed, already taken steps to acquaint the CCP Center and the CCP organizations elsewhere in China with the new strategy." (First Research Department of the CCP Central Party History Research Office, 2007) And Chen Yun, then lying low in Shanghai, only longed the more for Pan Hannian to come up from Hong Kong soon, so the two of them could talk it through, weigh it, game it out, and take its measure.

### 4. Boarding the Boat at Pudong

Before long Pan Hannian and Chen Yun were reunited in Shanghai.

At the same time Zhang Qiuyang had also found Qu Qiubai's widow, Yang Zhihua, and He Shuheng's third daughter, He Shisi (one account says He Shishan, He Shuheng's eldest daughter). Yang Zhihua and He Shisi were in touch with the Comintern's representatives in China. Through Yang Zhihua and He Shisi, those representatives passed word to the Shanghai Central Bureau and to Chen Yun and Pan Hannian that the CCP delegation to the ECCI held that "Shanghai at present has no need of any central organization," and that "many of the Party members left in Shanghai should go to the Soviet Union for the time being." (Chen Yun, 1982) That latest instruction from the CCP delegation to the ECCI in effect brought Chen Yun's mission to Shanghai to an end.

The "task" the Luding meeting had given Chen Yun was "to go to Shanghai and rebuild the Party's organization in the White areas." Chen Yun had come to Shanghai as the new supreme leader of "the White-area Party organization." Now that Shanghai "had no need of any central organization" after all, what reason was there for Chen Yun to hold the city? Chen Yun could "go to the Soviet Union for the time being," yet he still "decided to send Xiaokai on ahead," holding firmly that his own "task was to work in Shanghai, and so did not decide to leave at once." But the CCP delegation to the ECCI cabled again to press him, and, through the Central Special Branch, ordered Chen Yun to set out soon and brook no delay.

On August 10, 1935, the tenth page of the third sheet of *Shen Bao* carried another open report, that "the Hubei High Court has indicted the case of the mysterious Westerner in Shanghai," naming Walton and the others whose "crimes" were committed "at the direction of the GPU of the Communist Third International," in "collusion" with the Chinese Communist Party, "endangering the Republic," and "spying out China's political and military secrets." Only then did Chen Yun, on Song Qingling's arrangement, together with Chen Tanqiu, Yang Zhihua, He Shisi (or He Shishan), Zeng Shan, and Pan Weinian, and escorted by Ma Haide, board a Soviet freighter at Pudong bound for Vladivostok (Haishenwai). (Chen Tanqiu, given name Cheng, courtesy name Yunxian, was born in 1896, a native of Huanggang, Hubei, and was a delegate to the CCP's First Congress; he had served as a member of the Central Executive Committee of the Chinese Soviet Republic and concurrently head of its food ministry, secretary of the CCP Fujian Provincial Committee, and a member and organization chief of the Central Soviet-Area Sub-bureau. Zeng Shan, originally named Zeng Rubai, also known as Xiusheng, Xianpu, Zeng Shan, and Tanggu, was born in 1899, a native of Ji'an, Jiangxi, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1926, and was then secretary of the CCP Jiangxi Provincial Committee and concurrently political commissar of the Jiangxi Military Region. Ma Haide, a doctor of medicine, originally George Hatem, born in New York in 1910, was the first foreigner to join the Chinese Communist Party and, after the founding of New China, the first former foreign national to take Chinese citizenship; he served as an adviser to the Ministry of Health, and in 2009 was named one of the "100 Figures Who Moved China since the Founding of New China.")

"One day in the summer of 1935, Song Qingling asked me to escort two guests onto a ship bound for the Soviet Union. I put on a smart Western suit at once, drove a sky-blue Ford, and saw the two of them to the wharf. In the old China a foreigner like me was a passport that went anywhere, and so I got them off without a hitch. Not long after I got back to my clinic, Song Qingling telephoned to thank me, saying again and again that I'd done a fine thing. Later a messenger she sent brought a letter, and only then did I learn that the two I'd sent off were important Chinese Communists." (Ma Haide, 1981)

This was the first time Chen Yun had ever left the country.

No sooner had Chen Yun's party left Shanghai than the August 24 *Shen Bao*, on the eleventh page of its third sheet, ran a long report headed "The Case of the Strange Westerner Opens at the Hubei High Court," charging Walton directly with "colluding with Liu Suiyuan, Xiao Bingshi, Lu Haifang, and other organizing bodies to spy out China's political and military secrets and report them to the Third International," so that the authorities "indicted them severally under the Emergency Law for Crimes Endangering the Republic and brought them to trial."

Shen Zui said later that it was Lu Haifang whom he had "taken two agents" to arrest, at the East Asia Hotel on Dama Road. Lu Haifang, "that life-clinging, death-fearing piece of scum who had wormed his way into the revolutionary camp, saw at his interrogation how many instruments of torture they'd laid out, and without waiting to be put to them, after a single round of Dai Li's promises of money and rank, agreed at once to give up his superior," Walton. (Shen Zui, 1990)

Fang Wen (a pen name of Zhang Fang, who later renamed himself Liu Jinzhong) says that Lu Haifang, "that shameless creature," was the very man he had "introduced, as a revolutionary, into the intelligence group led by Sorge. In the end he managed to climb to an important post, which gave him the capital to trade for high office and rich pay. He could not round up every last member of the organization, but this revolutionary intelligence network of a hundred-odd people, created by Sorge and built up by Walton, reaching deep inside all of Chiang Kai-shek's military commands, was completely destroyed by the enemy."

The Walton affair "produced a violent shock in Moscow," so Victor Usov says.

Victor Usov says: "The repeated persecutions and disasters in Shanghai left the leaders in Moscow deeply uneasy." They took fright at the mere mention of it and became birds startled by the twang of a bow. They wanted only to "recall" everyone they could "recall," and "proposed" holding the number of "professors" (a figure for the agents of the ECCI's International Liaison Department and of the Foreign Department of the State Political Security Directorate) and "jurists" (a figure for the agents of the Intelligence Directorate of the Soviet General Staff) at each embassy down to one apiece. They did not want to see the CCP comrades "in touch with them hunted by the police and liable at any moment to be sold out by fifteen rampantly active traitors," and pressed again and again for Pan Hannian, who had not left with Chen Yun, to set out early. Pan Hannian "carried on his person the call signals, wavelengths, and time slots needed for radio contact," charged to "make contact directly with the Comintern's representative and try to restore radio communication." The Center didn't want him "to make contact directly with the CCP Shanghai Central Bureau," for the Center held that "since the CCP Shanghai Central Bureau had been broken up, there must be traitors hidden inside it." Moscow, full of misgivings and jumping at every sound, was thinking along the same lines, of "the struggle against terror and against spies," of how "the political reactionaries had been strengthened, traitor activity had multiplied, and the public-security committees and foreign intelligence services had adopted new, altered methods of investigation." (Lai An, 2007)

At the end of August Pan Hannian boarded the Soviet freighter *Dongfang*, under the alias "Mizuban Saburō," taking the same route Chen Yun's party had traveled: Shanghai to Vladivostok (Haishenwai) to Siberia to Moscow. A little over four months earlier, Chen Hansheng and his wife Gu Shuxing had hurried along the very same route.

On October Revolution Day that year, Chen Hansheng and Gu Shuxing were taken by two Soviet hosts to Red Square to watch the ceremony and to meet Stalin. Two other men from Wuxi also saw Stalin review the marching columns: Pan Hannian and, traveling with him, Yan Pu. Yan Pu was the father of Yan Weibing, wife of Lu Dingyi (born in 1906, a native of Wuxi, Jiangsu, who joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1925 and was then propaganda chief of the General Political Department of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army and chief editor of *Red Star*). Yan Weibing later mentioned, in her *Life of Yan Pu*, that Yan and Pan traveled to the Soviet Union together, and that when they reached Moscow it happened to be the great Red Square parade of November 7, at which Yan Pu had the good fortune to see the general secretary of the Central Committee of the CPSU(B) "for the second time." Chen Yun, who had reached Moscow earlier, in early September, had by then stolen a little time from his labors and, under the pen name "Lianchen," set to work on *Random Notes on the Western March* (1936), in which, in the guise of a "captured" "army doctor" "serving in a Red Army hospital," he told the world for the first time that "the Zhu-Mao Red Army marched all day, rain or shine, from Jiangxi through Hunan, Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou, Sichuan, Yunnan, and Xikang, and then into Lifan and Songpan in Sichuan. Their footprints crossed nearly all the land south of the great river; the march took more than eight months, ran to some 12,000 li, and passed through countless high mountains and great rivers."

"Our country's Communist forces have spread with great speed in recent years. The Red armies of Zhu-Mao, Xu Xiangqian, He Long, Xiao Ke, and the rest have become a mighty power in China. When the Red Army first arose it was but a single spark; today it has become a prairie fire. Now that China's two great Red armies have joined, Zhu-Mao with Xu Xiangqian, their momentum has swelled, and the center of gravity of the fighting has shifted from the southeast to the northwest. The work of suppressing the Communists grows ever harder, in operations and in transport alike, and the activity of the Red Army ever harder to check." (Chen Yun, 1936)
