## Code Words

Lin Shi lay on the bed. A little while ago, in the downstairs parlor, he had taken a great swallow of wine and then told the others he felt out of sorts and had gone back up to the sickroom to rest. After his release Qin Chuan'an had dressed his wound afresh; the bullet had not struck the bone of his leg, and after a spell of rest he could walk again, slowly. He had not been altogether lying: though the leg was not so bad as the others supposed, just now a chill was creeping over him once more.

He had a vague sense that there was something wrong with Cui Wentai — a matter of instinct, hard to put into words. A strained look on the face; one or two overdone gestures; a turn of phrase here and there. He was vexed with himself: he truly should not have let the business of the bank slip out. Five bars of gold. Funds like these did not come easily; perhaps comrades on some other front had bought them with their lives. To be so rash — how was he to face those people? If anything went wrong, the grave task the Central Committee had laid on him would be hard to carry through.

The hardest thing was not to clench one's teeth before the enemy; that he had long since settled in his mind, that he could give his all for the operation. But his present case was that he must keep silent before his own comrades and comrades-in-arms as well; whether they showed him warmth or suspicion, he could tell the secret to no one, nor talk it over lightly with anyone.

When the agents had come bursting into the meeting, Yi Junnian, to shield him, had slipped the dice into his own pocket; Lin Shi had seen it all, yet could neither say a word nor do a thing. He knew what Comrade Yi Junnian meant by it, but he could only keep silent, could only let his comrade stand in front of him for the time being.

In the lockup Yi Junnian had watched him with that look in his eyes. Lin Shi felt the man had all but guessed that he was the special commissioner, but he could not betray the least sign of it. He moaned and whimpered, feigning that his wound was grave, feigning a feeble will, feigning that he was wholly unprepared in his mind for the situation before him.

He had even made up for Yi Junnian a few passages of his past — first setting himself down as an old revolutionary who had taken part in the Guangzhou Uprising (which happened to be the truth), and then, on certain details, deliberately talking at cross-purposes, so that Yi Junnian should judge wrongly and take him for a braggart. In this way Yi Junnian gradually let his probing of him relax.

Some secret missions are fated to be carried out alone; and the loyalty they require is fated, too, to go cloaked in suspicion.

These last days he had seen that the two comrades, Yi Junnian and Ling Wen, were on close terms; revolutionary comrades often form an attachment in the course of their work, and there was nothing strange in it. He knew that Ling Wen believed her husband, Long Dong, had already given his life. But by what he understood, Long Dong was very likely still alive. Ling Wen had heard that after the Guangzhou Uprising failed, the Nationalist army had combed the city of Guangzhou for suspects, that anyone without a Cantonese accent might be shot on the spot, and that they had even stormed into the Soviet consulate. Long Dong, so the story went, had been inside the consulate just then, conferring with the Soviet comrades on the work of withdrawal, and after the Nationalists seized him he had been killed together with the Soviet comrades.

But Lin Shi knew that for at least a year after the Guangzhou Uprising, Comrade Long Dong had gone on working for the Party; under the enemy's brutal repression he had put together a small, seasoned underground group. Long Dong was a man of rare gifts — he could even draw secret intelligence out of the Nationalists' Guangzhou Municipal Public Security Bureau. In a few short months he had come to speak fluent Cantonese, an underground worker born to it. Lin Shi himself, in the end, was transferred out of Guangzhou because he could never learn to speak Cantonese.

He could not yet tell Ling Wen this news; nor could he tell any of them that he was “Old Kai.” He held himself back from sleep, for Chen Qianyuan had told him quietly, earlier, that Chen Qianli was coming to see him.

All at once a crowd had gathered at the roadside; firecrackers cracked and spat, and now and then a skyrocket shot up to burst high in the air, deafening. Along the street the second-floor households threw open their windows one after another and lit the firecrackers hung from bamboo poles. In the lane the people who had finished their New Year's Eve dinner, as if at some word of command, came out through their doors, grown-ups and children alike.

A single figure pressed in behind the crowd and came into the lane from under the overhead archway.

Chen Qianli found the clinic's back door and knocked softly, twice. Behind the door, Chen Qianyuan was waiting for him.

“I am looking for a Song-dynasty painting.”

“That won't be easy to find.”

“I am charged with it by another; find it I must, even if it can't be found.”

“Then tell me which one it is.”

“A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains.”

“Open the window and look outside.”

“Just so — these people here, they are the rivers and mountains.”

The visitor aboard the ship at Qingdao had told Chen Qianli that to speak this passphrase at the meeting would win him the complete trust of “Old Kai.” The passphrase had been devised by Comrade Shaoshan himself, and up to now those who knew it were surely no more than five.

“You have seen Comrade Shaoshan?” Lin Shi gripped Chen Qianli's strong, warm hands in excitement.

“Comrade Shaoshan is at Ruijin. He may not know your present situation. A comrade of the Central Liaison Bureau learned that something had gone wrong with the Shanghai action group, hurried to Qingdao, found me aboard the ship, and had me come to Shanghai to work with you.

“At Qingdao that comrade told me the situation in Shanghai was extremely grave; from the signs, the underground Party organization had been badly penetrated. He said the enemy had got wind of things in advance, before the meeting to convey the task had even been convened.”

“Judging by the Settlement's Municipal Police and the detective squad joining hands to make the arrests, the situation is indeed very grim.”

“But the ‘Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains plan’ was set in motion long ago; it is a task that cannot be called off. The Shanghai provisional action group is a crucial link in the plan, and so the organization decided, on short notice, to transfer me to Shanghai, charging me to root out the traitor within quickly and make sure the plan goes through.”

“There are five bars of gold in the bank's safe-deposit box, the funds the organization handed to the Shanghai group for this task. Now the box has been exposed, and they must be moved as soon as possible,” Lin Shi at once reminded him.

“You too must move at once,” said Chen Qianli. “The enemy may now know who you are. Once we have run the traitor down, they may start seizing people. You are the only one here who knows the ‘Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains plan,’ you carry a heavy charge, and you must move at once. There is one more thing I must tell you: Comrade Old Fang has given his life.”

“So I heard.” Lin Shi thought of what Yi Junnian had said a while before.

“Old Fang was making contact with me at the time, in his son's barbershop. To cover me he snatched up an agent's pistol and charged out. His son was arrested too. When I made contact with Comrade Yi Junnian, he told me he had learned of Old Fang's death from an inside source.”

“You would have me move first — but what of the other comrades?” Lin Shi nodded, then put the question to Chen Qianli.

“So long as you are not in the enemy's hands, the other comrades are safe for the moment. The agents will not move against them lightly; what they want more is to understand the plan behind us.”

“Even so, there must be a withdrawal scheme prepared in advance, in case the enemy can think of no other way and carries out mass arrests.”

Chen Qianli glanced at Lin Shi. He knew that Lin Shi thought he had spoken too lightly. He searched his own heart carefully: had he truly been too cavalier? Had he overlooked everyone's safety? Between completing the task and the lives of his comrades, had he fixed too hard on the first and plainly neglected the second?

He knew that the situation of this moment was forcing him to walk a tightrope, to strain every faculty he had to keep a dangerous balance. His comrades must complete the task under the enemy's gaze; each of them must feign utter unawareness of the agents at his back, and yet stay keenly alert, watch for the moment, and, in the instant the enemy's nerves slackened, act at once. And all the while they must be on guard against the enemy's turning, like a cornered dog, to desperate measures.

He himself was good at such things; in the operational psychology tests of his training he had always scored high. But the comrades downstairs — they might not be able to stay as cool as he. Was it that, because he had the ability to do it, he assumed others could as well?

“Qianyuan is —”

Chen Qianli said softly: “My younger brother.”

Lin Shi gazed at Chen Qianli, unable quite to make out just what kind of man this was who sat at his bedside. Cool, clear-headed, betraying nothing even with his own brother in peril. He did not know what sort of comrade-in-arms his superiors had sent. To handle a crisis like this took great wisdom. Only — a man of surpassing cleverness, might he not too easily, without meaning to, come to treat others as so many links in a chain of action? At a time like this one needed not only a head but a warm heart.

But now there was no time to hesitate any longer, and he said to Chen Qianli:

“First, I must lay out for you the whole of the ‘Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains’ plan. As far back as the August 7th Conference the Central Committee fixed the general line of land revolution and armed resistance to the Nationalist reactionaries; and our task this time, put simply, is to withdraw certain of the Central Committee's leaders safely from Shanghai, to move them to Ruijin, to move them into a broader world.

“Since last year the Party Central in Shanghai has had it harder and harder. We are growing, but the enemy has not been idle either. The Party Affairs Investigation Section, which the Nationalists keep expressly to deal with us, was lately enlarged again. Its chief, though he holds only a jianren rank, wields a power far beyond that of the jianren department heads and bureau chiefs. Among themselves they call it the ‘Special Operations Headquarters’; besides the agents on their own establishment, they post men into other organs, seize power inside those units, and turn them to the agents' use. By the intelligence the Central Committee has obtained, the Special Operations Headquarters very likely commands by now a vast number of agents, running a secret-police tyranny in the open, without shame.

“Little by little they have built up an effective anti-Communist intelligence net, using planted agents, and the opportunists and turncoats within the underground Party, to penetrate the organization without cease, so that the Shanghai Party organization has suffered grievous damage.

“This is a great removal. Besides the leaders, all else — the personnel, the organs, the documents, the wireless sets, the funds — must be arranged for accordingly. Some are to go over to local work and hold on in the underground struggle; some are to move along with the removal. To carry it out, the underground Party has convened several action groups in various places, and our provisional group is responsible for one part of the work.

“Originally, as the land revolution developed in the southern-Jiangxi and western-Fujian Soviet, the Central Committee spent great labor and resources to open four secret courier lines from Shanghai to the south. These lines are the red bloodstream of the Soviet; through them a great volume of people and matériel passes into and out of the Soviet areas, and so the enemy has forever schemed by every means to penetrate and wreck them. The old lines, long in use, are bound to be exposed. To ensure the withdrawal succeeds, and also to keep the red bloodstream flowing unobstructed in the harsher and crueler struggle to come, the Central Committee has decided to rebuild the most secret courier lines.

“We are charged with opening the stretch from Shanghai to Shantou. From Shanghai to Ruijin is more than three thousand kilometers; Comrade Shaoshan said, well then, let us call this operation the ‘Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains plan.’ He said it was not merely a courier line of a thousand li but a thousand li of rivers and mountains — our withdrawal from Shanghai was to scatter the revolution's sparks over the whole of China. Comrade Shaoshan said a single station on the courier line was worth a whole county of the Soviet area, and the line must be made good.

“By rights the Shanghai group is already exposed and unfit for such a task. Under the enemy's close watch it is hard to be sure of the safety of a newly built line. Only, the Central Committee and the Shanghai underground Party are in the greatest difficulty at present, and it would be hard, in so short a time, to muster fresh, seasoned people.”

“When your superiors set you this task, did they mention Comrade Haohan?”

“Comrade Haohan?” Lin Shi did not follow.

Chen Qianli told him of Old Fang's going to Pu'enjishi Road to make contact with Comrade Haohan.

“No wonder Old Fang never came to the meeting.”

When Chen Qianyuan had seen his brother into Lin Shi's sickroom and come back to the parlor, Tian Fei was arguing with Wei Dafu. Wei Dafu held that the organization had said all along that underground work must be masked by an open identity, and required every comrade to do superbly well at his open profession in society, the better both to shelter himself and to rouse the masses through it. So Lin Shi, even though he had just come out of the lockup, even with a gunshot wound, had reason enough to make a telephone call over bank business. Tian Fei did not care to reason with him at length; he only kept doggedly repeating that in his view there was certainly something wrong with Lin Shi.

Chen Qianyuan sat back down at the table and told them Lin Shi had lain down to rest. Qin Chuan'an rose and started for the kitchen: “Let him rest a while; I'll go and look in on him presently.”

Cui Wentai, still cradling his wine cup opposite, put in: “I'll go to the sickroom now, I'll look in on him too. It doesn't quite set the mind at ease, to leave him lying there alone.”

Yi Junnian, who was talking in low tones with Ling Wen, turned his head and stopped him: “What would you go for just now? Let him rest properly.”

For some reason Cui Wentai always felt a certain fear of Yi Junnian in his heart, above all when the man fixed his eyes on you — a gaze in which one could read no depth at all.

Qin Chuan'an made a turn about the kitchen and came out bearing a plate, on which was pressed a hemispherical aluminum pot-lid. Chen Qianyuan's interest was piqued: “What good thing is it this time?”

Qin Chuan'an smiled and said nothing, set the plate on the table, rubbed his hands, and lifted the lid: it proved to be a serving of glutinous eight-treasure rice with sweet red-bean paste, heaped over with dates, candied fruit and melon-seed kernels. Dong Huiwen cried out, “Oh, it's eight-treasure rice!” The words were scarcely out before Wei Dafu's chopsticks had already jabbed in and dug out a great lump of the bean paste.

Cui Wentai had no taste for sweets. All at once something came to his mind: “That special commissioner from above — how is it he's not coming today either? Has he heard the agents know of this place, and taken a bit of a fright?”

Yi Junnian said coldly, at his side: “What makes you think of him all of a sudden? A bit anxious for him to come, are you?”

“I'm just a worrier; I fret over everything.” Cui Wentai's laugh was rather dry, and he hastily took a mouthful of wine.

Liang Shichao had no liking for sweets either. He turned to Yi Junnian: “Spending New Year in Guangdong, did you ever eat fried taro puffs, or pork-and-radish cake?”

Ling Wen heard it, and asked Yi Junnian: “You've spent time in Guangdong? How is it I never heard you speak of it?”

Yi Junnian smiled: “Once you're transferred away, you oughtn't to speak to people of your former work.”

“Then how is it you told them?”

“Why, that was to pass the time in the lockup!”

“Old Yi worked in Guangdong for a good many years,” Liang Shichao told Ling Wen. “He was there at the time of the Canton–Hong Kong Strike. When we were in the lockup we spoke of some comrades who had given their lives, and several of them had been Old Yi's comrades-in-arms in those days.”

Ling Wen picked out a raisin with her chopsticks and chewed it a long while, her look heavy with care.

As they were talking, footsteps came on the stairs, and Lin Shi returned to the parlor on Chen Qianli's arm. As one the company rose, and the mood turned suddenly grave. Dong Huiwen came forward to help Lin Shi; Tian Fei, his face a little flushed with wine, gave up the chair behind him, but Lin Shi patted him on the shoulder and said with a smile that he needn't stir. Settling down beside Tian Fei, he said to them all: “Don't stand there, all of you — I've got to sit down quick myself.” Seeing it, Yi Junnian too laughed and waved everyone to their seats. Cui Wentai hurried to the kitchen to fetch a clean cup, and Qin Chuan'an filled a cup of wine and handed it to Chen Qianli.

“Comrade Chen Qianli, say a few words to everyone.” Lin Shi looked at Chen Qianli.

“Come, Qianyuan, let us two brothers wish the comrades a happy New Year together!” Chen Qianli had his brother rise and stand at his side.

“I guessed it long ago.” Wei Dafu laughed, tipped back his head and drank off a cup. The company raised their cups, rose, and toasted one another for the New Year.

When all had set down their cups, Lin Shi made the introduction: “Comrade Chen Qianli — our superiors have sent him to lead this group of ours.”

Chen Qianli looked each comrade over and said slowly: “I was first transferred here to work with Comrade Old Fang; Old Yi got the news that Old Fang has given his life.”

He glanced at Yi Junnian and went on: “Comrade Lin Shi is the Central Committee's special commissioner, code name ‘Old Kai’; he came on the Central Committee's behalf to convey the task to our Shanghai group. By the plan, once he had conveyed it he was to leave Shanghai at once, but he was wounded and cannot for the moment travel far. Until he can leave safely, he works together with us. Tomorrow morning three comrades — Lin Shi, Ling Wen and Cui Wentai — will go to the bank.”

Ling Wen and Cui Wentai glanced at Yi Junnian with some perplexity; Yi Junnian turned his face toward Lin Shi.

“In the bank's safe-deposit box there is a sum of funds the organization has set aside for this operation — five bars of gold,” Lin Shi added from the side. “Before we go further, the gold must first be taken out.”

“The greatest problem for the moment is that the enemy is very likely watching us.” Chen Qianli paused here. “Comrade Lin Shi and I judge that the enemy released the arrested comrades from the lockup not because they thought they had seized the wrong people — even had they truly seized the wrong people, they would not let them go so easily. We believe the enemy let everyone out because they know the underground Party is about to undertake an important operation; unable to learn the inside of it through interrogation, they released everyone in pretense, meaning to lull us into carelessness so that we betray ourselves in the course of the operation. Comrade Lin Shi and I both suspect that at this very moment, outside this house, there are agents keeping watch.”

Qin Chuan'an nodded.

“The two things we most need to keep safe are the gold in the box and Comrade Lin Shi. Yet we must take the gold out, and the gold must be fetched by Comrade Lin Shi in person. So, to make doubly sure, Comrade Lin Shi and I have just talked it over and worked out a plan in which the man and the gold go separately. Tomorrow morning at ten-thirty, Comrade Cui Wentai will drive Comrades Lin Shi and Ling Wen to the bank. The bank's main door is on the street corner; after they get out,” Chen Qianli turned to Cui Wentai, “you park the car on the road opposite the corner and wait. When Comrades Lin Shi and Ling Wen come out, they will put the suitcase holding the gold into the car but will not get in themselves; Comrade Cui Wentai will take the gold to the contact point at Laozha Bridge, and it will pass on from there by boat. Comrades Lin Shi and Ling Wen are not to leave at once; they will sit a while in the café at the mouth of Fucheng Li, across from the bank, and withdraw five minutes later.”

Chen Qianli turned to Yi Junnian: “Comrade Yi Junnian, you are to reach that café before half past ten, with a means of transport ready, and see to their safe withdrawal.”

Yi Junnian nodded: “Good!”

“And the rest of us?” Liang Shichao asked eagerly.

“The other comrades return to their own lodgings and await orders.”

Having said all this, Chen Qianli felt, for the first time, a faint unease in his heart. Was this decision, after all, the right one? Tomorrow, when the agents found that both Lin Shi and the gold in the box had vanished, they would surely mount a frenzied hunt. How much certainty, after all, did his arrangement hold?
