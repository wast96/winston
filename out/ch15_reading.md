## 1

Xu Delin met his violent end while his son, Zhongliang, was rehearsing Hamlet in the school's little assembly hall.

For more than half a month the drama club had gathered each evening on the dim stage, sighing and declaiming by turns. Zhongliang was playing Fortinbras, the prince of Sweden; his part was small, so he had dug a copy of Hamlet in the original out of the library and stood against one of the pillars of the stage, murmuring it under his breath, line by line. He had no taste for acting. What he loved was English.

To make something of yourself in Shanghai, you first had to speak fluent English. It was a line the dean of studies, back from his years abroad, often gave the students; now and then he taught English and vernacular composition himself. Zhongliang's own dreams ran shallower. He only wanted a clerk's post in a foreign firm after graduation, a Western suit and a necktie every day, his hair combed to a shine; for a postman's son that was already rising in the world. But by dusk the next day, Zhongliang understood all at once that the dream was finished.

The mission school's dining hall doubled as the students' chapel, a black crucifix on the wall at its center. As everyone sat at the tables, hands folded and chins propped, saying grace before the meal, a school porter led in a man in a short grey cotton jacket, who crossed the room quickly to Zhongliang.

Zhongliang recognized him: Zhou San, the doorman of the Jing'an post office. Yet what rose in his mind was his father's pallid face. He followed Zhou San out through the school gate, took the rickshaw waiting there, and reached home to find his father laid out rigid on a door-plank. Xu Delin wore a satin gown no one had ever seen him in, and a thin dusting of powder and rouge lay on his face. He might have been a sleeping actor.

By the police account, Xu Delin had died in a robbery; too many drifters were coming down from the north, and the concession was no longer the peaceful place it had once been. But the next day a busybody reporter at the Shanghai Times took another view. A robbery was not a kidnapping, and who would seize a postman, hold him two days, then kill him, all for the sake of robbing him? To go with the piece the paper ran a photograph alongside it: a man whose face could not be made out, slumped in a postman's uniform pulled open, in a doorway with granite steps.

Zhongliang knew the place at a glance: the front door of St. Thérèse's Church. For years Xu Delin had gone there every Sunday for Mass, sometimes with his son along. When he went into the confessional he would send the boy to wait at the door, on those granite steps. Zhongliang still remembered his father coming out one time, standing on the steps, suddenly taking his hand and saying to him in earnest, "Remember this. Before God, all men are born equal."

No one knew when Xu Delin had come to the faith, but in church he looked more devout than any born Catholic. There was a time when, home from a long day out, fed and washed, the shift-bell of the tram depot across the way already rung, he still could not settle down to sleep. He had to pedal his battered bicycle to the church, saying his Lord was waiting, that he had a confession to make.

One night Xu-sao finally could hold it no longer. She sat on the edge of the bed, watching him coldly, and said, "Your Lord is no wild pheasant." Xu Delin did not catch it at first; one hand on the door-bolt, he turned to look at his wife. Xu-sao said it again, straight into his eyes: "Only a wild pheasant waits for you in the middle of the night."

Xu Delin understood. He said nothing, only gave her one long, deep look, then softly drew the door open and went out, easing it shut behind him.

That Xu Delin kept women, and more than one, was an open secret at the Jing'an post office. The concession was full of sailors' wives, rich men's concubines, the dance-hostesses such men set up in flats; a postman carried letters into these households, and where the chance offered he carried himself into their beds as well. Lonely women wanted comforting, and a postman wanted the money more, to piece out his household; on his wages alone Xu Delin could never have put his son into a boarding mission school.

For her son's sake, Xu-sao bore it. The bearing gave a woman's eyes a depth no one could sound.

Father Brown of St. Thérèse's Church said the Mass before the burial, in a cramped side room of the International Funeral Parlor. This wrinkled Englishman had preached thirty years in China, close to ten of them in Shanghai, and still could not master the soft local speech. He held the Bible and read out a passage of the Gospel of Matthew in a flawless Tianjin accent, then narrowed his grey-blue eyes, gazed a while at the body in the coffin, drew a cross over his chest, and let the word out slowly, in two beats: A-men.

The congregation ringed the coffin and began to sing a hymn. All at once Xu-sao seized her son's arm, her eyes wide, staring at the solemn-faced women among them; her body was shaking, yet she bit down hard and would not let go. Xu-sao was certain that her husband's violent death in the street had to do with these women now murmuring their low, soft song.

Xu Delin had died hard. Not a mark showed on his skin, but washing the body, the mortician found both his testicles crushed, hanging in his groin like an unripe persimmon, and nine of his ten toes stripped of their nails. What had truly taken his life was the hole at the back of his skull.

The mortician fetched two rags to pack the hole full, then pried Xu Delin's mouth open and, as custom asked, set a copper coin inside. Again his eyes went fixed. He glanced back at Xu-sao, standing wooden as a block, hesitated, and said, "You'll want to have a mouth-piece bought for him." Xu-sao might have been deaf. The mortician stood, wiping his two hands, and said again, "The tongue's gone as well. How is he to cry out his wrong once he's down below?"

From first to last Xu-sao did not shed a single tear, nor once wail; she only bit down on her teeth. Not until the two funeral men in white came in, fitted the coffin lid, and wheeled it away did she suddenly wheel about and fling herself at the priest, dropping to her knees, both hands gripping the hem of his robe, crying out in a keening voice: "The police do nothing, your Lord does nothing. What am I to do? What is my son to do?"

Father Brown tipped his head back and let out a long breath, drew two crosses over his chest one after the other, then laid a hand on Xu-sao's head, shut his eyes, and said, "Let him rest in the Kingdom of Heaven."

In truth Father Brown had been the first to find Xu Delin's body. That morning he had pulled the church doors open as always, and, broom in hand, had barely stepped out when he saw Xu Delin slumped to one side. At first the priest took him for a drunk not yet slept off, and said, "Morning's here." But when he leaned in and saw Xu Delin's face, his mouth fell open and he turned sharply to look about him. The street was bare; it was the hour before dawn breaks light, and the lamps on the poles had already gone dark.

Father Brown ran his grey-blue eyes over the road once more, then slowly crouched and put a hand beneath Xu Delin's nose. A man who has been through seminary is half a physician; he ran over the body swiftly, rose, went down the steps at a run and across the street, and knocked at a shut door. He was more than a little shaken. "Go to the police," he said quickly, "go and bring them here."

By the time a constable pedaled up, a crowd had already ringed the doorway of St. Thérèse's Church. Every neighbor who had seen the hole in the back of the skull decided it was the fabled "opening a skylight," and, like "planting a lotus," the work of the city's gangs enforcing their own house law. Father Brown said nothing. He stood stock-still beside the body, a black statue set to guard the gate of heaven. Only when the constable wrapped the body in a white sheet and bore it away did his gaze drop to that corner.

A constable followed his eyes and looked as well, and said, "Not so bad, no blood on the ground." Then he turned, waved a hand at the onlookers below the steps, and said, "Move along, break it up, no gathering to gawk."
