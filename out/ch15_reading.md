## Chapter Fifteen. The Hour of the Rat, Second Half (midnight)

As he talked on, Xiao Gui had risen to his feet once more, and pressing himself forward against the crossbow-head, came walking on. Zhang Xiaojing dared neither pull the trigger nor draw the weapon away, and was forced to give ground step by step, until soon his back struck the door-frame with a thud.

The twenty-third year of Kaiyuan, the seventh month, the fourteenth day; the hour of the Horse.

The Protectorate of Anxi; thirty li north of Balhuan; the beacon-fort.

Not a wisp of cloud, not a breath of wind — only a single fierce sun riding high overhead, flinging down its endless heat upon this stretch of land without restraint. The whole desert steamed like a cage, the very sand scalding underfoot; yet steam as it might, it could not boil away the thick reek of blood and rotting corpses that hung upon the air.

A dragon-banner drooped from a flagpole split half through, long since so blackened by wolf-smoke that its color could no longer be told. Over the shattered battlements, above and below, bodies lay heaped — horsemen of the Türgesh of the Türk, and Tang soldiers alike. No one had gathered them up, for there was all but no one left.

Of those who still truly drew breath, there were but ten-odd soldiers. Every one of them was filthy in gown and armor, their topknots half come loose and hanging down, so that they looked no better than savages. They sprawled every which way in the shadow of a half-ruined blockhouse, keeping out of the direct glare as best they could — all but one man, who was still out among the heaps of the dead, rummaging for something.

Zhang Xiaojing bent and picked up a ring-pommeled saber, found the edge already chipped away, shook his head and flung it aside. He found next a long spear, but its shaft was gripped fast in the death-grip of a fallen Tang soldier, and try as he might he could not pry it loose. He could only strike off the spearhead and tuck it into his breast, then swept his eyes about, searching for some serviceable pole of wood.

"I say — why don't you get some rest, instead of larking about out there?" Wen Wuji, sheltering in the shadow of a broken wall, called out in a hoarse voice.

"The blades are all turned; if I don't find some to spare, then when the fighting starts again, are we to use our teeth?" But Zhang Xiaojing would not come back, and went on rummaging among the corpses. Wen Wuji and the several other old soldiers lying in the shade all laughed: "Give it up. Weapons or no weapons — what odds can it make?"

They had held out, bitterly, for nine days now. The Eighth Protectorate Company, three hundred men at full strength, had been whittled down by death to thirteen; even the commandant had fallen in the fighting. When next the Türks came on, there would likely be no one left to hold. And at such a pass a man grows, of all things, open-hearted.

"Zhang Big-Head — if you've the strength to spare, go find me some mint-leaf, will you? My hand's grown a touch unsteady."

The call came from the very top of the blockhouse, from a lean, dried-up archer with an eagle's-beak nose. He was binding a new string to a bow, the web of his hand long since split open from drawing too many times. Zhang Xiaojing raised his head: "Xiao Gui — how many have you killed?"

"Twenty-three."

"Kill your twenty-five, and I'll roll you one with my own hands."

"Can't you fucking give it me first? I'm afraid you won't live long enough to see it," Xiao Gui swore.

"Then wait till I dig one out of a dead man's mouth for you."

Zhang Xiaojing lifted his head to gauge the height of the sun. At high noon the Türks did not, as a rule, mount an assault; it would be past the hour of the Goat before they stirred. These few men had at the least one more shichen to live. So he wiped away his sweat and bent again to his rummaging.

Before long he came back into the shade with two long spears, three short knives, and a fistful of arrows in his arms, dumped them clattering on the ground, and lay straight down to catch his breath. Wen Wuji tossed him a waterskin; Zhang Xiaojing tipped it to his mouth, and only four or five drops trickled out, wetting the tip of his tongue like some nectar of the gods. All those around him licked their cracked lips without thinking — but there was not a drop more left in the skin.

"This wolf-smoke's been burning a whole day and a night now — even if the Protectorate's relief came crawling, it should have crawled here by this," said one soldier. Wen Wuji narrowed his eyes: "Hard to say. The Türks have raised no small stir this time; maybe there's fighting over at Balhuan too."

A silence fell in the shade; they all understood what that meant. Once Balhuan was locked in stalemate, there was no way this place could hold out until relief came. Wen Wuji looked around him and sighed all at once: "We've come the whole long way out here to the Western Regions, and it's odds we'll never get back. Now, brothers, let's have it agreed — whoever lives through it takes charge of gathering up the dead, and sending them home to their native soil."

Zhang Xiaojing, leaning aslant against a broken wall, said: "You wish. Old Wang's to go back to Hedong, Old Fan back to Jiannan, and then there's Commandant Gan, Liu the Clerk, Song Sixteen, Du Poluo... there's a long line to be sent home, and it'd be years before your turn came round. Better salt the corpses down first, and wait your while."

Wen Wuji went over to that heap of battered weapons and took them up one by one to look them over: "For my own part it makes no odds whether I go back or not — call it dying loyal to my country. Whichever of you lives, mind you marry my daughter, so she's not left all alone and forlorn in the world."

"With a face like yours, what sort of daughter would you get? I'd sooner fight the Türks to the death."

called out another soldier, and drew a spatter of feeble laughter. The very word "death" seemed to have been baked numb by the blazing sun; every man spoke of it lightly, for all the world like a party of young scholars out treading the spring grass.

Wen Wuji clicked his tongue: "Ai, you don't know — our whole Wen family art of blending incense, handed down through the generations, is in her hands now. They say that in Chang'an a single packet of rue-incense fetches fifty strings of cash; the two of you set up a shop, and it's as good as sitting on a mountain of gold."

"You've been to the city of Chang'an, then? What's it really like? They say that inside, the palace halls are bigger than this whole desert."

"Rubbish! Where would you find a roof that big? But I've heard there are a hundred and eight wards in the city! It's a vast, vast place!" Wen Wuji said, well pleased with himself.

The men cried out in wonder — Kucha had but ten-odd wards, and who would have thought Chang'an so great? One of them fell into a fond, faraway musing: "If we live through this, we really ought to go and see the great bright world of Chang'an. Best of all to catch your daughter's incense-shop just opened — we'll all go and offer our congratulations, and carry off a few good packets of incense while we're at it, and see whether you old bastard dare take our money."

Wen Wuji roared with laughter: "I'll not take it, I'll not take it — come, all of you, and I'll stand you sons of rabbits a cup of Xinfeng wine to taste besides. We brothers of the Eighth Company will have a fine gathering in Chang'an."

"I'm for the green bowers — I've never yet touched a woman!"

"I'll buy a box of flower-ornaments for my mother — she's never bought so much as a cake of face-powder in all her life!"

"A day of eating in every ward — I could eat my way through a hundred and eight days straight!"

"To Chang'an! To Chang'an! To Chang'an!" The whole band, carried away with the fun of it, beat their scabbards against the stones and set up a merry uproar.

A pang of sorrow rose in Zhang Xiaojing's heart, and all at once he spoke up: "Old Wen, better you go on ahead — go back and look after your daughter; this place will do well enough without you." The others too spoke up one after another, telling him to go. And as the talk ran on, someone let slip, offhand: "While the Türks are still not come — why don't we just all of us pull out?"

Every mouth fell shut at once. The thought had haunted many a heart there a long while, yet no one had ever dared say it aloud; and now, on the back of this talk, someone had at last poked through the paper of the window. The relief was slow, so slow in coming, and the enemy massed thicker and thicker; for the few of them that were left, to hold or not to hold made in truth no difference at all.

But Wen Wuji's face darkened, and he said sharply: "Who said that? Stand out here!" No one took him up on it. Wen Wuji jabbed his arrows into the ground: "The order we took was to hold the beacon-fort to the death. No acting as we saw fit, no acting as the moment served — just holding to the death. If the men are not all dead, and the fort is lost, do you call that holding to the death?"

"No one here clings to his life for fear of death. But it's come to this pass now..." Zhang Xiaojing plucked up the courage to try to argue.

Wen Wuji lifted his arm and swept it back behind him: "We fall back, and just behind us lies Balhuan, and Shayan, and Kucha, and the whole Protectorate of Anxi besides. If every man thinks so, is this war to be fought at all? It's not as if you'd never seen how savage the Türks are!" Zhang Xiaojing would have said something more, but Wen Wuji turned his back in a huff: "Pull out if you will — I'm staying here. This is the sovereign soil of the great Tang! I'll not budge a step!"

He put out his right fist and struck it hard against his left shoulder. This was the rallying-salute of the Eighth Company, and its meaning was "Nine deaths, no regret." The men's faces stiffened, and they made the same gesture too, which left Zhang Xiaojing rather at a loss.

From the roof of the blockhouse Xiao Gui called down lazily: "I say — quarrel however you please, but might I trouble you to send a man up with a bundle of arrows?" He had handed down a way out of the moment just in time; Zhang Xiaojing hastened to pull Wen Wuji's arrows out of the ground and carry them up the blockhouse.

Xiao Gui took the arrows and ran an eye over them: "This one's not quite straight — smooth down the fletching for me." Seeing Zhang Xiaojing say nothing, he swore at him again: "Zhang Big-Head, you really are a pig-brain. You know Old Wen's foul temper — what do you go stirring him up on purpose for?" Zhang Xiaojing took the arrow back and said, unconvinced: "It's not me that's pulling out! I was only urging him to go. His wife died early — and how old are the children at home?"

"To die in battle on the sands and be wrapped home in a horse's hide — that is a soldier's bounden duty. If we can keep this banner from falling before we die, then we've not failed the sovereign's grace; why fret over all the rest?"

He said it easily enough, but the sense of it was the same as Wen Wuji's: this was the soil of the great Tang, and there would be no falling back. Zhang Xiaojing stared at him: "For all your usual idle, slack ways, that you should come out with a thing like that — are you not afraid to die?"

Xiao Gui tilted back his head, leaning against the flagpole with a face of utter indifference: "What I'm more afraid of is having no mint-leaf to chew."

"Enough, enough — I've searched everywhere; there's not a single leaf left!"

Xiao Gui gave up his asking, and sat cross-legged to go on stringing his bow. Zhang Xiaojing, smoothing down the fletching, sighed: "I've no father and no mother, no son and no daughter; it's no great matter if I die. But Old Wen has a daughter, that's plain enough — and you, I seem to recall, have a sister still in Guangwu? Why will none of you go?"

"To hold fast and die in battle here is always better than to hold fast and die in battle on the walls of one's own home town." Xiao Gui said it slowly. "Every one of us must answer for his own—" His head tilted suddenly a little to the left. "—choosing."

The next instant a long arrow grazed past Xiao Gui's ear and lodged fast in a crack of the stone wall.

"Here they come!" Xiao Gui sprang up from the ground all at once, seized his long bow and took his stand by the parapet. Zhang Xiaojing hurriedly gave warning to the men below; Wen Wuji and the rest rose one after another, took up their weapons, and gathered in toward this side.

The Türks had, unlooked-for, moved ahead of their time; it seemed they too were sorely galled at being so long unable to break the deadlock beneath the beacon-fort. Xiao Gui's sight was uncommonly keen; shading his eyes with a hand, he saw thirty-odd Türgesh horsemen already galloping this way, the yellow sand flung up behind them, and a hundred or two hundred more riders at the least.

"Big-Head, come and help me!" Xiao Gui rose from before the parapet and stood bolt upright in a proper archer's stance.

Zhang Xiaojing, a knife in one hand and a shield in the other, stood firm guard at his side. Xiao Gui plucked the bowstring, and not a shaft flew wide; on the instant three horsemen tumbled from their mounts. The other riders scattered swiftly and shot back; but the range was too great, and by the time the arrows reached Xiao Gui their force was spent, and Zhang Xiaojing struck them down one by one.

Xiao Gui had trained himself to a fine hand at archery, and, standing on the height besides, could outrange even the Türks for all their mastery of bow and horse. But he had to hold himself bolt upright, without cover, and the guarding of him could only be left to others. Wen Wuji too came bounding up, and stood with Zhang Xiaojing to shield Xiao Gui, ready to meet a yet thicker storm of arrows. The rest held grimly to the foot of the blockhouse.

The Tang soldiers were but ten-odd men now; to look to them to hold the whole beacon-fort was out of the question. So they had drawn their line in to this one corner-bastion on the southeast side. This corner-bastion was the highest point of the fort; from it Xiao Gui, looking down from above, kept the whole place in awe, while the others ringed him about and held the foot of the bastion, to keep the enemy from drawing near.

So long as Xiao Gui's bowstring still sang, the Türks could not enter the fort with any ease of mind.

It was the tactic of last resort, and the only one left to a broken remnant that could still avail.

After the loss of seven or eight riders, the main body of the Türks reached the bastion at last. These Türk horsemen vaulted the collapsed stone wall and rushed the corner-bastion. In the earlier attempts they had already read the Tang soldiers' tactic, and knew that to match bow and arrow against the bastion's height was but to add to their own losses; so this time they came in heavy armor, charging the passage before the corner-bastion, meaning to draw the firewood from under the very cauldron.

Xiao Gui loosed and loosed again, and soon his arm began to show the signs of cramp — the fierce fighting before had spent too much of his strength. The veins started up on his brow; gritting his teeth he shot one more arrow, and this time struck only the instep of a single Türk soldier's foot. It was a dangerous sign, and Xiao Gui had no choice but to stop a while and rest. Zhang Xiaojing and Wen Wuji stood upon the high platform and, expressionless, warded off for him the arrows that came ever thicker.

Seizing the moment, the Türk soldiers swarmed up as one and rushed the slope beside the corner-bastion. All at once two broken chunks of wall came crashing down from above, and on the instant smashed the five or six men in front into bloody pulp. Then ten-odd ragged Tang soldiers came flinging themselves silently from every corner; first they struck right fist to left shoulder, then closed with the Türks in a tangled melee.

Their movements were not so nimble as the Türks', but the way they fought was to hold nothing back of their lives. No knife left — they bit with their teeth; no legs left — they clung with their arms, to make an opening for a comrade. And every man, as he grappled and killed, cried out hoarse and high: "To Chang'an! To Chang'an! To Chang'an!" Soon the cry ran on, voice upon voice, till it rang through the whole beacon-fort.

And amid that cry the Türks' assault was, once again and against all likelihood, pressed miraculously back.

But the cost this time was very great too: five more Tang soldiers went down in pools of blood, and the survivors besides could scarcely stir a limb.

"The Eighth Company — nine deaths, no regret!"

cried Xiao Gui, loosing his last arrow like lightning, and across the way a Türk soldier tumbled from the wall. He saw another wave of Türks come pouring into the fort, some thirty of them, and knew the final moment had come at last.

Wen Wuji and Zhang Xiaojing exchanged a glance and nodded together. Swiftly the two heaved aside a stone slab, laying bare a hole that led down into the blockhouse. And beneath that hole there was set an enormous wooden barrel.

Xiao Gui snapped his great bow in two with a crack, and then leapt down. In that barrel was the last of the fierce-fire thunder, made ready against this final moment; in the whole Eighth Company only Xiao Gui knew how to handle the dangerous stuff.

"Thirty finger-snaps!"

Xiao Gui said it coolly; this was the least time it took to set off a fierce-fire thunder. Wen Wuji and Zhang Xiaojing nodded, turned and took up shield and knife. They did not reckon how long they could hold — only that it would not end till they were dead.

The Türk soldiers began to climb the blockhouse like so many ants. The wounded at its foot dragged themselves up one after another with the last of their strength, hoping to delay the enemy if only by a single finger-snap. The Türks killed them without mercy, flung them off, and climbed on. They had but one goal: that eyesore of a dragon-banner of the great Tang.

But between them and the dragon-banner there stood, as ill luck would have it, two figures yet.

Zhang Xiaojing had little strength left; it was all one held breath that kept him up. His look began to grow dazed, and his arms to move stiffly. A sound of splitting wind came, but Zhang Xiaojing's reaction was a beat too slow, and he did not at once make out the quarter it came from.

"Look out!" Wen Wuji, beside him, shouted aloud and kicked him clear, and so made him dodge that killing arrow. At that very moment a Türk soldier had already climbed onto the blockhouse, and with terrible force hewed down with a sharp, broad-bladed cavalry saber; the blade cut through flesh, cut through bone, and at one stroke severed Wen Wuji's right leg.

Wen Wuji gave a piteous cry, and with the last of his strength seized the Türk soldier fast, thrust hard, and so the two of them pitched together off the blockhouse. Zhang Xiaojing, aghast, stepped quickly to the edge and craned to look, and saw the two locked in each other's arms where they had fallen on the heap of rubble, unmoving — whose brains it was that had run out he could not tell, staining a patch of the stone a yellowish hue.

Zhang Xiaojing felt something go off with a rush inside his skull, and a scalding, crimson current surged through his whole body. He gave a low roar, cast away his small shield, kept only a single knife in his hand, his pupils all suffused with blood, his movements like a thing gone mad and demon-possessed. Three soldiers who had just clambered up were startled by this sudden eruption, and Zhang Xiaojing hewed each one across the neck, a stroke apiece. Three fountains of blood spouted from the headless trunks, spattering Zhang Xiaojing from head to foot.

"Nearly there — fifteen finger-snaps to go," Xiao Gui called from down in the hole, his hands never ceasing their work.

But the knife in Zhang Xiaojing's hand was clean broken now, and that brief eruption had left grave after-effects. His oil was spent, his lamp burned dry; he could only lean against the flagpole of the dragon-banner, and sit slumped and gasping, waiting to die. Several Türk soldiers climbed up once more, and came at him spread out in a fan.

Just then a streak of pitch-black rock-oil came flying out of the hole and clung to those Türk soldiers. At once Xiao Gui sprang out of the mouth of the hole and flung a lit twist of mugwort tinder onto them; on the instant these men gave shrill, piteous screams, and fell from the roof as so many human torches.

Xiao Gui came stumbling over to Zhang Xiaojing's side and leaned, too, against the flagpole. He cocked his head, saw the tens of Türk soldiers climbing up one after another below, and laughed.

"Seven finger-snaps yet. With this many to keep us company, we've had our money's worth."

He drew from his breast a rotting mint-leaf and made to put it in his mouth, but his fingers cramped all at once so violently that he could not hold it at all. Zhang Xiaojing, with an effort, raised his arm and helped tuck it in for him:

"Where did you find that?" Zhang Xiaojing asked.

"Under the bottom of the fierce-fire thunder barrel. I told you long ago, you old bastard never looked properly at all." Xiao Gui swore, chewed a few times, then spat it out with a "pah!" — "Stinks of foul oil!"

Zhang Xiaojing closed his eyes: "A pity. We of the Eighth Company will never, after all, have our gathering in Chang'an."

"The netherworld's well enough too — at least the brothers are all there... Here, give me a hand."

Xiao Gui had drawn his bow too many times, and his arm ached now past the lifting. Zhang Xiaojing bent the right arm for him and set it against his left shoulder. Xiao Gui clenched his fist, struck it lightly against the shoulder, and split his mouth in a grin: "Nine deaths, no regret."

"Nine deaths, no regret." Zhang Xiaojing made him the same salute.

Below them the fuse of the fierce-fire thunder burned on with a hiss. The Türks still went on climbing up the blockhouse. The two of them, back against back, waited quietly for the last moment to come.

All at once Xiao Gui's ear twitched. He knit his brows, and straightened up with a jerk. Zhang Xiaojing, off his guard, was left leaning on nothing. Xiao Gui craned his neck sharply and looked away to the south of the beacon-fort.

Far off, it seemed, a dust-storm had risen. Xiao Gui cried out suddenly: "It's Protector Gai — it's Protector Gai!" His sight was very keen, and he could see, amid the dust, a great standard flying high, now hidden and now shown. In all the Western Regions there was no one who did not know that banner.

The main host of the Protectorate of Anxi had come up at last!

Xiao Gui was too overjoyed, and forgot altogether the pass they were in. Zhang Xiaojing gave a great shout — "Look out!" — and threw himself in front of Xiao Gui. A Türk soldier who had climbed onto the roof hewed viciously down with his long saber, and struck full upon Zhang Xiaojing's left eye; on the instant the blood spurted, and the eyeball was all but cut clean in two.

Zhang Xiaojing, his whole face awash with blood, looked like some fiend. He did not so much as cover the wound, but only clung grimly to the Türk soldier, crying out to Xiao Gui to be gone quickly. Since Gai Jiayun had come up, there was one last thread of life yet. Of the two of them, at least one might live.

Xiao Gui shot a glance at the mouth of the hole: less than four finger-snaps remained before the fierce-fire thunder would burst. With a crack he snapped the flagpole of the dragon-banner, gripped the broken half, and thrust it like a spear into the Türk soldier's body; then he seized Zhang Xiaojing's belt, tore down the dragon-banner to wrap about the two of them, and, without a backward thought, leapt from the outer side of the corner-tower toward the boundless desert beyond.

The two Tang soldiers traced an arc through the air, one corner of the dragon-banner streaming up in the wind; and at almost the same instant, the fierce-fire thunder within the corner-tower woke at last, and utterly.

This was a fierce-fire thunder that Xiao Gui had mixed with his own hands; there was no fear at all of its misfiring. In one instant the searing light and heat burst forth, till even the fierce sun in the sky was dimmed beside it. The whole corner-tower came thundering down in the roar of the blast, and amid the vast smoke and dust, numberless shards of brick and stone, wrapped about with raging flame, scattered on every side, and swallowed at one gulp every Türk soldier near at hand.

The violent shock-wave pushed the two of them, Xiao Gui and Zhang Xiaojing, a little farther yet through the air. Their bodies fell heavily upon the soft yellow sand. And then that battered, tattered dragon-banner came drifting slowly down to earth at last...

The third year of Tianbao, the fifteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Rat, second half.

Chang'an; beneath the Xingqing Palace.

"Xiao Gui?!"

A heavy, low growl rolled up out of Zhang Xiaojing's throat, and the crossbow trembled in his grip against his will. Never in his wildest thought had he dreamed that Long Bo, whom he had hunted so bitterly and so long, should turn out to be a comrade-in-arms of the old days, one who had gone through life and death at his side.

This unlooked-for turn left him utterly at a loss.

"We of the Eighth Company have met in Chang'an at last — though I never thought our reunion would be like this." Xiao Gui, who had gone under the false name of Long Bo, lay sprawled on the ground and let the crossbow press against his temple, yet his face showed the gladness of old friends met again.

Zhang Xiaojing did not draw the crossbow back, but pressed it a shade tighter still: "How can it be you?! How can it be you?!"

"Why should it not be me?" Xiao Gui returned.

Zhang Xiaojing's lips trembled faintly, his heart a tangle of raveled threads. He knew that what he ought to do now was to shoot this vicious, desperate criminal dead with one bolt, and then go and stop the plot up in the great lantern-tower; but his finger could by no means pull the trigger — for this was the very comrade to whom, in those years, he could entrust his own unguarded back!

Zhang Xiaojing could not well understand it: the Xiao Gui of those years, who had held the dragon-banner to the death — how had he become the savage Long Bo? Was not the thing he now sought to destroy the very thing he had once striven with all his might to protect? What in the world had befallen him?

"You... where have you been all these years?" This was the thing Zhang Xiaojing most urgently longed to know.

That day, Gai Jiayun's great host had reached the beacon-fort and broken the besieging Türgesh army. Clearing the field afterward, they found that Zhang Xiaojing and Xiao Gui had broken several ribs but still drew breath; and they found besides, in a cleft of the stones, Wen Wuji at his last gasp. When he had fallen from the corner-tower, a Türk soldier had broken his fall, and he had then rolled into a crevice between the stones, and so, by a miracle, had escaped both the fierce-fire thunder and the flying rubble.

The three surviving members of the Eighth Company were first sent back to Balhuan, then forwarded on to Kucha, the seat of the Protectorate of Anxi, for treatment. The army was well pleased with their hard fighting, and heaped praise and reward upon them.

Wen Wuji, having lost a leg, could not stay in the army, and so converted his reward into a scroll of Chang'an household registry — the fulfilling of a wish long held. Zhang Xiaojing, fearing there would be no one to care for Wen Wuji, used his own rank as a decorated Flying Cavalry Commandant to find himself a post in the selection of foot-archers under the Ministry of War, and went to Chang'an as well. As for Xiao Gui, he did not accept Zhang Xiaojing and Wen Wuji's invitation, but laid down his arms and set out for Guangwu. And from that time on, Zhang Xiaojing and Wen Wuji never heard word of him again.

Until this very day.

The Longshou Canal drove six huge water-wheels turning without pause, their low droning humming back and forth through the empty underground vault. The torch that had fallen to the ground had gone out at last, and the two men in the dark still stood unmoving, like a pair of stone guardians facing each other beside a tomb.

After a long silence, Xiao Gui's voice rose slow and drawn-out in the dark: "That year, after we parted at Kucha, I went to Guangwu to join my elder sister. I brought a great store of reward-money with me, and a warrant of appointment as a constable besides, and I hoped with all my heart to live a good life from then on. But when I got home and looked, I found the house already a heap of ruins. After much asking about I learned the truth: a certain county-aide of Guangwu had lusted after my sister's beauty, and had shamed and abused her to her death. Fearing the family would raise a great outcry, the aide even bought off a pack of scoundrels to set a fire, and burned my brother-in-law and my two nephews to death together in the house. I went to lay charges before the officials, and instead was framed — they said I was a horse-bandit, that all the reward-money I had brought back was plunder taken as a robber; and they destroyed my warrant of appointment."

He said it very calmly, as though he told of some other man's affair; yet a grim, chill hatred had long since sunk deep into every word. Zhang Xiaojing said not a word, only his breathing grew a great deal heavier.

"I had counted on the Area Command of Lanzhou to help clear my name, but they were all of one foul breath together; far from looking into it, they passed the word on and had me thrown into gaol. I lay in gaol more than a year. In the prison they used me as a stand-in for a condemned man, to be executed at the dead of night — but I spied a flaw in it, killed the headsman, and fled under cover of that same night. I stole a strong bow out of the armory, and shot dead more than ten officials great and small, the county-aide among them, till the Guangwu county office was emptied of them. I could get no footing there, and could only take my bow and flee, a fugitive, this way and that."

"Fleeing, a fugitive, this way and that" was an easy thing to say, but there was a boundless bitterness folded within it. Between the prefectures and counties of the great Tang the barriers were strict indeed: an ordinary man without a travel-warrant might not cross a pass or a ford, nor had he the right to lodge at an inn. A fugitive could only lie hidden by day and go abroad by night, forever in dread and fear, never seeing the light of day.

Xiao Gui could feel that, though the crossbow still pressed to his temple, the killing-intent upon it was next to nothing now. He smiled a little, put out a hand and gently pushed it aside, and slowly sat himself up.

"Why did you not come to Chang'an and find us?" Zhang Xiaojing asked.

"And what use would finding you be? Would you flee along with me?" Xiao Gui smiled. "Later, when I could get no footing in the Central Plains, I went to a garrison-town near Lingwu, and hid myself there, and have dragged out my life to this day."

At the two words "garrison-town" a light broke on Zhang Xiaojing. Those were places of chaos and no law, where men with a killing on their backs, like Xiao Gui, were thick as anything; and with his archery he could easily rise to the top among them.

No wonder, then, that this business of attacking Chang'an had the Shouzhuolang mixed up in it — the two had a bond of long standing after all.

At the thought Zhang Xiaojing's brow gave a twitch; he realized he had been led somewhat astray, and raised the crossbow again: "Then explain yourself — this present business of yours, what madness is this you're in the grip of?"

"That is a thing I should rather be asking you, is it not? What madness are you in the grip of?" Xiao Gui's voice turned grim and dark. "How did I end? How did Wen Wuji end? And whom have you to thank that you were flung into the death cells? Why, come to such a pass as this, will you still be a willing hawk and hound for the court?"

Zhang Xiaojing gave the crossbow's mouth a jerk: "This is another matter!"

"What other matter? The court's nature has never once changed." Xiao Gui sneered. "Never mind the far-off things; look at yourself now. With no small trouble you put paid to the Turkic Wolf Guards — and to what end? In the end were you not hunted through the whole city, driven to your wits' end? We fought for the court till we bled, and how did they use us in return? Ten years a soldier in the Western Regions, nine years a chief in Chang'an — and what have you got for it?"

Zhang Xiaojing was silent; there was nothing he could say against it, for it was a plain fact. Xiao Gui said: "So it is I who must ask you: what has gone wrong with your head, that you should strive so hard to shield this bastard that has wounded you from head to foot?"

Zhang Xiaojing spoke: "The court has done wrong, yes; but that is a thing between me and the court. You, for the sake of a private grudge of your own, have gone so far as to collude with the enemy of former days — how would the brothers of the Eighth Company, who died at the beacon-fort, think of that?"

Xiao Gui gave a scornful smile: "The Türks? They're not worthy of the word 'collude.' Those fools are no more than chessmen. I pushed them out to the front only to dig a great pit for the Khagan in passing, and hasten his dying a little." At this Xiao Gui suddenly heaved a long, long sigh: "When I was in Guangwu, it is true that for a private grudge of my own I wished every last soul dead. But what I am doing now has risen above such narrow hatreds."

"Eh?" Zhang Xiaojing knit his brows.

"After so long a fugitive in the Central Plains, and after knocking about the garrison-towns so many years, I found out at the last that the great Tang our Eighth Company swore to protect is sick. And who is it that lives in the garrison-towns? Merchants extorted into ruin; slaves and bondmaids abused into flight; farmers with their backs bent double under the rent-and-service tax; petty clerks bullied by their betters; and Hu foreigners without the money to return to their homelands... Do you know why so many follow me? They are all crack veteran soldiers — some from the Assault-Resisting Garrisons, some from the Protectorates, some even risen through the military examinations. Almost every one of them has the same story as I: after giving the court everything, they found in the end that the very people they had guarded had stabbed them in the back."

Xiao Gui's eyes grew fierce and glittering in the dark: "For one man to meet with such a fate — that, perhaps, is only ill luck. For five men to meet with it — that, one might say, is only the work of a few villains. But when a hundred, five hundred men all meet with the like of it, it shows that this court is sick! Sick past all cure! Look out over it, and there is nothing but the face of a flourishing age, all song and dance and peace — yet at the root it has already rotted through. It wants fire and blood to wash it clean, and to startle all men awake."

Zhang Xiaojing stared at this comrade of former days, and wondered whether he had not gone mad.

Xiao Gui spoke on, more and more worked up: "This is a mission the Shouzhuolang could never take up; they think only of dragging out their lives. So I have hastened from place to place, gathering up these veterans who have suffered such injustice. We are like so many Pifu — a single one is beneath notice, but massed together we have the force to shake the whole board!"

"What in the end... is it you mean to do?"

Xiao Gui threw back his head and cried aloud toward the roof of the vault: "I mean to make those great personages taste the strength of the Pifu, and to teach them that not every insect and ant may be trampled at will. I have not broken our Eighth Company's oath; I am still loyal to this great Tang — only that my way of serving it is a different one. I am a Pifu; I am the bitter medicine that heals."

As he said it he swept his arm hard through the dark, as though performing for the people up above ground. Zhang Xiaojing growled low: "To burn the city of Chang'an to ashes, to bring harm upon innocent folk — this is your way of serving?"

Xiao Gui suddenly burst out laughing: "No, no — to burn the city of Chang'an to ashes, that is the Türks' ambition; I could never take on so great a theme. My aim is but this one tower." His finger drew a circle in the air. "Only this Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower."

"Do you know what this tower cost to build? A full four million strings of cash! And all for three days of lamplight and the Son of Heaven's flourishing-age face. You cannot know how many extra taxes and labor-levies had to be wrung from every region for this tower — how many were ruined and beggared, their houses broken and their families destroyed, for it! So I mean to turn it into the brightest, most extravagant torch in all Chang'an, and let every man see just how the court of the great Tang burns away its money."

As he talked on, Xiao Gui had risen to his feet once more, and pressing himself forward against the crossbow-head, came walking on. Zhang Xiaojing dared neither pull the trigger nor draw the weapon away, and was forced to give ground step by step, until soon his back struck the door-frame with a thud. To see the force of the two of them, one would have thought it was Xiao Gui who held the weapon.

The tip of Xiao Gui's nose all but touched Zhang Xiaojing's face: "Do you know why, having lain hidden nine years, I have waited until this very day to make my move? It is because of you and Wen Wuji, no less..."

The corner of Zhang Xiaojing's eye twitched; he did not know why he should say such a thing.

"I have eyes and ears planted in the city of Chang'an too, and I knew of the wretched business at the Wen incense-shop. From that time on I hastened the making-ready of my plan, the better to win back some justice for the two of you. It happened that the Türks' Khagan had a mind to take his revenge on the great Tang, and had reached out to the Shouzhuolang. The Shouzhuolang had never dared set themselves against the government, and refused. So I sought out the Turkic Khagan of my own accord, and, borrowing their hands, laid this stratagem."

Only now did Zhang Xiaojing understand how the Türks had come to know the use of the fierce-fire thunder. Xiao Gui, in those years at the beacon-fort, had been the foremost expert on the fierce-fire thunder of them all. To think that the crisis he had run himself ragged over this whole day should, traced back to its very root, have sprung from his own self — in that instant Zhang Xiaojing seemed to hear Fate itself sniggering in his ear.

Xiao Gui stepped back half a pace, letting his overbearing air soften a little, and his tone grew gentler: "Think it over carefully. What is it that stands nearest the lantern-tower? It is the Qinzheng Wuben Tower of the Xingqing Palace, and up in it are the feasting Son of Heaven and all his civil and military officials. When the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower goes up, the only ones to suffer for it will be these very grubs that have done you such harm — how about it, Big-Head? Come and help me?"

At those words Zhang Xiaojing's whole body went rigid in an instant. This was a phrase he had heard countless times at the beacon-fort; unheard for years, it now carried an altogether different meaning.

What frightened Zhang Xiaojing more was not how terrible Xiao Gui's plot might be, but that he found he could not lay hold of a single reason to refuse.

Zhang Xiaojing had held a hatred for the court to begin with; the men who had done Wen Wuji to death still went at large to this day. If he had agreed to Li Bi's charge to run this matter down, it was wholly out of thought for the common folk of the whole city. But now his old comrade had said it: the Que-le Huo-duo was aimed only at these princes and great ministers — the very thing to avenge his wrongs and wipe them clean, without harm to the innocent, and then to let the Türks bear the consequences. How perfect it was.

And what was more, now even the Jing'an Bureau was no more. Li Bi, Tanqi, Yao Runeng, Xu Bin, Yisi — these people had either vanished no one knew where, or were sunk in prison; everyone connected with him had been cast out, or held in suspicion, and there was no longer a single soul to stand by him.

He could not find a reason to refuse, nor could he find a single reason to hold out any longer.

Zhang Xiaojing closed his eyes, and the crossbow fell clattering to the ground. He repented of having agreed to Li Bi's request; had he known, he would have done better to stay honestly in the death cells and spared himself the trouble. Xiao Gui gazed at his old comrade, and did not press him with more questions, but stepped back a pace and let him wage his own war between heaven and man.

After a long while Zhang Xiaojing slowly opened his eyes, and his tone was somewhat dry and cracked: "I'm in."

Xiao Gui's eyes lit up: "Good! That one word was all I waited for! We brothers-in-arms of the Eighth Company are gathered together again this time." In his excitement he threw his arms about Zhang Xiaojing, and laughed as heartily as in the days at the beacon-fort: "Zhang Big-Head — you and I will join hands and work one more miracle."

Zhang Xiaojing stood stiff and let him clap him on the shoulder, but his face stayed drawn taut the whole time, without a trace of a smile in its every crease.

Xiao Gui bent and picked up the crossbow, tossed it back to Zhang Xiaojing without the least misgiving, and made a gesture for him to follow. The two left the water-power hall, went up a narrow flight of steps some twenty paces, threw back a wooden cover, and so came out into the lowest story of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower.

What stands high must have a thick foundation. The whole Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower rose above a hundred and fifty chi, and even built all of bamboo, its total weight was very considerable, and it needed a solid earthen root to hold it down. So Mao Shun had simply made the lowest story of the tower into a broad Mystic Abbey of flying eaves, more than twenty bays across, its eaves all wrought in the shape of clouds — so that from afar it looked as if auspicious clouds bore up the lantern-tower, and it seemed the more like a thing of the immortals.

Climbing up from the water-power hall, they came out just into the rear hall of this auspicious-cloud Abbey. The hall was heaped now with qilin-arms unloaded from the carts, and a dozen or more men were bent to their busy work. They saw Xiao Gui come in but did not stay their hands, only went on with their tasks, heads down, in good order. As for Zhang Xiaojing, they did not spare him so much as a straight look.

The Longwu Army outside, it may be feared, did not yet know that the Pifu had quietly taken the whole great lantern-tower into their hands. It was no longer a wonder that would bring glory upon Chang'an, but a lethal engine of murder such as had never been seen before.

Where there is an abbey there must be tripods. In the very center of the rear hall of the Mystic Abbey stood eight small tripods, set out by the positions of the Eight Trigrams. They had been meant for ornament, but were now put to use as heating-vessels. In each tripod were laid some tens of qilin-arms. Charcoal fire burned beneath the tripods, and men kept taking up a small ice-phial and thrusting it into the bamboo tubes.

Without any telling, Zhang Xiaojing guessed it at once: this was the Que-le Huo-duo he had hunted so bitterly and so long, and here the last stage of the heating was being carried out. That ice-phial was in truth a slender-necked glass vial, shaped like an awl, with a column of ice set within it and a scale of marks upon the outside. Thrust into a bamboo tube, it let one judge by the speed at which the ice-column melted whether the rock-oil had yet reached the required heat.

Zhang Xiaojing had not thought they would have even such an instrument as this made ready. Xiao Gui marked the look in his eye: "This is what the Daoists use for firing their elixirs; I stole the trick of it. The fierce-fire thunder is hard to tame; keep the heat ill-controlled, and in one careless instant it goes off." Full of eager spirits, he stretched out his arm again to point beneath the tripods: "And do you know where this charcoal comes from?"

Zhang Xiaojing glanced at it; the charcoal was snow-white in color, and gave only firelight, without any smoke. Xiao Gui said: "This is burned by an old charcoal-seller up on the Southern Mountains. The charcoal that old man burns is snow-white as silver, full of heat, and with hardly a trace of smoke. Every year he used to haul a few cartloads into the city to sell; but the palace buyers would often take half a bolt of red gauze and a zhang of damask and carry off a whole cart of it by force — a thousand jin and more, mind you. So when the old man heard we meant to do a great thing, he came of his own will to help us burn it, and would not take a copper. Which only shows that this great thing we mean to do is truly what the hearts of the people yearn for."

Zhang Xiaojing kept a silence, only staring, lost in thought, at that charcoal fire. Xiao Gui said: "There, there — I know your mind won't come round all in a moment. Let's first go and pay a call on Deputy Director Li."

He led Zhang Xiaojing up to the second floor of the Mystic Abbey, which was partitioned into several Numinous-Officer chapels, all meant for the offering of prayers to suit the season, and so very plainly furnished within. Men came and went without cease, carrying out the qilin-arms that had been heated to the mark, and bearing them up into the lantern-tower by the passage here for their final setting in place.

Xiao Gui pushed open the door of one of the chapels; Zhang Xiaojing looked, and there stood a man within, straight-bodied and sword-browed — none other than Li Bi. He too had been smuggled into the lantern-tower; he looked utterly spent and dispirited, yet still struggled to keep up a last shred of dignity.

"Deputy Director Li, look and see who has come to call on you." Xiao Gui called out warmly, and threw an arm about Zhang Xiaojing's shoulders.

Hearing this, Li Bi looked their way; first he was struck dumb, then his two brows shot up at once, and he sneered again and again: "Good! Good!"

Zhang Xiaojing's face was without expression; he neither shrank away nor made excuse, but only stared at Li Bi, unmoving. Xiao Gui said with a smiling, narrow-eyed look: "Now here's a fine coincidence — who would have thought the Commander of the Jing'an Bureau should turn out to be an old comrade of mine from years back? At the beacon-fort, it was the two of us who rolled out alive from the heap of the dead."

"Eh?" Li Bi started.

"Just so. In all, three men of the Eighth Company came out alive, and in those days I still went by the name Xiao Gui. Oh — and there was one other survivor besides, called Wen Wuji. And where he is now, I think the Deputy Director knows well enough."

With Li Bi's wit he saw the whole chain of cause and effect at once. The look he turned on Zhang Xiaojing grew cold beyond all measure, and yet within that cold there was, too, a faint tincture of despair.

A brother-in-arms who had gone through life and death with him, or an organization that had again and again crushed and mistrusted him — which side Zhang Xiaojing would choose scarcely needed the saying.

Zhang Xiaojing avoided Li Bi's eye, lifted his arm, and flicked his finger lightly at the hollow socket of his eye. This was no unconscious habit, but done to make the moment a shade less awkward. Xiao Gui looked at Li Bi, then at Zhang Xiaojing, and grinned: "Deputy Director Li has a discerning eye for a pearl — at one glance he picked out this brother of mine. Had I not had a little luck on my side, he might truly have wrecked the whole thing! A pity only that you were fools, and could not carry your trust through to the end."

Li Bi said not a word. Xiao Gui pressed his own crossbow into Zhang Xiaojing's hand and said lightly: "Big-Head — to mark our reunion, how about pinning on a sprig of dogwood?"

"Pin on the dogwood?" At the phrase Zhang Xiaojing's face changed. This was no folk custom of wearing dogwood at the Double Ninth, but a byword of the armies of the Western Regions. The dogwood berry, when ripe, turns a purplish red; and "to pin on the dogwood" meant to draw blood.

Xiao Gui, brimming with smiles, jerked his chin toward Li Bi.

His meaning was plain enough. Half a shichen ago Zhang Xiaojing had still been the enemy Commander of the Jing'an Bureau; now that he had crossed over, to make men believe it he must hand in a pledge of blood-guilt — and the head of Li Bi, Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau, would serve for that better than anything else.

To kill his own superior would cut off any road of return for good and all; only so would he truly win the trust of the Pifu.

Xiao Gui watched Zhang Xiaojing, a smile on his face but a few glints of ill-will flickering in his eyes. Whether this brother, to whom he had entrusted his life and death, could be trusted on still — all hung on how this problem was solved. The several guards at his side glared like tigers, ready at any instant to draw and turn their blades against him.

The Numinous-Officer chapel fell silent for a time. Li Bi raised his head and simply stared at Zhang Xiaojing, neither pleading nor upbraiding. Zhang Xiaojing did not stir either; he stood silent and stiff, facing Li Bi, his one eye narrowed a little, so that no bystander could pierce through to what passed in his heart just then.

Seeing him so slow to act, the guards slowly felt toward their waists. Then, with a click, Zhang Xiaojing raised his right arm and set the crossbow to Li Bi's temple, his finger hooked tight about the trigger.

"Deputy Director Li, I am very sorry; I too have no choice." Zhang Xiaojing said it in a steady, level voice, without the least ripple in it.

"Let the greater cause weigh heaviest — where is the crime in that?" Li Bi closed his eyes. He smiled bitterly within: he had never thought that their talk at the Cibei Temple, of "killing one to save a hundred," would come true only a few shichen later. Still less had he thought that he himself would be the innocent one pushed forward to be offered up to the river-god.

Zhang Xiaojing, without expression, squeezed the trigger without a moment's hesitation.

With a soft thud, Li Bi's head jerked violently the other way as though smitten by a great hammer, and his whole body toppled to the ground in a ludicrous posture, and lay unmoving.

And so the Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau was shot dead, by the Commander of the Jing'an Bureau's own hand, within the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower.

Zhang Xiaojing lowered the crossbow and closed his eyes, knowing that from this moment on there would be no road of return left him. To save Chang'an he did not repent of the choice; and yet, for all that, it was wrong. Every wrong thing that ought to be done weighed his heart's burden a degree heavier.

The room was for a while utterly still; then all at once Zhang Xiaojing opened his eyes, feeling something was not quite right.

No — this was not how a bolt driven through the brain ought to leave a man. He looked at the crossbow in his hand, then cast his eyes toward Li Bi, fallen on the ground, and found on his temple a ring of purple-black bruised blood. Zhang Xiaojing's gaze swept to the floor, and his pupil could not help contracting.

The bolt he had loosed had, of all things, no head to it.

The shaft of a hand-crossbow bolt is not like that of a bow-arrow: its head is pared round, broad in front and narrow behind, because the hand-crossbow is generally used for cramped, twisting close-quarters work, where the stress is on force amid rough jolting. The bolt before him had no pointed iron head, but only an oval wooden knob. Struck against a man, the thing would give a terrible pain, yet it would deal only a blunt bruise, and could not kill.

Zhang Xiaojing looked in doubt toward Xiao Gui. Xiao Gui clapped his hands, his whole face aglow with a delighted smile: "Big-Head, my congratulations — you have passed the test."

"What is the meaning of this?"

"I had no doubt of you, Big-Head; but one must give the men under me their answer all the same." Xiao Gui bent and picked up the bolt-shaft. "I had thought you would waver — I never dreamed you would kill your own superior with so little mercy in your hand. My admiration, my admiration."

The last shred of his doubt of Zhang Xiaojing was gone at last. Whether a man had truly roused the will to kill could not be hidden from his eyes. The look in Zhang Xiaojing's eye just now, as he squeezed the trigger, had been brimming beyond all question with the intent to kill.

Zhang Xiaojing breathed lightly, his right hand trembling: "You took the head off the bolt before ever you gave me the crossbow?" Xiao Gui laughed: "That you could squeeze the trigger was proof enough of your heart; there was no need to truly take Deputy Director Li's dog-life. He has another use besides, and cannot die here for the present."

Just then Li Bi, coughing, tried to raise himself upright, but that blow just now had been too painful by far; his head still swam, his face was drawn in an extremity of pain, and blood ran from his nostrils. Xiao Gui lifted him by the hair: "Deputy Director Li — thank you for finding me back a good brother."

"Zhang Xiaojing!"

A great shout rang through the whole Numinous-Officer chapel. Li Bi, the blood trailing from his nose, had never been so angry in his life: "Am I still Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau, or am I not? Are you still Commander, or are you not?"

"You are." Zhang Xiaojing answered respectfully.

"The order I gave you was to stop the plot of the Pifu! I never once said your commander's life was to be spared! Is that not so?"

"It is so."

"That you kill me, this officer, matters nothing — but you are to save this city of Chang'an! The chief culprit stands right beside you; why do you not act?"

Xiao Gui gave a snort of derision down his nose — had that blow knocked Li Bi's wits astray? To be talking like an official at a time like this! Zhang Xiaojing walked slowly over, drew out the bronze tablet at his own waist, and, with all respect, tucked it back into Li Bi's belt:

"Deputy Director Li, I now resign to you my post of Commander. The man before you is no longer Commander Zhang of the Jing'an Bureau, but Zhang Big-Head who fought and bled with the Eighth Company; the buliang chief who cut down a county commandant and was flung into the death cells; the spy seized by the Right Xiao Guard; the condemned convict wanted through the whole city; an old soldier come to claim a reckoning of Chang'an!"

With each identity he named his voice rose a degree, until at the last he was all but roaring it out.

Li Bi's face went iron-gray. The reason for Zhang Xiaojing's imprisonment, and all he had gone through in these few shichen, he knew perfectly well, and he understood too what pressure and what wrong the man had had to bear. Now the resentment Zhang Xiaojing had stored up so long had burst forth at last, and that heaven-flooding, savage force came surging over Li Bi, till he could scarcely keep his eyes open before it.

And the trouble was, there was nothing he could say against it.

Having spat out these words, Zhang Xiaojing's shoulders sank, as if he had set down a burden of a thousand jin. Xiao Gui, at his side, smiled with satisfaction. As he saw it, Zhang Xiaojing's earlier conduct had been nothing but a knot he had tied for himself: his belly full of resentment against the court, and yet he must go running about for the sake of some empty name of righteousness — far too tangled a way to live.

Now that Zhang Big-Head had unburdened himself of his former scruples in one breath, and had, moreover, truly roused the will to kill against his own superior, Xiao Gui was at last set at ease. He clenched his right fist and struck it hard against his left shoulder; Zhang Xiaojing made the same movement, and the two said with one voice: "Nine deaths, no regret."

In that instant the glory of the Eighth Company seemed to come back before the eyes of them both. In the rims of Xiao Gui's eyes there welled up a little moisture.

Just then Li Bi forced himself to speak: "Zhang Xiaojing — you promised me you would take the culprit; do you mean, then, to eat your word?"

"No. What I answered then was: the man was yours to choose, the road was mine to pick, and each of us must answer for his own choosing."

Hearing this, Li Bi could not help a bitter smile: "You are quite right. I read you wrong, and I should bear the consequences of my own folly."

Zhang Xiaojing said: "You are not fit for this post of Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau; you would do far better to go back to your Daoism. Bow to the Three Pure Ones, pray to the Eleven Luminaries, cast the Eight Trigrams of your natal chart, make your pilgrimage to the Four Peaks and the Five Marchmounts — anything at all would be better than the Jing'an Bureau. Though should the Deputy Director wish to seek his revenge on me, I fear he would have to go down to the eighteen hells to do it."

Xiao Gui laughed aloud: "Well said. Men like us must surely go down to hell when we die, and no other place will suit. Big-Head, that name of yours, the Five Yamas — who knows whether it will serve you for anything when the time comes."

"I have said all I have to say; I beg young Master Li to weigh it well." Zhang Xiaojing made him a cupped-hand bow.

To call him "young master" meant that Zhang Xiaojing had utterly given up his Jing'an Bureau standing, and that the affairs of Chang'an were no longer any concern of his. At that form of address Li Bi gave over at last his effort to persuade, and hung his head in silence.

Xiao Gui gave orders to loose Li Bi from the pillar and set two guards to march him along behind, then beckoned Zhang Xiaojing to go on up into the lantern-tower.

"How is it that he goes too?" Zhang Xiaojing was decidedly ill at ease.

Xiao Gui said: "Did I not say just now? He has another use."

Only now did Zhang Xiaojing recall a point of doubt from before. When the Pifu stormed the great hall of the Jing'an Bureau, why had they gone to such pains to carry Li Bi off? To keep him alive must serve some use — but what in the end was that use?

Xiao Gui saw Zhang Xiaojing's doubt, laughed aloud, and said: Come, I'll take you to see a thing, and you'll understand.

The party filed out of the Numinous-Officer chapel one behind another. No sooner had Zhang Xiaojing stepped over the threshold than Xiao Gui's face changed all at once, and he shot out a kick at the small of Zhang Xiaojing's back. Zhang Xiaojing had not looked for him to strike so suddenly, and went down on the instant. And in the very instant of his falling, a cold gleam of light swept just barely past his scalp.

Yuan Zai was just now caught in a vast quandary. Half-leaning against a pagoda-tree, he stared at that blood-drenched gate and for a long while could not find his voice.

That killing-god had slipped away right under his nose, and had frightened the very wits and water out of him besides. Yet the words the man had spoken before he left weighed much on Yuan Zai's mind.

"If you have half a grain of sense left among you, get you to the front of the Xingqing Palace as fast as you can — the Pifu are all gathered there."

Was it a snare, or was it the plain truth? Yuan Zai did not know. If it were false — why, Zhang Xiaojing had no need at all to tell such a lie; and yet if it were true, would Zhang Xiaojing be so kindly disposed as to hand a clue, unasked, to the very men who were hunting him? That Yuan Zai could not believe.

He who had always prided himself on the keenness of his eye was, before this riddle of Zhang Xiaojing, at a loss after all.

He half wished he could simply find a chrysanthemum, pluck it petal by petal, and let Heaven decide for him.

Just then the Lüben Guard corporal at his side edged over and said in a low voice: "Shall we charge in and take them?"

Just now they had caught an apprentice fleeing from the yard, and had questioned him until they had the master of the house clear: he was called Chao Fen, and behind him stood the Japanese, Chao Heng. Within the yard there seemed, moreover, to be a gravely wounded Persian. Zhang Xiaojing had come running here on purpose; beyond doubt he was in league with them, and to seize them could never be wrong.

The Lüben Guard had at the least a dozen and more men laid out in this courtyard — an unheard-of, disastrous loss — and they were hot for revenge.

To this proposal Yuan Zai shook his head. He cared nothing for the Lüben Guard's face, nor did he fear Chao Heng; only he felt this business was not so simple as it seemed.

His subordinates did not know it, but Yuan Zai knew it perfectly well within: Zhang Xiaojing was no traitor within the gates; that charge had been trumped up only so that someone might conveniently carry the black pot. To use it for hounding a man was well enough, but to truly believe the conclusion and reason out the case by it would be to drive south while meaning to go north.

To drive south while meaning to go north?

Yuan Zai of a sudden slapped the trunk of the pagoda-tree hard, his eyes lit up, and in a flash his decision was made.

"Form up the ranks — to the Xingqing Palace!"

The Lüben Guard corporal was taken aback, thinking he had misheard the order.

"To the Xingqing Palace!" Yuan Zai repeated it, in a tone hard as chopped nails and sliced iron.

He did not know whether Zhang Xiaojing's words were true; but an instinct born in him told Yuan Zai that the greater uncertainties lay over at the Xingqing Palace.

Great uncertainties meant risk, and risk meant opportunity.

Yuan Zai believed that this night's luck had not yet wholly left him, and that it was worth a gamble.

In the instant Zhang Xiaojing hit the ground, Xiao Gui let out a furious roar: "Yuchang! What do you think you are doing?!"

Outside the Numinous-Officer chapel a black shadow came slowly to a stand, a narrow-bladed Yuchang short-sword in its right hand, the left hand hanging down. Only now did Zhang Xiaojing understand that Xiao Gui had kicked him aside to make him dodge that killing stroke. His mind was clouded now, his keenness dulled; had Xiao Gui not acted, he might well have died, he knew not how, beneath the Yuchang blade.

"I have said it before: I will take Zhang Xiaojing's life with my own hand." Yuchang said it in a hoarse voice, cold and grim.

Xiao Gui put himself between them, to keep Yuchang from striking again: "Zhang Xiaojing is one of us now; you need be his enemy no longer."

"And how do you know his surrender is not feigned?"

"That is for me to judge!" Xiao Gui said angrily. "And even were his surrender feigned — with our own men all around us now, what is there to fear?"

This explanation did not make Yuchang give an inch: "He humiliated me; he broke my left arm; he must die." Xiao Gui could only press it again, his words stern: "I say it once more — he is one of us now, and all the old scores between you are struck off in a single stroke!"

Yuchang shook his head: "It has nothing to do with which side he is on. I want only that he die."

Outside the Numinous-Officer chapel the air turned all at once very strange and uncanny. Scarcely had Zhang Xiaojing changed sides than he faced a feud within the ranks.

"This is the ninth thing I require of you! You are not to touch him!" Xiao Gui all but roared it out; he flicked up the hem of his gown and took up a string of red cord, and on that red cord were two copper coins. He slipped one off and flung it across. Yuchang caught the coin in mid-air, and his voice held no little astonishment: "For the sake of one enemy — you would spend this?"

"Have you heard me clearly? You are not to touch him." Xiao Gui said.

"Very well; but mark this — the bond holds no longer once you have spent your last copper coin," Yuchang stressed. "The day I have done the last thing for you, that is the day he dies."

Zhang Xiaojing took a step forward: "Yuchang, I give you my promise: when this business here is done, you and I will fight one fair duel, and let life or death fall as it may." Yuchang stared into Zhang Xiaojing's eyes: "And how am I to know you will keep your word?"

"You have no choice but to trust it."

Yuchang was silent a moment; likely he too judged that his chance of striking here was slight, and he nodded at last: "Good."

Yuchang's figure was soon lost in the dark, and then left behind a single sentence that drifted from no one knew where: "If you break your word, then I will go and kill Wen Ran."

Xiao Gui knit his brows, and turned to Zhang Xiaojing full of apology: "Big-Head, that scoundrel Yuchang is not like the others — he'll take a commission, but he won't be summoned to heel. When the great work is done, I will deal with this matter, and never leave you in a hard place over it."

Zhang Xiaojing said, without a flicker on his face: "I can look after myself; Wen Wuji's daughter cannot." Xiao Gui said savagely: "Let him dare lay a hand on Wen Ran, and I will settle him myself!"

As they climbed the steps up from the Numinous-Officer chapel, Xiao Gui told, in brief, along the way, the tale of where Yuchang had come from.

Yuchang had grown up from childhood in a garrison-town near Lingwu. No one knew his origins or his stock; they knew only that whoever gave Yuchang offense would, the next day, lie a corpse exposed in the wilds, with a single, exceedingly narrow wound at the throat. The local Shouzhuolang had meant at first to take Yuchang into their own service, but soon found the fellow far too hard to control, and set about doing away with him instead. But Yuchang struck first, assassinating several high officers of the Shouzhuolang one after another, so that even their chief narrowly escaped disaster. The upper ranks of the Shouzhuolang, in a fury, cast a great net to hunt him down. Yuchang was beset to the point of death, and it was only Xiao Gui's rescue that saved his life.

Zhang Xiaojing thought to himself: no wonder Yuchang had played the Shouzhuolang's fire-master so expertly — the two had a bond of long standing after all. If the Shouzhuolang knew that the assassin they had so nearly caught was none other than Yuchang, the matter would likely be far from simple.

Xiao Gui went on. After Yuchang was saved, he did not weep out his gratitude, but gave Xiao Gui ten copper coins strung together on a cord, saying that he would do ten things for the Pifu, and then the two of them would be quits. This was why Xiao Gui said he would take a commission but not be summoned to heel, and was not easily controlled.

By now Xiao Gui had spent nine of the coins, and only the last copper coin remained.

"Truly I am sorry to have made you waste one for nothing."

Xiao Gui said: "It's no matter — how can that count as waste? Besides, I have but one thing left that I need to ask Yuchang to do. Once it is over, I'll have no more use for him..." He ground his teeth, showing a cruel little smile, then in a flash put on a look of concern instead:

"Big-Head — the road ahead, now, you'll want to tread with care."

Zhang Xiaojing looked, and saw that above the Numinous-Officer chapel was the topmost loft of the Mystic Abbey. Above that loft, they passed formally into the base of the main body of the lantern-tower. The sight before them made both Zhang Xiaojing and Li Bi hold their breath despite themselves.

Above his head was a vast, magnificent dome, as intricate as a spider's nest. The whole Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower was framed on a skeleton of thick bamboo and timber beams crossing this way and that, and clothed without in figured silks, colored damask, and bamboo paper. Its inner space was astonishing in its size; thick pine boards were laid across between the beam-frames, lapping one over another, close-ranked as fish-scales, forming a series of none-too-trustworthy suspended bridges that spiraled upward. Nearby there hung down, besides, many ropes, pivots, and wheels, their uses unclear — mysteries that likely only a master such as Mao Shun or Chao Fen could read.

Treading the suspended bridges span by span, they wound their way upward, climbing to a height of seventy chi and more. All at once a night wind blew through the skeleton of the lantern-tower, and Zhang Xiaojing could feel the whole tower sway faintly, and give out a creaking, groaning sound.

The night wind lifted a patch of the outer covering, and Zhang Xiaojing looked out to the north through the gap, and found the Qinzheng Wuben Tower close at hand. He had known the two were no great distance apart, but had never thought them so near as this. One had only to throw across a well-rope of a ten-odd chi to join the two towers together.

From this distance Zhang Xiaojing's single eye could see clearly every detail of the banquet in the tower: the square caps on the guests' heads, the golden, crisp-roasted lamb upon the tables, the passing of wine-cups back and forth, and countless robes and skirts of gorgeous color flashing among them. Some, flushed with wine, had left their seats to stand at the balustrade, pointing this way toward the lantern-tower.

"Every one of them is waiting for the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower to light up — it will be a grand marvel such as no age has ever seen. I'll wager ten strings of cash that they've a good store of verses pent up in their bellies, just waiting to chant them out when the candles are lit."

Xiao Gui tossed off the jest and strode on. Zhang Xiaojing drew back his gaze, and noticed of a sudden that Li Bi's face was not good. His arms were bound fast, a stout man gripping him on either side; to walk the tottering suspended bridges in such a state, he could hardly keep his balance, and might at any moment fall.

He made to reach out and steady him, but Xiao Gui reassured him: "Don't fret, no harm will come to him. We've not gone to all this trouble to get Deputy Director Li up so high just to push him off and hear the thud." At this Xiao Gui raised his right hand high, then let it fall of a sudden, mimicking the sound with his mouth besides: "Wheeee — smack!"

The party climbed some tens of chi higher, and reached at last the central hub of the whole lantern-tower — the Tianshu tier.

This tier was a broad, ring-shaped space, its floor in fact one enormous wooden wheel laid flat, the face of it near as large as a drill-ground. At the very center of the bamboo wheel rose a great bamboo Tianshu, joined to the other parts, pieced together of timber and bamboo mixed, its larger gaps inlaid besides with iron corner-braces and bronze rings.

Many Pifu craftsmen were clinging to the framework, hacking with knife and axe all around this great wheel, changing out the qilin-arms. Beside each of them burned a small oil lamp, so that from afar they showed as scattered points of light, as though this great wheel were set all over with jewels.

Zhang Xiaojing could make nothing of it. But Li Bi, lifting his head to look, saw round about four or five lantern-chambers jutting out in outline, and understood in a flash.

This Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower, in its basic structure, was the same as that trial-piece Xiao Gui had shown him: a great hub-wheel at the center, a ring of separate small units all around, which, as the hub-wheel turned, would circle round and round in mid-air. The difference was that the trial-piece had used twelve paper-pasted compartments, whereas around this Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower there were twenty-four great lantern-chambers, open on all four sides, each with its own scene and theme within, and a branch-axle let into it, by which the lantern-figures could be driven to move of themselves.

One could imagine what an astonishing, splendid sight it would be when the whole tower was lit and the great wheel, reaching to the very sky, turned slowly, and these twenty-four lantern-chambers rose and fell and swayed in mid-air. The lantern-loving folk of Chang'an, seeing all this, would likely go mad with excitement.

A hunch-backed old man was crouched before the Tianshu, unmoving, now and again reaching out a hand to stroke it, as though he caressed a child of his own soon to die.

Xiao Gui went over and clapped him on the shoulder: "Master Mao, how do the preparations stand?" Mao Shun did not so much as lift his head: "Only let the turning-gear below engage with the water-wheels, and this main axle will turn, and set all twenty-four lantern-chambers revolving one after another." His humor was very ill; and no man's humor would be otherwise, learning that his masterpiece was to be blown to pieces.

Zhang Xiaojing started: "This is Mao Shun? Is he too one of your Pifu?" Xiao Gui said: "We thirst for talent, to be sure — but the master plainly sets more store by his own family." Zhang Xiaojing fell silent; most likely the Pifu had seized Mao Shun's household and forced him to work with them.

No wonder the Pifu had gotten in so smoothly; with Mao Shun to vouch for them, the whole way would of course lie open.

"What is it, in the end, that you mean to do?" Zhang Xiaojing asked at last, unable to hold back.

Xiao Gui seemed to have been waiting all along for the question. A man who has schemed out some astonishing thing with painstaking single-mindedness longs, whatever else, to show it off to someone. He pointed at that huge Tianshu, and, full of eager spirits, began to explain.

It turned out that this all-important great pillar of the Tianshu had already been filled full with rock-oil. In the twenty-four lantern-chambers around it, great numbers of rock-oil column-tubes had long since been set in place. Once the tower began to work, the lantern-chambers would take fire one after another. Those watching the lanterns would surely mistake it for a mere effect of the lamplight, and never suspect. When all twenty-four lantern-chambers were ablaze, the heat would be carried to the great central pillar of the Tianshu — and within that pillar was hidden the true, fully-mixed fierce-fire thunder. When the moment came and it burst, it might be called a sundering of heaven and earth. The Qinzheng Wuben Tower, so near at hand, would surely be scattered to ashes and smoke.

Zhang Xiaojing, having heard the whole account, could not find words for a long while. So this was the true face of the Que-le Huo-duo: it had never lurked or hidden itself at all, but had stood thus, brazen and open, within the very city of Chang'an.

What imagination, what obsession, must it take to bring such a thing to pass?

Xiao Gui was well pleased with Zhang Xiaojing's reaction; he threw back his head and said with feeling: "All this vast trouble is just to have a Son of Heaven, in the very instant of his greatest joy and highest pride, destroyed by the thing he loves best. That, now — that is the most meaningful revenge of all."

Zhang Xiaojing looked at this old comrade, and would have opened his mouth to say something, but in the end closed it again in silence.

"Oh — and before that, there is one thing I must trouble Deputy Director Li with. You wait here a while." Xiao Gui had Zhang Xiaojing stay by the Tianshu and talk a while longer with Master Mao, then hauled Li Bi away.

Leaving the Tianshu tier, Xiao Gui brought Li Bi into one of the lantern-chambers on the outer ring of the tower. These chambers were each of a separate design, open on all four sides, the better to be viewed from different quarters, and joined to the main body of the tower by a narrow passage.

The lantern-chamber Xiao Gui and Li Bi came to had for its theme "Tangdi," which told of brotherly love — of the elder brother's kindness and the younger's respect — and within it stood several lantern-figures, Zhao Xiao and Zhao Li among them. Xiao Gui pushed Li Bi in, and pushed him on until he was at the very edge of the chamber, his feet all but treading on empty air, before he stopped.

Li Bi looked down: he could not make out the ground beneath his feet at all — it was, at the least, a height of some tens of chi. With his hands bound, on this swaying, tottering tower, he could balance only by his legs, and it cost him dearly.

"Deputy Director Li, I've put you to much trouble." Xiao Gui split his mouth in a smile past all fathoming. He raised his hand and snapped his fingers.

Li Bi closed his eyes, thinking the other had some torment in store. But he waited a good while and nothing happened. Opening them again, he found that the two lantern-chambers adjoining the Tangdi chamber had each of them lit up.

The one chamber showed the Sage Confucius questioning Laozi, to set forth the way of civil governance; the other, Duke Li of Wei sweeping the Yin Mountains, to display the merit of martial might. As the lamps on either side were raised, they framed the Tangdi chamber exactly in the middle. The guests up on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, seeing lantern-chambers light up first, mistook it for the beginning of the show, and called to their friends to come and admire it together at the balustrade.

So it lasted some twenty finger-snaps; then Xiao Gui snapped his fingers again, and the candlelight in the two chambers went out together. The guests far off let out a sigh of disappointment, understanding only now that it had been a test.

"There — Deputy Director Li, your task is done." Xiao Gui hauled him back from the edge of the lantern-chamber. Li Bi, in the dark as to what it all meant, could only keep his silence.

When they had come back once more to the Tianshu, Xiao Gui summoned a guard and bade him march Li Bi down out of the lantern-tower and take him to the underground vault of the water-power hall; then he threw an arm affectionately about Zhang Xiaojing's shoulders and led him off to the far side of the Tianshu. From first to last, Li Bi and Zhang Xiaojing had not so much as the chance of a single glance at each other.

Li Bi, his hands bound behind him, was marched away from beside the Tianshu by that guard. Round and round the suspended bridges they wound their way down out of the tower, down into the Mystic Abbey, and down again into the vault below it. Those six huge water-wheels still turned, splashing, in the dark. Before long they would be linked up to Master Mao's mechanism, and bring the whole lantern-tower fully to life.

"Truly a workmanship to outdo Heaven's own." Li Bi, studying the great wheels, could not help the exclamation. Against all the splendor and extravagance of the tower above ground, it seemed to him that this part, hidden so deep beneath the earth, was where the real ingenuity lay.

The guard gave him a pitying glance — this official seemed still not to know his own fate, that he should have the leisure of mind to admire the view? He drew the knife at his waist: "Deputy Director Li, Master Long Bo bids me pass on a word: he respectfully sees the Deputy Director off to shed his mortal husk and ascend as an immortal."

Li Bi did not move — nor could he, his arms still bound fast behind him. But his look was calm and unruffled, as though he had long foreseen it.

The guard said with a savage grin: "It was just such a pretty-faced fellow as you who lured my wife away. Today you can pay the reckoning for that son of a rabbit, and I'll kill you as slowly as I can manage." His knife reached slowly toward Li Bi's chest, meaning first to carve off a strip of flesh over the heart.

All at once Li Bi moved. His two arms jerked violently, and the cords fell away at the shock. This young and frail-seeming official gripped a small iron file in his right hand and drove it savagely into the guard's temple. Taken suddenly by the attack, the guard lashed out with a reflexive kick and sent Li Bi sprawling into the corner of the wall.

This dying counter-blow had force and to spare, and Li Bi felt as if his very innards had been jolted apart, a thread of blood at the corner of his mouth. It was a long while before he could catch his breath, and then he struggled to his feet. The guard already lay on the ground, dead, the breath gone out of him; on his left temple only a short stub of the file's handle could be seen — that thrust just now had gone deep indeed.

With a clang, a bronze tablet fell from Li Bi's person to the ground. This was the waist-tablet Zhang Xiaojing had given back to him in the Numinous-Officer chapel just now, with the small iron file clipped inside it, tucked into his belt together with it. Apart from the two of them, no one had marked it.

Li Bi leaned against the earthen wall, rubbing his aching wrists, his heart a welter of feeling. Into his mind there rose again, unbidden, a certain abrupt speech of Zhang Xiaojing's:

"You are not fit for this post of Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau; you would do far better to go back to your Daoism. Bow to the Three Pure Ones, pray to the Eleven Luminaries, cast the Eight Trigrams of your natal chart, make your pilgrimage to the Four Peaks and the Five Marchmounts — anything at all would be better than the Jing'an Bureau. Though should the Deputy Director wish to seek his revenge on me, I fear he would have to go down to the eighteen hells to do it."

Zhang Xiaojing was no Daoist adept; the moment the words were out, Li Bi keenly sensed that some hidden device lay concealed within them. With his sharp wit, he had only to reason it out with care to know that the key of it lay in the numbers.

Three, eleven, eight, four, five, eighteen.

This was an ordering out of the Tang Rhymes, with which the people of the Jing'an Bureau were all thoroughly familiar. Three is a departing tone; rhyme eleven, dui; and there the eighth character is “退” (tui, “retreat”). Four is an entering tone; rhyme five, wu; and the character in place eighteen is “不” (bu, “not”).

Rendered out, it came to two characters.

This was Yao Runeng's resolve, and Tanqi's resolve, and the resolve, too, that Zhang Xiaojing had never once changed:

No retreat.
