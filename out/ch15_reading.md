## The Paulownia Crest

Maeda Gen'i, the Hōin, magistrate of Kyoto, polishes a hundred and eight walnuts every morning. He works the knees of his loose priest's robe wide apart, scatters the walnuts across them, and takes them up one at a time, the way a monkey plucks pebbles from a mountain brook, buffing each with a square of yellow cloth and setting it in its place on the sutra-desk. Every nut is well dried and blackened, its knobbled surface worn smooth, giving off the uncanny amber, half-translucent gloss of some strange jewel out of a barbarian land. This man had come up out of the priesthood, and he is versed in medicine besides. He knows perfectly well that rubbing walnuts wards off apoplexy. But when anyone asks, that is never the answer he gives.

"Making ready to die, you understand."

He lifts that ugly face, gnarled as one of his own walnuts, and smiles. Press him again, and he says:

"Beads for my coffin."

So he answers. Let it get about that a man spends his mornings at such work, and the world shows itself a simple creature. Gen'i-dono, being what his origins made him, keeps his mind afloat among the deep mysteries of life and death: so ran the reputation, unlooked-for, that people built up for him. It startled even Gen'i at first, but in time he half came to believe it of himself. The truth, needless to say, was nothing of the kind. This dogged labor of the fingers was no more than the outward shape of Gen'i's clinging, sticky hunger to hoard one more day of life. Maeda Gen'i knew the art of living at his ease in the hidden folds between those two spirits. He served Nobunaga first, then crossed to Hideyoshi; and some years after the time of this story, at the Battle of Sekigahara, he would make a show of holding with the Ishida camp while easing his weight toward Ieyasu before either army, east or west, had noticed. The walnut turning in his fingers was a fair emblem of the way the man lived.

That day, when he had polished about half of them, Gen'i looked up as though something had just occurred to him and called for Gohei. It was the morning after Tsuzura Jūzō had stolen into the residence. Gohei raised his handsome face before him, the eyes glinting now and then like a whetted blade. Setting one of the walnuts from his palm down on the desk with a small click, Gen'i said:

"Word came to me that Dōgen was found dead last night, by the pond at the Benten shrine. Did you know of it?"

"I—"

"And around the time of Dōgen's death, they tell me, there were figures moving busily about the grounds. Those would not be men of yours, I suppose."

"No, that— But suppose they had been my men. What then?"

"Then why set men loose about my grounds in the dead of night, without my leave?"

"With respect—"

Gohei, still bowed to the floor, lifted only his eyes a little.

"I am a man of Iga. And so long as my lord employs this one as a man of Iga, he must leave the man to the law of Iga. Once an Iga man is set to a task, he settles it by the law of Iga. Unlike your other samurai, he does not come to his lord for leave over every trifle."

"Very well. And so—did you kill that Kōga man?"

"No. The one who killed him was the intruder who broke into this house."

"What are you telling me?"

"Tsuzura Jūzō."

"The ringleader all along, then. And a man of your parts will have taken Jūzō, of course."

"Regrettably, no."

"Let him slip, did you. Well, well. 'Iga men,' one says, as though they were all of a kind, yet they seem to come in every grade. All those men set moving, and not one could lay a hand on a single intruder? Rather less than the boast, it would seem."

"You wrong me."

Gohei's pale face did not stir.

"There was a purpose in it. Seize him idly, and no ninja will ever open his mouth. And the man is as good as broken already: the wound I dealt him has half unseated his wits."

"And so? What came of it?"

"When I threw the kozuka he got clear of me for the moment. But later I caught sight of a shadow going over the moat, and I followed it in secret. And here is the surprising thing."

Gohei let a thin smile cross his face.

"Say it."

"I lost him around Komatsudani. Then a certain house drew my eye, and I judged he had likely slipped into it, so quietly I—"

"That would be the villa of Imai Sōkyū, of Sakai."

"What? My lord knew already?"

"Dōgen, dead now, had traced it that far. He kept digging at everything around Sōkyū of Sakai, but he could turn up no solid proof. In the end it came to this: there was no course left but to take an Iga man called Tsuzura Jūzō and make a living witness of him—and then, more's the pity, the fellow went and died. So. Did you get into the villa?"

"It was a blunder of mine."

"A blunder how?"

"I did not know the house was of such weight. Having once marked it, I meant to go back, put my arrangements in order, and set out again; and in the morning, when I returned and looked into it, I learned the place was Sōkyū-dono's villa."

"You worked it out at last, did you."

Gen'i said it to taunt him. Gohei could not so much as raise his face. He had been beaten by all of them—not the Kōga man Dōgen alone, but Gen'i, Jūzō, every one—and the wretchedness of it closed in on him. For a while he stayed bowed, shoulders low; then, from the pit of his belly, a pale anger he could not have said at whom came seething up, and—

"Though it cost me my own life, I will take Jūzō. I swear it."

"At the cost of your life? Fine words, for a jumped-up ninja. And in that heat—you don't mean to go breaking into Sōkyū's villa at Komatsudani, I trust?"

"Would you say I must not?"

"A poor move. Take Jūzō quietly, somewhere Sōkyū will never notice. You follow me?"

He understood. . . .

Gohei groaned inwardly. Whatever the Hōin Gen'i had meant by the words, Gohei believed, in his own way, that he could read the bottom of this man's heart.

For Maeda Gen'i, one of the five commissioners, to expose a plot by Sōkyū of Sakai was easier than pointing at his own palm. But Sōkyū, for all his mountains of money, was only a merchant, and no merchant of his station would dream of bringing down the ruler of the day. Behind him, without fail, some far greater figure lay hidden.

As Gen'i saw it, Hideyoshi's dominion rested on nothing but the man's own personal magnetism. Naturally, it would die as Hideyoshi's body failed. One who eyed the succession might be the adopted regent, Hidetsugu. But a shrewd old head like Sōkyū of Sakai, versed in the way things go, would never gamble his fortunes on a dull-witted youth in his twenties whose one claim was kinship with Hideyoshi. Look at it how you would, the shadow behind Sōkyū could be no one but Tokugawa Ieyasu, the Inner Minister. And if that was so, Gen'i had no wish to go worrying clumsily at the affair and put his own place in the coming age at risk.

Gen'i's pressing business was to lay hold of the assassin's weapon—the ninja himself—and learn all he could of what stood behind him. That done, he would draw up the design of the rest of his own life. Only, the thing had to be kept wholly secret; and for that, a man of Iga like Gohei was well suited to the taking. If Gohei bungled it, he was no more than a rappa kept on a throwaway pittance: the house need only disown any connection with him, and there was an end of it. Bowed to that same matting with his forehead pressed to the floor, Gohei had read the fine grain of the calculation to the full. Very well, he thought—he would let Gen'i use him, and use him to the hilt. But he did not miss the other road that had opened before him. It was this: he now knew that a man named Sōkyū existed.

(Money in this, he thought.)

Once Gohei had smirked over the thought, an elation rose in him that he could barely keep from showing to Gen'i. True enough, to wash himself clean of the rappa's lot he had once wanted a certain rank, a certain honor. He wanted them still. But gold, which had lately begun to move through the world, held for him a fiercer pull than any half-measure of warrior rank and stipend.

"I will take Jūzō. Depend on it."

So saying, Gohei withdrew from Gen'i's presence—though, in truth, Jūzō was for the moment the least of his cares. Leave the man be a while, and nothing would come of it either way. The first thing Gohei did was to write to Kisaru, who should still be at Shimotsuge in Iga. The moment he was back at the residence he shut himself in his room and, too impatient even to take up the brush cleanly, dashed off a letter. The small characters ranged down the page were, of course, all in the ninja script.

"Always, I have your happiness in my thoughts."

So Gohei began, at the head of the page.

"Soon, through a certain matter, I am to come into a great deal of gold. This is no lie. When it happens, you and I alike will be done with the despised lot of the rappa."

The characters danced on the page. He had not opened this way as a ruse, to hook the kunoichi with honeyed words; he had no such coolness left in him. It was, one might say, his own elation set down just as it stood. But from the next passage on, Gohei became the ninja again, and even his brushwork turned cold.

"On the night of the new moon in the sixth month, the back gate of the residence will be left open. Muster three genin of the Shimotsuge band at home, and come up to the capital in their company. The rest I will tell you at leisure once you are here."

He wrote so far, then tilted his head and let the brush hover in the air. Would this much on the page really bring the woman all the way to him—the woman he had all but thrown away? Such was his misgiving. Half in earnest, Gohei quietly added a line.

"Of late there are many days when I ache for you. Nor is it only I, I think; it must be the same for you. I long for the day—the sooner the better—when I may look on you as my wife, in my own house."

When he had finished, then—even granting the letter was a lie meant for the moment only—he thought of Kisaru's body with the violence of a thirst-maddened man baring his throat to a mirage of water. If Gohei ever began to love Kisaru, it may be that this instant was the first of it.

Gohei sent an express runner off to Iga at once, then made himself ready and slipped out of the capital in secret. He walked the seventeen ri in a single night, and by the time he reached the town of Sakai the next day the sun stood high.

As the ninja's rule prescribed, Gohei bought a woman at a pleasure-house, and over two days, in the pillow-talk with her, spent the time drawing out how things stood in Sakai—its gossip, its geography, the lie of it.

"And who else is there?"

Chin propped on one hand in the bed, Gohei listened to the talk of the Sakai merchants as though it barely touched his interest.

"If you were to count the top three on your fingers, it would have to be the Hōin of Minami-Zaimoku-chō."

"Sōkyū, you mean. Imai."

"Yes. Lately, they say, he has raised a stone stupa in his grounds, three jō high and of three tiers—quite the talk of the town."

"An odd thing to raise. And a great stupa like that—whose soul does he mean to pray for by it?"

"For the repose of Lord Oda Nobunaga, they say. That is all very well; but to go raising a stupa for the old lord out of the proper season for such rites, and in the Taikō's own day, only lays a man open to the suspicion of nursing some sulking grudge against the age—which is folly itself. The Tennōjiya of Kita-Zaimoku-chō and the rest of them, so I hear, pressed him hard to leave off."

"Grown homesick, I suppose, for the days of Lord Nobunaga, who was good to him."

"It's true. These days it's the Konishi riding their luck, and the Imai who look like a fire gone out. Ever since the Korea business started, they say Konishi has the gunsmiths of Sakura-no-chō all in one hand, and no war-money finds its way to Imai at all."

"That may well be. The Konishi—when you come down to it, they started in the very face-powder trade you women use; but now they deal in saltpetre and firearms and the like. The father, Ryūsa, holds the realm's coin as accounts-master of Ōsaka Castle, and the son, Yakurō, is Settsu-no-kami and vanguard general in the conquest of Ming. Call it the Toyotomi's age if you please—by the look of it, it may be the age of the Konishiya of Sakai. No wonder a man like Sōkyū envies them. Sōkyū had a son too, had he not?"

"That would be Sōkun-sama."

"That one holds neither rank nor office. Besotted with the tea ceremony, he is. Still, a father would want—even if he cannot reach so high as Konishi's Yakurō with his Settsu-no-kami—to see the boy set at least in some decent, profitable post. And the money? Is it Konishi first there as well?"

"Oh, hardly. For money it is Imai first still, I should say. Then Tsuda of the Tennōjiya. Konishi comes third. Lately, in all this war-fever, they say the gunmaker of Sakura-no-chō, Shibatsuji Riemon, put up ten storehouses at a single stroke—but he is nowhere near the old fortunes."

Ranking the town's fortunes was plainly a well-worn subject among the people of Sakai, and the woman appraised the insides of other men's storehouses with an air of authority.

For all this idle gossip, half of Gohei's mind was elsewhere. In his head the outward face of Sōkyū's mansion flickered in sharp outline. He had made sure of the exterior by daylight; but to turn the impression over by night and reckon out the placement and structure of the buildings within was a mental craft peculiar to the Iga man, one that belonged to none of the eighteen martial arts.

"What are you thinking of, my lord?"

The woman softly took Gohei's hand.

"Is it that pretty girl you left behind in your home country?"

"Something of the sort, yes."

Gohei turned to her as he said it, and all at once, with a rush that seemed to burst in him, he remembered Kisaru's body and her scent.

"Wicked of you."

She tugged hard at his hand and dug her nails into the back of it; Gohei paid it no mind. With one part of him he plotted the inside of Sōkyū's mansion, and with another steeped himself in the sharp, living sense of Kisaru's flesh.

(Will she come, I wonder?)

He meant Kisaru, who was to appear at the back gate of the residence on the night of the new moon in the sixth month. A man as sure of himself as Gohei caught himself waiting for it in a humbled frame of mind, almost as though he were praying for a stroke of luck.

(Not, of course, that waiting for the woman was any affair of love.)

Or so Gohei reasoned.

And in fact it was true. To get hold of Kisaru, and through her the genin of the Shimotsuge band who answered to her, was a thing Gohei's new work could not do without.

What he wanted—what he should have wanted—was only Kisaru's worth as a ninja. And yet, strangely, the image of her in his breast was taking on, more and more richly, the sense not of a ninja but of a living, breathing woman. Never before had Gohei thought of Kisaru like this.

Perhaps the Kisaru he now longed for was not the Kisaru he had once held. From the moment he cast her off, a new Kisaru must have been born in his memory and grown there little by little into something vivid and bright. Now, afresh, Gohei had begun to want her fiercely.

(So the edge is beginning to go off me.)

So he thought.

For a ninja, love was forbidden. A woman had to remain, to the last, what their cant called her: a kunoichi. A tool of deception, to be used; at most, now and again, a mere implement for the discharge of one's own lust. Nothing more of her was to be thought.

Somewhere far off, firecrackers were going. They seemed, indeed, to have been crackling away for some time. Between them a busy fluting, like a little snake writhing and knotting on itself, would ride across on the wind and then, for a spell, fall silent again.

"That flute—is it a Ming man playing?"

"A China ship come in, most likely."

"In the middle of this war?"

"War is a thing for gentlemen of the sword like yourself, sir. To a trader's eye there is neither war nor anything else. But that ship, now—it sounds like a Luzon vessel worked by Ming traders."

"How do you know that?"

"Between the Ming flute you can hear the hunting-drums the Luzon sailors beat. There—that sound, like a whip laid across a man's back. Can't you hear it?"

"That, just now—that would be the Luzon drum. A strange sound."

"Somehow it sets the blood stirring, does it not."

"Any tune the ear is not used to sounds uncanny. It is far into the night now. Go to sleep."

Wearying of it, and meaning perhaps to cut the talk short, Gohei made to roll away to the far side. Her hand came out to his bare chest, and the scent of the sachet in her bosom filled his nostrils. The throb of the Luzon drum must have driven the woman half mad; her body pressed at him, insistent, and Gohei took hold of her. In the same instant something in that body—something that was not Kisaru's—turned his feeling cold and hollow. Abruptly he let her go. He had always prided himself in secret on a ninja's hard indifference to women; this was a physical revulsion he had never known before.

(So the edge has truly gone off me now.)

In the dark Gohei gave a wry smile to himself.

It was about the time the woman had fallen asleep. The sea wind must have been rising; the rain-shutters rattled steadily under it. Early summer though it was, once the night air thickened toward the hour of the Ox the cold bit until the very pores drew tight. Gohei had not slept. For a while he lay measuring her breathing, then slipped from under the quilt like a breath of wind.

He slid coins quietly into the drawer of her box-pillow, put himself in order, and went out. The black mass of trees ahead of him, as he moved along the road like a shadow, would be the resting-shrine of the Sumiyoshi god. The lamps of the Shukuin lodging-houses, ranged eave to eave on either side of the shrine, had gone out. That night, just as Gohei had reckoned beforehand, not a star showed in the Sakai sky.

When he reached the Kaimachi crossroads a dog barked, off to the north. Gohei stopped short, and to put distance between himself and the dog turned deliberately toward the Mimura shrine. He went south along the shrine's earthen wall, then east again, and crossed Daishōji-dōri. At once the great roof of Jōraku-ji came into view; and when he set himself against the temple's outer wall, the wall ran straight on into the fence of the magistrate's office next door to the east. Past that office he was already in Minami-Zaimoku-chō. The residence of Imai Sōkyū, the Hōin, stood on a plot that took up a fifth of the whole district, a grand and sprawling establishment. Nor was it size alone. Round the walls ran a stone rampart, high at the base, its foot standing in the water of a moat; and the buildings were set out against an outside enemy exactly as a daimyo's would be. All to guard the gold and silver stored within.

Once, in his prime, just after the Honnō-ji affair, Sōkyū had received a secret letter from Akechi Mitsuhide, the Hyūga-no-kami—an overture that ran: "I will grant you the whole of the Sennan country; lend me your strength in raising the funds for the war." Sōkyū had laughed and torn it up. "If I wanted land badly enough for that, I would never have chosen to be a merchant. I could turn warlord tomorrow. That Koretō-dono should offer land to me, of all men, who love nothing but trade—the fellow must be badly muddled. Why does he not say, rather, that he will give me gold when the thing is done? Tell me I may take for myself every last harbor-toll of Sakai, and even I might give it thought. Koretō-dono has not the wit to preach his sermon to the man in front of him."

Afterward, when he heard that Hideyoshi had moved and struck Akechi down, Sōkyū loaded a train of congratulatory gifts and went to pay his respects at Hideyoshi's camp at Yamazaki. On the way home, in high good humor, he glanced back at his son Sōkun and said:

"Those loads will come back to my storehouses one day as ten thousand times their weight in gold and silver. That is what men mean by a business chance."

So he said.

Yet once Hideyoshi's age had come, the return on it, for Sōkyū, proved poorer than he had looked for.

(A warrior will stop at no cruelty to guard his lands and to widen them. By that reckoning, whatever means a merchant takes to widen his profit are fair, of whatever kind. Koretō Hyūga-no-kami killed Nobunaga to seize the realm; the Hōin Sōkyū kills Hideyoshi to seize the realm's profit. A shrewd stroke of business, is it not?)

Gohei, already stolen in past the walls, crouched among the trees at their foot, watching for any stir within the grounds, and turned over just such thoughts as these about Sōkyū.

(Set against that, an Iga samurai like this Kazama Gohei cuts a poor figure. A townsman's domain is his gold and silver, Sōkyū says; well then, from a man like that one ought by rights to strip everything, if it could be managed—yet I mean to squeeze out no more than enough to live at ease with Kisaru. Even in the amassing of a fortune, what a gulf there is between daimyo and merchant and ninja. And still, I am a cut above Tsuzura Jūzō. That man sweats buckets over work that comes to no more than playing errand-boy to a thief named Sōkyū, and—of all follies—throws his very life away on it.)

Gohei eased himself by degrees out of the trees. A ninja could earn his profit only within a ninja's narrow station; there was no help for it. Along the pale, clean ridge of his nose beneath the hood there hung a faint pride, and a faint self-mockery, both his own.

(Well then—shall I go and meet Sōkyū?)

He drew himself upright from the trees and set off across the garden, flitting like a black butterfly on the wing.

A sumptuous building in the study style spread its light cypress-bark roof out over the water, jutting to the very edge of the pond. Judging this to be where Sōkyū slept, Gohei worked a kunai in under a rain-shutter and lifted one of them free without a sound. He went along the corridor with a step that seemed to float above the boards. Within, the house lay deep in sleep. When he had made careful trial from outside of every room in turn, Gohei came to a stand before the cedar door of an inner chamber that looked to be Sōkyū's own.

On the cedar panels, in layer upon layer of mineral color, two civet-cats were painted at play beneath a grape-vine; on the other leaf, an old man like some holy sage of Tenjiku rode astride a white elephant.

Each leaf must have weighed a good five or six kan; drawn by hand, it would grate in its groove. Gohei lifted it away without a sound.

Next came a papered sliding-door. A faint light leaked from the transom above it and told him the room beyond was the guardroom where the night-watch sat. Gohei poured oil, and plenty of it, into the door's groove. Now it would slide without a whisper. The next instant—

"Ungh—"

The hired man on watch, a samurai by his look, was cut down before he had time to make a sound. In the same motion Gohei flung open the fusuma beyond, toppled a folding screen on purpose, and stood planted at the foot of Sōkyū's opulent bed, its down cased over in Zhejiang silk.

In the bed Sōkyū already had his eyes open, sharp and awake. Sensing Gohei planted there at the foot, he raised himself slowly upright on the bedding and said:

"Bring up a light."

—in a low voice. As though drawn to obey, Gohei took out flint and steel and carried a tinder-flame across to the candlestand at hand. Even as he did it he kept his eyes on Sōkyū, and said:

"Make no noise. You cling to your life like any other man, I don't doubt."

Sōkyū took no notice of that.

"A young voice still. And the way you move—springy as a yearling cat. You almost seem to be enjoying yourself. Well—luckily for you, I am too old. I'll raise no cry; you need not tell me twice."

In the brightened room Sōkyū smoothed the creases at his mouth and gave a slow smile. Only his eyes did not move: heavy, thick-fleshed lids cut in triangles hung low over them, and beneath them he watched Gohei's every move as though to pin it down. Then, quite at odds with that gaze, a voice like a man half yawning came out of the thick lips.

"So—you've killed a man."

"—Is that wrong?"

Even to himself it was a poor answer. Gohei felt how wholly the old man had the better of him.

"No, not wrong. But before we talk—can you not tell me your name?"

"I cannot."

"Then I shall say it for you. You are a man of Iga."

"……"

"Your house is the Kazama, gōshi of Ishikawa village in the Ayama country of Iga. Your name is Gohei. I am not mistaken, I think."

"……"

A retching impulse, one that could not even rise into a cry, pressed at the back of Gohei's throat. All the blood in him froze at once, and he barely mastered the trembling that ran up into his knees.

"H-how do you know that…?"

Startled into letting the words escape him, Gohei felt more wretched still. Sōkyū watched him with something like a sneer, and said:

"I'll tell you. Sit down first."

That he sank limply where he stood, just as he was bidden, might be called an inescapable spell cast by the sheer distance between the two men.

The smile had gone from Sōkyū's face as well. And yet he had no more ease of mind to spare for savoring Gohei's collapse than Gohei had for anything. Under the wrinkled lids the pale eyes glinted, narrow and sharp. A faint sheen of oil stood out on the sooty, all but lifeless skin of his face: proof that in Sōkyū's breast too a tension was gathering, the tension of a man crossing a blade's edge barefoot. For if the intruder were driven to the wall, he would turn killer on the instant—of that there was no doubt at all.

"Gohei."

Imai Sōkyū took a high and imperious tone.

"You'll know this already, but Sōkyū is no mere profit-grubbing townsman. For a time I held the magistracy of Sakai as well; my rank is Ōkurakyō, and the Hōin; and I have had the favor first of Lord Nobunaga and now of the Taikō, until I am versed in the inside and the outside of every lord and courtier about the palace. A man not so versed could never trade with the whole realm for his counter, as I do. The warrior houses loose their spies through the provinces. But it is not the warrior houses alone who must send out spies and learn how the land lies. We of Sakai, who reckon up the profit of the realm, keep spies at work always, so as to know the risings of the provinces before they break, and the temper of each daimyo, his cunning, and the goods each province yields. So it was that from my young days I came to have dealings with the men of Iga, and one Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon in particular has done my errands. Jirōzaemon, a rappa of the Shimotsuge band—do you know him?"

"I do not."

"The lie shows in your face. No matter; let me go on. In recent years, having some work for the man, I sent word into Iga; and Jirōzaemon answered that Iga was ruined now, and he himself too old to be of use—might he not set one of his disciples to the task instead? Only, that disciple, it seems, absconded from Iga not long after."

Sōkyū grinned and peered into Gohei's face.

"——"

"So Jirōzaemon, having no help for it, put to the work a man who had already left the shadow-trade and was playing at being a novice monk. Well? Does none of this come back to you?"

"'You have me there,' I'd like to say."

Gohei stepped briskly round to Sōkyū's bedside and settled cross-legged. He meant it for a show of boldness, but the fist he set on his knee was trembling, small and afraid.

"If you know so much, so much the quicker for us both. The work in question was to kill the Taikō—was it not?"

"Oho."

Sōkyū's eyes widened, and not for effect.

"His Highness? Kill him, you say."

"Ha—now it's the Hōin's turn to play the innocent. Let us both leave off the needless theater. I say it, and I am the man Jirōzaemon charged with the killing; there is no mistake in it."

"Well, well—the honor of the Iga men has fallen low indeed. In the old days a man would sooner have had his mouth torn open than name what he had been hired to do. That was the very thing that let the warlords of the provinces trust the Iga men with their work. And the gentry of Iga, every one, fed and clothed themselves by keeping that faith."

"No use telling me that. I am an Iga man no longer."

"Just so. You have already betrayed Iga."

"And so I have no such honor, either."

"Let me say this plainly: I have no recollection of asking Jirōzaemon for anything so grave as the murder of His Highness. You have misheard it somewhere, no doubt."

Sōkyū began to press the point, then all at once let the strength drain out of the end of his words. His face said plainly that speaking had grown too much of a trouble. The cold seemed to be reaching him; he sat silent, raised a hand to his breast, and slowly drew his collar closed. Then, at last:

"Is it money you want?"

he said.

"Call it a bargain."

"Hff—a scrap of an Iga man, and talking sharp. Were I a warrior, I would not let you leave this house alive. But I am a merchant. If a man can be killed with money, let me try killing you with it. What is your price?"

"A thousand pieces of gold. —Too little, would you say?"

Gohei said it with a movement of his thin lips. In those days a single debased copper coin would buy one rice-cake; a thousand pieces of gold, reckoned into such coin, came to some thirty-six million. About this time Hosokawa Tadaoki, lord of Tango and its one hundred and twenty thousand koku, being short of ready funds, had borrowed a hundred pieces of gold from the regent Hidetsugu. Later, when Hideyoshi cast a suspicion of treason upon him, Tadaoki—afraid the debt would mark him as one of Hidetsugu's faction—resolved to raise the sum elsewhere in haste and repay it; yet even knocking at the gate of so great a house as the Maeda of Kaga, he could not have it in time. In the end he went to Tokugawa Ieyasu, and the loan was at last granted; and it was murmured about the streets of that day that it was for this favor the Hosokawa sided with the Eastern army at Sekigahara. Such was the sum. And hearing it named, Sōkyū did not so much as stir an eyebrow.

"And that is all you want?"

he said, as though it were nothing, and went on:

"A thousand pieces of gold to kill one rappa with money—that is a grand sum, to be sure. But the night is cold, and if you sit there until dawn I shall freeze in my bed. To buy an hour's untroubled sleep with a thousand in gold—why, that has something of elegance about it, and I am a small hand at the tea way myself. I'll add another thousand and call it a blessing on the bargain. Only—can you carry it?"

Two thousand pieces of gold made a considerable weight. Half of it he might have shouldered; but he had to slip out of Sakai in secret, over the town gates, in the dead of night. For a man alone it was all but impossible. Gohei, however, seemed to have thought even this through, for he said:

"You'll know the place, maybe. At the foot of Mount Kannabi, in Yamashiro, the village of Kiwano was ruined in the great flood some years back; there is not one house left standing in those fields now. But at the heart of the village stands a Hachiman shrine. The hall itself is gone, but the torii should still be standing. Bury it under that torii by the night of the new moon in the sixth month. And mind—no clever tricks."

It is the night of that new moon in the sixth month.

The sound of the sixth-hour bells, heard here and there across the capital, always dies away on the last stray stroke of Jōraku-ji, near Uchino. Tonight, as though at that signal, the summit of Mount Nyoigatake—which floated up like a shadow-picture beyond the Kamo riverbed, seen from Gohei's residence—began to smolder gold. A slender thread of a moon was starting to rise.

Leaving the pines of the summit, the moon began quietly to sweep the darkness from the eastern sky. As though it had a will of its own, it slid its body up into the heavens. Watching that slow, creeping ascent over the wall, Gohei—

(She'll come. She will, that one. . . .)

—murmured, almost as a prayer. The longing for a woman shows, at times, a strange power: it can dye a man's very temper another color. In the breast of a man as cold as Gohei—a man who took a certain pleasure even in his own coldness—there had begun to flow a tenderness so keen that he half wanted to sneer at himself for it.

(Something's the matter with me.)

Gohei felt a fear that, going on this way, the shape-shifter's discipline would peel off him and leave him nothing but an ordinary young man. Perhaps, if he held her once, the feeling would cool of itself. Perhaps, if he took his fill of her one more time, he might come back to his old cold self, the way bad blood is let and drains away.

(But until I hold her I shan't know that, either. If I spend myself in the woman's body and the longing still remains—then it may be that my ninja days are at their end.)

What made him feel the danger of it was the ninja discipline drilled into him from boyhood. The Iga heart within Gohei, honed by that discipline to a still greater cruelty, watched his present agitation with a cold eye.

(Fool. Over a single slip of a girl.)

He thought that too, and had just twisted his thin lips at himself, when—

"Gohei-dono—"

By the time he spun round with a start, Kisaru was already there, in the shadow of the kimono-rack in the corner of the room, a kosode sleeve pressed to her mouth, holding back her laughter.

"Gazing at the moon—and what is it you're thinking of, with that solemn face?"

"—So you came."

Gohei stared at Kisaru's lovely face as if dumbfounded, and mumbled that feeble answer half under his breath. Kisaru, toying with the kosode of his that hung on the rack, said:

"And what's this talk about getting on in the world?"

"Mm. Later."

"You're not going to trick me again, now, are you?"

"Are you the sort to be taken in by me?"

"Taken in once, right enough—but I'll not bring it up. For Jirōzaemon's daughter, yesterday is already long ago. My father taught me so. A ninja has only today and tomorrow, he'd say."

"Then for the moment—what does Kisaru want out of tomorrow?"

"Shall I tell you?"

"……"

"I want to be married. Depending on what you're offering—if you'll take me for your wife, Kisaru will do anything."

"Odd. I'd have thought you were pining for Jūzō."

"It galls me, but I've given him up. I thought it all through, shut away at the house in Shimotsuge."

"So you've come to dislike Jūzō."

"Dislike him? Who said that? What I can't abide is the trade the Iga folk live by. Chase after an Iga man whose whereabouts you can't be sure of one day to the next, and a woman's happiness never comes at all. If it's a ninja I'm to love, I'd do better to love the wind. That's what I decided. Oh—is my face red?"

"It is. Something the matter?"

"—Whenever I think of Lord Jūzō—"

—it always comes over me like this, Kisaru said in a small voice, and pressed both hands to her flushed cheeks. Gohei found it too absurd, and said:

"If you're as smitten as all that, why not just go to Jūzō?"

But Kisaru lifted her face firmly, her sly eyes sparkling, and said, almost as though she were scolding Gohei:

"That won't do."

"Why not?"

"The folk of Shimotsuge village said it too. That man is possessed by the beauty of a ninja's whole life, they said; and to make that beauty perfect he all but hurries himself toward his death. Gohei—is it not so? Where, in a Tsuzura Jūzō who cares for nothing but to make his own life beautiful as a man's, is there any place for a woman to sit? Kisaru gave him up."

"And so—what will you do?"

"Become your bride."

"Well, I'm astonished."

And astonished he truly was; yet these wild leaps of Kisaru's mind stirred in Gohei a tenderness he could not put down. Kisaru went happily on:

"Kazama Gohei is another matter—so the folk of Shimotsuge said. Disciples of the same Jirōzaemon, and yet Gohei is nothing like Jūzō. That one thinks only of his own advantage. And after all, a woman becomes a man's wife for her own advantage as a woman too. It's by being taken by a man like that that a woman finds a place to live, and can help to push a man up in the world. So they told me."

"And who is this sage of yours?"

"Kiheiji."

"Of Kawabata."

"Aye. Seventy now, he is. He came through the Tenshō rising with his life by a hair, but powder blinded his right eye, and he's lame in both legs. No use for a ninja's work any longer. He beds down in the barn of our house at Shimotsuge, and cadges sake off the villagers in a chipped bowl, and eats and drinks himself sodden."

"Kiheiji of Kawabata—in his young days he served the Hōjō of Odawara, and slipped freely in and out of the lands of the Takeda of Kai and the Uesugi of Echigo, hard as those houses were on outsiders, and always sounded out the storms brewing across the Kō-Shin-Etsu country before they broke. A clever hand, by all accounts. There is nothing so wretched as the end of a ninja. Jūzō will come to that in time, too."

"That's just why."

Kisaru rolled her eyes round, and:

"—it's you I mean to throw myself upon."

"Slippery little thing."

Gohei laughed, took Kisaru's hand, and drew her to him. She let her body sink toward him with the pull, supple and twisting—but kept her face raised, and said:

"Never mind that—what about the work?"

"Mm. Did you bring the genin?"

"Can't you feel them, out there in the garden?"

Even Gohei, his wits gone vacant in the lively air the woman brewed about her, had failed to sense the four men who knelt with their faces to the ground in the shadows of the garden. The genin were, for the most part, risen from tenant farmers; and by custom, without express leave, they could not sit in the same room as a man of jōnin rank, born of the Iga gentry.

Gohei laid out for Kisaru the shape of the coming task, then leaned in close and whispered into her ear:

"These two thousand pieces of gold are only for the moment. The man is the Ōkurakyō Hōin of Sakai; he can be squeezed a second time, and a third. Once that's done, we can throw away any small ambition of rising as a samurai and still live out our days as rich folk, you and I, wherever we please. Well—not so bad a prospect, is it?"

"Not so bad at all."

Echoing the tail of Gohei's words, Kisaru gave a little laugh and hunched her shoulders, and:

"—and into the bargain, it'll put that woman they call Kohagi's nose out of joint."

"Kohagi? She is Sōkyū's adopted daughter, if I have it right. How do you come to know her?"

Kisaru took no notice of the flash in Gohei's eyes:

"She was my rival in love."

"Over whom?"

"Over Lord Jūzō."

"So Jūzō and Kohagi had an understanding?"

"I don't know."

Kisaru spat it out, vexed after all, and:

"Rake over the past, and it rots your very soul."

Even as she said it, on the dim floor Kisaru bit sharply at her lip.

(The woman is still drawn to Jūzō.)

From the back of Gohei's throat a bile welled up, metallic on the tongue, and a fury seized him to spit at her if he could; but telling himself that to torment the woman's feelings now would shatter the whole design, he made his face deliberately cold, and said:

"Tomorrow night, the second watch—understood? You know Mount Kannabi? At its southern foot lies the village of Kiwano—mind, there's not a soul living there now. There is a ruined Hachiman shrine, and a torii. Tell the genin to gather by the torii. And the evening after the gold's been dug up safe and sound—heh—that evening will be our—"

"—wedding, you mean?"

Kisaru and Gohei looked at each other and smiled.

By that time Sōkyū had already sent word of the matter—of Gohei's threat—to Kohagi in the capital, by an express courier.

"He is no more than a petty thief, but it will be a nuisance if the thing gets out. If it can be done, turn the emptiness of Kiwano to account and see to it that Gohei's whole band is cut down to the last man."

The handwriting, hoarse as Sōkyū's own voice, dictated the course Kohagi was to take. She read the letter, burned it to ash in the candle-flame, and understood that the moment for her own decision had come sooner—far sooner—than she had looked for. It was not, of course, that she meant to obey Sōkyū's command.

First, she would take Gohei's whole band in secret. Once they were taken, she would fasten on them the crime of the banditry that had troubled the capital since the year before, expose the plot of Sōkyū that stood behind these Iga men, and report the whole of it to the commissioner from whom Kohagi had received her charge as a ninja—Ishida, the Jibu-no-shō, Mitsunari. If Sōkyū were brought to justice, then Tsuzura Jūzō, who had left this house the other night still wounded, would have no need to fling himself into danger again. So she reasoned.

Kohagi called for the old nurse and ordered that twenty Kōga men be laid in ambush at Kiwano, night after night.

"And my lady herself?"

"I need not go, I think. But will twenty Kōga men be enough to settle it? Suppose I told Lord Jibu-no-shō the matter and borrowed a few men to hold the highway—how would that be?"

"There would be no need for that. On night-work, day-soldiers are only a hindrance. Each of them so hot for his own glory that his movements grow broad and showy, and the end of it is that the Iga men catch wind of them. I will go out and direct the Kōga men myself. First we must send the men off at once and bury the two thousand gold pieces. The setting of the ambush comes after that."

"The sooner the better."

"If this goes off well, my lady too may be at peace."

"When it is over, I want to go back to being only a woman. I have no mind to return to Kōga any longer. I have grown sick of the rappa's life. I want to live quietly, out among common folk, as one woman and nothing more."

"My lady was a daughter of the Sasaki house of Ōmi, and it was we of the Mochizuki house of Kōga who raised you. When your birth came to light and your life stood in danger, it was Lord Jibu-no-shō who saw you through. Set the Mochizuki aside; even the debt to Lord Jibu-no-shō will be paid off once this one thing is done. And yet—that you took the shadow arts into your very body, through being reared to womanhood in the Mochizuki house—that, I do believe, was for my lady a misfortune past mending. When this is over, we too would spend our whole hearts to see a woman's happiness come to you in full."

"Don't speak empty comforts to me."

Kohagi rose abruptly and opened the small window of the room. The sixth-month sun was still high, and the fragrant breeze that blew in at the window left a strange ache in her breast.

Before she had noticed, she was past twenty. A woman's prime was already going by. She had given up the flowering season of her life for Ishida Mitsunari, the Jibu-no-shō—a man to whom she was bound by no tie of her own—and there were times when the thought of it filled her with a frustration fierce enough to want to claw at her own breast. Her fate, perhaps—born and reared from the cradle in the Kōga country—she might resign herself to. But how was she to live out the rest of her life? As though she had read these thoughts in Kohagi's mind, the old nurse spoke from behind her:

"My lady."

she said.

"There is nothing for you to fear. Did not Lord Jibu-no-shō himself say that, once this is done, he would arrange you a match into any great house you please?"

"Nonsense."

Kohagi twisted her lips.

"He means only to sweeten me with such talk, and use Kohagi over again as a tool to work upon this great house or that. Kohagi too has grown wise enough in the world's ways to read the underside of men's hearts. It is true that, looked at alone, he is by no means a man who could do a shameful thing. But his head is too keen. When he plans the governing of the realm with that keen head of his, one like Kohagi seems to him not a creature of warm blood but a cold piece on a shōgi board. As a piece, a Kohagi reared in a ninja house has one good use after another. I owe him a debt. But so long as I go on living in the palm of his hand, there is no life of Kohagi's own at all."

"And so—what will you do?"

"If I knew that, I would not grieve over myself like this."

Having flung out the words, Kohagi felt—quite apart from all that—her cheeks slowly begin to color. With a feeling that surged up in her, she had remembered the warmth of Tsuzura Jūzō's thick chest. The lifeless tip of a pine cut across the blue of the early-summer sky framed in the window; and from somewhere in that desolate scene a choking smell of pollen had begun to fill the wind.

"It is no use."

Whether Kohagi murmured it while trying to make out what plant the pollen came from, she herself could not have said. There was only a stabbing sense of self-mockery in her. Why she should have to mock herself she did not know either; and told to the old nurse, it was not a feeling that would have carried across.

"You are in love, are you not."

The nurse's voice rang hollow to Kohagi. But the old woman went on in a tone sure of itself, in her own fashion:

"To lose your heart to a shadow-man is no good thing, my lady. It is like trying to take a heat-shimmer in your arms. Such men have hearts that are as good as no hearts at all."

Kohagi made no answer for a while, and stayed silent, looking out at the window; then, at last—

"No use in it—"

—she murmured, once more. It was not, as the nurse supposed, that Kohagi grieved over the emptiness of losing her heart to a ninja's, uncertain as a heat-shimmer. Rather, she had driven the needle of self-mockery into the very posture of her loving heart. So long as she loved, why could she not turn to another except as one bent on stripping away his body and his heart together? This pity for herself, for carrying a ninja's heart, was a thing she had only begun to grasp now that she loved.

"Go, now."

Still facing away, Kohagi gave the order. The nurse had her work to do—the men to be posted at Kiwano, in Yamashiro. She rose obediently and, all at once, laid a hand on Kohagi's shoulder. Kohagi shook it off with a force that was almost brutal; yet her voice alone was gentle as she spoke.

"As for me, you need not trouble yourself. It is only that this is a season when the heart grows heavy, I suppose."

"No."

The old woman let her lips part in a small smile.

"—I spoke amiss. If my lady feels so deeply as that, then I myself will guide you to Lord Jūzō of Iga. Even if you are ruined for love—why, perhaps a woman's life shines the brighter for just such a thing."

"That is meddling."

Kohagi whirled round and thrust the old woman away with both hands—

"Will you not be gone, and quickly?"

"—Yes."

The old woman left the room. When she had heard the fusuma slide shut behind her, Kohagi threw herself down sobbing on the matting. Tears not of love alone—tears she could not account for—wet the mats without end.

At that same hour, on the upper floor of the Echigoya—a shabby old inn some eight chō east and down from Komatsubara, on the bank of the Kamo at Gojō—a painter had spread paint-dishes across the whole room and was working at a commonplace design, a hawk lodged in a lone pine. At a footfall beyond the fusuma he looked up sharply. It was Tsuzura Jūzō, who since vanishing from Kohagi's villa at Komatsudani had changed his appearance to keep out of sight.

"Are you within, good Keikyokusai?"

Jūzō, playing the traveling painter, must have given himself that name.

"I am. And who is it?"

"This ragged monk of yours. You know my voice well enough—don't play the innocent."

The man was plainly a good deal of a nuisance to Jūzō; at the words, his thick brows drew faintly together.

"I'm playing at nothing—it's Dokutan, is it. Come in, and no ceremony."

Even as Jūzō spoke, the fusuma was already sliding open, and a monk of outlandish appearance had planted his foot boldly on the matting.

"Painting sliding-doors again? Diligent of you."

The monk said it looking down at Jūzō. He crouched as if to peer at the picture, but it was Jūzō's eyes he watched. A monk he might be, yet a razor had last touched his shaven crown a good two months since. He had a patched black robe hitched up to the shoulder, and the collar of the white under-robe was shiny with grime; for all the world he might have been a beggar.

"A hawk and a pine. Grim stuff. If it's the inn's doors they've set you to, why not paint them some jolly god of fortune—a Hotei, or a Fukurokuju?"

In years he was much of an age with Jūzō. His cheeks were oddly red, and his frame so brawny that he looked fit to turn highway robber on the spot. He was a wandering monk, tramping the provinces, and had happened to share the inn with Jūzō these several days past. A sociable soul, plainly, and one who had taken a great liking to Jūzō besides: he would wash the rice he had begged on his rounds and cook it, Jūzō's share and all. Jūzō found the kindness something of a nuisance; but inns of that day did not, as a rule, set meals before their guests, and so, for the mere convenience of it, he let himself lean on the monk's goodwill. A wandering monk, to be sure, would ordinarily go from one provincial Zen temple to the next and lodge at those. When Jūzō, wondering why a monk should put up at an inn rather than a temple, asked him, the man opened his red mouth in a great laugh—

"Temples? No, thank you. There's a Buddha in them, and there are priests in them. Too stiff by half. Out here I feel I can let my Buddha-heart roam free across the whole wide world—don't you see?"

—and roared with laughter. Nor was it only the begged rice he shared. Now and again, from where he never said, he would come by a dried fish and lay it on Jūzō's bowl. Jūzō, partly by way of return, would bring out the coppers he had earned as a traveling dauber and pay the day's inn-charge for the two of them; and at this the monk seemed to warm to him more and more. But now the wandering monk they called Dokutan, tiring of watching the picture, took up one of the pigment-dishes scattered by Jūzō's knee and put a question he seemed to have had in mind for some while.

"Now this—is this azurite?"

"It is."

"And that one—malachite green?"

"Just so."

"By the look of it, quite fresh."

"……"

"Azurite and malachite—these are the very pigments a man uses most in painting, are they not? And yours are not spent in the least. The white glaze of the dishes, too, looks as if it came out of the kiln only yesterday. A traveling painter, you told me you were; but that, I'll wager, is a tale you tell to gull the world."

"I am what I look—a man going the roads, scraping a living."

Jūzō said it low, bent over the door-paper, working blue-black ink into the hawk's eye. The wish not to be drawn into talk showed plain in the profile he kept lowered. But the wandering monk paid it no heed.

"Look at your own fingers. The fingers that hold the brush have not grown into the shaft of it. Dress yourself as a painter, paint as cleverly as you will—those fingers alone tell the truth. A painter clumsier than you, if he has kept at the craft ten years, fifteen, will have fingers that cleave to the brush as though possessed."

"A priest, and a face-reader into the bargain?"

"No—I'm fond of people, that's all. The kind who never tires of watching them, so such things catch my eye. And there is something in you, in particular, I seem to have taken a fancy to."

"Not a thing I'm glad to hear."

"No, no. I've walked the provinces looking for a true man within the gate of Zen, and never yet met one that a monk could lose his heart to as a monk. A monk is an impossible sort of workman: he carries all the flesh and appetite of a man, and yet sets himself to rise clean out of being a man. Whether we of the Latter Days can do what the Buddha himself once did—that I still do not know. It's because I do not know that I keep to the wanderer's road. And yet, watching you these few days, I've found a fellow who has done the thing. Call it a sixth sense. Just where in you it lies I could not say for certain. But at the bottom of that face of yours, the world I am striving toward seems to lie brimming, still as a lake."

"Think whatever you please."

Quietly steadying the tip of the brush, Jūzō finished dotting in the pupil of the hawk's eye. Below the hawk—which gripped a branch at the crown of a lone pine and swept its gaze around it—hung a pale, waning moon. With the pupil once set in by Jūzō's hand, the bird's plumage all at once took on a sheen, its down stirred in a wind out of heaven, and a power began to fill it to the last corner, as though it might that instant take wing after its prey.

"Ah—it lives!"

The wandering monk called Dokutan dropped his eyes to the picture and slapped his knee in delight. But at once he added—

"Mind, it's not because your painting is good that I struck my knee."

he said.

"It's only that, the moment you set in that pupil, the doubt in me melted clean away. That was what pleased me, and I struck my knee before I knew it. I praised you a while ago; but to speak plain, even in a man like you there is a darkness over the one half. To my eye, set against the clearness of your other half, it lies black as night. It vexed me, I'll own. A long while I turned it over—what could that half be? To know you through is to know my own self at the same time. And that, too, is part of the discipline."

"And so—what did you make of it?"

Jūzō said it, drawn in despite himself.

"Why, once you've grasped it, it's nothing at all. The darkness of your one half, I judged, is the darkness of a man who has no wisdom."

"How's that?"

"As I said a moment ago, a monk is an outrageous rogue: he carries all the body and appetite of a man, and yet sets his heart on rising clean above being one. —Here, are you listening?"

"I'm listening."

"And you, now—you have risen above the human splendidly."

"Have I?"

"By what road I couldn't say, but you must have hammered your mind on the forge a long while. That was what drew me to you. At first I felt as though I were looking at some strange furred beast."

"——"

"But when I turned it over again—you are a man of just that much, and no more."

"——"

"To be sure, to rise above the human is a rare thing. But that alone is no better than a tree, a stone, a bird, a beast. The tree and the stone, the bird and the beast, simply live according to their nature. They never fret over life and death. They were given no faculty even to be troubled by their own passions. If the whole bliss of liberation were merely to transcend life and death, then a man might as well turn himself into a tree or a beast. And a monk might as well leave off worshipping the Buddha and worship a pine instead."

"Let me say plainly—"

Jūzō said it without lifting his eyes from the painting.

"I have no wish to talk about myself."

"Very well. Whoever the painter may be is nothing to me. Only, it grieves me that your darkness is a darkness of wisdom. In our Buddhist world we call that wisdom prajñā. You do not know what a human being is. You never so much as try to think it. You never try to think of this world that bears mankind up and sustains it. You have surely never once thought even of your own happiness. And for all that, you carry a mind honed on life and death, beyond anything a hundred years of sitting in meditation could reach. Of all the creatures that wear a human skin, there is none so dangerous as that. Leave it alive a day, and a day's harm it will do the world."

"I see."

Jūzō lifted his face and looked steadily at Dokutan, but said nothing further; presently he lowered it again and went on with the brush.

"A man lives but fifty years."

Dokutan said it quietly, watching the haze of dawn spread and bleed from Jūzō's brush, where the azurite was thinned to a pale wash.

"Only by deepening the happiness of those fifty years does a man make it worth having been born a man. To weigh that worth is the true wisdom of a human being. —Will you not throw over that worthless way of yours?"

"This painter's trade, you mean?"

"No. Your real one."

Jūzō's fingers on the brush stopped short. In the same instant the blood through his whole body began quietly to cool. Could this monk know that he was a ninja?

"Who are you?"

"The wandering monk Dokutan."

"From whom, and on what charge, have you come?"

"I am charged by none. I know neither where I came from nor where I am going."

"This is no Zen riddling."

Jūzō flung down the brush and turned to face the monk. Dokutan only sat there, a smile on his ruddy cheeks.

(Still—it is not a face built to hoard a scheme behind it.)

So Jūzō judged, and felt a wash of relief. At the same time he was disgusted with himself for having doubted the man's goodwill—for the monk's earnest, shining eyes had force enough to shame Jūzō into looking inward. A wandering monk and a ninja, in the end, aimed at much the same thing in their training: to hold one's own mind under one's own command at will. And yet in all the Iga ninja Jūzō had ever known, not one had eyes with half the brightness of this monk's. The reason was plain. Ninjutsu was nothing but a devil's discipline. If Dokutan had it right, it was founded on having no prajñā—nay, on the utter denial of prajñā.

(This monk called me "dark.")

And true enough, set before the uncommon brightness of the monk's eyes, his own figure seemed of a sudden to blacken. Jūzō had studied the arts of concealment from boyhood. But those arts, he now felt he was being made to see, could not in the end hide the fated darkness of spirit that the Iga craft carried within it.

(Even the arts I learned in Iga seem to stand no chance against the light in this man's eyes.)

If a flowerless plant, that keeps its life only by fleeing the sun, could feel a faint yearning toward the plants that open their blossoms in the full light—then the something like yearning with which Jūzō glanced up at Dokutan's eyes was near enough that. But quite against that inward feeling, he spoke to Dokutan in a voice cold to the bone.

"So you suspect I have some real trade besides the painting? If you think so, say it out straight."

"That I don't know. I'm no fortune-teller."

Laughing, he cocked his head and peered into Jūzō's face.

"But when I open the eye of my mind, I can see your heart well enough. You are possessed of some mad, monstrous ambition. Were you a brother of mine in the monks' hall, I'd beat you half to death, if that was what it took, and drive the delusion out of you."

"Delusion, is it."

Jūzō hunched his shoulders, as though a draft had suddenly found him, and:

"You priests earn your rice by that trick, always."

"Just so—by putting the fear into people."

"You might, once in a while, put yourself in a layman's place. In a life of a bare fifty years, delusion may be the very thing a man lives for—may be his one pleasure."

"And is that you?"

"Is there harm in it?"

"You are calling hell down on yourself."

"It staves off the tedium a little better than living wrapped in smug enlightenment. It's only fifty years, whichever way."

"And that is why you say you'll try your hand at killing the Taikō?"

"……"

Jūzō turned a sharp, sidelong look on Dokutan's face. Somewhere along the way a long, thin iron paperweight had come into his hand. Flicked off with a finger, it could become at any moment a weapon to stave in the monk's brow.

"Priest. I was careless. So you were an agent after all."

"It slipped my tongue to mention it. Yes indeed—this humble monk is an agent."

And yet, even as he said it, Dokutan's own face was brighter than ever.

"Then know that you'll not leave this room alive. Who sent you?"

"All the devas and bodhisattvas of heaven."

The monk, sitting cross-legged, still did not drop his mocking tone. Only when Jūzō rose in one smooth motion in the shadow of the folding screen, and he saw the killing-lust that had settled in his eyes, did the monk at last raise his right hand.

"Do you truly mean to kill me?"

"What is there to startle you? Unlike a monk, we of the world below have no leisure to play at living. When interests fall out, there come times when a man must kill. You had only to stay outside the world, chanting your sutras; instead you stuck your nose, uninvited, into the world's affairs—and that is your crime. The world has its harsh law, the law of interest. It is a pity, but you'll have to submit to it."

"Wait. —Do you know a woman called Kohagi?"

"What?"

"Your own dear woman. You'll surely not tell me you don't know her. Monk though I am, I'm fond of the bustle of the capital, and every two years or so I come up to Kyoto on the pretext of asking after my old master at his sub-temple of Nanzen-ji. I don't lodge at his temple; I put up at an inn, like this, and beg my alms about the town. It was last spring, when I'd come up. Begging at a certain mansion in Komatsudani, I saw a certain woman. From that day, fool that I was, I lost my heart to her."

"And that was Kohagi, you're telling me?"

"It was. A monk is a man for all that. Show me one who can look on a beautiful woman and feel nothing stir, and I'll crack that lying skull of his open for him. To see her face I begged at that house near enough every day. The human heart is a strange thing. Standing at her gate with that in my breast, I must have struck some chord in the woman, for she would always come out and lay the alms in my bowl with her own hand. —Well? Does it make you jealous?"

"Don't be absurd."

"Let me make one thing plain here."

said Dokutan.

"It concerns what I said a moment ago. —Yes, I lost my heart to her, right enough. But I too am one who hangs, however far down, on the skirts of Zen. I certainly did not mean that I was led astray by the woman's charms. Let me put it more exactly: I was drawn by the strange power in her. That, in truth, is what I felt the instant I set eyes on her—as though I had touched something uncanny. And this strange power is the very thing I spoke of in you a while ago; it is in you too. A transcendence of the living flesh beyond anything we seekers of the Way can reach—shall I call it that? To put it once more by way of a figure: suppose the foxes and badgers that live in some temple garden were to ape the abbot, tread the Buddhist path, and come to a state that looked like liberation. Yet there is no prajñā in such liberation. At a glance it wears the look of the real thing, but liberation it is not. That is my meaning. Only, about such creatures there hangs an uncanny allure. It shows to no ordinary eye; only those of us who suffer along this Way of ours can catch it. Smell it, even. And so I am drawn to it."

"And so you fell for her."

Jūzō said it without interest.

"Ah, I did. Foxes and badgers have always drawn me. For the very reason I took to you, I took to Kohagi."

"And did you preach to her, as you've preached to me, that it is, in the end, no more than a beast's craft of mind?"

"Preach it I did. But that woman, unlike you, was not armored to the last corner of her heart in the way of beasts; and the more I preached, the more I found that a surprisingly human, living freshness remained in her. It became a delight to me, and I fell all the harder. Then last year I left the capital, and this year I came up again. The first place I went was not to my old master at Nanzen-ji, but to Kohagi's villa at Komatsudani."

"In love, it seems, layman and monk come to much the same thing."

Without noticing it, Jūzō had settled into the posture of a man drinking the story in.

"Just so, just so. And I—I was let up into Kohagi's room. Though that, mind you, is nothing new for me."

Dokutan chuckled, then broke off a moment to swallow. Beyond the paper screen the sun was at last beginning to fail.

"This year—though when I say this year, it was only the other day—when I came to see Kohagi, I found the human, living freshness in her plainer than ever, welling out of her. In the year I had not seen her, a good deal of the skin of the ninja-craft had peeled away. At first I thought, well, well. My heart leapt: had my preaching taken effect at last? —Why do you turn your eyes away? Are you listening?"

"I'm listening."

"Here is the heart of it. —But I was wrong. Wide of the mark, monstrously. Kohagi was in love with a man. She is a woman after all, you see. It was for the sake of that love, and that alone, that she had grown human past recognition. And she confessed it to me, clutching and shaking the knee of my robe, weeping."

"She did? She is not a woman to lose herself so."

"Well—there's a little exaggeration in it."

Dokutan licked his lips, unabashed.

Then he said:

"I heard it—the name of the man. His birth, too. I heard the whole of it. Kohagi told me with a face like one whose heart was being washed clean."

"—I see."

Jūzō looked up at the ceiling, gave a wry smile as though to say none of it mattered any longer, and let the paperweight in his palm drop to the floor beside his knee.

"As you say, reverend—the woman seems to have become a mere human being after all."

"A happy thing."

"Fool. —No, Dokutan. I'm a little tired of your company. I've passed a pleasant hour, for once in a long while. I hate to say it, but I mean to sleep until evening; would you leave me? We'll meet again."

Jūzō knocked the armrest aside and lay flat on his side. Dokutan, frowning openly, tugged at his sleeve, and:

"Come now. Why not at least hear me out?"

"No use in it. Every man has his own way of living. Talk between two men whose ways differ is amusing at the start, but it wears on you. And what you are about to say now, in particular—for me, it is breath thrown away."

"You can't know what it is I mean to say."

"How should I not know? Kohagi asked you this, did she not: go and find a tall man lodging at such-and-such an inn in the capital under the name of a painter—and shatter to pieces the ambition that man carries in him."

"A dear, pitiable woman, is she not."

"Nothing out of the way in it. Unless she's a fox or a goblin, a woman's coaxing runs mostly to the same thing."

"You fox."

"Me, you mean?"

"Who else."

"Fox will do, Dokutan. I'll say it again: a man has a thing called a purpose. Call it an obsession, if you like. The taste of that obsession is the taste of a life. Mine is like trying to heap lightning onto a little dish. That keen, clear taste is a thing you idlers of life will never come to know. And the wise man holds his tongue about what he does not understand. —Dokutan. It may be an odd thing to say, but I too have somehow come to like you. Still, talk on as we may, our words will never meet. You—if you're fond of your enlightenment, or whatever it is, then go there. I am fond of hell, and so to hell I'll go."

"There's no need to make a show of hell. An Iga man's heart is always there. The one misfortune is that, like some heretic ascetic of Tenjiku, you have trained up a craft of mind equal to enduring it."

"Will you not go? I can't sleep."

Jūzō rolled over and closed his eyes. Dokutan rose. He got as far as the fusuma once, but must have been sorely galled, for he came back, stood looking down at Jūzō where he lay, and kicked him in the shoulder with all his strength.

Jūzō did not even dodge it, and presently his breathing settled into the rhythm of sleep. Dokutan went. Jūzō had not been asleep at all. Behind the closed lids of this man the image of Kohagi breathed quietly on, wrapped in a pale-blue haze. Presently the image began to dissolve, ran down along his gaunt cheek, and dried there in an instant. And at that self of his, his face still that of a sleeping man, Jūzō let a cold, self-mocking smile rise to the surface.
