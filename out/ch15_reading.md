## Chapter 15. Resistance and National Salvation

In the early 1930s, at the very time when Chiang Kai-shek was zealously "exterminating the Communists," Japanese militarism was eyeing us like a tiger, bent on swallowing up our China; and under the Party's leadership, the movement to resist Japan rose like wind and swelling cloud.

The All-Circles National Salvation Federation, headed by Song Qingling, was declared founded at the National Salvation Congress convened in Shanghai on 31 May 1931. Its forty-one executive committee members included Ma Xiangbo, Song Qingling, He Xiangning, and Shen Junru. Within the National Salvation Association, every activity was openly called for by a council or executive committee made up chiefly of patriotic, democratic figures who were not Communists, while the concrete work of organizing and mobilizing was planned and prepared by a working committee in which Communist Party members were the majority. Comrade Enlai once said: wherever there is a Salvation Association, there are Communist cadres. To enable the National Salvation Association to gain an open, legal standing in Shanghai, Chen Zhigao supported the registration procedures for its founding. At that time the head of Shanghai's Bureau of Social Affairs was Wu Kaixian, and for the National Salvation Association to register openly it had to obtain the consent of both the concession police and the municipal Bureau of Social Affairs; the smoothing-over work with both was done by Chen Zhigao. So our close and deep cooperation with the National Salvation Association had its origins in 1931.

In 1932, Song Qingling further organized in Shanghai the China League for the Protection of Civil Rights, in which such well-known patriotic democrats opposed to Chiang Kai-shek's dictatorial rule as He Xiangning, Liu Yazi, Lin Gengbai, and Yang Xingfo all took an active part. The "League" publicly issued a manifesto opposing the Nanjing government's arbitrary arrest and killing of Communists and patriotic, progressive people, and actively and openly rescued arrested political prisoners. To coordinate with and support Song Qingling's work, the Party Central in 1932 also specially selected a comrade to serve as secretary of the League for the Protection of Civil Rights: this was Comrade Zhu Bochen, eldest son of Zhu Jianfan, principal of the Zhounan Girls' School in Changsha, who had joined the Party in 1930. Zhu Bochen had formerly been secretary of the Mutual Aid General Association and had worked together with me; his Chinese and English were both excellent, and he won Song Qingling's deep trust and esteem. The Party organization designated him to pass letters through underground couriers and to keep in touch with me and Pan Hannian, so that we might coordinate and carry forward the work of rescuing arrested comrades.

In mid-November 1936, the people of Shanghai were welcoming General Ma Zhanshan of Suiyuan, and General Feng Yuxiang sent a representative to Shanghai to say that he too supported a renewed Kuomintang–Communist cooperation. The Salvation Association thereupon launched a movement to aid Suiyuan. Among its many activities was a three-day charity performance in aid of Suiyuan, initiated by the wife of the lawyer Li Zuyu and held at the Daqian Shijie amusement hall. To celebrate the successful close of the charity performance, we held a small banquet at my home that evening. Everyone was in high spirits with the wine that day, and Zhigao had already drunk himself off to dreamland. In the dead of night, all at once Li Gongpu's wife Zhang Manyun, and then Shen Junru's son, telephoned one after another to say that Li Gongpu and Shen Junru had been arrested. Zhigao rushed at once to the police station and bailed out Li Gongpu and the others.

A few days later—on 23 November 1936—the Kuomintang authorities, in collusion with the police of the Shanghai concessions, secretly arrested seven leaders of the All-Circles National Salvation Federation on a charge of "endangering the Republic": Shen Junru, Zou Taofen, Zhang Naiqi, Li Gongpu, Wang Zaoshi, Sha Qianli, and Shi Liang. They were transferred and imprisoned in the detention house of the Jiangsu High Court at Suzhou, and the affair became the famous "Case of the Seven Gentlemen," which shocked China and the world and drew the concern of every circle at home and abroad. Progressive people of all circles and the broad masses protested one after another against the Kuomintang government's manufacture of a wrongful prosecution making "patriotism a crime." On 26 November, Song Qingling, in her capacity as an executive committee member of the All-Circles National Salvation Federation, issued a statement protesting the outrage of the Kuomintang authorities. On 16 December, she joined Ma Xiangbo and He Xiangning in issuing a jointly and personally signed manifesto, reaffirming that the National Salvation Association's position remained unchanged and demanding that the Nanjing government immediately and unconditionally release the arrested leaders of the salvation front.

To rescue the "Seven Gentlemen," the Shanghai Communist Party organization and the National Salvation Association's leaders, Song Qingling and the rest, jointly agreed to form a powerful defense team: twenty-one famous lawyers, Chen Zhigao among them, would defend the "Seven Gentlemen" without fee. Of these twenty-one great barristers, Zhigao was the youngest. In the prime of his vigor, he naturally felt himself in honor bound; for this case he went up to Nanjing and over to Suzhou, running about and crying out on every side, the most industrious of them all.

In the spring of 1937, when I went north to mediate the resumption of the Tongyi Company, I made a special point of carrying a letter from Zhigao to call on the barrister Zhang Shizhao; I first spoke of the situation of the Shanghai effort to rescue the Seven Gentlemen and asked him to respond. Zhang consented on the spot: not only did he, in his own name, submit a memorial to Chiang Kai-shek laying out the pros and cons and upholding salvation, but, to create a fair public opinion, he also telegraphed the National Bar Association calling for a united response. At the same time Professor Zhang Dongsun of Peking University, together with the teachers and students of Peking University, gave active support, and the international press responded as well. In this too I did a little to help expand the Party's public weight in the movement to rescue the "Seven Gentlemen."

As the Seven Gentlemen's case dragged on unresolved, Song Qingling went on to lead and launch a "patriotic imprisonment" movement: she and He Xiangning and fourteen others—sixteen in all—jointly signed and submitted a petition to the Jiangsu High Court, stating: "Shen Junru and the rest were arrested and imprisoned for engaging in salvation work. We are all members or directors of the National Salvation Association, or have engaged in salvation work together with Shen and the others. If to love one's country is thus a crime, then we are willing to be punished together with Shen Junru and the rest, and we ask the court to take us all into custody together; and if to love one's country is no crime, then we should enjoy freedom together with Shen Junru and the rest." On the morning of 5 July 1937, Song Qingling, ill as she was, brought along an amah (a maidservant) and led eleven people, Peng Wenying and Hu Yuzhi among them, to Suzhou; each carrying simple luggage (Song's amah even carried a commode chamber pot for her), they went to the Jiangsu High Court to request imprisonment of their own accord. This action forced the prosecutor to admit that the National Salvation Association was not itself a crime, and left the Kuomintang authorities in such an awkward plight that they dared not convict the "Seven Gentlemen." After the "July Seventh" Incident, the situation of nationwide united resistance had already taken shape. On 30 July, under the pressure of public opinion backed by our Party Central, the Jiangsu High Court at last had no choice but to rule that salvation was no crime and to release the "Seven Gentlemen" on bail. The "Seven Gentlemen" walked free with honor.

When the "Seven Gentlemen" walked free with honor, the people of every circle in Shanghai held a tremendous welcome rally. But the Kuomintang government still delayed and would not release the other political prisoners. Zhigao, together with Wu Gengmei, Shi Zhecun, Chen Gaoyong, Zhang Zhirang, Xue Muqiao, and others, made a joint appeal on behalf of Sun Xiaocun, who had already been held for more than eight months, going to Nanjing to petition and running about on every side until Sun Xiaocun was released without charge.

Not long after the outbreak of all-out resistance, the couple Liu Bochui and Zhao Wanhua were transferred away from Shanghai by order of the Central Committee, and I entrusted the two of them to ask Zhou Enlai to consent to my leaving Shanghai too, to study and work at Yan'an, where the Party Central was based. My reasons were: the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation had already taken shape; the great numbers of political prisoners arrested and imprisoned by the Kuomintang had been, or were being, released one after another, so that the special rescue mission the Party organization had originally given me was in the main accomplished; a new, safer, and more convenient line of communication had been established between Shanghai and the resistance base in northern Shaanxi; and I had rescued and cultivated many upper-level notables of political, legal, and financial circles, who could keep me safe along the way. I hoped to change my environment and throw myself into the front line of the anti-Japanese struggle under the direct leadership of the Party organization. But while I waited for the Central Committee's order, I had long since flung myself with all my strength into the blazing work of resistance and national salvation.

After the outbreak of the "July Seventh" Incident, the Nanjing National Government, under the pressure of the Japanese invaders' attack and the people's aroused indignation, was now at war with Japan; and so, toward the people's resistance-and-salvation movement in Shanghai, it no longer adopted its former policy of suppression and prohibition. But, fearing that the progressive forces would grow strong in the salvation movement and endanger its rule, it did all it could to impose a policy of monopoly and control over the mass movement. This still had its negative side, but was, after all, a progressive expression of the Kuomintang's resistance to Japan. Therefore our Party, and the progressive forces led and influenced by the Party, besides supporting and taking part in the anti-Japanese resistance-and-salvation bodies set up chiefly by the Kuomintang, also worked hard within them to expand the influence of the Party's policy of an anti-Japanese national united front—including support for the activities of the Shanghai All-Circles Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy.

On 22 July 1937, at the instigation of the Kuomintang's Shanghai Municipal Party Headquarters, thirteen official bodies and organizations—the Shanghai Chamber of Commerce, the Citizens' Federation, the General Labor Union, and others—took the initiative in founding the Shanghai All-Circles Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy, whose presidium was made up of magnates of Shanghai's industrial, commercial, and financial world and social notables such as Wang Xiaolai, Du Yuesheng, Qian Xinzhi, and Pan Gongzhan, together with members of the Kuomintang municipal party headquarters and the municipal Bureau of Social Affairs. Those in contact with our Party—Chen Zhigao, Cai Shuhou, Li Nanxiang, Jin Xuecheng, and others—also entered the planning committee of the Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy as representatives of various circles. On the same day, the well-known patriotic leaders of the women's former Salvation Association—He Xiangning, Xu Guangping, Shi Liang, Shen Zijiu, Hu Ziying, and others—also announced that, on the basis of the women's Salvation Association, they were founding the Chinese Women's Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy, calling on "all sisters who are unwilling to be the slaves of a conquered nation to rise as one and resist." Ever since the "July Seventh" Incident, Ms. He Xiangning had organized the purchase of towels, medicinal cotton, styptic pills, and a first batch of six hundred vests printed with the verse "The thread in a woman's hand becomes the clothes on a soldier's back; every stitch is charged with hatred of the foe, urging our sons to slay the Japanese enemy," and sent them to the 29th Army; she also spurred on Li Guang'an, head of the 29th Army's liaison office, saying: "When robbers break in, even the house-watching dog will bark its hardest. We are the dignified national army; at a time when the Japanese invaders press upon our borders and bully us so, if we do not come out and resist, how shall we face the nation and the people?" On 1 August, Song Meiling founded in Nanjing the Chinese Women's General Association for the Comfort of the Officers and Men Defending the Country in the War of Self-Defense and Resistance, and telegraphed the provinces and cities to establish branch associations. He Xiangning, proceeding from the larger cause of united resistance and taking no thought for her personal standing, decided to rename the Shanghai Chinese Women's Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy as the Shanghai Branch of the Chinese Women's Association for the Comfort of the Officers and Men Defending the Country and Resisting the Enemy (hereafter the "Women's Comfort Association"). Having obtained He Xiangning's consent, I used her name to link up and mobilize twenty-two women's bodies in Shanghai to take part—the Chinese Professional Women's Association, the Chinese Women's Mutual Aid Society, the Young Women's Christian Association, and others—and on 4 August founded the Shanghai Branch of the Women's Comfort Association. With He Xiangning as president, the Women's Comfort Association elected forty directors, a representative of every larger women's body serving as a director; among them were the women's Salvation Association's four famous "democratic big sisters"—Shi Liang, Luo Shuzhang, Shen Zijiu, and Hu Ziying—the Kuomintang legislator Wang Xiaoying, the Young Women's Christian Association representative Xie Zuyi, Liu Wang Liming of the Chinese Women's Temperance Union, Li Qiujun of the Women's Painting and Calligraphy Association, Gao Jingyi, wife of the industrialist Wu Gengmei, and the wives of some senior generals of the Northern Expedition period. He Xiangning's daughter Liao Mengxing and Elder Sister Xu Guangping served as secretaries of the Women's Comfort Association, whose offices were set up at He Xiangning's home, No. 8 Lafei Lane on Route Lafayette (now Fuxing Middle Road) in Shanghai. I was a managing director of the Women's Comfort Association and head of its comfort section, and, trusted by the venerable He Xiangning, served as her plenipotentiary representative both within and without. At that time our home was on Fuxing Middle Road (the site of the present Shanghai Grand Theater), not far from He Xiangning's residence, so I often went to her home. Because her age made it hard for her to get about, the ordinary external liaison activities were done by me on her behalf as her representative. Others holding concurrent posts in the Women's Comfort Association were the democratic big sisters Shi Liang, head of general affairs; Shen Zijiu, head of propaganda; Hu Ziying, head of solicitation; and Luo Shuzhang, head of first aid. But they were mainly busy with the work of the National Salvation Association and seldom came to the Women's Comfort Association. And so, during this period, the work of the Women's Comfort Association was mainly shouldered by a few of us: myself, Xu Guangping, Liao Mengxing, and Qi Yiying—representative of the Primary School Teachers' Federation, principal of the Daren Primary School, and deputy head of the comfort section.

The Women's Comfort Association was founded only after the outbreak of nationwide resistance; unlike such long-established bodies as the labor unions and chambers of commerce, it had no foundation, and its funds for activities were extremely tight, so we had no choice but to go and solicit donations from Shanghai's social notables and industrial and commercial magnates. Du Yuesheng, who at the time of the "April Twelfth" counter-revolutionary coup had been an accomplice of Chiang Kai-shek's, was one of the bosses of the Shanghai secret societies; later he moved into the financial world and took part in upper-level political activity, ranking third in Shanghai's financial world of the day (Qian Xinzhi was first, Wang Xiaolai second)—a man of great influence. After the "July Seventh" Incident, he too supported united resistance, and under the Party's policy of "letting bygones be bygones" within the anti-Japanese national united front during the period of the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation, he became one of those we sought to win over. It was therefore decided to raise funds for the Women's Comfort Association through him and by using his influence. Du Yuesheng was at that time chairman of the Zhonghui Bank, so, accompanied by Comrade Liao Mengxing, I went as the representative of President He Xiangning to see Du Yuesheng and ask him to support the Women's Comfort Association's activity funds. Du Yuesheng very readily donated a thousand dollars, and donated a further one thousand on behalf of Qian Xinzhi. Later, when the Shanghai Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy decided to send a delegation north to comfort the officers and men resisting Japan, the quota of collective fundraising apportioned to the Women's Comfort Association was eight thousand dollars. At the meeting I said that the Women's Comfort Association had no money and might not be able to complete this task. Du Yuesheng declared his position on the spot, saying he was willing to help the Women's Comfort Association and would go and raise the eight thousand dollars. He invited me to go and solicit donations at the home of another Shanghai "tycoon," Huang Jinrong. That day happened to be Huang Jinrong's birthday, and the well-wishers who came to offer congratulations were like clouds; seated at the mahjong tables were all the wives of the magnates of the industrial, commercial, and financial world. I made a round of several mahjong tables and raised the full eight thousand dollars in donations on the spot. I handed the money to Du Yuesheng, who concurrently served as deputy director of the Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy's solicitation committee (the director was Song Ailing), and so completed the Women's Comfort Association's task of soliciting comfort funds. Du Yuesheng wrote me a receipt, which I took back and handed to He Xiangning. He Xiangning said to me: "You must use your strength to send charcoal in the snow, not to add flowers to brocade." For at that time the progressive forces of every circle in Shanghai, under our Party's leadership and influence, had already broken through the various restrictions of the Kuomintang authorities, and, on the former basis of the National Salvation Association, salvation associations had sprung up one after another in every trade and profession. Under the hard conditions of getting no support from the government authorities, these associations were vigorously carrying on a mass movement of resistance and national salvation. I understood the meaning of the venerable He Xiangning's words: she wanted me not to put my main energies into the work of these official bodies supported by the Kuomintang authorities, like the Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy, but to give all my strength to supporting the popular salvation bodies of the various progressive masses, such as those of the cultural world, under our Party's leadership and influence. And so after this I no longer took part in the work of the Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy.

After the "July Seventh" Incident, our Party sent people to bring Guo Moruo secretly back to China from Japan. At that time the southbound anti-Japanese propaganda team that Guo Moruo's students Yao Qianxiu, Lin Lin, Yu Liqun, and others had organized in Beiping (with Yao as team leader) had already come to Shanghai. On the eve of the arrival at Shanghai of the ship Guo was taking, Yao came to find me and said that Pan Hannian wanted him to notify me that the Kuomintang authorities had not yet rescinded the warrant for Guo Moruo's arrest, and that I should find a way to protect Guo's safe landing in Shanghai. On 27 July, Zhigao and Cao Bingsheng rode in a police car, while Yao Qianxiu and I rode in Chen Zhigao's car, and together we went to the wharf to meet Guo Moruo. Because we had the police official car as escort, Guo was safely delivered to the Cangzhou Hotel to stay. Only then did Pan Hannian notify the old comrades-in-arms of the Northern Expedition period and the well-known figures of Shanghai's progressive cultural world to meet Guo Moruo formally. Sending out the invitations in my own name, we held a tea gathering at my home to welcome Guo Moruo; some forty or fifty people attended. At the meeting it was discussed to launch and found the Shanghai Cultural Circles' Salvation Association, "the Cultural Federation" for short (see A History of the Chinese Communist Party in Shanghai, pp. 941–942), and its founding manifesto was issued at the mass rally welcoming Guo Moruo's return to take part in the resistance. Guo Moruo was only forty-six when he returned, but his temples were already flecked with gray and his hearing was somewhat dull. He had quietly left Japan and reached Shanghai, and on nearing shore had composed a poem in the seven-character regulated form; after it was published, its lines "Parting from wife and casting off children, threads of gray at my temples; three days aboard ship, and I see the banners" were for a time on everyone's lips. All admired the heroic spirit and lofty resolve with which, braving danger, he had resolutely parted from wife and cast off children to return home and take part in the resistance. As far back as the Northern Expedition, Guo Moruo had held He Xiangning in great respect, and now, learning that I worked in the Women's Comfort Association headed by He Xiangning and enjoyed the venerable He's trust as her plenipotentiary representative both within and without, he was very glad and asked me to accompany him to call on He Xiangning. Later, each of us as a representative of the Women's Comfort Association and of the Cultural Federation, we went many times to the Songhu resistance front to comfort the officers and men resisting Japan, meeting almost every day. The mood then, as in the Northern Expedition days, was one of great excitement and vigor.

We also jointly saw off our old comrades-in-arms of the Northern Expedition as they set out for the anti-Japanese front. After the outbreak of the War of Resistance, through Guo Moruo's liaison, Comrade Qian Yishi (Jiepan)—who in 1926–1927 had done mass propaganda work at the Kuomintang's Hubei Provincial Party Headquarters—led, as team leader, a battlefront cultural service team attached to the army, going to the Fourth Army of the National Revolutionary Army commanded by Zhang Fakui (also called the "Old Fourth Army," to distinguish it from the New Fourth Army led by our Party) to carry on propaganda and agitation for resistance and national salvation. At the same time Xie Bingying and Hu Lanqi, of the Whampoa women's cadet corps in the Northern Expedition days, went respectively to the army service teams of Cheng Qian's and Hu Zongnan's forces. Representing He Xiangning, president of the Shanghai Women's Comfort Association, with Guo Moruo representing the cultural world, I saw them off to the front. On that occasion I gave these old friends of the Northern Expedition, as they raised the army's banner once more, these lines: "The vast sacred land is sinking; at the parting of the ways, the grief of farewell doubly deep; we stake our hot blood to repay those who know us; to the ends of the earth we share this one heart." In 1985, when I went to Guangzhou, Comrade Yang Yingbin of the Guangdong Provincial Committee, whom Elder Sister Yuan had told to come and see me, was one of the survivors who had set out for the front with Qian Yishi in those years; he still remembered the ardent scenes in which we, the representatives of Shanghai's all-circles resistance-and-salvation bodies, saw off our old Northern Expedition friends as they returned to the front to resist Japan and save the nation, and he said that the Shanghai papers of the day had all reported it. After Comrade Qian Yishi followed the army to Jiaxing in Zhejiang, he fell ill from overwork, and after many twists and turns was brought back to Shanghai, dying on 29 January 1939 when treatment proved of no avail. It was truly a case of "his high resolve unfulfilled, he died before his time, forever making heroes' tears wet their lapels!" In Shanghai we held a solemn memorial gathering to mourn him. Guo Moruo wrote an essay in his memory: "The political-work team that Mr. Yishi led was the first such team since the outbreak of the War of Resistance, and the first note of the revived Political Department. Yet Yishi, through the hardship of this work, fell ill and gave his life! Mr. Yishi thus became the first of the cultural men to give his life for the War of Resistance." (Boiling Broth Collection) Happily, his daughter and son-in-law (Xian Xinghai) and the rest all threw themselves actively into the work of resistance-and-salvation propaganda among the people; if the old comrade-in-arms knows it in the world below, he too will feel comforted.

The Women's Comfort Association had no large trucks and no pass to go to the front, so to comfort the troops at the front we could only borrow other people's vehicles when others went to the front to give comfort, and send the comfort goods up to the front. To guard against bombing by Japanese planes, the trucks were covered all over with tree branches as camouflage. The driver was Han Changfa, our family's chauffeur, a very quick-witted man. Each time we set out at night, without turning on the headlights, and, by the light of the Japanese army's searchlights, carefully drove the truck safely to the front. We not only delivered comfort goods but also had to rescue the wounded, bringing the wounded soldiers back early the next morning and taking them to the hospitals in the city.

At that time many of Shanghai's schools had become hospitals for wounded soldiers—some set up by the Red Cross, others temporary first-aid teams organized by student bodies themselves, in which both male and female students took part, rescuing wounded soldiers and doing comfort work as well, even writing letters home to reassure the soldiers' families. Chen Gaoyong ran a temporary training class on the fourth floor of the Sun Sun Company, and the students being trained even invited me to lecture on "how to do first-aid and comfort work." In the early days of the Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy, it had launched a drive to collect and donate scrap copper and old iron to send to the Hanyang Arsenal to be cast into guns and bullets. I formed the wives of the notables of the industrial, commercial, and economic world into a team and mobilized them to donate the useless copper and iron utensils in their homes to support the resistance. Zhigao's mother, the Elder Madame Pan, also answered the call and took the lead in donating the tall, heavy censers and candlesticks of cast copper and pewter from her home.

In the resistance-and-salvation movement, the dramatic world was very active, often using the ancient to allude to the present, and newly written historical plays were especially in vogue. After Guo Moruo returned to Shanghai, Comrade Tian Han organized patriotic figures of the dramatic and artistic world to hold a charity performance in aid of the resistance at the Xinguang Grand Theater: a newly written Peking opera, Liang Hongyu, telling the story of how Liang Hongyu beat the war drum to aid Han Shizhong in battle and resisted the invading Jin troops, so as to rouse the masses' patriotic passion for resistance. The famous Peking-opera actress Jin Suqin played the lead as Liang Hongyu. After Guo Moruo and I and the others had watched the play, we went backstage to give our regards to Jin Suqin. Guo Moruo even composed a poem on the spot and gave it to her: "Of old there was Liang Hongyu; today we see Jin Suqin; through the ages the same hatred of the foe, the same heart of a beautiful woman." Truly the words formed themselves into verse as he spoke—a talent brimming over. At that time the Peking- and Kunqu-opera world, the painting and calligraphy world, and even the athletes of the ball teams held charity performances, charity sales, and charity matches one after another, donating the money raised to the use of the resistance. In the painting and calligraphy world, He Xiangning (pen name the Elder of Twin Purity), heading it, held a painting and calligraphy charity sale with Li Qiujun (president of the Women's Painting and Calligraphy Association and a disciple of Zhang Daqian); famous painters such as Liu Haisu, and many unknown painters, eagerly brought out their own works to take part in the charity sale. Later, Li Qiujun, Qi Yiying, and Mao Wang Ruizhu, among others, together founded a home for the care and upbringing of disaster-stricken children, which held on until the liberation of Shanghai—no easy thing.

From 1937 to 1939, I served as a managing director of a dozen or more resistance-and-salvation bodies in Shanghai, and among these the activities of the Shanghai International Relief Association are especially worth mentioning. After the outbreak of all-out resistance, seven hundred thousand refugees from the areas around Shanghai poured into the concessions—supporting the old and leading the young, homeless, driven by hunger and cold, in a wretched plight—so that the work of relieving the refugees became an important part of Shanghai's resistance-and-salvation movement. Chen Zhigao, together with charitable figures such as the president of the Shanghai Chinese-Foreign Famine Relief Committee, the French Catholic priest Rao Jiaju (his Chinese name), had Father Rao come forward to found an International Relief Association. Father Rao had long been engaged in charitable relief work. Because one of his arms had been blown off by German artillery fire in the First World War, he was also called the "One-Armed Father." At the time, although China, France, the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union had not yet formally formed the Allied nations against international fascist aggression, they were each resisting the aggression of the German, Italian, and Japanese fascists in Europe and in Asia, and so in their larger aim they were as one. On the eve of the "August Thirteenth" Battle of Songhu, in order to protect the personal safety of the Western foreign residents in Shanghai, including the Jews, we had contrived to charter ships to send them away from Shanghai and evacuate them to relatively safe places; so the two sides had already had a relationship of friendly cooperation. After the International Relief Association was founded, it united all the units engaged in charitable relief work—those of the concession's Municipal Council, of the Protestant side, and of the Catholic side—along with certain warm-hearted foreigners. Father Rao was the foreign president of the International Relief Association; we invited the famous diplomat Dr. Yan Huiqing to serve as the Chinese president, and Chen Zhigao was one of the managing directors. Father Rao also served concurrently as director of the No. 1 International Shelter, with Chen Zhigao as deputy director; I was the general secretary, and the deputy general secretaries were Yao Qianxiu and Pan Da (Dacheng)—the few of us were the ones who did the actual work. Pan Da had originally been a student at Aurora University, knew French, and had his home in Annam (present-day Vietnam), then still a French colony. He had joined the Communist Party in Vietnam, and later came to Shanghai; seeing in the newspaper a notice placed by the Cultural Circles' Salvation Association—"Any patriotic youth wishing to take part in resistance-and-salvation work, please come to the newspaper office to register and fill out a form"—he went to fill out a form and apply to take part in resistance-and-salvation work, and happened upon Feng Wenbin, who knew Guo Moruo and me, who introduced him to come and find me. Because he knew French, we arranged for him to become a deputy general secretary of the International Relief Association's No. 1 Shelter as well. He took part in resistance work in China all along, and later worked with us at Qujiang in Guangdong, and together we welcomed the nationwide liberation; in the meantime he once returned to Vietnam. The head of the shelter's health section was Yu Liqun; among its members were Catholic nuns, who brought doctors and medicines and every day treated the refugees' illnesses at the shelter without charge, doing very good work. The head of the production section was Xu Keqiang.

On 12 November 1937, the Japanese army occupied Shanghai. On the 20th, the National Government announced the removal of the capital to Chongqing. Shanghai's concessions had now sunk into a "Solitary Island," and the leaders of the resistance-and-salvation bodies—Song Qingling, Shen Junru, Zou Taofen, Guo Moruo, and others—had to leave Shanghai secretly in batches. Since the Japanese army had already blockaded the mouth of the Yangtze, in order to cover their safe withdrawal, Zhigao, Jin Xuecheng, and others proposed to the Association for the Support of Resistance to the Enemy that the only course was to ask Father Rao to deal with the Japanese army headquarters and, in the name of evacuating refugees, to demand that a passenger steamer be let in, with the Japanese forces along the way not firing to intercept it. At that time Japan had not yet declared war on Britain, the United States, or France, and consent was, after all, obtained from the Japanese. Father Rao proposed that, to ensure safety, when the evacuees boarded the ship there should be no grand send-off at the wharf and no shouting of anti-Japanese slogans. On 27 November, I alone acted as representative to see them off at the wharf. The aged Ms. He Xiangning leaned on a cane; the youngest in the children's drama troupe were only around ten, the oldest only fourteen or fifteen—a whole shipload of "refugees," old and young, quietly withdrew from Shanghai. That day Han Changfa drove me there, and I waited until the ship gradually receded before going back. Guo Moruo wrote in his *Song of the Great Waves*: "In truth this steamer was quite worth the Japanese devils' dropping a few bombs on, yet they did not drop them. Only after we had passed the Wusong mouth did I learn that the ship was fully loaded with the Kuomintang's so-called party and government dignitaries. Of our own friends there were not a few either; Madame Liao and Zou Taofen both, unexpectedly, were on this same ship." Guo Moruo had come secretly to Shanghai on 27 July; four months later, again it was I who prepared Guo Moruo's luggage and helped him leave Shanghai secretly.

Yu Liqun's elder sister had originally had a special and deep affection for Guo Moruo, and out of discontent with the bondage of old-style marriage, she died a martyr to love. Yu Liqun put in order the poems and lyrics her sister had left behind and, through Yao Qianxiu, gave them to Guo Moruo. For Guo Moruo, the main thing was that, through his union with Yu Liqun, he wished to repay her sister's deep devotion to him. Some have said that the union of the two of them was arranged through my matchmaking; in fact this affair had nothing to do with me, which I explain here in passing.

Under the leadership of the Refugee Work Committee of the CCP Jiangsu Provincial Committee, the more than fifty refugee shelters of the Shanghai Federation of Charitable Bodies for United Relief (the "Charity Federation" for short), represented by Zhao Puchu, together with the Shanghai International No. 1 and No. 2 refugee shelters, became special positions for the Party's mass work. In the shelters the broad mass of refugees, through soliciting donations and saving themselves through production, had their livelihood provided for, and also received patriotic anti-Japanese education and class education. Our Party developed a batch of Party members among the progressive staff and the refugee compatriots in the shelters, and established and expanded the Party's organization. After a period of ideological education and organizational preparation, many young and able-bodied refugee compatriots whose consciousness had been raised proposed, one after another, at the rally on the first anniversary of a shelter's founding, to join the army and go to the front to fight the Japanese devils and avenge the wrongs done to nation and family. So, under the arrangement of the underground Party, they were secretly registered and given short-term concentrated training, with Liu Shaowen of the Eighth Route Army and Li Yimang of the New Fourth Army in charge of the training and dispatch. Outwardly, the International Relief Association proposed to the concession authorities: "In order to lighten Shanghai's food burden, we shall mobilize the young and able-bodied refugees in the various shelters to return to their home villages for production." This proposal was first seconded by the wife of the French counselor stationed in Shanghai and was passed at a meeting of the Municipal Council; then Zhigao again asked Father Rao to come forward and negotiate with the Japanese army headquarters that surrounded the Shanghai concessions, demanding that the Japanese forces not obstruct the refugees on their way home and guarantee their safety, and the Japanese agreed. For the refugees withdrawing from Shanghai by water, a French merchant steamer was chartered for safety's sake, with the Red Cross flag hoisted on it, so that they reached their destination smoothly. For this batch of "refugee compatriots" who went to join the New Fourth Army, a special send-off meeting was held—held at my home—and each small squad that joined the army was given a Red Cross first-aid kit, with encouragement to go to the anti-Japanese front, slay the enemy and win merit, and avenge the wrongs done to nation and family. Most of these refugees were orphans from the Zhabei district whose families had been broken and ruined after the Japanese occupation; every one of them had an account of blood and tears, and had no one left behind to worry about, so after short-term ideological education and training, they all signed up of their own accord to go to the anti-Japanese front.

The work in the refugee shelters was naturally carried on under the joint leadership of the Shanghai underground Party, the Eighth Route Office, and the representatives of the New Fourth Army; but it did indeed receive the coordination and support of upper-class patriotic figures of society and of the international anti-fascist united front, without which many things would have been very hard to accomplish. Shanghai had at that time several dozen refugee shelters, large and small, sheltering four hundred thousand refugees. Our International No. 1 Shelter alone, counting all who came and went, sheltered close to forty thousand refugee-visits in all.

In the work of relieving the refugees there was also one drive to deliver cotton-padded clothes that had a great impact. At that time, between the Japanese-occupied Nanshi district and the French Concession, there was a no-man's-land in between, called the Nanshi Relief Zone, where more than 15,000 refugees who wished to flee to the concessions from the various places in Shanghai's southern outskirts were gathered; but because several hundred thousand refugees had already crowded into the concessions, there was truly no strength to take in any more, and a boundary had to be drawn, so that no more of them could be let into the concessions. This batch of refugees lacked clothing and could hardly get through the bitter winter, so the municipal chamber of commerce and Xu Jiqing mobilized the whole city's cotton shops to donate cotton and cloth shops to donate cloth. By this time the Women's Comfort Association had been renamed the Women's Salvation Association, and it too donated all the cloth left over from the Women's Comfort Association's solicitations; fifteen sewing machines were gathered at the Sacred Heart Primary School, and the labor union mobilized workers of the sewing trade to make cotton clothes and trousers without charge. After two weeks of round-the-clock shift work in a concentrated push, more than 15,000 sets of cotton clothes and trousers were rushed out, and Zhigao, Father Rao, people from the French Concession's Municipal Council, and I went together to Nanshi to distribute them to the refugee compatriots.

In the work of relieving the refugees, we also strengthened the building of the Party's underground lines of communication. At this International No. 1 refugee shelter, the French Concession's Municipal Council issued each home-returning refugee a refugee certificate. With this refugee certificate, one could not only pass through the occupied zones held by the Japanese army, but also come and go between the rear areas under Kuomintang rule and the anti-Japanese guerrilla areas led by the Communist Party. Later, the refugee relief stations in the occupied zones everywhere also printed and issued "refugee certificates" of this kind, and this opened a breach for our Party's underground lines of communication, so that the communication network from Shanghai to all places could reach everywhere in all directions. It then spread so that underground communication and liaison points were established within every progressive resistance-and-salvation body. Some comrades who came from Yan'an to Shanghai (such as Feng Xuefeng) came and went through Shanghai by just such lines of communication. The person then in charge of central communications who remained on the Solitary Island was Comrade Gong Yinbing. This work was also commended by the Party Central, which regarded it as an innovation in underground communication work in the white areas.
