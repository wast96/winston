## Chapter 15

Bai Ping's temper and taste were like a mountain brook: lonely and apart, purling along of itself, rippling where it met the water-weeds, winding where the ground turned awkward, breaking into rapids at a boulder, plunging in a cataract at a cliff's edge. She drew you on forever, till you forgot the purpose of your life and pressed on at her side. Mei Yingzi was like a wave forever changing, now rising, now falling, startling and abrupt, dazzling your eyes and shaking your heart so that you were never your own master. But now, here before me, was a girl of another kind: like a river steady, level and even, without unlooked-for turns, without strange upheavals, existing free and at ease, so that you might moor upon her waters, or sail them as you pleased.

She had a bright, open forehead, long delicate eye-corners, a face full of life, and a lovely straight nose; her eyes were sea-blue, and her shy, girlish glance forever slipped away from anyone's gaze. Her lips drew a soft, gentle, well-marked curve, and again and again a dreamy smile took the place of speech, now and then showing the fine, small front teeth that were the emblem of her innocence and sweetness; and on her fair cheeks lay a faint scattering of freckles, so often the mark of a quiet and gentle nature.

This was Helen Manfield. She sat now across from me, in a bright, airy, elegant room lit up with lamplight. Her mother was busy over the dinner, and I began to talk with her—of school, of music, of America, of China. She told me her grandmother's family were in Canada, and that she had been born there; music, it seemed, was the art nearest to the temper of her mother's line. She had been studying singing five years now, and lately her progress seemed slow; her teacher said that once she was past this stretch she would come on quickly again, and told her not to lose heart in the least. I told her this happened in the learning of anything—it was what the psychology of learning calls a plateau, and it was a great pity how many drew back on reaching that plateau. There was a large piano in the corner of the room, and I asked whether she had studied it; she said her playing was very slight, and when I begged her to play a piece she would by no means consent.

She told me she was very fond of China, only she had not made many friends in Chinese households; her closest acquaintance just now was Miss Gao, though Miss Gao seemed to her no different from a European or American.

When the talk turned to films, her liking for them proved so slight it surprised me; at her age she ought to have been an ardent devotee of the cinema. But she said that for a story she would sooner read a novel, for acting sooner see a stage play, and for music sooner go to a concert—she loved all three of those, and cared only for the cinema not at all. She said, too, that Shanghai had no theatre, which left her little wish to go out, and that listening to the wireless at home and reading novels were her best amusements.

Dinner-time came, and Mrs. Manfield came out in a black evening gown. Helen went in, and returning had changed into a white one; she walked slow and soft, with a grace of a thousand kinds that bent one's heart. I wondered that an American family should keep so rigidly to this English custom in Shanghai, and learned only afterward that her grandmother had been English, emigrated to Canada, and that her mother had been reared all her life in an English household.

Mrs. Manfield had cooked the dinner herself, and it was a taste indeed not to be had in the common Western restaurants of Shanghai. Helen put on the wireless, and to that lovely music we savoured the evening pleasures of an English home. We spoke little, all of us, but again and again I caught the meaning that came through beneath Helen's dreamy smile: her spirit all the while opened and drew in again within the music.

When the coffee came, Mrs. Manfield turned off the wireless and began to ask about my family, my native place, my interests and tastes. She told me her husband was serving his country in the air force, and that her two sons were both in the American army. Her second son and Helen, she said, had the higher gift for music, and she set great hopes on Helen. She told me Helen now studied singing under Professor Paci, that she and her husband were dear friends of the Pacis, who set especial hopes on Helen and wished, when the war was over, to send her to Italy. Her own accomplishment in music, she said, had been sacrificed wholly to love and marriage and the bearing of children, and she hoped her daughter would achieve what she herself might have; she believed with all her heart in her daughter's future, and said that if only Helen were not hemmed in by love and marriage and children she would surely reap some marvellous harvest. Mrs. Stephen and the household of Professor Paci, she added, were forever encouraging Helen, and she hoped I too would often give her guidance and encouragement. An artist, she said again, ought to sacrifice all for art; and for a woman artist, her true husband ought to be art itself—Mrs. Manfield's warm sincerity and her hopes for her daughter moved me to deep sympathy.

Afterward Helen talked with me of novels—many that we had all of us read—and though her opinions often ran to one side, in many places she showed real discernment; she loved to hear my own views, and found much in them she had never thought of before. There were many books I had not read, which she carried out in her arms and asked me to give her my opinion of once I had read them; and many she had not read, of which she would say, if I had the book by me, that I must lend it her.

I left the Manfields' at ten o'clock; Helen made me up a parcel of books to carry off, and charged me to send her every book of mine she had not yet seen.

The next day, home again, I sent someone with books for Helen; but before I had so much as opened the ones she had lent me, she had sent them back, and with them a long letter discussing her impressions of her reading and asking what I had felt on reading the books she had lent me. This drove me to read her books in haste for the sake of that answer, and so our correspondence began; after that she came now and then to see me at my home, and I often went to hers.

This friendship helped me to carry out, firmly, the new life I had laid down for myself, and adorned that new life as well.

By now my life had settled. Each morning I could read to good purpose, and after noon I took a disciplined nap; toward evening I would often go out to walk and to drink tea, and now and then to call on Bai Ping, on Mrs. Stephen, on Helen, often taking a meal at one of their three houses; after which I went home and worked, every day, deep into the night. Helen always came to see me just as I was waking from my nap, and if I had not woken she would wait for me in the study; Bai Ping came to see me now and then, but rarely, and when she came it was always deep in the night, and often we talked till the fifth watch. Sometimes, at night, when I had brought a piece of writing to a stop and my spirits were still good, I would go and call on Bai Ping myself. For some months my mind was in fine balance and my work bore much fruit. Stephen came to see me no more now—which was of course Bai Ping's doing—and we met only by appointment at a café at dusk, now and then, to talk; Mei Yingzi I chanced upon rarer still, though when we met we laughed and talked as gaily as ever. All my friendships seemed now nicely adjusted, and gave me, it seemed, a greater faith in the single life, and a greater ease in it. Only Helen's friendship for me seemed slowly to be growing; in the course of her borrowing books from me its range seemed by degrees to widen, and had reached by now the range of philosophy. Of this I had never once thought—until one night in the tail of summer.

That afternoon Helen came to see me, and we talked on until dusk; then I walked with her nearby, and we dined at a Jewish restaurant on the corner of Route Winling and Avenue Joffre. After dinner I saw her onto the tram and came slowly home alone, sat down at my desk, and set to the thing I meant to do; but not an hour had passed when the telephone called me to answer it. Guessing it was Bai Ping, I took it up and said at once:

"Is that Bai Ping?"

But at the other end was a woman speaking English, in a voice so strange to me.

"Is that Mr. Xu?"

"It is."

"This is Mrs. Manfield."

"Ah, Mrs. Manfield, how do you do?" I said. "Is Helen home yet?"

But she seemed to pay these words no heed, and said:

"Have you time just now? I should like to come and see you at once."

"Very well, I'll wait for you," I said. Her voice seemed full of anxiety, and so I said: "Is anything the matter?"

"I'll come and see you at once," she said, and with that hung up.

What, then, could the matter be? Could something have gone wrong with Helen on her way home? And if not that—what? Could mother and daughter have quarrelled? What other cause could there be to bring Mrs. Manfield to see me at once? I could think of no reason, and so waited on tenterhooks, smoking; and when I had smoked my second cigarette to the end, there came the sound of a car outside. I hurried out to look—it had already flashed past—and so I strolled at the mouth of the lane. I waited while four cars went by; the fifth was a brand-new red Ford, coming quickly out of the distance, and I saw that the driver within was a girl dressed in red, who slowed, it seemed, as she reached me—it looked like Mei Yingzi. I watched her stop the car: quite right, it was Mei Yingzi, and she opened the door with a smile:

"Waiting for me, Mr. Xu?" Again I caught her rich, sweet fragrance.

Mei Yingzi came to see me on my own account very seldom, and several of those times had been in Stephen's company; so I supposed she was only passing, and had stopped to greet me on seeing me at the door. I said:

"So beautifully turned out—where are you off to?"

"When I am beautifully dressed, the first thing, of course, is to come and see you." It was long since I had seen her show those even, almond-tinted, glistening white front teeth.

"How lovely!" I took her hand and looked at her clothes. She wore a white silk blouse, a red jacket, a skirt of creamy white sprigged with little blue flowers and red hearts, and on her bare feet a pair of soft-soled white canvas shoes with blue trim and a half-heel. From her nose, her lips, her throat, her breast on down, every rise and fall was nature's loveliest curve. In wonder I praised her: "You really can carry off any dress in all the world."

"Thank you." About her there always breathed that fragrance peculiar to her—what scent she used I never knew. "Have you really come specially to see me?" I asked.

"Of course," she said.

"Thank you." I walked her into the lane, and went on: "I don't think I've ever seen anyone whom every kind of dress and adornment suits as it suits you."

"It's the first time I've heard a man praise me so," she said. "And would you use these same words to praise an innocent, pure young girl?" She leaned against me gravely.

"Perhaps I would," I said; "but so far I never have."

"Don't tell fibs," she said. "It's about that very story that I've come to talk with you today."

We came into the room; I switched on the light and the electric fan, and she sat down near the fan and said:

"You know, don't you, a certain young girl we all acknowledge to have a gift for singing?"

"You mean Helen Manfield?"

"Yes," she said. "But of late she has quite cooled toward her music."

"How so?" I said. "I shouldn't have thought it."

"At Professor Paci's tea-party today he said, with great regret, that Helen has lately been thinking of giving up music."

"Thinking of giving up singing?" I was utterly astonished. How was it Helen had never once spoken of it to me?—I thought.

"Yes," she said.

"Ah—"

"What is it?"

"Mrs. Manfield telephoned me a moment ago to say she was coming to see me—it must be about this very thing," I said.

"I daresay it is." She rose, took a few steps, and sat down close beside me. "Her mother is too grieved over it," she said. "You know, I suppose, what hopes she has for her daughter."

"Of course," I said. "When all of us have full marks of hope for Helen, her mother's must run past a hundred."

"And more than that," she said. "Do you know her mother's past?"

"That she too had a high gift for singing."

"Her family set the greatest hopes on her," she said; "but she fell in love with an American airman. Her musical family opposed it with all their might, and in the end she ran off with her lover and married him elsewhere."

"And that was Mr. Manfield."

"That was Helen Manfield's father," she said. "From then on she gave up music, and so on her gifted daughter she sets a hundred marks of hope more than the common run of parents." With that she rose again, went to the table, and took up a cigarette.

"You've taken to smoking too?" I asked.

"Just for fun, now and then." She held the cigarette and looked at it. "This kind I've never smoked, though."

"Era," I said, giving her a light; "I'm afraid you won't care for it."

She drew on the cigarette, went and leaned against the desk, blew a mouthful of smoke into the air, and watching it disperse said, calm, certain and slow:

"And yet now Mrs. Manfield's daughter is about, again, for love's sake, to fail the gift God gave her, and the hopes that men have placed in her."

"For love?" I asked.

"I alone know it, and I've told no one else: she is in love with a man," she said. "And I feel it right to tell you."

The door opened all at once, and the servant showed in Mrs. Manfield; her plump face wore a faint smile, and in that smile lay hidden a certain dejection, and at the sight of me she seemed to draw some comfort:

"Xu!" She shook my hand warmly, and shook Mei Yingzi's too. "How good of you—here about my affair even before I am."

I bade her sit down. She was too stout to catch her breath, and had walked a little way besides, so that she did not speak, and Mei Yingzi asked:

"Did you come by tram?"

"Yes."

Then, taking a sip of the soda-water I had poured her, she said:

"I expect Mei Yingzi has already told you—my daughter means, all of a sudden, to give up music."

As I listened to her, a thousand kinds of unease rose in me unawares; my heart beat fast, my eyes wished to escape her gaze, and I said not a word, but heard her let out a breath and say:

"You have been too good to her—lending her books, instructing her, opening her mind." She paused a moment and went on: "But she is a child too fond of using her thoughts, and now she is sunk deep in the books you lent her; she has no heart for her singing-practice, but reads all day and copies out notes, and lately keeps saying she wants to study philosophy." Of a sudden she changed her tone: "Xu, you mustn't for a moment take it amiss—I am not blaming you; but she trusts you greatly, and you can sway her, and so I have come to talk it over with you, and to beg you to find some way to persuade her not to give up music." And all at once she asked me: "Do you think she has a special gift for music?"

"Of course," I said.

"I am sure she is not suited to the study of philosophy."

"Of course," I said.

Mei Yingzi stole a look at me, with a mischievous smile, and I said:

"This is quite beyond anything I looked for. I talked with her of art, and it drew in questions of philosophy; she asked to borrow books, and of course I lent them. I had fully supposed that books of thought and philosophy would enrich an artist's soul—how could I have dreamed she would change where her interest lay?"

"I don't mean to blame you in the least," said Mrs. Manfield earnestly. "What I hope now is that you will be so good as to reason with her, and bring her interest back to singing."

"I will certainly reason with her—and I believe I can make her give up philosophy," I said. "This is no grave matter at all, Mrs. Manfield; set your mind at rest."

"I too think it a very simple matter," said Mei Yingzi, smiling archly at me. "I'm sure I can help you keep Helen from failing her gift."

"In the psychology of learning, I think, when we reach a plateau in our study, the slowness of our progress often makes us take an interest in some other subject; and when we reach the plateau of that other subject, we grow weary of it in turn," I said. "In short, it all rests on me, and I will surely bring her back to the future that lies before her in singing."

The knot of anxiety between Mrs. Manfield's brows seemed to loosen, and her moist eyes let out a gleam of gratitude; she nodded, and her double chin stirred with a soft, gentle motion.

"Mrs. Manfield, leave this affair to us, and let us talk no more of it now," said Mei Yingzi. "Suppose we go out and take the cool air?"

Mrs. Manfield made no objection, and I could hardly do otherwise than agree, so I came out to accompany the two of them. That red car was truly tempting, and I said:

"May I drive?"

"Very well," said Mei Yingzi. But as I was seeing Mrs. Manfield into the back seat, Mei Yingzi had already taken the place beside the driver's; I shut the door for Mrs. Manfield and got into the driver's seat, and Mei Yingzi said:

"This is the first time I've watched you drive."

"I'm afraid I'm quite out of practice," I said. "Where shall we go?"

"Jessfield Park," she said.

There were people enough on the street, and the way was bright with life; far off the moonlight was clear as washed jade, and the thronging stars shone bright, as I drove west at thirty-five miles an hour. All at once I felt within me a glory past telling—because, of course, Mei Yingzi sat beside me: her beauty, her elegance, that sweet fragrance she carried. It was the first time I had felt what scent can be to a person's spirit. I remembered I had once written a little essay on how modern culture is spread by the eyes and the ears alone—education pours in only through eye and ear, art performs only for eye and ear, politics too makes its appeal only to eye and ear—a very strange development, as though mankind had forgotten it had a nose at all. If we could take our cultural pleasures through the sense of smell, that would surely be a curious realm: we might invent books and journals of smell, whose ideas and meanings were only sets and sets of odours, to be grasped by a sniff of the nose; we might have rich and lovely smells, closely composed, breaking off and taking up again, like a symphony in music, for our noses to savour; a statesman, too, might brew a particular smell so that men, catching it, would believe in his doctrine. As things are, with only ear and eye admitted to the pleasures of culture, the nose is greatly wronged. But today Mei Yingzi's sweet fragrance was beside me, borne on the wind at the car window, now faint, now strong, rising to me by fits, and made me feel a strange enchantment; and though it gave me no fixed meaning as a painter's canvas or a musician's music does, it seemed nonetheless a temptation of its own, apart from sight and hearing. Mei Yingzi looked straight out of the window; I saw her profile aslant, and for an instant I truly could not believe I was in the world of men—when suddenly she said, with a laugh:

"Ai—ai—ai—what are you doing?"

I braked and turned my head: the car was already slewed across the road.

"What is it?" Mei Yingzi turned her head and laughed.

"Will you drive?" I was a little abashed, but composed myself at once, opened the door and got out, and went round to the left; by the time I got back in she had already drawn on a pair of white gloves and taken the right-hand seat; I sat down at her side, took out a cigarette, and lit it with my lighter. I said:

"It's long since I drove—I'm out of practice."

"I rather think the sunlight dazzled your eyes." She laughed, pressed down with both feet, and the car ran straight ahead; she sat with the ease and grace of a seasoned driver, comfortable and lovely, racing along the road at forty-two or forty-three miles an hour. I began to feel a kind of freedom: the smell of my cigarette had by now driven off her sweet fragrance, as though a counter-propaganda had taken effect, and let me admire the loveliness of her bearing from a better distance. There was a wind, and her hair swayed like wisps and flakes of cloud, and her collar and cuffs were like the rays of the sun about to rise. This red wave of her made me think of fire, of red roses filling a field, of the red silk a Spanish bullfighter shakes before the bull; I could not but turn my eyes from it—yet in the end I looked again at her profile, at the curve from her brow to her two knees, a harmony of softness and strength, a fusion of motion and stillness. Her two hands held the wheel, the speed-needle steady all the while at forty-two, forty-three; she took two bends, and then stopped short—we had arrived.

There was not too great a press of people in the park, and we saw the moonlight clearer and the starlight lovelier; beyond the reach of the lamps, moon and stars had touched the lawns to a softness like water. A few children ran about like the little deer and hares of a mountain wood, as though the black-green thickets were their very home. We strolled with Mrs. Manfield; washed by the cool wind of our racing drive, she seemed loosed from her heart's gloom, and the open prospect widened her breast the more, so that a smile came to her face. We walked and chatted idly, and I believed Mrs. Manfield no longer fretted over the matter of a while ago.

We sat down with Mrs. Manfield at an ice-stall, and after a little ice our spirits were all refreshed and our moods all glad; we spoke of no troubles of life, but only, in scattered snatches, of the people and stories of society, and there were many silences, as though we were all of us breathing the moonlight. In one such spell of silence I thought I would go and walk a while alone; I stood up, smoking, and said:

"I'll go over there a moment and come straight back."

I trod the soft, moist grass and strolled toward the pond. The chairs at its edge were all taken; a few of the pairs seemed lovers in their first love. On the pond the moonlight was uncommonly bright, and the water was strewn here and there with water-lilies; a little farther off a few blooms had opened, ghostly pale and exquisite, with a light, ethereal beauty. Standing by the pond, I began to notice how the lamplight behind me cast my faint shadow out to the pond's heart, where it wove and stirred among several others; and one of these was slowly lengthening, and slowly fading again. All at once it struck me that I seemed to know her; and looking up, I saw the back of a woman in a white dress, a black patent-leather belt at her waist, a black book and a light-coloured paper parcel tucked under her arm, drifting slowly toward the thickets. The moonlight had touched the grass to water; there was no one else at all, and walking upon it she was for all the world a water-lily. Looking, I thought she was really rather like Helen; looking closer, I thought her not like at all—but in the end I went round the pond after her.

She went in among the thickets, and I followed a full ten feet behind. Watching her pace over the moon-shadows, her head bowed as she wandered, I thought her now Helen and now not, until she came slowly out of the trees. Beyond was a stretch of lawn, and across the lawn a little stream; she looked up at the sky, and then, at her ease, kicked lightly at the short grass. By now I had settled it beyond doubt that she was Helen. With whom, then, had she come? Had she come alone? Or, like me, had she left the companions she came with to walk by herself? I thought to call to her, but of a sudden felt I would rather see where she was going, and so followed her still. The moonlight overhead was of a piercing clearness, and there was no one walking on the lawn; feeling myself only too easy a mark for her to catch sight of, I stood at the edge of the thicket and waited until she had put twenty feet between us before I moved—but I saw that she was not coming toward where the people were, but going straight on to the stream. Taking another line, at forty-five degrees to hers, I came toward the little wooden bridge at the stream's right end, watching her all the while. She stood a moment at the stream's edge, leaned against a tree, and gazed into the water's heart; and I, having reached the side of the bridge, saw her standing there without a stir, and so crossed by the bridge to the far bank, lit a cigarette, came opposite her, and, leaning aslant against a small tree, stole a look at her. She kept her gaze on the water's heart—whether she watched the stars and moon at its bottom, or the water-lilies on its face, I could not tell—a faint sorrow between her brows, and at the corner of her mouth a ripple, half a smile and not a smile. Her dress was white as the water-lilies, and under the moon she seemed a statue of white stone, standing without the least movement. When I had smoked my cigarette to the end and saw her still unmoving, I tossed the stub to the very spot she gazed on; the water gave a hiss that broke the stillness of the universe, and she seemed to start a little, and raised her head. I said in a low voice:

"Miss—has a star fallen into the water?"

"So it really is you, Xu." A dreamy smile rose at the corner of Helen's mouth.

"So it really is me?" I thought. "How did she know it was me? Can she have found out long ago that I was there watching her?" As I was thinking it, she said again from the far bank:

"Just as I was wondering how that star at the bottom of the stream was like you, you appeared."

"When I caught sight of you, I took you for one of the water-lilies stolen ashore to play."

She laughed, and looked for a way across the stream; at last, spying the little bridge, she ran to it as though dancing, and I ran to the bridge too, and we met at its crown, and I took her hand and said:

"Now I won't have you turn into a water-lily again."

Her hand was a little cold, and I let it go and said:

"Are you cold?"

"No." So saying, she wiped her hands with a handkerchief, walked at my side, took my arm, and said: "Did you come alone?"

"No," I said; "and you?"

"Alone."

"You're deceiving me," I said. "I saw your mother quite plainly, sitting at the ice-stall."

"Nonsense," she said, half laughing and half cross.

"Let's see, then, which of us is talking nonsense," I said, and walked with her straight toward the ice-stall, and asked: "Is it the artist come seeking the old traces of feeling? Or the philosopher searching out an object for thought?"

"I feel now that philosophy is the highest art of all."

"I have heard philosophy called the sum of all knowledge; I have heard it called the handmaid of religion; I have heard it, too, called the science of sciences," I said. "And now I hear it is an art."

"Then do you think what I say will hold?" she asked, as gravely as when we discussed questions out of books in the ordinary way.

"Perhaps," I said, more gravely too. "But it is only a conjecture. To prove such a conjecture one needs a rigorous method, and must temper it with wide and various material. That is the work of science."

"Then you think writing novels is the work of science too."

"Strictly speaking, the foundation of all art is scientific—is the training of music not a science?"

"Yes—the training of every technique is scientific," she said. "And so this art of philosophy, in its basic training, is scientific too."

"Then are all philosophers artists?" I asked in protest.

"Yes," she said. "Only, this kind of artist—his creation is a single whole; in his whole life he has but one work of art, and that work depends forever on his imagination to fill it out and revise it."

"And you would like to be an artist of that kind!"

"I can only say I have an interest in it."

"But everyone thinks you have a special gift for singing."

"Which is to say I have no gift for philosophy."

"I believe genius is a rare thing; to have one gift is already something wonderful."

"…" She smiled and said nothing, and I too fell silent. We walked on quiet and unhurried, turning at the edge of a thicket beyond which lay the ice-stall. But just at the turning I saw Mei Yingzi, standing alone by the trees, as though she had not seen us, and I called to her:

"How is it you're here all alone?"

"I'm listening to the stars and the water-lilies talk." Her words startled me greatly—could she have heard all our conversation? But half probing and half in jest I said:

"Talking love-talk, were they? Why, this is a tale of the sun and the moon."

"I didn't follow it," she said with a laugh; "for I am neither an artist nor a philosopher."

There was no sarcasm in this, and no jealousy; her bright, clear tone only showed that she had heard our talk—yet it seemed very strange to me.

"…" I wanted very much to ask when she had come over, but I did not.

"Even artists and philosophers are only mortals, and you are a fairy." Helen smiled at Mei Yingzi and walked on her left; I moved to Mei Yingzi's right and said:

"The sun's rays shine over all the day—but only today do I learn that they shine over the night as well."

We could see the ice-stall by now, and I had caught sight of Mrs. Manfield, when Mei Yingzi said to me:

"We waited and grew quite out of patience; we guessed you'd run into someone you knew—Mrs. Manfield guessed Bai Ping or Stephen, and I guessed Helen, and so I came to find you, and I've won, sure enough."

"So you came with my mother after all," said Helen. "Then how did you guess it was me he met?"

"I thought that if he'd met anyone else he'd have come straight back with them at once; only meeting you could have delayed him all this while," said Mei Yingzi.

"…" Helen seemed to take her to mean the particular feeling I had for her, and so said nothing. But I knew she meant that I had been alone with Helen, reasoning with her. Helen let go of Mei Yingzi and ran, as though dancing, to her mother.

"Were you following us the whole time?" I asked Mei Yingzi.

"…" She nodded and smiled.

"And did you discover anything?"

"A star at the bottom of the stream, keeping company with a pure white water-lily," she said, smiling in triumph.

On the way back—because I had arranged for Helen to come and see me at four the next afternoon, and Mei Yingzi had said she would come at ten that night to hear how I had fared—I slept badly the whole night through once I was home. I turned over how I ought to frame my words to bring her interest and her will back to singing; from that shallow probing talk of the night before, I had already found the thing was not so easy as I had supposed. Yet for the sake of my own dignity before Mrs. Manfield and Mei Yingzi, it seemed I simply must bring it off; and indeed, considering Helen's own future, for her to give up singing and study philosophy was truly a great blunder.

The next day.

In the morning I rose early and went to the flower-market to buy flowers; I bought up every kind of white flower to be had, and among them four pots of water-lilies. Home again, I set the room in order: I spread all the tables with white cloths, and made white the keynote of the room. After lunch I had a good nap, and woke at two; I read in the room, but kept thinking over the steps of the day's talk. At four Helen came, in a pure-white short-sleeved gown of ramie gauze, through the collar and cuffs of which I could see her cream-coloured silk chemise beneath. She carried a great bunch of bright-red roses, and coming in went at once to look for the vase on my stand—for she would often bring flowers to replace the withered stalks in my vase—but today the vase already held the white flowers I had arranged that morning. She looked about her and laughed, at a loss.

I took the white flowers out of the vase and gave them to the servant to find another vase upstairs, so that Helen's red roses might go into the empty one. I said:

"Is there a little of last night's air under the moon about the place today?"

"Mm…" Helen looked about her and said, "There is." And setting the red roses in the middle of the white cloth, she said: "Let them stand for Mei Yingzi's radiance."

"Is your mother still grieving over you?"

"That can't be helped."

"She hopes too much of you."

"Yes—too much of me." She laid weight on the word "too."

"Your mother came to my place yesterday," I said. "Did the two of you, mother and daughter, have some quarrel yesterday?"

"Of late she's often displeased that I read more and practise my singing less."

"And so you went off to Jessfield Park alone," I said.

"I wonder at it—why does she always suppose I have only the talent she handed down to me?"

"No—I don't know how she thinks of it," I said. "But in all our circle—Professor Paci's household, say, and Mrs. Stephen and Mei Yingzi—everyone feels your giving up singing would be too great a loss to us."

"Do you think so too?"

"Of course," I said. "What I mean is: for you, music at least can fill out your own life more than philosophy can."

"Not altogether."

"Is it that you've found your progress in singing too slight of late?"

"…" She was deep in thought.

"This is the plateau stage of learning," I said. "Every kind of study has this stage, and reaching it often lessens our interest in the study. In time, within philosophy, you will reach that stage too—and will you change again then?"

"It's very possible," she said. "I always feel you all hope too much of me. Why is it that if I study a little singing you expect me to become a singer, and if I read a few books of philosophy you expect me to become a philosopher? It is a dreadful thing."

"That is because what you show is genius."

"I don't know whether that is a compliment or an insult," she said. "In human society, parents, family, friends, society itself, forever bind a person with many duties and many titles, and bid one sacrifice oneself to them." She went on: "I don't love these things. I love singing, because it brings my soul a joy of rapture and of rising up; I love philosophy, because it leads me to think a little on what is more lasting, to think on questions wider and farther off, finer and more fundamental."

"But genius is a fact, not a title," I said.

"If that fact exists, it is only because my voice is fuller and sweeter than other people's—and how is that different from a person's having greater strength?" She had a strange excitement about her today, and ran straight on in one breath: "And on this you bid me not fail the gift—study, study, study!—so as one day to wait upon a crowd at a concert; and how is that any different from your working a strong man to the bone all day at hard labour for your own enjoyment?"

"Perhaps," I said; "but we live in the world to make it, so far as we can, perfect. We take our enjoyment of society, and so we must give to society in return. This is love. Many people love us, and we love others; our forebears left us beautiful things of their making, and we in turn make things for those who come after us."

"But I am not a machine, set to turn out toothpaste and bound to turn out toothpaste forever. Why may I not think of making toothbrushes instead?" she said, very indignant.

"Of course—how could I meddle with your interests, Helen?" I saw all at once that my manner had trespassed too far upon her own nature, and my voice went very low and soft. "If I talk to you of these things, it is really because your mother is so grieved for you, and your friends think it such a pity for you. And I have besides a guilt of my own—that your interest in philosophy was kindled by me. If it should ruin your future in music, how great would my sin be then?"

"Then you too have no faith in my talents in any other line?"

"I feel only that in the study of philosophy the road is still too long, and there are within it many, many tangled and toilsome paths; whereas in singing you have already put in your hard labour," I said calmly. "If it were philosophy you had laboured hard at in the past, and your mother now bade you study singing, I should certainly be against your mother's wish just the same."

The coffee and refreshments were brought in; Helen sat down in silence at the table, and I rose too, and said:

"It is because life is short, and we always hope to make something of ourselves."

Helen made no answer, nor looked at me; she poured my coffee and added sugar, and I watched her in silence, aware that my eyes were full of pleading and of hope. She stirred her own cup, watching the milk and coffee mingle, a yellow ring turning in the cup, paling from deep yellow to gold. Slowly she raised her head, gave me a glance, and looking at the red roses on the table, fingering them, said:

"It is because singing can no longer fill the emptiness of my soul; I feel, again and again, a loneliness past telling. Only when I have finished a book of philosophy, and am turning over in my mind the questions I have read in it, am I filled."

"Is it true, Helen?"

"…" She nodded, her eyes fixed on me, and her lids seemed a little moist.

"…" I turned my eyes from hers and was silent.

For a long, long while we were both of us silent, and then I said:

"Will you take a little refreshment?" And so saying I passed her the plate.

"Thank you." She took a piece and fell silent again. So after a while I said:

"It's strange to me that one who can sing should be unwilling to sing out the melancholy of her heart."

"I have no melancholy now—only emptiness," she said. "Melancholy is of a moment; emptiness lasts."

"Perhaps," I said in a low voice, groping for something to say, but not a word could I find. I had nothing to comfort her with, because I had nothing to comfort myself with. I let a heavy, heavy silence press upon our lips and our ears, while the light of the sky slowly darkened to grey.

Toward seven Helen said she must go home, and I saw her out; all the way we were silent. Usually I saw her to the bus-stop and went home only after she had boarded, but today, coming to the bus-stop, she did not halt, but walked straight on. Without a word I followed her, and as we neared the second stop she said:

"Go back now."

"Won't you dine out with me?"

"I should like to get home early."

"Then wait for your bus here."

"I'll walk a while."

"Then I'll walk with you a while."

"No," she said; "go back."

"No."

"Then I'll board here," she said, and stopped.

At last a bus came again, and I watched her board and take her seat, then walked back alone the way I had come. I thought of my appointment with Mei Yingzi, and was sorry now I had not pressed Helen with some deeper, weightier reasoning while I could.

But what use was any of this reasoning? All logical argument seemed now to be empty; hers was an emptiness and loneliness of the heart, and what we needed was to help her fill it. The sky was very dark by now, and a loneliness past telling stole over my heart; and with a start I recalled Mei Yingzi's words—could it be true that her feeling for me was something more than friendship? I was afraid, with a fear past telling. That fear proved the depth of my own feeling for her. In all our friendship till now neither of us had found it out, and yet in an instant it seemed to stand before me as a fact. It was the lamps—casting my shadow on the ground, turning it from behind me to my side, and then before me—it was the lamps; I thought of what Mrs. Stephen had said—it was the lamps, it was the lamps!

Home again, I was told Mrs. Stephen had telephoned; I rang her back, and she asked whether I had time that night and hoped I would come to her place to talk. I told her Mei Yingzi was coming, and she asked me instead to a plain lunch the next morning. I knew what she wished to talk of was Helen's affair too, and so I agreed.

At ten Mei Yingzi came, in a suit of soft yellow shot with silver thread; and coming in and seeing the white flowers all about and the white keynote of the room, she said:

"So your reasoning has failed?"

"I did not reason with her."

"Then my conjecture is proved."

"Not quite that either," I said.

"Then why did you not reason with her?"

"I found this is no matter to be settled by reasoning and argument, but a matter of the mind, and must be changed from her way of life. She is too still, too abstract, too without the tastes of the young," I said. "I think now only you can help her—take her through some lively days. She needs exercise, she needs society; you can take her to play tennis, to swim, take her into gay company."

"Yes," said Mei Yingzi, laughing; "if you can bear to give her over to me."

"Why speak of my bearing to?"

"What I mean is—if you are willing to give up the temptation of philosophy."

"I don't understand you."

She was silent, her two hands in her pockets, walking about, and then turned round of a sudden and said:

"I think that your setting such a mood to receive her is a temptation in itself."

"And what has that to do with her loving philosophy or singing?"

"It is a thing of the unconscious," she said. "Below her consciousness she simply loves you—and studying philosophy is her weapon."

"Don't speak of her so," I said.

"Then from today on will you seek her out no more, and see her no more—can you do it?"

"Perhaps…" I said.

"It is not a question of 'perhaps.'"

"Perhaps I really do love her?"

"You will destroy her whole future."

"Nonsense," I said. "I would create her future."

"Then you do love her?" She dropped her voice very low, and asked with a faint sigh, in earnest.

I was silent, and rose, and passing beyond her gaze, with my back to her, said:

"Very well—for three months I will not meet her alone. If your work comes to nothing, then give her back to me; and if you set her feelings right, let us take up our friendship again."

"Very well." She put out a hand like a narcissus, held mine tight a while, and smiled most sweetly; then she took her leave, and as she went kissed the red roses on the table. I said:

"Those were brought by Helen—she said they stood for your matchless radiance."

"And I rather thought you had set them there to stand for my red dress last night, breaking in upon the white mood of the two of you," she said, and plucking off one bloom came and put it in my buttonhole, saying:

"I give you my blessing."

I saw her spring up into the red car, and away she went as though in flight.
