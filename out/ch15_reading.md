### Opening a New Chapter (Part 2)

### Liu Shaobai: Tribute Scholar, Provincial Assemblyman, Enlightened Gentleman

As recounted earlier, Liu Shaobai had once made contact in Tianjin with Hu Egong and Yang Xianzhen and joined them in the effort to rescue the arrested leaders of the Party's Shun-Zhi provincial committee. He too was a figure of some legend. In the last years of the Qing he had been a tribute scholar; in the early Republic, a member of the Shanxi provincial assembly and a gentleman of the northwest of the province. Because he never stopped reaching for something better, searching out the way to save the nation and its people, he broke free of the old traditions at last and passed from a sympathizer of the Communist Party into a member of the Chinese Communist Party.

Yang Xianzhen, in the memorial inscription he composed for Liu Shaobai in 1987, described his origins and early years:

Liu Shaobai, given name Jiageng, styled Shaobai. Born June 1883 in Heiyukou village, Xing county, Shanxi. He entered a private school at six, quick-minded and fond of study, brilliant in his youth, and his father's hopes for him ran high. At his father's strict command he went to Xi'an to sit the Qing dynasty's last provincial examination, and was chosen a tribute scholar. In 1905 he passed into the Taiyuan Prefectural Middle School. During his studies he first took up the new learning, and his thinking changed sharply. Those were the years when the great powers pressed in on every side and the Qing court was rotten with corruption; Shaobai grieved for his country and its people, and resolved to save the nation.

In 1911, at the first rising of Wuchang, Shaobai, then still a student at Shanxi University, left off his studies and returned home to raise the county in response. The next year he was elected to the Shanxi provisional provincial assembly. In 1918 he graduated from Shanxi University with a bachelor's degree in law. After the 1911 Revolution the civil wars still came one after another and the foreign menace was not lifted, and Shaobai was bitterly disappointed. Through the long night he went on searching, high and low. Then the thunderclap of May Fourth woke the sleeping land of China, and under the surge of the new revolutionary tide he began to take up Marxism-Leninism, and grasped that China's one road out was to take Russia as its teacher.

In 1927, when Chiang Kai-shek launched the April Twelfth coup and slaughtered Communists on a great scale, many of Shaobai's own students were cruelly killed, and it stirred him to a towering indignation. Heedless of his own safety, he stepped forward and did all he could to shelter underground Party members and revolutionaries. Among those he helped to safety were Wang Hu, Zhu Zhihan, He Hangxiu, He Shuzhi, Chen Yuandao, Zhao Shilan, Li Hui, and many more. In this same period he braved the danger and resolutely joined the Party's outer organization, the Mutual Aid Society.

Liu Shaobai came of a landlord family, but in his youth he had read the works of the Western bourgeois thinkers, and he loathed the poison that the feudal rites worked upon the people; in his conduct he often did startling things. At the time of the 1911 Revolution he was studying at Shanxi University. (He had entered the school's preparatory law course at twenty-five, and there he both studied and taught; only in 1918, at thirty-six, did he graduate from the university's law and political science department with a bachelor's degree in law.) In October 1911, when the Wuchang rising succeeded, the whole country was roused and rose in response. Liu Shaobai was the first at his school to cut off his queue, and his eldest daughter was the first girl in Xing county with unbound feet. Before long, Qing troops forced the Niangzi Pass, and the uprising army was driven to withdraw from Taiyuan. Liu Shaobai went back to Xing county, organized a local militia, and struggled against the county officials. In his home district he spread word of the 1911 Revolution and did away with all manner of feudal customs. At the New Year he put up a couplet of his own composing: "Revolution is not a love of disorder; study is not for the sake of office." The Qing county magistrate charged him with stirring up revolt and even ordered his arrest, so that he had to slip away in disguise on New Year's Eve; only after the North-South peace settlement did he return to Xing county. After his parents died, he broke a thousand years of custom: he called in no geomancer, wore no mourning sackcloth, held no lavish funeral. He seized the occasion, too, to bring out every deed and contract the family held and burn them before the assembled crowd. He said: "In the past the ancestors did wrong, and should not have made others write out so many bonds. Now the old people have passed on; let these things go with them. This is the old people's property. We unworthy descendants have no right to inherit this estate." One year drought struck his home district and the crops in the fields failed; he taught his household not to press the tenants for rent, and gave out the family's stores of grain to relieve the famine-stricken.

For his active part in the 1911 Revolution, he was elected in March 1912 to the Shanxi provisional provincial assembly. That spring, on Hu Egong's introduction, he joined the Republican Party founded by Li Yuanhong. He soon found that the Republic did not belong to the people, and that the Republican Party was not in the least republican. The 1911 Revolution had only driven out an emperor; the whole nation still lived in fire and flood. The May Fourth Movement that broke out in 1919 worked a deep change in his thinking. In his home district he spread the new ideas, championed the new culture, opened schools for girls, and pushed education forward.

Liu Shaobai had studied in his early years at the Shanxi Military Preparatory Academy, in the same class as Yan Xishan. Yan Xishan gambled on the revolution, played at intrigue, seized the military and civil power of Shanxi, and set himself up as a local emperor lording it over the province. To pull the wool over the people's eyes, at the beginning of 1927 he raised the banner of "recruiting the worthy and gathering the talented" and held examinations for county magistrates. Liu Shaobai sat the examination. His gifts stood out, and he enjoyed considerable standing in society, so that passing should have been no worry. But the chief examiner put one last question: "Do you think communism is a good thing, or a bad one?" Liu Shaobai answered: "Communism is sound. The pity is that it is hard to put into practice in China." Naturally this was not to a certain Yan's taste, and in the end he was failed.

Judged by the answer he gave then, Liu Shaobai's understanding of communism was still shallow. But as the whole situation developed, his understanding deepened as well. His eldest daughter, Liu Yaxiong, was studying at Beijing Women's Normal University, and had taken an active part in the student movement, led by the Chinese Communist Party, to drive out the reactionary headmistress Yang Yinyu. (She joined the Chinese Communist Party after the March Eighteenth affair of 1926.) Liu Shaobai wrote from Taiyuan to support his daughter's stand against the outrage; and when the education authorities brazenly ordered the Women's Normal University dissolved, Liu Shaobai sent a letter to the Jing Bao exposing and denouncing it. After the March Eighteenth massacre that year, Liu Yaxiong was struck from the rolls by order of the Northern warlords' education ministry. The Party organization decided to send four women students who could no longer remain in Beijing, Liu Yaxiong among them, to study at Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow; Liu Shaobai hurried at once from Taiyuan to Beijing, and then from Beijing to Tianjin, to see his daughter off.

After the Great Revolution failed, Chiang Kai-shek slaughtered Communists without restraint. In the face of the White Terror, Liu Shaobai did all he could to support the revolution. One day Wang Ying, a leader of the CCP Shanxi provincial committee, burst into Liu Shaobai's house to escape the agents on his trail. Liu Shaobai took the risk without flinching and hid Wang Ying away. At dawn he saw Wang Ying to the Beiying railway station himself, and got him safely clear. It was in that same year that he joined the Party's outer organization, the Mutual Aid Society, and at the risk of his life sheltered a whole string of Communists and revolutionary youth.

In 1928 Liu Shaobai's friend Wen Shouquan, a leading figure of the Shanxi power-holders in the south of the province who opposed Yan Xishan, went to Beiping to take up the post of chief of the Hebei provincial construction bureau. On Wen Shouquan's recommendation, Liu Shaobai became a section chief in the construction bureau and also served as Wen Shouquan's secretary. That September Liu Shaobai moved his whole household to Beiping, renting as his residence the former home, at 60 Hufangqiao in the south of the city, of the Qianlong-era scholar Ji Yun (Xiaolan), the house people afterward called the "Liu Mansion." The next April, at Fu Zuoyi's invitation, he became head of the Tianjin commodity inspection bureau. His social standing had risen and his income was ample, but the darkness and corruption of officialdom that he saw about him left him in low spirits. In a letter to his old friend Niu Youlan he once wrote: "Working in this half-hearted revolutionary office, the wretchedness of my mood is beyond telling."

In early 1929 Liu Yaxiong returned from the Soviet Union to Shanghai. She first worked in the propaganda department of the CCP Jiangsu provincial committee, and in the autumn was transferred into the Central Organization Department. In early 1930 Liu Shaobai took a business trip as his chance to visit Liu Yaxiong in Shanghai, staying at the Dongfang Hotel. At that time the Kuomintang agents in Shanghai ran wild, hunting Communists everywhere. Zhao Shiyan, a leader of the CCP Jiangsu provincial committee, had been sold out by a traitor and killed, and his elder sister Zhao Shilan was now being hunted by the enemy. Liu Yaxiong and Zhao Shilan had been classmates and were close comrades, and she told Zhao Shilan of her father's lodging at the Dongfang Hotel. One day, Zhao Shilan and Li Hui, finding themselves shadowed by the enemy and in grave danger, ran to the Dongfang Hotel to find Liu Shaobai. He hid them away at once, and they escaped the enemy's pursuit.

In January 1931 Liu Yaxiong was transferred to the newly formed CCP Shun-Zhi provincial committee in Tianjin (that is, the Hebei provincial committee), as its secretary-general; her husband Chen Yuandao was head of the committee's organization department. At this time the committee decided to set up a liaison point at Liu Shaobai's mansion in Beiping. Besides putting the Shun-Zhi committee in touch with the underground Party leaders of Beiping city, this liaison point was charged with relaying and forwarding the operating funds sent from the CCP Center in Shanghai to the Shun-Zhi committee. That April the committee was broken into, and its leaders Xu Lanzhi, Chen Yuandao, Liu Yaxiong, Pu Qiuchao, and others were arrested. The CCP Center ordered the Shun-Zhi committee moved swiftly from Tianjin to Beiping, with Yin Jian taking charge of its work, and Liu Shaobai's mansion continued to serve as the committee's liaison point. Word of the arrests of Liu Yaxiong and the others was passed to Liu Shaobai, in the hope that he would use his connections in high places to find some way to rescue them.

Liu Shaobai, learning the news, was frantic with worry. He returned at once to Beiping, raised 2,000 silver dollars, and hurried back to Tianjin, where he took rooms at the Guomin Hotel and settled in for a long stay to set the rescue in motion. Drawing on his connections in the upper reaches of society, he first went to Fu Zuoyi, then to Zhang Xueming, the Tianjin police chief and younger brother of Zhang Xueliang. As recounted earlier, it was at this point that Hu Egong, Yang Xianzhen, and Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan came to Tianjin in succession, and Liu Shaobai went on using his social connections to help them rescue the arrested comrades. Through Liu Shaobai's hard efforts the rescue work gradually took shape: Zhang Xueming agreed that the prisoners could be freed for a large sum of money, and Hu Egong had already gone to Shanghai to report to the Central Special Section and bring the money back. Then came a sudden blow. Guo Yaxian, the new committee's secretary-general who had moved to Beiping, was arrested in early July and turned traitor; he gave up committee secretary Yin Jian and other leaders, so that they were arrested, and he gave up as well his connection with Liu Shaobai and the fact that the "Liu Mansion" was the committee's secret liaison point. The "Liu Mansion" was searched at once, and agents were planted inside to stake it out and seize everyone who came. Liu Shaobai, learning of this at the Guomin Hotel, left it swiftly and fled; Chen Geng and Yang Xianzhen lost touch with him, and the rescue work broke off there.

It was more than ten years later, when Yang Xianzhen and Liu Shaobai met again in Taiyuan, Shanxi, that Yang learned why Liu Shaobai had left without a word. Liu Shaobai also told Yang Xianzhen that on that day he had waited at the Guomin Hotel in an agony of suspense for Yang Xianzhen and Chen Geng to arrive. He waited from early morning until past ten o'clock, and still there was no sign of them; supposing that they too had run into trouble, he no longer dared to stay, and so he hurriedly paid his bill, slipped quietly out of the Guomin Hotel, and fled to Dalian to take refuge.

Now on that day, when Liu Shaobai came out of the Guomin Hotel, he boarded a steamer at once and fled to Dalian. In Dalian he had not a soul to turn to, and had no choice but to hide for the time being in a small inn. Dalian was by then occupied by the Japanese army, Japanese soldiers barged about the main streets, and traitor-spies swaggered everywhere. Though he kept close and seldom went out, he still drew the notice of a Japanese spy, who once questioned him about his words and doings under the pretext of a household check. Liu Shaobai felt Dalian was no place to linger, and moved on to Qingdao. Qingdao was not safe either: on the streets there he suddenly caught sight of the very Japanese spy who had tailed him in Dalian. He had no choice but to double back to Dalian, and only after much trouble shook off the spies' watch. Then he left Dalian in haste for Datong in Shanxi, and hid himself in the home of his fellow townsman and good friend Bai Yunxuan. The agents staking out the "Liu Mansion" in Beiping did not withdraw until September. Only then did Liu Shaobai have someone move his family to Datong.

Liu Shaobai's thoughts were always with the revolutionary young people suffering in prison, and he felt keenly the peril of a nation come to a moment of life or death. He had petitioned Zhang Xueliang more than once, urging him to put the interests of the country and nation first and to support the people's patriotic movement for national salvation. He lived three years in Datong; having no employment, his household was hard-pressed. In 1934 Fu Zuoyi became chairman of the Suiyuan provincial government, and Liu Shaobai, an old acquaintance of Fu's, went toward the end of that year to Suiyuan, where he became political director of the "Suiyuan Provincial Rural Education Training Institute."

Suiyuan was a frontier of the national defense that Japanese imperialism had long coveted, and Fu Zuoyi's ties with the Northeastern Army were fairly close; at that time Suiyuan became an important quarter for the Chinese Communist Party to win over and unite. By now Liu Shaobai was no longer merely a patriot of high principle: he stood, with some awareness, on the Communist Party's ground, willing to work with all his strength to realize the Party's program of resisting Japan and saving the nation. Between 1934 and 1936, the comrades the Communist Party sent to work in Suiyuan had for the most part close contact with him and received his help. From Fu Zuoyi he learned of Chiang Kai-shek's secret order to arrest Liu Lantao, and promptly warned Liu Lantao to flee at once. Afterward he also worked actively to help the Party organization rescue Wang Ruofei, held in the "First Model Prison" at Guisui (present-day Hohhot). He spoke with Fu Zuoyi many times, urging him to release Wang Ruofei. As a matter of tactics, he raised the release of Wang Ruofei together with that of his own nephew Liu Wuxiong, who had been mistaken by the enemy for a Communist and imprisoned in the 1931 Hufangqiao "Liu Mansion" case. Fu Zuoyi said: "The release of Liu Wuxiong can be arranged; the release of Huang Jingzhai" — that is, Wang Ruofei — "is a weighty matter, and truly hard to comply with lightly." In the end he only had Liu Wuxiong brought out of prison in Beiping and set free; but the rescue of Liu Wuxiong was not Liu Shaobai's chief aim. So he mobilized public opinion, lobbied widely among the upper class, and brought pressure to bear on Fu Zuoyi to secure Wang Ruofei's early release. Liu Shaobai also wrote to Wang Ruofei's uncle Huang Qisheng, far off in Ding county, Hebei. Huang Qisheng, on receiving the letter, hurried to Suiyuan; Liu Shaobai led him to see Fu Zuoyi and passed his letters along for him. But because the two sides could not come to terms, and Wang Ruofei refused Fu Zuoyi's unreasonable demands, the rescue dragged on.

In the summer of 1936 Wang Ruofei was moved under guard to the Taiyuan army prison. Through the Party organization's continued efforts, Wang Ruofei was released in April 1937. After his release, Wang Ruofei lived with his wife Li Peizhi and his younger brother Wang Jingren at No. 2 Yongding Road, Erdao Lane, east of Taiyuan's Great North Gate. This was the seat of the CCP Northern Bureau, and Yang Xianzhen, Liao Luyan, and others lived there too. By this time Liu Shaobai had resigned his post in Suiyuan and returned to Taiyuan, and he often came to Wang Ruofei's home as a guest for long talks. Wang Ruofei said to his family more than once: "Old Uncle Liu's daughter Liu Yaxiong is one of the earlier women Party members to have proved herself under trial; the man himself has done no little work for the Party, and I hold him in the greatest respect." After the Marco Polo Bridge Incident, Liu Shaobai formally applied to the Party organization to join. In early August 1937, sponsored by Wang Ruofei and An Ziwen, Liu Shaobai formally joined the Chinese Communist Party. To make his work easier, Liu Shaobai served as a secret Party member, not revealing his membership and keeping a single line of contact with the organization. Afterward he returned to his home district, where he carried on his public activities in the guise of an enlightened gentleman.

After the War of Resistance broke out, Liu Shaobai, following the Party's instructions, returned to his home district to serve as head of the economic department of the Xing county branch of the Second War Zone General Mobilization Committee, taking part in the work of building the northwest Shanxi anti-Japanese base area. He mobilized the gentry of northwest Shanxi to take an active part in the resistance, founded the Xing County Farmers' Bank, urged the government to plant cotton and set up textile mills, and made many contributions to the war effort. In the winter of 1942 he was elected deputy speaker of the northwest Shanxi provisional council. He went three times to Yan'an, and organized a delegation of gentry to visit Yan'an, where they were received by Mao Zedong, Zhu De, Zhou Enlai, and other central leaders.

In the early days of the War of Liberation, land reform began in the old liberated areas. When Liu Shaobai went to Yan'an for the third time, in May 1946, Mao Zedong explained to him the meaning of land reform and the Party's policy. Liu Shaobai returned to the Shanxi-Suiyuan border region and, following Mao Zedong's advice, gave up the family's land together with his younger brother Liu Xiangkun. The Shanxi-Suiyuan Daily, organ of the CCP Central Shanxi-Suiyuan Sub-bureau, ran the news on August 13, 1946, as its front-page lead: "Deputy Speaker Liu and His Brother Give Their Land and Houses to the Peasants," and reported that the administrative office's government council had passed a resolution to accept the gift and to commend them. But in 1947, when Kang Sheng came to lead the land reform in the Shanxi-Suiyuan border region and pushed an ultra-"left" policy, he went so far as to slander the Liu brothers as "false enlightened gentry" who "bought over the masses," and had them struggled against. When Mao Zedong saw the news of the struggle against Liu Shaobai, he ordered the CCP Central Shanxi-Suiyuan Sub-bureau to set it right. On September 15, 1948, Liu Shaobai wrote to Mao Zedong, laying out his views on the ultra-"left" deviations in the land reform of the Shanxi-Suiyuan border region and on the question of his own Party membership. On October 30, Mao Zedong wrote back to Liu Shaobai in his own hand:

Comrade Shaobai:

I have read with care your letter of the fifteenth of September. Our work has had its errors. Fortunately these have now in the main been corrected, and the correction is still going on. As you say in the second of your five points, once the situation is clear the matter is easily settled, and you may set your mind at rest. Your letter has been passed on to Comrade Peng Zhen; on the question of your Party membership, please consult with Comrade Peng Zhen.

With every good wish!

Mao Zedong, October 30

In 1947, while Liu Yaxiong was working in the Northeast, she read in the papers that her father was being struggled against. She wrote him a letter to open his mind and set it at ease, and asked Lin Feng whether it might be sent. Lin Feng passed the letter to Zhou Enlai. Zhou Enlai then had it delivered to Liu Shaobai, with a short note of his own to show his concern:

Comrade Shaobai:

Several times I have returned to Yan'an, but always in such haste, coming and going, that we have had no chance to talk; I count it a great regret.

Just now a letter from Yaxiong has reached me, come from the Northeast, passed along by many hands. I send it on to you herewith, in the hope that it will comfort your heart.

Written in haste; with my respects.

Zhou Enlai, 7/2

On March 1, 1948, in the Party directive "On the Question of the National Bourgeoisie and the Enlightened Gentry" that Mao Zedong drafted for the CCP Center, he wrote: "Men such as Liu Shaobai of the Shanxi-Suiyuan border region and Li Dingming of the Shaan-Gan-Ning border region gave us considerable help during the War of Resistance and in the hard years that followed…"

The frank warmth of Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai, and their earnest care, moved Liu Shaobai deeply, and he was thankful from the heart.

### The Pastor and the Lawyer

The intelligence section also built up all sorts of connections in the world of pastors and lawyers, and every one of them served the Central Special Section's work well. Among the pastors, the intelligence section's chief connections were Dong Jianwu and Pu Huaren.

The celebrated American journalist Edgar Snow, in Red Star Over China, told the story of a pastor who "went about among all sorts of civil and military officials" on the Communists' behalf, "lobbying" for them. This was the pastor Snow had come to know in Xi'an in June 1936, the man who had helped him slip through the blockade of the Kuomintang's anti-Communist forces and enter the revolutionary base area in northern Shaanxi in secret. Snow wrote:

Before I reached Xi'an I had never seen a Red soldier. The man who in Beiping had written me, in invisible ink, a letter of introduction to Mao Zedong I knew for a Red Army commander, but we had never met. That letter had been given me by a third party, an old friend of mine; apart from it, my one hope of making contact with the Northwest rested on a single thing. I had been told that once I reached Xi'an I need only take a room in a hotel and wait, and a man surnamed Wang would come to find me; but of this Mr. Wang I knew nothing at all, nothing save that he was to arrange for me to enter the Red district in Zhang Xueliang's private plane. That was what had been agreed for me.

After I had stayed some days at the hotel, sure enough a Chinese in a gray silk gown walked into my open room, greeted me in fine English, a big man, a little stout, but robust and of striking presence. He had the look of a prosperous, easy-living rich man, but he gave his surname as Wang and spoke the name of my Beiping friend, and I decided he was none other than the man I was waiting to see.

In the week that followed, I found that merely meeting this Mr. Wang made my trip to Xi'an worthwhile. Every day I spent four or five hours listening to his strange talk and his reminiscences, and to his ever more serious readings of the political situation. His being here was wholly unexpected. He had been educated at a mission school in Shanghai, had stood out among the Christians, and had once presided over a church, as I later learned; among the Communists they all called him Pastor Wang. Like many a successful Christian in Shanghai, he was a member of the Green Gang, and there was not a man he did not know, from Chiang Kai-shek at the top, himself a Green Gang member, down to the Green Gang boss Du Yuesheng. He had once been a high Kuomintang official; but even now I still cannot disclose his true name.

This pastor's true name was Dong Jianwu, a native of Qingpu county, Shanghai. In his youth, under his family's influence, he entered the Christian faith; in 1914 he enrolled in the theology department of St. John's University in Shanghai, and after graduating served as a pastor at St. Peter's Church on Avenue Road (present-day Beijing Road) in Shanghai. At the end of 1926, at Pu Huaren's invitation, he gave up his post at the St. John's church and went to the Northwest Army of the "Christian General" Feng Yuxiang to serve as an army chaplain, preaching among the troops; and there he joined the Chinese Communist Party.

Pu Huaren was a pastor too; he and Dong Jianwu had been classmates in the theology department of St. John's University. In 1921 Pu Huaren became secretary to Feng Yuxiang, then military governor of Shaanxi, and also served as his army chaplain. Afterward Feng Yuxiang's posts changed again and again, army inspector-general, defense commissioner of the northwestern frontier, commander-in-chief of the Allied National Armies, commander-in-chief of the Second Group Army of the National Revolutionary Army, and so on, and through it all Pu Huaren went on working in Feng Yuxiang's army: he served as head of the Yude School that Feng ran in Beijing, took part in the political department of the Allied National Armies, and in 1926 joined the Chinese Communist Party in secret at Wuyuan. In the memoir Feng Yuxiang wrote, he set down his impression of Pu Huaren: "I had a friend, Pastor Pu Huaren, whom I came to know when I was military governor of Shaanxi. A man of great warmth, firm and bold, he was long in my company. At Zhangjiakou he ran a school, and there too he did well. At the Wuyuan oath-taking I sent Lu Zhonglin to the Soviet Union on a mission of inquiry, and Pu went along with him. After reaching Moscow, he, an Anglican pastor, actually joined the Communist Party and became a devoted, active Party member. And yet I did not know it."

Dong Jianwu, having reached Xi'an, the seat of the Second Group Army headquarters of the National Revolutionary Army, at the end of 1926, took the alias Dong Xianwu; besides serving as army chaplain, he took part in the work of the political department. Before long Pu Huaren was appointed head of the workers' administration office of the group army's political department, and Dong Jianwu head of the peasants' administration office. At that time the Northern Expedition was advancing smoothly, sweeping fast through the provinces of the Yangtze valley. But as the next year came, Chiang Kai-shek, who had feigned revolution and gambled on it, gradually showed his reactionary face, and China's political situation began to turn. The April Twelfth and July Fifteenth counterrevolutionary coups followed one on the other; Chiang Kai-shek and Wang Jingwei carried out their purge of the Party and their war on Communism, slaughtering Communists and revolutionary masses without restraint. China's politics were turned upside down, and the White Terror settled over the whole country. That summer Feng Yuxiang joined Chiang Kai-shek in the "united anti-Communist front," and "saw off with courtesy" the Communists and Soviet advisers in his army. Pu Huaren did not leave at once, but stayed on to work a while longer; then he "departed without a farewell," left the army, and afterward wrote to Feng Yuxiang to voice his opposition to Feng's "joining with Chiang Kai-shek against Communism." Feng Yuxiang's memoir also records Pu Huaren's departure from the Northwest Army. After joining Chiang Kai-shek against the Communists, Feng had kept Pu Huaren and Dong Jianwu at his side. He wrote: "Before long he" — that is, Pu Huaren — "returned to the country together with Lu Zhonglin from the Soviet Union; Lu became acting chairman of Henan, and I at once gave him 3,000 yuan and sent him, with another friend named Dong Xianwu, to take charge of welfare work for the railway workers. One day he suddenly left me a note and quietly departed Zhengzhou, without accounting for the funds he had drawn. I did not believe he would make off with the money, and sent men to inquire; only then did I learn that he had given the money out to the poor workers, thirty here and forty there, and had not taken a cent for himself. His leaving, too, was a misunderstanding over the split with the Communists. In 1928, when I was in Nanjing, his old mother sent someone to see me, saying she wished to sell the family's land to repay the sum. I said: 'That money he gave out entirely to the toiling workers, and not a cent of it went into his own purse. Why should you sell land to make it good?' In 1931 I went to Shanghai and met Mr. Pu, who was then an editor at the Commercial Press. In 1936 I was in Nanjing, and he wrote to me from prison asking me to find a way to bail him out. I took his letter to see Mr. Chiang, and told him in full of Pu's origins, his character, and all the real circumstances; Mr. Chiang said he would look into it, and that if there was nothing to it he could be released. In the end, after several examinations by the gendarmerie headquarters, he was bailed out at the request of Lu Ruibo."

After Pu Huaren left, Dong Jianwu stayed on in the Northwest Army as Feng Yuxiang's secretary, and also as family tutor, teaching English to Li Dequan and the children.

In the autumn of 1928, the Kuomintang central party headquarters in Nanjing wired Feng Yuxiang that Dong Xianwu, that is, Dong Jianwu, was a Communist, and demanded that he be arrested at once and sent under guard to Nanjing; at this Feng Yuxiang sent him back to Shanghai.

After Dong Jianwu returned to Shanghai, he at once got in touch with Pu Huaren, and through Pu Huaren's contact with the Party organization was arranged to take part in the work of the Mutual Aid Society, chiefly rescuing arrested comrades, engaging lawyers for them, and relieving the families of those in distress. In the second half of 1929 he joined the work of the intelligence section of the Central Special Section; Chen Geng assigned Liu Ding and "Old Wang" to keep in touch with him, and at the time they called him "Fat Zhou the Second." The St. Peter's Church he presided over became a secret liaison point for the CCP Center, a place Chen Geng often frequented while sparring with the enemy; the Party organization and some of the left-wing groups it led also often used the church to hold meetings or carry on other activities. With the Special Section's help, this pastor also opened a "Songbai Antiques Shop" and a "Datong Kindergarten," and the Central Special Section used these places to shelter comrades, pass letters, and store some of the Party's documents. The Datong Kindergarten took in the children of Communists and the orphans of martyrs. In his guise as a pastor he drew close to the upper figures of the Kuomintang ruling clique, keeping up his contacts through religious activities. At that time he went every week to the mother of T.V. Soong, to accompany her at prayer, and often heard intelligence on the Nanjing government's financial and political affairs. Even when he heard no news, just seeing who came and went at T.V. Soong's mansion was very useful.

Liu Ding, deputy chief of the intelligence section, who had then been in touch with Dong Jianwu, said: "My contact with Pastor Dong Jianwu was on Chen Geng's instructions. There were several pastors at the time; some Chen Geng handled himself, some he handed to me. During the stretch when Dong was in touch with me, it was mainly to gather a little intelligence. We had him use his pastor's face to get close to T.V. Soong's mother and learn about the Soong and Chiang families. Besides that, he took on the communications work for several places. He also kept some documents at his place. After Gu Shunzhang turned traitor and Qian Zhuangfei and Li Kenong were pulled back, we wanted to learn Chiang Kai-shek's plan for the attack on Jiangxi, so we had him go to old Mrs. Soong's to sound things out, and he did pick up some word of the Kuomintang's financial and economic troubles."

In 1931, after Gu Shunzhang turned traitor and went over to the enemy, and Chen Geng left Shanghai for the E-Yu-Wan Soviet area, Dong Jianwu went on working in the Central Special Section. After the Central Special Section was reorganized, Pan Hannian, who took over from Chen Geng as head of the intelligence section, said: "The Special Section had a working connection named Dong Jianwu, a pastor with standing in religious circles. I had him draw in the upper ranks of the secret societies and make contact with the higher figures of the police and detective organs, so as to learn important news in good time and carry out rescue work as the chance arose. He won over Jiao Dingkai, a gang boss in the French Concession, which opened the way to bribing Lu Ying, the Shanghai police chief; he learned some of the circumstances of Xiang Zhongfa's arrest, and rescued a number of arrested people. After the Xi'an Incident, when Wang Jiaxiang and He Cheng were to go to the Soviet Union by way of Shanghai but could not get exit passports, Dong Jianwu obtained them through people inside the police, and the two of them went off to the Soviet Union without a hitch. Dong also kept Mao Zedong's two children hidden secretly in his home before they were sent on to the Soviet Union. But because he did too many left-leaning things, using the church to set up the 'Freedom League' and the like, his pastor's standing was stripped from him, and he could no longer play the part he should."

Among the lawyers, the Central Special Section's chief connections were Pan Zhenya, Fei Guoxi, Gu Jirong, and Chen Zhigao. All of them were connections the intelligence section made use of, or sympathizers of the Party. When comrades of ours were arrested, the Central Special Section would ask them to appear in court to defend and rescue them, or to pass word, and to carry on a legal struggle against the enemy. Pan Zhenya was a professor of law who also practiced; deeply sympathetic to the revolution, he would come to help the moment he was told of anything, and attached no conditions. On January 17, 1929, Ren Bishi was arrested in Shanghai; when the British Concession authorities opened his trial on November 22, the Central Special Section engaged the lawyer Pan Zhenya to defend him. After his arrest Ren Bishi used the alias Peng Desheng, never revealed his true identity, made no confession, and the enemy had no evidence at all. Though he was still sentenced to forty days' imprisonment on the trumped-up charge of "endangering state security," by Christmas on December 25 Ren Bishi was "released with his sentence reduced." The connection with the legal world can be shown through Chen Zhigao, as an example of the part these men played in defending and rescuing arrested comrades, and in other matters.

Chen Zhigao was a native of Haiyan county, Zhejiang. A graduate of Fudan University, he was a disciple of the famous patriotic lawyer Shen Junru. Though he had not long hung out his shingle in Shanghai, his youth and sharpness and his upright character had already made him quite a name in the legal world. He had once defended the workers of the French Tramway union in court without charge and won the case, and the French Tramway workers were deeply grateful and respectful to him. That Chen Zhigao could make his name in Shanghai at a stroke had much to do with his family background as well. His father, Chen Qishou, had served eighteen years as chief judge of the criminal court in the French Concession's Mixed Court, and had deep ties with the British and French consulates in Shanghai. He had once done the great gangster Huang Jinrong a good turn and saved his life. He also enjoyed the protection of Jiang Shang, a close friend of Du Yuesheng, that other great gangster of the Shanghai waterfront; Jiang Shang held great sway within the gangster circles of the French Concession authorities. What is more, Chen Zhigao's uncles on both his mother's and his father's side were judges. His family wielded enormous influence in Shanghai's judicial world.

So although Chen Zhigao was an ordinary lawyer, his capacity for action in Shanghai was very great. Whether with the foreigners at the top or the mixed crowd at the bottom, he could work them all. In his spare time he was fond of reading progressive books and journals, made many progressive friends, and his own thinking was fairly progressive. He was a member of the World and China Magazine society, an outer organization under CCP leadership. The magazine's manuscript reviewer was Sun Xiaocun, and Chen Zhigao handled its financial affairs; the editorial office was set up in the third-floor study of Chen Zhigao's home, where a good many progressive books were kept. He had bought over some connections in the Concession police stations, had defended arrested comrades of our Party in court many times, and played a considerable part in rescue work, helping to secure Guan Xiangying's early release from prison.

Between February 16 and 21, 1932, Kuomintang agents ran a forged "Notice of Wu Hao and Others Leaving the Communist Party" in several of Shanghai's large newspapers. Through this notice the Kuomintang reactionaries hoped to slander Comrade Zhou Enlai and to break up the revolutionary strength of the Chinese Communist Party in the Kuomintang-ruled areas.

By that time Zhou Enlai had long since left Shanghai and reached the Central Soviet Area, where as secretary of the CCP Soviet Area Central Bureau he led the Central Soviet Area's Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army in armed struggle against the Kuomintang. The CCP provisional Central leadership in Shanghai marshaled the Party press and scattered leaflets to deny the rumor and expose the enemy's slander. But the Party's papers and journals could not be published openly, and because of the Kuomintang government's suppression there was no way to issue a denial in the openly circulating Shanghai papers either. Only after much difficulty did the Center manage to place a plain advertisement in Shen Bao: "To Mr. Wu Hao: The advertisement you sent in on the 18th of this month could not be run, the Fuchang Bed Company having declined to stand surety, so that the formalities were not in order." Cleverly as this notice served to scotch the rumor, indirectly making known that Wu Hao had a statement denying and exposing the Kuomintang agents' fabrication, which could not be run only for want of a guarantor, the ordinary reader outside the Party would not necessarily have grasped its deeper meaning. And so Pan Hannian, then head of the Central Special Section's intelligence section, held urgent consultations with Li Yimang and other comrades of the CCP Shanghai cultural committee, and under the name of Zhou Shaoshan, an alias of Zhou Enlai's well known within the Party, prepared to write and publish openly in the papers a "Zhou Shaoshan Urgent Notice."

Pan Hannian knew that Huang Dinghui, also called Huang Mulan, had by then been transferred into the intelligence section of the Central Special Section and had once headed the special rescue department under the Party's leadership; in the course of rescue work for arrested comrades she had come to know Chen Zhigao. So he gave the task of placing this urgent notice to Huang Dinghui.

Huang Dinghui was just the right person for the business. A native of Liuyang, Hunan, she came of a distinguished family. Her father, Huang Yingchu, was an old member of the Tongmenghui, and with Tan Sitong and Tang Caichang made up the famous "Three Heroes of Liuyang" of the old democratic period. In the spring of 1926 she joined the Communist Youth League in Wuhan, and in November became a Communist Party member, and a member of the Kuomintang left as well; she had served as head of the women's department of the Kuomintang's Hankou special municipal party headquarters. In the days of the Great Revolution, "Huang Mulan" was a well-known figure in women's work in Wuhan. After the July Fifteenth counterrevolutionary coup she moved to Shanghai, where she served in the Center's internal courier work and as head of the special rescue department under the Party's leadership, and was later transferred into the Second Section of the Central Special Section. At this time she was got up as a fine lady, living in the high-class Chinese residential quarter on Avenue Joffre in Shanghai. Young and beautiful, warm and lively, she had had several dealings with Chen Zhigao in the course of rescue work, and he was now paying her devoted court.

Once given the task, Huang Dinghui went to make a date with the lawyer Chen Zhigao. On the pretext that a relative of hers had been framed, she asked Chen Zhigao to help place an urgent notice for her relative and get them out of trouble. Chen Zhigao had worked for the Communists many times before and had always agreed readily enough, but this time he felt the difficulty. He told Huang Dinghui that this was a weighty matter, one that the highest Kuomintang authorities were watching, and truly beyond what a Chinese lawyer could manage. After turning it over and over, he told Huang Dinghui that he was on good personal terms with Baihe, a well-known French lawyer in the French Concession, and that it would be safer to have Baihe come forward on her relative's behalf to place the notice. For by international practice a lawyer had the right not to disclose his client's name or address to anyone. Baihe was a foreigner, and lived inside the Concession as well, so that the Kuomintang could do nothing to him.

Huang Dinghui reported the matter to Pan Hannian and got his approval, then went with Chen Zhigao, the draft "Urgent Notice" in hand, to see the lawyer Baihe. Baihe had his misgivings at first, but after Chen Zhigao's repeated persuasion, and the promise of a heavy fee, he agreed. On March 4, 1932, this "Urgent Notice of Zhou Shaoshan, Placed by the Lawyer Baihe" appeared in a prominent position in Shanghai's Shen Bao.

After this, Pan Hannian had Huang Dinghui use her connection with the lawyer Chen Zhigao to try to build an inside line within the French Concession police. Huang Dinghui spoke with Chen Zhigao, and Pan Hannian himself spoke with Chen Zhigao several more times, encouraging him to go on working for our Party. Before long, through Chen Zhigao, Huang Dinghui came to know Zhao Zibai, a detective inspector of the French police station, who declared himself for the CCP and passed information to Huang on his own account. At this time an accountant of the national labor federation had absconded with embezzled funds. The man knew the federation's leaders and the details of its subordinate units, and if he gave himself up to the enemy the damage would be very grave. Huang asked Zhao to help find him. Making use of his position, Zhao worked with the detective departments of the British Concession and the Chinese-administered districts, and at last found the trail in a brothel; he had the man held on a charge of theft, and so averted great damage. Zhao Zibai told Huang of every case within the French police station's jurisdiction that was to be broken or arrested, and discussed with her ways to rescue those already taken. After Xiang Zhongfa's arrest, he told us the whole course of it, accepted the view we put to him, and had the French Concession authorities bribed so that the Chinese authorities could not extradite him. But because the Nanjing government threw all its weight in, the French Concession could not hold out, and Xiang Zhongfa was in the end extradited to the Songhu Garrison Command.

### Expanding into the Press

Following the line of shifting the Party's clandestine struggle away from the terror tactics that Gu Shunzhang had been so keen on and toward political struggle, the intelligence work of the Central Special Section steadily opened wide new avenues. The intelligence section gave attention to expanding into the press, and reaped rich results there too.

In leading the people through the revolutionary struggle, the Chinese Communist Party had always valued the role of the struggle over public opinion. In the early days of the War of Resistance, speaking of the relation between the struggle over opinion and the revolutionary war, Comrade Zhou Enlai once said: "The war of the pen is the forerunner of the war of the gun, and the backing of it as well." In carrying on clandestine struggle and intelligence work in enemy-ruled territory, the Party also took care to join it with the struggle over opinion: founding newspapers, setting up news agencies, using press organs to shelter the intelligence apparatus, and carrying on intelligence work in open, legal forms. This was not only a good way to shelter the clandestine struggle; it also made it easier to gather every sort of social intelligence, so that the sources of intelligence grew wider. Secret intelligence and open intelligence complemented and confirmed each other, giving a truer picture of the movements of the hostile forces.

In the early 1930s a vogue for founding news agencies was rising in the country's press. At this time Chen Geng, who was directing intelligence work in the Central Special Section, and some comrades of the intelligence section, gave thought to having the Special Section found a news agency, to gather intelligence on the enemy's activities openly, through the work of reporters, so as gradually to train up a body of their own reporters, and through them to unite and win over a number of figures in the press, to become the Party's eyes and ears.

Under the intelligence section's unified plan, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di, who had driven into the enemy's heart, in order to join secret intelligence with open intelligence, founded the Yangtze News Agency and the Minzhi News Agency in Nanjing and the Great Wall News Agency in Tianjin, and in Shanghai ran the "Shanghai Wireless Administration Broadcast News" to shelter their intelligence work; all this was recounted in detail earlier. Chen Geng, directing several comrades of the intelligence section himself, also made attempts along these lines. Chen Yangshan above all, who gave his whole life to intelligence work, often went about in the guise of a news reporter in the course of his intelligence activity. He was skilled at getting in touch, through his social connections, with some of Shanghai's press organs. After he came to the intelligence section he carried on his activity under cover as a Shen Bao reporter, and organized news agencies more than once, using open, legal forms to carry on intelligence work and obtaining a good deal of intelligence on the imperialists and the Kuomintang.

In the intelligence section's early days, "Liu the Big Han" (Ouyang Xin), nicknamed "the Big Fellow," often moved about in the guise of a news reporter and obtained much valuable intelligence. At that time the British, Japanese, and other imperialists entrenched in the Shanghai Concessions were often keen to draw in news reporters to do their propaganda. During the Great Revolution "Liu the Big Han" had served as interpreter to Eugene Chen, foreign minister of the Wuhan National Government, and had also studied artillery at a Soviet military school; his foreign languages were very good, and he turned the other side's designs to account, dealing with them in the guise of a news reporter and getting from them some intelligence on British and Japanese political trends and on foreign merchants' activities. Under the name of a reporter he also drew close to the Kuomintang's military organs and to the military and government officials passing between Shanghai and Nanjing, and struck up "friendships" with certain reactionary officers, obtaining a good deal of Kuomintang military and political intelligence. His capacity for action in society was fairly strong, and even if he were now and then caught off guard and arrested, he had ways to get clear. In the spring of 1930, Yang Dengying discovered that a Red Army traitor named Shi Shuyuan was using the "Yipin Xiang" Hotel as cover to make direct contact with the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal party headquarters and carry on a political deal to sell out the revolution. After Chen Geng reported the matter to the Central Special Committee, Zhou Enlai at once ordered the comrades of the Second and Third Sections of the Central Special Section all out, and threw a ring around the "Yipin Xiang" Hotel. The hotel's main door and the several nearby street corners were sealed off by the fully armed "Red Squad," and all the police were kept under watch. Chen Geng directed on the spot in person; Liu Ding, as Yang Dengying's specialist, went with him into the hotel to join the banquet, using it to reconnoiter and watch Shi Shuyuan's movements; and "the Big Fellow," in the guise of a news reporter, went into the hotel to "gather news," meaning to seize this traitor. In the end the enemy sensed that something was wrong, and the traitor bolted in a panic. This action miscarried, and "the Big Fellow" and several comrades at the hotel door were arrested. "The Big Fellow" stood tall and broad, spoke fluent English, and looked a little like a white man; in his pockets were the calling cards of many foreign reporters and diplomats, and he knew some of the foreign ambassadors and consuls stationed in China. The moment he was arrested he spoke no Chinese, poured out nothing but English, and kept protesting to the police that they had seized him without cause; he said he was a reporter, and demanded to telephone a certain ambassador and a certain consul. The enemy, unable to make out his depths and seeing that he "came from no small quarter," and having searched no suspicious evidence off him, let him go.

At this time there was a Nanhua News Agency run by a Cantonese, which, short of funds and short of hands, had, apart from its head office in Guangzhou, only a branch in Nanjing. (In fact both places had only two or three people and a signboard.) Shanghai was then the nation's political, economic, and cultural center; because no branch had been set up in Shanghai, business in Guangzhou and Nanjing was not easily developed either. Learning of this, Chen Yangshan went with Wang Anzhi, a reporter for Shanghai's Shishi Xinbao also known as Wang Xiong, formerly a Communist who turned traitor in 1931, to get in touch with the agency's proprietor. Learning that the man was short of funds but big in ambition, the Special Section subsidized him, provided people for him, and put up the signboard, quickly setting up a "Shanghai Branch of the Nanhua News Agency" and helping him establish a branch in Hankou as well. The Special Section used this agency to carry on its work, obtained much valuable intelligence on the imperialists and the Kuomintang, and gave the Special Section's comrades considerable convenience for their activities, serving as good cover. It was a pity that just as the business was steadily developing, the sudden affair of Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection broke, the White Terror settled over Shanghai, and this line of work too was quickly furled up and its drums stilled.

From 1928 until the Special Section wound up in June 1935, Chen Yangshan worked all along in intelligence in the Special Section. In September 1935, Chen Yangshan (under the alias Chen Zhongying) was sent from Shanghai to Chongqing, to open up intelligence work together with Chen Chang, who was already there. They found that the foundation for intelligence work in Sichuan was very good, the Party having fairly good relations with the Kuomintang, the local warlords, and every side, so that people could be sent in and there was great room for development; the trouble was only that the hands were too few, and it was very hard to open a way. For this Chen Yangshan went to Shanghai again, to seek help from the Party's liaison organ that remained there, and in December returned by boat to Chongqing together with Chen Kehan.

Just as the "Three Chens" were considering how to open a way in Sichuan and get their work going, they heard that the "New Sichuan News Agency," founded by the Sichuan power-holder Liu Hangchen, was preparing to close for lack of profit. Seizing the moment, they negotiated back and forth with Liu Hangchen, and at last, on the condition that they would relay his news dispatches first, had him hand the agency over to the "Three Chens" free of charge. On the further condition of providing some intelligence, they linked up with the political-training office of Chiang Kai-shek's Chongqing field headquarters, and so settled the question of funds. And so this news agency, with Chen Chang as director, Chen Yangshan as editor-in-chief, and Chen Kehan as reporter, began its work under a new face.

Chen Yangshan had originally had connections with some Shanghai press organs, and now came to Chongqing under the style of a reporter for the "Shishen News Agency"; Chen Kehan had been a reporter for Shanghai's Da Mei Wan Bao. To raise the agency's standing, besides the news they gathered themselves, they re-edited the news of the Kuomintang's Central News Agency and issued it as "this agency's special dispatches," and also, using Chen Kehan's connections in the press, had special contributions and "special dispatches" sent from Shanghai; the pieces they put out were welcomed by many papers. They also took the initiative to get in touch with the various newspapers and news agencies, working to unite the progressive figures of the local press, and quickly became the notable rising stars of the Chongqing press.

Chen Chang's wife Liu Qizhen, who took part in the work at the time, said: "Through several months of effort we obtained a great quantity of important intelligence, secret and open, illegal and legal, on military, political, and economic matters, and every kind of social intelligence besides. Because this intelligence came mainly from the Kuomintang's military and government leading organs, it was both secret and accurate and timely, and the work was very effective. And in these achievements Comrade Yangshan played an important part, for Comrade Yangshan was well on in years, had joined the Party earliest, and had the richest experience. At the time our operating funds were very hard, and the organization told us to find our own way to solve it; so Comrade Yangshan sold off everything he owned that was worth anything, and used it as the agency's operating funds. As a result he could not even guarantee his own basic living, and there were days he got by on a single sesame cake, very hard indeed. But his zeal for the work ran high; often he was editor-in-chief by day and a printing worker by night, taking the lead in every kind of task."

The "Three Chens'" efforts were not spent in vain: in no very long time, through this agency, they reaped rich results. In an essay he wrote afterward, "Recalling the 'New Sichuan News Agency,'" Chen Yangshan said:

Through several months of effort, we obtained a great quantity of important intelligence, secret and open, illegal and legal, on military, political, and economic matters: the party, government, and army movements of the Kuomintang Center and the local warlords; intelligence on the disposition of forces and on their struggles with one another; intelligence on the scrambles for power and profit between the Kuomintang's military and party organs and the various local factions; the conflict between the Chongqing business world and the Zhejiang capitalists; and every kind of social intelligence besides. This intelligence came mainly from the Kuomintang's military and government leading organs, secret, accurate, and timely, and for the most part we reported it to the Shanghai liaison organ in good time. Some, held up by circumstances, could not be sent out, which was a great pity.

Besides this, we also turned the enemy's contradictions to account, widening the breach and sharpening the dog-eat-dog struggles. When, for instance, Huang Yingqian, secretary of the Chongqing municipal government and a CC-clique member of the Kuomintang municipal party headquarters, seized people's houses to build a mansion for the newly appointed mayor Li Hongkun, a man of Liu Xiang's faction, Yuan Jiapei, commander of the Third Gendarmerie Regiment, got wind of it, and the "New Sichuan News Agency" ran the story and exposed it, setting the two sides at each other. Or again, we exposed the farce in which the Renaissance Society and the CC clique, fighting for control of the Kuomintang's Chongqing municipal party headquarters, came to blows in the headquarters itself. We also learned that Li Jiemin, a traitor and head of the news-censorship office of the Chongqing field headquarters, had used a fluoroscope lamp to intercept our secret method of chemical communication, and we reported it to the Center in good time, protecting the local Party organization and averting further damage.

Apart from the agency's work, we also secretly led and took part in local movements to resist Japan and save the nation, as in Chongqing's support for the Chengdu people's struggle against the Japanese setting up a consulate, and its aid to the Suiyuan people's anti-Japanese struggle; and in all this the "New Sichuan News Agency" stood at the front of the press. At the same time we took part in every kind of anti-Japanese national-salvation body and in the anti-Japanese national-salvation propaganda work of the colleges and middle schools. Some of the manifestos were drafted by Chen Kehan. He also planted himself as an editor in a newspaper run by Juntong.

This action, using an open news agency to shelter clandestine struggle, was a success. As Chen Yangshan said: "This 'New Sichuan News Agency' was paid for by the intelligence office of the Chongqing field headquarters, and it made wide use of the central party, government, and military organs of the Kuomintang and the forces of the local warlord factions, using them to keep in touch with people of every quarter, to unite the progressive figures of the press and cultural worlds, to gather a great deal of intelligence, and to carry on united-front work. With this utterly tangled political background and legal cover, who could ever suspect that it was a Communist intelligence organ!"

In July 1936, Chen Yangshan and Chen Kehan went from Chongqing to Shanghai to report on their work, just as the CCP Center was sending Comrade Feng Xuefeng to Shanghai to take up connections. After they had stayed a while in Shanghai, the organization decided to transfer them both back to northern Shaanxi. Chen Yangshan reached Xi'an in October, and the Party decided to keep him there, chiefly to take part in the work of liaison with the Northwest Army; still under cover as a reporter, he helped Wu Xiru run the Cultural Weekly, a journal funded by Zhang Xueliang to guide the movement to resist Japan and save the nation.

Before long the Xi'an Incident broke out. During this time, besides looking into how society was responding, Chen Yangshan was charged with setting up the Red China Agency in Xi'an. On December 19, Zhou Enlai telegraphed from Xi'an to Mao Zedong and Bo Gu: "It has been decided to set up the Red China News Agency in Xi'an. Please give attention to broadcast propaganda, and send out all open telegrams, letters, and propaganda material by broadcast; in arranging the broadcasts, any supplementary matter is also to be worked in."

The Red China News Agency was then set up in Xi'an, under Li Yimang, head of the propaganda department of the CCP Shaanxi provincial committee. Besides Chen Yangshan, the staff gathered included Chen Kehan and Bru (Chen Bo). After Bo Gu reached Xi'an, he went to the staff's quarters to talk with everyone. The Xi'an Red China News Agency was in the nature of a Xi'an branch of the Red China Agency, the forerunner of the Xinhua News Agency. Its task was to print and distribute to the Xi'an newspapers and public bodies the Red China Agency news taken down by the Xi'an Red Army office, along with the proclamations, manifestos, and the like issued by our Party. In his memoir Li Yimang also spoke of the matter: "Running the provincial committee's propaganda department in Xi'an was fairly simple at first. For at the time the Xi'an Incident was still unfolding, the political situation was changing greatly, and the attitude of the Party Center had great influence in society. Every day the Xi'an office took down the telegrams from Yan'an and passed them to us, and we edited them, mimeographed them into Xinhua News Agency dispatches, and sent them out to the party, government, army, and press organs of Xi'an. Two comrades, Chen Kehan and Bru, did this work, day in and day out… Later, once the Xi'an Incident was settled and the provincial committee moved out of Xi'an, mimeographing the Xinhua dispatches would no longer do, so Chen Kehan and the others all went to Yan'an… The mimeographed issue of these news dispatches of ours went on for about five months."

Chen Yangshan did not work long at the Xi'an Red China News Agency before he was transferred to the Xi'an intelligence station, where he took up intelligence and united-front work; in opposing civil war, in advancing the movement to resist Japan and save the nation, and in furthering the development of united-front work, he did hard work and won marked achievements.
