## On Du Yuesheng

{d} Narrated by Fan Shaozeng; set down by Shen Zui

My dealings with Du Yuesheng began around 1925. At the time I was commander of the Seventh Division under the Sichuan warlord Yang Sen, garrisoned at Wanxian. That Du was a boss of the Shanghai Green Gang I had heard long before, and that I was a Sichuan Paoge chieftain, he too knew. On the strength of this connection he was forever sending his disciples to me with letters of introduction, asking me to help them buy up opium within my garrison district. Each time one of his letters reached me, I did all I could to help.

Around 1928, when I was commander of the Fourth Division of the Sichuan Army under Liu Xiang, he also sent me a fellow gang brother of his, the morphine king Chen Kunyuan, who set up a morphine factory in Linshui county within my district, under my protection. To build a plant and manufacture morphine right where the opium was grown brought profits a dozen times and more greater than shipping raw opium from Sichuan to Shanghai.

In 1929 I went to Shanghai for the first time and met him face to face. Besides welcoming me warmly, he exchanged birth-cards with me and we became sworn brothers; and he kept me company in every pleasure Shanghai had to offer, sparing no effort to display the extraordinary power he wielded there.

After that I made time nearly every year to go to Shanghai for amusement, and each time he entertained me exactly to my taste.

In 1933, when Chiang Kai-shek moved Sichuan troops into Hubei to wage his anti-Communist war, I led my men into battle against the Red Army under the commander He Long and was gravely wounded. On hearing this, Du at once sent his disciple Zhang Songtao hurrying to Hankou to bring me to Shanghai for treatment. The moment the boat reached the wharf, he had Gu Jiatang there to meet me on his behalf and take me to the General Hospital by the Waibaidu Bridge, where he found an English doctor to treat me, so that my wounded right arm was saved from amputation. From then on we grew closer still.

During the War of Resistance he came to Chongqing; and though my own troops had been sent to the front, I myself was often back in Chongqing for amusement and much in his company. After the victory, when I was living in Shanghai, his second daughter Du Meixia was given to me as a goddaughter, so that we became relatives by adoption, and the two households visited back and forth constantly.

This account is for the most part what I myself saw in my dealings with him or heard from his own mouth; some of it was told me by Gu Jiatang, Ma Shiqi, and others long at his side; and some is what I have picked out, as the more reliable part, from the various tales told about him.

### I. Early Activities

Du Yong, styled Yuesheng, was born on the fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month in the fourteenth year of the Guangxu reign (1888) at Gaoqiao in Pudong, Shanghai. His family was poor, and while still in his teens he drifted to Shanghai, where he fell in with small-time hoodlums like Ma Shiqi and went in for "trade without capital." He and Ma often shared a single bed in some cheap inn; he dearly loved to sleep late, and only when his belly was cramping with hunger would he crawl up and beg Ma and the rest to send the gang out snatching other people's hats — what Shanghai folk called "tossing for a skullcap" — to sell for a little something to fill his stomach.

He knocked about like this for some years, and feeling it led nowhere, he set up a fruit stall at the gate of the "Great World" amusement park that Huang Jinrong ran, paring the fruit for his customers. He grew known for selling Laiyang pears, and many gave him the nickname "Laiyang Pear." At first he was rather pleased with this by-name: when people called it out he would answer readily, and he liked to use it himself when introducing himself. Only later, as he slowly rose in the world, did people stop calling it to his face. Yet many of the small hoodlums who had knocked about with him in the old days, when they came to him for money and were refused, would still bawl it at him without ceremony. Going with him in Shanghai to drink and carouse at the brothels of Huileli on Fourth Avenue, I saw this happen several times: the moment his car pulled up, a crowd of little hoodlums would swarm round with hands out, and he, hurrying away, would call to his men to be quick and give them money. Sometimes, when too little was handed over, these hoodlums would shout, "Laiyang Pear, give a bit more!" and his men would have to add to it at once before the pack of guttersnipes could be got rid of.

The first time I saw this I was much puzzled: how could this great Green Gang boss have no way to deal with such people? Only later, when Ma Shiqi told me the story above, did I understand that it was all a matter of this old history between them.

He kept up his fruit-paring skill ever after. Often, when only he and I were present, I would see Gu Jiatang pick up a piece of fruit and hold it out to him to pare, and he would always smile and pare it quickly, without the least sign of displeasure. He liked to joke with Gu in turn: since Gu had begun as a gardener, he would set him to arranging the flowers in his house.

Gu and he leaned on each other and played each other up. If guests were present, Gu would always put on a show of deep respect and obedience, but when the two were alone there was nothing they would not say to each other. Only after I had been long in their company did I see this special relationship between them for what it was.

Du had always been known for his gift for schemes, and in the gangs he was called the "staff adviser" or the "Zhuge Liang." Back in his fruit-paring days, many little hoodlums would come to him for a plan to extort and blackmail. It is said that at the time many shops, fearing the hoodlums would make trouble at their openings, would ask the police station to send men to protect them. Du would then set the little hoodlums to stealing the shop's signboard by night and coming back the next day to extort. And where a busy, thriving shop refused to pay the hoodlums off, he would set these men to brawling with each other at its door, flinging filth about and the like, until the customers scattered in avoidance and there was nothing for it but to pay a little to be rid of them.

Before long his name reached the ears of Huang Jinrong's wife. She had him sent for, and finding in conversation that he was indeed a man of many devices, decided to take him into the Huang household. From then on he and Huang worked hand in glove, and he became an important helper under Huang, doing untold evil in the French Concession.

During his years in the Huang household, while devising schemes for Huang he was also quietly laying much groundwork for setting up on his own one day. He used Huang's connections to make ties far and wide and to draw men of every sort to himself. When he judged his feathers full-grown, he broke away from the Huang house and banded together with Zhang Xiaolin, Jin Tingsun, Gu Jiatang, and others; he even drew off Huang's cook, Ma Xiangsheng. Huang was much displeased with him, and so he went and took another Green Gang boss, Chen Shichang, as his master.

Chen Shichang's own master was the head of the Ren Society, one Zhang Jinghu, a Shandong man who had been garrison commander of Tonghai. Many of his pupils were military and political chiefs — Yang Hu, He Guiyan, Han Fuju, and the like. Chiang Kai-shek, in his own drifting days in Shanghai, had also entered as Zhang's disciple; only later, when Chiang's station had risen, did he refuse to acknowledge this master.

I too had entered as Zhang's disciple and joined the Ren Society, so that by rights I stood in the same generation as Du's master Chen Shichang; but afterward, because Du held such power in Shanghai, no one any longer reckoned seniority of generation with him.

When Du first left the Huang house, knowing he was no match for Huang, he dared not fight him for his sources of income, and so looked to the commercial world for a different opening. His men would make trouble only at his bidding, so that businessmen had no way to peace but to seek him out to act for them — and in this way the foundation was slowly laid for his role as bodyguard to the capitalists.

Huang Jinrong went on making his money by the old crude methods of grabbing and extorting. When some official who had squeezed the people's blood and sweat in the provinces came to Shanghai to live at his ease, and Huang's men got wind of it, they would use every kind of means to blackmail him until the money he had brought was gone, then give him a little travel money to leave Shanghai. Du did not work this way: whenever a warlord or official from elsewhere came to Shanghai, he would take any chance to make his acquaintance, treat him as a friend, and act as his bodyguard. That is the reason he came to know so very many warlords, officials, and politicians, old and new, northern and southern.

On the surface he and Huang Jinrong were always civil to each other, but beneath it their struggle never for a moment ceased. I remember that after victory in the war Huang extorted two hundred million yuan from Qian Xinzhi, chairman of the Bank of Communications, and Qian, afraid of trouble in Shanghai, paid the whole sum over. Du was at the time a director of the Bank of Communications and on unusually good terms with Qian; learning of it, he was much put out, and complained that Qian Xinzhi had not told him beforehand. Not long after, Huang came forward again with a letter to the Chongqing Commercial Bank asking to borrow four hundred million yuan. I was a director of that bank, and I went to Du. Du gave me a plan: he told me to telephone Huang myself and ask him to his face, and see how he could have the nerve. I did as Du advised, and sure enough Huang would not admit it was his own doing but pushed it off on his men — and so the money was never taken.

Another reason Du's power in Shanghai gradually took shape was that the money he raked in was great, and he spent it freely too, always in with the left hand and out with the right, unlike Huang Jinrong, who took in but never gave out. So the men who played him up grew ever more numerous.

That he could make a name for himself owed also to a whole art of drawing men to him. With a man he wished to befriend, he would first approach that man's relatives and friends to voice his admiration and praise, so that the man would be glad to meet him. His way of making friends, too, was unlike other people's: at the meeting he would show himself very warm and modest, and once they had met, if he judged the man could be of use to him, he would by every possible means, in other company or before people connected with the man, deliberately sing his praises, so that the words would reach the man's ears and gladden his very heart and make him well disposed toward Du.

He was skilled at reading others' minds, and could deal with men of different temperaments by different methods, so that many, after meeting him, always loved to keep up the acquaintance.

When someone came to him for help, so long as he could later recover his outlay from that man, he would agree readily, willing even to lose a little for the moment. He often spoke to me of looking to the long view and not only to what lay before one's eyes; and so, when the time came for him to make use of a man, it usually went smoothly.

These methods of his made not only many hoodlums serve him body and soul; even men like Yang Hu, Chen Qun, and Gu Jiatang did his every bidding.

He went to great lengths, too, to take in and win over disaffected officials, politicians, and men of letters to serve him as advisers and play him up, and he took care to heed the counsel of these men who had seen great storms in the political world.

Among the secretaries he employed were Yang Du, foremost of the so-called "Six Gentlemen" who had got up the Chou'an Society for Yuan Shikai, and Xu Muxing, who had been secretary in President Xu Shichang's office. Many other old-style literati and foreign-trained doctors, too, were forever devising plans for him. In using these men of some former standing and reputation, he did not treat them as ordinary reactionary chiefs treat their subordinates; rather, he showed at every turn an attitude of humbly seeking their instruction, and was most punctilious in courtesy, so that these self-styled pure and lofty gentlemen served him loyally as his tools without even knowing it.

In his early years he had always wanted to model himself on Song Jiang of Liangshan Marsh, playing the "Timely Rain" in society and forever fond of small kindnesses and favors. And the disaffected officials, politicians, and men of letters who played him up, wishing to raise their own worth by leaning on him, hailed him as some "Lord Chunshen of our day" and "little Lord Mengchang."

In dispensing these small kindnesses he had another very clever trick, which in his own words was: "To spend one cash and get the effect of ten — that is the man who knows how to spend money." This was also the advice he often gave me when he saw me throwing money about without much thought to purpose. In helping others he would often keep himself out of sight: if he gave a man a sum of money, or solved some hard problem for him, then, once it was done, he would not admit it was his doing, and the man who had acted for him would then, more often than not, tell the beneficiary the truth of how Du had helped him yet refused to show his hand — so that the man helped was the more grateful, and would spread his praises everywhere.

Du Yuesheng's art of playing men up was indeed something to see, and it was of enormous help to his fame; he did it without leaving a trace, so that the man played up was delighted. I remember that after victory in the war there was a flood in Sichuan, and Xiang Chuanyi, speaker of the Sichuan Provincial Assembly, and He Beiheng went to Shanghai to raise relief funds. They first went to the mayor, Wu Guozhen, to talk it over, but Wu shrugged it off entirely, saying they had come a step too late — he had just raised funds for floods in northern Jiangsu and several other places, and there was no way to ask for donations again. Xiang and the rest then went to Du, who agreed at once and, thumping his chest, said, "We ate off Sichuan for a good few years; now that Sichuan is in trouble, what kind of men would we be if we did not help!" In a few days he had the matter well in hand. He might simply have handed the funds he raised over to Xiang and the others, but he seized the chance instead to play up H. H. Kung. He first explained to Kung the whole business of the Sichuan appeal and Wu Guozhen's refusal to help, and said he was willing to see to it himself and needed only Kung to appear in the matter. Kung was delighted to fall in with his arrangement, and invited Wu Guozhen and the others to dine at his house; He Beiheng, Xiang Chuanyi, and I were all there too. At table Kung said his piece just as Du had told him to, and adding a few words about Sichuan being his second home and that when it was in trouble he must do all he could to help, he pointed to Du and said that he had already charged Yuesheng to set about the matter at once and that they must not fail their fellow Sichuanese. Du then rose and declared, "Since Your Excellency the President cares so much about this, Yuesheng will certainly obey and do his utmost, and I hope you will all lend me every help." Those few of us who knew the inside of it, seeing this trick he played, which set Kung beaming from ear to ear, all raised our thumbs behind his back and said: Yuesheng was a big shot indeed.

He always took pains in playing up Kung, and so Kung was willing to play him up in return. In 1947, for instance, when his two sons Weiping and Weixin held their weddings on the same day at the Lido Garden in Shanghai, he asked Kung to be the marriage witness, and I heard Kung, before the go-betweens Qian Xinzhi and Zhang Shizhao and the more than a thousand wedding guests present — Yan Huiqing, Xu Jiqing, Li Shizeng, and the rest — declare that Du was "a man of enterprise rare in China, of far-reaching vision and a way of life that disciplined himself and helped others..."

Because Du fawned on Kung like this, Kung in many matters looked after him specially, giving him many chances to make money. After the war, for instance, when Kung once learned that Chiang Kai-shek had decided to dump the great quantity of cotton yarn Japan had surrendered, he secretly tipped Du off to unload at once the yarn he was holding, so as not to lose by it, and Du thereby made a great pile of money. Of the Four Great Families — Chiang, Soong, Kung, and Chen — it was Kung who was most susceptible to that flattering art of his, and their relations were the closest.

His notice in society really began in 1924. That autumn, when the two warlords Qi Xieyuan and Lu Yongxiang fell to fighting near Shanghai, country folk from all around fled into the concessions for refuge, and thousands upon thousands were left adrift in the streets. At the time the hoodlums inside the concessions were seizing the chance to swindle the refugees out of what little they had and to traffic in their women; but Du took a novel line, actually falling in with a set of so-called "charitable gentlemen" who lived off charity, and getting up refugee-relief work. Under the name of relief he sent his creatures out with subscription books to press and levy donations from merchants and townsfolk by force — a barefaced way of making money that not only brought him great profit but even won him some men's praise, while the refugees were most grateful for it; and so, at one stroke, Du Yuesheng was turned into a "great charitable gentleman."

Having tasted this sweetness once, thereafter, wherever some natural disaster or scourge of war occurred, he was always most zealous in promoting these "good works," raising subscriptions on a great scale in Shanghai. Because this way of making money without capital brought him both name and profit, he never tired of it.

I remember, too, that some year before the war, when a natural disaster struck somewhere or other, he was again presiding over "relief" work. By then he was at the very height of his fame in Shanghai, and to put on a benefit performance for the relief he and Zhang Xiaolin, Wang Xiaolai, and others took the stage as guest players, to draw a crowd. He played Huang Tianba, Zhang played Dou Erdun, and the top ticket was ten yuan.

That day I had invited a great crowd to go and play him up, but the moment he came on stage he set off a burst of laughter through the house. Though he was fond of Peking opera as a rule, and his concubine Yao Yulan was a famous performer, and he kept up all year with some of the great masters of the art, his own singing was wretched. No sooner had Zhang Xiaolin opened his mouth than his false teeth slid out; he snatched them up in a fluster, the house roared with laughter, and Zhang forgot even his lines. Jin Shaoshan, watching by the stage entrance and seeing things go wrong, hit on a quick expedient and hurried over with a teapot to give Zhang water, reading him his lines under his breath. By now some in the audience were grumbling and cracking jokes, and one man called out loudly, "Mei Lanfang's tickets go for only five yuan, and 'Laiyang Pear' charges ten and still clowns like this." Du's creatures, hearing this, seized the man who had said it and set to cursing him roundly. There was no fight on the stage, but below it a real brawl broke out, and the place was one great melee.

Du could win fame and the praise of so many chiefly because he was skilled at playing a double game. Let me here set out a few of the more important matters I saw for myself, to make this plain.

The first is the inside story of his mediation in the great strike of the water-and-electricity workers in the Shanghai French Concession in 1930. In that affair more than twenty workers were killed or wounded by the French imperialists, making the July 21 Massacre in Shanghai; and in this massacre he raised his own standing.

The affair arose because the French capitalists, relying on their privileges in Shanghai, exploited the workers cruelly. At the time the French staff of the water-and-electricity company drew at least two hundred-odd taels of silver a month, while the Chinese workers averaged only twelve yuan; and every time the company agreed to the workers' demands for better treatment, it never made good on them. In mid-June 1930 the workers went out on a general strike, demanding that the promise to raise wages be kept. The very next day the French side closed the factory gates and refused to let the workers in. The French Water and Electricity Workers' Union resolved to carry out the strike, formally putting forward as conditions for returning to work a raise of eight yuan a month per man and the abolition of the fine system; the French imperialists would accept none of it. The Nationalists' Shanghai municipal Party office several times invited the two sides, labor and capital, to mediation, but the French refused to take part, their attitude utterly overbearing, and they announced that all workers who had joined the strike were dismissed without exception, hiring instead a batch of White Russians and new workers to take over the work, so that the conflict between the two sides sharpened.

Du Yuesheng at first feigned sympathy for the workers, sending them a sum of money in support and agreeing to their request that he mediate. At this point the French capitalists' agent, Shen Shumei, comprador of the French water-and-electricity company, also came to him, asking him to find a way to stop the workers from holding out in their strike, and to this too he readily agreed.

The strike at first was limited to the engineering department; but after the French imperialists set Vietnamese police to open fire on Huacheng Road, killing a worker on his way to a meeting, the traffic department's workers joined the strike too, so that in the French Concession the electric lights went dark, the trams stopped running, and the running water failed, and the French national-day revels set for July 14 had to be put off. On July 21 the French police shot down workers meeting at the club of the water-and-electricity union, killing and wounding more than ten on the spot; this massacre roused the fury of the whole city's workers, and the other unions rose as one to support them.

The French consul-general, Gan Gelin, and the police commissioner Fei Woli asked Du to step in and find some way to keep the labor unrest from going on, though they would not accept the workers' demands. To curry favor with the French imperialists, Du set his disciple Lu Jingshi and others to organize a "Strike Support Association," ostensibly to help but in fact to split and undermine the strike.

At this time the mayor of Shanghai, Zhang Qun, and the garrison commander Xiong Shihui also posted notices strictly forbidding the workers to strike on their own and forbidding other factories to support the strike. The police bureau even sent out large numbers of police to protect the White Russians whom the French imperialists had sent into the Chinese quarter to repair the water mains leading to the French Concession — which stirred the workers' anger the more. For the French imperialists' reason for refusing the mediation of the Nationalist municipal Party office was that their company was not registered with the Nationalist government, so the Nationalists had no right to interfere; yet the Nationalist government, unbidden, sent police to protect the work, and made wholesale arrests of the Chinese workers who tried to stop the White Russians from repairing the mains.

Chiang Kai-shek, fearing that if the workers held out it would harm Sino-French relations, hastily sent the Nationalist Central Committee member Li Shizeng to Shanghai to mediate. Li, long in collusion with the French imperialists, on reaching Shanghai went first to Du, and Du agreed to everything, willing to share Chiang Kai-shek's toil and cares, and pressed on the harder with his tricks to divide the workers.

The French imperialists' attitude remained utterly savage: they declared martial law in the French Concession, sent out more armored cars on patrol, and went on arresting the workers who led the strike; and when these men fled to the Chinese quarter, the police bureau arrested them just the same.

The unrest held out until mid-August; and only because the French Concession's water and power supply grew tighter by the day and tram traffic was cut off did the French authorities at last allow a raise of two yuan and forty cents a month per man — but they insisted on dismissing the forty workers who had led the strike.

The moment Du Yuesheng and Li Shizeng heard the French side agree to a small wage rise, they took it for a splendid victory and insisted the workers go back to work. For the forty men the French had dismissed, Du promised to be responsible for placing them in union work, with their wages paid by himself; and the striking workers had no choice but grudgingly to agree to return.

At the signing, the workers demanded that the several dozen men arrested one after another be released before they went back; the French side insisted on returning to work first and deciding afterward as the situation warranted, and it nearly came to another deadlock. In the end it was Du who signed to bail them out, and so brought the labor unrest to an end. As for the forty the French wanted dismissed, Du gave them two months' wages and packed them off, achieving the French aim of dismissal; and the few who would not return to work were even given a beating by his creatures.

This affair gave Li Shizeng face, relieved Chiang Kai-shek of his worry, made the French imperialists prize him the more, and even won him the gratitude of some workers who did not know the truth. Du had killed four birds with one stone.

The second is the matter of the Nationalists' election of a vice-president in 1947. Du and I were then both in Nanjing; I was staying at the Central Hotel, and Zeng Kuoqing kept me company all day long, afraid I would go out and get up to things, for I could command a good many of the votes of the Sichuan National Assembly delegates. Zeng wanted me to support Sun Ke, but as Li Zongren had treated me well in the past, I meant to support Li. One morning Chen Lifu came to me and said Chiang Kai-shek had summoned me. I did not want to go; that afternoon he sent me Chiang's written order that I must be there on time.

When I saw Chiang, I learned it was likewise to have me support Sun Ke. Not having expected that Chiang would take the field himself to canvass for Sun Ke, I told him honestly that I had promised to cast my vote for Li. Chiang then instructed me that my own one vote might go to Li, but that all the other votes I could command must go to Sun. I was somewhat undecided about it, and went to Du Yuesheng, who told me quite frankly that no matter who asked him for support, he agreed to it — not like me. On such occasions, he said, one should be nimble: not only must one not offend anyone over such a thing, one should seize the chance to win men over — that was the way. After he had passed on this secret of his to me, I watched carefully, and sure enough every man standing in the election pinned his hopes on Du. The votes he could actually command were few to begin with, and split up could have no effect at all; yet he himself gained a great deal by it.

In his early years his hand was very open, and he took great care to use gifts to cut a figure and draw men to him. Before the war, when Chiang Kai-shek was promoting "Save the Nation through Aviation," Du at once made a show of responding, buying a French trainer aircraft and presenting it to the Shanghai Aviation Club. Later, when Sun Tongxuan's brother Sun Tonggang came back from studying aviation in France, he bought another plane and gave it to him. In those days to give a man an airplane was still a rare thing, so the newspapers trumpeted this act of his to the skies — which was exactly his aim. In presenting these two planes he named each after himself: the one to the Aviation Club he called the "Yuewen," and the one to Sun Tonggang the "Yuehui," the intent needing no telling.

Other gifts — cars, concubines, and the like — were commoner still: the cars used in the Shanghai mansions of quite a few reactionary chiefs were all given by him. To many foreigners working in the police stations, too, he gave cars and such. To the many Chinese and Western detective-inspectors and detective-sergeants he would send a rich present at every New Year and festival, often of a thousand or two thousand yuan.

Zhang Yishu, who in the early days made his ties with the French imperialists for him, praised this touch of his most of all. Zhang was a returned student from France who had been a Chinese member of the French Concession's Municipal Council, and it was said that he had connections in the French cabinet: matters the French Concession authorities would not grant him, he could take straight to the relevant men in the French cabinet by letter. Du did well in the French Concession in the early days, and Zhang Yishu had helped him not a little. When his disciples were arrested for offenses within the French Concession, or when others asked him to put in a word with the French police to have someone bailed out, a single telephone call or a name-card from him would settle the matter. For especially grave matters, or ones it was awkward for him to appear in himself, Zhang Yishu would go about the bribery on his behalf, and to great effect.

He made friends very widely: whenever a warlord or official or politician, from north or south, or a famous foreigner, came to Shanghai, he would through the proper channels get in touch and entertain them. His proudest boast was that when the Japanese elder statesman Saionji came to Shanghai he showed Du great good will. When he told me that he had kept up ties with Saionji ever after, I half believed and half doubted him; but later, when my son Fan Zhiqing went to study in Japan, Du wrote a letter for my son to carry to Saionji, and he was indeed well looked after, so that I came to believe Du was not merely boasting. When the Lytton Commission came to Shanghai, too, he entertained it lavishly; and the foreigners who ran the Chinese customs and posts kept up regular dealings with him. As for the warlords and officials of the various provinces and the Nationalist military and civil officials, when they reached Shanghai he entertained them as a matter of course.

In less than ten years he had surpassed Huang Jinrong and the other big-gang chiefs, men of far longer standing than he. As he gradually gained some name in society, wishing to deal with the so-called upper crust, he had to change his old manner — cap askew, sleeves rolled up, shuffling along with his shoes trodden down at the heel — and put on the air of a cultivated gentleman. He told me himself that he had worked hard at imitating it, and was very ill at ease at first; but afterward, whenever he appeared in any public place, or even received guests at home on the hottest day, he would put on a long gown, and to call on men of standing he would add a riding jacket over it. Not only did he do this himself, he required his disciples and their disciples to dress so too, and he often said to me, "With food and clothing enough, rites and right conduct should flourish; we can't have people take one look and be disgusted and afraid."

His schooling was about the same as mine: he could still make out some characters in the regular script, but anything with a touch of running or cursive hand he could not read. Yet on writing his own name he had spent real effort, and to see his signature you would think he had had some years of schooling. He was never hasty in speech, and later put on an even more refined manner; in public and before guests he did not know well he would not talk freely at random, for fear of giving himself away. So a man meeting him for the first time who did not know his origins could not at a glance make out his true face.

For all these reasons he did not like to be called "Boss" as Huang Jinrong was, but preferred to be called "Mr. Du."

He had always been praised by his brothers of the greenwood for never blocking another man's road to riches. Whenever a kidnapping or extortion case occurred in Shanghai and someone asked him to help settle it, though he knew perfectly well which crowd had done it, and though his mere stepping in would have set all right, he would never agree to do such a thing. On the one hand he did not wish to offend those desperadoes, fearing they might later do him harm; on the other he was afraid of being said to be in league with bandits, which would hurt his own name.

Yet in helping others to make a fortune he was a very good friend indeed, as I myself found on several occasions.

In 1946 I heard it said that the Nationalists were auctioning off the enemy and puppet property they had taken over in Shanghai, that a whole warehouse could be sold at once, and that if one could find the way in and buy up a warehouse, one could make a killing. I talked it over with Du. He had me invite the director of the Enemy and Puppet Property Administration Bureau, Liu Gongyun, to dinner, and undertook to put in a word for me himself, so that Gu Jiatang, Zhang Junsheng, and I went in together on the deal, three of us. Liu Gongyun and I were already acquainted, though not close; and following Du's advice I laid on a banquet at my house for Liu, with Du to keep him company. When the wine had flowed and we were well fed, I put my request about buying a warehouse to Liu; and before Liu could answer, Du at once cut in, "These things are all in his grip — what more is there to be said?" Liu had no choice but to agree to let me buy one warehouse. There was no need even to pay cash at the time; I merely had my own Fuhua Bank issue a promissory note, and the thing was settled — as good as an outright gift.

The bales of cloth, cotton yarn, honey, hides, and the rest in the warehouses were beyond our expectations in quantity and variety. Since being taken over, these warehouses had never so much as been inventoried; ask the warehouse keepers, and they could not tell you the quantities, nor even clearly what things were there. The degree of corruption was truly astonishing. That one time I made over three thousand taels of gold.

Du held the concurrent post of chairman of the Shanghai Stock Exchange; when Gu Jiatang and I wanted to do some speculating, we would go to Du for guidance, and with a word of his we could make several dozen or several hundred taels of gold in the twinkling of an eye. Later, when Gu and I asked him for a broker's number at the exchange, he said he feared it would set tongues wagging, for too many people wanted the thing, and on the black market one already sold for several hundred million yuan; but in the end he still gave the two of us one between us. Afterward, too lazy to run to the exchange, we sold the number for several hundred taels of gold. That I had so much money to throw about in Shanghai after victory in the war was mostly thanks to Du's help.

After leaving Huang Jinrong's house and setting up on his own in Shanghai, he took the greatest care to avoid making enemies. Still, many looked down on him, and some, fearing he might one day turn on them, struck at him at every turn. He told me that when he first came up he had suffered no little bullying, and the one he hated most was Wei Tingrong and his brother. Wei belonged to the Zhejiang faction, and his power in Shanghai had taken shape earlier than Du's; in the French Concession too he was a man of the moment. During the mixed warfare of the Jiangsu and Zhejiang warlords, when no few stragglers and disbanded soldiers slipped into the concessions and ran wild everywhere, and even the imperialists found them hard to handle, Wei Tingrong got up and founded the "Chinese Volunteer Corps" in the French Concession, making himself its commander and helping keep order there, which won him the French imperialists' high regard; afterward he served as chairman of the chamber of commerce and as a Chinese member of the French Concession Municipal Council. When Du was just beginning to make his mark, Wei, fearing future rivalry, held him down at every turn, and Du, his feathers not yet full, could only bear it as best he might, while inwardly hating him to the bone. Later, when Du had made his connections with both the imperialists and the Nationalists and his power grew mightier by the day, he took his revenge on Wei, actually sending his creatures to kidnap him; and when that failed, he made ready to assassinate him. Wei, his own strength dwindling and no longer prized by the French imperialists, had no choice but to leave Shanghai and lie low for a time. Later, when Du's position in Shanghai was secure, he no longer used such savage means of revenge, and when Wei afterward returned to Shanghai, Du was still outwardly very civil to him.

Du's health had never been good, yet he was fond of women, and he also took to smoking opium, of which he had a great craving, needing two or three taels a day. But he smoked at set times — thrice daily, morning, noon, and night, and no more — so it did not much interfere with his social rounds and his work; when he had promised to go somewhere, he generally kept the appointment, and later, even when his asthma came on badly, he would force himself, ill as he was, to keep his engagements. He often said to me that whatever happened one must be as good as one's word, or else better not promise at all. I remember once, at the opening of the Shanghai branch of the Sichuan Salt Bank, when he and I and Gu Jiatang went together to offer congratulations, his asthma came on so badly in the car that he could scarcely get his breath; yet as soon as he was a little better he insisted on going on. When we arrived, I hurried to tell Liu Hangchen how things stood, and Liu, without letting him get out of the car, asked him to go home.

He did not care for Western suits, but loved Western food, and was fond of snacks and sweets. He had five wives. Of his first wife I know little; I only heard that his eldest son, Du Weifan, was not his own, but a child his first wife had taken in from someone else. He was very superstitious, and since taking this son into the house everything had gone smoothly, so that he looked on the boy as a "treasure that brings the family fortune" and doted on him, more than on his own sons.

Later he took two more concubines, his third and fifth wives, from the brothels, and, still not content, had Yang Hu arrange the whole thing to force a famous Peking-opera actress, Yao Yulan, into becoming a concubine of his. After victory in the war he took up as well with the famous Peking-opera actress Meng Xiaodong; and after the liberation of Shanghai, when he fled to Hong Kong, though he was already past sixty, he even held a formal wedding with Meng. Wives though he had, he still loved to go often to the "long-three" houses of Huileli for his amusements; reckoning in the silver dollars of before the war, whenever he and I stood a round of "flower wine" at a long-three brothel it always cost five hundred to a thousand yuan. Later, when he had got his asthma and even climbing a single flight of stairs was a labor to him, he would still want to go to such places, and when he could not manage the stairs he had men carry him up and down in a rattan chair.

For all his own days and nights of wine and revelry and his life of boundless dissipation, he kept a very strict watch on his women. I heard it said that his first wife had been on good terms with him at first, until someone told him that she had an improper relationship with one of his own gang brothers. In a fit of rage he had his creatures cut off the man's foot; and afterward, when it came out that it was all a false rumor he had heard, and he was afraid of what people would say, he had no choice but to take the man, now crippled for life, into his own house to keep, and before long the man died. His first wife, too, died, unable to bear the ill treatment.

Nor was it only toward his own wives that he was so. During the War of Resistance, in Chongqing, when he heard that a concubine of Yang Hu's called "Little Tiger," left behind in Shanghai, had a relationship with Yang's disciple Wang Pan, he and Dai Li insisted that Yang Hu find a way to have Wang Pan killed, saying this was a grave breach of the gang's rules and that the man must be put to death. Later, for some reason I do not know, Yang Hu did not kill Wang, and Du showed great displeasure at it.

Besides Weifan, who was adopted, his children — borne later by his third and fifth wives, Yao Yulan, and the others — were seven sons: Weiyuan, Weiping, Weihan, Weining, Weixin, Weishan, and Weisong; and two daughters, Meiru and Meixia.

His several wives all kept no small private hoards, and when I sometimes ran short of money to spend I would even go to his wives to borrow. Once I borrowed 20,000 American dollars from Yao Yulan, and she said there were only 19,000; taking it, I wondered how it came to be exactly a thousand short, until Gu Jiatang set me straight: "When you pay it back, won't you just make up the round sum for her!" Only then did I realize that my adoptive relative, the mother, had docked the interest in advance.

### II. The Special Power Built on Anti-Communism

That Du Yuesheng could enjoy his so-called "renown" in old China for more than thirty years, long trusted by the imperialists and by Chiang Kai-shek and the rest and backed by the great capitalists, turned above all on his anti-Communism.

In the spring of 1927, before the Northern Expedition army reached Shanghai, several hundred thousand Shanghai workers had already organized under Communist leadership and set up a General Labor Union with its own armed pickets. When Sun Chuanfang's Zhili-Shandong Allied Army fled in rout, the workers rose up and seized great quantities of the routed soldiers' arms and ammunition, took many counter-revolutionary troops prisoner, kept order in Shanghai, and put out the fires the fleeing soldiers had set before they left — for which the common people of Shanghai loved them greatly.

After the workers put forward the two slogans of "Down with imperialism" and "Take back the concessions," the imperialists of both the International Settlement and the French Concession were thrown into panic and dread. They wired their home countries in haste, asking for more troops to be sent to Shanghai, and at the same time entered into collusion with Bai Chongxi, then front-line commander of the Eastern Route Army, and with Chiang Kai-shek, who reached Shanghai afterward.

In early April, eight imperialist powers — the United States, Japan, Britain, France, and others — assembled more than fifty warships and brought them to Shanghai; Britain even brought an aircraft carrier bearing several dozen planes to the mouth of the Wusong; and the French imperialists drew several battalions of French and Vietnamese troops from Paris and Haiphong into the French Concession. From March the two concessions had begun to enforce martial law, closing the iron gates leading to the Chinese quarter, setting up cannon and machine guns in the streets, and sending armored cars patrolling everywhere.

There were many rumors at the time, and the imperialists were forever fabricating facts, saying that workers somewhere were trying to force their way into the concessions with arms, and lodging protests with Bai Chongxi over it. By Du's later recollection, the imperialists in the concessions were then very fierce in their arrogance: officers and men of the National Revolutionary Army who pasted up propaganda slogans beside the concessions' iron gates were often seized by the concession police. At that time Wei Daoming was practicing law in Shanghai, and each time the police station sent the captured officers and men to the concession's court to be tried and sentenced, it was Wei Daoming who defended them; at each trial, as often as not, the arrested officers and men were forced to admit they had strayed into the concession by mistake and to sign a written repentance before they could be released on bail. Chiang Kai-shek then issued a written order strictly forbidding officers and men to enter the concessions, on pain of punishment for disobedience if found out.

Du's power in Shanghai at the time, though less than Huang Jinrong's, was extraordinarily active. Chen Qun, director of the political department of the front-line command; Yang Hu, chief of the special-services section of Chiang Kai-shek's general headquarters; Wu Zhongxin, commissioner of the Songhu Police — all had close dealings with him; and after Chen Qun took him to see Bai Chongxi and Chiang Kai-shek, both Chiang and Bai praised him highly and even gave a banquet for him and Huang Jinrong, Zhang Xiaolin, and the others.

Among the imperialist chiefs, it was first the French commander Ba'erxue who thought highly of him; later Chen Qun introduced the British commander Dengkan and the American commander Baiduolou and others to deal with him. From Du's mouth they could learn the state of things in the General Labor Union and with Chiang Kai-shek, Bai Chongxi, and the rest, and so all at once he came to seem important.

In early April Chiang Kai-shek held several secret meetings in Shanghai with Bai Chongxi, T. V. Soong, Li Jishen, Wu Zhongxin, and others, and Du learned from Chen Qun that Chiang was preparing to purge the Communists from the Party. By then rumors were already flying outside: some said the Communists meant to form a government, overthrow the Nationalists, and take over the concessions by force. By Du's account, this rumor was put about by Chen Qun's political department. Another had it that the Nationalists meant to expel the Communists and dissolve the General Labor Union and the pickets — this was the leaking-out of the anti-Communist plot of Chiang Kai-shek and his set.

The most laughable thing, Du said, was that the Communist leader Chen Duxiu, on hearing all this, actually went to Wang Jingwei to look into it; Wang of course flatly denied it, and the two issued a joint declaration to deny both versions. Chiang Kai-shek even presented the pickets with a silk banner reading "Struggle Together," and the Shanghai Municipal Government Committee praised the pickets as "the armed force of the people"; and who would have thought that a few days later a great anti-Communist massacre would break out.

After Du had word from Chen Qun and Yang Hu to mobilize the Shanghai hoodlums to join the anti-Communist action, he first talked Zhang Xiaolin round, and when Zhang agreed, went on to persuade Huang Jinrong. Huang at first could not make up his mind, for he saw that the slogans and program the Communists put forward had many supporters, and feared the Nationalists might not be able to deal with them; he wanted Du to wait and see who would win before deciding. Du held that the chance must not be lost, and that only by joining the anti-Communist action would there be political standing to be had later. It is said that Huang was won over in the end when Du argued that a Communist victory would do them no good at all, but would only mean perishing together with the Nationalists; that being so, better to throw in wholly with the Nationalists against the Communists and make the Nationalists win, for only then was there a future — and so Huang decided to take part.

A part of Du's creatures had long since worked their way into the General Labor Union and the pickets, and they had made in advance a good number of imitation armbands of the kind the union used; and Du had arranged beforehand with the concession side that the hoodlums and secret agents be allowed to gather within the concessions and act together with the counter-revolutionary troops to disarm the pickets. Once Chiang Kai-shek had everything in place, he left Shanghai for Nanjing on April 7.

Before four o'clock on the morning of April 12, Du, Huang Jinrong, and Zhang Xiaolin each mustered their followers: Du's creatures — Gao Xinbao, Rui Qingrong, and the rest — were led by Gu Jiatang; Huang's disciples by Tang Jiapeng and Gu Zhuxuan; and the agents of Yang Hu's special-services section by Peng Bowei. All were dressed as workers, wearing armbands with the character gong, "worker," and carrying weapons, and in separate bands they rushed from the concession's iron gates toward the pickets' posts in Nanshi and Zhabei and toward the tramway union at the Sanshan Guild Hall, the Huzhou Guild Hall, the Shenxin Cotton Mill, and other places.

These agents and hoodlums disguised as workers, while the pickets were still asleep, seized the guards' guns. In some places they tricked the pickets and searched out a quantity of arms; in others clashes broke out and the workers fought back in haste. As arranged beforehand, the 26th Army under Zhou Fengqi, its chief of staff Zhu Shaozhou commanding four regiments of the army's First Division under Wu Wenyuan and Second Division under Si Lie, surrounded the pickets separately and attacked them; at the same time, on the pretense of mediating the clash between the two sides, they ordered both to lay down their arms, and seized the chance to cheat the pickets out of all their weapons. In the clashes more than a hundred pickets were killed on the spot and several hundred wounded, and the chairman of the General Labor Union, Wang Shouhua, was murdered. The hoodlums plundered the pickets of everything and withdrew into the concessions by the way they had come. Du Yuesheng and the others had not left the concessions but waited inside the iron gates, and only when they saw all their men return safely did they leave the gates.

The next day (the 13th), the General Labor Union published the true story of what had happened as a "Special Urgent Notice" in Shen Bao; the Martial Law Command at once sent men to strike out the notice, and put in its place a Special Urgent Notice of its own, denying that any armed hoodlums had rushed out from the concession side, and saying that the affair was purely a dispute between local hoodlums and rabble and the pickets, and that to keep order the arms of both sides had merely been taken into temporary custody. Du Yuesheng and the others were most displeased at the Martial Law Command's calling them hoodlums and rabble, and Chen Qun hastily came forward to correct it, declaring that the affair was a brawl between two factions within the General Labor Union.

When the news got out, the Shanghai workers were furious beyond measure; every factory struck, and the workers gathered and held meetings in Nanshi, Zhabei, and elsewhere, demanding severe punishment of the murderers and the ringleaders and the immediate return of the seized arms; after the meetings they held a great demonstration. Gu Jiatang and the rest went out again in force the next day to help hunt down the leaders of the General Labor Union and the pickets. Gu himself watched from the door of the Renshan Girls' School at the Baoshan Road crossing in Zhabei and the headquarters of the Second Division, as more than a hundred of the demonstrating workers were shot dead by the fully armed soldiers of that division, their corpses strewn everywhere. The reactionaries then leveled a new slander against the workers, saying that the Zhili-Shandong Allied Army was hidden within the General Labor Union, that a great quantity of Zhili-Shandong Allied Army insignia and papers had been found, and that there was "proof of collusion with the enemy." Using this as a pretext, they hunted down and slaughtered the workers on a great scale.

This plot, too, was one of Du Yuesheng's proud achievements. For when the hoodlums, having plundered everything of the pickets', went back and checked it over, there was little of value, and the documents and the like they gave to Yang Hu. In sorting through these things, Du found that when the workers had seized the arms of the routed Zhili-Shandong soldiers they had also taken their insignia and papers, which had not been destroyed; and Du suggested using these things as a pretext to arrest and kill the workers — this was the origin of the so-called "proof of collusion with the enemy." In the two days of confusion the reactionaries said now one thing, now another, and many people still did not understand what it was all really about. On Bai Chongxi and Chiang Kai-shek's instructions, Chen Qun drew up a circular telegram, had it sent out over the signatures of the three men Du, Huang, and Zhang, and printed several thousand copies to scatter about Shanghai. On the 14th the telegram appeared in the newspapers of Shanghai, Nanjing, and some other places. In it they did their utmost to slander and vilify the Chinese Communist Party, and at the same time attacked the leaders of the General Labor Union and the pickets with all their might. But the text gave itself away, saying such things as: "We, your fellows, have risen in haste to gather our comrades, raising poles for banners and hewing wood for weapons, to wipe out these ferocious brutes and spare our descendants the harm; and we especially hope that the elders of the whole nation will, father charging son and elder brother urging younger, rise together and root them out. We, your fellows, holding to the principle that every common man shares the responsibility for the nation's rise or fall, come forth to struggle." It confirmed exactly the plot of Chiang Kai-shek to collude with hoodlums in the anti-Communist action. That evening Bai Chongxi went to Nanjing to report to Chiang Kai-shek, and before leaving he again called Du and the others in to encourage and commend them — so that Du's zeal grew all the greater.

The Shanghai workers were not cowed by this great massacre. Besides going on holding meetings and demonstrations, they printed and distributed many documents exposing the great plot, headed by Chiang Kai-shek, of betraying the revolution and slaughtering the Communists. Du in turn set his creatures to spying out Communists everywhere and passing black-lists to Yang Hu at every turn, so that the arrests of Chinese Communist personnel never ceased.

Even before Du's own circular telegram had gone out, he had approached a number of organizations connected with him, and Chen Qun too set on some organizations linked to the Nationalists, gathering sixty or seventy in all; and on the 15th they published in the newspapers a circular telegram addressed to Chiang Kai-shek and Bai Chongxi, extolling these few hoodlum thugs as "righteous men who had saved the nation," saying that on the morning of the 12th they had personally led "dare-to-die comrades" to seize the General Labor Union's guns and disband the pickets, to the joyful celebration of Chinese and foreigners throughout the city, and expressing the hope that organizations in every province and city of the whole country would follow Shanghai's example and fall into line with them — this by way of a trial of public opinion.

On the 16th, on Chiang Kai-shek's instructions, Chen Qun had Du and the others come forward openly to receive the press and give anti-Communist statements. Only after this whole series of soundings did Chiang Kai-shek and Bai Chongxi drop their disguise and openly put forward anti-Communist utterances.

Du often said that his life's career was founded on anti-Communism: it won him the favor of the imperialists, earned him the esteem of Chiang Kai-shek and his set, and gained him the support of the capitalists — three gains at a single stroke.

Many years later, at his sixtieth birthday, the Central Daily News extolled him in these words: "In the sixteenth year, when the national army had just secured the southeast, Shanghai was full of hidden brigands, men's hearts were for a time unsettled, and order was in disarray; the gentleman, holding the pacification of the region foremost, spoke out for justice together with Huang Jinrong and Zhang Xiaolin, made his proclamation to the nation, and quietly contrived what was fitting, so that within ten days the disaffected were brought to peace — herein lay a service to the Party and the state at its very beginning." His merit in acting this time as the anti-Communist vanguard was never forgotten.

After Chiang Kai-shek's betrayal of the revolution had succeeded, he showed particular good will toward the Shanghai Green Gang bosses; before long, on a visit to Shanghai, he gave a grand banquet for Du, Huang, and the others, praising them to their faces as men who "deeply understood the great principle" and were "heroes who knew the times."

When Chiang stepped down the first time and was staying at Yang Hu's club in Shanghai, Du entertained him lavishly, and it is said even gave him money. But of the matter of giving money to Chiang, Du never spoke to me himself, and when others asked him about it he would not answer directly, for he feared Chiang would be displeased to know of it.

For many years Chiang and he kept up a very special relationship. In a great crowd he never showed any excessive warmth toward Du, but when only the two of them or a few trusted intimates were together he was extremely close with him. One year before the war, when I went to Shanghai, I happened on his being invited by Chiang to Nanjing, where Chiang had asked him and Qian Xinzhi and two other men of the financial world to dine together, and at table was most warm toward him. They fell to comparing ages, and Du being the youngest, Chiang particularly encouraged him, saying his future was boundless; he came back delighted. By his own account, Chiang Kai-shek had told him that whenever he had a difficulty he might come to him at any time. Du was boundlessly grateful for this and often flaunted it to show his relations with Chiang; but unless he met with some especially grave matter bearing on his own vital interests, he would never lightly play this trump card. He was on deep terms with many of Chiang's subordinates, and ordinary matters could be settled without going through Chiang at all. In the past I heard him speak of only two matters that many people had thought hopeless, on which he had gone straight to Chiang and got them settled.

One was the great morphine case at Taiping Lane in Nanshi, Shanghai, before the war. This huge morphine factory was one Du had had Gu Jiatang take on. Later, when it was cracked open because of an unequal division of spoils with the military police and secret agents, the Military Police Command sent a company of military police to guard it. The upshot was that the whole company, from the company commander down to the private soldiers, made off with the morphine and all fled. Chiang Kai-shek was furious and insisted on a thorough investigation, throwing all Shanghai into an uproar, with quarrelling among the reactionaries too that would not cease. Du, knowing that if the investigation went on it must lead straight to him, had no choice but to hurry to Nanjing with a great sum of money; and, on the one hand buying over those around Chiang and on the other seeing Chiang himself, he begged that the matter be pursued no further — and so this case that had made such a stir came to a limp, tame end.

Another was the case of Xu Maochang, brother of Xu Maotang, his richest disciple. In the year the war broke out, Xu Maochang, colluding with bureaucratic capital in Shanghai, dealt in cotton yarn and stirred up a great storm on the market. Chiang Kai-shek ordered Dai Li to arrest Xu in Shanghai; and at this time Du's disciple Wang Zhaohuai was chief of the detective brigade at the Songhu Garrison Command, which Dai Li controlled, and on receiving the order he went secretly to inform Du. Xu Maotang pleaded with Du, and Du, for the sake of the money, at once flew to Lushan to see Chiang and begged that Xu and the others not be arrested. Chiang accepted his view and told Dai Li to consult with him in handling it. Afterward, because H. H. Kung's wife was involved, the matter could not be pursued even had they wished, and so it came to nothing.

The ten years from 1927 to 1937 were the most triumphant period of Du's life. Relying on the imperialists of the Shanghai concessions, he was willing to serve as their accomplice; he was also in league with the reactionary chiefs; and as both sides needed to make use of him as a tool, his position was raised. After Yang Hu became Songhu garrison commander for the second time, he did Du's every bidding all the more.

To control the main departments of the Garrison Command directly, Du recommended his favorite disciple Lu Jingshi for the post of head of its military-law department. This scab, who had never worn a uniform in his life, became at a stroke a full colonel in the army. Another of his disciples, Wang Zhaohuai, was chief of the command's detective brigade; and within the Garrison Command many people were more deferential to Du than to the commander himself.

In the concessions, his power had at first been limited to the French Concession, but now it extended to the British Concession as well. The Chinese chief inspector of the French Concession police, Jin Jiulin, did entirely as he arranged; and the hoodlum boss of the International Settlement, Ji Yunqing, and Ji's disciple Lu Liankui, chief inspector of the British Concession police station, held him in great respect too. Add to this that the chamber-of-commerce chairman Wang Xiaolai was a man he had backed into office, and that he himself held not a few posts as chairman of the board, managing director, and general manager of this and that — and so in commercial and financial circles too he had great power.

In his heyday he was lavish with money, throwing it about by the ten thousand without a trace of grudging. Yet had he relied only on the proper income from the enterprises, banks, and posts he ran, he could never possibly have done so. Where did all his money come from? Here I must say a word about his ways of making money. Though he did not deal in opium directly himself, his great income came from giving protection to the trafficking of opium and other narcotics. At the time every kind of narcotic sold in Shanghai had to have his protection, and this "insurance fee" was a startling sum. Each year several thousand piculs of drugs were shipped to Shanghai from all over to be sold and processed, and from this he could take in more than a million in "insurance fees." Not only could he guarantee that the goods would come to no trouble; even the men who ran this trade were under his protection. Gu Jiatang once told me that when Dai Ji was garrison commander in Shanghai, his agents once seized the morphine king Chen Kunyuan on an upper floor of the Sun Sun Company building in the British Concession, meaning to spirit Chen back to the command without going through the British Concession police; a prostitute who knew Chen saw this and at once telephoned to report it to Du, and Du immediately had Gu Jiatang take eight armed toughs, burst into the room, and wrest Chen by force from the agents. In escorting narcotics they always carried pistols too, ready at any moment to resist the inspectors — deaths and bloodshed they did not shrink from.

Another of his large sources of income was the protection of gambling: he had taken charge of many of Shanghai's big gambling dens. I went to only one of them, the largest, at No. 181 Tongfu Road, where Gu Jiatang had posted a dozen or so men — Gu Miaogen and the rest — with pistols, to guard it. It was a fairly large garden villa, its iron gate kept shut, and only cars that were known could drive straight in; strangers were even frisked, for fear of trouble from someone bringing in a grenade or the like. Small hoodlums who came to make trouble they beat, in the milder case, and killed, in the graver.

Once inside the den, you changed your money for chips at the counter, from one yuan to a hundred; the main game was roulette, and there were also odd-and-even, big-and-small, huimen tan, mahjong, playing cards, and so on — Chinese and Western gaming gear of every kind. I played roulette and big-and-small there, and lost more often than not. The stakes were quite large: a single win or loss ran from several hundred to ten thousand yuan. Besides living off its winnings, the house also took a cut by the "one in ten" method, so that a thousand yuan, once it had passed out and in ten times, was as good as gone.

When some big gambler from out of town had lost all his money, the house would, as a matter of course, buy him a train or boat ticket home, so that he might go back and fetch more money to gamble, and at the same time to keep him from doing away with himself in the den or playing the welsher. Those who won a great deal the house would also send men to escort home — but this happened very rarely.

The dens were fully equipped, providing free of charge top-grade opium, Chinese and Western meals, hot and cold drinks and the like, so that at rest one could eat and drink one's fill — all to draw the gamblers in. From this one den Du Yuesheng could take thirty or forty thousand yuan a month, and Gu Jiatang five thousand a month.

Du himself did not come to such places to gamble; he always gambled at home. When a guest won, he had to tip the people of Du's household, and the sum was considerable too; some, wishing to curry favor with Du, gave more even than the house's rake. His usual small game was "wahua"; those who played with him were Liu Hangchen, Kang Xinzhi, Gu Jiatang, myself, and others, and small game though it was called, each win or loss still ran to over a thousand silver dollars. The big game was paijiu, at several tens of thousands to several hundred thousand silver dollars a round. After victory in the war, because the fabi had depreciated, they reckoned in American dollars, settling accounts at the close by converting into fabi at that day's black-market rate for the dollar. At this big game he won nine times out of ten, and won a great deal; he himself worked no tricks, but relied on others in league with him to cheat. Men like Zhu Rushan, son of a great salt merchant of former days, and the lawyer Xu Shihao had a very fine set of skills. I once went partners with him at the big game: because his health was poor, he would gamble only until around ten at night and then go to rest, leaving me to keep watch through the night, and often we did not break up until dawn. While he was at the table he always won, and the cheats like Zhu Rushan lost money just like everyone else, so that it was not easy to draw others' notice. The one who lost most in those days was Wu Qiding, chairman of the Ningbo Commercial Bank, who lost more than ninety thousand American dollars at Du's house all told (the rumor outside at the time was that Wu had lost more than nine hundred thousand, but as I recall it was more than ninety thousand). There were also Liu Hangchen, Kang Xinzhi, and Kang Xinru of the Sichuan faction, and Du's little fellow-townsman Lu Genquan, boss of the Lu Genji Construction Company, and others, who lost no small amounts too. Of the money won, Du and I divided it three parts to seven, and I could sometimes take several thousand or more than ten thousand American dollars in a single night.

Du's skill at cheating with cards was not, in fact, first-rate. Once Dai Li gave a performance together with us at my house, and even so Dai Li's methods were cleverer than either of ours: whatever dice he took up, he could throw at will the very points he wanted. Du and I both praised this "consummate trick" of his.

In Shanghai, Du not only gave protection to the gambling dens run by Chinese but even took a cut of the "big swindle shows" the foreigners ran at the time. Before liberation, the horse racing, dog racing, and jai alai of Shanghai cheated the Chinese out of who knows how much money; of the many women I knew, no few loved to bet on the jai alai, and many were brought to utter ruin, some even to suicide.

These public gambling establishments at the time were under the protection of the concession authorities, and though they did not send him hush money as the Chinese-run dens did, they too had to get on terms with him to be left in peace. In the past I loved to buy race tickets, and each year, when the spring and autumn "Champions" sweepstakes were drawn, I would also ask Du to preside. In those days the top prize had once been as high as two hundred and fifty thousand yuan, but never once did a Chinese win it — the winners were always foreigners not in Shanghai. At first I could not make out the reason, until Du told me later that these foreign top-prize winners were all fakes: if such a huge prize were really paid out, what would those foreigners have left to live on? So he never gambled on these things himself, but only took from these few big foreign gambling houses a not inconsiderable sum in "carriage money."

The number of concurrent posts he held was truly astonishing. He himself founded not many firms: the Zhonghui Bank, for instance, was nominally run by him, but in fact his disciple Xu Maotang and others held more of the shares than he did; and the Tongshang Bank he snatched from others' hands. This Tongshang Bank, it is said, had been founded in the Guangxu reign, an old bank that had once issued banknotes. His way of snatching it was vicious: he first sent men to deposit money in the bank, and once the bank had lent this money out, he sent men on a single day to withdraw cash and change banknotes, while the bureaucratic-capital banks would not help it with its short-term funds. In this way the bank was caught off guard and hard put to pay out; and as soon as word got about, depositors rushed to withdraw and redeem in a run, and with the bank on the verge of collapse its managers had no choice but to ask him to help keep it going. At first he deliberately made excuses and put them off, but once he had agreed to become chairman of its board, a word from him brought the withdrawals and the run to a quick calm. Later Qian Xinzhi and others put more government shares into the bank, and so the Tongshang Bank was snatched by him. The course of it at the time was very complicated; I remember only this bare outline.

Besides these he held the posts of managing director of the Bank of China and the Bank of Communications, chairman of the board of the Pudong, Guoxin, Yadong, and other banks, and concurrent posts in some other banks as well.

In the transport world, he was chairman of the National Steamship Trade Association and concurrently of the Shanghai Steamship Trade Association, chairman of the board of the Dada Steamship Company, and a director of the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company and of the Minsheng Company.

He understood that to make a name he must also rely on the newspapers to trumpet him, and so he found ways to worm his way into the press as well. He was chairman of the board of Shanghai's Shen Bao, a director of the Xinwen Bao, and a managing director of the Central Daily News. Many of the small papers his disciples ran — such as Lu Jingshi's Shanghai Zheng Bao — were connected with him too.

Other important posts he held concurrently included: president of the Shanghai Local Association, vice-president of the Red Cross Society of China, standing supervisor of the Shanghai Chamber of Commerce, standing director of the Pudong Native-place Association, chairman of the board of the Chinese Merchants' Electric Company, chairman of the board of the China Textile Company, chairman of the Stock Exchange, chairman of the board of the World Book Company, and presiding director of the Dadong Book Company. Posts of this kind that Du held came to about a hundred, so many that at times even he could not keep them all straight. From these posts of his, the "carriage money" alone came to a considerable sum each month, and with the dividends on his sinecure shares added in, the amount was greater still. These many concurrent posts of his were for the most part gained by the use of hoodlum methods and by leaning on the collusion of the imperialists and the reactionaries; some people used his "name-plate" to deal with unexpected trouble, and at times he too used these posts to achieve his own private ends. When the war began, for instance, Chiang Kai-shek wanted to sink some steamers in the Yangtze as a blockade line, and many private steamship companies were unwilling to give up their ships; Du Yuesheng, concurrently chairman of the Dada Steamship Company, was the first to answer the call, giving up several ships to be sunk at Jiangyin, so that the other companies too had no choice but to make the painful sacrifice, and the Yangtze blockade line was completed in this way — and Du thereby came off with the name of a "patriot."

In 1932 he founded the Heng Society. This, under the name of a social organization, was in fact a gang combine, its members all his disciples. Besides the so-called morality and good faith of the ordinary gang, the aims it professed made a special point of "serving society and being loyal to the country," the better to deceive people and please the reactionary rulers. He often boasted that he had "eight thousand disciples, sharing weal and woe." This was pure bragging: in fact there were not two thousand. Even counting in the disciples of his disciples — the Qing Society of his disciple Lu Jingshi, the Chang Society of Zhang Kechang, and the like — the total came to fewer than eight thousand. What these men were to him he knew very well himself: they merely borrowed his power for a shield, and apart from the crowd who had knocked about with him in the early days and depended on him for their living and were often at his side, most of the commercial men merely made mutual use of one another. Among his disciples there were very few military officers, for he understood Chiang Kai-shek's temper well: this dictator of hoodlum origins most abhorred having his own students and subordinates go and take another as their "old man." Such general-grade officers as were in the Heng Society, like Wang Zhaohuai, had entered his gate only on Dai Li's instructions. Though he had dealings with many reactionary officers as a rule, these were mostly friendships, and he did not draw them in to be his own disciples.

At that time he had to pay out a very large sum of money each month to keep a set of disaffected officials and politicians. By the end of each month, before the takings from every quarter had even been transferred into his account, a part was already being sent out. I once saw a list of those who received his money, and no few of them were men who had once had some name — Zhang Shizhao among them.

### III. The Completion of the Ancestral Shrine and the Sixtieth Birthday

Since coming to know Du, I took part in the two most spectacular occasions of his life. One was in June 1931, when I attended the dedication ceremony of the newly built Du family ancestral shrine at Gaoqiao in Pudong. The other was on August 30, 1947, his sixtieth birthday. The first was when his fortunes were at their height; the second, splendid as it too was, came when he was already on the downward road.

In May 1931 I received his invitation, but did not reach Shanghai until early June, so that I missed the work of his great preparatory committee and went only to see the spectacle.
