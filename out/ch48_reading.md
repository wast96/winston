## Chapter 48

"Thank you, ladies and gentlemen; tonight the peoples of Greater East Asia have had the loveliest of gatherings. It is now five o'clock, and after three more dances we shall declare the party over. All night long we have worn our masks; we now ask you all to take your masks off. There are three dances left, and we mean to enjoy them to the full with our truest smiles. Well then, please, everyone, take off your masks."

At five o'clock, just as Miko and I finished a dance, someone clapped his hands and began to make this announcement. At that a roar went up, everyone applauded, and then everyone threw off their masks. Just then I was in a most anxious state of mind to see Asamura Toshimiko's true face, but I had no way to find her.

At last I saw Mei Yingzi, and when the music began I danced the first with her, and I said:

"Have you seen the blue-tailed snake?"

"Isn't she just in front of Bai Ping?"

"And Bai Ping?"

"Over there."

Sure enough, I saw Bai Ping, and the one dancing with her was Dr. Philip. Much surprised, I searched carefully up ahead. I could not see people's skirts, so I danced over there with Mei Yingzi, and now I saw the one Bai Ping was following close behind, and at once I saw on her skirt the blue ink stain, and I was anxious to look closely at her face. I pressed through—ah, sure enough a gentle face, the corners of the mouth seeming to hold a look of pity throughout, the chin full of an engaging mildness; but suddenly my eyes met hers, and it dawned on me that I had seen her somewhere. I searched in my mind, but could not for the life of me place it.

At the second piece I danced with this girl, and I asked:

"Miss, have we met somewhere?"

"Have we met somewhere?" she said, laying stress on the words, but in unpracticed Mandarin.

After this I could find nothing to say. When the dance was over I saw Bai Ping, with Honsa Jiro beside her; I knew he had just finished dancing with Bai Ping, and I went over and asked Honsa:

"Who is that lovely woman? I seem to find her a little familiar."

"What a bad memory you have." Honsa laughed. "You have forgotten even someone who dined at the same table."

This gave me no small start, but I still put on a humorous manner and said:

"Ah, it's Miss Miyama Yoshiko—she has changed her gown, and I did not know her at all!"

Miyama Yoshiko! It was simply not to be believed—how could she speak such good Mandarin, and now call herself Asamura Toshimiko? Could such a shy girl, like a young lady of the old classical breeding, be the very woman who had done that fearful business in the room, and also this Asamura Toshimiko with so gentle a face and so pitying a mouth?

But there was no room for doubt: the blue ink stain was plainly on her skirt, and the voice, speaking fluent Mandarin and telling me she was Asamura Toshimiko, was plainly still at my ear. Can the human world truly be so fearful and so unfathomable? My whole soul shuddered at it. Through the third piece of music my thoughts did not leave this tangle. I was in a daze, as if beside myself, right up to the end of the piece.

I saw Umetake declare the party over; people came and went, crossing one another in a moment's uproar. I did not see Bai Ping, nor Miko; I saw only Mei Yingzi at Umetake's side, but I had no way to speak with her, and it seemed there was no need to. On the other hand, Honsa and the rest were looking for me to say goodbye, and I found Miyama Yoshiko among them; they had come together and so were leaving together. Sophie was now beside me too, and I of course had to leave with her; she was toying with the silver mask in her hand as she and I took our leave of Honsa Jiro and the others. When they had crowded off to talk elsewhere, it occurred to me that I ought to see Sophie home early and go early to keep my appointment at Bai Ping's, and I asked Sophie:

"You wore a silver mask too?"

"Yes."

"I never did find you—do you remember whether I danced with you?"

"I think we did dance."

"Where were you sitting?"

"Over there," she said, taking me across. "Don't you remember this gold-trimmed handbag is mine?"

Just as she was fetching her handbag it dawned on me that her seat had been at Miyama Yoshiko's left hand—so the person I had mistaken the first time I was looking for the blue-snake lady had been her. My heart gave a jolt: in all that time I had never once sought out Sophie, or I should surely have found this so-called blue-snake lady earlier than Bai Ping did.

We fetched our coats and hats and greeted the host and our acquaintances along with the many people dispersing outside; my mind dwelt all the while on this strange crossing of chances. I thought: had I known beforehand that Miyama Yoshiko was sitting next to Sophie, then when I discovered the blue ink stain on her, would not my talk with her while we danced have gone much more easily? I did not know whether Sophie knew that Miyama Yoshiko was beside her; and as car followed car, driving toward Hongkou along the broad asphalt of the city center, I asked:

"Sophie, did you know that the one sitting on your right was Miyama Yoshiko?"

"Of course," she said with a smile. "It was she who called me to sit over there."

"You mean you were not sitting there at first, but moved over later," I said.

"No," she said. "She saw me walk over looking for a seat, and called me to sit beside her."

"And then she told you she was Miyama Yoshiko?"

"Yes," she said. "We both remembered dining together."

"Doesn't she speak no Mandarin?"

"Well enough," she said. "Because she doesn't speak it well, I suppose, she won't speak it in front of many people."

"What did she talk to you about?"

"Odds and ends—and she spoke of you, too."

"Spoke of me?"

"She asked where you worked."

"What did you say?"

"I said I didn't know."

"Very good."

"What is it?" Sophie asked.

"Nothing," I said. "Japanese women are the most snobbish; they always love to ask about a person's occupation and income."

Not wanting to talk much with Sophie, I hastily turned it off with other words, saying:

"Are you sleepy?"

"A little."

"Rest a while, then."

She did not answer.

"Would you like a cigarette?" I said. "In my overcoat pocket."

She reached into my overcoat pocket for a cigarette, and I watched her smoke it. The car had reached Hongkou; up ahead the many cars were scattering off in all directions, and the streets were very desolate. The night was on the point of waking; a night-soil cart or two crept slowly along the road. In the thin mist, where the headlamps shone, one could see it steaming and stirring.

Without a glance elsewhere I saw Sophie to the mouth of her lane. When Sophie had got out, I drove straight to Bai Ping's.

Ah Mei answered the door with sleep-heavy eyes, and asked:

"And them?"

"They aren't back yet?"

"No."

"They will be soon, no doubt." I came in and said: "You go to sleep first; I'll answer the door for you."

So saying, I went into the room I had once lived in, and sat smoking on the sofa waiting for Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi to come back; but three cigarettes had turned to ash and still they had not come. I took up a book at random to read, and after I know not how long the words on the page slowly blurred, and I dozed off on the sofa.

It seemed I still faintly heard music; I was aware of others dancing, and my body was very uncomfortable, curled up and unable to settle. I found myself hidden under the round table; it seemed moonlight was streaming in at the window, and I suddenly saw a blue snake glide past the edge of the table. It's Miyama Yoshiko, I thought—ah, this must be a ridiculous dream. But the snake glided quietly past and suddenly thrust its head under the table and said:

"I know you are hiding there; I saw it all."

I gave a start, but suddenly found the voice very familiar; it seemed there was no snake at all, but a smile, like a lily just opening, and the person was there in the room, and in the moonlight she said:

"Come out; I saw it all."

I crawled out from under the table, and looking, it was indeed Bai Ping; as if my mind had been set at ease, I said:

"So it really is you."

"And if it is me?"

"If it is you," I said with a laugh, "and I had a gun, I'd fire."

"I have one, I have one." Laughing, Bai Ping handed me a gun; I took it and, as if in jest, fired a shot at the ceiling.

"Bang!"

But Bai Ping did indeed fall at the sound, and for a moment I was aghast; I went over and pulled at her arm, crying:

"Bai Ping, Bai Ping!"

But just then the door suddenly opened, and in came Mei Yingzi in a white evening gown, laughing, showing her almond-tinted front teeth, and she said:

"Well acted, well acted!"

"Well acted, well acted!"

The one standing before me was indeed Mei Yingzi; I woke from my sleep and found that I had already slid to the floor, and Mei Yingzi was standing at the door. My heart was still pounding, and I hurriedly got up from the floor and said:

"When did you come?"

"Just now." She came in laughing. "You really are something—shutting the door as loudly as that and you didn't wake, and still saying you'd keep the door for Ah Mei."

"Was it you who said, 'Well acted, well acted'?" Not weighing her words closely, I sat down on the sofa with my hands over my face.

"I heard you calling out 'Bai Ping' straight through your sleep-talking."

"Did Ah Mei open the door for you?"

"Of course—did you think I'd fly in?"

"I rather thought you'd have slipped in like a snake," I said with a laugh. "And Bai Ping?"

"Now you turn and ask me," she said. It flashed on me that Mei Yingzi's shutting the door might have been the gunshot in my dream, and I asked:

"Did you shut the outer door very hard?"

"Yes." Mei Yingzi sat down facing me and said mockingly: "But you still didn't wake, did you?"

"I heard it," I said. "That was the gunshot in my dream."

"You were dreaming?"

"How is it Bai Ping is not back yet?"

"You seem very anxious about her."

"It was the very sound of your shutting the door—I dreamed Bai Ping fell at the sound," I said. I had a strange presentiment, feeling there was something ill-omened in Bai Ping's not coming back. I said: "Don't you think that, her not being back yet, she may have met with something?"

"Strange," she said.

"You find it strange too?"

"What I find strange is that our philosopher should be so superstitious." Mei Yingzi was smiling all the while, but my heart grew uneasy. I rose and went to the window. I drew back the heavy curtain: the sky had already grown bright, and I opened the window to look out on the winter-morning street. There were scattered passers-by, but no cars; I hoped Bai Ping's car would come flying up just then, but it did not.

Ah Mei brought in the tea and refreshments, and only then did I shut the window and go back to my seat. Mei Yingzi was pouring tea for me, but my thoughts were elsewhere, and I sat there blankly. Suddenly Mei Yingzi lit a cigarette, waved the match before my face, and said:

"Set your mind at rest; Bai Ping will be back presently."

"Then you know where she has gone?"

"I think you ought to know beforehand."

"She never told me."

"Does she need to tell you?" said Mei Yingzi. "At this juncture, whoever first learns where Miyama Yoshiko lives has scored a kind of merit."

"But isn't that a very easy thing to learn?"

"How would you go about learning it?"

"Ah, I have not yet told you—last night I dined at the same table as Miyama Yoshiko at Honsa Jiro's, and after dinner, because I had to see Sophie home, I did not come with them; and Miyama Yoshiko came together with Honsa Jiro and the rest. A single question tomorrow and it's done, isn't it?"

Mei Yingzi suddenly knit her brow, as if pondering, and said:

"Why did you not say so at the ball?"

"By the time I found she was Miyama Yoshiko the party was nearly over."

"This really is..."

"And by then I could no longer find any of you," I added. "Did you not see Miyama Yoshiko leave in the same car as Honsa Jiro and the rest?"

Mei Yingzi seemed very grave just then; she leaned back on the sofa and smoked, taking no notice of my words, and after a long moment suddenly looked at me and said flatly:

"No—I think Honsa Jiro is not likely to know her exact address."

Mei Yingzi's words might be right, might be wrong, but I did not argue with her, and said:

"Even granting Bai Ping went to find out Miyama Yoshiko's address, she ought to be back by so late an hour; and this is nothing so important that it must be learned this very night."

Mei Yingzi still sat there gravely, as if lost in thought, taking no notice of my words, and after a long silence she said, as though out of patience:

"I am much surprised you still do not understand Bai Ping's character."

"Truly," a burst of laughter came suddenly; she said, "how is it that after all this while you still do not understand my character?"

One glance and I saw the white figure, and gave a start: Bai Ping was already standing at the door. Mei Yingzi's position was level with the door, and so she had not seen Bai Ping, and she seemed not at all startled by this dialogue coming so suddenly. Welcoming Bai Ping, and at the same time as if reporting to Mei Yingzi, I rose to my feet and said:

"Bai Ping is here."

Bai Ping stood at the door without moving, the smile of a lily just opening on her face, and I marveled that Bai Ping's air should be so radiant.

Mei Yingzi suddenly rose, kindling in an instant from her gloomy manner into excitement, and looking at Bai Ping she said:

"I was just trying to guess the result of your work from the bearing with which you came in; and now I dare come, quite certain, to congratulate you on your triumph."

Bai Ping came in laughing, like a white sea-bird alighting on the rocks of an island, and in her pure-white dancing dress sank down onto the sofa. She said:

"Guess what time I came back?"

"You have been listening to our talk a good while."

"I am much surprised," said Bai Ping. "You knew Honsa Jiro was acquainted with Miyama Yoshiko—why did you not say so earlier?"

"I only learned it at the dinner table, and besides, how could I have imagined that someone like a young lady of a great house would be..."

"Tell your result first, Bai Ping," said Mei Yingzi.

"What you guessed was quite right," said Bai Ping. "The address Honsa Jiro knows is not Miyama Yoshiko's address."

"You found it all out?" I asked excitedly.

"Honsa Jiro saw her to Yuyuan Road," said Bai Ping. "In fact she lives on Youheng Road."

"Where is Youheng Road?" Mei Yingzi asked.

"Just a few streets on from North Sichuan Road."

"I went all the way there to look," said Bai Ping. "It is a very ordinary house."

"You met her?" I asked.

"No," said Bai Ping. "I only stood outside the house alone and watched."

"Is there a map of Shanghai?" Mei Yingzi suddenly asked.

Bai Ping rose and went to the writing desk, and took a map from the drawer; Mei Yingzi went to the desk too just then and turned on the desk lamp, and Bai Ping spread out the map and showed her where Youheng Road was, and showed us where Miyama Yoshiko's house was: outside a place called Juxian Village, the balcony of the house just beside the mouth of the lane, and the road right in front.

Then they discussed how to spy out the truth about Miyama Yoshiko; at all costs several questions must be answered by tomorrow:

First, who lived together with Miyama Yoshiko—how many people lived in that house?

Second, was Miyama Yoshiko often at home, and were there often guests there?

Third, when had she come to Shanghai, and what was her chief task?

Fourth, what was her history—what had she done before coming to Shanghai?

Fifth, how were we to watch her movements closely from now on?

Sixth, how could we get close to her, so that she would be willing to tell us her address and let us become regular callers at her house?

In short, our conclusion was that our aim lay not in the documents alone, but in Miyama Yoshiko; for this failure to steal the documents was one thing, while the mystery of Miyama Yoshiko was a lasting threat to our work hereafter.

For me, though, it became a problem. I had originally decided to go to Beiping after this piece of work, and had made an appointment with Helen; but now this work had ended by having no result for a result, and the matter it dragged in was work still longer and more distant. There was an indescribable feeling and sorrow in my heart; but when Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi were gravely and earnestly discussing the work, I had, just then, no way to bring up my own private concerns.

We parted at seven o'clock, agreeing to meet again at ten that night to see what everyone had gained.

I went back to my lodgings and lay down at once, but I could not sleep, for my own private affairs. I felt that while this work had reached no stopping-point, there was truly no way to propose taking Helen to Beiping. And this work now dragged Miyama Yoshiko in; if the work on the documents was a total failure, without hope, could I then get free? No! Though it was a stopping-point, I still could not get free—the reason was a subtle one, chiefly my own state of mind. If this failure ended in my being wanted by the authorities, I might get free; otherwise it must be victory, with merit standing to my name; failing that, this failure would end in my being caught and killed, and then my getting free would not be going with Helen to Beiping, but going with Stephen to the grave. At the thought of Stephen, his stiff body, his lightless eyes, his purple lips floated before my eyes; for this lively, guileless friend, in my life of late, whenever I was tired or lonely, I always thought—not necessarily of his look at the moment of death, but of any scene from when I first knew him and roamed about with him. In my impression he was always a strong, lively, cheerful, guileless man; however I might try to reason out the mystery of the work he had taken on, I never felt that he had any other dreadful cunning or crookedness or dark quality of character. Every time I thought of him I felt a grief and a shudder, and then an anger. And it was just this anger, then, that made me think of the wretched fate of the thousands upon thousands of our people, and I felt I ought to hold on, at least until our work should become clear. I was no expert spy, but between Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi, under this consciousness of mutual suspicion and rivalry for credit, my existence was not without meaning.

In this settled state of mind I was without hesitation or worry, and grew at last very firm; and to carry on the night's work, I went, with a fixed purpose, to find Honsa Jiro.
