## 8

Qin Shuji appeared before Yanzhi all at once, on a morning when the thin mist had just burned off. Yanzhi was bent over in the cabin steaming her face, a habit that never failed, for a moment, to make the past feel like a dream. Just then Old Mo called from beyond the door for the Boss, saying the brothers were all back and last night's haul had been good. Yanzhi did not stir; no one was allowed to break in on her morning face-steaming. Old Mo's voice turned a little hesitant. "We've brought someone back," he said.

It was a good while before Yanzhi lifted her head from the basin. She wiped the water slowly from her face and began, at the mirror, to dress her hair. Everything was long since routine, and not a flicker of expression showed on her face. But when she slid the cabin door open, that face flushed a sudden deep red. She stared at Qin Shuji standing at the prow as though the whole world had come crashing down in an instant.

Qin Shuji's hands were bound behind him, and beads of water from the morning dew still clung to his hair.

Old Mo said, "Boss, this fellow swore he'd see you if it killed him."

Yanzhi made no sound. She closed her eyes gently and reached out a slow hand to steady herself against the door-frame.

Qin Shuji said, "I've searched this marsh for you three days and three nights."

Three days and three nights? Yanzhi tipped her face up, let out a long breath, and turned back into the cabin. Her voice came out only after a long while, so weak and so hoarse. "Untie him," Yanzhi said. "Ask him in."

The cabin was forbidden ground to the men; except for her daughter, no one had ever set foot in Yanzhi's cabin. Qin Shuji rubbed his wrists and, ducking his head as though coming home, slipped inside and sat down in a rattan chair. Qin Shuji had come to win Yanzhi over. Back in Shanghai he had been a liaison for the underground Party, charged with passing intelligence and turning men to the cause. Betrayed by an informer, his flight had begun the morning he left the flat on Jing'an Temple Road. From Shiliupu he took a boat to Suzhou, and from Suzhou he walked the whole way to southern Anhui. Now Qin Shuji let out a long breath, as though he had crossed mountains and rivers without number once more, looked at Yanzhi, and was a long time in saying, "I've seen you at last."

"You didn't come only to see me." Yanzhi gave a faint smile and, not waiting for him to speak, went on: "You know? The Japanese have come to me. The Central Army sent men too, and they brought gold bars, hard silver dollars, a commission, the whole lot."

Qin Shuji started. "But you didn't go with them," he said.

"And I won't go with you either." As she said it, Yanzhi raised her face, yet lowered her eyes.

The next day Yanzhi shut herself in the cabin the whole day long, seeing no one and saying nothing. Not until evening did she suddenly bid Old Mo lay out wine: she meant to give Qin Shuji a meal. At the table Yanzhi brought out thirty silver dollars and pushed them, with meaning, across in front of him. Qin Shuji asked what she meant by it. Yanzhi seemed not to hear; she went on to take out a jewelry box, opened it, and said, "Take these back too. They're for your wife."

"I'm not even married. Where would a wife come from?" Qin Shuji laughed all at once. He told Yanzhi that the Mrs. Qin of those days had been a sham, a thing the revolution required: the two of them were a false married couple. "She and I fought side by side," Qin Shuji said. "We were comrades in the cause."

Yanzhi looked at him, listened quietly to the end, and asked, "Then what is there that's true?"

Qin Shuji said, "You wouldn't believe me if I told you."

"Then say nothing at all. Go."

"But if I don't, I'm afraid I'll never have the chance to tell you as long as I live." Qin Shuji thought a moment, then said, "With you, I am true. I can't cast you off again."

It was a long time before Yanzhi felt a single tear turning in her eye. She waited without moving, waited for the tear to seep slowly past the lid and slip gently down her cheek, and only then let out a long breath. Yanzhi agreed to rise with them three days on. After daybreak she rowed a small boat to see Qin Shuji out of the Xiangfu marsh. Their boat drifted and rocked on the water with the wind and would not hold still, like a parting of the living from the dead. Yanzhi buried herself deep against Qin Shuji's arm. "Why won't the boat sink?" she said. "Let us just die like this."

Qin Shuji said, "We have to live. We still have tomorrow."

Yanzhi said, "I don't want tomorrow. I want now."

Qin Shuji said, "The life of the two of us is only just beginning."

Yanzhi said, "I really ought to shut you up in my cabin and pillow my head on your arm every day."

"I have to report to the leadership. Three days from now, no one will be able to part us again." As Qin Shuji spoke he raised himself and pointed ahead. "Remember: three days from now, I'll be waiting for you all at the Fenshui Pavilion."

Yanzhi said, "And if I don't come?"

Qin Shuji said, "I'll go on waiting."

Yanzhi said, "And if I never come at all?"

Qin Shuji said, "Then let me turn to a stone."

"I don't want a stone." As Yanzhi said it, she stopped his mouth with a kiss.

The small boat began to rock on the water again, so violently it seemed bent on grinding the endless glitter of the waves to pieces. When Yanzhi rowed it back to her own big boat, every one of the water bandits sat cross-legged on the deck; not one rose to meet her, and Old Mo's eyes were as sullen as a fishing-cormorant's. Ever since Qin Shuji had stepped into Yanzhi's cabin, all these days, Old Mo had watched Yanzhi with just such a look.

Yanzhi said, "Have you nothing better to do?"

Old Mo looked up at Yanzhi. "Boss," he said, "your hair's come undone."

Yanzhi's face darkened. "Are you telling me what I may do?" she said.

"I'm only afraid you're being taken in." Old Mo got to his feet. "Boss, we can't trust a pretty-boy like that."

"Bullshit!" Yanzhi said loudly. "The man's shown us a straight road. We can't go on licking blood off the knife's edge our whole lives."

"And what part of this isn't licking blood off the knife's edge?" Old Mo said. "We wouldn't do it for the National Army, so why do it for the guerrillas?"

Yanzhi said, "Because I'm the one who runs this house, that's why; and off this boat, you are nothing at all."

Old Mo glanced back at the others, then said to Yanzhi, "Boss, speaking honestly, could the New Fourth Army's guerrillas ever measure up to us? Do they have opium? Can they let the brothers spend a night at the Xingchun Lou?" And at the last Old Mo said, "Throw in with the New Fourth Army, and the brothers are nothing at all."

"So you settled it among you long ago." Yanzhi nodded and drew her gaze back from those faces bit by bit, then swung round into the cabin. When she came out with her daughter in her arms she was like another person. A bundle hung from one hand, and on her back she carried the painting. She looked at no one and said nothing, like a young wife driven from the house, biting her lower lip, her eyes fixed only on the far distance ahead.

Old Mo had a small boat carry her to the bank, and Yanzhi's near six years as a water bandit ended the moment she stepped ashore. In the Fenshui Pavilion by the lake she waited from noon till dusk, her daughter asleep in her arms, and she held the girl without stirring, then sat on from dusk till daybreak. For six days running Yanzhi sat there with her daughter each day, growing unkempt and haggard, but Qin Shuji never came. It would never have crossed Yanzhi's mind that Qin Shuji was at that hour a hundred li away in the Tianmu Mountains. The Japanese sweep had begun the day after he rejoined his unit; the fighting ran from day into night and from night into day again. Qin Shuji broke out this way and that with the column, fighting through blood, until a grenade burst not far from him and drove splinters deep into half his body.

When Qin Shuji came to he was lying on a stretcher, borne through a stretch of wooded hills. He asked a soldier where this was. The soldier said it was the Tianmu Mountains; they had been marching two days already. Qin Shuji said, "I want to see the commissar."

The commissar was a man with a full beard, his gray cloth uniform caked with dust and blood. He took one of Qin Shuji's hands. "Don't talk," he said. "Rest and heal."

"I have to speak." Qin Shuji said, "At this very hour I ought to be at the Fenshui Pavilion, meeting them."

The commissar said, "The situation has changed."

Qin Shuji said, "But our promise to them cannot change."

The commander bowed his head and brooded a while; yet when he raised his face again his eyes were set hard as iron. "War is that cruel," the commissar said. "This is a debt to be laid at the Japanese devils' door."
