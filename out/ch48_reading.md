## Saishō-an

Oyuki went by palanquin.

Toshizō walked at its side as though guarding it, and before long they came to the slope that climbs from Shimotera-machi up to Yūhigaoka.

On either hand ran temple walls.

There was no one passing on the slope. City though this still was, the neighborhood lay on a plateau where many hundreds of temples, great and small, stood crowded together, and so it was hushed and still as a forest.

"What slope is this?"

"Aye, hereabouts they call it Kuchinawa-zaka, Snake Slope,"

{j} the palanquin-bearer answered.

"An odd name,"

"Not so odd, really. Climb to the top of the slope and look, and you'll see,"

And sure enough, when he had climbed to the top and looked down at the slope, it had the shape of a slender snake winding along.

"So that's why it's Snake Slope,"

{j} Toshizō was amused at the matter-of-fact way this place named its places.

At the top too it was temples, temples.

There is a famous nunnery called Gekkō-ji. When the palanquin came round to its rear gate, there was no temple there, but a dense, dark wood.

In the wood stood a crossbar gate, with a stylish eaved lantern hung out before it.

Saishō-an,

{j} a fine restaurant.

"Aye, here we are,"

{j} and the bearers set the palanquin down, and one of the pair went running up the path from the gate—to give word, no doubt, that guests had come.

"You've done well,"

{j} and Toshizō tipped them handsomely for their drink-money.

The street-palanquins of Naniwa were a convenient thing:

{j} —Somewhere a man might talk at his ease, is there such a house?

{j} say only that, and they would take the measure of the man and woman with them, guess even at the state of one's purse, carry you off to just the fitting place, and settle the terms with the house besides.

"Oyuki-dono, please,"

"Yes,"

{j} Oyuki began walking with her eyes cast down. Tree-roots crept across the little path.

At the Saishō-an they were shown to a room on the western side.

(A good room.)

Toshizō sat down.

With the war-uproar toward Fushimi, a house like this seemed to have no guests, and within it was quiet.

About the time the wine and food were brought, the westering sun fell on the paper of the lattice-screen.

With that setting sun there came, from the temples near and far, the sound of wooden fish-drums, and the voices chanting the sunset gatha at this hour reached faintly even into the room.

"It is quiet, is it not,"

{j} said Oyuki.

"Quiet,"

"I feel as though we were living deep in some far-off mountains,"

Oyuki rose, knelt beside the lattice-screen, and turning toward Toshizō,

{j} (May I open it?)

{j} she showed him with her eyes. The look on her face was very dear.

"By all means. It might be a little cold, though,"

"I want to see the garden,"

She slid it wide.

"Oh—"

Garden there was, one might say, none. A narrow plot of only moss and stepping-stones and a low brushwood fence, and beyond the fence it fell away into a sheer cliff.

Far below their eyes, the town of Naniwa spread out.

Beyond it, the sea.

The mountains of northern Settsu and of Hyōgo could be seen; the sun, this very moment, was about to go down, leaving red clouds behind it.

"A tremendous sunset,"

{j} and Toshizō too rose to his feet.

"Is that why they call this place Yūhigaoka, the Hill of the Evening Sun?"

Oyuki was well versed in the lore of poetry.

Seeing the place-name and the sunset together, she seemed to call it freshly to mind,

{j} "Yes, yes—this place,"

{j} she murmured. In the old court days there was a poet named Fujiwara no Ietaka, who won undying fame for helping compile the Shin-Kokinshū; in his last years he built a hermitage here on the Yūhigaoka of Naniwa and passed his days in the discipline called nissōkan, the sunset contemplation, gazing at the setting sun.

{p} By some bond of karma I have come to lodge in the village of Naniwa,

{p} and here have worshipped the sun as it sinks into the waves.

"That Yūhigaoka, then, is this,"

"Now that you say it, the mistress of the house was saying a while ago that there's a burial-mound somewhere about here,"

Toshizō put on the garden clogs and trod the moss. Oyuki came after him.

Going west out of the garden, there was a brushwood wicket. Opening it and passing through, an old camphor tree cast a deep shade beneath its boughs, and the grass beside it rose a little high.

There stood a five-ring stupa. Beside it was a stone tablet, and on it he could make out

{j} "Karyū-zuka,"

{j} —the tomb of Ietaka.

"I'm unlettered and know nothing—but who was this Ietaka?"

"A poet of the far past, who must have loved dearly the evening sun one sees from here. I know only that he did nothing but gaze at the sunset,"

"A man fond of splendid things, it seems,"

"The evening sun, splendid?"

Oyuki thought Toshizō a strange man.

For one thing, Lord Ietaka, seeing here the solemn grandeur of the sun going down into Osaka Bay, had come to believe that Amida's Original Vow was a real thing, and in his death-poem too he sang: "Making of the Naniwa sea a bank of clouds, I gaze—and the land of Amida is not so far off after all." Even from that poem, Ietaka, who loved the evening scene of this hill, can hardly have thought the setting sun a splendid thing.

"Splendid, is it?"

"It is,"

{j} said Toshizō.

"The most splendid thing in this world, I should say. And if it is not splendid, then it is a thing that ought to be made splendid,"

Toshizō seemed to be speaking of something else.

"Ah—"

{j} the cry Oyuki gave was when, making to come down from the mound, she slipped her footing. The day had already gone dark.

"Careful—"

{j} and Toshizō swiftly caught Oyuki up under the right arm and steadied her.

Naturally, by a very natural turn of things, Oyuki came to be leaning against Toshizō. Toshizō held her in his arms.

"These lips,"

{j} and Toshizō, setting a hand to Oyuki's chin, gently raised her face.

"I'm going to kiss them,"

(What a foolish man.)

{j} Oyuki thought.

Where in the world is the fool who announces it beforehand?

Toshizō found that Oyuki's lips were very sweet.

"What have you got in your mouth?"

"Why, nothing,"

"Then Oyuki-san's lips are sweet of themselves?"

{j} Toshizō asked it in dead earnest. It was too dark to see his face, but there was the voice of a boy in his tone.

Inwardly Oyuki was astonished. Hijikata Toshizō of the Shinsengumi—no one in all the land, surely, would know that at such a time he brought out such a tone as this.

"You are a great connoisseur of women, Hijikata-sama, are you not?"

"Once I thought so. But since I came to know you, Oyuki-dono, I feel that all I thought I knew of women until now was an illusion,"

"How gallant,"

"No—I mean no gallantry,"

{j} His voice had gone back to its surly self.

As the night deepened, the room grew cold enough to freeze.

At first the two of them drew their beds together. Then they doubled the quilts over, and it was only after they lay down in a single bed that they were at last at ease.

"I am ashamed,"

{j} —at first Oyuki shrank from it: from lying with her bare skin uncovered, that is.

"Oyuki-dono. I—"

{j} Toshizō said in all seriousness.

"I have held you in reverence. I am the youngest child, who grew up with no memory of my mother's face—raised, you might say, by my elder sister Onobu—and you had something of her look about you. That was the reason I was drawn to you; and yet at the same time it made you somehow hard to grow close to. But as we came to know each other more deeply, I saw that you, Oyuki-dono, are like no one else on this earth, the one and only woman there is for me. —I—"

Toshizō had grown more talkative than was his wont.

"I have been—how shall I say—rather a disagreeable fellow; no, how should I put it; yes, a man with something in him that never lets another hear his true heart, in any case whatever. I have known women in the past. But the abandon of a man and a woman together—that I do not know,"

". . . th-that—"

Oyuki was a woman of samurai upbringing, who had once been a samurai's wife. Her eyes went wide.

"Are you telling me to be the one?"

"I am,"

{j} Toshizō said, without a change in his tone.

"I'm thirty-four. Come to this age, and I still don't know the abandon of a man and a woman,"

"Yuki does not know it either,"

"That—"

{j} Toshizō was brought up short.

"That may be so.—But, Oyuki-dono, I am not saying such a thing. I do not think for a moment that the height of love between man and woman lies in wild and wanton play. Only, with you, Oyuki-dono, I want to forget everything and become simply a naked man and a naked woman,"

"I do not think I could,"

"We have two nights,"

"Yes—two whole nights,"

"Be it fifty years side by side, or only two nights, I would believe the depth of the bond no different. And within these two nights, surely—"

Toshizō checked his words. After a silence,

{j} "I seem to be saying embarrassing things. Let us leave it,"

{j} he said.

"No,"

{j} This time it was Oyuki who shook her head.

"Yuki will lose her senses from this very moment,"

"Lose your senses?"

"Yes,"

"Come now—"

{j} Toshizō gave a little snort of laughter.

Her samurai upbringing, it seemed, would out after all.

"You laughed,"

And yet Oyuki herself had let slip a stifled laugh.

"This is a fix,"

"A fix indeed. Steel myself and declare that I shall lose my senses from this moment—and still Yuki is Yuki, when all's said,"

Yet even as she said it, Oyuki was aware that, by the stifled laugh she had let slip in spite of herself, something somewhere in her heart had snapped cleanly open.

"Yuki thinks she can, after all. But only if you will put out the lamp,"

"It stays lit,"

"Why?"

"There'll be no wild and wanton play,"

"In the dark Oyuki would turn herself into another for you—and yet like this it comes to nothing,"

"It stays lit,"

"I won't have it,"

Before the words were done—or rather, while they were still enjoying this exchange—Oyuki had her sash untied, her arms slipped from the long under-robe, and her underskirt drawn away.

"I shall call you 'somoji,'"

{j} Toshizō whispered at Oyuki's ear.

Oyuki nodded.

Presently she parted her lips, faintly.

"Did you say something?"

"Danna-sama,"

{j} Oyuki whispered back.

"I only wanted to say it,"

"Say it once more,"

"You want to hear it?"

Oyuki laughed mischievously.

"I've gone through life a single man,"

{j} said Toshizō.

"But ever since I was a boy I've dreamed of being called by that word one day. You, Oyuki-dono, may have used it before, perhaps,"

"Is that a barb?"

Never had Toshizō been so fiercely jealous of Oyuki's dead husband as tonight.

"I mean it in earnest,"

"Danna-sama,"

"Who do you mean by that?"

"Who else is there but you, Toshizō-sama?"

"Inside this body,"

{j} and Toshizō touched her.

Oyuki hastily pressed the back of her hand to her lips.

A cry had been about to escape her.

"He's in there,"

". . ."

"Tonight I'll knead him away and wipe him out,"

Something like half a toki went by.

A wind seemed to have risen, and the rain-shutters began to rattle violently.

Cold.

But Oyuki did not notice it.

When another half-toki had passed, Oyuki at last

{j} "A wind seems to have got up,"

{j} —she said, as if fearful.

"It's been up this good while,"

{j} Toshizō turned on Oyuki a private, amused smile.

"Didn't Oyuki notice it?"

"No,"

Oyuki was pouting on purpose.

"I have known this good while. And what of it?"

"No—nothing at all,"

{j} Toshizō went back to his scowl.

The one night was soon over, and dawn came.

When Toshizō woke, Oyuki's bedding was already folded away and she was gone.

Toshizō hurriedly put away the bedding and went down to the well-side.

(A fine clear day again today.)

Presently Oyuki brought in a tray set out with the meal. Just like Oyuki, Toshizō thought.

She had borrowed the kitchen and made it herself, no doubt.

Oyuki slipped off her sleeve-cord and, with a natural grace, set her fingers to the floor in a bow.

"Good morning,"

"You've left something out,"

"What have I left out?"

"The word of address.—"

"Ah,"

{j} and Oyuki flushed red.

"Danna-sama,"

"Mm,"

(He's making fun of me.)

{j} —with such a look, Oyuki widened her smiling eyes.

(So this, perhaps, is what a husband of the world is like.)

With such a look on his face, Toshizō sat with his knees properly squared, not slackened.

(The shogunate, the Shinsengumi—for this one day I will forget them and live.)

"Please,"

{j} and Oyuki held out the tray.

Toshizō hastily took up the rice-bowl in his right hand.

"There are no chopsticks,"

"You have them in your left hand,"

"So I do,"

Toshizō changed them from hand to hand. A husband of the world, too, must now and then make such blunders.

"Oyuki,"

"Yes?"

"Let's eat together. I grew up among a crowd of brothers and sisters and nephews, so food tastes wrong to me if I don't eat it with someone,"

"With brothers and sisters, that would be so. But we two are husband and wife,"

"Ah—of course,"

At being a husband, Toshizō was not yet practiced.
