## 7

The training camp of the Jiangxi Youth-Cadre class stood at Liyuan village, on the outskirts of Ganzhou. Hills at its back and water before it, its ancient trees towering into the sky, it was like some haven out of the world, far from the war. Each day Jiang Yongnan drilled the trainees in firearms and hand-to-hand fighting on the parade ground before the little ancestral hall; sometimes he went next door to the nursery to serve as the children's doctor, or sat under the old banyan at the village entrance and gave free treatment to the country folk.

Hardest of all to bear, though, were the nights when the moonlight ran like water. Wind skimmed the surface of Xibei Lake and combed the treetops, giving off a howl like a wolf's. It was in that desolate sound that Jiang Yongnan took to drink. Often he would walk alone along the old city wall into town, to a bathhouse—also, as it happened, called the Huaqing Pool—and each time drink until he no longer knew where he would sober up, or when.

Ever since Chiang Ching-kuo brought his New Deal to Gannan, the brothels, opium dens, and gambling houses of Ganzhou had been swept clean away; even the taverns were forbidden to open at night.

"This place is like the Communists' Yan'an." Jiang Ruoshui said it once, over their cups, leaning close to Jiang Yongnan's ear.

He was the English interpreter at the airfield in the southern suburbs; back in Chongqing he had once visited Yan'an in the train of an American military advisory mission. It was then that Jiang Yongnan had come to know him, in passing. This delicate-featured southerner looked nothing at all like a soldier. He brought the airfield's pilots and mechanics here to soak and drink, stacked the foreign liquor, cigarettes, and tinned beef they smuggled in by plane in the cellar out back, then partitioned off half a changing room and built a sauna, kept for the American servicemen posted at the field. Jiang Ruoshui had not only turned the bathhouse into an underground air-force club; he had swiftly made himself a partner in the place.

One time, watching Jiang Yongnan sit cross-legged alone in a corner, cutting the local rice wine with American vodka and shaking it into a cocktail with the air of a melancholy apothecary, Jiang Ruoshui was suddenly put in mind of much of his own past, and could not help carrying his cup over to sit beside him. "What's her name?"

"It has no name." Jiang Yongnan shook his head and filled the other man's cup with the milky liquid. "I think of it as a glass of liquefied potassium cyanide."

"I mean the one you're carrying in here." Jiang Ruoshui jabbed theatrically at Jiang Yongnan's chest, his eyes sweeping the half-naked men around the room. "Look at them. Every last one is pining for home, for a woman at home. Who'd come here to drink himself blind every night otherwise?"

"I have no home, and still less a woman to pine for." Jiang Yongnan touched his glass to the other man's and drank it down in one.

Jiang Ruoshui tossed his own back, his face flushing crimson at once; he opened his mouth and blew out several hard breaths before he could speak. "That's a chemical reaction." Jiang Yongnan laughed and shook his head again. "It's the base spirit that's wrong. I'll never taste it the way it used to be."

"Then forget her." Jiang Ruoshui spoke with the air of a man who had been through it. "Find a new woman. Try a new flavor."

Jiang Ruoshui's own latest woman was an art teacher at the provincial middle school. When Nanchang fell she had fled south with her husband, a man who lived by his brush; outside Ganzhou the painter had lost his footing, tumbled into the Zhang River, and drowned. Jiang Ruoshui had gathered her into his arms for the price of two pairs of glass-silk stockings and a few tins of American food.

Jiang Yongnan first met Shen Jinzhu at the home of this woman called Shufen, at a dinner Jiang Ruoshui had arranged on purpose. The four of them sat around the square table trading toasts, nothing said outright and yet all of it understood. Lovers in the flush of passion are forever delighted to match up other couples, though it is really only to add a second pair of playmates to their own pleasure.

The second time, Jiang Ruoshui took the two women out of town for a spring walk. Picnicking in the woods beyond Liyuan village, gazing at the backs of the two of them where they sat by Xibei Lake, he said, from the heart, "A wartime wife is a wife too. They need a man. What they need still more is tinned Texas beef."

Shen Jinzhu was a small, unlucky woman. Not long after the wedding her husband marched off with his unit and never came back. Two years later, when the death notice reached her, her father—only just promoted to anti-smuggling commissioner—was being tried in public for graft and abuse of office. On the very night he was marched to the execution ground and shot, Japanese aircraft raided Ganzhou. Shen Jinzhu watched, helpless, as her mother and younger sister were pinned beneath a fallen beam and burned alive.

One night Jiang Yongnan woke to the sound of the woman's weeping to find Shen Jinzhu curled under the quilt with a hand clamped over her mouth, her cold tears already soaked through the sheet. Jiang Yongnan could find no words of comfort; he could only reach out and gather her in. The small woman wiped her eyes, tactful, and rolled up on top of him. Her desire was always so turbulent, and in its heat there was a note of yielding, too.

Much of the time, looking up at this woman riding astride him, Jiang Yongnan felt he had become her dead husband.

The day Shufen came hurrying to Liyuan village, Jiang Yongnan was in the middle of explaining the workings of the Thompson submachine gun to his trainees.

Jiang Ruoshui had been arrested. The garrison command's guards had burst into Shufen's house the night before and hauled him from his bed, and at the same time they had ransacked the Huaqing Pool. Shufen got all this out in a breathless rush, swaying on her feet. She clutched hard at Jiang Yongnan's sleeve. "You have to help him. You're the only friend he has here."

In truth, Jiang Ruoshui had foreseen this day himself. He had told Jiang Yongnan that once he had made a little more money, he would take Shufen and leave, find some place no one ever came to, and live the life of a peasant in the wilds. Jiang Yongnan had said a life like that needed no money at all. Jiang Ruoshui had laughed: the war would end sooner or later, and everything he was laying by was for that day.

But Jiang Ruoshui would never live to see that day. The morning after their arrest, he and the owner of the Huaqing Pool were executed in public without trial, on the open ground before the bathhouse; a single rifle bullet blew his brains across the dirt.

All Jiang Yongnan could do was claim the body. He hired men to bury him beneath an earthen slope outside Ganzhou.

Early the next morning Shen Jinzhu went to call on Shufen. Before she had even reached that room of hers, its walls papered with fine-brush flowers and birds, she saw the front door standing open and Shufen, sleeves rolled up, in the middle of a great clean-out. Many of Jiang Ruoshui's belongings had been piled under the eaves outside.

When a person leaves, the tea goes cold—and how much more when the person is dead? That night, lying beside Jiang Yongnan, Shen Jinzhu was overcome, and having said it she could not hold back her tears.

Jiang Yongnan, his head pillowed on his own two hands, said suddenly, "Marry me."

Shen Jinzhu's mouth fell open, and a long moment passed before she said, weakly, "Never mind that. I've already married one soldier."

Jiang Yongnan thought about it. "Then I'll take off this uniform."

Shen Jinzhu buried her cold face in the hollow of his arm. "They'll shoot you."

Three days later their wedding was held in the little ancestral hall at Liyuan village, plain and yet solemn. Besides the instructors and trainees of the Youth-Cadre class, the children from the nursery next door came too. At the end, the wedding closed on the massed child voices singing the "Song of Marching to Battle."

After the wedding Shen Jinzhu gave up her post at the provincial middle school, moved to Liyuan village, and became a nurse at the nursery. When spring came, the couple broke a patch of wasteland on the slope behind their house and planted it with all manner of vegetables and melons. What the two of them could not eat, they traded to the villagers for glutinous rice, and from the rice they brewed liquor at home.

Only, Jiang Yongnan could never again find that fierce American vodka. Not a drop. Jiang Ruoshui's death had wiped out, along with him, the whole trade in smuggled foreign liquor across Gannan.

One evening Jiang Yongnan showed a rare good humor. He cooked the meal himself, with many kinds of vegetables, chilies, and soybean paste, a handful of river clams dredged from the lake, and boiled up a pot of sauce-brown soup in the water rice had been rinsed in.

Shen Jinzhu had never tasted anything like it. Across the table she looked at her husband with wondering, delighted eyes.

"This is doenjang stew. Back home, we drank it every day." Later in that meal, for the first time, Jiang Yongnan told his wife about his life—from Jeju Island, where he was born, all the way to the Iwai Surgical Clinic in Hankou.

By the time he finished, the sky had gone fully dark. Shen Jinzhu came to as if waking from a dream, found the matches, and struck one. In the leaping flame she looked at her husband's long, narrow eyes and said, archly, "Either way, I'm yours."

The next summer, in the flood season, the Gan River rose in spate, and the whole Youth-Cadre class, teachers and trainees alike, was called into the city to man the dikes against the flood. It was then that a man leaning on a bamboo staff came weaving into Liyuan village, asking his way as he went, and knocked at Jiang Yongnan's door. Shen Jinzhu, one hand on the door frame, did not make out his face until he lifted off his rain hat—and then she started as though she had seen a ghost, and for a long while could not get out a single word.

The man was her first husband, back from the dead. He had not fallen in battle but been taken prisoner, held all this while in the Japanese camp at Shangrao, then marched from battlefield to battlefield across Jiangxi as forced labor. He had thought he would die, like countless others, in a trench dug by his own hands—but he had not. A guerrilla raid had freed them. Seated on a bench in the main room, gazing up at the wife who had haunted his dreams, the man said he had been searching Ganzhou for two days already. He had gone to their old home, gone to his father-in-law's house, now burned to rubble, and found the provincial middle school only at the last; he had not waited for the rain to stop but come straight on. At the end the man, worn through by all he had suffered, let two lines of hot tears fall. "Jinzhu. What I feared most was that I'd die on the road to reach you."

Shen Jinzhu did not answer. Though she leaned against a wall, she looked as if she had long since collapsed to the floor, weaker even than the man. Now the man stood, and leaning on his staff he limped once around the main room, then went to the door of the inner room and looked in—and understood everything. He picked his rain hat up off the floor, took one last look at Shen Jinzhu, and limped back out into the rain, off the way he had come.

The next day Jiang Yongnan came home worn to the bone, and got no rest. In silence he washed himself clean in cold water, in silence opened his medical case, and there in the little ancestral hall, on a door laid flat for a table, performed a successful amputation with a nurse from the nursery.

It turned out the man had been running a high fever the whole way home, and had fainted to the ground not long after passing out of Shen Jinzhu's sight. The villagers carried him into the little ancestral hall, and only when they stripped off his soaked clothes did they find that one of his legs was long since a pulp of flesh and blood, crawling with maggots. The rain that had fallen for more than a week finally stopped, and a rainbow hung in the sky. Jiang Yongnan had the man carried back to his house and laid on his bed. That evening he built a fire on the open ground outside, to dry the washed bandages. In the sweat hissing off him, Jiang Yongnan said, "I've made up my mind. I'm giving this home back to him."

"This home isn't his. This home is ours." When Shen Jinzhu had said it, a light like flame flickered in her eyes. Then all at once she said, "Let's leave here. I'll go back to Jeju Island with you."

"Haven't you seen it?" Jiang Yongnan let his gaze rest on Shen Jinzhu's face. "You are his home. Wherever you are, there his home is."

The light in Shen Jinzhu's eyes dimmed, little by little. She rose without a word and walked, with heavy steps, back into the house.

That night Jiang Yongnan sat before the fire the whole night through, until the east grew pale and the fire had burned to ash.
