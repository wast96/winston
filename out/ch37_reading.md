## Chapter 37

Many scattered small matters, jumbled together here, would surely obstruct the development of my story; yet here I cannot help supplying one thing. A day or two before Christmas I had given presents to relatives and friends, and Bai Ping, Mrs. Manfield and Mei Yingzi were of course among those I gave to. For on returning to my lodgings the first thing I did turned out to be, not the sleep I had counted on, but the discovery that Mei Yingzi had sent someone round with a present for me. It lay on my sofa, a large box wrapped in Christmas paper. Seeing Mei Yingzi's name on it and the words of Christmas good wishes, I opened it at once: inside was a dressing gown, grey ground with black stripes and red trim, of very fine wool, English goods. The colour I liked too. Taking off my evening clothes and changing into my pyjamas, I tried the gown on, and found both the size and the cut suited me. It was a rather costly, rather ceremonious present, and I began to feel that what I had sent her was too paltry.

This, of course, was no great matter, and I soon put it out of mind; wearing the gown, I sat on the sofa and smoked a cigarette, then washed and went to bed, the gown left hanging by my bedside.

The moment I lay down on the soft bed I fell asleep, with no notion at all of how the time was passing.

I dreamed. I dreamed that the dressing gown flew of its own accord, dazzling with light, and it was as though someone told me this was the holy robe of Atri in a novel by Flaubert; even in the dream I seemed quite to believe it a thing of mystery. I actually went walking down the street wearing it, to see whether anyone would praise my boldness; but the whole street burst out laughing, and someone splashed red ink on my gown, and then everyone was splashing it, as though it were some superstitious warding-off of misfortune; some flung it down from upstairs windows, till I was drenched all over in red, and I watched the gown turn from patch upon patch of red light into red from head to foot, with drops of water, thick and turbid and heavy, dripping from my hem.

"Tap! Tap! Tap! Tap!" I heard the sound of the dripping water, exactly as though someone were knocking at the door.

I woke; the sun filled my window curtains, red as blood—this was the very apparition of the gown in my dream—while the gown itself still hung by the bed, grey ground, black stripes, red trim.

"Rap! Rap! Rap! Rap!" There really was someone knocking at the door.

"Who is it?" I asked.

"Me." It was a woman's voice; naturally this was Mei Yingzi. I suddenly remembered yesterday's appointment—could the appointed hour already have passed?

I rose, and threw on the gown with pleasure: it would surely be amusing, I thought, to let Mei Yingzi see the present she had given me; I smoothed my hair with my hand and went out to open the door.

But the one standing at the door was not Mei Yingzi. My drowsy, half-shut eyes began to clear—this really did astonish me too much.

—It was Bai Ping.

How could Bai Ping know this address? All at once I thought of that figure the other day, standing in the lane mouth opposite, who on seeing me had turned and walked in; so she had ferreted out my address long before.

She stood at the door and did not come in at once, wearing a forced, cold smile; beyond that not a single movement, not a flicker of expression; her face bore no trace of powder or rouge, showing the wan pallor left by last night's vomiting. She wore a large, loose overcoat of coarse herringbone tweed, last night's handbag tucked under her arm, both hands thrust into her coat pockets, a white silk scarf wound about her neck, hiding the collar of the evergreen-ground, red-square-flowered woollen cheongsam within. I stepped back two paces and said, feigning composure:

"I never dreamed you would come to see me."

"I should think you might have dreamed it." So saying, she took a step in, and pushed the door-lock shut with her elbow, her hands still in her pockets.

"You've recovered today?" I said.

"Thank you." But still she stood without moving.

"Won't you take off your coat?" I said, going nearer.

"No," she said gravely. "I have only come to ask you to give that thing back."

"What thing?" Her fierce air forced me, despite myself, to step back.

"Don't play the fool." She said it with a cold, cutting sneer.

"I really don't know." I was lying; I meant to hold out through this stretch of time, so that in the afternoon I could fetch the document back from Mei Yingzi and return it to her. But she said:

"From Jiangwan to Route Prosper Paris, my handbag was right there beside you."

"Your handbag?" I said, feigning thought. "Ah, that was next to Mrs. Manfield the whole time."

"Then Mrs. Manfield has lied." So saying, she pressed a step closer, and in a changed, rueful tone said: "I foresaw that you would go over to the side of my enemies. And now..."

"Bai Ping, now you must let me..." I was on the point of speaking frankly with her outright, of urging her to turn back and give it a try. But she cut me off, and said savagely:

"Tell me honestly—is that thing still here with you now, or not?"

"Search me," I said.

"So you have already gone to my enemies to claim your reward! Very well!" So saying, her right hand suddenly came out of her coat pocket—it was a pistol!

"..." I was just about to speak, but she shook her head, and, as if in regret, raised the pistol at me and said:

"Today my duty is to have you die!" With a roar she pulled the trigger.

This should have struck me full in the chest, but in that instant, by instinct, I twisted aside to dodge, and the bullet went in through my left arm. I shrank my body like an animal, my right hand pressed to the wound; there was a sentence in my heart, but several times it came up to my throat and I swallowed it back. I found that the fear of my own instant was now all upon Bai Ping: her face ghastly white, her eyes flashing red, her whole body trembling. She seemed to be steadying herself, and in a stern, and also quavering, voice she said:

"Our friendship gives me the courage to take on this task of carrying out your death sentence, because you know..."

"I know—I know that you will come to regret it." Unable to bear the pain, I leaned back against the window bay and said it with conviction. I saw the blood on the gown, mingling with the grey ground, black stripes and red trim into a fearful purplish red, and I thought of my dream!

"No! No!" she suddenly said, as if to herself. "I ought to have courage."

Then she raised the gun—I had not even time for fear—and with a roar she pulled the trigger.

I believe I fainted; I could not tell where this shot had struck; and the fearful blood on my left arm, as it convulsed, came welling out, faster than the sleeve of my gown could soak it up. Unable to hold myself up, by instinct I sank limply to the floor; but my mind was still very clear. In that instant I had no more fear, no alarm; I did not even wish to make any declaration to Bai Ping. I shut my eyes and waited for the coming of Bai Ping's third bullet, praying that it would bring me at once to my final rest.

But the third shot never came. Suddenly I felt Bai Ping at my ear; she was stroking my brow, and calling, anxious and full of pity:

"Xu! Xu!"

I turned over and opened my eyes; I saw her half-kneeling at my side, tears hanging at the alarmed corners of her eyes, her hair falling down against my cheek. She said:

"Xu..."

"Rap, rap... rap, rap." This knocking cut off Bai Ping's words, and she began to panic. With my right hand I pressed her down, and, raising myself a little and gathering my strength, I lifted my voice and asked:

"Who is it?"

"Is anything the matter?" It was the servant's voice.

"Nothing," I said, putting on a look of annoyance. "I've only just got to sleep—don't come disturbing me."

As I raised myself to answer, Bai Ping's arm was pillowed under my neck; now my head sank limply down again, and still she let me lean on her. Those few words had made my pain flare up all at once, and I found that the second shot had struck me in the left shoulder. The crimson-purple blood had already stained my left breast and spread all over my left arm—which put me in mind of the dream just now—and I could not help a bitter smile. But in that instant I saw Bai Ping's pistol right beside me, and, waking suddenly to it, I said:

"Go, quickly—go out through the bathroom door."

Bai Ping's panic had already dazed her; she did not know what to do; a glistening teardrop fell onto my lips. I reached out and felt for the pistol, and said:

"Go, go quickly! I'll say I did it to myself."

Bai Ping rose staggering, but steadied herself a moment, then bent down again, her left hand bracing my right arm, her right hand pillowing my neck, gazing at me with glistening, tear-filled eyes, the corners of her mouth faintly quivering. She said:

"Promise me: from this day on, give your great soul to the nation."

"Great though my soul may be, it belongs always to the nation... in the past, in the present, and in the everlasting future to come."

"..." She was astonished.

Showing a strange light of excitement, her gaze shot straight into the depths of my eyes; and at last she laid her mouth on my lips, and she wept, and sobbing said:

"Forgive me!"

She sprang to her feet, took up the handbag from the chair behind, and hurried into the bathroom; and then I heard the sound of the back door closing.

Now I had a clear mind and a quiet world in which to think. Of these two wounds, the one on the shoulder blade ached unbearably, but the one on the arm bled the harder; using the belt of my gown, and by the strength of my right hand and my teeth, I bound it tight above the wound on the arm. I tried to struggle to my feet—it was very hard—and once up, what could I do anyway? I thought of calling someone, and felt that was no solution either; so I lay down calmly, wanting a spell of quiet thought, to seek the quickest and most convenient way of getting myself to a hospital.

The cover of suicide I had thought of just now seemed, on reflection, too childish. First, both these gunshot wounds had been fired in from behind. Second, if it were suicide, one should surely aim at a fatal spot; even with two shots gone astray, there would all the more be need of a third. Third, a man who has killed himself has no right to have others send him to hospital.

The most convenient thing, of course, was to call someone—but how was I to explain myself? Best of all was to let no outsider know, and so spare the newspapers their various conjectures. Suddenly I thought of Mei Yingzi's noon appointment; by now it should already be...? My watch was in my clothes pocket; judging by the sunlight, it should already be half past eleven. So I thought the best thing would be to telephone Mei Yingzi to come, and have her fetch Dr. Philip to take me to hospital. But my telephone was up on the writing-desk, some ten steps from where I lay, and I would have to drag my wounded body across to it.

I put the whole weight of my body onto my right arm and turned myself onto my side; I drew up my knees and tried to stand, but it was so heavy and so hard—the wound in my left shoulder wrenched up an unbearable spasm of pain that would not let my neck turn—and I slumped down aslant against the floor again. Half a minute later I made a second attempt: I knitted my brows, clenched my teeth, kept my left arm pressed close to my body to hold the balance of my upper half, and from the side let my right arm push up off the ground, while at the same time I braced my bent right leg up from the floor; but my slippers and the floorboards were both too slick, and when I was two feet off the ground my right foot slipped, so that my right arm could not hold me, and I fell to the floor again. This jolt wrenched up in my left arm and left shoulder another spasm of pain there was no resisting, and more blood came flowing out; my head swam, and there was a trickling sweat at my temples and in all my limbs. I could only shut my eyes and lie still a long while.

But I had a lucid mind, which made me feel I must first find something to hold on to before I could get up; so, using my right hand as a rudder, I steered my body slowly toward the window panelling, and as I drew near the window frame I made a third attempt. I gripped the window panelling with my right hand and braced my right foot against the wall; I held my breath, letting the left side of my body not shake in the slightest; and in that instant I felt that the human body, on the ground, is no different from a great tree taking root—and that we can only sink down into the earth, and cannot grow up out of it.

At last I did get up, and, clinging to the wall like a crawling thing, I went step by step toward the writing-desk.

Just at that moment there came the sound of footsteps passing from the next room into the bathroom, and while I stood startled and doubtful, someone had already come out of the bathroom.

Very tall, a moustache on his upper lip, his eyes bright and gleaming, an overcoat draped over his arm, half hiding the handbag in his hand. Behind him followed a young, sturdy man.

They came over, grave and composed, and only now did I recognize Dr. Philip. Without a single word he directed the young assistant to help him take off my clothes, and supported me to the sofa to sit.

There was radiator heat in the room, but it was not warm enough; Philip himself moved the electric heater in from the bathroom and set it before me. I said:

"Was it Bai Ping who fetched you?"

He took no notice of me, and directed the assistant to clear up the bloodstains on the floor. He himself went back into the bathroom, and I heard the sound of washing; then, in his shirtsleeves, his cuffs rolled high, he came out, opened the handbag, and wiped his hands with spirits. By now the blood on my arm had more or less clotted, but the plasma welled in great clots at the wound, with fresh red corpuscles welling up over it; the wound on my left shoulder I could not see myself, but there too were fresh red corpuscles hanging below the arm; needless to say, my chest and the backs of my hands were stained with a good deal of blood as well. In an instant my nerves could no longer bear these traces of blood; I fell silent and slumped, gazing into Philip's eyes, and said:

"Is it serious?"

He did not answer, and gave a slight shake of the head. From the handbag he took out a hypodermic and drugs, had the assistant press up the vein in my right arm, and began to give me an injection; then he gave me a glass of boiled water and two tablets and told me to swallow them; at last he looked at the wounds and swiftly brought out gauze and bandages to dress them.

"The bullet?" I asked.

He took no notice of me, and only bound my wounds up tight. At last he had the assistant fetch my trousers, my leather shoes and my shirt, and help me on with them. Then he himself drew the overcoat over me and set the hat on my head, and had the assistant stuff the pistol and my bloodstained clothes into his handbag.

The deftness of this whole procedure was astonishing; from the time he came in until now was, I think, no more than the space of two or three cigarettes. Through all these many movements I was in some pain, but by now my wounds were bound to numbness; and while the assistant was putting the pistol and my clothes into the handbag, he went back into the bathroom again. From the assistant's wristwatch I saw the time was already a quarter past twelve.

When Dr. Philip came out, dressed, calm and refined, I said:

"Mei Yingzi—you...?"

He nodded, letting out the faintest smile, and stopped me from talking; he took out a cigarette case, put one between his own lips, and put another between mine. Then he flicked open the lighter, lit mine, and lit his own. He suddenly caught sight of the scarf, glanced at the assistant, and the assistant, taking his meaning, brought it and wound it about my neck.

Then, from the right, he lifted me up and, tenderly and with force, supported me; his assistant, carrying the handbag and with the overcoat over his arm, was already opening the front door for me.

Out the door, his assistant walked on my left side, and Philip, as if on purpose, kept puffing his cigarette out ahead of us; and when we met the servants he laughed and said loudly:

"I told you you were drunk yesterday and you still wouldn't own to it."

"I certainly drank more than you did," I said, forcing a smile.

His car was waiting at the door, and in less than half an hour I had reached the Gaolang Hospital on Gaoye Road.

Mei Yingzi was waiting for me at the door of Ward Twelve.

Ward Twelve—all at once I thought of Stephen: his iron-grey cheeks, his deep-purple lips, his tightly clenched teeth, his slightly parted eyes...

And I lay down on this very bed, on which Stephen's life had once ebbed away.
