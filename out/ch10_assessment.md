# Chapter Ten — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 201–218. Sections 35 ("Dai Yunong Spreads His Net in Shanghai"),
36 ("Who Is the Informer"), 37 ("The Feint on Route Père Robert"). Chapter
title: "Wang Yaqiao Walks to Maicheng" — the idiom for a hero's undoing, from
Guan Yu's last defeat.*

**Coverage note.** Synopsis covers printed 201–213, read off the OCR with names
and numbers checked. The last few pages were not yet processed when this was
written and are not summarized.

---

## What happens

**Chiang and Soong Mei-ling in the garden.** She is indignant about the attempt
on the Lytton commission — does the man not understand the first thing about
diplomacy? Chiang confirms it was Wang Yaqiao's Iron and Blood corps that
disturbed the commission's quarters at the Cathay by night, and frets about
what it would have meant internationally had it succeeded.

**Dai Li briefs his Shanghai people.** Three names on the board: Wang Yaqiao,
Yang Xingfo, and Soong Ching-ling. The last two are to be watched only; Wang is
to be killed, and quickly. His officers push back — Wang has more than a dozen
addresses, comes and goes without a trace, and finding him in Shanghai is
fishing a needle out of the sea. One notes that he is no longer merely a gang
boss operating out of the Anhui guildhall as in the old days.

**The turned man.** Dai Li has recruited Wei Yiming, a squad leader in Wang's
own corps, who protests that even he rarely sees Wang — only the wife and the
driver know where he sleeps. Dai Li is content to wait: a long line for a big
fish.

**Wang works out who it is.** His car is followed on Nanjing Road; his bodyguard
notices the tail. Wang gathers his inner circle and has each man write a
character on his palm, then calls them up to the lamp — every hand says *Wei*.
He makes them give reasons rather than take the coincidence as proof, and they
do: Wei went down to buy cigarettes every time Wang left the building; one man
followed him and found him talking to somebody at the shop.

**The trap.** Wei is walked into a dark lane by two of Wang's men, understands
too late what is happening, and finds two more behind him.

## How it holds up

The pattern by now is familiar, and this chapter fits it exactly: **the
furniture is accurate, the people are real, and Wang Yaqiao's connection to any
of it is undocumented.**

### The furniture is right, including two things I had wrong

- **Route Père Robert.** My OCR read 姚神父路; the street is **金神父路**, Route
  Père Robert, laid out in 1907 and absorbed into the French Concession in 1914,
  renamed **Ruijin Er Road** in 1943. Added 姚/金 to the mangle map.
- **And the street matters.** Guangci Hospital — Hôpital Sainte-Marie, today
  Ruijin Hospital — stood on it. That is where Yang Xingfo was carried to die.
  The chapter's location and its central killing are the same address.
- **The Shanghai station personnel are real, and my uncertain OCR readings all
  resolve to documented people:** the district chief is **Yu Lexing** (not Cai
  Lexing), sent by Dai Li in autumn 1932 when the Shanghai station was upgraded
  to the East China District; **Chen Zhiqiang**, Whampoa third class, head of
  the First Group; **Wang Changyu**, Whampoa fifth class, head of the Second
  Group, whose speciality was penetrating anti-Chiang circles. Yu Lexing is a
  striking figure in his own right — an early Communist who studied security
  work in Moscow before defecting — and he is the man who recruited his
  brother-in-law Shen Zui into the service, which matters below.

### Yang Xingfo's killing is documented, and is not Wang Yaqiao's story

Yang Xingfo was shot on the morning of **18 June 1933** outside the Academia
Sinica publications office on Avenue du Roi Albert, in the French Concession.
He was general secretary of the **China League for Civil Rights** — Soong
Ching-ling chair, Cai Yuanpei vice-chair, Lin Yutang publicity — founded in
December 1932 to campaign against political imprisonment, torture and
extrajudicial killing. His fifteen-year-old son was in the car; Yang threw
himself over the boy and took the bullets. The driver was killed too; the son
survived with a leg wound. Sources disagree on whether he took three rounds or
ten and on whether he died at the scene or in hospital.

The League **ceased to exist within weeks**, which was the point of killing him.

Nationalist secret-service responsibility is the near-universal view. But the
granular version — Chiang signalling in April, Dai Li coming down in June to run
it himself, a named triggerman team — descends almost entirely from **Shen
Zui's memoirs**, written decades later in PRC custody by a former Juntong
officer with every incentive to tell the story a particular way. It should be
attributed to him, not stated as record.

### Soong Ching-ling: the book's framing is right in substance

There is no evidence of an actual plan to kill her. What the record supports is
exactly what the book describes — heavy permanent surveillance rather than
assassination, because killing Sun Yat-sen's widow was politically impossible.
The book is retailing the standard Chinese account here rather than inventing.
The specific three-name directive, though, is not documented.

### Wei Yiming does not exist

Searched against Wang Yaqiao, against the Iron and Blood corps, against the
Hatchet Gang, and generically. **Zero hits, in any form.** He appears in no
reference work and in none of the recycled popular accounts.

There *is* a documented informer in Wang's organization, and he is a different
man in a different year: **He Yong'an**, who while organizing an attempt on
mayor Wu Tiecheng informed on the plot and named Wang as the planner of the
T.V. Soong shooting — 1931, not 1933. And the betrayal that actually destroyed
Wang came later still and by another route entirely: Dai Li took his lieutenant
Yu Likui, could not turn him, and bought **Yu's wife** instead, with money and a
promise of her husband's release. She lured Wang to Wuzhou on **20 October
1936**, where he was shot.

So the chapter's whole engine — a traitor inside the corps in 1933, exposed by
the trick with the palms — has no counterpart in the record.

### No link between Wang Yaqiao and Yang Xingfo, or Wang and Soong Ching-ling

Searched directly. Nothing, in either direction. The League's literature does
not mention him; the Wang Yaqiao literature does not mention Yang Xingfo. Where
the chapter puts them in a shared political orbit, that is the author's
construction.

### One anachronism of nomenclature

The body Dai Li ran in 1933 was the **Special Services Section** of the
Renaissance Society, established March 1932, with its East China District at
Shanghai. It was not yet Juntong, which dates from 1938. Chinese writing about
this period uses "Juntong" loosely and backwards; where the book does the same,
it is a label out of its time.

### Wang in 1933, which the chapter is building toward

He left Shanghai for Hong Kong in **August 1933**, contacted Li Jishen and the
southwestern faction, and went to Fuzhou that November for the **Fujian
Incident** — the 19th Route Army revolt that proclaimed a rival government on
20 November 1933 and collapsed inside two months. All corroborated. The bounty
figure the popular literature gives — a million silver dollars — has no
documentary basis and is almost certainly legend. For scale, Dai Li bought the
betrayal that actually killed Wang for a tenth of that.

## The source problem, stated plainly

This chapter is where it becomes clearest that the whole Chinese popular
literature on Wang Yaqiao is **one body of story wearing many hats**. It
recycles a small set of narratives and leans ultimately on Shen Zui's memoirs
and on 文史资料 recollections gathered decades after the events. A claim
appearing in a dozen articles across a dozen outlets is not corroborated; it has
been copied twelve times.

That is why the checking in these assessments has repeatedly had to go outside
Chinese-language popular sources — to Japanese naval records, Korean
independence-movement scholarship, Hansard — to find out whether anything is
actually underneath. When it does, the answer is consistent: the events are
real, and Wang Yaqiao's authorship of them is not.
