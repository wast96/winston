### Sage Kang Runs the Guoshi Bao

In the Wuxu coup at the close of the Qing, old Mr. Kang was truly the moving spirit of the affair. Once the plot leaked, his younger brother Guangren, together with Tan Sitong, Lin Xu and the rest, met their end at Caishikou, while the old man, with his beloved disciple Liang Rengong (Qichao), fled abroad and so kept his life. Thereafter he simply took up the "protect-the-emperor" signboard and carried it, trumpeting it high and low among the overseas Chinese wherever he went. By the early years of the Republic, when Yuan (Shikai) and Duan (Qirui) held power one after another, Liang had served several turns as a high official, but Kang swore he would never take office. I recall that once Kang wired Yuan, heading the message with the six characters "President Weiting, my young brother" — proof enough of the way he traded on his years.

Kang's style was Changsu, and he was also called Nanhai (he was in fact a native of Nanhai in Guangdong; once a man's fame grows great, his native place stands in for his sobriquet — as Li Yuanhong is called Cen Xilin, and Cen Chunxuan called Li Huangpi). By the fifth or sixth year of the Republic he wearied of his wanderings and came home, and at the corner of Maijiaquan and Jiaotong Road in Shanghai he opened a paper, the Guoshi Bao, whose editorials pressed with all their might for the veneration of Confucius and breathed not a word about the affairs of the Republic. When Zhang Xun's restoration came, Kang had gone secretly north beforehand and had a hand in the plot, serving one brief stint as a deliberating minister. Only when the restoration was snuffed out and the Republic made whole again did he slip abroad once more to live as an idle exile, and this Confucius-touting Guoshi Bao thereupon shut up shop.

In the end he still set up as a sage, now south, now north, now east, now west, his comings and goings past all fixing. Four years ago he died of illness at his lodging in Qingdao, and since then there has been no more to be seen of the old man's doings and sayings. Someone remarked that, had Kang not died, the premiership of the puppet state today would beyond a doubt have fallen to old Kang.

Kang had a gift for calligraphy; his admirers styled it "unbridled and vigorous, first in all China." In truth the characters he wrote kept to no rule: unbridled they were, but vigorous not necessarily. The late university lecturer Li Shicen wrote in strenuous imitation of Kang's manner — which is to say that the benevolent see benevolence and the wise see wisdom, and tastes will differ.
