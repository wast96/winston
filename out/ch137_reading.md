### Buying a Mark

There is a breed of swindler forever setting up some sham foreign firm or some sham silk-and-satin shop, printing off tens of thousands of gaudy many-coloured catalogues, giving out that it holds some grand-opening celebration, say, or is enlarging its business and clearing its stock, all in the smoothest of honeyed words, with the prices marked exceedingly cheap. These catalogues they post out from the post office, one by one, to every province and every port; and people up-country, on receiving them, take it that there really is such a firm and such a silk shop in Shanghai holding its grand opening and expanding its trade, and, coveting the bargain, straightway remit their money and order by post. But once the money is sent off it is like a stone dropped into the sea: never do they get any cheap goods or any cheap silk. After a long while they beg some friend or kinsman sojourning in Shanghai to go to the address and look into it, but the sham firm and the sham silk shop have long since vanished without shadow or trace, and no one knows where.

Yet, strange to say, how do these men come to know the surnames and addresses of people in the out-ports? Once the thing is laid bare, there is nothing strange in it at all. For beforehand they pay heavy money to some clerk in a great pharmacy or a great publishing house, one who keeps the carbon-copy ledger, and secretly have him copy out a list, which they then post off to the addresses on it. This piece of business they call "buying a mark." Once they have their marks bought, they need only sacrifice a little more in postage to cheat a great deal of money and stuff their own purses. Nowadays, though many of the gulled have grown wise to it, China is vast and the marks past counting: today a Zhang San is taken in, tomorrow it is a Li Si's turn to be the dupe, round upon round, without ever an end. Ah me!
