Chapter 18. Thirty Days a Refugee
Before the Pacific War broke out in the winter of 1941, Lin Gengbai and his wife Lin Beili flew from Chongqing to Hong Kong, meaning to use their wide connections with overseas Chinese to found in Hong Kong a newspaper supporting patriotic resistance, the Overseas Chinese Daily. When they heard that the Japanese had launched the Pacific War, there was no time to withdraw to Chongqing, and so they moved into my home on Kimberley Road in Kowloon to stay. The place was small, and they had to sleep on floor-mats. Zhigao told me to hurry off to Qujiang and had already bought a plane ticket for December 14. But the war began on December 8, and by the 12th Kowloon had fallen, so that I, along with Lin Gengbai and his wife and Zhu Libo and her daughter, were all trapped in the now Japanese-occupied Kowloon. Luckily I had prepared well in advance: the household had stored up two months’ worth of grain and side dishes — dried bamboo shoots, mushrooms, wood-ear fungus, dried preserved greens — as well as pickled vegetables, canned goods, and salted vegetables I had put up myself. On December 9, one could no longer take the Kowloon-to-Hong Kong ferry across the harbor without a special pass, but there was still a kind of small ferrying motor-launch, and I took one across the harbor to the bank, withdrew part of the deposits of the South China branch of the Chinese Women’s Temperance Association, and prepared to disband the women refugees at the refugee shelter. Zhu Libo happened to come back to my home, and after the two of us conferred we decided to give each refugee three months’ disbandment living allowance, and we paid the shelter’s staff a disbandment allowance as well. After the refugee shelter was closed, Zhu Libo and her daughter came to live with us. On the 10th, Yang Yunshi’s youngest son — “Old Fifth Yang” — managed to get hold of a ferry pass and came to fetch his stepmother to the Hong Kong city area, which had not yet fallen. He offered on his own to take the three of us, mother and daughters, along to shelter in the city too. I felt I could not abandon Lin Gengbai and his wife and Zhu Libo and her daughter, who were sheltering here, so I would not go with him. But Old Fifth Yang said: “The Japanese spies have already established that you, Huang Dinghui, are an underground Communist Party member; there is a name on the wanted list the Japanese army posted after occupying Kowloon, and it is yours. If you go on living in your own home, I’m afraid there could be danger. And if anything happens, the others living with you will be implicated too.” Hearing this, everyone else said it would be best for me to cross the harbor and lie low for a while. So I entrusted Zhu Libo with running the household and looking after Lin Gengbai and his wife on my behalf. But once in Hong Kong, where was I to take shelter? In my extremity a thought struck me, and I made myself an uninvited guest at the home of Dr. Deng Xianyu, who handled finance for the South China branch of the Women’s Temperance Association. Deng Xianyu was a Christian; her husband, Chen Di, had once been captain of the warship Zhongshan, and had retired home to recover from a stomach ailment. To be so warmly welcomed by the Chen couple amid the chaos of war, I felt deeply comforted. Also sheltering in their home were the wife and children of Chen Di’s nephew, General Chen Ce, everyone crowded in together. Chen Ce was a Kuomintang navy admiral who had had a leg blown off in battle. Deng Xianyu and I arranged that I would disguise myself as a servant in her house, a nanny to her children. The house belonged to Deng Xianyu and her husband; a bed was added to the large back room where the nannies lived, and the three of us, mother and daughters, settled in there. The Chen household had suddenly taken in many people seeking refuge, and with no way of knowing when the fighting would end, everyone naturally worried the food supply would run short, so no one dared eat much; we could only cook a little thin rice porridge or noodles and save grain as far as possible. Not many days later, Chen Ce sent people to take his family away.
On the eve of the fall of Hong Kong Island itself, the already-fallen Kowloon and Hong Kong shelled each other across the harbor, and the bombardment on the night of the 21st was the fiercest. Deng Xianyu and the others took shelter in the air-raid dugout every day. For the first two days I too took my two daughters into the dugout, but it was packed with people, the air was foul, and Dazhong was running a fever besides. The third time, as I was setting out for the dugout with Dazhong on my back and Yunzhong by the hand, we had just stepped out the Chen family’s front gate when a shell came flying over and blew a passerby on the road ahead into four pieces, the bloody limbs and trunk hanging on the wall by the roadside — a sight too dreadful to look at. I thought, if we had gone a few steps sooner, very likely we would have been blown up along with him. If we were to die, then let us just die together — why bother sheltering in the dugout at all? So we retreated back into the house. In those days every household hung blackout curtains of light-proof black cloth over the windows. Downstairs in the Chen house was a narrow passage running straight back to the kitchen, and I put a square table in it, padded above and below with quilts, laid Dazhong to sleep under the table, and took Yunzhong to hide in the passage with me. On the night of the 21st, a shell came shooting straight down from the fourth floor of Chen Di’s house and smashed the bathtub in the bathroom at the back; smoke filled the rooms in an instant. Yunzhong, already six, knew enough to be afraid, and clung to me, crying “Mama” over and over, and all I could do was keep comforting her not to be frightened. Dazhong, running a fever and asleep under the table, was too dazed to know fear. Because the child was ill, if we had hidden in the dugout she could not have got even a mouthful of hot water; keeping the children with me hidden in the house, I could give her medicine from time to time to treat the cold and bring down the fever, and I also boiled a big pot of porridge for everyone. When the shelling stopped and those who had sheltered in the dugout came home, everyone greatly admired my courage and composure. The shelling ceased on the 22nd, and on the 25th the British garrison in Hong Kong surrendered to the Japanese and the two sides signed an armistice, and only then did we pass a few somewhat calmer days.
On the evening of the (Western) New Year’s Eve, Japanese soldiers came knocking at Chen Di’s door. We and our neighbors had agreed in advance that if we found Japanese soldiers coming to harass us, we would bang an enamel washbasin to warn the neighbors on every side. Hearing the Japanese soldiers knock, we at once began banging the basin. The interpreter accompanying the Japanese knocked on the door and shouted loudly: “Don’t be afraid, we are men of the Japanese army command, come to call on General Chen Ce.” Mr. Chen Di went and opened the door, and once the Japanese came in they checked the household registration, first asking whether a General Chen Ce lived here, saying the Japanese command wanted to invite him to work on the peace-maintenance committee. We all said we did not know where Chen Ce was, and did not say that his family had once lived here. The Japanese said they knew he had lived here, and pressed us about when he had left and where he had gone. We still said we did not know. The Japanese then seized Chen Di and took him away. When they questioned me that night, I said I was the wet-nurse of the Chen family’s children. After the Japanese had questioned everyone in the house one by one, they took flashlights and searched everywhere — the kitchen, the back courtyard. As that interpreter followed me to search the back-courtyard room and saw that the Japanese soldiers were not beside us, he quietly said to me: “You are Huang Dinghui, aren’t you? I know you. You must leave here tomorrow, or you’ll be in danger!” He also told me the name of a place on the Hong Kong shore (which I have now forgotten), saying that an old fisherman would be there with his boat waiting for us, to ferry us across the water to the home of Boss Gu Jiatang (one of the secret-society chiefs who had fled from Shanghai to Hong Kong); this fisherman was Gu’s disciple, everything had been arranged, and when the time came a boat would take us on to Chaozhou; and he told me to hold a newspaper in my hand at the time as a signal, and the fisherman would come to take us aboard. As he spoke these words to me in a low voice, he kept breaking off to bellow abuse at me, so as not to arouse the Japanese soldiers’ suspicion. They searched the Chen house thoroughly, and finding nothing suspicious, they left. Afterward I talked it over with Deng Xianyu and the others, and we felt this interpreter was very likely an underground worker we had planted, and decided to take the risk and go as he had said. But Madame Yang did not trust the interpreter and would not come along, and I could not very well force her. So through the night I got our things ready, put two thin padded gowns on the children — clothes by day on the road, quilts by night — and took along some fried rice-flour mixed with sugar, in case the children got hungry along the way. The next day, carrying my second daughter on my back and leading my eldest by the hand, I walked to the appointed spot on the shore, and sure enough there was a small boat; someone came down to help us aboard, and neither side said a word to the other. The little boat slipped past the Japanese sentry posts and rowed to the far shore. Once landed, I knew that Gu Jiatang’s home was just behind Du Yuesheng’s, having been there before. When we arrived, Du Yuesheng’s wife Yao Yulan was already there to meet us. Yao Yulan told me that someone from the Central Relief Commission had come and made arrangements: Gu Jiatang would front the operation and, through the Chaozhou Native-Place Association, deal with the Japanese army command to charter a steamer under the name of sending refugees home, to set out in a few days by way of Danshui to Huiyang. We stayed at the Gu home for several days; because there were so many people taking refuge there, we all had to sleep on floor-mats.
Around January 19, we went to the dock to board. The line of refugees stretched long, and Gu Jiatang too had disguised himself as a refugee, carrying his own luggage, going aboard the same ship to the rear. The Japanese had drawn a white line on the ground as a boundary, and whoever stepped past the white line was whipped. The people lined up to board crowded together, and Yunzhong, queuing behind me, was squeezed until she began to cry. A Chaozhou refugee asked me something in the Chaozhou dialect, which I did not understand; then he asked in Cantonese, and I told him that because the children’s father was a Chaozhou man, I was taking them on this ship back to their father’s old home. He said he too was going back to Chaozhou on this ship, and that he could help me carry a child aboard, but I would have to give him a little money. I said that would be fine, and gave him fifty dollars to carry Yunzhong. I gripped Yunzhong’s clothes tightly so we would not be squeezed apart. In this way we managed to crowd aboard at last. Because we had boarded late, we could only sit on the floor at the stern. When night came, Dazhong needed to relieve herself and called loudly, “Mama”; the people beside us said, “Eh, they’re Shanghai folk — how are they going to Chaozhou with us?” I explained to them in Cantonese that the children’s father was a Chaozhou man, but the children had grown up in Shanghai from infancy, and that I was their wet-nurse, entrusted by my master to take them back to their father’s old home in Chaozhou. These refugees were really quite good: they passed over a spittoon, let Dazhong relieve herself, then passed it out to empty into the sea. People sharing hard times can always still look after one another. Ah-tao, a servant of the Du household, went about the ship with a flashlight searching for us, and having found us, took us to a cabin. Yao Yulan and Madame Gu had each already taken a berth, and had kept a berth for me. I let the two children sleep on the berth and sat myself on a stool. In the middle of the night the ship met a great storm at sea and pitched violently; from the cabin came the sound of the passengers all being seasick and vomiting. I never get sick in a car, a plane, or a boat, but the children were frightened into crying, and I said to them: “In the past, when you listened to my stories of heroes, you said you wanted to learn to be heroes too; now, if you can’t bear even this little bit of hardship, how will you ever be heroes in the future?” Hearing this, they stopped crying. I cleaned up the vomit in the cabin and went to look after the ladies of the Du and Gu families.
The next afternoon, around two or three o’clock, the ship reached a dock near the town of Danshui. Getting off, because I did not know that the secret society had sent sedan chairs to meet us women, I carried and led my two children off the ship by myself, and only after reaching shore did I realize I had become separated from the Du and Gu parties. I could only follow along with the great crowd of refugees, not even needing to ask the way. Because I was alone with two children, I could go no more than a few li before I could walk no farther, and had to sit down by the roadside to rest. Just then I saw an old woman carrying a pair of empty baskets, and I went up to ask her whether she could use her two baskets to carry my two daughters the twenty or thirty li on to the town of Danshui. She said all right, but each child would cost fifty dollars in carrying fees. I said that was fine. On the flight I had brought plenty of money with me, and since I was in disguise and had no luggage, I looked very much like a real refugee and drew no notice, so no one came to rob me of my money. In this way we went all the way to the town of Danshui. In a ruined temple with only a few broken walls left standing, the Japanese had set up a porridge station, and many refugees crowded there waiting for the porridge. I told the children: we will eat only the fried rice-flour we brought ourselves, and not the porridge the Japanese hand out! When we were thirsty, we bought a few stalks of sugarcane by the roadside and chewed them in place of tea.
Toward evening, I shone my flashlight trying to find the two ladies, Du and Gu, but could not; instead I saw a woman with two children sitting not far from us, weeping bitterly. I asked her why she was crying. She said her surname was Zhu, that her husband worked in Chongqing, that she had brought a servant and carried two sets of bedding and was going to the great rear to find her husband, when all the money she had on her had been robbed by bandits. Because she had permed hair and wore sneakers, one could see at a glance she was a well-off intellectual, and she had a servant following her besides, so she drew much notice. The bandits had robbed her not only of the Hong Kong dollars and U.S. dollars strapped to her body, but even of the jewelry and diamond rings on her head and hands. She said she was truly at the end of her rope. I said, don’t cry; I still have some money on me. We are all refugees; tomorrow we’ll travel together and share good and bad alike. She was overjoyed to hear this, wiped away her tears, and thanked me over and over. I said that with the nation in crisis, we ought to share hardship together. The next day, just as the sky was faintly light, we set off, and after six or seven li we all felt very tired. I saw ahead of us a kindly-looking old woman sitting in the doorway of a house, and I went over and told her our plight in Cantonese, saying that we had to press on and needed two pairs of baskets and two people to help carry our four children along the road. This old woman, surnamed Huang, at once called out from the house her two strong young sons, who put the children into two pairs of baskets and even set in the baskets two big basins of hot, freshly boiled sweet potatoes. The four children ate to their hearts’ content. After eating the sweet potatoes and setting out again, Dazhong’s cold got better. Chatting with the two young men on the road, I learned they were men of the East River guerrillas. They asked me what relatives I had in Huiyang, and I said the children’s father was with the Nationalist government’s Central Relief Commission, that there was a relief station the Commission had set up there, and that when we arrived someone should receive us. The two of them said they were still young, and had wanted to go off with the guerrillas to fight the Japanese devils, but their mother was old and there was no one else to look after the home, and they did not want to be press-ganged into the Kuomintang army as conscripts, so by day they hid in the house and did not come out, and only at night came out to carry on resistance activities; whenever their mother saw refugees who were old, weak, women or children, she would always have them escort them around the Japanese sentry posts to a safe place, and this was a duty they owed. The two of them delivered us safely to a small boat on the riverbank, and we finally reached Huiyang directly and in peace.
At Huiyang, Li Hanhun, chairman of the Guangdong provincial government, and Chen Zhigao had long been waiting there for us. The two children, who had suffered and been frightened all along the way, the moment they saw their father both threw themselves into Zhigao’s arms and shed tears of grievance. Zhigao said he had sent many people to Hong Kong to meet us, but there had been so many refugees on the road that of course they had all missed one another; as for the two ladies, Du and Gu, the secret-society men had carried them over by sedan chair, and they had reached Huiyang long since. That evening Li Hanhun laid on a banquet to welcome the two ladies, Du and Gu, and the rest of us all together. I had thought that, having now reached Huiyang, bathed, and eaten a good meal, we would no longer have to live in the fear and hardship of the refugee road; but in the middle of the night we again heard the alarm that the Japanese were attacking Huiyang, and everyone had to get up at once and withdraw toward Qujiang. This time, though, there was no trouble: Zhigao had come to Huiyang with a work team, and there were team members to help carry the children for me, and we reached Qujiang smoothly, bringing to an end the drifting, homeless life we had led since the fall of Hong Kong.
Later I learned that the Japanese, unable to find Chen Ce, wanted Chen Di to turn traitor and come out to head the peace-maintenance committee. Chen Di’s stand was firm, and he refused to take the puppet post. The Japanese then cruelly subjected him to the torture of forcing chili-pepper water down his throat, and Chen Di, already weak and ill, could not endure it and in the end died a martyr’s death. Afterward Madame Deng Xianyu raised her children through bitter toil. Her son Chen Xiaojiang joined the People’s Liberation Army after the founding of New China, and after transferring to civilian life became a reporter for the Xinhua News Agency; he had first joined the China Democratic League and later the Communist Party; during the “Cultural Revolution,” however, he was persecuted as a “descendant of Kuomintang reactionaries” and put through severe trials. After his rehabilitation, he got in touch with me through our good Democratic League friend Zhu Libo, and I wrote for him the certifying material about how his father had been seized by the Japanese army in Hong Kong and died a martyr, praising Deng Xianyu’s righteous warmth in sheltering us in Hong Kong and our unforgettable friendship. He wrote to say that he had once been named a model Party member, that he had now retired, was settled in Guangzhou, and was taking part in cultural and historical work. Every New Year he sends me a card with his greetings. Later, when I was staying as a guest in the home of my elder sister Yuan Puzhi, he and his beloved Huang Cheng made a special trip to visit me. That an old friend should have descendants, that the flower should bloom and bear fruit, is a real comfort, and I set it down here in passing. I also learned that in 1950 his cousin General Chen Ce had lent some support to the PLA’s cross-sea operation to liberate Hainan Island. In his letter he also told me that Chen Ce’s younger brother had once served as vice-chairman of the Hainan Island provincial political consultative conference.
