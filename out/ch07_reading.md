## 7

On Christmas Eve, to mark the first anniversary of the signing of the Hong Kong armistice, the streets were hung all over with the flags of Japan and its military ensigns, and geisha in kimono had taken the place of the white-bearded Father Christmas. Everywhere were Japanese soldiers making free with their pleasures.

Zuo Qiuming came on foot to the King's Hotel on Lockhart Road, and the moment he stepped into the lobby he saw that the place was blown; but he did not leave at once. Instead he went straight to the telephone booth, swallowed a slip of paper, dialed a number, and hung up without waiting for an answer. He drew a pistol from inside his coat, worked the bolt, dropped it into his overcoat pocket, and, gripping it tight in his hand, pushed the door open and came out.

It was just then that the plainclothesmen closed in. Zuo Qiuming drew and shot two of them down, ran behind a pillar, and set the pistol to his own temple. But before he could pull the trigger a bullet caught him in the chest, and he pitched violently to the ground.

An hour later, while Suzuki Masao was operating on him, the door of the operating room was shoved roughly open. In came a Japanese army lieutenant in full uniform. He flashed a Tokkō credential, bowed to the army surgeon, and said in Japanese, Be so good as to cut open his belly; the intelligence we want will have been swallowed down into his stomach.

Suzuki Masao signaled the nurse to take off his mask, spread his two hands, and said, That would take his life.

His life is not important, the lieutenant said. What is important is the intelligence.

But I am a doctor, Suzuki Masao said. I cannot do this.

You are first of all a soldier of the Empire, the lieutenant said. You must obey orders.

Suzuki Masao stood a moment with his head bowed, then went to the operating table and took the scalpel from the nurse's hand.

The lieutenant was growing impatient; he stepped up and threw back the surgical sheet over Zuo Qiuming and said, Be quick, if you please.

Suzuki Masao paid him no heed, but had the nurse put another dose of anesthetic into Zuo Qiuming's vein before he opened the belly with a single stroke.

Two days later Zuo Qiuming came, miraculously, back to life — and then chose to end it himself. Deep in the night of the third day, when the doctors and nurses on their rounds had all gone, he pulled off the oxygen mask, drew out the drip tube in his vein, worked both hands under the quilt, and tore open by force the two wounds on his body. Then, eyes open, he lay quietly in the dark and let his blood drain away, drop by drop, through the searing pain.

They sat in the hall of a teahouse, and Suzuki Masao told Lin Nansheng the whole of it. When he had finished, Suzuki Masao rose, made a deep and solemn bow to Lin Nansheng, then sat and said, Pang-san, I have failed your friend.

Lin Nansheng said nothing, and only when he had slowly drunk the scalding tea in his cup to the last did he set the cup down and say, You are mistaken; he was not my friend.

I saw him come to visit you, and more than once.

Talk like that could get me arrested.

I am your doctor, and your friend as well. Suzuki Masao said in earnest, I asked you out only to tell you this: a brave man deserves an honorable burial, not to lie in a mortuary.

Lin Nansheng said calmly, Suzuki, to pity your enemy is to betray your Empire.

I have no enemies. Suzuki Masao looked up at Lin Nansheng and said, As a doctor, I have only patients.

Not a trace of expression could be read on Lin Nansheng's face; he only went on drinking tea and refilling the cup, and after he left the teahouse he went back to the office, to his coming and going as before. He took this meeting with Suzuki Masao for some kind of Japanese probe — until, a few days later, he saw in the newspaper the notice calling for someone to claim an unnamed man's body. Looking at the likeness of Zuo Qiuming in the photograph, Lin Nansheng was seized all at once with a rising and falling of the heart.

That night he sought out the Juntong's highest officer in Hong Kong. When he had said his piece, the officer pulled open a drawer, took out a sheaf of files, and said, You are the intelligence analyst; you judge.

The files held many photographs, all of the places Zuo Qiuming had been and the people he had seen. On one of them Lin Nansheng saw Ji Zhongyuan's profile, and understood at once; but he said all the same, What does this prove?

That is why we must still sift it through; this is the last chance.

He is a dead man already.

But we must know whose martyr he is. The officer heaved a long sigh, stood, went to a globe, gave it a hard spin, and said, If I have not judged wrong, someone will come to claim his body.

Lin Nansheng said not another word. Leaving the officer's office, he went to the bars on Queen's Road and drank there without stopping, and without stopping traded jokes with the bar girls; then, half a bottle of liquor in hand, he came drunk to the mouth of the lane where he had met Ji Zhongyuan, and, like a man with no home to go to, lay drunk there five deep nights running.

Deep in the night of the sixth day a pedicab, having circled once, stopped before him. Ji Zhongyuan, got up as a pedicab driver, helped him aboard, and Lin Nansheng let out a long breath and said, So you have shown yourself at last.

Pedaling hard, Ji Zhongyuan said, And if I had not shown myself?

Lin Nansheng asked in return: Would you have given it up?

As the sky was about to lighten, Ji Zhongyuan took him across the water to a burial ground on Lantau Island. Standing before a new grave with no headstone, he said, I know the two of you were friends of many years.

When did he begin working for you?

He worked for no one. Ji Zhongyuan said, He was only doing the duty of a Chinese agent.

Lin Nansheng stood a while with his head bowed, then went back to the boat, and all the way said not a word, sitting alone and silent at the bow, into the rising morning sun and the sea wind. Only after he had gone ashore did he turn to Ji Zhongyuan and say, Putting together the messages that pass back and forth, the Japanese army will soon move troops against Guangzhouwan.

And the movements of the French forces?

The British could not even hold Hong Kong; can the French? With that Lin Nansheng turned to go. But before he had gone many steps he came back, looked at Ji Zhongyuan, hesitated, and said, I hope your people in Shanghai can help find out what became of her...

Rest easy. Without expression Ji Zhongyuan cut him off and said, She is our comrade, and my wife as well.

To receive the intelligence Lin Nansheng sent over, Ji Zhongyuan opened a special line for the purpose and took delivery of it himself. But such days did not last long; all at once a transfer order came, and Lin Nansheng was recalled to Chongqing to take up a post as a technical instructor at the Sino-American Cooperative Organization.

Before he left, Lin Nansheng said goodbye to no one, nor did he make contact with Ji Zhongyuan; alone and forlorn he came to the pier and followed the other passengers aboard the liner. But in the instant he stepped into the cabin, the very first thing he saw was Ji Zhongyuan. Dressed in the white uniform of a steward, a tray of hot towels in his hands, Ji came smiling up to him and said, Sir, wipe your face.

Lin Nansheng said coldly, Do you mean to see me off to Chongqing?

Still all smiles, Ji Zhongyuan handed him the towel, and with it a slip of paper, and said, Any time you need to reach us, run this in the Central Daily News.

On the slip was a seven-character regulated poem, 'Ode to the Plum Blossom,' signed: Huang Shanyun.

Leaning on the ship's rail, Lin Nansheng read the poem over once in silence, then tore it to shreds and threw it into the sea. He closed his eyes, and heard the blast of the steam whistle.
