## Chapter Seven

It was three days later. From early morning there had not been a breath of wind. The sky was packed with grey masses of cloud that hung there dull and motionless. Now and then the pale-yellow sunlight showed its face for an instant, then hurriedly hid itself away again. Dragonflies danced in swarms about the treetops, and now and again flung themselves against the green wire-gauze window; that would startle the flies crawling there, and with a buzz they would all rise up, wheel about before the window for a while as though they had nowhere to go, and then settle again on the gauze, thrusting out their two hind legs and slowly rubbing them together, as if their minds were heavy with care.

Inside the wire-gauze window was the small, richly appointed drawing-room of the Wu mansion. Wu Sunfu was pacing there to and fro, quite alone. The look on his face was much like the sky outside the window. He took a few steps, then suddenly stood still, threw a glance at the big clock in the room, and muttered to himself:

"Eleven o'clock! Why doesn't the telephone ring?"

He was anxiously awaiting a telephone call from Zhao Botao and Du Zhuzhai. Their speculation in government bonds was to be decided, won or lost, this very day! Since the day before yesterday the market had been full of news that the Central Army had turned the tables along the Longhai line. Yet people were still holding back and watching; only a few scattered small holders were buying, and no rising wind got up. Yesterday the newspapers had made much, in the boldest type, of the Central Army's victory, and the moment the morning session opened on the exchange every kind of bond had gone up two or three dollars; the heads were packed on the floor layer upon layer, and the shouting was like a charge at the front, so that not a word could be made out, and one saw only that every palm thrust up on the floor was turned upward. But Zhao Botao and his people had put out no more than two million before the price fell back again, and in the end it stood only about half a dollar above the day before yesterday's. This, they said, was because the big bears still meant to fight it out, and would wait until today, to see how the wind blew, before covering. The victory or defeat of Wu Sunfu and his partners would therefore be told within these twelve hours. Tomorrow was settlement day!

Wu Sunfu knitted his brows, looked out at the murky sky, and then, with a faint smile that seemed to say "let it go as it will," paced out of the small drawing-room and went into his study to telephone Tu Weiyue at the mill. On this front Wu Sunfu's victory was the surer bet; but today too was the last five minutes that would decide the match. This very forenoon Tu Weiyue and Mo Gancheng were to settle, once and for all, the half-hearted go-slow that had already dragged on for the better part of a week.

He had just taken the receiver in his hand when the study door opened, and an oblong face with a little tuft of moustache below the chin poked in through the crack, as though asking leave to enter. Wu Sunfu hung up the receiver and called out:

"Xiaosheng, come in! Have you any solid news?"

Fei the little-moustache, however, made no answer; he sidled in, softly shut the door behind him, tiptoed up to Wu Sunfu, and, with his two eyes fixed on the ground, said slowly and in a low voice:

"There is. And it's bad! The bandits have pulled back, but they're massed in the country round about, and none of the shops have opened for business. The troops the province sent are still garrisoned in the county town; they don't dare march down to the market town—"

"Never mind troops or bandits! How much have we lost, in the end? Out with it!"

Wu Sunfu shouted impatiently. A wave of vexation rose in him, and the room seemed to him gloomy and wretched; he reached out and snapped on the big electric lamp with its pale-yellow silk shade that stood on the writing-desk. A wash of yellow light fell on Wu Sunfu's face and showed it purple shot with a livid green.

Above his ferocious eyes the two thick brows were twitching and quivering.

"The losses, now—they're not yet clear. But what one can see is no small matter already: the Hongchang pawnshop, the Tongyuan native bank, the oil-press, the power-plant—"

"Pah! Plundered, the lot of them, is that it?—I hardly need you to tell me that! What I want is a detailed reckoning of the losses, not a lump figure! Do you mean to say that you went back to the town for three days and have brought me nothing but these few words? Three days! And still nothing clear?"

The more he said, the angrier he grew, and he brought his hand down with a slap on the desk. It was not, in truth, that the size of the loss angered him—no; a loss of a hundred or two hundred thousand in silver he could meet with no more than a slight knitting of the brows before setting it calmly aside, such was his largeness of manner. What angered him now was rather that Fei the little-moustache went about his business so slowly, so unpractically. And besides, Wu Sunfu was in a hurry to know just how much of his native place was left over after the sacking, so that he might lay his whole plan to meet the money-pressure that grew tighter as the old-calendar Dragon-Boat Festival drew on.

Seeing that Fei the little-moustache held his tongue, Wu Sunfu went on to ask:

"The money we've laid out—how much of it, do you reckon, can still be got back?"

"Why—six-tenths, at the least. The market town itself wasn't so very badly knocked about. Only the rice-shops and the cloth-shops were cleaned right out. The other trades didn't lose much. Of the accounts we've put out, a good six-tenths can be recovered. And what's more, the county town came to no harm……"

"Why didn't you say so at once!"

Wu Sunfu again cut Fei the little-moustache short, but his tone was far milder now, and a shadow of a smile flitted across his face. Of his three problems—the go-slow at the mill, the battle on the exchange, and the upheaval in his native place—one at least had now taken shape: six-tenths of it survived. That was to say, he still had sixty or seventy thousand in ready cash to dispose of; and though the sum was a trifle, yet, like a great general moving up his troops for the attack, once he knew his own strength he could find a way to draw up his order of battle.

"One of the motors at the power-plant is wrecked—"

Fei the little-moustache went on, slowly as before, his eyes still on the ground. But before he had finished, a flash of lightning suddenly swept past the window, followed by a rolling crash of thunder that seemed to shake the very walls of the study. A downpour like a galloping horse came hard on its heels. Fei the little-moustache's too-low voice was swallowed up altogether in these roarings of nature. And at that very moment a man burst into the study, his goat-face beaded all over with fine drops of sweat: it was Du Zhuzhai.

"What a monstrous clap of thunder! No wonder the telephone's gone dead! Sunfu, your telephone's out of order, is it?"

Du Zhuzhai spoke as he came, and sat down in the sofa opposite Sunfu; then he took out a large handkerchief, laid it over his face, and rubbed hard. This was a posture habitual with him whenever he met with some knotty affair, and its purpose was not merely to wipe away the sweat.

Wu Sunfu threw Du Zhuzhai a glance and understood that things on the exchange were not going smoothly; he gave a faint smile, and grew, if anything, the calmer within. Defeat or victory would be told within a minute or two, and a man of his great spirit was, as a rule, all the steadier for that. He turned back to Fei the little-moustache, waved a hand at him, and gave his orders:

"Xiaosheng, you must go straight back to the town, gather in every last dollar of the ready cash—whatever there is, that's what there is—and bring it here at once! One of the motors at the power-plant is wrecked? I'll send a man to look at it tomorrow; it can surely be mended.—This very night you must get to Shuangqiao! Go and hire a naphtha-launch, for yourself alone, and it must cast off before one o'clock! There now, be off!"

"Yes—"

answered Fei the little-moustache, his face woebegone. It was not yet an hour since he had left the steamer, and he had not had time so much as to sit down and stretch himself; and now he was to go straight aboard some naphtha-launch again and be tossed about—and back, at that, to a town ringed round and watched by armed peasants. He was truly none too willing; but Wu Sunfu's temper was hasty like that, and would brook no accommodation, so Fei the little-moustache could only swallow his grievance whole and, with a shake of his clothes, take himself off. And now Wu Sunfu and Du Zhuzhai fell to talking over how matters had gone on the exchange.

Lightning flashed, thunder pealed, the rain roared, filling all the space between, so that speech could scarcely be heard. Wu Sunfu made out the general drift only from the movements of Du Zhuzhai's lips. In a moment when Du Zhuzhai's lips came briefly to rest, Wu Sunfu suddenly cried out in a loud voice, with a cold laugh:

"Are there still new bears throwing themselves in? The devil is in them!"

"That is just what makes it so strange! I've never seen a market gone so mad! We must wait for the afternoon round!"

"How much have we still in hand?"

"Four or five million! The moment we let it out, the rising wind will turn straight into a slump! And if we don't put it out—what's to be done?"

"Put the whole lot out, then! We shan't be out of pocket anyway!"

"Shan't we, though! Have you forgotten we've already paid out three hundred thousand?"

"Of course I remember. Not quite eighty thousand in silver apiece—we'll count it our contribution to the army's pay and be done!"

said Wu Sunfu coldly, rising and taking a few turns about the study. By now the thunder had ceased, but the rain came down the harder, and the wind had got up too; the sound of wind driving rain, and with it the raging of all the trees in the garden. Du Zhuzhai sat silent, and it was as if he were back once more in the seething uproar of the exchange floor: hundreds upon hundreds of tense, sweating faces swam before his eyes, in air so foul it made the head reel and the eyes redden. And all of it was the doing of himself, Zhao Botao and two others—four men working their mischief behind the scenes, who had now brought it about that they were setting a fire to burn their own bodies too; it looked like no small matter! Du Zhuzhai shook his head and, with a sudden sigh, said:

"I truly don't understand it—so many of the big bears fighting to the death and refusing to cover! Tomorrow is settlement, and this very forenoon there were still new bears throwing themselves in!"

"New bears throwing themselves in, indeed—might it not be some conjuring-trick of Zhao Botao's?"

Wu Sunfu had turned round suddenly and, fixing his eyes on Du Zhuzhai, said this, at the same time bringing his right hand down with a slap on the table. Du Zhuzhai started up in a fluster, and his face changed too; he had truly been dazed by the clamour and the reek of sweat on the exchange, and had never once guessed in that direction.

In anger and alarm at once, he said:

"Oh! Oh! That—that may well be it! Why, it would be monstrous!"

"We've been taken in! Ha-ha!"

Wu Sunfu laughed savagely at the sky and cried out in a loud voice. Just then another thunderbolt came down like a heavy lid clapped over everything, and every human voice was drowned. Du Zhuzhai took out a cigar and lit it, drew hard at it a few times, and said slowly:

"If it really is that sort of business, old Zhao is too little of a friend, and we'll not let him off lightly! But, Sunfu, let us wait for the afternoon round; how it stands in the end can only be known in that afternoon round, and there is no telling for certain yet."

"We can only hope so!"

"It is not just hoping—there are a few grounds for confidence yet! I'll go and find old Shang. When I've had my midday meal I'll go on to the exchange and watch the market!"

Du Zhuzhai rose as he spoke and left; Wu Sunfu followed, and quitted the study too. But when they had reached the steps before the great drawing-room and Du Zhuzhai was on the point of getting into his motor-car, he suddenly turned back, drew Wu Sunfu into the small drawing-room, and asked gravely:

"And what did Fei the little-moustache have to report when he came? How great are the losses?"

"Of the details, not one thing is yet clear."

"Didn't you just now tell him to go straight back to the town?"

"I told him to go back and collect what's left and move it all to Shanghai. What I mean to do now is to concentrate my strength, and make that trust company my base of operations for a great stroke!"

Wu Sunfu answered with a smile, and the sombre look was swept clean from his face once more. Du Zhuzhai pondered a good while, then asked again:

"And in Zhu Yinqiu's affair, then, you are set on pressing forward? Have you reckoned that there's no risk?"

Wu Sunfu did not answer, but only threw Du Zhuzhai a glance.

"As for running factories and the like, I'm no hand at it; but as things look, the prospects of industry can hardly be viewed with any hope. And besides, Zhu Yinqiu is no fool either. His machinery and mill-buildings and the rest are worth more than five hundred thousand now; can he fail to see that we mean to take them over for three hundred thousand—how should he consent to it? The man is a slippery, obstinate customer, and to dig anything out of his hands will be trouble enough, I fear. These last few days he's been putting it about everywhere that we're out to cheat him; and just now a word let slip by Zhao Botao showed that Zhu Yinqiu is in treaty with old Zhao too, hoping to pledge his machinery for a hundred-odd thousand and pay off the cocoon-loan he owes us here, which falls due a month from now—"

Here Du Zhuzhai paused a moment, flicked the ash from the cigar in his hand, and turned his face to look out of the window. Rain-threads thick as chopsticks hung densely all across the window, yet the sky seemed to have brightened a little. As Du Zhuzhai turned his eyes back, he saw a savage grin come glowering over Wu Sunfu's face, and Wu asked abruptly:

"Has old Zhao agreed to it?"

"He's probably still considering it. For the moment old Zhao, since he's busy floating the company with us, is very civil on the surface; he gave me to understand that if the loan Zhu Yinqiu is arranging on his side were to injure our own claim, then he would refuse—"

"Zhuzhai! Be sure to tell old Zhao to refuse!"

"That is the very thing I want to talk over with you. It seems to me that, with the silk trade in such poor shape just now, we'd do better to hold back for the present. If Zhu Yinqiu can raise something from old Zhao and clear off the hundred and fifty thousand he owes us on mortgage, then let us leave it at that."

"That won't do! Zhuzhai! We can't be so faint-hearted!"

cried Wu Sunfu, springing up abruptly. Just then a shaft of sunlight shot suddenly through a rift in the clouds, passed through those dense curtains of rain, and fell straight into the small drawing-room, staining Wu Sunfu's face an ochre yellow. The rain still came steaming down, and in a ringing voice that overrode the sound of it Wu Sunfu went on:

"We've spent the strength of nine bulls and two tigers to squeeze Zhu Yinqiu's cocoons out of him; now that the cocoons are as good as in our grasp, how can we give them up again? Zhuzhai, we must on no account be faint-hearted! Tell old Zhao to refuse! When it comes to lending to Zhu Yinqiu, our trust company has the prior claim—that is written plain in the contract for the hundred-and-fifty-thousand dried-cocoon loan. Zhuzhai, it was for this one clause that we made so great a concession on the interest, and asked no more than five and a half per mille a month. Zhuzhai, tell old Zhao that he ought to respect our claim!"

Du Zhuzhai gazed at Wu Sunfu's face for a while, then drew the cigar from the corner of his mouth and said, letting out a breath:

"We'll have to take one step and then see. The pressing thing at the moment is the exchange, so I'll go and find old Shang."

The rain had lessened, but it had turned into something like a thick fog, and the sky grew greyer still. In Wu Sunfu's heart, too, there seemed to hang a lump of lead. The bond market changed from instant to instant, so that hope was not altogether cut off; yet, judging by yesterday and this morning, there was much of a tendency to "slaughter the bulls," and that was passing strange. Of such a thing there was but one explanation: word had already leaked out! Wu Sunfu, who at bottom put little trust in Zhao Botao, could not, do what he would, help suspecting that Zhao Botao had some devil's trick at work within it too. "To go and try one's luck on the bond market is not, in itself, necessarily dangerous; but to have dealings with old Zhao—there the danger runs very high!" So Wu Sunfu thought, without ceasing, as he paced to and fro with his hands behind his back.

The clock already stood at half past eleven, and the telephone call reporting Tu Weiyue's victory, looked for as it was, had not come. Wu Sunfu had to set Zhao Botao and the bonds aside and rouse himself to deal with the mill. He bade Gaosheng put through a call. But his telephone really was out of order and would not connect. In a burst of anger Wu Sunfu got into his motor-car and went to inspect the mill in person.

The fine rain, turned to thick fog, wrapped everything more than fifty feet off in a blurred and swimming husk. Here and there a cloud-topping tall building showed, through the mist, only its topmost few storeys, and from the rows of window-holes, like giant eyes, a wan yellow lamplight glimmered and flickered—seen from afar, they were like a mirage floating in mid-air, without a whit of majesty. And this thick fog was boundless and shoreless; the motor-car pushed forward, breaking through the smothering damp, and the glass of its windows turned to frosted glass, so that even figures close at hand became blurred and grotesque; everything had lost its clean outline, everything was caught up in a blurred distortion.

Wu Sunfu leaned back into the right-hand corner of the carriage, stretched his left leg up to rest aslant on the cushion, glanced from time to time out of the window, and breathed hard. A thought such as he had never had before rose all at once into his heart: in the world of enterprise he was a fierce champion, forever thrusting forward; yet was not what lay before him a wild, unreal mirage half-floating in the air? Were not those about him figures of blurred and distorted outline? Even as he now sat in this motor-car and charged forward through the blinding fog!

And so a thread of cold spread out from his spine, until his face went pale, until the brave, keen glitter had faded from his eyes.

The motor-car drove into the mill, passing along the side of the reeling-room. The wan yellow electric light was thrown back from the many window-holes of the reeling-room, and the sound of the reeling-machines turning mingled into one soft, sliding hum that filled the damp air. In ordinary times, how instantly all this would have quickened Wu Sunfu's spirits, and how readily his practised eye and ear would have told, from that light and that hum, whether the work was going briskly or slackly. But now, though he still saw it and still heard it, a film of fog clung to his brain, and a lump of lead hung at his heart.

Not until Old Guan the bodyguard had opened the car door, and Mo Gancheng and Tu Weiyue stood together before the car to receive him, did Wu Sunfu slowly step down. His ashen, ferocious face set Mo Gancheng's heart pounding. Wu Sunfu threw Mo Gancheng a cold glance, looked too at Tu Weiyue, and went straight on into the manager's office.

The first to be called in and questioned was Tu Weiyue. The young man's face was all coolness, and, without waiting for Wu Sunfu to open his mouth, he spoke first:

"The telephone at the Third Master's residence was out of order; it was put through only ten minutes ago, and by then the Third Master had already left. A pity the line was mended a touch too late."

Wu Sunfu knitted his brows a little, then deliberately smiled again. He had caught the meaning behind Tu Weiyue's words: that in coming here he was meddling where he need not. This proud, self-confident young man plainly held that for Wu Sunfu not to have waited at home for the tidings of victory (which had been agreed on beforehand), but to come rushing off to the mill instead, betrayed a want of absolute trust in the men who worked under him—which ran counter to the principle of "employ no man you doubt, doubt no man you employ," and was not the bearing of one who does great things. Wu Sunfu watched Tu Weiyue's face, inwardly approving the young man's stubbornness and shrewdness; yet aloud he would not admit that it was unease that had brought him here. He gave another faint smile, and said, very calmly:

"Isn't it nearly twelve o'clock now? I take it my commander-in-chief at the front has by this time won all along the line. I've come by surprise, to make a speech to the prisoners."

"That is still a little too early."

Tu Weiyue answered with the utmost decision, his face still uncannily cool.

"What! Do you mean the noise I heard just now from the workshop is not the machines truly running, but is the same as these last few days?"

"If the Third Master will go and take a look, he will know."

said Tu Weiyue, slowing his voice; yet his manner was wholly frank and open, and at the same time wholly composed.

Wu Sunfu gave a snort through his nose. His gaze, fixed on Tu Weiyue's face, grew ever sterner, like two swords. But Tu Weiyue drew himself up straight, still smiling, and unexpectedly put a counter-question:

"I must ask the Third Master's instructions: do we still hold fast to the aim of a 'peaceful settlement'?"

"Of course we still mean a 'peaceful settlement.' But my patience, too, has its limits!"

"Just so!—The limit is today, as the Third Master said the day before yesterday. But the women workers are living people too; they have minds, they have feelings, and, worse still, they have rather complicated minds and feelings like raging fire. Three days ago, for instance, they still put great faith in one of their own, Yao Jinfeng of No. 12 reeling frame; but from early this morning their tune has changed, and they curse Yao Jinfeng for a running dog who has sold out the workers' interests, so that things have turned bad in a moment. The Third Master will likely still remember this Yao Jinfeng—a lean, tall slip of a woman with a small round face, a few fine white pock-marks, and past thirty; she has been three years and six months in the mill, and it was she who opened fire in this go-slow—"

"I remember the woman. And I remember that you used a little stratagem to soften her."

"And so today she has earned a new title: running dog! Once she is a running dog known to all, she is of no use whatever! Our pains of these last few days are wasted."

Wu Sunfu gave a snort through his nose, and said nothing.

"Our business was managed very secretly—only three or four people knew of it; and on the face of things Yao Jinfeng still spoke up for the women workers. I dare say the women never dreamed that their leader had already been bought over by the Third Master. So it is plain as day that someone inside our own ranks has been making trouble!"

"Ha! Such a thing! Why haven't you looked into it?"

"I've already found out that it was Xue Baozhu, the No. 9 overseer, who let the secret out and wrecked our scheme!"

"What? The No. 9 overseer? She wanted to curry favour with the workers—has she taken leave of her senses?"

"It is wholly out of jealousy—the two of them are old enemies. Xue Baozhu is envious that Yao Jinfeng got the credit!"

"Go and bring the two of them in to see me!"

Wu Sunfu sprang to his feet and gave the order in a voice and countenance both stern; but Tu Weiyue sat where he was and did not stir. He knew Wu Sunfu would come to himself in a moment and take back this thoughtless order; he waited for the Third Master's fit of anger to pass before he spoke again. Wu Sunfu looked keenly at Tu Weiyue for a good while, then, his face gradually clearing, sat down once more, and, grinding his teeth, said to himself:

"The scoundrel! More hateful than the women who make the trouble! Doesn't she want to eat my rice any longer?—Ai, Weiyue, tell Mo Gancheng to lay the Xue woman off!"

"Has the Third Master, do you think, a better plan still?"

"What is your view? Out with it!"

Wu Sunfu's tone had turned stern again, as though his patience had truly reached its limit.

"Let the Third Master post a notice: a day's merit-bonus for the workers at the Dragon-Boat Festival, Yao Jinfeng dismissed, and Xue Baozhu promoted to inspector."

Tu Weiyue drew himself up straight and brought it out almost word by word. Wu Sunfu waited for him to finish, then, narrowing his eyes savagely and gazing into the air, mused a while, and suddenly laughed and said:

"Is this your stratagem of the double agent? Are you sure of it?"

"I am sure of it. From eight o'clock this morning I've been using every kind of method to retrieve the deadlock that Xue Baozhu made. It has begun to take shape. A day's merit-bonus at the Dragon-Boat Festival the Third Master long ago allowed; what I must still ask the Third Master to permit are just these two things—Yao Jinfeng's dismissal and Xue Baozhu's promotion to inspector—and later the order can still be revoked, counting as a concession to the workers, and so the go-slow disturbance will be settled at a stroke. We've managed, with much trouble, to plant a root among the women workers, and we surely can't throw it away lightly."

Just then a steam-whistle suddenly shrilled, hoo—hoo, ringing through the whole mill, and Wu Sunfu started violently, his face changing a little. When the workers rose in revolt in the mill, they too would often sound the whistle for a signal—was it not so? But he thought at once that this was the midday break letting the shift off, no accident at all, and he took the chance to smile, by way of tacitly assenting to Tu Weiyue's plan.

"This afternoon the labour trouble can be brought to an end. As for how the several men who did capable service should be rewarded, let the Third Master give his orders."

Tu Weiyue went on, taking out a sheet of paper and laying it before Wu Sunfu. Wu Sunfu gave it a careless glance, then knitted his brows and asked:

"Qian Baosheng and Gui Changlin are union men—are they to be specially rewarded too?"

"Yes. The backgrounds of the two of them differ, so they belong to two factions. But this time they've been able to act as one in serving the Third Master—"

"As one, indeed? In coming to me for money they're as one; when it's a scramble for the union they're not as one; and caught in the go-slow disturbance, when each wanted to use the workers to topple the other, they weren't as one either. To tell the truth, that this go-slow has dragged on to nearly a week is in small part owing to those two dog-heads not being as one—not as one in serving me, not as one in dealing with the workers!"

"But these last two or three days they have been as one. Qian Baosheng in particular, on hearing my mediation, gave way to Gui Changlin."

"That is no serving me in earnest either—it is the selfishness of a man who trims his sail to the wind. Money of mine I'll not give to such people!"

Wu Sunfu retorted with decision, snatched up a pen and struck the names of Qian Baosheng and Gui Changlin off, wrote at the foot of the sheet the single word "Seen," and handed it back to Tu Weiyue. He rose, looked out at the women workers coming and going beyond the window, and suddenly, remembering something, let a shadow spread once more over his face; but he recovered himself at once, and, giving Tu Weiyue his orders as he went, walked out of the office:

"By tomorrow this thing must be settled without fail! My patience ends today!"

These two sentences, again stern in voice and face, threw into a fright all the clerks gathered outside the office door. By the time they had chewed over the weight of the two sentences, the motor-car carrying Wu Sunfu had already chugged out of the mill gate. A few women workers standing by the gate watched the imposing car and gave a scornful laugh.

Tu Weiyue at once called together some four or five of the more important staff, Mo Gancheng and those under him, to talk over what should be done. Among them was Gui Changlin. The labour trouble was to be settled by tomorrow, and Wu Sunfu's forbearance had come to its last step: such news had already spread through the whole mill. The inspectors and the overseers all took it as a sign that Wu Sunfu meant to use strong measures, and their spirits ran especially high. They all knew that if the "Third Master's" policy turned from "peaceable" to "strong," that would mean the shrinking or the end of Tu Weiyue's "regime." Though they dared not openly oppose Tu Weiyue's "regime," they were never quite easy in their hearts about it.

Tu Weiyue, who understood all this perfectly well, therefore first reported Wu Sunfu's dissatisfaction with Qian Baosheng and Gui Changlin, and then came to the main point:

"The Third Master has now given three orders: a day's merit-bonus for the workers at the Dragon-Boat Festival, Yao Jinfeng dismissed, and Xue Baozhu promoted to inspector."

Everyone opened their eyes wide in astonishment. Gui Changlin said indignantly:

"Isn't this beating a dog that is already in the water? The Third Master isn't being fair. What merit has Xue Baozhu, that she should be promoted?"

"Yao Jinfeng is truly wronged! But Mr. Tu, you ought to put in a good word for Yao Jinfeng before the Third Master. Have you done right by her? You were the one who told me to go and win her over. And now she ends with a dismissal, while Xue Baozhu, who made all the mischief, gets a promotion and a reward instead—how is one to give out such a tale!"

Wang Jinzhen, the No. 2 overseer, came too to cry out against the injustice; she was wholly kept by the Third Master, and, not daring to oppose the Third Master, could only grumble at Tu Weiyue.

But Tu Weiyue did not answer; he drew himself up straight and smiled very calmly.

"The Third Master rails at me for setting myself against Qian Baosheng—true enough, Qian Baosheng is my mortal enemy. The union's rice is for everyone to eat; Qian Baosheng wants to swallow it all alone, and I'm bound to oppose that! Since the Third Master doesn't concern himself with the bulls-and-horses goring one another inside the union, and only wants the labour trouble settled soon, then why must he promote and reward Xue Baozhu? That Xue Baozhu made the disturbance at Qian Baosheng's prompting, out of jealousy and to spite me—who doesn't know it!"

Gui Changlin got out this long speech with froth all round his lips and his eyes gone red. Yet he was, after all, being polite. For among the people present he alone was of the union side—ate the union's rice—while all the rest ate Wu Sunfu's rice, and so of course dared not, before Tu Weiyue, criticize Wu Sunfu for handling it wrongly.

Tu Weiyue still smiled his cold, still smile, and said nothing at all. It was Mo Gancheng who now spoke up:

"What the Third Master wants done, we can only do. But, Mr. Tu, the labour trouble is to be settled this very day—how is it to be managed?"

"That is the real business we have to talk over!"

Tu Weiyue had spoken; his alert eyes rested on pockmarked Li the inspector and another woman overseer. These two were watching Tu Weiyue in their turn, a shadow of a smile playing about their lips. They counted as intimates newly taken into Tu Weiyue's confidence since he had come to "power." Tu Weiyue drew himself up, let his gaze sweep over the faces of them all, and said in a loud voice:

"The matter of Yao Jinfeng and Xue Baozhu we'll speak of later. The Third Master has always been fair. For any man who serves the Third Master with a true heart, I can stand surety that he'll come to no loss. The Third Master has said this thing must be settled today. A day's merit-bonus at the Dragon-Boat Festival the Third Master has already granted. What's to be feared is that the hot-heads among the workers—He Xiumei and her lot—will still make trouble. We've no choice but to deal with them without ceremony! Old Li, this task I leave to you. You need only give them a fright, and that will do.—"

"Just leave it to me!"

pockmarked Li the inspector broke in, his two thick brows jerking up. He was a Hongmen brother, and could at any time muster ten or so of his sworn brethren to come out and brawl.

"A fright, and that will do? You make it sound too easy! He Xiumei and that pack of bad eggs are not to be frightened off!"

Wang Jinzhen, the No. 2 overseer, put in a grudging protest.

pockmarked Li, much put out, opened his eyes round and was about to speak, but Tu Weiyue cut him short:

"What Wang Jinzhen says has reason in it too. Old Li, you just watch your chance to seize hold of He Xiumei and hustle her off to the theatre! She's gone out to her midday meal just now; go and see to this at once, and mind you do it cleanly. You haven't eaten yet—take ten dollars from the counting-house first; and when the thing's done, treat your brethren to a meal out.—This matter must be kept secret!"

"Kept secret? Those two rascals Qian Baosheng and Xue Baozhu are not to be trusted anyhow—and, after all, there's something to be gained by not keeping it secret!"

said Gui Changlin, curling his lips and muttering.

pockmarked Li took the money from Mo Gancheng's hand and went off in high spirits. Tu Weiyue fixed his eyes on Gui Changlin for a moment, but said nothing; then he turned his head and asked the woman overseer of No. 10:

"A Zhen, how did the business you handled turn out in the end?"

"Half the workers believe Yao Jinfeng has been wronged. They curse Xue Baozhu for spreading lies, saying she was the capitalists' running dog all along and worked a wicked trick. And they say that He Xiumei and her lot want to make a show of themselves, and are jealous of Yao Jinfeng."

"Well done! He Xiumei won't be coming to the mill this afternoon; so you put the word about that He Xiumei has been asked by Mr. Mo to go and see a play—"

"Hey, hey, how do I come into it? My good fellow, don't play your tricks on me!"

Mo Gancheng cut in hurriedly. Gui Changlin, Wang Jinzhen, and even A Zhen all burst out laughing. But Tu Weiyue did not laugh; he patted Mo Gancheng on the shoulder and said very earnestly:

"Of course it is you who invite her to the theatre. You must go out now and find pockmarked Li. He'll surely be somewhere near where He Xiumei lives. Settle it with him: just wait for that gang of loafers to hustle He Xiumei off to some quiet spot, and then you go and rescue her. After that, you invite her to see the play."

"And if she won't go?"

"Then you'll have to put in a little effort. You just say she can hide a while in the theatre until those loafers have scattered. You're an old fellow—she'll have no suspicion, and will surely be willing to go."

"If it gets out and reaches the Third Master's ears, it'll be no joke!"

"I'll answer for it before the Third Master! Off you go! A Zhen, you go and see to your part; and don't give the game away!"

Now there were left in the room only three men—Tu Weiyue, Gui Changlin, and Wang Jinzhen. Tu Weiyue smiled coldly, his alert eyes fixed on Gui Changlin's face. It was an oblong face, close on forty, with something of the rogue about it, and a pair of small, ill-matched eyes. Under the piercing keenness of Tu Weiyue's gaze, an uneasy, sheepish look came slowly over that oblong face.

Suddenly Tu Weiyue laughed, and asked coldly:

"Changlin, do you really mean to be Qian Baosheng's mortal enemy?"

There was no answer; Gui Changlin gave his body a shake, set his two hands akimbo, and threw Tu Weiyue a fierce, savage glance.

"Think it over for yourself—how much weaker is your strength than Qian Baosheng's?"

"Hmph! Strength, damn it! It's only a dog trading on its master's rank!"

"Just so! And on that one point you come off worse. Your Mr. Wang, too, is far away in Hong Kong."

Gui Changlin's face changed at once, and the fierce light in his eyes turned to a look of wavering doubt and fear.

"Set your mind at ease! Here there is only Wang Jinzhen, who has always been on good terms with you. And I'll tell you more: Boss Wu, too, has dealings with Mr. Wang's friends. Reckoned up, you might call them men on the same road; so in the mill you ought surely to help Boss Wu with all your might—is that not so?"

"If Boss Wu understands it all, then why dismiss Yao Jinfeng and promote and reward Xue Baozhu? And besides, in this labour trouble have I really not exerted myself for the Third Master? I've half a mind to ask the Third Master to his face."

"In this matter the Third Master has really been unfair. Mr. Tu, do go and speak to the Third Master about it—the notice hasn't gone out yet, in any case."

Wang Jinzhen put in. She fancied this speech very neatly turned, siding with Gui Changlin on the one hand while on the other deferring to Tu Weiyue. But, to her surprise, Tu Weiyue's face suddenly darkened, and he gave a very stern rebuff:

"No more of your 'the Third Master this, the Third Master that'! Does the Third Master trouble himself with such trifles? It's all I, Tu, who lay down the terms! I say Yao Jinfeng is to be dismissed and Xue Baozhu ought to rise; the Third Master gave a nod, and there's an end of it!"

"Then you're much in the wrong!"

Gui Changlin sprang up shouting, and thrust out his fist too. Wang Jinzhen hastily plucked at the hem of his coat. But Tu Weiyue only threw back his face and laughed aloud, as if he had not seen the fist, big as a bowl's mouth, waving before his face. The fist drew itself back of its own accord about half a foot from Tu Weiyue's face, and then came a spiteful snort. Tu Weiyue stopped laughing too; his face was cool as before, without a trace of expression, and, as though spitting something out, he said:

"Pah, you ruffian! As simple as that! Can't you consider what the workers will do when they hear Xue Baozhu has been promoted and rewarded? They too will feel it an injustice, and the crowd will turn right round and rally to Yao Jinfeng.—"

"But Yao Jinfeng has already been dismissed—what use rallying to her now!"

"Changlin! Can't you speak a little slower? Didn't I say long ago that the Third Master always means to do people justice in the end?—What you should do now is go and get busy; jabbering away in front of me is no use at all. As for Qian Baosheng's mouth, we mean to give it one public slap! You must trust that I am helping you along!—Understood? Off you go!"

When Tu Weiyue had finished, he took up a sheet of paper and wrote out the notice he had settled on.

Just then the steam-whistle shrilled again through the whole mill. The women workers came filing back in one after another. The voices in the workshop surged up like a tide; but this time the tide, all unawares, had entered upon the very road that Tu Weiyue had laid out.

Leaving the mill, Wu Sunfu went on to the Bankers' Guild. Sundays excepted, he came here every day for his midday meal, and to touch base with his friends into the bargain. What with the pleasant give-and-take of talk and laughter, this luncheon of his would, as a rule, take up something like an hour. Today, as he entered that splendid dining-room, he felt oppressed the moment he came in. Today was unlike the usual: no familiar smiles and greetings broke out to announce his arrival. There were, indeed, seven or eight people in the dining-room, but all of them strange faces. Several, amid the clink of knife and fork, were talking of the weather, of the war, even of the greyhound track and the dancing-girls, showing that they had nothing serious to say and could only pass the mealtime hour in this loose, rambling fashion. By the window three men sat together at one table, all of them middle-aged, their faces brimming with the air of country landlords long used to living off rents and usury, yet putting their heads together and whispering over something. Wu Sunfu chose his own seat opposite these three, two tables away.

Outside the window it was still a dense half-rain, half-fog, one white blur, as if the bustling industrial Shanghai had vanished away and only this one corner of the dining-room's high storey were left. And in this dining-room there were only four or five modish young gentlemen, listless and sunk in dancing-girls and greyhounds, three feudal country landlords, and Wu Sunfu—and Wu Sunfu at this moment was himself under the threat of three lines of fire.

Wu Sunfu let out a heavy breath and bade the waiter bring brandy, of which he took several fierce gulps in a row. That he was caught between three lines of fire was a fact; that he had already racked his brains to meet them was a fact too. To dream, before victory or defeat was decided, of how he would press the attack once he had won—that was too airy and vain; to plan how he would fall back and hold on after defeat, or make ready to counter-attack—that his present mood would not allow; and besides, since he did not yet know to what degree he had lost, he felt he had no way to lay hold of any future plan. And so for now he could only drink a few mouthfuls of wine, by way of make-do. His mood was somewhat like that of a condemned prisoner awaiting execution.

The wine went down mouthful by mouthful, and his heart seemed to grow a little livelier; at the least, his hearing had turned uncommonly sharp again. Among the three whispering over there, one had a rather higher voice, and a few phrases full of hidden meaning fell clear and plain into Wu Sunfu's ear:

"It's come to this—in for a penny, in for a pound; I mean to fight it out! Victory, indeed—that's a lie got up by the bull side. You know that this fellow Zhao is a big bull; it's he who's rigging the market there! I simply won't believe he has the appetite to swallow it all!"

The man who said this had his side turned toward Wu Sunfu—a long, narrow face, with a few crescent-shaped strands of yellow beard. His two companions kept silent for the moment, one propping his chin, the other holding his coffee-cup and lost in thought. Later these two spoke at once, but in voices so low and confused that only from their manner could one tell they had fallen into a dispute with the crescent-bearded man. These three were all men steeped in bond speculation, and were plainly playing the bears.

Wu Sunfu looked at his watch: it wanted ten minutes of one o'clock. People kept coming in, yet, strange to say, not one was an acquaintance. He worked his knife and fork mechanically, while all that turned over and over in his mind were those few phrases of the crescent-bearded, long-faced man. Did they stand for the mind of the mass of the bears? Wu Sunfu could not be sure. But that the state of the market was still one of mutual jostling, still in chaos, was already perfectly plain. It occurred to him that the reason he met no acquaintances here today might well be just this. There was no sense in his lingering here alone. So he pushed his dish away and was about to rise and go. But just as he lifted his head, he saw two men pass in front of him—familiar faces! One was Han Mengxiang, an exchange broker and a confidant of Zhao Botao's; the other was Li Yuting.

Han Mengxiang had seen Wu Sunfu too, and, with a smile, came near and said quietly:

"Neither side gives way—old Zhao is losing his temper!"

"What—losing his temper?"

Startled though Wu Sunfu was, he could quickly master himself, so that the latter half of this question stayed mild enough not to draw notice.

"He'll have none of the small fish—he wants the big fish; he'd sooner have nothing! Wait and see: this two-o'clock round will show who wins and who loses!"

Han Mengxiang still spoke low, and with a smile turned his eyes to look at Li Yuting. Just then one of the three over there—a plump, short white-faced fellow—rose abruptly, calling out again and again, "Brother Mengxiang!" The crescent-bearded one and the other went on talking head to head. Han Mengxiang nodded to Wu Sunfu, then turned and walked over to them. A lively talk began, on the state of the exchange, needless to say.

Here Wu Sunfu invited Li Yuting to eat with him, and they talked idly of one thing and another that had no bearing on anything. The wine was well up in Wu Sunfu's face now, and, suddenly recalling the matter of Zhang Susu, he asked Li Yuting:

"The day before yesterday I heard from Peiyao that things have changed between you and Susu?"

"There was never anything in it; there can be no question of any change."

Li Yuting answered with embarrassment, and, recalling the mocking words that Fan Bowen and Wu Zhisheng and the rest had spoken, felt ill at ease again within. But Wu Sunfu took no notice; he drank a great mouthful of soda-water and said, smiling:

"A Su is free and unfettered, just like her father. Quick and shrewd, like her dead mother. Yuting, you're no match for her!"

Li Yuting only gave a dry laugh, and, lowering his head, set about the chicken leg.

From the table over there came Han Mengxiang's laughter, and then the tangled, crisscrossing argument of four men. But amid it one steady voice came, not in the least blurred, with this: "Yunqing, you've only to press out a few more piculs of rent-rice, and won't that settle it?" Whereupon the crescent-bearded, long face was seen to give a start, and answered, most wretchedly: "This year it won't do—there's rent-resistance and rioting everywhere!" After that came again the jumbled sound of the four men all speaking at once.

Wu Sunfu knitted his brows, laid his hand over the mouth of his wine-glass, and, watching Li Yuting's face, asked:

"Have you heard any special news?"

"I've heard that a great plan is afoot, and that it has to do with you."

Li Yuting laid down his knife and fork, wiped his mouth with the napkin, and said carelessly.

"A great plan that has to do with me? Why, I know nothing of it myself!"

Wu Sunfu, too, answered offhand, and smiled lightly again. He supposed that these words of Li Yuting's must be a hint at their trust company. This was, after all, no matter that need be kept secret; but that it should have got about so fast did rather surprise Wu Sunfu. Yet what Li Yuting said next was more startling still:

"Ah, you've got it wrong—it's not like that. Among those setting the great plan in motion, you have no part; but among the objects of the great plan, you are included. When I say it has to do with you, that is the kind of connection I mean. I thought you must have had word of it long ago!"

"Oh—but honestly I know nothing at all of it."

"Whether they'll bring it off is by no means certain, but I hear they really do have such an ambition. To put it in a single word: the finance capitalists mean to extend their power into industry. They want to follow America's example—finance capital ruling over industrial capital."

Wu Sunfu half-closed his eyes and gave his head a slight shake.

"You think, no doubt, that they overreach themselves? But last year the total surplus of the Shanghai banking world was two hundred million, and such surplus capital must of course seek an outlet."

"The outlet is the bond market; or else real estate, city buildings. Their eyes will never stray beyond these two circles!"

said Wu Sunfu with great contempt, his wine-flushed face grown yet more shining. His scorn for his adversary was perhaps just because the wine had already gone a little to his head. But Li Yuting, who had the same few cups inside him, was for once talkative too. As if unwilling to give in, he said:

"Sunfu, you rate them too cheap. They have this ambition, only the ground of fact is not yet fully ripe for it. But the plan now brewing is well worth watching—the more so because behind it American finance capitalists are lending their backing. I hear the first step of the plan is for the government, in the name of relieving industry, to float a very large issue of industrial bonds. That would be the beginning of finance capital's rule over industrial capital—and in fact it is very possible—"

"But the government floats bonds and still hasn't enough to meet its military and administrative costs—how can there be any talk of construction?"

"That is the state of things at present; at present there's still the civil war. They hope that the outcome of this war will be a victory for the Central Government, a true unification of the whole country. Naturally the Americans hope so too. And that hope may well become fact. Then can you say their plan is nothing but a fantasy? With America's experience and money for backer, can you say this plan of theirs has no chance of being realized? Sunfu, for finance capital to swallow industrial capital is a common thing in the countries of Western Europe—how much more so when Chinese industry is so young, so withered, and when, besides, the American gold dollar is looking to push outward—"

"Ah! This is simply the ruin of China's national industry, no less!"

said Wu Sunfu, clenching his teeth in a sudden fury. The wine had left him; he could no longer smile his cool, disdainful smile. His face went white, but his eyes were fearfully red. Li Yuting was struck dumb, having never thought Wu Sunfu would take it so much to heart and grow so angry. After a while, as though to ease the air, he said, half to himself:

"It probably won't come off, though? America can't yet have her own way alone in the world, and in the East especially she has two powerful rivals."

"You mean England and Japan? So the outcome of this war may not, after all, be quite what the financial world hopes for."

said Wu Sunfu, gazing blankly out of the window. His thoughts at this moment were tangled indeed. But one thing was certain: the righteous indignation, on behalf of national industry, that had just flared up in him was slowly shrinking away, while his concern for his own private interests was slowly swelling, until at last his mind was wholly fixed upon it. Was it not just as Li Yuting said—that the foundation of Chinese industry was weak? The weak must in the end be swallowed by others, and so it is in the world of enterprise; was not Wu Sunfu himself even now planning to swallow the weaker Zhu Yinqiu? And now he found that he too stood in danger of being swallowed—and that just when he himself was hemmed in by three lines of fire, with victory and defeat still undecided. So Wu Sunfu thought and thought, his compass narrowing and narrowing, his mood growing ever darker.

Suddenly someone startled him out of his brooding. It was Han Mengxiang again, his whole face full of high spirits; he gave Wu Sunfu a greeting and then hurried off. The three at the table over there promptly went out after him. The crescent-bearded, long-faced man called "Yunqing" dragged his steps heavily and came last of all.

"They've all gone off to the exchange. Today's exchange is just like a battlefield!"

said Li Yuting softly, with something of a sigh in it, gazing at their retreating backs; and at the same time he threw Wu Sunfu a glance.

The waiter brought the coffee. Wu Sunfu took a sip, then set down the cup and asked Li Yuting:

"The prime movers of this great plan are American capitalists, by the look of it; but on the Chinese side, who are the people? Doing this business of letting the wolf in at the door!"

"Shang Zhongli and Zhao Botao, I hear."

answered Li Yuting without lifting his head, drinking his coffee the while. Wu Sunfu's face changed all at once. Old Zhao again! Wu Sunfu felt that this time he had surely been taken in, and set it down on the spot that the whole so-called "bond bulls' company" was a snare. He gave a snort through his nose and could get out not a word. Yet his dark mood suddenly scattered instead, leaving only fury, only the wish for revenge; now that he reckoned defeat unavoidable, he grew calm again, and his courage came back to him. His one hope was to learn, the sooner the better, to what degree he had lost, so that upon the ruins of defeat he might build up his order of counter-attack.

After parting from Li Yuting, Wu Sunfu drove straight home. In the car his thoughts turned as fast as the wheels. He chewed over that piece of news of Li Yuting's afresh and in detail. A feeling close to self-comfort was the first to creep into his head. He could not believe that such a thing could really come about, and come about just as they wished. It was most likely a fantasy of Zhao Botao and his sort, with the megalomania of the American bourgeoisie thrown in. Had not some European scholar once said that the megalomania of the American bourgeoisie, since the Great War, had swollen almost to the point of unreason? And had not the economic crisis of the whole world struck America too?…… And yet, no: America had her Dawes, and her Young. There was no guaranteeing there would be no second Dawes Plan applied to China. Once China had a unified government, and one held fast in the grip of the American fellows, a second Dawes Plan would surely be hard to escape? And then—the clash of interests of the three great powers in the East?—Wu Sunfu gave a savage grin. Even as his thoughts reached this point, the car had already driven in at the gate of his house, its wheels purring coquettishly along the asphalt road.

What met him as he stepped from the car was another burst of downpour. The sky had darkened almost to dusk. Every lamp in the house was lit. The young mistress, the Fourth Young Lady, and Du Zhuzhai's eldest son Xintuo were all in the drawing-room. Wu Sunfu put them off hastily with a few words and hurried into his study. He was unwilling to let anyone see through to the trouble weighing on his heart; and, besides, he had a stack of letters to answer.

Several letters that were wholly matters of business he answered all; the last he replied to was a long letter from a friend who ran a cotton mill in Wuxi and, meaning to add to his spindles, was coaxing Wu Sunfu to take up shares. It fell on just the wrong moment, and all the darkness that filled Wu Sunfu's breast came flowing out at the tip of his pen. When he had finished and read it over, he was startled himself at how he could have said such dispirited things. He tore up the letter-paper, dared not write again, and went back once more to the great drawing-room in front.

Lin Peishan was sitting at the piano, playing; the melody was strangely mournful. The yellow lamplight, falling on her tall, slender form in its deep-blue silk gown, looked gloomy and oppressive too. Wu Sunfu, frowning, was about to speak, when he suddenly heard the young mistress sigh. He turned his face, his brows knitting the tighter, and saw that the rims of the young mistress's eyes were a little red, and that two tears had fallen. At the same moment he heard Du Xintuo say dreamily:

"Man's life is like the morning dew! This piece expresses just that mood. In this rainy weather, under this dreamlike lamplight, it is most fitting to play this air!"

Wu Sunfu's face changed utterly. The evil omen turned into sharp steel claws and clawed straight at his heart. He was in such a fury that he nearly burst out cursing aloud; but just then the servant Gaosheng came up and said another thing that set the heart pounding:

"Master, there's a telephone call from the mill!"

Wu Sunfu turned and ran inside. A call from the mill! Was it good news or ill? As he took up the receiver, his hand, without his knowing it, was trembling a little. But a minute later his face suddenly lit up, and in a clear, ringing voice he said aloud:

"Very well done!—Since you plead for him again on his behalf, then let Gui Changlin have a half-month's rise in pay! Tomorrow at nine o'clock I'll come to the mill to inspect."

The labour trouble at the mill was settled, and Wu Sunfu had won; he had no more cares at his back to trouble him!

Wu Sunfu set down the receiver, smiling. By now the downpour was over, and a sheet of golden sunlight slanted in at the west window of the study. Looking out through the window, the leaves in the garden were a lovely green, dripping with water-drops as if in rhythm. Wu Sunfu walked lightly out of the study, rounded a stretch of veranda, and went along the asphalt path of the garden, washed so clean by the rain, feeling that the air now was fresher than it had ever been. As he drew near the front of the great drawing-room, he heard the wild honking of a motor-horn; a car drove straight up to the stone steps of the great drawing-room, and before it had properly stopped, Du Zhuzhai had already leapt out of the carriage. Never had he been so impatient, so keyed up!

"Zhuzhai, how did it go?"

Wu Sunfu hurried forward to ask, his heart in a great flutter. But before Du Zhuzhai could answer, he knew it was victory: the elation showing through the weariness was written plain on Du Zhuzhai's goat-face. As they ran up the stone steps of the great drawing-room together, Du Zhuzhai said softly:

"In this afternoon's round the bears all came in to cover, the rising wind was fierce beyond measure, and it nearly touched the limit. If we had taken in another two or three million earlier on, we'd have unloaded it today just the same! A pity we were too cautious at the start! And now, when we reckon it all up—"

"Well, never mind—this is a lucky opening! We'll try again another day!"

said Wu Sunfu with a slight smile. The sun slanted on his face, throwing back a bright red glow, and the sombre look that had come and gone since morning was now quite gone. He had broken through the encirclement and won on both fronts; and that great plan Li Yuting had reported—one might as well call it a great plot—could, in this light of victory, no longer threaten Wu Sunfu now.
