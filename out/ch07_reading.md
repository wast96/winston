## Chapter Seven. The Great Hermit Hides in the City

### 1. The National Congress of Soviet Areas

When we call Shanghai the cradle of the people's congress system, it is because the forerunner of that system was the workers', peasants', and soldiers' soviet, and the workers', peasants', and soldiers' soviet was itself first proposed at the National Congress of Soviet Areas.

There were reasons why the Party Center chose to convene the National Congress of Soviet Areas in Shanghai in May 1930.

The Shanghai of that year is caught by the writer Ding Ling in her "Shanghai, Spring 1930" (1930): "Scores of newspapers were hawked in a clamor across the city, their banner headlines carrying news of the fighting on every front, all of it unreliable, contradictory news. The pretty young ladies and gentlemen of good family had all changed into their fine spring clothes, a flush on their faces and a special brightness in their eyes; they strolled the length of the avenues, thronged the amusement halls, and scattered out to the suburbs and the nearer beauty spots, seeking a little more pleasure for their pampered bodies and their untroubled hearts. Such amusements would keep them young and handsome, and give them all the satisfaction life could offer. And the workers? They had come through the bitter cold of winter, but the pressures of life arrived with the long days of spring: the price of rice and grain rose, rents were raised, working hours grew longer; they toiled harder, tried harder, and grew thinner still; the aging had their wages cut or were dismissed outright; and the children, the children who had never once eaten their fill, were taken on to fill the gaps, though neither their age nor their strength met what the law required. They suffered too much; they had to fight back, and so the struggle began. News of strikes, news of workers beaten and killed—fresh reports came without letup, day after day, and so many revolutionary youths, students, the ×× Party, threw themselves into a frenzy of activity, sympathizing with the workers, aiding them, and under some guiding hand running about, sweating, burning with excitement.... Spring had deepened—a soft wind, an intoxicating air! And yet all the wickedness, the suffering, the struggle and the strife played out beneath that same mild and sunny sky."

Likewise, Mao Dun wrote in his Midnight (1933): "The din of machines, the stink of motorcars, and a fashionable young woman perched high on a rickshaw, one bare white leg cocked up as if she wore no trousers at all. ... In the exchange, tiers of men, faces flushed crimson, eyes bulging and threaded with red, veins standing out thick as earthworms, shoved forward as if charging into battle, raising a thunderous uproar of numbers, heedless that the pale-blue gauze dress of the woman beside them was already soaked through with sweat, two rosy points showing at the breast. ... Peasants in threes and fives, a strip of red cloth around each neck, weapons of every kind in their hands, running wild through the main streets. ... A shrill police whistle split the air, then an armored car and a mounted patrol scattered a knot of the crowd; but still the demonstrators came on and on, a mixed column of students and workers, strewing handbills as they went and shouting slogans like thunder. ... Outside the mill a single shout went up, and countless women workers surged forward, choking the road before the gate to a standstill; the police came running too, braving a squall of flung gravel and clods of earth, seizing one for every one they saw."

The Comintern accordingly proposed that the CCP gather the delegates of the major soviet areas, the delegates of the Red Army, and the delegates of the various revolutionary bodies to confer with the Party leadership on the great questions of the day: how to strengthen the Party's leadership of work in the soviet areas, and how to drive the nationwide soviet movement—armed separatist bases, the countryside encircling the cities—to a new high tide.

On January 20, 1930, on the Comintern's proposal, the Party Center decided to convene the National Congress of Soviet Areas: first a preparatory meeting in Shanghai, then the formal congress in a soviet area.

On February 4, in order to "convene the National Congress of Soviet Areas," the Party Center issued its Circular No. 68 to the whole Party, calling on "all Party organs at every level, and rural local branches above all, to proclaim openly among the masses that this year's May Day will see the opening of a National Congress of Soviet Areas, to bind together the soviet areas and the Red Army across the country, and to unify the direction and action of the Chinese revolution." The circular declared: "The convening of this congress will have as its chief sponsors the All-China Federation of Trade Unions and the Chinese Communist Party, with the soviet areas and the Red Army also invited to lend their names. Its task, beyond all doubt, in the present tense state of the revolutionary struggle, will be to carry a still greater historic mission; and the first thing this congress must resolve is a more unified joint action between the soviet areas and the Red Army across the country. ... The sponsors will notify the localities of the arrangements. The Center will designate the principal delegates from each soviet area and from the Red Army, who will first assemble in one place for a preparatory meeting to discuss and draft all the resolutions, and only then will the congress proper open in some designated soviet area, together with a nationwide demonstration in support of the Chinese soviets and the Red Army. As for the delegates, apart from the trade-union delegates, each soviet area shall have no fewer than two principal delegates, each Red Army corps no fewer than one, and the other guerrilla areas and areas of peasant struggle likewise no fewer than one each; as for the other mass delegates and Red Guard delegates, these shall be chosen locally at discretion, but no area may have more than five in all."

With the Party Center's Circular No. 68, which he had gone to endless trouble to get his hands on, Xiong Shihui read over and over the words plainly set down there—"the Center will designate the principal delegates from each soviet area and from the Red Army, who will first assemble in one place for a preparatory meeting"—and was simply beside himself with glee.

For he had already made certain that the "one place" where they would "first assemble ... for a preparatory meeting" meant Shanghai, and he had decided this was a golden chance to smash the leadership core of the CCP Center and the command hub of its Workers' and Peasants' Red Army once and for all—and a rare opening to vault himself to fame and fortune.

So he summoned every one of his spies and secret agents, threw down on the spot a huge bounty of 500,000 yuan, and ordered them to find out for certain the exact dates and the exact address of the National Congress of Soviet Areas.

At the same time, the Central Special Branch, which had full charge of security for the congress, had already had accurate intelligence of its own: it knew that Xiong Shihui's eyes and ears, drawn by the 500,000-yuan bounty, had cast their net across the whole city and were prowling everywhere like hawks and hounds.

Hold the congress as planned, or cancel it at once—a hard choice now faced the Party's highest leaders.

The Center would have dearly liked to countermand its own order, but no one had the authority to cancel the congress or to change where it would be held. Above the Center stood the Comintern. The CCP Center had already reported formally to the Comintern and had Moscow's approval and consent. And besides, the delegates from the major soviet areas and from the Red Army had all set out and were already on the road to Shanghai.

The arrow was on the string; there was no holding it back.

So the congress preparatory group and the relevant members of the Central Special Branch met in secret in a little Western-style house on the corner of Avenue Road (today Beijing West Road) and Carter Road (today Shimen No. 2 Road), racking their brains and laying their plans to fix on a venue with room enough and yet nothing to draw undue notice.

### 2. The Secret Hospital

Li Weiwei says that as her father and his comrades prepared for the National Congress of Soviet Areas, they "made a great many very careful arrangements, and looked over many places, all of which the Center rejected as unsuitable. The Center held that the White Terror in Shanghai was very fierce, that the reactionary authorities watched the Communists' every move closely, and that, with the number of delegates actually taking part so large—there might be more than a hundred—the site could not be too out of the way. The more central, the busier and more bustling the district, the easier to stay hidden and the less likely to draw the enemy's notice. And with plenty of people about, it would be easier to guard as well."

Li Weiwei is Li Yimang's daughter. In March 2007 I went to see her at Nanshagou in Beijing, the old house where her father had lived out his last years. Many high leaders of the Party and state had lived at Nanshagou, Li Weiwei told me, and many eminent public figures as well. Most of the old people are gone now, and their children too are no longer young—Qian Zhuangfei's grandson Qian Hong, Gao Chongmin's son Gao Dahui, Ai Siqi's son Li Xindong, and the like.

Li Yimang joined the Party in 1925 and the Red Army in 1932, walked the Long March, and served as one of Mao Zedong's secretaries; in his life he went on to hold such posts as ambassador of the People's Republic of China to Burma, deputy director of the State Council's Foreign Affairs Office, standing deputy head of the Central Committee's International Liaison Department, and standing member of the Central Advisory Commission.

Li Weiwei is very like her father: gentle, low-key, unassuming. "My father braved death a hundred times and struggled all his life," she said, "yet the couplet he wrote for himself at eighty was this: 'Withdrawn at the city's edge, the sound of books and the notes of the lute; at ease beneath the trees, birdsong and the scent of flowers.' Just because he took neither honor nor disgrace to heart, held rank and fame lightly, and wished only to be a nameless hero, he used to say: I am no more than an ordinary rank-and-file Communist; through the whole course of the Chinese revolution I leaned above on the Party's line and below on the masses, and had no merit of my own—there is truly nothing you could call an achievement or a contribution. And even the few things worth the telling are no more than passing clouds, drifting, at most, slowly and lightly in the depths of one man's memory...."

In the "depths of memory," "drifting slowly and lightly," of Li Yimang lay the National Congress of Soviet Areas. Speaking of it in his old age, Li Yimang recalled: "In May 1930 a National Congress of Soviet Areas was held in Shanghai. It was convened in the name of the Central Committee of the CCP and the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, so the preparatory work was all in the hands of Lin Yunan of the Federation. The address for the preparatory work was on the corner of Avenue Road and Carter Road (today Nos. 690 to 696 at the mouth of Beijing West Road and Shimen Road). As for the place where the congress actually met, it was in a row of buildings behind the Carlton Theater, on Burkill Road (today Fengyang Road)." (Li Yimang, 2001) But Zhang Wenqiu, in her own late-life recollections, insisted again and again that the little Western-style house on "the corner of Avenue Road and Carter Road" was not only the congress's "preparatory-work address" but also "the very place where it met." (Zhang Wenqiu, 2002)

Zhang Wenqiu's life was extraordinarily eventful. She lived through many great events of modern Chinese history and CCP history—including being assigned by Zhou Enlai as an assistant to the famous Soviet spy Sorge—and she was also the mother of Liu Siqi (Songlin) and Shaohua, and Mao Zedong's kinswoman by marriage. What is more, the little Western-style house on "the corner of Avenue Road and Carter Road" had in the first place been the "home" of her and Lin Yunan.

Lin Yunan, born 1898, his ancestral home the Lin Family Bay at Huilong in Huangzhou, Hubei, joined the Party in 1921 and held such posts as member and secretary of the Central Committee of the Communist Youth League of China, propaganda chief of the Hubei Provincial Federation of Trade Unions, and executive member, standing member, and secretary-general of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions.

Zhang Wenqiu—ancestral home the Zhang Family Bay at Qingshuling, Sunjiaqiao, Jingshan, Hubei—was born in 1903 and joined the Party in 1926. In 1927 she married Liu Qianchu (then head of the social section in the propaganda department of the political department of the Eleventh Corps of the National Revolutionary Army); in 1928 she was propaganda chief of the Shanghai-West District Committee under the CCP's Jiangsu Provincial Committee; and in 1929, having changed her name to Chen Mengjun, she became an executive member and head of the women's department of the CCP's Shandong Provincial Committee, working together with Liu Qianchu (by then secretary of the CCP's Shandong Provincial Committee) until she was arrested, sold out by a traitor.

In January 1930, released from prison, Zhang Wenqiu returned from Jinan to Shanghai, took the new alias Zhang Yiping, and, with Lin Yunan—who under the alias Li Jingtang was got up as a returned overseas Chinese—posed as husband and wife and moved into the little Western-style house on "the corner of Avenue Road and Carter Road." Zhang Wenqiu recalled: "After we had talked a little, Lin Yunan said to me: 'I have come today to discuss work with you. Right now armed struggle is developing fast all across the country, and soviet governments are springing up everywhere. To consolidate and extend the fruits of the revolution, the Center means to found a Chinese Soviet Republic, to strengthen its leadership over the local soviet governments. To that end the Center has decided to set up a Soviet Preparatory Committee, to draft all the necessary laws, decrees, and policies and submit them to the National Congress of Soviet Areas for review and adoption. This congress was originally to be held in the Central Soviet Area, but with the reactionaries' blockade and encirclement it would be hard, and unsafe, for the delegates from all over to get there, so it has been moved to Shanghai. The organization has decided that you and I are to take part together in the work of the "Prep Committee." I will pose as a wealthy overseas-Chinese capitalist from the South Seas, and you and I, under cover of a false marriage, will first set up the office and then get the work going.' ... Fang Lin, one of the Center's responsible people, also said to me: 'I have come to say a few words to you on the Center's behalf. The staff of the Prep Committee have all been chosen and assigned; they will arrive one after another. The Center has decided that you and Comrade Yunan will set up the office as a false married couple. You must lose no time: pick out a good house somewhere in the Shanghai concessions and settle in as fast as you can, so the work can begin. From now on you must play the part of the housewife to the full, keeping on good terms with the landlord and the neighbors, staying alert, and keeping the office's secrets safe.'" (Zhang Wenqiu, 2002)

Fang Lin was an alias used by Deng Fa in September 1937, when he succeeded Chen Yun as the CCP Center's representative in Xinjiang and head of the Eighth Route Army's Xinjiang office. If the "Fang Lin" Zhang Wenqiu speaks of was indeed Deng Fa, then at the start of 1930 Deng Fa could hardly have been in a position to convey a "decision of the Center" to Zhang Wenqiu in Shanghai "on the Center's behalf." For at that time Deng Fa was only a standing member and organization chief of the CCP's Guangdong Provincial Committee and secretary of the Hong Kong Municipal Committee—far from "one of the Center's responsible people"—and was moreover still in a Hong Kong jail, enduring the torture and forced interrogation of the British colonial authorities.

So who was it, then, who paid sixty taels of silver a month to rent the little Western-style house on "the corner of Avenue Road and Carter Road"? And to what use was it put? Was it truly "the very place where the congress met," or merely a "preparatory-work address" for the "Prep Committee," and an over-lavish home laid on for Lin Yunan and Zhang Wenqiu, that false married couple?

Hong Yangsheng, in "The Work of the First Section of the Central Special Branch" (2018), says: "Setting up an office and arranging a venue is concrete, painstaking work. Whenever the Center held an important meeting, everything—fixing the site, renting the house, arranging the venue, seeing to the sundry chores—was the First Section's charge. In May 1930 it was the First Section that arranged the venue for the preparatory meeting of the National Congress of Soviet Areas. This meeting had many people taking part, ran a long time, and made for a heavy workload. Two venues were made ready, so that if trouble arose at one the meeting could move to the other. One was a row of Western-style buildings behind the Carlton Theater (now the Changjiang Theater); the other was a Western house at the mouth of Avenue Road and Carter Road (now the mouth of Beijing West Road and Shimen No. 2 Road). In fact both venues were used, one after the other."

Just because "both venues were used, one after the other," Zhang Wenqiu could say: "After nearly a month's preparation, on May 20, 1930, the National Congress of Soviet Areas opened in secret, in solemn and stately fashion, in the three-story building of two joined houses at the mouth of Avenue Road and Carter Road in Shanghai. Lin Yunan, at the head of all of us on the 'Prep Committee' staff, warmly ushered the delegates from every part of the country and the leaders of every quarter into the second-floor meeting hall of the west house, and it was there the congress met. To meet any mishap that might arise, we had also fitted out a birthday hall in the ground-floor reception room of the east house. In the middle of the birthday hall hung a great golden character shou—'long life'—flanked by a couplet: 'May your fortune be as boundless as the Eastern Sea's long-flowing waters; may your years outlast the ever-green pine of the Southern Mountains.' Beneath the shou stood an offering table draped in deep-red felt, set out with red longevity peaches, white longevity noodles, and golden Buddha's-hand citrons. The chairs for the birthday guests were ranged in neat order. To lend this lavish celebration the fullest air of truth, we even brought in the father of one of the comrades to play the father of Zhao Yuqing (that is, Lin Yunan)—'Old Master Zhao'—waiting in the room next door, ready at any moment to enter the birthday hall and receive the guests' congratulations and their bows. Before the meeting, every delegate and staff member had long since settled on a single story: whatever happened, they were to swear blind that they had come to wish 'Old Master Zhao' a happy eightieth birthday."

Li Yimang recalled: "The place where the congress actually met was in a row of buildings behind the Carlton Theater, on Burkill Road (today Fengyang Road). Before the meeting, the Center decided that I should act as master of the house where it would be held. Zhao Yimin and Li Yichao (a woman, also called Li Kuntai) had just come back from the Soviet Union not long before—they had both returned in the winter of 1928—so I had one of them play my younger brother and the other my younger sister. Of course I had my wife and two little boys with me as well, and put all together we naturally looked very much like a family."

By Li Yimang's account, the Central Special Branch finally settled on a building at the junction of Park Road (today Huanghe Road) and Burkill Road (today Fengyang Road) as the venue for the National Congress of Soviet Areas.

I am convinced that Hong Yangsheng and his helpers did two things at once. One was to disguise, most ingeniously, the building right up against the Carlton Theater as a hospital. The other was to fit out the "reception room" of "the three-story building of two joined houses at the mouth of Avenue Road and Carter Road" as a "birthday hall." Since this second "birthday hall" was set up directly in the "home" of Zhang Wenqiu and Lin Yunan, and this "eightieth birthday of Old Master Zhao" was so "lavish," is it any wonder that, as time went by, the impression sank so deep in Zhang Wenqiu that she came to believe it true?

### 3. His Own Spear Against His Own Shield

The venue chosen for the National Congress of Soviet Areas lay in the busiest quarter of the International Settlement, hard against the day-and-night revelry of the Carlton Theater—plainly very much to the Center's wish, which was to be "the great hermit who hides in the city," where "the more central, the busier and more bustling the district, the easier to stay hidden and the less likely to draw the enemy's notice." Under the very eyes of the reactionaries, they would play a game of "fighting poison with poison," of "his own spear against his own shield." And yet Lin Chengxi and Xu Rongsheng write: "The Special Branch threw itself in with all its strength and laid careful plans: first it rented a four-story red-brick building on Hart Road in the International Settlement, and set up a temporary 'hospital' there." (Lin Chengxi and Xu Rongsheng, 1996) Li Tao likewise says: "After much repeated study, the Special Branch leadership decided to rent a four-story red building on Hart Road in the British Concession, disguise it as a hospital, and use it as the venue for the National Congress of Soviet Areas." Li Tao was one of the authors of Liu Ding (2002); he had been head of the Ordnance General Bureau of the former Central Ministry of Heavy Industry, secretary to Liu Ding (deputy minister of the Second, First, and Third Ministries of Machine Building), and deputy director of the Economic Research Institute of the Ministry of Ordnance Industry. Lin Chengxi, Xu Rongsheng, and Li Tao all follow Wu Dianyao. Wu Dianyao, in Liu Ding (1990), says the venue of the National Congress of Soviet Areas was "a four-story red building on Hart Road in the British Concession, the red building disguised as a hospital." Others who say the venue was "chosen in a four-story building on Park Road, behind the Carlton Theater on Hart Road in the British Concession," include Yue Xian and Qin Shaozhi in The Tiger's Den and the Dragon's Pool (2003). All of these derive from Mu Xin's General Chen Geng (1985). Mu Xin says: "At the start of 1930 the Party Center convened the National Congress of Soviet Areas on Hart Road in the British Concession of Shanghai. To ensure the congress's safety, the Central Special Branch made the following careful arrangements: behind the Carlton Cinema on Hart Road it rented a four-story red building and set up a temporary 'hospital.'"

The Hart Road of those days is today's Changde Road. Hart Road too, it is true, lay within the bounds of the International Settlement, and touched a little on Bubbling Well Road—only, along that road the Carlton Theater stood at the far eastern end and Hart Road at the far western: the one hard by the Racecourse, the other hard by Jing'an Temple.

The Carlton Theater—also called the Carlton Playhouse or the Carlton Cinema—opened in February 1923 and was renamed the Changjiang Theater in December 1951; it was the foremost of the Shanghai cinemas the British opened in the twentieth century, clamorous from dawn to dusk, its crowds coming and going in an endless stream, all bustle and life. Nearby Burkill Road, by contrast, was a winding, secluded little lane, lined with ordinary dwellings, Chinese and foreign mixed together, "seventy-two tenants" to a house—every kind of person under the sun.

Now a word more about Li Yimang and his "younger brother" Zhao Yimin (later deputy head of the International Liaison Department) and his "younger sister" Li Yichao. After the National Congress of Soviet Areas ended, they still had a few private meetings, and then lost touch. Only after the founding of New China, when Li Yimang saw the film Zhao Yiman, did he learn that Li Yichao was none other than the anti-Japanese martyr Zhao Yiman. Zhao Yiman—originally named Li Kuntai, courtesy name Shuning—was born in 1905 in Yibin, Sichuan. She joined the Party in 1926 and went in 1927 to study at Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow. She returned to China in 1928, and in 1932 went to the front line of the resistance in the Northeast, holding such posts as secretary, organization chief, and acting secretary of the Manchuria General Labor Union, member of the CCP's Zhuhe Central County Committee, secretary of the Tiebei District Committee, and political commissar of the 2nd Regiment, 1st Division, 3rd Army of the Northeast People's Revolutionary Army. In November 1935 Zhao Yiman was captured, wounded. In August 1936 she died a hero's death at the execution ground by the Little North Gate of Zhuhe. Before her death, as the Japanese devils paraded her through the streets on a cart, she asked for paper and pen and wrote a last letter to her still-underage son: "Your mother and you will never in this life have another chance to meet. I hope you, my Ning'er, will grow up quickly and come to comfort your mother beneath the earth! My dearest child! Your mother will teach you not with ten thousand words but with deeds. When you are grown, I hope you will not forget that your mother gave her life for her country!" In his memoirs Li Yimang wrote with feeling: "Some accounts of Zhao Yiman's life today—whether in Shangzhi County, where she died, or in Yibin County, where she was born—leave out this stretch when she took part in the Congress of Soviet Areas as a member of my Li household. There is probably no one left now who knows of this short passage of her life." (Li Yimang, 2001)

### 4. Layers of Insurance

In mid-May 1930 the hospital behind the Carlton Theater opened for business. To an outsider it was just one more newly opened private hospital, making no great show, drawing no crowds to its gate, nothing out of the ordinary at all. No one guessed that all of it was the careful handiwork of the Central Special Branch. Li Yimang said: "The kitchen was theirs to run, the cars were theirs, the furniture and fittings in the house were theirs, the guard inside and out was set by them. We did nothing but sit downstairs, grown-ups and children, playing mahjong and running the gramophone, to cover the meeting. ... And of course, besides this makeshift family of ours, there were the staff taking part in the meeting; they did not live there, but came and went."

In fact the doctors, nurses, clerks, and odd-job men here—including Zou Zhishu, who saw to the catering—were all members of the Central Special Branch. Over the white coat, many of them wore a stethoscope; under the white coat, they carried a Mauser.

Zou Zhishu, also called Zou Zhiying, was born in 1897 in Jiaxing, Zhejiang. She graduated from Zhuangshi Senior Middle School in 1912, joined the Xincheng Reading Society in 1924, and joined the Party in 1926. In 1927 she took part in the Northern Expedition and came with the army to Shanghai.

Set beside Huang Mulan and Wu Xianqing of the Second Section, Zhou Huinian of the Third, and Pu Qiuchao of the Fourth—all of them, like her, Special Branch members, all of them educated women—Zou Zhishu all but vanished on the wind, lost in the withered grass and the boundless waves of history's haze. Even Shen Wan (Shen Anna) and Li Yun, of the Central Special Branch's later years, are far better known, far more celebrated than she. Yet her standing was an old one. By the end of 1927 or the start of 1928 she had already been transferred to the First Section of the Special Branch by Gu Shunzhang, and by figures higher still—by Zhou Enlai. The Meilong Gazetteer (1986) says: "After 'April 12th,' Zou Zhishu went to the Party Center's organ at No. 36 Zhangjiazhai, on Avenue Road and Carter Road (today Beijing West Road and Shimen No. 2 Road) in Shanghai, to do courier-cover work in the First Section of the Central Special Branch under Zhou Enlai." But in the Directory of Shanghai Firms and Streets (1947), on Zhangjiazhai Road—west of Carter Road, east of Medhurst Road (today Taixing Road), south of Xinzha Road, north of Avenue Road—there is no No. 36, only Lane 36.

Because Zou Zhishu had followed Hong Yangsheng throughout, when people write her life today and come to her achievements, they can only follow Hong Yangsheng's recollections and put it simply: "In May 1930, during the Congress of Soviet Areas held in a row of Western-style buildings behind the Carlton Theater (today the Changjiang Theater), Zou Zhishu took part in the work of the congress." (People's Government of Meilong Township, Shanghai County, Shanghai, 1986)

Zou Zhishu, a woman like a riddle, only deepened the mystery of the secret hospital.

Besides Li Yimang, Hong Yangsheng, and Zou Zhishu, there was Liu Ding at work on the edge of the secret hospital. Liu Ding rented a separate suite of rooms next to it, in the same row of great Western houses up against the Carlton Theater, and set up an office there to serve as an escape route by which the delegates could withdraw fast in an emergency.

Liu Ding—originally named Kan Sijun, courtesy name Zunmin—was born in 1902 in Nanxi, Sichuan. In 1924, during a stay in Germany, he joined the Chinese Communist Party on the introduction of Sun Bingwen and Zhu De. In 1926 he went to study at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow, improving his Russian, studying Marxism, and receiving military training. At the end of 1929 he returned to China and reached Shanghai, where he became deputy head of the Second Section of the Central Special Branch, assisting the section head, Chen Geng, in intelligence work.

Yi Hui says: "At the end of 1929 Liu Ding came back secretly, in disguise, to Shanghai, reported to Zhou Enlai, and was sent by Zhou Enlai to the Central Special Branch to be deputy to the Second Section head, Chen Geng, working solely on intelligence. He put together a very complete set of materials giving a full picture of every side of Shanghai. To learn exactly how many men were inside the concession barracks, Liu Ding would climb to the top of a tall building nearby to look. The barracks were closely guarded—sentries at the gate, high walls, barbed wire—so what was to be done? The only way was to climb to the roof of the building beside it and, looking down from above, study the reflection of the barrack blocks, and from the reflection reckon their area and how many soldiers they could hold. They say that when he went up he even took along a little biesan—a street urchin—to keep watch for him. Very thorough." (Yi Hui, 2002)

Yi Hui was Liu Ding's wife, a veteran of the Eighth Route Army, and before her retirement an adviser to Institute 210 (the Intelligence Institute) of the Ministry of Ordnance Industry. Yi Hui told me that Liu Ding and his helpers also set up a street stall outside the foreign barracks, posing as sellers of sweets, cigarettes, and snacks, and from there spied out the men and vehicles coming and going, and so reckoned up the strength and equipment within. Yi Hui said further: "Besides the barracks, they went out separately and reconnoitered the administrative buildings of the concessions' Municipal Council and French Municipal Council, and reconnoitered the banks, the granaries, the ports, the prisons, and other vital points. They also got hold of a very, very detailed set of maps of Shanghai. To get that set of maps Liu Ding went round every bookshop in Shanghai. The big shops had none, so he tried the small ones. The Chinese shops had none, so he tried the foreigners'. At last, in a secondhand shop kept by a foreigner, he found a set—a very complete set, foreign-language originals, a great tall stack, better than he had imagined—and he was simply overjoyed; he did not even haggle, but bought the whole lot and carried it all home."

All of this was for the sake of carrying out the "Shanghai uprising." The so-called "Shanghai uprising" was a product of Li Lisan's "Left" adventurism. At the time Zhou Enlai had gone to Moscow, and Xiang Zhongfa, chairman of the Standing Committee of the Politburo of the CCP Central Committee, let himself be led by the nose by Li Lisan, standing member of the Politburo and head of the Central Propaganda Department. Under the guidance of his mistaken notion of "victory first in one province or several," Li Lisan concentrated on preparing uprisings in Nanjing, Shanghai, Wuhan, and elsewhere, and kept Liu Bocheng—just back from the Soviet Union—in Shanghai, appointing him head of the staff department of the Central Military Commission to draw up in detail an insurrection nationwide, grandly styled "preparing a great armed uprising of all China, for a final, decisive battle with the imperialists and the Kuomintang." Later the CCP Center convened the enlarged Third Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee in Shanghai, and checked in time some of the errors that were the chief marks of the "Li Lisan line." Liu Ding's compiled and submitted materials were sealed away with it, shelved and forgotten; but Liu Bocheng, with his rare eye, still set great store by the latent value of those things. And so, after the founding of New China, the moment Liu Bocheng saw Liu Ding in Beijing he said: "That set of materials you put together in Shanghai was really something—no small feat." "That was what Li Lisan had us do," said Liu Ding. "That's another matter entirely," said Liu Bocheng, "not to be confused with this. The materials themselves are precious. We've used them all along like a dictionary: whenever there was a doubt we looked it up, and everything could be found there. Truly rare."

I asked Yi Hui to describe in detail the escape route Liu Ding had laid in advance for the National Congress of Soviet Areas. Yi Hui said: "In his old age Liu Ding often spoke of this. He said he had rented a separate suite of rooms beside that hospital. If ever the front door there were blocked, the Central leaders and the delegates could go through an upstairs window, jump across into the rooms he had rented, and slip out safely by another door."

But the two suites—one with a low roof, the other with a high window—were several meters apart, and to cross in safety one needed a ladder. So Liu Ding hit on another "perfectly natural" idea. He had the hospital side pretend its window was broken and set about repairing the window; his own side pretended its roof was broken and set about repairing the roof. The windows on both sides were forever opening and shutting, shutting and opening, and a ladder stood there for several days on end without drawing any more notice.

Beyond this, the Central Special Branch laid on a double safeguard. The medical staff moving to and fro on the hospital's ground floor were only the second line of defense for the whole venue; the first line was led in person by Gu Shunzhang and Chen Geng, spread around the hospital's outer ring. Chen Geng had come out of Whampoa and had no lack of combat experience. He had his "Red Squad" men dress as peddlers and passersby, standing watch in shifts, scattered through the streets and alleys on the lookout; at the first sign of trouble they were to draw and open fire, and fight to the death. At the same time they were to set alight the great stores of petrol and alcohol heaped beneath the hospital, covering the withdrawal of the delegates as they scattered out and got away fast in the cars rented in advance.

### 5. In by Groups, No One Allowed Out

As the work of the Central Special Branch and the congress preparatory group pushed steadily on, and Red May drew nearer by the day, Xiong Shihui could sit still no longer.

Every sign showed that the National Congress of Soviet Areas would surely be held in Shanghai, and in the concessions—and surely in the International Settlement at that. The trouble was that the vast International Settlement, running from the Huangpu River in the east to Jing'an Temple in the west, bordered by the Yangjingbang in the south and backed by Suzhou Creek in the north, spread over some tens of square kilometers, full of tall buildings and mansions, neighborhoods and lane-houses—so under which roof of them all would there gather so many people that merely to dream of them would keep a man awake the whole night through? And however knowing the concession authorities might be, they still could not tear off the mask altogether—throw fairness, openness, neutrality, and human rights all to the winds—and simply let Xiong Shihui's soldiers, police, gendarmes, and secret agents go house to house on concession ground, hunting and arresting, digging three feet into the earth to search.

At his wits' end, Xiong Shihui still pressed his No. 4 secret investigator, Song Zaisheng, to shoulder part of the burden and ease his pressing straits.

Xiong Shihui ordered Song Zaisheng to slip quietly into the heart of the concessions and make a thorough survey of the key points.

Song Zaisheng—originally named Song Qirong—was from Zhuji, Zhejiang. Honest and quick-witted, capable and thorough in his work, he had also served in a concession police station and was on good terms with the concession police, able to talk with them and trade intelligence—truly the ideal first choice. Song Zaisheng was moreover Xiong Shihui's fellow disciple: both were pupils of the Republican elder statesman Jiang Boqi.

Jiang Boqi too was from Zhuji, Zhejiang. He graduated from the Japanese Army Officers' Academy in 1904, joined the Tongmenghui in 1905, took part in the Revolution of 1911, and afterward held such posts as military affairs chief of the Guangdong military governor's office, military governor of Zhejiang, and chief examiner of cadets at the Baoding Military Academy—a man, one might say, of established fame and high repute.

It was while studying at the Baoding Military Academy that Xiong Shihui took Jiang Boqi as his teacher. When Jiang Boqi warmly recommended his own favorite pupil, Song Zaisheng, how could Xiong Shihui think of refusing?

Song Zaisheng too threw himself in with a will, never one to cling to life and fear death or play deaf and dumb. Seeing Xiong Shihui frantic as an ant on a hot pan over the coming National Congress of Soviet Areas, he set his men in plain clothes, disguised as peddlers hawking their wares along the streets, to thread through lane and alley into the heart of the concessions on their secret inquiries.

Just as Song Zaisheng's underlings were slipping into the heart of the concessions on their secret inquiries, the delegates from the major soviet areas and from the Red Army were arriving in Shanghai one after another, in a steady stream.

Li Weiwei says: "Following the CCP Center's instructions, the Central Special Branch first put the delegates up in small hotels near the venue, checked each one's identity, and then helped them make up and dress the part. The men put on long gowns and riding jackets, the women changed into qipao and silk stockings and added gold and silver jewelry, got up as young mistresses and wealthy wives. Then, group by group in stages, they were quietly brought to the venue and taken one by one into the hospital behind the Carlton Theater."

The Biography of He Changgong (2000) says that when He Changgong reached Shanghai he "first stayed at the appointed Qichang Hotel, waiting for someone to make contact—about two or three days before the connection was made. At the meeting, according to each participant's features, he was made up afresh and given a code name, and this He Changgong became 'his lordship.'"

Yi Hui too says: "The moment a delegate entered the hospital and took a sickroom, he was cut off entirely from the outside world, absolutely forbidden to go out at will. The Special Branch fighters got up as doctors, nurses, cooks, and attendants were all the same: none allowed into the street, none allowed to leave his post."

Hong Yangsheng, in "The Work of the First Section of the Central Special Branch" (2018), says: "This congress laid down that once a delegate had entered the venue he could not come out, but ate and slept on the spot, for safety's sake. The catering was seen to by the First Section, which specially assigned Zou Zhishu and two comrades to it."

### 6. The Internationale Rises

At about nine on the morning of May 5, 1930, the National Congress of Soviet Areas opened on time in the "hospital" behind the Carlton Theater, and all the assembled delegates struck up the stirring strains of the Internationale.

Li Weiwei says: "By my father's recollection, this congress had forty-seven or forty-eight people actually present. The Red Army delegates were two per corps, one officer and one soldier, and since they all came from the ranks he did not know a one of them. But he did remember two comrades: Teng Daiyuan, from Jiangxi, and He Changgong, from Hubei."

He Changgong, then secretary of the Fifth Column Committee of the Fifth Army of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army, took part in the National Congress of Soviet Areas in a double capacity: as military delegate for the Southeast Hubei district and as delegate of the Fifth Red Army. Traveling with He Changgong to the congress were several county Party secretaries and soviet delegates from Southeast Hubei, among them Wu Zimin, the CCP Hubei Provincial Committee's resident representative in Southeast Hubei; Cao Daquan, soviet delegate for the district; Yi Jinbo, secretary of the CCP's Tongshan County Committee; Fang Buzhou, secretary of the Yangxin County Committee; and Yu Haixia (Xu Ze), delegate of the industrial workers of the Daye Iron Mine. Wu Zimin was head of this region's delegation.

Wu Zimin, He Changgong, and the others also brought the Center a quantity of gold. This gold had been weighed on native scales in the base area, but weighed again on foreign scales in Shanghai it came out, astonishingly, several dozen taels over. The Center told He Changgong to use the surplus gold to bring out his wife and children, still in Huarong, and planned to place his wife, Meng Shuya, in work in Shanghai and send the child to the Soviet Union to be raised. He Changgong at once waved this away and firmly declined, again and again. This gold, He Changgong said, had not come easily; we have brought the Center this gold that the people of the base area gathered by hard toil, so let the Center use it to serve the Party's cause, use it to rescue the comrades who might at any moment be cruelly murdered by the enemy. He Changgong's sincerity moved the Center. With this gold the Center saved no small number of people from the reactionary authorities' prisons.

During the National Congress of Soviet Areas, the Fourth Red Army too sent funds to the Center. Xiao Ke, then chief of staff of the First Column of the Fourth Red Army and head of its training unit, remembered that in 1930, "in early May, the corps headquarters sent Xiong Shouqi, Party representative of the First Column, to Shanghai, to hand the Center some of the gold we had raised when we took Nanxiong and Dayu. It happened to fall just as the National Congress of Soviet Areas was opening, and so he took part as a delegate of the Fourth Red Army. But after the congress ended Xiong Shouqi never came back. In the spring of 1950 I ran into him in Hankou, and only then heard him tell it: on his way back from the Center in Shanghai to western Fujian he had been detained by the enemy at Xiamen; his family bailed him out and sent him to study two years at Fudan University in Shanghai, and later to study in Japan, after which he went to Sichuan to do social work." (Xiao Ke, 1997)

On the convening of the National Congress of Soviet Areas we may also read a piece the writer Ding Ling wrote in memory of her husband, Hu Yepin: "The Life of a True Man: Remembering Hu Yepin" (1950).

Hu Yepin, born 1903 in Minhou, Fujian, joined the Party in 1930 and held such posts as executive member of the League of Left-Wing Writers of China, chairman of its Worker-Peasant-Soldier Literature Committee, and chairman of its Worker-Peasant-Soldier Correspondence Committee; he took part in the National Congress of Soviet Areas along with Rou Shi, Feng Keng, and others, all of them as delegates of the "Left League."

Jiang Zulin, the son of Hu Yepin and Ding Ling, was a shipbuilding expert trained in the Soviet Union. Before he and his wife, Li Lingyuan, and their daughter Xiaoyan (Hu Yanni) moved to Beijing, they lived on Huating Road in Shanghai, not far from my own old home on Beijing West Road, and we saw a good deal of one another. Jiang Zulin said that his father, "when he came home from the National Congress of Soviet Areas, did not say much, and said nothing of what the congress was about, for it was a high Party secret and he could not speak of it freely. He only described the mood of the place. He said the hall was hung with the sickle-and-axe flag, and that at the opening they sang the Internationale softly—very solemn, very grand. He said too that at this congress he had seen some Red Army men, come from the soviet areas. He talked with them, and got some sense of life in the soviet areas and the Red Army, and was very taken with it, and very eager to write something out of the material."

Ding Ling, for her part, wrote in full in "The Life of a True Man: Remembering Hu Yepin" (1950): "Yepin suddenly went off to a meeting, keeping it even from me. He only told me he would not be back that night, and I did not ask him. Two days later he came home, and handed me a letter from Qu Qiubai. I guessed what he had been doing—I knew they had met—and then he told me that yes, there had been a meeting. The Communist Party leaders from all over had taken part, and he described the place to me. They had held it in the strictest secrecy. He said it was in a very grand foreign house, all like a mansion downstairs. Ladies were forever coming and going, playing cards and running the gramophone. The delegates from out of town went in one after another, and once in were shut up on the third floor. Up on the third floor the windows were kept shut most of the time. The Shanghai comrades went in last. Once in, the meeting began. The hall was hung all over with red sickle-and-axe flags—extremely solemn. Afterward the out-of-town people left first. As for what the meeting was about, Yepin never told me a word, so to this day I am not very clear what kind of meeting it was. But I could see it had stirred in Yepin a still deeper interest in politics."

The "Shanghai comrades" of whom Ding Ling speaks included not only Rou Shi, Feng Keng, and Hu Yepin, but also Xiang Zhongfa, chairman of the Standing Committee of the Politburo of the CCP Central Committee; Li Lisan, standing member of the Politburo and head of the Central Propaganda Department; and Xiang Ying, standing member of the Politburo and chairman of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions.

On Hu Yepin's part in the congress, Xia Yan writes in *Lazily Seeking Old Dreams* (2006): "In late May 1930, after Hu Yepin and Feng Keng had taken part in the Congress of Soviet Areas held in Shanghai, the 'Left League' decided to give all its members a report on it; but at the time it was very hard to find a venue that could hold forty or fifty people. I told Ozaki about it and asked him to help. At the time the Japanese were very powerful in Hongkou, and their premises even the Municipal Council dared not touch. He said readily: a fine chance—this month it is my turn to be in charge of the Japanese Journalists' Club in Shanghai; the club is generally empty except on Saturdays and Sundays, with only a single Chinese steward looking after it. Once you have fixed the date, I can send the steward away—but it must not run past six in the evening, for after that people may come to the club. And so it was: at the Japanese Journalists' Club near Zhapu Road in Hongkou we held a full membership meeting of more than fifty people. Ozaki Hotsumi was a most exact and thoroughly careful man; when he handed me the club keys he urged me again and again not to talk loudly, to tidy everything away after the meeting, and to leave no trace. But once the meeting began, and Hu Yepin gave his report, that restraint was broken. Some people shouted slogans—'Long live the soviets!' 'Defend the Soviet Union!'—which sent me running up and down, in a great state of nerves. Luckily nothing went wrong at that meeting."

### 7. There Is Only One Truth

On May 6, 1930, the National Congress of Soviet Areas entered its second day, and the delegates listened attentively to the reports of Xiang Zhongfa, chairman of the Standing Committee of the Politburo of the CCP Central Committee, and of Li Lisan, standing member of the Politburo and head of the Central Propaganda Department.

Li Weiwei says: "Because the congress was held against the background of the 'Li Lisan line,' all its documents bore the color of the 'Li Lisan line'—unavoidably, and there was no help for it. When Li Lisan gave his report, he departed from his text to say one thing: 'When we've taken Wuhan, then we'll tell the Third International.' This piece of bravado ran through the whole house in an instant, so that even my father and the others downstairs heard of it and knew of it—a remark that truly electrified the room."

Upstairs the report "electrified the room," and drew burst upon burst of applause.

Downstairs the Central Special Branch staff were on the highest alert—loose without, taut within.

Li Weiwei says: "The whole ground floor had turned into a gambling den. On the tables were pai gow tiles, dice, and mahjong, and my father and the others played the part of men playing cards, chatting, drinking tea. Even if someone came to look from outside, they could find no crack in it."

On May 7, the National Congress of Soviet Areas entered its third day. Teng Daiyuan, for the Military Commission of the Fifth Red Army, gave a long speech of nearly 15,000 words, while He Changgong set out, under seven main heads, the situation of the Fifth Column's opening of the Southeast Hubei base area. These seven were: local mass work and the local Party; the army's Party and the local Party; expanding the Red Army; economic life and the sick and wounded; the replenishment of military and political cadres; land revolution and class differentiation in the countryside; and the courier network between the Center and the provincial committees. He Changgong held that "the Fifth Column, for all its faults, was in its spirit of tough endurance, its heroic and unyielding revolutionary will, and above all its use of the Party branch as the core of leadership among the rank and file, a model worthy of study by all the brother units."

He Changgong's speech struck a chord with the delegates. They vied with one another to analyze and sum up the lessons of building the soviet areas, and called out strongly for the Center to correct the inconsistencies in its policy and lay down a practical, workable, all-round plan for strengthening the Party's organization and expanding the soviet areas. But the presidium could not let everyone speak their fill and get all that was in their hearts off their chests. For Gu Shunzhang had brought bad news: he said suspicious figures had already appeared around the venue.

The suspicious figures Gu Shunzhang spoke of were Xiong Shihui's henchmen and the informers of the concession police station.

For Xiong Shihui had made his final resolve—urgent matters handled with urgency, every light green—and, breaking his own rule, had approved a plan of targeted removal, agreeing that his henchmen and the concession police should join hands, split into two parties, and, along Park Road and Burkill Road, enter every door and question every person, choosing to search wrongly rather than miss anything at all.

And so the reactionary authorities' hawks and hounds at last fixed their target on the "hospital" behind the Carlton Theater.

The gate of the "hospital" was flung open with a heavy crash by the police-station detectives and Xiong Shihui's henchmen.

They found the whole consulting hall empty and still, not a soul in sight.

They rushed on into the rooms on either side, and found in each all manner of tables, chairs, and stools, tea things and fruit dishes and gambling gear of every kind—only not the shadow of a single person.

Hearing that it had all come to nothing—water drawn in a bamboo basket—Xiong Shihui simply stamped and beat his breast and raged like thunder. But then, turning to see his men come back beaten, more crestfallen and more heartsick even than he, he forced himself to show a great commander's bearing, produced 100,000 silver dollars on the spot, and rewarded the police-station detectives and his own henchmen with it separately.

Even Lord Guan, he said, lost Maicheng; even Kongming lost Jieting.

They, he said, "have no merit, but hard toil at least; and if no toil, then weariness at least."

Those money-grubbing lackeys all fawned on Xiong Shihui and swore their loyalty, calling their commander their father and mother reborn. For their commander they would serve heart and soul, before the saddle and behind the horse, to their last drop of blood.

Xiong Shihui never could make out how the delegates of the National Congress of Soviet Areas had got wind of the raid and withdrawn in time to escape.

He may have imagined many possibilities, but the one man he never suspected was Song Zaisheng.

For there was no reason to.

From first to last Song Zaisheng had worn himself out, forgotten sleep and food, been loyalty itself—how could he possibly be a "mole" who would "turn the tables"?

But this is precisely the spy war—the laying of traps and the countering of traps, the secret blow and the blow against it.

Chen Geng had arranged Zou Zhishu's marriage to Song Zaisheng, and Zou Zhishu had brought Song Zaisheng into the Party. From then on Song Zaisheng was an important source of intelligence for Chen Geng. So when Xiong Shihui had Song Zaisheng lead men to investigate the National Congress of Soviet Areas, Song Zaisheng turned around and reported it to Chen Geng. Chen Geng laid his plans at once, turned danger into safety, and did much to safeguard the smooth holding of the National Congress of Soviet Areas.

On June 10, 1930, the CCP Center issued its Circular No. 81, for a "movement to broaden the propaganda of the National Congress of Soviet Areas." The circular's very first sentence was: "The First National Congress of Soviet Areas has now closed, and this congress was a complete success." And the plainest mark of that "complete success" was the timely birth of the "soviet congress of workers, peasants, and soldiers."

The Central Special Branch had made an indelible contribution to "the correct road of the national revolution toward a soviet China"; but later that year Zou Zhishu was killed, an innocent victim, perishing cruelly at the brutal hands of Gu Shunzhang.

After the liberation of Shanghai, Zou Zhishu's daughter—only three years old when her mother was killed, and named, in a play on the safeguarding of the National Congress of Soviet Areas, Song Baosu, "Protect the Soviet"—who had once been given away, was found again through Zhou Enlai's warm concern. Song Baosu's son, Wu Guolin, is now in Shanghai; he says he asks for nothing else, only that his grandmother, who lies at rest in the Martyrs' Cemetery in Minhang District, Shanghai, be left in peace—no longer to be honored now as a hero and now spat upon as a degenerate.
