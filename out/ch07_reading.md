## Section 2. A Startling Debut

### 1. A Sudden and Urgent Sanction Order

In March of 1933 the Japanese Kwantung Army, heedless of the censure of world opinion, brazenly pushed into our country's Rehe province, and with small detachments launched harassing attacks severally at the passes beyond the Great Wall.

The Kwantung Army's deputy chief of staff, Itagaki Seishirō, was stationed at Tianjin, carrying out a secret scheme he had drawn up himself and that had the full backing of the Japanese military.

Itagaki's method was to use large sums of money to buy up our side's leftover warlords, disaffected politicians, local ruffians and hooligans, and to stir up trouble everywhere in the North China region centered on Beiping; his design was first to wreck public order, and then to plant a puppet regime that would take its orders from Japan.

By Itagaki's fond reckoning, even should he gain nothing, he could still sit back and watch Chinese kill one another, brewing civil disorder, and so reap the effect of gradually sapping China's national strength.

It was a fairly vicious plot.

The account above is a conclusion we worked out from the mutual corroboration of a great deal of intelligence material, checked once more against the general and conspicuous run of things.

Our working guideline was drawn up precisely against this conclusion.

Still, the “Beiping Station” was not large in scale, and what it could do, as it bore upon the larger situation, was in proportion very limited; besides “intelligence gathering,” listed as our basic task, we added a further line of work, “boring in and drawing out.” By “boring in” is meant finding a way, contriving a means, to get inside the seditious organizations that were plotting mischief; “drawing out” is to move those engaged in seditious activity to turn from their evil and come back to the right road, to return from a road once lost.

This task the “Beiping Station” assigned to Qi Nanpu. In Qi Nanpu's work report he mentioned that he set about it first by joining the secret societies. At that time the Beiping Branch of the Military Affairs Commission had issued orders to all the military, police, and gendarmerie organs within its jurisdiction to enforce precautionary measures—strict guarding against troublemaking by petty criminals, the banning of assemblies and marches, and the like. Little though we understood, this much was enough to reflect how uneasy and unsettled the times were.

At this juncture the head of the “Tianjin Station,” Wang Tianmu, who went under the cover name Zheng Shisong, moved his whole household to live in Beiping so that his daughter might attend school, and so our chances of contact with him grew more frequent than before. Once we were all well acquainted, we changed our address for him and called him “Elder Brother Wang.”

During his time in Beiping he often found the time to instruct us. He once said: “A man in secret-service work need not be a specialist, but he must be an all-rounder. For in the course of the work he must often place himself amid all sorts of different surroundings; must come into contact with persons of every different station; and will meet with all manner of different mishaps. So he must be widely informed and rich in common knowledge, that he may adapt to each turn as it comes and meet every case with ease.”

He would cite concrete instances too, expounding the local ways and customs of this place and that, down to the whole living panorama of every class of society. He held that even the matters of food and drink, of men and women, of the pleasures of the demimonde, all had to be known and understood. For instance: he took us along to have Western suits made, took us out to eat foreign-style banquets; and led us as well to go touring the brothels, and so on.

The “Xiao Fengxian” we often see in films and plays—part of that plot describes the story of General Cai E touring the brothels in the city of Beijing in the early Republican years. But the scenes and settings I saw on screen and stage were none too true to life, perhaps because, more than a dozen years apart, things had changed, or had been deliberately prettified to suit the dramatic form.

Beiping's rather famous “Eight Great Hutongs” was the so-called red-light district, called in some places the Pingkangli. In Beiping, though, these names were not used.

The “Eight Great Hutongs” lay outside Qianmen; they were several little lanes crossing lengthwise and crosswise—little alleys, that is—nearly all adjoining one another. Whenever the bright lamps first came on, two or three good friends would go down the main streets and through the small lanes, walking at their leisure; peering this way and that—this was what was called “touring.” Where the gateway was brightly lit and hung with a signboard bearing the four small characters “pure-singing house,” that was a first-class brothel.

If you wished to tour the brothels, you had only to go on in.

On through the gate—most had a spirit-wall—a bend to the left, a turn to the right, and going on inward, someone would lift the door-curtain and usher you toward the room, always first asking, as was their habit: “Have you an acquaintance here, or are you calling to view?” If you had an acquaintance, you named who it was; if not, he would raise his voice and give one long call, “Calling—to—view.” Why the long-drawn shout? So that the upper floor and the front and back courtyards could all hear it.

When the girls came out to be viewed, each in a different pose, meaning to entice, it still rested wholly on the customer's liking. As a girl came to the door, an attendant stood to one side and sang out her name, one for each that came, until the viewing was done; if the house had ten girls and only eight had been shown, the attendant would let the curtain fall and say in a low voice: “One is out on call and not yet back, one is ill and begs your leave.” Having thus explained, he would then ask you: “How do you find them…,” drawing the word “find” out very long, meaning to wait for your reply. If you had a mind to one, you told him straight out which; if not one pleased you, you shook your head and walked out, with no need to show any apology, for this was an everyday thing. Should you truly not have seen clearly, you could even have a “second viewing.” But that turned on whether you were spending “in earnest” or making trouble “on purpose.”

Once you had chosen a girl, the customer was first shown to a seat in the girl's “home” room. When you were settled, the maidservants would bring in a plate of melon seeds, open a tin of cigarettes, pour tea to honor the guest, ask your honorable surname, and begin to find a phrase or two for a little talk; and then, naturally: the customer studies the girl's looks at leisure; the girl slowly reckons up the customer's purse. This was called “mounting the tray” or “opening the tray,” and by a name peculiar to Beiping, “beating the tea-circle.”

By the going rate in 1933, at a first-class house the “tea-circle,” whatever the size of the party, was one silver dollar. If you opened a tin of the famous English cigarettes “Garrick” and had a dish of fruit brought as well, you usually gave two dollars. Now and then, meeting a “greenhorn” who did not know the ways and still gave only one dollar, they would never haggle over more or less. When Elder Brother Wang took us to open our eyes, he laid out five dollars—not to play the grandee, but to let us see something of the world.

Beiping's first-class houses were divided into two sections, the “fair beauties of the southland” and the “rouge of the north,” differing in style and in flavor. The southern houses, whether from Wuxi or Changzhou, all called themselves Suzhou women, but there were never any from north of the Yangtze; the northern houses were nearly all Beiping natives. As for those from the outlying counties near the capital, most sank into the second and third grades. This had not much to do with the fineness or plainness of their looks; it was owing to poor families, without the wherewithal to fit themselves out in clothes.

The girls of the southern houses always coaxed their own patrons, and found friends present rather in the way; the northern houses were otherwise—they treated their own patron like a husband at home, intimate yet mutually respectful as guest and host, and flattered the patron's friends in every way, only never to the point of impropriety. These were all arts of drawing custom, but in the customer's own feeling each had its merits.

The southern houses had endless devices for making the customer spend—today the “opening of business,” tomorrow the “unrolling of scrolls”—to show support you had to loosen your purse, and once you had opened your mouth, how could you but nod? Otherwise you would lose face to enter that door again. The northern houses kept a sense of measure and never opened their mouths before the time was ripe, unless they met a real sucker. They would even, with a show of frankness, think up schemes for you, how to manage so as to save money. In the end the money was spent, and yet they left you feeling very much at ease.

This is but the ABC of Beiping's flower-world; it may be taken as a piece of old hometown lore.

One evening Elder Brother Wang again took brother Bai Shiwei and me, three of us together, to tour Hanjiatan. Hanjiatan is a little lane, one of Beiping's “Eight Great Hutongs.” This quarter was nearly all Suzhou pure-singing houses. By “pure-singing” is meant selling one's song and not one's body—setting a high price on oneself, that is all. In Hanjiatan there was a “Flower-Cultivating House,” where Elder Brother Wang was acquainted with someone whose flower-name was Hanchun. This beauty was past the first bloom of youth, her charm at its finest, her talk and bearing not bad, though she could never quite escape a certain reserve. This was not necessarily a fault; some held that just this was dignity. It was rumored that Zhang Zongchang had once meant to take her out of the house, and she had stubbornly refused; the procuress-mother, tempted by the money, urged her to consent, but she would rather die than comply.

Elder Brother Wang egged me on to call for one too. This person's name was oddly chosen—decades on, I still remember she was called “Feilong,” Flying Dragon. Chubby and plump, of no great looks; but she had a mouthful of soft Wu speech, and a Suzhou woman speaking the Beiping dialect above all, all sticky-sweet and pleasant to the ear. We pressed brother Shiwei too to call for one as the mood took him, but not one caught his eye, and he begged off, saying he would wait for another house.

We sat in Hanchun's parlor talking, and Elder Brother Wang spoke of how he had come to know Mr. Dai Yunong in 1928 and afterward joined the work. Feilong, meanwhile, asked us again and again to sit in her room; we thought that sitting in anyone's room was all one, but it was not so—it turned out that for a first-time new guest not to go to the girl's own room did not count as a formal “mounting the tray,” and so one could not well ask for money; this too was a rule of theirs.

Just as we were laughing and carrying on in a fog of gaiety, someone who looked like Elder Brother Wang's driver was outside asking the attendant for us; Elder Brother Wang looked, and sure enough it was Old Xiao, and beckoned him forward. Old Xiao said that Mr. Zheng Jiemin had been looking everywhere for us, had been at it a good while; it was Mr. Zheng's own driver at the mouth of the lane who, seeing Elder Brother Wang's car and asking about it, had learned we were all here. Now Mr. Zheng was waiting for us in his car just outside the mouth of the lane.

Elder Brother Wang and I had not even the time to take out money and settle the tray; we asked brother Shiwei to stay and wait a while, and hurriedly took up our hats and rushed out. We had not gone far past the gate when we saw Mr. Zheng, alone, walking toward us in our direction.

On seeing Mr. Zheng, we both felt rather ashamed of ourselves. But Mr. Zheng had Elder Brother Wang walk him back, and so we returned once more to Hanchun's parlor.

Brother Shiwei rose, meaning at first to make some show of courtesy, then hesitated and held back; I guessed it was because a bow would not do and a handshake would not do either. It was Mr. Zheng who spoke, telling everyone to sit down so we could talk at ease.

Hanchun, Feilong, and the maidservants and little maids all came up to wait on us. Mr. Zheng asked: “Brother Tianmu, is there anywhere convenient to talk here?” Elder Brother Wang turned to ask Hanchun; Hanchun nodded and led us within.

This was a little inner suite, quite tastefully furnished, kept especially for guests to play at cards and show their support. There were just four of us, and they perhaps mistook our meaning and were about to draw up a table and set out the gaming things; Elder Brother Wang, seeing that things were amiss, at once whispered a word or two to Hanchun, and Hanchun, generous enough, poured the tea, brought in two more dishes of fruit, drew the door to, and they all withdrew.

Brother Shiwei looked carefully about him once more, front and back.

Mr. Zheng put away his smile, lowered his voice, and gravely delivered his instructions. He said:

“A little after five this afternoon the highest military and political authorities in Beiping summoned me before them and, face to face, charged me with a task whose importance bears upon the safety of the whole North China region;

“In the name of our organization, I have taken it on;

“This is a piece of action work; the object of the sanction is the former military governor of Hunan, Zhang Jingyao;

“The term set is one week reckoned from today, that is, seven days;

“The lead given us is that Zhang Jingyao has now slipped into the Legation Quarter of Beiping and is engaged in seditious activity. The further particulars we must reconnoiter for ourselves.”

After a slight pause, Mr. Zheng Jiemin earnestly encouraged us further, saying:

“This is a fine chance to rid the nation of a scourge and win glory for our body; success can steady the North China situation, and its effect, one may imagine, is great—we should spare no cost and throw our whole strength into it;

“This piece of work I have decided to entrust to the Tianjin and Beiping units to carry out jointly, with their strength concentrated; as for the division of the task, let you two, brothers Tianmu and Gongshu, weigh it out between yourselves;

“I shall at once telephone Mr. Dai to report the matter;

“At ten sharp tomorrow morning we shall meet at Fuyou Street to confer; the relevant matters we shall then discuss in full detail.” Mr. Zheng's declaration finished, and it was not yet midnight by the clock. He said he would go back first to send the telegram, and hoped we would exchange views at once and set to work.

This was a sudden and urgent sanction order, an action assignment for which we had no mental preparation whatever; hearing it all at once, we were struck blank and at a loss, altogether at our wits' end. For at this time our working organization was still in its founding stage, everything being felt out, trying to make a start. And the Beiping-Tianjin unit, only lately set up, was still small in scale, then bending its whole strength to laying out and opening up its intelligence lines; for action-sanction work it had not only no hands to spare, but lacked the equipment and gear it ought to have had, and—more important still—was utterly without experience of action work. Now, at a single word of command, the thing came up all in a rush. Before we knew it a myriad tangled threads came crowding in at once, for all the world like a duck driven up onto a perch. But where the order lay, duty allowed no turning back; we could only do our utmost and press bravely onward.

We saw Mr. Zheng off, and after we had all fallen silent a while, the people of the house came in again. Elder Brother Wang was quite bent on taking Hanchun out; he whispered in her ear a long while, but Hanchun seemed unwilling, and for the moment things came to a deadlock. Feilong went on asking us to her room, and we took the chance to rise, to shift our surroundings a little and spare everyone the awkwardness.

As Elder Brother Wang came out, he drew out two ten-dollar notes and laid them on the table; Hanchun had the maidservants give thanks, and came along with us to Feilong's room.

The style of Feilong's place, set beside Hanchun's, was a good deal poorer.

My mind was a bellyful of cares; I could scarcely wait to leave at once, and had no patience even to sit and drink a mouthful of tea. But Elder Brother Wang had his plan well formed; he pulled me down to sit and said: “I have a way; only keep your nerve.”

Elder Brother Wang turned again to Feilong: “We should like to ask you out for a bite to eat, and see you back once we've done; what do you say?” For all that Feilong spoke soft and limp, she answered crisply enough; she said in the Beiping dialect: “Good—I'll go with you anywhere, what of it? Only wait a moment for me, gentlemen, while I tell my mother and throw on a cloak; I'll be right along.” Elder Brother Wang, with Hanchun standing there, feared she would feel slighted, and had to smooth it over, saying this was all to bring us two together, and that he would surely come another day to show his support.

By now it was already twelve, deep in the night; out on the road beyond the lane there were still one or two little Jiangsu-Zhejiang eating-houses that catered to the late-supper trade, their lamps still bright, doors open and not yet shut. We ordered a few things at random, unable to fathom what fine scheme Elder Brother Wang was hatching.

When at last we had all finished eating, Elder Brother Wang spoke up: “In the Legation Quarter there are only two most likely places—one is the Japanese Legation, one is the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits. As for that other, apartment-style hotel, I don't think he would go there.”

And he said to me: “I mean to make a trip there right now; if by chance I can take a room, a night's stay might turn up some signs. What do you think?”

What views could I have? Had I been sent to take a room, I would not even have known how to walk in at the door of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, let alone go probing for anything.

Then Elder Brother Wang set out his concrete plan more fully: “It is not that your elder brother lacks self-respect—and you mustn't take it amiss—but it would be best if Miss Feilong here would put herself out and, quite openly, come along with me. Think: a single man at this hour taking a room without luggage is bound to draw suspicion; but a man and a woman—that is far, far better.”

At this Elder Brother Wang turned to Feilong again: “Just now Hanchun would not agree to come out, and there was nothing I could do; now I ask you to lend a hand this once. Afterward you will know what a deed of hidden merit it was!”

And pointing at me he said to Feilong: “He is my younger brother, and you shall be my younger sister-in-law; your elder brother here would never do a thing to wrong a friend—you have only to come along with me this once. As for whatever rules you have, you say how it is to be done and I'll do just that.”

Feilong looked at me, hesitated a while, and asked me, her face reddening: “And what do you say?” “I say do as our elder brother says; every word of his is the plain truth.” Feilong signaled her assent, only she must have a word with her mother.

Elder Brother Wang said to her: “We'll go first to the Legation Quarter and have a look; if we can take a room, there'll be time enough to telephone then—we'll see you home in any case.”

The hour was late and there was no delaying further, so Elder Brother Wang took Feilong into the car and went off first. Brother Shiwei and I hired a rickshaw and returned to No. 18 Beichang Street.

Brother Shiwei and I said nothing for a good while. I was reckoning how I might be of use, and meaning as well to send first thing in the morning for brother Qi Nanpu, to talk it over and arrange the many things that would need doing.

Brother Shiwei sat alone in a chair, smoking hard, neither sleeping nor speaking. When he had made up his mind, he rose all at once and asked me: “Brother Gongshu, tell me—would it be well for me to be the one to do it?”

I too was fretting over just this. For the “Beiping Station” had as yet no action-work section, and all told there were but three of us—Yang Ying, Old Qi, and I; Wang Yunsun, newly come to the work, handled the paperwork and naturally could not manage such a thing; brother Shiwei dealt with intelligence material, and I had never thought to set him to action work. Our closeness came of getting on well together and finding each other good company, so that we often went out and came in together, with nothing else behind it. Had I asked him on my own initiative, it would have squared with neither feeling nor reason, and had there been the least reluctance it would have been more awkward still. Now that brother Shiwei had volunteered of himself, it was of course the very thing I could have wished.

I said: “Good—the very best.” I wanted to find a fitting word or two more to show what was in my heart, but I truly did not know how best to put it.

Bai Shiwei, courtesy name Zilian, was a Shandong man, long since settled in Beiping. A seventh-class graduate of the Military Academy, he had done Party work in Funing county, Hebei; his parents were living, he was married, with two daughters. His old father and elder brother were both in trade, and the family was not especially well-off. Qi Nanpu and Bai Shiwei were classmates of the same class; meeting in Beiping, Qi urged him not to go back to Funing, and so he stayed on and joined our work.

When brother Shiwei joined the work, we had just gathered a batch of material on the Northeastern Army and the volunteer forces beyond the passes, and we asked him to help put it in order for the time being, to be assigned other suitable work later.

Now, with this chance come up all of a sudden, I naturally hoped our “Beiping Station” might show what it could do—the more so as the matter bore upon the larger situation. That brother Shiwei feared no hardship or danger and dared to press bravely to the fore was, win or lose, our glory.

What with our tangled feelings and our over-excitement, Shiwei and I never slept easy that night.

Before seven the next morning I telephoned and asked brother Nanpu over. When we met, I gave him in brief the matter Mr. Zheng had charged us with the night before, and asked him at the same time to make a solid reconnaissance of Zhang Jingyao's movements as fast as he could.

I knew that at the west end of the Legation Quarter—that is, entering the Quarter's west mouth from Hubu Street and turning left—there stood a row of three-story houses, among them an apartment-style hotel that, in grade, could rank no higher than second-rate; on my judgment, it was most unlikely Zhang would put up in such a place. Still, to avoid regret afterward, one ought to go and see for certain. I put this notion to brother Nanpu.

Another matter, also urgent, was to have brother Nanpu find the driver Mr. Dai had hired for his car, get the car out of the repair shop, and give it a trial run—it might come in useful.

I told brother Nanpu once more what brother Shiwei had said plainly to me the night before—only five or six hours since. Brother Nanpu was of course overjoyed, delighted beyond measure.

At ten in the morning I was to go over to Mr. Zheng Jiemin's for the conference. I asked brother Shiwei whether he wished to come with me; he thought it fitter to stay here and await our decision.

Just before I left, I suddenly thought of a pistol Mr. Dai had given me as a keepsake; I hurriedly found it out and, together with the only six cartridges I had, handed the whole to Shiwei.

Brother Tianmu and I reached Fuyou Street two or three minutes apart, one after the other; Mr. Zheng had long been waiting for us in his little office.

First, Mr. Zheng told us he had already wired the main points of the matter to Mr. Dai. Now he would hear our report; and then go to Zhongnanhai to report back to Minister He.

Brother Tianmu reported his reconnaissance and its results: the night before, after we had parted, he had taken, in the name of the Japanese Ōkura Company, a room on the second floor of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits in the Legation Quarter, and still kept it, not yet given up. In his dealings with the front desk downstairs and with the head steward—the foreman—on the second floor, he had made no discovery as yet.

Brother Tianmu added that, since in the hours from deep night to morning ordinary comings and goings were sparse and still, it was hard to discover anything, and he meant to go back and go on reconnoitering.

What brother Tianmu thought most troublesome was the question of whether Zhang Jingyao might be hidden inside the Japanese Legation. On this point none of us could pass judgment offhand.

The first thing I reported was that Comrade Bai Shiwei had offered himself and would willingly undertake the “execution”; before the “Beiping Station” had any dedicated action personnel, this solved the greatest difficulty. As to whether he was equal to it, there was no need to worry at all, for he had every qualification and was willing and glad.

The second thing concerned the disposition of the work, divided into two steps: what was necessary beforehand—that is, what we should set about now—and what would be fixed once the target was found; for both the “Beiping Station” could take full charge.

We who lacked experience would, of course, seek instruction at every turn from Elder Brother Wang at Tianjin.

Our conference came here to a pause.

Mr. Zheng told us he was about to go up to see the highest authority in Beiping, both to report our preparatory work and reconnaissance and, secondly, in hopes of learning a little more of what was to follow.

Mr. Zheng disclosed to us what he himself understood: “The source of the intelligence very likely comes from a mole taking part in Zhang Jingyao's seditious activity.”

### 2. Reconnaissance Deployed with All Our Might

At its founding the “Beiping Station” was truly starting from nothing; not only were hands few, but much of the equipment it should have had was wanting—to take our conveyances, for one, we had but a single bicycle.

In this there was no special reason; our early work, from the Bureau headquarters at Nanjing down to the units in every province and city, high and low, was all pinched for want of funds.

Even so, Mr. Dai did not like his subordinates to ask anything of others. Were we now to put any request to the authorities in charge, reasonable though it was, it always felt not quite fitting at just this critical pass. So I thought for my own part, and Mr. Zheng Jiemin was much of the same mind; and so we decided to solve our own difficulties ourselves. This, indeed, was the meaning of doing all that lies in oneself.

A little after ten that morning, when we had held a first consultation with Mr. Zheng, he made ready to go up and see Minister He (acting chairman of the Military Branch Council). Elder Brother Wang and I went by the same car to Beichang Street to join up with brother Shiwei.

We three, on the principle just discussed, exchanged views once more and agreed to proceed each on his own part:

brother Shiwei to go with Elder Brother Wang to the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits and watch for any stir;

I to arrange with brother Nanpu the necessary further preparations.

All that needed doing had to be done; having arranged to meet brother Nanpu, we went first to “make a round” of Beiping's special district, the “Legation Quarter.”

The “Legation Quarter” in Beiping might be reckoned a land “beyond the pale,” a humiliating brand left by the Boxer Protocol, commonly called the “legation district.” By 1933, though many foreign legations had already moved to Nanjing, this place still kept its special status, and our government could not exercise all the powers the law conferred.

Hence those harboring evil designs and plotting mischief made use of this speck of ground, sheltering under the wing of foreign power, to carry on their unlawful doings.

The “Legation Quarter” was not large; its whole area was only about two square kilometers. It lay between Beiping's Zhengyangmen (Qianmen) and Chongwenmen (Hademen).

Its bounds on the four sides were these: the eastern entrance was on Chongwenmen Street; the western entrance on Hubu Street; to the north it fronted East Chang'an Street, and between East Chang'an Street and the entrance lay a great parade ground where foreigners often played football. As a boy I would often ride my bicycle through here by a shortcut, and the memory is with me yet. On the south stood an old city wall in which, near the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, a gap had been opened, with no formal name; it was generally called the “Water Gate.” In truth, a hole opened in a city wall may well be called a gate, but there was not so much as a ditch hereabouts, and where the “water” came from, no one knew.

Out of the “Water Gate” was the platform of the East Railway Station, and further on there were rails, but no level-crossing gear, so vehicles could not pass. The East Station was the starting point of the Beining line and the terminus of the Beiping-Tianjin through trains. Foreigners getting off the train mostly entered the Legation Quarter by the “Water Gate,” and all sorts of mysterious personages could take this hidden byway too.

Within the “Legation Quarter,” besides the resident units of the several nations' legations and their attached organs—the “counselors' offices,” the “military attachés' offices,” and so on—there were a few troops stationed as well. These were generally called “barracks,” though in fact they were the legations' “guard detachments.” As of 1933 there were still the two, the American barracks and the Japanese barracks; whether there were also others, British, French, and the like, I cannot at the moment say.

There were police within the district too, few in number, seldom to be seen on the street, one or two at most. As to what uniform they wore, my impression is now blurred; but I remember they carried only truncheons and no firearms. There seemed to be one loitering about at the door of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, who would sometimes vanish again.

Within the “Legation Quarter” all the roads were asphalt, and the most unforgettable was that east-west trunk road. To my mind, wherever in the world a road may be, the smoother the better; only this one had been deliberately worked to be uneven, with a raised ridge running across the road-face about a hundred meters apart—a perfectly good road turned into a wave-pattern, a marvel indeed. Turning it over, one supposes it was to check speeding vehicles to good effect, and at the same time it reflected the shortage of police. By 1938, when I again walked the “Legation Quarter” in Beiping, this queer road had already been paved smooth.

Of vehicles running in the “Legation Quarter,” about three kinds were commonly seen: motorcars, rickshaws, and bicycles. These vehicles coming and going did not all necessarily belong within the district; some merely borrowed the way to pass through, and in such cases they were let through, no one minding. Within the “Legation Quarter” it was hard to hail a taxi; to telephone into the city took a special arrangement, and to have one come at call was a matter of luck. The rickshaws within the “Legation Quarter” were licensed by the district itself and had a good many points of difference. For instance: the bodies were all painted the plain color of the wood, and every cushion was white bordered with blue, so that they looked very clean. The pullers, though they wore no uniform, were all neatly dressed. They had fixed stands and never touted for passengers about the streets. At the door of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits there were regularly three or five waiting for hire. I rode in them too; the fares were much dearer than outside the boundary. The pullers here seemed to have a foreigner-flattering turn of mind: the moment a foreigner got in, they showed themselves specially hard-working, whereas to pull a Chinese was as though they had suffered a wrong. Motorbicycles were not many in those days; the odd one seen was nineteen in twenty of military use. Bicycles were plenty, mostly used by the district residents' servants or by the schoolchildren.

At the two boundaries between the Beiping city district and the “Legation Quarter” there were wooden barriers, but no one guarding them. For a spell, at twelve sharp, midnight, the northern barrier-gate onto East Chang'an Street would be closed, leaving only the east-west way open; but at other times it was thrown open all night, passage unhindered.

Brother Nanpu and I, seated in that car not yet taken away, driven by Mr. Dai's driver Old Zhang, went round and round several times on the few roads there were within the “Legation Quarter”; we not only learned the lay of the roads but also readied ourselves in mind.

I worried that once there was a stir—a gunshot, say—the wooden barriers at the several entrances might suddenly be shut and block our way. Then again I thought: why should this be a worry? Barriers merely for form's sake like these, with a car like ours, one press of the accelerator would smash them to bits; how could they hold us?

Going along the “Water Gate” wall, we suddenly found there was yet another road one could take. Coming out of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits and going south thirty to fifty paces, one reached the “Water Gate”; a turn to the right and one was on this newly discovered road, driving straight west, the high city wall on the left and, on the right, mostly the back doors of the buildings on that main street in front. Where we passed there was a sentry gate—the back gate of the “Japanese barracks”; and one or two hundred feet on, another American sentry post, the back gate of the “American barracks.” Reaching the end, one could only turn right, go on, then turn left, and one was at the exit. The main road out was Hubu Street, already outside the bounds of the “Legation Quarter.”

This was a trial run, and also a first survey of the ground and its features.

We returned to Beichang Street and from afar saw Mr. Zheng's car parked under a great tree. It turned out Mr. Zheng was already waiting for us—surely on some urgent matter.

Mr. Zheng told us that, according to word received, “Zhang Jingyao is indeed staying in the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits in the Legation Quarter.” He directed us to inform brother Tianmu at once, and hoped we would do our utmost to complete the mission.

It was now a little after four in the afternoon; to telephone Elder Brother Wang was not quite proper, and for me to go to the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits to find him and pass it on face to face might draw notice—we had agreed to meet at six that evening, and since it was but an hour and more off, better to wait for him.

Brother Nanpu and I, the two of us, used this little time to lay a further plan between us:

As for the term, there were still six days; now that Zhang's whereabouts were fixed, we had at least a grasp of the main direction, and on the face of it time was not pressing.

The first thing to accomplish was to reconnoiter out the room Zhang was staying in, and the sooner the better, lest a long night breed dreams.

Before we acted, we must on no account startle the snake by beating the grass.

Once Zhang's traces were found, the other attendant problems could all be solved step by step.

We talked a good while, and it all came in the end to one sentence: to find the “target” early.

At five-fifty brother Shiwei telephoned to say that he and Elder Brother Wang were waiting for me at the Wufangzhai in the Dong'an Market to eat together; I promised to come with brother Nanpu.

Elder Brother Wang and brother Shiwei wore grave looks, with no heart even to talk; no need to ask—there had been no progress.

I repeated word for word what Mr. Zheng Jiemin had just come to Beichang Street to tell us.

Then I added, with weight in my voice, my own opinion: “I hold that the news Mr. Zheng conveyed must be reliable; that old rascal we are after is beyond doubt in the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits.”

Brother Nanpu then, in his unhurried way, went over once more those self-evident points the two of us had planned. Elder Brother Wang and brother Shiwei would have thought of them without our saying.

A meal hastily done, and what was to be said pretty well said, we looked again at Elder Brother Wang's and brother Shiwei's faces, which had cleared and brightened.

We shared out once more the tasks each was to bear, the weight of it still on the reconnaissance Elder Brother Wang and the others would do on returning to the hotel.

We agreed to keep No. 18 Beichang Street as our liaison center, where I would stay on watch the whole time, awaiting good news.

Brother Nanpu's reconnaissance arrangements outside the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits were all to be called off. The spare car was put in brother Nanpu's charge, and he was to keep in close touch with me at all times.

After we parted, I made a telephone call and reported briefly to Mr. Zheng.

On the first day of the term, a full twenty-four hours' floundering brought no result; on the second, though we knew for certain Zhang Jingyao must be staying in the not-large Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, we still could not find a trace of him.

A thing that looks easy—only in the doing does one taste how it is not simple; or else we were too stupid.

I fretted alone that, should the term run out and we not lay hold of so much as a shadow of the man, that would be to lose face indeed.

Mr. Zheng, for his part, was quite composed; there was not even a note of urging in him, and what he charged us to do he said but once. Whether his mind was truly so, that was very hard to guess.

Mr. Dai wired his reply to Mr. Zheng, and did not telegraph us directly—this, of course, was to preserve the integrity of the chain of command.

By noon of the third day, when I met with Elder Brother Wang and the others, we still looked at one another blankly, still far from reaching a happy pass.

Elder Brother Wang counseled us: “In a matter like this one can only fret inwardly and must not let it show. If we stay on and still cannot make out any thread of it, I am half of a mind to risk startling the snake and go to the front desk to inquire once more; but perhaps that would be too clever by half and turn out ill instead.”

At this point Elder Brother Wang suddenly recalled that the day before, going upstairs, he had once seen the back of a man very like Zhao Tinggui, who had been Zhang Jingyao's chief of staff; but in the blink of an eye the man had gone up, and he had not seen clearly, so he could not yet be sure it was he.

I turned it over in my mind: since Elder Brother Wang's room was on the second floor, to see that man “go up” meant either the third floor or the fourth. If it were really Zhang's chief of staff, then Zhang Jingyao, if not on the third floor, must be on the fourth.

Was this not a discovery of value?

Brother Shiwei told me too that in the guise of Elder Brother Wang's “attendant”—his “man,” that is—he went in and out with Elder Brother Wang, a thing so commonplace in a great hotel that the stewards thought nothing of it. While Elder Brother Wang was shut alone in the room laying his plans, Shiwei would seize the chance to chat idly with the stewards, but he could chat nothing to the purpose out of them either.

Brother Shiwei said further: “Elder Brother Wang charged me to keep a special eye on that man who looked like the chief of staff, Zhao; I too kept watching the staircase, my eyes going up and down, but I never saw a man such as Elder Brother Wang had described.”

I thought this was all gain; though we had not yet found the target, it could not be counted as no progress at all.

Elder Brother Wang wanted to make a trip home to change his clothes and shave. He asked brother Shiwei too to find a place to rest a while, and when he came back they would go together to the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits again. He charged us as well to study the thing over well once more.

Elder Brother Wang went home, and brother Shiwei had meant to take a nap to refresh himself, but not only could he not sleep, he could not so much as close his eyes, and so he sat up to talk; and thus we came again to speak of the “Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits.”

The “Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits” was of no small fame in Beiping, but not large in scale, being no more than a four-story old-style building. In its appointments it fell far short of the “Hôtel de Pékin”; in value for money it was no match for the “Central Hotel” or the “Chang'anchun Hotel”; only, being the one such hotel in the “Legation Quarter,” it seemed a rarity—especially to those Chinese who had their own peculiar uses for it.

The “Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits” faced west with its back to the east, its main door on the street; to go in and out one climbed a dozen or more stone steps. What left the deeper impression was that revolving door, turning round and round.

In at the door was a spacious lobby, the floor laid with a scarlet woolen carpet; set off so, it gave one all at once a sense of the high-class—the imported foreign gimmick, just that.

Along the long counter was the reception clerk who managed the cars, in a Chinese-style long gown and sleeveless jacket and a black-satin skullcap with a red knot—the ugly getup by which the “imperialists” looked down on Chinese as slaves, offensive to see.

As for this ground floor, what else was set out here I no longer clearly remember—probably there were no guest rooms.

The “Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits” had no elevator, perhaps because it was only four stories. But the staircase was broad, wide enough for four abreast going up and down. The width of the second-floor passage was much the same as the staircase, all carpeted, so that unless one skipped and hopped, no footstep could be heard.

The second floor was shaped like an inverted U—that is, there were rooms on only three sides, and the side near the stair-head was just a passage. On the passage stood a small counter, the central point where a steward was on duty, waiting for guests to call.

The stewards were all got up alike, a rare enough sight: they wore long gowns, but cut short by a stretch, reaching only to the knee—called a half-gown—with a long satin sash at the waist, old-style trousers tied at the ankle, white cloth socks, and a pair of long-nosed leather-soled cloth shoes of black gongni serge, and a melon-rind skullcap too. Look at it—what sort of figure was that? It was simply a degradation of a man.

And to see further their cringing, knee-bending image as they waited on foreigners was truly a disgrace for Chinese. But they too did it to earn money and keep their families fed.

Elder Brother Wang's room was in a corner of the second floor, some fifty or sixty paces from the staircase, at the end of the right-hand corridor. Going further in, there was a row of transverse rooms, and turning round them one came to the left-hand corridor—hence its being called an inverted-U shape.

This room was not large, but the bed was; the mattress and sheets laid on it ran to several layers, thin and thick. Brother Shiwei told me: “That pistol—it is not convenient to carry on one's person going in and out, so sometimes it is tucked under the pillow, and Elder Brother Wang knows it too, so that if any sudden chance should come, either of us could take it up and use it.”

Brother Shiwei was an attentive man; he heard a steward say: “There's a fire escape at the back too, unused for years now, all crammed with old broken things.” So said, the fire escape that might have served as a way out had fallen into disuse, and need not be reckoned with.

The layout of the third floor was much like the second. The fourth floor was quite another matter: it was said that on this fourth floor the rooms available for hire were only half those of the second and third, the other half being for long-term guests to live in, and differing in appointments, room size, and rent. Shiwei had several times wanted to go up and look, but he could never find a pretext that held together and would not draw suspicion.

Just as we were talking of this, Elder Brother Wang came back; he and brother Shiwei would go to the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits again—they must, after all, find some thread or trace.

Time slipped away fast, and as the term drew nearer and nearer, besides the fretting of our hearts it was as though we had lost something. Unlooked for, on the fourth day the road wound round the peak and a first light broke.

It was thus:

At noon, as Elder Brother Wang and brother Shiwei were coming downstairs, meaning to go to eat, just as they went to push the door, out from within turned an acquaintance, a little blue-cloth bundle tucked under his arm—it was the proprietor of the tailor shop, Manager Ying; who would have thought to meet him here?

Elder Brother Wang asked Manager Ying: “What brings you here?” Manager Ying, offhand, gestured with his hand up and down on the right side of his chin and said: “He's had two suits made, and told me to come today to fit them; he's probably up by now?” From Manager Ying's gesture and words Elder Brother Wang had already caught his full meaning, so without asking more he signaled brother Shiwei to push the door and go out, at the same time giving Manager Ying a light nod of greeting.

They gave up going to eat and hurried to Beichang Street to tell me what had just happened.

Elder Brother Wang explained: “The one with a tuft of hair on his chin is Zhang Jingyao; just now in the hotel it was not convenient to ask much; let's go eat something first, and by the time we've done, Manager Ying will be back too, and we'll go find him and ask him the whole thing in detail.”

We three came again, as usual, to the South Street of Dongsi Pailou, where Manager Ying's Yingyuantai tailor shop was. This tailor shop had but a single frontage, rustic and unadorned, with nothing to draw a customer if one were not an acquaintance. But at the craft of cutting he was a first-rate hand.

We had meant to make clothes for a pretext, to find a chance to chat more with him, but before we could open our mouths Manager Ying said: “These two days I have work to rush; if you gentlemen want to add to your wardrobe, I'm afraid you'll have to wait some days. I must first get out these two suits for Superintendent Zhang, and then there are several other customers'. Better pick out a few fabrics now, and when I am free I'll cut for you.”

Elder Brother Wang asked Manager Ying in passing: “Superintendent Zhang is waiting to wear them?” Manager Ying said: “That's right; he told me to have them done and delivered by noon the day after tomorrow without fail, saying he might be going back to Tianjin within a day or two.”

We three lingered a while more with Manager Ying around this matter, and the weightiest few sentences that came out of Manager Ying were: “Superintendent Zhang is staying on the third floor, three rooms in a row, numbers 231 to 235; besides Superintendent Zhang himself, there are his chief of staff and his aide. Where I go to fit the suits is the middle one of the rooms.”

“It came without the least trouble”—in truth, it was both coincidence and lucky chance.

For now, though Zhang Jingyao's whereabouts were had, close on their heels came many hard problems still; what we had not thought of was that Zhang was not alone.

Zhang had taken three rooms—he lodged in one, the chief of staff in one, and the other was the aide's. The so-called aide might well be a guard. Manager Ying had not said how many aides; there might be more than one, and reckoning them all up, at the least three, at the most five or six—could brother Bai Shiwei, “single spear and lone horse,” deal with them? Even if Elder Brother Wang joined the fighting line, it was still no match in numbers.

Their rooms, three in a row—leaving aside for the moment which one Zhang Jingyao lodged in—at the first stir would surely rouse those to left and right at once; with the strength we now had, we could send in at most two men and a single gun, and if the other side put up the least resistance or drag, then to get clear of the scene became a problem; that is, in a case of the few against the many, only a surprise blow would serve, and once it came to a standoff, we were bound to be at a disadvantage.

Furthermore, they were on the third floor, we on the second; reckoning from the moment of mounting to the third floor, one had first to cross a corridor scores of paces long; supposing no obstruction, there was still the door to be got open by ruse or by force, the target to be found; even if one pushed the door and came upon him and fired at once, then, the shots sounded, even granting no one dared come near, one had still to make from the third-floor corridor straight for the staircase and down again by the second. At the most hopeful estimate, the time exposed was still too long. So whether one could safely walk out the main door needed yet closer planning.

That revolving door was the sole way out; should an accident occur, would it not shut of itself? I believe there must be such a device—this was the more worth special heed.

What made us most anxious was that Zhang Jingyao was about to leave, so that we must act quickly and could do no otherwise; time did not allow us to lay long plans or make a leisurely disposition. A thing like this could not be set out so four-square and even; too much caution, on the contrary, would tie our hands and feet. To gain the advantage of time, what we needed most was to carry it through in one bold breath.

We three together reported to Mr. Zheng what had passed in this while, and Mr. Zheng was uncommonly pleased and encouraged us again.

Elder Brother Wang too declared his resolve.

Brother Shiwei was as high in spirit as ever, rubbing his fists and wiping his palms, only waiting for the startling first cry.

My own greatest worry was the fear that brother Shiwei would be alone and outmatched; a means of support had to be devised.

After we took our leave, in the car Elder Brother Wang comforted us again and again, saying he had ways in plenty, and urging us on no account to be over-hasty. He lowered his voice and said: “I can work on that chief of staff, Zhao; if the back I saw the day before yesterday was really his, then there is a fine article to be made of it. Wait till I go back and sketch it out carefully—maybe this is the best road to take.”

Elder Brother Wang was very cool, quite with the air of a man with the bamboo already formed in his breast.

I too was thinking of schemes at every moment, but I could put forward nothing concrete and good, for a thing like this could not be “tried out,” and besides I was truly without any experience.

Before we parted, we made several necessary agreements on what could be foreseen.

Elder Brother Wang and brother Shiwei were to go back to the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits.

I decided to go first and seek out brother Qi Nanpu. I meant to ask him whether he could at once find one or two men to be brother Shiwei's helpers; and I hoped too that he could contrive to get hold of a gun over there—borrowed or bought, no matter which.

When I had seen brother Nanpu and explained my purpose, what he said had reason in it too. He said: “In the past we simply had no plan for action work, nor had the higher-ups given the least hint of it; and now, to do a thing the moment it is said, to have a thing the moment it is wanted—where, in a flash, is one to find usable men? One cannot just drag in one or hire one. Now, since it is to lend Shiwei a hand, for the public good and the private alike I have a duty I cannot shirk; so I am willing to join the scene, and as for which task I take, only assign it to me.”

Hearing him declare himself so, I was very glad at heart—here indeed was a timely rain, a fresh reinforcement.

I raised the matter of borrowing a gun again; he said: “Borrow—where's one to borrow? Buy a knife; is it not just as serviceable?”

Brother Nanpu and I agreed that from now on we would keep in touch at all times, and as for how the work was to be shared, I would let him know once I had conferred with Elder Brother Wang and reached a decision.

The car in brother Nanpu's charge was to stay on call, and that driver Zhang too was to be well treated.

I returned alone to Beichang Street, and it came to me all at once that four days had already gone by. I looked for good news from brother Shiwei, watching his chance at the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, but through the whole long night there came not even a wrong-number call.

### 3. The Old Saw Proved True: Heaven's Net Is Wide

This day was 7 May 1933.

Early in the morning the courtyard and the rooms were all quiet, without the least disturbance. My whole heart hung on brother Shiwei and Elder Brother Wang and the others in the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, not knowing whether they were still in a hard case or had made some new progress. I thought: before long they must telephone.

Then another turn of thought: I could not wait like a fool, looking to others; better that I too do my share of the work.

I ought to send for Yang Ying too; I needed to talk it over with him and hear as well how he viewed the thing. For I had a notion I could not put into words—how, that is, to do all that lay in me to strengthen our force, since our manpower was truly too thin. Should there be no very complete good plan, then as a last resort we could all throw ourselves in and go at it with everything!

I telephoned Yang Ying and asked him over for a talk. It was not yet nine when, just as Yang Ying came in at the door, the telephone rang. I listened—it was Elder Brother Wang; he said: “In fifteen minutes I can be there; please wait for me.” I had been waiting for him all along; this meant there was a new development.

Yang Ying had a bit of a mule's temper; for all his bookish, scholarly air, prick him and spur him and what we could do he would do too. Before I had time to talk with Yang Ying, brother Nanpu, waiting with the car at the door of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, telephoned again to tell me: “They've gone out.” He meant Elder Brother Wang and brother Shiwei. I answered him: “They're coming here; you can cruise about, and in an hour—that is, before ten-thirty in the morning—come back to your place.”

As I spoke, Elder Brother Wang and brother Shiwei arrived too.

Elder Brother Wang set forth the two courses he had already worked out ripe:

He held that the surest way was to fetch to Beiping from Tianjin an old friend of his close enough to count on, a man surnamed Hou, a Henan man, who had knocked about the rivers-and-lakes many years and had now washed his hands of it; if this man would nod his consent and join hands with brother Shiwei, then however many the other side numbered, it would be no matter.

Elder Brother Wang added: “I know he has a piece—a gun—in hand, so we need not trouble over that; whether he agrees or not, I mean to send your elder sister-in-law over—she'll catch a train before noon and be back before supper; even if he won't, it will hold up nothing of ours.”

Excellent. True enough, I had once met this man at Elder Brother Wang's house, a little over forty, solidly built; by his dress and gear he seemed well-off, and the fear was only that he would not care to run the risk again. Still, this at least was a plan.

To speak fairly, this was only the first half of a plan.

Elder Brother Wang went on: “Before the action, I can go up to the third floor and knock, asking for the chief of staff, Zhao. If an aide answers the door, I say I've come to call on the chief of staff; if Zhao himself answers, we're acquainted, and he can make nothing of what I'm at now, so I'll play to his liking and hunt out just the words he likes to hear; if Zhang Jingyao himself comes to open the door, whether or not he still remembers such a one as me, I'm still there to see his chief of staff. In this way our re-check is done.”

“By my judgment, it won't rouse their suspicion; and even should suspicion arise, it will no longer matter.

“The moment I come down from the third floor, brother Shiwei and Old Hou, seeing my hand-signal, rush straight up to the third floor and do it; however they guard themselves, they'll be caught off their guard.”

Just so; I thought the same. Blend my thoughts with Elder Brother Wang's—whether brother Nanpu, brother Yang Ying, or even the invited Mr. Hou were the one to second brother Shiwei, or all took the stage together—this was a fine plan.

We may as well hear Elder Brother Wang's other plan too.

Elder Brother Wang said: “Didn't Manager Ying say that tomorrow he'd deliver the two rushed suits to Superintendent Zhang at the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits? We'd take the chance to follow Manager Ying up the stairs; Zhang is bound to try them on, and the moment we see the target we do it; this too can gain our end. But then it would drag in the innocent. Only if there were no other choice—so long as there is some other way to follow, this is the worst of bad plans.” Of course we were all agreed the first plan was best. And so it was decided to act by that plan.

Just as Elder Brother Wang and brother Shiwei were about to leave, Yang Ying too said that if he could be of use, he was willing to go with Elder Brother Wang to the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits at once.

The better to settle in detail the working steps thereafter, I asked Elder Brother Wang to stay a few minutes more; I summed up and added to what had just been said, so that the whole order of the affair should be thus:

Brother Shiwei to go home with Elder Brother Wang first, and ask elder sister-in-law Wang to take the trouble of a trip to Tianjin.

I had a thought of my own, and asked Elder Brother Wang to weigh it well: should Mr. Hou not agree, might his piece be borrowed for our use?

And further, if Mr. Hou did not come but would only lend his gun, then would elder sister-in-law Wang, a woman, dare to bring it back?

Once elder sister-in-law Wang had set out, Elder Brother Wang and brother Shiwei were still to go back to the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits. They might use this stretch of afternoon to make further necessary arrangements and preparations.

It was reckoned that by half past six in the evening elder sister-in-law Wang could be back—but that depended on whether Mr. Hou came or not.

If he came and was glad to draw his blade to help, we would proceed by our original plan.

If brother Hou did not come but was willing to lend a weapon, that too was well; brother Nanpu and brother Yang Ying would take the stage together, and fight it out to a finish come life or death.

If Mr. Hou neither came nor lent a gun, then truly, as Qi Nanpu had said, we could still go in with knife and axe and shed our blood and sweat together!

Elder Brother Wang nodded again and again. When I had finished, he answered that earlier remark: “What I can be sure of is that, even should Hou not come himself, he'll surely lend the gun. And don't underrate your elder sister-in-law—to carry a gun, what is that to her?”

This talk done, we all shook hands once and agreed to gather at Elder Brother Wang's residence at seven that evening.

Brother Shiwei and the others went back to the Wang house first, together.

Qi Nanpu telephoned; I asked him whether he had bought the things he had said the night before he would buy. He said he had bought two or three sorts, and when the time came would use whichever came handiest. I asked him to stay on from twelve noon at the door of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits to receive us, and to keep a special eye on that revolving door.

It came to me too to ask him to come to Beichang Street at six-fifteen in the afternoon to fetch me, and go with me to the Wang house.

From the night before until now I had eaten nothing, and I asked Yang Ying to go out and buy a few baked sesame cakes and youtiao—fried dough sticks—to come back and eat together.

A little after noon, at three quarters past twelve, the telephone rang; I took up the receiver and listened. It was Shiwei calling. His voice was a little different from usual, thick and clipped; he said: “The thing's done; Old Qi and I are upstairs at the Qinghuayuan.” I said: “Good, good, I'll come at once.”

For a moment I did not know where best to set down the receiver in my hand, and my heart was pounding hard. I told Yang Ying: “The thing we planned may already be done; please stay here for the time being, while I go to hear the news, and when I have it clear I'll let you know.”

I meant to report to Mr. Zheng at once, but on second thought—no. I should understand it clearly first; to report just now the one line “the thing's done” would not do, the more so as, before I had seen Elder Brother Wang, for me alone to report first to Mr. Zheng was not fitting either. Since it was done, to report a little late did not much matter.

Though the Beichang Street where I lived was not too far from the Qinghuayuan at Bamiancao where they were bathing, even by rickshaw it took more than fifteen minutes; the more anxious my heart, the slower the pulling seemed, and it was a hard while before I arrived.

The Qinghuayuan was one of the first-rank bathhouses in Beiping, and for that day quite up-to-date in its fittings. I went through a passage and straight up to the second floor; the moment the attendant saw a guest come up, he bowed me toward a private box, and I asked him: “There's a Third Master Bai, come just a while ago—go and ask for me.” The attendant at once, in that habitual singsong, called out loud: “Third Master Bai, a friend to see you.”

Shiwei answered from within the room; the attendant lifted the door-curtain, and I saw them wrapped in big towels, resting on the beds. Shiwei was smoking, Old Qi kneading his feet.

I stepped in and first made them a round-arm bow, left and right, by way of congratulation, and they answered smiling. Though we had said nothing yet, one could already be sure how the thing stood: at this my heart was greatly settled.

Shiwei said: “Elder Brother Wang went down first, and is probably home by now.” I was eager to know how the thing had gone, but here one could not speak one's fill; better first to telephone Elder Brother Wang, and once the four of us were gathered, go together to Fuyou Street and tell it all before Mr. Zheng—would that not save a deal of to-and-fro?

The telephone was a long while ringing through; I asked Elder Brother Wang: “We'll wait at the door of the Qinghuayuan, right here on Bamiancao Street; if your car is at home, best come and fetch us to go together to Fuyou Street. What do you say?”

Elder Brother Wang agreed to come for us, and could be there in about ten minutes.

In Elder Brother Wang's car, what Elder Brother Wang asked was just what I wanted to ask. It was Elder Brother Wang who spoke first; he asked Shiwei: “Is he down?” Shiwei took him up: “Gave him three of them; I reckon he's eight parts done for.” Nanpu, smiling, said nothing; what I wanted to hear was just these two lines.

Mr. Zheng Jiemin, seeing the four of us come together, was not surprised, but he could never have thought we had already accomplished the mission.

When brother Tianmu, on our behalf, had first reported the general course of it, Mr. Zheng was overjoyed indeed; he grasped Shiwei's hand and shook it many times over. He drew brother Tianmu too to sit at his side, and shook hands again with me and with Nanpu, and then gravely declared: first, on behalf of the higher command, he gave us comfort and cheer and offered his hearty congratulations. After hearing a fuller report, besides wiring Mr. Dai at Nanjing, he would at once go up to see the Beiping authorities and report back.

The development and shifting of events lay not within our original plan, and one may say it went far more smoothly than we had foreseen; only, the one point not to be blotted out is this: had there been no plan beforehand, the later good fortune would not have come about either. The whole true course of it was thus:

We had settled our plan at Beichang Street and resolved to work in full concert and strike upstairs.

Elder Brother Wang, together with Shiwei, went home, and at once charged elder sister-in-law Wang with the plan we had weighed out, begging her by all means to make the train that left Tianjin a little after four in the afternoon and come back.

That done, Elder Brother Wang and Shiwei went back once more to the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits.

By now it was nearly noon.

To settle the possibly-coming Mr. Hou, and to make it easier to approach the target, and still more to set up one more foothold, they decided to take another room, the most desirable being of course on the third floor.

Elder Brother Wang called the steward and told him: “A Japanese colleague is coming from Tianjin, may arrive this afternoon; reserve a room for him. He likes quiet, so the third or fourth floor would be best.”

The steward promised to go down first and ask at the counter. After a while he came back and said, in his well-practiced manner: “To answer you, sir: the counter has checked, and just at present there is nothing empty on the third or fourth floor; tomorrow a room may free up. I see there is one ready on the second floor—better book it first, and when the third floor frees up tomorrow we'll change it over; it will not hold up your use in any case. What do you say?”

Whether it suited or not, it had to suit, and Elder Brother Wang booked the ready room on the second floor. Taking the chance, he tipped the steward five dollars to buy his goodwill and many conveniences.

On a sudden impulse, Elder Brother Wang thought of going to look at the new room; he said nothing, but pulled Shiwei along and went.

This newly booked room was on the transverse stretch, at the meeting-point of the two corridors, left and right, not far from the room Elder Brother Wang lodged in—only some twenty-odd paces; going on further and turning left, one came to the corridor on the opposite side.

When Elder Brother Wang and brother Shiwei had looked over the booked room, they still grudged its being too far from the staircase—for us it was really too inconvenient; but for the moment there was no better plan.

The room seen, the steward handed Elder Brother Wang the room key and, with repeated thanks, went off ahead. Elder Brother Wang, as if not master of himself, followed behind the steward and went on forward too; Shiwei, at a loss to know why, could not but follow Elder Brother Wang's steps and go forward as well.

By now they were already walking along this opposite corridor.

A dozen paces on, among a row of rooms on the left of the corridor, there was one with a half-leaf of window open, and inside a man was seen, seated sideways on the edge of the bed, his head tilted back, facing the window, fiddling in his hands with some small object that could not be made out clearly. This man had a long rectangular face, a high-tilted nose-tip, thin hollow cheeks, two little wisps of mustache, and under his chin a tuft of long hair besides. Elder Brother Wang's eyes lit up, as though a flash had struck—why, was that not Zhang Jingyao!

Fearing his eyes had played him false, Elder Brother Wang stopped his steps, turned his body, and took another glance, coming just face to face with the man—not the least mistake, it was he!

Brother Shiwei, seeing Elder Brother Wang pause, followed his line of sight and turned to look leftward too, and saw only a man sitting there. Turning his head again to look at Elder Brother Wang, he saw him pointing with his hand into the room, nodding again and again, saying under his breath: “That's him.” Then he walked with quick steps toward the stair-head.

Shiwei had already caught his meaning, and now heard it plain besides; he planted his feet firm, lifted the skirt of his lined gown, drew out the gun, aimed at the chest of the man in the room who had just half risen; bang, bang, bang—he fired three shots in a row.

In an instant, the sliver of a scene caught at the corner of his eye: the man was toppling down.

Gun in hand, Shiwei strode toward the staircase. Just as he reached the stair-head and was about to step down, he chanced upon a steward darting up from the ground floor. Shiwei made a motion with the gun; how would the steward dare bar him? He hid quickly to one side and watched Shiwei walk down the stairs step by step.

Reaching the ground-floor lobby, Shiwei turned and looked up: that steward was gone, and no one came down after him. Looking round the lobby again, in twos and threes there were a good many eyes fixed on him.

Shiwei paid them no heed; with head high and long strides he walked straight for the main door.

Now for Elder Brother Wang: he had gone down but five or six steps when he heard the noise above; so he quickened his pace, came to the lobby, saw some looking up and some talking, and, with no time for any of that, hurriedly pushed the door and went out.

Out the door, a sweep of the eye showed Nanpu come to receive them, the car parked across the road; and several rickshaws stood on either side of the door waiting for hire; that patrolman who used to drift about outside the door was nowhere to be seen. The road was calm as usual; the shots from upstairs seemed not to have startled the people outside.

Elder Brother Wang went down the stone steps, beckoned and hired a rickshaw, sprang up and was off.

Nanpu perceived that Elder Brother Wang was deliberately not greeting him, and, thinking things amiss, gave the driver a few words and in two or three strides darted up the steps; he first pushed the door with his hand—still turning—then carefully watched the stir within.

Just at this juncture Shiwei was coming out the door. With one hand he seized Nanpu's arm and, without a word, made straight for the roadside. The two of them, one left and one right, opened the car doors severally; before they were well seated, the driver pressed the accelerator, and in an instant they had left the spot, driving off toward the “Water Gate,” then, with a sharp turn hard after, sped west along the foot of the city wall.

On this road there was not a single vehicle; save this one of ours, none came toward us, and none trailed behind.

As this car passed the back gate of the “Japanese barracks,” the great fear was that they would come out and stop us; Shiwei kept a bit of caution, laying the gun on his knee and covering it with the hem of his lined gown against mishap. But the Japanese soldier standing guard with his rifle did not stir and made no response at all.

On some hundreds of paces, passing the back gate of the “American barracks,” there was no need for worry: that neatly turned-out American soldier, acting as traffic policeman, signaled us through with a wave of his hand.

Following the road, a turn right and then a turn left, was a memorial archway—this was the boundary. Entering Hubu Street, we had come onto land where we held sovereignty.

From the finding of the target to this moment, the whole course had taken, all told, less than five minutes.

Brother Nanpu told driver Zhang to drive first to the Dong'an Market on Wangfujing Street; he and brother Shiwei got out of the car, telling driver Zhang to drive the car back as before to the repair shop for servicing, and to rest a day first—any business could wait till tomorrow. Getting out, brother Shiwei patted driver Zhang twice by way of thanks; but this driver Zhang perhaps still did not understand what it was all about.

Nanpu and Shiwei walked, amid the coming and going crowds, to the Qinghuayuan at Bamiancao.

That telephone call I received at Beichang Street was the one they made from the Qinghuayuan.

After coming out of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, Elder Brother Wang got down first at the door of the Peace Cinema at Dongdan Pailou, then changed to another rickshaw and went home. He had just wiped his face and was meaning to rest a moment when he got the telephone call in which I asked him out for us all to gather.

This scene ran off at flying speed, with rhythm and with measure, every thread fitting close, without a flaw. Though it was all fact, it might as well be enjoyed like a cartoon.

At this there remained one suspense urgently to be understood: what had become of Zhang Jingyao?

Besides, there were of course many after-matters that needed to be made clear.

When Mr. Zheng Jiemin had heard everyone's report, he at once drafted a telegram and wired it to Mr. Dai Yunong at Nanjing; and, dressing meanwhile, went straight to Zhongnanhai to lay the matter before acting deputy chairman He in person. As he rose to go, he made an appointment to gather with us that evening.

Elder Brother Wang, short of sleep several days running, meant to go home for a sleep, to be ready toward evening to go in person to the railway station to meet elder sister-in-law Wang, and that Mr. Hou as well.

Brother Shiwei was to go out of the city, home to pay his respects to his two aged parents.

Brother Nanpu was to go again to inquire after news bearing on the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits affair.

I was to telephone first to Yang Ying, waiting at Beichang Street. I had a mind too to write a detailed written report to Mr. Dai. Toward afternoon, the evening papers of that day carried no news report of the matter. Toward dusk, Qi Nanpu reported: “An ambulance came before the door of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, and drove off again straightway.” That night Mr. Zheng Jiemin got the sure word from the Beiping Military Branch: “Zhang Jingyao died of his grave wounds at three in the afternoon at the German Hospital.”

The whole passage was but the fifth day of the one-week term.

### 4. What I Call a Flawless Piece of Work

In the daily paper of 8 May 1933, the news printed ran in substance thus: “The great merchant Chang Shigu was shot dead within the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits in the Legation Quarter; the assassin fled without a trace.” Some also printed the name “Chang Shiwu”; only the three characters “Zhang Jingyao” were nowhere to be seen.

“Chang Shigu” and “Chang Shiwu” were two cover names, close in sound but different in characters, very likely a foreign transliteration used in the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits register.

I dimly recall that in the news feature of the time Zhang's family were still covering the thing up, spreading it about that he had died of an accidental electric shock.

In fact Zhang had taken two bullets, both about the diaphragm in the chest and belly; he did not die at once, and the hotel called an ambulance and sent him for emergency aid to the nearby German Hospital; but his wounds being grave, with too much blood lost besides, he lingered until three in the afternoon and died.

We knew that the “Chang Shigu” the paper printed was the former military governor of Hunan Zhang Jingyao—there could be no mistake.

Zhang's confederates—that is, the chief of staff Zhao and the aides lodged on the third floor—and the fellow-plotters who had dealings with Zhang, must likewise have known that “Chang Shigu” was Zhang Jingyao.

The reason the newspapers of the time did not mention Zhang Jingyao was that they simply did not know the truth, not that they had anything to shy from saying. After another spell of days, it gradually came out.

In that interval, the position of the highest North China authorities and of the Beiping Military Branch was such that, from any angle whatever, they could not make the matter public, could not even acknowledge it—the more so for fear of the Japanese side making an unreasonable row or seizing the pretext to stir up trouble.

The Japanese military who had egged Zhang Jingyao on to raise a revolt were like “a mute eating goldthread”—they could not hum out a single word, for to say anything would expose their own plot, as good as confessing unbeaten.

So this strange case soon fell silent and unheard-of.

I have not looked up how many years it was, but suddenly people were again discussing this case in the papers and magazines, and from then on it kept coming up, down to the present day. This is where its influence runs deep.

Later intelligence confirmed that Zhang Jingyao had indeed taken three rooms on the third floor; besides the chief of staff Zhao, there were an aide and a groom-orderly, one each, and counting Zhang himself in, four persons in all. They had been staying in the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits over half a month, with a fair amount of contact with the outside; though they did not come and go often, their goings and comings were not few. It shows how far short our reconnaissance still fell. As to whether Zhang's attendants carried weapons, that was left a riddle needing no answer now.

Zhang Jingyao had an opium habit, and no small one; he was fond of little antiques—jade carvings, snuff bottles, and the like; and he had one more failing, laziness: too lazy to sleep at night, too lazy to rise in the morning, too lazy even to see a caller. His taking a small extra room on the second floor was, to put it in today's terms, to possess a little world wholly his own; beyond that, it is hard to find any other reason.

It cannot be said that Zhang Jingyao himself had no wariness, for all he did were things that wronged his conscience; but he never dreamed that his hour had come, that the end of his life was before his eyes. As the saying goes: “Did one know the death-pit lay there, one would not sleep the whole night through.”

To tell the truth, not only did Zhang Jingyao's side not foresee it; our side too, who put him to death and were glad of it, likewise did not foresee it. Had there not been so many coincidences, how it would have ended was still in the lap of the undetermined.

From composite intelligence material it was further learned: among those who meant to raise a rebellion and plotted with Zhang Jingyao was the former “commander-in-chief of the Five-Province Allied Army,” Sun Chuanfang, who was at that time also in the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits.

Sun Chuanfang, courtesy name Xinyuan, was called “Marshal Xin” in his days in power. During the Northern Expedition of the National Revolutionary Army, Sun held Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui and the rest, five provinces, in check, and styled himself “commander-in-chief of the Five-Province Allied Army.” When the Northern Expedition succeeded, Sun withdrew into seclusion at Tianjin, and though he chanted sutras and worshiped the Buddha and repented his former wrongs, he never laid down the butcher's knife.

It was said that Sun Chuanfang lodged on the fourth floor of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, with attendants; between Sun and Zhang there was not only frequent coming and going, but they often held “secret councils” together.

When we made our re-check on the strength of the intelligence, Sun had already slipped away. It was only heard that on the very night Zhang Jingyao met his end, Sun moved out of the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, whereabouts unknown—most likely, of course, fled back to Tianjin.

There was another, different account: that Sun Chuanfang had refused the Japanese side's overtures and would not run with Zhang Jingyao. And after Zhang Jingyao's death it was again rumored the Japanese once more tried to talk Sun Chuanfang round, but he politely declined.

Speaking with the correct attitude in handling intelligence, neither of the above two statements can be affirmed. But the point that the Japanese side had had dealings with Sun Chuanfang can indeed be affirmed.

In 1934 Sun Chuanfang, while performing a Buddhist service at a certain “lay Buddhists' grove” in Tianjin, was at last killed by Miss Shi Jianqiao. Everyone knows it was a filial daughter avenging her father. Her filial deed was like that of Shisanmei, He Yufeng, in “A Tale of Heroic Sons and Daughters”; a cut above He Yufeng in that He Yufeng plotted but did not carry it out, whereas Miss Shi had already fulfilled her heart's wish. The affair came about because Miss Shi Jianqiao's father, Shi Congbin, had once been a division commander under Sun and had been shot dead by Sun Chuanfang at Bengbu, Anhui, and so a “blood-feud for a slain father” was formed; there may have been no political factor in it.

There is a book that says Shi Jianqiao was a working member of the “Juntong,” acting under orders. The present writer was then at work in Beiping and Tianjin and knew nothing of this; and lately, having asked round all my old friends, none knew of it either; nor, besides, is there any concrete material to confirm it. Still, if Miss Shi could be at once loyal and filial, that would naturally be all the better.

Now to the question of the intelligence source in the Zhang Jingyao case: as already made clear above, the intelligence that Zhang Jingyao was staying in the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits in the Legation Quarter, fomenting revolt and plotting mischief, was handed down from the Beiping Military Branch. My own guess is that whoever furnished the intelligence to acting deputy chairman He of the Military Branch must have had contact with Zhang Jingyao—very likely was one Zhang meant to win over, or even one to whom Zhang had made some promise. Otherwise he could not have known the inside of it, seen it as clear as fire. In the jargon of intelligence work, this kind of source is called an “inside line.”

Decades on, we have never known who the man was; and if I were to hazard yet another guess, then the answer to the riddle is this: a man with old ties to Zhang, of no low standing, or holding military command, and deeply versed in the greater righteousness.

In volume nine, page ninety-four, of the complete translation of “The Secret Records of President Chiang,” serialized in Japan's “Sankei Shimbun” and rendered and printed by the Central Daily News, there is this passage, which runs:

“Itagaki first meant to draw out Duan Qirui or Sun Chuanfang, but failed; then he made contact with old warlords—Zhang Jingyao, who had in earlier years been military governor of Hunan, and Shi Yousan and the rest.

“Zhang Jingyao boasted and bragged of deep ties with Song Zheyuan and showed off before Itagaki; Itagaki, believing him without a doubt, once gave him three hundred thousand silver yuan of working funds.

“On 18 April (1933) Itagaki wired the Kwantung Army in secret to report: ‘Zhang Jingyao is set to launch a coup on 21 April, Song Zheyuan responding at the same time.’ And he asked the Kwantung Army, to cover Zhang Jingyao's action, to press the attack southward harder; but the Kwantung Army received on the nineteenth an order to withdraw, so that this scheme was never realized.”

We cite this passage to give the reader something to refer to.

We have not gone deeply into the reliability of this passage.

According to the book's “editorial principles,” material quoted from official documents is set within “……”; on this basis, the passage in Itagaki's secret wire to the Kwantung Army—“Zhang Jingyao is set to launch a coup on 21 April, Song Zheyuan responding at the same time”—is quoted from an official document, that is, one may verify that Itagaki did indeed send such a wire. As for why it was not later realized—whether Zhang Jingyao was boasting, or someone within changed his mind—that is very hard to say.

Does it hint that the one who furnished the intelligence to the Beiping Military Branch was Song Zheyuan? Impossible; for on the strength of the above passage alone, it is not yet enough to pass such a judgment offhand.

After the event, who it was that furnished the intelligence had ceased to matter; but what people Zhang Jingyao had in fact won over—how weighty a matter that was at the time—how could it not be pursued?

It is learned that—leaving aside for now how Zhang Jingyao boasted to Itagaki Seishirō—if one is willing to believe he had “secured an inside response” within the two great military and political organs, the “North China Political Affairs Council” and the “Beiping Military Branch,” then luckily, once Zhang Jingyao was dead, these people lost their prop all at once and ceased to have any effect. And the military and political authorities, having already cut off the chief culprit and dissolved a great upheaval that might have occurred, held it best, in adapting to their hard circumstances, not to probe too deeply.

This belongs to matters of high-level “political maneuvering,” and should not be set out further in depth.

Now look at the Japanese military: their basic policy in this phase was to pair military threat with so-called strategic maneuvering, the true aim being to fabricate in North China a “second Manchukuo,” a puppet regime, and so realize by degrees their aggressive ambition. So the Japanese military supported this scheme wholesale. What is meant here by “wholesale” includes the Japanese General Staff, the War Ministry, the Kwantung Army, the Tianjin Garrison Army, the Tianjin special-service organ, the Beiping special-service organ, and the military attaché's office of the Japanese legation in China, among other units. What should give one pause is this: the evil “reckoning of men” is no match for the righteous “reckoning of Heaven”; with Zhang Jingyao's head taken, the claws and fangs lost heart and the petty villains hid themselves away, and the whole plot was shattered. And thereby the tense situation in North China was eased.

The above assessment may be taken as a self-examination of the gains and losses of success and failure; perhaps, from different angles, there are yet other views.

In the Zhang Jingyao case we received a double commendation, from the highest military and political authorities in Beiping and from Mr. Dai Yunong of the Nanjing Special Services Department (the Second Department).

What gladdened me and gave me a very great sense of ease was more than this alone; for I had always looked on the “Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits” as a symbol of imperialism, and to sanction a traitor within the “Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits” I reckoned to be a killing of two birds with one stone—both smashing “that” and ridding us of “this.” Such a notion cannot but betray a touch of childishness; then let me be allowed my own quiet pleasure in it.

Though brother Shiwei, in describing the scene afterward, showed himself quite excited, thereafter he seldom spoke of the matter again, and was even unwilling to touch on such questions any more. I understand: this is a kind of dead-knot of the mind; one who has never done action work, never with his own hand killed a man, can hardly appreciate the taste of it.

If anyone should say, So-and-so has guts and dares, So-and-so is good at assassination work, so let him do more of this kind of work hereafter!—that would be a mistake. I believe a man of normal mind would never look on killing a man as eating a dish of food. So I must gravely stress one word: on no account slight the factor of “political faith” or “conviction in the work,” for this is the very motive force.

Elder Brother Wang Tianmu grew more open-minded still; at the time, on him, it seemed as though this thing had never happened. In his eighty-fifth year, when I asked him whether he still remembered that affair at the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits of more than forty years before, he spoke of it with relish, and vivid as a painting.

Mr. Zheng Jiemin set special store by this matter; besides commending us in many ways, in his talks, speeches, lessons, and admonitions he often cited it as an example, to spur those to come. In short, in his eyes the few of us were all good men. Toward me especially he was all tender care and added affection. Some years later, once, halfway on my road toward “the City of Fengdu,” with one hand he pulled me back out of the ranks of the dead. This is a true thing, and will be written again in the fourth part of “Nameless Heroes.”

Not long after the Zhang case was done, I received formal notice that I had been raised from a “member” of the “Revolutionary Youth Comrades Association” to a member of the “Three Principles of the People Lixingshe.” At the same time, Wang Tianmu and Bai Shiwei were both permitted to join the “Lixingshe” directly as members. This, of course, was owing to Mr. Zheng Jiemin's “recommendation.”

Bai Shiwei and I were put into the same cell; it was only since joining the “Revolutionary Soldiers' Comrades Association,” and after the “Military Association” and the “Youth Association” had merged into one, that I began to take part in cell activities.

I remember that in our one cell there were the gendarmerie's third-regiment commander Jiang Xiaoxian, the fourth-regiment commander Ji Zhangjian, the gendarmerie battalion commander Han Wenhuan, the third-regiment adjutant Ding Chang, and others, and the cell leader was elder brother Ji Zhangjian. That we were all put into the same cell was probably owing to being “of the same trade”?

What left the deeper impression was that every cell meeting was a serious affair, never sinking into mere form.

Writing to this point, there is a remark I have kept bottled up in my belly for decades, and I think it best to speak it out. Just after the Zhang Jingyao case had succeeded, Mr. Dai came to Beiping again, and as we spoke of “Lixingshe” matters he once instructed me: “What matters is still the work of the ‘Beiping Station’; I hope you will not spend too much time taking part in ‘organizational activity.’”

As to the meaning of this remark, I could not press him deeply; the result of my own working-out was an eight-character reading, namely: “One's own post above all; cooperate and divide the labor.”

This was the situation then. In some years after, I heard some “idle talk” too: it was said that among the senior cadres inside the “Lixingshe” there was no avoiding a “striving for the upper hand” and a “contending for mastery.” In truth, this sort of thing happens in any body whatever, and really counts for nothing.

On this northern trip of Mr. Dai's, he decided to enlarge the establishment of the “Beiping Station”: an “Action Group” dealing solely with action work was set up, with Comrade Bai Shiwei as its group leader. Social activity was strengthened too, with Comrade Qi Nanpu in charge of it. Afterward, more basic cadres were sent to Beiping to strengthen our force.

The Zhang Jingyao case here came to a stage's end; in the work of the “Juntong,” it had already been entered on the first page.

What remained was one small loose end, rich enough in human feeling that it must be set down.

One day Elder Brother Wang, accompanying brother Shiwei and me, went to call on Ying Yuanxun, the proprietor of the Yingyuantai tailor shop.

At first he would not so much as mention that affair of Zhang Jingyao at the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits—not that he did not understand, but very likely from worldly prudence, unwilling to court trouble. In the end it was Elder Brother Wang who, in roundabout terms, conveyed our apology and our thanks.

Ying Yuanxun said nothing at all; head lowered and silent, he walked to the back, brought out two finished new suits, laid them lightly on the counter, and only then said: “Take a look, gentlemen—these two suits, where am I to deliver them? From whom am I to collect the money?” That said, he broke into a great laugh, and would insist on taking us to eat, saying he would play host and spread for us a “banquet of consolation.”

True enough, it was because Zhang Jingyao had clothes made and came to fit them that his hiding-place was exposed. The proprietor of the Yingyuantai tailor shop, all unwitting, gave us enormous help, and only so did we succeed. This was truly a “coincidence,” and might as well be called “Heaven's will.”

That a tradesman could be so bold and open-hearted was rare indeed.

In 1938, when Beiping had fallen, I came to Beiping again on a mission; though I passed his door, I dared not go in, for fear of implicating him.

In 1948 I was again, amid storm and stress, quartered with my troops at Beiping, and had a Zhongshan tunic and a military uniform made at his shop. I remember that at the Spring Festival of that year Manager Ying invited a few of us to a meal of the rather famous “Tan-family cuisine.” Present, about then, was brother Bai Shiwei; at that time brother Shiwei was deputy chief of the Beiping Municipal Police Bureau, his charge being to keep local order. At this juncture the “Legation Quarter” was still the “legation district” as before.

In the twinkling of an eye it is thirty-odd years again; I do not know whether old Mr. Ying Yuanxun is well. I wish him much blessing and long life, food and drink enough. Yet I always worry that, fine a hand though he was at cutting, he too would have no business left to do.

Besides, there is one matter of a vow made and a vow discharged, which too must be accounted for to the person concerned.

It was that on the night the urgent order came, Elder Brother Wang, to take a room at the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits, needing a good cover and a made-up pretext, spent no little breath before he got the girl of the “Flower-Cultivating House”—Miss Feilong—to go along as a screen to carry off the deception. And sure enough he smoothly took a room, without rousing the least suspicion.

Now, that night, when Elder Brother Wang went with Miss Feilong to the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits and, having reached the second floor, stayed a little while, he then sent the driver to take Feilong back. As she left, Elder Brother Wang coaxed her, saying: “In a day or two we'll surely come to show our support.”

Now that a matter of a human life had occurred, Feilong must be in the know; should she noise it abroad, it would be bad, and the more so as she had, after all, done a little to help—so we could not be easy until we had made a trip there.

And so we chose a day when business was slack, and at the hour when the bright lamps first came on, the three of us again went in company to the “Flower-Cultivating House.” When Feilong saw the three of us, before a word she kept pressing her lips in a smile, as if to say: “A fine thing you've done.”

Elder Brother Wang drew her aside, meaning, I suppose, to give her a word of caution, but before he could open his mouth she got in first: “Set your minds at rest, gentlemen; little schooling though I have had, I would never be so wanting in sense.”

She was clever indeed; that evening we quite openly made her a “flower-tip,” which was, as it were, to give her a small bonus.

From then on she and I had a good deal to do with each other. When I moved to Bicai Hutong and shared a rented house with brother Shiwei, this girl often came to linger, and we even spoke of marrying; only, because her mother demanded a sum of old-age keep that could not be met, it fell through—which too, one may say, was a matter of fate.

As it turned out, this girl was under a lucky star; had she really married me, she would have known fright and fear and suffered a lifetime's misery. A year later someone redeemed her from the register—an officer, it was said; man and woman took their joy, bore sons and daughters, and so she left the life for good and became “a decent household's woman.”

At this the case leaves but a very little regret, namely that when elder sister-in-law Wang Tianmu went to Tianjin to fetch reinforcements, Mr. Hou, being ill, could not come.

Of course, among the factors of success there is no avoiding some measure of luck; or one may say, it too was half of “Heaven's” will and half of man's!

Looking back over the whole, the case's smooth completion cost no sacrifice and suffered no mishap; it implicated no one and failed no one. Truly it was a piece of work perfect and complete, whole from head to tail.

In the ten years thereafter, among the more than two hundred acts of action-and-sabotage work under my command, never again was there one so flawless.

The bizarre shooting-death that occurred on 9 November 1934 within the Guomin Hotel in the French Concession of Tianjin was the first close-quarters clash between the work units under the “Juntong's” leadership and the Communist Party in the North China region.

This was of course no ordinary killing, nor a simple piece of traitor-rooting work; in substance it should be reckoned a piece of armed political struggle, banners clear and battle-lines sharply drawn.

Some years and months after the event, the Communist Party's propaganda organs puffed up Ji Hongchang—a traitor who had suffered the law—to the skies, not only styling him a “martyr” but declaring, shameless, some such thing as “a roc that darkens the sky, brought down by mole-crickets and ants”; when in truth, in the eyes of every patriot, it was precisely “a rebel minister and a bandit son, whom any man may put to death.”

Ji Hongchang had been a Northwest Army general, and had held such posts as army commander, commander-in-chief, and chairman of Ningxia province. First he had secret dealings with the Communist Party, then willingly let himself be driven by them. In 1934, under the name of resisting Japan, he raised a great armed force in the Chahar-Rehe country; before long his true shape showed itself and he actually turned his guns about, openly opening war on the Nationalist forces, and that for more than fifty days. After his rout and surrender, on the way while being escorted back into custody, he broke free again and slipped back to Tianjin, and there, with the Communist chiefs Xuan Xiafu, Nan Hanchen, and others, organized the “Chinese People's Anti-Fascist Grand Alliance,” and went on fomenting disorder.

Ji Hongchang's doings had proof of guilt beyond dispute, and the government had long since put out an open warrant for his arrest on record; and putting it to the mass of the people, they too “all said he might be killed.”

As for the development of the case—treacherous as shifting clouds, with many a rise and fall; the tracking of the quarry like fishing a needle from the sea; and when it came to the blow struck upstairs, again, by a freak crossing of wires, a plum died in the peach's place. So it is that this makes up this ever-shifting, quite legend-rich record of the true facts.
