## Chapter 7. In Longhua Prison

After I was transferred out of the Central organs, the Jiangsu Provincial Committee arranged for me to go and work as an apprentice at the Japanese-owned Naigai Wata No. 13 Mill in the Puxi district. In those days even landing a place as an apprentice in a mill was no easy matter: you had to hand money to the "Number One" (in the pidgin English of Shanghai, the foreman) to bribe him, and after he agreed you still needed the approval of the mill's boss. It was the responsible comrades of the Party's underground branch in the mill who found a way to get hold of an apprentice's tally for me, and that was how I got in.

Naigai Wata was a spinning mill, and an apprentice's work was to do cleaning and sanitation all day long, sweeping the workshops and, above all, the toilets; they never let you learn any real skill. In theory an apprentice served out her time in three years and became a regular hand after three years, but during that period you got no wages at all. The mill hands of that time worked twelve hours a day: you had to be up by half past five in the morning and hurry to line up and go in. At five fifty-five the mill blew its whistle, and once the whistle stopped they shut the main gate; if you tried to get in after that, you were late. Being late meant a demerit, and if you were late several times you were punished, had your wages docked, or even dismissed, so no one dared be late. Through the whole twelve hours of work there was no rest period; even at midday we ate in shifts, the people stopping but the machines never, and going to the toilet you were allowed a few minutes at most. I brought some pickled vegetables and rice in a lunch box, and that was my food for the whole day. I had long steeled myself for this kind of life, and seeing that all my worker sisters lived the same way, I did not feel it a hardship, only especially exhausting. Besides working themselves to the bone, the women hands were forever being ordered about by the foremen and even cursed and beaten. To the young women hands the foremen would sometimes say vile, off-color things, and even paw and molest and insult them, and the sisters simply had to endure it. All of this let me feel in my own person the miserable life of the working masses under the exploitation and oppression of the capitalists and their foremen. In those days, when He Chang came back to Shanghai now and then from inspecting the work in the provinces, the two of us could still meet at a hotel. In late June I discovered I was pregnant again.

That year, on International Labor Day, the first of May, the underground Party organization handed out leaflets, and I took part too. After I had finished distributing my leaflets I kept only the very last one in my lunch basket, meaning to hold it back for my own study and propaganda work, but a policeman happened to find it on inspection. He questioned me: "What is this?" Not in the least flustered, I answered without any change of expression, "I picked it up off the ground." He asked again, "What did you pick it up for?" I said I was a woman hand in a spinning mill, and that the shuttles on the machines often sent cotton flying that stuck to your clothes and hair; seeing this piece of paper was so clean, I picked it up meaning to wrap my comb in it. He of course did not believe me, and hauled me off to the police station. The Naigai Wata No. 13 Mill lay within the International Settlement, jointly administered by Britain, the United States, and other countries, and the police station observed the Sunday day-of-rest system; that day was a Saturday, so it was not until Monday that they sent me before the court of the International Settlement for trial. The court of the Settlement in those days styled itself a "civilized court," and had a system of duty defense lawyers taking turns to serve pro bono. I saw that the name of the duty lawyer posted on the board that day was Jiang Yiping; he was the son-in-law of the Shanghai magnate Yu Qiaqing and very well known in Shanghai legal circles. The judge asked me how old I was and what I did. I said I was an apprentice at a spinning mill, seventeen (though I was already in my twenties, being short and small I could pass for seventeen and no one could tell). He asked me again, "Can you read?" I said I had only just come from the countryside to work in the mill and could not read. Then the judge, very sternly, rebuked and questioned me: "Where did this leaflet come from? Do you not know that handing out leaflets is a crime? It is the crime of disturbing the public peace! Inciting the workers to strike is the doing of the Communist Party. Who put you up to it? Tell the truth and we can deal with you leniently!" I lifted my head, put on a blank, uncomprehending look, spoke in a very small voice, dull and rustic, and answered slowly: "Your Honor, sir, I don't understand what the master is saying. What party? I've only just come from the countryside, I know nothing, what does it say on this paper? I only thought this scrap of paper was nice and clean, and picked it up to wrap my comb in…" Because I played the part so convincingly, Jiang Yiping the lawyer spoke up beside me in my defense: "The defendant is under the legal age of eighteen, and considering that she is illiterate and ignorant, her offense does not warrant a sentence. There is no point in keeping her in custody any longer. I ask the bench to be lenient—admonish and reprimand her a few words in court and then release her!" Hearing the lawyer say this, and seeing that I really did look like a country person who understood nothing and had only just come to the mill to work, the judge agreed to discharge me then and there.

After I was let go, I at once reported to the leadership of the underground district committee the whole course of what had happened before and after my arrest. The comrades said: "This time you were not only cool and composed, giving good answers in the deposition in court and playing the part convincingly, but you also happened to have the famous lawyer Jiang Yiping defend you pro bono—it was truly a fright without any danger. You were lucky!" In fact it was because I already had experience as an underground courier that I was able to keep my head in a crisis and adapt to events with quick wits.

This demonstration of handing out leaflets had been directly led by Comrade Luo Mai (Li Weihan), a member of the Central Committee and secretary of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee. Although I had been released and sent back to the mill, I was after all someone the police station had once hauled in, and to go on being active at the Naigai Wata No. 13 Mill would draw attention and be inconvenient. The district committee reported my situation to the Jiangsu Provincial Committee. After studying it, the Provincial Committee decided to move me from Puxi to Pudong, where I entered the Hengfeng Spinning Mill as an apprentice. This time a woman hand named Liu Axiu had taken sick leave to go back to the countryside, and I went to stand in as her replacement. Of course the underground Party had again to send money and gifts to the foreman; only after he had been bought off could I get into the mill under Liu Axiu's name, taking her place. In those days the workers' movement led by the Party in the White areas, under the guidance of "Left" erroneous thinking, did not understand that in a situation where the enemy was strong and we were weak the fighting strategy had to be to "keep hidden and keep our cadres sound, lie low for the long haul, and wait for the moment"; it always liked to go in for activities that were formalistic and superficially rousing, such as marches and demonstrations, handing out leaflets, and strikes, which easily exposed one's own strength and brought on losses.

Not long after I entered the Hengfeng Spinning Mill, to mark "July First," the Party's birthday, the Pudong district committee again gave orders to hand out leaflets and called for a response to the general strike of the workers of the spinning and silk mills across the whole city—even half a day's strike would be good. The underground Party and Youth League branches at the Hengfeng Spinning Mill had altogether only three Party members and five League members, and we convened a meeting of the Party and League members to discuss how to carry out the district committee's orders. In the end we decided to hand out leaflets in the mill on the eve of "July First," propagating the Party's declaration, backing the general strike of the spinning and silk mills, and supporting the demands and conditions they had put forward. The concrete method was this: one Party member worked in the factory canteen as the purchaser in charge of buying vegetables, and went into the mill earlier each day than even the cooks, so we had him hide the leaflets in the three-wheeled cart he used to buy vegetables and bring them into the kitchen while the sky was still dark; one League member was an electrician, so we had him cut the wires before daybreak; and the other comrades, taking advantage of the dark confusion of the blackout, would take the leaflets from the kitchen, run to the drying terrace on the highest floor of the mill, and scatter the leaflets down from all four directions.

All of this was carried out in pitch darkness, with no one else seeing. As day was breaking, the workers going home from their shift and those coming in for the day shift saw leaflets all over the ground in the mill; some picked them up without a word and slipped them into their pockets to take home, some picked them up and hid in the toilets to read, and some simply carried them into the workshops for everyone to pass around, the illiterate women hands asking those who could read to read them aloud. Soon a hushed discussion spread through the workshops: "Oh, the workers at Such-and-such Spinning Mill and Such-and-such Silk Mill are on strike already!" People were all excited and stirred up. From the May Fourth and May Thirtieth Movements to welcoming the Northern Expedition army with three armed uprisings, the workers of Shanghai had a glorious tradition of struggle. Many of the older hands, though not Party or League members, plainly leaned toward the Communist Party in their thinking and cherished a deep feeling for the Party; understanding one another without words, they whispered: "The Communist Party hasn't forgotten us workers—they've come to care about us again and are leading the strike!" So it went on until dawn, the workers in the workshops walking to and fro spreading the word and talking, many of them with no heart left for work. And with that, the tasks of handing out the leaflets and staging the so-called half-day strike could be counted as accomplished.

The mill's boss, the foremen, and the soldiers and police who came racing up in cars treated it as if facing a great foe, coming into the workshops to search for leaflets. Because some had already been carried home by the workers coming off the night shift, some had been secretly hidden away, and many were simply left unretrieved, scattered all over the ground everywhere in the mill, they fussed about for a good long while and found nothing; nor did they know just how many Communist Party members there were in the mill, that such a great "commotion" could be raised, and they were beside themselves with alarm.

Afterward the organization sent me to the silk mill that was then on strike, to bring comfort and solidarity to the striking sisters and to make contact about their supporting one another's strikes. In those days only the officially recognized unions in the mills (what we called the yellow unions) could operate openly and legally. The organization decided to have the Party members and cadres who had taken part in the Great Revolution all join them, precisely so as to win over and make use of the legal standing of the yellow unions to lead the strike. After I got back from making contact at the silk mill, I reported to the union, and the union decided that the Hengfeng Spinning Mill would strike as well and put conditions for returning to work to the management. I was chosen by the strike committee to be the representative of the apprentices, and together with the other worker representatives went to the Dazhong Tea Garden in Zhabei to take part in the negotiations. The eight representatives put forward by the union, plus the workers who came to observe, numbered at most one or two hundred, but the mass of workers ringed around outside the tea garden came to one or two thousand. The boss and the management's representatives sat facing us, with a good number of soldiers and police alongside—both to intimidate the worker representatives and to protect the management's representatives. We had agreed beforehand that I, in my capacity as the apprentices' representative, would be the first to stand up and speak. Being short and small, I stood on a square stool and said: "I am the representative of the apprentices. We apprentices come into the mill and work hard every day, yet get no wages at all. Our families are all very poor, and still we are made to bring our own food into the mill every day to work for the boss. We ask the boss to give each of us three dollars a month for meals. Think about it, bosses—the maidservants you hire to help in your mansions, who eat and live in the mansion, still get three dollars a month in wages! We labor twelve hours a day in the mill and eat food we bring ourselves, yet are not given even three dollars a month for meals—is there any sense in that? We are called apprentices, but in fact we have no chance at all to learn a skill. Some apprentices have worked three years, even five years, in the mill, and still are never allowed to learn the machine-tending and spinning trade. We demand that from now on it be changed so that in the first year in the mill one does cleaning work, in the second year one begins to learn the skill, and in the third year one can be made a regular hand. Our third demand is that we oppose the foremen bullying us women sisters, oppose the foremen beating, cursing, and insulting the women hands at will. Our fourth demand is that we oppose the body-search system on entering and leaving the mill. Every time we go in and out of the mill we are searched—are we workers thieves, then? And also…" Before I could finish stating the conditions, the soldiers and police alongside rushed forward all at once, seized all eight of us worker representatives, and pushed us onto the cars parked outside the tea garden. The mass of workers ringed around outside the tea garden shouted in protest: "You may not arrest people!" "Release our representatives!" "If you don't release our representatives, we'll never go back to work!" They pressed forward in a throng to stop the cars from moving, but were held back by a great force of soldiers and police. Though we eight had been arrested, seeing with our own eyes how mightily the masses supported us, we felt it a great honor in our hearts.

The first day of our arrest we were locked up for a night at the public security bureau of the Nanshi district. The next day we were transferred to the detention house of the Longhua Garrison Command, a place for holding political prisoners. The eight of us women hands were locked in one large cell, sleeping on a big communal plank bed like the heated brick kangs of the north; the window was very high, so from inside you could not see out at all. There was already a woman prisoner held here: her husband had been sentenced to life imprisonment for robbery, and she had been arrested along with him by association. After being held for a time she could actually have been released, but she did not wish to leave, and begged the warden to let her stay on in the prison to look after her husband, at the same time washing and mending clothes for the detention house's soldiers and prisoners, and she had in fact obtained the warden's "gracious permission." We asked her why she did not go out and work as a maidservant, doing housework for others, which surely was far freer than being locked up in jail. She said: "You don't understand. To be a maidservant you have to go through an employment agency and need a guarantor—who would stand guarantor for me, and who would want a convict's wife like me for a maidservant in their home? Here I can still earn a little washing and mending for the soldiers and prisoners, and improve my own and my husband's meals a bit!" Hearing this, we all pitied her greatly. Her husband too was a poor man, driven to a dead end, who took the desperate road of stealing and robbing only when there was no way out. She was only a housewife, and now, dragged down by association, "voluntarily" stayed on in the prison to eke out her days bitterly—truly pitiable. Not like us: though we had been arrested and jailed, outside there was still the union supporting us and finding ways to rescue us, and there would be a day when we got out. The poor of the world are one family; having come to know her in prison, we were like daughters to her, and so we all called her "godmother," and told her we were willing to help her wash and mend without charge, all the extra money we earned going to her. That woman prisoner was probably in her forties, and hearing our words she was so moved that tears ran down her face. We also told her things about the outside, saying that this unjust world would one day change, that we would all have our day to hold up our heads and would not sit in jail till we wore its very floor through, and that she and her husband too would surely one day get out of prison and be reunited… Our taking the initiative to build good relations with her was also of great benefit to us. She was a "voluntary convict" who could come and go freely in the prison, and the prison lamps were kept burning all night long; sitting under the lamp every evening mending and patching, she could keep watch for us and cover our activities and struggles in prison.

Not long after we entered prison, Comrade Peng Pai too was unfortunately arrested, in late August 1929, and held in Longhua Prison. He was a member of the Central Political Bureau in both the fifth and the sixth Central Committees, a "major Communist criminal" for whom the enemy had offered a heavy reward, and he had been arrested through betrayal by a turncoat. During the Great Revolution he had led the peasant movement in the Hailufeng region of Guangdong, was the illustrious "Great King of the Peasants," his identity long since exposed; once his person had been verified, the matter was reported up, and soon the order would come down for him to be shot. So after he entered prison, he openly admitted that he was a Communist Party member, and, seizing the last of his time, propagated among the soldiers of the detention house the Party's aims and the idea of the poor common people striving for liberation, doing work to win them over. He told the soldiers that in those years, leading the peasant movement in Hailufeng, they had established a red political power; though it had failed, the workers and peasants were the most numerous, and when their strength grew great in the future, who would rule this world was still not certain! As it happened, among the detention house soldiers there was a squad leader who was a Guangdong man and had once taken part in the Hailufeng peasant movement. After the Great Revolution failed, because his mother and elder brother were both in Shanghai, he had gone there to join his kin, and through his brother's connections had come to know a minor bureaucrat in the Kuomintang, who introduced him into the detention house of the Longhua Garrison Command as a squad leader, in charge of twelve soldiers (the whole detention house had several squads). He knew Comrade Peng Pai, and now, seeing Peng Pai become a serious offender on death row, he felt both grieved and ashamed, and at their first meeting he hung his head low. Peng Pai's memory too was very good, and one look at this squad leader and he found the face very familiar. Because Peng was a condemned prisoner, held in a single cell, he was watched with special, added strictness. It happened that one evening the squad leader brought him tea, and Peng quietly asked him in a low voice whether he recognized him. The squad leader said he did, and said he knew Peng had been arrested and already condemned to death, and that his heart was very sad. Peng Pai said: "That's very good. You took part in the Hailufeng peasant movement—it seems you haven't forgotten your roots." Through their talk, Peng learned that among the squad three soldiers were on very good terms with the squad leader and could help cover for them, and so, while the squad leader was on duty, Peng Pai, through a little window opening in the iron-locked cell door, talked further with him quietly, heart to heart. Peng Pai said to the squad leader: "It's very hard for our Party to get people planted in here. Could you, for the sake of the old comradeship of having once made revolution together, use your authority as squad leader to help look after the political prisoners, and as far as lies within your duties, treat them a little better?" The squad leader readily agreed. Peng Pai said to him further: "Please go and pass this word to them: I am Peng Pai, I have been arrested, and I ask the comrades to be sure to note—first, absolutely do not expose your own identity; second, in prison do not be rash, you must be patient, do not make trouble for the guards on duty over petty matters of daily life, or you will only bring more trouble on yourselves and heavier punishment; third, our Party is certain to be victorious, everyone is certain to see the light, and you must have confidence in victory for the future of the revolution; if you have social connections outside, get out on bail through them as far as possible, and go on striving to work for the Party."

At that time the cells holding the male prisoners ran along three corridors in all, the great majority of those held being political prisoners. The squad leader conveyed Peng Pai's three charges separately to all the male and female political prisoners. Though the comrades had been arrested and jailed, they were still full of revolutionary optimism, and hearing the squad leader convey Comrade Peng Pai's three charges, they were all the more greatly educated and heartened. When we eight women convicts helped "godmother" wash clothes, we would hum songs as we washed—of course we could not sing revolutionary songs, but folk airs and little tunes like "Meng Jiangnü Weeping at the Great Wall." This singing was heard by Comrade Peng Pai, and he asked the squad leader: "I hear there are political prisoners held in the women's cell too; I heard them singing while they washed clothes!" The squad leader said: "Yes, eight women hands from a strike are locked up." Peng Pai asked him: "Is there anyone among them who can read?" The squad leader said: "They all say they can't read, but I have a feeling that among them there's one, short in stature, who probably can." Peng Pai then asked the squad leader to find a way to bring that woman convict who could probably read to talk with him. So the squad leader, on a night when the soldiers of his squad were on the night watch, put up money himself to have the soldiers on duty go off to play cards and gamble, and arranged for me to meet Comrade Peng Pai through the window of the cell door. Because we already knew each other well, he told me that this squad leader had once taken part in the Hailufeng peasant movement, was very reliable, and was also helping him pass letters to the outside.

In 1964, when I was visiting the Museum of the Chinese Revolution, I saw a letter in Comrade Peng Pai's own hand, in which there were two places that mentioned me. One place said: "Besides, we are also in communication with Mulan, and have likewise charged her to work on the special-agent squad guarding us." Another place said: "…directing Mulan to be active from within…" In the 1980s, Yuan Puzhi wrote to say that when the Guangdong Provincial Party History Committee compiled and published the Collected Works of Peng Pai, this letter too was included. She also sent me the galley proofs of that book (pp. 99–101) and asked me to write an article recalling the situation of Comrade Peng Pai leading the struggle in Longhua Prison in those years.

In those days the letters passed out from prison to the underground Party organization or to families were all sent, by prior arrangement, to a shop opened by the Mutual Aid Society. The forerunner of the Mutual Aid Society was the Relief Society, which had been jointly initiated and organized by the Kuomintang and the Communist Party during the Northern Expedition war. After the Great Revolution failed, the Relief Society changed its name to the Mutual Aid Society and became an organization led by the Communist Party specially responsible for rescuing arrested revolutionary comrades and comforting and relieving the families of comrades who had been arrested or killed. The couriers of the Mutual Aid Society, on receiving a letter passed out from prison, would usually give the person who brought it five dollars as a reward. In those days a soldier's monthly pay was only three dollars (enough to buy one picul of rice), and even the squad leader had only five. So each month, in passing one or two letters out for us, they were not only helping us do a good deed but also making a bit of extra income. In the officialdom of the old society there were two sayings: one was, "The yamen gate opens wide; with right but no money, don't come inside," and the other, "Deceive those above, not those below." Their carrying letters for us was of course unknown to those above, but among those below it was understood without a word. What is more, the soldiers of the detention house all worshipped the 108 heroes and good fellows of Liangshan Marsh in the *Water Margin*. The squad leader propagandized to the soldiers, saying: "Peng Pai is worthy of respect just like the heroes and good fellows of Liangshan Marsh. We all value the loyalty of the greenwood—even if our heads are cut off we must never sell out a friend." So under the squad leader's leadership, four soldiers in all passed letters out for the comrades in prison. After the letters were sent out, some comrades' families would send clothes and food and such to their kin in prison—of course they first had to give gifts or a little money to the warden and the guarding soldiers. The warden too, like the soldiers, deceived those above but not those below. And so, in prison we were able to keep contact between inside and outside unbroken, and, following the organization's instructions, established constant, unbroken contact with the underground Party organization from within the cells.

Before long, Comrade Peng Pai gave his life heroically. After his arrest, through the secret channel of the prison's outside contacts, he had resolutely told the Central Committee not to carry out any plan to storm the prison and rescue him: the enemy's guard was tight and severe, and a failed jailbreak would only cause even greater losses. When he was escorted from the cell to the execution ground, he shouted slogans all along the way: "Long live the Communist Party! The revolution is certain to be victorious! I am Peng Pai, and my sacrifice is worth it—comrades, take care of yourselves! Long live communism!" The reactionaries treated it as if facing a great foe, sending a platoon of soldiers to escort Peng Pai to the execution ground to be shot. It is said that some of the soldiers' hands were shaking as they raised their rifles on the execution ground, and it took several shots in a row before the execution was carried out.

Peng Pai's heroic sacrifice was a great shock and a great lesson to us fellow sufferers in prison; some wept quietly, some hummed the "Internationale" under their breath, and some were filled with grief and indignation and were silent; many were so grieved that they could not eat for several days on end, but no one was cowed by the enemy's atrocity. The Central Committee conveyed to imprisoned comrades-in-arms all over the country the three-point instruction of Comrade Peng Pai's concern for his comrades before his execution. Everyone, obeying Comrade Peng Pai's charge and in the spirit of enduring humiliation and bearing heavy burdens, held fast to patience and avoided the practice of hunger strikes in prison as a form of resistance, so as not to invite repression and even death to no purpose. As Lenin said: "Prison is a school that tempers the will of the revolutionary fighter to be strong."

The eight of us who had been arrested together were very united in prison and looked after one another well. The prison clothes issued to the convicts were all very large; being short and small, though I was already pregnant, when I put on such large prison clothes no one could tell. But the comrades noticed I could not eat and was always vomiting, and so they knew I was pregnant, and would no longer let me go with them to the well to draw water and wash clothes; instead they had me stay with "godmother" and help her with light work like mending and patching. Before we washed the clothes the convicts sent to be washed, we would very carefully turn out the pockets to inspect them, and sometimes would find little notes that political prisoners had written in pencil on rough paper. In prison the convicts were not called by name but each was assigned a number to be called by. For instance, one note read: "I am No. 704, please help me find No. 705, arrested at the same time, and ask him what he said in his deposition to the enemy?"—plainly so that the two men's depositions would agree, to deal with the enemy. We would then put No. 704's little note into the pocket of No. 705's already-washed prison clothes, and after No. 705 had written a reply, put it back into the pocket of the cleaned clothes to pass to No. 704. It was the squad leader who had me go with "godmother" to deliver the clean clothes back to the convicts, so that we could use the chance of delivering the washed clothes to secretly link up the fellow sufferers in prison and pass news among them. So the political prisoners all welcomed us warmly when they saw us, saying we were like fairy maidens come down from heaven. I remember one comrade, who after his release worked at the Anhui Provincial Committee, even wrote two lines of verse: "Beyond the wall, peach blossom; within the wall, blood— / both alike bright, both alike red." The verse expressed the comrades' strong will and revolutionary optimism as they underwent the severe test in prison.

We were held for a hundred days, during which we were subjected to only one court hearing. The judge who interrogated us was very young, and asked us why we had gone on strike. We said we had to work twelve hours a day, which really was too bitter and too exhausting, and we asked the mill to institute an eight-hour workday; if it truly could not be done, then at least to change it to a ten-hour workday, so that we would have time to eat and a little rest. Several elder sisters who had already borne children also proposed that the mill should let them have a quarter-hour to nurse their babies; and further, that women hands should be given a maternity leave, before and after childbirth, of two months on full pay. We also said that workers were human beings too, that the mill must not beat, curse, or dismiss workers at will, and we demanded the abolition of the body-search system… These demands and conditions all accorded with ordinary human feeling and carried no political leaning whatever. That young judge probably still had a bit of a sense of justice and sympathy, for after hearing us he did not rebuke or curse us, but only said we must put our mark to the deposition, and asked whether we could read. We all answered together that we could not read. The judge then had us make a cross on the record of the deposition. As I made mine I even deliberately made my hand tremble, so that I drew even the cross all crooked and askew, very like a country person who did not know a single character. From the eyes and expressions of the soldiers who escorted us to trial one could also see that they sympathized with us, because they all now knew that we had only opposed the body-search system and asked for shorter hours and a slight improvement in our lives—what crime was that? Of course, we ourselves protested all the more, at every turn, that we had been jailed innocently and wronged. So in the end that judge ruled that we be acquitted and released.

Another important reason we could be released was that the strike struggle outside had already won victory. Because the national bourgeoisie supported Chiang Kai-shek in establishing the Nanjing National Government and had just mounted the political stage, they had tasted some sweetness from it. On some small points of detail they made some concessions, satisfying part of the striking workers' demands, in the hope of resuming work as soon as possible and grabbing more profit. The leadership of the strike committee also considered that if it dragged on in a long stalemate—holding out for more than a year, as in the Canton–Hong Kong Strike of those years—the workers would have no wage income, and what would become of the old and young in their families with nothing to eat; besides, they did not, like Guangdong, have the support of a revolutionary government, and so they too thought the thing should be stopped while it was still timely. After both sides made some compromises, the order was given, and the workers of the spinning and silk mills went back to work one after another.

The eight of us underwent a severe test and good tempering in Longhua Prison, and when we came out the union sent people to receive us and take us back, with something of the flavor of a triumphal return. Not only had this strike of ours won victory: in that year of 1929, fierce civil wars broke out among the various factions of the Kuomintang's new warlords, the revolutionary forces recovered somewhat, and the strike struggles the Party led in various places almost all won victories of one scale or another. This created the objective cause for the second commission of the "Left" adventurist error (also called the "Li Lisan line") during 1930, when Comrade Li Lisan presided over the work of the Central Committee and wrongly estimated the balance of forces between the enemy and ourselves.

At the scene of the strike struggle before I entered prison, I had seen Wang Ming come to conduct interviews in the capacity of a reporter for the magazine *Red Flag*. Later, in Longhua Prison, Wang Ming had also come to visit us in the name of the General Labor Union, giving us two silver dollars and a package of preserved kohlrabi as a token of comfort. In those days he was not at all well known in the Party.

It was only after I got out of prison that I learned Comrade He Chang had already been transferred and dispatched by the Party Central to work at the Southern Bureau, established in Hong Kong (first as head of the propaganda department, later as secretary). The reason the Party Central was then established in Shanghai was that Shanghai had the French and Japanese concessions and the International Settlement jointly administered by Britain, the United States, and others. The concessions were governed by foreigners, and the Nanjing Kuomintang government could not arrest or search at will within them, so setting the Central organs in Shanghai made it possible to use this special environment of the concessions to more easily conceal the Party's underground work. The Southern Bureau was a Central dispatched organ leading the revolutionary struggle in the provinces of Guangdong, Guangxi, and Fujian, and for the same reason it was established in Hong Kong under British rule. He Chang wired the Central Committee requesting that I be transferred to work at the Southern Bureau. The Central Committee considered that I had just come out of prison, was in very poor health, and was pregnant, so that working in Shanghai was inconvenient and there was no one to look after me, and that a change of workplace was indeed necessary; and so it approved He Chang's application and agreed to send me to work at the Southern Bureau.

On "July First" of 1990, a forum of the Longhua fellow sufferers was held in Shanghai. Xia Zhengnong, a member of the Central Advisory Commission, attended the meeting. Those who took part had all been held in Longhua Prison at various times, and not a few comrades were not released until the full-scale War of Resistance. Although we had not known one another before, we all had the experience of the struggle in Longhua Prison, and we felt like old friends at first meeting, all agreeing that this was an unforgettable and glorious chapter in our lives!
