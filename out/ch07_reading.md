## Old Fang

Around ten in the morning, Old Lu, the day-shift attendant at the Shenxin Hotel, showed the guest up to a room on the second floor; by day the rooms on this floor were all in his charge. He set the two leather cases on the rack, shut the window, turned on the room's steam heat, and stood there without moving. Two days more and it would be New Year's Eve; guests were few, and a good many of the long-staying lodgers had left as well. Not until the thirtieth of the last month was past would guests come to take a room for their card-playing.

The guest's name was Chen; Mr. Chen tucked fifty cents into his pocket. Not the open-handed sort of guest, but one who knew the rules and understood how things were done. Had it not been so near the New Year, he would most likely have brought out no more than a single twenty-five-cent note. Old Lu said with a broad smile: “Mr. Chen, I'll bring you hot water for your tea by and by. There are telephones at both ends of the corridor; for calls outside the hotel, we've a girl at the switchboard.”

“Good—thank you.”

“Is there anything else you require?”

The guest shook his head. All the way up the stairs Old Lu had made a little conversation, but the guest was a man of few words and hardly took it up. He looked thoroughly worn out, his face lean and thin; he must have gone several days without shaving. That heavy woolen overcoat with the fur-trimmed collar gave off the smell of a long journey, yet on him it looked very fine indeed.

He had said only that he came from ‘Xinjing,’ and that he was a merchant in the antiques trade. Ever since the puppet state of Manchukuo was set up the year before, Shanghai's hotels had often seen guests from up there; these newly risen northeastern grandees of doubtful provenance were all well-off, and there was no telling what they came to Shanghai to do. Had this Mr. Chen been a guest fond of a chat, Old Lu might well have talked with him about the Japanese army's recent seizure of Shanhaiguan.

The guest seemed suddenly to come to himself: “Oh yes—Old Lu, I'd like a haircut. Is there a decent place nearby?”

Old Lu warmed a little to this guest who had worn so blank a face all along; the hotel's guests usually just called out ‘boy,’ and no one cared what a servant's name might be.

“There's one right here in the hotel—the barber should be on by now. Or outside: go out and head north, follow this road at the door straight down to Third Avenue, turn the corner, and you'll see one.” Old Lu gave careful directions.

“Thank you.”

Chen Qianli went downstairs and took a copy of Shen Bao from the table in the front hall. On the front page he read first the news that Chiang Kai-shek had gone in person to Nanchang to take command of the ‘Communist-suppression’; then he turned to the advertisement page and ran his eye over it, seemingly at idle—all small notices of premises to let, used cars wanted, calligraphy and paintings for sale. At the hotel door he slid the paper into his overcoat pocket and, following the attendant Old Lu's directions, set off toward Third Avenue.

The New Year was near, and the street was hung with the smell of salted fish and cured meats. Outside several woolen-and-silk shops flew cloth banners calling for a boycott of Japanese goods. He slowed his pace and walked at his ease, like the other passersby on the street.

Crack—a sharp report from the lane on the left, and a small boy came shooting out, bundled up round as a ball, his new padded jacket already smeared with dirt. In his left hand he clutched a few firecrackers, in his right a lit paper taper, and he barreled straight into Chen Qianli, who stepped aside and caught the child. He looked at his watch, then noticed a tobacco-and-sundries shop by the mouth of the lane, a public telephone on its counter. He paid, dialed a number, and waited a good while before the other end took up the receiver…

He saw the barbershop the attendant Old Lu had spoken of, right on the corner opposite, but he did not go over; instead he sprang aboard a tram at the stop in the middle of the road.

A month and more before, Chen Qianli had left the training school at Khabarovsk, where he had lived for three years. After three years even the hard, unsentimental instructor had felt the pang of parting; that day he had called Chen Qianli up out of the icy water where he was winter-swimming, and the two of them had drunk the night through in the instructor's quarters. Then came the long march of ten thousand li: first a train of six hundred kilometers, through the forests of Siberia all the way, to Vladivostok, where—who would have thought it—he was held up half a month.

It was winter; a thick sheet of ice had formed over the sea of the outer harbor, and only a few freighters carrying goods the Soviet Union urgently needed could pass in and out of port, an icebreaker clearing the way before them. It cost him no end of trouble to find a freighter that would take a passenger.

When he set out from Khabarovsk, his instructions had been to slip into Fujian and the two Guang provinces and work to split and break up the upper ranks of the Kuomintang warlords, preparing the ground in advance to smash the enemy's next ‘encirclement and suppression’ of the Central Soviet. He was to have disembarked at Hong Kong; but at Qingdao an unexpected visitor came aboard and called at his single cabin. This man had him change his plan for the time being and make Shanghai his destination instead.

“‘Old Kai’ of the Central Liaison Bureau has been arrested in Shanghai; the agents surrounded the meeting place. Five other comrades were taken with him. Counting ‘Old Kai,’ the meeting was to have been attended by twelve comrades, who were afterward to form a temporary action team and carry out a secret mission.”

The visitor told Chen Qianli that the task they were to carry out bore on a momentous decision the Central Committee had lately taken—a top-secret plan that, even within the organization, only a very few comrades knew of. The higher-ups were transferring Chen Qianli over for the time being, with instructions to help ‘Old Kai’ and carry that task forward. The particulars would be conveyed to him by someone after he reached Shanghai, and the comrade who conveyed the content of the mission to him would very likely still be ‘Old Kai.’

Chen Qianli asked: “Is there any chance the arrested comrades can be got out of prison?”

“Not all who came to the meeting were taken. By what we've learned, the agents rushed the place before the meeting had even begun. Among those arrested, no one but ‘Old Kai’ knows the content of the task. And so far no one knows which of them ‘Old Kai’ actually is. ‘Old Kai’ is a seasoned comrade, and the organization believes the relevant intelligence has not, as yet, leaked.

“The Party organization is bringing every force to bear to rescue them. As things look now, the hopes are high. By the time you reach Shanghai they may well already be out; you are to find a way to make contact with ‘Old Kai,’ who will pass on to you the Central Committee's top-secret plan and the particular task the temporary action team is charged with.”

Chen Qianli gazed out the porthole at the harbor lying in the night. “For the Shanghai group to be arrested at the very scene of a secret meeting means the underground Party has very likely been penetrated. I need to know more.”

The visitor picked up his teacup and warmed his palms against it. “The organization judges the same. Of late, underground Party bodies in Shanghai have been broken one after another. The agents have even burst into the Party Central's most secret organs; there is evidence to confirm that someone, once arrested, has turned traitor.

“These past two years, that Special Operations Headquarters in Nanjing seems to have hit upon some knack; it's said that, for the frequency with which they have wrecked our Party's underground bodies, they have won more and more of Chiang Kai-shek's trust. The secret-service apparatus has been greatly enlarged these two years, and the situation of the struggle just now is grave indeed.

“From an inside line the higher-ups have obtained intelligence that an agent under the cryptonym ‘Xi Shi’ has very likely been planted within our ranks. The source does not know when he first wormed his way into the Party. It was only because the Special Operations Headquarters, in its self-congratulation, boasted of its ‘Communist-suppression’ successes that the word slipped out. Our intelligence section has made an analysis, and has the sense that this ‘Xi Shi’ seems at times a long-buried agent, and at times more like a newly turned traitor.”

Chen Qianli had just been turning the same question over: strictly speaking, if a Party underground body had been penetrated and damaged by the enemy, then that part of the system ought to be frozen and left unused for the time being.

“Since there's a traitor within, why not build a separate action team and lay out the task afresh?”

“Time presses hard. The task the Shanghai temporary action team is to carry out is part of the Central Committee's top-secret plan; its members were picked in haste by the organization from people in all manner of trades, with an eye to the circumstances the task might meet, and such a body cannot easily be reassembled at short notice. What is more, ‘Old Kai’ has already met with them. In sending you over, the higher-ups hope you can form a precise judgment of how far the underground has been penetrated, root out the traitor as soon as you can, and at the same time help ‘Old Kai’ complete the task.”

“Before the action team was called together, how many people knew of this?”

“Apart from ‘Old Kai,’ at that time only Fang Yunping—the comrade in charge of the Shanghai underground Party—was in the know. Those who came to the team had merely received notice of a meeting. The higher-ups had meant Old Fang, too, to join the action team, as its head, working with ‘Old Kai’ to complete the task. But word has it he did not go to the meeting that day.”

“Everyone was at the meeting place—why did the agents take only six?”

“The market was in great confusion, and it all happened suddenly; some of the comrades slipped into the crowd and got away.”

“Of these people, whom do the higher-ups reckon can be trusted?”

“The higher-ups await your judgment.”

“That Old Fang would seem to be open to suspicion?”

“If there were something wrong with Old Fang, the enemy would have made its arrests earlier and cast a wider net. The organization does not, at present, incline that way.”

Chen Qianli thought this reasoning none too tight.

On the visitor's instructions he came to Shanghai. The steamer lay a night at the mouth of the Wusong River; in the morning, after the tide went out, a pilot came aboard, and the Settlement's foreign police came up with him. A policeman questioned Chen Qianli and set him down on the register as a merchant in the antiques trade. Ashore, he had a rickshaw man pull him to the Shenxin Hotel, and once settled he went at once to the contact point to find Old Fang.

Chen Qianli got off the tram before the North Sichuan Road Bridge, crossed it, and turned west along the embankment of Suzhou Creek, circling the Post Office building to come back to North Sichuan Road. He looked about him, made sure no one was following, and then walked north along the road. This was a quarter he had once frequented; he wondered whether the Shuimo Bookshop and the Xinken Bookshop in Gongyi Lane were still open—Lu Xun, Feng Xuefeng, and Chen Geng had once taken part here in the doings of the magazine The Outpost. Gongyi Lane was a gathering-place of Cantonese; to its northwest stood the Yihong Garden, which Mr. Sun had visited more than once, and just now before its gate a wedding party—bride and groom, kin and friends—was making ready a modern-style ceremony.

Chen Qianli threaded through the crowd, went into the lane on the other side of the road, and found the barbershop.

A customer lay with a hot towel over his face in a barber's chair tipped back on its hinge. The barber, a man in his twenties, stood razor in hand, about to give him a shave. Seeing another customer come in, he pointed with the razor to the other chair alongside, and Chen Qianli sat down. The shop went suddenly quiet; none of the three men spoke. After a while the customer being shaved spoke from under the hot towel: “This gentleman's not a local, I think?”

“I've only just come to Shanghai by ship from Qingdao.”

“The New Year's nearly here—come to see relatives, have you?”

“On business.”

“With the year's end so close, and in this bitter cold—there's still business to be done?”

“The antiques trade keeps no season. Wherever there's a fine piece, there you must hurry.”

As they spoke, the barber went and shut the shop door. The customer being shaved pulled off the towel, gone cold now, and turned his face toward Chen Qianli—

He said he was Old Fang.

The barber took up the kettle and stood outside the shop door, where a stove by the door was kept for boiling water. He had shut the door, and now there were only the two of them in the shop.

Old Fang lay back in the chair and drew the cooled towel over his face again, leaving only his two eyes uncovered, fixed on a small mirror that hung on the wall opposite. This was no doubt the mirror to be handed to a customer once his hair was done. Following Old Fang's gaze, Chen Qianli looked at the mirror too. It hung with cunning, turned a little to the side, so as to face just the window by the shop door; through the window one could see all that stirred outside.

“Here—you don't feel it's safe?” Chen Qianli asked softly.

“Here there's no trouble. That's my son.” Old Fang pointed outside the door.

After a moment he added: “The enemy has got hold of most of our places; where I lodge was surrounded too, and I fled. This spot has never once served as a contact point, and none of those arrested know of it.”

“He seems none too willing?” Chen Qianli looked at the door; the crack of it went now bright, now dark.

“He wants to take part in the work; he wants to do great things.”

Outside the door, Old Fang's son was turning away a regular from nearby who had come for a haircut: “We're too busy—two customers just sat down. Come back later.”

“Everyone who comes here for a haircut is a resident of the lanes hereabouts,” Old Fang said in a low voice.

Chen Qianli stood, went to the door and looked out, then turned back, took up a white barber's cloth folded on the stool beside him, draped it over himself, and sat down. “Better not be careless all the same. You didn't go to the meeting that day?”

“I was to have gone, but I was late. When I got near the market I saw the Municipal Police's van, and afterward I ran into Cui Wentai, who had fled from the meeting place.”

“You called the meeting—how came you to be late?”

Old Fang hesitated. “The night before the meeting, the higher-ups sent someone to tell me to make contact, at six the next morning, with a comrade at a bun shop by the mouth of Pu'enjishi Road, aslant across from the Lyceum Theatre. When the bun shop opens in the morning it lays its door-boards out flat in front, and those eating breakfast can use them for a table; the comrade would be sitting there at the time.

“The moment I got there I felt something was wrong: there were far too many idlers about, and so early in the day there ought not to have been so many people dressed like that on the street. From a distance I saw that the table outside the bun shop was empty; no one was sitting there.

“I had to do something, to warn that comrade off. On the road to the east a man stood smoking against the trunk of a plane tree—just such a suspicious-looking idler as I've described. I went up to him to borrow a light, and while he was off his guard I patted his clothes—sure enough, there was a pistol underneath. Before he could react I gave him a punch, drew the gun, and fired a few shots into the air.

“A little way east and Pu'enjishi Road comes to an end; ahead is Xiaobangwan, and the lanes run through, so that from inside you can go straight on to Shengmuyuan Road next to it. I ran east, firing as I ran, then threw the gun away in Xiaobangwan. They did not catch me. I waited a long time before circling back, got hold of a hat and put it on, and asked around near the bun shop; they say the plainclothesmen took no one.”

“Who was so important as to make you run so great a risk?”

“Do you know who that comrade was?”

“Tell me.” Chen Qianli watched Old Fang closely.

Old Fang turned his head. “That was Comrade Haohan.”

Chen Qianli knew the name, of course, though it was only a working alias.

“The higher-ups' original instruction was for me to make contact with Comrade Haohan, arrange a safe house, and await word. A few days ago someone in the Central Committee's most secret organ turned traitor, and Comrade Haohan had to withdraw; the situation was critical. So I asked the higher-ups: since it's so dangerous, why not move Comrade Haohan out at once, instead of having me make contact with him? My own place is none too safe either. And they said: wait till after tomorrow's meeting and you'll understand; ‘Old Kai’ will tell you what to do.”

“When did you notify everyone of the meeting?”

“Starting the afternoon of the day before, I made contact with each of them one by one, running about the whole afternoon through; it was evening before I had notified all ten. With me, and with ‘Old Kai,’ that made twelve in all. The choice of who was left to me as well; I called them together at short notice, saying only that there was an important task. Six were taken, five got away. Those five I all know—so ‘Old Kai’ must have been arrested.”

“Was the place your arrangement?”

“Yes—it was a new place; we'd only just got hold of it, and hadn't yet—”

Now and then someone passed outside the door, and they would break off their talk, like two somewhat impatient customers—one cracking his knuckles, the other endlessly crossing and recrossing his legs.

Old Fang thought a moment, and said with great gravity: “I am willing to submit to the organization's investigation.” From inside his padded jacket he drew a letter and handed it to Chen Qianli.

Brother Fang, as though we met face to face: the circumstances of Old Yi and your sisters, I trust you already know in full. Having now entered the hospital, we are resolved to fight it out. Our hearts are quite at peace; the worst that can come is no more than a single death. The weather is bitterly cold; may you and the others take good care. Please also convey word to our honored father and mother, and may they keep themselves well. Your sister Ling and the rest.

Chen Qianli turned the sheet over and found a passage of secret writing, in very small characters, written moreover on the back of the text on the front, so that it was hard to find.

All comrades are resolved. The matter of the dice is out; there is a traitor.

“This came out from the comrades in prison?”

“From the two comrades, Ling Wen and Dong Huiwen.” Old Fang pointed out to Chen Qianli a few inconspicuous ink dots in the lower corner of the sheet. “Their position and their number—the enemy could never counterfeit them.”

“What are the dice?”

“The way of making contact was the pai gow and the dice. Each man would bring a few dominoes, and when all had come they would make up a set of pai gow between them. When the meeting formally opened, ‘Old Kai’ would bring out a pair of dice. I don't know ‘Old Kai’ by sight myself, and so we used this method. The enemy does not yet know about ‘Old Kai,’ but they do know about the dice.”

“Who knows about the dice?”

“In this I was careless. I should not have told anyone about the dice. I was to go myself, so there was no need at all to tell the others. At the time I only thought that once the meeting began they would all know anyway. Going back over it carefully, several people knew about the dice, and I don't know whether Old Yi may have told Ling Wen. It was a recognition signal; they had been drafted into this team at short notice from all the different lines, and I told them that the man who brought out the dice at the meeting would be the comrade sent from above to assign the task.”

“Might they tell others in prison?” Chen Qianli shook his head almost imperceptibly. “This letter—was it safe to collect?”

“We rent boxes 137 and 138 at the Xujiahui post office; 138 is right below 137, and there's a contrivance in the partition—give it a light pull and the letter in 137 drops down. Even with someone watching alongside, it's not easy to notice the person opening box 138.”

“It's too great a risk all the same—” Chen Qianli examined the letter closely once more.

“My courier is Cui Wentai; it was he who actually fetched the letter, and he handed it to me only this morning.”

“Tell me first about the comrades who came to the meeting.”

“I'm in charge of the local Party organization, and I know these people well; every one has stood severe tests. Ling Wen is a woman writer; she took part in the Left League and knows a good many people. Her husband died a martyr. I feel she ought to settle to being a writer—our Party needs comrades like her too—but she says she means to carry on the work her husband left unfinished, and is willing to run the risks of underground work.

“Liang Shichao took part in the Nanchang Uprising, and was gravely wounded in the fight against the ‘encirclement and suppression,’ so that he had to be sent to Shanghai; Comrade Qin Chuan'an was the doctor who treated his wound.

“Wei Dafu—the organization placed him on purpose in a house-letting agency; many of our secret contact points were rented through his hands. Had there been something wrong with him, the underground would have been exposed long ago.

“Comrade Yi Junnian was transferred to Shanghai by the organization in July of the eighteenth year of the Republic. Already in Guangzhou he had been in intelligence work all along; he is rich in experience of the secret struggle, and more than once he got word out ahead of the enemy's moves. A reliable comrade.

“Tian Fei works at the library above the market; it was he who found the meeting place. I've thought hard about whether there might be something wrong with him, but I can find no sign of it.”

Old Fang seemed to grow agitated, pinching endlessly at his knuckles. “They are all reliable comrades; their trades may differ, and some of them want experience, but I cannot doubt their loyalty to the Party.”

“We are not doubting them, but examining them,” Chen Qianli corrected him.

“I agree. They are willing to sacrifice everything for the Party's cause. Li Han's elder brother, Ling Wen's husband—both died as martyrs. Chen Qianyuan and Dong Huiwen are sweethearts, the two of them full of ardor—” Old Fang looked up suddenly at Chen Qianli, wondering whether he had really seen his eyes give a flicker. What a strange chance!

“What is Chen Qianyuan to you?”

“He's my younger brother.” Across Chen Qianli's mind flashed the way Chen Qianyuan had looked in the old days. “We've not seen each other in nearly three years. All these people, my brother among them—what I want most to understand is what they were before. History—” He looked at Old Fang in the mirror. “A man's face is hard to see clearly; it is painted, layer on layer, out of his history—”

“In White-area work there's no keeping files on comrades.” Old Fang found this comrade sent by the organization sharp-eyed, yet a touch dogmatic and finicky in his thinking. “These few years the Kuomintang has built up its secret-service bodies like men gone mad; one careless step in underground work and the enemy finds you out. System after system has suffered losses; many comrades, their original line of work broken, have lost touch with those above and below them. They seek out the Party with the greatest revolutionary ardor, and are then assigned to some other line. By our discipline, within a new working system no one should refer to the past. Yet it is loyalty and trust between comrades that are the very ground on which the work is done.”

“Yi Junnian—is his source of intelligence reliable?”

“When Old Yi came to Shanghai the Great Revolution had been in ruins two years, and the underground was hard pressed. The organization transferred him precisely to rebuild the intelligence network. Over these years he has done the work bit by bit, and furnished the Party with a great deal of valuable intelligence.” Old Fang added: “Once, there being no time to warn the comrade charged with a task, he dealt with a traitor by his own hand, and averted a serious loss.”

“Tell me more particularly.” For the first time Chen Qianli let Old Fang feel he took some interest in the subject.

“The organization looked into it; the traitor was highly dangerous, and Old Yi acted with decision—he did right. Afterward the agents took great revenge, and one comrade in Old Yi's intelligence network died for it too.”

“So that's how it was—” Chen Qianli retraced, step by step, the threads of Old Fang's account. “How far has the rescue got now?”

“Through connections in the Nineteenth Route Army, the Judge Advocate's office has already had us submit surety papers. I'm arranging it now—the plan is for Qin Chuan'an to stand surety for Liang Shichao; he keeps a private clinic and has a good standing in Shanghai society.”

“He too is a member of the team, and went to the meeting at the market—is it fitting to have him come forward?”

“By rights, indeed, he ought not to; but he says none of the others in the team know him, and since Liang Shichao has all along helped out at his clinic, for him to stand surety is proper and aboveboard. Dr. Qin came out of the German Hospital and has practiced these many years in the Settlement, making the acquaintance of no few high officials and notables. So long as one is not seized red-handed by the enemy, or the evidence is not conclusive, they cannot simply make arrests as they please within the Settlement.”

Old Fang said with anxiety: “The sureties for the other comrades aren't yet settled; I'm trying to arrange them, but these days the agents are looking for me everywhere—my likeness is posted on the iron gates of the Settlement, and I can't move about by day. To keep the organization safe I've cut most of my working ties, and have been waiting all this while for you.”

“On the one hand letting them out on bail, on the other hunting you down—what is the enemy playing at?”

Both men fell silent.

Someone pushed at the door—it was Old Fang's son, who put his head in and said: “There are two strangers loitering about in the cross-lane.” With that he drew back, and the door closed.

Chen Qianli rose on the alert and went to the door again. “Have these comrades had any lateral working ties among themselves before?”

Old Fang rose too, glancing out the door and, without thinking, picking up the razor beside him—holding the blade by his fingertips and tapping the handle softly on the little table. “Ling Wen and Wei Dafu are Yi Junnian's subordinates, the inside staff of the intelligence network. I chose Old Yi on purpose; he has strategy, and he's brave as well—” Old Fang looked at Chen Qianli. “From certain angles, I do think he's rather like you. We've gone to great lengths to smooth the way for the rescue, and they should be released from Longhua any day now; when the time comes you can make contact with him—the two of you might hit it off—”

“We must get out of here at once.” Chen Qianli cut Old Fang short.

A figure stirred at the doorway; someone shoved the door open with an elbow, hands drawn up into the sleeves of a padded long gown, and stepped inside. Behind him Young Fang called out: “No time to do you today—come early tomorrow, sir—” and with the same motion nudged the man a step further in, stepped in himself, and shut the door with a hand behind his back.

Seeing how things stood, the man let his drawn-up hands fall, lifted the hem of his padded gown, and reached inside with the movement—

In two strides Chen Qianli was behind him, pinning his hand, feeling along it, and said to Old Fang, one word: “Gun.”

“Be sensible—the detective squad's whole force is right behind, they'll be here any moment; come along with us—” Before the man had finished, Chen Qianli took the razor from Old Fang's hand with the motion and drew it across the agent's throat.

Chen Qianli held the fellow up and slowly let him down onto the floor.

“There's a crowd coming at the mouth of the lane,” Young Fang urged. “You two go out the back door first.”

Old Fang bent and drew a Smith & Wesson revolver from the agent's waist, and, breaking it open to check the rounds in the chambers, looked gravely at Chen Qianli. “You go by the back door—find Old Yi first.” He stepped out the door, then turned back to Chen Qianli and said: “Take my son with you!”

Old Fang went out into the lane, looked back once at his son, raised his hand and fired a shot into the air, and ran off into the depths of the lane. Standing behind the door, Chen Qianli saw through the window that he had just reached the mouth of the cross-lane when he stopped short and turned, as if meaning to run back; at that a burst of wild gunfire came from the direction of the lane-mouth, and a bullet struck Old Fang in the shoulder. He staggered a few steps and ducked into the cross-lane.

But the back door was blocked too. Young Fang pushed it open, put his head out for only a moment, and drew back. There were plainclothes agents at the lane-mouths on either side of the back door as well. The two of them went upstairs and climbed by a dim, narrow flight to the drying-terrace. Young Fang ran to the parapet of the terrace, put his head over to look outside, and, pointing beyond the wall, said to Chen Qianli: “You go down, crawl along that wall—over the roof is the neighbors' drying-terrace.”

Two more shots rang out in the lane, then a burst of wild firing, and then quiet. A flock of someone's pigeons started up from the rooftops into mid-air, and somewhere a window was hastily shut.

Chen Qianli got up onto the top of the parapet and called back: “Keep close behind me.”

The guns sounded again. Young Fang waved him off. “I'm going back for the old man.” He turned and dashed for the head of the stairs, and in the blink of an eye was gone.

Chen Qianli hesitated a moment, then edged forward. The parapet joined the gable-end of the house next door, more than half a man's height below the roof; he set his hands to it, hauled himself onto the roof, stepped lightly onto the tiles, crawled a few paces over the ridge, and sure enough, below was a drying-terrace.

He lay flat, and heard from the drying-terrace next door a din of struggle—someone tumbling down the stairs, someone else cursing, then a scramble of footsteps, as if many pairs of leather shoes were treading the wooden stair-boards; then more fighting, and someone suddenly began to shout and curse—it was Young Fang the barber, his voice broken and muffled, as though it came from very far off, as though from a face pressed against a cement floor.

He crouched by the wall and thought: the enemy perhaps knew there had been three men in the barbershop. If so, they would soon search every household in the lane, and he could not linger here too long. Chen Qianli stood up on the drying-terrace and gazed about him: this stretch of shikumen houses ran roof to roof, eave to eave.

After climbing over two more drying-terraces, he found a road beyond the wall. Chen Qianli took off his overcoat, turned it inside out to show the lambswool within, put it back on, and went down the stairs from the terrace, slipping quietly along the passage. No one. He went down, crossed the courtyard well, and pushed open a door; behind it was a tea shop giving onto the street. There were no customers within, and the proprietor stared in astonishment as, paying him no heed, Chen Qianli walked out of the shop door.

A crowd had gathered at the mouth of the lane where the barbershop was; several policemen brandished batons, cowing a few young men who had come close to gawk. The batons struck against padded jackets, and wadding flew up in wisps. Chen Qianli did not leave at once, but stood quietly a while at the back of the crowd, and heard someone say: “The young one's in a dreadful state, his face all blood. The old one was beaten to death on the spot.”

On the road Chen Qianli thought again and again of what Old Fang had said just now: “Take my son with you!”
