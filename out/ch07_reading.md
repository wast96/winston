## Juraku

The Jurakudai stands in the Uchino, in the capital.

For all that it was Hideyoshi's palace in the capital, the Jurakudai had the look of a vast lowland castle: a moat drawn round it — Ōmiya to the east, the Jōfuku-ji to the west, Ichijō to the north, Shimochōjamachi to the south — flagstones laid, and loopholes cut into its white walls. But that spring the master of the palace, having issued the order to make ready for the crossing into Korea, had moved to Ōsaka to oversee a military government suddenly grown busy, and of late the Juraku had the look of a house with its fire gone out.

Now, Jūzō had thrown Watanabe Satoru off behind the Chōsokabe mansion on Sakaimachi; and by the time he had run the few chō to the eastern corner of Shimochōjamachi, it was already past the last part of the Hour of the Rat.

There was still an hour to go.

His appointment with Kazama Gohei was for the first part of the Hour of the Ox.

(That cost me more time than I'd bargained for.)

Pressing himself against the black board-fence of a townhouse, Jūzō gazed at the huge structure of the Jurakudai across from him, and in his mind there was no longer any thought of the assassin of a while before.

When a rappa has set himself inside a piece of work, other rappa — friend and foe alike — slip in from every quarter; and to let himself be caught up only in guessing at the breed of the shadow-shapes flitting before his eyes is to risk losing the main thread of the thing altogether. Even so, what puzzled Jūzō was that the man of a while ago had been no rappa, and had used the correct, well-schooled swordsmanship of the Kantō.

(Gohei would never use a plain samurai. He'd use a rappa. So whoever set the man on, it was not Gohei.)

Among the disciplines of Iga there is one called flowing water: a way of living in which the heart is kept forever moving like water, never let to settle. But the Jūzō who had come into Shimochōjamachi was no longer the Jūzō who had turned a bare blade at Sakaimachi. He put the assassin out of mind and bent his whole attention on the great pile before him. To slip in here tonight was not only to meet Gohei. Before the day came for his final object — entering Hideyoshi's private chambers — the one thing he had, above all, to know was the lay of the ground within the Jurakudai's walls.

There are pines.

Beyond the pines stood a white rampart wall, and above it a single cluster of star-mist rose climbing into the sky. Stare hard, and it seemed almost to shimmer with a greenish fire. The shinobi of Iga had always read it so: when a blue light rises at night to the northwest of a castle, the garrison is weak. Jūzō let his brows relax a little.

From the edge of the moat he sank quietly under the water.

When he had swum to the foot of the stone rampart, from Jūzō's hands under the water a single long rake went snaking up the stones like a serpent, lengthening as it climbed. The tool is called a shinobi rake: lengths of thin bamboo cut at each joint and threaded on a hemp cord, so that a pull on the cord makes it stiffen at once into a pole and sets the rake-head at its tip against some high place.

Over the wall and inside, Jūzō wrung the water from his ninja garb, folded the rake away and buried it in the earth, and when he had crossed a further small wall of the inner enclosure and passed within, he caught his breath in spite of himself at the grandeur of the design. Under the starry sky an artificial landscape of black hills and waters spread out without end.

Jūzō followed the course of a garden stream, trying to find the heart of the palace. As the water fell it changed shape from pond to pond, until at last it poured into a blue pool that held the mirrored stars.

At the center of the pool was an island, and on the island rose a three-storied tower of a strange shape Jūzō had never seen before, soaring toward the sky.

It looked like a heavenly maiden come down to earth and dancing in her longing for the sky; and it was like, too, one of the auspicious birds said to dwell on the isle of Hōrai, spreading its wings to lift now from the ground.

Out of the night sky the building carved for itself, a soundless music seemed to well up without cease.

In Iga there is a saying: to tread the tally of castles. As a shinobi Jūzō had trodden the tally of many castles; but set beside this Jurakudai, which the master of the realm had raised on the ground of a royal capital, they were meaner than a peasant's hut in the country. Standing before such beauty, he felt a strange numbing of the heart, as the uncanny powers he had trained into himself as a shinobi thinned away like mist.

There is a hushed delight that any man of Iga feels, sinking by night into the darkness of an enemy's castle and its waters. By day the castle belongs to others; but the castle in darkness, for as long as the darkness lasts, is his own — that is the lonely pleasure peculiar to the shinobi. What Jūzō was tasting now was no such trifling thing. Through the beauty of this vast castle and its waters, he felt himself seated alone, just the two of them, face to face with Hideyoshi, the master of all under heaven. A common samurai is bound within the net of rank; the shinobi stands always alone, facing the realm. And of the splendor of that solitude, surely no man had ever tasted on so vast a scale as Jūzō did now.

(Now then — the First Palace…?)

Roused at last from his intoxication, Jūzō looked slowly about him. What he had named the First Palace was surely no proper name but a common byword for whichever building stood at the center of the complex. It could only be that three-storied tower out in the pool.

There was no bridge across the pool.

The island floated at the center of the water.

For fear of an enemy stealing in, they seemed to keep to boats as a rule: where the reeds thinned he made out a small landing of stacked stone. No light burned in the tower. By that, it seemed likely that only a handful of guards were on watch within.

The watch being so thin, there was no particular need to wet himself and go under the water. Judging so, Jūzō drew out the water-gear he had ready and quietly filled it with air.

The thing he had readied is called a leather water-spider.

It is a float of deerskin. To either wing of it are fixed a hoop of whalebone shaved thin and bundled, and a piece of paulownia wood; lower yourself onto it and it makes a handy little raft.

He set the leather water-spider among the reeds; the reflected stars broke faintly on the water's face, and Jūzō slid soundlessly across the dark pool.

Reaching the island, he passed inside the tower's balustrade, drew out a shinobi pick, and opened the door — and got into the tower more easily than he had thought.

First floor, second, third: through pitch-dark rooms Jūzō climbed the stairs, opened a window on the third floor, laid a hand to the eaves, and in one motion drew himself up onto the edge of the roof.

"Jūzō?"

It was Kazama Gohei.

He had come ahead and was already stretched out at his ease on the gentle pitch of the cypress-bark roof.

"So it's Gohei."

"Mm. I've been waiting a while."

Gohei's pale face broke, in spite of everything, into a fond smile.

"Nine years, is it?"

said Jūzō.

"No — ten, now."

Gohei answered, and went on:

"It's been that long since you hid yourself away on Otogi Pass. Back home — is the master of Shimotsuge keeping well?"

"Hale as ever. Speaking of the Shimotsuge master — Gohei, you'll have met Kisaru here in the capital, I think."

"Kisaru. …No, I haven't."

"No use lying. It's written on your face that you have. She was set on running the traitor Gohei through with her own hand; but seeing you here whole and hearty, it seems you took Kisaru and made a kunoichi of her."

"She's my betrothed. No one anywhere can lay the blame on me for that."

"Never mind. So — what are you doing in the capital?"

Jūzō raised himself and looked hard into Gohei's face. Gohei wore a thin smile under the starlight. Of course, Jūzō already knew, from Kohagi, that Gohei had taken service with Maeda Gen'i, the magistrate of Kyoto.

"I grew sick of the ninja's life. I took service."

"Mm."

"Which makes me, in short, the man whose office is to take you."

"Now that's a fine turn."

Jūzō gave a low chuckle.

"A secret agent of the Kyoto magistracy, no less."

A star streaked and fell.

"Ah — it's gone."

"A star?"

"Kazama Gohei, that's who."

By the law of Iga, Jūzō was bound to cut down, with his own hand, a Gohei who had cast off Iga to take service with a daimyō. But his heart shrank so from the thing that he had to take shelter in that bantering tone. Every memory of the years he had grown from child to young man was bound up with Gohei.

To cut Gohei down would be to set a blade to the half of his own life — a fierce, aching pain. It was something rawer than friendship, even.

"What brought you to sell out Iga?"

"I wanted a quiet life. I wanted a fief, to be looked up to. I wanted to find a sweet woman for a wife, get children, and pass my days looking at the garden trees. A man like Tsuzura Jūzō wouldn't understand that. Or would you?"

"No. I wouldn't."

Jūzō threw it out coldly, and went on:

"Fine, you've had your fill of the ninja's life. But why does such a man turn spy-catcher?"

"I could hardly become an accountant, could I? There's nothing for it but to sell the one skill bred into me."

"And the stipend?"

"Two hundred koku."

"So for a paltry two hundred koku you've sold your master, sold your comrades, and sold a ninja's freedom into the bargain."

"It won't stay two hundred forever. A thousand in time — and who knows, even a lord of ten thousand koku is not past dreaming of."

"And what am I worth?"

"Two thousand koku, at the least — that much is safe."

"How cheerless."

Jūzō lay flat on his belly and laughed low, a soft chuckle, as though mocking himself. Through his belly the day's warmth still held in the cypress bark came up to him, and for the first time in months he tasted a pleasant slackening of the spirit, almost drowsy. Talking on like this, after all, Kazama Gohei's very scent carried — for Jūzō alone to catch — the sweet homesickness of an old comrade.

"In that case I'll cut you down before I'm sold."

"You won't find me easy to cut."

Gohei laughed, the thin flesh of his face folding into creases.

"Gohei. Just to be sure — on whose word did you come here?"

"A woman's. I don't know her name."

"Kohagi, then."

"Oh? So she was your genin after all?"

"She is not."

"Then who is she?"

"Even I can't make out what she truly is."

"Whose side is she on — mine, or yours?"

"That, too, I don't know."

"Two men called the finest shinobi in all Iga — and we're being made fools of, then, aren't we?"

"Well — something like that."

Having said it, Jūzō went on in a lighter tone, as though something had shifted in him:

"But leaving that aside, Gohei — you know the work that brought me to the capital, don't you."

"To kill the master who lives beneath this Juraku roof, I should think."

"Mm. Come to think of it, this was once your own trade. Won't you turn your heart around and go back to being a rappa? Or is it still the two thousand koku you're after? However much you sell me for and pocket Gen'i's reward, you'll never rise as high as Hideyoshi, who began a mere foot-soldier. Better than gnawing a scrap of stipend, go back to the rappa's life — staking your life man against man with the Kampaku Hideyoshi. A man of your mettle — even at two thousand koku, it's a waste to be another man's kept hound."

"Ha-ha-ha-ha. Jūzō, wherever did you come by so glib a tongue? I'll never go back to the rappa's life, not for anything. It's enough if my two hundred koku swells to three hundred. I mean to lay the whole of the plot bare — the one Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon of Iga took in hand — and make it the seed of my own rise. Jūzō: tonight, as your old comrade Kazama Gohei, I let you pass, this once. But from tomorrow it will be otherwise. As the hound of the Kyoto magistracy I'll hunt you — through the capital, through Ōsaka, through Sakai, over the sea to Tenjiku and to Luzon."

"Big words."

As though the pleasure had gone out of it, Jūzō raised himself, and said to Gohei:

"I'll be paying you a call before long. See you wash your neck well."

With that he slid smoothly down the pitch to the eaves of the roof, and glanced back up at Gohei. Gohei was already gone. The sky over the capital is a changeable thing. Clouds had come up from who knew when: through the slope where Gohei had lain, not a single star showed now in the southern sky.

Jūzō gripped a rafter with one hand and the third-floor corner-post with the other, ran lightly down, did the same at the second floor, and from the first floor down leapt two jō into the dark of the night.

When he came to the pool's edge, the water-spider he had hidden was gone.

(Gohei — a neat touch, just as one would expect.)

Gohei must already have used it and glided off across the pool in another direction.

Jūzō sank into the water, and as he lifted his head on the far bank, the ground off to his right went suddenly bright.

A small tea-pavilion that stood in the trees a little under half a chō off had burst crackling into flame.

(Gohei — he's set it alight, the devil.)

Raising himself a little from among the reeds, Jūzō ran, a black whirlwind, toward the pool of darkness away from the fire.

The night air must have been very dry. The pavilion caught well. A pillar of fire stood up to the sky like a hemp-stalk torch.

Inside the palace grounds an uproar began. By the fire he could see the shapes of several men running this way and that.

(He's done it—)

Jūzō could only marvel at Gohei's gift as a rappa.

As Jūzō read it, Gohei of course bore no grudge against the Jurakudai. Nor had he set the fire out of any loyal zeal, to warn the palace guard of Jūzō's intrusion. Had that been his aim, he would have alerted the palace beforehand and spread a net to run him down.

For now, Gohei would do Jūzō no harm. His calculation, rather, could only be this: to let Jūzō's Iga men range through the capital to their hearts' content, wait until the affair had swelled large in his superiors' eyes, and then, with his own hand, sweep up the whole conspiracy at a stroke. The larger the catch he landed, the fatter his reward — that much went without saying.

(Take service though he may, the rappa in his bones won't wash out.)

Threading among the plantings, the buildings, the hollows as he ran, Jūzō twisted his mouth.

(Knowing that man, he'll have killed a few along the way.)

And so he had: around the blazing pavilion Gohei had been plying a glittering, lightning blade. Several of the guards fell without so much as a cry. Even the men who came to fight the fire did not find their dead until near dawn. By then, needless to say, neither Jūzō nor Gohei was anywhere in the Jurakudai.

It was a good half hour after daybreak when Jūzō came back to Kuroami.

"I'm for sleep."

Pushing open the back door and stepping into the outbuilding, Jūzō spoke low to Kuroami, who stood on the packed-earth floor.

"You'll be weary."

Kuroami, it seemed, had waited the whole night without sleep. Folding his knees at Jūzō's pillow, he said:

"This fire that broke out at the Juraku — was it Master Jūzō's doing?"

"News reaches your ears quickly."

"No quick ears of mine. Since morning the whole town's been full of the talk."

"That was not me."

"Whose, then?"

"Kazama Gohei's."

"Ho. A strange sort of helper. Why did you not cut him down?"

"I want to give him some line to swim on. Setting the Juraku alight and raising an uproar was, in the run of our work, a thing I ought to have done myself. Clever Gohei broke a sweat and did it for me."

"Which means—"

Kuroami cocked his small face, and said:

"Then we had best make quick use of the fire's opening."

To let only the fire burn and nothing come of it would be a waste, he muttered, low, half to himself. His thought was that the twenty hired rappa mustered in Rakshasa Valley must now, all together, be set to spreading rumor abroad.

Drawing the box-pillow to him, Jūzō said:

"Leave that to me. I mean to sleep my fill, so don't wake me before morning."

With a look that said there was no help for it, Kuroami drew a thin summer quilt over his master.

The next morning, with the sun already high, Jūzō went out the back door of the Iseya behind the Hōkō-ji. He had a mind to walk the town.

The capital that year was hot beyond the common.

The padded cord of his deep sedge hat grew soaked and creaking with the sweat that pooled at his chin, so that the hat meant to keep off the sun became instead an instrument of the heat.

Jūzō had been drifting along peering under the eaves of the sideshow booths near the riverbank; unable to bear it any longer, he loosed the cord and walked on through the crowd holding the hat up over his head like a shade.

He came out on Shijō.

At the foot of the bridge a monkey-act was being shown, and as he tried to peer at it from behind the ring of onlookers, a small man of no clear sort, neither samurai nor idler by his getup, turned abruptly out of the crowd to face Jūzō.

"Ah — it's you, my lord!"

"Oh — you, from that time. Keeping well?"

"Yes, my lord. Thanks to you."

"Come along. Let's take a breather at that teahouse. It's hot."

"Thank you, my lord."

Jūzō stepped into the shade of the reed screen and, making a place for the man as well, said:

"How it blazes down. Is it like this out toward Luzon too, I wonder?"

"Yes, my lord. No — that is… since then I came up to the capital and spent a good long while searching for you."

The man was Kumobei. In the ruined temple within the Tōdai-ji precincts, Jūzō had cut down Matsukura Kurando; and this man, whom he had spared, had come up to the capital just as Jūzō had bidden him. Spared, and even given money besides, the timid fellow seemed to feel himself deeply in Jūzō's debt, and:

"Is there nothing I might do for you?"

he said, edging close. His clownish face strained with every feature to show gratitude and submission.

"Do for me — how?"

"Anything I might do, my lord."

"There is, as it happens. For a while, take on as errand-runner to the sword-polisher Iseya Kahei, behind the Hōkō-ji. On the side, store up in your ears whatever gossip goes round the samurai houses. Now — is there any interesting talk about the town just now?"

"The Juraku—"

Kumobei dropped his voice as though it were a weighty matter:

"It burned, they say."

"Mm."

"The talk all over town is that it was the secret work of some daimyō with a grievance against the Kampaku."

"What daimyō? Who?"

"Well, now — some say Mōri, some Chōsokabe, or Tokugawa, or Hosokawa; there's even talk it was the Kampaku's own adopted son, Lord Hidetsugu. There's no pinning it down. But there is one rumor that's rather interesting."

"Out with it, then; don't make a show of it."

"You'll know of Yakurō, my lord?"

Kumobei was a Sakai man.

Yakurō too was the son of a Sakai apothecary-merchant. He and his father Ryūsa alike had won Hideyoshi's extraordinary favor, and Yakurō had risen to be Konishi Yukinaga, Settsu-no-kami, lord of Uto at two hundred and forty thousand koku; yet the local merchants, in a mingling of envy and scorn, still called him nothing but Yakurō.

"If the Kampaku goes into Korea, they say, the father, Ryūsa, stands to lose his profit. After all, he's a merchant who built that whole fortune peddling face-powder to the Koreans."

"Willing or not, the son — Yukinaga, Settsu-no-kami — is prized for knowing the ground, and the rumor runs that of all the daimyō he's the one chosen to lead the van into Korea, is it not?"

"Ah, there's the sore spot — a thing to make you wince either way. Leave aside the generals bred of proper samurai; no one knows the folly of the Kampaku's Korean venture better than that man, jumped up from a tradesman. Yet with the Kampaku's favor upon him, to remonstrate is out of the question. So — the story goes — he had no choice but to fire the Kampaku's palace while its lord was away, to let it be known, sidelong, that the country has not yet given the Kampaku its whole heart. That's the scheme they lay to him."

"I see. There's a thread of sense running through it."

"And more than that — it seems Yakurō has secretly joined hands with a woman named Hei, the mother of the Korean king Sŏnjo."

"Oh?"

Jūzō was taken aback.

It was, of course, utterly without foundation. He had supposed the sowing of these rumors to be the work of Kuroami's band; but even so, Kuroami's men could hardly know the name of the Korean king. One dog barks at a shadow and ten thousand dogs bark it into fact, as the saying goes. What startled him was that the street talk, "barking it true," carried far more of the ring of reality than the falsehood Kuroami had first put out, and had grown fins and a tail of its own.

"I see. However grand a front the Kampaku Hideyoshi may put on, with troubles at his back he cannot send a great army overseas. Yakurō's reckoning — it may even hit the mark."

Even as he made noises of agreement, Jūzō found himself thinking of something else.

It was this: might not the Ōkurakyō Hōin Sōkyū himself be secretly clasping hands with the Korean king? Not Sōkyū alone: several of Sakai's great merchants, it seemed to him, while outwardly bending to the Kampaku, were keeping their secret channels to Korea open and trying to head off the expedition. Rumor, even when it is false report, will sometimes hold within it an insight one would never have looked for.

(A strange business, this. If my work goes off well, it will save not only the merchants of Sakai and Ieyasu, but even the lives of Korea's weak-kneed soldiers.)

Jūzō found it funny. He did not, of course, lay his plans thinking so far ahead as that. For a rappa to weigh the results or the reach of his work was, in a manner of speaking, an impurity.

"Kumobei."

As he set down the tea-money, Jūzō quietly pressed a grain of silver into Kumobei's palm. Kumobei bobbed his head in a flurry of thanks, but watched uneasily as Jūzō rose from his seat:

"M-may I not come with you, my lord?"

The man seemed to have begun to feel a strong pull toward Jūzō himself. There was a pleading look on his face; but—

"We'll meet again."

Jūzō shook the plea off, and added:

"You'd best lodge at the Iseya. Only, those clothes won't do. Have Kahei fit you out like a proper tradesman's man."

He rose from the camp-stool and turned his steps east, unhurried. He crossed the bridge. His aim was to see Kohagi. He had already had Kuroami shadow her and make sure of her villa in Komatsudani.

What Jūzō felt in going to see Kohagi was not clear even to himself.

Was it that he wanted the woman in Kohagi? Or did he go, as his employer's adopted daughter, to learn how the work stood? Or was it, quite apart from either, a curiosity to peel back even a little of Kohagi's baffling true self? Each time he turned it over, Jūzō felt a faint disgust at the opaque thing within himself. It was the sense that it might be luring him to his own ruin. The disgust came coupled with a foreboding.

Climbing the slope toward Amidagamine, he turned north into the pine wood.

Among the trees the light roof of the villa gate showed. The path bent toward the gate; and just about there, no sooner had he caught the sound of something brushing the grass than a samurai popped abruptly into view.

A brown robe, hakama tucked up, a heavy copper-red war-sword thrust in his sash with its fittings unaltered: at a glance one could take him for a swordsmanship trainee from somewhere in the Bandō. The path was narrow.

One of them would have to give way; but the samurai, a few ken off, halted.

It was the shrine-man of Kashima, Watanabe Satoru.

At a single look Jūzō knew him for the attacker of the other night; but the other did not remember Jūzō, who on that dark night had worn ninja garb.

He had halted, it seemed, out of caution; and Jūzō, reading the other's mind, stepped carelessly into the pine wood and yielded the path.

The man went by with his hips held coiled and wary. Jūzō gave a wry smile. The plainness of a swordsman lost in nothing but the craft of cutting men down stood out in him, pitiably clear.

The gate stood open.

When he asked at the entrance to be announced, an old woman came out; but at the sight of Jūzō she drew a doubtful frown between her brows.

"And who might you be?"

"Well, now…"

As he spoke, Jūzō looked about at the garden and the rest with an air of curiosity.

"Who is it, if you please?"

"Mm. Tell her, if you would, that it is Tsuzura Jūzō."

"Master Tsuzura. Of what house?"

"Of none. A vagrant."

The old woman withdrew, put out; but when she came again it was with a wholly altered courtesy:

"This way, if you please."

Slipping on straw sandals, she led the way through the garden, and he was shown into an elegant tea-house. The building was made up of two-odd detached wings, as though in imitation of the layout of the Awata academy.

The old woman vanished. When he sat down, a cool wind blew through the openwork above the paper screens on three sides. The place must be used only for summer living.

Kohagi's footsteps sounded under the eaves. The way she slipped in between the screens showed a liveliness rare in this woman.

"Why — what wind is it that has blown you here? How good of you to come."

"That too I owe to my still being alive."

Jūzō chuckled.

"Your life?"

"Near the gate I passed a man who looked like one of these swordsmanship trainees so much in fashion of late. I had marked him once before, the other night. That evening I came within a hair of losing my life."

"Oh—"

"No cause for you to be startled. The one who got the fright was me."

Jūzō looked at the Hōin's adopted daughter.

As ever, a smoky smile hung about her eyes.

"It was you, wasn't it."

"Now, who can say."

The smile did not fade.

"Don't toy with me."

"I am not toying."

"I came to get to the bottom of it. I want to hear why you set a blade to me — to Jūzō. Speak."

"Then I will tell you. I wished to see the color of your blood, Lord Jūzō."

"Hmm."

"Do not be angry. Kohagi is a woman."

"That I have long known."

"You do not know it. The strangeness of a woman's heart — even Kohagi cannot fathom it. There is no way Lord Jūzō could."

Kohagi gave her strange reason for the killing in a tone that seemed to bear down on him, and Jūzō listened with a cold face. It could be taken for a confession of love. But no rappa is fool enough to believe another rappa's words.

(And it is not Kohagi alone. Woman is a born rappa. That glib tongue of hers works its guile not on the man before her only, but on her own heart as well.)

"Kohagi."

He said the name flatly, without a title.

"So you'd reckoned in advance that I would be cut down."

"Yes. 'Music comes up from the capital, swordsmanship down from the east,' as they say. That man is an eastern swordsman. However much the Iga of the Kamigata is called the land of the shinobi—"

"You thought I'd be no match for him? The rappa's art is not, like swordsmanship, for contesting who is the stronger fighter. It is, you might say, an art of getting away. As it fell out I managed to slip that trap; but had I not, I'd have been cut clean in two."

"No. The one who all but came to be cut in two was that man."

"You were watching?"

Despite himself, Jūzō's voice roughened.

"Yes. From the shadows."

"Kohagi."

"Yes."

"I'd thought it would come out in time, without my making a rough business of the asking; but now is the very moment — answer me: which side are you on? At bottom you are no ally of Sōkyū's. You've gulled him into taking you even as his adopted daughter, but your true self, I judge, is a rappa out of Kōga. What are you scheming, unwomanly as it is? Who is it that engaged you? Say it — or else."

"Will you cut me down?"

"Fool. What are you?"

Coloring with anger, Jūzō seized the sword at his waist.

Kohagi pressed a hand to her breast. Doubling forward at the waist, she laughed softly aloud, a rippling laugh. Her voice had a moist, throaty quality that was hers alone.

"Ho-ho-ho-ho. Have you forgotten your own words, Lord Jūzō? — Kohagi is a courtesan."

"You talk nonsense."

With his left hand Jūzō swept the scabbard away. The bared blade left in his right cut the air and stopped at the base of Kohagi's throat.

"It grieves me, but I'll cut you down."

Once he had said it, Jūzō thought, even to himself, what a clumsy way of pressing her it was. She was not, after all, a woman to be handled by any ordinary means. And in truth he had found the will to cleave the white flesh before him clean in two.

That killing intent of Jūzō's ran down the blade's point and reached Kohagi.

At that, even Kohagi's color changed.

But the smile alone did not freeze; narrowing her eyes above the blade and looking straight at Jūzō, she said:

"Since I am a courtesan, if it is your wish, Lord Jūzō, I will serve you at your pillow even now."

There was a faint tremor in her voice. Whether she feared Jūzō's killing intent, or was drunk on her own strange desire — a desire, even beneath that killing intent, to draw Jūzō into her bed — he could not grasp the depths behind this shifting woman.

Of itself, the sword's point sank.

(I've lost.)

At that, if anything, Jūzō's heart grew lighter. A woman on whom threats got no purchase — a woman who, even threatened, pressed close to the other's heart and felt desire — was more than his slender experience of women could master.

At that moment Kohagi, swallowing a cry, fell hard.

Jūzō had kicked her.

Even as she took the kick on her shoulder, the way Kohagi fell had, too, the air of one taking pleasure in the blow.

Neither of them spoke.

Fallen, Kohagi's narrow upturned eyes held Jūzō steadily. Propped on both elbows, her body twisted at the waist, her legs showing white where they slipped from the hem.

The smile had gone from her cheeks, and the eyes that watched Jūzō burned hard and glittering, strangely bright. They were quiet eyes, in which there was no telling what she thought or might do — quiet, and yet mad.

A few shaku off, Jūzō stood.

The already dark-flushed face of this man had begun to redden as if spouting blood — and it was not, in truth, from anger. He had let the strength go out of him and given her a kick, yet Jūzō's will could command nothing of his own hands and feet.

For the first time Jūzō felt for this woman a longing that all but knocked him flat. He had loved this woman's body before. But now a feeling that was more than that alone filled his heart. It was as though this man, who had lived only in the world of the rappa, had found for the first time a peerless equal who could play him like a ball in her palm. Had the other been a man, the astonishment would have turned at once to hostility or to awe; but toward the woman Kohagi it turned, all at once, into a fierce rush of love. And because he already knew and remembered the fallen woman's body, that feeling came coupled with a lust that smelled of blood.

Looking at the woman on the tatami, Jūzō weighed whether to take his fleeting pleasure of her here and now, or to cut her down.

He had come to feel love for her, true; but there was no knowing how utterly the feeling of this moment might be undone by tomorrow. Well and good if it changed; but if he went on cherishing this love, then Jūzō the rappa would fall into a place of death. To be toyed with by the woman to the end, and lose his life for it — that would be the likely finish. There was no two ways about it: the right thing was to kill her while he had the chance. A rappa hopes for nothing eternal; and yet, if he killed her now, just as she was, his own fleeting love would live on in his breast as something that would last forever.

Jūzō put strength into the hand that gripped the hilt. The blade shifted a little and flashed in the sun that struck through the gap in the screen. Sensing it, perhaps, the big toe of the woman's foot, in its summer tabi, curled faintly.

But the point of Jūzō's sword groped at the woman's hem and of a sudden flicked it up. The dark map of her came into view. The woman lowered her lashes. By now she must have believed in her own victory. Over the swell of her breast a soft wave passed.

Seeing it, Jūzō felt his own resolve give way. And in the same instant, anger at his own lapse made his right hand catch the pommel and reverse its grip.

The sword came down, top to bottom, straight as a white waterfall, and soundlessly drove through the fat at the root of the woman's right thigh. Its point stitched into the tatami.

The woman made no sound.

Jūzō threw down the sword and was gone from the villa.
