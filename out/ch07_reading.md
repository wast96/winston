## Chapter 7. Rooting Out Traitors: The Deep and Intricate War of Counter-espionage

China's War of Resistance was a war against invasion, a resistance mounted only after the blows had already fallen; from the start it was fought on the back foot. The secret front was no different. There too the overall posture was defense—counter-espionage.

### 1. The "Double Agent" Who Tried to Kill the Commander-in-Chief

During the war the Eighth Route Army's security department took a new name: the Anti-Traitor Department. Its work was to root out spies, and by spies it meant one thing in particular—Chinese who served the enemy, the men commonly called *hanjian*, traitors to the nation.

So the security department's main enemy was the traitor.

A work report of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region government summed up the enemy this way: "In the great war of national liberation we must fight the open fight, and we must also fight the hidden fight. The hidden fight, because it is hidden, most people scarcely notice; and precisely because it is hidden, its wins and losses often count for more than the wins and losses of whole divisions and armies, and the strategy and tactics of it are often more intricate and profound than those of the open fight. This—the struggle against enemy scouts, against traitors, against anti-Communist agents—is what we call anti-traitor security work."

In the Communist Party's lexicon the "open fight" was the "military struggle" and the "hidden fight" the "covert struggle." The enemy in that hidden war came in three kinds: the "enemy scout" meant the Japanese agent; the "spy" meant the traitor; and the "anti-Communist agent" was in plain words the "Nationalist agent," *guote* for short. To have called the hidden fight "intricate and profound" was already to have seen into its secret.

For it turned out that this "rooting out of traitors" was not only a matter of clearing away collaborators. It also meant hunting agents: behind the collaborators stood craftier men still, the special agents who pulled the strings.

In August 1937 the commander-in-chief, Zhu De, was about to cross the Yellow River eastward from Hancheng in Shaanxi to take command of the Eighth Route Army at the Shanxi front. The Japanese intelligence apparatus ordered a Chinese killer, code-named "Skull," to assassinate Zhu De at Zhichuan, the river-crossing town.

Skull sat in the teahouse at the mouth of the town, looking down on the welcoming crowd below, meaning to open fire the moment Zhu De arrived. He was confident he could bring off so perilous a job. For Skull was at the same time a Juntong man, and Juntong had agreed to back the operation: an action team was to loiter in the street, and once Skull had struck, these men would cover his getaway.

Only Skull did not know that the street team's orders were something else again. After he had hit Zhu De, they were to turn their guns on Skull. Dai Li wanted the killer silenced, so that the whole blame for wrecking the Nationalist-Communist alliance could be shifted onto the Japanese.

Skull and the Juntong action team each had their own calculations, and neither reckoned right. The man who rode into Zhichuan was not Zhu De but Yang Qiqing, chief of the Anti-Traitor Department at the Eighth Route Army's forward headquarters; Zhu De had long since gone straight to the ford and crossed. Yang Qiqing had known of it in advance, and he not only kept Zhu De safe, he took Skull alive.

The notice announcing Skull's execution called him "a double agent of both Japan and the Nationalists."

A "double agent"? A foreign-and-Chinese double spy, an enemy of a kind the Communist security men had never faced, intricate and profound beyond anything before.

With a stroke of his pen, Yang Qiqing struck out a few words and added two. And so Skull's title became "Japanese collaborator" instead. This was a decision of deep policy: when Yang Qiqing moved to seize Skull, he had let the Nationalist agents in the street go. During the united front, one kept the larger picture in view.

And so the security department, though its main work was now against Japanese and Nationalist agents alike, was still called the Anti-Traitor Department.

Whether "Japanese scout" or "Nationalist agent," all of it was folded under "rooting out traitors." Any agent action aimed at the Communist Party or at the Border Region was aid to Japan's invasion, was a traitor's work, was illegal, and was to be hoed out. "Counter-agent" and "counter-espionage" work might still carry a whiff of party rivalry; "rooting out traitors" was the plain cause of the nation, righteous in name. That the Communists used "rooting out traitors" as the name for their wartime counter-espionage work shows how shrewd their counter-espionage strategy already was.

Yan'an too was holding public trials of traitors.

On the evening of March 27, 1938, a public trial was convened on the parade ground of North Shaanxi College in Yan'an. Along the front of the ground a row of long tables stood in a half-moon. At the center sat the presiding judge, Lei Jingtian, president of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region High Court, with Cheng Fangwu and Mo Wenhua beside him; alongside were people's assessors from the Anti-Japanese Military and Political University and North Shaanxi College. At the edge of the ground three anti-aircraft machine guns pointed at the blue sky. Within it, nearly ten thousand cadres and civilians listened in rapt silence.

The case was a devious story. A man who called himself head of the Eighth Route Army office's political-training section had been recruiting young women students to come to Yan'an, mapping the country as he went and making the women share his bed along the road. When the party lodged at Yan'an's reception house, he was seen through by Cheng Yonghe of the Border Security.

The man's real name, it emerged, was Ji Sigong. He had joined the Japanese intelligence apparatus in 1936, using work at a "geographical study society" as cover for his intelligence activity. This time he had used the false title of political-training chief to lure his victims: partly to have others screen his mapping of Yan'an's military terrain, partly to prey on women along the way.

Before the eyes of the multitude, the traitor Ji Sigong was shot.

Traitors were loathsome. From Kawashima Yoshiko to Ji Sigong, Japan's invasion always sent collaborators ahead to clear the road, and China never seemed to run short of them.

In the Northeast there was Puyi, the former Qing emperor; in Nanjing, Wang Jingwei, the former Nationalist vice-president; in North China, Wang Kemin, chairman of the Provisional Government council—prominent men of China, openly setting up puppet regimes for the Japanese. Lesser collaborators were legion too: across the occupied countryside "maintenance associations" sprang up everywhere, local gentry stepping forward to keep order for the Japanese. A dozen Japanese soldiers could hold a whole county, for they had puppet troops and collaborators at their service.

The Japanese intelligence apparatus also used collaborators to carry on its work, worming its way by every means into the Nationalist-controlled areas and the Communist base areas.

The headquarters of the Japanese North China Area Army's group army in Shanxi kept intelligence organs at Taiyuan, Fenyang, Lishi, Lanxian, and Linfen, and intelligence posts at Linxian, Xingxian, Qikou, Jundu, Liulin, and Sanjiao. The Japanese apparatus worked chiefly by buying Chinese and sending them into the Border Region as spies, some high-level agents, some low-grade scouts. From 1939 to 1941 Yan'an's security organs cracked 73 cases of collaborators dispatched by Japan. The Taiyuan Japanese apparatus trained one Gao Ziwen and, with a special fund of 3,000 yuan, sent him expressly to Yan'an to assassinate Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai. The Shanxi apparatus trained an orderly named Li Yongmao and sent him into the Border Region to steal documents, steal ciphers, and poison. Among the dispatched agents caught by Yan'an over time were Bai Mingyao, Song Changling, Luo Honggou, Cai Changgeng, Han Yushu, Li Tianxin, Li Juchuan, Gao Ziwen, and Wang Zhisheng, and besides them the couples Lin Suguo and Wang Zhe, sent to Yan'an by the headquarters of the Japanese Kwantung Army.

There was another kind again: the "indirect organizations of the Japanese bandits," in which collaborators joined hands with the secret societies. The Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region had its Black Army and its Star of Heaven Party; the Huaibei region had its Flower-Basket Society, its Way of Former Heaven, its Black-Robe Society. From 1937 to 1938 there were more than a hundred bands of brigands inside and around the Border Region: Li Qingwu's band ambushed Zhou Enlai at Laoshan, Chen Houzi's band welcomed the Japanese at the Yellow River's edge, and around Suide and Guanzhong there was an Elder Brothers' Society outfit calling itself the "Black Military Government." From 1938 to 1939 the security men cracked such Elder Brothers' Society bodies, bought by the Japanese, as the "Inquiry Committee," the "Local Inquiry Squad," the "Anti-Communist Committee," the "Special Agent Committee," and the "Broadsword Society of Righteousness."

Collaborators of every stripe became a "fifth column" for Japan's invasion. The Nationalist government's Military Affairs Commission and the Border Region government both issued Regulations for the Punishment of Traitors; the Communist Party's Central Social Affairs Department issued one "anti-traitor directive" after another. In the army the security departments were called Anti-Traitor Departments; local governments set up Anti-Traitor Committees; even the children's corps, shouldering red-tasseled spears and checking road passes at the village mouth, were doing anti-traitor work. The war against Japan was a national war, and punishing traitors became the common task of the whole nation. Rooting out traitors, in short, became the main task of the Communist Party's wartime intelligence and security organs.

"Special agent," "scout," "spy," "secret agent." The layman finds it hard to sort out the differences among these terms of art. By convention, a "special agent" covers a wide range of work; a scout's work is chiefly to spy out military conditions; a "spy" in the narrow sense means a man who betrays the camp he belongs to and gives intelligence service to the enemy; and "secret agent" is strictest of all, meaning one planted inside the enemy's camp to steal intelligence.

In the Communist security system, this work was called "counter-scouting" in the years of war generally, "rooting out traitors" during the war against Japan, "counter-agent work" in the early years after the founding of the state, and, in today's standard usage, "counter-espionage."

Security work had many tasks. Counter-espionage was, from first to last, the most important core of it.

### 2. A "Good Devil" Comes to Da'anzhuang

Japan had a rich experience in the use of secret operatives. Its army had a special-service department, its police a Tokkō section, and on the battlefield it had "special assault killing squads" besides.

In May 1942 the Japanese North China Expeditionary Army assembled three divisions and a brigade, more than thirty thousand men, for a surprise assault on the Eighth Route Army's headquarters at Matian. In the vanguard of the assault were the Japanese agents of the "special assault force," and it was local collaborators who guided them to the Eighth Route Army. On the 25th the headquarters was encircled at Shiziling, the escape paths all blocked—the collaborators, of course, knew the ground.

The Eighth Route Army's chief of staff, Zuo Quan, was killed in action, the highest-ranking commander the Communists lost in the whole war. Zuo Quan had studied in the Soviet Union and ran the operations department at headquarters; he was an outstanding commander, veteran of a hundred battles. And a famous general died at the hands of collaborators and Japanese agents.

At Yan'an's Japanese Workers' and Peasants' School there was a Japanese soldier-student who was strikingly active. He was seconded to help at the Border Region High Court, sat in on classes at the Anti-Japanese University, went to watch the troops train, and would even step up to speak of his own accord at Yan'an's mass meetings. Later he fell under suspicion and was arrested. Two soldiers were escorting him when he snatched a gun, shot both of them dead, and fled toward the Yellow River. On the road he forced peasants to cook for him—and was quietly reported to the anti-traitor authorities.

Just as this Japanese agent was hurrying along with a pair of trousers stuffed full of steamed buns slung over his neck, a squad sent out by the Yan'an municipal public-security bureau arrived in time and shot him.

The truth was that the Japanese army feared this school above all for the way it shook the morale of its own troops, and had made a point of sending agents to wreck it.

The Eighth Route Army's Shandong Column, fighting on amid heavy Japanese encirclement, laid particular stress on subverting the enemy's forces.

A young Guangdong man named He Qingyu, having trained at Yan'an in the General Political Department's enemy-work course, was sent to work in Shandong. The Shandong Column set up an enemy-work section with an armed work team under it, threw itself into work among the enemy and puppet troops, and ran nine rounds of a residential training class to school agents inside the enemy camp. The work among the puppet troops bore great fruit: it won over Han Shouchen's unit of eight thousand men, who came over in revolt, and took the walled town of Ju County by a coordinated stroke from within and without.

Work directed at the Japanese themselves was harder: on this side of Shandong only one Korean cadre, Li Ming, knew Japanese. Fortunately there was a "Japanese Anti-War League." More than ten Japanese prisoners of war—Kojima Kinnosuke, Kobayashi Kanchō, Sakatani Masazō, Kaneno Hiroshi, and others—had resolved, after the Eighth Route Army's schooling, to turn against the war. In Shandong the "Japanese Anti-War League" threw itself into anti-war propaganda, slogans and leaflets, singing and shouted appeals across the lines, and left the Japanese units badly unsettled.

One day the enemy-work chief, He Qingyu, received a report: "A good devil has come to Da'anzhuang!"

The people had lately been saying that a good-natured devil had come to Da'anzhuang—smiled at everyone, treated the sick, even cut the grain levies the puppet troops imposed. When the Japanese carried off two village heads, it was that same Mizuhara Kiyoshi who had them brought back.

A devil who was a good man? Such an odd figure drew the notice of the Column's political department: could the man have some other, political background? The Shandong Column's base lay in the Yimeng mountains; along the base's edge ran the Yi River, and on the far bank stood Da'anzhuang, garrisoned by the Japanese—a place that had always been a focus of the Column's enemy-work section.

He Qingyu went to the front himself to investigate. Around Da'anzhuang there had been a string of killings of anti-Japanese elements, and that same rescued village head had now been assassinated too. The situation was murky, the loyalties confused; it had to be probed to the bottom.

He Qingyu's Yidong armed work team were all crack shots, every man a two-gun hand. They moved like lightning, secretly seizing Mizuhara Kiyoshi's courier and taking a coded letter Mizuhara had written to the Yishui office. So this Mizuhara Kiyoshi, it turned out, was the chief agent posted by the Jinan apparatus to the Yishui office. At Da'anzhuang he had set up an "experimental zone," meaning to win the hearts of the Chinese by soft methods and so realize a strategy of "intensified pacification."

A double agent of a kind he had never met stood before him, and He Qingyu racked his brains: how to expose the fellow?

Just then Mizuhara Kiyoshi, still unaware that He Qingyu had the measure of him, put out word that he wished to meet the Eighth Route Army commander.

A lone sword to the parley. He Qingyu, taking the Japanese-speaking Li Ming with him, slipped into Da'anzhuang under cover of night and met Mizuhara Kiyoshi face to face.

Mizuhara Kiyoshi respectfully professed sympathy for the Communists and a willingness to cooperate with Eighth Route Army intelligence. He Qingyu played along with the ruse, asking Mizuhara to supply intelligence on the Japanese mopping-up operations, and asking too for travel passes.

The counter-mopping-up battle opened. The travel passes were genuine and usable; the intelligence was false, and to act on it was certain death.

That proved it: this double agent had surrendered in name only. The Shandong Column resolved to arrest Mizuhara Kiyoshi, this Japanese agent, and try him in open court.

The latest case showed that the Japanese army not only struck and killed ferociously, it could also work by deception. It was not only we who used two-faced tactics to plant men on the enemy; the Japanese agents were using two-faced tactics to deceive us as well.

Struggle against foreign agents was still a new subject for the young Communist intelligence and security organs. Did they know the inner workings of a foreign intelligence service, or a foreign language? Could they get inside a foreign agent's mind, and be sure of not being taken in?

### 3. A "Party Branch" Inside Juntong's Main Station

If the Communists knew Japan's intelligence machinery imperfectly, the Nationalist apparatus was an old adversary.

The Communists entered Yan'an at the end of 1936. In May of the following year Chiang Kai-shek sent a senior staff officer, Lieutenant General Tu Sizong, to lead a delegation to Yan'an, and the army system's operative-work chief, Yang Wei, mixed in with them. That winter Xu Enzeng again sent men to Yan'an to make "an exploratory expedition," coming away with a book, *The Party's Line of Strategy*, said to be teaching material Zhang Hao had used in his lectures at the "Anti-Japanese University." There was also a Juntong agent, Shen Zhiyue, who claimed to have slipped into Yan'an and served as Mao Zedong's secretary, and who went round the various Nationalist agencies telling of his espionage exploits, cutting quite a figure for a time.

A Nationalist agent had been Mao Zedong's secretary? That would be a great spy indeed, of the first importance. In fact Shen Zhiyue had genuinely entered Yan'an, studying in the second class of the Anti-Japanese University in August 1937 and worming his way into the Communist organization; but later, seeing how strict Yan'an's political vetting was, the man quietly slipped away. As for serving as Mao Zedong's secretary, that was so much bragging.

Whatever the water in these boasted espionage feats, Zhongtong and Juntong did put real effort into their Yan'an work.

Around the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, Zhongtong had had provincial-level station-rooms in Shanxi, Shaanxi, and Ningxia, and had set up prefecture-level station-rooms at Luochuan, Yulin, Binxian, and Xifeng besides. The post-and-telegraph offices at Yan'an, Ganquan, Fuxian, and other places all held Zhongtong operatives, and one Zhongtong agent, a man named Mei, even served as director of the Yan'an telegraph office.

Juntong's forerunner organization had been active in Shaanxi and Gansu since 1932. When the Red Army reached the Northwest on its Long March, Juntong set up a Northwest District at Xi'an, with jurisdiction over the four provinces (or regions) of Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia, and Qinghai, the largest of all Juntong's districts in the country, under a district chief, Zhang Yanfo. The district's apparatus soon spread across five provinces, with stations at Xi'an, Lanzhou, Yulin, southern Shanxi, and Taiyuan and more than twenty cells; a Xi'an radio sub-station had more than twenty sub-stations under it, whose chief, Wang Keyi, based himself at Yan'an in the guise of director of the Yan'an telecommunications office. When the political department of the Tianshui Field Headquarters sent an inspection team to Yan'an, Zhang Yanfo planted a Communist renegade, Guo Ziming, among them; Guo Ziming came back and wrote a report on conditions in Yan'an running to more than twenty thousand characters, and was at once promoted to chief of the Communist section in the Second Bureau of the Juntong headquarters.

When the Xi'an Incident broke out, Juntong's Northwest District collapsed for a time, then quickly revived. In 1941 Juntong carried out a great reorganization aimed at Yan'an. The old Northwest District was made over into a Shanxi-Shaanxi District covering the two provinces of Shanxi and Shaanxi; a new Northwest District was created covering the five provinces and regions of Gansu, Ningxia, Qinghai, Xinjiang, and Tibet. The two districts formed a pincer from east and west to squeeze the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region. The Shanxi-Shaanxi District had cells at Yulin, Luochuan, Yan'an, Malan, Sanbian, and elsewhere, and it raised the Yulin cell at Suide to a full station to seal off the Yellow River fords. The Northwest District was headed by a former Communist, Cheng Yiming, with stations under it at Lanzhou, Pingliang, Wuwei, and Ningxia, and cells at Xifeng, Dihua, Qinghai, Lhasa, and Jiuquan besides, penetrating the Border Region from the west.

In the war of resistance the two parties had become openly "friendly parties," and the agent activity between them ought by rights to have died a natural death. But open was open; underneath lay another scene entirely.

Though the Nationalists had had all their organs of power driven out of the Border Region, they were not without a foothold there: the Communists had left the postal office and the telecommunications office in place. Knowing full well that the postal office held Zhongtong agents and the telecommunications office Juntong agents, the Communists kept the two offices open all the same—the people's life needed them. And with the highway traffic unbroken, between Yan'an and Xi'an there were always the "three links": mail, transport, and trade.

The "three links" were good for travel between the people and good for commerce. The only ones who worried about them were the security organs. The letters reaching Yan'an every day came by the sackful—ordinary family letters, government dispatches, and documents written in secret ink alike. The telegrams sent to Yan'an every day came in plain code and in cipher both. And among all this mail and wire traffic there were, of course, the secret communications of Japanese and Nationalist agents.

To watch and control the agents' communications, the Security Office set up a mail-and-telegraph inspection station. Its head was Chen Shiqi; its members Gao Jiquan, Zhu Guifang, Yong Jinbing, Lin Song, Li Shisheng, and Qin Ping, with an orderly, Bai Xuesheng—nine in all. Breaking telegraph ciphers was highly specialized work, and the inspection station only made copies of the wire traffic and sent them to the Military Commission's Second Bureau to handle. The Second Bureau also kept a set near the telecommunications office to monitor the wireless traffic.

The volume of mail inspection was very heavy. Letters to Yan'an came from all over the country, from the Nationalist areas and the occupied areas, and even from overseas, from the South Seas and elsewhere. Most were ordinary family letters, but some parcels carried opium, and letters sent out by Nationalist organs stationed in Yan'an held a great deal of intelligence besides. The station inspected every letter from the occupied areas and from overseas, and made spot checks of the mail from Nationalist areas. Apart from the head, Chen Shiqi, who had a good deal of worldly experience, and Zhu Guifang, a student of Beijing Normal University, the staff were all very young and short on such experience. The technical means were simple too: only two chemical reagents, iodine tincture and soda water. Yet even under such crude conditions the station made its discoveries.

The mail carrying opium was mostly sent by opium dealers, with the secret mark "wu-ji" on the outside of the parcel. Spotting that mark and inspecting it greatly speeded the work.

The most alarming discovery was a letter to Mao Zedong's secretary that carried secret ink. A brother and sister named Hua, from a Wuxi capitalist family, had come to Yan'an, and after graduating from the Anti-Japanese University, Hua Ming had been chosen as one of Mao Zedong's secretaries. Hua Ming's father doted on his children and wrote often to Yan'an; the letters read in a merchant's idiom, yet in the blank spaces there was secret ink. The station analyzed the secret writing in the Wuxi letters and found no sign of agent activity. All the same, Hua Ming was moved away from Mao Zedong's side to work in the Border Region's highway department. After the founding of the state Hua Ming served as director of the Anshan Iron and Steel Works.

Besides the mail-and-telegraph inspection, the Security Office ran inside informers against the Nationalist-controlled postal and telecommunications offices. Liang Ji's father had a schoolmate who was the postal office's oldest letter-carrier, and through this tie the postmaster recommended Liang Ji to the head of the telecommunications office. From inside the office Liang Ji established that the director of the Yan'an postal office was a Zhongtong agent, and that Juntong, after the war broke out, had used a "reorganization of the military post" as a pretext to replace the key telecommunications staff with Juntong agents.

Even the Nationalist-controlled departments were no monolith. A young trainee in the postal office resigned of his own accord to study at the Anti-Japanese University, and the telecommunications office too had underground Communists in it. The Nationalists had an international radio station in Shanghai that had long since been penetrated by the Communist underground. Yang Lianzong, working at that international station, was secretly brought into the Party by Fu Yinghao; transferred to the Yan'an telecommunications office as duty officer, he made contact with the Security Office. Once it had the true picture, the Security Office secretly arrested the agent director, Li Jun, put him through a sudden interrogation, and won him over to supply intelligence to the Border Security. It went on to win over the clerk Wang Le, the postmaster named Wang, and the postal assistant named Xu as inside informers. The station's Qin Ping met the informers in secret and drew, at regular intervals, copies of the telecommunications office's entire outgoing traffic, then passed them to the cipher-breaking department.

And so the Nationalists' postal and telecommunications offices in Yan'an, far from serving the Nationalists as intelligence outposts, became instead a channel by which the Communists gathered intelligence. Internal documents and operative papers that the Nationalist Shaanxi provincial party headquarters and the Shaanxi investigation-and-statistics room mailed into the Border Region fell, one after another, into the Border Security's hands.

The "three links," everyone agreed, were a good thing that eased exchange among the people. But the security organs also feared they were a bad thing, giving the adversary his chance to gather intelligence. Still, the channel was there for anyone; either side could work it, and a bad thing could be turned into a good one. There was no reason to oppose the three links.

The facts proved that the Nationalist agents were always at work in secret to undermine the Communists. Yet now, after all, it was a Nationalist-Communist alliance against Japan, and the term "enemy scout" belonged to the Japanese agents alone.

The Nationalists' agents could not well be called enemies. So let this adversary be called simply "the Nationalist agents," *guote*.

The Nationalist agents carried on targeted work around the fringes of the Border Region. "First," ran one report, "in the counties surrounding the Border Region, from party affairs, administration, military affairs, communications, and cultural organs at the top down to the village *baojia* at the bottom, all are so-called 'loyal-cadre talents' who have been trained, whose whole business is to wreck the Border Region—organs of this kind numbering more than three hundred in all. Second, they organize special-agent bodies among the masses. Third, they secretly set up so-called anti-traitor nets, relay nets, intelligence nets, and the like. Fourth, they manufacture brigands—men such as Zhao Laowu, Zhang Tingzhi, Xia Laoyao, and Chen Laoda, who constantly harass the Border Region and are all supported by them; and beyond that they openly loose their own troops to turn brigand, as the Xifeng and Qingjian security corps once robbed and killed travelers, and as He Shaonan's former security corps has lately been preparing to invade the Border Region."

The Nationalist agents also "laid out open and hidden points and lines inside the Border Region." "First is the open, legal organs and bodies used as the open points and lines that disguise their direction of the agent apparatus; next is the effort to plant men inside us, whether by sending men in directly or by buying so-called hidden points and lines (inside informers) with money and beautiful women, and by setting up secret *baojia*—this is the anti-Communist elements' chief strategy for wrecking us, and in three years we have uncovered more than a hundred of them."

As the Border Region's counter-espionage organ, the Border Security had a full and sober estimate of the Nationalist agents, and issued a steady stream of documents to guide anti-traitor work. Facing an ever-heavier counter-espionage task, the Border Security stressed strengthening its investigation-and-research work, set up its own monitoring station, took on technical and cipher-breaking staff, and even founded an intelligence bulletin, *The Books-and-Press Brief*, to report the enemy-agent situation to the central organs and other departments at regular intervals.

Against the Nationalists' intelligence posts around the Border Region, the Border Security set up posts of its own. Facing Nationalist intelligence forces on all four sides, south, west, north, and east, the Border Security in 1941 added four sub-offices, at Guanzhong, Longdong, Sanbian, and Suide.

The Border Region's anti-traitor work also took care to join professional work with mass work. The old government-run "Anti-Traitor Committee" was turned into a mass body, its seven members chosen from among the citizens by the township assembly, with the township head, the anti-traitor chief, and the self-defense-force company commander as ex officio members. The Communist organization was strong: party, government, army, and people all mobilized; checkpoints on the borders searching passersby, the organization departments vetting within, the masses watching day to day—an all-covering net, open and hidden. Whether Japanese agent or Nationalist agent, any who were sent into the Border Region reckoned it a road to dread.

As for how to slip operatives into the Border Region, Zhongtong and Juntong tried no end of tricks. They drew people in along the border zones to set up secret organizations, cultivated ties among traveling merchants and peddlers to mix men into Yan'an, and trained intelligence men expressly to burrow into the Communist organization—but none of these methods bore much fruit.

In espionage against the Communists, the Nationalists' past successes had come mostly from Communist renegades. Zhongtong therefore set special store by its "shock" policy. By "shock" was meant the secret arrest of Communist members, forcible persuasion, their recruitment as inside informers, and then their secret release. The shock campaigns ran, in turn, as the "inside-informer shock," the "surrender policy," and the "one-and-two movement." In 1940 Zhongtong sent down word to launch a "high-grade special-intelligence shock competition" across the station-rooms of every province, taking as its targets the senior figures at every level of the Communist branch bureaus, provincial committees, district committees, prefectural committees, and county committees, and of the rear liaison organs; it set ten to twelve months as the shock period, put each province's station-room chief and experimental-zone chief personally in charge, and rated one to two men per unit a pass.

"Special-intelligence" work was hardly a sport, and yet they were running a "competition." It shows how eager and how pressed the Nationalist apparatus was in its espionage against the Communists. The Central Social Affairs Department noted: "The Nationalists have a long history of rule in China, and their vast agent apparatus makes use of a considerable number of politically seasoned renegades from our party, and of other conveniences besides. We must therefore take a firm hold of policy, split their social base, and so achieve the aim of purging inner traitors and smashing the enemy."

There are generally two ways to build an inside secret agent: to plant one in, or to pull one out.

To plant in is to send one's own secret intelligence man into the enemy's agent apparatus as an inside informer. To pull out is to win over a man of the enemy's apparatus to serve on our side as an inside informer. The practice of espionage proves that "planting in" is far harder than "pulling out." A secret agent who has passed the political vetting and joined the organization must still get through many a barrier before he can be moved to a post that commands real intelligence. Planting an agent to build an inside line is therefore long and arduous work, slow to bear fruit.

To pick a man at a key post inside the enemy's apparatus and lead him around to serve us, by contrast, works like a pole casting an instant shadow. The Nationalists had long since tasted the sweetness of using Communist renegades, and so set special store by "pulling out" against the Communists. Early in the war they pulled out Zhang Guotao, a member of the Communist Politburo, and pulled out Xu Mengqiu, a senior Red Army cadre. Holding the resources of state power, the Nationalists found the strategy of "pulling out" comparatively easy to work.

The Border Security's field post at Fuping, at the southern tip of the "pouch" of the Guanzhong sub-district, was the very front line of the struggle facing Xi'an. One day late in 1941 the head of the Guanzhong theatrical troupe, He Zhide, came hurrying to the field post to report: his own clansman, a certain He, had defected and was trying to draw him over to the enemy.

Qin Ping, the Border Security field officer who received He Zhide, was startled: this certain He was a famous progressive figure. Around the time of the Xi'an Incident he had been a leader of the "Northwest Youth National Salvation Association," one of the far-famed "Five Youths of the Northwest." How could such a progressive have defected?

Xi Zhongxun, secretary of the Guanzhong prefectural committee, and Qu Jixian, head of the Fuping field team, weighed it and decided: lure the certain He into a trap.

He Zhide feigned agreement with the certain He's demands and got word to him through a go-between to set a meeting; the moment the certain He came, he was secretly arrested. A sudden interrogation revealed that after his arrest by the Nationalists the certain He had surrendered and defected, joining the Zhongtong agent organization. Given the certain He's revolutionary past, the Border Security field team meant to turn him and send him back into Zhongtong as an inside informer. But the certain He, seizing on a lapse by his guards, suddenly bolted, and was caught by the soldiers sent after him.

Even a famous revolutionary youth could be pulled out by the Nationalists to serve as an agent. The fact drove the lesson home: anti-traitor work had to guard against the enemy's agents "planting in" and against their "pulling out" both—and in a sense, one might say, guarding against the "pulling out" was the more important task.

Everyone knew "pulling" worked better than "planting"; so, if others could pull men out of our ranks, surely we could pull men out of theirs.

It was hard. The hardness lay not in others forbidding it but in our own unwillingness to do it. When Zhou Enlai founded the Special Branch, he took particular care to use the strategy of "pulling out," and it did great good. But the "left"-deviationist leaders who held the center one after another saw nothing but mortal foes: within the party they waged "ruthless struggle," at the middle forces they aimed "merciless blows," and as for the enemy's camp, well, that was "one solid slab of iron." When even one's own people were being pushed out, "pulling out" was out of the question.

During the war against Japan the Communist center corrected the old "left" line in secret work as well. After reaching northern Shaanxi, Mao Zedong had proposed, in "On Tactics Against Japanese Imperialism": "If they can pull the bad elements out of our ranks and make them run off, then of course we can pull the 'bad elements' out of their ranks and make them come over."

The Communists, skilled at active defense in war, took the same active-defense line in the intelligence war, meeting "pulling out" with "pulling out." In December 1941 the Communist center issued a "Notice on Improving Intelligence Work": "If we do not make use of everyone inside the enemy who can be used—progressives, wavering collaborators, opportunists, the disaffected, renegades, and the like—then inside-line work cannot achieve great results." With this imperial sword from the center, the intelligence departments set to pulling in the enemy's camp with a free hand.

The Nationalists had begun as a revolutionary party and had appealed to revolutionary ideals in recruiting operatives too. But the Nationalists who held power were sliding into corruption, and so were losing their power to appeal to the progressive. And so the progressive youths who had already entered the Nationalist agent apparatus came to feel "their bodies in Cao's camp, their hearts with Han." The Communists, though they held no nationwide power, led the whole country's progressive tide—and this was the peculiar advantage of "pulling out."

One night in the autumn of 1939, two uninvited guests burst into the Eighth Route Army office at Zengjiayan in Chongqing—officers of the Nationalist Juntong radio station, Zhang Weilin and Feng Chuanqing, wanting to go to Yan'an.

Zengjiayan stands on a stretch of red rock in the outskirts of Chongqing, and is also called the Red Crag. The organ there was called the "Eighth Route Army office" to the outside world; within, it was still the Communist Southern Bureau, directing the Communist underground of the Southwest and of South China. The Southern Bureau's military group received the two Juntong officers and decided to have them go on working within the Juntong system to gather intelligence. Before long the two were developed as secret Communist Party members.

Juntong's main telecommunications station stood at the Yi'ai Shrine below Futuguan at Lianglukou in Chongqing, a modern telecommunications center built with American aid. The telecommunications sent out from here directed several hundred secret intelligence organizations of Juntong at home and abroad, and several hundred thousand secret operatives. Feng Chuanqing's post at the main station was second only to the station chief's; he had charge of several hundred of Juntong's radio sets at home and abroad and more than a thousand telegraph operators, and could command Juntong's core secrets. Zhang Weilin served in the telecommunications-monitoring section of the Chongqing garrison district, responsible for monitoring the wireless signals of the Chongqing area—which put him in a position to protect the Communists' secret stations there.

These two inside forces pulled out of Juntong made up an intelligence group lying hidden within Juntong, and its role was of the first importance. To keep them safe, the Southern Bureau's military group forbade them to come to Zengjiayan again. Who, then, was to be their contact? The head of the Southern Bureau's military group, Ye Jianying, had at hand one more important woman intelligence worker—Zhang Luping, just sent from Yan'an by the Central Organization Department.

Zhang Luping's brother-in-law was a Sichuan Army division commander, and the Central Organization Department had meant to send her back to Sichuan to do united-front work among the Sichuan Army's upper ranks—just as Ye Jianying here was short of a hand. And so Zhang Luping became Zhang Weilin's "younger sister," and in the course of the work she went on to develop Zhao Ligeng, Yang Guang, Chen Guozhu, Wang Xizhen, and others, building a Communist branch inside Juntong, with Zhang Luping, just twenty, as its branch secretary.

This secret branch soon obtained the call signs and wavelengths of every Juntong station, laying all of Juntong's secret radio traffic open to Communist monitoring. On one occasion, from a coded telegram Dai Li sent Hu Zongnan, they learned that Juntong was preparing to send an intelligence group with a radio set into the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region. This intelligence went ahead to Yan'an, and the Juntong group had barely entered the Border Region when the waiting Border Security men caught it. The Communists made the case public as evidence of the Nationalists' espionage against a "friendly party," and put Dai Li badly on the back foot.

Espionage warfare has something in common with open warfare: attack is the best defense. Practice proved that meeting "pulling out" with "pulling out" was the most efficient way of all.

### 4. A "Double Agent" in Guanzhong

Whether the Nationalists' "pulling out" or the Communists' "pulling out," both sides were in the business of manufacturing "renegades." China's ever-shifting political stage never seemed to run short of them.

What the Communist security organs hated most was the renegade. During the Second Revolutionary Civil War the Nationalists cracked most of the underground Communist organizations in the White areas by relying on a mass of renegades like Gu Shunzhang; the renegade was the greatest security threat, and it was renegades that the Special Branch's "dog-beating squad" was made to strike.

During the war, in the second Nationalist-Communist alliance, Communists would again meet former renegades in the course of their work, and could not suppress their revulsion. Yan Xishan of Shanxi exploited this friction, deliberately packing his Sacrifice League with renegade cadres to hold the Communists down. How to treat the renegade became a test of the Communists' policy sense.

In December 1942 Mao Zedong drafted a party directive for the Communist center, summing up the policy on intelligence work, and stated plainly: "As for renegades, apart from those guilty of monstrous crimes, so long as they do not go on opposing the Communists, they are to be given a road to remake themselves; and if they can turn back to revolution, they may even be received—but are not to be readmitted to the Party." The central policy lifted the misgivings below; the Communists in the Sacrifice League took care to unite with and win over the former renegades, and the two forces joined to deal with the diehards of Shanxi, bringing the Sacrifice League and the Shanxi New Army effectively under control.

If even renegades could be used, what taboos were left in intelligence work? Yan'an's intelligence and security departments were in a ferment of ideas, opening a debate on the counter-espionage line. The Border Security's Bu Lu put forward "turn the enemy into ours, turn ours into the enemy"; Chen Long, Mu Fengyun, and others of the Social Affairs Department disagreed—the relation of use between enemy and self could not always be turned into a complete conversion. Later the line was fixed as "turn the enemy to serve us," and it was decided to make bold use of Nationalist agents who came over and of usable suspects. The Border Security also put forward a line for building the intelligence organization—"weight pulling over planting, weight the inside over the outside, weight the top over the middle and bottom"—stressing the pulling out, stressing the building of inside lines, stressing the upper ranks.

Once thinking was set free, the work came alive. The Central Intelligence Department and the branch-bureau social-affairs departments carried on their work boldly, actively building special-intelligence forces inside the enemy's apparatus.

Wang Chaobei, then setting up a secret radio station in Xi'an, came upon a lead. In 1941 the underground member Wu Shaowen told Wang Chaobei that an old acquaintance, Li Maotang, hoped to work for the Party again. Li Maotang and Wang Chaobei had both been members in the Great Revolution years; later Li Maotang was arrested by the enemy and had even led a party to hunt Wang Chaobei down. In truth Li Maotang's body was in Cao's camp but his heart was with Han: after the war broke out, Li Maotang, then working at Juntong's Lanzhou station, was anxiously searching for the Party, and entrusted his cousin Wu Shaowen to seek out Wang Chaobei. Wang Chaobei referred the matter to the Xi'an Eighth Route Army office for instructions. With Yan'an's agreement, Wang Chaobei made contact with Li Maotang.

To do better work for the Party, Li Maotang got himself transferred from Lanzhou to Xi'an as deputy chief of the Zhongtong Shaanxi provincial station-room. From then on the minutes of the Tianshui Field Headquarters' "party-government-military-special joint intelligence conference," which oversaw agent activity against the Border Region, were sent to Yan'an complete, not one meeting missed—and Yan'an even had Zhongtong's codebook.

Wang Chaobei and Li Maotang conferred with Chen Zijing, who lay hidden in the Nationalist Shaanxi party headquarters, and exploited the feud in the Nationalist upper ranks between the "Zhu faction" and the "CC Clique": leaning on Xu Enzeng of the CC Clique's Central Organization Department, they backed Yang Daqian, Li Youlong, and others of the Shaanxi party headquarters against Wang Jigao, the Shaanxi party secretary-general who was a trusted man of the organization minister Zhu Jiahua. The Shaanxi quarrel was carried all the way to Chiang Kai-shek: Chen Guofu took over Zhu Jiahua's post as organization minister, and Gu Zhengding took over Wang Jigao's as chief of the Shaanxi provincial party headquarters. The CC Clique won a sweeping victory, and in the sharing of the spoils Li Maotang was appointed chief of the Zhongtong Shaanxi station-room and Northwest Bureau commissioner, taking hold of the highest power in Shaanxi Zhongtong.

The Nationalists kept several concentration camps in Xi'an, expressly to hold Communist underground members and the young students hurrying to Yan'an. Gu Zhengding devised a vicious scheme: send in a renegade to win the fellow prisoners' trust by the ruse of self-injury, then incite them all to flee, so that the camp authorities could shoot every prisoner on the pretext of stopping a jailbreak.

Just as this fellow was leading the way up the high wall, the fellow prisoners hauled him down, gave him a savage beating, and killed him on the spot. The inside truth had long since been seen through by Li Maotang, Gu Zhengding's own trusted man.

The commanding general of the espionage war had turned into the other side's man; the script of *The Ruse of Self-Injury* was played out as *Jiang Gan Steals the Letter*, and Zhongtong's espionage war on Yan'an was lost in utter confusion. Xu Enzeng, the Zhongtong director who had sworn lifelong hostility to the Communists, sighed that the war against Japan, in bringing on the Nationalist-Communist alliance, had handed the Communists a chance to come back from the dead.

What he did not see was that the chance had been created. Policy answered to the chance: the "turn the enemy to serve us" line fixed by the Communist center let the Communist intelligence departments build important inside forces within the enemy's apparatus and seize the initiative in the espionage struggle.

The Nationalist agent apparatus employed a mass of Communist renegades. Some had gone over to the enemy in true earnest; most had left the party (lost their contact) but had never sold out the organization, and once the moment came these lapsed members would turn back to serve the Communists again. A few had from the start feigned being "renegades" to burrow into the Nationalist agent organization, and lay hidden deeper still. Of the two provinces with which Zhongtong ringed the Border Region, both provincial station-room chiefs—Li Maotang in Shaanxi and Miao Zhuanglin in Shanxi—secretly returned to the Communist camp.

The espionage war, of spying and counter-spying, is a tangled affair. Whose policy is the more supple, his methods are the more; whose methods are the more, his gains are the greater.

A tangled espionage case appeared.

In the winter of 1941 a married couple came back to Yan'an from the occupied areas—agents trained and dispatched by a Soviet Army intelligence group, who had slipped into Shenyang and returned.

The Social Affairs Department secretary Wang Chu kept receiving rejection slips from the *Liberation Daily*, all for pieces by this couple newly returned to Yan'an; the pieces held nothing of substance, and seemed only signals sent out that the pair had reached Yan'an. The station-room chief Wang Jinxiang and the investigation-section chief Chen Long began looking into it, following the couple's route back to Yan'an and questioning each courier station; the replies all said that once the couple arrived, they published an article or a short poem in the paper. The Social Affairs Department also inquired of Shenyang, and learned that the two had come back to Yan'an without their leaders' approval—an unauthorized act.

Interrogated separately, the wife, a certain Tian, was a simple worker who answered whatever the organization asked her truthfully. It came out that the two had been sent by the Soviet Army intelligence group to lie hidden in Shenyang, where their radio set was picked up by Japanese monitoring. The Japanese agents resolved to turn them, sending the two back to Yan'an to lie hidden for the long term—generally making no organizational contact, only publishing articles in the paper as signals.

The man, a certain Zhang, was arrested; the confessing Tian was sent to study in the intelligence training class at Northwest College, and later even joined the Party.

By Bu Lu's account, this case was "counter-counter-espionage": we sent out an agent to scout, the enemy's counter-espionage turned him, and then we took counter-counter-espionage measures in reply.

The Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region's security cadres were mostly of worker and peasant stock, their lives fairly plain, and the coming of the war set an espionage struggle of unheard-of complexity before them all at once.

Zhitian township in Xunyi County lay on the border between the Border Region and the Nationalist areas, and here the Yang family and the Xiao family were sworn enemies. When the Xiaos joined the revolution, the Yangs squeezed them until they could not stand their ground. To save his clan, a certain Xiao surrendered to the Nationalists to kill his enemy with a borrowed knife, yet he did not oppose the Communists either, passing intelligence from time to time to the Xinzheng County security section. Out of a private quarrel he even denounced a renegade named Zhang to the Nationalist agents. This certain Xiao lodged charges with both sides, until the security organs of both sides dared not trust him. In an anti-traitor campaign the Border Security arrested the certain Xiao, and he confessed the whole story in floods of tears, declaring that he had defected purely for his clan's sake and would never harm the Border Region. Later the certain Xiao did in fact move his family into the Border Region, became an ordinary commoner, and worked for the Nationalist agents no more.

In the border zones enemy and self were interlaced, and men shifted at any moment among the roles of soldier, brigand, agent, and civilian. The intelligence and security cadres had to understand the concrete social conditions and grasp policy exactly.

The Border Security's cadres began to take up a tangled subject: the double agent.

A "double agent" (a two-faced agent, a turned agent) has two senses. In the one, a single agent serves at once two mutually hostile agencies—a foot in each boat. This kind of double agent gives an agency the worst headache: whoever gives the milk is mother to him, and who can tell whose man he truly is? In the other, he reaches true service to one side through false service to the other—body in Cao's camp, heart with Han. This second kind too keeps an agency on its guard, lest the man be pulled over to the other side. Whichever the kind, the double agent ranks among the higher and harder grades of espionage work.

The Border Security got a double agent of its own. Yang Hongchao, a primary-school teacher in the Border Region, received a subversion letter sent from the Nationalist areas, and the Border Security seized the chance to plant this Yang Hongchao inside Zhongtong. To win Yang Hongchao Zhongtong's trust, the field team fed him some false intelligence and false special-intelligence besides. Sure enough Zhongtong appointed Yang Hongchao a "full-time investigation worker," and he brought back no little intelligence. Later, in an inspection of the work, the Social Affairs Department criticized the Border Security: the false special-intelligence handed over in Guanzhong had been pitched too high—even a county committee secretary had been made out to be a Nationalist agent—which was to swell the enemy's strength, and had to be pulled back. The Guanzhong prefectural committee and the field team weighed it and decided that the Xinzheng County committee secretary, Li Ke, and others would publish a statement in the paper to quash the rumor. At this Yang Hongchao's footing on the Zhongtong side turned somewhat awkward. Before long the field team head, Qu Jixian, who had had single-line contact with Yang Hongchao, was recalled to Yan'an, and Qin Ping took over the work. But the intelligence Yang Hongchao sent grew scarcer and scarcer, and was sometimes no more than street gossip.

Just then Zhou Chongde, director of the Border Region Bank's Guanzhong branch, sent a man named Wen Bin to Qin Ping for vetting. In the course of talk Qin Ping quickly found the flaw: Wen Bin had served under Zhou Chongde at the Longdong branch, yet Zhou Chongde had only lately been transferred to Guanzhong—how could Wen Bin have known so soon?

Under questioning Wen Bin gave up the inside story. He had gone back to his old home in the Nationalist areas to marry, was secretly arrested by the Nationalist Xunyi County party headquarters, and there the Zhongtong agent Pu Yujie and Yang Hongchao had forced Wen Bin to return to the Border Region and subvert Zhou Chongde.

Yang Hongchao subverting Zhou Chongde?

Lately the border situation was tense, the Nationalists might launch a military attack at any time, and some waverers were casting about for another way out. Had this Yang Hongchao changed too?

Shi Zhe, chief of the First Bureau of the Security Office, led a party to Guanzhong and secretly arrested Yang Hongchao. Later the Yang Hongchao case became a model case in Yan'an's cadre-examination campaign; Yang Hongchao's uncle Yang Yuanyao was a veteran party member of northern Shaanxi, and the case reached into the Shaanxi party as well.

The espionage war is the most tangled of struggles, and the double agent the hardest figure to place.

Yang Hongchao was only a young primary-school teacher, made a double agent all at once, without the training to handle a tangled situation. If the intelligence tie he brought to the Nationalist agents was publicly quashed in the papers, could he still be trusted by the Nationalist apparatus? A man who was not trusted could not obtain secret intelligence; and a man who could not send back secret intelligence could not be trusted by the Communists either.

The double agent, trusted by neither side, is always in a hard place. On the moral coordinate of espionage, every choice is a temptation: steadfastness, wavering, evasion, a foot in each boat, surrender, defection, going over to the enemy.

To whom, then, was the double agent Yang Hongchao loyal? A later sifting found that when Zhongtong dispatched Wen Bin to subvert Zhou Chongde, Yang Hongchao had at the time reported it to the Border Security; but because contact was difficult, the intelligence had not reached Qin Ping's hands in time, and so the misunderstanding arose.

The standard for judging an agent is a question of real difficulty. The Social Affairs Department documents of the day once listed several standards. In short, look at the quality of the intelligence he brings back. Whoever gets the truest, most accurate, highest-grade intelligence is the one he is loyal to. All the rest—signatures, fingerprints, verbal declarations—is empty.

The Communist Ji-Re-Liao district committee was holding a three-level cadre conference at Yangjiapu when it was suddenly encircled by Japanese troops. The Jidong intelligence station chief, Ren Yuan, had already broken out, then charged back into the ring to rescue his comrades, and was gravely wounded and captured.

Ren Yuan had joined the revolution in 1936 in his home district of Suide, taken part in 1938 in the first class of the Border Security's intelligence-and-scouting course at Qilipu, and later followed Xu Jianguo to work in Jin-Cha-Ji. The Social Affairs Department had spared no pains to open a channel to the Northeast, and that task had fallen to Jidong. Ren Yuan, dreading that he might not hold out and would give something away, resolved to kill himself—but his grave wounds left him unable to do the deed.

The Japanese, finding they had caught a great Communist agent, took him very seriously. First came harsh interrogation, then favored treatment to induce surrender. Ren Yuan concocted a confession, supplying at a stroke a list of some thirty-odd names. Thinking Ren Yuan had wavered, the Japanese relaxed their guard, and Ren Yuan seized the chance to make contact with the Communist underground and slip away when the moment served. In truth the list Ren Yuan supplied was a scheme to kill collaborators with a borrowed knife.

Counter-espionage was indeed intricate and profound work.

Everyone knew it was very hard to plant an agent inside the enemy's apparatus; but in a sense, one might also say counter-espionage was no easier than planting agents. In planting an agent, I am in the dark and the enemy in the open, and I can lay schemes to lure him. In counter-espionage, the enemy is in the dark and I in the open, and it is hard to make out the adversary's true identity.

Social life is complex, the espionage war more complex still, and the simple-minded are the ones who suffer for it.

### 5. "Why Doesn't the Thunder God Strike Mao Zedong?"

The Communists' management of society was solid enough. Criminal and public-order cases in the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region were quite few; In Yan'an, nothing dropped on the road was pocketed and no door was locked at night. Across the length of China the people could scarcely make a living; only this Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region was truly an earthly paradise.

The Nationalists were unwilling to see Yan'an grow strong. In the autumn of 1940 the Nationalist government cut off the Eighth Route Army's pay and blockaded the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region's supplies. A Border Region of 1.4 million people now had to feed troops and organs numbering in the hundreds of thousands. The Communists, so skilled at war, ran into an economic crisis, and the Border Region faced the prospect of running out of both ammunition and grain.

The scarcer the grain, the more had to be levied. On June 3, 1941, the Border Region government convened a joint conference of county heads to discuss the grain levy. Dry, rain-starved Yan'an was suddenly struck by a downpour; with a great crash a lightning bolt hit the conference room, wounding eight of those present and killing the acting head of Yanchuan County, Li Caiyun, on the spot.

Chinese folk belief has always held to a correspondence between Heaven and man, and word of an ordinary natural disaster passed from mouth to mouth among the common people: "The county head was struck down by five thunderbolts from Heaven—he must have done something to burden his conscience!"

Some went further: "Heaven's eyes are shut. Why doesn't the Thunder God strike Mao Zedong dead?"

This northern Shaanxi was no ordinary place: in its day the rising of Li Zicheng had begun with the revolt of northern Shaanxi's disaster victims. The Border Security had always kept a close watch on the mood of society, and hearing of talk that attacked Chairman Mao, it at once listed the matter a counter-revolutionary case for stern investigation.

Mao Zedong, learning of it, was not in the least surprised, and first asked whether the speaker had any political background. Told the man was no more than an ordinary peasant, Mao Zedong stopped the Security Office's investigation.

A peasant himself by origin, Mao Zedong understood that peasants do not curse people for no reason. Mao Zedong judged: this shows that something has gone wrong in our work. After looking into it, Mao Zedong found the peasants' main grievance was that the grain levy was too heavy.

Defending a poor and weak Border Region called for a strong force, yet the vast host of men taken off production would drain that poor, weak Border Region dry—a contradiction the cadres within the party thought insoluble. Then, unexpectedly, at the Border Region's second assembly, a non-party assemblyman, Li Dingming, put forward the proposal for "crack troops and simple administration."

Crack troops and simple administration—the target of the trimming was bound to be the army and the organs, the Communists' core strength. In the history of the Republic, armies had been reorganized many times, and not one attempt had succeeded. No ruler gives up vested interests willingly. Yet Mao Zedong thought highly of this non-party man Li Dingming's advice. The Border Region government at once set up a reorganization committee. In December 1941 the Border Region government's administrative council decided on a sweeping cut in personnel: for 1942 the public grain levy was set at only 600,000 dan, 40,000 less than the previous year, and the public fodder at only 16 million jin, 10 million less than the previous year.

Saving with one hand and opening new sources with the other, Mao Zedong also called for a Great Production Campaign. Across the whole Border Region, from the chairman down to the clerks, everyone labored—breaking new ground and planting, spinning thread and weaving cloth. This lean, capable body of men in Yan'an actually met most of its needs by its own hands.

A single "Thunder God" affair brought many consequences. The security departments not only avoided a wrongful case, they were learning how to treat political dissent, how to treat the citizen.

How to tell contradictions of different natures apart was not only a question of policy but a question of competence. In the Longdong sub-district a tangled money-theft case appeared.

That year Longdong suffered a great drought, and the Qingyang County government drew special funds to relieve the victims. When 8 million in border currency, carried by the county civil-affairs section chief Li Lianchen, went missing, suspicion of the theft fell on two men. This Section Chief Li was a Nationalist holdover; that Secretary Li was fond of opium. In the course of the inquiry Secretary Li went to Section Chief Li's home and swindled 2 million out of his younger brother, forcing Section Chief Li to make good 6 million. Section Chief Li insisted that the 2 million was money a relative had entrusted to him to buy opium, and demanded Secretary Li hand over the 8 million.

The case was tangled, and the Longdong security section took Section Chief Li and Secretary Li into custody together. Just then the Qingyang County deputy head, a man named Luo, suddenly came to the security-section chief Li Fushan and handed over a letter showing Secretary Li in collusion with the Nationalist agents at Pingliang.

The theft case rose to an agent case. The Longdong court decided to arrest Secretary Li and release Section Chief Li without charge.

The Border Security's security chief, Bu Lu, came on a tour of Longdong, and received word from cadres and masses that Secretary Li was an honest man, while Section Chief Li and Deputy Head Luo were brothers-in-law—officials siding with officials, commoners with commoners, one turtle cozy with another. Some grumbled that the Communists were no different from the Nationalists after all, still taking the side of the officeholder.

Bu Lu had always loved to study material evidence, and this time, with a Nationalist agent's secret contact letter in hand, he grew fonder of it still. Turning it over and over, he at last found the flaw. It emerged that Section Chief Li and Secretary Li shared one cave dwelling, and that Section Chief Li had deliberately asked Secretary Li to write the letter for him. Once the letter was written, Section Chief Li had burned away the signature and a few words with a cigarette end, and this ordinary letter had become a secret agent-contact letter. The clever notion had come from his brother-in-law, Deputy Head Luo, learned from the old opera *Jiang Gan Steals the Letter*.

The case was retried; Section Chief Li and Deputy Head Luo were arrested, and Secretary Li released without charge. In Qingyang, liberated barely a year and more, feeling ran high, and all praised the Communists as just and honest.

As it happened, another money-theft case turned up in Qingyang before long.

The allowance funds for all the county's cadres vanished from the finance section's very room. Suspicion pointed within, and the county security-section chief Hao Su set the scout Wang Fulin to get the lay of things from outside first; he learned that the county head's wife had lately been spending suddenly more. The security section's woman cadre Huang Youqun drew close to the county head's wife and found she often went to the finance section to chat. Hao Su called the county head's wife in for a talk; a simple woman, she gave herself up at the first question.

The case reached into the family of the county head, Feng Zhiguo, and Hao Su told Feng Zhiguo how things stood. This old revolutionary of northern Shaanxi had a strong sense of party discipline: "This is the security section's business."

The county head's own wife had been caught for theft. Among the common people of Longdong the word went round: the Communists put justice above kin!

The Longdong commissioner, Ma Xiwu, also served as court president, and by trying and mediating many a tangled case in open court he won the people's hearts—the folk of Longdong called him "Ma the Blue Sky."

How a party is to hold power, how power is to serve the people—Yan'an gave its answer.

This learning did not come easily. The Communist security organization had learned from the Soviet Cheka, and "Cheka" was a short form: its full name was the "All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage."

Purging counter-revolutionaries is a contradiction between the enemy and ourselves; sabotage is a contradiction among the people. Yet a single organ handled both kinds of contradiction.

And so the Soviet purges often ran to excess; and under the Soviet influence the Communist purges too came to run to serious excess.

Now the Communists were beginning to change: contradictions among the people were not to be blown up into contradictions between the enemy and ourselves.

### 6. The Counter-espionage Stratagem

Yan'an had a set of espionage-war buffs who were forever debating the fine points of the craft.

Bu Lu reeled off a string of new terms: "scouting, counter-scouting, counter-counter-scouting…" This "scouting" was our side's action to scout the enemy; this "counter-scouting" was the enemy's action to guard against our scouting; this "counter-counter-scouting" was our further scouting action against the enemy's guarding action…

Talk it round and round, and one loses the thread. They all said Bu Lu quibbled over terms; Bu Lu, for his part, never tired of it. Espionage warfare was growing ever more complex, and the methodology had to keep pace.

In truth, even this tongue-twister of professional jargon still fell short of describing the complexity of the espionage war.

At the end of 1938 Li Qiming went to Suide to do field work, and set up the Lu Nan intelligence group among the Nationalist garrison, successfully exposing the corruption of the Nationalist Suide commissioner He Shaonan. To pull special-intelligence out of the enemy's ranks and win the struggle by using it was already one successful piece of espionage work. But the Border Security did not stop there. When Suide was liberated in 1940, Li Qiming used a prisoner exchange to send Lu Nan and Gong Zhen into the Nationalist areas.

After Lu Nan reached Suiyuan, he burrowed into one Nationalist organ after another and gathered a great deal of intelligence. But an officer who had fled from Suide recognized Lu Nan; Lu Nan admitted only to being a Communist intelligence contact, and at the same time contrived to send word back to Yan'an.

Juntong set great store by the Communist agent in its hands, and moreover meant to turn Lu Nan and plant him inside the Communist security organs. Li Qiming, who had long since learned of Lu Nan's arrest, decided to strike back once more, and sent Lu Nan a coded letter in reply. Juntong, thinking its turning had taken effect, gave Lu Nan still heavier responsibilities, and so Lu Nan could go on supplying intelligence to the Border Security.

In the spring of 1942 the Juntong boss Ma Hansan sent Lu Nan to burrow into Yan'an, and Li Qiming likewise notified Lu Nan to return to Yan'an to take part in the Rectification—the agent bosses of the two parties happened to think alike, and Lu Nan got safely back. Afterward he burrowed once more into Suiyuan and became secretary of the "party-government-military-special joint intelligence conference," able to command all the important intelligence of the Suiyuan region, and played a still more important part in the War of Liberation.

To develop an intelligence force out of the enemy's camp and use the intelligence to counter the adversary; when an intelligence man is caught, to go on lying hidden by taking advantage of the enemy's turning of him; at the right moment to prompt the enemy to dispatch him and so bring the intelligence man safely back; and at a crucial juncture to send the intelligence man in again to burrow into the enemy anew, giving long intelligence service—

The complete case of the Lu Nan intelligence group showed that Yan'an's counter-espionage work had reached a considerable level.

Counter-espionage means catching the enemy's agents; the counter-espionage stratagem means turning the enemy's agents to use—and the highest level of the espionage war is still the "counter-espionage stratagem."

Chiang Kai-shek was a master of the counter-espionage stratagem. In 1932 the Communist Xu Jishen and others were ordered to found the E-Yu-Wan base. This Whampoa cadet was a bold and able fighter, and left Chiang Kai-shek, his old teacher, without a countermove. Chiang Kai-shek sent two agents, posing as members of the Nationalist Reorganization faction, into the Soviet area to make contact with Xu Jishen. The two agents fell into the hands of the security bureau and confessed that Xu Jishen was a Nationalist inside line. And so Xu Jishen, whom the Nationalists could not defeat, died under the knife of an internal purge.

Luochuan, lying between Yan'an and Xi'an, was the very outpost of Nationalist-Communist espionage warfare.

Luochuan's Zhongtong boss, Shan Buyi, sent a party functionary, Wang Zhongqi, to make a false surrender to the Border Security. The Border Security's field team head, Zhao Qufei, was just then working a counter-espionage stratagem, and let it be understood that the renegade who had fled to his side was in fact a mole.

Having disposed of the renegade, Zhao Qufei went further, forcing this "surrendered" Wang Zhongqi to supply a list of hidden agents, and turned up that Shan Buyi was subverting a Border Region assemblyman, a certain Fan.

Wang Zhongqi, who had given away a great Zhongtong secret, dared not keep up the pretense, and fled by night. Zhao Qufei arranged that his men were not to give chase, so that it would look as though they had let Wang Zhongqi go.

Sure enough, Shan Buyi suspected Wang Zhongqi's return had come too easily, put him to savage torture, and secretly buried him alive.

Having done away with the false surrenderer Wang Zhongqi, Zhao Qufei was still not finished, and put out word to make it plain that Wang Zhongqi had fled of his own accord. The Nationalist men at Luochuan grew restive, and the Border Security agents Shi Zhiwen and Cheng Yonghe seized the chance to stir them up, saying that Shan Buyi, an outsider, made a point of persecuting the local men. At this Shan Buyi found Luochuan too hot to hold him, threw over his work, and left without leave.

With Zhongtong's Luochuan investigation-and-statistics room left leaderless, the Border Security moved at once to seize it. The Zhongtong full-time investigation worker at Yichuan County, Nie Mingxi, was also a Border Security inside line, and without waiting for higher appointment came to Luochuan of his own accord, "stepping forward to take charge." From then on the Zhongtong apparatus at Luochuan was wholly in the Border Security's hands.

A deft counter-espionage stratagem cleared away not just an agent or two but seized control of the enemy's whole apparatus. The young Border Security cadres learned quickly enough. The studious Zhao Cangbi held: "In the covert struggle, both sides—enemy and self—must act according to their own schemes and plans, and the schemes and plans of both are aimed at defeating the other." "This contest of wits between enemy and self, these schemes and plans set point against point, we call the 'divergence of wits.'"

The Border Security threw itself into the "struggle of the divergence of wits."

The field posts at Suide and Mizhi contrived to build inside lines within the Nationalists. The wife of Wei Liang, colonel and commissioner of Juntong's Yulin-and-Suide district, was a Mizhi woman, and through her the Border Security made contact and won Wei Liang as an inside line. Wei Liang supplied the movements of Hu Zongnan's forces and exposed a Juntong scheme to sow discord among the units of the Northwest Army generals Deng Baoshan, Gao Guizi, and Gao Shuangcheng. The secretary-general of the Nationalist Yulin district, Li Wenfang, also became a Border Security inside line, supplying the Border Security field officer Cao Hongbi with Zhongtong's telegraph ciphers and with intelligence on Zhongtong's reliance on Hu Zongnan to enter the Border Region.

By the end of 1942 the Border Security's field posts had already built up an intelligence force of more than a hundred men in the Nationalist-controlled areas opposite, and had gathered several hundred pieces of intelligence—among them the Zhongtong Shaanxi station-room's special-intelligence shock-competition directive and the work plans of the Luochuan, Yulin, and other station-rooms—and had taken control of the Nationalist agent posts at Luochuan.

In the espionage-war contest between the two parties around the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, the Communists were step by step taking the initiative.

This counter-espionage work was truly an intricate and profound problem.

The complexity of the espionage struggle lies in the blurring of enemy and self—the enemy has us in him, and we have the enemy in us. And so whoever can turn the enemy to serve us is the one who wins the espionage war.

Both sides were trying to win the agent over; which side an agent caught in the middle would serve was the question.

The most profound thing in this world is not the craft of the work but the human heart. An agent too is a human being, with his heart set somewhere. And an agent's heart is deeper than an ordinary man's.

The Communists, in the weaker position, could take the realm only by winning men's hearts. Could an agent's heart, then, be turned toward the Communists too?

### Principal Sources

Wang Xiaohua, Meng Guoxiang, Zhang Qingjun, et al., eds., *A Record of Rooting Out Traitors in the Nationalist-Communist War of Resistance*. The book collects many collaborator cases, and its study of the "anti-Trotskyist purge" is fairly systematic.

Liu Xingyi, *A Life of Yang Qiqing*. Its subject, Yang Qiqing, was a veteran Communist security cadre and a counter-espionage expert. The book interviews many of Yang Qiqing's colleagues and is vividly told.

Liang Ji: former deputy director of the Shanghai Maritime Transport Bureau and concurrently public-security chief; interviewed October 26, 2000. Liang Ji worked long in the Yan'an municipal public-security bureau, which at the time bore the task of guarding Japanese prisoners of war.

He Qingyu, *Years of Beacon Fire*. He Qingyu worked for many years in the army's liaison and security work, matching wits directly with the Japanese agent Mizuhara Kiyoshi.

Ling Yun: former Minister of State Security; interviewed May 27, 1998. After the Nationalists withdrew to Taiwan, Shen Zhiyue rose to be deputy director of the Investigation Bureau of the Ministry of the Interior—perhaps connected to this "secretary" episode. Ling Yun, long in charge of counter-espionage work, knew the course of Shen Zhiyue's penetration and withdrawal from Yan'an.

Central Social Affairs Department, "Directive to All Localities on Countering the Nationalist Agents' High-Grade Special-Intelligence Shock Competition"; from the archive kept by Zhou Xing. From this Social Affairs Department document one can judge the original intent of the Zhongtong "high-grade special-intelligence shock competition."

*Work Report of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Government* (Republic years 28–30, i.e., 1939–1941); from the archive kept by Zhou Xing. This report's summary of the enemy-agent situation reflects the Border Security's understanding of the enemy at the time. Unfortunately, with so much time elapsed, the author could only select the important cases among the many counter-espionage cases the report mentions, and was in no position to verify each one.

Hong Lin: former cadre of the secretariat section of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office; interviewed August 19, 2010. Hong Lin was a schoolmate of Zhang Luping at North Shaanxi College; when Zhang Luping suddenly left Yan'an during their studies, rumor among the students had it that she had turned renegade, but Hong Lin held firmly that Zhang Luping was no bad person.

Ming Jun and Di Jiu, eds., *Secret Records of Communist Operatives* (Dalian University of Technology Press). This book records the deeds of many Communist agents, among them the story of the secret branch inside Juntong's main telecommunications station. Zhang Luping's secret branch was later exposed, its whole membership imprisoned in the SACO camp and killed by Nationalist agents on the eve of liberation. Because their underground identities were secret and they had changed their names, the true story of Zhang Luping came to light only in the 1980s.

Li Tao and Yi Hui, *Liu Ding*. When Gu Shunzhang defected, the Special Branch's Liu Ding had nowhere to hide, and with his leaders' approval feigned surrender, then later secretly escaped back to Yan'an.

Wang Chu: former deputy bureau director of the Ministry of Public Security; interviewed December 7, 1998. Japanese agents able to burrow into Yan'an were rare. This Japanese-couple agent case that Wang Chu handled is a twisting and curious one.

Li Qiming: former standing secretary of the Yunnan provincial party committee; interviewed October 29, 1995. The Yang Hongchao case had several people involved, but they all later broke off their working connection with it, while Li Qiming kept the whole picture throughout. Li Qiming personally developed that important intelligence tie Lu Nan, and sponsored Lu Nan into the Party.

Qin Ping: former deputy secretary of the party committee and concurrently security chief in the Ministry of Petroleum apparatus; interviewed October 5, 1994. The author talked with Qin Ping many times, and checked against the memoirs Qin Ping wrote himself, gaining much important detail. The case of the certain He is seen only in Qin Ping's telling; the Yang Hongchao case later grew into a model case of the Rescue Campaign. After the founding of the state Yang Hongchao served as a department head in the Shaanxi Public Security Department; he was persecuted during the "Cultural Revolution" and rehabilitated in the 1980s.

Ren Yuan, *The Test* (Writers Publishing House). The book records the extraordinary experience of a Communist intelligence cadre after his capture by the Japanese.

Jin Chongji, ed., *A Life of Mao Zedong* (Central Party Literature Press). The book gives a full account of the hard course of Mao Zedong's leadership in building the Border Region, and its telling of the "Thunder God" affair is quite vivid.

Li Fushan: former deputy president of the Shanxi provincial procuratorate; interviewed March 1998. On the money-theft case, Li Fushan, then Longdong security-section chief, remembered that a Longdong cadre, Wei Yiwu, had also come to give him his views, but that the case was tried by the court.

Lü Huang: former cadre of the All-China Women's Federation; interviewed March 3, 1997. Lü Huang went with Bu Lu to Longdong and took part in cracking the money-theft case.

Hao Su, "Recollections of Public-Security Work in Longdong." This short piece, written at the invitation of the Longdong public-security office, sketches the security work of the Longdong region and recounts several cases besides, among them the Juntong Hanzhong special-training-class case, the Qingyang county head's wife's money-theft case, and the successful Han Zhanlu case and the mistaken Yao Jingbo case.

Liu Wenge, comp., *A Brief Revolutionary History of Qingyang*. The book records the revolutionary course of the Qingyang region in detail, including Xi Zhongxun's opening of the Nanliang base, the great case of the Juntong collaborator-training class uncovered in Longdong, and "Ma the Blue Sky" and "Liu Qiao'er."

Huang Youqun: former deputy-bureau-level cadre of the Ministry of State Security; interviewed March 1, 1995. Yan Fu and Huang Youqun met and married in Longdong, both veteran security cadres; Huang Youqun once served as secretary of the Qingyang security section.

Zhu Shitong: former deputy chairman of the International Cultural Exchange Foundation; interviewed August 23, 2001. Zhu Shitong's father, Zhu Yunshan, supported his student Xu Jishen and others in founding rural bases, and, on Zhou Enlai's instructions, took part in Deng Yanda's action to reorganize the Nationalist Party. Because Xu Jishen was wrongly killed, Zhu Yunshan indignantly left the Communist organization, and later, active among the democratic parties, remained a loyal friend of the Communists.

Zhao Qufei: former deputy governor of Heilongjiang province and concurrently head of the provincial Public Security Department; interviewed September 24, 1998. With no written materials and no preparation beforehand, Zhao Qufei told the tale of the Luochuan battle of wits, and told it to the life. Colleagues such as Zhang Jiping can confirm and add to this account. After the founding of the state Shi Zhiwen served as president of the Xi'an Geology Institute, and Cheng Yonghe as head of the Second Bureau of the Yunnan Public Security Department, and later as deputy secretary-general of the Yunnan provincial political consultative conference.

Zhao Cangbi, *An Outline of Political Special Cases* (Masses Publishing House). As a scouting expert, Zhao Cangbi spent four years after stepping down as Minister of Public Security writing this monograph, filling a gap in Chinese public-security theory, and in it he set out the concept of the "divergence of wits" in the covert struggle.
