## The Edo Dōjō

At the top of the slope at Yanagichō stood Kondō's Edo training hall.

Hereabouts there was much greenery.

Far off one could see the woods of the Mito lord's mansion (the Kōrakuen of today); round about, the residences of the lesser hatamoto were packed close together; and at the back spread the vast precincts of the Denzū-in.

That the neighborhood ran to such gloomy trades as ritual-goods shops and flower-sellers was because it adjoined the Denzū-in; but for a place in the middle of town there were a good many small birds, too.

At dusk especially, the crows behind the hall set up such a clamor that the sharper-tongued townsfolk nearby had a nickname for it—

{j} the Crow Dōjō,

{j} they would sneer behind its back.

It was at dusk of the day after next that Toshizō came back from the Tama country, and the famous crows were cawing away oddly.

(A hateful voice they've got.)

Even so grim a man had his likes and dislikes, it seemed; crows, at least, he did not care for.

He went straight round to the back of the hall, and was washing his feet at the well when Okita Sōji came up.

"You took your sweet time, didn't you,"

{j} he teased, in his usual way.

"..."

Toshizō went on washing his feet, head down. Okita, all but peering up into his face,

{j} "Master Kondō was full of praise for you—well done, well done, he said."

"What's that?"

{j} Toshizō turned the whites of his eyes on him.

"The business at Bubai Bridge—did you tell Kondō?"

"Of course I didn't, as if I would."

"Then what's this 'well done'?"

"For teaching fencing, that's what."

"What are you on about."

There was no getting the better of this young man.

"Anyhow,"

{j} Okita went on peering,

{j} "a great to-do has blown up. I'd hate to alarm you the moment you're back, but this one thing I've got to get into your ear, or even a man of parts like Master Hijikata will lose his head when it comes to the point."

"What is it?"

{j} Toshizō began to wash his face. Okita glanced at the nape of his neck,

{j} "You're filthy with the dust of the road."

"What is it, this great to-do?"

"Wash your face first."

"Out with it."

{j} He plunged his face into the basin with a splash.

"The fact is, only a moment ago, a country swordsman of some school came asking, 'I beg you would grant me a bout of instruction'—"

"What of it, an inter-school match?"

Nothing to remark on.

It was the fashion of late.

Men confident of their arms would mark out the second- and third-rate little halls of Edo, and come to squeeze a little sandal-money out of them.

At Kondō's Tennen Rishin-ryū hall, in such cases, it fell to the deputy masters, Hijikata and Okita, to take the field.

In order of strength, this hall was an odd one: the young master, Kondō, was rather clumsy; Okita Sōji was the strongest, and then came Hijikata Toshizō and Kondō Isami in that order. (This, of course, was with the shinai; with live blades, how the order might run, one could not say without trying.)

"With the shinai,"

{j} it was said; but the truth was that the Tennen Rishin-ryū was a rough brawler's fencing, and Kondō, as if it were the one thing he knew,

{j} "First, fighting spirit; second, fighting spirit. Push on with spirit, and with live blades or wooden swords our school will always win,"

{j} he would say.

But in the hall's bouts it was weak.

The teaching of swordsmanship had, in these last years of the shogunate, made unheard-of advances.

As teachers, the men of the old days—Tsukahara Bokuden, Itō Ittōsai, Miyamoto Musashi—were, beside the masters of the great late-shogunate halls, Chiba Shūsaku, Saitō Yakurō, Momonoi Shunzō, so simple and unsystematic as to be no comparison at all.

Chiba Shūsaku in particular had an exceedingly fine analytical mind—a man who, were he alive today, could serve just as he was for the head of a college of education. He did away entirely with the mystical language common to the old schools, examined the various styles from the side of mechanical reason, threw out what was needless, and for teaching used no grandiose, unfathomable terms but a plain, logical speech that anyone could follow.

For this reason, counting his two halls of the Hokushin Ittō-ryū together, at Kanda Otamagaike and at Okechō, several thousand fencing students swarmed at his gate. (Of Chiba's Genbukan it was said that a skill which took three years at another school was reached here in one year, and an art of five years perfected in three.)

But the Tennen Rishin-ryū was another matter.

It was Kondō's beloved

{j} "fighting spirit"—

{j} that was the thing.

And so a shinai bout in the hall, in mask and wrist-guards, could not but come off worse against the styles of the day.

Naturally,

{j} the inter-school match was its weak point; and when someone rather strong came calling, they would send in a fluster to a hall of another school and borrow a stand-in.

Against just such an occasion there was a standing understanding with the hall of Saitō Yakurō of the Shintō Munen-ryū, and it was from there that men came. This was a style of the day, reckoned one of the three great halls of Edo, so it was rich in talent.

This hall had at first been at Iidamachi, very handy for borrowing a man; but after that, having suffered a fire, it had moved to distant Sanbanchō.

So that at need a servant would dash out from Kondō's hall, run the ten-odd chō to Sanbanchō without stopping, and fetch a swordsman by palanquin. A fee was paid, of course.

"To Sanbanchō,"

{j} Toshizō said, raising his face,

{j} "you've sent to fetch someone?"

"Kondō,"

{j} Okita said, cocking his thumb,

{j} "since he said, do that, it seems Hijikata and Okita won't manage it—so I sent a man off. Though the match is at the fourth hour tomorrow, before noon, so there's time enough yet."

"And is the swordsman lodging somewhere near, till then?"

"He keeps his lodging hidden; but about now he'll be somewhere near here, drinking his sake and listening to the same crows."

"Who is he?"

"You mustn't be startled,"

{j} Okita said, with a low chuckle,

{j} "the school is the Kōgen Ittō-ryū; the hall, the Hiruma hall of Hachiōji in southern Tama,"

{j} he said.

Toshizō's hand stopped on his face. Was it not the very man he had had that great brawl with only the other night, at Bubai Bridge on the edge of the Fuchū station?

"So he's come riding all the way to Edo, has he."

"He has."

"Who is it? The name."

"Shichiri Kennosuke.—"

{j} and having said it, Okita leapt back. For Toshizō, saying,

{j} —you fool,

{j} had flung the basin-water over him.

"Why did you say nothing till now?"

"I said nothing? It's your being so late back that's to blame. Here I've been, waiting and waiting on your return, to bring you the report."

"All right, all right."

Toshizō was thinking of other things.

"Sōji—you're sure of this? Kondō hasn't the least notion, has he, that we crossed swords with Shichiri at Bubai Bridge?"

"He's a fine one, the master."

"Fine in what way?"

"A trifle like that, he knows nothing of. He's a big man, after all—not like you, Hijikata."

"What are you on about."

{j} Toshizō thought a moment,

{j} "and Shichiri, too—is he wiping his mouth and playing the innocent?"

"Shins with something to hide—that's true of both sides, so Shichiri won't tell. More likely his notion is to take a fair and open win in a hall bout, then spread it about the Tama country, and bring down the good name of the Tennen Rishin-ryū at a stroke."

"I won't face him."

With the shinai, in a formal match, Toshizō had no confidence of a sure win. It was not that Shichiri frightened him—better to say that the Tennen Rishin-ryū was ill suited to a shinai bout.

"But if it's a sequel to Bubai Bridge, I'll gladly go another round."

"That I'll beg to be excused,"

{j} Okita said, and went off laughing.

Supper, at Kondō's earnest wish that they take it together, was eaten in his room.

Kondō's wife, Otsune, waited on them; but she was silent and gloomy, and when this woman served, it seemed the rarest delicacy would turn to ashes.

Toshizō was fussy about his food, and anything ill-seasoned he would leave after a single touch of the chopsticks.

But Otsune was hopeless at cookery. So that rather than eat at the Kondō house he far preferred to order in dishes he liked from the caterer nearby who served the manservants; but such feelings of Toshizō's were beyond Kondō.

Tonight's stew was an odd little fish he had never seen, all bones and no flesh. A single touch of it and the tongue shrank with the salt, but Kondō, unconcerned,

{j} "Eat, eat,"

{j} was tucking in heartily. The rice was four parts barley to six parts old rice.

Rice that a fastidious craftsman would have spat out, Kondō ate six and seven bowls of. His lower jaw was strangely large, so he could crunch up a small bone or two; and, his jaw being so square, the sight of him eating gave the impression of a whole face pulverizing its food.

"Toshi—what's the matter? Upset your stomach?"

"No,"

{j} he said, sourly,

{j} "I'm eating. It's good."

"So it is. Otsune's come on a good deal of late, too. Eh, Otsune?"

(Eh?)

{j} Otsune raised her eyes with that look.

"You hear that—Toshi's praising it so. If a man like this praises it, your cooking's no small thing."

(Listen to him. A good fellow, but his tongue's made of cowhide.)

Thinking so, he was staring hard into Kondō's face when of a sudden it said,

{j} "Did you hear, about Sōji?"

"Hear what?"

{j} Toshizō played dumb.

"Why, this afternoon an odd one came from the Hachiōji station—that Shichiri Kennosuke fellow. A disagreeable sort, but skilled."

"Mm."

"With that Rokusha Sōhaku business, I took him for someone come to pick a quarrel with you, and dealt with him so; but no. It's a match he wants."

"I heard about it."

"Did you."

Kondō at last ate the last of his rice, and, as was his habit, rose to relieve himself after the meal.

"Thank you for the meal."

{j} When Toshizō gave Otsune a bow, she, clearing away the dishes,

{j} —Not at all.

{j} answered in the back of her throat. That was all. Toshizō found this wife hard going, somehow.

Presently Kondō came back to his place. The moment he sat down he opened a letter he held.

"Rihachi, the errand-boy, is just back from Sanbanchō. At Sanbanchō—the Saitō hall—they've taken on tomorrow's business, it seems."

"Who will come, I wonder?"

"The man newly made head student. Young, but wickedly good, by all accounts."

"His name?"

"Katsura Kogorō, it seems to be."

"..."

Neither Toshizō nor Kondō had heard of him.

Not that Katsura's fame as a swordsman had not already rung through the halls of standing in Edo; but to this rustic little hall at Yanagichō it had not yet carried. Such is the sorrow of the outskirts.

(To be head student of the Saitō hall—that must be a brilliant thing.)

Toshizō thought. It was not that he envied it; but though the same name of head student was hung on him too, somehow he began to feel his own lot a shabby one.

(A man is his background and his birth, after all.)

Thinking such things, he withdrew to the sleeping-quarters off the hall, where Okita Sōji sat head-down in the shadow of a dim lamp. Looking, he had his undershirt turned inside out and was picking off fleas.

"Stop that, Sōji."

He had grown irritable. Toshizō, too, picked off a flea now and then, but here Okita's posture suited this third-rate hall all too well, and it was more than he could bear.

"What's the matter?"

{j} The face Okita raised was startlingly bright. Toshizō felt somehow saved by that brightness,

{j} "The man coming here tomorrow to earn his pocket-money is one Katsura Kogorō, they say. Have you heard of him?"

"I have."

{j} Okita, sure enough, was a knowing one.

"I heard of him from Nagakura Shinpachi—a house-guest at Kondō's hall, holder of the full transmission of the Shintō Munen-ryū, the same style as Katsura but a different school. A sword quick and fierce as a demon, they say. Once, at a great match at the Momonoi hall, he cut down nearly every swordsman of every style, and only at the last was put out when Sakamoto Ryōma, head student of the Chiba of Okechō, of the Hokushin Ittō-ryū, got a thrust in on him—though most likely he was tired, is the story. His domain is Chōshū."

"Chōshū, is it."

The name of the domain stirred nothing in particular in Toshizō. Chōshū itself was still an unremarkable domain; the radical loyalist movement that some years later would throw the political scene into chaos had not yet arisen. And Toshizō, for one thing, was not yet vice-commander of the Shinsengumi.

"And in Chōshū, what rank is he?"

"The Katsura were a house of one hundred and fifty koku originally, it seems, but by some turn of the inheritance they're down to ninety. Still, in that domain they're upper samurai, full and proper. In learning, too, he's a great prodigy, and stands high in his lord's regard. A young man blessed in everything, all round, you might say."

"Mm."

Toshizō did not like it.

An ordinary man, hearing rumor of one he had never seen—

—blessed in his teachers, his lord, his birth, his talents, in every point—

would say to himself, well, so he is not as we are, give a wry smile, and there an end. But Toshizō's heart was a little twisted. That a man should be blessed to excess—that very thing he did not like.

"Sōji—you seem to praise him mightily."

"I'm not praising him. I only say what I heard from Nagakura, that's all."

"No, you're praising him. But Sōji, you too—had you been born not a rōnin's son but into an upper-samurai house of a great domain, you'd have had the proper education, become a proper fine man in the proper way, stood high in your lord's regard, and been deferred to by your fellows. A man born different shines different."

"..."

Of course, Toshizō was saying it less of Sōji than against himself.

"Isn't that so."

"Is it, I wonder?"

Such things seemed of no interest whatever to Okita.

The next morning.—

At the appointed hour, Shichiri Kennosuke came.

The flab of his face had its usual soot-grimed look, but the eyes alone were sharp to the point of menace.

Those eyes were smiling. With those same eyes he stood at the entrance of the hall.

He was alone. He had brought no pupils.

It was a boldness that rather flustered the pupil of Kondō's hall who received him.

"I beg you would announce me to Master Kondō. I am Shichiri Kennosuke of Hachiōji, who had the honor of your acquaintance yesterday."

"This way."

Kondō was already waiting in the hall.

Beside him sat, in a row, the head student Hijikata Toshizō; Okita Sōji, holder of the full transmission; Inoue Genzaburō, of the mokuroku; and the house-guests Harada Sanosuke and Nagakura Shinpachi.

"Well, now,"

Shichiri Kennosuke, in a shabby cotton crested robe and cotton-striped riding hakama, was got up for all the world as a country swordsman of Bushū or Jōshū.

When the round of greetings was done, Shichiri turned his smile toward Toshizō,

{j} "Why, Master Hijikata—we met in an odd place the other day."

"On that occasion.—"

{j} Toshizō, with a fierce face, gave a slight bow.

"Ah, on that occasion we were, both of us, most discourteous. Oh—and there beside you, that will be Master Okita. How glad I am to see him."

He was a man who made game of people.

Presently there came in, alone, unannounced and unushered (avoiding such handling on purpose, no doubt), got up to look for all the world like the lowest of the hall's own pupils, a man who entered by the far door.

Toshizō saw the man for the first time.

It was Katsura Kogorō.

The man seated himself, unhurried, at the lowest place.
