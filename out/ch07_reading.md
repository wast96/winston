## Chapter Seven. The Hour of the Monkey, Second Half (4 p.m.)

{v} The hour for lighting the lamps and loosing the night had not yet come, but the people of Chang’an, the old and the young in tow, had crowded early into the streets, jostling amid the ox-carts and mule-carts draped with colored silk.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Monkey, second half.

{d} Chang’an; Chang’an County; the West Market.

The change came so suddenly that it caught every one of them off guard.

Two Lüben Guards soldiers roughly pinned Zhang Xiaojing to the ground, bound his wrists with an oxsinew binding-cord, and then thrust a wad of hemp into his mouth, so that Zhang Xiaojing was left wholly unable to resist, unable even to make a sound. Throughout it all Cui Qi kept his right hand on his hilt, watching Zhang Xiaojing’s every movement, coiled and ready to spring—as though at the least sign of resistance he would cut him down where he lay.

A few quarter-hours before, this same man had stood on the edge of collapse, pitifully looking to Zhang Xiaojing to save his life; now his face had changed altogether. Zhang Xiaojing could not speak, but his neck could still turn. He lifted his head and glared at Cui Qi out of his single eye. Cui Qi turned his face away, yet the corner of his mouth twitched a little—within, he was not so calm as he labored to appear.

A few of the buliang men still wore their fawning smiles, frozen where they stood in bewilderment. They could not make out what had happened at all. Was this lord not the great hero of the hour? How had he in one instant become a prisoner?

It was not that Zhang Xiaojing had never reckoned the Jing’an Bureau might kill the donkey once the grinding was done; what he had not reckoned was that they would not wait so much as a moment to do it.

The people on the far bank were confounded by this turn as well; the water was too broad, and they could not clearly see what was afoot. They saw only Zhang Xiaojing helped up onto the bank in the distance, and then pinned down. Xu Bin’s eyes were poor, and in his agitation he kept tugging at Yao Runeng’s sleeve, bidding him look closer. Yao Runeng strained his eyes wide and could just make out two soldiers marching Zhang Xiaojing away, an officer hard on their heels. This little party turned behind a stretch of stacked timber and vanished from the far bank’s view.

“It’s the Lüben Guards...”

Yao Runeng murmured. Two white braids hung beside their shoulder-guards; there could be no mistaking them.

The moment Xu Bin heard it was the Lüben Guards, his eyes filled with confusion: “Impossible! What would they be seizing their own men for? Isn’t there some misunderstanding in this?” He paced anxiously back and forth along the embankment, meaning to go over and get to the bottom of it, when his foot slipped and he all but tumbled into the water. It was only by catching Yao Runeng’s arm that he barely kept his feet.

Yao Runeng’s heart was rising and falling like a swell. Simple and straightforward as the young man was, he was no fool. The Jing’an Bureau’s stance toward Zhang Xiaojing had always been an equivocal one—admiring his skill at getting things done, yet wary of him as a condemned man. Never mind He Zhizhang; even Li Bi, who had pushed the matter through with all his strength, kept his guard up against Zhang Xiaojing, or he would never have sent Yao Runeng to watch him.

The Lüben Guards were troops under the Jing’an Bureau’s direct command, and Cui Qi took orders from Li Bi alone. Yao Runeng guessed that those above were unwilling to let it be known abroad that the whole Jing’an Bureau had needed a condemned man to bring the thing off, and so were trying at the first instant to blot out the taint of it—but it was too shameless by half!

Zhang Xiaojing had just risked his very life to save half of Chang’an; how could a hero be treated so?

Yao Runeng shook out the hem of his robe and, step by step, made for the earthen slope nearby. Li Bi and that maidservant of his were standing at its top, gazing likewise across the water. He drew a deep breath and resolved to go to Li Bi and have the matter out.

To question a superior openly was a mad thing to do; it might well cost him any footing in Chang’an from that day on. But it stuck in Yao Runeng’s throat like a fishbone, and a knot of fire burned in his breast. Xu Bin marked what he was about, hesitated a moment, and then followed after.

Hearing the footsteps, Li Bi swept a stern gaze their way. Xu Bin at once halted where he stood and gave Yao Runeng another tug. But by now Yao Runeng had already taken one great stride forward, the indignation on his face not to be hidden however he tried.

“Deputy... Deputy Director Li.” Xu Bin decided to smooth the air first.

Li Bi looked the two of them over and said coldly: “If you are here to ask about Zhang Xiaojing, then I should like to know myself just who it was that gave Cui Qi the order.”

Yao Runeng and Xu Bin were struck dumb on the instant. So this had not been Li Bi’s order?

Then whose could it be? In the whole Jing’an Bureau, only the Deputy Director and the Director himself had the standing to give Cui Qi orders; but Director He had already gone home to his sickbed to recover, and could not possibly have kept pace with the moment-by-moment changes on this side. And as for Cui Qi’s acting on his own initiative—where would he have found the nerve?

Li Bi’s face darkened, and he waved a hand: “This is no place to talk. Back to the Jing’an Bureau first.”

By now the residents and traveling merchants of the West Market were gathering in from every side, buzzing with talk as they eyed the canal. The string of disturbances just now had raised too great a din, and had drawn in all these lantern-goers. The clerks of the West Market Office were straining every nerve to keep order, but it was a cup of water on a burning cart. It was no fit occasion, in truth, for talk.

Only a single street divided the Jing’an Bureau from the West Market. As Li Bi’s party crossed the corner, they saw a great crowd of servants clearing away the several corpses of the Wolf Guards. Ma Ge’er’s gross bulk lay on a flatbed cart like a wild boar’s, the eyes staring wide. A few commoners spat at him in disgust, yet dared not go near, but flicked salt about him from a distance with willow sprigs.

These picked warriors of the steppe now lay thus in the streets of Chang’an, loathed and cast aside like so much refuse. Yao Runeng had no great pity for them; but it came to him that if the hero who had done these Türks in were to come to the very same end, it would be an irony indeed.

The words Zhang Xiaojing had said to him rose again, unbidden, in his ears: “In Chang’an, if you do not become a monster like it, it will devour you.”

The party returned to the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau, where the taut atmosphere that had hung over the place for a dozen double-hours had eased at last. The great foe was destroyed; the weary clerks and the hoarse-throated criers alike wore looks of relief. Not a few had begun quietly to put away their scrolls and implements, meaning to go home early and take their families out to see the lanterns. It was, after all, the Lantern Festival, the merriest night of the whole year.

Li Bi flushed with displeasure: “The daughter of the military commissioner Wang is still nowhere to be found, and here is such slackness—what will outsiders think, to see it!”

With the Wolf Guards annihilated, the abduction of Wang Yunxiu had become the most pressing matter the Jing’an Bureau had to resolve. Wang Zhongsi was a great minister of the court, and should any harm come to his kin, it would deal a heavy blow to the heir apparent. Li Bi could by no means allow such a thing to happen.

Xu Bin hurried over and, kicking at the corners of the desks, urged them all to rouse themselves. The petty clerks could only spread their satchels open again and sit back down, though in their hearts many thought it beside the point. They all reckoned she must have died in the blast at Changming Ward, without so much as bones left, and that there was no need to trouble further over it.

Li Bi paid the men no further heed. Heavy with care, he walked past the great sand-table of Chang’an and came straight to his own desk. On it stood seven or eight document-cases of varying make, each holding the dispatches, records, and papers that came in from every quarter. The most sumptuous of them was a purple-figured brocade case, kept for official correspondence alone. It had all along stood empty; but now within it lay a silver-edged document.

Tanqi was certain the case had been empty before they set out. She took up the receipt-slip beside it—and indeed it had been delivered not long since.

Li Bi broke it open, swept his eye over it, and could not help a cold laugh: “I had not even gone looking, and they have sent the answer over first.” Then he tossed it into Xu Bin’s hands. Xu Bin took it and glanced it over: the document came from the Right Xiao Guard, and said that, in view of the danger of the imperial city’s being harried by bandits, it had temporarily requisitioned Cui Qi of the Lüben Guards to seize the persons concerned for a thorough inquiry, and hereby so notified the Jing’an Bureau, and so forth.

To an outsider this was but a plain notice; but in the eyes of one who knew the ways of officialdom, it held a great deal of meaning.

The Jing’an Bureau had charge of Chang’an within the walls and without, while the Right Xiao Guard had charge of the outer security of the imperial city; the two offices’ duties did not overlap, nor did one stand subordinate to the other. However great a stir the Türks might raise, it fell within the Jing’an Bureau’s remit.

But the Wolf Guards had crossed the death-line of Guangde and Huaiyuan, and that had made everything different.

Once the death-line was crossed, they posed a direct threat to the imperial city, and the affair became on the instant a great case of “alarming the imperial presence,” so that the Right Xiao Guard had the right to step in at once and investigate. Raising that gilded signboard of “pursuing a case,” they could requisition whomever they pleased; and whoever dared not cooperate in the inquiry was guilty of “treason.”

So if the Right Xiao Guard demanded that Cui Qi arrest Zhang Xiaojing, then, overstepping their bounds though the act was, a mere petty officer like him could in no way bear up under the pressure.

Yet in this Cui Qi was not without fault: he could well have told the Jing’an Bureau beforehand and let Li Bi make ready. Instead he had said nothing and sprung a surprise, and had seized Zhang Xiaojing and delivered him straight to the Right Xiao Guard—an act that was nothing short of betrayal.

Yao Runeng was not surprised at Cui Qi’s betrayal. From the letting-slip of Cao Poyan at the West Market on, a string of grave blunders had made Cui Qi a bird startled by the twang of a bowstring, in the utmost dread and unease. The Wolf Guards’ crossing of the death-line was the last straw on the camel’s back. Cui Qi reckoned that to stay in the Jing’an Bureau was a road with no way out; better to clasp the leg of the Right Xiao Guard, and at the least earn some credit for coming over.

Li Bi took no interest in where Cui Qi would land. He rapped the desktop with a finger: “Why does the Right Xiao Guard want to take Zhang Xiaojing?”

This was the very heart of the riddle. What did the Right Xiao Guard stand to gain by braving a clash with the Jing’an Bureau and seizing a man by naked overreach?

No one answered. The matter touched on court factions; Yao Runeng was of too low a grade, and Xu Bin was in a muddle, so that neither of the two could offer any counsel of worth. Tanqi stood quietly to one side, a fingertip to her chin, her lovely eyes fixed vacantly on the sand-table. All at once she gave a soft little cough, and put out a slender finger, seeming to point, as if by chance, at Pingkang Ward on the sand-table.

Understanding flashed suddenly before Li Bi’s eyes.

Tanqi was a house-bred maidservant, and dared not speak out on such an occasion; but her hint was clear enough. Pingkang Ward held more than its houses of pleasure—there dwelt within it a great personage besides: the Right Minister, Li Linfu.

One of the most famous political spectacles of the present reign was the standoff between Li Linfu and the Eastern Palace. This chief minister, whose power tipped the balance of the realm, had always borne the heir apparent enmity, only never brought it into the open. From the shadows he watched unceasingly for the Jing’an Bureau’s slips and lapses, that he might use them to assail the Eastern Palace; he was the heir apparent’s most dangerous foe at court.

From the Right Xiao Guard’s sallying forth to Zhang Xiaojing’s arrest there had been but the briefest interval. That the enemy could seize the flaw in an instant and bite down on the very vitals of it—such uncanny insight and dispatch were beyond anything those soldiers of the Right Xiao Guard could have thought out; there must be an old hand behind them, prompting. And of those who would do this, and had the power to do it, there was only the Right Minister.

Following this thread through, the whole of the motive grew of a sudden clear.

Should Zhang Xiaojing fall into Li Linfu’s hands, his very identity would be enough to make a great essay of it: Why did you insist on employing a condemned man? On what grounds did you judge him fit to trust? The Wolf Guards fought their way to the very edge of the imperial city—was that his incompetence, or his willful conniving? Had another loyal and capable officer been put to the work, might not these disturbances have been avoided? Without a perfect certainty you took the risk all the same—have you reckoned the safety of His Majesty as anything at all?

Li Bi pictured in his mind the several faces of Li Linfu’s questioning and could not help a “Hah.” Just as Li Heng had warned earlier in the Pure Land Cloister, He Zhizhang was the pavilion-roof that kept off the wind and rain; and with him gone, the open spears and the hidden arrows came rushing in at once.

The upshot of this affair of the Türk Wolf Guards was an equivocal one. Call it a success, and it was a success: the villains had been slain to the last man. Call it a failure, and it was a failure too: these steppe barbarians had at one point pressed close to the imperial city and alarmed the throne, and the Jing’an Bureau’s having failed to forestall the trouble before it arose was a dereliction of its own.

In other words, whether the Jing’an Bureau had “held up the heavens and guarded the imperial carriage” or had been “negligent of its duty” hung wholly on which side wielded the greater strength at court. With Zhang Xiaojing in the Right Minister’s hands, the Eastern Palace would be on the back foot indeed.

Small wonder the Minister Li had struck so fast.

Yao Runeng and Xu Bin stood where they were, not daring to draw a full breath. They could not see into it as clearly as Li Bi, but one look at their superior’s face told them how great a trouble this was.

Li Bi explained it briefly; Xu Bin’s face dimmed, and he hung his head. Yao Runeng bit his lip in anger, unable to fathom how the thing could be so tangled. Was a hero who had saved Chang’an to be seized at will, only for the mutual jostling of officials? This was no aspect of a flourishing age!

“You’ve been in Chang’an too short a while. Things like this... ai, ai.” Xu Bin shook his head. But Yao Runeng looked to Li Bi and said loudly: “Deputy Director Li, we cannot abandon Commander Zhang—it isn’t right!”

Li Bi motioned him to be calm and not fret. His right hand, from habit, went to grasp for something, and found it closing on air. Tanqi took the fly-whisk from where it lay nearby and set it in his hand. Li Bi gripped the fly-whisk and said in a heavy voice: “We shall not abandon Zhang Xiaojing—for the business of the Türks is not yet over!”

At the words all three started. Were the Wolf Guards not all dead already?

Xu Bin took it that Li Bi meant the progress of the search for Wang Yunxiu, and hurriedly turned to take up a report-scroll: “The Lüben Guards are at this moment conducting a... ai, ai... thorough search of Long Bo’s dwelling in Huaiyuan Ward, the empty house in Xiuzheng Ward, the depot in Changming Ward, and the rest, but so far no trace of Wang Yunxiu has been found.”

But Li Bi shook his head: “It is not Wang Yunxiu I mean; it is the business of the Türks.”

“That?” said Xu Bin in surprise. “What is there left for the Deputy Director to worry over?” Li Bi glanced at him: “Recorder Xu, your memory is not poor. Do you recall, when Su’s Cart-and-Horse Company came into the city, how many casks of Yanzhou rock-oil there were, declared at customs under the guise of ink-stock?”

The figures were graven in Xu Bin’s memory, and he blurted them out: “Three hundred casks, loaded on thirty great flatbed carts.”

“Three hundred casks of rock-oil are three hundred casks of fierce-fire thunder. Those three carts just now held only fifteen casks in all—which is to say that two hundred and eighty-five casks, and twenty-seven flatbed carts, are still unaccounted for.”

Li Bi let the reminder fall lightly, and all around him were struck through with alarm.

Just so—what the Wolf Guards had taken along was but a small part of it. The force of those mere five casks had already turned the West Market upside down, and here were two hundred-odd casks whose whereabouts none knew. This city of Chang’an—heaven above... And in every mind at once there rose the same four syllables: Que-le Huo-duo.

At this Yao Runeng put in: “But the Türks have suffered so grievously in dead and wounded—even if a few slipped the net, they could hardly have hands enough to haul off these two hundred-odd casks, surely?”

Li Bi’s face was between a smile and none: “And who said it must be the Türks who do it?”

Yao Runeng was stunned for a moment, and then broke out in a cold sweat all over. Zhang Xiaojing, Li Bi—it was all one; they never shrank from reading a matter by its darkest thread, as though there were not one good man in all the world. And the more fearful thing was that they were very likely right.

Said Li Bi: “And so we have need of Zhang Xiaojing still. In this matter, save for him, there is no one who can do it.”

As of one accord they all glanced at the sand-table. A fog wreathed the city of Chang’an; while all rejoiced in the victory, the true monster still lay crouched in the dark, and had only just bared its fangs. Zhang Xiaojing alone could hope to cleave the fog apart and drag that monster out into the light of day—and he at this moment was sunk in a prison woven by his own side.

Yao Runeng hesitated a moment, then stepped forward: “Your servant is willing to go and treat with the Right Xiao Guard.” Xu Bin, beside him, wrung his hands in his agitation: “...Ai, ai, you fool! What standing have you? The Right Xiao Guard could grind you to death without so much as a flutter of an eyelid.”

“Then still I must go and try! If it truly comes to nothing, I’ll... I’ll...” Reaching this point, Yao Runeng unfastened the tally at his waist. “I’ll break the jail! Rest easy, Deputy Director—I’ll resign my post and go as a plain commoner; I’ll not implicate the Jing’an Bureau in any wise.”

“Be calm, and do not fret; it has not yet come to that.”

Li Bi motioned him not to be so wrought up; but Yao Runeng caught the sense beneath his words—it has not yet come to that, which was to say that, should it truly come to that, the breaking of a jail was not out of the question after all?

Li Bi set the fly-whisk down hard on the desk, a keen light shooting from his eyes: “This matter I shall see to myself. The rest of you—hold your posts strictly for me, go on with the search for Wang Yunxiu, and let there be not a hair’s breadth of slackness!”

A murmur of grumbling and disappointment rose in the hall, but under Li Bi’s glare no one made bold. The petty clerks, yawning, spread out the shelves again, and the servants, bent low, blew the smothered braziers back to life. The criers went flying out of the hall to carry the unwelcome news to the watchtowers all around.

Li Bi set Xu Bin, Yao Runeng, and several other recorders to press on with the search, and then turned and went through to the rear hall. There Tanqi had already laid out his outer robe and his reckoning-pouch.

“Young master, will you truly go and storm the Right Xiao Guard?” Tanqi asked in a low, anxious voice.

“No—that would fall in exactly with the Minister Li’s wishes; he is hoping for nothing so much as that I should tear into the men of the Southern Command.” Li Bi looked her full in the face. “The one to go is not I. It is you.”

“I?” A sudden flurry took Tanqi. “Why—why me?”

Li Bi leaned to Tanqi’s ear and said a few words softly. Tanqi looked at her young master in astonishment, taking it for a jest. But Li Bi gave a firm nod, to show that he had not gone mad.

“You are a clever girl. To pour the tea and carry the water and shift the pieces on the sand-table here—it is a waste of your gifts indeed.”

The sudden praise flushed Tanqi red to the ears, and she quickly bowed her head. Li Bi smiled and patted her shoulder: “There are not many I can trust at my side; for this work there is no one but you.”

“Then where will you go, young master?” Tanqi asked.

Li Bi drew on his outer robe, hung the reckoning-pouch at his belt, and adjusted the set of the silver fish-pouch on his sash before he answered: “There is only one man who can break the present deadlock. I go now to find him.”

“Who?”

“Director He.”

Li Bi’s tone was level, but Tanqi knew that this was the hardest decision her young master had yet made.

Feng Dalun had two loves: one was to drink in the Yixiang Pavilion, and the other was the Yixiang Pavilion itself.

This little pavilion was fifteen paces both in breadth and in length, no great space; but it had one marvel about it. Into the plaster of its four walls had been kneaded the shredded yunhui fragrant grass of Khotan, together with musk and frankincense. Should the sunlight shift into the pavilion, a faint, strange perfume would rise within, growing sweeter the longer it stood, so that one felt as though lodged in an orchid chamber.

By now the sun had gone down in the west, yet the fragrance lingered still. Feng Dalun, smiling narrow-eyed, raised the bronze goblet in his hand and said aloud: “I greet the sage.”

To call the clear wine “the sage” and the cloudy wine “the worthy” was a jesting turn of phrase among the lettered set. The host being now in his cups’ good humor, the guest facing him took up his own goblet, returned a “I greet it likewise,” and then, with a sweep of his broad sleeve, drank it off at a draught.

The one kneeling opposite was a young man named Yuan Zai. He was of a scholarly, upright cast, his forehead broad and flat as a terrace, grave to look upon. He was that Evaluator of the Court of Judicial Review whom Prince Yong had recommended; and in official grade he stood a head above Feng Dalun.

Yuan Zai set down the bronze goblet when he had drunk and let slip at once: “Fine wine—this is the Langguan Clear of the Toad Barrow?”

Feng Dalun put up his thumb: “Evaluator Yuan has a fine palate—it is indeed from the Toad Barrow in Changle Ward.” He took up the wine-ladle and filled the other’s cup again, and said, unhurried: “There is a droll tale behind that name. In Changle Ward there stands an ancient tomb, east of the ward’s inner street. It is said to be the grave of the Han sage Dong Zhongshu; disciples of the Confucian school, coming to it, would dismount to show their respect, and so it was also called the Dismount Barrow. But the common herd, knowing nothing of the teaching, passed the error mouth to mouth until it became, of all things, the Toad Barrow—which is laughable indeed.”

Long in the building trade, he had the lore of Chang’an’s ward-names and old sites at his fingertips. Yuan Zai gave a laugh: “When first I came to Chang’an I wondered how there could be so odd a place-name; today, hearing Brother Feng’s account, I have it at last.” Holding the bronze goblet, he glanced about him and then said with feeling: “Brother Feng knows how to enjoy himself indeed. There is thought in every corner of this Yixiang Pavilion; it may fairly be counted one of the wonders of Chang’an.”

Feng Dalun marked keenly that where Yuan Zai’s eyes swept—over the aloeswood roof-beams, the crystal curtain-weights, the purple-red gauze hangings, and all the lavish appointments—there was a hot light in them, though it showed only for an instant and was gone. He had read men beyond counting, and knew that this one harbored a rampant greed within, yet could master and hold it in; he would surely be a formidable piece one day.

Just then a knock came from outside the pavilion, and a loafing youth stood on the threshold and passed in a slip of paper. Feng Dalun opened it, glanced at it, cocked his right brow, tucked it away in his breast, and said to Yuan Zai: “I asked Evaluator Yuan here today over a small matter. There is a condemned man in the Chang’an County jail; I must trouble you to issue a document requisitioning him away.”

“Oh?” Yuan Zai tilted his head. “Requisitioned to where? The jail of the Court of Judicial Review?”

“On whatever pretext you like—so long as he is kept there three or five days, then sent back to the county jail just as he was, that will serve.” Feng Dalun made it as light as he could.

Yuan Zai found the request rather unexpected—not because it was hard, but because it was too easy. He had supposed some noble house wished a man fished out; he had not looked for so odd a request. His eyes turned, and he could not help smiling: “This man—I fear he is not in the county jail at present?”

For were the prisoner still in custody, the jail could simply hand him up, with no need for all this circumstance. Only if the prisoner were held by some other office would it be necessary for the Court of Judicial Review to issue a formal writ of requisition to the county jail, which would then take the writ and go to demand the man.

Feng Dalun had not looked for Yuan Zai to catch on so fast, and gave a somewhat awkward cough: “Just so; the man was taken away by others today, and Prince Yong wishes him to go quietly back and stay put.”

“Which office was it that took him away?” Yuan Zai asked.

Feng Dalun stiffened his face: “It is a trifling small matter; Evaluator Yuan need only issue the document, and there is no call to make branches grow out of the twig.”

Yuan Zai studied Feng Dalun. He much liked to observe others, and to read out from them the true feeling they hid. This one tried to seem all composure, yet an anxiety showed through in his tone. That he stressed over and over it was a trifling small matter was the very proof that it was no small matter at all.

Another man would merely have sent out the document and pocketed the bribe, and cared for nothing further—but not Yuan Zai.

“Recorder Feng, you might be a little more candid,” he said.

Feng Dalun’s face changed a little: “What do you mean by that?”

Yuan Zai gave a laugh and leaned his body a little nearer: “For Prince Yong himself to take the matter up, this man’s standing must be no simple thing...”

“That is not a thing for you to ask about.” Feng Dalun’s composure was at last beginning to give.

But Yuan Zai took no offense at all; he wagged his forefinger gently, his gaze sincere: “You might tell me the whole course of it. If your servant knew a little more, perhaps he could be of a good deal more help.”

Only now did Feng Dalun understand how, young as he was, Yuan Zai had already risen to the eighth rank. The fellow’s nose for an opening was too keen by far; after but a few exchanges he had sniffed out the deeper meaning in it, and meant to make a great favor out of a small one. Feng Dalun was minded to refuse, but on second thought—the Jing’an Bureau was a strong and freakish thing, and one document might not answer; better to hear what the fellow had to say.

Men who are greedy and yet know how to hold themselves in are, more often than not, clever to the last degree.

“What is it you wish to know?” Feng Dalun asked.

Yuan Zai smiled: “For instance—who exactly is this man? And why is he in jail?”

Feng Dalun hesitated a moment, then spoke: “The man to be requisitioned is called Zhang Xiaojing. He was once a soldier in the Western Regions, and for his merits was promoted to buliang chief of Wannian County. In the tenth month of the second year of Tianbao, the court, meaning to build a hostel for the envoy come from Little Balur, requisitioned land in Dunyi Ward. A shop called the Wen’s would not remove, and men of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau went to treat with them; whereupon the shopkeeper, one Wen Wuji, died in some unaccountable way. This Zhang Xiaojing was an old comrade-in-arms of the shopkeeper’s, and insisted the man had been done to death by villains, and would pursue it to the very bottom; in the end he fell out with his superior, the county commandant of Wannian. The fellow killed his superior, and was hauled off to jail.”

Yuan Zai listened, the smile on his face unchanging. Feng Dalun’s account was neither whole nor honest—this “building a hostel, requisitioning land,” for one thing, hid within it who knew how much profit; and the Forestry and Crafts Bureau’s “treating” with the master of the Wen’s shop was hardly likely to have been so gentle. As for the part Prince Yong had played in it, Feng Dalun had not breathed a word...

But... none of it mattered. Yuan Zai cared not a whit for the truth; the thing that mattered was what Prince Yong wanted.

He tapped the rim of the bronze goblet with a fingernail: “A capital sentence passed in the tenth month last year should, by rights, have been carried out that same winter; how is it he is still alive now?”

“The re-memorializing was not yet finished, was it—and so he has been held in jail all along.” Feng Dalun was rather at a loss over it.

Yuan Zai nodded in understanding. Since Emperor Taizong’s reign, the court had urged caution in punishment and mercy in penalty, and a capital case in the capital required five re-memorializations. For a case to drag from last year into this before it was carried out was nothing rare.

Feng Dalun went on: “Today, from the Wannian County jail, Zhang Xiaojing was taken away by men of the Jing’an Bureau, his cangue and shackles struck off before all eyes, and he walks abroad among the wards and markets—as good as pardoned!” As he said it, without meaning to he gripped the wine-ladle tight. Yuan Zai marked that his agitation had grown.

“The Jing’an Bureau...” Yuan Zai chewed over the unfamiliar name. “What do they want with Zhang Xiaojing?”

“I don’t know. But by any means he must be got back into the county jail.” Feng Dalun said it with a touch of strain. That case last year had cost no end of trouble to get that Yama behind bars; on no account could he be let go free again.

Yuan Zai had already, dimly, guessed at the whole of the affair, cause and consequence. That “falling out” of Zhang Xiaojing’s, it seemed, had put such men as Prince Yong and Feng Dalun in great dread, fearful that he should be a free man again. With this crux understood, the other particulars were of no great moment. Yuan Zai took up the bronze goblet, savored another fine mouthful of the Langguan Clear, and set his thoughts in order.

“For the Jing’an Bureau to be able to fish a man out of the county jail, its authority must be no low one. Were the Court of Judicial Review alone to come forward, I fear it would be turned back.”

“Then in your honor’s view...?”

“Better to set the censors in motion, and have them lay an impeachment...”

“No, no.” Feng Dalun hastily dissuaded him. “Prince Yong has said he does not wish to stir up those mad dogs of the Censorate.”

The men of the Censorate had for their very office the finding of fault, and they found it with anyone at all. To hope to use them for a blade, one must take care not to cut oneself first. “You put me up to making trouble for another? Eh? That shows you have something amiss yourself, and I must look into you too!”—such was the whole cast of the censors’ thinking. Put kindly, it was “holding all to a perfect standard”; put unkindly, it was a pack of mad dogs.

Seeing the awkwardness on Feng Dalun’s face, Yuan Zai laughed aloud: “Brother Feng is a master of building, but a layman in matters of litigation. Every case the Court of Judicial Review handles must be reported for the record to the Censorate. So we need only find a pretext to have the Court take up the case, and your servant will play a small trick or two in the reporting document—and there will be some restless censor of himself to go and make trouble with the Jing’an Bureau on our behalf...”

Feng Dalun nodded and nodded as he listened. Worked in this way, it left indeed no trace, and no one could trace it back to Prince Yong’s side. He pondered a little, then asked again: “What pretext would serve?”

The pretext had to be big enough to warrant the Court of Judicial Review and the Censorate taking it up, and yet must not draw in himself or Prince Yong.

Yuan Zai dipped a finger in the clear wine and wrote a few characters on the table: “A man guilty of clinging to evil and defying right—how can there be any reason for him to be let out unpardoned?” Feng Dalun was overjoyed, and said “good” again and again. These few characters skirted the eviction and spoke only of Zhang Xiaojing’s killing of the commandant, while hinting that someone had bent the law for private ends and openly shielded him. The four characters “let out unpardoned” above all—the censors, seeing them, would be as a swarm of flies at the sight of tainted blood.

A mere sixteen characters, and several layers of meaning, every side of the thing covered—here was a pen-and-ledger scrivener grown old in his craft, and no mistake.

Once the censors were on the move, there was no fear but the Jing’an Bureau would hand the man over. And whether Zhang Xiaojing were seized back into the county jail, the jail of the Court of Judicial Review, or the Censorate’s own lockup, was all one.

Yuan Zai clapped his hands, smiling narrow-eyed: “Once the Lantern Festival is past, your servant will see to it at once.” Hearing this, Feng Dalun grew anxious: “This—it were best done today...” Yuan Zai had not looked for him to be so pressed; but it was now the hour of the Monkey, and the officials of the Court of Judicial Review, high and low, had long since gone home to make ready for the lanterns—who was there still on duty?

Feng Dalun cupped his hands: “When the thing is done, there will surely be a rich reward.” He bit down hard on the last two syllables. So long as Zhang Xiaojing was not done away with, he would not eat or sleep in peace one day the longer.

Yuan Zai turned it over and over, and sighed: “The matter comes up in a rush; if we would have Zhang Xiaojing seized back today, we lack one more ingredient—a drug-conductor to carry the dose.”

“A drug-conductor?”

“The one who set Zhang Xiaojing on to his violence was the Wen Incense Shop, was it not? If someone of that house would come forward of their own will and lay a charge, then there is a footing in name, and the Court of Judicial Review can make an exception and take up the case this very day.”

Feng Dalun clapped his hands and laughed aloud: “Why, this is a case of no coincidence, no story! The daughter of the master of the Wen’s shop has just this moment been fetched back by my men, and is right next door. I’ve not yet had time to go and see to her—shall we go and take a look together?”

Yuan Zai knew that Feng Dalun wore, among his identities, that of chief of the Bear Fire Gang. The Bear Fire Gang dared not set itself against the Jing’an Bureau, but bullying the common folk was their daily bread. He did not say so aloud, and gladly assented.

The two men rose and left the Yixiang Pavilion, crossed the courtyard, and came before a low woodshed. A few loafing youths of the Bear Fire Gang stood guard at the door. Seeing them each grimed head to foot, Feng Dalun knit his brows and asked how it was that the mere seizing of a woman had come to such a pass. The loafing youths looked at one another and, one saying this and another that, could not for a good while make the how and why of it clear.

While they talked, Yuan Zai took the moment to push open the woodshed door first. Within, a woman in foreign robes lay bound on the ground, her cloud of hair in disarray, her look one of terror, a wad of hemp stuffed in her mouth so that she could make only a muffled moaning.

Yuan Zai’s eyes met hers, and of a sudden he noticed several kingfisher-blue花钿 of twisted silver at the woman’s cheek, and, thrust into her coiled hair, a phoenix-tail nanmu hairpin; his expression could not but stir.

He stood where he was, a flicker in his eyes, and then did a strange thing—he turned and pulled the door shut behind him.

The affairs of this world are marvelous strange; every sip and every peck is foreordained.

Only a little while before, Li Bi had, and left no trace of it, vexed He Zhizhang into a sickbed at home; and now he was forced to steel himself and go beg the old man to come forth again.

The Right Xiao Guard’s holding of Zhang Xiaojing was a most delicate thing, like an egg hung upon a string. Were Li Bi to come forward, or the heir apparent, the frail balance would break on the instant and the egg come crashing down. He Zhizhang—his repute lofty, the imperial favor toward him undimmed, and yet already openly withdrawn into retirement—was the one man alive who could lift the egg down and not break it.

Were there half a chance of it otherwise, the proud and high-spirited Li Bi would not wish to bow his head to that old man. But he had within him a strong foreboding that Chang’an was yet in the utmost danger, and that some great crisis was surely gathering, quietly, still.

The pressure of the hour left him no choice but to set his own honor and shame, his likes and dislikes, aside.

He Zhizhang’s dwelling lay in Xuanping Ward of Wannian County, no short way from the Jing’an Bureau: east through six crossings, then south through three. The streets now surged with a tide of people, and but for his horse’s right of passage he might not have reached it before the dead of night.

Li Bi gripped the reins tight and galloped his horse down the great street. The hour for lighting the lamps and loosing the night had not yet come, but the people of Chang’an, the old and the young in tow, had crowded early into the streets, jostling amid the ox-carts and mule-carts draped with colored silk. The lantern-frames of the several wards were still at their last preparations, but the hundred entertainments beneath the lanterns had already begun their shows, unable to wait. All along the way there was ball-and-sword juggling and wrestling, horse-tricks and cockfights, a marvelous bustle. A layer of greasy roast-mutton fragrance hung in the air, borne off into the distance with the spring airs of the foreign music-troupes, answering the tread-and-sing of the singing-girls that carried faintly from afar.

This was but one small block of streets; further off, one ward and market after another was sinking, each in its turn, into the very same merriment.

The city of Chang’an was like a bolt of plain silk flung into the dyeing-vat, the clamorous dye seeping across its crisscrossing streets as across layer upon layer of warp and weft. One saw the whole face of the cloth slowly wetted through and steeped, the colored haloes spreading by degrees, until soon every last thread was stained with that festive spirit. The whole bolt of plain silk changed its color, and shone with a joy that rose to the heavens.

Amid all this hue of gladness, Li Bi alone was like some stubborn, ill-timed blot, his lips pressed tight, pressing on against the flow of the crowd. He worked his horse’s head this way and that, straining to shoulder a path through the confusion.

Looking on these faces, one and all bright with gladness and excitement, looking on these bustling, thriving wards and streets, Li Bi knew he had no other choice. For the sake of the whole city’s people, for the sake of the heir apparent’s realm to come, he could only lay down his pride and do a thing he was in the utmost loath to do. It was at once a duty and a promise.

“Let it be counted a trial in the world of dust, a whetting of the Daoist heart,” Li Bi thought wearily, as the hooves ran ever onward.

The ground about Xuanping Ward stood high, its slope rising gently, so that from afar it looked like a flat-topped hillock heaved up out of nowhere in the midst of the city. This rise was called the Leyou Plateau, and upon it lay the four wards of Xuanping, Xinchang, Shengping, and Shengdao, whence one might look down over the whole quarter of the city. Its ashen-grey ward-walls wound and stretched along the hillside, roses and clover planted thick at the wall-feet, and green willows besides all over the plateau, in spring and summer riotously lovely, the prospect beyond compare.

The Leyou Plateau and Qujiang Pool were named together as “the hills and waters,” the country scenery the people of Chang’an might enjoy without going beyond the walls. Music-houses, playhouses, and wine-shops covered the plateau, and there were great temples too—the Ci’en Temple, the Qinglong Temple, the Chongzhen Abbey—and nearby, in Jinggong Ward, a polo ground, one of the few places in Chang’an open to public view; it was among the finest spots in the city for pleasure and diversion.

Xuanping Ward, where He Zhizhang lived, stood at the northeast corner of the Leyou Plateau. He had chosen the place partly because there were so many willows here—the trees the old man loved best—and partly because in Shengping Ward to the south there was set a physic-garden of the Eastern Palace. The heir apparent held this venerable elder in especial reverence, and had granted that the Eastern Palace’s physic-garden might supply him with medicines at any time.

After He Zhizhang had retired from office, he had sold off all his properties in the capital, keeping only this one—by which one might see how greatly he loved it.

Li Bi drove his horse up onto the plateau, straight up along a broad and level road of yellow earth, the scene rising higher by stages as he went. The willows on the plateau grew so thick that it had even the by-name of “the Willow Capital.” Winter being just past, the dead branches were too many, and the authorities had strictly forbidden the lighting of lanterns; so neither within the wards nor along the roadsides were there any colored lanterns raised on high. But the ground here stood high and swelling, and from the height, at a glance, all the bright lamps of the whole city fell within one’s view; and so not a few families of officials had come up onto the plateau early to seize a good place. All along the way there was the din of carts and horses and an unbroken chain of song, not a whit behind other quarters.

Li Bi barely hacked his way out of the press and came to the southeast corner of Xuanping Ward. There were few residences here, but every lintel was studded with four gate-pins alike—a sign that each householder was of no common birth. He Zhizhang’s house was easy to know: a great stand of willows was planted before the gate. Li Bi went straight to a residence behind the green wood and knocked at the corner door. The servant within recognized his standing and, not daring to be slack, led him all the way through to the rear court.

A son of He Zhizhang’s was in the courtyard taking stock of the medicines. He was a wooden, taciturn man of middle years, named He Dong; he was not He Zhizhang’s own issue but an adopted son, and bore no title but that of vice-director in the Forestry and Crafts Bureau. Yet He Dong’s name stood high: after He Zeng, He Zhizhang’s own son, had gone off to a garrison post, he had stayed on in the He household to serve his adoptive father with his whole heart, and the world praised him for his pure filial piety.

He Dong knew Li Bi for who he was; ignorant of the falling-out between his father and Li Bi, he came forward warmly to greet him. Li Bi, with a touch of awkwardness, asked after the illness. He Dong’s face changed a little and took on a look of worry: his father’s mind, he said, was still tolerably clear, but the dizziness had not passed, so that he could only keep to his bed, and found speech somewhat hard—by He Dong’s manner, He Zhizhang had, it seemed, said nothing to his household of the Jing’an Bureau’s affairs.

“Your servant has an urgent matter and would call upon Director He; might it be?” Li Bi pressed further: “It is a matter of the court.”

He Dong hesitated, then gave a nod, and led the way before him. The two of them went on to the door of He Zhizhang’s bedchamber, where He Dong went in first to ask a word, then came out, nodded, and asked Li Bi to enter.

Li Bi stepped into the chamber, steadied himself, and made a deep bow: “Li Bi pays his respects to Director He.” He saw the old man reclining listlessly on his couch against a round bolster of gilt-figured hide, his white brows drooping low, and could not but feel a pang of guilt rise in him.

He Zhizhang’s eyes were clouded; with an effort he raised a hand and made a sign. He Dong bowed and withdrew, shutting the inner door fast behind him. When only the two of them were left in the room, He Zhizhang spoke, a string of muddled, phlegmy sounds rolling out of his throat, which Li Bi could only just make out:

“Changyuan—how stands it?”

Plagued by his dizziness, He Zhizhang could keep his words only few and to the point. Li Bi hastily gave a rough account of the case. He Zhizhang heard him out in silence, but passed no judgment. Unable to make out what the old man truly thought, Li Bi stepped up to the couchside: “Director He, the situation now is unquiet; I must beg you to raise your ailing body and go treat with the Right Xiao Guard to free Zhang Xiaojing, or Chang’an will not be quiet, and the heir apparent will have no peace.”

He Zhizhang’s eyes were pressed deep among the folds of wrinkles, so that one could not even tell whether he slept. Li Bi waited a long while, and, getting no answer, reached over and shook him by the body. Only then did He Zhizhang stir his lips and softly let fall a few more words: “It cannot be. The Right Minister.” Then his palm rapped once on the couch-frame.

Li Bi grew greatly agitated. This answer of He Zhizhang’s was still the thinking of court faction: he feared that to save Zhang Xiaojing would give Li Linfu more grounds for attack, and would have the Jing’an Bureau cut itself clear of this condemned man—and so, having gone round a circle, they were back at the two men’s old contradiction. Li Bi, to do the work, must sweep the obstacles from his path by any means; He Zhizhang, to guard against men, must be watertight, dimming his own light to mingle with the dust.

Outside, the water-clock dripped drop by drop into its pail. Li Bi could not help raising his voice, pressing on him that little time now remained, that a great store of fierce-fire thunder was still unaccounted for, that Chang’an was in peril as a stack of eggs. But He Zhizhang was unmoved, and still, stroke by stroke, rapped his palm on the couch-edge.

His meaning was plain enough: the thing must be done, but not by the hand of Zhang Xiaojing.

Before he had come, Li Bi had foreseen that the matter would not be easily resolved. Without a moment’s hesitation he gathered up his gown and knelt on one knee to the ground: “If Director He holds it against me still, your servant is willing to... bear the bramble-rod and beg punishment, and submit to whatever disposal you please. But the time does not wait for me, and I would beg Director He... to hold the greater good foremost.”

He had used the death of Jiao Sui to vex He Zhizhang deliberately into retreat, and in that he had indeed been at fault first. To bring He Zhizhang forth again, Li Bi could do without this much of his face. He held to his humble posture of confession, his long brows knit tight, his fair face flushing faintly. The mortification of this humiliation all but stopped his breath, yet he held on, gritting his teeth.

He Zhizhang, his white brows lowered, heeded it as though he heard nothing, and still rapped his palm, stroke after stroke. The slap of flesh on the wooden couch echoed round the room. It was a gesture of pardon, and it was a gesture of refusal both. The old man would not take revenge out of private grudge; but your method is a poor one, and there can be no accommodating it.

Seeing this answer, the corner of Li Bi’s mouth twitched a little, and a chill spread through his heart. Were it only a contest of interests, he could yield the advantage; were it only a private grievance, he could bow his head. But He Zhizhang acted purely out of concern for the common weal, and it was only that the two men held to different principles—so how was he to give way?

Slap—the palm struck the wooden couch hard once more. This time it was with full force, the posture resolute, no room left for turning.

Li Bi turned his head aside, glanced at the sky beyond the window that had begun to darken, and his breath came short and fast. The road lay there plain before him, yet the old man’s obstinacy stood athwart it like a great boulder, blocking the way airtight.

He rose abruptly to his feet. There could be no more delay; the moment demanded that he decide, and decide now!

Mount Hua has ever had but one road, and though it grind body to powder and bone to dust, one can only walk it to the end.

The offices of the Right Xiao Guard stood within the imperial city, set between the Chengtian Gate and the Zhuque Gate, and were made up of eighteen halls with overhanging-gable roofs. All the other offices within the imperial city stood with their gates thrown open to the outside; but the Right Xiao Guard was unlike the rest, and had built around its halls a further ring of sharp-ridged wall in ashen red. Looking in from without, one could barely make out the rooftops and a few flag-poles, so that it seemed a place of some mystery.

This was because the Right Xiao Guard had charge of the several gates in the south side of the imperial city, and quartered there the year round a great body of Leopard Cavalry. Arms are instruments of ill omen; and so a wall was needed to bar off the baleful air, lest it mar the auspicious calm of the imperial city.

Tanqi stood beside the horse-rail before the inner gate of the Right Xiao Guard, keeping to a graceful posture. On her head she wore a curtained hat, a ring of thin silk hanging from its brim to veil her expression. Yao Runeng, beside her, was much on edge, turning his neck from time to time to look off in a certain direction beyond the imperial city.

They had waited here a good while, yet had not gone in, as though they still waited on something.

The setting sun was sinking in the west now; another double-hour, and the merriest Lantern Festival of all Chang’an’s year would begin to raise its candles. Most of the people from the many offices of the imperial city had already gone, and the few who left late from their rotas hurried their steps, fearful of missing the revels. These two, standing idle upon the imperial way, seemed strangely out of place.

All at once a roll of drums came from far off. Yao Runeng quickly roused himself and, by the last glow of the setting sun, read the flag-signal. This signal was not long; a single character came through. His face grave, Yao Runeng turned his head to Tanqi and said: “B!”

The curtained hat swayed gently. This one character meant that the young master’s endeavor on the Leyou Plateau had failed, and that they must now fall back on Plan B.

In silence Tanqi went over every particular once more, drew a deep breath, and still her heart beat hard. This was a bold plan, a dangerous one, and one whose aftertroubles were without end; only when there was utterly no way left would one do such a thing. Let a single step go amiss, and all of them would be doomed past all redemption. Yet she did not regret it, for it was the young master’s asking.

If the young master’s whole heart was for the heir apparent, then her whole heart was for the young master alone. She was willing to do anything for him, dying not excepted.

“Mistress Tanqi—do we carry out the plan?” Yao Runeng asked.

“Think it over carefully once more—is there truly no oversight in it?” Tanqi was not wholly easy. Li Bi had given the plan his nod, but the working-out of it was Yao Runeng’s; and in this green, blundering youth Tanqi had not the confidence she had in her young master.

Yao Runeng struck his chest, to say there was no need to worry.

“Very well; let us go.” Tanqi forced down her unease and, with Yao Runeng at her side, walked in through the inner gate of the Right Xiao Guard.

The guards had not looked for callers at such an hour, and warily slanted their halberds across. Yao Runeng stepped forward and flourished the waist-tally in his hand: “We’ve come to the Guard on business.” And he made to stride in. The guard hastily barred the way with his halberd: “The passes of this office are strictly kept; none may enter without surrendering a fish-pouch. I beg your pardon.” The tally shone with a silver light, and the guard, not knowing the depths of it, spoke very courteously.

Yao Runeng said: “We’ve an appointment with Adjutant Zhao, on an urgent matter to discuss.”

“Might I ask the honored guests’ names?”

“Of the Pingkang residence.”

The guard turned to rummage through a string of bamboo door-slips hung under the eaves, and after a rattling search reported: “There is no door-slip here for the honored guests.” Yao Runeng put on a look of puzzlement: “Surely not—Adjutant Zhao plainly made the appointment with us; look again?” The guard, holding his patience, went through them once more, and still there was none.

Yao Runeng’s face darkened: “So important a matter, and not even a door-slip made ready beforehand? What kind of work do you call this!” The guard grew somewhat flustered: “We have charge here only of the passes; it is the Stores Section that changes the door-slips each day.” Yao Runeng said angrily: “I care nothing for what tangles you have within the Right Xiao Guard—do not waste our time!” And with that he made to force his way in.

The several guards all tensed at once, some leveling their halberds, some drawing their blades. Tanqi spoke up of a sudden: “Do nothing rash.” Only then did Yao Runeng sullenly halt his steps, draw back beyond the inner gate, and toss over a calling-card: “Fine, fine—we won’t go in; you call out Adjutant Zhao.”

The guard let out a quiet breath of relief; the Stores Section’s blame was not one they cared to carry. That the callers would give ground was the best thing possible, and he hastened to pass the word within and spare himself trouble. So he took up the calling-card, ran in to report, and before long a plump official in a blue robe came hurrying out.

This official wore a look of bafflement, at a loss where these two guests had sprung from. But when he had come to the inner gate and looked them over, he hastily cupped his hands and gave a “yes, my lord,” his manner all courtesy.

The young guard in front was one thing; but the woman behind him wore a curtained hat of gauze and a broad, dark-hued brocade robe besides. To wear so thick a brocade robe in the present weather was somewhat strange, but the outfit was of no small worth.

Adjutant Zhao reasoned it out clearly: those who had the standing to enter the imperial city were either rich or noble; and one who dared stand at the gate of the Right Xiao Guard and call an adjutant out by name, more than that, had reach and grasp that touched the very heavens. He was but an eighth-rank officer, and could not lightly give offense to the powerful.

“The bright lanterns are about to be lit; what brings the two of you here?”

Tanqi did not lift her curtained hat, but simply handed over a jade pendant. Adjutant Zhao started at first, then quickly took it. The pendant was the size of a palm, carved into the shape of a spray of plum blossom. The plum blossom is white, the white jade clear and pure, and the two joined so seamlessly it was as though wrought by nature itself—a marvel of workmanship that stole the very craft of heaven.

The jade was of the finest, and rarer still was the workmanship. Adjutant Zhao held the plum-blossom jade and for a moment was at a loss. Tanqi said: “Zhao Qilang, my master wishes to come and take away a certain person.”

Hearing this young woman call out his own ranking-name at a stroke, and looking down again at the plum-blossom jade and the “of the Pingkang residence” on the card, Adjutant Zhao’s eyes grew suddenly excited: “Your honor... can it be you come from Pingkang Ward?” The gauze on the curtained hat trembled, but made no sound. Adjutant Zhao caught the meaning at once, handed the jade back, and then, with the utmost deference, ushered the two of them into the office.

The guard was just about to hand up the gate-register for them to sign in, when Adjutant Zhao waved a great hand and drove him off.

They passed through a long corridor and came to a quiet room kept for receiving guests. Adjutant Zhao shut the door fast, then turned and said with a smile: “I had not thought my own lowly name could come within your honored master’s eye.”

“Heh, heh—my master has said that there is a fine strength of character in Zhao Qilang’s Tangdi Collection; a pity it is not more widely known.”

Adjutant Zhao’s face all but bloomed with delight. He had once played at elegance and printed a collection of poems, though only to be passed among kin and friends; he had never dreamed that personage had actually read it. Overwhelmed by the favor, he hastily gathered himself: “And might the Right Minister...”

“Mm?”

Behind the gauze Tanqi gave a sound of displeasure, and Adjutant Zhao hastily changed his words: “Your master—your master. Might I know whom your master has sent his honored envoy to take away this time?” Tanqi said: “Zhang Xiaojing.” Adjutant Zhao started, and Yao Runeng added: “The very man you brought in half a double-hour ago.”

Of the tumult in the West Market Adjutant Zhao had heard, and he knew a man had been brought back. But he had never thought that this affair should have stirred even the Right Minister.

“But this—this is a wanted criminal of the court...” Adjutant Zhao did not understand the tangled situation behind it, but he knew at the least that the man was of great moment. Tanqi said: “This man is called Zhang Xiaojing, and was arranged between my master and your Right Xiao Guard from the first. Otherwise, why would the notice sent to the Jing’an Bureau not so much as leave a name?”

Her tone was unhurried, level, and yet carried within it a trace of the reserve and pride of a high and lofty house.

Hearing this, Adjutant Zhao pondered a moment, tapped his right hand lightly into his left palm, and his face cleared in sudden understanding: “So that... so that is how it is!” In Tanqi’s and Yao Runeng’s hearts both, a tension eased at once: it had worked.

This Plan B was to have Tanqi pass herself off as a house-bred maidservant of Li Linfu’s, slip into the Right Xiao Guard, and collect Zhang Xiaojing away. The heart of the whole plan lay in that one document the Right Xiao Guard had sent to the Jing’an Bureau.

The arrest of Zhang Xiaojing had been done by the Right Xiao Guard at Li Linfu’s secret bidding, and so the document had said only such words as “seize the persons concerned for a thorough inquiry,” and had written no name. In this way the Minister Li could take the man away and leave no trace; and should the Jing’an Bureau come to demand him, the Right Xiao Guard could fob them off with any other man they pleased—we have seized only the persons concerned, but never said the one seized was that one you are looking for.

Li Bi knew these games of words upon paper to the marrow, and so turned about and contrived to use them. Since you can only take the man in secret, and do not wish it noised abroad, then I will steal a march on you: passing myself off as you, I will carry the man off.

That jade pendant was, in truth, a gift from Li Heng to Li Bi. The plum blossom stands for the Li surname of the imperial clan. And as it happened all three of these men were surnamed Li, so that to use it as a token of Li Linfu’s showed not a single flaw—it caught to perfection the art of crossing the sea under the very eye of heaven.

So the moment Tanqi showed the plum-blossom jade and the alias “of the Pingkang residence,” Adjutant Zhao took it into his head from the first that the callers had been sent by the Minister Li. And when they named besides, at a stroke, the particulars of the Jing’an Bureau’s document, Adjutant Zhao suspected nothing further, and at once “worked it out”: ah, so the Minister Li and this Guard are in secret concert, and here they come to take the man away.

This string of hints seemed a matter of luck, but was in truth the fruit of the Jing’an Bureau’s “art of the Great Archive,” worked out with the utmost pains.

Seeing the moment ripe, Tanqi pressed him: “The lantern festival is close upon us; I must ask the adjutant to take us to fetch the man as soon as may be.” At the mere thought of forming a tie with the Minister Li, Adjutant Zhao felt his very frame go light, and he assented over and over.

Adjutant Zhao led the two of them deeper into the Guard’s offices. Here side-galleries, inner rooms, and store-halls interlocked ring within ring, running every way, so that without a guide one would surely lose oneself. Rounding a corner, they met a squad of soldiers coming toward them. Adjutant Zhao stopped short and gave a soft “ai.” Tanqi’s heart and Yao Runeng’s skipped half a beat, taking it that some flaw had shown. Yao Runeng slid his hand toward his waist, where an iron ruler lay hid.

But Adjutant Zhao said fawningly: “Further on the way grows dark and the eaves low; I fear the honored envoy’s curtained hat may be a hindrance, so pray take good care.” Tanqi let out a breath. To walk so narrow a passage behind a veil of gauze was awkward indeed. She lifted the gauze of the curtained hat aside, and disclosed a face of matchless beauty.

Adjutant Zhao was startled at her beauty, and dared not look long, but quickly turned away. It was said the Minister Li was sunk in wine and women, his chambers full of concubines and maids—why, even a house-bred slave-girl of his was as lovely as this. Having already fixed it in his mind that the callers were the Minister Li’s envoys, he read every particular in that light, and grew the surer of it.

They went on until they came to a small courtyard, and there stopped. Call it a courtyard, but it was near enough indoors, hemmed on every side by the broad eaves of the great halls close by, so that it seemed cramped and dim. At the far end of the courtyard were two great iron-banded doors, and five or six guards stood at the courtyard’s entrance.

By Adjutant Zhao’s account, the Right Xiao Guard had no proper prison of its own. Behind these iron-banded doors was a storehouse, used in ordinary times for goods; that guards had now been posted showed plainly it was serving for the moment as a cell, to hold a prisoner of weight.

Adjutant Zhao went over first and muttered a few words to the soldiers through the rail, glancing back their way from time to time as he did.

Yao Runeng marked that Tanqi’s cuff was faintly trembling; to send a frail woman to break a jail was, after all, asking too much. The plan was in truth a hasty patchwork thrown together in a rush, with many an uncertain link in it that must lean on a little luck.

“It’s no great matter to be found out. At the worst we simply fight our way in and snatch Commander Zhang out.” Yao Runeng’s eyes were on the way ahead, the iron ruler gripped in his hand, and there was a touch more of a Zhang Xiaojing-like ferocity in his tone.

To shake off her tension, Tanqi lowered her voice and asked: “Why do you take so to heart that lecher?”

Tanqi bore Zhang Xiaojing no goodwill, and had come here purely for the young master’s sake; and so she could not well understand why Yao Runeng had volunteered to tread this place of peril. Yao Runeng said: “He is a hero, and ought not to be treated so. Breaking a jail is against the law, but it is a right thing to do.”

“Is he truly thinking of the whole city’s people? He does not mean to seize the chance and flee?” Tanqi asked back, curious.

Yao Runeng knit his brows as though at an insult: “If Commander Zhang wished to get away, there is no one in all Chang’an who could stop him.”

Tanqi sighed: “And the young master too—at his very first meeting with the man, he dared entrust him with so great a charge. I truly cannot understand it: here is plainly a brute who killed his own superior, and how is it you all trust him so?” Yao Runeng had all along been curious about the charge against Zhang Xiaojing, and hearing this he quickly pressed: “Mistress, do you know for what he was jailed?”

“The young master mentioned it a little—that he killed his own superior.”

Yao Runeng started. Zhang Xiaojing’s superior was the county commandant, an official of the junior eighth rank, lower grade; for an inferior to strike down his superior—small wonder it was a capital crime. He pressed further as to why he had killed the man, and Tanqi shook her head and said she did not know. Yao Runeng found it very strange. By his observation, this Zhang Xiaojing was a man of deep and hidden thought, not the impulsive sort—and, granting every allowance, even had Zhang Xiaojing set his heart on killing the commandant, then with his skill how should he have been caught red-handed?

“No, it can’t be so simple; there must be something else behind it.” Yao Runeng shook his head.

“Hmph—an idle lecher like him, what could there be behind him?” Tanqi still bore a grudge for the insolent way he had looked at her.

Just then Adjutant Zhao came back, and the two of them hastily hushed. Adjutant Zhao wore a look of helplessness: “This matter—it’s a little hard to manage.” Tanqi’s fine brows knit: “What is the trouble?”

Adjutant Zhao said: “Were it an ordinary prisoner, my word would serve. But this prisoner was taken by General Gan’s own order, sealed with the great seal besides; by the rule, there must be his signed leave to release him... Your honored master will have told the envoy of this, surely?” Reaching this point, a trace of doubt showed in his eyes.

By rights, if the Minister Li sent an envoy to take a man, he should first have gotten word to General Gan, and had him furnish a document or a token. These two had only a plum-blossom white jade of doubtful meaning, and so a little suspicion stirred in Adjutant Zhao.

Tanqi’s response was very quick; she lifted her chin and set on a face of displeasure: “This matter touches a secret of the court, and my master does not wish it noised about. To set it down in a signed writ—do you fear the whole world may not come to know of it?”

Clapped with so great a hat, Adjutant Zhao shuddered with fright: “I would never dare, never dare; but the Right Xiao Guard goes by military law, and I have no authority to release the man.” Seeing the displeasure on Tanqi’s face, his eyes turned: “The General is at present out patrolling the city; better the two of you give me your master’s token, and I’ll send a swift-legged trusted man out; in less than half a double-hour he can surely bring back a signature from him.”

In saying this Adjutant Zhao was at once smoothing things over and testing them. Were they truly the Minister Li’s envoys, they should have no fear of a confrontation with the General.

Tanqi dared not go to the General, and hastily raised her voice: “My master has urgent use for this man, and cannot brook a moment’s delay. Should the great matter miscarry, are you willing to bear the blame for it?” She deliberately said nothing of the Right Xiao Guard, and struck only at Adjutant Zhao the man, laying the whole weight of the pressure on him.

The sweat ran off Adjutant Zhao like rain, yet he would not give way.

The situation was jammed fast at a stroke, and an anxiety began to burn in Tanqi’s heart. She had kept up her high-pressed bearing all along for fear that Adjutant Zhao, coming to himself, would spy the flaw. Seeing things slide toward the worst, Tanqi quietly pinched the back of her own hand with a fingernail, and let the sharp pain steady her mind before she spoke: “Then let it be thus—you take us in to see him, for my master has a few words to ask of him.”

This was a plan that served both sides: it neither broke the military order nor left the envoys without an answer. Adjutant Zhao had no authority to bring the man out, but to take them in to see him he still could. So he let out a breath, gave the warders a few words, and opened the great door of the storehouse.

Before entering, Tanqi gave a soft little cough. Yao Runeng glanced over and saw her raise her right hand and draw a handkerchief from between the armlets on her left arm, and dab at the corner of her mouth. This plain, unremarkable gesture stiffened Yao Runeng’s movements a moment, and then his eyes turned keen and hard.

The gesture meant that Plan B could no longer be used either, and that they must fall to Plan C—a plan that had come not from Li Bi’s hand nor from Yao Runeng’s, but had been put forward by Tanqi herself.

The three of them followed the warder into the storehouse, and were met first by a stale, moldy smell of straw. Within, it was dim, the light so faint one could scarcely see. On the ground lay a great scattered heap of bamboo mats and stray pieces of armor, a few battered chests set in the corner, and along the wall-foot, ranged in a row, seven or eight wooden pinioning-frames.

A few crisscrossing chains of black iron bound a man fast to one of these wooden frames: it was none other than Zhang Xiaojing.

Zhang Xiaojing was still as he had been when he crawled out of the canal, his topknot wet and loosened, his lapels marked yet with water-stains and scorch-marks. It seemed that, having seized him and brought him in, the Right Xiao Guard had not yet found the leisure to put him to hard torture. Hearing the footsteps he lifted his head, and finding that those who came were, of all people, Tanqi and Yao Runeng, a keen light flashed in his single eye.

“There—this is the man,” said Adjutant Zhao.

Tanqi said: “I must ask him a few words on my master’s behalf; would that be convenient?” Adjutant Zhao took the meaning and at once bade the warders all go out; he was about to leave himself, but Tanqi said: “Adjutant Zhao is one of our own; there is no need for you to withdraw.” Hearing this, he was secretly delighted, and barred the door from within.

With the great cell door shut, the room grew darker on the instant. This had been a storehouse, with but one small air-vent left in it, and no observation-hole in the door; so that once the door was closed, not even the soldiers outside could see the goings-on within.

Finding it too dark, Adjutant Zhao bent to feel for the candlestick beside him. Yao Runeng edged over and said he would strike the light. Thinking nothing of it, Adjutant Zhao passed him the candlestick. But instead of drawing out a fire-steel, Yao Runeng drew an iron ruler and struck it hard at the back of his head.

Adjutant Zhao gave a smothered grunt and pitched to the ground. The candlestick Yao Runeng caught in one hand, so that it made not a sound.

Yao Runeng stuffed a wad of hemp into Adjutant Zhao’s mouth, then laid his ear to the door and listened warily for any stir without. After a good while he made a sign, to show the soldiers had not been alarmed.

Tanqi crossed quickly to stand before Zhang Xiaojing and said in a low voice: “The young master sent me to save you.” Zhang Xiaojing grinned: “I knew he would surely come to save me—it isn’t yet time to hide away the bow and boil the hound, after all.”

Tanqi paid no heed to his mockery, and began to undo the robe-buttons at her breast. Zhang Xiaojing was taken aback: “What’s the meaning of this? Would you keep my seed?” Tanqi’s face flushed red, and she spat a low, furious word at him: “Lecher! No ivory ever came out of a dog’s mouth!” She stamped her foot, turned, and went off to the corner.

Yao Runeng hurried over: “Commander Zhang, that was too forward of you; Mistress Tanqi ran a great risk to get in here.” Even as he grumbled, he drew out a sweat-cloth, wrapped it round the joints of the chains, and, without a sound, freed Zhang Xiaojing from the pinioning-frame.

Zhang Xiaojing worked his wrists and his neck, moved to no little feeling within. To force one’s way into an inner guard of the imperial city and carry off a prisoner besides—in ordinary times this was a case to shake the heavens.

That Li Bi, to save him, should go so far as this?

But Zhang Xiaojing felt no great gratitude. That the young Deputy Director should do this was by no means out of any principle of right; more likely the situation had shifted again, and he had urgent need to lean on Zhang Xiaojing’s help.

The pressing thing of the moment, however, was how to get out.

These two fledglings had plainly gotten in by passing themselves off as someone; but the crux was how they meant to get themselves out of the Right Xiao Guard.

Zhang Xiaojing turned his head and saw that Tanqi, over yonder, had already taken off the brocade robe and laid it on the top of a chest nearby, and was pulling loose the ring of thin gauze around the curtained hat. That flippant remark had truly angered her, and so Zhang Xiaojing, knowing better, did not go over, but waited patiently where he was.

Puffed up with anger, Tanqi finished with the curtained hat, then threw it, together with the brocade robe, to Zhang Xiaojing, and said coldly: “Put them on.” Zhang Xiaojing felt the hat and found that the gauze within had been changed for a thicker ring. It was the same color as the old thin gauze, but of a far denser weave. With this on, one need only let the veil hang down, and those outside could not make out the face at all.

Zhang Xiaojing understood their plan at once.

He and Tanqi were much of a height; drape on the brocade robe and the curtained hat and walk out bold as brass, and no outsider would ever dream that the one inside the robe had been swapped.

Zhang Xiaojing pinched the hat-brim and squinted at Tanqi: “A fine plan—the plum tree withering in the peach tree’s stead. But then does it not mean leaving you alone in this tiger’s den?” The most vital point of the plan was that Tanqi must stay behind in Zhang Xiaojing’s place; for the number of people leaving the cell had to tally, or the guards would take alarm.

Tanqi did not so much as look at him: “That is no concern of yours; the young master will come to save me himself.”

Zhang Xiaojing shook his head and reached out to set the curtained hat back on Tanqi’s head. This insolent act startled Tanqi so that she nearly cried out. She flinched away by instinct, but Zhang Xiaojing caught her by the arm and grinned: “It won’t do; this plan is not to my taste.”

Tanqi, somewhat vexed, tried to shake off his hand, but that hand was like a pair of fire-tongs, and she could not break free of it at all. She could only lower her voice and shout in a breath: “Do you want the young master’s efforts to go for nothing?”

“No—it is only that I am not in the habit of letting a woman go to her death in my place,” Zhang Xiaojing said, all earnestness.

Tanqi gave up her struggling and glared at Zhang Xiaojing, unreconciled: “What a gentleman. Then how do you mean to leave?” Zhang Xiaojing put up a finger and waggled it, and smiled: “As it happens, I have a way to get everyone out safe.”

Outside the cell the soldiers chatted on and off, envying those comrades who had the luck to join the first day of the lantern festival. But the festival ran a full three days; once today’s rota was done, tomorrow they could go out and make merry. The guards were just at the height of their talk when one of them wrinkled his nose: “Where’s someone cooking? The smoke’s drifting all the way over here.”

Soon all the ring of men round about smelled it too, and following the scent they looked down—and saw, to their alarm, thick smoke pouring out through the crack of the cell’s great door. Hastily they hammered on the door, bang, bang, bang, to make out what had happened within.

But the door had been barred from within by Adjutant Zhao’s own hand, and without a ramming-log there was no opening it from without. Seeing the smoke and fire grow ever thicker, and even flame-tongues faintly showing, the soldiers grew frantic on the instant. The halls of the Right Xiao Guard stood packed close together, and all of timber; let there be but a little open flame, and it might spread over a whole stretch.

Before the cell all was confusion: one said fetch water quick, one said find some way to open the door, and one said better report first to those above—and then was shouted at, wasn’t the man above right there inside! Not one of them knew what to do.

Happily, before long the great door was flung suddenly open from within. First a mass of thick smoke rushed out, and then Adjutant Zhao and three others came running out grimed and wretched... wait—three? Looking more closely, the soldiers saw that the prisoner too was, of all things, among them, trussed up hand and foot in chains, and led along behind by Adjutant Zhao. Only, the black smoke was everywhere, and they could not see the particulars clearly.

The moment Adjutant Zhao was out, he shouted in a frantic fluster: “In there the candle set the straw alight—call men to fight the fire, quick, we can’t let it spread!” He was the highest in rank present, and at his word the soldiers steadied at once. Adjutant Zhao gave the prisoner a tug and, moving outward, cried: “This important prisoner I’ll take first to a safe place; you sound the gong and raise the alarm, hurry!”

No sooner had he spoken than the firelight in the cell flared up all at once. The blazing head of the flame surged toward the side-chambers on either hand. The soldiers had not looked for the fire to be so fierce, and, with no thought now for anything else, cast about everywhere for fire-fighting gear. Not a few praised the adjutant in their hearts for his sound judgment in getting the prisoner out in time; had the man truly burned to death inside, the doorkeepers would all have come to grief.

Soon the fire-gong rang out, and wave upon wave of soldiers ran in, their footfalls in disorder. The fire grew fiercer still, ashen smoke spreading everywhere, so that all covered their mouths and noses and pressed on with lowered heads, coughing. Adjutant Zhao’s party went out against the flow of the crowd, and in the wreathing smoke no one marked them at all.

Adjutant Zhao walked in front, his face stiff and iron-grey. Though the prisoner hung with chains, his right hand was unbound, gripping something, and never left the small of Adjutant Zhao’s back. Tanqi and Yao Runeng followed close behind, their hearts full of alarm and admiration both.

Never in the world had they thought that Zhang Xiaojing would set the whole cell alight at a single stroke.

The stratagems the two of them had thought of were all of how to hide their figures and go quietly about it; but Zhang Xiaojing was the very opposite—there’s no hiding a figure? no matter; raise a far greater to-do and draw the eye away.

The method was crude and brutal, and yet it broke cunning by main force. Never mind Tanqi and Yao Runeng—not even Li Bi had so ruthless a boldness as this, to burn the whole of the Right Xiao Guard for the saving of one man.

“Only, with such an uproar, I fear the young master’s troubles hereafter will be greater still.”

Tanqi sighed to herself, yet bore no great resentment toward the fellow ahead. After all, it was to spare her from sacrifice that he had chosen this way. Just what manner of man was this lecher? Tanqi lifted her eyes toward Zhang Xiaojing, but his retreating back was blurred and indistinct behind the veil of black smoke.

Soon the party came back to Adjutant Zhao’s room. Once inside, Adjutant Zhao dropped down onto the felt rug, his face iron-grey. Zhang Xiaojing shook off the chains and said with a smile: “Your honor has cooperated well. Next, you must help me find a change of clothes.” Knowing that more words were of no use, Adjutant Zhao rose in silence, opened a cabinet, and dug out a spare set of eighth-rank everyday robes.

Zhang Xiaojing, careless of company, changed his clothes bold as you please and was about to go out the door. Adjutant Zhao suddenly called him back: “You’ll just go, like that?” The three of them turned; they did not take his meaning. Adjutant Zhao cocked his head and pointed at his own neck: “Do me the kindness—land one here, would you; then I can bear a little less of the blame.” Zhang Xiaojing laughed aloud: “Just as you command.” Then he raised his palm and struck a hard blow, and Adjutant Zhao fainted away, well content, on the instant.

The three dared not linger, and, leaving the room, made straight outdoors. By now the fire was growing greater and greater, the whole standing garrison of the Right Xiao Guard roused, and everywhere one could hear men shouting “Fire! Fire!” In the confusion no one paid the least heed to this small party. Bold as brass they went on along the corridor, and came to the inner gate without the least hindrance.

Once through the inner gate, they were as good as out of the jaws of death. Yao Runeng and Tanqi could not help letting out a long breath; the time just past had not been long, but it had been a torment indeed, and they longed to catch their breath.

Just then an armored man came running toward them from the other end of the corridor, likely hurrying too to fight the fire. The corridors of the Right Xiao Guard were very narrow, wide enough only for two to walk abreast, and the three of them had to turn side-on in advance to let him by. The light was dim, and they could not see the man’s face clearly; but as he turned, Yao Runeng caught by chance, beside the man’s shoulder-guards, two white braids, and hastily made to warn the other two—but it was already too late.

As the man’s body crossed Zhang Xiaojing’s, their eyes met square, and on the instant both of them were struck still.

It was Cui Qi.

It fell out by chance. After Cui Qi had brought Zhang Xiaojing to the Right Xiao Guard, he had not left. He knew he could no longer stay in the Jing’an Bureau, and was eager to speak with the officers of the Right Xiao Guard about a place and terms for himself. But the officers were all out, and he could only wait in the room in fretful unease. When the fire-gong sounded just now, he felt he could not sit idle, and thought to come out and make a show of himself—never dreaming that the moment he stepped out he would run into an old acquaintance.

Craven though Cui Qi was, his reactions were first-rate, and he grasped at once what had happened. Without hesitation he fell back three swift steps, and, drawing his blade, bawled at the top of his throat: “The great criminal has escaped!”

Zhang Xiaojing was no slower; he sprang forward and drove his elbow hard into Cui Qi’s belly. In a flash of lightning and flint-spark the two exchanged several passes. Both fought in the army’s way, hard and fierce and direct, and for a moment were matched drum for drum. But though Zhang Xiaojing could hold Cui Qi’s movements down, he had no leisure to stop the man’s mouth.

Cui Qi had never meant to bring Zhang Xiaojing down quickly; he needed only to drag out the time. Fighting and shouting both, before long he had roused the soldiers at the inner gate, who came charging over. This squad was a good ten-odd men, every one in full harness—more than Zhang Xiaojing could deal with, though he had three heads and six arms.

Yao Runeng and Tanqi shut their eyes in anguish; having overcome hardship upon hardship, they were undone, of all things, at the very last step—the work all but done, and ruined on the threshold.

Cui Qi felt the other was near enough to be taken, and slowed his movements. Then he noticed all at once, at the corner of Zhang Xiaojing’s mouth, a grim smile showing, and knew it for a bad sign. Whenever this fellow bared such a smile, something was sure to happen. Cui Qi hastily drew back, guarding against a sudden violent onset.

But Zhang Xiaojing did not press the attack at all; he stood where he was and roared, in a still greater voice: “The Lüben Guards are breaking the jail!!”

Cui Qi’s face changed in a flash. He wore the armor of the Lüben Guards, while Zhang Xiaojing wore the everyday robes of the Right Xiao Guard; which of them the Right Xiao Guard soldiers would side with at first blush needed no thinking about.

Cui Qi turned quickly to explain, but the whole affair was too tangled to be made clear in two or three words. What cared the soldiers for that? Three or four of them came up and pinned Cui Qi down. Zhang Xiaojing and the other two seized the chance to slip past them and ran for the inner gate.

Cui Qi dared not resist, and could only cry over and over that the man was an impostor. At last a soldier heard something amiss and made to stop Zhang Xiaojing and get to the bottom of it—whereupon Zhang Xiaojing flung up his right hand, and a great cloud of white lime-powder went flying through the air, so that the several soldiers nearby, in wretched pain, clapped their hands over their eyes and crouched down.

This was lime-powder scraped from the corner of the storehouse wall; before leaving, Zhang Xiaojing had gotten a packet of it and tucked it in his breast, and sure enough it had come in useful. Yao Runeng, watching from the side, thought Zhang Xiaojing nothing less than a demon-man, forever breaking out, at every last extremity, from some angle beyond all imagining. He half suspected that, even without his and Tanqi’s venturing in, the fellow would have found a way to escape all the same.

Seizing this rare opening, the three broke by main force through the encircling press and ran for all they were worth. Tanqi ran foremost; she felt she had never run so hard in her life, her lungs all but bursting. The inner gate was in sight ahead, and the bows and arrows hung upon it could be clearly seen.

It was but a dozen-odd paces, with no obstacle left. She summoned all her strength and was first to burst out through the inner gate—and in the very next instant stood stock-still where she was. Behind her, Yao Runeng and Zhang Xiaojing could not check their steps, and all but ran into her back.

The two of them did not ask why she had stopped so suddenly, for the answer already lay before their eyes.

Outside the offices, dozens of Leopard Cavalry came galloping up, a dark, dense mass sweeping on like a storm-cloud, the close and low thunder of hooves beating on the ground. In the very instant the three of them burst out through the inner gate, the Leopard Cavalry too came charging up. These well-drilled horsemen reined in swiftly and ringed the inner gate in a half-circle. Horse-legs stood thick as a forest, long blades were raised on high, and from the rear ranks came the sound of bowstrings drawn taut.

The three of them stood with their backs to the inner gate, not knowing what to do for the best. Were Zhang Xiaojing the reincarnation of a Heavenly King himself, he would have no answer to such an array.

Tanqi trembled all over, her legs barely holding her up. She did not fear sacrifice; but to die at the spot nearest to success was beyond what she could bear. Zhang Xiaojing put out a hand and laid it on her shoulder. This time Tanqi did not shrink away; his palm was burning hot, and its warmth passed on into her body, melting her terror away bit by bit.

“Just now in the cell I spoke too forwardly; I beg the mistress will pardon me.” With the great foe before them, Zhang Xiaojing said this one thing of no consequence at all.

Choosing such a moment to apologize, he left Tanqi for a moment not knowing whether to forgive him or curse him back.

Behind them, Cui Qi and the guards came rushing frantically out of the offices, and, seeing the Leopard Cavalry had blocked Zhang Xiaojing at the gate, were overjoyed past measure. What he feared most was that this dangerous fellow should win his freedom again. Now the Leopard Cavalry were massed, which meant the General had come in person, and the fellow would surely not get away. Hefting a binding-cord, he turned over in his mind how to pin Zhang Xiaojing down fast—but on second thought, might this not steal the General’s thunder? And so, hesitating, he set the binding-cord down and waited to see how things stood.

Just then the horsemen at the middle of the half-circle parted to either side with a rush, and a general of commanding stature and lofty bearing came riding slowly forward, the reins in one hand and a riding-whip in the other, coming on at an unhurried pace until he stopped before the inner gate. Yao Runeng recognized him: it was none other than Gan Shoucheng, general of the Right Xiao Guard.

Gan Shoucheng’s mount was a fine steed from the Western Regions, and from on high he looked down on these three quarry in the jar, giving no order at once to seize them. He toyed with the tip of his whip, his eyes sweeping from these people’s faces to their feet, and then to the inner gate, and of a sudden a few threads of regret showed in them—the regret of one whose quarry slips away in the very instant before the bow is loosed.

The black smoke behind the offices grew ever thicker, yet Gan Shoucheng fell to musing on his horse.

A brief silence fell before the inner gate; no one knew how this general, whose offices had been set ablaze, would deal with these villains, and all waited. At last Gan Shoucheng slowly raised his right hand, his face without expression. The Leopard Cavalry knew the General was about to give an order, and their horses stirred restlessly.

Gan Shoucheng’s hand did not come sweeping down with force, but fanned quickly to either side. It was an order not to be mistaken: make way. The horsemen did not grasp his meaning, but a military order is as a mountain, and they at once opened a passage leading outward.

Neither the three of them—Zhang Xiaojing and his companions—nor Cui Qi could tell what medicine the General was selling out of his gourd. But Gan Shoucheng had no mind to explain; he repeated the gesture once more, then turned his gaze off toward a certain point beyond the imperial city and gave a cold snort.

Yao Runeng was the first to grasp it: that was the watchtower of the Jing’an Bureau nearest to the imperial city.

Waking as from a dream, Zhang Xiaojing helped up the limp and trembling Tanqi and, with Yao Runeng, left along the passage. The horsemen on either side glared like tigers; let the commander but give the word, and they would tear these three villains to shreds. But right up until they had wholly passed from sight, the General made no sign at all.

Cui Qi could scarcely believe his own eyes; he waved his arms about, thinking the General’s order had gone wrong. But however he urged them, the Right Xiao Guard soldiers were unmoved. Cui Qi dropped down on the ground, his face the grey of dead ashes. From this morning on he had gone on making one wrong decision after another, right up to now.

Gan Shoucheng’s gaze rested a moment on this wretched creature, and lightly he gave an order. A start of bewilderment took Cui Qi, and there rose on his face a look that could not be told for joy or for shock.

Wang Yunxiu felt this had been a thoroughly wretched day.

First she had met with a carriage wreck, then been dragged about hither and yon by her captors, and there had even been a savage brute who tried to kill her. Now she was flung like refuse into this filthy woodshed, her two hands bound fast, a wad of hemp rudely thrust into her mouth besides.

Wang Yunxiu had cursed them times beyond counting in her heart. Who under heaven were these damnable worms and dogs? Did they not know I am the daughter of Wang Zhongsi?

Unhappily, it seemed they truly did not. No one came to the woodshed all this while, and she could make no sound, but could only lie there alone on the ground. The floor was very cold, and Wang Yunxiu’s body soon shivered with the chill; her fine, tender wrists were chafed raw and aching by the rope, and the aftereffects of the wreck left her head swimming. She had never suffered such indignity; after struggling a while, spent and exhausted, she turned instead to weeping in silence, and soon her tears too ran dry, so that she could only stare dully at the roof-beam and pray for the nightmare to end and let her wake.

Just as Wang Yunxiu felt herself burnt to the wick, the door-boards sounded, and someone came into the woodshed.

With an effort she lifted her head, and before her was a strange, square face, the forehead large, the complexion fair and the beard short, dressed in an official’s blue robe. Wang Yunxiu remembered that at her own home she had often seen men so dressed coming and going, every one of them bowing and scraping before her father.

A low creature like this, and he dares to affront me? A knot of anger gathered in Wang Yunxiu’s breast. Fixing on the fellow before her as the very author of it all, she made to open her mouth and rail at him in fury—but the wad of hemp stood firm-blocked in her mouth, and her words beyond number all turned to a muffled, garbled moaning.

The man did not come near, but only stared at Wang Yunxiu and studied her a while, and then did a strange thing—he turned and shut the door. Wang Yunxiu’s heart gave a lurch: what did he mean to do?

Yuan Zai shut the door fast, turned back, set his eyes again upon the woman before him, and his mind ran at speed.

He had a native instinct for costly things, and the moment he entered he had marked it: what was stuck to this woman’s cheek was a花钿 of twisted silver. The material of the ornament itself was of no great worth, but to twist fine silver into the very texture of a kingfisher’s plumage was a craft worth, at the least, some tens of bolts of fine damask; and the phoenix-tail nanmu hairpin on her head, plain though its shaping was, had a grain in the wood like so many threads of gold, even and close-set—one glance told it was the finest golden-thread nanmu.

These two things, falling under the common man’s eye, might amount to no more than the two words “worth money.” But under the eye of a connoisseur like Yuan Zai, one could savor, in the fine details, the very air of a house of the first rank.

The daughter of an incense-shop keeper might well be decked in gold and silver, but she could never possibly own ornaments such as these.

Yuan Zai stepped over, put out the thumb and forefinger of his right hand, said “your pardon,” gently parted Wang Yunxiu’s lips, and drew the wad of hemp softly out. In the next instant a voice of the utmost fury rolled from her throat:

“You dog’s carrion! I’ll have my father strike off every one of your dog-heads!”

“Just so...” Yuan Zai said within himself. That air of command, of ordering men about with a jerk of the chin—it was never bred in a commoner’s household. Unhurried and unruffled, he asked: “Might I make bold to ask your father’s name?”

Wang Yunxiu sneered: “The name of a General of the Cloud Banner—are your ears even fit to hear it?”

At this Yuan Zai drew a sharp cold breath. A General of the Cloud Banner was of the junior third rank among the unattached military ranks, the highest grade below the four Grand Generals. In all Chang’an—no, in all the Great Tang—those who could bear the title were not above twenty men, and every one of them a great minister or a personage of note.

Feng Dalun’s men had surely seized the wrong person. Not only the wrong person—they had brought back a hot potato besides. Likely Feng Dalun himself had not yet looked her over, or he would long since have found the fatal blunder.

To dare kidnap the kin of a General of the Cloud Banner—ten Bear Fire Gangs would not be enough to pay with their lives!

Yuan Zai could not help bearing Feng Dalun a little resentment. He commits the great blunder, and drags me into it too! This woman had already fixed it that he was in league with the Bear Fire Gang. By her temper, she was hardly likely to hear any explaining out; and once she was let go back, he feared she would take a mad revenge—and I’ve bloody done nothing at all! A calamity out of a clear sky, this was!

It was as well that Yuan Zai had just now decided on the instant, and, the moment he found her identity in doubt, had shut the door first, leaving himself a thread of room to maneuver.

By common sense, Yuan Zai ought to have hurried to tell Feng Dalun to release her at once and make his apologies... but Yuan Zai saw that this would do him no good. His mind reckoned at speed how to slip clear of this perilous pass—nay, whether there might not be a way to turn his hand about and squeeze some profit out of it.

Yuan Zai was of humble birth, and firmly believed in one maxim: “Fame and rank show themselves only after hardship; wealth and honor are sought in the midst of danger.” The more perilous the pass, the greater the riches—it hung wholly on whether one had the daring to stake it. It was by his utter keenness for opportunity, and his tenacity, that he had come step by step to where he stood today.

Long as these thoughts are in the telling, they turned in Yuan Zai’s mind but an instant. His mind made up, he bent over Wang Yunxiu, darkened his face, and barked low: “Be quiet!”

Wang Yunxiu was struck dumb despite herself. In all her life, never had anyone dared speak to her so. She was on the point of flaring up when Yuan Zai thrust out a domineering hand and covered her mouth: “Do you want to get out alive? Do you want to see your father again?” A start showed in Wang Yunxiu’s eyes, and she quickly nodded. Only then did Yuan Zai loose his hand, his tone grave: “You are at this moment sunk in the utmost peril, and I alone can save you out of it! Do you understand? Say it back to me!”

Wang Yunxiu was in no mind to heed him, and shook her head with all her might. Yuan Zai gave a cold “heh,” rose, and made as if to leave. In fright she hastily cried out: “I’ll say it, I’ll say it!” Yuan Zai came back, looking at her coldly and saying nothing. Fearful of letting this last chance slip, Wang Yunxiu forced out a low repetition: “You alone can save me out of it...” the last note lifting a little, with a touch of doubt in it.

Yuan Zai let out a quiet breath of relief. Wang Yunxiu had the spoiled temper of a great house’s young lady, and could only be met with a still harder tone thrown back at her. That she would repeat his words showed the strategy had begun, at least, to answer.

He took up the wad of hemp between his fingers and stuffed it back into her mouth: “Listen: from here on, what I require is absolute obedience. If you cross me even once, I leave on the spot. If you agree, then nod.”

Wang Yunxiu had no other choice, and could only agree.

“Rest easy; having met me, Yuan Zai, today, you’ll come to no more harm.” Yuan Zai said it with the flat finality of one who cuts nails and slices iron.

Wang Yunxiu’s body ceased its trembling. After so much torment her spirit was near collapse, and to hear such words all at once was nothing short of a music from heaven. In her daze, she felt that this man’s way of speaking was somehow like her father’s—all in the mood of command, hard beyond measure, yet carrying withal a deep solicitude.

Having soothed Wang Yunxiu, Yuan Zai rose and pulled the door open again—and there was Feng Dalun stepping in toward him. Yuan Zai, his face dark, barred his way: “Recorder Feng, a disaster has come upon us both, you and me.”

Feng Dalun started, not knowing whence the words came. Yuan Zai turned half his body aside: “Take a look—is this Wen Ran?” Feng Dalun craned his head to look, and his face changed. The woman lying in the room was not, of all things, the least bit like Wen Ran. Yuan Zai went on: “Look closer still.”

Feng Dalun too was a man used to the sight of luxury, and after a few sweeps of his eye recognized at once the uncommon quality of the silver ornament and the nanmu hairpin, and his face went iron-grey on the instant. Yuan Zai made a sign for him to come out and talk. Feng Dalun hastily backed out and shut the door.

A few of the small rogues edged over, only to be kicked hard to the ground by Feng Dalun, one apiece. These plague-stricken fools had surely lost Wen Ran somewhere along the way, and had kidnapped some house’s womenfolk, heaven knew whose, to make up the number! He was about to demand the particulars when Yuan Zai said coldly at his side: “Recorder Feng, never mind all that for now; we must think how to mend the thing.”

Sweat beaded on Feng Dalun’s forehead, and he explained in a rush: “I’ll go and get to the bottom of it at once, and let her go quickly...”

“If you truly do that, then great ruin will indeed be upon your head.”

Feng Dalun too was a clever man, and needed only a hint from Yuan Zai to grasp at once where the danger lay. The noble womenfolk of Chang’an knew nothing whatever of any way of mercy and forbearance. Let her go with one foot, and with the next their private troops would come surrounding him. Prince Yong was cold and thin by nature, and would lend him no helping hand.

With Zhang Xiaojing at large before him and a great house glaring like a tiger behind, Feng Dalun felt this day was truly a wretched one.

“What if... we silence her for good?” The possibility struck Feng Dalun of a sudden, and he blurted it out. Yuan Zai gave him a pitying glance: this gang boss wore, for all that, a ninth-rank seal at his waist—how was it his every notion ran in a bandit’s grooves?

He patted Feng Dalun’s shoulder: “Brother Feng, be not so rash; silencing her is out of the question altogether. Your servant has thought of a plan to kill two birds with one stone: it can both do away with that Zhang Xiaojing, and so fulfill your heart’s wish, and send this hot potato smoothly on its way besides, with no aftertrouble at all.” Having said it, he narrowed his eyes, with an air past all fathoming.

Yuan Zai had already reckoned it out clear: he must grip this chance fast and play a great game. Played well, it would prove the largest opening of his official career thus far.

Feng Dalun clutched at the life-saving straw, overjoyed past measure: “Brother Yuan, I beg you, instruct me!” Yuan Zai said: “If we are to work this plan, you must tell me the truth of that case of Zhang Xiaojing’s last year, the whole of it from first to last, and hide from me not the least jot.”

“Er... and can Brother Yuan warrant it will go without the least slip?”

“You’ll not be disappointed.” Yuan Zai smiled, and his laugh was full of confidence.

Feng Dalun did not mark that Yuan Zai had never said whose disappointment it was.

When the three of them—Zhang Xiaojing, Tanqi, and Yao Runeng—had left the imperial city, they hastened at once back to Guangde Ward. Each was full of unspoken doubts, and all the way there they did not exchange a word.

The lantern festival was near now, and the air of the streets was thick with it. At the Guangde-Huaiyuan corner, the scene of the recent clash had been swept clean away, and was now given over to a few Kucha players, the harp pitched high and the pipa ringing clear, with a great crowd of onlookers gathered round, singing and dancing. The disturbance a little while before had only briefly broken in on the residents’ good spirits—like a drop of ink fallen into water, thinned away to nothing in an instant, and leaving no trace.

They passed through the throng and came to the gate of Guangde Ward, where they found Xu Bin leaning against the flag-pole beside it, peering their way. The moment Xu Bin saw Zhang Xiaojing, he was seized with an unspeakable joy, rushed over and caught him by the arm, the very creases of his face all but shaking loose with excitement.

The stir of their leaving the imperial city had plainly been relayed back to the Jing’an Bureau by the watchtowers, and Xu Bin had run out at the first moment to meet his old friend.

Zhang Xiaojing clapped his good friend hard on the shoulder with both hands: “Old Xu, you might have waited in the Bureau; why come to greet us at the ward gate?” Xu Bin put up a forefinger and made a sign at his lips: “Hush—I came on purpose to wait for you; ai, ai, come with me.”

By his air of mystery, it seemed there was some secret matter to be discussed. Yao Runeng said: “Then I’ll help Mistress Tanqi back to the Bureau first, and leave you to talk in private.” Xu Bin shook his head: “You two go along as well... ai, ai!” Realizing he had misspoken, he clapped himself on the head, hastily hushed, and urged them to hurry along.

Halfway along, Zhang Xiaojing caught him by the sleeve: “Youde, tell me first—has Wang Yunxiu been found?” He had kept Wen Ran in mind all along; by a cross-grained mischance she had been seized off by the Türks as Wang Yunxiu, and was still nowhere to be found. Xu Bin shook his head, and said the Deputy Director Li had made it the first priority, and the Jing’an Bureau had set a great body of officers to searching, but there was as yet no good news at all.

“But there’s no bad news either—no one has found a body.” It was all the comfort Xu Bin could offer.

Within Guangde Ward, besides the offices of the Jingzhao Prefecture, there stood temples too—the Cibei Temple, the Changfa Temple, the Shengguang Temple—set about the four corners of the ward, so that one might say the Buddha’s law wreathed the place. Xu Bin led them winding this way and that, and came at last, going round, to the Cibei Temple at the northeast of the crossroads.

This Cibei Temple had a history of some note. In the last years of the Sui, a monk from the Western Regions named Tanxian had relieved the poor here day by day. Afterward, when Emperor Gaozu founded the dynasty, moved by his good works, he raised this temple for him, and named it “Cibei,” Compassion. And so the gates of the Cibei Temple stood open the year round, and at every festival gruel and food were doled out, and beggared and destitute folk were often gathered at its door.

Today, being the Lantern Festival, the Cibei Temple was as usual handing out plain-oil fritters at its gate. These were an indispensable little treat of the Lantern Festival in Chang’an: wet dough rolled into balls and fried in oil, richly fragrant. Many residents had come early to wait here, and a few guest-prefect monks stood on the steps keeping order, not for the moment letting sightseers into the temple. The monk at their head, seeing Xu Bin, intoned a Buddha’s name and, asking nothing at all, let him straight through. Something stirred in Zhang Xiaojing’s mind: it seemed Xu Bin had made ready beforehand, and this was no notion of the moment.

They passed through the temple gate, went by the bell-tower and the drum-tower, and rounded the west side of the great hall of the Buddha to a side courtyard. Beside a lotus mercy-release pond linked to the transport canal stood a mean thatched hut of a meditation cloister. Behind the hut pagoda-trees rose thick, secluded and still, and beyond the pagoda-tree wood one could dimly make out a low wall of grey brick.

Zhang Xiaojing reckoned up the bearings and found that the other side of this wall should be just where the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau stood. The Jing’an Bureau used Sun Simiao’s old residence, which stood, as it happened, but a single wall from the Cibei Temple.

This was strange indeed; going round so great a circle, whatever was Xu Bin about?

Xu Bin gave no explanation, but only, bent low, kept urging them to walk faster. As they neared the thatched hut, they saw a man standing by the mercy-release pond, his hands clasped behind his back.

“Young master.”

The first to cry out was Tanqi. Her breast full of grievance, her eyes grew moist. But she soon checked her tears, and found to her astonishment that in the short half a double-hour since they had parted, Li Bi seemed a changed man: his face pale, his eyes shot through with red, and several more furrows between his brows, as though carved there with a knife, both deep and long.

This look of his might be matched, perhaps, only by Wu Zixu, whose hair went white with care in a single night. Tanqi knew her young master was under great strain, but what manner of strain could turn him so fast into this? Her heart ached, and she was about to speak when Li Bi raised a hand, signing her to hold her tongue for now, and turned his gaze to Zhang Xiaojing:

“How came Gan Shoucheng to let you go?”

Zhang Xiaojing described the scene for him, and Li Bi narrowed his eyes: “Commander Zhang, you are the Five-Faced Yama indeed—you’d dare set even the Right Xiao Guard ablaze at a stroke.”

Zhang Xiaojing gave a slight smile: “I have not repaid one part in ten thousand of the court’s kindness to your servant.”

Tanqi’s face changed; the lecher’s words came near to treason. She looked to her young master, but Li Bi gave no reaction at all, and only waved a hand, signing them all into the thatched hut. It seemed to Tanqi that her young master’s edge had somewhat scattered, that he was listless and spent, as though he had just come through some most grievous ordeal.

In the hut were only a sitting-couch and a few rush mats, with several Buddhist scriptures laid on a rattan rack. In the very middle of the hut stood a three-tiered water-clock, plainly just moved in, which happened to screen off a Locana image behind it.

The several of them knelt and settled, and none spoke; each waited for Li Bi’s explanation.

Li Bi stood outside the window with his hands behind him, keeping his face turned deliberately from the others’ sight: “I found Gan Shoucheng a while ago, and made a wager with him. Should you still be within the inner gate when he rode back to the offices, then he might do with you as he pleased; but should you have already passed the inner gate—though it were but a single step out—then he might make no pursuit of it whatsoever.”

Zhang Xiaojing understood: this still turned on that document of detention. Since the writ had named no clear name for the prisoner, it became a two-edged blade—the Right Xiao Guard had seized a man and could disown it; but if the man got away, neither could they go after him.

The dividing line in it fell exactly at the inner gate of the Right Xiao Guard. Within the inner gate, the offices’ word was law; beyond the inner gate, it was no concern of the offices at all.

But Gan Shoucheng was not an easy man to deal with; since he meant to curry favor with Li Linfu, how should he be willing to make such a wager with the Jing’an Bureau at all?

“How did you persuade him?” Zhang Xiaojing asked.

Li Bi looked out the window and heaved a long sigh: “It was not I. It was Director He.”

Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his single eye: “Eh? He agreed to help, then?”

Li Bi said: “I went a while ago to call on Director He. When Director He heard that the Right Xiao Guard had of its own accord detained a meritorious officer, he was so angered that his malady seized him and he lost consciousness on the spot. His adopted son He Dong and I went to demand an accounting of Gan Shoucheng.”

He gave a brief account of his meeting with Director He, and all present were struck with alarm. Director He was already eighty-six; angered so, he would very likely, eight parts in ten, not keep his life.

But on further thought—unseemly though it was to put it so—He Zhizhang’s falling ill was of more effect than his coming forward in person could have been. For the Son of Heaven had, but ten days before, set out a canopy on purpose to see the old man off, so deep was the imperial favor. Should the Son of Heaven hear that He Zhizhang had been vexed to death, alive and whole, by Gan Shoucheng’s rashness, and let fall a wrath of thunder, a mere general of the Right Xiao Guard could never bear the brunt of it.

Gan Shoucheng bore Zhang Xiaojing no deep grudge; he had only been doing the Minister Li a favor. For so small a gain he had no wish to shoulder the blame for He Zhizhang’s death. And so, under the press of Li Bi’s overbearing force, with He Zhizhang’s son aiding beside him, Gan Shoucheng at last, unwilling, made his concession.

Simple as the thing was in the telling, the scheming and the crossing of wits in it had cost no little wear of spirit.

Li Bi’s fingers gripped the hem of his robe, and he murmured one abrupt sentence: “From of old, Mount Hua has had but one road.”

Hearing this, Tanqi and Yao Runeng could not but press their breasts and sigh. They had ventured, at their peril, into the offices, and had made ready to be alone and without aid; and all the while, it turned out, Li Bi too had been running about outside, and had never given up. Both sides had strained to the utmost, and only so, as by a miracle, had Zhang Xiaojing been fished out.

But why could Zhang Xiaojing not return to the Jing’an Bureau?

Li Bi clicked his tongue and showed a face of contempt: “Gan Shoucheng, having swallowed this reverse, is none too resigned to it. He has put it about that Zhang Xiaojing you are not to appear openly at the Jing’an Bureau, or he’ll detain you again on the charge of an imperially-named criminal—petty-minded indeed. So I could only find the abbot of the Cibei Temple and get this thatched hut a single wall from the Jing’an Bureau, and Xu Bin will see to the liaison between the two sides for the time being.”

“Commander Zhang will have little chance to stay in the hut in any case; let it pass for humoring General Gan out of his pique.” Yao Runeng, stroking his rush mat, said it sarcastically.

At the thought of a full general of the Right Xiao Guard playing the rogue like a child to save his face, they all laughed, and the atmosphere eased a little at last.

Zhang Xiaojing did not laugh; his elbow on his knee and his chin in his hand, he had fallen deep into thought.

He was not thinking of the Türks, but of Li Bi.

In his time as buliang chief Zhang Xiaojing had handled too many cases, and heard too many depositions. In this account of Li Bi’s there were a great many things that jarred and contradicted.

He Zhizhang had all along opposed the use of Zhang Xiaojing; how should he be so angered by this affair as to swoon away? At the time there had been only Li Bi and He Zhizhang in the room; He Zhizhang’s malady had seized him suddenly, and then Li Bi had come out and declared that the Right Xiao Guard had vexed the old man into it—from first to last, the whole story rested on Li Bi’s word alone.

What was the true cause of He Zhizhang’s collapse? What had really happened in that room?

From of old, Mount Hua has but one road; if you would go up it, you must be resolved to clear away every obstacle. What did that mean?

Zhang Xiaojing gazed into Li Bi’s blood-shot eyes, and realized all at once that he was not, after all, working a case, and that there were things it did no good to make too plain. So Zhang Xiaojing cupped his hands: “Deputy Director Li once said he would stop the Türks whatever the cost; and true enough, his word once given is kept.”

Li Bi caught the meaning beyond his words, and, without explaining further, asked back mildly: “And is Commander Zhang, I wonder, still as he promised at the first?”

“Of course—else I’d not have come back.” Zhang Xiaojing said: “The court is the court, and the common people are the common people.”

The two men looked at each other, and each saw something in the other’s eyes, understood without a word said. Beyond the meditation court a bird’s cry suddenly rose, and the two of them broke at once into a wry, rueful laugh.

“Enough; let the idle talk end here. We’ve wasted half a double-hour on fools already; let us to the real business.” Li Bi rapped the couch-edge, and the others hastily straightened themselves.

He laid out for Zhang Xiaojing the whole of his doubt as to the number of the fierce-fire thunder. Zhang Xiaojing nodded: “Great minds run alike. When I crawled out of the river, I meant from the first to put this very point to the Deputy Director—judging by the scale of the depot, the number of fierce-fire thunder the Türks hold is not too great but too small. They must have some larger plan besides, and it is being carried out even now.”

Li Bi glanced at Xu Bin, who hastily rose and said: “Ai, ai, the people in the streets today are truly too many; near the two markets, the East and the West, alone there are several hundred beast-drawn and hand-drawn carts, and the number of carts in all the city’s streets is no fewer than ten thousand. By the watchtowers alone, it is flatly impossible to trace the flatbed carts the Türks use to haul the fierce-fire thunder. And now we’ve been held up... ai, held up by the Right Xiao Guard more than half a double-hour; I fear—I fear it has already been carried to wherever it is they want it.”

“I have a notion—has the Deputy Director marked it, I wonder?” Zhang Xiaojing’s voice grew grave. “I have all along a feeling that behind the Türk Wolf Guards there are others yet.”

“Is that not a matter of course? The khagan on the steppe—as if you need tell us!” There being few in the hut, Tanqi too grew bolder.

But Zhang Xiaojing shook his head: “No—I mean within this city of Chang’an.” He drew a few strokes with his finger in the dust before his rush mat: “Think on it: the Türk Wolf Guards went to Cui Liulang for a ward-map of Chang’an, because they were not familiar with Chang’an—is that not so?”

Li Bi’s face was grave, and he said nothing, but his hand tapped, stroke by stroke, at the couch-edge.

“But let us think back over the whole course of the pursuit. The Türk Wolf Guards had already planted a great many hands beforehand—there were the safe-houses, there was the depot for their mustering, and they could reach the out-of-town haulage-porters besides. To say nothing else, the mere choosing of that disused depot in Changming Ward showed a fine eye: the site secluded, no great way from the busy quarter, and with two entrances, handy for moving bulk goods unseen. A man with such an eye must be most familiar with Chang’an—what need would he still have to go looking for a ward-map?”

Yao Runeng ventured a guess: “Perhaps they wished to have the plan carried out with more precision?”

“If the Türk Wolf Guards meant only to raise a chaos in the city with the fierce-fire thunder, why, the thriving quarters of Chang’an are but a dozen-odd wards; where’s the need of any ward-map? Drive the carts and ram north, and there’s an end of it.” Zhang Xiaojing took up a cup of clear water and drank it off at a draught.

Yao Runeng thought a moment; it was true enough. The force of the fierce-fire thunder was too great; there was no need to place it just so—fling it anywhere and it took out a whole stretch.

“The whole plan of the Türk Wolf Guards gives me a strong feeling that it is made up of two sorts of men, utterly unlike in style: one sort most familiar with the city of Chang’an, with wide connections, able even to plant an inside man in the Zoroastrian temple of Huaiyuan Ward half a year in advance; and another sort wholly strange to Chang’an, forced to turn at the last to a ward-map, and mounting a rushed and hurried assault.”

After a slight pause, Zhang Xiaojing put up a single finger: “To put it simply, in one sentence: the Türks are no more than a down-at-heel house of the steppe; where would they get the power, on their own, to cross a thousand li and come to Chang’an and mount so intricate an assault?”

Hearing this, Li Bi’s gaze grew suddenly sharp, and following Zhang Xiaojing’s thread, a fearful inference rose in his mind: “Then your conclusion, Commander Zhang, is that someone is helping them?”

Zhang Xiaojing set the cup down hard on the ground and said with a wry smile: “I fear... that besides the Wolf Guards, we have to face a stronger enemy yet, one most familiar with Chang’an, to whom the Türk Wolf Guards are only a blade, a chess-piece.”

When this sentence was out, a fearful stillness fell over the hut. One could hear how every man’s breathing had grown heavy. So the Türk Wolf Guards were, of all things, only a beginning? And there was a stronger enemy still? The news was enough to make all their eyes go dark.

Li Bi had marked something of this before, but had not thought it out so far as Zhang Xiaojing. The more he thought, the more reasonable it seemed, and the more reasonable, the more it chilled him. What manner of enemy could it be, that would work through the Türks’ hands to destroy the city of Chang’an? The Great Tang had enemies many, but one so savage and so cunning at once was rare as phoenix-plumes and unicorn-horns.

A thread of regret even flashed through Li Bi’s mind. Were Director He still by, then with his experience of the court he might well have seen more in it. He shook his head at himself in self-mockery and drove the tangled thoughts away: “Xu Bin, is there any progress now?”

Xu Bin wrestled with it a good while, and in the end brought out but one word: “None...”

Since the annihilation of the Türk Wolf Guards, most reckoned the great matter settled. Save for Wang Yunxiu, the other inquiries were the routine tying-off of loose ends; the investigators would not much bestir themselves, still less turn up any clue of worth.

Li Bi was about to give the order to press them to search again, but Zhang Xiaojing stopped him: “It’s no use. If it is that mysterious enemy, he will have left us no trace to follow at all.”

Somewhat vexed, Li Bi rose and paced back and forth in the hut. With such labor they had done away with the Türk Wolf Guards, and now a mysterious foe cropped up. Now they knew for a certainty that he lurked in the very heart of Chang’an, and yet there was no trace of him at all. He was like a lizard that had cast off the Wolf Guards as a tail and slipped straight away into a deep, deep fog.

“No clue? Then force a clue out! Set everyone to searching with all their might! When the Türk Wolf Guards fled the West Market before, did we not later find a trail all the same?” Li Bi barked at Xu Bin; having paid so dear a price, he could by no means give up here.

Xu Bin wiped the sweat from his brow and went through the documents at hand once more, trying to find in them some slightly better news. He looked a good while, then with an effort raised his head: “There’s just one thing... ai, ai, that might barely pass for a clue... we’ve caught Cao Poyan.”

Zhang Xiaojing, beside him, started. He remembered that in the clash at Changming Ward he had run Cao Poyan through with his own hand; how had the man come back to life?

Li Bi was overjoyed at first—this Cao Poyan was a man of weight among the Wolf Guards, and must know something; then he grew angry: they had taken so important a man, and why had Xu Bin not reported it sooner? Xu Bin brought his eyes close to the document, looked a few times, and raised his head with a wry smile: “Ai, ai, the reason it wasn’t reported is that when we found him he was already gravely wounded and at his last gasp, of no worth for questioning.”

To hope that a Wolf Guard would speak of his own will was hard indeed. And Cao Poyan besides was at his last breath, so that hard torture could not be used on him. Small wonder the Jing’an Bureau had not counted this a thing of worth.

“Then let me go and question him once.” Zhang Xiaojing worked his fingers, letting a killing air brim out of him freely. Li Bi said doubtfully: “In his state he could never bear your Five-Faced Yama’s methods.”

“To pry open a man’s mouth does not always call for force.” Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye narrowed. “Besides, this is our only chance. Time—there is little of it left now.”

No sooner had his words fallen than a clear, crisp sound came from the water-clock in the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau just over the wall. At once the great bell of the Cibei Temple boomed out too, and from near to far the drums and bells of the several wards rang out in their turn, grand and deep and far-carrying, resounding over the whole of Chang’an. A myriad thousand lanterns raised their candles all at once, and the darkening sky brightened again, radiant and glittering—trees of fire and flowers of silver.

The hour of the Rooster had come, and the grandest Lantern Festival of Chang’an’s whole year had begun.
