### Deep into the Tiger's Den

### The Investigation Section and Xu Enzeng

The adversary the "Three Heroes of Longtan" worked against was the Party Affairs Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, and its chief, Xu Enzeng.

After Gu Shunzhang was arrested and turned traitor, he wrote a manual for training Kuomintang agents, The Theory and Practice of Secret Service Work, in which he said: "The crudeness of China's secret-service work no one can deny, and the reason is simply that its history is too shallow. ... Secret-service work in China arose only after the Nationalist-Communist split of 1927; but it began with the C.P., and was formidable indeed, thoroughly conversant with the military, political, and social conditions of the whole country. The Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters, by contrast, had paid secret-service work no mind before; only lately, pressed by the times, has it formally set about it. Yet in these founding days everything is very rough, and trained men are especially scarce. ..."

Back in the Northern Expedition years the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, then seated in Guangzhou, already had a Party Affairs Investigation Section, given over to gathering and compiling every kind of material on Party members. After the move to Nanjing the section took on a further, secret-service task: investigating members' thinking and factional loyalties. Even so it still looked only inward, into the Kuomintang itself. Then, in the autumn of 1927, Chiang Kai-shek stepped down for a time and went to Japan, and the Nanjing government fell into the grip of a "Central Special Committee" got up by the Guangxi clique's Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi together with He Yingqin and Li Liejun. Chiang's own man Chen Guofu, under the banner of opposing the "Special Committee," founded a "Central Club" — in English, Central Club, abbreviated C.C. — drew in the pro-Chiang forces, and built the innermost core of the C.C., squeezing out and holding down the other Kuomintang factions one by one. That December the "Special Committee" fell; Chiang came back from Japan and resumed his posts, and as C.C. stalwarts took up official appointments across the country, the section's leadership passed to Chen Guofu and his younger brother Chen Lifu. It became a vast factional machine the two brothers ran at will, and gave rise to the saying, "the Chiangs own the country, the Chens own the Party."

In January 1928 Chiang Kai-shek won his contest for the leadership of the counter-revolution. At the Fourth Plenum of the Second Central Executive Committee, convened that February, he reorganized the Kuomintang Central Committee, the National Government, and the Military Council, seized the whole of party, government, and military power, and set up a dictatorship. Chiang again took the post of commander-in-chief of the Kuomintang armies, made himself chairman of the National Government, and held the office of the party's Central Organization Department head as well, with Chen Guofu as his deputy and Chen Lifu as his confidential secretary. Out of all this maneuvering Chiang drew a lesson: to hold absolute power for good and secure his reactionary rule, it was not enough to suppress the Communists and the people harshly; he had also to strike at the Kuomintang's rival factions, and for that he had to build up secret-service work and clear out those who stood in his way. So within the Central Organization Department he set up, afresh, a Party Affairs Investigation Section with an entirely different mission: to use party investigation as cover for secret-service work, to put down the people's resistance, to slaughter members of the Chinese Communist Party and other revolutionaries, and to purge the Kuomintang's own rival factions. This was the forerunner of the Kuomintang's Central Bureau of Investigation and Statistics — the "Zhongtong" — and the first secret-service organization the Kuomintang ever established.

The section's first head was Chiang's confidant Chen Lifu. It had two subsections, Collection and Compilation, run respectively by Yang Jianhong and Wu Dajun. Once Chen Lifu rose to be chief secretary of the Central Party Headquarters he no longer held the section chief's post himself; but this great arm of the Kuomintang secret service stayed firmly in his hands throughout. Chiang Kai-shek, needing to purge rivals within his party and to fight the Communists, the Soviet Union, and the people, kept a personal hand on secret-service work from the first. Zhang Wen (Zhang Guodong), once head of the First Group of the Zhongtong, recalled: "After the anti-Communist massacre of April 12, 1927, Chiang Kai-shek, to consolidate his fascist rule, decided to make Chen Lifu chief of the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, and moved the whole apparatus of intrigue from the General Headquarters' Confidential Section over to the Investigation Section, turning it into an organ for strong secret-service control over party, government, and army. From the very start Chiang took pains to nurture the Investigation Section and push its growth. In June 1928 he picked, from the graduates of the Kuomintang Central Party Affairs School, ten students judged the most loyal and reliable — I was one of them — and sent us to work in the Investigation Section; in 1930 he chose a dozen or more graduates of the sixth class of the Central Military Academy who had been trained as 'political police' and sent them to the section as well. These two batches later became the hard core of the Zhongtong. Chiang kept raising the section's operating funds, too: whatever men it wanted it got, whatever money it wanted it got — his favor was boundless. And so the Investigation Section's secret-service apparatus grew, little by little. ..."

After Chen Lifu, the Investigation Section's second chief was Zhang Daofan; when he was promoted to secretary of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, the third chief was Wu Dajun. Wu held the post half a year before rising to head the Central Statistics Office, and Ye Xiufeng took over from him. In December 1929 Xu Enzeng replaced Ye Xiufeng as the section's fifth chief, and he ran this secret-service body for fifteen years without a break.

Xu Enzeng, courtesy name Kejun, was born in 1898 in the town of Shuanglin, Wuxing county, into a family of great landlords and great capitalists. He was distantly related to the brothers Chen Guofu, born in 1892, and Chen Lifu, born in 1899 — their "elder cousin" and "younger cousin" — and he had been a fellow student of that "younger cousin," Chen Lifu, in America; the well-known Zhejiang financier Xu Xinliu was a close kinsman of his. These were the men who would later back Xu Enzeng's rise, and his lifelong good fortune in office was inseparable from the Chen brothers.

Xu Enzeng's father died young, and the four brothers were raised by their mother alone.

Xu Enzeng was the third son. His eldest brother, Xu Enyuan, was an official under the Northern Warlords and the family's financial mainstay, paying for the three younger brothers' schooling; he died in 1932. Xu Enzeng's kinsman Xu Xinliu, after graduating from Nanyang College, studied in Britain and France, and on his return served as secretary to Liang Qichao, finance minister of the Beiyang government, before becoming a noted banker. This man had a certain influence on Xu Enzeng.

Xu Enzeng finished primary school in Shuanglin, and in 1917, after graduating from Zhejiang Provincial Huzhou Middle School, entered the electrical engineering department of Nanyang College in Shanghai. He graduated from Nanyang in 1921, and in 1922 went to the United States on a government scholarship. He interned first at the Westinghouse works, then studied factory management at the Carnegie Institute of Technology, taking his master's degree in 1925. During his American years he joined an association of Chinese students in America called the "Jian Society," founded and led by Chen Lifu and Zeng Yangfu. Chen Lifu later became head of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, and Zeng Yangfu communications minister in the National Government — the two of them big men of the C.C. clique. It was precisely through the patronage of Chen Lifu and Zeng Yangfu that Xu Enzeng rose, in turn, to be chief of the Central Organization Department's Investigation Section and vice-minister of communications.

In 1925, back in China, Xu Enzeng took a post as engineer at the Shanghai South City Waterworks. In April 1927, after the Kuomintang's Nanjing government was formed, it decided to build a Central Broadcasting Station. On the recommendation of Chen Guofu, Zhang Jingjiang, and others, Xu Enzeng joined the station's construction, while also serving as head of the business subsection of the Construction Commission's radio engineering office. That August, when the Central Broadcasting Station went on the air, Chen Guofu put his name forward, and the Kuomintang's propaganda minister Ye Chucang formally appointed Xu Enzeng the station's first director. In building the station Xu Enzeng first set foot across the threshold of the Kuomintang's C.C. clique, and became a hound of the House of Chiang.

Shanghai had an International Radio Administration, originally a government commercial body under the Construction Commission. In the latter half of 1928 Chen Lifu, then the commission's secretary-general, reached his hand into this administration to widen his private power and plant his followers, and installed there his kinsman and confidant Xu Enzeng as its chief. Xu Enzeng set up a radio training class, and it was at this time that Qian Zhuangfei came into the administration. At the end of that year Xu Enzeng was further appointed chief of the Shanghai Radio Administration Bureau. In 1929 he was transferred for a time to head the Zhejiang provincial Construction Department, and he took along to Hangzhou the family of Qian Zhuangfei, who was by then secretary of the Shanghai Radio Administration Bureau. Thanks to Qian Zhuangfei's diligent planning, the grand West Lake Exposition the Zhejiang Construction Department mounted was a fair success and won praise on all sides. This has been recounted earlier.

Afterward Xu Enzeng moved into the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters as chief of the general affairs subsection of the secretariat. In December 1929 Ye Xiufeng, the head of the Party Affairs Investigation Section, fell out with Chen Lifu, sparred with him, and in a fit of pique took leave to "rest" in Hangzhou. His going was abrupt, but he did not formally resign — he simply "overstayed his leave" — and the nature of the Investigation Section was such that it could not go a single day without a head. Ye Xiufeng meant to use this to squeeze a concession out of Chen Lifu; Chen Lifu flew into a rage and ordered Xu Enzeng, who had long coveted Ye's post, to stand in as acting head of the Party Affairs Investigation Section in his capacity as general-affairs chief, taking temporary charge of the section's work. Before long Chen Guofu sent Ye Xiufeng off to a post in the Kuomintang's Zhejiang provincial party office, and in the winter of 1929 Chen Lifu had Xu Enzeng formally resign as general-affairs chief of the secretariat and devote himself to the office of chief of the Central Organization Department's Party Affairs Investigation Section.

From that point Xu Enzeng ruled this secret-service arm of the Kuomintang for fifteen years. In 1936 the body was reorganized from the "Party Affairs Investigation Section" into the "Party Affairs Investigation Office," and in 1938 expanded into the "Investigation and Statistics Bureau of the Kuomintang Central Executive Committee" — the notorious "Zhongtong." Its personnel grew from a dozen or two at the outset to a sprawling, tangled machine of two hundred thousand men. Its work reached into every crack and every corner, a plague upon all China that spread even overseas. The Zhongtong coexisted with the later-founded Juntong, and "Zhongtong kingpin" Xu Enzeng vied with "Juntong demon" Dai Li for supremacy. But that is a story for later.

### Zhou Enlai: "Bring It Over to Us"

By the time Xu Enzeng took over as chief of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department's Party Affairs Investigation Section, the section's targets had long since ceased to be mere factional and party investigation within the Kuomintang. The Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army movement in the Soviet areas had by now become a major force in the country's politics, and the Chinese Communist Party's organizations in Kuomintang-held territory had recovered a good deal of ground. Chiang Kai-shek meant to wipe out the Red Army at a stroke and to sabotage the Communist organizations in the White areas root and branch, and he looked to his secret-service chief Chen Lifu to lend him a hand. Chen Lifu, for his part, saw a chance to enlarge his own power, and set about turning this Party Affairs Investigation Section into a secret-service organ for clandestine surveillance and interrogation. Chen Lifu had founded the body, but he was still a novice at this trade, so he handed it to his confidant Xu Enzeng to run; and Xu Enzeng, greener still, in turn brought in Qian Zhuangfei to plan it.

By this time Qian Zhuangfei had already won Xu Enzeng's deep trust. Through his arrangements Li Kenong had become a broadcast-news editor at the Shanghai Radio Administration Bureau. Near the end of 1929 Xu Enzeng told Qian Zhuangfei that he was to head the Party Affairs Investigation Section and set about expanding the secret-service organization, and asked for Qian's help. Xu Enzeng also let slip that he and Chen Lifu meant to "soften up" the Communists' waverers and use turncoats as their spies, planting them inside the Communist Party for clandestine work. In other words, on the foundation of the Investigation Section the enemy meant to build an anti-Communist secret-service organ to crush revolutionary activity.

What to do? Qian Zhuangfei and Li Kenong reported the situation to the Party Center in good time. At the time the Central Special Committee held two views on whether to break into the Kuomintang secret service: one held that our comrades should be sent in, driving into the heart of the enemy to fight him there; the other opposed breaking in. The Central Special Committee's Zhou Enlai gave the word: "Bring it over to us!" And so the Special Section decided to send Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di into the enemy's highest secret-service organization. Li Kenong and Qian Zhuangfei were still in Shanghai at the time, and their Party organizational ties thereafter passed to Chen Geng.

The moment Xu Enzeng took up his post in Nanjing he set about swelling the ranks, raising the budget, adding new offices, strengthening the interior, and sending agents into the Kuomintang's military and police organs. What Xu Enzeng cared about most just then was how to make full use of Yang Dengying in Shanghai, and how to set up grassroots secret organs for surveillance across the reach of Chiang Kai-shek's power — the towns along the Yangtze, for instance. To make it easier to direct secret-service work nationwide, he charged Qian Zhuangfei, under the title of confidential secretary, with building a secret command organ in Nanjing, setting up a clandestine radio station, and establishing grassroots branches around the country. Chen Lifu and Xu Enzeng insisted again and again that besides gathering intelligence on the Communists, the men were to collect intelligence on the other parties too. Acting on the Central Special Section's instructions, the "Three Heroes of Longtan" thus went on to "help" the Kuomintang build out an ever-wider intelligence network across the whole country.

From then on Qian Zhuangfei held the Kuomintang's supreme secret-service command organ nationwide; Li Kenong held the intelligence at the Shanghai Radio Administration Bureau; and Hu Di was sent to Tianjin to hold the North. Hu Di first ran the "Minzhi News Agency" in Nanjing, and was later posted to Tianjin to found the "Great Wall News Agency." To strengthen the Party's leadership, the Central Special Section had Chen Geng keep in regular contact with Li Kenong; whatever came up in Nanjing, Li Kenong relayed promptly to Chen Geng. Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di, the three of them, formed a Party cell, with Li Kenong as its head; major questions in the work were decided by the cell and carried out separately. At the time the Sanming Photo Studio on Yunhai Road was an important secret Party base, where Chen Geng often operated in the guise of the studio's proprietor; whenever Li Kenong got hold of important intelligence, he came here to make contact with Chen. To get the intelligence to the Party Center in good time, the organization also assigned Song Zhijia — one of Li Kenong's students at Minsheng Middle School in Wuhu, who took the name Song Jiren after coming to Shanghai — as Li Kenong's underground courier. Song Zhijia brought Li Kenong his meals in the guise of a "servant." Everyone in the bureau knew this Director Li put on great airs and had a fierce temper, forever dressing Song Zhijia down for bringing the food late or ill-cooked. And it was under cover of that scolding that the intelligence passed, without a soul the wiser.

Many years later Song Zhijia (Song Jiren) recalled those days: "In 1929 Li Kenong broke into the Kuomintang secret service. He pretended to have a stomach ailment, and I brought his meals every day in the guise of a 'servant.' He was always putting on the master's manner, cursing me at the drop of a hat for cooking the rice too hard or the dishes too salty, as though nothing ever satisfied him. I would play the meek, cringing wretch. And in just this atmosphere the intelligence passed smoothly. When we got back to our quarters and shut the door, we would often double over laughing at the day's performance."

Song Jiren also spoke of how he carried intelligence for Li Kenong in 1931, when he lived in Yongyu Lane on Baoding Road in Shanghai:

"Li Kenong would have me take the documents he had obtained to a spot between Zhengjiamu Bridge and Sanyangjing Bridge, where someone else would take them from my hands. And how did Li's documents reach my hands? I made use of the chance to bring him his lunch each day, carrying the documents back in the meal box. If there was intelligence, Li would give me a wink as he handed the box back. I used an aluminum pot to carry the food, because if I had used an ordinary meal box a beggar might stop me and dump out the leftovers — we called that 'breaking the line' — and that would have ruined everything. In those days the Party's intelligence system had a contact point at the 'China Park' by Sichuan Road Bridge. Following orders, I would keep watch in the park's pavilion for the person coming to meet me. There was another contact point at the market on Tiema Road, today's Pengze Road. Whenever the appointed contact time came, rain or wind, bitter cold or blazing sun, I had to keep the appointment and hold my post. I remember one time the Party Center decided to hold a meeting in Shanghai, and the Kuomintang got wind of it and prepared to break it up. Li Kenong got hold of this important intelligence and reported it to the Center through me at once; the meeting was called off, and a loss was averted."

The Party Affairs Investigation Section's offices had originally been inside the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters at Dingjia Bridge in Nanjing. Xu Enzeng found the place too disorderly to keep secrets, so he chose a two-story Western-style building at No. 5 Zhongshan East Road as the secret organ's "headquarters." There were not many people here; Qian Zhuangfei staffed it with only a few confidential men, who on the one hand ran the radio station and doubled as secretaries and accountants, and on the other served as the secret couriers linking him and Li Kenong — among them Qian Zhuangfei's son-in-law, Liu Qifu. Xu Enzeng was a wastrel of a gilded youth who whiled away his days in dance halls and brothels. He shoved everything onto Qian Zhuangfei. After the Investigation Section moved to the new address, every document and telegram addressed to Xu Enzeng was forwarded here by the Central Party Headquarters. The first to see these papers was Qian Zhuangfei. After he had gone through them and proposed how each should be handled, they went to Xu Enzeng, who merely signed his name and handed them back to Qian Zhuangfei to see to. Because Qian Zhuangfei's working conditions were so good, and because he coordinated with Li Kenong and Hu Di, the three gathered a great deal of Kuomintang intelligence — military, political, and secret-service — that let our Party Center grasp the Kuomintang's every move. The intelligence work of this period was done superbly.

After the Kuomintang's Third National Congress, Chen Lifu became secretary-general of the Central Executive Committee and the Central Political Council. From his privileged position as secretary-general, he passed along to Xu Enzeng the orders and troop dispositions for Chiang Kai-shek's First Encirclement Campaign against the Jiangxi Soviet, planned for September 1930, and the Second, launched in February 1931 — all the important documents. Qian Zhuangfei obtained from Xu this top-secret strategic intelligence on the two campaigns, and Li Kenong passed it to Chen Geng and to Zhou Enlai, secretary of the Central Military Commission. The intelligence was accurate and timely; relayed by the Center to the Central Soviet, it played a major part in the Red Army's victories in the field.

### Exploiting Xu Enzeng's Weakness to Seize the Top-Secret Codebook

Xu Enzeng's looks and character were much discussed by the secret-service men who had spent longer under him and seen more of him. "Refined without, venomous within" was the verdict on every tongue. One said: "Xu Enzeng stood about 1.72 meters, with a square face and glasses for his nearsightedness, refined in appearance, sparing of words, for all the world a pale-faced scholar." Another: "Xu Enzeng was refined enough on the surface, but sinister in the extreme underneath. In his manner of living he did his utmost to ape the West in food and daily use. He dressed wholly Western, though now and then he wore a Sun Yat-sen tunic. He loved motorcars, and new-model cars above all: let him see a new style and he would find a way to get hold of it. ..." He was also said to give private dances often: "Xu's dancing was nothing special; his fondness for it was, first, to show himself off as a 'high-class Chinese,' and second, a means of seducing and toying with women." Another said: "Xu Enzeng's appearance was plainly unlike Dai Li's. Dai Li was dark-faced, with heavy brows and big eyes, a hooked nose and a wide mouth, a fierce cast to his features; whereas Xu Enzeng was fair without question, with gold-rimmed spectacles, cultivated and mild. Mr. Zou Taofen spoke of Xu Enzeng in his book Since the War of Resistance Began. Mr. Zou and Xu had been fellow students at Nanyang University, and he said Xu was handsome and kept his quarters spotless in his school days. In the early war years the democrats Zhang Naiqi and Li Gongpu ran into Xu Enzeng in Kunming, and expressed their astonishment: how could so refined a man be a Kuomintang secret-service boss? In truth, refined as Xu's exterior was, his heart was uncommonly wicked, and there was nothing he would not do."

Zou Taofen and Xu Enzeng had been "classmates" at Nanyang College. When he mentioned Xu Enzeng in Since the War of Resistance Began, Zou said: "Mr. Xu's bearing and the office he held seemed entirely mismatched. On the surface he looked a gentle, cultivated, pale-faced scholar; whether in Western suit or Chinese gown he was always handsome and neat, and in school his room was the most spotless and tastefully arranged of all, admired by his fellow students. I spoke with him several times in the wartime capital. Once I asked him, unthinking: you studied electrical engineering, you ought to be an electrical engineer, yet here you are doing ✕✕✕✕✕✕✕✕ — it is beyond all expectation. He smiled and said: What choice have I? The Generalissimo insists I hold this post; I shall have to wait until the revolution succeeds and then go back to my engineering. I am at it now, and can only carry on!" During the war, when the Kuomintang launched its second anti-Communist high tide, Xu Enzeng's agents sealed up all fifty-five branches of the Life Bookstore; Zou Taofen, in boundless indignation, sought out this "classmate" to protest. Seeing him, Xu Enzeng launched into a "torrent of abuse" against the Communist Party. Zou Taofen "asked him: in the friendship of old schoolmates, we can each speak plainly — you run the secret service; by the material you have gathered, am I a Communist or not? He smiled and said: I have 'shadowed' you for seven years and cannot prove you are a Communist. I said: then why say so much to me about the Communist Party? He answered bluntly: at this stage, whoever is not Kuomintang is Communist. There is no neutral ground between, no such thing as a people's standpoint! You cultural people, if you do not join the Kuomintang, you are working for the Communists! ..." Xu Enzeng further urged him to be "sensible," pressed him to join the Kuomintang, and tried to force him to merge the Life Bookstore with the secret-service-run Zhengzhong Book Company. Zou Taofen sternly rebuffed these blandishments and threats: "An upright citizen can only do upright things of use to nation and people ... I will be no one's lackey, neither any private man's nor any private party's! Truckling to another's will is a thing this stiff spine of mine cannot manage!" They parted on bad terms; Xu Enzeng went off in a huff and sealed the Life Bookstore all the same, driving Zou Taofen to flee to Hong Kong for his fourth exile. In a few strokes Zou Taofen drew this secret-service boss to the life.

Kuomintang high officials of the day were corrupt and dissolute, their private lives debauched, and the privileged secret-service boss Xu Enzeng was an outrageously greedy and lecherous man, a practiced trifler with women who spent his days haunting dance halls and brothels — a thing known to every Kuomintang official and every secret-service organ. It is said that in 1945, when Xu Enzeng had already climbed to the high post of vice-minister of communications in the National Government, Chiang Kai-shek suddenly ordered him "stripped of all posts, never to be employed again" and cast down, and that this failing had something to do with it. One of the charges against him was that, together with Wang Suqing — the woman he had kept and then made his second concubine — he had traded in gold and American dollars on a grand scale in Chengdu under the "Vice-Minister Xu" name, smuggling foreign goods wholesale over the Burma Road and lending at usury, and the like. This weakness of his the "Three Heroes of Longtan" could naturally turn to account in their contest with him.

Not counting his countless mistresses, Xu Enzeng had three wives under his own roof. His first wife was a Wuxing woman surnamed Zhang, who lived with Xu's mother and was "a housewife of heavy feudal cast and no little superstition." The two are said to have got on well enough at first, but from 1928 their feeling soured. In 1929 a friend of Xu Enzeng's, going to Britain to study, entrusted his wife, Wang Suqing, to Xu's "care." Not long after the friend left, Xu Enzeng took up with her, and she bore him children. When the friend came home from his studies and found the deed done, cowed besides by this secret-service boss's power, he dared not raise the matter.

Later Xu Enzeng took up with a Hubei woman named Fei Xia, a turncoat who had studied in the Soviet Union. When Wang Suqing, the concubine so lately brought in, found out, she cursed Fei Xia roundly and even came to blows. Xu Enzeng had Wang Suqing sent off to Chengdu and consigned to the cold palace, and married Fei Xia in Hankou. Many of Xu Enzeng's mistresses and paramours were not merely playthings for his pleasure but tools of his counter-revolutionary work, bait to lure the weak-willed in the revolutionary camp into apostasy. Fei Xia, for one, used her charms to help Xu Enzeng win over and buy off Ren Zhuoxuan (Ye Qing) and other Soviet-trained students, who then served the Kuomintang reactionaries willingly.

At times this failing of his we too could turn to account. When Xu Enzeng first took up with his friend's wife Wang Suqing, for instance, he feared a row with the head wife at home, and asked Qian Zhuangfei to find him a suitable house to "hide the beauty" in. Qian Zhuangfei reported it to the Party organization. After some study, it was decided to "give him what he wanted": the Qian family would let this kept woman have the front upstairs room of the Shanghai house where they themselves lived. This both let Qian Zhuangfei win further favor and trust from Xu Enzeng, and gained the Qian family a thoroughly safe political umbrella, useful as a Party contact point and a meeting place. According to Qian Zhuangfei's wife, Zhang Zhenhua, Xu Enzeng truly did treat Qian Zhuangfei as a "bosom friend" then. She said: "In those days we lived at No. 4, Xinxingshun Lane, Rue Lafayette, off Ganshi East Road in the French Concession, sharing a house with Xu Enzeng's mistress 'Miss Wang.' I remember 'Miss Wang' later grew big with child and went into hospital for an abortion. When she came out, she stayed at our home, sleeping at my bedside. ..." Just then Xu Enzeng and Wang Suqing were still keeping their liaison secret, and he was thoroughly pleased with Qian Zhuangfei for arranging the "help" himself, all the more taking him for a "bosom friend," leaning on him as his right hand and treating him as "family."

After Xu Enzeng set up the Party Affairs Investigation Section's "headquarters" at No. 5 Zhongshan East Road in Nanjing, a secret radio station was built there, and they began, too, to manufacture small, portable transmitter-receivers. So a signboard reading "Zhengyuan Industrial Company" was hung at the door as cover for this mysterious organ. Xu Enzeng's secret radio station was set up here as well, and the investigators later posted to Hankou, Jiujiang, Anqing, and elsewhere all reported to the station here. Every telegraphic report and every piece of intelligence for Xu Enzeng passed first through Qian Zhuangfei, who read it over and proposed how it should be handled, Xu Enzeng merely signing his name atop it. And so Qian Zhuangfei held all the secrets of the place.

By this time, trust Xu Enzeng though he did in Qian Zhuangfei more and more, there was one thing he kept in his own tight grip. It was like a talisman to Xu Enzeng: whenever he went out he carried it on his person, tucked in an inner pocket against his skin. It was the codebook he used to communicate with the Kuomintang's high officials. By the order of Chiang Kai-shek and Chen Lifu, this top-secret cipher was to be kept and used by Xu Enzeng alone, and handed to no one. Qian Zhuangfei knew that without this codebook the core secrets of the Kuomintang's supreme ruling clique could not be got at. He had long wanted to copy it, yet could never lay hands on it. What was to be done? Qian Zhuangfei and Li Kenong studied Xu Enzeng's fatal weakness — his corruption and dissipation, his lust for women and whoring — and laid a plan. Once, when Xu Enzeng came to Shanghai for a meeting, they seized the chance to play up together, to him, that there was a beautiful girl in such-and-such a place. Xu Enzeng, hearing it, was moved at once and made to go. Qian Zhuangfei said, on purpose: "You can't go — how can you go carrying a thing like that?" Xu Enzeng promptly drew the codebook from his shirt and handed it to Qian Zhuangfei; and the moment Xu was gone, Qian Zhuangfei copied and stowed it away. With this codebook, much of the most secret and important cutting-edge intelligence fell into our hands as well.

### "Use His Passport and Money for Our Intelligence; Use His Hand and Gun to Kill the Detectives We Hate Most"

For the Kuomintang's own needs, and for the convenience of our work, Qian Zhuangfei went a step further and "helped" them set up in Nanjing, Tianjin, and elsewhere a set of open intelligence organs under the cover of "news agencies": the "Minzhi News Agency" on Danfeng Street in Nanjing, the "Great Wall News Agency" at No. 5 Qiushan Street in the Japanese concession in Tianjin, and, as the command organ over these, the "Yangtze News Agency" on the fourth floor of the Central Hotel in Nanjing — next door to No. 5 Zhongshan Road — run by Qian Zhuangfei. In name these bodies were subsidiaries of the Kuomintang secret-service organs; in fact they were, from top to bottom, wholly in our Party's hands. And building them up in earnest was, in itself, one of the ways Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di won further trust from Xu Enzeng. After Hu Di set up the "Great Wall News Agency" in Tianjin, he used the Kuomintang's name and the Kuomintang's money to range about gathering intelligence, and often sent what he collected to Qian Zhuangfei in invisible writing, to be passed on to the Party organization. The method of secret writing then was to write the message in lemon or orange juice in the cracks of an ordinary letter, and pass a flame over it to bring it out; sometimes intelligence was written across a newspaper in rice water, which, once received, was brushed with iodine to make it appear.

Once the Yangtze News Agency existed, the work of Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di grew easier still. Each time Li Kenong came to Nanjing he put up on the fourth floor of the Central Hotel, and the three would confer there on the work and live the life of the Party. The Party documents the Kuomintang agents gathered all funneled into the news agency, where Qian Zhuangfei had only to stamp a seal on them and hand them off to be filed away, and that was that. Whatever intelligence the Kuomintang wanted, Qian Zhuangfei fabricated for them — exaggerated stuff, such as how many people the Communists had turned out for a march or demonstration. And all of it went out through Xu Enzeng's own hand.

In April 1930, after Chiang Kai-shek's tangled war with Feng Yuxiang and Yan Xishan, Chiang grew deeply uneasy about the Northeastern Army and schemed by every means to get intelligence on it, for everyone knew the Northeastern Army's weight in the national balance. The situation in the Northeast was tangled then: Zhang Xueliang was emperor and Northeast king there, and Chiang's power could not reach in at all. The Kuomintang had gathered intelligence on them before, but of the several agents it had sent to the Northeast, all had been done in by the Japanese. So Xu Enzeng entrusted this "grave charge" to Qian Zhuangfei. Getting word of it, Qian Zhuangfei told Chen Geng he meant to go in person, taking along Hu Di, who was then in Tianjin, and organizing a small team to go to Shenyang. Our Center, weighing the matter, decided the Party's intelligence work in the Northeast must also grow — it needed both to gather the situation there and to sound out the intelligence apparatus. The Central Special Committee therefore attached great importance to the affair and sent Chen Geng along with them, at the same time arranging for the local Party organization in the Northeast to throw its full weight behind it. Chen Geng and the others thus seized this rare chance: using the passports of the Kuomintang secret-service organ as cover and spending the Kuomintang's money, they took the occasion to make a tour of the Central Special Section's work in the Northeast and North China, and with the help of the local Communist organizations along the way brought back to the Party a great deal of important secret intelligence. At the same time they set up in the Northeast a secret organization that was nominally the Kuomintang's but in fact supplied intelligence to the Central Special Section. To satisfy the Kuomintang, Qian Zhuangfei returned to Shanghai and, together with Li Kenong, clipped and pasted news from the papers, added some of the genuine, well-documented inside facts on the Northeastern Army gathered this trip, and wrote a detailed report of more than forty thousand characters on the situation in the Northeast. The intelligence organs they had developed were real, staffed by certain people living in the Northeast, standing openly as the Kuomintang's organs there. So when Qian Zhuangfei got back to Nanjing and produced all this, he performed a great service — intelligence the Kuomintang had never dreamed of. Xu Enzeng praised him again and again — "remarkable," "where did such fine material come from?" — professed himself "utterly impressed" by Qian Zhuangfei, and rewarded him on the spot. Because of all this, not only could Xu Enzeng claim the credit, but Chen Lifu could show off before Chiang Kai-shek. In Xu Enzeng's eyes Qian Zhuangfei was, from then on, a founder in name and in fact of the secret-service organization of the Chen Lifu–Xu Enzeng system, all the more prominent there, his position all the firmer.

This Northeast trip of Chen Geng and Qian Zhuangfei's was an extraordinary success. As some comrades put it: using his money and his name to keep all the intelligence in our own hands — this was the best way to do intelligence work. It was the finest single instance of that year, though not the only one.

As Qian Zhuangfei did in Nanjing, so Li Kenong, under cover of the Shanghai Radio Administration Bureau, sent all the intelligence he obtained to Chen Geng. At the same time, the intelligence Li Kenong supplied to Xu Enzeng — everything bearing on our Party's activity — was mostly overblown and wide of the mark. And Xu Enzeng never doubted the false intelligence Li Kenong sent him, but passed it all up the line unchanged.

And so, under the Center's leadership, the three comrades Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di broke into the Kuomintang's highest secret-service organ, took hold of the entire activity of the Kuomintang's national secret-service apparatus, and supplied the Party with much important intelligence. Above all, after Gu Shunzhang was arrested and turned traitor, when the enemy meant to net our Party Center's organs and its leaders at a single haul, they made an immense contribution to safeguarding the Party Center.

Besides drawing off the enemy's intelligence and mastering his movements, the "Three Heroes of Longtan" also made use of the enemy's own knotted internal rivalries to kill, by the enemy's hand, certain vicious anti-Communist thugs who gravely endangered our Party. There was a detective-squad captain in the Kuomintang's Shanghai Public Security Bureau, a thoroughly reactionary man, savage toward the Communists, who had arrested many Communists and revolutionaries. The Central Special Section was set on eliminating him, and had sent the Red Squad several times to do it, without success. Chen Geng consulted Li Kenong and "Old Song," who had gotten inside the Songhu Garrison Command; they first rented a house in Shanghai, moved some Communists in, and left there a batch of Party documents that the Kuomintang had already seized and that were now worthless, then leaked word of this lead to that detective-squad captain. But when he was about to go straight there to raid it, Chen Geng, through Yang Dengying and by other channels, tipped him off from the side not to move at once, but to "cast a long line for a big fish." A few days later, when the detective captain went to make his raid, the place had long stood empty — not a soul to be seen — and he retrieved only a few worthless, expired documents. When his superior learned of it, he demanded of him: you knew all along, so why didn't you move at once? Did you deliberately let the Communists get away? The man could not clear himself, could not answer; and so he roused his superior's suspicion, and after a period of "watching," the detective captain was secretly done away with by the enemy as a "Communist spy." The enemy still thought he had eliminated a real "Communist accomplice," never knowing he had been taken in by Communists. This was in 1929.

At times they also used the Kuomintang organ's privilege — no one dared cross it — and the Investigation Section's special standing, to escort many Party cadres out of danger, and to help the Party organization send underground workers in to break into the enemy's own ranks. Whenever a responsible comrade had to go to, or pass through, Chiang Kai-shek's stronghold of Nanjing, the Center left the escort to Li Kenong.

In early 1930 Liu Bocheng, just back from the Soviet Union to Shanghai, took on the Party Center's charge of raising an armed uprising in Wuhan, and it was decided that he would be commander-in-chief; he had to reach Wuhan quickly. But the White Terror was severe then, and the enemy's blockade on the road from Shanghai to Wuhan was tight. To get Liu Bocheng to Wuhan swiftly and safely, the Central Special Committee decided that Li Kenong would use his special standing to shepherd Liu Bocheng to Wuhan by way of Nanjing. Li Kenong's biographer vividly records this escort of Liu Bocheng:

At the appointed time, Li Kenong came through the rain to the contact point at the Bund Park.

Li Kenong, in a blue silk gown, stood waiting with a black umbrella held aloft, glancing now and then at his watch, his face composed but his heart on edge, his wary eyes fixed on the park entrance.

A Chevrolet sedan drove up from the distance, stopped at the park's edge, and two men got out and came straight toward the park. Seeing them, Li Kenong walked out to meet them; and when he was a few meters off, he stopped, took off his glasses, breathed on them, drew out a handkerchief and wiped them and put them back on. This was the prearranged signal, that there was no "tail," that all was well. When Li Kenong and the two men met, not a word was said; he only gave a look with his eyes to one of them, a familiar face — Li Lisan — and the three climbed into the car one after another.

Once the car started, Li Kenong, in the front seat, turned around at last and shook hands and exchanged greetings with Li Lisan.

Li Lisan pointed to the man beside him and said: "Old Li, this is Comrade Liu Bocheng; I hand him to you — see him safely off."

Li Kenong reached over quickly and gripped Liu Bocheng's hand: "Long have I heard your name; I never thought to meet you here."

Liu Bocheng gave a slight, unruffled smile: "And I only today set eyes on the true face of Mount Lu!"

In the first-class carriage of the train, because Li Kenong carried special credentials, he deftly handled the agents' surveillance and the military police's searches, and reached Nanjing safely. Afterward, though Chiang Kai-shek and his secret-service organs did come to know that Liu Bocheng had left Shanghai, they never for a moment imagined it was Li Kenong who, single-handed, had brought Liu Bocheng to Nanjing.

Speaking of this later, Li Kenong said the escort of cadres had been part of the work, too: "Our Party cadres bound for Nanjing were always sent by me. Liu Bocheng, too, I sent. Li Lisan handed him to me in person, telling me to 'see him safely off.' When I escorted cadres I rode first class; we had a Pass, and once aboard we cut a fine figure, telling people to make way, make way. Reaching Nanjing, we would put up on the upper floor of the Central Hotel. ..."

### A Model of "Breaking Into" the Kuomintang's Highest Secret Service

Under the direct leadership of Zhou Enlai, the Central Special Section sent Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di at the end of 1929 to break into the Kuomintang's highest secret-service organ, where they took hold of the enemy's core secrets and supplied the Party with much important intelligence. Chiang Kai-shek's several attempts to destroy the Communist central leadership organs stationed in Shanghai all failed; above all, the men's timely report of the defection of the Central Special Section chief Gu Shunzhang made an outstanding contribution to safeguarding the Party Center's leadership organs, and won high praise and reward from the Party Center. This small group achieved immense results, and stands as the model of the Central Special Section developing intelligence ties inside the enemy — of "breaking in."

Of why the fight in the enemy's very heart could win so great a victory, Li Kenong said: "Neither side had any real experience then. The enemy was the ruling class; he stood exposed in the open. Once our Party went underground, much could be done. With the right policy and reliance on the masses, clandestine struggle could be carried on.

"In the end, we were underground then, hidden, studying the enemy's situation day in and day out, waging tit-for-tat struggle against him, while the enemy stood exposed. Besides, they lacked skilled men, and we seized the moment to break in; the surveillance points the enemy set up in Shanghai, Nanjing, and Tianjin were all under our control."

As for this trial of strength on which the fate of the two parties hung, the Kuomintang secret-service boss Xu Enzeng, faced with the incontrovertible fact, had no choice but to concede defeat. In the memoir he wrote nearly thirty years later he gave himself away unbidden: "The Communist Party's underground organization was extremely tight-knit. In my first months in the post I was like a blind man groping in a dark cave; but by the end of that year, 1930, there was a breakthrough, and I began to feel my way to the door of the Communists' secret organization. ..." Four months later he discovered that his "ablest" hand, the "model employee" who kept "our top-secret documents" — Qian Zhuangfei — "was in fact a Communist spy," which left him "thunderstruck" and cost him dearly. In the 1940s, running into Li Kenong in Chongqing, Xu Enzeng said with an awkward look: "See, the lot of you turned my hair white." It was the helpless lament of a man who had taken a savage beating.
