## A Preliminary Inquiry into the History of the Hongmen

{d} Jiang Hao

### I. The Origins and Development of the Hongmen

The Hongmen arose out of the "overthrow the Qing and restore the Ming" organizations of the late Ming and early Qing, evolving from the Heaven and Earth Society, the Three Harmonies Society, the Elder Brothers Society, and the like. Legend gives its first ancestor as Hong Ying, also called Hong Shengying (and by some Yin Hongsheng), a man of Taiping county in Pingyang prefecture, Shanxi. He took the metropolitan degree in the fourth year of Chongzhen (1631), and in the seventh year of Chongzhen was engaged onto the staff of Jiang Xiang, garrison commander of Datong in Zhili, where he took part in military planning. Shrewd and capable, generous and given to fair dealing, he drew many chivalrous men of the day who came from all quarters on hearing his name to take him as their master, among them Cai Dezhong of Pucheng county in Tongzhou prefecture, Shaanxi; Fang Dahong of Huailai county in Xuanhua prefecture, Zhili; Ma Chaoxing of Zhuozhou in Shuntian prefecture, Zhili; and Hu Dedi and Li Shikai of Jiangzhou in Shanxi.

In the fourteenth year of Chongzhen, Hong Ying went south with his disciples and transferred his allegiance to Shi Kefa of the Ming Ministry of War. Shi was then commanding the defense of Yangzhou against the Qing army, and he sent Hong to Yanjing (present-day Beijing) to scout out its strength. All along the way Hong made contact with anti-Qing men of resolve—Gu Yanwu, Wang Fuzhi, Fu Qingzhu, Huang Lizhou, and others—and together they founded the "Hanliu" ("Remnant of Han") organization and carried on the resistance against the Qing. After Shi Kefa died a martyr at Yangzhou, Hong Ying went to Wuhu in Anhui and threw in with the Ming general Huang Degong. When Huang was defeated and cut his own throat and his men scattered, Hong Ying gathered up more than twenty thousand of Huang's troops and fought on. But the two sides were too unevenly matched; beaten again and again, he died on the thirteenth day of the fifth month of the eighteenth year of Chongzhen (1645) at Sanchahe, some sixty-odd li from Guiju and Si.

Afterward Hong Ying's son Hong Xu, together with Hong Ying's disciple Cai Dezhong and the others, went to Hangzhou to join the Ming Prince of Lu; but the Prince of Lu surrendered to the Qing, so they went instead to Zheng Chenggong. In the seventeenth year of Chongzhen the Qing troops took Beijing, and thereafter, though the various regions of the south went on resisting, they were destroyed piece by piece by the Qing army.

In the eighteenth year of Shunzhi (1661), Cai Dezhong and the others followed Zheng Chenggong in holding Taiwan. To bind his officers and men more closely together, Zheng Chenggong founded the "Jintai Mountain" and the "Minglun Hall"—the idea taken from Liangshan, so that there was both a "mountain" name and a "hall" name; later groups within China used the mountain name, while those abroad used only the hall name and not the mountain name—and this was the earliest "mountain" and "hall" organization of the Hongmen. To build up the secret anti-Qing organizations on the mainland, Zheng Chenggong sent Cai Dezhong, Fang Dahong, Ma Chaoxing, Hu Dedi, Li Shikai, and the others in disguise to the Shaolin Temple on Jiulian Mountain in Putian county, Xinghua prefecture, Fujian, where they took ordination under the abbot Zhitong; these five are honored in the Hongmen as the "Former Five Ancestors," which is why tradition holds that the founding patriarchs of the Hongmen were monks.

The monks of the Shaolin Temple were masters of the martial arts and famous throughout the country, and men came from everywhere on hearing their name to study, so that the temple became a school of martial arts. In the Kangxi reign, Zheng Chenggong's nephew Zheng Junda took refuge at the Shaolin Temple with his wife and sister, and once led a monk-army of one hundred and twenty-eight men, raised at the Qing court's summons, on a campaign against the Xilu, returning in triumph. The monks claimed no credit and asked only to go back to the temple to cultivate the Way; the Qing emperor rewarded them richly, and apart from keeping Zheng Junda on as a regional commander, let the rest return to the temple. Later the emperor, believing slander, sent troops to besiege the Shaolin Temple; through a traitor's betrayal the temple was burned, and of the monks, eighteen escaped, among them the five—Cai Dezhong, Fang Dahong, Ma Chaoxing, Hu Dedi, and Li Shikai—who got away.

After Cai Dezhong and the others fled the Shaolin Temple, they were pursued by Qing soldiers along the way, and at the mouth of Shawan five brave men—Wu Tiancheng, Fang Huicheng, Zhang Jingzhi (also written Zhang Jingzhao), Yang Zhangyou (also written Yang Zhangzuo), and Lin Dajiang—held off the pursuit and let them escape; these five are called in the Hongmen the "Middle Five Ancestors." Wu Tiancheng and the rest then went with Cai Dezhong and the others to Guangdong, where they were again hard pressed by Qing troops and were rescued from danger by five monks of the Baozhu Temple in Huizhou—Wu Tianyou, Hong Taisui, Yao Bida, Li Shidi, and Lin Yongchao; these five monks are called in the Hongmen the "Latter Five Ancestors."

Cai and the others went from Guangdong through Jiangxi to Hubei, where at that time Wan Yunlong, abbot of the Wanyun Temple on Wanyun Mountain, swore brotherhood with Chen Jinnan in the common cause of overthrowing the Qing and restoring the Ming. Wan Yunlong had been a general under the Ming Prince of Lu and had come there after his rising in Shandong failed. Chen Jinnan was a scholar of the Hanlin Academy, a Hubei man, who had opposed the emperor's burning of the Shaolin Temple, and finding his remonstrances useless, resigned his office and roamed the country. In the ninth year of Yongzheng (1731) he had organized the "Hanliu" at Yazhou in Sichuan and founded the "Jingzhong Mountain"; later he returned to his native Hubei and studied Daoism in the White Crane Cave, styling himself the "Hermit of the White Crane Cave." Sympathizing with what the Shaolin monks had suffered, and meaning to avenge them, he took on the disguise of a wandering diviner, made friends among the bold men of the land, met Cai Dezhong and the others along the way, and welcomed them into his home, settling them nearby at the Red Flower Pavilion in Xiaputan.

On the twenty-fifth day of the seventh month of the twelfth year of Yongzheng, with Chen Jinnan presiding over the incense, they swore the oath of alliance and rose at the Red Flower Pavilion, the earlier comers reckoned as elder brothers and the later as younger. Those who came from every quarter to the gathering included Wu Tiancheng, Fang Huicheng, and Zhang Jingzhi of Fujian; Wu Tianyou, Wu Tinggui, and Hong Taisui of Guangdong; Huang Changcheng and Zhong Yuying of Jiangxi; and loyal survivors of the Ming. Among them was one Zhu Hongzhu, said to be a grandson of the Ming Chongzhen emperor; taking him for the legitimate Ming line, they raised him up as their alliance chief, took the character "Hong" as the surname of their brotherhood, and split the character "Hong" into "three, eight, twenty-one" as a secret sign within the society. They made the "Red Flower Pavilion" the birthplace of the brothers and the "day of the oath" the brothers' birthday. This gathering was called the "Great Meeting of the Hong Family," and from it the name Hongmen arose, the "Hanliu" organization thus evolving into the Hongmen body.

Once the Hongmen body had formed, it went on to raise an army, under Chen Jinnan's command, with Su Hongguang as vanguard, the five Baozhu Temple monks led by Wu Tianyou as the center guard, and the five brave men led by Wu Tiancheng as the reserve; they advanced from Xiangyang in Hubei and won battle after battle. But at Wuchang they were defeated by the Qing general Yu Chenglong; Wan Yunlong fell in battle and Zhu Hongzhu vanished. Chen Jinnan had no choice but to fall back and hold Xiangyang, and after three months and more without progress, he changed his strategy, dispersed his men to found mountains and set up halls on their own throughout the country, so as to build up strength for the future and join forces again later. At parting he gave them a poem to serve as a password when they should meet again, its lines running: "Five men divide a single poem; that Hong Ying is upon them, none can know; this we pass to all the brothers, for the day we meet and are made whole again."

In the fourteenth year of Qianlong (1749) the Hongmen met at the Gaoxi Temple in Huizhou, Guangdong, to plan a rising; Chen Jinnan had by then died, and it was led by the five men headed by Wu Tianyou. After Wu and the rest died one by one, the leadership passed to Su Hongguang. He changed his name to Tianyou Hong and named his organization the "Three Harmonies Society," taking Heaven as father, Earth as mother, and sun and moon as sisters, the meaning being to unite the three—the timeliness of Heaven, the advantage of Earth, and the harmony of men—into one. Tianyou Hong led the Three Harmonies army against the Qing troops, took a good many notable cities, and shook the seven provinces of the southeast. Later Zhou Hao (also named Yuying), a nephew of the Ming regional commander Zhou Yuji, came over with his troops—Han Long, Han Hu, Li Changguo, and others—and so Shi Jianming, a nephew of Shi Kefa, was made military adviser, the woman-knight Guan Yuying was made commander of the women's army, and Zhu Hongzhu was honored as "Former Emperor"; with a great show of force they raised troops at Xiangyang and marched into Sichuan. At that time Wang Chunmei, the Qing governor-general of Sichuan, seeing how strong the Three Harmonies army was and how hard to meet head-on, laid a scheme, sending his trusted men Fu Si and Tian Qi to feign surrender to the Three Harmonies army; Tianyou Hong took it for genuine and placed Fu Si as the fourth rank of the Three Harmonies Society and Tian Qi as the seventh, serving as deputy military advisers of the army. When they reached Chongqing and fought the Qing troops, Fu Si and Tian Qi acted as the Qing army's men inside the Three Harmonies army, so that it was caught between enemies within and without and suffered a great defeat. Tianyou Hong was wounded by a stray arrow in the fighting, and the whole army fell back in rout to White Tiger Mountain; Fu Si and Tian Qi were both taken and put to death. This is the reason the Hongmen thereafter forbade the fourth and seventh ranks.

Afterward, to purge the ranks within, they revised the society's charter and drew up rules such as the "Thirty-six Articles," the "Ten Prohibitions," and the "Ten Punishments," and set up a strict system of reward and penalty. Tianyou Hong soon died of his wounds, and Su Hongyu and Lin Lie led the Three Harmonies army on in resisting the Qing, taking several counties in a row; later they were surrounded by the Qing army, Guan Yuying died going to their relief, and it ended once more in defeat. When the Sichuan governor-general Wang Chunmei was reassigned to Guangdong, Su Hongyu slipped into the governor's residence one night and stabbed Wang to death, and was himself killed in the melee. By now Lin Lie stood alone with too little strength to hold on, so he disbanded his men and turned to spreading out across the whole country, founding mountains and setting up halls, carrying on the resistance by guerrilla methods, and extending the Hongmen organization into overseas Chinese communities.

In the eleventh year of Jiaqing (1806), the "Zhuqing Mountain" was founded in Guizhou, the man in charge being Lin Huaiming. The next year Lin raised a rising at Hua county in Weihui, Henan, breaking the society's chief Li Wencheng out of prison, and several departments and counties around Tai'an in Shandong answered the call. After this Lin Qing raised a rising in Beijing; he had meant to act jointly with the Henan members on an agreed date, but the dates were mistaken, and Lin Qing, moving ahead of time, gathered two hundred men and entered the city through the Xuanwu Gate, attacking the East Flower Gate and the West Flower Gate in separate parties with eunuchs acting as men on the inside; but the palace guards discovered them, and seeing the situation was against him, Lin withdrew at once, and the affair failed. The Henan members then rose and were also beaten. The members had no choice but to scatter in all directions, and the risings across Henan were at last put down.

In the twenty-eighth year of Daoguang (1848), Guo Yongtai of Xianning county in Fujian obtained from a fisherman the "rules" Zheng Chenggong had drawn up when he founded his mountain. These were what Zheng Chenggong's grandson Zheng Keshuang, hard pressed by the encircling Qing army, had sealed in an iron chest with other important documents and sunk to the bottom of the sea. Thereafter the Hongmen called it the "Seabed," and it came into general use among Hongmen bodies everywhere. But so much time had passed that the original was lost, and the copies in circulation did not entirely agree with it in content.

The Taiping movement raised by Hong Xiuquan—from the rising at Jintian village in the twelfth month of the thirtieth year of Daoguang (1851) to the taking of Nanjing in the third year of Xianfeng (1853)—had the Three Harmonies Society taking part in it from the first, and at the same time the Society's members in various places, seeing Hong Xiuquan's rising succeed, rose one after another as well. In the thirtieth year of Daoguang, the Three Harmonies Society of the two Guang provinces rose in many places, and by the fourth year of Xianfeng had taken Zhaoqing, Foshan, Dongguan, and other towns, all but surrounding the provincial capital of Guangdong. In Fujian the members held Xiamen for a time. At Shanghai, in the ninth month of the third year of Xianfeng, Liu Lichuan, a Guangdong man, and Li Xianchi, a Fujian man, and others rose and took the walled city of Shanghai county; their organization was called the "Small Sword Society," a branch of the "Heaven and Earth Society," and it later came under the leadership of the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom. In the fifth year of Xianfeng, Liu Lichuan fell heroically in battle, and the county city was at last taken by the Qing army.

In the twelfth year of Guangxu (1886), three thousand men of the Three Harmonies Society rose at Ningshan in Huizhou prefecture, Guangdong, with four hundred stonemasons coming to their support, but were unluckily beaten by the Qing army. In the twenty-fourth year of Guangxu, the Three Harmonies Society leaders Li Liting and Hong Zhennian rose in Guangxi and, apart from Wuzhou and Guilin, swept the whole province, staying active for more than ten months before the Qing army suppressed them.

The Elder Brothers Society was formed in the Qianlong years of the Qing and was in origin only another name for the Three Harmonies Society; it flourished in Hunan and Zhejiang, and after them in the provinces along the Yangtze, and its members were very many.

The Hongmen spread widely among the overseas Chinese, with organizations both in the South Seas islands and in the Americas. In 1893, in Honolulu in the United States, Sun Yat-sen made the acquaintance of the Three Harmonies Society leader Zheng Shiliang, and through Zheng's introduction joined the Hongmen organization; he later reorganized it as the "Chee Kung Tong," founded the "Revive China Society," and set up the "Dalu Mountain," sending members such as Zheng Bichen, Lu Haodong, and Chen Shaobai to various places to link up with men of the brotherhood and carry on revolutionary work.

In the twenty-sixth year of Guangxu (1900), the leaders of the three bodies—the Revive China Society, the Three Harmonies Society, and the Elder Brothers Society—met in Hong Kong and jointly formed the "Revive Han Society," acclaiming Sun Yat-sen as their head; and with this the Hongmen was joined to Sun Yat-sen's revolutionary organization.

Huang Xing, an important figure in the 1911 Revolution, in the second month of 1904 joined with Chen Tianhua, Song Jiaoren, Tan Renfeng, and others to found the "China Arise Society" in Changsha, and was acclaimed its head. At the same time they formed the "Common Enemy Society" specially to make contact with the secret-society brotherhoods. Huang went in person to meet the Hunan Elder Brothers Society leader Ma Fuyi, and they resolved to rise on the sixteenth day of the eleventh month of that year (the tenth day of the tenth lunar month), on the "Longevity Festival" of the Empress Dowager Cixi's seventieth birthday. But word leaked out, and the rising failed before it could be launched; Huang Xing was put on the wanted list and fled to Japan. In 1905 Sun Yat-sen joined with Huang Xing, Chen Tianhua, Song Jiaoren, and others to found the "Chinese Revolutionary Alliance" in Tokyo, with its head office in Japan and three great bureaus within China, of which Wuchang was the central one; it put forward the revolutionary program "Expel the Manchu barbarians, restore China, found a republic, and equalize land rights." Its office was set at Hankou, with Song Jiaoren acclaimed to manage its affairs, and men were sent to set up branches throughout the South Seas; Hongmen men joined it in great numbers.

In Sun Yat-sen's leadership of the revolution—from the Guangzhou rising of 1895, the Huizhou rising of 1900, and the Yellow Flower Mound affair of April 1911, and so on—the anti-Qing armed risings he planned and organized came to ten in all, and laid the foundation for the victory of the Wuchang rising. In these armed struggles the strength of the Hongmen played an important part. So although the Hongmen, with its long history and its mixed membership, and being under Qing rule, consistently took a stance of opposition and sabotage, and although some Hongmen men forgot their origins and did things harmful to the people, still, on the whole, the Hongmen kept its national integrity in the struggle to overthrow the Qing and restore the Ming, and played a positive part in the 1911 Revolution.

### II. The Organization of the Hongmen

Within China the Hongmen has gone by various names—"Hanliu," "Three Harmonies Society," "Three Dots Society," "Heaven and Earth Society," "Elder Brothers Society," "Big Sword Society," "Small Sword Society," and so on—and abroad by names such as "Chee Kung Tong," "Hongshun Hall," and "Yixing Hall"; but its internal structure was much the same everywhere.

Every Hongmen body had its own mountain name, hall name, water name, and incense name, its inner and outer passwords, and a poem besides. Because in the Qing the Hongmen was a secret anti-Qing organization, it used many secret signs to keep saboteurs from slipping in. Take Liu Chuanfu's Jinhua Mountain as an example: its mountain name was Jinhua Mountain, its hall name the Renyi Hall, its water name the Four Seas Water, its incense name the Myriad Blessings Incense, its inner password "Righteousness weighs at the Peach Garden," its outer password "Heroes stand established," and its poem, "One stick of incense on Jinhua Mountain; the names of the Five Ancestors spread everywhere; the heroes of the land swear brotherhood together, and the Three Mountains and Five Peaks make firm the realm." When challenged by a stranger, the two sides would use these cant phrases as their question and answer.

Members of the Hongmen stood in a horizontal relationship to one another, in imitation of the sworn brotherhood of Liu, Guan, and Zhang in the Peach Garden in the time of the Three Kingdoms, and were called elder-and-younger brothers. The brothers were divided into ten ranks, marking the high and low of their standing. The first rank was called Elder Brother or Big Master, and included the mountain-master, the deputy mountain-master, the incense elder (who presided over the incense at the opening of an incense hall), the oath-witness (who took down the oath at the opening of an incense hall), the presiding master (also called Left-Minister Big Master, who managed the affairs of the whole camp), the accompanying master (also called Right-Minister Big Master), the punishment master (who administered the penal law), the executive master (in charge of organization and training), the ritual master (in charge of teaching the observances), the discipline master (in charge of promotion, demotion, reward, and penalty), the arrow-keeper (also called Sword-Guarding Big Master, who kept the command-arrow), the seal-keeper (who kept the seals), and the "new deputy" (also called the confidant, generally a newcomer whom the mountain-master valued highly or a man of talent). Of these the mountain-master and deputy mountain-master were the chiefs, called Dragon-Head Elder Brother and Deputy Dragon-Head Elder Brother; the incense elder and oath-witness were temporary offices at the opening of an incense hall, called "holding a place but no office"; and all those from the presiding master down to the seal-keeper who held both office and place were called the Inner Eight Halls stewards, something like the organization of the eight great ministers of the Ming. Below them were the Outer Eight Halls, the "new deputy" ranking last in the Inner Eight Halls and first in the Outer Eight Halls. The second rank was called Sage Second Master, or Military Adviser Outside the Capital, in charge of strategy; the third rank was called Steward Third Master, or Grain-and-Pay Comptroller Outside the Capital, in charge of receipts and payments; the fifth rank was called Manager Fifth Master, or Red Flag, in charge of general affairs; the sixth rank was called Flower-Crown Sixth Master, or Wind-Patroller and Scout, in charge of patrol; the eighth rank was called Worthy-Tablet Eighth Master, or Discipline-Post, in charge of recording merits and faults; the ninth rank was called River-Mouth Ninth Master, in charge of new brothers and of promotions and posting the name lists; the tenth rank was called the Camp Gate, also called Big Old Youngest or Big Full, in charge of odds and ends, and the rest were all called Little Full, that is, the small fry. The fourth and seventh ranks, because spies had come from them before, were reckoned to the "yin" side and so took only women, not men. All from the "new deputy" down to the tenth rank, except the fourth and seventh, made up the Outer Eight Halls.

The brothers' ranks above were not fixed: one could be promoted for merit, and could rise three grades at once. A newcomer was placed according to his social standing and his learning and reputation, but no higher than the Outer Eight Halls. One might also rise to the top at a bound and be a "new deputy" Big Master the moment he joined.

### III. The Initiation Ceremony

The rite by which a new member entered was called "opening the incense hall": one had to have offered incense before he counted as formally admitted. Incense halls were of two kinds, the "great incense hall" and the "small incense hall." The "great incense hall" was used when a larger number joined at once; its observances were elaborate and took a long time. The "small incense hall" was simpler and suited to a small number. In the Qing, because the Hongmen was a secret anti-Qing organization, the incense hall was mostly opened in secret inside a temple in the countryside. After the 1911 Revolution the brotherhoods could be open, but even so the incense hall was not opened before a crowd. Under Kuomintang rule the brotherhoods were banned on the surface, so they took the form of ordinary social associations, and the incense hall too was opened in a half-public way.

For the setting of a great incense hall—sometimes called the "Red Flower Pavilion"—the seat of Lord Guan was set in the center, with the tablet "Loyalty and Righteousness Hall" hung above it, and in the middle an altar table of three tiers: on the upper tier the seats of the two men Yang Jue'ai and Zuo Botao; on the middle tier the seat of Song Jiang of Liangshan; and on the lower tier the seats of the founding ancestor, the Five Patriarchs, the Former Five Ancestors, the Middle Five Ancestors, the Latter Five Ancestors, the Five Righteous, the male and female military advisers, and the former sages and worthies, each written on red or yellow paper.

Among the objects used in the incense hall were an incense burner (bearing the four characters "fan you fu lei," a cipher for "overthrow the Qing and restore the Ming"), and also a candlestick, a Seven-Star sword ("the Manchu overturned, the Ming arisen"), an abacus (to reckon the time until, the Qing destroyed, a Ming emperor should ascend again), a ruler (to measure the members' conduct and to reckon the inch and fraction of Heaven and Earth made one), a peck-measure and steelyard (standing for justice and fairness), a mirror (its sense: to see through all good and evil), scissors (the sense: to cut open the dark clouds), a peach branch (symbolizing the sworn brotherhood of Liu, Guan, and Zhang in the Peach Garden), and a string of beads and a wooden fish (set to encircle a single sword, forming a vaulted shape). Red lanterns were also hung—three on the outer layer, eight on the middle, and twenty-one on the inner—again splitting the character "Hong" into "three, eight, twenty-one." Below was set a bridge, meaning the place where the Five Ancestors met danger in their flight. The setting and observances of the small incense hall were simpler, and need not be described in full.

As for the ancestral tablets enshrined on the lower tier of the incense hall above: the founding ancestors were Hong Ying, Fu Qingzhu, Gu Yanwu, Huang Lizhou, and Wang Fuzhi; the Five Patriarchs were Shi Kefa the Civil Patriarch, Zheng Chenggong the Martial Patriarch, Chen Jinnan the Proclaiming Patriarch, Wan Yunlong the Attaining Patriarch, and Su Hongguang the Majestic Patriarch; the Former Five Ancestors were Cai Dezhong, Fang Dahong, Ma Chaoxing, Hu Dedi, and Li Shikai; the Middle Five Ancestors were Yang Zhangyou, Fang Huicheng, Wu Tiancheng, Lin Dajiang, and Zhang Jingzhi; the Latter Five Ancestors were Li Shidi, Hong Taisui, Wu Tianyou, Lin Yongchao, and Yao Bida; the Five Righteous were Zheng Junda, Xie Bangheng, Huang Changcheng, Wu Tinggui, and Zhou Hongying; the Five Heroes were Zheng Daode, Zheng Daofang, Han Long, Han Hu, and Li Changguo; the Three Heroines were Guo Xiuying, Zheng Yulan, and Zhong Wenjun; and of the military advisers, the male adviser was Shi Jianming and the female adviser Guan Yuying.

### IV. The Rules and the Family Law

The Hongmen was tightly organized and thoroughly disciplined, with rules such as the Twenty-one Articles, the Ten Prohibitions, the Ten Punishments, the Eighteen Statutes, the Ten Articles, the Ten Clauses, and the Ten Deliberated Precepts, and so on. Take the Ten Articles as an example, and one may see the general shape of its discipline. Their content was: first, be loyal and serve the country; second, be filial to one's parents; third, keep the order of elder and younger; fourth, live in harmony with one's neighbors; fifth, walk the upright path; sixth, uphold benevolence and righteousness; seventh, brother-in-law and sister-in-law treat each other with respect; eighth, the elder brother kind and the younger righteous; ninth, obey the incense-hall rules; tenth, trust and help one another.

Under all these rules, anyone who broke them was punished very severely. Because it was all carried out internally, it was called the "family law." Its penalties were: capital punishment—death by slicing or by the knife; heavy punishment—being buried alive in a pit, or drowned in water; light punishment—"three knives and six holes," or forty strokes of the red staff; degrading punishment—demotion, or hanging an iron tablet on the offender; and dark punishment—being seized and made an outcast, or demoted to the "raw hall," never to be used again.

The knife used in the family law above was called the "Little Treasure," and the staff the "red staff" (three chi six cun long). The penalty of "three knives and six holes" was used on the upper four ranks, and the penalty of "forty strokes of the red staff" on the lower four ranks and the tenth-rank youngest. On the whole, punishment followed the "Five Punishments" according to the gravity of the offense.

### V. Secret Documents, Cant, and Signs

The secret documents and the cant and signs handed down in the Hongmen were very many, mostly passed on by word of mouth with little set down in writing, and some copied and recopied so that errors were bound to creep in. Of these the "Seabed" was more concrete in content, though, being passed on in secret, it too had its gaps and errors. Its chief contents included the cant for making one's respects and forming ties at a new "dock"; the fundamental questions and answers of Liangshan; the questions and answers on the origins of the founding ancestor Hong Ying; the terms for "conquering the wine"; the terms for "presenting the treasure" (that is, honoring loyalty); the ties formed in going abroad to visit friends; the ties of the forty-eight-line master poem; the ties of a send-off; the "three and a half sticks of incense" (the first stick, Yang Jue'ai and Zuo Botao's bond of life and death; the second, the sworn brotherhood of Liu, Guan, and Zhang in the Peach Garden; the third, the benevolence and righteousness of Song Jiang of Liangshan; the half stick, loyalty to Lord Liu unto death); the ties of setting out; the questioning of an innkeeper; the ties of "washing the face" (also called "opening the light"); the accompanying master's passing of orders; the felicitation on a fifth-rank's promotion; the mountain-ridge order; the praising of the knife and slaying of the sacrifice, and the offering to the banner; the order to found a mountain; the setting of the Red Flag in its place; the mountain-quelling order; the seating of guests; the words for conferring rank on brothers of every grade; the audience with the oath-witness Big Master; and so on, besides many other kinds of cant and sign.

### VI. The Mountain-Lodges and Societies Founded

The aim of the Hongmen was to overthrow the Qing and restore the Ming, and so the great majority of Hongmen organizations were founded under the Qing. To make guerrilla activity easier, each organization was independent, answering to the others when need arose. From the materials I have gathered, the Hongmen bodies founded under the Qing and after the 1911 Revolution include the following.

### VII. The Hongmen's Activities in Shanghai

#### (i) Hongmen Bodies in Shanghai

Hongmen bodies were spread over the whole country, most thickly in the regions of the Yangtze valley and in Fujian and the two Guang provinces. Shanghai being a mixing-place of people from every quarter, Hongmen men from all over moved to Shanghai and brought their Hongmen bodies with them—some bringing a whole body along, some setting up a new outfit in Shanghai under the old body's name, or founding a new mountain. For these reasons many Hongmen bodies appeared in Shanghai, with a strong local-clique coloring at first—one body a Hubei clique, another a Guangdong clique. But later the newly recruited brothers were not confined to a single province or place but drawn from all over the country, so that the local-clique coloring gradually faded.

Under Kuomintang rule, because the brotherhoods were banned on the surface, those that had gone by mountain-and-hall names took association or society names for outside purposes, registering with the Kuomintang government organs as ordinary social bodies to gain legal standing and be able to act openly. Before the War of Resistance there were around forty Hongmen bodies in Shanghai; in the eight years of the war, some ceased activity and some carried on, and Bai Yushan of the Hubei clique took the lead in running a Hongmen federation. After the victory the Hongmen federation dissolved of itself, and the old Hongmen bodies each resumed activity on their own. Here I list those bodies and the men in charge of them as I can recall: the Wusheng Mountain (Xiang Songpo, Zhang Zilian, and others), the Xiayi Society (Zheng Ziliang), the Zhongnan Mountain (Bai Yushan), the Shilong Mountain (Shi Zhenjiang, Long Qingquan), the Rivers-and-Lakes Federation (Shi Zhenjiang, Long Qingquan), the Tianshou Mountain (Yao Gengbai), the Changbai Mountain (Zhang Shusheng), the Chujing Mountain (Li Wenhui, Li Qiyun), the Zhengcheng Society (Wang Zhiben), the Wuxing Mountain (Wang Yucheng, Xu Yimin), the Guyun Mountain (Xu Langxi), the Dahong Mountain (Wang Tiemin), the Xinshenghe Athletic Club, the Jushenghe Athletic Club, the Five Ancestors Shrine (an organization of the Chee Kung Tong in Shanghai), the Fushou Mountain (Wang Tiemin), the Jinhua Mountain (Lan Shaoting), the Dalong Mountain (Zhou Zhenkun), the Taihua Mountain, the Minxing Society (Wang Yucheng), the Xingzhong Mountain (Yang Hu, Cheng Zhuang), the Minguo Mountain, the Fuxing Gong, the Langgan Mountain, the Fuming Mountain, and the Hongshun Mutual Aid Society (Yang Wendao), among others.

The Chee Kung Tong was originally a Hongmen body among the overseas Chinese, and it built a Five Ancestors Shrine of its own on Avenue Haig in Shanghai, originally for sacrifices to the Five Ancestors of the Hongmen, but in fact an organization of the Chee Kung Tong in Shanghai.

The Wusheng Mountain had five halls—Benevolence, Righteousness, Propriety, Wisdom, and Faith—each with its own hall-master: the Renwen Hall under Zhu Zhuowen, the Yiheng Hall under Mei Guangpei, the Lide Hall under Mingde, the Zhisong Hall under Xiang Songpo, and the Xinlian Hall under Zhang Zilian. Of these five, Zhu Zhuowen and Mei Guangpei had been of the Guangdong clique, Mingde was a monk, Xiang Songpo was of the Hubei clique, and Zhang Zilian was a Hangzhou man who ran the Sanxing Cotton-and-Iron Works in Shanghai and had a retail branch on Nanjing Road.

The Wuxing Mountain grew out of the Minxing Society of the Sichuan clique, which its mountain-master Wang Yucheng enlarged into the Wuxing Mountain before the war, though it went on using the name Minxing Society for outside purposes. Wang Yucheng was an Anhui man who had taken part in the 1911 Revolution, serving as captain of the guard when Sun Yat-sen was inaugurated Provisional President at Nanjing in 1912; afterward he held a post in the Shanghai police bureau and was in charge of general affairs at the Shen Bao office for many years, and during the war took refuge in Hong Kong, where he died of illness. After the victory the Wuxing Mountain passed to the former deputy mountain-master Xu Yimin, who rose to be mountain-master. Xu was a Western-medicine doctor trained in the United States, with a thriving practice in Shanghai, and he brought a number of his American-educated classmates into the Wuxing Mountain—men such as Wei Jingzhou (deputy mountain-master, who before the war had been head of the Central Mint in Shanghai and during the war served as head of the Customs Administration of the Ministry of Finance in Chongqing, on close terms with T. V. Soong), Fan Wenzhao (deputy mountain-master, a well-known architect), Wei Banguan (before liberation chief accountant of Jardine Matheson), Sun Boqun (before liberation manager of the Fufeng Flour Mill), and Zhou Sizhong (before liberation deputy head of the China Travel Service). So there were many intellectuals within the Wuxing Mountain.

#### (ii) The Shanghai Hongmen in the 1911 Revolution

Many Hongmen men took part in the 1911 Revolution. Chen Qimei (styled Yingshi), who carried on revolutionary work in Shanghai, had ties with men of both the Green and the Hong gates. When he set off the Zhaohe warship uprising, for instance, Yang Hu, who served under him, had been a Hongmen man. Wang Yucheng, spoken of above, had also taken part in the 1911 Revolution. Besides, the noted Hongmen man Xu Langxi contributed a great deal in the 1911 Revolution. A Shaanxi man by origin, he had joined the Revolutionary Alliance while studying in Japan, and returned to China in 1910 to take up revolutionary work; to rouse the secret-society brotherhoods he too joined the brotherhood organizations, with the Hongmen as his chief focus. When the Nanjing provisional government was formed, Sun Yat-sen made Xu front-line commander-in-chief of the Northern Expedition allied forces; and soon, when north and south made peace, Xu resigned and came to Shanghai to found the newspaper Shenghuo Ribao, at the same time carrying on brotherhood work, chiefly in the Hongmen, and taking in a good many brothers. He also held important charges in the anti-Yuan movement of 1914 and the campaign against Duan Qirui in 1918.

#### (iii) The Hongmen in the Northern Expedition

Since not a few Hongmen men had given their strength in the 1911 Revolution, and because of this historical connection, Hongmen men took part in still greater numbers in the Northern Expedition, especially among the worker and peasant masses—some in mass activity, some in armed struggle. Xu Langxi, for instance, had organized an armed force in Shanghai; when Chiang Kai-shek disarmed and seized the weapons of the armed pickets, Xu went into hiding for a time, and though Chiang afterward expressed his regret to him, Xu was left dissatisfied with Chiang, and thereafter would not serve as an official of the Nanjing government, but founded the Xinhua Special School of Art in Shanghai and made himself its principal—yet without giving up his Hongmen activity, presiding over the affairs of the Guyun Mountain.

When Chiang Kai-shek launched the April 12 counterrevolutionary coup, some of the Hongmen men who had taken part in the mass movement became its victims, and some served as its executioners and accomplices. Yang Hu, the Kuomintang's Songhu garrison commander at the time, for example, was both of the "Tong" generation of the Green Gang and a chief of the Hongmen, and on April 12 he slaughtered no few Communists and revolutionary masses. Many men in the anti-Communist "Progress Society" belonged to both the Green and the Hong gates; so in the Northern Expedition the Shanghai Hongmen men included both revolutionaries and the executioners and accomplices who suppressed the revolution.

#### (iv) The Cantonese-Clique Hongmen Organizations in Shanghai

The Cantonese-clique Hongmen organizations of Shanghai were concentrated along North Sichuan Road in the Hongkou district; they were rather numerous and rather complicated. Part of them were what had come down from the Three Harmonies Society and the Small Sword Society, but the people who joined later were a mixed lot, some bad elements among them who took up improper trades—even kidnapping children, running gambling dens and brothels, and peddling narcotics. Because their interests clashed, they sometimes came to armed fights among themselves.

The Cantonese-clique Hongmen organizations bore such names as athletic clubs (like the Xinshenghe Athletic Club and the Jushenghe Athletic Club), societies (like the Xiayi Society), and associations (like the Hongshun Mutual Aid Society). Of these the largest was the Xiayi Society headed by Zheng Ziliang; after the victory in 1945 it held a demobilization rally at the Canidrome greyhound track, with four or five thousand attending, and its sphere of power reached not only the Hongkou area but into the French Concession as well. The opium and gambling of the French Concession had been in the hands of Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng, and Zheng Ziliang, active in the French Concession too, demanded a share of the spoils. During the war he had ties with the Juntong and the Loyal and Patriotic Army, and after liberation he was suppressed.

Among the Cantonese-clique Hongmen, most were businessmen in legitimate trades. Yang Wendao of the Hongshun Mutual Aid Society had joined the national-salvation resistance movement before the war and had been sentenced to ten years' imprisonment, getting out only after the victory; he went on running the Hongshun Mutual Aid Society and opened a Hongshun Primary School in the society's premises on Zhongzhou Branch Road in Hongkou.

#### (v) Hongmen Activity During the War of Resistance

During the August Thirteenth Shanghai fighting, many Hongmen men supported the resistance—setting up hospitals for the wounded, comforting the frontline troops, relieving refugees, and the like. Some ran guerrilla units too: Wang Yucheng of the Wuxing Mountain and I, for instance, ran the Southeast National Anti-Japanese Self-Defense Army. Some Wuxing Mountain brothers were then in Shanghai county, Jiading county, Jiangyin county, and elsewhere, making contact with parts of the units left over from the local defense corps, meaning to form guerrillas; Wang Yucheng and I planned to gather them under one command, and using the name Southeast National Anti-Japanese Self-Defense Army, sent Yang Mingxiao down to the countryside to make contact, and I reported the situation to the Kuomintang center by secret radio. Later the Chongqing Military Affairs Commission sent men to Shanghai to make contact with me, and I turned over to them the connections at several places; the Jiangyin unit among them had more than three hundred men and over a hundred rifles, mostly former local defense-corps members. Afterward I heard that these units all joined the New Fourth Army.

#### (vi) The Hongmen United Body in Shanghai

The Hongmen bodies had been independent, with no united body among them. In 1935 I joined the Chujing Mountain of the Hunan clique in Shanghai, and the next year transferred to the Minxing Society of the Sichuan clique. Its mountain-master, Wang Yucheng, an Anhui man, had a certain standing within the brotherhood. He had taken part in the 1911 Revolution, and felt deeply that the Hongmen had gradually lost its spirit of national revolution and sunk into a gangster force for evil, and he urged that the Hongmen organization be put in order. I felt the same, and so we set about organizing a united Hongmen body; winning the agreement of Xiang Songpo of the Wusheng Mountain and others, we launched the preparations together and that same year founded the "Hongxing Association," its meaning being "of one mind and joined strength, to revive the Hongmen." More than thirty Shanghai Hongmen bodies took part, each sending five representatives as members; the founding assembly was held at the Lord Guan Temple at Laoximen, where more than thirty directors and alternate directors and more than twenty supervisors and alternate supervisors were elected—the chief among them being Wang Yucheng, Xiang Songpo, Cheng Zhuang, Jiang Hao, Zhang Zilian, Wang Zhiben, Zheng Ziliang, Mao Yun, Chen Peide, Bai Yushan, Shi Zhenjiang, Wang Tiemin, and Long Qingquan—and Wang Yucheng and Xiang Songpo were made chairmen of the board. By the usual arrangement there is only one chairman, but within the Hongmen, in point of reputation Wang Yucheng ranked highest, while in point of strength the Wusheng Mountain was greatest, so the two men Wang and Xiang were made joint chairmen; though when it was reported to the Kuomintang party and government organs, Wang Yucheng alone was named chairman.

When the Hongxing Association was founded it had no source of funds; its office was borrowed within the Wuxing Mountain's premises in the Pudong Building, and it lived on the members' dues for its daily expenses. After its founding the Association strengthened the ties among the various Hongmen bodies, holding a meeting of the directors and supervisors once a month, which the heads of all the bodies came to, exchanging news, drawing closer, and clearing away estrangements; and after that there were no more fights and brawls between the cliques.

The year after the Hongxing Association was founded—1937—the August Thirteenth resistance broke out. After Shanghai fell, the Association ceased activity. It reconvened after the victory; Wang Yucheng having by then died of illness, Xiang Songpo was made chairman, and most of the former directors and supervisors, being in Shanghai, were elected again, with the office borrowed within the Zhengcheng Society on Weihaiwei Road.

During the war the Juntong too dabbled in brotherhood activity, setting up a "People's Mobilization Committee" in Chongqing and engaging as members Yang Qing (Shanghai clique), Fan Shaozeng (Sichuan clique), Tian Desheng (Sichuan clique), Yang Qingshan (Hankou clique), and Wei Zuomin (then chief technical inspector of the Ministry of Communications), with Dai Li making himself a member as well. After the victory, He Congliang (also named Xu Weibin, a Wuxi man) founded the China New Society Construction Promotion Association in Shanghai, nominally a national body with branches in various places and a branch in Shanghai too, engaging the heads of the Shanghai Green Gang as members and commissioners and taking in the heads of all the Hongmen bodies; but in fact only Zheng Ziliang had deep ties with the Juntong, having run Loyal and Patriotic Army activity during the war. This Promotion Association spent a great deal of money and had all the airs of a military organ, but was really just an empty shell.

#### (vii) How the Global Hongmen Fraternal Convention Came to Be Held in Shanghai

The Hongmen abroad had organizations under various names—the Chee Kung Tong, the Hongshun Hall, the Yixing Hall, and others—of which the Chee Kung Tong was the more widespread and influential in the Chinese communities of the Americas. Among the overseas Chinese there had long been a kind of occasional gathering called a "fraternal meeting," bringing together Hongmen representatives from various places for friendly activity. In the spring of 1946 the American Hongmen leaders Situ Meitang and Zhao Yu came to Shanghai one after another and proposed calling a Global Hongmen Fraternal Convention, the aim being to bring together Hongmen representatives from home and abroad in a common gathering. After representatives from home and abroad had conferred, more than twenty men were chosen as preparatory committee members—Situ Meitang, Zhao Yu, Yang Tianfu, Zhu Jinbai, Cheng Zhuang, Zhang Shucheng, Wang Zhiben, Wang Tiemin, Zheng Ziliang, Chen Mansheng, and others—with Situ Meitang presiding and myself as secretary-general in charge of the practical work. Since the representatives from home and abroad could not all arrive at once, it was decided to hold a preparatory meeting first and call the full convention at a suitable time later. On July 14 that year the preparatory committee entertained the city's press in the Western dining room of the YMCA, and I gave an account of the history of the Hongmen and the meaning of holding a fraternal meeting. On the seventeenth of the same month, a fraternal rally was held in the great hall of the Hu Society on Guizhou Road, attended by representatives from home and abroad and by the leading men of the city's Hongmen bodies, about a thousand in all; the guests included Zhao Zukang, then head of the municipal government's Public Works Bureau, Zhu Ping, head of the Land Bureau, and the dramatist and Fudan University professor Hong Shen, among many others. The presidium of the rally was Situ Meitang, Zhao Yu, Cheng Zhuang, and others; at it Cheng Zhuang reported on the history of the Hongmen and the meaning of holding the fraternal convention, the guests then offered their congratulations, and the meeting passed the proposals the preparatory committee had put forward—to build a Hongmen Shrine of the Fallen, to found a Hongmen middle school, to set up a communications office at 476 Avenue Haig, and to compile and print a global Hongmen directory—and ended with an entertainment. It had been intended to hold the enlarged fraternal convention at a suitable time, but because the situation changed, it was never held again, and the resolutions above were never carried out.

The author was in the Hongmen only a short time and saw only a little; errors and omissions in this piece are bound to occur, and I earnestly hope that historians and those in the know will supplement and correct it.
