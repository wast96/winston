## Chapter 7

"There — you're late!" Bai Ping greeted me with a lily of a smile; the café clock already showed three-fifty. I said:

"I'm sorry — have you been here long?"

"Today I came especially early, like a man waiting for his sweetheart."

"Then I have been playing the lady on purpose."

"A cup of coffee," I told the waiter, taking off my coat as I spoke.

"Why, you've dressed so smartly," she said, looking at my clothes.

"Ah," I said. "Is it because I forgot to say that very thing to you?"

And truly, today Bai Ping shone with an uncommon brilliance. She wore a white satin cheongsam worked with embroidery, a camellia of white silk in her hair, a platinum watch on her right wrist and a pure white jade bangle on her left; the diamond ring I had given her gleamed on her right hand, her nails seemed freshly lacquered white, and on the table lay a white handbag and a handkerchief of pure white linen. As though everyone around were envying me, I felt all at once an unspeakable pride. I said:

"Have you dressed like this just for me?"

"For the dinner-dance you are taking me to."

"What?" It struck me suddenly — could Stephen, knowing I would ask her, have got up this dinner-dance on purpose? I said:

"Did Stephen tell you?"

"What?" she said. "Didn't you ask me to go with you to a formal dinner-dance?"

"I did." I handed her the card.

"Stephen has a wife?" she asked the moment she read it.

"It's the first I've heard of it too."

"What — have you a wife as well?"

"If I had a wife, would I be asking you?" I said, laughing.

"Then I'm to pass for your wife?"

"No," I said. "Having no wife, I've asked a good friend to come along."

"These are matters of manners," she said. "You ought to have warned me beforehand, so nothing goes wrong on the spot."

"Thank you," I said. "We'll see how things stand when we get there — the whole business baffles me too."

Having spent a pleasant afternoon, we went off to spend an astonishing night.

No. 41308 Route Lafayette was a small foreign-style house running along the road, its garden not large but lush with flowers and trees; the roses and China roses were in their full busy bloom just then, and a wooden fence went round it, newly painted by the look of it, green as spring. There I rang the bell. When the door within opened I knew at a glance it was Stephen — Stephen in full uniform, glowing with spirit, coming lightly down the steps and saying with a smile:

"What splendid guests!"

He shook our hands, and taking Bai Ping on one arm and me on the other, walked us from the outer door to the inner. He said:

"A surprise, isn't it? It is my wife's birthday." He brought out the word "wife" especially loud.

There in the corridor, by the coat rack, I took off my overcoat, and with Bai Ping followed Stephen into an exquisite drawing room.

There were already a good many men and women in the room. Stephen first presented us to his wife, saying, half in earnest and half in jest:

"Mr. Xu and Mrs. Xu."

Bai Ping shot me a glance, her smile a lily just opening; and awkward as I felt, I could hardly deny it.

Mrs. Stephen held out a lovely hand to us in greeting, a faint sweet smile rising to her face, and said:

"Mr. Xu, it is a great honour that you would grace us. Stephen speaks of you often; I hope you will feel tonight as if you were in your own home."

Then she introduced the guests to me one by one, but always presented Bai Ping under the name "Miss Bai Ping," as though she had long known that "wife" was a name meant in jest. Half the company were American naval and army officers, most with women on their arms; besides them were people from the consulate and the embassy, a few friends from banking and commerce, and some lawyers and doctors, among whom I met Dr. Philip — a very tall man of forty-odd, with a moustache on his upper lip, of most grave and cultivated manner, his wife too gracious and warm. Of Chinese, apart from myself, there was only a Mr. Gao, the manager of the Weibai Hotel. His wife was a handsome American, much at ease in company; we had met before in many places, and today she had brought her daughter along — already a lovely girl of twenty, and so tall that, but for the introduction, I might almost have taken her for her mother's younger sister. Several of the young women guests, all pretty, seemed on close terms with Miss Gao; schoolmates, I supposed, from the American school. But of all these women the one who caught my attention most was Miss Mei Yingzi, who possessed the beauty of both West and East. Most of tonight's guests she seemed already to know, yet she made a point of mixing with those newly met, and in this new acquaintance she broke through the other's reserve at once. When our host presented me to her, she said:

"Mr. Xu, is it? It seems we ought to have known each other long ago."

"A great honour," I said, and was already being led off to meet someone else.

But I saw that Mei Yingzi's mingling never ceased. When the cherry aperitif came round she was talking with Bai Ping. I happened to be standing beside Miss Gao, and said:

"Did you know Mei Yingzi before?"

"I've met her a few times."

"At your home?"

"No," she said. "At gatherings at the Weibai Hotel."

Just then Mr. Gao, beside us, said:

"She grew up in Japan."

"Are her parents American?" I said.

"No." Mr. Gao smiled. "Her mother is American."

"Then her father is Japanese?"

"No," he said. "You've guessed wrong all round. Her father is Chinese, but has lived in Japan all along."

"Have neither of her parents come today?"

"Her father died in Japan, her mother in China; she is quite alone now."

Just then Miss Gao went off to talk with another young lady, and Mr. Gao, watching her back, said to me in a bantering tone:

"You seem to take a great interest in Miss Mei Yingzi?"

"I seem to take an interest in any woman — but only this much interest, always," I said.

"You know she is now a lady of international Shanghai, the object of pursuit by the young men of England, America, France and Japan alike," he said.

I answered him with a faint, guarded smile and began to turn the talk elsewhere.

After the drinks a servant came to call us to dinner, and we went through to the dining room.

Tonight I seemed to be the least familiar guest, and so was seated at Mrs. Stephen's right, while Bai Ping sat at the other end, at Stephen's right. Beside me was a brown-haired lady; Miss Mei Yingzi sat diagonally across from me, Dr. Philip on her right and a very handsome American officer on her left.

Before me stood a vase of fresh flowers, but it did not hinder my study of Mei Yingzi. She had the eyes of the East and the lashes of the West, the mouth of the East and the chin of the West; her nose was straight but not coarse or high, her cheeks soft, her brows lovely, her forehead open and clear, and above it lay her glossy black hair. She talked with those on either side of her in every variety of smile and tone. She wore a short-sleeved cheongsam of pure white satin, its buttons of diamond, set all round with small green jade; on her fair skin I could see no trace of powder, and her lips seemed touched with the faintest rouge. An indescribable grace flowed from her throat down to her breast, so that the evening gowns of the Western women present were all outshone before her — though I believe that in a Western gown she would have looked finer still than she did tonight. At last I began to notice that many men's eyes were stealing looks at her, and all at once I felt a strange shame; I turned from my own stolen glances and attended to my food.

So I began to talk with Mrs. Stephen. Her voice was soft and low and fine, her expression pale and gentle — a temper, it seemed, wholly unlike Mei Yingzi's. She must be twenty-six, I thought; she had a very beautiful figure, a long neck, and a fine, delicate face, and was so still and grave that when she did not smile she seemed hard to approach — altogether out of keeping, one would think, with Stephen's lively, innocent, bright, easy manner. Yet in the corners of her brows and eyes I could read no trace of inward grief or pain, and in her talk her love for Stephen showed only the deeper.

But why did Stephen always like to seek me out alone to go about with? That was my question. Naturally I would not speak to Mrs. Stephen of our revels together; yet she seemed to know the story of our outings, and so, within what she knew, I did not deny it. At last she said:

"I hear you are a confirmed bachelor?"

"I am."

"Does that mean you take an interest in no girl at all?"

"Or perhaps that I take an interest in every girl?"

"Then it is another name for a romantic trifling with the world," she said, smiling at me as if in mockery.

"No." But I said it gravely: "The interest goes no further than admiration at a distance."

"Have you no sweetheart?"

"In the past, of course, I have."

"Been crossed in love?"

"That too, once."

"Then it is a case of sour grapes," she said, smiling mockingly again.

"Perhaps."

"But surely you have also been loved?"

"It seems I have."

"Yet you believe none of it?"

"Because one day I suddenly found that I had never loved anyone — I had loved only my own imagining; and that no one had ever loved me either — they too loved only their own imaginings."

"You think that people, like Narcissus loving his own reflection, forever love only their own imagining?"

"It is all unrequited love!" I said.

"And so you despaired?" she said. "And prayed for love no more?"

"I have only my penitence," I said. "And so I hold to bachelorhood."

"How interesting," she said. Then, suddenly, watching Bai Ping pass before us, she lowered her voice and said to me with a smile:

"Then Miss Bai Ping, too, is loving her own imagining unrequited."

The remark struck me as very abrupt, and I saw at once that it was a broadcast of one of Stephen's jokes. I said:

"Do you always so believe your husband's jokes?"

"Did you not notice me talking with Bai Ping just now?"

"…" I let a smile stand in for the difficult reply.

"But I think," she said, "that tonight you have been dazzled by a new and strange radiance."

"…?" I questioned her with a silent gaze — but at once I felt Mei Yingzi's radiance flickering in my heart.

"It is Mei Yingzi, of course," she said. "She is forever bright as the sun."

"But I have always loved the lamp — because I love my own shadow in its light."

"Yet sunlight, at night, is the lamp; and lamplight, by day, is the sun."

"…" I began to perceive the beauty of Mrs. Stephen's spirit — how fine and keen her sympathy, her feeling were. Here was a temper wholly unlike Stephen's; were they, then, a happy pair?

I noticed Stephen rise to turn on the wireless: very fine music. Everyone grew quiet. Debussy, I thought — but listening on, it seemed not to be; and then Mrs. Stephen asked me softly, all at once: "Do you like Debussy?"

"A clever composer," I said. "But without depth or weight, more's the pity."

"Then you are well schooled in music."

"I would not claim that," I said. "But I love music, just as I love nature."

"…" She made no answer, knit her brows, mused a while, and then, as if drawn away by the music, wore in the corners of her eyes an unapproachable gravity. I fell silent.

After dinner we went to the reception room, now arranged like a little ballroom; Stephen found music on the wireless, and a few officers began to dance first. I asked Mrs. Stephen, close at hand, to dance.

"Forgive me," I said as we turned. "Mrs. Stephen, do you dislike this jazz?"

"I don't care much for it," she said. "But to take a turn now and then with friends is a pleasure to me all the same."

As I danced through the crowd I saw Mei Yingzi on that handsome officer's arm, a sweet smile on her face; I turned aside from her gaze and swung away, and next came upon Bai Ping and Stephen. Bai Ping tonight shone with a special brilliance, in intimate talk with Stephen; of all the couples on the floor, theirs was the handsomest.

When the piece ended, Mrs. Stephen said to me:

"You ought to dance more with Mei Yingzi tonight."

"Why?" I said.

"Because I believe you will like her."

"…" I said nothing; but when the second piece struck up I danced with a very young lady. She was shy, and unsure of her steps, yet she had a peculiar gentleness that none of the women I had known possessed. And so I said:

"May I ask the young lady's name?"

"Helen Manfield."

"A great honour, to dance with you tonight."

"…" She was silent; I could not see her face, but at my chin I felt her shy nestling, the soft strands of her hair brushing my skin. As though charged with some unlooked-for duty, I set my steps most carefully to the beat of the music, and passing through the crowd I thought suddenly of the Mrs. Manfield who had been introduced earlier. I said:

"You must be Mrs. Manfield's daughter, I think."

"Yes, sir."

"Then I hope I may see you often hereafter."

So for the next piece I asked Mrs. Manfield to dance, and said:

"Only you could be Miss Manfield's mother."

"She is still a very shy child."

"But she has a soul hard to come near."

"I hope you will guide her often."

"I hope for the honour of being your friend."

"The honour is all ours," she said.

"May I call on you?"

"You are welcome any time," she said. "My home is at No. 341, the Bakou Apartments."

In that short piece of music I found Mrs. Manfield to have a most kind and genial nature. Her husband and two sons, she told me, had gone home to serve in the army, and only this daughter kept her company, so that she was very lonely and much wished a Chinese would come often to see her. She was a stout middle-aged woman with a rich fund of smiles; and reasoning from her daughter, I thought she must have been beautiful in her youth.

Which piece it was I do not know, but I danced with Bai Ping. She said:

"You haven't danced with Mei Yingzi yet?"

"What — you watch me so closely?"

"I've noticed you take a particular interest in her today."

"…" I could find no words to answer. How came she to the very same observation as Mrs. Stephen? Was there something in my face that gave me away?

"Have I guessed right?"

"I think not."

"But you do not deny it."

"I am only wondering," I said, "on what you base your words. I have not danced a single dance with her, nor spoken a single word."

"On just that."

"But among the others," I said, "there are those I've also—"

"Against too strong a light one sees nothing, and against the dark one sees nothing either." She laughed, with a charming drollery and a sweet mockery both.

"…" I fell silent, and turning it over in myself, felt that Mrs. Stephen's saying at table that I had been dazzled by a new radiance was the chief reason I had not danced or talked with Mei Yingzi — and that my not dancing or talking with Mei Yingzi was in turn the chief reason Bai Ping now said the same. Had Mei Yingzi's radiance truly dazzled me? Did I take a particular interest in her? I did not know myself; but when I resolved inwardly to ask Mei Yingzi to dance the next piece, my heart grew all at once uneasy.

And in that unease, as a waltz struck up, I went to ask Mei Yingzi to dance — a thing that astonished my own feelings. When I had led her out and was just marvelling at the scent she wore, she said:

"I said to myself there was one distinguished man tonight who had not yet asked me to dance. So it was you."

"Was it I?" I said, low.

"I thought you meant to hold yourself aloof to the very end and never ask me at all."

"But I have asked you in the end," I said.

"Did someone warn you not to come near me?"

"Why should anyone warn me so?"

"They say, it seems, that any man who comes near me cannot help becoming one of my satellites."

"No one, it seems, fears my becoming your satellite."

"Then have you wagered with anyone," she said, with a light, golden laugh, "that not asking me to dance would be your victory?"

"Perhaps," I said. "A wager with myself."

"A wager between feeling and reason?" she said, soft as a coquette.

"No," I said. "A wager between my feeling and my will."

"But your feeling has won."

"It is my will that has won."

"Is it that your feeling did not wish to dance with me?" she asked, questioning.

"My feeling tends to halt at the distance of the aesthetic."

"I find there is no explaining it."

"For me," I said, "when I love the colour of an orange, I do not wish to eat it — that is my feeling."

"Then does your feeling not wish to come a little nearer the light?"

"A light too strong, naturally one does not wish to approach — just as I will not look the sun full in the face."

"And so you use your will to look upon the sun."

The music stopped, and as I saw her to her seat she said:

"The next piece, I shall be waiting for you still."

"Very well; thank you."

For the next three pieces I danced with Mei Yingzi. I never once asked her address, nor gave any sign of wanting her for a friend. But I found that she seemed bent on drawing one more satellite to her, to conquer me.

Later, when I was with Mrs. Stephen, she asked me:

"Beside the sun, do you still long for the lamplight?"

"I do," I said. "I love my own shadow in the lamplight."

"I think Miss Helen Manfield is like the lamplight." She glanced at Helen Manfield and said: "Now I am easy that you won't lose your head over Mei Yingzi," she added, smiling.

Mrs. Stephen seemed wholly put up to it by Stephen; the whole conversation seemed bent on probing the psychology of my bachelorhood, dealing my bachelorhood every kind of blow and threat and gibe, till I repented of having talked so much.
