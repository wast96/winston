Chapter 6. Secretary to the Central Committee
Traveling by the same boat with me from Jiangxi to Shanghai was Rao Shushi of the Jiangxi League Provincial Committee, who was being transferred to the League Central to serve as secretary-general. While I was working at the Jiangxi Provincial Committee, I had often met him in connection with the work, but we spoke only of work.
After I reached Shanghai, I was assigned to work in the Secretariat of the Party Central. When the Central Political Bureau held meetings, I was the secretary who kept the record, and at the same time I served concurrently as a confidential courier of the Party Central. During this period the actual person in charge of the Party Central was Comrade Zhou Enlai, a member of the Standing Committee of the Central Political Bureau, secretary-general, and head of the Organization Department. Chen Tanqiu had been transferred to the Central Organization Department as confidential secretary and had become an important assistant to Zhou Enlai. The reason I was transferred to work in the Central Secretariat was that Comrade Chen Tanqiu had recommended me to the Party Central. While I was working together with Comrade Chen Tanqiu in Jiangxi, I had seen him often drawing a circle or some other symbol in an account book. When I asked him what it meant, he said this was a shorthand of his own devising, imitating the ancients' way of keeping records by knotting cords: using the daily dates in the account book, he set down the important matters of each day with symbols such as circles, X-marks, crosses, and the like, to make them easy to recall afterward. After I became a secretary in the Central Secretariat, I learned this shorthand method of his for taking the minutes of meetings, and afterward would put them into written text and send them to the responsible comrade who had chaired the meeting for approval.
Though as a secretary I was only an ordinary working cadre, and only for a short few months, it greatly widened my horizons and taught me much experience of struggle in the white areas. In particular, after the leading comrades of the Central learned of Xiyan's death, they were all very sympathetic and caring toward me. Seeing that I had some talent and, more than that, revolutionary aspiration, they paid all the more attention to fostering and educating me. From their teaching by word and example as they discussed problems and studied the work, I was deeply benefited, and I felt that these few months of practical work gained me even more than studying at a Party school would have. My later ability to do underground work in the white areas single-handed was inseparable from the education I received in these few months.
I also served concurrently as a confidential courier of the Central, often making contact with the underground couriers who came to Shanghai from the various provinces to connect with the Central. In charge of the Party's underground courier work at that time were Comrades Wu Defeng (director of the Central Liaison Bureau) and Gong Yinbing. Those who did the Party's underground courier work in the big cities were mostly women, because they could conveniently make use of all sorts of social connections, disguise themselves as women of every kind of standing, and adapt to every kind of change, being less likely to draw the enemy's notice. But those who went to the mountain districts and rural base areas were mostly male comrades, since a woman comrade long accustomed to city life could never look quite like a peasant woman when disguised as one.
While I was working in the Central organ, I again ran into Comrade He Chang, whom I had already come to know back at Wuhan. He Chang was a native of Lishi in Shanxi, a member of the Fifth Central Committee of the Communist Youth League and secretary of the Hubei Provincial Committee; at the Party's Sixth Congress he was elected a member of the Central Committee at the same time as Comrade Guan Xiangying. Tall and elegant, brimming with talent, he was much admired by everyone. He too knew that I had once worked at Wuhan. At this meeting he first spoke of Xiyan's death as a heavy loss to the Party, and consoled me not to grieve too much and to be sure to restrain my sorrow... At that time, although he was a member of the Central Committee, he did not work in the Party Central organ but was often sent by the Central to inspect the work in Hunan, Hubei, and other provinces, and when he came to Shanghai to attend meetings convened by the Central he stayed at a hotel. I remember he stayed at the Mengyuan Hotel (later renamed the Shenjiang Hotel), because the odd-job man at that hotel was a comrade of our underground Party, so staying there was comparatively safe. As a confidential courier of the Central, I had contact with all who came from the provinces to find the Central. When Comrade He Chang came to Shanghai, I too often took documents to the hotel where he stayed, and so we gradually grew familiar with each other.
I was often ill after childbirth, and having learned of Xiyan's death I grieved too much, so that I grew ever more haggard and thin. After several meetings with He, our understanding deepened, and he sought me out for an individual talk, giving me an education in the revolutionary outlook on life. He said: "You should not always be so grieved, so haggard. Though you work actively and hard, you should not always carry this sort of sorrowful mood. We took an oath to join the Party, willing to sacrifice ourselves for the cause of communism; revolutionary struggle is long and arduous, and can never be all smooth sailing. Bear in mind Comrade Lenin's teaching: the weapon by which we the proletariat defeat the enemy is to organize, organize, and again organize; to study, study, and again study; to fight, fight, and again fight! We should not sink into dejection at a setback, but face difficulties head-on, firmly believe that the revolution will certainly triumph, and steadfastly keep a spirit of revolutionary optimism; only so can it do good to the revolutionary work and to one's own body and mind. A proletarian revolutionary woman, moreover, must oppose feudal ideas; she must on no account hold the old ritual notions of 'faithful to one husband unto death' and of raising chastity arches—though of course she must also oppose an undisciplined, unorganized, dissolute romantic style of life." He also told me of how Soviet women rouse themselves to self-reliance and strength, to encourage me to throw off my dejected mood and rally myself early.
Such talks, both warm and caring, showed his noble character all the more, and I was moved and heartened by them, so that not only did my spirit grow daily brighter and a smile begin to come to my face, but the feeling between us deepened all the more. At last, one day, he asked me frankly: "Comrade Mulan, do you think it possible that I, like Comrade Xiyan, might join with you as a revolutionary companion?" I nodded and said: "Let me report and ask the organization for instructions before deciding, shall I?" He said: "It is right to treat it so seriously; I await your answer."
After turning the matter over again and again, I reported it to Comrade Zhou Enlai. Enlai said: "Very good; Comrade He Chang is worthy of your love, and I agree to your union." And so, with the organization's approval, and again without holding any wedding ceremony, we began to live together in a hotel.
He Chang and Guan Xiangying had both taken part in the work of founding the League, and while working at Anyuan had been well acquainted with Li Lisan, Liu Shaoqi, Chen Tanqiu, and others; he especially revered Comrade Zhou Enlai. For at that time, though Xiang Zhongfa was nominally the number-one figure of the Party Central, in real virtue and ability he was not equal to it. In those days the Party Central practiced collective leadership, and the chief responsible person directing the actual work was Comrade Zhou Enlai, who not only had very rich practical work experience but enjoyed very high prestige within the Party. Li Fuchun and Deng Xiaoping, who worked in the Party Central, had joined a communist group while on the work-study program in France, and after returning home had taken part one after another in the Central's work; He Chang had had working relations with them too. These leading comrades all knew the two of us and all warmly approved of our union, so that after we married the comrades came one after another to congratulate us both.
Only Rao Shushi, learning of it, sneered at me with cold irony. Once he mocked me, saying: "Well now! You've climbed up onto a high branch—keeping the minutes for the Central Political Bureau's meetings and married to Central Committee member He Chang; how could you still have eyes for a small person like me?" Hearing this, I truly could not bear it, and thought: I, Huang Mulan, could work independently just as well even if I had not married He Chang—did I marry in order to climb a high branch? So I retorted on the spot, saying that what he said was sheer subjectivist nonsense, measuring a gentleman's heart with a mean man's yardstick. After some wrangling we broke up in mutual displeasure. When I got back I was still very angry, and I told the leadership of the matter, and proposed that from now on I was unwilling to go on doing the work of connecting with the League Central and making contact with Rao Shushi, and asked the Central to change to someone else. But that day I did not meet Comrade Zhou Enlai and could not report to him directly. The next day the Central Political Bureau met again, and there was no chance to bring the matter up. After the meeting, riding a rickshaw home, I was still sulking to myself, dwelling on how this Rao Shushi was a man with no moral sense, who did not respect women cadres and was always thinking the worst of people. Such irony and mockery I could say I had never met with since joining revolutionary work... The more I thought the more wretched I felt, and getting down from the rickshaw I carelessly left my little leather handbag behind on it. There was not much money in the bag, but it held a book of meeting minutes. By the time I remembered, the rickshaw had long since vanished.
This was no small emergency, and I hurried back along the way to give chase. I was living then on Xinzha Road, and the Political Bureau meeting had been in the upper story of an antique shop run by our own people on Nanjing West Road. I chased all the way there but did not catch the rickshaw; my heart truly full of anxiety and regret, wretched beyond words, I walked at a loss from Nanjing West Road all the way to the Bund, and sat down on an iron bench by the Huangpu River. I could not help recalling many past things: why should a woman comrade who comes out to make revolution still have to suffer this kind of undeserved wrong? The leading comrades of the Central, and Comrade Zhou Enlai in particular, had all warmly congratulated me on my union with He Chang and had encouraged me greatly. I had never thought this Rao Shushi could be so narrow-minded, that he should mock and attack a revolutionary comrade like this. Thinking back, my union with He Chang I had considered with all seriousness, and it had had Comrade Enlai's encouragement and support; on what grounds did that Rao Shushi insult my character and wound my self-respect... Thinking this way and that, it stirred up all the more my longing for Xiyan, and so there arose in me over the twists of my own fate an unspeakable, unbearable mood of anguish. What most galled me was this: since childhood I had striven to be first in everything and had never made a mistake; I had changed many posts, and the leadership always thought highly of me; this transfer to be secretary was an even greater trust and cultivation—how could I be so useless as, in a daze, to lose the minute-book on a rickshaw? Although the symbols I recorded no one else could read, to have made so great a blunder was sure to earn criticism and disciplinary action. Even if the leadership could forgive me, I myself felt I had failed the organization's trust and truly had no face to meet the leadership or He Chang. And so there arose in me the muddled notion of taking my own life; I thought, enough, enough—with a heart full of anguish and nowhere to pour it out, I might as well end this life. In that flash of a thought I threw myself into the Huangpu River, seeking only to die and have done. It was still the chilly early spring, when warmth and cold alternate; I had on a thin silk-padded cheongsam. As I bobbed in the water, I was spotted by the police of the water public-security bureau and rescued, and sent to a relief home for disaster victims for the time being. The police who saved me from the water and the people at the relief home asked me: "So young—why did you throw yourself into the river to kill yourself?" By this time my head had cleared, and I understood that I must on no account reveal the truth, lest I implicate the organization and my comrades. So I made up a story, saying that I had lost heart in love and, wretched, had come from Nanjing (where the Kuomintang reactionaries' "Central Government" was) to Shanghai to find my cousin, but that he had moved and I had not found him, and my handbag had been stolen by a thief, so that penniless and a stranger in a strange place, without even the fare back to Nanjing, in desperation and unable to think it through, I had for a moment thrown myself into the river. They asked me my cousin's address in Shanghai; I said I did not know the new address, and that if I had known it I would not have thrown myself into the Huangpu. They asked my cousin's name, and by then I ardently wished to return to the embrace of the organization, so I gave the alias of Comrade Zhang Guohua, an underground courier I regularly kept in contact with, as my cousin's name. Because I had made up this story plausibly, and it accorded well with my age and disguised standing, the water public-security bureau and the relief home all believed it and were very sympathetic toward me. The next day, in the "Local News" column of the *Shen Bao*, there appeared a report headed "Young Maiden Saved from Suicide over Lost Love." The moment Comrade Enlai saw this report, he thought of how I had not come home the day before and my whereabouts were still unknown, and how the "cousin's" name given in the report was precisely the alias of the underground courier Zhang Guohua, and judged that this "young maiden" who had thrown herself into the river was in all likelihood me. He waited two more days, and I still had not turned up, which the more convinced him that this maiden was I; so on the third day after the report appeared in the paper he had Zhang Guohua go, in the standing of my "cousin," to the relief home to bring me back. When Comrades Enlai and Deng Yingchao had heard my account of the general course of the affair, they gave me a serious criticism: "Comrade Mulan, we transferred you to work at the Central out of the Party's care for you, wishing to foster you and raise your working ability and level of struggle—how is it you cannot get rid of the incorrect petty-bourgeois moods? When you walked out of your family to join the revolution, though at first it was only to oppose the old-style arranged marriage, yet once you had set out on the revolutionary road you should have put work first and observed organizational discipline. What Comrade Rao Shushi said was wrong, and he should not have mocked and taunted you like that; but what is that worth making so much of? Every one of us has shortcomings and errors in the work, and every one is severely criticized by the Party—far more severely than what Rao Shushi said. If a bit of criticism or a little 'wrong' sends you off to throw yourself into the Huangpu, how many lives would you have? Having given our lives to the Party, they are no longer our own, and we must never lightly kill ourselves the moment things go a little against us. To take your life so lightly is a sign of an impure Party spirit. Suicide is a petty-bourgeois impulse of emotion; though only a muddled thought of a single instant, it is a grave, mistaken act. Do you not know Comrades Qu Qiubai and Yang Zhihua? A pity you do not yet know of the exemplary conduct of the two of them when they married: when they were preparing to unite, Comrade Zhihua notified her former husband Shen Jianlong (son of Shen Dingyi, an early Party member), and on receiving the letter Shen hurried to Shanghai to offer congratulations, and the three of them jointly published in the newspaper a notice of the joining and parting of their marriages, declaring that they would remain good friends thereafter—that is noble friendship. Besides, you had already retorted upon Rao Shushi at the time and paid him back in his own coin. We need not raise the matter with Rao again; I believe he will himself come to regret it, that he should not have spoken those cutting, mocking words to you in so crude a manner and wounded your self-respect, so as to bring on so great an affair. But this too is only a question of attitude; you need not bear him a grudge, and from now on, when you meet him, do not go criticizing him again. You must be sure to remember that, as a Communist, no matter when you meet a setback, you must report to the organization in good time, and do as the organization tells you." I put in a word: "That day's affair was one I had no time to report to you; had I reported it a little sooner and been able to receive your education and help, I would not have had such muddled thoughts." Comrade Enlai went on: "Good, it is well that you understand. You should understand the importance of mutual respect and stronger unity among revolutionary comrades. Sometimes misunderstandings arise among comrades, and the other's criticism is too severe, the words too heavy; so long as it is well meant, you should examine yourself modestly; and even if it does not accord with the facts, you should take the attitude of 'correct it if you have the fault, guard against it if you have not.' You must neither feel resentment against the one who criticizes you, nor take revenge with mockery, still less give rise in yourself to a pessimistic, negative mood. Having given yourself to the revolution, you must proceed in all things from the overall revolutionary situation, and not haggle over personal grudges. You are one who has read the old books well, so you should have the cultivation of 'demanding much of oneself and asking little of others'—that is, of being strict with oneself and lenient toward others. Hereafter, in whatever you meet, you should be calm and think more with reason." I said I was willing to accept the organization's disciplinary punishment. Comrade Enlai said again: "You have already confessed to the organization, made clear the whole course of the affair, and recognized your own error. As for the lost minute-book, though it cannot be recovered, we have long known that you learned from Comrade Tanqiu and devised a set of symbol-shorthand ciphers of your own that no one else can read. So it will not leak the Party's secrets—you may set your mind at ease. What is more, the things you said to the public-security bureau and the relief home under questioning were all very clever and adroit, revealing not the least of your true identity or the Party's secrets, and you did not forget that you wished to return to the Party's embrace. Had you wanted to leave the revolution and the Party organization, you would have written to your parents and gone back to them. But you had no such thought." (In truth, how could I not have thought at that time of going home?) Comrade Enlai seemed to see through my secret thoughts, but without laying them bare, he went on: "If you had wanted to go home and leave the organization, you would not have said that Zhang Guohua was your cousin and that, he having moved, you could not find him. This shows that at the relief home you had already recognized your error in your mind and were doing all you could to get back into the organization. This time it was only a momentary muddle in your thinking, not a wavering born of pessimism and despair over the revolution; you have examined yourself, and even asked for punishment, so we consider that to give you a criticism, to help and educate you, is enough—there is no need to give you a further disciplinary penalty. But judging from your present mood, I think your petty-bourgeois tail has not yet been wholly cut off, that you have not been thoroughly remolded. In this you must be sure to strengthen your Party sense of organization and discipline and your firm faith in the victorious future of the revolution, and put in effort to study and work hard, strengthening the tempering and cultivation of your Party spirit." Comrade Enlai's earnest teaching, at once grave and warm, weighty with meaning, made my heart suddenly clear, and I have never forgotten it. From then on, however great the hardships and turns I met on the revolutionary road, however grave the setbacks, blows, or misunderstandings, I could bear them with a strong will, and never again did the thought of taking my own life arise in me.
When He Chang learned of it, he did not criticize me, but only asked whether I felt dissatisfied after marrying him, and regretted it. I said that this was by no means my meaning; it was only that I could not bear Rao Shushi's mockery and insult, and regretted all the more the loss of the minute-book. He did not criticize Rao Shushi either, but only said: "We two married because we are of one mind and one purpose; naturally there will be those who grow jealous, and there will be narrow-minded people who mock and taunt you—this too is unavoidable, and you need not take it to heart any longer." Hearing He Chang say this, and thinking of his largeness of heart and his loving comfort of me, my heart eased greatly, and so I let the matter go. After this I saw Rao Shushi only once, when I went to the East China Bureau just after Shanghai's liberation, and thereafter I had no further contact with him at all.
In the spring of 1929, a strike struggle was brewing among the Shanghai workers, and the Party Central mobilized the cadres of its organs to go down to the grass roots to experience how labor is the source that creates everything, and to be tempered in real struggle. I asked the organization to send me down to a factory, so that my revolutionary will might be tempered stronger in real struggle. The Party organization approved my request. And so I ended my secretarial work in the Party Central organ and threw myself actively into the front line of the workers' movement.
