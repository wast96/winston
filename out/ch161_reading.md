### Rubber Checks

The checks that banks and native banks issue to their depositors carry, on the bank's side, the obligation to pay them out, and on the depositor's side may be used in place of ready cash, the sum written in at will, great or small—so that they are highly convenient. But in these days of declining morals, your common run of sharpers has turned this very convenience to account, writing out rubber checks by the score to work their swindles. What is a "rubber check"? Suppose that party A holds a checking account at some bank, but has long since drawn out his balance and left only the barest sliver of silver on deposit, and yet goes on writing checks at will for this sum or that in place of cash, passing them off all over town. When the holder takes such a check to the bank to draw the money, the bank, finding no funds left in A's account, of course refuses to pay—and that piece of paper is what is called a "rubber check."

Nowadays the old, established banks, before letting a depositor draw a checkbook, require a first deposit of five hundred dollars—three hundred at the very least—and an introduction by someone known to them besides (this person being one of some standing with the bank), before they will issue it. To draw a checkbook at a native bank, one needs an acquaintance to stand sponsor still more surely (if, say, one wishes to run an account for a certain sum, the sponsor first guarantees it, so that should the account later be left unsettled, the guarantor is made to pay the whole; for dealings at a native bank are all on overdraft). Only, since this year, certain native banks such as the Fuyuan and the Yintai, in expanding their business and to court their customers, will issue a checkbook without any acquaintance's introduction—though such an account is not an overdraft one.

Those who have suffered by rubber checks are past all counting—most of them like the mute who swallows the bitter coptis root: the bitterness is there, but he cannot tell it. Some, where the sum on the check is very large, have no choice but to go to law for a remedy. But by the time it comes to that, the drawer will as likely as not have gone to ground, keeping out of sight, or fled clean away, so that there is no serving him a summons; and in the end the holder can only put it down to his own bad luck. Hence in the dealings of the marketplace nowadays, and between private persons too, whenever a check changes hands men look only to the everyday credit of the shop or the person, and settle by that whether to take it or refuse it. If the shop or the person has always been of shining repute, and the check is sure to be honoured when it falls due, with no rubber-check trick to be feared, then it is gladly taken. But if the shop or the person has always treated the word "credit" as child's play, then even a check that is no rubber check but has real funds behind it will find no one who dares trust it and take it. The payee, where money is concerned, cannot but deal warily, that he may not be cheated.

There is yet another sort of rogue, who gathers a gang, sets up on the spur of the moment a native bank of his own—a sharper's bank—and makes it his whole trade to pass rubber checks and rubber "cashier's notes" (the note a native bank draws on itself is called a cashier's note) all over town to swindle men of their goods. The takers, supposing that a note the bank draws on itself can carry no rubber-check flaw, accept them every one. But when the notes fall due and are brought in to be cashed, the bank has already failed and shut its doors, and the swindlers have long since slipped far away, none knows where—and the takers can only cry out to no purpose. A sharper's bank as carefully planned as this, before it closes its doors, looks to the eye for all the world like a grand and stately financial house, so that men are easily taken in by it; but its true inner workings are nothing more than a native bank run on the Empty-Fort ploy.
