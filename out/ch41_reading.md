## Toshizō at Fushimi

Fushimi —

A post-station of seven thousand houses.

Three ri south of Kyoto down the Fushimi road, a town where even at high noon in summer the mosquitoes are fierce. Coming down the highway into this post-town, the first quarter is Senbon-chō. Then,

{j} Torii-chō,

{j} Genba-chō,

{j} they follow one on another, and presently there is a barrier gate.

In Nabeshima-chō, past that barrier gate, stands the grand building of the Fushimi Magistrate's office, which for two hundred years and more since Ieyasu had boasted the authority of the Tokugawa. Its grounds, broad enough to have become a military barracks after the Restoration, were enclosed by a gray plastered wall.

When Kondō, Toshizō, and the rest moved into this Fushimi Magistrate's office and,

{j} "Shinsengumi Headquarters"

{j} hung up the placard, the corps had dwindled to a bare sixty or seventy men. Just as Toshizō had foreseen, many, seeing that night how the times were turning, had in the end never come back to the barracks.

The main body of the shogunal army was at Osaka. The front line against the "Palace party" — the Satsuma han and the rest — in Kyoto was the Fushimi Magistrate's office. That the Shinsengumi guarding it numbered sixty or seventy men (even granting that a part of the Aizu troops were there too) was surely too thin by far.

Besides that, one cannon.

"There's no managing with this."

Even Kondō was aghast, and in great haste he negotiated with the shogunal-army officers at Osaka and with the Aizu han, and had them pick out able hands from among their men to reinforce him.

A strength of one hundred and fifty men.

"There now — this puts us into some sort of shape at last."

{j} and Kondō, too, was relieved.

Okita Sōji, even after they entered the new barracks, kept to his bed.

Many were the days he scarcely set his chopsticks to what the commissary carried in to him on the tray.

"Sōji, won't you eat?"

{j} Toshizō would come into the room once a day and say, with a hard face.

This past month, Okita's wasting had grown marked.

"Don't eat, and you'll die."

"I've no appetite."

"Are you taking the Koryōsan?"

It was the medicine handed down in Toshizō's family home.

"Yes. When I take it, I seem to feel a little life come back into my body. Though it may be only my fancy."

"It's not your fancy. It's the medicine I once peddled about. It works."

"Yes."

He was smiling.

The First Unit that Okita had once led was now held in addition by Nagakura Shinpachi, captain of the Second Unit.

"Shinpachi was saying he's hard-pressed with the load, and needs you to get well soon."

"Is that so."

The look on his face as he nodded was already weary. That so little talk should be a burden to him meant the sickness had advanced very far indeed.

Presently the Chōshū troops began landing in force at the beach of Nishinomiya in Settsu and moving into Kyoto.

"Chōshū?"

Kondō, from his shogunate-supporting stance, hated Chōshū above all. It was no wonder he was startled to hear of the Chōshū troops landing at Nishinomiya. Chōshū was a han that, for the crime of throwing Kyoto into turmoil at the so-called Hamaguri Gate affair in the summer of Genji 1, had had its lord stripped of court rank at the shogunate's pressing of the Court, and stood now in submission, awaiting its punishment.

And now, without so much as waiting on an Imperial command, it had moved its troops as it pleased, landed at Nishinomiya without leave, and, more than that, was coming into Kyoto.

"What do they take the shogunate for?"

{j} Kondō raged.

But the Satsuma han, already in Kyoto, had worked upon the palace, and Chōshū's treatment had wholly reversed. Not only was the court rank of the lord and his son restored, but,

{j} "Enter the capital and guard the Nine Gates"

{j} — such an Imperial command had gone out.

It was four years since men of Chōshū had entered the capital, the first time since Genji 1. The common folk of Kyoto had always favored Chōshū, and when on the twelfth of the twelfth month of Keiō 3 the Chōshū Kiheitai marched proudly into the capital, the townsmen of Kyoto, seeing the house crest on their ammunition-boxes,

{j} "It's the Lord Chōshū!"

{j} cried out in wonder, and there were some who wept and pressed their palms together in worship,

{j} "Fearful, fearful,"

{j} while others whispered. Now that the Chōshū army had entered the capital, the people of Kyoto must have judged, from all they knew of that han's temper, that war could no longer be escaped.

From that day, Chōshū units entered the capital almost daily, until on the seventeenth the main force under the commander-in-chief Mōri Heirokurō (Kai-no-kami) landed at Uchide beach in Settsu and, hauling its gun-carriages, began moving toward Kyoto.

And these Chōshū units passed in state right before the gate of the Fushimi Magistrate's office, where the Shinsengumi was quartered.

"Are we to let this pass?"

{j} and from the early dawn of the eighteenth Kondō made to form up a column and set out to lay his views before Nagai Genba-no-kami Naomune, the shogunal chief inspector, who at that time still remained at Nijō Castle.

"Give it up, now."

Toshizō stopped him. For all the politics-loving Kondō's dashing about at this late hour, the situation was past the handling of a man who was a mere country politician. The shogun Yoshinobu had already returned to the throne the power held since Ieyasu, and the proclamation of the Restoration of Imperial Rule had gone out besides.

"Toshi, there's a thing you don't know. This Restoration of Imperial Rule is all a Satsuma plot. The shogunate has been outdone."

{j} So Kondō told Toshizō the inside of the political situation, picked up at second hand; but such things held no interest for Toshizō.

"Kondō-san, the time for parleys and go-betweens and debate is past."

{j} said Toshizō.

"It's by war that the thing will be settled now. It's come to that."

"I know that. It's to urge just that on Nagai Genba-no-kami that I'm going."

"It's needless."

"What?"

"You're the commander-in-chief of this headquarters. Go dashing about and the men won't hold together. War might begin this very moment, mind you."

"Toshi, you're a fool."

"A fool?"

"In the Shinsengumi, and knowing nothing of the affairs of the realm. Knowing nothing of the strategy of the realm. It is by ordering one's strategy before the battle that one wins."

"I know that. But we are no more than one corps of the shogunal army. The strategy of the realm is not for the commander of a single corps to make; leave it to the great men at Osaka. You — do not move."

But Kondō set out.

Mounted on a white horse, with an escort of twenty corps men, all of them raw recruits, he made for Kyoto and rode along the Takeda Highway.

***

The Magistrate's office had a watchtower.

It was a structure just like a smaller version of the drum-tower of the Hongan-ji, and from the top one could see at a glance the forest of the Gokō-no-miya before one's eyes, the hills of Momoyama, and beyond them the streets of Fushimi.

At noon that day Toshizō climbed the watchtower.

It was because along the highway below, yet another echelon of Chōshū troops had begun to pass.

They numbered a little over two hundred.

It was a strange sight. In Western dress with white sashes bound about them, wearing the two swords, every man shouldering a new-model Minié rifle of fifteen-millimeter bore, and even the officers carrying guns.

(Got up like a lot of ragpickers.)

{j} thought Toshizō.

But for that very reason their movement would be nimble, he thought; and remembering how, four years before, in Genji 1, the Chōshū troops who had come storming up this same highway had had a general in a wind-folded court cap and a war-surcoat, wearing a brocade court-robe beneath armor handed down through generations, with all his followers in Warring-States harness and no firearms but matchlocks, he could not but feel the gulf between then and now.

(Four years, since then.)

A mere four years. Yet Chōshū's armament had utterly changed. This expel-the-barbarian, West-hating han, in the course of enduring the shogunate's first and second punitive expeditions, had of necessity converted its military system to the Western style. The Satsuma and Tosa units in Kyoto were equipped the same as this Chōshū.

(It seems the world is changing.)

Toshizō looked down on their array with the feeling of a man waking from sleep.

The gun-carriages went rumbling by, hauled along.

These, too, were new-model ordnance. Four-kin mountain guns, they were called; the bore inside was cut with a spiral groove, and the shell flew more than a thousand meters.

Against that, the single cannon the Shinsengumi possessed was a home-made piece from the Edo ordnance works, its bore smooth, with an effective range of only some seven hundred meters.

Set beside the look of these Chōshū soldiers, the shogunal army's equipment was that of the Chōshū of four years before.

The shogunal infantry, true, were on the French model, but the hatamoto units and the han troops — Aizu and the rest — were almost all in the Japanese style, their main weapons sword, spear, and matchlock; and the few Western guns they did have were of the Dutch Gewehr type, crude old muskets with not even a rear sight.

(Can we win, I wonder.)

But in numbers the shogunate's side would probably be more than ten times over.

(Push with numbers, and we can win.)

{j} Toshizō thought again.

As the Chōshū soldiers passed, a light midday rain came pattering down.

The sun was shining.

(Strange weather.)

{j} and as he made to leave the watchtower window, of a sudden, on the road below, he caught sight of a woman opening a snake's-eye umbrella with a light snap.

(Ah — is that Oyuki?)

{j} and by the time he thought it, the woman had already turned into an alley that led through to Kyōmachi-dōri.

Toshizō ran down.

He flew out the gate.

"What is the matter?"

{j} and one of the corps men by the gate came running up.

"Nothing."

Toshizō wore a sour face. But standing planted in the road with that same look, unable to check the feeling boiling up in him,

{j} "H-here —"

{j} he said, the words bursting out of him.

"Did you not see a woman, just now, after the Chōshū men had passed? Young — no, young, but a woman past her first youth, I should think. Her eyebrows not shaved. She carried a snake's-eye umbrella. Such a woman passed by about this gate and vanished into that alley there. Did you..."

"Hijikata-sensei."

{j} The corps man had evidently felt, after all, that there was something strange in Toshizō's behavior.

"We have been standing here all this while watching the Chōshū soldiers. But a woman such as that —"

Toshizō had already begun to walk.

That alley —

{j} Once he stepped into its mouth, the corps men's eyes were on him no longer.

Toshizō broke into a run down the dark alley, heedless of how he looked.

He came out onto Kyōmachi-dōri.

(Not here.)

To right and left the street was almost too bright.

(Was it an illusion?)

No — the very sound of the umbrella snapping open still lingered in his ears. And yet, come to think of it, could the sound of an umbrella opening really carry from that high watchtower?

Oyuki, about that time, was staying at a low-eaved inn on Kyōmachi-dōri called the Yutōya.

Since receiving Toshizō's letter, Oyuki had come secretly to Fushimi twice already.

She had no intention of meeting him.

(He did not come to say goodbye. He had written that he wished to go to the battlefield as a samurai should, without meeting her. And yet in the same letter he had written that, if they met, he might perhaps be changed.)

This was the first time Oyuki had seen Toshizō's hand, and what surprised her first was how very feminine the character of his writing was.

(Is this the Hijikata Toshizō who made the streets of Kyoto tremble?)

{j} was the thought his prose gave her. Not even a woman would write in a manner so lingering and unbroken.

(He is by no means a gentle man. Nor a warm-hearted one. And yet — how is one to put it? Could there be a man so weak of heart as this?)

Oyuki wished to see this weak-hearted man Toshizō just once, if only from afar, and then to part from him. That wish had brought her to this town.

(Is he no longer here, I wonder.)

Within the plastered wall of the Magistrate's office it was always so still that one could not believe several hundred men were there.

Today, in the morning, she had watched from under the eaves as Kondō rode out.

At midday she had watched the Chōshū men pass.

But Toshizō himself, alone of them all, was never anywhere to be seen.

(Perhaps the bond between us was thin from the first.)

Oyuki was beginning to give it up. For just one brief span of a long life, that man had passed by like a shadow. Perhaps the bond was no more than that.

Toshizō took a late midday meal.

He napped a while.

A gunshot sounded in the distance and echoed off the mountain behind, but it was a single report, and then silence. Toshizō woke. The watch in his breast pointed to half past four.

"What was that just now?"

{j} and he went out onto the open veranda.

As it happened, Nagakura Shinpachi was in the garden.

"Who knows,"

{j} said Nagakura.

"Some han or other must be at drill, I suppose."

"A drill of a single shot?"

Toshizō cocked his head.

And well he might.

For this single gunshot was fated to place the command of the Shinsengumi thereafter in Toshizō's hands.

At that same hour —

{j} Kondō, with some twenty corps men before and behind him, came along the Fushimi road to Sumizome.

There stood the Fushimi residence of the Owari Tokugawa house.

Beside it was an empty house, its old lattice exposed to the highway.

That from between the slats of that lattice a single gun let its muzzle peep faintly out, not one man of the column noticed.

Inside the empty house lay in wait the remnants of Itō Kashitarō's band — Tomiyama Yahei, Shinohara Tainoshin, Abe Jūrō, Kanō Michinosuke, Sahara Tarō.

They had learned that Kondō had set out for Kyoto that morning, and had fixed on today as the day of their revenge.

They had spied out even this — that Kondō, this day, had called at Nijō Castle and at his mistress's house on the Horikawa, and that past two o'clock he had come onto the Fushimi road.

"There are twenty corps men."

{j} said Shinohara.

"But look at their faces and there's not one I know. They seem to be nothing but useless new recruits. Loose off a single shot and charge in, and they'll scatter and run..."

They meant to avenge the Aburanokōji on the Fushimi road. Every one of them, having made a name as a swordsman in their Shinsengumi days, felt no fear of the numbers of Kondō's party. They were all, now, quartered in borrowed lodging at the Satsuma residence in Kyoto.

Presently Kondō came on, urging his horse at a brisk clip-clop.

(He's come.)

{j} and Shinohara Tainoshin closed his left eye. Squeezing his finger, he let the trigger fall.

With a roar an eight-monme ball leapt out.

The ball bit into Kondō's right shoulder as he sat his horse, and split the shoulder-blade.

"Now!"

{j} and Itō's remnant band sprang out into the road.

Kondō, true to himself, did not fall from the saddle; he bent low over it and set off down the highway at a flying gallop.

Shinohara and the others gave chase, cutting down two or three corps men in a twinkling, but in the end they could not bring a blade to bear on Kondō himself.

Kondō sank down into the seat of the saddle and, pressing a hand to the wound in his right shoulder, galloped on.

The instant he galloped in through the gate of the Fushimi headquarters, he threw off the horse and went in at the entrance hall.

He met Toshizō in the corridor.

"What's happened?"

"Fetch a doctor. Yes — a surgeon."

Kondō went into his own room and only then let himself collapse. The blood had begun to wet the matting.

Toshizō ordered Nagakura and the others to search the town of Fushimi, and, until the doctor came, stripped off Kondō's clothes and washed the wound with shōchū.

"Toshi — how bad is the wound?"

His face was twisted with pain.

"It'll be nothing much."

"I should have listened to you and given it up today. How's the bone — the bone? If the bone is done for, I'll never use a sword again."

Oyuki, about that time, passed before the barracks and slipped quietly back to the Yutōya.
