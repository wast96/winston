## Chapter 41

While I was still waiting, the nurse came in and said someone had come to see me. At once I thought of Bai Ping; but the nurse held the spring-door open and stood there—this was the posture she often took when bringing in a meal—and the person she now showed in carried in one hand a square rattan basket and cradled in the other some pink-hued camellias, the blossoms hiding her whole face; yet from the figure I recognized her as Ah Mei, and a kind of disappointment stole over my soul, for this settled it that Bai Ping would not come today. And ever since Mei Yingzi's talk with me the day before, I had longed to see Bai Ping as a suckling babe longs to see the wet-nurse it has long been parted from; in the course of one night I had woken, to say the least, seven or eight times.

The nurse closed the door, took the bunch of flowers from Ah Mei's hand—Ah Mei showing an eager, obliging smile—and set down the rattan basket.

"Miss Bai Ping told me to bring it to keep you company." So saying, she bent and undid the cords; it turned out there was a parcel tied to the basket's lid as well. She set that parcel on a chair, and then opened the lid; I had supposed it was some kind of food, but to my surprise it was that pure white Persian cat, Jimi.

Jimi mewed and jumped out uneasily, sniffing all around; at last, hearing the sound of my voice calling it, it sprang onto the sofa where I sat.

"Miss Bai Ping is not coming, then?"

"She has a letter for you," said Ah Mei, and she took up the parcel from the chair and handed it to me.

I opened the paper parcel; inside were two silver-covered diary volumes and a letter. The letter ran thus:

"Xu: I have had Ah Mei bring Jimi to keep you company, thinking it may call to your mind the scene of the days when you lived in my house—whenever I was not at home, it was always Jimi that kept you company as you steeped yourself in philosophical thought. I believe still that this is your proper life.

"The other night Mei Yingzi stayed at my place, and she said, 'Jimi has the air of a philosopher.' I said, 'That is perhaps caught from Xu.' This too was one motive for my sending it to keep you company.

"Would it not be better for me not to come and see you for a while? For when I see you, I can think of no words with which to comfort you. And besides, I cannot imagine with what kind of eyes you would look at me...? Astonishment? Hidden hatred? Forgiveness? Grief and reproach? All of these I dread to see.

"You have perhaps prepared questions and materials to ask me; but in the flurry of the moment you will not be able to get out a single word, and I too shall answer beside the point.

"I have never shown my diary to anyone, nor has anyone known that I keep a diary; but now I let you know it, and let you see it, thinking that everything you wish to know you may then know. I do not wish, when we meet, to bring up the past again, to speak again of these misunderstandings and heartaches. That is to say, I forbid you to ask me again about the things of the past, and I shall refuse you by giving no answer.

"I repent to myself, and pray for you. Now that I hear you can be wholly well again, I have no second frame of mind; I wish only to prepare a lovely celebration, to welcome you out of hospital.

"Bai Ping. P.S. I do not yet wish a second person to see my diary—do you respect my wishes still, the same as ever?"

Jimi jumped down from the sofa, and the nurse held it and played with it, and she and Ah Mei fell to talking about Jimi. With paper and pen I wrote a letter in reply to Bai Ping; I remember it ran thus:

"Bai Ping:

"I ought to thank you, for my wounds have become my glory. And henceforth it is for the light and the victory of the road ahead that I pray. Forever I gaze at you with reverent eyes, and follow you with the feeling of faith.

"Xu. P.S. The diary is safer in my keeping than in yours; besides myself, the one who may see it should be Jimi."

After Ah Mei had gone off with this letter, I was just about to look through Bai Ping's silver diary when Mrs. Stephen came. She was as stately and gracious as ever; I put the diary behind me and welcomed her, and though I still called her Mrs. Stephen, I no longer regarded her in that character. I truly marvelled now at my early childishness and stupidity; for in her cerulean eyes I ought surely to have discovered long since that she was not Stephen's wife.

She gave me only a stately, composed greeting, neither asking after the course of my wounding nor speaking of Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi, but talking rather of Helen and her singing. In passing I told her the news in Helen's letter and her plan to go to Beiping; she seemed much in favour of it, and, on hearing of it, said that if Helen came back she would certainly ask Helen to her house once. Then we talked of music, and talked of art.

In the course of such talk I had no way left to believe in her identity; I could not tell how many layers there were to the fabric of her life.

Mrs. Manfield came, and our talk turned again to Helen. Mrs. Manfield knew, of course, of Helen's plan to go to Beiping; and, just as Helen had foreseen, she did not wish to let Helen go on alone first. Both Mrs. Stephen and I urged her to think of Helen's future above all. And, once Helen had found her a place in Beiping, she could go there at any time.

But our words did not win Mrs. Manfield over; I saw that in these days when Helen was away she had grown lonely enough, and she looked with wavering eyes at the white bunch of flowers she had brought just now—the nurse had already set them in a vase—and, as if dissatisfied with the way they were arranged, she went to set them right again, and then said, as if in sorrow:

"I have already left my husband, and I have already left my son; I have no courage now to leave this daughter of mine too."

"But this is no battlefield," I said.

"But it is a strange place."

And so we all fell silent. An indescribable air pressed down on the whole sickroom. It came to me suddenly that Mrs. Manfield's husband and son were both serving in the war, a thing Mei Yingzi seemed to know; then to use Helen again as a stepping-stone for the work, with never a thought for Mrs. Manfield—how cruel that was. I thought by association of Bai Ping's attitude, and felt that she was indeed more generous and merciful than Mei Yingzi. Running through the whole of Bai Ping's heart was a kind of great human feeling; whereas Mei Yingzi had only a will like steel. Was this difference because Bai Ping was purely Chinese, and had a certain breadth peculiar to the Chinese?

In the silence Mrs. Stephen took her leave. Mrs. Manfield went on talking with me a good deal about my wounds and her own conjectures. She still believed, to this day, that I had been shot and hurt by a Japanese soldier; I felt there was no need to explain to her, but she worried over the danger to me after I left hospital, and so urged me that I had better go with her to Beiping and stay a while.

I said this shooting had come wholly of a mishap after drink, and was no hard-to-untangle hatred against me, and begged her not to worry on my account. In the end I still urged her to let Helen go to Beiping first; I told her Shanghai was not far from Beiping, and that in my free time I could of course go to see them at any time. If, by summer, Helen had still not found her a suitable place in Beiping, I would certainly go with her to Beiping to pass the summer, and then we could think of some other plan. Then the parting of mother and daughter would be at most half a year—which brought to Mrs. Manfield's face a smile of consent, a smile that held in it pleasure and comfort and gratitude; and so she promised me she would no longer hold stubbornly to her own preconceptions.

As she left, she looked at me with grateful eyes and shook my hand warmly besides. I realized that from the moment she came in she had been wavering over her daughter's future and her own happiness, and had perhaps meant to leave the matter to my decision.

I watched Mrs. Manfield's retreating figure disappear, and again saw the pure white roses before me, and drank myself, alone, into this great mother-love. It was not until Jimi wound round my feet that I was reminded of the diary set behind me; I took it in my hands, and at once an indescribable feeling took hold of me—it was the plain silver of the cover that called to my mind that silver-clad girl, her love of silver, that called to mind the atmosphere of the day she fell ill on our return from Hangzhou, when I first discovered the desolation hidden in the very quality of silver.

It was dusk; there was no more daylight in the grounds, and the room was beginning to darken. The nurse was not there; I thought of turning on the light, but was too lazy to rise, and in the moment of sitting there dazed I felt my loneliness, and, enduring it, let the sky darken, darken, till I was buried in the darkness—but sleeping at my feet was Jimi, that Persian white cat.

At last I roused myself and went to the bedside to turn on the light; that silver diary slid to the floor, which seemed to startle Jimi awake. When I had turned on the light, the sudden brightness in the room made it stand up; I went over to pick up the diary beside it, and the diary lay open at a certain page.

So, sitting down in my former place, I began to read that page of the diary:

—

"I would sooner go to the battlefield and fight hand to hand than idle here among ghosts!

"That Mei Yingzi is a lovely demon's-hand is now a fact past all denying; I can find no reason why she should keep up so warm an intimacy with us, unless she has already made me out for her enemy!

"To take an interest in Xu—this is an ingenious screen. Stephen says, 'She merely wants to conquer Xu, to take him for a satellite of hers, that is all.' These are very stupid words, and also very clever ones. Stupid, if what he points to is only the very letter of the sentence; clever, if the 'satellite' he speaks of has another meaning.

"Only on some occasion where there is one 'ghost' the fewer would she take notice of Xu, I think.

"At first I took Xu for no more than one of the 'self-flattering-in-love' sort; now I feel rather that he has a loyal soul besides, and so I think of going to warn him—being a man who studies philosophy, is it not too great a pity that he should idle among ghosts here and play the clown?

"E, L, P and the rest all think I ought to make use of Xu to check Mei Yingzi; but I think this would do no more than hasten Xu's being made use of by Mei Yingzi the sooner.

—"

The nurse came in, and the meal was brought in behind her, and I put the diary away, meaning after the meal to read Bai Ping's diary again from the beginning.

I wondered at Bai Ping's sending me Jimi—what a troublesome thing it is to keep a cat in a sickroom; luckily this nurse was very fond of cats, and said she would see to everything, and so while I ate she carried the cat away; after the meal the nurse brought it back again, saying she would come to take it out again when I went to sleep.

At nine o'clock I read Bai Ping's diary over again; the room was very quiet, and my spirits were good too; I sat on the sofa, smoking and reading in silence, and knew nothing of the passing of the time. At half past ten the nurse came in to take the cat, and told me to go to bed early. After she had gone out, I lay on the bed and still went on reading Bai Ping's diary.

In her diary I saw her novel and rich life, her keen feeling and her original insight. In many places she used queer expressions and simple symbols, which I had to guess at carefully to understand, and some of which I could not make out at all.

This is an interesting and valuable diary, and to me especially; in the days that followed I could not help copying out some extracts from it. But here it is still hard to quote all I copied down, because in this story of mine its bearing is only a very small part, and the story here bears on her life only in a very small part. Take the matter of killing, for instance: here, the attempt on my life seems a very great event, but in her life it was only a very small matter. In this very point the real affairs of the world often differ from fiction; when a novelist sees, in one small matter, an eternal truth, he must set that small matter in the central place; whereas in real life it is perhaps often overlooked. That each kind of event should be decided by another event is almost enough to fill us with a sense of the immeasurable. An earthquake is perhaps a small matter in the universe, but among mankind it is a great one; walking, we tread to death who knows how many ants, but we never notice it, whereas in the society of the ants it is perhaps a great event. I believe that, had my wound truly been beyond saving, what few extra words there might be in Bai Ping's diary, I cannot imagine. For a while she would perhaps have had much bitter grief and vexation; but the waves of her life were, in her, no fixed thing—one day one wave breaks in, and my impress would be no more than a footprint on the beach, washed away to nothing in an instant.

And the mercilessness of time was ever scouring her away too, and she seemed especially sensitive to this; how many pages were tinged with this feeling of hers. Human nature is truly complex! At times she was intensely competitive, intensely strong, intensely brave; at times again intensely passive, intensely sorrowful, intensely decayed; she had the will to live, and yet looked on life as the morning dew, and toward death she seemed to have no more fear, and so at times did not feel an act of killing to be a punishment of the wicked.

For the sake of my understanding of her, I gained a greater understanding from her diary, and now have a great interest in reading her diary, in copying out extracts from her diary. I think the friends who read this book, apart from a very small part of it, will surely have an interest in reading it too; but here I cannot append all the part I now have, and, to help the development of my story and to fill in the gaps of the story that my own dullness has let slip, I cannot but copy out a little below.
