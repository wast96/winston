## Appendix III. A Brief Life of Comrade Liu Shaowen

— Excerpted from Luo Qingchang and Chai Chengwen, "A Nameless Hero, a Model for a Thousand Ages"

(People's Daily, 10 June 1996)

Comrade Liu Shaowen, originally named Liu Zizhang, was a native of Xinyang, Henan. In his youth, influenced by the "May Fourth" movement, he threw himself into the local anti-imperialist and anti-feudal struggle. In March 1925 he joined the Communist Youth League, and in June of the same year the Chinese Communist Party. In October he went to study at Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow in the Soviet Union, serving as party branch secretary for his year. In February 1927, assigned by the organization, he returned to China as interpreter accompanying a delegation of the Soviet Communist Party. From the latter half of 1928 to the first half of 1931 he was head of the translation section of the Central Secretariat, responsible for liaison work with the Communist International and fraternal parties and for managing the related funds.

After 1931 he resolutely set out for the anti-Japanese front at Zhangjiakou. In February 1934 he reached the Central Soviet Area in Jiangxi. He took part in the Long March, serving in secretarial work at the Zunyi Conference. On 27 July 1936 he became secretary-general of the Northwest Bureau of the CCP Central Committee. During this tenure, together with Comrades Zhu De and Ren Bishi, he waged a resolute struggle against Zhang Guotao's opposition to joining forces with the Party Center.

After the "Xi'an Incident," Comrade Shaowen long worked in the dangerous conditions of the White areas, quietly carrying out one task after another entrusted to him by the Party. Much of this work was of great significance and far-reaching influence in the military and political struggles of the time, yet little known.

After the "July Seventh" Incident he was transferred to the CCP office in Shanghai and then became secretary-general of the Eighth Route Army's Shanghai office. After the "August Thirteenth" Incident, as Comrades Li Kenong and Pan Hannian left Shanghai one after another, Shaowen took charge of the office's overall work, keeping up the struggle on the Shanghai "solitary island" until, at the end of 1939, he left Shanghai for Yan'an to report on his work. His chief contributions in this period were: (1) maintaining contact with leading figures of the various resistance organizations and conveying the Party's positions and policies by various means; (2) organizing the printing and distribution of resistance publications and guiding local anti-Japanese activities, and directing and supporting the translation and publication of Snow's Red Star Over China (Random Notes on a Journey to the West) — the publication of the Complete Works of Lu Xun was also carried out with his active backing, and How the Steel Was Tempered, that Soviet work of biographical literature of great influence in China, was in 1938 entrusted by Shaowen, as a task assigned by the organization, to Comrade Mei Yi, who completed the translation; (3) organizing and directing the people's anti-Japanese armed forces around Shanghai; (4) organizing support for the New Fourth Army, sending them cadres and raising money and goods; (5) leading the underground confidential radio and intelligence work, and establishing communications links with the Party Center, the Southern Bureau, and the New Fourth Army.

In July 1940 he was sent by the Southern Bureau to Hong Kong. He later served as a member of the CCP Hong Kong–Macao Work Committee and as head of the Hong Kong–Macao office of the Central Liaison Department, responsible for liaison, communications, confidential matters, radio, and funds. Besides keeping up contact with Shanghai, Shaoguan, Guilin, Hainan Island, and other places, he also established independent communication links with certain regions overseas. On 8 December 1941 the Pacific War broke out; after Hong Kong fell, the Japanese army hunted down patriotic resisters in a frenzy, and several hundred influential patriots then in Hong Kong — He Xiangning, Liu Yazi, Zou Taofen, Liang Shuming, Mao Dun, and others, together with famous figures of the arts such as Xia Yan and Jin Shan, and their families — fell into peril. The CCP Central Committee and the Southern Bureau wired the heads of the organs stationed in Hong Kong — Liao Chengzhi, Pan Hannian, and Liu Shaowen — to spare no effort in the rescue. With the help of the Guangdong Provincial Committee, the local guerrillas, and others, they organized and mobilized every force that could possibly be used and at last successfully moved these patriots in secret to safe areas. For his outstanding performance in the rescue work he was commended by Comrade Zhou Enlai and given an award registered with the Center.

In August 1942, under orders, he returned to Chongqing, where, under the alias Zhang Ming, he worked in the Southern Bureau of the CCP Central Committee, serving successively as head of the Liaison Department, head of the Organization Department, member of the Chongqing Work Committee, and member of the Southern Bureau. In this tenure he directly organized the laying of the Sichuan–Shaanxi transfer route and the secret line from Chongqing to the Central Plains Liberated Area. He also directly led the work of the Association of Chinese Young Scientific and Technical Personnel, winning over and uniting many scientific and technical workers and building up a store of construction talent for the country. In 1945, during Comrade Mao Zedong's negotiations in Chongqing, Shaowen took the post of head of the intelligence department of the Southern Bureau, providing intelligence support for the negotiating struggle and for the holding of the Political Consultative Conference, and on many occasions arranging and accompanying Chairman Mao in receiving revolutionary patriots. At the end of February 1946, as an aide to Comrade Zhou Enlai, Shaowen accompanied the three-man group from Chongqing on flights to Beiping, Zhangjiakou, and other places to inspect the implementation of the ceasefire agreement.

In March 1946 Comrade Zhou Enlai sent Shaowen ahead to Nanjing and Shanghai to prepare for the move of the CCP delegation (that is, the Southern Bureau). Soon afterward he was ordered to go underground. In January 1947 he became a member of the CCP Central Committee's Shanghai Sub-bureau, with responsibility for united-front, propaganda, and intelligence work.

In October 1948 Shaowen withdrew to Pingshan, where the Party Center was located, and was appointed deputy head of the Social Affairs Department of the CCP Central Committee.

In 1949, together with the leaders of the Shanghai underground party, he made detailed arrangements for the underground party to coordinate with and assist the People's Liberation Army in liberating Shanghai. On 27 May he entered Shanghai with the army, serving as a member of the CCP Shanghai Municipal Committee and as deputy director of the Financial and Economic Takeover Commission of the Shanghai Military Control Commission, concurrently head of the Light Industry Department. After the founding of New China, he held such posts as member of the East China Military and Political Commission and head of the East China Textile Industry Department, organizing and leading the recovery and development of the light-textile industry in Shanghai and East China.

Shaowen was one of the pioneers of the intelligence work of our Party and our army, and an outstanding leader on the hidden-struggle front. The working organs he built and led in enemy territory were one of the important intelligence systems under the direct leadership of the Party Center, and the leaders of the Center and of the intelligence departments held him in high esteem. Under the Party's leadership, Shaowen and his comrades-in-arms, with all their heart's blood, built yet another Great Wall to defend the safety of the motherland and the people — an underground Great Wall.
