## Chapter 9. Reflecting on Past Pain, the Future Still to Redeem

In the sub-title of the first section the writer has used three phrases—“hearts uneasy,” “the road rough,” “time slipping away”—to sigh over the prospects of this unit of ours.

When the brigade set out from Beiping, all that was known was that it would go first to Shanghai; where it would go thereafter, none could tell. And so it went: every three or five days a change of station, every ten days or fortnight another setting-out—from Shanghai to Shaoxing to Ningbo, then back again by way of Shanghai, taking ship across to Xiamen; and from Xiamen on to Haicheng and Zhangzhou, and at last a withdrawal to Penghu. This was to be their terminus. Yet there was an order forbidding them to be quartered on Taiwan. And here the “First Brigade of the Pacification Corps” came to its end; only the fighting spirit lived on.

To speak of the Beiping-Tianjin war is to feel the ache of it still: a great army of five or six hundred thousand, splendidly equipped, was—without any real fighting—eaten up piecemeal. The blame for this must fall upon Fu Zuoyi, the commander-in-chief of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, who made light of the grave charge the government had laid upon him for that frontier region: irresolute and weak at the first, he sat idle and let slip the good chance of moving east; and thereafter, his command and dispositions thrown out of balance, laying too much store by ties of native place, he brought at last his whole army to ruin—truly a thing to be regretted as few are under Heaven.

Among the documents there is, upon the fall of Beiping and Tianjin, a passage of “lessons drawn from experience,” of which I here set down the substance in abstract: “In large-army warfare, the first thing is the posture of the decisive battle. When the situation in the Northeast turned for the worse, common sense alone could foretell that the bandit Lin’s main force would surely pour its whole strength in through the passes, and that the massed bandit armies of North China would then hold a position of absolute superiority. Yet at that time the several armies under the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters were scattered in their stations, their posture from the first most disadvantageous; there was urgent need to readjust the dispositions with all speed, to concentrate the main force in the Tianjin-Tanggu region, with the sea for a rear, drawing upon the combined fighting power of the three services and guiding the action along interior lines. Even could no decisive victory be gained for a time, one might at the least first stand upon unassailable ground; and the more so as, with the seaport of Tanggu and command of the sea in hand, one might advance to attack or fall back to defend, and had initiative enough and to spare. It is to be lamented that Fu Zuoyi did not so plan, but, blinded by the present state of things, chose the most disadvantageous and passive of expedients, gave battle piecemeal, and in the end met with utter defeat.” The document points out further: “Inaction and hesitation must needs let the good chance slip. Fu Zuoyi’s conduct of the Beiping-Tianjin operations was not settled, and that but grudgingly and amid much wavering, until the middle of the eleventh month of the thirty-seventh year; and just as it was about to be put in hand, he hesitated again and did not straightway carry it through. When the advance columns of the bandit Lin Biao’s forces crossed to the north of Beiping, he mistakenly supposed the bandits meant to fall upon Beiping, and altered his plan again and again—not only going against his own resolve to move east, but throwing away all initiative, and bringing the situation at Beiping and Tianjin to a pass past mending. As early as the beginning of the eleventh month, the ‘Battlefield Inspection Group’ that the Presidential Office had posted to that command had likewise urged upon Fu: ‘First concentrate nine armies, take the offensive against East Hebei, and shatter the bandit Lin’s preparations for attack.’ For at that time Nie Rongzhen’s field main force was still far off about Zijingguan, and the bandit Lin was still beyond the passes; in the East Hebei region there lay only two columns of Lin’s advance corps, lurking about Jixian. Had Fu Zuoyi accepted the counsel and resolutely carried it out, the National army might, with overwhelming superiority, have wiped out those bandits—not only leaving the bandit Nie a single palm that could raise no clap, but making it hard for the bandit Lin to relieve the urgency within the passes; and our own main force might, seizing the moment, have moved east to Jin-Gu and given battle at its ease. But how could it be, when Fu Zuoyi was still irresolute, resolving and not acting? In the direction of a battle, the whole must be held foremost in view; and when the course of the fighting falls into confusion, whoever is commander of a quarter must keep his thinking cool in the highest degree, weigh the whole gain and loss, and hand down the fittest resolve for the good of the action as a whole—this is the cultivation a high commander ought to have. The success or failure, the safety or peril, of the Beiping-Tianjin operations lay with the commanding general.” The foregoing is sober and plain speaking, and worth the reading.

The hundred faces of society at Beiping during the siege are snapshots of the actual scene, and a mirror, too, of the people’s hearts; I make bold to think this a sort of “keeping one’s composure amid upheaval” of a form all its own—whether rightly or not I do not know.

### 1. The Corps Moves South: Hearts Uneasy, the Road Rough, Time Slipping Away

The order for the unit’s transfer had reached Beiping. Since our “First Brigade of the Pacification Corps” had begun its work about the Beiping-Tianjin region in the autumn of the thirty-sixth year, only a year and four months had passed.

Had the whole course of the war not turned against us, we might well have earned a rating of “excellent work”; but now there was no talk of any such thing. Yet the comrades of the whole brigade had valor to spare, and not a man among them was willing to own himself beaten.

At the same time, the other directly subordinate units stationed at Beiping—the “Military Police Regiment” and the like—were likewise ordered south. As I understood it, this was but a small part of an overall readjustment of dispositions. As for the combat troops, there was no great movement of them. The chief reasons were, besides the limits that conditions set upon both sea and air transport, above all a matter of strategic policy.

At this moment the writer was still bending every effort to the disposing of the “stay-behind work,” and the business of the unit’s transfer he handed over to Deputy Brigade Commander Li to deal with at his full discretion. The matters to be seen to were these three: first, that the units great and small dispersed in various places—from the command rooms down to the several small groups and the directly subordinate personnel—were, one and all, to come back and concentrate at Beiping within a set time; second, that by formal official dispatch, with a copy of the Ministry of National Defense’s telegram appended, report be made to the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” for the record.

Third, to negotiate with the Railway Bureau for the allotment of freight cars to carry the men from Beiping to Tianjin. In days gone by such things were of the utmost ease to arrange and counted for nothing; but at this time they cost no end of trouble. To take first the working units posted about in various places: the most vexing thing was that communications were already cut, and though there were still means to be devised, the timing of them could hardly be controlled, so that there was no bringing the men back to Beiping within any set term. One had hoped the sooner the better; but as things now stood, haste was of no use—yet neither could one say that those who could not get back in time should simply be cast aside.

The “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” was of itself beset with troubles enough; a matter like ours, that could only add to its vexations, and touching moreover on the movement of troops, was the more unwelcome. The brigade headquarters sent a lieutenant-colonel staff officer bearing the dispatch to deliver it to the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters,” and to offer, into the bargain, a verbal explanation to the “Second Section” and the “Third Section” with which we had dealings in the way of business. At the time no answer was given; it was said that a thing of this kind must be submitted for Commander-in-Chief Fu’s sanction. After some days the endorsement came down: grudgingly it was passed, and we were let go.

According to what the officer handling the matter at the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” let fall, Fu Zuoyi was much displeased as he went over the dispatch, and muttered as he did so: “So the ones the Central Government sent up are all leaving now—” This was only hearsay; whether it was to be believed or not was by this time of no great importance.

The men were now assembled, the way was cleared; what remained was only the freight cars that were to bear the whole brigade’s officers and men on their way.

Day after day we pressed the Railway Bureau, but with military transport so unceasing there were truly none to be spared; daily we urged them, and patiently we waited—and this for no more than the two hundred-odd li from Beiping to Tianjin, the very first stage of the whole journey.

This day the waiting was at last rewarded: a train stood at Beiping’s West Station. Ordinarily the through trains for Tianjin, and the passenger and goods trains of the Bei-Ning line, all put in at Beiping’s East Station, so that the boarding and alighting were done there too. That this time it should halt instead at the West Station was perhaps a matter of the marshalling of the rolling stock. But the route being different, the going meant fetching a great circuit round—what on the railway they call a “shunting-over”—that is, a shift from the Ping-Han line onto the Bei-Ning line.

After the unit had formed up and marched out from Fuxue Lane, the writer had not meant to go to the station to watch them depart. But once they were gone, the whole brigade headquarters was cold and still, and all at once I felt it empty, and myself adrift with nowhere to settle; and so, unable to help myself, I hurried after them to the West Station beyond the Qianmen gate.

Alone I stood far off, not wishing them to see that I too had come.

How long that train was! Passenger coaches, open flat-cars, iron box-cars—every kind of car was there; and though I did not count them one by one, there must have been several dozen in all. I saw only the heads swarming thick upon them, and it seemed the men were not yet all aboard.

As I gazed, of a sudden I made out that there were womenfolk aboard as well; I rubbed my eyes and looked again more closely—not women only, but children too. Ah!

Then I understood: they must be the men’s dependents. Though to take one’s family on the march was a thing the regulations did not allow, yet at such a time, if a man cast off his whole household, young and old, who was there left to look after them?

In any case I could by no means interfere; better far to make as though I had not seen. And besides, since it was the deputy brigade commander who led the column, there was the less call for me to inquire into it.

This was a thing of forty years ago. Only later did I learn that the dependents who went south with the army at that time numbered some thirty or forty households; borne on through a thousand li of wandering, they all in the end reached Taiwan. The young and comely wives of those days are now risen to the rank of grandmothers; the little children have taken husbands and wives and are every one grown up. Of those who pursued their studies, many have won the master’s degree or the doctor’s; and those who flung aside the pen and took up arms are lieutenant-colonels and colonels now. How swiftly the bright hours roll on and away!

Touching the unit’s southward transfer, my fellow-student Wang Hongzhu has left, under the title “The Brigade Withdraws into the Southland,” a passage of record, which I here copy out below: With the Northeast fallen, there came to Beiping to rejoin us some thirty-odd men of the Second Brigade, my fellow-student Wei Ning among them; and with Taiyuan left standing alone, a dozen or more of the Fifth Brigade too gathered at Beiping, so that the numbers of our First Brigade grew daily greater. We were just in the midst of the overall assignment of duties when, all of a sudden, the order came for the whole body to be transferred south to Shanghai.

The brigade headquarters, by an urgent order, assembled at Beiping the whole of the several command rooms and working groups. Under the command of Deputy Brigade Commander Li Yulin, four teams were formed for the time: the First Team under my fellow-student Lin Li, the Second Team under my fellow-student Chang Shaozeng, the Third Team under my fellow-student Wang Zhaofen, the Fourth Team under my fellow-student Wang Hongzhu; and the several team leaders took the duty-officer’s watch by turns, beginning from our setting-out from Beiping.

At Tanggu the whole body of officers and men looked, day after day, for a ship; and only after nearly a month of bitter waiting did we by good fortune get hold of a certain vessel. By this time the flames of war spread everywhere: the bandit Lin Biao’s forces had already crossed the Luan River and taken Tangshan, and his main force was pressing straight on toward Tianjin. Our own 208th Division (of the 87th Army) had withdrawn to Lutai, and its leading elements were about to reach Tanggu. Our whole body had boarded the ship, but with the ship small and the men many there was truly no more room to set a foot; and, as ill luck would have it, we struck the ebb tide, and looking from the shore one saw the ship’s hull already listing, with every look of being stuck fast and, at the least stir, ready to capsize.

Beyond all imagining, there even fell out a thing wherein a friendly force sought to seize the ship by force—of which I cannot speak in fuller detail, for in no wise ought one to lay bare one’s own shameful sores. Deputy Brigade Commander Li Yulin, seeing it with his eyes, was cut to the heart, and was indeed at his wit’s end. Some marked how he, alone, thrust both hands within his greatcoat, upon the belt, and, with head bowed, paced to and fro along the bank; and the fellow-students, fearing lest in his over-great wrath some harm should come to him, hastened forward to crowd about him and soothe him with gentle words, and all begged him to come back aboard.

The duty officer, having sought Deputy Brigade Commander Li’s instruction, straightway gave the order to cast off! Very grudgingly the ship began to stir, slow and slow. At this juncture, had it not been for the “assault team” in full equipment standing upon the bow, we might yet have met with hindrance, and, wish as we might to break free, not been able to break free.

The ship did at last, slow and slow, draw away from the wharf; but it had gone no great distance in its sailing when the bandit guns began again to boom far off, and one saw shell after shell burst in quick succession upon the water close at hand, sending up columns of spray to the sky. We would fain have fired a round or two in answer, but could not make out where the target lay. The unit tossed upon the ship for the better part of a week, passing through storms and billows true and terrible, before we reached Shanghai.

At dusk we came ashore to bivouac. A pack of young northern lads, being now come to the great city of Shanghai, could not but feel, every one, the freshness of it all. And truly, the very first thing was fresh indeed: the great compound we had moved into turned out to be a funeral parlor, a place for the ordering of men’s last affairs.

Round about the courtyard were rows upon rows of little rooms, clean and orderly, rather like an inn of the middling sort and better. Some rooms stood with their doors open, some were locked; there were old coffins that had stood there many a year, and new coffins whose lacquer was not yet dry. In the dressing-room a man was even then making ready a body for the coffin; and the great motor-tricycles that came and went—no need to ask—must surely be the corpse-carts. The living bustling for the dead: what a lively scene it was.

The funeral parlor was of two storeys, the upper rooms set apart for the bereaved families to chant the sutras and do the Buddhist rites. This was just the place, as it fell out, to lodge our “women’s team.” This team had been formed, on our coming south from Beiping, out of the dependents who traveled with us, with Zou Yi (a wife of the Northeast team) appointed its leader; and just because leader Zou was uncommonly capable, we owed much, all along the road, to her devoted care.

The fellow-students, for their part, lodged severally beneath the eaves and along the corridors; and though the weather was cold, it was warmer, at all events, than at Beiping and Tianjin. In the funeral parlor there was a keeper, a Tianjin man, who, when we fell to talking, proved a fellow-townsman; and from him I heard a little tale, curious enough that I may as well set it down here in passing. He lodged in the room to the left as one came in at the great gate, and, very conspicuously, there hung upon his wall several leather whips. These, he said, were for the express use of “number-checking.” Number-checking is to say patrolling: by day or by night, the keeper was bound, as a matter of course, to go the rounds of the mortuary and inspect it. If he heard any stir or sound, he would take a whip and lay on a while toward that quarter; and the strange thing was that, so soon as the whip cracked, all fell quiet at once and nothing more was to be heard. He told it with great relish, as though it were some solemn affair, and the fellow-students, too, listened rapt.

Here we lodged several days, and never shall I forget it: this funeral parlor stood at Xujiahui Road and Madang Road.

The next stage was to go from Shanghai by the Shanghai-Hangzhou-Ningbo railway across the great Hangzhou bridge, and then, changing at Xiaoshan into small boats, on to Shaoxing. All along the road the whole of the guard duty was still borne by the “assault team,” at the cost of no small toil.

After a short spell of training at Shaoxing, we were again ordered to move to Ningbo. The brigade headquarters was set up in a small primary school at “Yinjiangqiao” in the southern outskirts of Ningbo; and save for a few of the administrative staff left on watch, the main force moved into “Xiaolingfeng,” “Xikou,” and the like, to guard against the incursions of the local Communists about the “Siming Mountains.” The above is one section of my fellow-student Wang Hongzhu’s long record, the whole of which is not yet ended; that part which tells of the “guarding of the late President Chiang’s native place” shall be set out in the final chapter, together with the piece “A Record of the Xiaolingfeng Garrison” furnished by my fellow-student Feng Zhijun.

The writer appends a note: with the brigade going south, the writer, being at Beiping to dispose of the stay-behind work, did not travel with it, so that there is much of which I am not clearly informed. This journey, however, went hard indeed with our deputy brigade commander, my brother Yulin. Leading this body of men, he crossed a thousand hills and ten thousand streams; and it was not the hardship alone, but that he suffered every kind of injustice. Here, from the bottom of my heart, I set down to him my apology and my thanks.

### 2. The Ache Remains: Notes on the Fall of Beiping and Tianjin

In the seventh and eighth chapters above I have set down how the Thirty-fifth Army, going to the relief of Zhangjiakou to save the Eleventh Army Group under Sun Lanfeng from its straits, was in the end wholly destroyed at the town of Xinbao’an on the Beiping-Zhangjiakou line, its commander Guo Jingyun taking his own life; and how the Provisional Third Army under An Chunshan, having failed to bring the Thirty-fifth Army the succor it rushed to give, then fell, on its way back, into an ambush and was well-nigh cut to pieces; and how thereafter the Eleventh Army Group under Sun Lanfeng at Zhangjiakou, its break-out at last a failure, was likewise routed into a rabble—such, in the main, was the course of it.

Now to say on: after Zhangjiakou and Xinbao’an fell to the bandits, of all North China there remained only the four strongpoints of Beiping, Tianjin (Tanggu), Taiyuan, and Datong. The situation grew from day to day more unfavorable, and the outlook less cheering still.

While Fu Zuoyi, clinging to his own private designs, gave his whole mind to the fighting on the Ping-Sui railway and the Ping-Zhang stretch, the bandit “Northeast People’s Liberation Army” (the field forces)—twelve infantry columns under Lin Biao, together with newly-formed engineer and artillery units and the like—had already, with all speed, come pouring in through the several passes of the Great Wall, each by its own way.

At that time the Beiping-Tianjin railway was still open and unobstructed, and within a range of some tens of li on either side of the line there was, save for a few bandit militia, no trace as yet of any bandit combat force. To have made sure of its safety and kept it open would have been no hard thing at all. Had it been so, the Beiping garrison would yet have had the hope of moving east to Jin-Gu, and would not have been penned up as in a jar, with never a hand’s-breadth of room to turn.

The thing that ought not to have happened happened at last: at daybreak on the eleventh of the twelfth month of the thirty-seventh year, the great iron bridge on the Beiping-Tianjin line, between Yangcun station and Douzhangzhuang station, was blown up and severed by the bandit forces, and communication between Beiping and Tianjin was of a sudden cut off.

Beiping and Tianjin linked together were far better than each standing alone, and the two could answer to each other. And to hold fast the seaport of Jin-Gu was to have still a way out; and with a way out one was, in any case, alive. Some several hundred thousand men in defense there could not be said to have been of no avail whatever.

The old-style soldier always takes the armies of the state for his own capital, wherewith to sit in high office and hold his patch of ground; and Fu Zuoyi, though of the plainer sort, had yet not shaken off the old notions and old habits. These—the Eleventh Army Group under Sun Lanfeng, worsted of late in its break-out at Zhangjiakou; the Thirty-fifth Army under Guo Jingyun, surrounded and wholly destroyed at Xinbao’an; and the Provisional Third Army under An Chunshan, ambushed as it took a short cut through the Miaofeng Mountains—were all of them Fu Zuoyi’s “own line,” his so-called “sons and younger brothers’ troops.” That these three forces should have collapsed, and each dragged down by another, was wholly a collapse the one entangled with the next; had Fu Zuoyi not been so full of private self-regard, how could things have fallen to such a pass?

Fu Zuoyi, in the excess of his grief, let his wits be mastered by the moment and his self-possession fall into utter disarray, so that his every move was a losing stroke, and half the rivers and mountains were thus thrown away by his hand. Even setting military action aside, a commander’s wrong decision brings on not only the needless death of officers and men, but affects the whole. So to say that Fu Zuoyi must bear the greater part of the blame is no overstatement.

Now to say on: after the Beiping-Tianjin railway was cut on the eleventh of the twelfth month, hard upon it, the bandit forces under Lin Biao that came in through Xifengkou (north of Zunhua county in Hebei, and south of Pingquan county in Rehe)—five columns in all, the First, Third, Fifth, Sixth, and Tenth—advanced from the twelfth by way of Jixian, Sanhe, and Tongxian upon Beiping.

The other bandit forces under Lin Biao that came in through Shanhaiguan and Lengkou—six columns, the Second, Seventh, Eighth, Ninth, Twelfth, and the artillery—pressed on, from the thirteenth, along the Bei-Ning railway line toward Tianjin and Tanggu with all haste.

The forces under Lin Biao that thus came in through the passes by the two routes numbered ten infantry columns and one artillery column. His remaining Fourth Column and Eleventh Column had come in ahead, penetrating within the passes in advance—these being the main bandit force that had waylaid our Provisional Third Army under An Chunshan at the Miaofeng Mountains.

By this time the “Northeast People’s Liberation Army” under Lin Biao—by now perhaps renamed the “Northeast Field Army”—had come wholly within the passes (later to be renamed again the “Fourth Field Army” and to go south).

On the fourteenth of the twelfth month of the thirty-seventh year, some of the market towns about Beiping—Haidian, Xiangshan (Biyun Temple), the walled town of Wanping county, the town of Fengtai (that region where the winter vegetables are grown)—fell one after another. The nearest incursion came to within two or three li beyond Deshengmen at the northeast corner. Two or three days later, Shijingshan (where the power plant stands), Mentougou (where the coal mines are), Wanshoushan, and other towns fell to the bandits. By the seventeenth the whole city of Beiping was ringed about by the bandit forces.

At this time the writer, too, was within the city of Beiping. Though we were then still going on with the gathering of intelligence, we were by no means so clearly informed of the matters set out above as I now write them. When the bandit forces had taken Fengtai and then the Nanyuan airfield, there came in, among the reports brought to us, one that touched the writer himself, the substance of which was this—“Zhu Zhankui, the former inspector-commissioner and peace-preservation commander, who betrayed us to rejoin the bandits, appeared suddenly at Nanyuan; and toward the city he cried out: ‘Chen so-and-so of Unit 0760, hear this! We, the People’s Liberation Army, have you now tightly hemmed in; come out of the city and surrender with all speed. Come early, and you shall have the chance to redeem your sins before the People; come late, and it is but to seek your own destruction!’ These few words Zhu Zhankui cried over and over for a day or two.” This was the bandits’ unvarying trick; there was nothing for it but to curse him a time or two and, in anger, let it pass. As for what Zhu Zhankui was about at that moment, the report did not say; and thereafter he appeared no more.

Now to say further how the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” readjusted its dispositions in the face of this situation, and how it sought to meet it.

In the main, following the present state of the troops in the two places of Beiping and Tianjin, they were divided into two great defense zones—the “Beiping Defense Zone” and the “Jin-Gu Defense Zone”; and the “Jin-Gu Defense Zone” was in turn split into two, that is, into the “Tianjin Defense Zone” and the “Tanggu Defense Zone.”

Their personnel, unit designations, strengths and the like were roughly as follows: (1) the “Beiping Defense Zone”: its commander the commander of the Fourth Army Group, Li Wen (one of the deputy commanders-in-chief of the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters”); the Thirteenth Army under Luo Zhenshao, with two newly-formed divisions attached; the Sixteenth Army under Yuan Pu, about two divisions strong; the Ninety-second Army under Huang Xiang, lacking the Forty-third Division; the 101st Army under Li Shilin, lacking one division; the Provisional Seventeenth Division of the Thirty-fifth Army (which had not gone with the whole army to the relief of Zhangyuan); the 151st Division of the Sixty-second Army; the 205th Division; the New Fourth Cavalry Division; local peace-preservation troops to the number of ten thousand-odd—the whole a strength of some 180,000 men.

(2) the “Jin-Gu Defense Zone”: its commander the commander of the Seventeenth Army Group, Hou Jingru (concurrently); (1) the “Tianjin Defense Zone”: its commander the Tianjin garrison commander, Chen Changjie; the Sixty-second Army under Lin Weichou (concurrently deputy commander); the Eighty-sixth Army under Liu Yunhan; the Forty-third Division under Wang Zhixi.

(2) the “Tanggu Defense Zone”: its commander the commander of the Seventeenth Army Group, Hou Jingru (concurrently); the Eighty-seventh Army under Duan Yun (concurrently deputy commander); the Independent Ninety-fifth Division under Zhu Zhiyi; the Transport Police Corps (two); the Jin-Gu Waterborne Peace-Preservation Force; the Navy’s Third Fleet—the whole a strength of upward of 150,000 men, together with a number of naval vessels.

The reason the “Jin-Gu Defense Zone” was split into two was that there was still a stretch of some distance, about forty li, between Tianjin and Tanggu.

Tianjin fell on the sixteenth of the first month of the thirty-eighth year; Tanggu was given up after the main force of the Seventeenth Army Group, thirty thousand-odd men, had boarded ship and withdrawn to Qingdao; and the sinking of Beiping into the demons’ grasp came on the twenty-first of the first month. Let me now set down the course of each in turn.

The battle for Tianjin, primarily a military engagement.——Tianjin had of old a city wall, but it had long since been destroyed in the disorders of the Boxer year, and now there remained but ruined ramparts and broken foundations.

During the Japanese occupation, simple fieldworks had been thrown up round the city.

In the thirty-sixth year the “Tianjin Garrison Command,” to guard against a bandit incursion, threw up afresh a ring of blockhouses forty kilometers long about the city. Along the outer side of the blockhouses a moat was dug, some five meters wide and three meters deep; from the highest point of the moat’s inner wall to its bed was about seven meters. Within the inner wall, at every thirty meters, stood a blockhouse; along the moat there were, all told, three hundred and eighty large blockhouses, which, with the works ranged in depth through the city, came to more than a thousand in all. Each large blockhouse could hold a squad; and the blockhouses were joined to one another not only by field telephone but by rifle-trenches, dugouts, and communication trenches. About the city was drawn, besides, a barbed-wire and electrified-wire entanglement, with but eight openings left for entrance and exit. To make the moat more effective, water was led in besides from the source of the Grand Canal, so that a depth of three meters could be kept up constantly.

These works had been completed by the beginning of the thirty-seventh year; only, because the whole structure was of brick and stone and lacked concrete, their power of resistance was somewhat wanting.

The city of Tianjin runs about twelve kilometers north and south, and but five kilometers wide east and west; the Ziya River and the Northern Grand Canal meet to the north of Tianjin and cut through the center of the city, so that the city, of itself long and narrow north to south, is made, under the dividing of the Hai River, the more fragile still.

The above is the geographical lie of Tianjin and the defense works that were already there. Now to say on how the bandit forces came to the attack.

The five columns under Lin Biao that came in through Shanhaiguan and Lengkou, with an artillery force equal to one more column, reached, in advance, north of Tangshan on the tenth of the twelfth month of the thirty-seventh year. Just at this time the defending Eighty-seventh Army was in the midst of withdrawing from Tangshan toward Tanggu; the bandit forces, reckoning there was an opening to be seized, did not wait for the whole body to assemble, but pressed hard upon its heels in pursuit. Happily the Eighty-seventh Army was able to make use of the rivers and salt-fields as natural obstacles and put up a stout resistance; and after a week of fierce fighting the bandits had not, up to then, gained their end.

The bandit forces, sensible that the ground about Tanggu was peculiar, and knowing it hard to take by force in a moment, then shifted their main strength and bore in upon Tianjin.

On the sixteenth of the twelfth month of the same year, the bandits first, with two columns, attacked the outskirts of Tianjin, their weight of effort pointed at Junliangcheng and the Zhangguizhuang airfield. The defending Forty-third Division fought them valiantly, and it was not till the eighteenth that it gave up Junliangcheng; the bandit forces then pressed hard, on its heels, upon Zhangguizhuang.

Another body of bandits, about one column strong, took Yangliuqing on the Jin-Pu line; of the two railway-guard brigades defending it, four regiments in all, only one regiment fell back to Tianjin.

While the fighting on the outskirts of Tianjin was at its fiercest, the operations-staff officer in charge at the Ministry of National Defense urgently proposed, on the eighteenth, to Chen Changjie, the Tianjin defense commander—that, before the bandit forces had closed the ring, it were better to break out and fall back upon Tanggu, and thence go by sea to Qingdao. As for the ships needed and the reckoning of time and space, the withdrawal of ten divisions would be no problem. But commander Chen Changjie had deep confidence in the works about Tianjin, and held that he could hold out three or four months; and so he was not able to accept the counsel to fall back upon Tanggu and pass on to Qingdao.

On the nineteenth of the twelfth month the bandit forces hurled themselves upon the Zhangguizhuang airfield, and after a fierce fight it fell at last to them, so that the air passage was cut. Therefore another site was chosen, the city racecourse, and a runway laid out there, that the air supply might be kept up.

On the twentieth the bandit troops were already at the foot of the walls, and the defense of Tianjin was thus joined.

The bandit forces that came against Tianjin numbered six columns, and held the advantage over the defenders. From the latter part of the twelfth month of the thirty-seventh year they were all in probing attacks, and it was not until the seventh of the first month of the thirty-eighth year that they began a general assault along the whole line. Though the defenders were at a disadvantage, their fighting spirit ran high; and with the moat girdling the whole city, and under cover of the linked blockhouses, the bandit forces had up to then been unable to advance a single step over the forbidden line. So they fought fiercely for seven days, and the bandits’ dead and wounded were even more grievous than ours. Yet a part of the city’s defense works had by now been destroyed by gunfire.

On the morning of the fourteenth of the first month, in a thick fog that would not lift, the bandits, taking advantage of the dimmed sight-lines, launched a fresh assault upon the city. They used their tanks for ferries: under cover of more than a hundred guns of every kind, they first drove the tanks into the frozen moat, and then swarmed up and over. Though the defenders fought valiantly and closed in bitter hand-to-hand combat, yet in the end, few against many, they could not hold back the assault; and hereupon Heping Gate on the west of the city and Minquan Gate on the east were breached one after another by the bandits. Straightway street-fighting began, and from morning till the sun went down it did not cease.

By the morning of the fifteenth a body of the bandit forces broke suddenly into the Garrison Command; commander Chen Changjie, wholly bent upon directing the fighting, had no time to make head against them, and was taken by the bandits. There being now none to command, organized resistance came thereupon to an end. Not until the sixteenth did the scattered fighting wholly cease.

Tianjin thus fell into the enemy’s hands.

A lone army had fought bitterly for a full month; the officers and men gave their utmost, and though they could not keep Tianjin, the fault was not the fault of the fighting.

Writing thus far, I cannot but call to mind my friend Qi Qingbin (former secretary of the “Shanghai District”). At that time he was at Tianjin, being the deputy chief of the “Tianjin Municipal Police Bureau.” Some while before, when I had passed through Tianjin on an inspection of the “Third Command Room,” we had met once, and had spoken too of what were to be done should such a day as this ever come. Old Qi said that, being a local official, he had no choice but to hold on to the last and let things take their course.

Tianjin fallen, old Qi, it was heard, “lay safely hidden” in the house of Zhang Shude. Zhang Shude was a nephew of the former Tianjin mayor Zhang Ting’e; he himself had joined one of the so-called “democratic parties”; and Zhang Shude’s wife was none other than Jiang Yunqing.

Jiang Yunqing was the eldest daughter of the old-line Communist Jiang Hao, and the younger sister of the old-line Communist Jiang Zhenhuan who died at Tianjin (see my own work, “Rooting Out Traitors in the North”). As for whether Elder Sister Jiang was herself a Party member, that cannot be affirmed for certain; but that she had old ties with many a veteran Communist cadre there was no doubt whatever.

Zhang and Jiang had lived at Tianjin some years, and their days went well enough; to shelter one or two persons in their house was the easiest of things—the more so as the couple were on very good terms with old Qi. I think that in lodging with them old Qi had, at the least, some measure of safety in the choice.

At first no one made trouble for old Qi, and so a year or two passed. But by the fourth month of the fortieth year of the Republic (?), the Communists, following upon the “Three-Anti and Five-Anti,” set going yet another thing called the “Campaign to Suppress Counter-Revolutionaries.” Zhang Shude could scarce keep himself safe, and as for Jiang Yunqing, what was she reckoned? They could of course not maintain old Qi’s safety, and he was “hauled out,” fastened with the crime of being a “Nationalist secret-agent chief,” and shot.

Such is the Communist Party: trusting to luck, given over to wild fancy!

My old friend Qi Qingbin twice escaped the torments of the Japanese army, only to meet a cruel death at the hands of the Communist Party; and at every mention of it, how can I master my grief!

Its reason for existence gone, Tanggu abandoned.——After Tianjin fell to the bandits on the sixteenth of the first month of the thirty-eighth year, Tanggu had lost its value as a seaport, and the Supreme Command straightway ordered severally the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” and the “Naval Command” to withdraw the garrison forthwith by sea, that its strength might be preserved.

Hou Jingru, commander of the Seventeenth Army Group of the “Jin-Gu Defense Zone,” on receiving the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” instruction to withdraw, completed the detailed arrangements with the Navy’s “Third Fleet” and sent out a covering force; and, before the bandit forces at Tianjin had swung back, on the afternoon of the sixteenth, under the double cover of naval gunfire and of the covering force, boarded ship at a stroke and drew off. In this action—save for the units detailed to the covering, which could no longer break off from the field of battle, and one lighter-load of troops that was cut off by the bandits, and more than two thousand men of the “Transport Police Corps” that lost touch—the main force of 36,000-odd men, with their equipment, arms, ammunition, and other important stores, all reached Qingdao safely on the nineteenth of the first month.

And here Tanggu was given up.

The writer would fain, by means of the record above, set forth a thing or two of his own seeing. It is said in the text: “the covering force could no longer break off from the field of battle”; then were not those charged with the covering work most unlucky? Of one and the same unit, some had the good fortune and some the ill; and on the other hand it seems, too, to betray a want of even-handedness. Yet in truth, under the great premise of seeking the “good of the whole body,” this is a thing not to be avoided. In “military action,” or in “secret-service work,” affairs of this kind often fall out, and are no marvel.

Psychological, political, and united-front warfare under the siege.——When the bandit forces took Tianjin on the sixteenth of the first month of the thirty-eighth year, the lone city of Beiping had already been besieged a month; not only were all the four outskirts held by the bandit forces, but the city’s water and power too were in the bandits’ control. Happily the spirit of the men was as high as ever, and officers and men were resolved to make their last stand with the city at their backs. The pity was only that Fu Zuoyi was, first and last, not made of the right stuff.

The bandit forces, besides “ringing Beiping without assaulting it,” on the pretext of preserving the cultural relics and ancient monuments from the ravages of war, secretly, under the borrowed name of “the popular will,” got up and organized a “North China People’s Peace-Promotion Movement Association,” to cry up a “local peace.” At the time Fu Zuoyi treated it as though he saw it not, letting it spread unchecked and putting no hindrance in its way.

As I understood it, there were at this moment men busy at their threading of the needle, in the work of persuading Fu Zuoyi to lay down his arms and surrender.

What came out into the open, amid these secret dealings, was a joint letter from Lin Biao (commander of the Beiping-Tianjin Front) and Luo Ronghuan (political commissar of the Beiping-Tianjin Front) urging Fu Zuoyi to surrender, and setting forth a so-called “Method for the Local Peace of Beiping,” the substance of which was as follows: “Let there be an immediate order to all under your command to lay down their arms of their own accord, with a guarantee that the cultural relics and monuments be not destroyed. That the Communist spies be not slain, and public property, arms, ammunition, official files and the like not destroyed. If all this be thoroughly and wholly carried out, we may guarantee the safety of the life and property of Fu himself and of his officers and men.

“If you will not lay down your arms, then you should be moved away from Beiping into a designated region, and there, by the system of the ‘Liberation Army,’ receive reorganization.

“For the above method four days are granted (from 0100 hours on the seventeenth of the first month to 1400 hours on the twenty-first) for consideration; and if before the term is out the said proposals be refused, the assault on the city will begin.” This letter, full of menace and of enticement, was handed over on the sixteenth, at Lin Biao’s “Beiping-Tianjin Front Command,” to Fu Zuoyi’s secret negotiating representatives, Deng Baoshan and Zhou Beifeng. On that same day, moreover, Deng Baoshan escorted Lin Biao’s representative into the city to meet with Fu Zuoyi.

On the other hand, the “North China People’s Peace-Promotion Movement Association,” led on the Beiping local side by Zhang Yinwu, Xu Huidong (the council speaker), He Siyuan (a retired mayor), and others, likewise met in the Beiping municipal council chamber, and put forward Lü Fu, Kang Tongbi, Liu Hongrui, Guo Shutang, and others first to meet with Fu Zuoyi, and then to go out of the city and treat of terms with Ye Jianying.

Fu’s peace-seeking was wholly a private act, and no report of it was made to the central authorities; and when the central authorities, from intelligence, got wind of the true state of things, they straightway detailed the Minister of National Defense, Xu Yongchang, to fly to Beiping by special plane on the twenty-first of the first month (landing at the temporary airfield within the city at Dongdan), and go directly to see Fu and inquire into the truth of the matter. But Fu still went on glozing over the fact, pleading in excuse that “the aim in feigning to treat with the Communists was chiefly to keep his strength intact. So long as the strength was there, it might hold the bandit forces in check and be of advantage to East China and Central China.” Minister Xu Yongchang set the great principles before him again and again, but Fu Zuoyi was no longer to be moved.

Minister Xu returned that same day to the capital to make his report; and the next day Fu Zuoyi—ever irresolute and weak—straightway and resolutely accepted “reorganization,” proclaimed a cease-fire, and on the twenty-fourth of the first month, after the city’s defense had changed hands, this cultural and ancient city fell wholly to the bandits. Of the other particulars I shall say nothing; but the greater part of the “reorganized” troops was used by the bandit forces as the “first-line sea of men” in their seventh general assault upon Taiyuan in Shanxi—a thing that aches in the telling.

### 3. Scenes on My Hometown Streets During the Siege

This long passage of record that follows the writer holds especially dear; for save that the observation could not go to the inmost of things, and that the memory can hardly be without its errors, it should be the most objective, the truest, the rarest of the softer tales of the time of war—and the more so for a certain fondness for the past that the writer bears toward Beiping. The span of time is from the middle of the twelfth month of the thirty-seventh year to the beginning of the first month of the thirty-eighth. The background of the fighting: the old capital of Beiping lay hemmed in the heavy siege of the bandit forces, with five million inhabitants within the city and 180,000 National combat troops. The besieging enemy was the “Beiping-Tianjin Front Command,” formed jointly of Lin Biao’s forces of the Communist “Northeast People’s Liberation Army” and Nie Rongzhen’s forces of the “North China People’s Liberation Army.” Its total strength was given out as a host of a million.

The state of personnel: the troops under my command had, several days before the siege of Beiping, been transferred to the Southland, and there were still more than fifty men of the old brigade headquarters left on watch; the “stay-behind work” had come to a stage of completion. At that time, though it could not be said that the task was done, yet, communications being cut, save for waiting upon the air-transport there was, wish to go as one might, no going, and haste availed nothing.

The state of mind: this is to speak of the writer’s own frame of feeling. When a man has been tense and busy for a spell, and it is over, he will feel himself a little eased, and have besides some time to spare to look about him here and there. Among colleagues one could now chat freely, without constraint, and exchange one’s understanding of things.

Of what I came in touch with, of the things that left the deeper impression, I here set them down, divided into a number of heads, one by one, as follows: (1) The general state of the market and daily life.——Beiping is a purely consumer city; save for a few small enterprises, it has no large-scale productive industry, still less any wealth of resources to speak of. At the time of the victory in the War of Resistance its resident population was about four million; thereafter it increased year by year, and by the thirty-seventh year the refugees fleeing in from every quarter grew more and more, so that, by an unofficial reckoning, it stood in all at about five million. Yet of these five million, some were penned within the city and some caught outside the walls; the proportion we may set, say, at four to one.

Grain, it was said, might be made to last some months; the trouble was rather the vegetables. In ordinary times these were wholly supplied from the outer towns and the outskirts, but now, with the four gates shut fast and the source cut off, one felt little of it in the first days, but afterward the want was felt more with each day. It was now the depth of winter, and the consumption of vegetables was not great; yet to have none at all would not do either. Though the dealers could bring out and sell what they had in the “cellar,” it was after all but a limited store. (The writer appends: in that day Beiping had no large cold-storage plant, and once winter came, by the old traditional method certain things could be laid up in a cellar. The commonest was ice, and this was called an ice-cellar; and then vegetables, and this was called a vegetable-cellar. This “cellar” was a pit dug some feet into the ground, with a cover laid over, and the things put within; the air not circulating, they could be kept long and unspoiled—cabbages, radishes, and the like all served. Among the street-names of Beiping there is one indeed called “Ice-Cellar Lane.”) Afterward it was found that the bandit forces only now and then loosed a gun to disturb men’s minds, and did not press the assault, so that the situation seemed to ease a little. The military authorities then tried, by selection, opening a few of the city gates each morning, to allow the townsfolk to come and go. Our brigade headquarters was in Fuxue Lane, no great way from Dongzhimen and Chaoyangmen; and we sent staff officer Mao Yilu and others out of the city in disguise to gather intelligence, and they brought back not only a “flat-cart” (an unpowered wooden-boarded, rubber-tired cart that could be pulled or pushed) of Chinese cabbages, but bought too a little lamb.

The electric power of Beiping came from the Shijingshan power plant. Shijingshan had been taken by the bandit forces on the seventeenth of the twelfth month; yet they did not destroy it, and within the city the power went on without a break. This uncommon conduct was plainly, once again, so much of a hold gripped in the hand, that might well be used as a tool of united-front work.

Water, on the other hand, was no great problem; for there were wells everywhere in the city, and even were the piped water cut off, well-water could be made, after a fashion, to serve for drinking.

Even in ordinary times, a full third of the city’s households still used well-water.

The telephones within the city rang in and rang out as usual; whether the telegrams that flew to and fro were as of old, I did not mark—a thing of no moment in the daily life of the ordinary inhabitant.

The chief conveyance of the Beiping townsman was the tramway that ran along several of the main thoroughfares of the city, from morning till night, and handy enough. Next came the man-pedaled pedicab; the rickshaw (the man-pulled cart) that filled the streets a few years back had by now been gradually driven out. As for hired motorcars, there were some, but they were not common; on the main streets one could not flag one down, and if one had need, one telephoned and waited the better part of an age before it came. By now very few called a hired car. Besides these, the most serviceable of all was still the bicycle, which the Beiping man loved best.

Our own unit’s motor vehicles had already been sent away, and when we went out we took, for getting about, either a pedicab or a bicycle, so that we paid no heed even to whether the use of gasoline was under control.

The post office took in and forwarded only letters for the city itself; the broadcasting station, with its talk and its singing, went on the air all day as before; the schools were on their winter holiday; and the municipal offices kept their hours as usual.

(2) The gold-yuan note and the currency in circulation.——The gold-yuan note, its reform not half a year old, was not merely unwelcome in the market but had in fact ceased to pass current. The tradesmen dared not say outright that they would not take it, but with one voice put it off with the polite excuse, “We have no small change.” This was a great setback for the government authorities.

Nothing was more welcome in the market than “foreign money.” What the Beiping man called “foreign money” was the silver dollar, the very thing that in the south is commonly called the “Yuan big-head.”

To ride a pedicab, to eat a snack in the street, to buy this small thing or that, one needed subsidiary coin; and if one tendered a dollar (a silver dollar) to be changed, what came back? At that time Beiping still used the copper coins minted in the days of the Northern government, called in the local speech “coppers,” which are what about Beijing and Shanghai are called “copper cash.” I remember that in the days when the “fabi” was used, one dollar (the silver dollar being on a par with the fabi) could change for four hundred copper coins, each of double value (counting as twenty cash), which the local people called “a big one,” so that there were in fact only two hundred.

There was besides a kind of “small copper” of ten cash, which on the contrary was not used in the market. Yet it was just this “small copper cash” that passed current in Tianjin, Shanghai, Nanjing, and the like.

Before the War of Resistance, the Beiping local bank issued “hair notes,” one dollar changing for ten dimes, a dime being just a subsidiary tenth of a dollar. Earlier still, the “Official Money Bureau” had issued “copper-cash notes,” of two denominations, “twenty coins” and “forty coins” (?). These are no longer used now.

In sum, the Beiping man trusted no paper money whatever; for having been bitten and grown afraid of the biting, and dreading to be taken in again, he loved hard coin the more.

During the siege prices did not greatly fluctuate: such things indispensable to daily life as the “mutton stall” and the “pork bench,” the general stores that had rice and flour, the oil-and-salt shops that dealt too in greens—though the sellers knew well that once sold there was no getting more in, yet not one of them raised his prices. And this is just the honest kindliness of the Beiping man.

As for how the banks and the money-houses did their business—that, it may be, was the concern of the well-to-do; but I am sorry to say we had no touch with it.

What did leave a little impression was this: from Fuxue Lane, two of us went by pedicab to the Dong’an Market, a distance of about two kilometers; it was already toward dusk, and the fare was forty coins, the tenth part of one silver dollar.

And again, the three of us ate our fill of a hot-pot at the Donglaishun, and it came, all reckoned, to but a dollar (silver) and a little over. (3) The emergency repair of an airfield inside the city.——This was a thing of the latter part of the twelfth month of the thirty-seventh year. Beiping had of old two military airfields, “Nanyuan” and “Xiyuan,” but as both lay in the near outskirts beyond the walls, they were lost at the very beginning of the siege. There was no help for it but to find a place within the city and set to building day and night—a thing not thought of beforehand.

The building-work was first carried out on East Chang’an Street (from the Dongdan memorial arch to the south end of Wangfujing Street). This ground had been the great drill-field of the Legation Quarter, used at times too as a ball-field. It fell just opposite the Dongdan-arch night market, across a single road.

To speak of “constructing” or of “laying out” seemed alike somewhat overblown; in fact it was no more than a leveling of the ground and the pulling-down of a few wooden fences. But limited by the lie of the ground, being neither broad enough nor long enough, it could serve, and that barely, for the taking-off and landing of light aircraft only; for such as the C-47 transport it would not do.

So a second was hurriedly built within the “Temple of Heaven.” Here a word of explanation is needed: the “Temple of Heaven” lies within the city of Beiping, and the airfield was built within the “Temple of Heaven.” Think of it—how great, then, is Beiping!

The “Temple of Heaven,” whose proper name is the “Altar of Land and Grain,” is one of the famous scenes and ancient monuments of Beiping. Its main hall is three storeys high, in the shape of a round canopy; beneath the golden dome hangs a tablet whereon are written the three characters “Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests”; and were this drawn out as a design, it is the very emblem that stands for Beiping.

The “Temple of Heaven” lies northeast within the Yongdingmen gate and southeast of the “Tianqiao” market. It is the place where the emperors of the two dynasties of Ming and Qing sacrificed to Heaven and prayed for a rich year, and the largest of all the altar-and-temple buildings in the country.

The “Temple of Heaven” was designed and begun in the fourth year of Yongle of the Ming (1406), and completed in the eighteenth year of Yongle (1420), the whole taking fourteen years. In the fifteenth year of Guangxu of the Qing (1889) it was struck by lightning, and rebuilt the year after.

The outer circuit of the altar is 1,700 meters east and west, 1,600 meters north and south, covering about two hundred and seventy hectares, an area more than twice as great again as the old Palace.

Within the altar, from the “Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests” on the north to the “Imperial Vault of Heaven,” there runs a north-and-south terrace of dressed stone, three hundred and sixty meters in length; yet even this was not enough to serve for a runway.

The runway of the temporary airfield was, likely enough, built on a piece of open ground to the right, of a length upward of a thousand meters. Whether so, I dare not too firmly say, for at the time I had no chance to look closely, and, sitting within the aircraft, the field of view was too narrow to take in the whole.

The moment the aircraft left the ground it was already beyond the walls, and the bandit forces’ guns thereupon opened up—a scene to try a man’s nerves.

The Temple-of-Heaven airfield once flew out a regiment’s strength in withdrawal to Qingdao; but thereafter it came under the fire of the bandit forces’ guns, and so was given up. And here the whole air passage of Beiping to the outer world was likewise cut.

(4) The pawnshops kept open.——In the city of Beiping there was no publicly-run pawnshop; every pawnshop was privately owned.

The writer had, all along, no liking for pawnshops, the chief reason being their bleeding of the poor. Whenever the “appraiser” in the shop, perched high above, in that tone all his own looked down upon the man standing beneath the counter with upturned face to pawn his goods, and asked, “How much to pledge it for?”—there was a great air about him of a judge opening court to try a case, and that man holding up on high his bundle of clothes was, in very truth, like a prisoner awaiting sentence. That the pawnshops kept open with the enemy at the walls was no great outpouring of mercy to bless the humble folk of peck and pint; it was that there were still men come to redeem their pledges, and they could not but open. Only, the hours of business were shortened, but a few hours each day, from nine or ten in the morning to two or three in the afternoon.

The Beiping folk, many of them cut off from all production, were, save for the official families not yet fallen into decay, on the whole poor enough. There were not a few who, a day without earning, went a day unable to scrape up their food and drink—men who pedaled pedicabs, or kept little stalls, or played their trade at the temple fairs, or did coolie-work here and there; and, add to these those who could not get out of the city and had lost their living, there was nothing for it but to pawn.

The poor and hard-pressed multitude—where should they get watches or fountain pens? It was nothing but clothes and bedding; and in the “nine-nines” cold of deep winter, a whole household, old and young, huddled together, pawning the padded quilt to buy “mixed-grain flour” to eat—of such there was no lack either.

(5) The boom of guns, and the townsfolk unafraid.——From the seventeenth of the twelfth month, from morning till night, the gunfire within the city did not cease. The strange thing was that the people in the streets and lanes neither took fright nor lost their heads: he who had something to do did it, and every man seemed to go about at his ease.

The writer must point out that this was a “keeping one’s composure amid upheaval” of a special import, quite other in its nature from a cool meeting of the case.

Almost all who had long settled and dwelt at Beiping held a certain notion: they believed that, whoever fought whom, every time the fighting came to the very foot of the city walls, the peril would of itself turn to safety, and no side would destroy this cultural and ancient city whose allotted span was not yet run.

And this time—was it not the selfsame thought?

It was not that in their hearts they looked for the Communist army to enter the city the sooner, nor that they hoped the National army would the sooner be gone; in very truth, all they prayed for was that the fighting should go on no longer, that everyone might live out his days in peace and quiet.

Of politics they had, at bottom, no clear notion; they could not so much as make out the very frame of the state. And no great wonder either, when one comes to think of it: since the founding of the Republic, though it was not yet forty years, this place Beiping had already, who knows how many times, changed to a “new order of things.”

Least of all did they dream that this would prove a calamity without precedent, unlike, every whit, all that had gone before. O men of Beiping—on what day shall you win through to your deliverance?

(6) The markets and temple fairs, for the most part as usual.——The markets and temple fairs, within the city of Beiping and without, seemed uncommonly many—perhaps truly more than in other places. For many years they had held to one and the same form, unchanged to this day. At this moment, though what went on outside the city was unknown, within the city it was as of old.

Markets: reckoned by the hour, there were morning markets and night markets; whether there were noon markets too I do not know. Reckoned by kind, there were flower markets and bird markets, and it seems there were even cat markets and dog markets and the like. Now, of the streets that take their name from a market, there are the Pearl Market, the Mule-and-Horse Market, the Crock-and-Tile Market, and so on, which prove that of old too each had its market. So far as the writer knows, after the victory in the War of Resistance there appeared besides a “Dongdan night market.”

Temple fairs: they were past all counting; those I remember are the Dongyue Temple, the Baiyun Temple, the Baita Temple, the Huguo Temple, the Longfu Temple, the Yonghegong, and others. To put it simply: save that at the New Year and the festivals, on the first and the fifteenth, they were of a special liveliness, on the other days, from the first of the month to the thirtieth, round and round again, there was a temple fair every day. And in truth each temple fair was much of a muchness with the rest; the difference was only that at some places they burned incense and worshiped the Buddha, and at some there was left but eating, drinking, and merriment.

Of the several million inhabitants of the city of Beiping, not a few, surely, lived by this; and for the general run of townsfolk the going to market and the strolling of the temple fairs had grown into a habit—to say it a little strongly, had come even to be reckoned a part of daily life. At this time, though the gates were shut fast and the gunfire ceased not, yet on the ninth and the tenth of each month the Longfu Temple in the eastern city was still thronged as thick as ever with sightseers.

The peddlers of the Dongdan night market stood in their rows. In former days it opened from nightfall to about ten; now it had been moved forward to three or four in the afternoon, and by not yet eight the market broke up. Across the road from the night market was the newly-laid temporary airfield. By day there were aircraft taking off and landing; at night, there being no equipment for night flying, it lay still one pitch of blackness. By rights this was a military installation; yet it was under no control, and the passers-by—save for such as, in idle curiosity, strolled the night market and looked at the aircraft—wore, toward the business of the war, a very air of its being no concern of theirs.

(7) Beyond reason, yet within the common run of human feeling.——The writer holds some of these phenomena to be abnormal; the Beiping man does not think them so. The reason of it is not hard to make out. One evening, cold of weather and empty of belly—though it was not that we were pinched with cold and hunger—the writer had made an appointment with my brother Yuanshen and two colleagues besides; seated by twos in two pedicabs, we took counsel to find some place to eat a warm and comfortable meal, and so bethought us of the Donglaishun, no great way off. Only, none of us knew whether it would still be open or not.

The cart drew up at the great gate of the Dong’an Market at Jinyu Lane; the sightseers strolling the market were not many. In at the gate and to the left, three or five paces off, was the Donglaishun. One saw the master of the “sliced meat,” as of old, standing in his corner with head bent slicing the meat, and the waiter at the door already crying out at the top of his voice, “Inside—if you please!”

The Donglaishun mutton house had, first and last, been enlarged and rebuilt many a time. It is now of three storeys, each storey having its open seats and its private rooms, some ten and more chambers great and small, roomy and to spare. It is said that, master and men together, up and down, there were more than a hundred souls; and the greatness of the outlay may be imagined.

Here was one peculiar thing: though the fittings of the upper floor and the lower were unlike, yet the prices were the same. And the local regular patrons, for all that, all loved to gather on the ground floor. By any fancy, with the enemy now at the walls, the door should be so still one might net sparrows before it—that would suit reason; how came it, then, still to be all ablaze with lamplight, every floor full above and below? Can it be that a love of good eating is human nature itself?

On beyond the Donglaishun was the Jixiang Theater. Before the siege, day and night alike, there had been every day a play by day and a play by night; now, straitened by the hours and by the traffic, it had stopped its performances.

The main street of the Dong’an Market, the whole street of it, was given over to the selling of fine little delicacies; now a good half were not set out, and the strollers of the market were but two or three at a time.

Within the market was another place, the Danggui Bazaar, all bookshops and book-stalls; now it was cold and deserted, not the shadow of a soul to be seen.

Since we have come to speak of human nature, perhaps someone will ask: “And how of the ‘Eight Great Lanes’?” There is an answer to that: the “Eight Great Lanes” are but a few little lanes running crosswise one upon another, comprising, it is said, Shitou Lane, Baishun Lane, Boxing Lane, the Greater Li-Shamao Lane, the Lesser Li-Shamao Lane, the Wang-Guangfu diagonal street, the Fire-God Temple by-lane, and Hanjiatan. These places even the old Beiping man could mostly not name; he knew only that they were a red-light district lying all together in one quarter. And the red-light district of Beiping was not this one place alone; only the “Eight Great Lanes” had the loudest name.

Not all in the lanes were houses of pleasure; those that made it their trade must needs hang out a signboard. Their grades were of three: the first was called the “pure-chant little troupe”—as the name declares, professing to sell song and not the body; the second was called the “teahouse,” where one did but drink tea and chat; the third was called the “resting-place,” and this it was that was your lordship’s house of ease.

In days gone by, when the bright lamps first were lit, that was the very hour of bustle and press, of carriages thick as flowing water and horses like a moving throng, the doors and courts like a marketplace. Now it was otherwise: at the first-grade houses the lamps were not lit, for the men of money, the men who would spend it, were either gone off to take refuge or shut close within doors; the second-grade teahouses were as cold and forsaken; only the third-grade resting-places, on the contrary, flourished past all wonder.

What observation yielded was, in very truth, the fact; and, being past explaining, I set it down under the head that “food and sex are of nature.” (8) Walking the whole city over, to take in the atmosphere of the war.——The city of Beiping was, after all, ringed round by the bandit forces; and besides the booming gunfire that every ear could hear, there were, to be seen, of course, many a scene of war.

(1) Just outside the Qianmen gate of Beiping, on the left hand and the right, stand the East Station and the West Station. The East Station is of the Beiping-Tianjin railway, the West Station of the Ping-Han railway. In former days the East Station was busier than the West; the comers and goers thronged, and even by night there was no break in the passers-by. Now, no train could get out at all, and the two stations, east and west, wore a look of cold and utter desertion. And so, by the chain of it, the out-of-work must needs have grown the more.

How it fared with the Ping-Sui railway outside Xizhimen, being unable to get out of the city I could not see; but it too, surely, was in the same desolation.

There was no going further to make it out; and the rolling stock upon these several railways was, I fear, well-nigh all lost.

(2) Sitting in a pedicab, I passed by Chongwenmen.

Chongwenmen is also called Hademen; and I remember there was a cigarette brand of the common sort that was likewise called “Hademen.” “Hade” was a foreign general in the time of the Eight-Power Allied Forces’ attack on the city of Beijing, who was killed, and after whom it was named to force his remembrance upon men. Herein lies an element of “national humiliation.” The Beiping folk, not knowing the ins and outs of it, still mostly call it “Hademen.” (Here the writer may be in error; let the reader consult the piece by Mr. Li Qiusheng in the 303rd number of “Biographical Literature,” “‘Hademen’ Has Nothing to Do with ‘National Humiliation,’” by which I have been set right.) Chongwenmen is a gate of the inner city—that is, one is still within the city when one has gone out of the gate—so that whether it were opened or shut, closed or not, came all to the same. Yet here a “checkpoint” was set, to search the passers-by, to no real purpose whatever—a thing quite superfluous.

Chongwenmen in the eastern city stands far opposite Xuanwumen in the western city; how it fared over there with the searching of the passers-by, on this side one could not know. (3) The police of Beiping were noted for the special excellence of their manner of service.

To speak of it more thoroughly, one should divide the Beiping police into the two grades of high police officer and rank-and-file patrolman. The high officers changed with the shifting of the general state of things; but the rank-and-file patrolmen there were who did the work a lifetime long, through every vicissitude. For instance, there were not a few who had gone on from the days of the Northern warlords, through the governance of Duan Qirui and the coming of Zhang Zuolin within the passes, through the five-colored flag giving way to the white sun in the blue sky over a field of red, through the eight years of the Japanese usurpation, on to the recovery at victory, and are in office still. And just for this reason their political consciousness was rather the dimmer. Yet a faithful minding of their duty was, first and last, their strong point.

Strange to say, from the time Beiping was besieged, the state of public order within the city was on the contrary better than of old: not only were marches and demonstrations seldom seen, but even ordinary criminal cases, and traffic accidents and the like, were few; and the police of the several districts kept warning the householders, in an admonishing tone, “It’s not quiet outside—if you’ve no business, go out little, put the lamps out early and sleep.”

(4) The newspapers came out as usual, but it was the delivery, on the contrary, that grew unusual. Of the papers, great and small, published at Beiping there were perhaps several tens; those I remember with the most subscribers, and the widest circulation too, were the two great papers, the “Beiping Chenbao” and the “Shijie Ribao.” As for the “Yishi Bao” and the “Dagong Bao” published at Tianjin, in the past they had reached Beiping early in the morning; now, communications being cut, even were they published as before, they could not be brought in. The most regrettable thing is that I did not keep the papers published at that time; if I could have a few of them now, to open and look over, how good that would be!

Beiping is too vast, its scenes past all taking in; and even could I hold them in mind, I could not write them out to the end!

### 4. Gone for Good: Farewell Beiping, Farewell Home

In the early part of the first month of the thirty-eighth year, Mr. Zheng Jiemin flew to Beiping by special plane from Nanjing, bearing an order from above. The aircraft landed at the very “Temple-of-Heaven airfield.”

Mr. Jiemin told me that he came under orders of the late President Chiang, to deliver a letter in the President’s own hand to Fu Zuoyi, the commander-in-chief of the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters.”

What was in the letter? Mr. Jiemin did not disclose it. Nor, of course, ought he to have disclosed it. But out of curiosity, and out of the weight I laid on it too, and since an intelligence man cannot but be given to much surmising—in the jargon, to “analysis and assessment”—as to how I made it out, I dared not say at the time, though there is no harm in writing it now. Most likely it was to instruct Fu Zuoyi, on the one hand to hold Beiping fast, and on the other, so far as might be, to draw off troops and fly them to Qingdao; and next, to sound out the possibility of leading his forces to break out by their own strength and go south or go west. Besides this, it held too the intent of steadying Fu Zuoyi’s own resolve.

Whether I guessed right, in the end, there will be no answer to tell.

Mr. Zheng Jiemin stayed but a day at Beiping, and had his several meetings with the commanders of the garrison and the heads of the intelligence-and-security units. I fancy it was not that Mr. Zheng sought them out of his own motion, but that they wished to hear a little news that touched them nearly; for Mr. Zheng’s errand did not lie in this.

There are two things whereof the writer may not remember quite exactly—it may be an over-fineness of the nerves, a wild guessing.

The first: Mr. Zheng had taken, from a safe-deposit box in the American National City Bank in the Legation Quarter, two small caskets of things, which, he said, were all important documents; nor were they, as I judged, gold or silver or jewels. What in the end they were, and why they should be taken out at just this time, I never knew. Yet these, it may be, were not Mr. Zheng’s own private property?

The second: he had met with Fu Jingbo, the secretary of John Leighton Stuart, then the American ambassador to China (Fu being a Chinese, without diplomatic standing). On the face of it Fu was a leftist, reckoned even a fellow-traveler of the Communists; but in fact his allegiance lay elsewhere. This was an “intelligence undertaking” of a rather higher order, of whose content I have no knowledge. And now to speak of matters bound up with my own work.

I went to see Mr. Zheng to report on the business of the “stay-behind work.” I had prepared a small booklet, wherein was set down the whole of the personnel of the “stay-behind work,” and the ways of making contact with each, and the like. My meaning was to ask Mr. Zheng to take this booklet away with him, to serve as the ground of the communication to be carried on hereafter.

I set the greatest store by this booklet; it held the lives and the future of a good many men!

Mr. Zheng took it from my hand, turned it over, looked it through, and gave the booklet back to me again, asking, “Have you any other task at Beiping?” Whether I had a task or no, how should Mr. Zheng not know? His meaning I understood: he pointed to my holding, besides, the post of leader of a “Baomiju directly subordinate section.” I said, “No more; the moment there is a plane, I am going back to the unit.”

Thereupon he decided: “Set your affairs in order, and tomorrow morning at nine go back with me first to Nanjing; we shall talk it over at length on the way.” I went back to the brigade headquarters at Fuxue Lane, sent for my brother Zhang Luying, the brigade-attached officer, and told him what Mr. Zheng had just decided. At the same time I asked him to take charge of the brigade’s affairs after my going, and to look well, too, after the several dozen comrades left on watch.

As to how the several dozen comrades remaining at Beiping were to be withdrawn, I on the one hand reported it to Mr. Zheng, to devise a means and press the Ministry of National Defense to the utmost for air transport; and on the other, asked brother Luying to negotiate on the spot with the air-force base.

Besides this, I entrusted my brother Yuanshen with some private things, to carry to Shanghai when occasion served; and the affairs of the “Beiping directly subordinate section” I handed over to comrade Mao Yilu to manage in my stead. When I left Beiping, I had but the one suit of grass-green cotton uniform on my back and a padded greatcoat. This was because, to go by military plane, one must show oneself a soldier and not a common citizen.

The night passed without a word, and early the next morning I hurried to the “Temple-of-Heaven airfield,” far off in the southern city.

The thing I set down below is true past all doubt, and deep-graven in my memory; there can be no mistake in it.

Before boarding, a certain commander, one of my classmates and a good friend, entrusted to me a pistol he had carried on his person the year round, to give, when I reached Nanjing, to his family, and that was all—no other word besides. I surmised that this held the parting sense of “seeing the thing as seeing the man.” It was understood between us without the speaking. This was the prelude; what follows is the true matter.

I mounted the aircraft; Mr. Zheng Jiemin was already in his seat. The aircraft had a dozen and more places, but save for Mr. Zheng and the writer there were only two or three men I did not know.

It had been set to take off at nine o’clock sharp; but now nine was past, and still there was no stir. In a little while someone came out of the pilot’s cabin forward to ask Mr. Zheng something; I could not clearly hear what Mr. Zheng said, but it was, likely enough, to bid them wait a little.

A few minutes more, and the man from the forward cabin came again to ask his word; and one saw someone get down from the aircraft, and thereupon there was heard the sound of hailing on the ground.

The voice was thrown out, amplified, through a microphone, and could be heard even in the aircraft. The word passed was: “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, Deputy Commander-in-Chief Deng, please board the aircraft; we are about to take off.” Twice it was cried, with no response. Mr. Zheng rose from his seat, and a man I did not know rose up too and followed him. One after the other they climbed down from the aircraft, to inquire into the truth of the matter, one judged, or else to go and telephone.

They were gone a good fifteen minutes, and then Mr. Zheng and the other man came back to the aircraft. His face was blank, his brows knit tight; he knocked at the door of the forward cabin, to tell them, likely enough, that they might take off.

It was now 9:35—more than half an hour late of the set time of departure.

After a good spell of deafening din, the aircraft sprang aloft; and it felt as though it were on the point of striking the city wall close before our eyes; in the blink of an eye it was over the wall and beyond the city. How high above the ground, I could not measure; but the men stirring below could still be dimly seen.

Were it not under a state of emergency, a man could not lightly come by such an experience as this.

Looking down, the ground was one stretch of earthen gray, the very look of the northern country in winter, with truly nothing to see.

In the flying, at the first, the talk aboard was low and could not be heard; after a while it was much better; only, now and again, a word or two was still not quite to be caught.

Mr. Zheng, as if by chance, told me: “Commander-in-Chief Fu had promised to ask Deputy Commander-in-Chief Deng to go with me, on his behalf, to Nanjing, to make his report and set forth his views to the Leader; and Deputy Commander-in-Chief Deng had appointed with me to my face that we should all gather here this morning and board together, and take off at nine. How comes it to have gone awry? Deng even asked me whether, if he brought two more men along with him, there would be room; and I said there would be no problem. Is not this a jest?” As Mr. Zheng spoke, there showed in his look a great bewilderment; and I, hearing and seeing it, could truly make nothing of it, head nor tail.

After a pause, Mr. Zheng said further: “Just now I went to telephone Commander-in-Chief Fu; he said that he too could not find Deputy Commander-in-Chief Deng at the moment, and that if there was no waiting for the time, we might as well fly first, and he would arrange another aircraft.” And after another pause, Mr. Zheng said one thing more: “I don’t know what tricks they are playing?” The writer thought to himself: of the nine deputy commanders-in-chief of the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters,” there was but one surnamed Deng, and it must surely be Deng Baoshan. Deng was an old general of the Northwest Army; now, save for the name of deputy commander-in-chief of the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters,” he had under his hand no troops he could command.

In the past there had, in truth, been in the intelligence material a report that “Deng Baoshan is in collusion with the Communist bandits”; but as there was no factual ground for it, it was hard to judge whether it were true; and the more so as, he being a “deputy commander-in-chief,” intelligence of this kind was the harder still to deal with.

In fact, the one who threaded the needle for Fu Zuoyi’s going over to the Communists and surrender to the enemy, who brokered the match at both ends, was afterward proved to be none other than Deng Baoshan.

Think of it: within this affair too there is much matter for thought. Why should Fu Zuoyi wish to send Deng Baoshan to represent him at Nanjing? Of his own will? Or against it? Or was it even that the letter named Deng by name, to be carried off?

At that time, had it already been determined that Deng Baoshan was a source of harm, then plainly this was, in a case past all remedy, a drawing of the firewood from under the cauldron, a guarding against the trouble before it came.

As for why Deng should have gone back on his word and thrown us over—the ready answer is known to all, and needs no further guessing.

Yet, say what one will, what we understood was, after all, but a scattering of stars and points of light.
