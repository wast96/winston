## Chapter 47

Now I felt an utter emptiness and loneliness. My heart was at once grieved, and vexed, and conscious of an unfathomable mystery; my feelings were at once alarmed, and stifled, and conscious of a subtle excitement.

Was this girl, after all, an enemy or a friend? If an enemy, why should she come so stealthily to take these secret documents? If a friend, then whose side's agent was she? Why should she also set a bomb in the safe?—I felt sure it was a bomb. This was what I could not understand, and I had no time to seek the answer.

Had I come a step earlier, had I taken the documents first, what would she have done? Told the Japanese to come and search me out? Had she discovered me, what would she have done? I had not seen her carrying a weapon. Had I come a step later, and jumped in just as she was opening the safe, what then would she have done—would she, like me, have hidden under the table?—

These reflections tangled and jumbled dimly in my mind. I put away the fountain pen and came out from under the table. Walking to the window, my fear had lessened and my tension too had eased. I looked out from the window; below there was still not a soul, and the ladder was still where I had set it. So I drew the curtain, slipped out from the window, and stood on the ladder; I began to pull the window tight and came softly down.

When at last I set foot on the ground, I laid the short ladder flat, very quickly it seemed, back where it had been, and, seeing there was not a soul about, my heart began to grow calm.

But where was Mei Yingzi? Had she not arranged to meet me here? I was anxious to see her, to report to her how it had gone, and yet she was not there. I waited in expectation for a full three minutes, and I was just planning how to slip in when someone should come out from inside, when I saw a shadow turn out from the corner of the wall. I flattened myself against the wall of the house, watching keenly; yes, it was a woman, in a black-fox coat—but at her neck too I saw the gleaming ring of pearls. In great excitement I brought myself out into view.

"Did you get it?" said Mei Yingzi, coming toward me with a smile.

"..." I hesitated.

"I saw you long ago from among the trees, and had to pick the very best moment to come over," she said in a low but very excited voice. "How did it go?"

"A failure, an utter failure." I took the key from my pocket and handed it back to her, saying it dejectedly.

"You forgot the combination on the safe?" She turned in an instant to a grave manner. Behind the mask her eyes gave out a strange, keen light that bore down on me; in the darkness this gaze was a little terrible, and I avoided it, pressing my body against the wall below the window, and I said:

"Someone else got in first."

"Someone else?" She took off her mask, showing her lovely face, and said in astonishment.

"Yes," I said. "A girl, and she even set a bomb in the safe—a bomb, I think it was."

I do not know why, but in that instant I again grew suspicious that the girl had been Mei Yingzi herself; I watched Mei Yingzi's figure, thought of the movements just now in the room, and believed it very possible that she had taken off the pearl collar to do this work. I looked at her indignantly, but she seemed to be pondering, and suddenly said:

"Can there be so strange a thing?"

"I rather thought it was you," I said with a cold smile.

But she paid me no heed; she was thinking, and after a long moment suddenly said:

"Her figure was very like mine?"

"Yes."

"Then it must be Bai Ping."

"But she had not the ring I gave her."

"A ring can be moved," she said, still thinking.

"Then it was that she feared I could not carry out this work."

"Nonsense." She showed a strange look and said: "She is too competitive!"

"Competitive?"

"She still keeps up a distinction between us; she must be after the credit, I think," she said.

Mei Yingzi's words astonished me greatly, and it dawned on me suddenly: although we were doing the same work, in their standpoints what Bai Ping represented and what Mei Yingzi represented were not the same. And I—I belonged to Mei Yingzi, and so Mei Yingzi used the word "us" in setting herself against Bai Ping. Mei Yingzi mused and thought, and I felt a kind of pain; in an instant it came to me that the reason they had contended to take on the work was, after all, not the lofty, pure, unselfish thing I had imagined, but "the credit"! Then Bai Ping's urging me, alone, to let her have the work had not been "sympathy" for me or "care" for me, but "the credit"! In such an arena of struggle, no matter that what we represented were two nations, it was always one ideal, and still we were after "the credit"—"the credit," just as the members of one and the same football team each want the credit for themselves. How can the hearts of the people of this world be forever so narrow and so small!

I did not know what Mei Yingzi was thinking, and I said gravely:

"But we can very easily prove whether this person is Bai Ping."

"..." Mei Yingzi raised her head and looked at me.

"I secretly sprayed ink on her skirt."

"You?" said Mei Yingzi, showing her almond-tinted front teeth. "Really? Now that is a splendid piece of work..." She seemed about to say something more, but the lights in the corridor came on, the garden already showing a stronger brightness. Mei Yingzi stopped speaking at once, put on her mask, and craned her head out from the corner of the wall; I followed her to look. Many people were crowding into the corridor, and then four or five couples came into the garden and very quickly walked over, scattering to different places. Mei Yingzi at once slipped her hand through my arm and led me out of this passage.

I felt this was a very rash thing to do; it would surely have been more prudent to lie low and go out following the people when they went back in. And in fact, in the very moment we came into the garden, though no one took notice, there were indeed two couples who saw us, and I was much worried, and I said in a low voice:

"You don't believe they saw us?"

"Silly," said Mei Yingzi with a faint smile. "In the Garden of Eden, apart from Adam and Eve, all are naturally angels."

This remark, of course, meant that those people were all there to help her get back in. Mei Yingzi's arrangements astonished me; I looked at those few couples and followed Mei Yingzi over to the neighborhood of one of them, and I saw on that woman's hand the ring with the white cross in a red frame—it was Miko, no question. But I felt a desolate, ill-omened fancy: what rose before me was not the Miko my reason knew, but Stephen, buried in some strange corner of my consciousness! I had been in the garden a long while, and was a little cold; I gave a shiver, and Mei Yingzi asked:

"Cold?"

"They say that in the Garden of Eden one does not tell cold from heat," I said, and at once thought of the terrible shadow in my consciousness: "But besides angels there are also devils."

"That is the serpent!" Suddenly I heard Miko saying to the man beside her.

"Don't be afraid, miss," the man said. "How could there be a snake in winter? Perhaps it's the shadow of a tree."

"It is the serpent!" Mei Yingzi said to me in a low voice. "Then it is the one your ink is on."

—

Mei Yingzi led me into the back veranda; the dance music had not yet begun, and we sat down there and called for two drinks, and Mei Yingzi told me to wait while she herself went in. I now had a rather easier mind for smoking, and as I smoked among the crowd all around I began to search for ink stains on the women's skirts. But this was only a way of passing the time, not an urgent task, for in fact I naturally could not watch every woman guest too closely, which would make people wonder. At last Mei Yingzi came, sat down quietly, and asked me for a cigarette to smoke; there were a few water marks on the table just then, and, half absently, with her narcissus-like fingers she traced in the water, and all at once wrote out the two words "not it," then lightly wiped them away and wrote the two words "Bai Ping"—plainly meaning she had gone to observe Bai Ping's skirt and there was no ink stain on it. Then it must be someone else, and perhaps that person belonged to the enemy, who, suspecting that someone would come tonight to steal the documents—but then why not guard against it openly, instead of secretly setting a bomb? And if she did not belong to the enemy, then whose was she? Never mind for now whose side she was on; there was no reason at all for her to set a bomb after taking out the documents—could she mean to assassinate Umetake too? Perhaps what she had put in was not a bomb; then what was it? I smoked in silence, these questions circling in my mind. Mei Yingzi too was silent; I believed she too was guessing at that strange person, or thinking how to spy out this ink stain on the skirt. Suddenly our eyes met, and it came to me with a jolt that I had not yet told her the details of what had happened; I thought I should at least tell her about the bomb. But the music sounded, the lights in the corridor dimmed, and I led her in to dance. In the crowd I said:

"Need I tell you the details?"

"No," she said crisply. "Keep your eye on the ink stain you left."

But the lights dimmed again, and there were so many people, that there was truly no way to observe, no way to search. We kept silent, until the dance piece ended.

After this I danced three or four pieces running with other people; I had already given up hope of the search, and put almost no great effort into watching.

Perhaps some twenty minutes later I found a chance to dance with Bai Ping, and I said:

"You know it all?"

She nodded, and for a long while said nothing, but at last she said:

"When you leave here, remember to go to my place first."

"Is there nothing more for me to do?" After a pause I added.

She nodded again.

After this I passed this lively night as one ordinarily does, seeming half-consciously to avoid dancing with Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping. In two hours I danced only twice with Mei Yingzi and once with Bai Ping, and said nothing much; Mei Yingzi only charged me to watch for the ink stain and to tell her when I found it, while Bai Ping did not say even these few words.

Just then it came to me with a jolt, this so-called "credit." Was what Mei Yingzi guessed at wholly her own overwrought nerves, or did Bai Ping truly have this consciousness of "the credit," so that she wanted to find the ink stain herself and did not think to charge me with it?—I was tormented and uneasy over this!

Ever since Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi began to suspect each other, I had been made a fool of in every kind of way between them, and had borne countless wounds; and once I was wounded, it seemed they could from then on fully cooperate—who would have thought that the beginning of their cooperation would be the beginning of a rivalry for credit? Then what is born of this rivalry can, beyond doubt, be jealousy and suspicion, and then my wounds would have been paid for by nothing. If one day I left them, the distance between them would surely grow ever wider, until they kept secrets from each other and could not cooperate, or could even come to entrap each other; and what a bitter thing that was for me. Thinking so, I suddenly found this lively gathering, this teeming world, gray and hopeless; I walked in silence out to the corridor, and in the dim light drank alone at my wine, letting the world inside boil in its music while the long night vanished before my seat. Even when the interval came and people came out from inside, I could pay no heed. Suddenly, behind me, a hand was laid on my shoulder, and she said: "Tired, child?"

I gave a start, but at once saw on the fingers laid on my shoulder Bai Ping's ring, and I said:

"It is about time to be tired."

She sat down beside me; the waiter brought drinks, and she took a glass of lemon-colored wine, raised it, and said to me in a low voice:

"With this pale-yellow wine I drink to the victory of that blue rattlesnake of yours."

I did not understand, and hesitated a long while, when she said:

"Drink this cup! I salute you and bless you."

She drained it at a draught, and I emptied mine too, and only then did I grasp that she had already found the ink-stained woman.

When the music sounded I rose to dance with her, and I said:

"You have found her?"

"Keep your eye always on your left."

From then on I kept close to Bai Ping, and before much time had passed I saw, on the skirt of a woman on the left, the ink stain, very small, seven or eight dots like a dotted line, like... a little snake—I do not know why, but I gave a shiver. I kept close behind that dancing couple with Bai Ping, and slid to the front of them; I watched her face. Below the silver mask the chin she let show seemed to belong to a very gentle type of face—how was it that she was engaged in this business? I could almost not believe that the woman I had seen in the room just now was she. They drew away to my right, and I had an impulse to dance one with her at the next number, and so I asked Bai Ping:

"Do you know where she sits?"

"Diagonally opposite me, I think."

Bai Ping's words "I think" seemed not to be very certain, but recalling that gentle chin I felt I could find her in the seats. ...This was because in such a gathering we men seemed to have no right at all to bend down and study a woman's skirt, though we could study a woman's face. So I no longer forced myself to search through the crowd, but waited until this piece of music ended, and when the second piece began I hurried to the place diagonally opposite Bai Ping; but I could not find the gentle chin, could find only silver masks. Nor did time allow me to hesitate and search closely, and just then I danced, at random, with a girl in a silver mask; but in the very instant I began to dance I discovered that in the seat over on the right a woman, in the moment of rising to dance, was dragging her skirt into motion, and on that skirt were fixed the little blue snakes. I noted her seat at once: it was the fourth seat to the right of my partner, and I believed that at the next number I could surely find her to dance.

Sure enough, at the next piece of music I danced with her, and when she rose I made out closely the blue snake on her skirt. That was it—the one now at my side was my adversary of the room just now. I was over-excited, and could not say whether it was gladness or fear; I strove to keep calm, wanting to find a phrase to open talk with her, but truly did not know what to say. After a long moment I began by asking:

"Miss, do you remember whether I have danced with you before?"

"No," she said. "I think this is the first time."

"Then am I entitled to inquire your honorable name?"

"My name is Asamura Toshimiko," she said with a smile.

"What a lovely name!"

"Thank you."

"Have you been in China long?" I asked.

"Not so very long, I think."

Her cool reply left me unable to find anything more to ask, and so after a long moment I said:

"In such a gathering, the distance between us is too great."

"You think so?"

"Of course," I said. "Masks, nationality, and all sorts of guardedness and suspicion."

She made no answer. I said again:

"Perhaps it is the progress of the age, perhaps the retreat of mankind, that even a lovely, dear young lady now learns cunning, secrecy, and worldliness—pitiable and laughable at once."

"Is it fitting to say such things to a girl who is a stranger?"

"I beg your pardon," I said. "But when I asked how long you had been in China and you said 'not so very long,' I felt very sad."

"Strange," she said sarcastically.

"What I mean is that the meaning of tonight's masked ball is only to add, beyond the distance in our hearts, one more veil of a mask."

She made no answer. I said again:

"It seems that even when people have covered their faces, they still cannot treat one another with sincerity."

"You mean you want to know how many years, months, and days I have been in China?"

"If it is not so very worth keeping secret."

"But ten years and ten days seem to make no difference for us."

"How so...?"

"I mean that before we met, the past had no bearing on either of us; have we not been acquainted only a few minutes?"

"It is just because we have been acquainted only a few minutes that the past seems to me worth recalling; for if you had been ten years in China, then I should really wonder where I had been living all through those ten years."

"You guess I have been ten years in China?"

"At least, I think."

"No," she said. "Only two years."

"To speak such good Mandarin," I said.

"Did it never occur to you that before I came to China I had spent ten years in Manchukuo?"

"Ah, I beg your pardon, miss—it never once occurred to me that Manchukuo was not Chinese soil."

"I beg your pardon," she said.

Then the music stopped, and in the pieces that followed I danced with her from time to time, but she never said much. Silent, calm, gentle. Though I used many sarcastic and teasing words to rouse her interest, she was patient and silent throughout, letting not a trace of feeling or expression show.

Once, during an interval, I took her to the corridor for a drink; she sat beside me, and by the brighter light I looked, through the eyeholes of the mask, at her jet-black eyes, and again, below the mask, at her gentle chin, and I felt she must be a very beautiful woman.

The dance music started again, and everyone went in; we sat rather longer, and I said:

"I think I must have seen you somewhere."

"What is strange in that?"

"No, what I mean is that I want to know where I have seen you; may I ask you to take off your mask a moment?"

"I hear that at the end of the ball we are all to take off our masks."

"You mean you will not allow it?"

"Then why ask me at all?" she said. "Will you dance with me?"

"Thank you."

And I led her again into the ballroom.
