## Chapter 50

Now I began to understand the reason for Mei Yingzi's listlessness and dejection. I read the letter in my hand through twice and gravely handed it back to her. Without looking at me, she took a cigarette and set it between her lips, lit it with the lighter on the table, and then set light to the two sheets of paper. She gazed into the glowing flame, lost in thought, and in that instant I saw what lay stored in the depths of her eyes: an infinitely deep secret, flickering with a light of worry, care, and unease.

Only when both sheets of the letter had turned to ash did she raise her head and look at me; but I had no courage to look at her so any longer. I turned my eyes instead to Bai Ping, who was still as radiant as before — what a startling contrast it was. I had no wish to look for long, and, drawing in my gaze, I bowed my head and kept silent.

"So what do you mean to do?" Bai Ping began, to Mei Yingzi.

Mei Yingzi answered in a low voice; I did not attend to what she said, being taken up with the words of that letter. Through all these many days, Mei Yingzi had not noticed in the least, had never once thought, that someone was spying on her from behind — what a dreadful thing that was, and what a dreadful figure Miyama Yoshiko was! I said:

"Though we have in fact proved how formidable Miyama Yoshiko is — she saw through what we were about — in the upshot it has only shored up Umetake's trust in her; and I think Miyama Yoshiko's failure is no less than ours."

"And besides, our failure or success is not yet decided," said Bai Ping, suddenly fervent. "What decides it is whether or not we get the documents."

"The documents." Bai Ping's words rather startled me, and I said: "I thought your problem now was Miyama Yoshiko, not the documents."

"Do you think we ought to give up this work now?" said Mei Yingzi gently.

"Of course," I said. "We're in no position now to bother with these documents. To retrieve a thing that has already failed is harder than to win a fresh victory…"

I did not finish. What was in my mind was that, after last night's talk, I had long since granted the documents a lost cause, and in my sense of it we had all seemed to give the aim up too; but, to my surprise, Bai Ping broke in on us with sudden gravity, saying:

"But I won't hide it from you — I have everything arranged. Tonight, at two o'clock, I am going to fetch those documents."

"You?" I asked.

"What is all this?" asked Mei Yingzi.

"I've bought over Miyama Yoshiko's most trusted maid. She has promised to steal the documents out to me tonight at two, and to come out and take them back tomorrow at ten."

"Is such a thing possible?" said Mei Yingzi, suddenly roused.

"Why didn't you say so sooner?" I said, getting to my feet. "Bai Ping, you're astonishing — in a single afternoon you've made a whole new world. I must toast you with wine."

So saying, I went to the dining room for wine; I took three different kinds and fetched three glasses. Coming back in high spirits, I did not catch them talking, but I sensed they were both happy and excited. Yet when I had filled their glasses and looked from Bai Ping to Mei Yingzi, I found Mei Yingzi silent in thought, the smile at her lips gone stiff. Why should that be? At once I thought of the so-called squabble over "who gets the credit," and guessed at the jealousy in Mei Yingzi's heart: Bai Ping's achievement far outstripped anything Mei Yingzi had gained, and it had been carried off so brilliantly besides. A girl's heart is a narrow thing, and even one as brilliant and beautiful as Mei Yingzi was not above it — so I thought to myself, but I feigned at once not to know it, and, raising my wine cup, said to Mei Yingzi:

"Mei Yingzi, let us drink this cup together now to Bai Ping's glorious victory."

Bai Ping raised her cup and I clinked mine against it, but only then did Mei Yingzi lazily lift hers and touch it lightly to mine. I drained mine at a draught, but Mei Yingzi held hers without drinking, and said suddenly, gravely:

"Bai Ping — but this is a thing you must think over more carefully."

Bai Ping smiled, thought a moment, and said, easily:

"I've considered everything already."

Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping raised their cups once more, and the two of them drank off their wine; for a moment everyone was silent. Just then it came to me that the reports the two of them had made today were each the gains of the working group to which each belonged. Though the object of our work was the same, the groups were different, and if there was an edge of rivalry in it, it was just like the rivalry between Umetake's view and Miyama Yoshiko's: failure and success would, they say, soon be borne out, but what is borne out is not necessarily to be trusted — just as we had confirmed Umetake to be right when in fact the right lay with Miyama Yoshiko. For myself, by my cover I stood on Bai Ping's side, while by my work I stood on Mei Yingzi's; and now these plainly jealous words of Mei Yingzi's shifted my sympathy, little by little, onto Bai Ping. I said, excited again:

"Another cup, Bai Ping — I wish you victory tonight."

"Wait a moment," said Mei Yingzi again. "Bai Ping, I feel all the more now that this calls for caution. Would you tell me the full account of how you got in touch with this maid?"

"It was like this," said Bai Ping. "We know a cook, a young fellow from the maid's home district; they grew up together, and he brought the maid to meet me…"

"So that's it." Mei Yingzi suddenly bowed her head in thought, paused a while, then said: "Then why not have her bring the documents out to you?"

"She says she overheard Miyama Yoshiko on the telephone: the documents are to be delivered tomorrow at lunchtime. And she can't get out at one in the night."

"Then why not have that cook go to her and fetch them?"

"That would be far too unreliable," said Bai Ping.

"Was it you yourself who said you'd go and fetch them?"

"Yes," said Bai Ping. "There was no time then to think of anyone else, and the whole thing is my responsibility."

"And what is the signal?"

"We set our watches together, and agreed to meet just inside the entrance of their house."

"Did she say it had to be you yourself who went?"

"She said it would be best if it were me, so that she could account for it."

"No, no." Mei Yingzi said suddenly, with decision: "Bai Ping, you absolutely must not go."

"Why not?"

"I don't believe Miyama Yoshiko's own trusted maid could be bought over by us so easily."

"It's through that cook."

"I'm afraid this cook may himself be someone they've bought over."

"How could they have foreseen that we'd find our way to him?" Bai Ping smiled throughout.

"The strangest thing is that she insists you go yourself," Mei Yingzi said again.

"She did not insist that I go myself."

"Then," said Mei Yingzi, "could we not send someone else to receive the documents, while we wait together some way off?"

"What difference is there between sending someone else and going myself?" said Bai Ping quietly. "They don't take me for anyone important — you are the important one — and if they had some wicked scheme, they would surely have asked for you."

Mei Yingzi was silent a long while, seeming to fix her mind on something. I had not said a word. Of the two of them, my sympathy tonight was with Mei Yingzi, but through this exchange of theirs it went to Bai Ping. Her grave, composed manner seemed to have everything in hand; while in Mei Yingzi's words there was a fretful unease and an anxiety of spirit that were quite beyond understanding. A moment ago I had thought it sprang from jealousy, but now, in that fixing of her mind, I saw at the very center of her deep dark eyes an inexpressible flickering light — unease, wavering, worry, alarm, dread, and fear too. I was moved by it despite myself, and felt that what she said might not be jealousy at all, but a matter of her overwrought nerves. I said:

"Mei Yingzi, is it because your nerves are overwrought that you feel so uneasy, so unable to set your mind at rest?"

"Forgive me." Mei Yingzi said, with anger in her voice: "I have no wish for you, too, to sign on as an unpaid assassin and help bring Bai Ping to her ruin."

What a weight of a thing to say! I nearly clashed with her over it, but in the end I held myself down and, changing to a calm tone, said:

"My dear friend, can't we keep our feelings a little calmer?"

"Then I hope you'll understand the feeling between me and Bai Ping," said Mei Yingzi. "I won't hide it from you — I heard the name Lang Diyi long ago. She is no incompetent enemy."

I fell silent, and everyone was silent, and Bai Ping slipped quietly out. Only the two of us, Mei Yingzi and I, were left in the room. I thought Mei Yingzi would surely have some instruction for me now, but no — she only sat, grave and silent, without so much as looking at me. I could think of nothing to say either. What, I wondered, was Bai Ping thinking just now? Did she too feel Mei Yingzi's counsel sprang from jealousy? To send another person meant sending one of Mei Yingzi's own, meant sharing in Bai Ping's credit; and Bai Ping's grave, composed refusal of Mei Yingzi's proposal plainly had in it a certain self-respect and pride, the source of which was, beyond doubt, a distrust of Mei Yingzi and an unwillingness to let anyone else share in her success. I said:

"Mei Yingzi, treat me as one of your own people — have you really no instruction to give me?"

"Today, anyone who is Bai Ping's friend," said Mei Yingzi gravely, "ought to be dissuading her."

"You mean — you want to carry out this task in her place?"

"I?" Mei Yingzi raised her lowered eyes. "If the two of you put me forward, I would of course not decline."

Just then Bai Ping appeared in the doorway. She had changed into a cheongsam of black silk, trimmed along the edge with a jade-green band, and had changed the drops at her ears for jade-green pendants; the two greens matched exactly, as though chosen to go together. She came in with a light, charming smile, her earrings swaying, and looked in silence at Mei Yingzi; and Mei Yingzi's eyes suddenly flashed with fright. She said:

"Bai Ping, believe me — let us give up this work, won't you?"

"What talk is this?" said Bai Ping. "I've prepared everything — look, I've already changed my clothes."

At these words of hers, what I noticed was her feet: she was wearing a brand-new pair of soft-soled leather shoes; while Mei Yingzi's attention went especially to her head, and, as though struck by something odd, she suddenly asked:

"You're still wearing earrings?"

"They're my lucky earrings," said Bai Ping.

"Then," said Mei Yingzi suddenly, "if this work can't be given up, could I not be the one to receive the documents, while the two of you watch me from some way off?"

"What talk is this?" said Bai Ping. "If this is a dangerous thing, how could I hand the danger over to you?"

There the sentence broke off; had it gone on, it should have been: "And if there's no danger at all, then wouldn't this success be handed over for you to share for nothing?" Somehow that came to me at once, and I saw plainly that Bai Ping's mind ran just as I had supposed. If that was so, then every added word of dissuasion from Mei Yingzi was one more thing to set her against it. I did not know what to say for the best. For Mei Yingzi's sake, to heed her words could hardly be wrong; for Bai Ping's, to dissuade her would at least lessen her danger. So, reckoning my dissuasion could do no harm, I said:

"Bai Ping, can't we give this matter longer thought?"

"There's no time left," said Bai Ping. "I'll have a little something to eat and then set out."

"Have you arranged for anyone else to go with you?" asked Mei Yingzi.

"No," said Bai Ping. "I go alone."

"Do you mean you don't want us to come along either?"

"If you think it too dangerous." Though Bai Ping spoke with the same quiet gentleness as before, I caught in her voice her pride and self-respect.

"If you've decided," said Mei Yingzi, "then however dangerous it is, we'll go with you."

Mei Yingzi said this and glanced at me, and I nodded. Then, almost pleading with her eyes, she looked at Bai Ping and said:

"Bai Ping, believe me — believe that I have a cooler heart for judging such a thing. To stop this work is not to say we lack courage; you should know that only the bravest can rein in at the cliff's edge. Listen to me, Bai Ping."

"Mei Yingzi, I'm grateful for your kindness, but what I must carry out is the decision of the group I belong to; and if you think that judgment lies too far from your own, then I'd rather you didn't come."

Since Bai Ping's group held throughout to an optimistic judgment, then what Bai Ping followed and trusted, I thought, was her group; and in that light Mei Yingzi's view stood on the outside. I began to admire Bai Ping's resolve greatly, but I felt a strong distaste for her arrogant manner; while Mei Yingzi's pleading with Bai Ping just now made me feel a boundless earnestness and pathos, so that now my sympathy was wholly with her. I said:

"Bai Ping, couldn't you consider Mei Yingzi's view a little more coolly?"

"No." Bai Ping said firmly. "I've decided. I hope to see you once when I get back; otherwise, we'll meet again tomorrow after eleven, once I've returned the documents."

"If you're as set on it as this," said Mei Yingzi steadily, "then of course we'll go with you, and wait some way off, and watch you take the documents and come back together."

Just then Ah Mei brought in tea and refreshments. Bai Ping ate with good cheer, and I too had a few pieces of cake, but Mei Yingzi drank only a few mouthfuls of tea. At last she poured out three cups of wine, and said:

"Let us drain them." And she raised her cup and said again:

"With my most heartfelt prayer I wish you victory."

We all clinked cups with Bai Ping. Bai Ping did not hesitate, but drank hers off at a draught.

After that Mei Yingzi had no more words to dissuade Bai Ping. She watched Bai Ping's every movement and smile, and then charged her with a great many cautions: she told Bai Ping that the moment she got there, first, she must note where the car stood; second, if the person was slow to keep the appointment, she must on no account wait long, but come straight back to the car — and at the last, struck suddenly by the thought that none of our cars would serve, she went out to make a telephone call, and came back to tell us that a more suitable car could be sent round. She told Bai Ping not to fret.

But Bai Ping, glancing at her watch, did indeed grow uneasy, and yet would not refuse Mei Yingzi's kindness; she paced back and forth in the room, while Mei Yingzi kept her eyes on Bai Ping's moving shadow, and I too, restless, looked from Bai Ping to Mei Yingzi.

Silence, everyone silent — expectant, and on edge. That minute of silence was dreadful, and an agony too, and even now, looking back, my heart cannot help but tremble a little.
