## The Northern March

Toshizō and the men of the Shinsengumi had returned to the Kantō. Naturally the writer, too, takes up a fresh sheet here and heads it

{j} "The Northern March,"

{j} for a new part of the tale.

It was in this period, most likely, that Hijikata Toshizō came fullest into his own.

History, at the boiling-point that was the close of the Tokugawa years, threw up two strange men, Kondō Isami and Hijikata Toshizō; what they contributed to history, I cannot say.

Only this: they resisted the current of the times, fiercely.

Ever since Toba-Fushimi the lords of the realm, who till then had held to a neutral stance, vied with one another to ride the "current," the current whose figureheads were Satsuma and Chōshū, and almost all of them became the "Imperial army."

Not the Three Houses alone, Kii, Owari, and Mito, but the collateral houses, and even the Ii, first among the hereditary vassals, became the Imperial army.

They joined in the destruction of the Tokugawa.

Put so, these domains riding the current look all self-interest, and comical besides; but there was this too, that the need to build a unified state around the Kyoto court had grown plain to every eye.

They now became a part of

{j} "Japan,"

{j} and in that they took their share.

Since the age of the warring domains, it was the first time the domains had held any consciousness of the nation.

But one set of men resisted, holding that this was no "Japan" at all, but merely Satsuma and Chōshū.

By resisting, they sought to show their own

{j} "chivalry."

{j} That was the wish.

Said thus, it turns schematic and loses, on the contrary, all feeling of truth.

Well, there is nothing for it but to write it as a novel.

It was on the twentieth of that First Month that the Shinsengumi, after their spell of garrison at Shinagawa, moved into the official residence of Torii Tango-no-kami on the Daimyō-kōji at Marunouchi in Edo.

The troopers numbered forty-three. Of these, however, the wounded had been housed in a foreign-run hospital at Yokohama.

The surviving officers were, besides the three who had been with the corps since its founding, Nagakura Shinpachi, Harada Sanosuke and Saitō Hajime, such men as Ogata Shuntarō, called the most cultivated man in the corps, and Ōishi Kuwajirō, called a master hand at killing.

Okita Sōji was under treatment.

Kondō's wound, once he was back in Edo, mended by leaps, until he could even go up to the castle by palanquin.

"Toshi, it's the water of Edo that suits me after all,"

"A good thing,"

{j} they were saying, and in the course of it, within Chiyoda Castle, Kondō fell in with a curious personage.

The man was a great hatamoto, ranked for the Fuyō Chamber, with a house stipend of four thousand koku that, with the emoluments and office-lands of his post, came to ten thousand.

He was Satō Suruga-no-kami, administrator of the Kōfu garrison.

What was strange was not the man himself. It was the substance of what he whispered into Kondō's ear.

"Kondō, I have a matter to speak of in confidence,"

{j} he said.

The truth was that, with the collapse of the shogunate (though the Tokugawa house, its castles and waters and its directly held domains remained), Satō had come back to Edo to consult the senior councillors on how he was to conduct himself henceforth as administrator of the Kōfu garrison.

But the council of elders had no time for such things and gave his suit no proper hearing, only saying,

{j} "As seems best, as seems best,"

{j} and no more than that.

Satō was at a loss.

Kōfu was a million koku.

It was the old domain of the Takeda house of the warring years, and had since become the private land, the heaven's-domain, of the Tokugawa. Satō Suruga-no-kami was the "governor" who administered that domain of a million koku.

"At this moment Itagaki Taisuke of Tosa is coming down the Tōsandō at the head of a great army. The chief aim of this Tōsandō force is to take that Kōfu of a million koku into the Imperial army's hand,"

"Hm,"

It was plain enough. Call it the court's army as one might, it was a joint household of many domains, and the Kyoto government had no domain-land of its own.

There was nothing for it but to seize the shogunal lands.

"So. Kōfu, is it,"

"Left as it stands, it will only be taken by the Imperial army,"

In Kōfu Castle there were two hundred garrison samurai out of Edo, but nearly all had drawn back to Edo; what remained were twenty mounted yoriki, who by the shogunate's table of offices were as good as non-combatants, and a hundred dōshin besides, and both being local functionaries, they would take no part in a fight.

"It is an empty castle, near enough,"

"Ho,"

Kondō listened with his arms folded, as was his habit when he was thinking.

"And what would you have me do about Kōfu Castle?"

Seize it by the Shinsengumi's own hand, was what Satō Suruga-no-kami meant.

(This is interesting.)

{j} And Kondō went, together with Satō, to the senior councillors' antechamber.

"Very well,"

{j} said the senior councillor Kawazu Izu-no-kami Sukekuni.

In point of fact, the Tokugawa house had returned the reins of government, but it had not yet lost its four million koku of Tokugawa land, nor the seven million that included the hatamoto holdings.

The new government pressed it to give up its lands as well, and this wrangle was one cause of the opening of the fighting at Toba-Fushimi.

The Tokugawa refusal was, as reasoning, only natural: having returned the government and become one daimyō among others, there was no reason the Tokugawa alone must give up its land when no other daimyō had returned a single tsubo.

For one thing, if it gave up the land, would not the eighty thousand hatamoto be cast onto the streets?

On the other side, the new government's dispatch of its "Imperial armies" down every highway to make war upon and destroy Tokugawa Yoshinobu had, hard-pressed as things stood, one practical object: the taking of

{j} "land."

{j} It was land they were after.

And yet.

The essential Tokugawa Yoshinobu held to submission and self-confinement to the last, and had at length left Edo Castle and withdrawn to the Daijiin of the Kanei-ji at Ueno.

He had no stomach to defend the Tokugawa lands by force of arms.

(So Kōfu, a million koku, hangs in the air. The Imperial army comes only to pick it up. Then if we hold it before the Imperial army arrives, it is ours.)

{j} so Kondō thought.

The councillors were of the same mind.

"Can you hold it, by the Shinsengumi's hand?"

{j} said Kawazu Izu-no-kami.

"We can,"

"Then hold it, we would ask. Funds for the campaign, firearms and the rest, we will furnish so far as we are able,"

At this, one of the councillors, Kawazu or else Hattori Chikuzen-no-kami, seems to have said, in jest,

{j} "If you will secure Kōshū for us, we will portion off five hundred thousand koku to the Shinsengumi,"

Whether he meant that it was a work worth that much, or whether it was a joke out and out, in either case, to Kondō's ear it sounded as

{j} "We will give you your share,"

{j} so he heard it.

The men who said it were of no great account, either. A senior councillor after the collapse of the shogunate was no longer a minister of government but merely a steward of the Tokugawa house. In rank, too, where such men had once been chosen from among the hereditary daimyō, they were now chosen from the hatamoto, and these to a man incompetent; nay, even had they been able administrators, things had come to such a pass that there was no knowing how the Tokugawa house was to be managed at all.

It was, in a word, all confusion.

(Five hundred thousand koku.—)

Kondō rejoiced almost past his wits.

***

"Toshi, five hundred thousand koku, he says,"

{j} said Kondō, dropping his voice, the moment he was back at the Shinsengumi quarters on the Daimyō-kōji.

"Never mind that, how's the wound?"

"Doesn't hurt,"

Wound or no wound, it was past mattering to him.

"Toshi, we get up a force at once and push out for Kōshū Castle,"

Kondō, in the last days of his Kyoto time, had kept company with the go-between agents of the domains and, under the sway of such men as Gotō Shōjirō of Tosa, had been turning into a passable man of national affairs; but the base metal showed through after all.

Kondō too had held a fair enough view of the times; but this one business of the grab for five hundred thousand koku of Kōfu turned our Kondō Isami back into a warring-domains warrior of three hundred years before. To him this Boshin war had come to seem like the age of the warring domains.

"Kondō-san, are you in your right mind?"

{j} and Toshizō peered into his face.

"Back in the Kyoto days, when you were forever preaching your Union of Court and Camp—that reverence for the throne was all very well as reverence for the throne, but that the governing was the Edo shogunate's to do by the court's commission, and puffing such reasoning at the go-betweens of every domain, I told you once that it did not suit you. This time the wind seems to have shifted,"

"Toshi, the times have changed. It's a thing you can't grasp,"

"The times, is it,"

{j} he said; but to Toshizō, a brawler born, the great brawl of pushing into Kōshū to hold down a million koku held, in a sense different from Kondō's, an unbearable allure.

(This time I'll do it Western-style.)

In his breast he had that same Infantry Handbook.

"Toshi, raise troops at once,"

"Let's do that,"

{j} and Toshizō set about busying himself with it.

Kondō too went up to the castle daily and, meeting the councillors, negotiated to raise as large a force as could be managed.

To recruit a great army, the commanders needed rank first of all.

The shogunal council made Kondō the equivalent of a wakadoshiyori and gave Toshizō the rank of yoriai-seki, and obtained the sanction of Yoshinobu, then in his confinement.

"We're daimyō,"

{j} said Kondō.

It was so. A wakadoshiyori was a hereditary daimyō of under one hundred thousand koku. And Toshizō's yoriai-seki was a great hatamoto of over three thousand koku.

But the shogunate had already ceased to exist. To the Tokugawa house it must have cost nothing to lavish whatever dignities it pleased on the two of them.

The councillors,

{j} "Flatter them and they will serve,"

{j} must surely have thought. Kondō was indeed a man who, once he had ridden the tide and been flattered, could do a great work beyond his real measure.

Kondō took now to going up to the castle each day in a long-shafted, sliding-doored daimyō's palanquin.

Toshizō, for his part, put on a Western-style uniform.

"Toshi, what's this—a man of yoriai rank, got up like a rag-and-paper picker,"

{j} and Kondō knit his brows.

"For war this is the best thing there is,"

On the field of Toba-Fushimi he had seen the nimble movements of the Satsuma-Chōshū side, and envied them.

The uniform was an officer's coat of woolen cloth got from the shogunate's Army Institute, cut in the French army style.

The recruiting did not go easily.

But Matsumoto Ryōjun, head physician of the Tokugawa house, who was treating Kondō and Okita,

{j} "What if you moved Asakusa Danzaemon?"

{j} said to Kondō and Toshizō.

Danzaemon was the head of a class set apart and held in contempt under the shogunate's system of ranks.

Kondō negotiated with the councillors, had the discrimination against this class abolished, and further took the steps to have Danzaemon raised to the hatamoto.

Danzaemon rejoiced greatly, and,

{j} "Let me put men and war-funds at your service,"

{j} and placed ten thousand ryō in money and two hundred men under Kondō's command.

Toshizō dressed these new levies in Western uniforms and put them through a hasty Western drill.

The drill, though it was only the handling of the Minié rifle (a breech-loader), had Kondō,

{j} "Toshi, when did you master that?"

{j} exclaiming in amazement.

From the Tokugawa house came two cannon and five hundred small-arms, the foundation of an army was roughly laid, and the name was fixed:

{j} "the Kōyō Chinbutai,"

{j} so they styled it.

The officers were the old troopers of the Shinsengumi.

Besides those hospitalized under treatment, a dozen and more had deserted, so that they had dwindled to fewer than twenty men.

Yet Kondō was in high good humor every day.

One day, when Toshizō came back from drill,

{j} "Toshi, here is a plan of Kōfu Castle,"

{j} and he spread it out to show him.

"Hm,"

Toshizō had a fair notion of it already, and the road from Edo to Kōfu (the Kōshū-kaidō) he had traveled back and forth past counting in his young medicine-peddling days.

As a prospective battlefield there was no district so convenient.

"I've been thinking over what each man's stipend should be, when we've taken Kōshū,"

"Hm,"

Toshizō looked at Kondō's face.

It was melting into a broad grin.

"For me, one hundred thousand koku; that much won't shift. To you, Toshi, I'll give fifty thousand,"

"………"

"Sōji, sick as he is, thirty thousand for him. To the assistant vice-commanders' jokin, Nagakura Shinpachi, Harada Sanosuke and Saitō Hajime, thirty thousand as well. To the inspectors, Ōishi Kuwajirō and the rest, ten thousand; to the corporals, Shimada Kai and the others, five thousand; and to the plain troopers, evenly, a thousand koku apiece,"

"Ho,"

"Well? The above I've put to the councillors too. And gotten their assent,"

"You're a good man,"

Toshizō thought it from his heart.

The man who, at the collapse of the shogunate, thought of becoming a daimyō, was surely Kondō Isami alone.

"Born into the age of the warring domains, you'd have been the master of your own province and castle,"

"Is that so,"

"Only, this is not the age of the warring domains. Even if we smashed Satsuma and Chōshū and brought back the Tokugawa world, the daimyō system would not revive. There'd be a turn, as in the country of France, to a system of prefectures and counties; I've heard there were some in the shogunal council, even before the return of government, who thought so,"

"Fool's notions, from men gone dizzy over Western barbarians. There's the ancestral law, handed down since the Gongen-sama,"

"Well, it's all one either way,"

Toshizō was sunk deep in the plan of campaign. The fatal thing was the shortage of troops. He wanted two thousand at the least.

(Can Kōshū really be taken with a bare two hundred-odd men?)

Once they were into Kōfu Castle, the plan was to call on the farmers of the district and add to their numbers. Whether that would go well, he did not know.

"Come, it will be all right. Take the castle, and we are as good as lords of a million koku already. Set the country samurai and the village headmen to picking stout fellows in every village, and we'll gather them by the ten thousand,"

{j} Kondō was all optimism.

Perhaps it was even so, Toshizō thought. The state of the world having fallen into such chaos, there was no way of reckoning anything except by trying it.

Before setting out, Toshizō took a few of the plain troopers and went to look in on Okita Sōji, who lay in a corner of the Igakusho by Izumibashi in Kanda.

To look in on him, rather, was not quite it; the Igakusho was all but closed now and its physicians gone, so it was to move Okita's body to another place.

Sōji's one blood-kin, his elder sister Omitsu, was there too, and with her Omitsu's husband, Okita Rintarō (a trooper of the Shinchōgumi held in ward by the Shōnai domain).

The new place of convalescence was to be a rented detached wing at the house of Heigorō, a nurseryman living at Ikebashi-jiri in Sendagaya, an acquaintance of Rintarō's.

Okita was quite wasted with illness, but his voice alone had an unlooked-for strength, and,

{j} "Hijikata-san, thirty thousand koku for me, they tell me,"

{j} and he gave a low chuckle.

"What, did Kondō tell you that?"

"No—it was Sōma Kazue, who came to visit the other day, let me in on it,"

(So Kondō seems to have told them all.)

For Kondō, it would have been to lift their spirits that he let it out.

But Sōma and the like had deserted on the very heels of their visit to Okita. The dream of ten thousand koku, of a thousand koku, was proof already that the corps could no longer be baited with it.

"Sōji, get well,"

"Yes—for the thirty thousand koku's sake, too,"

{j} and Sōji gave another little chuckle.

When Toshizō had seen Okita to the nurseryman's at Sendagaya, he went straight back from there to the quarters.

Tomorrow they set out for Kōshū.

(This time, with Western guns, I'll fight them on even terms and pay them back in full for Fushimi.)

The heavy-lidded double eyes glittered, bright as ever.
