### A Dinner for Three Hundred Dollars

There is a good old saying: "Born in Suzhou, dressed in Hangzhou, dining in Guangzhou, buried in Liuzhou." For the Cantonese are wholly indifferent about everything else, and only over the matter of eating are they lavish beyond measure and particular beyond measure: a single spread of food and wine running to several hundred dollars, a single bowl of shark's fin to twenty dollars and up, all of it in Cantonese eyes the most ordinary and trifling thing — which is why the saying "dining in Guangzhou" has long been on everyone's lips.

Of the restaurants in Shanghai the Cantonese houses are reckoned the most numerous, and the price of a full spread the highest. A top-grade banquet is fixed at three hundred dollars — which to us poor devils must seem a thing to click the tongue at in wonder, and yet, since such a thing figures on their bills of fare, there must be those who come to try it. This lavish, sumptuous fare, they say, runs above fifty dollars a spread, and its materials leave out the everyday meats of pig and sheep and chicken and duck, and use only the rare delicacies of mountain and sea, strange fowl and curious beasts and the like costly things; the greater the price, the dearer the materials chosen. The ancients said: "One feast in a rich man's house, half a year's grain for a poor man." And by the reckoning of a three-hundred-dollar spread, it would come to more than several years' food for a poor man.
