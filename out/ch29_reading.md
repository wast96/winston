## Toshizō the Hated

The general secretary of the Shinsengumi, Yamanami Keisuke, left a note addressed to Kondō and deserted the corps; it was in the dark before dawn of the twenty-first day of the second month, the first year of Keiō.

(Yamanami? …)

{j} —so Toshizō heard the report, in his pitch-black room where the night still went on. The one who brought it stood in the corridor. It was the inspector Yamazaki Susumu.

"Yamazaki-kun. Is this certain?"

"Well, now. There is a note left behind; his long and short swords and his baggage are gone from the room, and the man himself is not there. From that, I ask you to judge."

"Let me see that note."

{j} Toshizō lit a splint of kindling and, as he moved the flame to the paper lamp, said it without a thought. But Yamazaki did not come in, did not so much as lay a hand on the shōji.

"What is it?"

"No—I should have said sooner, but it is addressed to Kondō-sensei."

"Ah. So it is."

He was being shut out. But Toshizō, forcing himself calm, said:

{j} "Yamazaki-kun. The messenger to Kondō-san's retreat has gone, I take it."

"Not yet."

"Why haven't you gone at once?"

"I will go from here now. It was only that I thought—Hijikata-sensei first."

(A clever man.)

{j} He does not break the order of precedence. He understood well the professional feeling of Toshizō, whose post was vice-commander. An organization always demands men like Yamazaki, so Toshizō thought.

By the time Toshizō had finished changing his clothes, the dawn bell rang, and along the corridor the rain-shutters were run open one after another. But outside them it was still dark; the night had not fully broken.

(Cold. …)

For the second month, it was too cold a morning. Toshizō went out past the gate, alone, to make his way to Kondō's retreat. There were no frost-columns standing up out of the ground as in his native South Tama of Bushū, but it was cold enough to freeze the bone.

Before he knew it, Okita Sōji had come up beside him.

"A bad business."

{j} said Okita, in a low voice. This over-bright youth's voice was, for once, subdued. Okita, since the days of the sweet-potato dōjō in Edo, had been on good terms with Yamanami. Yamanami was thirty-two. Ten years the elder, he had doted on Okita like a younger brother.

"He was a good man, wasn't he."

{j} and looked at the side of Toshizō's face.

He said nothing.

To Okita, Toshizō had turned hateful to look at.

(Yamanami-san, hating this man past bearing, broke the corps law and deserted.)

{j} So he saw it. And not Okita alone. Anyone in the corps was bound to see it so.

The one was general secretary.

This one, vice-commander.

In rank they were equals. But the direct command of the troopers was in the vice-commander's grip, while the general secretary had become something like an adviser to the commander, Kondō. It was Toshizō who had made the structure so. Yamanami Keisuke had been shelved. Or rather, this man of Sendai had been left on the shelf to gather dust.

(Yamanami-san had hated this man utterly.)

Not only that.

Yamanami's thinking was different. His origin lay in the Hokushin Ittō-ryū. This school, since Chiba Shūsaku, had kept deep ties with the Mito Tokugawa house; many of the Chiba line were taken into service as upper samurai of Mito, and its pupils were mostly Mito men.

Naturally the dōjō was thick with the color of Mito learning, and its students, along with the sword, took in the Mito-style, argument-loving discipline of the revere-the-Emperor, expel-the-barbarian creed. How many men of action of that expel-the-barbarian stamp came out of that gate cannot be counted. Even of those Okita knew, there were the dead Kiyokawa Hachirō and, newly joined, Itō Kashitarō.

(Yamanami-san, too, is at root a man of that faction.)

{j} thought Okita, walking the Bōjō avenue as it slowly brightened.

(But this man is different.)

Toshizō held that doctrine could go to the devil. As an entertainer loses himself in his art, he was, near to innocence, given wholly to the strengthening of the Shinsengumi he had brought forth. That was the very thing Okita liked in him; but the man of learning Yamanami Keisuke could not, it may be, endure that ignorance in Toshizō, so void of principle or thought.

—It is a hard place to live in.

{j} Yamanami had once grumbled it to Okita, after the affair at the Ikedaya.

—I have stopped understanding why the Shinsengumi must kill men. Were we not bound, at the first, by a covenant to become the vanguard of expelling the barbarian? And that same Shinsengumi goes about seeking out and cutting down the very men who would die to expel the barbarian. Do you not think it strange, Okita-kun?

—Yes.

{j} Okita Sōji, at that, put up an ambiguous smile and gave a vague assent.

"Okita-kun."

{j} Yamanami, this time, was worked up as he seldom was, and would not let it go. Why did he not speak his mind plainly? So he pressed.

"It's awkward for me. …"

{j} said Okita, and scratched his head. At the Ikedaya it was Okita who had cut down the most. Yamanami had not taken part in that raid.

"What do you make of the Shinsengumi?"

"—Me?"

Okita was at a loss.

"My elder brother Rintarō was an old pupil of Kondō-sensei's predecessor, the aged Master Shūsai; and my sister Omitsu kept up an acquaintance near to kin with Toshizō-san's family. If such men as Kondō and Toshizō-san were going up to Kyoto, then of course I had to go up to Kyoto too. So the expelling-the-barbarian, or the revering-the-Emperor—"

"—have nothing to do with it."

"Yes, that's it. …But."

Okita, after a bashful laugh,

{j} "I am content with that,"

{j} and, for the first time, laughed brightly.

"You are a strange young man. When I talk with you, I cannot help feeling you are some child sent into this world by the gods, or by all the heavens."

"That sort of thing—"

{j} Okita, flustered, kicked a stone. In this youth's own way, he was abashed.

—Toshizō-san.

{j} Okita, this time too, kicked a stone. In a small voice—"Say, now"—he spoke to Toshizō. He wanted to sound out how Toshizō meant to deal with Yamanami.

"What are you going to do about Yamanami-san?"

"Ask me, and how should I know. A thing like that, you had best ask the master of the Shinsengumi."

"Kondō-san, you mean?"

"The corps law."

{j} That is the master of the Shinsengumi, Toshizō said. And that Code of the Corps, and the fine print of the corps regulations, Yamanami himself had helped decide in council.

(Seppuku, then.)

Okita thought. But at once he said, in a loud voice:

{j} "You are hated by everyone, Toshizō-san. Yamanami-san, of course, hated you utterly. Like a viper and a scorpion, you might say."

"And what of it?"

He was unruffled.

"Nothing of it. Only, everyone fears you, everyone hates you. That much, at least, might be well for you to know."

"They do not hate Kondō, surely."

"No—Kondō-sensei is beloved. Among the troopers there are some who look on Kondō-sensei with a feeling as for a father, unlike you. …"

"I'm the viper and the scorpion."

"Oh—so you know it."

"I know it. Sōji, let me tell you: I am the vice-commander. Think back. Since the founding, every hateful order, every measure that drew the corps tight and made it strong, has come out of my mouth. Has one, even once, been made to come from Kondō's? I have always kept Kondō, the general, in a seat like a god's or a Buddha's. Sōji, I am not the corps commander. I am the vice-commander. The vice-commander takes on all the hatred. He always keeps the commander the good fellow. This thing, the Shinsengumi, is by nature a rabble. Loosen it a little and it is built to fall to pieces at any moment. Do you know what sort of moment it falls to pieces in?"

"Well, now."

"It is when the vice-commander begins to mind the troopers' liking for him and to court their favor. When the vice-commander wants to be the good fellow, like Yamanami or Itō Kashitarō, the bitter orders come out of Kondō's mouth. Naturally the hatred, the praise and the blame, go to Kondō. Kondō loses the troopers' trust. And the corps falls to pieces."

"Ah."

{j} Okita apologized honestly.

"It was thoughtless of me. I never knew you took such pains to make yourself a hated child."

"Give over."

{j} Coming from Okita's mouth, it sounded as if he were being teased.

"There's my nature in it, too."

{j} he said, with a sour face.

***

Kondō, sure enough, went dead pale. Yamanami was a guest-retainer of Kondō's Edo dōjō, a comrade since the covenant. And he was one of the corps's highest officers. His desertion could be called a silent censure of the corps's whole way of going.

"An old comrade, but I cannot forgive it."

{j} said Kondō. Were desertion forgiven in Yamanami Keisuke's case alone, the corps discipline would slacken all at once, desertions would follow one on another, and in the end there would be no bringing it back under control.

"What is the reason?"

"He hated me. That alone will do."

{j} said Toshizō.

"No."

{j} put in the inspector Yamazaki Susumu, with a mediating look, saying an unexpected thing:

"Yamanami-sensei, these past few days, hearing the rumors of how the Mito Tengu Party was dealt with, has seemed terribly cast down."

"The Tengu Party's?"

{j} Kondō let his eyes drift into the air. Indeed, the rumor that the Mito Tengu Party was being put to death at Tsuruga in Echizen, not far from Kyoto, was the talk of the whole corps.

The fierce men of the Mito revere-the-Emperor party, under their leader Takeda Kōunsai, a former executive of the Mito domain, had raised a righteous army as vanguard of the expel-the-barbarian at Mount Tsukuba in Hitachi; and after many turns, running west to petition Yoshinobu, the shogunate's representative in Kyoto, their strength gave out on the road, and on the seventeenth day of the twelfth month last year they surrendered to the Kaga domain. The Kaga men treated them as gentlemen of honor. For they were not, particularly, advocates of toppling the shogunate; they had only sought to make expelling the barbarian a reality by the shogunate's own hand.

But, come this year, the junior councillor Tanuma Genba-no-kami came up from Edo to handle the affair, and, while cajoling the rōnin, took away their weapons, stripped the clothes even from the whole eight hundred of them and left them stark naked, treated them like beasts, and packed them into the herring-sheds of Tsuruga. The handling in the cells was cruel past all measure.

Not only that.

Come this second month, they dug five grave-pits three ken square in the precincts of the Raikō-ji at the edge of the town of Tsuruga, and, dragging the naked rōnin out beside the pits, struck off their heads and kicked the corpses in. Twenty-four men on the fourth of the second month, one hundred and thirty-four on the fifteenth, one hundred and two on the sixteenth, seventy-six on the nineteenth, and so, all told, it came to three hundred and fifty-two men. It was a great slaughter such as had not been seen since the shogunate began, or rather, seldom seen in the whole history of Japan.

And most of them were retainers of the Mito Tokugawa house, men who preached expelling the barbarian but were no rebels seeking to do anything against the shogunate itself. Those men they killed like insects.

—Has the shogunate lost its mind?

{j} Such voices filled the realm. It may be said that this was the moment the realm's extremist opinion turned from expelling-the-barbarian to toppling-the-shogunate. By what justice was such an engine of murder to be kept alive?

"I have come to hate being given rice and salt by the shogunate."

{j} Something to that effect Yamanami had let fall to someone in the corps, Yamazaki said.

Whether it was truly so, one cannot know.

But that Yamanami must have taken a hard shock can be readily imagined, from the fact that among those put to death there were seven or eight men of like grief, old acquaintances from Edo.

Yamanami had despaired of both the times and the Shinsengumi.

—It says he is going back to Edo.

{j} said Kondō, when he had finished reading the letter.

Hearing it, Okita was relieved. Yamanami, though he had grown so close to that Itō Kashitarō and had chimed so with his doctrine, had not fallen in with Itō to raise a party-within-the-party. He goes back to Edo. Yamanami was only going home. There was in it no political smell of any kind.

(He is a fine fellow after all.)

{j} Okita, gazing vacantly at the garden of the Kondō house, thought of that samurai with the Sendai accent. But just then Kondō's expression moved. His lips made to say something. Taking the words from him, though—

"Sōji,"

{j} it was Toshizō's cold voice that said.

"You'll do. You were close to Yamanami-kun. Ride after him now, and you'll catch him about Ōtsu."

"—A pursuer?"

{j} was the look Okita Sōji's face made. Surely a flinching color rose in it. In skill Okita was the better; it was not in that sense that he flinched.

"You won't?"

{j} Toshizō gazed fixedly at Okita.

"No."

{j} He smiled a little. It turned, all at once, into a bright smile. Somewhere within his body he must have cut off his feeling for Yamanami.

Okita ran back to the barracks.

He mounted.

He galloped.

It was cold. The chill air pushing in at his mouth and nose set Okita coughing, up on the saddle. Carrying Okita's cough, the horse galloped east along the Sanjō avenue. About Awataguchi he pressed the back of his hand-guard to his mouth. The cloth grew wet. Faintly, blood had seeped into it.

(I, too, may not have long.)

{j} When he thought that, the green of Kachōzan passing on his right struck his eyes with a strange vividness.

When he had come to the edge of the post-town of Ōtsu, from within a single teahouse a voice called,

{j} "Okita-kun,"

{j} came the call.

It was Yamanami. He held, cradled carefully in both hands, a large teacup filled with kuzuyu.

Okita leapt down from the saddle.

"Yamanami-sensei. I will see you back to the barracks."

"It was a surprise, that the pursuer should be you."

{j} Yamanami looked at Okita with those friendly eyes of his.

"If it is you, there is no help for it. Had it been the inspectors at Hijikata-kun's beck and call, they'd not have let me back to Kyoto alive."

"It does not matter. If you say you must go back to Edo whatever comes, then draw your sword. I will be cut down here."

"How so—it is I who would be cut down. I would be no match for your arm either."

The sun was still high. It was not that they could not get back to Kyoto that day; but Okita could not bear to hurry Yamanami. Deciding to return to Kyoto the next morning, they took lodging that night in Ōtsu.

The two lay down side by side to sleep.

"A cold night."

{j} said Yamanami.

Okita was silent. He was vexed—why had this luckless man of Sendai let himself be caught up with by him?

For one thing, the fineness of the man Yamanami was in this: withdrawing from the corps, he did not try to hide where he had gone; in his note, too, he had plainly set it down—I am going back to Edo. Not only that: from the teahouse at the town's edge, it was he himself who called out the name of his pursuer. It was a clear, clean way of doing, like Yamanami.

That night Yamanami spoke of none of it—not his dissatisfaction with the corps, nor what he had meant to do on going back to Edo.

He spoke of his native country. And that too was all trifling stuff: how in Sendai, at high summer, hail fell about one sun across; how, for the by-work of the foot-guards, digging wild yams paid the best; only such talk.

"Did you dig wild yams too, Yamanami-sensei?"

"Ah, as a child. No—that was mostly children's work. It is an amusing thing, too. In the season while the mountain yam is still young, you go into the hills, find the spot where it grows, and sow barley there. By the time the barley has grown up, the yam too has swelled underground. Using the barley for a mark, you dig it out. That is the way of it."

"—And in Edo,"

{j} what had he meant to do? When Okita began to ask it, Yamanami said gently,

{j} "Let us leave off talk of Edo. For my life, it is a land that has ceased to exist."

{j} he said. Likely, even had he gone back to Edo, he had no scheme of any weight.

Two days after that, on the twenty-third day of the second month, the first year of Keiō, Yamanami Keisuke, in a room of the Maekawa mansion fronting the Bōjō avenue of the Mibu barracks, quietly, and by the correct forms, accomplished his seppuku. His second was Okita Sōji.

Yamanami had a woman. A courtesan of Shimabara named Akesato; and Nagakura Shinpachi of the corps, who knew the circumstances, had sent her word of Yamanami's trouble. The woman, the day before the seppuku, came and stood by the row-house gate fronting the Bōjō avenue.

—Yamanami-san.

{j} the woman said, weeping, and laid her hand on the bay window. In the room of that bay window Yamanami was confined.

Yamanami, from inside the room, took hold of the fingers of the woman who gripped the lattice.

They stayed so a while; from the shadow of the gate, by chance, Okita saw it. The woman's face he could not see. Only the black-lacquered fair-weather clogs and the white tabi stayed in Okita's eyes.

Okita hid himself at once within the gate.

(Her soles were small.)

{j} Even after he had struck off Yamanami's head, only such a thing, oddly, kept coming back to him.
