## Chapter 10. Troubles Never Come Singly; Pillars Snap, Beams Fall (Part 1)

### 1. It Was I Who Ruined His Bright Future

Ever since those two traitors, Zhang Xiaolin, the magnate of the Shanghai concessions, and Fu Xiao'an, the puppet mayor of Shanghai, had been killed one after the other, the running dogs of the enemy and the puppets were forever taking fright at shadows, at every bush and tree a soldier; the killings had indeed worked their deterrent effect.

This unbroken chain of operations not only spread our fearsome name through the fallen zone, but lifted men's spirits for the whole of our war of resistance.

It is a matter for regret that this striking power could not be brought to bear without a break, and that we could not effectively check, or closely guard against, the counterblows of the enemy and the puppets; so that our own organization was struck down time and again.

From the beginning of 1941, the First Section of the Wang puppets' "Special Operations Headquarters," acting in concert with the Special Higher Section of the "Shanghai Japanese gendarmerie," actually set aside all else and gave themselves solely to a carpet-search of my "Shanghai District," and to arrests within it.

Had we, for our own safety, wished only to dodge the blade's edge, there would be no more to say; but not one of our comrades would give the least ground for a charge of cowardice. By an incomplete count, in the ten months from January to the end of October 1941, hazardous as our circumstances were, we still brought off some sixty-odd cases of action and sabotage—on average one every five days. Of course it became a common thing for our comrades to be seized and to give their lives for it. Of such a spirit of dauntless valor, in all the decades the writer has seen, none was ever displayed more finely than in the war of resistance against Japan.

It was just in the spring of 1941, in those very days, that my little son—born only after we came to Shanghai—broke out in measles. In itself it was nothing; but unexpectedly he ran a high fever that turned to pneumonia, and now the trouble was grave. I called in Mr. Nie Chonghou, an eye doctor, and asked him first to look the boy over, hoping he might recommend a pediatrician to treat him. One glance and he judged it already too late; on his recommendation Dr. Zhu Shenzhi was brought in to consult, and though we used the newly come chemical medicine "Xiaozhilong," in the end it could not raise the dead.

Apart from my friend Hu Yongquan, Dr. Nie Chonghou was the second friend ever to come to my home.

With the boy dead, the mood at home was bound to be desolate, and my wife urged me to go out and take my mind off it—a change of scene might do me good. So I dressed myself up and went out alone, out of the lane and strolling on at random, and came in time to the racecourse. That day happened to fall on a grand race meeting, and I went in with the crowd to watch, and, following the others, bought two race tickets on impulse—one to win, one to place—and, as it turned out, both came in. I spent all the winnings I had drawn on more tickets, and, would you believe it, won again: on a stake of four dollars I had gained more than two thousand. Good luck, people called it. But who could know what the taste of it was in my heart?

Coming out of the racecourse and turning up a street, I was not far from Hu Yongquan's house; and it struck me all at once that, with money in my pocket, I might as well ask Yongquan and his wife out to a meal, and so, in all sincerity, thank them for their long care of me. When I reached the Hu house, the Third Mistress came out to open the door; I asked after Yongquan, and she said he had gone off to Hong Kong again some days before, and there had been no word from him since—she did not know how he was faring. From that time on I never set eyes on Hu Yongquan again.

I thought that Yongquan, who was forever going back and forth between Shanghai and Hong Kong, would soon return, and so I gave it no more thought.

Leaving the Hu house, I meant to find some diversion but truly did not know where to go; after long thought, I decided I might as well pay a private call on Liu Junqing while he was home from work, and chat a little of home—that would do very well.

Junqing's other residence was on Route Vallon in the French Concession; I arrived just as he too was coming home, and we met, as it happened, right at his gate. He drew me upstairs and asked in a low voice whether I had some urgent business, and only when I had explained my errand was he at ease. He insisted on keeping me to a plain meal, and would have me drink a cup or two with him—which was, in fact, just what I had in mind. We talked a good long while; then I suddenly thought I ought to telephone home so they would not worry, and, having drunk a few cups too many and being unsteady on my feet, I let Junqing help me downstairs to the telephone. The telephoning set off a train of thought: in the past, whenever there was an alert at the International Settlement police station, it was Junqing who notified Zhang Zuoxing, and only then did it pass on to district headquarters—a serious case of help coming too slow for the need. If Liu Junqing could telephone me directly, would that not be far quicker? On the impulse of that single thought, I told Junqing my home telephone number to his face; and on the way home, when the cool wind struck me, I suddenly felt that I had gone against my own rule—but since I had already told him, I let it be.

Of late the district's affairs came thick and various, every kind of question among them. Just because I was too busy to spare a moment, the small misfortune at home faded into the background.

In the third month of the thirtieth year I received a telegraphic order from bureau headquarters, running roughly thus: "It is noted that Comrade Liu Yuanshen, assistant secretary of your district, an outstanding graduate of the Linli class, has in three years of work at Shanghai given a distinguished account of himself. This bureau now specially recommends him to the Advanced Education Class of the Central Military Academy for training, that he may perfect his studies. On receipt of this, kindly at once direct the said officer to report, without fail before the end of the sixth month, directly to the headquarters of the Military Academy at Chengdu; let there be no delay, and be sure to report his date of departure."

Would Yuanshen truly reach the Advanced Education Class of the Military Academy at Chengdu for his training? Because of one measure of mine, Yuanshen was made to suffer every kind of wrong, and I ruined his bright future besides.

The affair took a winding course, with troubles rising one upon another; having now obtained Yuanshen's consent, I have asked him to take up the pen himself, which will surely be the more faithful for it.

The passage that follows is Yuanshen's own, in the first person, recounting in plain strokes this extraordinary chapter of his experience.

That year I was just turned twenty-four, in charge of action work in the "Shanghai District," and my duties were heavy and pressing. As a rule a telegram of this kind must first pass through my hands, with my draft opinion for its handling endorsed on it, then be sent up for the countersignature of the district secretary, Mr. Qi Qingbin, and after that be laid before the district chief for his ruling. But because this telegram bore on a matter of my very own, I could not well take it upon myself to say yea or nay, and so I sent the original along to Mr. Qi to deal with.

Two days later the district chief, Mr. Chen Gongshu, spoke with me alone. He began: "Congratulations! I have already seen that telegram from bureau headquarters. This summons of yours to train in the Advanced Education Class is splendid news, and bears on the whole future of your career. I approve of it, naturally, and ought to encourage you to go..." He lit a cigarette, brooded a moment, and went on: "But at present your work here is heavy, and you know the business inside out and handle it with an easy hand; your liaison with and guidance of the several field units is done, too, very soundly and rightly. Once you were gone, I truly cannot think of any fit person to take it over. You understand—there would then be bound to be a gap, a lean season with no one to fill it. In these days, when the tasks of our 'Shanghai District' grow daily harder and the arrogance of the enemy and the puppets daily more unbridled, we can in fact allow ourselves not the slightest slackening or break. And so I have been thinking—for the sake of the work at hand, could you consider giving it up and not going? Better to stay on. To be sure, this is a personnel order from bureau headquarters, and for you it is a rare chance too; I have no wish to press you too hard. It is not that I am selfish; but to meet the needs of the actual work I cannot but sound out your view. As for going or not going, the decision must still rest with you."

I understood that Mr. Chen's words were earnest and yet tactful, and that all he said was the plain truth. This question of going or staying put me in a dilemma, and for the moment I could not bring myself to decide.

It was on the very night of the great Changsha fire, November 12, 1938, that I was summoned before Mr. Dai at the Yueyun Middle School on Baonan Street, and, in a private talk of about half an hour, was appointed to his face to take charge of a party and lead seventeen fellow students to Shanghai to join the work. At that time the former head of the "Shanghai District," Mr. Zhou Daosan (Weilong), had through a careless mishap already left his post and gone elsewhere; the vacancy was filled provisionally by the deputy district chief and concurrent chief of the action corps, Mr. Zhao Lijun, and was at last taken over by Mr. Wang Tianmu. Reckoning the time, Mr. Wang came from Tianjin, while I and my fellow students came by way of Jinhua and Wenzhou and across the sea, and we reached Shanghai at nearly the same moment. We seventeen students put up in several hotels across the International Settlement and the French Concession, and only after waiting some ten days did we make contact with the district secretary of the time, Mr. Zheng Xiuyuan. The upshot of that meeting was that I was kept on in the "Shanghai District" to do intelligence compiling and reviewing; though in truth I had then no working experience at all, and did no more than learn by following the senior comrades. Besides these, six others—Tang Yuyuan, Zhang Xueli, Zhang Yutan, Wu Jusheng (now in Taiwan), Yang Jizhi, and Zhang Weixian—were assigned severally to inside and outside duties. Most to be lamented and grieved over was that the remaining ten students—Di Xiting, Li Yushun, Liu Shiyu, Ding Lüjing, and the rest—were all sent to serve in the "Loyal and Patriotic Army." As ill luck would have it, this party of theirs—unfamiliar with the route and the lay of the land, unversed in the Shanghai dialect, and perhaps too little careful in their dress and their cover—had no sooner crossed the Huangpu and set foot on Pudong soil than they were lured into capture by Japanese soldiers and the puppet Peace Army; and of them all, save Ding Lüjing, who saw his chance and got away by good fortune, the other nine were cruelly put to death at one stroke. These students were the finest of patriotic young men of learning; with hearts full of ardor they had given themselves to the revolution, had undergone strict training in the rear, and yet, without so much as a single day's work done, laid down their lives for nothing, becoming the vanguard's "nameless heroes"—it was, in the very words, "dead before the campaign was won"; their deaths bought nothing, and truly they died with their eyes unclosed! Whenever I recall their voices and their smiling faces, each one as if before my eyes, my heart is cut as with a knife, and my mourning knows no end!

Comparatively speaking, I may count myself, after all, one of the lucky ones. I had worked in the "Shanghai District" not half a year, and my own proper work was gradually getting into its stride, when I saw the pressure on us from the enemy and the puppets and the concession authorities growing greater and greater, while our organization itself seemed somewhat to have aged, unsound in many places, wanting in a fresh keen vigor and a lusty will to do. For the sake of putting the organization in order, and out of care for our safety, we one and all went most warily, with the feeling of men treading beside a chasm or upon thin ice. And so at this stage the work of the "Shanghai District" can be said to have made no headway at all.

Least of all did I dream that on a day in July of that year (1939) the leading ranks within the "Shanghai District" would of a sudden meet with a vast upheaval, a very bolt from the blue that left men at their wits' end. Without doubt it was a most grievous blow, one that directly threatened the very existence of the "Shanghai District." Happily, though the affair dragged on in turmoil a long while, it was at last quieted, thanks too to the comrades' good sense in keeping out of harm's way. Thereupon, in August 1939, Mr. Chen Gongshu came to take charge of the "Shanghai District," and the situation slowly steadied at last. He gathered up the broken pieces and set the ranks in order anew; and before long the work of the "Shanghai District" rose to another new milestone.

By March 1941—that is, the day I was ordered to the Advanced Education Class for training—I had followed Mr. Chen at his work for two years. He had come out of the fifth class at Whampoa and was a senior man of our bureau; at this time he was but thirty-two, in the very prime of life, and already held the key to that quarter which was the most complex and most vital ground of our bureau's behind-the-lines work—Greater Shanghai. Because his thought was close-knit, his experience rich, and his manner crisp, decisive, and bold as one who wields the great axe, under his leadership the behind-the-lines activity of the "Shanghai District" ran like quicksilver spilled across the ground, like driving rain and howling gale, scoring one fine success after another, its fearsome name ringing far, and raised such a high tide of killing the enemy and rooting out traitors as had never been seen before, so that in the fallen zone the people's hearts were stirred and the enemy and the puppets lost their nerve. In the handling of business he was full of reason; in questions of personnel he knew men and used them well; and he showed an enlightenment, an uprightness, and a rich warmth of human feeling. On these grounds he kindled the more, in all the comrades within and without, a sense of responsibility and of honor, and drove the working record of the "Shanghai District" into a period at its very peak. And there is a point more important still: young as I was, Mr. Chen's trust in me and reliance on me were growing by the day, so that I felt keenly the grace of one who knows another's worth, and in my heart there had already grown up toward him a deep affection. This is why, when he set the present work of the "Shanghai District" first and hoped I would give up going to Chengdu for training, adding the words, "As for going or not going, the decision must still rest with you"—afterward one inside comrade said to me behind his back: "Mr. Chen's way of putting it is really no different from putting you in 'check'; I think you had best plainly refuse." But I did not think so. I felt only that his words were earnest and every one of them agreeable to hear, and that they showed plainly, too, how truly I was valued by this my immediate superior; the more, then, did I find it a kindness hard to refuse. And so, after a moment's hesitation, I said resolutely: "Very well, Mr. Chen, I will do as you say! In any case there should be other chances for this training later on. Only, you must not tell the bureau that I of my own accord defied the order and would not go, but rather that the work here needed me and I truly could not be spared." Mr. Chen smiled and said soothingly: "That goes without saying; I shall see to it. So, then, we have settled it thus, and I shall presently wire bureau headquarters a truthful reply."

The matter had been decided in haste, and on the face of it Mr. Chen and I were both well pleased. But at night, lying on my bed, I could not help turning it over and over: to be ordered to train in the Advanced Education Class was a thing I knew not how many cadre comrades longed for waking and sleeping, and I, as though I had never used my head, had lightly thrown it away on a moment's rush of feeling. Every young man has a heart set on rising; to give up so fine a chance now was surely too unwise, and it slighted, moreover, all the deep intent with which Mr. Dai had nurtured me. Yet the word was out of my mouth and I could not take it back; and so, torn within, I lay awake the whole night through.

Why do I bring up Mr. Dai here? First: when I trained in the Linli class, the Leader was its principal and he was the class director; I graduated with high honors, and this he knew. Second: as far back as October 1938, when I went to Wuhan for practical training, out of a practice corps made up of a hundred students I was picked as one of the five practice committee-men. Mr. Liu Peichu, the corps leader, had the whole in charge; the team leader was Mr. Zhang Shuxun of the fourth Whampoa class, with Chen Xianzhou and Dong Wei managing affairs. At that time Mr. Dai was attending on the Leader, quartered at the Bank of China by the mouth of Huangpi Road in Hankou, and every Monday at early morning he would come, as a rule, to the practice corps to preside over the Memorial Week. When the Memorial Week was over he would always summon me and inquire into such matters as the subjects, the progress, and the results of the training, and give many instructions. He was, at the same time, most concerned for the students' daily life and comfort; to all I answered he would nod in satisfaction, and never once did he reproach me. So I knew his impression of me was rather good. Only once—the weather was turning cold, and each student had but two army blankets, one to lie on and one for cover, so that by night it was truly too cold to bear—I raised the matter without much thought, and to my surprise his face changed on the instant; he called Chen Xianzhou over and, before my very eyes, gave him a thorough tongue-lashing, which left me in the utmost embarrassment. Afterward, though each man was issued two more blankets, Chen Xianzhou sought me out to apologize again and again, saying: "For things of this kind hereafter, just tell me directly, younger brother, and be sure never to tell Mr. Dai again! You saw that scolding—he had me drenched as with a bucket of dog's blood." I said I had meant nothing by it, and begged his pardon too. He bared his big front teeth in a rueful smile, and there the matter ended. That the young know nothing of the ways of the world may be seen from this.

On the night of the great Changsha fire, when I was appointed to lead seventeen fellow students of my class to Shanghai to join the work, Mr. Dai even came in person to see us off at the railway station. By that time fires had already been set within the city of Changsha; with a "boom" a tongue of flame would leap up, and the sound of "boom, boom" fell without cease on the ear; before long the flames all over the city joined into one, and the great fire spread at once. The people gathered thick at the station to flee were a very sea of heads, their faces aghast, none knowing what it was about; and Mr. Dai too kept turning to look back. His face was heavy, and he said not a word; nor did I understand whether he had known beforehand of this setting of fires. By now the houses on both sides of the station had caught fire too, and the flames on either hand slowly joined to form a very archway of fire. The train had to be gone. As I boarded, he charged me once more, in particular, to look after my fellow students with care and to work hard. In the flickering firelight I gazed on his face; his look was grave and yet kindly. I shall never forget the few words with which he last heartened me. He said: "You are to work in Shanghai three years; if you give a good account of yourself, I shall recall you when the time comes—otherwise, never come to see me again!" But things fell out against my hopes; in the years after, I met again and again, for my work, the calamity of the prison bonds, endured every kind of torment, came several times to the brink of death, and in the end disappointed him. All this is a later tale, and I set it aside. This summons of mine to train in the Advanced Education Class fell just as my three years' work in Shanghai were complete; so I alone knew in my heart that it must have been his doing. In fact, though bureau headquarters did indeed use the work assessments of field comrades as one ground for promotion and transfer, I could not say of myself that my record was very good. And at bureau headquarters, apart from the two gentlemen in charge of personnel, Mr. Li Xiaobai and Mr. Zhou Kang, I knew not a single soul; and so my judgment was that, unless it were Mr. Dai, no one else would take such delight in me or cherish me so.

Sure enough, Mr. Chen's proposal—that I be kept on to go on working in Shanghai and give up the Advanced Education Class—was submitted twice running, and neither time was it approved.

I remember that bureau headquarters, in its last reply telegram, said: "As regards the transfer for training of Comrade Liu Yuanshen, it was Mr. Dai who chose him in person, and all formalities with the Military Academy have already been completed; the matter may not be altered. It is hoped that, in accordance with the earlier telegram, the said officer be directed to proceed to Chengdu on schedule." At this, headquarters being so firm, Mr. Chen, wish as he might to keep me, could keep me no longer.

In the twinkling of an eye it was April. Mr. Chen said: "Since things stand thus, there is no help for it but that you go. Now, to travel from Shanghai to the great rear, one must first fix upon a comparatively safe route; along the way it will be unavoidable to pass through checkpoint after checkpoint of the enemy and the puppets, and to submit to all manner of tests and searchings; and with your age, your looks, and your bearing, it will no doubt be a good deal more troublesome for you. For these problems full preparation must be made in advance, to keep you altogether safe. But you need not fret; I shall go and look into it and arrange it for you. At the same time there are certain things—your disguise of identity, your cover story, and the like—over which you too must rack your own brains. Luckily there are still two months to the opening of the class, and there is time for every preparation." At that time I had not yet learned to smoke, but Mr. Chen's craving for tobacco was great; now he was smoking again, and what he smoked was always one of the famous tinned brands of the day, such as "Garrick" or "State Express 555." He had, besides, a habit in his smoking: after only a few puffs he would toss the greater half of the stub away on the ground as he pleased. Seeing it often, I thought it no strange thing, and even found this habit and gesture of his dashing and endearing. Watching him smoke, and seeming to muse a while, he went on: "These next few days you may wind up the business in your charge and hand it over for the time to Comrade XX; and some of the working connections you must, as circumstances warrant, severally introduce and bring together face to face, so that later on the contact will go smoothly. Besides... I think that in these days while you wait to set off you should not sit idle either. You know our First Action Brigade, with its three sub-brigades under it—has not the post of brigade commander all along been held concurrently by the district chief? In truth I do but hold the title in name; I have no time at all to look into it, so that the personnel and the state of the work in the several sub-brigades have all grown estranged from me, and for a long while now they have shown nothing to speak of. My meaning is that, in the two months before you leave, you should use the time to go, in the capacity of acting commander of the First Action Brigade, and put it thoroughly in order. Such things as adjusting the personnel, replenishing the weapons, or how to resolve their difficulties and lift their spirits—all these you may do with a free hand. Of late the first and third sub-brigades have each sent up a draft plan for a sanction; you might plan it out too, near at hand, and help bring it to pass. This notion of mine—or say, rather, a decision—is it not the best of both worlds? Think it over and see." As it was, I was to hand over my duties within days, and might well have given my whole mind to nothing but preparing for the journey; now Mr. Chen had of a sudden laid on me this "temporary task," and I had grounds to decline it gently. But then—first, he treated me too well, and our feeling for each other ran deep; and second, so long as I had not left Shanghai for a single day, I still counted as a man of the "Shanghai District," and I could not be so cold and calculating. In any case, in this month or two while I waited to set off, idle was idle; and perhaps, with a parting glance over my shoulder, I might set some threads in order for him, which would be a very fine thing. So whatever he said, I agreed to.

### 2. Secret-Service Work, Where Human Feeling and Reason Interweave

The passage below, taking up where the last left off, is still from Yuanshen's own pen; the original text is as follows:

A secret-service man's movements are always clean and quick. In mid-April I took my leave of Messrs. Chen and Qi and several other inside comrades; the two maidservants, Amah Zhao and Cai'ai, wore a look of parting sorrow too. From then I moved out alone from the district headquarters in the Ping'an Apartments on Seymour Road in the International Settlement, and into an apartment house at the corner of Avenue Joffre and Route Delastre in the French Concession. The proprietor of this apartment house was a German Jew of about fifty, tall of frame, wearing a great grizzled ring of beard, of so quaint and antique an aspect that one was easily put in mind of a figure out of the film "The Arabian Nights." The apartment had but three rooms, of the family sort, fully fitted out, with cold and hot water supplied without lack; only the bath was shared, nor could one cook for oneself, there being no common kitchen. All the furniture within had an air of antique elegance; a blood-red carpet was spread on the floor, and on the wall hung a great striking-clock that kept very true time and, whenever it chimed, gave out a droning as of old music played. Above the fireplace hung a large oil painting, of still life and flowers. On the long mantel-shelf stood a Western painted vase and a jade-white marble statue of "Venus" in the round, about a foot high, its lines soft and fair in relief, its face beautiful—an old thing too, by the look of it. There were, besides, several other small ornaments, enough to please the eye.

What remained was the question of eating. Now it was not as before: a bachelor on my own, I had to go to a restaurant for every meal, Chinese or Western; I ate my way through nearly the whole length of Avenue Joffre and the whole quarter round about Baxianqiao, Yu Qiaqing Road, and Fourth Avenue—a waste of time and of money both. I thought these days would not last too long, and made do as best I could.

Within a week I met in turn with the first sub-brigade leader, Liu Quande, the second sub-brigade leader, Xiang Qiangwei, and the third sub-brigade leader, Zhou Xiyuan.

Let me first present Liu Quande. The man was loyal and staunch and fierce, his marksmanship uncannily sure, and at the scene of the work he flung himself in heedless of his life. He was an outstanding action man. But this only within the bounds of a particular target assigned him; ask him to seek out intelligence leads himself, or to plan a case, and he was rather at a loss. So he was no more than a reliable executant. He was a Jiangxi man, and from his teens had followed the Communists in their old lair at Ruijin as a "Little Red Devil." His two great eyes, as they opened and closed, showed a lurking glint of blood that struck fear into any who looked on him; and with this went a slightly husky voice, rough movements, and a body of great spring and agility, so that it is not hard to imagine how, at the moment he came to sanction a traitor, he had no need to draw his pistol—the other was already frightened dumb, his legs gone weak. I do not remember when or where he joined our work; I know only that he was a good comrade, loyal to the nation and loyal to the organization, a man of his word, who strove hard to prove himself. At that time this sub-brigade of his was very small—six men in all, himself included—yet every one of them was fierce as dragon and tiger, and earnest in the work. I asked whether the unit had any difficulties, and what he thought of the work at present. His answer was very plain and simple: he said all was well, only that they had gone so long without work that the idleness had made them somewhat restless; and he felt, too, the shame of "drawing rations and doing nothing." I understood that his words came from the heart, and at once comforted and heartened him, bidding him be a little patient, that before long there might be a task to hand him. He showed himself at once greatly excited, and began somewhat to rub his fists and wipe his palms. I said that if he had any requests he might put them forward at any time, and that whatever was reasonable and fitted the needs of the work I would support him in and help him settle. Our meeting was satisfying and pleasant, and built up a mutual confidence. To tell of it now, it is already a thing of forty years ago; afterward, when the government removed to Taiwan, he volunteered once more and was sent by the bureau back to Shanghai to carry out some task, and before long word came of his death—he fell at last to the poison hand of the Communist Party, and mounted the roll of the "nameless heroes."

Next I should present the second sub-brigade leader, Xiang Qiangwei. Xiang was a man of Sheng County in Zhejiang, sprung from the greenwood, rough and unlettered, yet a fellow who would cut off heads and let the blood run. He was a senior comrade with a good working record—he must have been in his thirties then—simple and honest and of few words; at every task he did the thing with his own hand, pressing ever forward. He and Liu Quande seemed men of one stamp; but where Liu Quande would sometimes bluster and shout, he was wholly silent, and at the work he was fiercer even than Liu. He had a deputy named Luo Chengjin, a student out of the Hangzhou Police Academy, and a fierce fighter too. Luo was short and stout of build, honest and simple of aspect, and, being better lettered and having had training, was more of a head on his shoulders than Xiang. I believe that in first setting Luo as Xiang's deputy there must have been an intent to have wit and courage aid each other. When I met with Xiang Qiangwei, Luo too was present; learning that he had come out of the Hangzhou Police Academy, I could not but feel the closer to him. Here I may as well tell an old tale: in the latter half of 1940, Luo met with mishap and was seized and held in "No. 76," the puppet Special Operations Headquarters. He suffered every cruel torture; on the "tiger bench" they wedged as many as three bricks beneath his legs, till the sinews of his feet and the bones of his knees were near torn asunder, and he swooned as if dead several times over, yet from first to last gave not half a word of confession. A man of high resolve and benevolence, of a will so firm that even the ruffians of "No. 76" could not but stick up a thumb and call him an "iron man." His legs, hurt under the torture, left him lame; whether he ever recovered I have kept always in my thoughts. When the war of resistance was won, Mr. Dai, alas, met his death on duty in an air crash over Nanjing on March 17, 1946; his loyal remains were laid in the cemetery of the Linggu Temple in the outskirts of Nanjing, and I heard that Luo Chengjin kept watch there over the grave all the while, until Nanjing fell to the bandits, when the man's whereabouts became unknown. Here I offer from afar my humble prayer: may my old friend be safe and well.

Xiang Qiangwei's second sub-brigade was all in order—eight men, all old followers of Xiang's, valiant in a fight and trustworthy, with Luo Chengjin to lend a hand among them; I had nothing to be uneasy about. They too, like Liu Quande, had long shown no work, and their hands itched with idleness; again and again they made it known that they were whetting their blades in readiness, awaiting the order to move.

Last I must speak of the third sub-brigade's Zhou Xiyuan. Zhou was a Jiaxing man, going under the assumed name Feng Xian; four years before he had been transferred to Shanghai from the Loyal and Patriotic Army, and in those four years had shown not a single result—he did but hold a post and eat his bread for nothing. The traitor Wan Lilang had come likewise out of the Loyal and Patriotic Army, and so the two were old acquaintances and often kept up a private intercourse. When Wan turned his coat and went over to the puppets, taking the post of first-section chief in the puppet Special Operations Headquarters, he made it his whole endeavor, in order to court merit, to wreck my "Shanghai District" with all vigor. Reckoning Zhou a good chessman lying hidden within the "Shanghai District" organization, he pressed the closer to draw Zhou in. Zhou was a muddlehead, and, with Wan's baiting and threats besides, was duly hooked. The two had secretly kept up a covert connection for a long time already, and Zhou, having no course but to do as he was bid, went so far as to hand over to Wan the whole of his unit's weapons and every kind of instruction document sent down from district headquarters. The one good way to wreck the "Shanghai District" was, of course, to seize men. But our courier network was tightly knit and nimbly worked, and Zhou could get no hold on it at all. And then for a long time past none of the higher leading personnel had met with Zhou face to face; so, wishing to strike, he yet had no object to strike at, and could only wait quietly for his chance.

What needs adding here by way of explanation is this: of Zhou Xiyuan's unsteadiness and his various signs of treachery we had long since received, bit by bit, a number of reports bearing on it; only they were wanting in concreteness and lacked forceful proof. Weighing the sources of these reports, the greater part came from Zhou's own unit secretary, one Zhu Min. In his reports Zhu Min flatly charged that his brigade leader, Zhou Xiyuan, was in secret collusion with "No. 76," plotting mischief, and that it were well to guard against him early, or to deal with him decisively, so as to cut off future trouble. This was, of course, a thing to appall the ear; for what our revolutionary body most abhorred was the rising within the organization of the double-hearted and of traitors—the more so in this sacred war of resistance against Japan, when the soldiers and people of the whole nation were fighting the enemy to the death with a Great Wall of flesh and blood, and any Chinese with the least conscience or sense would never be content to serve as the running dog of the enemy and the puppets and sell out his own country. How much less, when Zhou was a junior cadre of our own revolutionary organization! So the thing was past all comprehending. Yet, looking closer at Zhu Min's reports, one could not be without one's doubts either:

First: put favorably, that he denounced his own brigade leader's plot of treason might prove him a faithful comrade.

Second: by the common rule of our appointment of personnel—especially between the head and the secretary of a field unit—though they need not always be kin or connected, in nineteen cases out of twenty there is some prior tie between them, or else they work together seamlessly and trust one another; without which there can be no laying bare of hearts, no gaining that use whereby the arm answers the finger. What the original relation of Zhu Min and Zhou Xiyuan was cannot for the moment be looked into, but I believe it was no exception. Yet now he had actually come forward to denounce Zhou's treasonous doings; the inside of this was worth pondering.

Third, a first set of suppositions: 1. That Zhu had indeed discovered, or got hold of, the fact of Zhou's plotting, and, to safeguard the organization, had no choice but to lay a denunciation before his superiors. 2. That Zhu and Zhou, differing perhaps in their views of the work, had over time bred a hatred and pushed each other out; and that Zhu, striking first to master the other, had one-sidedly filed "tale-bearing reports" on Zhou, deliberately manufacturing a pretext to deal Zhou a blow. 3. That between Zhu and Zhou there might be a clash of interests or a private grudge grown past all untangling; or else that Zhu, unwilling to remain long beneath Zhou, coveted Zhou's post of brigade leader and thought to take his place, and so did not scruple to frame him. 4. That Zhu and Zhou had both gone over together to "No. 76," and, taking their orders from Wan Lilang, were playing a "two-man turn," using the show of a "falling-out within the nest" as a bait, to lure our superiors into sending a man directly to look into and mediate the matter, and so achieve their aim of seizing him on the chance and thereby widening the wreck of the "Shanghai District." Were this last supposition true, it was plainly a great conspiracy, and might be called the most dangerous supposition of all.

Judging by the facts and by reason, there was something in each of the suppositions. As to the first, the crimes Zhu Min's report laid on Zhou were of the gravest, but its substance was hollow and not to be credited, and it was not free of the suspicion of malicious slander; as to the second and third, these were guesses at the feelings and reasonings of men, yet their possibility could not be ruled out either; only the fourth was most to be marked, and its likelihood greater too. But in my own mind Zhou Xiyuan might be counted an old comrade, and for him to forsake the light for the dark was hard to understand; and moreover Zhu Min's report was, in effect, a warning served on us beforehand. If we, for fear of breaking the vessel while pelting the rat, should never send anyone to make contact with him, what could they gain thereby? Would it not have wrecked their scheme of the two-man turn? Turned over closely, there was a contradiction in it too.

Before I moved out of district headquarters to take over the First Action Brigade, Mr. Chen, Mr. Qi, and I had twice taken counsel over the question of Zhou Xiyuan. When I set out the views and thoughts above, Mr. Chen said: "Weigh the inside of it as you will, it hardly falls outside this range; but as for the fact of Zhou Xiyuan's treachery, we have data reported from other quarters as well, enough to corroborate it." Mr. Qi said: "To rid ourselves of this canker at the heart, the man must be sanctioned, and early! We have already reported this case to the bureau for a ruling, and the moment approval comes, it shall be carried out." I demurred, saying: "To put to death one of our own comrades is no light thing; one must have hold of reliable, forceful proof, or else, should we kill a man wrongly, the remorse will be past all bound. This time when I go down, I mean without fail to meet Zhou Xiyuan face to face and make a direct observation and reckoning of him, to learn the truth. The move is, to be sure, somewhat of a risk; but I think that in the time and place of the meeting and in every other point I shall contrive to do it deftly and tightly, so that even should they wish to play some trick, they will not be prepared for it. Of course I shall be especially on my guard against the unforeseen." Mr. Chen and Mr. Qi, hearing me out, did not agree, and said with one voice: "That is too great a risk; it wants careful thought first." I said: "Now that I am going to lead the First Action Brigade, they will not know of it until I have sent word and fixed a recognition signal for the meeting. And before we have met, we are strangers to each other; he cannot be sure who it is that comes, while the so-called recognition signal is one-sided—that is to say, I shall know him at a glance, but he cannot know me, and must wait until I come forward to speak and give the password. Before that, I can take the chance to observe the surroundings and the movements of the people, and only when I judge it safe go up and fall into talk with him. So I believe this first meeting should pass off without mishap. Besides, if they really meant to seize a man, they need not be in such haste over it. And there is a point of weight still: not one man of our 'Shanghai District' knows what manner of man Zhou is, or what he looks like. If our superiors approve the sanction of Zhou, there will still be no way to lay a hand on him; only after I have met Zhou and talked with him can I quietly point him out to the action men. Is it not so? To meet Zhou is needful, even at some risk." I did not wholly persuade Mr. Chen; at last he said, with reluctance: "I think the sanction of Zhou Xiyuan is a thing that must be done. You might as well go first and set about planning and arranging it, and only wait for the bureau's reply telegram to arrive to strike at once. But you—you must take the greatest care!" Mr. Qi too said: "Take the greatest care." I laughed to myself that they worried overmuch. And at the same time, for the matter of Zhou Xiyuan's treachery, I had to that day seen no hard and solid proof; to kill him thus, in a muddle, would it not be too great a wrong to him! And so in my heart I felt it much amiss.

Just because the matter had passed through these two councils, the question of sanctioning Zhou Xiyuan seemed a foregone conclusion, and I had known in my heart from early on how it stood; which is why, at my last meeting with Liu Quande, I had comforted and heartened him with the words, "Be a little patient; before long there may be a task to hand you"—it was this I had meant.

On a day late in the fourth month I met with Zhou Xiyuan at last. His unit's office was set up in a garret room upstairs at No. — Xiafei Lane, off Avenue Joffre in the French Concession. It was I who changed my mind at the last and arranged to meet him directly at his unit office. Beforehand I had conceived the idea of taking him unawares and had made thorough arrangements, and went with my plan full-formed in my breast. At the time both Zhou and Zhu Min were present. Zhou was twenty-nine, about one meter seventy-eight in height, with a big broad face and big hands and feet, and no other mark to speak of. In talk his words were clumsy and his smile childish; the first impression he gave was of a good-for-nothing without a mind of his own, without backbone, without any doing in him. Zhu Min was very young, about twenty-three or so, his cleverness plain on the surface, handsome of feature, like a university student still at his books. The mood of the talk seemed very good, and I did my utmost to show myself sincere in manner and ready to shoulder responsibility. Coming to the questions of personnel and funds, Zhou said all was as usual and there was no difficulty; only that the unit's present weapons (revolvers and Mauser pistols among them) were all old, and though not yet past use, might fail at the critical moment and spoil the great matter, and so he asked that they be replenished as seemed fit. I made a show of hesitating and said to him: "It may be considered; but so far as I know our stock of weapons is not large, and I shall have to make a separate application to my superiors—which, I expect, will be granted; set your mind at rest on that."

Zhu Min seemed to have his scruples and said very little. Presently the talk turned to the case of sanctioning Xu Liqiu. Xu Liqiu was the director of the "South China Evening News," Wang Jingwei's propaganda organ in Hong Kong, and was, in May, to pass through Shanghai from Hong Kong on his way to Nanjing to attend some important meeting of the puppet Propaganda Ministry. By Zhou Xiyuan's finding, it was Xu Liqiu's wont, every time he came to Shanghai, to stay at the Wengzhou Hotel on Xi'aixiansi Road in the French Concession; Zhou had already planted an informer at the hotel, and meant, once Xu reached Shanghai, to sanction him. Zhou had already reported this to the district for record in early March. We held Xu to be no important target; but as Zhou showed himself very sure of it in his report, and partly for the sake of a show of work and on the plea of lifting the unit's spirits, we allowed it to go forward for the time. Zhou said: "Within days I shall have a photograph of Xu, and can make several copies to report to the district for reference and give to the executants, that they may know him; the one problem now is the replenishing of the weapons, which truly brooks no delay." I nodded in agreement, thinking in my heart: you have given away the whole of your unit's weapons to Wan Lilang, and now you would swindle a few more out of us to make him a present of them—the man is truly past saving!

After talking with Zhou and Zhu, I had reached a first understanding and conclusion. To judge Zhou's character, on the surface timid and afraid of trouble, dull as a deer or a pig, one might declare with confidence that he was in no way the stuff of one who would dare to rise in revolt of his own accord; but by the same token he was a stupid lump very easily led by the nose. So, as to the matter of his treachery, one could only say the likelihood was great, and that it must be confirmed by further deep talk and by gathering proof. Zhu Min, on the other hand, though sharp of aspect, was in his doings deep and hidden enough.

Coming out of Xiafei Lane, I boarded a tram bound for Rue du Consulat—first, to avoid being "tailed," and second, because I meant to go to the "Wufu Lou" at Baxianqiao for my midday meal. Sitting on the tram, my mind ran all the while on this affair.

In May the weather of Shanghai gradually grew hot. Being busy with the affairs of the First Brigade, I had, without knowing it, set aside for a time all the business of preparing to go to Chengdu for training; and when it came suddenly to mind, I could not help feeling a rush of anxiety. Happily, though I had been transferred for the time to field duty, I still had no lack of chances to meet Messrs. Chen and Qi directly. Besides reporting or asking instructions on the work, I had to remind Mr. Chen, as the day of my own going to Chengdu drew nearer by the day, not to forget to arrange it for me in earnest.

For the half-month and more that followed, besides regularly using couriers to pass orders and messages to Zhou Xiyuan, I met with him two or three times more; perhaps I was too careless and thought too little of him, but whether we spoke of business or of private things, I found nothing amiss. Did I not say that Zhu Min had hinted I meet him alone? One afternoon a little past three, I took "surprise action" and appeared all at once at Zhou's sub-brigade office. Zhu Min seemed just awake from his noon nap and was washing his face. Seeing me come so suddenly, his look was very mixed—on the one hand startled and glad, on the other wearing the air of "this was foreseen all along." I said: "Passing this way, I stepped in to see how you are"—and by "you" I meant to include Zhou Xiyuan. Zhu Min said: "I live here at the sub-brigade office; unless there is business or an appointment, the brigade leader does not often come." I smiled to show I understood. Zhu Min lowered his voice and said: "I report to you, Brigade Commander: on the matter of our brigade leader, I have already written several reports, and our superiors have all along given no reply..." I stopped him and said: "We have seen your reports; but because the matter is so grave, and the substance of your reports rather hollow, they were not free of doubt. Now we may as well talk the thing through to the bottom, so that I may learn the truth." Zhu Min composed his face and said: "You, Commander, of course know better than I that Wan Lilang now holds great power at No. 76 and has become a chief enemy full in our front. The trouble is that our brigade leader was drawn over by Wan half a year ago and more. The occasion of it, to tell it, was simple: when our brigade leader was a sub-brigade leader in the Loyal and Patriotic Army, Wan Lilang happened to be his political instructor, and because of this tie the two hit it off at once in Shanghai. The brigade leader is at bottom a muddled man; Wan had power of every evil kind, and worked on his feelings besides, and the brigade leader could only bow his head and do as he was told." I put out my hand to stop Zhu's talk and asked: "Slowly—how do you know all this?" Zhu said: "He has always taken me for one of his own and hid nothing from me; sometimes, meeting a difficulty, he would even seek me out on purpose to talk it over. But ever since he gave away the whole of our unit's guns—three revolvers, two Mausers, and one Browning—to Wan, I opposed it; I said to him: 'Doing this, you leave yourself without the least power of self-defense, that is the least of it; but should our superiors hand down a case, what will you carry out to execute it? Is that not to lay your own weakness bare? Once our superiors inquire into it, we all suffer for it together!' He pounded the table at me on the spot, cursed me for wanting in loyalty and resolve, said I should meddle less in his affairs hereafter, and the like; and sure enough, after that he took less counsel with me on such things, and his comings and goings turned secret. But one thing I know for certain: he and Wan Lilang meet without fail once every month or so—the place and time unknown. There is, besides, a set liaison man, who, at the hour of afternoon tea every Monday at three, meets without fail at the café of the Sun Company on Nanking Road to pass word or exchange intelligence. If you doubt it, you may send a man to verify it on the spot!" Zhu Min's manner was grave, his tone plain and direct; what he said stood to reason and feeling both, with evidence and grounds, so that one could not but believe him. I clapped him on the shoulder and said mildly: "Our district chief, reading your reports, long ago praised you as a good comrade, young and able, pure and unselfish, faithful and to be relied on; and this talk today, face to face, moves me greatly, and proves too that the district chief's judgment was not wrong. Only I cannot for the life of me make out what your brigade leader means by doing this. After all these years in the work, has he not the least sense of right and wrong, the least discernment of duty from gain? To be sure, what you have brought to light can no longer be doubted for its truth; yet I still hope, for the best, that it may not be a fact... And afterward I shall, of course, send a man to verify it in secret." Zhu Min bowed his head and said, with deep meaning: "I report to you, Commander: if there be any falsehood in my denunciation, I willingly take the 'counter-penalty' upon myself! And if it be found true, how then should he be dealt with?" I looked into his eyes, thinking: "Here it comes! The first masterstroke is played out after all—this is plainly casting a stone to feel out the road!" Still supposing them to be playing a two-man turn, I chose my words on purpose and said: "That is not certain either. To begin with, I am, in principle, against killing our own men; and besides, the law is not apart from human feeling. The 'house law' is stern indeed; but you must not forget the words 'the body is the family, the comrade as one's own hands and feet'—how hard it is for us to rear a single comrade! No man can help erring; it is a question of what his motive was—did he mean it or not?—and of how grave the fault he committed, to be weighed and dealt with by the circumstance. The comrades working in the fallen zone, crossing blades at close quarters with the enemy and grappling flesh to flesh, are not the same, either, as those in the rear." By Zhu Min's look he was far from agreeing with what I said; he would have argued back, but held himself in. The little fellow was truly a formidable character. But he seemed to have lost his patience, and came straight to the point: "Zhou's treasonous conduct is by now very plain, and I think he will act at any moment; to guard against trouble before it comes, I ask you, Commander, to lay down a concrete plan of countermeasures—that is, what am I to do?" It looked as though he were forcing me to show my hand. I thought a moment and said, fancying myself clever: "Since we have come this far, let me first make my own stand plain. First: I will on no account harm Zhou. I firmly believe that his collusion with Wan Lilang was a moment's muddle, or forced on him by some turn of circumstance, done in spite of himself. Second: that he gave the weapons to Wan Lilang was likewise a thing done under the same duress, for want of any other course. At least to this day he has done nothing to wreck the organization or sell out a comrade. I see this man as good at bottom, and worthy of sympathy. I am not a man without principle; but for the sake of cherishing and saving an old comrade who has strayed into the wrong path and not yet sunk deep, I mean to ask him to a frank and open talk, heart laid bare. To say I know nothing of his treasonous conduct—he would not believe it; and for each to hide it in his breast only breeds more mistrust; so it is better to lay it plainly open, to clear the ghost from his heart, and, in a spirit of utter candor, to bring home to him the great cause and move him by feeling, and wake him to turn back before it is too late. Do you think this clumsy method will serve?" A look of astonishment crossed Zhu Min's face, and he shook his head at once: "That will absolutely not do! He is not so much the gentleman, nor so high-minded, as you imagine; to speak the thing out to his face is as good as fixing on him the great crime of betraying the organization. He will not take your kindness, for he will not believe you truly mean to clear him; and if it goes wrong he may turn on you then and there, and the consequence would be past imagining!" In truth these words of mine were half true and half feigned; were it feasible, I would truly have been willing to do so! But I could foresee that this road was closed, and the way too foolish besides; my real aim was only to sound out by it how Zhu Min would react. Little did I expect that Zhu's words were straightforward, with no sign in them of any cunning or contrivance. I asked offhand: "What you say has reason in it too; how, in your view, should it best be done?" Zhu Min composed his countenance and said: "Commander, I feel the present situation is very troublesome and very urgent, and my own responsibility the graver for it. I dread that, once he acts, we shall be caught unready; that I myself be dragged in is a small thing, but should it touch the safety of the organization, or bring harm on other important comrades, the responsibility is great, and none could bear it! So I ask you, Commander, to lay down a plan of countermeasures early, that we may meet the turn." I said gladly: "Just so! I too have thought of it. Now, I have a plan in mind; what we are to do may be divided broadly into three parts. First: my principle in handling the Zhou question is unchanged—I am resolved to help him and to bring him to his senses; we cannot 'drive the dog into a blind alley,' but must give him a chance to make a fresh start. On this point there must first be, between you and me, a tacit understanding, and we must both bend our strength toward this end. If you and I, aiding and supporting him, can within a short while bring off the sanction of Xu Liqiu, to atone for his past fault and show where his heart lies, that would be better still. Second: should he indeed prove not his own master, or persist blind in his error, then I have resolved to move him away from Shanghai, to cut him off from Wan Lilang; so that Wan may have no way to work his arts, and Zhou too may slip his yoke, and not be forced to go on in his mischief. This, I hold, is a way that parries and strikes in one, and may well be adopted. As for Zhou, he can hardly escape some punishment from our superiors—that is to be looked for; but we shall at least have done our utmost to save him whole, which fully accords with my first intent of cherishing a comrade. Third: from now on you must take more pains, sounding out his attitude from the front and from the flank, and keeping close watch on all his doings; if you find him making any move, report it at once, that trouble may be forestalled. At the same time, of the personnel, the business, the funds of this sub-brigade, and its present working connections, you are, of course, the one who knows them best of all. You may set about now, in secret, to put it in order, to tighten your hold and prepare to take it over; and the moment Zhou's case cannot be cleared, or it is decided to move him elsewhere, I shall at once recommend that you take over as third sub-brigade leader."

As for the means of dealing with Zhu and Zhou, in speaking thus I had indeed spent some pains, fancying that I had guarded both attack and defense and covered every side. Though whether Zhu Min was in league with Zhou Xiyuan was still hard to be sure of, yet to soothe them, to slacken their wariness, and to wait for the sanction order to come down, was needful.

While I laid down the so-called "countermeasures" above, I watched Zhu Min's reaction closely; I saw only his brows and eyes stir, his look mixed, as if startled, as if glad, and it was very hard to judge what his heart was thinking. When the talk was done, Zhu Min said: "Yes, I shall do just as you direct, Commander." On the road home, savoring over my own words, I found in them not a little that was ill-judged. Supposing Zhu and Zhou really were in collusion, then my saying that I meant to move Zhou to the rear to take his punishment boded far more ill than good, and was as good as sending him to his death; for our bureau's discipline was very strict, and toward men of treasonous conduct above all it never lightly forgave—was it likely they did not know it? So my talk of moving Zhou to the rear, far from slackening their wariness, would rather spur him to launch his revolt the sooner—was this not to overreach and undo myself! The more I thought, the more it was wrong; for the holes in the middle of this so-called "countermeasure" were too many, and I could not but rail at myself for being too green. But things having come to this pass, there was nothing for it but to let matters take their course and find some other way to patch it over.

From late May to early June, I kept up my contact with Zhou Xiyuan and Zhu Min; all was quiet, with nothing at all amiss.

As to my own being ordered to Chengdu for training, the date to report was near at hand; but the matter of the travel route from Shanghai to the rear, which Mr. Chen had promised to arrange, and all the preparations, seemed to go forward none too briskly—for to that day there was no firm word—and in my heart I burned with anxiety.

At this juncture my contact with Zhou Xiyuan centered, on the face of it, on the case of sanctioning Xu Liqiu and the replenishing of the unit's weapons as its chief topics. By Zhou's report, Xu Liqiu would arrive in Shanghai from Hong Kong within days, and would tarry in Shanghai two or three days at most, so that the time must be seized and no good chance let slip. I promised him that I would fix a day, a time, and a place, and introduce him face to face to the comrade in charge of conveying the arms and ammunition, after which the two sides would themselves agree on the manner of handing over the weapons. At the same time I charged him firmly to gather Xu's photograph and the relevant follow-up material complete without delay, and report them for record. Two days later Zhou reported again that Xu had, on the thirteenth of that month, hurried through Shanghai on his way to the capital, having to be in time for the session of the puppet Central Propaganda Ministry opening at Nanjing on the fifteenth. The chance being missed, there was nothing for it but to wait and carry it out on Xu's return journey after the meeting. Such shifts in the state of things happen often enough, and I did not look deeply into it; yet I could not but suspect that he was putting me off with a pretext on purpose, and my guard against him rose the higher for it.

Shortly I received a written instruction from the district office, running in substance thus: "It is noted that in the case of the third sub-brigade leader Zhou Xiyuan's collusion with Wan Lilang to plot treason, the proof being conclusive, the matter has again been reported to bureau headquarters with a request for his sanction, lest we nurse the abscess and hand on the trouble; we believe approval to carry it out may be had within days. Be sure to press on with the arrangements and await the order to act. It is hoped, too, that you, elder brother, will be prudent in your movements, and by no means let yourself be caught out—this is of the first importance!"

On receiving the order, I at once met with the first sub-brigade leader, Liu Quande. I told him that a traitor had appeared in our organization, a man by the name of Feng Xian (Zhou Xiyuan's alias), whose dealings with the enemy were proven, and whose sanction was now under request to bureau headquarters. He was at present, under the false name of sanctioning a traitor, asking for a replenishment of weapons; I had feigned to grant it, and would within days meet him in company with Liu. "When the time comes," I said, "you may pass yourself off as the man in charge of the arms depot; I shall introduce you between us and promise a replenishment of two revolvers and sixty rounds; the introduction done, I shall withdraw first, and as for the conveying and handing over of the weapons, the two of you shall settle it separately afterward. In truth this is only a blind; the purpose is that you take the chance to mark your target well, and await the order to act." I described, besides, Zhou Xiyuan's age and appearance in detail, and gave the whereabouts of Zhou's unit office, so that the action might be prepared for beforehand.

As late as June 22, Zhou Xiyuan reported that Xu Liqiu, his business at Nanjing done, had come to Shanghai and was now lodged in Room 301 of the Wengzhou Hotel, meaning to spend a week in Shanghai in feasting and sightseeing before returning to his post in Hong Kong. Xu's every move was already under close watch and control; and so the matter of replenishing the weapons truly brooked no delay—if the weapons were in hand by morning, the action could be taken that very night, and Xu would be as good as a fish swimming in the pot.

On the twenty-fourth I severally arranged for Zhou Xiyuan and Liu Quande to meet at a café on Bubbling Well Road, and all went off smoothly according to the plan laid down. Liu Quande marked Zhou Xiyuan well and awaited the order to act; Zhou Xiyuan went back to prepare to receive the weapons.

On the twenty-fifth Zhou sent through a courier another report, saying in brief that Xu Liqiu's photograph, the personnel data due to be reported, and the course and content of Xu's attendance at the puppet Central Propaganda Ministry meeting on this trip, and so forth, had all been gathered complete. As to the replenishment of weapons, it had been agreed with the source that they would be received and conveyed into hand on the twenty-eighth of that month, so that the party could set out on the twenty-ninth; three action men, So-and-so and the rest, had now been detailed—one to keep watch on the outer ring, one to do the deed, and the third to cover the withdrawal. Only there remained other technical questions, and sundry proposals—how, once the task was done, to strengthen the unit's working capacity, adjust the ranks of its personnel, and further lift its spirits—that awaited instruction face to face; all Xu Liqiu's material he would present in person without fail, and so on. I sneered inwardly, thinking: this man looks a fool, but his craft at counterfeiting is very finished; this is "letting the kite out on a long string," meaning to make a show of completing the Xu case to fix our trust in him, and to squeeze a little blackmail out of us besides. It was my own fault to be careless for the moment, and to underrate him too; in fact this thought of mine was itself amiss.

I sent word to Zhou Xiyuan to come himself, at ten in the morning of the twenty-eighth, to his unit office, that we might talk it all over face to face, and that I would take charge of settling every question for him.

In the two years from the autumn of 1939 to the winter of 1941, our behind-the-lines action in Shanghai—those fierce blows struck at the running dogs of the enemy and the puppets, at the great traitors and arch-villains, and at the opportunist small fry—did indeed work its deterrent effect. Its influence reached so far that our fearsome name spread wide through the fallen zone, and men's spirits were lifted for the whole future of our war of resistance.

Yet because our striking power could not be brought to bear and built up without a break, and because we lacked effective means to check and guard against the counterblows of the enemy and the puppets, our own organization was struck down again and again, while the arrogance of the enemy and the puppets grew daily more rampant.

From the beginning of 1941, the Wang puppets' "Special Operations Headquarters," joining with the "Shanghai Japanese gendarmerie," each detailed one authorized unit, given solely to visiting a thoroughgoing search and mass arrest upon my "Shanghai District."

Hazardous as our plight was, we understood deeply where our duty lay, and truly neither dared nor wished to shrink back; by an incomplete count, in the ten months that followed we still brought off some sixty-odd cases of action and sabotage—on average one every five days—and so the number of our comrades seized, and even killed, grew step by step.

At this point, to make up our strength, I set vigorously to putting in order the "First Action Brigade," which held deep potential yet had shown very little. On this matter I took counsel with the assistant secretary Liu Yuanshen—already ordered away for training, though not yet gone—entreating him to lend the "Shanghai District" a hand once more, for two months, under the title of acting commander of the "First Action Brigade." Moved by a call he could not in honor refuse, Yuanshen readily gave his consent. Little did I think that, because this measure of mine was not guarded against with care enough, Yuanshen would be duped by a petty clown and fall into enemy hands. Though in his prison bonds he suffered every kind of torment, yet he held fast to his resolve and his honor, kept the organization safe, and set up a model of one whom might and force could not bend—a hero and a stout fellow indeed.

By the end of October, on a night when alarms came thick and fast, through a want of steadiness in myself I made once more a whole train of wrong judgments, and actually cast myself into the net and fell into the trap—to my deep shame.

At first they did not know that I was the head of the "Shanghai District"; but afterward, bringing men time and again to identify me, they laid bare my true identity in the end. Things having come to this, what I could yet hold to was that I must on no account lose the dignity due to one who had once been chief over a thousand men. As for how they would deal with me, that was their affair.

Yet the behind-the-lines work at Shanghai, under the leadership of the "Juntong Bureau," did not cease for my dereliction and capture.
