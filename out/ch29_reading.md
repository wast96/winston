## 15

That morning Zhongliang left the house as always, but he did not go to work at the Jing'an post office; he walked straight in through the gate of the Shanghai Municipal Public Security Bureau. He set the silver crucifix down on the desk in Section Chief Chen's office and said it all in one breath: “My code name is Catfish. I was once the courier of Comrade Su Lina. I can vouch for who she is.”

The whole morning long, it was Zhongliang alone who talked. At noon Section Chief Chen stood up and cut him off: “Eat first. We'll go on after.” In the afternoon Zhongliang talked on until nearly dusk, and again Section Chief Chen rose. “We have indeed checked that intelligence, and we know there were these two code names, Catfish and Cuckoo. But on what grounds am I to believe what you tell me?”

Zhongliang thought and said, “There's someone else who can vouch for me.” Just find Father Kruger, he said, and he can prove that I am Catfish. Section Chief Chen laughed. “You'd have us go looking for an agent of American imperialism to vouch for you?”

A month later Zhongliang walked into Section Chief Chen's office again. Section Chief Chen opened a case file and said, “We have confirmed that you are the son of the martyr Xu Delin; that in 1936 you took over from him as a postman at the Jing'an post office; that you knew our underground agent Comrade Zhou San; and we have learned, too, that you distinguished yourself in the battle to liberate Shanghai and were very nearly killed in the fighting for the China Merchants warehouse. But none of this proves that you are Catfish.”

“Then what did you call me here for?”

“To tell you the result of our inquiry.” Section Chief Chen said, “Comrade Xu Zhongliang, I understand how you feel.”

“I don't want to be understood. I want it proved.”

Section Chief Chen said, “All we can prove is that in the old society you were a postman, and that you are a postman still.”

Zhongliang nodded and said not another word. It took him a whole half-day to get himself home.

That night Zhongliang did not bend over the table at his calligraphy; he took up his pen and wrote a long letter to Deputy Mayor Pan Hannian. But to his surprise, the moment Su Lina rose the next day she tore the letter up, saying he had better let it go — that to be alive at all was enough for her. Zhongliang said, “It's not enough. I won't have you carry a name like this.”

Su Lina's eyes went suddenly bright and hard. She stared at him a while, then lowered her head and said, “Then I'll go. I'll find somewhere no one knows me.”

Zhongliang caught her hand in alarm and stood before her, at a loss for what to say.

Su Lina slowly raised her face, reached out like an aged mother and touched Zhongliang's cheek, and all at once she smiled. “You foolish man. Think of all the dead. We're lucky just to be alive.”

But Zhongliang would not be reconciled to it. Often, after work, he sat in the post office gatehouse writing letters — not only to Deputy Mayor Pan Hannian, but to Mayor Chen Yi, and to Luo Ruiqing, the Minister of Public Security; only he never once had a reply.

One day You Kechang gave a sigh and warned him that going on like this would bring trouble down on him. Zhongliang flew into a sudden fury and glared at him. “Even you get to have a Chinese name — so why should she bear the name of a suspected enemy agent?”

You Kechang sighed again, held his tongue, and sat to one side, silently watching the setting sun beyond the window.

Not long after the first National Day of New China had passed, Su Lina received a visitor of a particular kind at home. When the knock came she was sitting at the table pasting matchboxes — the work the neighborhood had found her, by way of looking after her.

Su Lina started, rose to open the door, and knew Zhou Chukang at a glance. He stood there in a crisp officer's uniform of the People's Liberation Army, waited a moment at the threshold, and said, “Won't you ask me in to sit?”

Su Lina, like a woman made of wood, held the door and stood aside.

Zhou Chukang looked round the room and sat down before the matchboxes heaped like a hill. “I came to see you,” he said.

Su Lina made no sound. The only thing she could hear was her own heartbeat.

Zhou Chukang said again, “I know I shouldn't have come.”

Still Su Lina said nothing. Under the brim of Zhou Chukang's cap she saw the white hair at his temples, and a crowd of old memories stopped up her chest at once. After a long silence Su Lina forced out a single sentence: “I've married.”

“I know,” Zhou Chukang said. “I wanted to come and see you all the same.”

Su Lina grew calm by degrees. She sat down across from Zhou Chukang and, over the matchboxes, asked how he had found his way here. Zhou Chukang said he had known the address half a year ago, and known her circumstances too. The Shanghai public security bureau had come to his unit on two occasions to make inquiries; they had wanted to learn about Su Lina's life before 1937. “If I had found you back then,” Zhou Chukang said, “you would never have come to this.”

Zhou Chukang had gone looking for her in Shanghai on two occasions before. During the great Battle of Changsha, when his eye was wounded, he had broken his journey to Hong Kong for treatment and stayed ten days, and had all but combed the whole concession. The second time was at the victory over Japan, when his unit was airlifted from India to Shanghai to take the surrender; Zhou Chukang had called on the military and on the Shanghai gangs alike, and still had not managed to find Su Lina. Later his unit set out for the Northeast, and in the Battle of Siping he led his men over to the other side. Now Zhou Chukang was a deputy division commander of the People's Liberation Army's Fourth Field Army.

“I thought you were dead.” Zhou Chukang took off his cap and dragged a hand hard through his hair. “I searched every graveyard in Shanghai back then.” “You should have taken me for dead.” Su Lina said it lightly. “You shouldn't have come.”

Zhou Chukang nodded and said, “I know.”

After a long silence Su Lina stood and said, “Go now. He'll be back soon.”

Zhou Chukang got to his feet, looked at the matchboxes on the table, and said, “What can I do for you? I'll do everything I can.”

Su Lina shook her head and said, “There's no need.”

But at the door Zhou Chukang put on his cap, fixed his eyes on hers, and asked suddenly, “In all these years, have you thought of me?”

Su Lina was startled, but did not answer. She stood in the doorway and slowly straightened her back, and the look on her face turned little by little languid and indifferent, as though she had gone back to those years and become once more that graceful, elegant officer's wife.

Su Lina watched Zhou Chukang turn and go out through the shikumen gate, and only after a long while did she let out a long breath, drained, as if the whole of her had been emptied out. When she had shut the door she fell face-down on the bed, pulled the quilt over herself any which way, and still felt cold.

Su Lina shivered with cold, and that very night fell gravely ill.

Two months later Zhongliang read in the newspaper the news of Zhou Chukang's death. He was the first deputy division commander of the Volunteer Army to fall on the Korean battlefield. Home again, Zhongliang said to Su Lina, “You remember, you once had me ask after Zhou Chukang.”

Su Lina stopped her work and looked at him blankly.

“There's a deputy division commander of the Volunteer Army by that same name.” Zhongliang said, “The paper says he's been killed.”

Su Lina lowered her head, pasted up with slow care the matchbox in her hands, then looked at him and said, “One day we will all die. But I mean to die before you.”

Zhongliang asked, “Why?”

Su Lina said, “I won't have you leave me alone in this world.”
