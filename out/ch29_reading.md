## The Dyeworks Drying Ground

Chen Qianli had guessed that agents would come to his door today, but he had not expected them so soon.

Today was the fourteenth of the first month. First thing in the morning he had gone out, threading through the streets and lanes, pretending he knew nothing at all of the two tails behind him. At Laoximen he went into a teahouse, ordered a plate of steamed buns and a plate of shredded dried tofu, and asked for tea. While the shadowing agents were not looking, he borrowed a newspaper from a guest at the next table and found the advertisement.

The figures that appeared in the advertisement were, in order, three, one, eight, five, three, four, six, six; in Morse code these eight figures were the two characters ‘Haohan.’ Below the advertisement was a telephone number, and the moment Comrade Haohan dialed that number, he would be in danger.

Back at the house on Menghua Street, he went up to the third floor. The night before he had forced himself to sleep, right here in this third-floor side room. He stood at the rear window and looked out. Below the rear window ran a narrow gap-lane, very narrow, and on its other side was a wall; within the wall was the drying ground of a dyeworks. On the square drying ground stood row upon row of drying frames, rising well above the wall, and hung with hundreds upon thousands of lengths of blue cloth that drifted in the sun. Chen Qianli reckoned the distance: if he stood on the windowsill, he could jump onto the wall opposite, and once over it, cross the drying ground and get away from this house. He set a chair beneath the rear window, sat down facing out, and once more ran through everything in his mind, satisfied that the plan he had conceived aboard ship could still be carried through.

He knew agents would burst in today. Ye Qinian was not Lu Zhongde; he would not lightly let him bluff his way past. Yesterday, on the wharf, he had put on a show, so that Lu Zhongde would think he was not yet exposed, and the agents had neither fired nor rushed up to seize him. But last night, the more Ye Qinian turned it over, the more uneasy he would grow; today he would still send men to take him, or simply to be rid of him.

Yesterday afternoon he had had Lu Zhongde arrange lodgings for him. He knew that so long as he did not go to meet Lin Shi and the comrades, the agents might for the time being not seize them. Leaving them at large was what let ‘Xi Shi’ carry on his ‘work.’ Before going to Guangzhou, he had had Wei Dafu secretly find a place, meaning within these two days to move everyone there; before disembarking he had already sent Liang Shichao there to stand by.

In the evening he had gone to Chen Qianyuan's, but he did not go up, nor did he enter the lane. He pretended he had noticed someone tailing him and taken fright, made a great circle about the place, and came back.

Now the agents had come. He heard a confusion of footsteps below, and something clattered rolling down the stairs. Just before he came up, he had picked up two battered aluminum pots from the kitchen and set them on the staircase. He lived alone on the third floor; no one would come to call on him. The stairwell was very dim, and when the agents rushed up they would not notice two pots underfoot.

He pushed open the window, stepped onto the sill, and leapt.

There was moss on the wall, and his hand slipped, but he still caught hold. Over the wall, he dropped down into a great expanse of blue cloth. Some of the pieces were already dry, some half wet; in the air was a faint sour smell. He moved slowly among the blue cloths, knowing the thing was not over. Yesterday evening a batch of cloth had been taken down from the drying ground, leaving a stretch of open ground near its gate; from a distance he had seen two men standing at that gate. The house had been found by Lu Zhongde, who of course knew the lay of the land around it well.

Chen Qianli crouched down and watched from beneath the cloth. After a while he saw a pair of feet moving, but a second pair was slow to appear.

Behind him men were shouting; he could wait no longer, and began to run fast toward that pair of feet, weaving through the gaps, keeping the cloth from swaying as much as he could. Now he saw it — through the blue cloth he saw the figure, bent low, a gun in his hand, looking left and right, unable to decide. He ran at him, and as he ran snatched up a length of cloth and flung it at the shadow. He did not stop, but drove straight up to the agent and struck a fist into his Adam's apple; before the agent could cry out he threw a second straight punch into the hollow between his ribs. He did not let the agent fall straight to the ground, but reached out, gripped him by the lapels, and lowered him slowly down, taking the pistol from his hand.

He guessed the other agent was still standing at the gate of the drying ground. The gate was on the right, but he ran to the left, hauling hard at the cloth so that the frames rocked violently. Along the passage between two rows of cloth he ran swiftly to the right. From the frames to the gate was another seven or eight paces; he shot out from the last row of cloth and rushed the gate, roaring aloud, Don't move — but his feet never for a step stopped. In his left hand was the gun, its muzzle leveled at the agent, who had still not made up his mind. He did not fire, but ran past the agent, and with a sweep of the dagger in his right hand it was as if he had merely brushed a hand along the man's chin, as if his arm, swinging as he ran, had touched him by accident. Not until he had already leapt out through the gate did the man feel the thread of cold at his throat.

Still he did not stop, but ran on to the left down the lane, then turned right into a straight alley. On the right of the alley was a high wall; within the wall came a hubbub of voices, but the little alley outside it was very quiet. Out of the alley's mouth was a broader street; he did not turn, but plunged straight into the alley opposite. Beyond the next alley-mouth was another wide street, and at last there were some people.

Chen Qianli slowed his pace, steadied his breathing; his mind began to work, and all at once he had an idea. He did not run for the main road outside, but went instead to Xueqian Street, and from the mouth of the lane on Xueqian Street entered the cross-lane of Puyu Li. He turned into the straight lane, followed it through two or three rows of houses to the lane's mouth. Outside was Penglai Road, and Lu Zhongde's painting-and-calligraphy shop stood just to the left of the lane-mouth. He went into the shop, seized Lu Zhongde by the hand and hauled him toward the back room, saying as they went: “Old Yi, agents burst into my place; I finished two of them and got out. You'd best clear out too, and fast.”

“How would the agents know about that place of mine?” Lu Zhongde asked suspiciously.

“Last night I meant to go and find Chen Qianyuan, and two agents were on me. They may have found the place. Leave the shop at once.”

“I'm in no hurry to withdraw. Even if that Menghua Street house is blown, they still can't find me; the man who rented the house has gone back to Sichuan for the New Year. To pull back and go underground, I'd have to change my whole identity over again — and we have an important task, no time to prepare all that. Let's hold out as long as we can.”

Chen Qianli came out of the painting shop and slipped into the crowd pressing about the outside of the National Products Market on Penglai Road. At the stop of the French tramway company on Fangxie Road, he glanced at the telegraph pole beside the platform and found Liang Shichao's slip of paper, pasted beside a notice about a missing person. The spot had originally held a small advertisement, most of whose picture had been torn away — the medicine bottle was gone, and only half a bald head was left. On the slip was a telephone number, and, as if some schoolboy on his way home had scrawled it crookedly in pencil in the blank space, a line of abuse: Lu Zhongde is a bastard.

He boarded a No. 5 tram, bought a ticket and had it punched, and squeezed into the third-class trailer at the back. This foreign-run tram had only first class and third class, no second: evidently the foreigners in the Settlement felt that between themselves and the Chinese lay more than a single grade.

He meant first to go to Zhaojiabang and tell Lin Shi and Li Han to move at once. He calculated in his mind that Qin Chuan'an, Tian Fei, Dong Huiwen, and Chen Qianyuan all lived in the Settlement, and that even if Ye Qinian wanted to seize people, he could not move so fast. He reckoned that the withdrawal of the Guangzhou and Hong Kong liaison stations might have put Ye Qinian on the alert. At the Gonghexiang Wharf he had, it was true, deceived Lu Zhongde successfully, but today's move to arrest him showed that Ye Qinian had changed his mind. That being so, he ought not to let the other comrades run risks any longer.

The conductor announced each stop once in French and once in Chinese. The tram turned west at Xieqiao and, passing Rue Marco Polo, Lujiawan, the Dapu Road bridge, and the Panjia wooden bridge, reached the Avenue du Roi Albert stop, where Chen Qianli got off; the stop was right on the bank of the Zhaojiabang. At a tobacco-and-paper shop on the corner he telephoned Liang Shichao, then walked on along Zhaojiabang Road.

Outside the Maochang Coal Company there was no stir. Workers were carting coal across the bridge, two of them, one hauling in front like a tracker and one pushing behind; the flatcar was piled with coal balls just made. The plank bridge had no railing, and as the coal cart went over, he turned sideways and barely kept himself standing on the edge of the boards.

From the yard workshop came the creak of a rolling conveyor belt. The sun shone on the silt of the riverbank; some green algae floated on the surface of the water, and a flock of sparrows foraged over the coal heaps. Within the coal yard, ringed by its wire fence, not a soul was to be seen. He rounded the coal heaps, which rose like small hills, and by the west wall of the single-story building a man stood bent over with his back to the outside; as Chen Qianli drew near he had just finished pissing, and turned to ask, who are you looking for. He was looking for Li Han.

The man waved a hand toward the shacks where the workers lived. “Li Han was on night shift; he's only just gone back.”

He knew where Li Han lived. At the northwest corner of the coal yard a stretch of the wire fence had been pulled open; it lay hard against the riverbank, with waste ground all around, so that outsiders never came. The torn wire had been carefully rolled back to leave a hole, and beyond the hole ran a little path — not one specially made, but one trodden out over the years by people's feet. All around the path were weeds, growing from the earth mounds down into the earth pits; on the mounds clothes hung drying, and the pits had become rubbish tips. The path wound crookedly toward a row of shacks. Most of the coal-yard workers lived in these shacks.

There were two rows of shacks, thrown up any old way along the river, one here and one there; a few had brick walls, but most were earthen, though the walls had all been whitewashed. The outer row of shacks faced the waste ground, and the patch of ground before their doors had been leveled and spread with cinders and earth — the little square of the shack-dwellers, where in summer they ate their supper and took the cool air. Now, in the cold, there was nothing on it but drying laundry and stray cats and dogs. On the south side of the ground, some twenty-odd meters from the shacks, a well had been sunk, where ordinarily a few women were always washing clothes and vegetables.

Li Han lived in the inner row, close to the riverbank. Between the two rows of shacks was a long strip of open ground, and against the door-walls of the various households along it had been built up some water-troughs and cooking stoves; on the walls hung chili peppers, garlic, and dried string beans. But outside Li Han's shack hung a large slab of cured pork.

This slab of cured pork was the safety signal; seeing it, Chen Qianli turned out from the wall and walked in. No sooner had he entered the long strip of ground than a feeling of danger rose in his heart. Li Han's shack was shut; he knew that ordinarily Li Han seldom shut his door, even when he went off to work in the coal yard. In fact the dwellers in these shacks all rarely shut their doors — except at night, they left them open even in the cold, so that the shacks would be brighter and they could save on lamp oil.

Chen Qianli hastily stepped back a few paces and slipped around behind the shacks. The rear walls of this row of shacks pressed hard against the riverbank, which fell away in a steep slope of silt piled up over many years; between the slope and the rear walls of the shacks there was left only the narrowest strip of level ground, on which a man could barely stand sideways. Keeping close against the rear walls, he moved slowly forward — one shack, two, three… He knew Li Han's was the seventh. The rear walls of the shacks had no windows, so the people inside had no need to guard their backs; but Li Han's neighbor had a rear window. He pushed it open and quietly climbed in.

The moment he was inside he knew what had happened. There were agents at Li Han's. Next door, Li Han and Lin Shi were speaking by turns, one asking for water, the other wanting to piss, while someone reprimanded them both in a low voice. Chen Qianli went to the door — made of long boards nailed together, with gaps between the boards — and looked out through a gap. The rear wall of the front row of shacks was like this one: some had rear windows open, some not. He suspected there might be men behind those windows too.

He did not know how many men were in the next room, or how many guns. He could only wait.

He waited a long time. The sun had already slanted westward, and light came in through the gaps in the door-boards; out on the open ground children ran chasing past, and then all was silent again.

He knew the next room was a trap, set especially for him. Having failed to take him on Menghua Street, Ye Qinian had guessed he would surely come here. What worried him now was whether Ye Qinian would see through the false front he had put up before Lu Zhongde. That the agents had come to the Maochang coal yard to make arrests seemed to show that Ye Qinian no longer cared about exposing ‘Xi Shi.’ Li Han had escaped from the market and not been taken; Cui Wentai had never come to the coal yard; so had they come to the coal yard to make arrests because they no longer wanted Lu Zhongde to go on hiding within the underground Party organization? Had he underestimated Ye Qinian's judgment? Would the withdrawal of the Guangzhou liaison station make Ye Qinian think that nothing new could be dug out along this line, and that it was time to draw in the net? This was a tangled game of chess, in which he had not only to guess what his opponent's next move would be, but to guess how the opponent was guessing his next move. In any case, he must not yet let Lu Zhongde feel he was exposed.

The chance came. The door of the next room opened, and someone came out, stood across the open ground, and, humming a little tune, pissed against the foot of the wall. Chen Qianli saw his shoulder twitch, and gave the door a light push outward, then pulled it to again. The pissing agent spun round and peered suspiciously this way. Nothing stirred, and the agent was reassured. Buttoning himself up, he took up his tune again and made to go back to Li Han's shack.

The door opened and shut once more, this time with a larger motion and a louder sound. This startled the agent, and he shouted: “Who's there?”

A wooden window set at an angle across from Li Han's shack was pushed open, and two agents thrust out their heads; one of them shouted sternly: “What's all the fuss? Get back inside.”

The pissing agent pointed in Chen Qianli's direction, and two more agents came out of Li Han's shack; several pistols leveled together at this door, yet none dared rush it. After a long hesitation, at last one bold enough for anything kicked the door open and charged in, gun raised — but there was no one in the room, only the rear window standing half open.

“So it was the wind!” The agent, still shaken, cursed aloud to bring himself round.

Now Chen Qianli knew where the agents of the front row of shacks were hidden. He climbed out through the window, followed the strip of path only a foot wide around to the east end of the shacks, and waited there a while until all was quiet once more. Then he began to run — like a cat, or a leopard, running without a sound underfoot. He ran up to the door of the shack where the agents were hidden, never once stopping, and by sheer momentum burst the door open, driving straight to the rear window. Before the two agents there could react, the dagger in Chen Qianli's hand had opened one agent's throat; the man only stared blankly at Chen Qianli, then at his comrade. After a moment his legs began to give, and he fell. The other was the very fellow who had shouted the reprimand just now; he stared at the agent on the ground but dared make no sound, for a keen dagger was pressed to his neck, the point pricking his Adam's apple, and he felt his throat go icy cold, as if some liquid were seeping out.

The point had not gone in. Chen Qianli said in a low voice: “Have the men in the house opposite come out.”

He did not take his meaning.

Chen Qianli jerked his chin toward the window. “Call them out.”

The agent began to shout, All of you come out, all of you come out. Three agents came out of Li Han's shack; Chen Qianli did not wait, but snatched the agent's pistol, chambered a round, fired, and fired again, the movements all of a piece. Three agents fell to the ground. Seeing Chen Qianli shoot, the agent in the room lunged at him from behind to seize him; without turning his head Chen Qianli drove his bent elbow back into him, then spun and fired again.

Chen Qianli leapt out through the window and ran for the shack opposite. Bursting through the door, he found a fight already under way inside. There were still two agents in the shack; at the instant the shots rang out they froze for a moment, and Lin Shi and Li Han seized the chance to strike. Li Han grappled one agent and rolled with him on the ground, throttling him by the neck. Lin Shi caught up the teapot from the table and rushed to smash it down on the other agent's head, but his leg wound had not yet healed, and the blow did not knock the agent out. Seeing the agent raise his pistol, Lin Shi flung his whole body at him and, like Li Han, went rolling on the ground together with the agent.

Chen Qianli darted over in a single stride and kicked at the small of the back of the agent who was grappling Lin Shi. The kick was enough to break the man's spine — but before it landed the agent fired, the gun pressed between the two men's bellies, so that the report sounded somewhat muffled.
