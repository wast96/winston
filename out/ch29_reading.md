## Chapter 29. Parting the Wild Grass to Seek the Doubtful Dust

The winter of 1932.

On the desk of Xiong Zida, chief of the Detective Division of the Central Shanghai Commandant’s Office, lay a stack of newspapers and a single official document.

Outside the window ice-flowers tumbled and heavy snow whirled down; the passers-by in the street struggled along, one foot deep, one foot shallow, the very image of Xiong Zida’s mood at that moment, chilled through and through.

His adjutant, Liu Yun, was carefully packing his briefcase for him and putting in order the documents to be handed over; the adjutant’s hands were light and deft, but when they touched the newspapers on the desk, Xiong Zida gave a snort.

“Leave those,” Xiong Zida said.

Among that heap of papers were an issue of Pravda, printed and published in the Soviet Union, and an issue of the Xin Zhonghua Bao published by the Chinese Communist Party; both carried an article by Mao Zedong, “Peace, Democracy, and the War of Resistance.” They also ran, in bold headlines, the news that “the CCP Central Committee has successfully convened the National Special-Committee Work Conference in Shanghai.”

These newspapers were the direct cause of Xiong Zida’s fall from office.

“I made one fatal mistake,” Xiong Zida said.

Adjutant Liu looked puzzled.

“I trusted people too readily. Everyone around me was a subordinate that fellow Du had planted here.”

Adjutant Liu lowered his head.

“Isn’t that so, Adjutant Liu?”

“Chief…”

“No need to explain.” Xiong Zida let out a long breath. “I don’t believe Du Luning can hold this seat for three years. His end will be worse than mine.”

Adjutant Liu grew doubtful. “You mean… you intend to come back?”

“No, not I. The Communists. The Communists will rob him of his peace at meat and in bed.” Xiong Zida gave a cold smile and pushed the window open.

Snowflakes drifted in; the wind cut like a knife to the bone.

From far down the street came the cry of a newsboy: “The China League for Civil Rights founded in Shanghai, demanding that the Nationalist Government release political prisoners and safeguard the people’s democratic right to resist Japan…”

“Listen to that!” Xiong Zida said. “Not every paper leaves a blank window where the censor has been.”

“Chief?”

“Do you hear it? That is the voice of the Communists… You cannot control it. They are like the wind, like drifting cloud, forever shifting, everywhere at once, seeping into every crack… As the saying goes: ice three feet thick is not the work of one cold day.”

“Do you think we can catch them?”

“What do you think? Can you catch the lightning-flash and the thunderclap?”

Adjutant Liu felt a jolt in his heart.

“These words of mine you needn’t report to your new superior; he would think them beneath his notice.” Xiong Zida took the briefcase from Adjutant Liu’s hands and said, with heavy meaning, “I am leaving. Let these newspapers stay, as a reminder to Du Luning.”

Xiong Zida was gone; his retreating figure, in the corridor of the Detective Division, looked utterly worn and spent.

Yu Xiaojiang arrived, riding a military motorcycle, dashing and spirited. This day happened also to be Yang Muci’s first day back at work after his recovery.

Seeing Yu Xiaojiang spring down from the motorcycle, Yang Muci at once came to attention where he stood and saluted her.

For the first time, Yu Xiaojiang’s face showed Yang Muci a bright and gentle smile.

A day of sudden snow, the weather turned abruptly cold, the plane trees on both sides of the street clothed in silver; Yang Muci and Yu Xiaojiang strolled side by side through the streets. Snowflakes drifted at random about their ears and hair, the fresh air lingered around them, and in both their hearts welled a faint warm current of “spring,” as though some long-missed tenderness had returned.

“You knew about me all along, didn’t you?” Muci asked.

“What do you think?” Yu Xiaojiang returned.

“I feel a little short of breath in front of you,” Muci said, smiling.

“That’s only natural; after all, I was once your instructor,” Yu Xiaojiang said. “The organisation took it into account, too, that you and I have the bond of teacher and student, which makes contact between us more convenient.”

Muci nodded.

“The organisation holds in the highest esteem the courage you and Comrade Ronghua showed at this ‘Special-Committee conference.’ With your own lives you bought the safety of the National Special Committee. The organisation has resolved to recognise Comrade Ronghua, posthumously, as a martyr of the revolution…”

Yang Muci stopped abruptly. Yu Xiaojiang looked up in surprise and followed his gaze: across the road hung the signboard of the Huamei Bookstore.

The shopfront had been repaired and looked wholly new. Within, the shop seemed very quiet; a young man in a thin, pale-blue cotton gown stood bowing at the door, seeing a reader out. His hair was neatly combed, and there was something in his features that resembled Ronghua, only his face wore an added, servile smile, and that made Muci uneasy.

“You miss her very much, don’t you?” Yu Xiaojiang said; she could feel the pain surging and echoing in Muci’s heart.

“Yes. I would rather believe she is still there.”

“Would you like to go in and walk about a little?”

“No. The Huamei Bookstore is, for me, still a minefield.”

“It seems your vigilance is stronger than your sentiment.”

“For which I have your teaching to thank.”

“Fang Zhitong’s defection this time dealt a heavy blow to the Party organisation’s underground contact network. For the free and unobstructed flow of intelligence, we must restore our secret transmitter as soon as we can.”

“I’ll find a way,” Muci said, lighting a cigarette.

“There is one very particular matter on which I should like to seek your answer.”

“Go on.”

“The Juntong signals office has lately detected unidentified radio waves in the neighbourhood of Yuyuan Road.”

Yuyuan Road? Something started in Muci’s heart.

“You mean…”

“Yes. I suspect there is a secret transmitter hidden in your house.”

Muci said nothing.

“You are not in the least surprised.” Yu Xiaojiang’s own expression was one of great surprise.

“I had found it once, but I couldn’t be sure,” Muci said.

“Who do you think is the likeliest suspect?”

“My mother.” Muci’s answer was both calm and decisive.

Yu Xiaojiang was briefly silent.

Mustering his courage, Muci asked, “Whose do you think this secret station is?”

“The Japanese.” Yu Xiaojiang answered with certainty. The cigarette ash burned Muci’s fingers; his chest felt stopped up.

“If my supposition is correct, then your position is a very hard one,” Yu Xiaojiang said.

“How does Du Luning see it?”

“His attitude is very ambiguous. As you know, he thinks highly of Wang Jingwei’s so-called policy of salvation by a roundabout path. He harbours illusions about the Japanese; perhaps he has plans of his own.”

Plans of his own? Muci stopped walking.

“What is it?”

“Surely he wouldn’t go so far as to throw in his lot with the Japanese?”

“It’s hard to say.” Yu Xiaojiang lowered her eyes to the footprints left across the snow, each one distinct. “We cannot reckon just how far he will go.”

“If it should turn out that my mother is a deeply buried Japanese spy, I find that beyond all imagining,” Muci said.

“You are her son. How much do you understand of her?”

Muci fell silent.

He did not, in truth, understand his mother well.

In childhood, his mother’s coldness; in his boyhood, the long years of boarding away; in his youth, the “rebellion” of never coming home; all of it had kept him and his family forever in a state of half-nearness and half-distance, and all of it left Muci feeling estranged from his mother, and at a loss before her.

“Perhaps I ought to pay a call on my… my mysterious ‘elder brother,’” Muci said. “Perhaps he can give me an answer.”

“The question is whether you trust him.”

“A man who has once saved your life has surely earned your trust!” Muci said.

In the pitch-black of winter, night hung low; the cold moonlight cast down a faint reek of blood and, looming from on high, bore down upon the Yang family garden, where the trees huddled in the desolate cold wind. A ghostly figure came out of the lingering snow and into the little shrine-hall in the garden.

“Xu Yuzhen” knelt on the prayer cushion in the shrine-hall, brought her palms together, and bowed her head low, as though all things in the world were void.

She seemed to be at confession.

She was no ordinary woman.

Her original name was Koyama Eiko, and she was an Imperial Flower of the China Expeditionary Army under the Land Survey Bureau of the Japanese General Staff. From the moment she took up her mission, she had suffered grievous wounds, in spirit and in body alike.

Her face had been cut by the surgeon’s knife into the likeness of another woman; her chastity she had given to a Chinese man she did not love, Yang Yuhua. In the world within her, the honour of an Imperial soldier stood above all else. She would not shrink from sacrificing her private feelings, nor even from violating her own flesh, to protect her identity; without the least sense of good or evil she had killed the women and children of the Yang household and destroyed the evidence, so as to lie hidden for the long term and to drive out the fear within her.

She was like a whore who would take any man, numb and unfeeling.

She had lost the man she loved, and yet her heart grew ever more hard.

And all this was no more than the basic price a soldier of the Japanese Empire paid for the sake of seizing the territory of China.

Koyama Eiko felt “honoured” by it. All night long her thoughts were of how to distinguish herself in this war, how, with more of the innocent blood of the common people of China, to pave her own way to the throne, to become the queen of flowers among the Imperial Flowers.

Whenever her thoughts reached this point she would grow feverishly, nervously excited, as though she saw the exalted Emperor of Japan and lay prostrate at his feet, and the Emperor was about to bestow on her the sword of a “god,” so that her name would go down forever in the annals of the Eastern Isles.

Koyama Eiko rose from the cushion and turned the underground switch hidden behind an infant’s photograph; a dark door split open with a snarl, and a gloomy, long, sloping underground passage opened before her eyes.

With practiced ease she switched on a torch, walked in, and shut the door of the hidden room fast behind her.

By the time she entered the cellar it was just midnight; she set up the transmitter.

On the stroke of midnight she sent out the safe call-sign: “The Imperial Flower calling…” Some tens of seconds later the receiving station answered.

“The Imperial Flower’s report concerning military dispositions in Shanghai, the movement of military supplies, and the recent trend of Shanghai’s economy; the report follows…” With practiced fingering she sent off each item of intelligence at speed; when she had finished, she received fresh instructions. She took off the headphones, pushed the transmitter, the codebook, and the cipher-sheets all to one side, and, her expression cold, poured herself a glass of wine; borrowing the warmth of the red wine, she pondered her plan for the next move.

Koyama Eiko opened a small wooden box, in which she treasured many lovely memories of her girlhood: a comb she had used, a lock of her lover’s hair, withered cherry blossoms. Suddenly her face changed utterly: something was missing from the box. A plain student photograph from her girlhood, the one photograph, kept back in secret from her superiors and privately preserved, that still held her own true face. Horror seized her. Who? Who had broken into the forbidden ground? Who had taken this photograph away? Once an intelligence agent’s traces are exposed, it foretells the end of her career in espionage, and hints, too, that the road of her life is soon to end.

In an instant she felt a panic and an anguish that soaked into her very flesh and marrow.

She must act at once and get the course of events firmly under control, or she would die with nowhere left to hide.

At this same moment there was another whose heart, like Koyama Eiko’s, was full of a burning unease and an incredulous panic, the very person who had taken Koyama Eiko’s photograph. The face in the photograph was unfamiliar, yet the eyes were altogether familiar; the eyes are the windows of the soul, and hard indeed to disguise, all the more so in a plain, innocent student portrait.

Yang Muci’s heart heaved like a tide; he could not believe it.

It was before midnight that he had entered the shrine-hall and found the mechanism of the cellar. He had discovered the secret transmitter, and the codebook. He recorded on microfilm everything he found. He could find no reasonable explanation for his mother’s furtive behaviour, until he saw a photograph kept in the box.

He felt terror.

For these eyes belonged to his own mother!

He felt a greater terror.

For this face belonged to another, a stranger!

Under heaven there could be no stranger thing than this, none more beyond imagining. A chill went straight from Muci’s head down to the soles of his feet. In his mind a rough frame of the story had already taken shape; he needed to “verify” it, he had to get to the answer, he would use evidence to “attest” the fact.

Muci drove straight to No. 7 Plum Blossom Lane.

Plum Blossom Lane was uncommonly still and secluded. Muci’s mood, strangely, began little by little to settle and grow calm again. He cut the engine, then lit a cigarette and rested his head against the back of the driver’s seat, turning over everything that had happened from first to last. He had already reached a preliminary and fairly clear judgment: this “mother” who lived in his house could not be his own birth mother. Then where had his own mother gone?

He opened the car door, pressed close to the foot of the wall of No. 7 Plum Blossom Lane, and climbed up; the lingering snow had made the wall slippery, and he nearly lost his footing on the empty air.

Muci’s movements were too broad, his lean too wide; as he vaulted down from the wall he struck a little potted plant at its corner, which gave a crisp clatter. Muci felt that he had indeed lost his composure, that he could blunder even at so simple a thing as getting over a courtyard wall.

Sure enough, a light came on in the courtyard.

“Come in, no need to skulk about.” A-Chu’s voice came from within the house, even and mild, with no sign of anger in it. Muci, thoroughly embarrassed, simply stood in the courtyard, stamped his feet, rubbed his hands, breathed out a puff of warm air, and said, “Forgive me this intrusion at such a late hour. Your younger brother Yang Muci has more than once been rescued by your helping hand, and has not dared to forget it; it has weighed on me, and I have come on purpose to thank you.”

The door opened, and Yang Muchu stood there with graceful, unhurried bearing, saying only, mildly, “Come when you wish to come; you needn’t find pretexts.”

Muci gave a small laugh.

A-Chu saw the fallen leaves trodden down at the foot of the wall and said, “You’ve ruined my snow-bamboo. Tomorrow you’ll go to Chen’s hothouse and buy me a new one to plant in its place.”

“Oh.” Muci, made uncomfortable by this, glanced back at the sorry state of the potted plant and hastily answered, “I’ll make it up to you.” Even as the words left his mouth they felt awkward, and he gave a perfunctory smile.

“You laugh a great deal?” A-Chu said, walking inward as he spoke.

A-Ci followed close behind. “You are very grave. Are you always so grave? Or only in front of me, putting on a ‘lofty air’ on purpose?”

A-Chu stopped.

“If you don’t care to hear it, then take it as unsaid,” A-Ci said.

“Mr. Yang, I did not invite you to my home as a guest; you have come uninvited. You have broken into a private house, I could call the police.”

“Mr. Yang.” A-Ci said. “I have come to call on you in all sincerity. Please believe me.”

“You’ve stopped calling me Mr. Chu?” A-Chu asked, with a sly edge.

“It is your blood that runs in my veins,” A-Ci answered, deftly.

“Come in and sit,” said A-Chu, his look softening, his tone gentle.

For the first time Yang Muci stepped into A-Chu’s study. Its furnishings were simple; the shelves were filled with books of every kind, chiefly on medicine and philosophy. On the wall hung an elegant ink landscape, “Green Bamboo, Spring Dawn”; on the desk were laid the four treasures of the scholar’s studio, and the ink in the inkstone was not yet dry, giving off its fragrance.

“Tea, or wine?” A-Chu asked.

“Have you red wine?” A-Ci was studying the painting. The bamboo stems and leaves upon it were shaded with nice gradation, the brushwork supple, carrying a delicate, feminine air.

“I have.”

“A glass of red wine, then.”

“Very well.” A-Chu opened the lower compartment of the bookcase, where several bottles were kept, drew one out at random, and poured two glasses. “The colour of this wine suits your mood tonight very well.”

“I don’t consider myself agitated; on the contrary, I consider myself quite calm.”

“Is that so? So calm that you must climb a wall in the dead of night to get here?” A-Chu walked to the scroll of “Green Bamboo, Spring Dawn” and said, “This painting is my wife’s work.” He handed A-Ci a glass. “I know little of painting. In the old days, when I was with the eldest young master of the Rong family, I learned a few strokes from him; but my gifts for calligraphy and painting are only middling, I take as beauty no more than what pleases the eye.”

“Your good lady has an orchid’s heart and a fragrant grace; her talent overflows.”

“A pity she chose me,” A-Chu said, with a complicated feeling within.

“Your good lady?” A-Ci was about to offer the polite request that he might be allowed to pay his respects to her, but the thought that it was now about one in the morning made him draw the words back. He said, roundabout, “I fear I may have disturbed your lady this evening?”

“It’s nothing; she is already asleep,” A-Chu said. “Tell me the purpose of your visit tonight.”

“I have three photographs I should like you to help me examine.” Muci came straight to the point.

A-Chu switched on the desk lamp and signalled A-Ci to produce the photographs. A-Ci took three photographs from his pocket and laid them out in order on the desk. The first was of Xu Yuzhen chasing butterflies in a courtyard of pear blossom, taken in the early spring of 1910; the second was an artistic portrait of “Xu Yuzhen” in a qipao, taken at the Great Shanghai Photo Studio in the summer of 1922; the third was that student photograph of unknown provenance.

“Can you make out anything from them?” A-Ci asked.

“What is your own sense of it?” A-Chu returned.

“One cannot tell the human from the ghost.”

A-Chu studied the photographs closely, several times over, then moved the first one forward and said, “Mother.”

A-Ci stared at him wide-eyed, startled that A-Chu should, before his very face, call his mother “Mother” so plainly and without reserve; A-Ci’s thoughts were thrown into some confusion.

A-Chu pointed at the second photograph and said, “The disguised false ‘mother.’” Pointing at the third, he said, “The false ‘mother’s’ true face.”

“Why are you so certain?”

“The eyes,” A-Chu said. “Look at their eyes, not their faces. Then you will know of themselves which is true and which false. Where did this student photograph come from?”

“From home, the cellar beneath the shrine-hall.”

“You are in danger now,” A-Chu said.

“I mean to know the truth.”

“The truth I know is no more than a scale here and a claw there. Do you understand? What you mean to investigate is an unsolved case of more than twenty years ago, intricate, its roots and branches tangled together. What we lack is a strong living witness to give us the leads of those years, so that we cannot trace the case from beginning to end. Everything I know of the story is arrived at by conjecture.”

“Then tell me the story you know by conjecture, from first to last, just as it is. And I can go on to do…”

“Do what?”

“Check the time and place of the crime, reason out the details, hunt for the offshoots. And if we are lucky, we can play the story through again.”

“You are very sure of yourself.”

“It will take the efforts of us both.”

“What I want to know now is: in your heart, what am I to you?” A-Chu asked in earnest.

“A friend!”

“I am your kin,” A-Chu said, with emphasis.

“Let us be friends first.” A-Ci’s manner was full of sincerity. He raised his glass and said, “I hope that in the years to come we may come to understand each other, and that friendship may ripen by degrees into kinship. I drink first, in respect to you.” When Muci had drained his glass, he turned it mouth-downward.

A-Chu answered him at once, and finished the red wine in his own glass.

Then A-Chu told A-Ci, in full detail, a fairly complete and credible story, the same story he had told Han Zhengqi. Yang Muci’s face showed by turns astonishment, wonder, anger, and a fluttering of the heart. A-Chu saw that his younger brother, before him, hid nothing of what he felt and held back neither joy nor anger, and he felt a heartfelt gladness; they were, after all, of one blood, kin of the same bone and flesh.

“Now that you have heard this strange and tragic story, what have you made of it?” A-Chu asked.

“Mother and Han Zhengqi are the key figures of the story, and the Ciyun Temple is a key place, no, I should say the scene of the crime,” A-Ci said. “All of it was to open the road for a substitution, the plum tree dying in place of the peach.”

“And so?”

“And so we need to go and survey the Ciyun Temple.”

“When?”

“Now.” Muci stood up as he spoke.

“All right.” A-Chu put on his coat and said, “Wait a moment; I’ll make a telephone call.”

A-Chu put through the call, waited a while, and someone answered. A-Chu said, “Amah A-Yue? Tell Rong’er I’ve gone to the Ciyun Temple.” He hung up. “Let’s go,” he said.

Muci stopped short. “I must make a call too.” He dialled straight through to the duty line of the Detective Division at Garrison Command and left a single message for Du Luning: “Teacher, I’ve gone to the Ciyun Temple.”

“Why do you notify the people at the Detective Division?” A-Chu asked.

“For the same reason you notify your people, to guard against the unforeseen,” A-Ci answered with a smile.

The two came out together. Muci got into the jeep; A-Chu, standing at the car door, asked, “Do you know the road to the Ciyun Temple?”

“I know the general direction,” Muci said.

“Take my car.” A-Chu walked straight to his own car, opened the door, and got in. A-Ci had no choice but to follow and take the passenger seat.

The two brothers exchanged a glance, and both, without prompting, felt an inexplicable tenderness, A-Chu especially, who was uncommonly sensitive to nearness and distance in kinship, so much so that being close to Muci made him a little uneasy.

“We are too alike,” Muci said suddenly, out of nowhere.

“Do you remember the first time we met?” A-Chu started the car.

“It gave me a fright at the time,” Muci said. “I thought I’d seen a ghost.”

A-Chu wrenched the wheel hard, and the car took a sharp turn that flung Muci’s body over, tipping and swaying, so that he cried out; then the car was running smoothly again along the cement road, and in the rear-view mirror Muci caught a flicker of a smile passing through A-Chu’s eyes.

The Ciyun Temple was built beside water, amid rank, luxuriant grass, secluded and still. Because the air here was fresh and the incense-fires unbroken, many high officials and men of rank had once loved to come here to escape the summer heat. But some twenty years ago a fire had broken out at the temple; ten or more pilgrims, it was said, were burned to death, and all at once the Ciyun Temple became a place of ill omen, with few pilgrims coming or going. It was precisely by turning people’s dread of the Ciyun Temple to account that Amah A-Yue had, in the beginning, lived hidden here so long.

A-Chu and A-Ci reached the mountain gate of the Ciyun Temple at three in the morning. A-Chu stopped the car before the gate; since it was A-Ci’s first visit to the Ciyun Temple, the moment he got out he began a careful study of the whole layout of the temple.

The mountain gate of the Ciyun Temple was vermilion; below the threshold lay a patch of damp moss. The temple was old, its red-lacquered pillars long unpainted, the lacquer peeling away to bare a wretched ashen face, forlorn to look on. Outside the gate stood a huge and grotesque ancient tree, its branches spreading every way, trunk and leaves alike so dim that their colour seemed unnatural; even the leaves gave off a sense of stiffness.

“Come, let us go into the great hall.” A-Chu patted A-Ci on the shoulder and pushed the door open, going in first.

A-Ci followed behind him, glancing back out of habit to see whether anything stirred outside the temple, then stepped backward a few paces before entering the hall.

In the hall, to their surprise, several oil lamps were burning. The chief adornment within was a wall full of murals of gods and immortals; some of the figures had been gilded with gold leaf. Murals and altar alike, like the gate outside, had had their divine lustre eaten away by the pitiless years, leaving only a mottled, heavy black-grey.

“What do you see?” A-Chu asked.

“This is a dwelling-place of gods, and also a haunt where phantoms move,” Muci said. “Someone was here just now.”

Without meaning to, A-Chu took a step backward.

“No need to be nervous,” A-Ci said.

“It’s you who are too nervous,” A-Chu said.

“What’s this?” A-Ci had found several wooden tablets hanging beneath the drapery of the altar.

“For praying for blessings, I suppose.” A-Chu reached out and tore off one of the wooden tablets to look; on it was written: Expel the Demons.

Muci had, all at once, a sense of ill omen.

A chill wind soughed; the temple’s prayer-banners stretched out their cold corners and began to dance. Alert, Muci pulled A-Chu in close beneath a pillar of the gods, and they heard faint footsteps, slow, as though the feet trod on crusted snow. Looking out through the hall’s carved window, Muci saw clearly a ghostly, loose-haired figure swaying beyond the blackened pane, light and drifting, like a paper garment hung outside the window to lure the passing souls of the dead.

Muci suddenly grew anxious for A-Chu at his side, afraid he might cry out; he turned deliberately to reassure him, only to find him quite composed. A-Chu spoke to Muci with his eyes, and the sense of it was: don’t worry about me, put all your strength into catching the ghost.

The sound of wind came in through the chinks of the window; the oil lamps in the hall swayed beneath it, their flames now small, now large, and under the flickering light the whole hall took on a look of panic, until even the faces of the gods on the murals turned grim and dreadful.

A woman’s sobbing came from deep in the hall; A-Ci’s gaze shifted swiftly from beyond the window to the depths of the shrine. A-Chu and A-Ci fell into a back-to-back stance, and without intending it had formed a single mind to protect each other, while the shrill, bone-piercing weeping wound round the whole shrine and seeped into every corner of the hall.

Yang Muci quickly judged that the weeping came from beneath the altar table; he signalled A-Chu to approach it with him, moved the table aside, crouched down, and listened.

This time, they heard a woman singing…

“There should be a door here,” Muci said low, pressing on the grey bricks laid beneath the altar.

“Perhaps a door, perhaps a trap,” A-Chu warned A-Ci. “There’s someone below, and perhaps someone beyond the window too.”

“The one beyond the window is the bait; the one below is the mechanism,” Muci said, tapping lightly on the grey bricks of the floor.

“You’re so certain?”

“I am a professional.” Muci smiled. He pointed at a smooth, clean grey brick on the floor and said, “This brick is the one to knock on; it is unlike the others, without the least speck of dirt.”

“You’re sure?”

“I’m sure,” Muci said.

“Try it,” A-Chu said.

Muci straightened up and stepped back two or three paces; he looked over the hall to left and right, picked up a long bamboo pole from which a prayer-banner hung, then came back and, testing, struck the “door-knocking brick” hard with one end of the pole. The unforeseen happened: with a rumbling crash, the grey bricks under A-Chu’s and Muci’s feet caved in, and overhead the beam that held the oil lamps snapped across the middle and came straight down at A-Chu’s head; calamity full upon him, and no evading it…
