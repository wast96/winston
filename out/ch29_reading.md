## Chapter 29

The pastor gave his address, and had us sing a hymn for the dead, and pray. Here I saw all the guests who had been at Mrs. Stephen's birthday party.

Accompanying the coffin, we went all the way to the International Cemetery and watched over it until it was buried. Before the cross, we laid our flowers in silence.

So many souls, and only one grief.

The people dispersed one after another, and I dragged myself back with infinite melancholy and heavy steps; I could not free myself from this sorrow and grief. Before my eyes there rose the lively, young Stephen — at the corner of Malang Road, at Dr. Philip's clinic, at the window of my old lodging, on the sofa in my room, in the Liti Café, in the Paramount ballroom, at Mrs. Stephen's birthday party, and in the hotel at Hangzhou — his movements, his talk and laughter, his dancing; and then I saw him lying stiff on the sickbed: tousled hair, a disordered stubble, an iron-gray cheek, deep-purple lips, the mouth clenched shut, the eyes half open — and now he lay long beneath the earth, and hereafter the world would have no more of this liveliness, this laughter, this grace, and the dauntless, heavy work and duty hidden within it.

This soldier for love, for freedom, for ideals and for dreams.

I loved him, I revered him, I mourned him; I had a nagging disquiet and words unspoken; I regretted my going out that day, and regretted the more my going late the next evening. Yet this could never be undone now. With the touch of my fingers I recalled his eyelids, and with the sense of my eyes I recalled the closing of his half-open eyes. I firmly believed this was an expectation and a tacit understanding within our friendship, and again I could not keep back my tears.

At seven o'clock the next morning I went alone to the International Cemetery with a bunch of flowers. A misty fine rain was falling, and the cemetery paths were already wet; head bowed, I walked quietly beneath the locust trees, and where the path turned I raised my head and looked off toward Stephen's grave. I was surprised: so early, and there was already someone paying respects before it.

It was a woman in black, but she did not look like Mrs. Stephen, nor did she seem to be Mei Yingzi. I gazed at her as I slowly drew near, and the more I made sure it was neither of them, the less I could tell who it was. I thought: since Mrs. Stephen was not his real wife, then this must be some true love of his whom I had never met.

I did not disturb her, but came up quietly. She seemed already to have laid her flowers, and stood before the grave with her hands clasped, gravely bowing her head. I fixed my eyes on her from behind and went up, but when I had come to about five or six paces from her, I was startled, and cried out in spite of myself:

"Helen!"

She turned her head and was taken aback; then she leaned against my chest and began to weep.

"Helen!" I patted her on the back, but could find no more to say. She wept the harder.

"Helen!" I stroked her golden hair and said: "The dead are dead; let us who live, live bravely."

She did not answer, and sobbed a long while; when I saw her master herself a little, I pushed her from me and wiped her tears with my handkerchief, and said:

"Take courage, Helen; we must live bravely."

"Yes," she said haltingly, and then wiped her own tears with her handkerchief. I left her to lay my flowers at the head of the grave, and stood before it to bless Stephen. Ten minutes later, when I turned, I found Helen standing gravely beside me. I mused a while, and thinking of some light, casual remark, said with a smile:

"You were even earlier than I."

"I was troubled; I did not sleep the whole night." As she spoke she wept again: "I am wretched! When I think of my socializing every day with Japanese soldiers — think of it — how ashamed and disgraceful I am before this hero who died for his country!"

Helen's words were so far beyond my expectation that they astonished me, and for a moment I could find nothing to answer. I walked at her side, treading the wet path, and felt the nobility of Helen's inmost heart. I recalled Jessfield Park, and how she had wandered alone in the moonlight while I followed behind her — so still, so languid, like a water-lily untouched by dust treading the flowing water, like a fairy treading the drifting clouds, pure and flawless and yet grave and noble. Now I saw again this soul; this sacred soul God had given her together with her beautiful voice, and afterward, adrift in the dusty world, it had lost its dazzling luster, and now in an instant it had come alive again in her heart — it was Stephen's spirit that had wakened her, and brought her back to her former radiance.

"Yesterday I really wanted to kill myself," she said.

"Helen, what talk is this?" For an instant I thought of telling her that all her chances and doings were Mei Yingzi's arranging and contriving, and that all this arranging and contriving was a part of Stephen's work. But what would come of it? With a character like Helen's, she would feel at once that such arranging and contriving was an insult, and it might even, who knows, drive her to abandon herself and go to ruin. And besides, suppose I had the power to explain it all to her in detail, and to make her fundamentally understand this contrivance — would she then go on living her present life as though it were work? Most important of all was Mei Yingzi's judgment, and I had to respect the discipline of the work. I did not speak.

"I am ashamed; I don't know how I could have sunk to this! I want to kill myself!" she said in penitence, drawing close to me. We paced along the little cemetery path. After a long silence I said:

"We've taken the wrong path."

"There's a way round on that side too." Helen looked about and pointed the way for me.

"Then, Helen," I said, "you have only taken a wrong path; what place is there that one cannot get round?"

"Thank you." She let out a beautiful smile, her eyes shooting a strange light, and said: "Then lead me out."

I nodded, but found I could not think of the way.

"As you led me out that night from Scott Road," she said.

"That was Bai Ping's doing."

"You were the one who found it out first."

"Yes," I said. "And now, too, I have only found it out."

"Only when it is just the two of us do I feel that the life I have been living is not the life of my soul."

"That is my honor."

We lingered on in the little path; on the bare, withered locust trees a flock of sparrows chirped, the air was damp, the ground gleaming wet. The fine rain had stopped, and in the east a weak yellow sunlight showed through; a few old women were laying flowers at strange graveheads, devout and lonely. This corner of the world, I thought, could surely have no more strife or envy or resentment with the clamorous world of men; it was a true love that joined all here.

"Let's go back," she said.

I did not answer, but quietly walked out with Helen. We ate breakfast at Jing'an, and in our silence what ran through our hearts was a transparent understanding and sympathy.

At the table Helen suddenly broke the silence, and said:

"How do you wish me to live now?"

"Faithfully," I said. "We can only live faithfully and bravely."

She said nothing, and at the corners of her mouth there rose a dim, faint smile; this smile was the one that belonged to her soul — it had once stirred many imaginings in me, but since she had learned the fashionable manner of smiling, I had actually forgotten that she had once left me this particular, true smile. This smile showed that she had wholly awakened, had seen through the forms of life to its content. I said:

"We must laugh faithfully, weep faithfully, sing faithfully, sigh faithfully..."

"Then you think all my past was unfaithful."

"Only the smile," I said.

"The smile?"

"Yes," I said. "I believe every person ought to have their own way of smiling; but the smiles of today seem to have formed into schools — everyone learns from one another and promotes it, so that the manner of smiling too has become a fashionable adornment."

"Perhaps that is the power of American films," she said.

"Films ought to learn from real life, but now the people in real life are learning from films."

"I think that is the progress of mankind," she said. "The laughter in films is made by refining the beauties of society's smiles and cutting out their ugliness."

"I think that's true, but everyone vies to imitate it, and the upshot is that each person's own peculiar beauty is lost."

She laughed again. Perhaps it was a lovely bit of screen, but it was not Helen's own beauty. Unconsciously I laughed out loud.

She seemed to know what I was laughing at, and was a little abashed. And in a moment of reserve, she unwittingly let out again that dim, faint smile of hers.

Now I saw it clearly: perhaps only an infant's smile is the voice of angels, and so in many sacred paintings the Madonna is forever grave and silent, while the countless little cherubs all wear the smiles of infants.

I saw her home at six o'clock, and after that did not see her for several days. But of a sudden I received at home an invitation to a Christmas evening party, put forward in the name of Rear Admiral Umetake of the Japanese Navy Ministry. I had never met Umetake, and this naturally made me think of what Helen had said that day, and I concluded that it was Helen who had given them the direction.

And so one evening at dusk I went to her home.

Her home was arranged as before, but Helen's dress and manner had changed entirely. Her hair was combed smoothly and hung down behind, without a trace of oil or the constraint of hairpins, tied lightly behind with a woolen band, and this band was of the same material as her dress — a small blue-and-white check on a white ground; her powder and eyebrow-paint were quite gone, and I saw a few faint freckles beside her nose. On her person, apart from a belt of yellow patent leather, there was no other adornment. The soft stuff of her dress floated freely as she walked, and this change was like classical ballet liberated by Isadora Duncan; I felt she had become natural and young. She seemed to have recovered the impression she had left on me when I first met her, but she had none of her former shyness and tenderness; grave, calm, and easy, she bade me sit down with a bearing like Mrs. Stephen's, so indifferent that she seemed to have lost all feeling, her eyes forever avoiding my gaze, not a trace of expression on her face. I could not find in her a single link with the remorse of that day at the cemetery, or the grief of that day at Scott Road. I said:

"Well, then? Any changes?"

I kept my eyes from Helen, to let her answer more naturally. Suddenly I saw the photograph on the wall: it had been changed to a group portrait of her father, her brother, and the two women, mother and daughter — three seated, two standing. I meant to ask, but —

"Life," she said. "I mean to be faithful and brave."

This brought me back to my mood of that day at the cemetery, and I said, quiet and calm: "You have given up your social round."

"Not only the social round," she answered calmly, "but I have given up my post as well."

I was not surprised, for this was an expression of the special quality in Helen's character, a character I understood. I gave a faint sigh, and then everyone was silent. In that very silence I suddenly recalled the purpose of my coming, and drew the invitation from my inner pocket and handed it to her, saying:

"Then why have them send this to me still?"

She frowned slightly, and then, with a sudden dawning of ease, took the invitation in surprise, gave it a cold glance, and said slowly:

"It has nothing to do with me."

I knew then that I had mistaken it: the sending of this card could be Mei Yingzi's doing, or Bai Ping's doing, or the doing of any of the Japanese; only because Helen had spoken of it to me had I concluded it was she. I said:

"Then they must have sent it themselves; didn't you receive one?"

"They sent one; I told them I was going to Beiping, and returned it."

"So of course you're not preparing to attend."

"I shall attend no engagement whatever."

"Living quietly retired, cultivating your nature?" As I spoke I saw a few books lying in disorder on the piano, and asked: "Reading?"

"Yes," she said. "Lend me a few more books another day."

"And singing?"

"Yes."

"Practicing?"

"Yes," she said. "To enrich my own life."

"To enrich your life." These words made me realize all at once the change in Helen's life; this was Mrs. Stephen's way on the surface, the heart of a lovely recluse. She read, she sang, she played the piano, but not for the pursuit of truth and art, nor as a refuge for her anguish, still less lured by vanity, but for life, for the enriching of life. It seemed she had wholly awakened from the clamorous, disordered life, come to her senses out of the striving, struggling life, and been disillusioned of her countless ardent pursuits. She had come to taste the savor of quiet and plainness, the serenity of stillness; she had given her life over to nature, as fallen blossoms are given to the flowing water, as the planets are given to the void of space. The world held no more hope for her, nothing among all things was strange to her; this state of heart was perhaps a beautiful one, but was it one that ought to belong to a woman of her years?

I was silent, silent as a fish.

Clouds drove past outside the window, a light breeze stirred the window-gauze; Helen set the curtain straight, and drifting lightly to the piano, played on the keys, dim and quiet, like an Italian nocturne, and made me aware that dusk had already seeped through the window-lattice.

When the music stopped I said:

"Thank you for the beautiful nocturne you have given me." I stood up to take my leave, went before her seat, and said uneasily:

"Forgive me for saying a commonplace thing. If you should need my help, please treat me as your good friend and do not stand on ceremony."

"I thank you for your pure and beautiful friendship," she said, raising her head. "Won't you wait for my mother to come home?"

"Your mother?"

"She works now at the Huimei Hotel."

"I'll come again another day to see her."

Helen saw me out, and at the door she said:

"Thank you for your concern for us, and thank you for coming to see us."

"Thank you for the beautiful nocturne you have given me," I said. "Tonight I shall pray for you devoutly."

On the way back it struck me all at once: today Helen had not let slip a single smile.
