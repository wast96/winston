### The Sixteenmo Magazines

Sixteenmo bound periodicals are once again all the rage, and their number has already passed a hundred titles. When people speak of the state of the press, they are sure to say how very many tabloids there are; but for all their number, the tabloids run only to a few dozen — how should they match the sixteenmo magazines for multitude and vigor?

Among these, one hundred titles and more, the two of longest standing are the weekly Libai Liu and the weekly Shenghuo. The Libai Liu began in the twelfth year of the Republic, and by now is a full twelve years old; the Shenghuo was started in the fourteenth year, and has also been going several years. But when the Libai Liu and the Shenghuo first appeared, both were quarto loose sheets, like the tabloids in vogue today, and it was only after many years that they changed to the bound sixteenmo form.

Speaking of all these many magazines, those that are truly of no party and no faction, and that speak for the common people, are in fact very few; nearly all have some backing behind them — backed by politics, or by religion, or by this faction or that clique. There are even those that champion a single man and serve as his gramophone. But any magazine with a backer is bound to be short-lived, and so, of all these many titles, apart from the Libai Liu and the Shenghuo, most spring up and die away, and very few last beyond two or three years. (The Shenghuo Weekly, too, has now suspended publication for reasons of its own.)

As for how often they come out, there are three-day papers, weeklies, ten-day papers, fortnightlies and the like, but the weekly, coming out once in seven days, is by far the commonest. Because they are made by taking a single newspaper sheet and folding it down into sixteenmo, readers generally call them sixteenmo magazines. The newly issued ten-day paper the Shiritan uses instead an octavo binding, with an unusually large page — it is run, I hear, by Shao Xunmei and others — their notion being merely to show themselves a little different from the sixteenmo kind; later it too changed to sixteenmo, and now it too has suspended for reasons of its own. Through the whole of the year 1934 this sort of magazine flourished mightily, so that people dubbed it the "Year of the Magazine," which is an apt description indeed.
