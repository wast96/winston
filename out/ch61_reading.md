## The Seizure of Matsumae Castle

It was on the twentieth of the tenth month of the year Boshin that the fleet slid into the Funka Bay of Hokkaidō.

There is a fishing village called Washinoki. Off its shore the ships, one after another, let go their anchors. From this moment there would begin, in the history of the year Boshin, an affair to shake the realm.

Toshizō stood on the Kaiyō's deck. Before his eyes the hills and fields he was to go ashore upon spread out, blanketed in snow.

Already, with Enomoto, Matsudaira, and Ōtori, the plan of operations after the landing had been settled.

They would divide into two corps and attack Hakodate. The commander of the main body was Ōtori Keisuke; the commander of the flanking corps, Hijikata Toshizō.

"Hijikata — we were both of us born in Bushū, and here we have come to a preposterous place. But think of it all as the soil of our own government, and it grows dear,"

{j} said Enomoto Takeaki, coming up to Toshizō's side.

Toshizō was peering through a telescope.

(Ho — there are houses.)

And a hundred and forty or fifty of them, no less. This astonished him.

"Enomoto, there are houses."

"Indeed, I am astonished too. I had heard people lived at Washinoki, but I supposed they would be Ezo natives; the ways of the world are past knowing."

When they went ashore, they found, just as at a post-town on the Tōkaidō, a proper honjin and all, and were the more astonished when the master came out to meet them in crested formal robes and Sendai-hira silk.

More astonishing still, this honjin house was of Japanese build, with as many as seven or eight rooms, and even a raised dais-room for the receiving of great persons.

Even Enomoto was astonished.

"It is no different from Japan."

For a man like Enomoto, who said he had come to Matsumae once, at about eighteen, to be so struck — Toshizō, Ōtori, and Matsudaira were fairly dumbfounded.

"Hijikata, I had thought it wilder country than this,"

{j} said Matsudaira Tarō.

"Not at all — come to think of it, the Matsumae domain has struck root here these several hundred years. Still, it is astonishing."

The young Matsudaira was all smiles.

The next day they set out for Hakodate.

Ōtori's army had for its mainstay the old shogunal infantry, with the Yūgekitai, and took under its wing, for close combat, the Shinsengumi (the Shinsentai). This was by Toshizō's arrangement.

"The Shinsengumi belong to the new government; they are no private army of mine."

Toshizō's own army, on the other hand, was a wholly Western-style force, so that they had, as it were, traded troops.

Straight south from Washinoki it is ten ri to Hakodate. This way Ōtori's army took.

Hijikata was to go the long way around by the coast, crossing snow-laden mountains from Kakkumi to Yunokawa, and strike Hakodate from the east.

At Hakodate there was a government tribunal (the administrative office) headed by the noble Shimizudani Kinnaru, seconded by one Chōshū samurai and one Satsuma samurai; and quartered as a defense force, as the government army, were domain troops of Matsumae, Tsugaru, Nanbu, Akita, and the rest.

The armies of Ōtori and Hijikata broke these in place after place, and Shimizudani Kinnaru fled to Aomori.

The occupation of Hakodate was completed on the first of the eleventh month, some ten days after the landing.

The Enomoto army raised the shogunal war-flag, the Rising Sun, within and without the town of Hakodate, and from the warships that had come into the harbor fired a salute of twenty-one guns apiece, proclaiming this occupation to Japanese and foreigner alike.

Its seat of government they set in the former Hakodate magistracy at Motomachi, making Nagai Genba-no-kami Naomune the "mayor"; and the Enomoto army's military headquarters they fixed at the Western-style fortress the old shogunate had built at Kameda, in Hakodate's northern outskirts — the "Goryōkaku."

Taking the occasion of the fall of Hakodate, the Enomoto army meant to hold a celebration, inviting the consuls of the various foreign powers who kept legations in the town; but the Matsumae domain, the one domain in all Hokkaidō, still held its castle-seat twenty-five ri west of Hakodate with its domain troops and would not yet "surrender."

"Hijikata is a master of castle-taking,"

{j} said Matsudaira Tarō at the council of war.

Toshizō held his peace. He must have meant the reduction of Utsunomiya Castle.

"A hard task, but will you go?"

{j} said Ōtori Keisuke as well. Ōtori did not care for Toshizō, but unless the Matsumae domain were brought down, it became a question of their standing with the foreign legations.

At this time Toshizō had only just finished the fighting of the twenty ri from Washinoki to Hakodate, and his men had scarcely rested.

"The sooner it falls, the better,"

{j} said Enomoto too. Enomoto saw this as a political war. By the swiftness of this reduction, the trust of the foreign legations and trading houses in the Hakodate regime was bound to deepen.

"...In that case,"

{j} the one unlettered man among the commanders of the new regime nodded, sour-faced.

He was satisfied.

His comrades were almost all scholars of Western learning. Each had a deep grounding in the Chinese classics too, and as occasion arose would compose Chinese verse, or talk of Dutch and French studies; but into such idle talk Toshizō could not enter.

Skill at fighting, he believed, was the one and only meaning of his existence.

"I will go,"

{j} Toshizō nodded.

Toshizō set out at the head of seven hundred men — the Shinsengumi, shogunal infantry, the Gakuheitai (the Western-style corps of the Sendai domain), and deserters from the Shōgitai among them.

The Matsumae domain was, among the three hundred lords, the one domain that held no rice-assessed fief. Its economy was met by the produce of Hokkaidō.

The former lord, Matsumae Takahiro, was a man of parts enough to have become the shogunate's commissioner of shrines and temples, commissioner of sea and land forces, and even a senior councillor; but he was now dead of illness.

The lord of the day was Norihiro, the eighteenth of the line. Sickly, without the strength to see to the domain's affairs, he had by now been ridden over by the loyalist party within the domain, who kept the throne empty within the castle. Yet, for all that, they were setting out to crush a whole domain; and whether seven hundred men were enough for it was doubted even by the Enomoto army's French advisers.

Small domain though it was, the castle was a considerable one.

It was a newly built castle, only just completed in Ansei 2 (now a national treasure): an area of 21,374 tsubo, the keep of three storys, roofed with copper, its walls done in white plaster; and, being a new castle raised after Perry's coming, it mounted a gun-battery on the castle's south face, toward the sea.

"Well, they will be enough,"

{j} said Toshizō. At Toba-Fushimi they had lost to the Minié rifles of Satsuma and Chōshū, but this time it was our side that held the Minié rifles, while the foe had only matchlocks, and Gebel guns scarcely better than them.

If anything, it was the march through the snow that plagued them.

As far as Tōbetsu, Kikonai, Shiriuchi, and the Shiriuchi pass they could billet in people's houses, but the day after that they bivouacked.

"Keep the fires blazing,"

{j} was the only order to give; there was no other way of bivouacking.

Toshizō too pulled his greatcoat over him and lay down beside a great bonfire, but the snow beneath him melted and, far from warming him, left his body as if freezing to the ground, and he thought this was past bearing.

In the dead of night he roused the whole army, and

{j} "Think that there is nowhere to sleep but by taking the enemy's ground,"

{j} began a night march.

The enemy's first line was at the harbor town of Fukushima, a place of a thousand souls; by the scouts' report the garrison was three hundred.

The whole army, of one mind to sleep, fell upon the town, and after a fierce fight took it; but the Matsumae troops, knowing the hardship of a bivouac in the snow, set fire to the town and withdrew.

That night they slept among the burnt ruins, but in the small hours the wind and snow grew savage, and again there was no bivouacking.

"Up,"

{j} Toshizō roused the men in the night, and

{j} "Fix your beds at Matsumae Castle. Take it that it is either take the castle or freeze to death, one or the other."

They all marched half-fainting.

At last Toshizō and his men came out onto high ground from which they could see the keep of Matsumae Castle.

Toshizō first chose a small hill, Hokkeji-yama, six or seven chō off from the castle, set two four-kin mountain guns there, and had them open fire on the castle.

The enemy too shifted the mountings of the twelve-kin cannon at their Tsukishima battery on the castle's south and fired back, and it became an artillery duel.

Keeping the gunners at their covering fire, Toshizō set the Shōgitai and the Shinsengumi against the main gate, and gave the Western-style corps, the infantry and the Gakuheitai, the assault on the rear gate.

He himself took command from the saddle.

The castle had a mountain, Jizō-yama, at its back, and around its front a river thirty ken wide.

They came to the riverbank.

The enemy fired briskly from across the water and from within the castle. But the Gebel gun, with its flintlock firing-piece, was slow to work and wretchedly poor at hitting the mark.

"That is all noise. I learned it at Fushimi,"

{j} Toshizō laughed.

"Think of it as fireworks going up. Jump into the river,"

{j} and kicking his horse's flanks he rode into the current himself.

The Shōgitai went foremost.

The Shinsengumi came on a little behind them, downstream.

(Tch.)

Toshizō was on tenterhooks, but for the command of the whole army he could not rally the Shinsengumi alone, and it chafed him.

"Ichimura Tetsunosuke,"

{j} he called his page to his horse's side, and

{j} ──Tell Saitō. Say to him: remember Kyoto.

Ichimura, running along the sandbars, crossing the shallows, and now and then swimming through the deep current, came up to the Shinsengumi commander, Dakusai — Saitō Hajime — and when he told him this,

{j} "You cannot be serious,"

{j} Saitō bawled in the rain of bullets.

"Even in the Kyoto days I never once swam the Kamo. Tell that to the man. I never thought I should be swimming rivers in a Hokkaidō winter."

The whole army swarmed up onto the far bank.

Once the hand-to-hand fighting began, over the knot of the Shinsengumi there seemed always to hang a mist of blood; they were the strongest of all.

With the Shōgitai they drove the enemy to the main gate, but the retreating foe shut the great gate fast at last.

"This will not do."

With the sword, there was nothing to be done against a gate studded with iron rivets.

Before that gate Saitō Hajime took counsel with Shibusawa Seiichirō and Terasawa Shintarō of the Shōgitai, and,

{j} "Unless we go round to the rear gate, there is no fighting to be done,"

{j} changing on his own the disposition Toshizō had fixed, they broke into a run.

On the way they met Toshizō on horseback.

"You two corps — what are you about?"

{j} Toshizō bawled, and Saitō Hajime, running past his side, told him the reason in a rush.

"Just so — the gate will not be broken. I too shall go to the rear gate. Every man of you, come after me,"

{j} and he set off at a run below the castle wall.

Musket-balls were fired down from the wall, but, pitiably, they did not hit.

There the enemy were taking a curious tactic.

Inside the castle gate they had set two guns side by side; loading them, they would fling the gate open, fire both at once, and close the gate again.

At this the Gakuheitai and the infantry were baffled, and lay here and there, barely guarding themselves from the bursting of the shells.

Toshizō rode into the skirmish line and called for the Gakuheitai's commander, Hoshi Juntarō.

Hoshi wore the gaudy Gakuheitai uniform, of bright-red wool worked with gold thread.

"That gate — how many times has it opened now?"

"The fourth time."

"From opening to opening, how long does it take?"

"Let me see — about as long as one might draw twenty breaths, perhaps."

"Then lend me twenty riflemen. The rest of you make ready to charge."

Presently the gate opened for the fifth time, the two guns spat fire together, and blew away eight infantrymen who stood behind Toshizō.

At once the gate closed. Only the smoke of the discharge remained.

"Come on,"

{j} and Toshizō ran with his twenty riflemen, closed to just before the rear gate, and had them take a standing-fire posture.

"The instant the gate opens, all fire together, aiming at the gunners."

Their comrades to the rear, flat to the ground, held their breath to a man.

If the guns fired the sooner, the twenty men, Toshizō among them, would be smashed to splinters.

Presently the gate opened.

Out poked the two guns.

And in that same instant twenty muskets spat fire and dropped the Matsumae gunners at the guns' side, one after another.

"Cut your way in,"

{j} and the first to spring in was Terasawa Shintarō of the Shōgitai, then Saitō Hajime, Matsumoto Sutesuke, and Nomura Risaburō of the Shinsengumi.

By then the shells our own gunners had fired from Hokkeji-yama were beginning to set fires within the castle.

The whole army poured in. The domain troops abandoned the castle and fled in rout to Esashi.

Toshizō ought to have ordered a pursuit, but they had taken Matsumae Castle in order to sleep.

"Sleep,"

{j} he ordered.

Having ordered it, he took only the Shinsengumi, made himself the scout, and began to climb the byroad at Ōno-guchi that led toward Esashi.

Going two chō or so up the mountain path, there was a woodcutter's hut, and there — why they had come was a puzzle — old shogunal infantry had already arrived before him.

Seeing Toshizō, they were thrown into confusion.

"What is this?"

{j} he asked, and it seemed they were after some women who had fled the fallen castle.

Toshizō stepped into the earth-floored room of the hut. There five girls in the manner of palace maids were guarding a young woman who looked to be an invalid, each gripping a dagger with a terrible look on her face.

Toshizō gave his name and rank, said he meant no harm, and asked them to tell him how the case stood.

"Lord Hijikata Toshizō?"

The women all knew the name of this samurai famed in Kyoto. But by what impression that name stood recorded in them was hard to tell.

The woman in the middle was with child. Barely past twenty, no beauty, but with an air of breeding.

"Tell him my name,"

{j} she said to the maids.

She was the principal wife of the Matsumae lord, Matsumae Shima-no-kami Norihiro.

Here Toshizō did a thing very ill-suited to such a man, a thing full of human feeling.

"Lord Shima-no-kami should be at Esashi, I take it,"

{j} he said. Already, by a spy's report, he had heard that just before the assault he had gone off to Esashi; why the pregnant lady of the lord alone had been left behind, the whole of that he did not well understand.

"I will have my troopers see you as far as Esashi,"

{j} he said.

For those troopers Toshizō, on a snap judgment, named names.

Saitō Hajime and Matsumoto Sutesuke,

{j} the two of them. And were not both of them commanders of the Shinsengumi (the Shinsentai)?

And moreover,

{j} "Attend her all the way to Edo,"

{j} he ordered.

"Hijikata — are you in your senses?"

{j} Saitō knit his brows.

"In my senses."

"I refuse. You and I have gone on together since the founding of the Shinsengumi. Just when Hokkaidō is only now beginning, I will have none of this going to Edo."

"Once you reach Edo, go home to your own country."

"..."

Saitō and Matsumoto were more astonished than ever.

Toshizō glared them down with a fearful face, and

{j} "Have you forgotten the law of the Shinsengumi — that he who defies a corps order is cut down?"

{j} brooking no refusal, he had them consent; then, calling up the corps' baggage-master, he gave them a parting gift.

But there was a difference in the parting gifts: for Matsumoto Sutesuke, ten ryō; for Saitō Hajime, thirty.

Asked the reason, the difference had a reason of Toshizō's own. Both were from Minamitama district (Saitō was the son of a rōnin of Akashi in Banshū), but Saitō had no family in his home country; Sutesuke had both parents living and a house and fields besides.

"That is why,"

{j} Toshizō said, and no more.

The two slipped away from Hokkaidō by Esashi and lived on, thereafter, to about the end of the Meiji years. To make them live was the chief reason for the parting Toshizō forced upon them.

──He was an odd man.

{j} So, even into his old age, Yamaguchi Gorō (as Saitō Hajime renamed himself) would speak of Toshizō.
