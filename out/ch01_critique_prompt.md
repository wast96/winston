CHAPTER UNDER REVIEW

Chapter 1. "The Gun" and "The Knife": Terror and Repression Breed a Special Organization

In the modern world, no organ is more dangerous or more shrouded in mystery than the intelligence and security service. America's CIA, the Soviet Union's KGB, Britain's MI5, Israel's Mossad: these are the perennial objects of the world's curiosity.

China's own are a stranger story.

When Chinese people bring up this trade, their first reaction is a startled cry: "*Tewu*!"

This is because the Nationalists' Juntong agents had so fearsome a name. Out of power, the Communist Party denounced the Nationalists for running a "rule by secret police"; in power, it made "catching secret agents" a central task of consolidating its hold. And so this word, *tewu*, came to seem a term of abuse.

But definitions are always a matter of form; it is the real thing that has substance. To talk about secret agents, one should first find China's earliest "secret-service" organization.

1. Modern China's First "Secret Service" Organization

Search for the "secret service," search high and low, and you find it not among the Nationalists but among the Communists. On the record, in China's intelligence and security world, the first appearance of the word *tewu*, and the first organization actually called *tewu*, both trace back to the Chinese Communist Party Central Military Commission's "Special Work Section" of May 1927.

Let us look at Zhou Enlai's calendar for 1927.

On March 21, the CCP launched the third armed uprising of Shanghai's workers, with Zhou Enlai as commander-in-chief.

On April 12, Chiang Kai-shek engineered a coup and struck at the workers' pickets; Zhou Enlai, going in person to negotiate, was seized by Nationalist troops and got free only through the efforts of comrades.

In the second half of May, Zhou slipped away to Wuhan, attended a meeting of the Standing Committee of the CCP Central Political Bureau, was appointed head of the Central Military Department, and in the same period set up the Military Commission's "Special Work Section."

On July 26, Zhou hurried to Jiujiang in Jiangxi to launch the Nanchang Uprising of August 1.

In early and mid-October, Zhou led the uprising's forces in a fighting march into Guangdong, met with reverses, lost contact with the main body, fell gravely ill, and reached Hong Kong by small boat.

In early November, Zhou reached Shanghai and attended an enlarged meeting of the provisional Central Political Bureau. At year's end, he founded the intelligence and security organization known as the Central Special Branch.

The year 1927 was, for the young Chinese Communist Party, a blow struck full in the face. The Nationalists' massacres taught the Communists a lesson: in China, with no arms of your own, you have no right to live. At the August 7 Conference, Mao Zedong spoke a line that shook the world: "Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun!"

The CCP's "gun" was Zhou Enlai's charge, as head of the Central Military Department. Even as he threw himself into military work, Zhou was secretly building an intelligence and security system as well. In the CCP's own language, the army was the "gun" and the security apparatus the "knife": Zhou Enlai raised a gun in one hand and gripped a knife in the other.

Open military work and secret intelligence-and-security work, the one in the light and the other in the dark, were both matters of the first importance to survival.

Modern China's first intelligence and security organ, then, was cut into being by the terror of the ruling Nationalists.

The newly formed Central Military Commission was a lean body: Zhou Enlai its secretary, Wang Yifei its secretary-general, Nie Rongzhen its chief of staff. Its offices stood at No. 12 Yujili in Hankou, a *shikumen* house of three rooms above and three below with two wings: the organization section in the lower west wing, the secretariat in the upper west wing, Zhou Enlai working in the upstairs parlor; the special-service section was right next door in the east wing, under Gu Shunzhang.

The Special Work Section was divided into four subsections.

The intelligence subsection was to gather military and political intelligence and follow the enemy's movements. Its chief was Dong Xingwu, nicknamed Fatty Dong, at the time the commander of the Nationalist government's detective brigade for the three towns of Wuhan. Intelligence work was easy in those days: Wang Jingwei's Wuhan government was still cooperating with the Communists, and even the Wuhan police chief, Wu Defeng, was a Party member.

The security subsection was to protect the central organs and the Soviet advisory group; its chief was Li Jianru. When Borodin, head of the Soviet advisers, was forced to return home, the security subsection detailed thirty men to see him off, and once the job was done they stayed on in the Soviet Union to study security work.

The special-operations subsection was to punish traitors and spies and to handle special tasks assigned by the Center; its chief was Li Qiang, with only a handful of members, Cai Fei, Chen Liansheng, Wang Zhuqiao, and a few others. This action unit had once put to death a British spy who tried to get close to the Soviet advisory group, and had wounded a mole planted inside the group, Eugene Pick.

The bandit-liaison subsection had the task of bringing armed bandit bands over to the Party; its chief was a man they called Huzi, "Whiskers."

In its earliest days the Special Work Section was intensely busy, and it turned up no small amount of important intelligence.

A young fellow named Yang Gongsu, sent out to scout, confirmed that Xia Douyin's troops were massing at Yichang. And so, before Xia Douyin raised his Yichang mutiny and marched on Wuhan on May 17, the Communists had already made ready. When Xu Kexiang mutinied at Changsha on May 21, and when Wang Jingwei broke with the Communists at Wuhan on July 14, the Special Work Section got word of it in good time. During the Nanchang Uprising the section also worked closely with the troops to tear up the Guangzhou-Hankou Railway, and it secretly delivered the rifles of the departing Soviet advisers to the 24th Division, commanded by Ye Ting. Through merchants it even ran a little financial speculation, trading in treasury bonds, banknotes, and silver dollars, to earn secret operating funds for the Center.

This was modern China's earliest "secret-service" organization.

The Communists set up a secret-service organization before the Nationalists did because the CCP had long been outlawed, working underground, and had to guard its own safety with the greatest care.

From the very day of its founding the CCP took security work seriously; one might say that "where there is a Party, there is intelligence and security work." The First National Congress of July 1921 met under strict secrecy, was interrupted mid-session by a patrolman's inspection, and had to shift from the streets of Shanghai to a pleasure boat on South Lake at Jiaxing, in Zhejiang. The Party Program adopted at that First Congress laid it down: "While the Party is in a state of secrecy, its important positions and the identities of its members must be kept secret." In the First United Front of 1924, many members' identities became public: Yang Yin, of the Guangdong District Committee, served as an adviser to the Guangzhou municipal police, investigated the assassination of Liao Zhongkai, and in 1926 took charge of security for the Nationalists' Second National Congress, choosing men from the Guangzhou workers' pickets and the Nanhai peasant army to form a security battalion. The great strikes the CCP led — on the Beijing-Hankou Railway, at the Anyuan mines, at the five Kailuan collieries, the Canton-Hong Kong strike — all set up their pickets, inspection squads, reconnaissance squads, investigation squads, and other security bodies; and the peasant movements in Hubei, Hunan, and elsewhere set up peasant self-defense corps far and wide to keep order in the countryside.

Still, these early security measures and security bodies had not yet grown into a tight working system. In its underground period the CCP had called this, among themselves, "secret work." Later, once there were rural base areas — the "Soviet areas," meaning the Soviet-held regions — security work set up open organs, and the work in the areas outside came to be called "White-area work" or "urban work." In 1925 the CCP Center picked three men, Gu Shunzhang, Chen Geng, and Lu Liu, and sent them to the Soviet Union to study intelligence and security work in earnest, in preparation for founding an intelligence and security system of the Party's own. Not until the Central Military Commission's "Special Work Section" was formed in 1927 was there a dedicated organ.

The word *tewu* is plainly a modern coinage. In the *Ci Yuan*, the dictionary of classical words, the term *tewu* is simply not to be found. The nearest word in meaning is *jiandie*, "spy," glossed as "to scout the enemy's affairs in secret." For an example, the *Records of the Grand Historian* says of the general Li Mu's campaigns that he "trained in riding and archery, kept careful watch by the beacon-fires, made much use of spies, and treated his soldiers generously." There is another word too, *jianren*, meaning "scout, spy"; and Sun Tzu's *Art of War* has its chapter "On the Use of Spies."

The 1979 edition of the *Ci Hai* defines *tewu* this way: "A person who joins a special-service organization, or accepts a task from a special-service organ, to carry out counter-revolutionary activities such as spying out intelligence, covert injury, sabotage, and the spreading of rumor and incitement." This definition hands the *tewu* over wholly to the enemy. In truth the name *tewu* carried no shameful sense at first. In January 1937 the Northwest Political Security Bureau issued its "Points for Special-Service Personnel," which called a leader's bodyguard a "special-service man" — a thoroughly honorable post.

The 1990 *Grand Dictionary of Language* gives four senses of *tewu*, including the English meanings: "1. (special task or duties) In an army, one charged with special duties such as guard or transport, as in special-duty man, special-duty company, special-duty battalion. 2. (special agent) Personnel engaged in espionage. 3. (spy) A spy: personnel that one hostile side sends into the other to gather intelligence and carry out sabotage. 4. (stooge) A subversive agent who serves one government against another." This definition makes *tewu* a neutral word, covering our side and the enemy's alike, and every kind of agent. It does leave out one reconnaissance role in the military category, though: an army's special-duty company always contains a reconnaissance platoon.

A 1996 book, *China's Secret Agents*, held that all these definitions were unscientific, and redefined the term: "Personnel who, for the benefit of a state, a class, or a political group, and on the appointment of an organization or a leader, quietly carry on intelligence work, or operational work such as arrest, assassination, and sabotage, and other secret work to safeguard the interests of their own country, class, or political group." The business of a secret agent, it said, comprises intelligence work, operational work, turning-and-subversion, and psychological warfare.

This *tewu* really is "special," then: no definition, however framed, comes out quite rigorous or quite complete.

So why did both sides in China, Nationalist and Communist, come to call their own intelligence and security organs "special service"? In the Soviet Union, an "All-Russia Extraordinary Commission" was set up in 1917 and renamed the "State Political Security Bureau" in 1922, whose organ was known for short as the GPU. In America, the Justice Department set up a Bureau of Investigation in 1907, enlarged in 1924 into the "Federal Bureau of Investigation." Neither in the Soviet Union nor in America did the government's intelligence and security organs use the name "special service."

Military intelligence organs, by contrast, have always been called "the Second." The U.S. Army's intelligence bureau is abbreviated Army G-2, the Air Force's A-2. In 1906 the Qing government set up a "Military Consultative Office" with powers like those of a general staff, and under it seven bureaus, the "Second Bureau" charged specially with foreign intelligence and espionage. The Nationalists had their "Second Bureau of the General Staff Headquarters," the CCP Central Military Commission likewise had a "Second Bureau," and the general staff too had a "Second Department." The senior man is always the operations department; for the intelligence department to rank second is distinction enough. The "special-duty companies" and "special-duty battalions" that take on special tasks in an army include reconnaissance platoons, and those belong to this "Second Department."

Seen this way, the name "special service" that the Chinese Communists first gave their intelligence and security organ in fact came out of military organization. It is worth noting that the Japanese army kept a dedicated "Special Service Department" whose very duty was intelligence and security work. The Chinese, learning modern science and technology, often took their translations of Western terms secondhand from the Japanese; and the name *tewu* may well have reached them by that route.

China's earliest secret-service organization in fact lasted only three months. After Wang Jingwei broke with the Communists at Wuhan, the CCP Center moved to Shanghai, and the Military Commission's "Special Work Section" wound up its work in August 1927. Yet the closing of this "section" did not mean the end of "special work." Before long the CCP set up another secret-service organization, at a higher level still.
2. The Central Special Branch

Within the CCP's intelligence and security system, the name "Central Special Branch" rings loud.

The Central Special Branch was founded in Shanghai, the birthplace of the Chinese Communist Party. Shanghai set no end of Chinese records: the largest city, the widest foreign concessions, the greatest press of citizens — and the most tangled, most ferocious spy war of all. The National Government's Songhu Garrison Command and the Shanghai police; the patrol houses and paid informers of the British and French concessions; the Green Gang and Red Gang of the lane-ways and the wharves; the Comintern's intelligence stations and the CCP Special Branch's dog-beating squad — Shanghai was, by common consent the world over, an "adventurers' paradise."

When the First United Front broke apart and the Communists went underground once more, the central organs moved back from Guangdong to Shanghai. In November 1927 Zhou Enlai returned to Shanghai from the East River front and was elected to the Standing Committee of the provisional Central Political Bureau. At his proposal, the Central Special Branch was set up in Shanghai that same month. In October 1928 the CCP Center also formed a three-man Central Special Committee headed by Zhou Enlai — the other two members being Xiang Zhongfa, the general secretary, and Gu Shunzhang, the Special Branch's working head — to direct the Branch's work at first hand.

If the earlier Military Commission "Special Work Section" had been a short-lived, makeshift body, this "Central Special Branch" was now a professional CCP intelligence and security organ.

The Nationalists set up a secret-service organization of their own only a step behind, a matter of a little time between them.

In February 1928, three months after the founding of the Central Special Branch, the Nationalists' Central Organization Department set up an Investigation Section under Chen Lifu, dedicated to hunting down and killing Communists. In the summer of 1930 the Investigation Section added a "special-service group" within it, aimed squarely at CCP activity.

In March 1932, Whampoa cadets close to Chiang Kai-shek, modeling themselves on Italy's Fascists and Germany's Brown Shirts, organized a secret society in China, the "Chinese Nation Revival Society"; its members dressed in blue, and it was also called the "Blue Shirts Society." Chiang, who took pains to cultivate his intimates and chose the closest of the close, set up within the Revival Society a special "Special Services Department." That department was made up of ten Whampoa men, with Dai Li as its head. Its tasks were intelligence, turning-and-subversion, and operations — precisely the standard functions of a secret service. April 1, the day it was founded, later became the "April 1 anniversary" of the Nationalist secret-service organization Juntong; every year on that day Chiang Kai-shek would attend the commemoration in person.

And so the Nationalists too had an organization called a "special service." As it happened, both of China's parties, Nationalist and Communist, gave their intelligence and security organs the very same name: "special service."

Small and half-formed as these early secret-service bodies were, they were the forerunners of the vast organizations that would later shape China's politics. The "Investigation Section" grew into the notorious "Zhongtong" — the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Nationalist Central Organization Department. The Revival Society's "Special Services Department" expanded into the notorious "Juntong" — the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Nationalist Central Military Affairs Commission. Among the Nationalists' huge apparatus of army, police, gendarmes, and agents, this "Zhongtong" of the Party wing and "Juntong" of the military wing were always the core secret-service organizations.

The CCP "Special Branch" grew by stages, setting up four sections in turn. The First Section, general affairs, under Hong Yangsheng — earlier called "headquarters" — saw to the guarding of the central organs and to other business. The Second Section, intelligence, under Chen Geng, was to penetrate the enemy's detective organs and secure intelligence. The Third Section, the "Red Squad" (the dog-beating squad), had as its main task the armed protection of the organs and the punishing of traitors and moles, under the squad leaders Cai Fei and Tan Zhongyu. The Fourth Section, radio, was to build secret wireless communications, under Li Qiang and Chen Shouchang.

An important task of the general-affairs section was to safeguard central meetings. The CCP Center of those days ran high with revolutionary zeal; outlawed though it was, it still had to convene national congresses often. The 1930 preparatory conference for the National Congress of Soviets, and the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee in 1931, each drew several dozen. The general-affairs section would first rent a building inside a foreign concession and move its own people in as cover, then lodge the delegates, dozens of them, separately in hotels. During a session, plainclothes Red Squad men cycled patrol outside as lookouts, a "wife" played cards downstairs as a lookout, and general-affairs "servants" waited on the rooms above. The delegates met on the upper floor; at the first sign of trouble the outer ring would raise the alarm and hold off the intruders while the delegates slipped away by a hidden passage. The general-affairs section was also charged with rescuing arrested comrades. With the CCP's covert backing, a public body was formed under the chairmanship of Song Qingling — the "China Mutual Aid Society" — to rescue arrested comrades in the open, through legal channels. Ren Bishi, a member of the Central Political Bureau, was arrested twice, and both times the Special Branch bought off the concession police and got him out. In August 1929, when the Central Military Commission members Peng Pai and Yang Yin were arrested, Zhou Enlai planned in person to storm the execution ground. The enemy took Peng Pai and Yang Yin off to Longhua for execution, and the Red Squad, disguised as a film crew, moved to intercept them on the road. But the route was heavily guarded, the weapons came late, and at the last critical moment the empty-handed Red Squad could only watch the prison van roll past, with no way to strike. After Luo Yinong went to his death, the Special Branch braved the danger to gather up and bury his body, and even raised a gravestone under the assumed name "Tomb of the Gentleman Luo Siwei."

The Second Section, in charge of intelligence, was pure "special-service" work. Its men — Chen Geng, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di, Pan Hannian, Chen Yangshan, Ouyang Xin, Liu Ding, Li Yuchao, and the rest — were prodigies of CCP intelligence. The section chief, Chen Geng, was one of the "Three Heroes of Whampoa," had saved Chiang Kai-shek's life during the Eastern Expedition, and had studied security work in the Soviet Union. The Second Section cultivated one important intelligence connection, Bao Junfu. When the Nationalists first set up their Investigation Section in Nanjing and looked to build a secret-service organization in Shanghai, the special agent they chose to post there, Bao Junfu, happened to be a close friend of the CCP Special Branch's Chen Yangshan. And so the very Nationalist agent system meant to ferret out the CCP central organs in Shanghai was in fact in Communist hands. When the Special Branch rescued Ren Bishi, it was through Bao Junfu that the money reached the concession police. When Guan Xiangying was arrested in the French Concession and his top-secret handwritten papers fell into enemy hands, the French, who could not read Chinese, asked Bao Junfu to find an "expert examiner" — whereupon the Special Branch's own Liu Ding was put in charge of examining Guan Xiangying's documents and books. Liu Ding quietly recovered the secret papers, Bao Junfu handed the French an assessment certifying that the man's books had nothing to do with politics, and Guan Xiangying walked safely out of jail. The Special Branch's secret connections reached into every quarter. Yang Du — one of the "Six Gentlemen of the Chou'an Society" who had backed Yuan Shikai's bid for the throne — also joined the Communist Party in secret, with Zhou Enlai's approval; he worked hard to save Li Dazhao, and supplied intelligence more than once.

The Third Section, the Red Squad, was the action arm: a good twenty-odd stout fellows who braved life and death on the Shanghai streets. When Luo Yinong was betrayed by the turncoat couple He Jiaxing and He Zhihua and went bravely to his death, the Red Squad swore to avenge him and hunted the pair across the city; the two had only just gone to ground on Bushi Road when the Red Squad came to their door the next day and dealt out its reckoning. Bai Xin, the turncoat who had sold out Peng Pai, had once served as secretary of the Central Military Commission and knew a great deal of the inner workings, and the Center ordered the Red Squad to eliminate him. But Bai Xin knew the patterns of CCP secret work and dodged the Red Squad's assassins time and again. The Special Branch laid careful plans, Chen Geng and Bao Junfu scouted the ground themselves, and just as Bai Xin was setting out to leave Shanghai, the Red Squad shot him dead at the very door of the police inspector Fan Zhengbo's house.

The Fourth Section's radio work was set up and built under Zhou Enlai's own hand. In the past the CCP had passed intelligence mainly by post, through mails the Nationalists controlled and censored — most unreliable. Sending letters by dedicated secret couriers still meant running the gauntlet of police searches, and the risk stayed high. So the most secret letters had to be memorized by the courier and recited out again on arrival. Crossing mountains and rivers, slipping through the enemy's blockade, this primitive method often took months to complete a single exchange — far too slow. By the lessons of secret work abroad, the surest and quickest means of contact was still the wireless. Yet a radio set, that piece of modern equipment, was hard for the CCP to lay hands on. In October 1928 the CCP Center resolved to build its own wireless. Zhou Enlai assigned Li Qiang to assemble a set himself, sent Zhang Shenchuan to sit the entrance exam for the Nationalists' radio school, and picked Tu Zuochao and three others to study radio technology at the Frunze Military Communications School in the Soviet Union. In the winter of 1929 Li Qiang assembled the set in the British Concession in Shanghai; in early 1930 Tu Zuochao came home to help, and Li Qiang carried the set into Kowloon and reached Shanghai from Hong Kong — the CCP's first long-range wireless link, and the quiet birth of its first radio station. In September 1930 Zhou Enlai personally arranged a training class in Shanghai to train radio cadres for the Soviet areas and the Red Army units. A group of people from around the country gathered to study in secret at the Fuli Electric Company's works in Shanghai. The concession police, finding this factory rather odd, raided it and arrested five instructors and fifteen trainees. Those who escaped arrest — Li Qiang, Mao Qihua, Wu Yunfu, Zeng San, Tu Zuochao — scattered at once and carried on training radio operators.

This "Central Special Branch," founded in November 1927, marked the birth of the CCP's central intelligence and security system.

In summing up the history of the CCP's founding, one usually says that Mao Zedong opened up the rural base areas and created the Red Army. Then one ought to add that Zhou Enlai opened up the urban secret work and created intelligence and security work.

In the spring of 1928 Zhou Enlai ran a training class himself, drilling Special Branch personnel for more than twenty days. He also laid down the Special Branch's rule of "three tasks and one prohibition": to do intelligence, to punish traitors, and to carry out every kind of special task including fund-raising — and never to spy on one another within the Party. Zhou also compiled the CCP's first cipher himself, the "Hao code," with Deng Yingchao serving as the cipher clerk.

Secret though the Special Branch was, it still needed firewalls. Zhou Enlai set up other, parallel secret bodies whose people never crossed with the Special Branch's. The Central Liaison Bureau handled the secret conveyance of documents, intelligence, supplies, and personnel; the Central Military Commission's own intelligence organ scouted chiefly for military intelligence.

The CCP had run internal courier work from its founding. In 1927, when the Special Branch was created, it also created an internal courier section and an external courier section, and built three secret courier lines out to the Party organizations around the country. At the end of 1928 it brought in Wu Defeng, a member since 1924, to build in full the secret courier links between the central organs in Shanghai and the base areas. Wu Defeng, a master of secret work, put together a courier network in two months; in 1929 it carried out 5,523 central documents and brought in 4,687 reports from around the country. In October 1930 it was raised into a Liaison Bureau directly under the Center. Its director, Wu Defeng, threw everything into opening the routes to the Central Soviet Area in Jiangxi, laying three lines — east, west, and south. The Liaison Bureau not only carried secret mail but was also charged with escorting cadres and moving supplies and funds.

The whole Party was outlawed, and it was not only the intelligence and security system that needed secrecy. Zhou Enlai, working with Li Weihan, Ren Bishi, and Deng Xiaoping, drafted "Central Circular No. 47: On the Consolidation, Development, and Secret Work of Party Organizations under the White Terror," which fixed the guiding line and the methods of secret work. Every CCP organization in the White areas took to underground, clandestine methods. Even in the Red base areas the Party organizations were not made public, and the Party membership of men in the army was kept secret too.

Given the founding and the record of the Central Special Branch and the other secret bodies, one may say that the CCP's intelligence and security work began from a high starting point.

A Nationalist publication, "The Theory and Practice of Special-Service Work," assessed them this way: "Though they had no experience to speak of, yet because the right men were in charge, and in a spirit of study and invention, they laid out a whole plan and, by measured stages, pressed forward with their feet on solid ground. In less than three years they achieved astonishing growth and singular results. Standing on objective ground, even we cannot but admire their fighting spirit!"

For all its systematic organization and outstanding record, the Central Special Branch, this newly founded intelligence and security body, was still relatively green. One had only to look at the man it chose as its first working head, Gu Shunzhang, to see a fatal flaw. Then again, when had Xu Enzeng, the man in charge of the agents on the Nationalist side, ever been free of fatal flaws of his own?

Soon enough, the intelligence and security organs of the two sides would get their chance to cross swords head-on...
3. Showdown at the Summit

Both sides in China's life-and-death struggle, Nationalist and Communist, took up intelligence and security work early enough. Perhaps it came of the Chinese gift for "using spies"; perhaps it was borrowed from the international experience of America and the Soviet Union; but more important than either was the plain need of the struggle itself.

In the early 1930s the focus of the two sides' intelligence and security struggle was the CCP central organs. The CCP Center had operated all along right under the enemy's nose, with no legal right to exist, protected entirely by the Special Branch. And the Nationalists, holding the power of the state, threw in both their open military police and their secret agents to crack the CCP Center open.

The chance to rewrite history appeared out of nowhere.

In January 1931 the CCP Center held the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee in secret in Shanghai; Wang Ming, back from Moscow, entered the central leadership with the backing of the Comintern representative Mif. Divisions within the Party sharpened by the day, secrecy grew slack, and Lin Yunan, Li Qiushi, and others were arrested. In April, Gu Shunzhang, escorting Zhang Guotao to the E-Yu-Wan Soviet, gave himself away by performing magic in public in Wuhan, was arrested on the 24th, and defected that same night.

Gu Shunzhang had been bold and fierce from boyhood, a student of the martial arts; in the May Thirtieth Movement he had charged in without flinching, and after joining the Party he trained the workers' pickets. Back from studying security work in the Soviet Union, he became one of the CCP's earliest security specialists, serving as bodyguard to the Soviet adviser Borodin and as commander of the workers' pickets in Shanghai's third armed uprising. Gu Shunzhang could kill a man silently with his bare hands, was a master of disguise, of shadowing and of shaking off a shadow, and stood second to none among China's agents as an operations specialist. In 1927, when right deviation was under criticism and the Comintern pressed the CCP to give more weight to worker stock in its reorganization, Gu Shunzhang began to move into the central core: member of the CCP Central Political Bureau, member of the Central Special Work Committee, and working head of the Central Special Branch. Gu was quick and capable — on stage he could perform magic, off stage he could work hypnosis — but he was also dissolute and unbridled, forever calling friends together, gambling and whoring, and breaking Party discipline on the pretext that an agent's work demanded it. In May 1930 Zhou Enlai sent Nie Rongzhen into the Special Branch expressly to keep a political check on Gu Shunzhang.

And just as Zhou Enlai was weighing whether to replace the Special Branch's leadership, Gu Shunzhang came to grief in Wuhan.

Gu Shunzhang had long run the Special Branch's day-to-day work and knew every one of its people; his own relatives all lived in the central "live-in stations" and held many secret addresses; if Gu once opened his mouth, the CCP central organs would be swept up in a single net. But Gu Shunzhang, scheming for his own future, meant to hold out for a price: he would surrender all his intelligence only if he could meet Chiang Kai-shek face to face. And the Wuhan Field Headquarters detective office that had caught him, itching to claim the credit, was in a hurry to report straight to the Investigation Section. So secret telegrams went out, one after another, to Xu Enzeng at the Central Party Headquarters in Nanjing.

Right at the elbow of Xu Enzeng, head of the Nationalists' highest secret-service organ, three intelligence officers of the Communist Party's Central Special Branch lay concealed. In 1928 Chen Lifu had told his cousin Xu Enzeng to open a radio training class and enlarge the Investigation Section's agent system. Getting wind of it, the CCP Special Branch sent the Party members Qian Zhuangfei, Li Kenong, and Hu Di to sit its entrance exam and form a secret Party cell. Zhou Enlai arranged for the three to conceal their identities and bore deep into the vital points. All three, brilliant students, took important posts in turn: Li Kenong held the headquarters of the Radio Administration, Hu Di was moved to Tianjin to hold the northern organ, and Qian Zhuangfei actually became the confidential secretary of Xu Enzeng, the head of the Nationalists' agents.

Li Kenong was born in 1899 in Chao County, Anhui. His father held a post in the Wuhu customs house, and the family was comfortably off. Chen Duxiu and Hu Shi, the leading men of the New Culture Movement, were both Anhui men, and Li Kenong, while at school in Wuhu, threw himself into the progressive student movement, and was arrested in 1919 in a struggle against taxes. Together with a band of May Fourth stalwarts from Anqing, and at the CCP's commission, Li Kenong founded the Minsheng Middle School, which became a base of the revolutionary movement in Anhui. In 1926 Li Kenong joined the Chinese Communist Party. When the Northern Expedition reached Anhui, the Communist Li Kenong served as propaganda chief of the Nationalist county party in Wuhu. In Shanghai, Chiang Kai-shek joined with the Green Gang to crush the Communist-led labor unions, and at the same time put the Wuhu Green Gang boss in as police chief to fight the left for power; the CCP's Wuhu Special Branch assigned Li Kenong to penetrate the Green Gang. When Chiang launched his coup in Shanghai on April 12, 1927, and wired the Wuhu Green Gang boss on the 14th, Li Kenong secretly reported it to the CCP Wuhu Special Branch that very night. By the time the counter-revolutionary coup broke out in Wuhu on the 18th, most of the revolutionary forces had already been moved to safety. A wanted man, Li Kenong perfected in Wuhu the art of transformation — now a headmaster, now a vagrant, now a teacher, now an army officer — fencing cleverly with the enemy, and so began his career in secret work.

In early 1928 Li Kenong moved to Shanghai, and in the winter of the following year he, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di sat the exam for Xu Enzeng's radio school and penetrated the Nationalist secret-service organs. This three-man cell obtained the Nationalist agents' core secrets and supplied vital intelligence for protecting the underground organizations and the Red Army in the base areas.

Now the Communist Special Branch's own head had surrendered to the Nationalists, and Communists lay hidden at the very elbow of the head of the Nationalist agents... the first summit duel in the history of modern Chinese spy warfare.

In a single night, Wuhan sent six urgent telegrams one after another to Nanjing, and every one fell into Qian Zhuangfei's hands. Xu Enzeng, off at a brothel, had let his authority slip away — he had even given Qian Zhuangfei the codebook to keep. Qian Zhuangfei at once sent his own son-in-law, Liu Qifu, to Shanghai to warn Li Kenong, and Li Kenong scoured the city for his handler, Chen Geng...

The turncoat Gu Shunzhang, arriving in Nanjing by boat from Wuhan, set the Nationalist agents rushing upon the CCP central organs in Shanghai — only to find empty rooms. Zhou Enlai, half a step ahead, had had the entire central apparatus moved.

After they had won power across the country, the CCP's central leaders, looking back on the perils of half a lifetime, all said the same thing: that was the Center's most dangerous hour.

Small players had rewritten great history.

The three who worked this marvel — Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di — were called by the CCP intelligence world the "Three Heroes of Longtan". Li Kenong held that it was the fruit of the Special Branch's turn from security to intelligence, and on to counter-surveillance.

This heart-stopping bout of secret struggle laid bare the state of both sides' secret-service organizations at that moment. The CCP Special Branch, thanks to the higher caliber of its team, escaped catastrophe, yet it too took a heavy blow and could not shake off the defensive. Gu Shunzhang took his revenge like a madman: he fingered Yun Daiying in a Shanghai jail, went to Guangzhou to seize Cai Hesen, and the CCP general secretary Xiang Zhongfa was arrested and defected.

Zhou Enlai moved decisively to reorganize the Special Branch: of the intelligence connections planted inside the enemy, some were arrested and some withdrawn, and every Special Branch cadre Gu Shunzhang knew was moved. Here the firewalls set up in advance proved their worth: the Liaison Bureau saw to moving the cadres, and cadres from the Military Commission and the Jiangsu Provincial Committee were brought in to take the Special Branch over.

Chen Yun, a member of the Central Political Bureau, took over as head of the Special Branch, and Zhao Rong — Kang Sheng — was moved in as second-in-charge. Chen Yun also held the First Section's general affairs, Pan Hannian ran the Second Section's intelligence, and Kang Sheng also oversaw the Third Section's Red Squad. In early 1932 Chen Yun was transferred to be Party-group secretary of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, and Kang Sheng took the Special Branch over; before long Kang Sheng moved to the provisional Central Organization Department, and the Special Branch fell to Pan Hannian once more. From then on three nicknames went around the CCP intelligence world: "the Teacher," "the Boss," and "the Young Master."

Battered as it was, the Central Special Branch carried on in hardship and reordered its work.

The Second Section's old intelligence connection, Bao Junfu, could no longer be used, so it contrived to penetrate the Songhu Garrison Command. When the CCP general secretary Xiang Zhongfa was arrested on June 22, 1931, the Special Branch's Ouyang Xin used his connections to steal the whole confession out of the Garrison Command, and Huang Mulan cross-checked it through connections of her own: Xiang Zhongfa had indeed defected.

In February 1932 a "Notice of Wu Hao and Others Leaving the Communist Party" appeared in all the major Shanghai papers. At that moment Zhou Enlai, whose code name was "Wu Hao," was on the road to the Central Soviet Area in Jiangxi; the notice was plainly the Nationalist agents' work, a stratagem to sow division. Through its connections the First Section placed, in the Shen Bao of March 4, an "Urgent Notice from the Lawyer Bahe on Behalf of Zhou Shaoshan," refuting the rumor on Zhou Enlai's behalf.

Xu Qiang, Li Yun, and others newly moved into the Special Branch went to Henan to build up military work, and obtained the Nationalists' operational plan for the fourth "encirclement and suppression" of the Central Soviet Area, aiding the Soviet's counter-campaign.

Though the Special Branch did all it could to recover its strength, the situation in Shanghai grew ever graver, and the CCP central organs and the Comintern's Far Eastern Bureau were forced to suspend operations. In January 1933 Bo Gu and the rest of the provisional Central Political Bureau leadership left Shanghai for the Central Soviet Area in Jiangxi under Mao Zedong, and the Special Branch's head, Pan Hannian, went with them. The Special Branch left behind in Shanghai came under the Shanghai Central Bureau, in Li Zhusheng's charge, and worked mainly on military intelligence. In February the Nationalist secret service set up a special "Shanghai Action Zone," and the Special Branch's Red Squad promptly shot its head, Ma Shaowu, dead. The Special Branch's leadership changed hands often: in May Wu Hujing, from the old Military Commission intelligence system, was brought in, and in October Liu Zihua after him. The new leaders knew military-intelligence work; Wang Shiying sent Xie Fusheng expressly to work the intelligence connection at the Wuhan Field Headquarters, obtaining the Nationalists' intelligence on the "encirclement" of the E-Yu-Wan base and helping the 25th Red Army break out to safety.

Because of the Center's "Left" leadership, and with experienced intelligence cadres withdrawing one after another, the Special Branch's work too began to fail. On June 26, 1934, the secretary of the Shanghai Central Bureau, Li Zhusheng, was arrested and defected, and the Shanghai Central Bureau and the Jiangsu Provincial Committee were broken up six times over. The Special Branch's Red Squad fought back to the death and put down the traitors in the open, but exposed the organization in doing so, and most of its members were arrested. When the Red Squad's Wang Deming, Kuang Hui'an, Ou Zhiguang, and others were taken to the Longhua execution ground and the authorities brought in a clergyman to pray over them and talk them round, the brave men lifted their heads and sang "The Internationale," and went to their deaths unbowed. In September 1935 the surviving Special Branch members mostly moved to Tianjin, where Wang Shiying led them in the work of the North China Liaison Bureau under the Northern Bureau. On November 19, the head of the Special Branch's Shanghai office, Qiu Jifu, was abducted, and the Shanghai Special Branch lost contact with the Center.

This was a wretched season of defeat in CCP history. It was not the Central Special Branch alone that took heavy blows: the leading organs of the Party, the Youth League, and the unions in Shanghai were smashed on a large scale, over and over, until the Party organization in Shanghai was all but wrecked.
4. The State Political Security Bureau

And just as the Shanghai Special Branch was passing through its ordeal, over in Jiangxi all was flourishing.

The Autumn Harvest Uprising forces led by Mao Zedong and the Nanchang Uprising forces led by Zhu De joined up at Jinggangshan and opened a large base area in the borderlands of Hunan and Jiangxi. The Jinggangshan base straddled 28 counties across the three provinces of Jiangxi, Fujian, and Zhejiang, held 15 county towns, covered more than fifty thousand square kilometers, counted a population of more than two and a half million, and fielded a Red Army of more than forty thousand troops. The CCP Center resolved to make this, the largest of the Soviet areas, the Central Soviet Area. On November 7, 1931, the anniversary of the October Revolution, the First National Congress of the Chinese Soviets opened at Yeping in Ruijin, Jiangxi; the congress resolved to found the Chinese Soviet Republic and elected Mao Zedong its chairman. On ground of its own the Chinese Communist Party had set up a government of its own, to stand as the Nationalists' equal.

This Chinese Soviet Republic adopted the political system of a modern state: a procuratorate, courts, an interior ministry, a judicial department — a fairly complete legal order.

One department was out of the ordinary: the State Political Security Bureau.

The Outline Constitution of the Chinese Soviet Republic provided: "The State Political Security Bureau is a part of the Soviet organization, a special organ of the Soviet's special organization. Under the leadership of the government, this organ carries on open and secret struggle against all military, political, and economic counter-revolution, and is an organ for the defense of Soviet power." The Organizational Outline of the State Political Security Bureau of the Chinese Soviet Republic provided that the Bureau, "under the jurisdiction of the Council of People's Commissars of the provisional central government, shall carry out the tasks of reconnaissance, suppression, and elimination of all counter-revolutionary organizational activity, and the detection of bandit crime." "The State Political Security Bureau, its branch bureaus, and its special commissioners represent state power in investigating, taking up, and disposing of all counter-revolutionary cases." Which is to say: ordinary criminal and civil cases fell to the other judicial departments, while counter-revolutionary cases came under the Political Security Bureau alone.

The CCP's intelligence and security work had always been secret work before; this "State Political Security Bureau" was an open organ. In the past, when people spoke of "legal struggle" and "illegal struggle," the law in question was always, when all was said and done, the Nationalists' law. From now on the Communists' own republic had promulgated laws of its own, and would govern in the open and by law.

The chief struggle the new regime faced was the struggle of revolution against counter-revolution, and so this State Political Security Bureau, a "special organ of a special organization," held very great power. The Bureau's investigation branch had full-time investigators who inspected the people and goods passing in and out of the Soviet areas, and it sent intelligence officers deep into enemy country besides. It ran a network of its own besides, planting eyes and ears in every Party, government, army, and mass organization. The Bureau's executive branch handled arrest, detention, and preliminary interrogation. The Bureau also had an armed force directly under it, the "Political Security Guard," charged with the safety of the Party, government, and army organs and their chiefs; the bodyguards of the Party Center's, the government's, and the army's leaders were all "special-service men" assigned by the Bureau, and the men, horses, sidearms, and sabers issued to leading cadres at every level were all fixed by the Bureau. The Bureau's organizing principle was to be "the embodiment of complete centralization": "The relations between the higher and lower levels of the State Political Security Bureau, barring special obstacles, form a consistently vertical system, in which the lower absolutely obeys the orders of the higher." The Bureau kept branch organs or special commissioners in the local governments and the Red Army units, and no local government or Red Army command had the power to alter or halt its orders.

The Bureau's members ranked high too: even a leader's bodyguard, a private's post, was filled by a cadre specially transferred in. The Guard had the best weapons as well, both rifles and sidearms. The Red Army's officers and men wore red collar tabs; the Bureau wore green tabs with a red border. On his chest a Bureau man also wore a silver badge bearing three foreign letters, "GBW." In the plain ranks of the Red Army, the security men, trim in bearing and singular in dress, stood out sharply from the rest. A saying went around inside the Bureau: "The Party is iron discipline; the Bureau is steel discipline." So great was the Bureau's power that, in special circumstances, it could arrest military and government leaders of its own rank. Chen Fusheng, special commissioner of the Fifth Red Division, once arrested and shot a division chief of staff who tried to flee. Xiao Chi, chief special commissioner of the Red Army's medical school, stopped an escape plot got up by the school's principal, Chen Yihou.

In scale and in power alike, this State Political Security Bureau plainly far outstripped the "Special Branch" that Zhou Enlai had founded. Study the Bureau's system and its powers and you see it copied the Soviet Union almost whole. Not only were its principal cadres trained in the Soviet Union — even those foreign letters on the badge were the Russian letters for "GPU".

This State Political Security Bureau had a prehistory of its own too.

As an ancient country of long history, China had always had organs that performed similar functions; the Yuan dynasty's "Bingma Si" was the equivalent of a modern police bureau. But the CCP of those days had no intention of inheriting the political institutions of the feudal age. The founding of the CCP's security organs plainly took its cue from the experience of the Soviet revolution. On August 1, 1927, the CCP and the Nationalist left jointly formed a Revolutionary Committee and launched the Nanchang Uprising. Under the Revolutionary Committee and the uprising army's command a Political Security Office was set up, with Li Lisan, a member of the CCP Front Committee, as its head. On December 11 the CCP launched the Guangzhou Uprising, and the Guangzhou Soviet government set up a Committee for the Elimination of Counter-revolutionaries. On March 10, 1928, the CCP Center issued a circular requiring that Soviet governments, wherever established, set up such a committee beneath them. The CCP's Sixth Congress, held in Moscow on July 10, adopted the "Resolution on the Organization of Soviet Power," proposing that town and country alike in the revolutionary base areas set up suppression and security organs. On April 5, 1929, the Fourth Red Army under Mao Zedong set up a security section within its political department. On April 15, southern Jiangxi's first county government, the Xingguo Revolutionary Committee, set up a suppression committee and a judicial department beneath it. In January 1931 a Central Political Security Office was formed, with Wang Jiaxiang, director of the Red Army's General Political Department, doubling as its head, and only five members. Before long Deng Fa took Wang Jiaxiang's place as head.

This Central Political Security Office evolved, in November of that year, into the "State Political Security Bureau." In November 1931 the First All-Soviet Congress established the state organs of the Chinese Soviet Republic, and under the Central Executive Committee it placed the "State Political Security Bureau."

Deng Fa, who had once gone to the Soviet Union to study security work, became the Bureau's first director. A Guangdong man of worker stock, Deng Fa had served as an orderly in a police bureau, had joined the anti-imperialist marches of the "May Fourth Movement," and had led workers' pickets in the 1922 Canton-Hong Kong strike. He took part in the Northern Expedition in 1926, and in the 1927 Guangzhou Uprising served as deputy commander of the Fifth District's Red Guards. After the uprising failed he went to Hong Kong to build a Special Branch organization and served as secretary of the CCP Hong Kong Municipal Committee. In 1931 he entered the Jiangxi Soviet, and served for a time as secretary of the Fujian-Guangdong-Jiangxi Special Committee.

Under the State Political Security Bureau were a investigation department, an executive department, a Red Army work department, a White-area work department, and the Political Security Guard. Those who served in turn as department heads included Ouyang Yi, Zhang Ranhe, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Li Yimang, Wang Jinxiang, and Tan Zhengwen; those who served in turn as commanders and commissars of the Political Security Guard included Li Yutang, Wu Lie, Hai Jingzhou, Ma Zhulin, Zhuo Xiong, and Tan Zhenlin.

The State Security Bureau's reach covered every system, Party, government, and army. During the fourth counter-encirclement campaign, a bodyguard of Zhu De, the Red Army's commander-in-chief, suddenly died of poisoning. The State Security Bureau at once sent an investigator in as a groom to get close to the people around Zhu De, and quickly cracked the case. The Red Army had captured an army doctor from a Nationalist unit, and this rare and needed talent was at once assigned to serve at headquarters. But the man was a Nationalist agent, and he secretly poisoned the boiled water Zhu De drank. As it happened, Zhu De's bodyguard, being thirsty, drank the commander-in-chief's water first. Zhu De's life was charmed, and he escaped assassination. But the Bureau grew warier for it, and tightened its guard work.

In the past, CCP work had fallen into two great systems, the Soviet areas and the White areas. Security and intelligence work in the White areas was run by the Special Branch, and in the Soviet areas by the suppression committees. After the State Political Security Bureau was founded, it took charge of intelligence and security work in both, and the Soviet areas across the country reorganized their suppression committees into political security bureaus, branch bureaus, and offices, all subordinate to the State Political Security Bureau.

The old "Central Special Branch" had still been a secret organization working underground; this "State Political Security Bureau" marked the turn of the CCP's intelligence and security work to being carried on in the form of state power.
5. Purging Counter-revolutionaries in the Soviet Areas

"Suppression" and "security": different words for the same task. The newly founded State Political Security Bureau brought the security bureaus and the suppression committees at every level under one organization, and gave one leadership to security work and suppression work alike.

The suppression campaigns in the Soviet areas are a thoroughly tangled matter as well. Three great "Left" deviations have appeared in the history of China's public security: the "suppression" campaigns in the Soviet areas during the Second Revolutionary Civil War; the "Rescue Campaign" at Yan'an during the War of Resistance against Japan; and the political campaigns after the founding of the People's Republic, the "Cultural Revolution" above all.

Just as no great historical event is ever an isolated thing, these three great errors in the history of China's public security are bound up with one another. Was the "Cultural Revolution" foreshadowed by the "Rescue Campaign"? Was the "Rescue Campaign" sourced in the "Soviet-area suppression"?

The Jiangxi Soviet, first to begin its suppression, began with a purge of the "AB Corps," and rose to its height with the "Futian Incident."

In June 1930 the CCP's Southwest Jiangxi Special Committee, acting on an informer's tip, arrested a committee cadre named Zhu Jiahao, and Zhu Jiahao in turn gave up a batch of members of a reactionary organization, the "AB Corps." As more were arrested and interrogated, the "AB Corps" grew with the catching, and by September more than a thousand had been purged out of the thirty-odd thousand Communists in southwest Jiangxi. On October 4 the Red Army took Ji'an, and among the enemy documents seized they found an "AB Corps" badge, and a receipt written to a landlord by the father of Li Wenlin, secretary of the Jiangxi Action Committee. The General Front Committee was aghast, and on the 14th its secretary, Mao Zedong, wrote to the Party Center to report that a great many "AB Corps" elements had been found in southwest Jiangxi.

Just then the Nationalists launched their first "encirclement" of the Jiangxi Soviet; Mao Zedong and Zhu De bent all their attention to directing the fighting, and the power to conduct the suppression was devolved entirely. Li Shaojiu, secretary-general of the First Front Army's General Front Committee and head of its suppression committee, drove the purge hard, weeding more than 4,400 "AB Corps" out of a Red Army of over forty thousand. In December, on the General Front Committee's order, Li Shaojiu led a security detachment to Futian, seat of the Jiangxi Action Committee, and that very day arrested the provincial action committee secretary Li Wenlin, the 20th Red Army commander Zeng Gulin, and six other main leaders, eight in all. Li Shaojiu extracted confessions under savage torture, seizing more than 120 people and executing more than 40 in five days. Then Li Shaojiu led his detachment on to the 20th Red Army's station at Donggu to carry the purge there. Liu Di, political commissar of the 174th Regiment stationed at Donggu, made common cause with the army political department director Xie Hanchang, raised the army headquarters' own independent battalion in revolt, arrested the 20th Red Army commander Liu Tiechao and Li Shaojiu, and freed the prisoners. This was the "Futian Incident" that shook the Soviet areas.

The leaders of the Futian Incident marched their men out and raised the slogan: "Down with Mao Zedong, up with Zhu, Peng, and Huang!" They also sent letters, separately, to Zhu De, Peng Dehuai, and Huang Gonglüe, claiming that Mao Zedong had arranged for others to forge evidence against the three of them, meaning to do them harm. Peng Dehuai recognized these as forgeries done in imitation of Mao Zedong's hand, and issued a declaration in support of Mao Zedong as secretary of the General Front Committee. The Red Army's General Front Committee issued a proclamation of its own.

The suppression had provoked the Futian Incident, and the Futian Incident set off a fiercer suppression still. The handling of the Futian Incident swung back and forth, cooling and heating, until at last every cadre of the 20th Army from the commander down to the deputy platoon leaders was arrested, its soldiers were parceled out among other units, and its designation was struck off; a great many cadres were executed. The suppression spread swiftly everywhere, and cadres in the Soviet areas went in fear for their lives, every one. Chen Yi, commander and commissar of the 22nd Red Army, was marked out by Li Shaojiu too; leaving home for a meeting, he told his wife, Xiao Juying: "I am going to a meeting. If I am not back by six this afternoon, go home to the village and hide." Ambushed by bandits on the road back, he was delayed, and Chen Yi did not reach home until eight — to find that his wife had already thrown herself down a well and killed herself.

The unit that carried out the suppression was the Political Security Bureau. Under the relevant rules the Bureau had, in principle, no power to kill. "It may arrest counter-revolutionary suspects directly"; "the power to sentence and to carry out sentence on counter-revolutionary criminals and suspects in general belongs to the judicial organs, while the Political Security Bureau stands in the position of the prosecution." But once the frenzy of a campaign was on, and in wartime above all, the Bureau's power swelled without limit: from investigation to interrogation to sentence, a single body performed all the functions of police, procuratorate, and court together. Answerable to no one but its own superiors, and checked by no other organ, the Bureau reached the point of killing even without the approval of the military and government chiefs of its own rank.

Power without check led on to action beyond all check. The pioneering "experience" of southwest Jiangxi ran: "Unless the most cruel beating is used, they will never confess; one must use methods hard and soft together, keep up the harsh interrogation without cease, weigh the source of what they say, find the thread, chase down the trail with more questions, and above all make them give up the AB Corps organization, so that it may be rooted out at the source." This method, later summed up in the phrase "coerce, confess, believe," worked all too well: the chain reaction it set off made the "counter-revolutionaries" more and more numerous.

The "Futian Incident" broke out in the Jiangxi Soviet at the end of 1930, and the West Fujian Soviet began its suppression next; after it, the Hunan-Jiangxi, the West Hunan-Hubei, and the E-Yu-Wan Soviets all carried out one wave of suppression after another. The targets of the purge widened at furious speed: great numbers of Party members, League members, Red Army cadres, and soldiers were put to death, and some base-area leaders were killed as well — West Hunan-Hubei killed Duan Dechang, Zhou Yiqun, and Liu Zhixun; E-Yu-Wan killed Kuang Jixun and Xu Jishen... It was not until the Red Army was forced to abandon its base areas and set out on the Long March that the purges everywhere gradually subsided. Even so, Zhang Guotao killed the senior cadre Zeng Zhongsheng on the Long March road. By October 1935, when the suppression had stopped in all the other Soviet areas, it was only then getting under way in northern Shaanxi.

Though there is as yet no way to verify the full toll of those killed in the suppression, this much is certain: the killing of so many cadres, and of senior cadres above the county and regimental level in particular, was more than the enemy could ever have accomplished. The over-widening of the suppression also split the ranks within and sowed doubt among the people. Out of fear of the suppression, the people in some places no longer supported the revolution, and some even "turned their coats" and ran after the enemy. One may say that the consequences of the suppression shook the very existence of the revolutionary base areas.

The suppression that ran through the whole Party was plainly bound up with the "Left" leadership of the CCP Center at the time. Through the suppression years the leadership of the CCP Center was, in turn, Li Lisan and then Wang Ming, and the whole line grew more and more Left; that the suppression should lean "Left" too is no wonder.

There was an international background as well. After China's Great Revolution failed, Trotsky joined with Zinoviev, Kamenev, and others in criticizing Stalin's guidance as mistaken, and Stalin, with the support of the majority, struck back at Trotsky. In the parade marking the anniversary of the October Revolution, Trotsky's supporters shouted slogans against Stalin, and among them were some Chinese students at Sun Yat-sen University. The Soviet Party at once began its struggle against the "Trotskyists," sending the Trotskyists among the Chinese students home. Back in China, these Trotskyist students formed a few small Trotskyist groups, and gradually gathered around Chen Duxiu, who had been stripped of his Party posts; in 1929 they set up a "Left Opposition of the Chinese Communist Party" and elected Chen Duxiu its secretary. In the Soviet Union at this time a new view took shape: that an opposition within the Party could shake the convictions inside the revolutionary ranks, and was therefore more dangerous than the enemy. The struggle against the Trotskyists escalated swiftly — expulsion from the Party, deportation, physical elimination — fiercer even than the struggle against the enemy. The Chinese Party, as a branch of the Comintern, had a current phrase of its own: "Against wavering thought within the revolutionary ranks, there must be cruel struggle and merciless blows!" Speaking of struggle within the Party, Mao Zedong once put forward "Five Fearlessnesses": not to fear criticism, not to fear punishment, not to fear expulsion from the Party, not to fear one's wife divorcing one, not to fear the loss of one's head. Do not suppose this was said only to startle: every one of these "Five Fearlessnesses" was aimed at things that had actually happened in the suppression.

In looking back on a historical error, one ought to examine both the subjective and the objective conditions. There truly was, in that time, a fierce and cruel struggle of life and death, and the newborn Communist Party truly faced a powerful and ruthless enemy. Without these extremely unfavorable objective conditions, no one would ever have thought of resorting to so violent a measure as the suppression. In the Red Army's early days, from a division commander like Yu Shadu down to the men of the battalions, companies, platoons, and squads, defection and desertion never ceased. Once a security system was built, the mutiny of whole units was put to an end. And yet, even after the suppression, there were still defections, from an army commander like Gong Chu down to battalion and regimental cadres. The facts prove that the errors of the suppression cannot be laid entirely to objective conditions: a lesson must be sought in those who carried the suppression out, as well.

The Soviet-area suppression that took place in China was directly bound up with the "Left" leadership of the CCP Center, and of the Comintern above it.

In August 1929 the CCP Center issued its Circular No. 44, requiring the whole Party "to follow, in organization, the resolutions of the Comintern and the highest principles of the proletariat, and resolutely to eliminate any activity of the opposition within the Party." On November 13 the Center further instructed the Jiangxi Provincial Committee to "actively widen the struggle against the AB Corps Grand Alliance." On January 11, 1930, the Central Political Bureau passed a resolution, "On the Nationalist Reorganization Clique and the Tasks of the Chinese Communist Party," requiring the whole Party to make the struggle against the Reorganization Clique one of its central tasks. On April 10 the Center sent an instructional letter to the Fujian Provincial Committee, making the struggle against reformism, against the Reorganization Clique, against liquidationism, and against the Right an urgent task. In June the West Fujian government promulgated regulations for the punishment of counter-revolutionaries. In August Li Shaojiu became head of the First Front Army's suppression committee. Thereafter both Hunan and Jiangxi built suppression committees from top to bottom. The CCP cadres and the security system that led the suppression in the Soviet areas were still at a green stage, still without experience in dealing with moles within. On October 14 the Red Army's General Front Committee reported to the Center that a great many AB Corps elements had been found in southwest Jiangxi; on December 12 the Futian Incident broke out...

Zhou Enlai entered the Central Soviet Area at the end of 1931 and quickly saw that the suppression had been carried too far. Zhou Enlai, who had led the CCP's intelligence and security work all along, was relatively familiar with the enemy's situation and with the international scene, and he decisively reined in the suppression in the Central Soviet Area. Mao Zedong, who had taken part in the Jiangxi Soviet's suppression, had opposed the Center's "Left" leadership all along, and saw that the suppression here in the Jiangxi Soviet had its "Left" problems too. But so long as the central leadership went on turning "Left," and so long as the Comintern went on demanding a cleansing of the Party, the CCP could not wholly halt the errors of the suppression, still less draw up any overall reckoning of it.
6. The Red Army's "Trump Card" on the Long March

With a powerful enemy bearing down from without and the suppression shaking its unity within, the Red Army had no choice but to leave its base areas and set out on the long road of the Long March.

The Long March's greatest difficulty was not the thousand mountains and ten thousand rivers alone, but the breaking of its human intelligence. Before this, the Red Army had won four counter-encirclement campaigns one after another, and behind each victory lay the work of intelligence. When Chiang Kai-shek launched the first "encirclement" in 1930, Qian Zhuangfei got hold of the Nationalists' operational plan right there in Nanjing. When the Soviet intelligence officer Sorge reached Shanghai, he mixed closely with Chiang Kai-shek's German advisory group and got hold of the "encirclement" plan too. Mo Xiong, a Guangdong veteran of long standing within the Nationalist ranks, secretly supported the Communists; while serving as garrison commander at De'an in Jiangxi he took on a batch of Special Branch men, Xiang Yunian among them, at his side. When Chiang launched the fifth "encirclement" and ringed the Central Soviet Area with blockhouses, the Red Army got the intelligence in time just as the ring was about to close, and broke out suddenly through a narrow gap.

The enemy's situation shifted from moment to moment; even Zhou Enlai did not know where he would camp that night, and a courier bearing intelligence had little chance of finding Red Army headquarters. The Shanghai Special Branch did what it could: Mo Xiong made a show of eagerness and had himself transferred to Guizhou to block the Red Army, and Lu Zhiying, of the Military Commission intelligence system, went with Mo Xiong to his new post to look for the Red Army. Yao Zijian, a secret Party member burrowed into the Nationalist General Staff Headquarters, was in charge of the military maps. From the moment the Long March began, he read the papers every day, and wherever the Red Army reached, he stole the map of that place and passed it up the line. Only, he too had no way of knowing whether his intelligence would reach the Red Army in time...

The passing break in human intelligence did not turn the Red Army blind and deaf. Mao Zedong said, with satisfaction, that the Long March was "walking a night road with a lantern lit."

For the Red Army held another "trump card": radio technical reconnaissance.

In the early days the CCP had relied on human hands both to gather intelligence and to carry it. Human courier work was bitterly hard; every trip had to break through line after line of blockade and took ten days or a month, so that it could scarcely keep pace with a situation that changed by the moment. To carry intelligence promptly and easily, the wireless was needed.

In Shanghai, Zhou Enlai had told Li Qiang to buy parts here and there and assemble a radio set himself. The CCP's first piece of modern communications equipment was "cobbled" up just so.

The Red Army of the Jiangxi Soviet captured a radio set in the fighting at Wenjiashi, only for a soldier who took the clattering thing for a monster to smash it to bits. Mao Zedong at once told Guo Huaruo, head of the Fourth Red Army's staff office, to add a line to the next operational order: all Red Army columns were to take care to collect radio sets and radio personnel. So when the great victory at Longgang, where the enemy commander-in-chief Zhang Huizan was taken prisoner, yielded another set, it was saved — though not quite whole. A soldier had brought a rifle-butt down on it before he could be stopped, and it could no longer transmit; it could still receive, and from this "half a radio set" the Red Army's wireless communications got their start.

The Red Army captured several technicians from the Nationalist army, and Mao Zedong received them in person: "The wireless is a new technology; your having learned it is very useful, and very rare." Wang Zheng and Liu Yin, who joined the Red Army of their own will, went to work at the set in high spirits, and the very next day picked out the enemy's dispositions from the enemy army's radio signals.

The wireless could gather intelligence too.

On January 28, 1931, Zhu De, commander-in-chief of the First Front Army, and Mao Zedong, its general political commissar, issued an "Order on Detailing Students to Study the Wireless." Mao Zedong himself taught the first training class: "Wireless communication is our thousand-li eye and our downwind ear."

In March, the Center sent Wu Yunfu, Zeng San, and Tu Zuochao to Jiangxi carrying a cipher. From then on the Jiangxi Soviet was in wireless contact with the Center in Shanghai, and intelligence could arrive in an instant.

In June, Mao Zedong ordered a set assigned specially to reconnaissance, no longer to be used for communications. The dedicated reconnaissance set was extraordinarily productive, taking down great quantities of enemy telegrams. But by now the enemy had discovered that the Red Army had radios and had begun to send its telegrams in cipher. The telegrams taken down could not be read, and the intelligence was still not accurate, so Zeng Xisheng, chief of the intelligence section at Red Army headquarters, spent his days puzzling over how to break the ciphers.

The Red Army used the wireless as a means of reconnaissance, listening to open broadcasts to compile reference reports, and monitoring the enemy's communications to learn the situation on the battlefield. This kind of work, unlike the old human reconnaissance, was known inside the trade as technical reconnaissance.

At the very same time, Chiang Kai-shek, supposing the Red Army had no radios at all, directed his operations in plain-language voice traffic and gave away a great deal.

While on the Communist side, a revolutionary leap in the means of intelligence was under way — reconnaissance by science and technology.

In October 1932 the Red Army set up a Second Bureau, dedicated to radio technical reconnaissance. The First Bureau for operations, the Second Bureau for intelligence — a high standing. On November 16 of that year the Second Bureau broke its first cipher telegram.

At Red Army headquarters there was a room that burned bright all night long. Zeng Xisheng and Cao Xiangren puzzled over the ciphers side by side, and the men holding the lamp beside them were Zhou Enlai and Zhu De. Zhou Enlai knew the patterns by which ciphers were built, and Zhu De knew the Nationalists' military terms; this codebreaking team worked at an exceptional level and pace.

Before the Red Army the enemy had no more secrets. Mao Zedong praised them: "Fighting Chiang Kai-shek, we are betting on cards laid out in a glass cup — we can see them clearly, and we win!" The Second Bureau's men said, pleased with themselves: "We are the ones with the treasure cup!"

With the treasure cup in hand, the Red Army fought with more initiative. In the fourth counter-encirclement campaign, Zhu De and Zhou Enlai held their forces still and did not move until the Second Bureau had obtained the intelligence, then struck all at once, dealing a heavy blow to the enemy's "Iron Army" under Wu Qiwei.

The Nationalists' reverses put them on their guard, and Chiang Kai-shek engaged foreign experts specially to compile a "special edition" cipher — which the Red Army broke all the same. In 1933, to mark the August 1 Army Day, the Second Bureau's deputy chief Qian Zhuangfei drew a "Hundred Beauties" picture: the Red Army had by then broken a hundred of the enemy's ciphers. On Army Day 1934 the Red Star Medal was conferred, and two of the Second Bureau's men were honored, Zeng Xisheng in the second class, Cao Xiangren in the third.

Intelligence work, too, had to prize its men of science and technology. Mao Zedong, general political commissar, drew a monthly allowance of five yuan — but the technical cadre Wang Zheng drew fifty silver dollars.

At about this same time, the American secretary of state Henry Stimson ordered the "American Black Chamber" closed, and its master cryptographer, Herbert Yardley, was thrown out of work.

Through the Long March the Military Commission's Second Bureau was at the very peak of its powers: every enemy telegram sent was a telegram taken down, and every enemy cipher issued was a cipher broken.

Breaking out of the encirclement, the Second Bureau found the holes in the enemy's dispositions, and the Red Army slipped through the seams.

At the turn of the march at Tongdao, when the highest leaders could not settle their dispute, Mao Zedong won them over with the Second Bureau's intelligence.

Intelligence is the most objective thing of all: even a man in a "Left" frenzy had to bow to objective reality. Mao Zedong, who knew the Second Bureau well, used its intelligence to persuade his colleagues. After the Zunyi Conference, Mao Zedong directed Zeng Xisheng's reconnaissance still more directly.

Yet the first battle Mao Zedong directed after regaining command did not go well. At the battle of Tucheng the attacking Red Army was counterattacked by the enemy, and even the commander-in-chief took up a rifle and went to the line. It was thanks to the Second Bureau's obtaining intelligence on the battlefield — that the enemy's dispositions had changed — that Mao Zedong hurriedly ordered a withdrawal from the fighting; even a commander's will has to bow to intelligence.

Mao Zedong's authority was challenged once again. At the battle of Dagu Xinchang, Mao Zedong argued against fighting, while everyone else argued for it. Isolated and unsupported, Mao Zedong again used intelligence to win over Zhou Enlai, and to win over them all.

The Four Crossings of the Chishui were the proudest stroke of Mao Zedong's life: within a small compass of thirty kilometers the Red Army moved in and out, drew the enemy away by main force, and won a week's grace to cross the Jinsha River in safety. Little did people know that here too lay the subtle use of intelligence. At the moment of the third crossing, the enemy had already closed the ring, and the Red Army could find no gap to break through. Zeng Xisheng used a Red Army radio to send a telegram to the enemy, and, borrowing Chiang Kai-shek's own cipher, commanded the two columns of Zhou Hunyuan and Wu Qiwei to open a way through.

The strife on the grasslands, too, was a moment of peril in Mao Zedong's life — who could tell what ruthless move Zhang Guotao might make? Mao Zedong led a small force north by forced night marches, and before setting off he gave particular notice: "Bring the Second Bureau!" Ye Jianying had Zeng Xisheng, Cao Xiangren, and Zou Bizhao go on ahead while he himself brought up the rear. All three were master codebreakers.

Arrived in triumph in northern Shaanxi, Mao Zedong received the whole of the Second Bureau in person, and set a high value on its work. When the Red Army was short of grain, Mao Zedong wrote in his own hand: "The grain of the comrades of the Second Bureau must be provided for, whatever it takes." When Lu Xun had cured meat carried to Mao Zedong, Mao passed this delicacy on to the Second Bureau. When the codebreaking expert Cai Wei died of illness, the Military Commission issued regulations on allowances to secure the living conditions of its technical men. When someone at a Red Army set held private radio conversation with a Nationalist army's set — a crime of treasonous contact punishable by death — Mao Zedong let it pass: the man had been a Nationalist army technician before and did not understand Red Army discipline, and could be forgiven. This enlightened policy set the technical men's minds at ease in their Red Army work. Their minds at ease; but the Red Army soldiers' minds were not always at ease, and some asked to leave the Second Bureau for the front. Mao Zedong spoke with them himself: "Yours is the unit that guarantees the Red Army's victories."

Young soldiers of little schooling found it hard to grasp what technical reconnaissance did. So Mao Zedong told them the story of the master builder Lu Ban. A great stone bridge was near completion, but the center of the arch still wanted one stone, and no fitting one could be found anywhere. Just then Lu Ban arrived, and cut a stone with his own hands, neither too big nor too small, exactly right. This stone was called Lu Ban's stone; and the Second Bureau was the Red Army's Lu Ban stone.

Now they all understood: radio technical reconnaissance was an indispensable part of an army's fighting strength.
7. The First Step to a Foothold in Northern Shaanxi

On October 19, 1935, the Central Red Army — battered, its ranks thinned, spent to the last of its strength — reached Wuqi Town in northern Shaanxi after a Long March of ten thousand li. The great army of a hundred thousand that had set out was now down to eight thousand; but fortunately there was a base area of its own people ahead in the northwest, where it could at last set down and rest. The CCP Center sent the Shaanxi-born cadre Jia Tuofu, together with Li Weihan, head of the Central Organization Department, on ahead with a radio to make contact.

Li Weihan and Jia Tuofu reached Xiasiwan in Ganquan County and found Guo Hongtao, deputy secretary of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Shanxi Provincial Committee, and learned: a suppression was under way here in the northwest, more than two hundred Party, government, and army cadres had already been killed, even Zhang Qingfu, a Special Branch cadre sent down from above, had been seized, and Liu Zhidan, Gao Gang, Xi Zhongxun, Ma Wenrui, and the other founders of the Shaan-Gan border base had just been arrested.

When Zhang Wentian, general secretary of the CCP, and Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai, chairman and vice-chairman of the Central Military Commission, who were then directing the fighting at Zhiluozhen, got the report, they wired back at once: "Stay the blade! Stop the arrests!"

On October 24 the CCP Center reached Xiasiwan in Ganquan, seat of the Shaan-Gan Border Soviet government, and on November 3 the Standing Committee members Zhang Wentian, Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai, Bo Gu, and others heard the reports of Guo Hongtao and of Nie Hongjun, chairman of the Northwest Military Commission. The Center resolved on the spot to abolish the Central Delegation that had directed the suppression, and the Shaanxi-Gansu-Shanxi Provincial Committee, to set up a CCP Central Northwest Bureau, and at the same time to form a five-man group of Dong Biwu, Li Weihan, Zhang Yunyi, Wang Shoudao, and Guo Hongtao to re-examine and settle the northwest suppression. To keep the suppression from going further, the Center also sent Wang Shoudao, director of the State Political Security Bureau, straight to Wayaobao to look into it on the spot.

Before he set out, Mao Zedong charged Wang Shoudao gravely: "Beheading is not like cutting chives; chives grow back once they are cut, but a head that falls to the ground never joins on again. If we kill the wrong man, if we kill a comrade of the revolution, that is a criminal act. Be careful; do your investigation and study well."

Mao Zedong had his reasons for being so tense about the suppression. Since 1934, all the red base areas across the country, this one in the northwest excepted, had been forced to give way. Behind this lay, to be sure, the external cause of a powerful enemy's "encirclement"; but the deeper internal cause was the "Left" error of the central leadership, and the wrongful killings of the suppression above all — a bitter lesson in tearing down one's own Great Wall. On the Long March road, in January 1935, the CCP Center had held the Zunyi Conference, which changed the line. The reshuffled central leadership had come to some recognition of the "Left" errors of the past; and Mao Zedong, only just come to the core of power, loathed dogmatism with a particular passion. Though the Zunyi Conference's conclusions still rested on the military side and had not yet summed up the political errors as a whole, the new leadership already had a common mind about the error of over-widening the suppression.

Once the pain had been taken to heart, the CCP Center could not let the errors of the suppression wreck northern Shaanxi, this last patch of ground to stand on.

On October 30 Wang Shoudao rode hard to Wayaobao, at once proclaimed his order, took over the security bureau's work from the former director Dai Jiying, and reopened the case of Liu Zhidan and the others. Wang Shoudao, when he was secretary of the Hunan-Jiangxi Border Provincial Committee, had once been dismissed for resisting a wrongful suppression; coming now to re-examine the northwest suppression, he quickly saw the problem.

Here in the northwest there had been two revolutionary base areas: the northern Shaanxi base founded by Xie Zichang and others, and the Shaan-Gan border base founded by Liu Zhidan and others. In August 1934 Xie Zichang was wounded by a bullet in the chest, and that winter the Nationalist army opened a great "encirclement" of the northwest Soviet. The Northern Shaanxi Special Committee wrote to the Shaan-Gan Border Special Committee proposing that the two armies fight jointly. Liu Zhidan led his forces to northern Shaanxi in February 1935, the two special committees held a joint conference, and they resolved to form jointly a Northwest Work Committee and a Northwest Military Commission to give unified leadership to the two base areas. Not long after the conference Xie Zichang, chairman of the Northwest Military Commission, died of his wound, and Liu Zhidan took unified command of the fighting.

In July 1935 Zhu Lizhi came to northern Shaanxi from the Northern Bureau as a central representative; in August the Shanghai provisional Central Bureau sent Nie Hongjun to northern Shaanxi; and on September 15 Xu Haidong led the 25th Red Army into northern Shaanxi. After the forces joined, it was resolved to form a Shaanxi-Gansu-Shanxi Provincial Committee with Zhu Lizhi as secretary and Guo Hongtao as deputy secretary; to make Nie Hongjun chairman of the Northwest Military Commission; and to form a 15th Red Army Corps with Xu Haidong as its commander, Liu Zhidan as deputy commander and chief of staff, and Cheng Zihua as political commissar. In October the 15th Red Army Corps won a great victory at Laoshan, wiping out most of the 110th Division and one regiment of the 107th of the Nationalist Northeast Army. The Northeast Army brigade commander Zhang Hanmin was an underground Communist and helped the Red Army destroy his own force; but the Red Army's officers and men could not for the life of them believe that an enemy brigade commander could be one of their own, and they shot this comrade dead all the same. What is more, the connections with the 26th Red Army's leaders that Zhang Hanmin had named to prove his identity became, of all things, evidence in the suppression for wrongly persecuting Liu Zhidan and the rest.

While the fighting was on, the suppression began in the rear at Wayaobao. The Northern Bureau's central representative told the Shaanxi-Gansu-Shanxi Provincial Committee that Huang Ziwen, Cai Ziwei, and others were rightists. The security bureau director Dai Jiying wielded the whip himself to force confessions, and pronounced that Liu Zhidan, Gao Gang, Yang Sen, Zhang Xiushan, Xi Zhongxun, Liu Jingfan, and other Red Army leaders were rightists too. And so the Provincial Committee sent word to the front to arrest Liu Zhidan, Gao Gang, and Zhang Xiushan.

A Provincial Committee courier riding hard to the troops with the letter met, on the way, none other than Liu Zhidan, deputy commander of the 15th Red Army Corps, and handed this chief the Provincial Committee's letter to the front military commission. He opened it and read — an order to the Northwest Military Commission to arrest Liu Zhidan. He could, at that moment, have held the letter back and gone on to the 81st Division, an old unit he had led out himself that would surely have protected its own chief, even at the cost of a fight. But Liu Zhidan sealed the letter up, handed it back to the courier, and came of his own accord to the seat of the military commission: "Isn't it me you want to arrest? Here I am."

The case widened further. When Xi Zhongxun, chairman of the Shaan-Gan Border Soviet, also received notice of a meeting, Tu Zhankui, leader of the Provincial Security Bureau's special squad, hurried to stop him: "Don't dare go! They've arrested Old Liu and Old Gao, both of them!"

Tu Zhankui was a local; the Northwest Military Commission and the 25th Red Army's headquarters were quartered in his very village, Zhanghuaiwan, and his family had told him: Old Liu (as the people of northern Shaanxi called Liu Zhidan) and Old Gao (Gao Gang) had been shut up by the 25th Army in the cave dwelling of your second sister-in-law's house.

But Xi Zhongxun said: "Never mind him; it will all be cleared up in the end." As he set out he charged everyone: "I am going to the meeting; I may come back, and I may not. Whoever comes to lead, you are all to do your work well." And he charged Tu Zhankui, who had once been his own bodyguard: "Whatever comes up, don't go talking or acting rashly!"

At Zhanghuaiwan, Xi Zhongxun's bodyguard was disarmed, and Xi Zhongxun too was shut in a cave dwelling.

Liu Zhidan's younger brother, Liu Jingfan, was ill at the time; he went the twenty li to the "meeting" on his own, and when he truly could go no farther and rested for a night, he was arrested in the open by the security bureau's pistol squad.

Where in the world were there moles like these? The soldiers and people of the Shaan-Gan Border Soviet could not for anything believe that these leaders, who had braved life and death to fight the base area into being, could be spies. The northwest Red Army — the 26th, the 27th — had already begun to wonder: was the 25th Red Army, come from outside, a White army? When the people of the Hongyi district heard that their leader Ma Wenrui had been arrested, more than seven hundred households fled to the White areas. The enemy seized the chance to stir things up; in the Sanbian district guerrilla units mutinied, and some of the people raised the white flag and "turned their coats."

Conflict was on the point of breaking out within the Red Army; the consequences of the wrongful suppression were destroying the northwest Soviet.

The CCP Center reached Wayaobao and at once heard the five-man group's report. Mao Zedong stated plainly: "The arrest of comrades Liu Zhidan and the rest is entirely wrong; it is a trumped-up frame-up, it is opportunism, it is a 'madness'!" Mao Zedong also called on all cadres, soldiers, and people to strengthen their unity and face the enemy as one. On November 7 the Center held a banquet to release the eighteen — Liu Zhidan, Gao Gang, Xi Zhongxun, and the rest. Liu Zhidan spoke on behalf of the arrested, thanking the Center for their deliverance and criticizing the errors of the former Central Delegation, and at the same time urging everyone not to hold it against the comrades who had run the suppression. On November 30 the Center resolved to discipline the two men responsible for the suppression, Dai Jiying and Nie Hongjun.

History had contrived so hair-raising a scene, and it was true.

In October 1935 a wrongful suppression was breaking out in the northern Shaanxi Soviet, and the CCP's last foothold, its last inch of standing ground, was on the point of utter ruin. And at that very moment the CCP Center came to northern Shaanxi — and the Center that came was no longer the old "Left"-leaning Center.

Northern Shaanxi was fortunate: its leaders, Liu Zhidan and the rest, kept their lives.

The CCP Center was fortunate: its foothold held firm at the base.

What if it had been off by a few days? Had northern Shaanxi killed Liu Zhidan a few days earlier, or had the CCP Center reached northern Shaanxi a few days later, what would the outcome have been? The Center saved northern Shaanxi, and northern Shaanxi saved the Center; after all its lessons written in blood, the Chinese Communist Party too was owed one stroke of luck. The CCP Center that reached northern Shaanxi in October 1935 made its first act the halting of the wrongful suppression — and by that first act won the hearts of the Party, the army, and the people of northern Shaanxi.

But the whole Party's full recognition of the errors of the suppression was still a long way off. The "Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of Our Party," adopted by the Seventh Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee on April 20, 1944, proposed: "All comrades who, on investigation, are confirmed to have been wrongly dealt with and falsely accused should be vindicated, restored to Party membership, and honored in memory by their comrades." On May 30, 1945, Mao Zedong said at the Party's Seventh Congress: "The suppression walked a most painful road. Counter-revolution ought to be opposed; but while the Party was still immature, it took a wrong turn on this question and committed errors." In 1954 the Interior Ministry, with the Center's assent, issued a "Notice on the Handling of Those Wrongly Killed in the Suppression During the Second Revolutionary Civil War and of Their Families," and everywhere they set about vindicating those wrongly killed in the suppression. Duan Dechang, Liu Zhixun, and a number of others wrongly killed in the suppression were all declared revolutionary martyrs. But at the time the truth of how these people had died was not disclosed, and even their families believed their kin had fallen in the struggle against the enemy.

The errors of the suppression came to light by degrees; after the "Cultural Revolution" the CCP Center began to deal in bulk with the false and wrongful cases of history, and those wrongly killed in the suppression at last received public vindication and the restoration of their names. The "History of the Chinese Communist Party," published in 2002, drew the full official conclusion on the Soviet-area suppression: this suppression struggle was carried on not only in the Central Base Area but also, separately, in E-Yu-Wan, West Hunan-Hubei, and other base areas. The circumstances of the suppression differed from base to base, but all of them, in varying degree, committed the serious error of over-widening, doing enormous harm to the revolutionary cause. This error arose in direct connection with the "Left" mistaken guidance of "cruel struggle" and "merciless blows." In the fierce revolutionary struggle the enemy was forever contriving ways to wreck the revolution from within, and it was necessary for the Party and the Red Army to keep up the suppression struggle. But in the harsh conditions of war, with class struggle extremely complex, the broad mass of cadres lacking experience in struggling against a hidden enemy, the Party's policy imperfect, and the leaders in some areas moreover prone to a serious subjectivism and warlordism, the errors in the suppression developed grievously and caused painful losses. The comrades wrongly killed in the suppression displayed a lofty revolutionary spirit, loyal unto death to the Party and to the communist cause. Later they were vindicated one after another, and honored with the respect and remembrance of the Party and the people.

In October 1935 the CCP Center, only just arrived in northern Shaanxi, had already seen the grave errors of the northern Shaanxi suppression, but it did not rush to draw a political conclusion; rather it avoided dispute and set things right first. The CCP Center resolved to appoint Liu Zhidan deputy chairman of the Northwest Office of the CCP Central Revolutionary Military Commission. This Northwest Office, headed by Zhou Enlai, was in fact the central leading organ. The CCP Center, its footing in northern Shaanxi not yet secure, meant to shrink its profile.

With that, the former "State Political Security Bureau" was renamed the "Northwest Political Security Bureau." The old Central Special Branch had, during the Long March, been called the Special Work Section, known to outsiders as the Fourth Section of the Central Organization Department, under the section chief Du Liqing (Xu Jianguo). When the Northwest Security Bureau was formed, Du Liqing also served as its deputy director. At some point — no one knows just when — the cadres and men of the Northwest Security Bureau no longer wore the green collar tabs or the silver badge. Ever since the suppression, the Bureau's iron-and-steel image had gathered dust.

The Northwest Political Security Bureau set about its work with dispatch all the same. The Center reached northern Shaanxi on October 19, 1935; on the 24th it resolved to reorganize the Northwest Security Bureau; on the 30th director Wang Shoudao took over the work; and five days after that, on November 5, the new Northwest Security Bureau issued its Order No. 1. Director Wang Shoudao was a southerner; his secretary was the northern Shaanxi cadre Geng Hong, on loan while Li Qiming was held in the suppression. In February 1936, when the Red Army made its eastern expedition into Shanxi and Wang Shoudao was moved to the front, Zhou Xing took over as director of the Northwest Security Bureau. The whole bureau had some fifty-odd cadres: Han Xianqi as head of the investigation department, Chen Fusheng of the Red Army work department, Tan Zhengwen of the executive department, and Li Fushan and Liu Haibin as Party general-branch secretaries. Under the Northwest Security Bureau were five provincial security branches: the Northern Shaanxi Provincial Security Bureau under Liu Ziyi, the Shaanxi-Gansu Provincial Security Bureau under Zheng Zixing, the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Provincial Security Bureau under Li Woru, the Guanzhong Special District Security Bureau under Niu Hansan, and the Shenfu Special District Security Bureau under Huang Zhengming.

Small as the team was, its cadres were all old Red Army men and old security hands. Chen Fusheng had been, before the Long March, special commissioner of the Fifth Division of the Third Red Army; Tan Zhengwen had been, before the Long March, head of the Jianning Garrison District and the Fujian-Jiangxi Military District, and security bureau chief of the Fujian-Jiangxi Province; Li Fushan had been security bureau chief of Qingjian County in northern Shaanxi; and Liu Haibin had been special commissioner of the Second Division on the Long March.

Zhou Xing, short of men, at once ran a training class for security cadres. The trainees came from the counties of the border region, and though all had done security work, most had loyalty to spare and craft in short supply. For the graduation test, Zhou Xing set an unusual task: to find, somewhere in the classroom, a reactionary slogan. At once the trainees grew tense; some turned the tables over, some picked at the cracks in the walls, but nowhere could they find it. Only Zhao Cangbi, secretary of the Yanshui County Security Bureau, kept calm, looked about him, and let his eye come to rest on a slip of paper on the tabletop. On it were written four idioms: "Yesterday's chrysanthemum; daily a myriad affairs; storm and tempest; nimble in action." Everyone supposed there could be nothing wrong with four phrases stuck up in plain view, but Zhao Cangbi said: "This is a head-hiding reactionary slogan; read the first character of each idiom together, and it spells 'Uprising tomorrow'!"

Zhou Xing admired Zhao Cangbi's ready wits, sponsored his entry into the Party during the class, and after it kept him on as a secretary at the Northwest Security Bureau, a post later taken over by Li Qiming.

Whether as the "director" of the Northwest Security Bureau or the "chief" of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office, Zhou Xing's name was never a loud one — but his authority was of the first importance: he was the man in charge of security at the side of the Party Center and Chairman Mao.

Zhou Xing was born in 1906 in the county town of Yongfeng, in the mountains of western Jiangxi. After three years at a normal primary school he went to work as an apprentice in a dye-house. In 1925 Huang Oudong, the earliest Communist in Yongfeng, brought Zhou Xing into the Chinese Socialist Youth League, and in 1926 he crossed over into the Party. Yongfeng's unions and peasant associations received the Northern Expeditionary Army and took hold of the whole county's government. When Chiang Kai-shek marched on Nanchang and the Nationalist right raised a coup in Yongfeng, Zhou Xing and other Communists were seized overnight and paraded through the streets. The pain taken to heart, the party of western Jiangxi drew the lesson and set about grasping "the gun." The young Zhou Xing was sent to Nanchang, entered the instructional regiment under Zhu De as its commander, and took part in the Nanchang Uprising; after the uprising failed he slipped back to Yongfeng to carry on guerrilla struggle. When Mao Zedong led the Red Army in building the base at Jinggangshan, Zhou Xing was, in 1930, transferred to be secretary of the Jiangxi Provincial suppression committee, and there, at Chairman Mao's side, his career in security work began.

Unluckily, Zhou Xing's immediate superior was a zealot: Li Shaojiu opened a great slaughter in the suppression, until in the end the Provincial Security Bureau was down to two men, director Li Shaojiu and secretary Zhou Xing. Just as Li Shaojiu, having killed Zhou Xing's own younger brother, was preparing to move against Zhou Xing too, the Center corrected the error of over-widening the suppression. The crime-laden Li Shaojiu was severely disciplined, and Wu Defeng, who took over as director, held that Zhou Xing had been no more than one who carried out orders, and kept him on.

Zhou Xing's original name was Liu Weixin; he changed it to "Zhou Xing" on joining the revolution, and a security cadre sharing a name with a cruel official of the Tang could not but draw some mockery. Zhou Xing bitterly regretted the lesson of the suppression, and always entered in his file: "Received a half-year's probation within the Party after the Soviet-area suppression," and would now and then remind young comrades of his own lesson in the course of his work. Not until the "Cultural Revolution," when the rebels traced Zhou Xing's history, was it discovered that this punishment had never been carried out.

Correct in his Party character, Zhou Xing went on enjoying the organization's trust, serving in turn as the security bureau's secretary-general, investigation department head, and executive department head, and on the Long March as deputy director of the First Red Army Corps' security bureau, special commissioner of the State Political Security Bureau, and special commissioner of the Red Army Cadre Regiment. The officers and men of the Red Army Cadre Regiment were all squad and platoon leaders marked for promotion and cadres of company-and-battalion rank and above, every one of them holding a post above his grade; Zhou Xing assisted the work of the regiment commander Chen Geng and the political commissar Song Renqiong. Wang Dongxing was at the time political instructor of the Cadre Regiment's Third Company, and did his security work so carefully and thoroughly that he caught Zhou Xing's notice. At the forcing of the Jinsha River, when the Third Company held an important battle position, Zhou Xing went to the spot himself to check it and to arrange the control of the enemy's firing points on the far bank. During the Zunyi Conference, Zhou Xing was in charge of the outer guard, and led the guard company to break up bandit forces three times at Hongtupo, a kilometer from the conference site, capturing two Nationalist agents, and was commended by Zhou Enlai, vice-chairman of the Military Commission.

In July 1936 the American journalist Edgar Snow slipped in to Bao'an in northern Shaanxi, and while interviewing Mao Zedong and the other CCP leaders he fixed his eye on Zhou Xing, the security bureau director — this was, most likely, the first "foreign affairs" activity in the history of CCP security work.

Zhou Xing gave a concise account of how the Bureau's tasks now differed from what they had been in Jiangxi: "The task of the State Security Bureau is to defend the fruits of the revolution. Counter-revolutionary elements do not stop their activity when the Red Army arrives, and to counter them the Bureau relies on the help of the broad masses of the people, and uses the method of education to change the minds of those who are not yet dead-set on counter-revolutionary activity." "The Bureau is not so fearful a thing; it merely defends Soviet power, and now it protects and widens the united front." "In Jiangxi the definition of counter-revolution was very severe; now, in a time of the united front, it is not so severe. In Jiangxi no one spoke of cooperating to oppose imperialism; now it is different (now one may speak of cooperating to resist Japan). In Jiangxi, whoever disagreed with us would be counted a counter-revolutionary and his property confiscated, and if he opposed us he would be stripped of every right, though he had not yet taken direct action against us." "In Jiangxi our policy was to protect directly the interests of that main part of the population that actively took part in and supported the revolution; now we recognize some new categories among the people, people who on the one hand oppose Japan and on the other do not sympathize with the Communist Party. Such people, so long as they do not attempt to overthrow Soviet power by force, ought not to be arrested, punished, or ill-treated. We are educating them and carrying on ideological struggle with them, only without resorting to force and suppression. Nor, further, have we confiscated their land; we let them till it themselves. And finally, in policy we give them a fitting political consideration." "In a word, the Bureau now defends not only the fruits of the revolution's victory but also the foundation of the united front."

Zhou Xing's account shows that, hard on the heels of the Party Center's overcoming of "Left" adventurism and closed-doorism, the intelligence and security departments too had begun to change the "Left" practices of the Jiangxi suppression.

Zhou Xing went on to set out four differences between the Bureau and the Nationalists' gendarmerie (or political police): "The former merely carries out the demands of the masses; without the demand of the masses we do not kill, still less kill in secret — whereas with the Nationalists it is otherwise." "Another difference is this: in the Nationalist-ruled areas, when one man commits a (political) crime, his whole family is arrested, and even the children may be killed. But the Soviet does not allow such a policy. We hold that one man bears his own crime, and that it has nothing to do with his family, his relatives, or his friends. Still less do we strip these people of their political and economic rights." "A third difference is the Soviet's lenient policy toward political prisoners: those people (the political prisoners), so long as they accept education and show themselves well, are soon released and restored to all their rights." "A fourth difference is that a prisoner's rations are the same as our staff's; they are issued the same clothing and food as our staff, and they are not beaten and abused as they are in the Nationalists' prisons."

Zhou Xing's account shows a fairly high level of policy, and one in keeping with internationally accepted principles of justice. Snow set Zhou Xing's answers down carefully and wrote them into his book Random Notes on Red China.

The journey to northern Shaanxi changed Snow's view: in Snow's mind the Communist Party had gone from bandits to the star of China's hope. And Snow's book Red Star Over China (also titled Random Sketches of a Journey to the West) made the CCP known to the world.

The old security hands of Yan'an all knew this story. Snow asked Mao Zedong straight out: "The Nationalists take Zhou Xing for the CCP's spymaster?" Mao Zedong answered with a smile: "I should say Zhou Xing is the proletariat's sword!"

The security cadres all knew that Stalin had once praised: "The Cheka [the suppression committee] is the proletariat's drawn sword!" Zhou Xing explained to the comrades who asked him: "The proletariat's sword that Chairman Mao spoke of does not mean me as a person, but that organization then under the Party's leadership, the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office." He Long, commander of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia-Shanxi-Suiyuan Joint Defense Army, put in a word: "If the Security Office is the sword, then what is the army?"

And so the CCP's security system never styled itself the "sword." After the founding of the People's Republic, the public security minister Luo Ruiqing proposed the "shield" as the emblem of public security. And the emblem of the state security organs holds both together: the "sword and shield."
8. The Mysterious Man Who Secretly Met the Young Marshal

Facing the new situation of Japan's invasion of China, the CCP Center changed its old policy of class revolution and turned instead to the line of an anti-Japanese national united front.

In July 1935 the Comintern held its Seventh Congress and resolved to change its old line of closed-doorism and build an international anti-fascist united front. At this time the CCP Center, still on the Long March, had lost its wireless contact with the Comintern and could not coordinate positions, so the CCP delegation to the Comintern drafted, in the CCP Center's stead, the "Letter of the CCP to All Compatriots on Resisting Japan and Saving the Nation" (the "August 1 Declaration"), calling for Nationalists and Communists to resist Japan together. So great a change of policy urgently needed a unified mind, and in August the CCP delegation to the Comintern sent Zhang Hao home to restore contact.

From the Soviet Union to the Soviet areas of the Chinese interior, the road would pass through the domains of the Mongol princes and the Nationalist-ruled territory, and two men had already been caught and killed making it. For secrecy's sake Zhang Hao carried no written material at all, but committed the main content of the Comintern's Seventh Congress documents and the "August 1 Declaration" to memory — and, above all, recited by heart the whole of the cipher the Comintern had supplied. Zhang Hao and his companion rode camels across the great Mongolian desert, reached Yinchuan in Ningxia, and were held for a time by the Nationalist garrison on account of his Hubei accent. Just as Zhang Hao entered Dingbian in the Shaan-Gan Soviet, the CCP Center too had only just ended its Long March and reached northern Shaanxi.

On November 7, 1935, the anniversary of the October Revolution, while the CCP Center was holding its banquet at Wayaobao to release Liu Zhidan and the others, a telegram suddenly came from Dingbian: a suspicious man wanted to see the Center. The tall, dark Zhang Hao was brought under escort to Wayaobao, and everyone recognized him: this "old Tartar" was the Communist Lin Yuying.

Mao Zedong, only just come to the central leadership, was fortunate: he needed a base area, and there was the northern Shaanxi Soviet under his feet; he needed international backing, and Zhang Hao dropped from the sky. The CCP Center had only just corrected the "Left" military line, there were still serious differences in the discussion of the political line, and Zhang Guotao had gone so far as to set up a rival Center openly. Zhang Hao, holding the Comintern's imperial sword, gave Mao Zedong powerful support and stopped Zhang Guotao's splitting.

At this time the Nationalists, driven to the wall by Japan, were themselves seeking to work with the Soviet Union. Chiang Kai-shek felt out a channel to the CCP Center and entrusted the task to his brother-in-law T.V. Soong. Just then the Shanghai Special Branch system had lost contact with the CCP Center, and Xu Qiang and the others who kept up the struggle had also asked Song Qingling to help them reach the Center in northern Shaanxi. As it happened, T.V. Soong likewise asked Song Qingling to introduce a connection for reaching northern Shaanxi. So Song Qingling went to her eldest sister's husband, H.H. Kung, obtained a blank Finance Ministry travel pass, borrowed a sum for the journey, and entrusted Dong Jianwu with carrying a letter to northern Shaanxi. Dong Jianwu had once been a member of the CCP Special Branch; his public identity was that of a pastor, and he was also T.V. Soong's university classmate — a messenger acceptable to both Nationalists and Communists. Song Qingling charged Dong Jianwu: this journey was important, and would in time do the nation no small good. As they parted, Song Qingling took out one of her own name cards and told Dong Jianwu that if he ran into difficulty he could seek the help of Zhang Xueliang's wife, Yu Fengzhi.

Dong Jianwu took with him on this journey an attendant who kept his identity hidden, Xu Guanghan of the Shanghai Special Branch. In January 1936 Dong Jianwu and Xu Guanghan reached Xi'an; Zhang Xueliang then had them flown to Fushi (Yan'an), and from there local Northeast Army cavalry escorted them, a journey of six days, to Wayaobao. After meeting the CCP central leaders, Xu Guanghan stayed behind to report on the Shanghai Special Branch, while Dong Jianwu returned to Shanghai in the company of Feng Xuefeng.

The central representative Feng Xuefeng, reaching Shanghai, first met Xu Qiang in secret and arranged for the Special Branch to stop its alarm work and change over to intelligence work. Then Feng Xuefeng went to call on his old acquaintance Lu Xun. Learning that the Red Army's Long March had ended in triumph, Lu Xun was greatly excited, and at once introduced Feng Xuefeng to Song Qingling.

History ought to record it: the second Nationalist-Communist cooperation in modern Chinese history took its first step from the exchange of word between the highest leaders of the two sides at the start of 1936. And history should not overlook that this exchange used an intelligence network.

Nanjing had put out a feeler to northern Shaanxi; how could Xi'an, near at hand, fall behind? At this time, apart from Chiang Kai-shek's own direct-line troops under Hu Zongnan, the main force "encircling" the northern Shaanxi base was Yang Hucheng's Northwest Army and Zhang Xueliang's Northeast Army.

The CCP Center resolved to develop work energetically toward these three forces. Back in the days of the Central Soviet Area, Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai had argued for joining with Cai Tingkai's 19th Route Army against Chiang; had it not been vetoed by the "Left" leadership, there might have been no need to abandon the Central Soviet Area for the Long March at all. The central leadership of those days missed its chance and steered the revolution into crisis; the CCP Center of now seized the historical opportunity decisively and set about united-front work toward the Northeast Army and the Northwest Army on its own initiative.

Zhang Xueliang, the Northeast Army's Young Marshal, had long wanted to establish contact with the CCP but had been at a loss how to begin. Back in 1928, Zhang Xueliang's father Zhang Zuolin had led the Northeast Army in occupying Beiping, marched openly into the Soviet consulate, and seized and strangled the Communist leader Li Dazhao. Later the Japanese military assassinated Zhang Zuolin, and Zhang Xueliang, taking over the Northeast Army, tried to establish contact with the Communists.

Given the Northeast Army's fierce wish to resist Japan, the CCP Northern Bureau had, from the September 18 Incident of 1931 on, sent Liu Lanbo to work within the Northeast Army. But the guiding line for enemy-army work at the time was to want the men and not the officers, so no Party members were recruited among the officers.

Zhang Xueliang, appointed by the Nationalists deputy commander-in-chief for suppressing the Communists, led his troops off to Shaanxi. Chiang Kai-shek's forcing the Northeast Army to fight the Red Army instead of the Japanese stirred deep resentment among the Northeast Army's officers and men. As newcomers, the Northeast Army and the Northwest Army did not get on well together either. This awkward state of things made Zhang Xueliang anxious to find a way out.

At the start of 1936 Zhang Xueliang sent men to Shanghai to seek out the Communists, and Song Qingling introduced to them Liu Ding, who had just escaped from the enemy camp and had not yet restored his organizational ties. In March Liu Ding went to Xi'an and talked with Zhang Xueliang for several days, then went to northern Shaanxi to report to the CCP Center.

Yang Hucheng, commander of the Northwest Army, had long leaned toward the Communists, and the CCP Central Military Commission's Shanghai rear office had set up a Northwest Special Branch to work within Yang's forces. When Yang Hucheng was garrisoned in Henan in 1929, he already had the underground Communist Song Qiyun as his secretary. In 1930, when Yang Hucheng was in charge of Shaanxi-Gansu military and civil affairs, he further invited the Communist Nan Hanchen to be secretary-general of the Shaanxi provincial government. Nan Hanchen in turn introduced his fellow townsman Li Zhifeng to be confidential secretary of the Xi'an Pacification Headquarters, where he secretly monitored and broke cipher telegrams. Yang Hucheng's guard regiment commander Zhang Hanmin was an underground Communist too, and had escorted Pan Zili to the Fourth Front Army in northern Sichuan, Wang Feng to the 25th Red Army, Zhang Qingfu to northern Shaanxi, and Chen Gang to Xi'an. Yang Hucheng's gendarme battalion was a gathering of the Northwest Special Branch's Communists: the battalion commander Jin Minsheng, the deputy commander Tong Lusheng, the instructors Xie Hua, Wang Genzeng, Xu Binru, Li Mu'an, Du Yuhua, and others. Later Chiang Kai-shek removed Yang Hucheng from the chairmanship of the Shaanxi provincial government and forced the expulsion of Nan Hanchen.

In August 1935 Wang Shiying led the remnants of the Special Branch from Shanghai to Tianjin and worked energetically toward the Northeast Army and the Northwest Army, while Nan Hanchen carried on the work toward Yang Hucheng through Shen Bochun.

In the winter of 1935 Mao Zedong sent Wang Feng to Xi'an to make contact with Yang Hucheng; Yang Hucheng, fearing Wang Feng might be a Nationalist agent, first held him in custody, and only after Wang Shiying and Nan Hanchen had vouched for him did he sign an agreement with the CCP side. Mao Zedong also sent his own secretary Zhang Wenbin to stay in Xi'an as a Red Army representative, his public identity being chief secretary of the political department of the 17th Route Army headquarters.

The CCP Center set great store by the united-front work in the direction of Xi'an, and set up a dedicated CCP Central Liaison Bureau (also called the Northwest Liaison Bureau), its director none other than Li Kenong, foremost of the Central Special Branch's "Three Heroes of Longtan."

Li Kenong, who had done intelligence and security work, now entered a new field too — united-front work. The CCP Central Liaison Bureau made the Northeast Army and the Northwest Army its focus, and Li Kenong began with work on the Northeast Army's prisoners. Gao Fuyuan, a Northeast Army regiment commander, had been taken prisoner in battle, and grieved that he had not died on the field against Japan only to suffer humiliation at Communist hands. Li Kenong, skilled at writing for the stage, rewrote a play himself, "You Took the Wrong Road," telling the story of a Nationalist regiment commander who, taken prisoner by the Red Army, turns his gun around to fight Japan — and it moved Gao Fuyuan to weep aloud. The next day Gao Fuyuan asked of his own accord to see Li Kenong, and said he would try to persuade Zhang Xueliang to join the Red Army in resisting Japan. Li Kenong laid careful plans and had Gao Fuyuan act by stages. Back in the Northeast Army, Gao Fuyuan reported directly to Zhang Xueliang, and was then sent by Zhang Xueliang to the Red Army's station to negotiate. And so contact between the Communist Party and the Northeast Army was opened.

In February 1936 Li Kenong, as a Red Army representative, went to Luochuan, where the Northeast Army was stationed, to negotiate. On March 3 Zhang Xueliang, back from Nanjing, flew a plane to Luochuan himself. The two sides talked frankly. Zhang Xueliang proposed that the Communist Party should change its line of "opposing Chiang while resisting Japan," and Li Kenong set out the Communist Party's strategy for fighting Japan. As their differences narrowed, on March 5 they reached a preliminary agreement between the Red Army and the Northeast Army to halt the civil war and resist Japan together.

On March 9 the CCP's plenipotentiary Zhou Enlai came in person to the city of Yan'an, held by the Northeast Army, and held talks with Zhang Xueliang in a Catholic church. These talks changed not only the political scene of the northwest but the history of China. In 2001, the hundred-year-old Zhang Xueliang still said, in Hawaii: "Zhou Enlai is the man I most admired in all my life!"

The CCP Center set great store by the united-front work at Xi'an, formed a Northeast Army Work Committee led by Zhou Enlai, and pressed the work on the Northeast Army hard. It also sent Ye Jianying to Zhang Xueliang's forces as the CCP Center's resident representative, to carry on united-front work toward the Northeast Army, the Northwest Army, and the whole northwest region.

With a great enemy at the gate, joining to resist Japan had become the wish of the whole nation. But Chiang Kai-shek went the opposite way, coming to Xi'an in person to press Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng into a war of suppression against the Communists. The friendly armies of the northwest would not slaughter their own kind, and on December 12, 1936, Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng remonstrated with Chiang Kai-shek by force — the Xi'an Incident had broken out.

The situation changed in an instant. The Red Army, the Northeast Army, and the Northwest Army negotiated with Chiang as a tripartite unity, and as a tripartite unity prepared for war against the central army. The Northeast Army moved troops to Tongguan to bar the central army from entering Shaanxi, and Zhang Xueliang, of his own accord, yielded Yan'an to the Communists.
9. "Special Zones" and "Special Agents"

Yan'an was a "big" place in northern Shaanxi, from of old the seat of a prefecture, a prefectural-grade city. When the CCP reached Yan'an in those days, it was like coming upon a treasured spot at the very end of the road.

The Communist Party's foundation lay in its rural base areas, the Nationalists' strength in the cities and communication lines. The Central Soviet Area had once been the Communists' largest base, holding 15 county towns and two and a half million people; but as for prefectural-grade cities, it had only ever taken Zhangzhou and Ganzhou, and had pulled out of them after a few days. When the CCP Center reached the northwest base, this foothold amounted to no more than six county towns — Yanchang, Yanchuan, Bao'an, and the rest — with guerrilla zones spread over seventeen counties of northern Shaanxi and eastern Gansu. And prefectural cities like Yan'an, Suide, Dingbian, and Qingyang were driven like nails into the guerrilla zones, where the Nationalist troops holding the prefectural seats could send forces into the countryside to sweep it at any time.

On December 12, 1936, Zhang and Yang raised the Xi'an Incident, and the very next day it was decided to give up Yan'an. On the 14th, Zhang Xueliang flew a plane to Yan'an to welcome Zhou Enlai. Zhou Enlai came from Wayaobao to Yan'an with Ye Jianying, Li Kenong, Zeng Shan, and others; Zhou, Ye, and Li at once flew on to Xi'an, leaving Zeng Shan to take Yan'an over.

Zeng Shan moved into the Yan'an Normal School and ran head-on into the dean of studies, Lin Disheng. Lin Disheng had been in the same Party branch as Zeng Shan in Shanghai; he had later studied in Japan, and when he came back to Shanghai the Center had already withdrawn, so, unable to reconnect his organizational ties, he had come to Yan'an to look for the Party. Lin Disheng told Zeng Shan that the moment Zhang Xueliang left, the Northeast Army had withdrawn from Yan'an; still in the town were the Nationalist county party headquarters and a security corps, and, hearing that Communist cadres were lodging at the Normal School, they meant to move in the night. And so Zeng Shan, the great takeover officer, had to slip away by night once more. Only when the Red Army's main body hurried up on the 17th did Zeng Shan march into Yan'an at last, with his head held high.

Yan'an was in fact the communications hub of northern Shaanxi. The high road south led to Xi'an, to the northwest it ran toward Dingbian and Ningxia, and to the northeast it joined Suide and Inner Mongolia. The streets of Yan'an ran crisscross, a tall city wall embraced the whole town, roads reached everywhere and could take motor vehicles, and outside the East Gate there was even an airfield.

Yan'an held the advantage of its hills and waters. The Nanchuan River came up from the south, the Yan River came down from the northwest, the two rivers met and flowed east into the Yellow River. At their meeting stood three hills — Qingliang to the north, Fenghuang to the southwest, Jialing to the east. Yan'an, rising on the river delta, was truly a place of scenic splendor. Legend has it that in one of his former lives the Buddha Shakyamuni "gave his own flesh to save a dove" here, and from this Yan'an took its old name, "Fushi." The Ten Thousand Buddha Cave on Qingliang Hill holds a myriad carved Buddha figures in relief; the old town of Yan'an, at the foot of Fenghuang Hill, backed by hill and facing river, lay like a jade dish; and the Tang-dynasty pagoda on Jialing Hill kept the floods of the Yan River in check. On Pagoda Hill the celebrated Song figure Fan Zhongyan left a great inscription, "First to Worry, Last to Enjoy," a shortening of his line "to worry before all under Heaven worry, and to take joy after all under Heaven have taken theirs." Yanzhou, "the throat of five roads," had been a military stronghold of China's northwest from ancient times. Mizhi had produced a Li Zicheng, who ran downhill from northern Shaanxi to Hebei and marched his army all the way to take the city of Beijing.

A place blessed by wind and water, where the wind rises and the waters stir. Having come by such a Yan'an, Mao Zedong was bound to work it with all his heart.

After the Xi'an Incident, the situation in China turned. Chiang Kai-shek accepted the call to unite with the Communists and resist Japan, the Nationalists' Bandit-Suppression Headquarters was abolished, and peace came to the northwest. Having marched into Yan'an, the CCP Center wired the Nationalist Center proposing to halt the civil war and resist Japan as one. It further proposed, of its own accord: to end the standoff of two rival regimes, and to rename the Soviet Workers' and Peasants' Democratic Government the "Special Zone Government" of the Republic of China.

Could there be different regions within a single country? After the Zunyi Conference the CCP Center's thinking changed greatly, opening and inventing. No longer to call itself the "Chinese Soviet Republic" was to show its unity under the central government; to call itself the "Special Zone Government" was to show that it still differed from the other Nationalist-ruled areas. To accept the leadership of the Nationalist central government and at the same time keep its own independence — this was the "special" in the "Special Zone" government.

In 1937 the Nationalist-Communist "special zone" was born at Yan'an. In 1979, China had another "special zone," Shenzhen, of reform and opening.

When the Guangdong Provincial Committee secretary proposed to the Center an export-processing zone at Shenzhen, Deng Xiaoping, looking at Xi Zhongxun, that cadre out of Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia, thought of the old Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia special zone and declared on the spot: "We can mark out a piece of ground and call it a special zone. Shaan-Gan-Ning was a special zone, after all. The Center has no money; you'll have to do it yourselves, blaze a trail!"

Thousands of li apart, forty years apart in time, and history proves it: the founding of both special zones marked a major change of policy for the Chinese Communist Party. The growth of both special zones brought extraordinary success to China's revolution and construction.

And what of the "special agents" inside the "special zone"?

The Special Zone Government of February 1937 had its capital at Yan'an. Lin Boqu served as chairman, Zhang Guotao as acting chairman. Zhang Guotao proposed that, since they were accepting the central government's leadership, the security bureau ought to be abolished.

Zhou Xing fought hard for it: however the organization might change, security work could not be abolished.

So it was decided to place the security bureau under the garrison command, renamed the Security Office of the Special Zone Garrison Command — to outsiders no more than one office subordinate to the garrison command. Below it, the various districts and counties still called theirs security bureaus, a grander name than their superior's. The Special Zone Government was "special," and the intelligence and security front more "special" still: the knife stayed always in Communist hands.

In September of that year the "special zone" was renamed and reorganized as the "Border Region," its jurisdiction taking in the Eighth Route Army's recruiting area, and, formally passed by the Nationalist government's Executive Yuan, it had 26 counties, 130,000 square kilometers, and two million people. The security office was constituted as the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office. Whatever its rank to outsiders, within it was always the leading organ of intelligence and security work. Zhou Xing served as its chief and Du Liqing as deputy chief, and under them: the secretary Li Qiming; the First Section (intelligence) under Bu Lu; the Second Section (reconnaissance) under Xie Ziqun; the Third Section (criminal) under Zhao Cangbi; the Fourth Section (pre-trial) under Tan Zhengwen; the Fifth Section (organ security) under Chen Fusheng; the Sixth Section (public order) under Liu Huping, who also served as chief of the Yan'an municipal police; the deputy chief of the Red Army work section Wang Taihe; the general-affairs section chief Hui Xili; and the security battalion commander Hu Youcai.

The security office laid out Yan'an's perimeter defense, setting up checkpoints on all four sides of Yan'an — at Qilipu, at Yongping in Yanchuan, at Yanshuiguan, and at Chafang in Fu County — and anyone coming to or from Yan'an out of the Nationalist areas had to hold a pass issued by the security office. The security office also set up a postal-and-telegraph inspection station in town, stationed right inside the Nationalist-controlled post and telegraph office. And the security battalion directly under the office reinforced with special care the guard on the seat of the central organs.

It is worth noting that at this time, among the CCP central organs there was neither a "State Political Security Bureau" nor a "Central Special Branch," only this "Security Office."

As China's political landscape changed and was reshaped on a great scale, the CCP's intelligence and security system too entered a period of great transformation and readjustment.

Lecturing at the Red Army University on "Problems of Strategy in China's Revolutionary War," Mao Zedong summed up the combat experience of the ten years of civil war. "A commander's correct dispositions stem from a correct resolve, a correct resolve stems from a correct judgment, and a correct judgment stems from thorough and necessary reconnaissance and from pondering over the various reconnaissance materials in their connection." The judgment that this "reconnaissance" and "pondering" arrive at — that is "intelligence".

Mao Zedong, who prized intelligence above all, soon had his chance to put his intelligence gift to use.

On the night of July 7, 1937, the Marco Polo Bridge Incident suddenly broke out. The next day the CCP Center was the first to wire the whole country, demanding that "the whole nation carry on the war of resistance". On July 14, Mao Zedong and Zhu De ordered the Red Army to "complete its preparations within ten days and await the order to resist Japan."

Yan'an became, with that, the headquarters from which the CCP Center directed the war against Japan.

When the Red Army began its Long March in 1934, "going north to resist Japan" had still been only a slogan. That it set down in northern Shaanxi in 1935 was first because this was the only place with ground held by its own people, and second out of a thought that northern Shaanxi lay near the Mongolian border and could open a line to the Soviet Union. The times made the ground: this northern Shaanxi had now become, in truth, the finest position from which to strike at the Japanese invaders.

After the Yan'an Red Army was reorganized into the Eighth Route Army, it struck directly into the enemy's rear and built base areas in Shanxi, Hebei, Shandong, and elsewhere. By the time victory in the War of Resistance came and the lost lands were recovered, the Nationalists, far off in the southwest, could not reach so far, and the Communists took a great deal of northern ground in passing.

This Yan'an was a place of commanding advantage.

Still, the security office dared not grow careless. Years of war and chaos had left public order in northern Shaanxi in disarray. If the Communists were to strike root in Yan'an, they would have to govern it, and govern it in earnest.

Wang Shiying led the remnants of the Special Branch to Tianjin and, with the CCP Northern Bureau having suffered enormous damage, preserved and built up an important force; the foundation of work he laid within the Northeast Army, the Northwest Army, and the Shanxi-Suiyuan Army helped the CCP Center gain its footing in northern Shaanxi. Wang Shiying's son Wang Minqing lived with his father from the age of five and took part in many secret activities, and after 1949 served in turn as the personal physician of Jiang Qing and of Deng Xiaoping. Song Qiyun was secretly arrested by Juntong in 1941 and killed together with Yang Hucheng on the eve of Chongqing's liberation in 1949; his young son Song Zhenzhong was the model for "Little Turnip Head" in the novel Red Crag.

Li Yun: former secretary-general of the China Welfare Institute; interviewed June 26, 2005. After the head of the Shanghai Special Branch, Qiu Jifu, was abducted, Xu Qiang led the remaining personnel in carrying on the work and trying to restore contact with the Center. Xu Qiang's wife Li Yun was assigned to liaise with Song Qingling and long served as Song Qingling's secretary. Xu Qiang's cousin Xu Guanghan was sent to northern Shaanxi to make contact with the Center. After 1949 Li Yun long served as secretary-general of the China Welfare Institute, of which Song Qingling was president.

CCP Hubei Provincial Committee Party History Research Office, ed.: Wu Defeng, CCP Party History Press. Wu Defeng was a leader of the CCP's secret courier system, founding the Liaison Bureau during the Second Revolutionary Civil War and rebuilding it during the War of Resistance against Japan.

Chen Fusheng: former deputy-bureau-grade cadre of the Ministry of Public Security; interviewed June 19, 2001. Chen Fusheng entered the State Security Bureau in 1932 and served as a division special commissioner before the Long March. The only division-grade cadre of the Third Red Army still living, blind though he was, he had a remarkably clear memory, vividly describing the look of the security bureau's people in the Red Army days, and telling too of how he himself put down a division chief of staff who tried to flee.

Xie Ziqun: former Wuhan municipal Party secretary and chairman of the political-legal committee; interviewed May 27, 2005. Xie Ziqun had served as investigation section chief of the First Front Army's security bureau and knew the case of the Nationalist agents' attempt to assassinate Zhu De. After the Long March he was investigation section chief of the Northwest Security Bureau, and after 1949 long served as chief of the Wuhan municipal police, in charge of the guard work for Mao Zedong's 31 visits to Wuhan.

Zhuo Xiong: former Fujian provincial Party secretary; interviewed November 14, 2005. A native of Tai'he in Jiangxi, he had taken part in the Jinggangshan struggle and served as security bureau chief of the Ninth Red Army Corps, the only army-grade Red Army security cadre still living. He held that the "Left" over-widening of the Soviet-area suppression was not the fault of a few individuals but had been fairly general.

Zou Yu: interviewed January 4, 2013. Zou Yu had taken part in Yan'an in drafting the "CCP Report to the Comintern on the Northern Shaanxi Suppression Question," for which he interviewed Xi Zhongxun specially and examined his injuries.

Liu Siqi, ed.: Mao Zedong in the Central Soviet Area, Dongfanghong Series, China Bookstore Press. The book's editor was once the wife of Mao Zedong's son Mao Anying, and it records faithfully the state of the suppression in the Jiangxi Soviet during Mao Zedong's time as secretary of the First Front Army's General Front Committee.

Liu Bingrong: A Full Documentary of the Soviet-Area Suppression, Huashan Literature and Art Press. With a preface by Li Zhenjun, political commissar of the Chinese People's Armed Police Force, it gives a full account of the suppression in each of the main base areas, including a list of "martyrs who did not die under the enemy's butcher's knife" — proof of how grievous the loss of cadres from the errors of the suppression really was.

Yao Zijian: interviewed February 25, 2010. The Central Special Branch's Wang Xuewen brought Yao Zijian into the Party, and he lay concealed for years in the Nationalist army headquarters in Nanjing, right up to the nation's liberation.

Ouyang Yi: former deputy political commissar of the artillery; interviewed October 2002. Ouyang Yi was one of the most senior CCP intelligence and security cadres still living; when the Central Political Security Office was set up in January 1931, Ouyang Yi was one of its five members. During the War of Resistance he served as deputy head of the General Political Department's traitor-elimination department, and after 1949 as deputy political commissar of the artillery.

Liu Yin: Growing Up in Battle, and Recollections and Historical Materials of the Signal Corps, PLA Press. Wang Zheng and others not only founded the Red Army's wireless work but also began radio technical reconnaissance, opening an important new path for the CCP's intelligence work.

Wu Xing: "An Inquiry into Some Questions in the History of CCP Radio Technical Reconnaissance," CCP Party History Studies, 2010, no. 1. This essay rarely lifts the veil on the founding and the great contributions of the Military Commission's Second Bureau.

Dai Jingyuan: Unforgettable Years. The author once served as director of the Military Commission's Second Bureau.

Li Weihan: Recollections and Studies, CCP Party History Materials Press. Li Weihan took part in 1935 in the CCP Center's handling of the wrongful suppression in northern Shaanxi, and in 1982 was entrusted by the Center with chairing a symposium on northwest historical questions.

Guo Hongtao: former deputy director of the State Economic Commission; interviewed November 28, 2002. This man, one of the leaders of the northern Shaanxi base, recalled that the northern Shaanxi suppression had been carried out on the instructions of the "central representative resident with the Northern Bureau." The CCP Center was then on the Long March, and this representative had been appointed by the CCP delegation in Moscow. When the suppression reached the point of arresting the founders of the Shaan-Gan border base, Liu Zhidan, Gao Gang, and Zhang Xiushan, Guo Hongtao felt something was wrong and said to Zhu Lizhi: "Cut off my head, and still I will not believe that Liu, Gao, and Zhang are counter-revolutionaries!"

Nie Hongjun: "The Making of the Liu Zhidan Frame-up," Revolutionary History Materials (1), Literary and Historical Materials Press. This essay frankly admits the wrongful suppression he himself carried out.

Recollections of Ma Wenrui, Shaanxi People's Press. Ma Wenrui had worked in both of the northwest's base areas and lived through the forced-confession suppression himself. At the time, all cadres of the 26th Red Army from battalion level up, and all cadres of the Shaan-Gan border base from county level up, were locked up, and more than 200 people were killed. In the courtyard where Ma Wenrui was held, a pit had even been dug, ready to bury people.

Tu Zhankui: former deputy head of the Qinghai provincial machinery department; interviewed 1999. Tu Zhankui joined the army in the founding days of the Shaan-Gan border Red Army and had served as bodyguard to Liu Zhidan and Xi Zhongxun; a security cadre himself, he nonetheless loathed the wrongful suppression.

Geng Hong: A Loyal Heart for the People. Geng Hong, former deputy head of the Shanxi provincial public security department, recalled that many local cadres in northern Shaanxi could make no sense of the suppression at the time: "Had the Center not arrived in time, the northern Shaanxi Red Army and the 25th Red Army would surely have come to blows." Geng Hong served as a secretary when the Northwest Security Bureau was first set up.

Pei Zhouyu: former political commissar of the armored corps; interviewed April 12, 2011. Pei Zhouyu had long done army security work and had served as special commissioner of the 28th Red Army; in the eastern-expedition fighting he witnessed the army commander Liu Zhidan fall to a bullet.

Jin Chongji, ed.: A Biography of Mao Zedong, Central Party Literature Press. This book, together with other memoir essays, shows that the CCP Center's attitude in halting the wrongful suppression in northern Shaanxi was resolute and its measures effective; but because the pressures of war left its footing unsteady, a deeper reckoning with the errors of the suppression had to wait for later.

Wang Dongxing: former CCP vice-chairman, director of the Central General Office, and head of the Central Guard Bureau; interviewed April 24, 1995. Zhou Xing's wife Yang Yuying took the author to interview Zhou Xing's old comrade-in-arms Wang Dongxing. During the Long March Zhou Xing had been Wang Dongxing's superior; when Zhou Xing died of illness, the CCP vice-chairman Wang Dongxing kept watch at his bedside, and later approved the move of Yang Yuying and her whole family to settle in Beijing.

Snow: Random Notes on Red China, CCP Party History teaching reference material. There is no dedicated chapter on Zhou Xing in Snow's book Red Star Over China. In fact Snow also wrote Random Notes on Red China, whose sixth chapter deals specially with the CCP's security organs, introducing first Zhou Xing's brief biography and then recording "Security Questions and (the System of) Justice." The written record in Zhou Xing's own kept archive agrees entirely with Snow's Random Notes but for a few words — which suggests that Zhou Xing may have prepared something in writing before the interview.

Li Haiwen and Wang Xin: "Nationalist-Communist Relations from 1935 to Early 1937," CCP Party History Materials (42). This essay recounts in detail the secret exchanges between the Nationalist and Communist centers, in which the Special Branch cadre Zhang Zihua traveled secretly between northern Shaanxi and Nanjing many times.

Kai Cheng: Li Kenong: An Outstanding Leader of the CCP's Hidden Front, China Friendship Publishing Company. This biography, vetted by the Ministry of State Security, is the authoritative account of Li Kenong's intelligence career.

Gao Dahui: son of Gao Chongmin; interviewed March 27, 2007. Gao Chongmin came into contact with communist ideas in his early years, once persuaded Zhang Xueliang to release the arrested Manchuria provincial Party secretary Liu Shaoqi, accepted the Communist Party's leadership in 1935, supported Yan'an economically, and took part in launching the Xi'an Incident.

Nan Yanbin: grandson of Nan Hanchen; interviewed March 24, 2007. Nan Hanchen took part in Shanxi's 1911 Revolution in his early years, was on close personal terms with the Northwest Army's generals, and did secret work in turn in the CCP Central Military Commission's intelligence department, the Central Special Branch, and the Northern Liaison Bureau.

Jiang Shuchen: The Hundred-Year-Old General Tong Lusheng, China Federation of Literary and Art Circles Press. Its subject was a man as old as the twentieth century, who took part in the May Fourth Movement in his early years, joined the CCP during the Great Revolution, then went to do army work in Yang Hucheng's northwest forces, took part in the Xi'an Incident, and long did military united-front work afterward.

Li Li: Remembering My Father Li Kenong, People's Press. Li Kenong's son Li Li joined the Party early in the War of Resistance, knew the affairs of the CCP's hidden front well, and served as deputy head of the General Staff's communications department; the Li Kenong memoir Li Li preserved reveals the secret of whether Zhang Xueliang joined the CCP.

Liang Ji: former deputy director and police chief of the Shanghai Maritime Transport Bureau; interviewed October 26, 2000. When Zeng Shan walked into the Yan'an Normal School, Liang Ji was studying at that very school. As one of the few Yan'an-born cadres in the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office and the Yan'an municipal police, Liang Ji always took care to know Yan'an's local conditions and history.

Li Qiming: former standing secretary of the CCP Yunnan Provincial Committee; interviewed October 18, 1995. Of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office leaders still living, Li Qiming had held the highest post and served the longest, and his memory was excellent. Li Qiming and Luo Qingchang jointly edited Historical Materials on the Intelligence of the CCP Northwest Bureau's Social Affairs Department, and the author interviewed Li Qiming many times, seeking his guidance face to face on many doubtful points.

NOTES (these are prose too; judge them):

- [their first reaction is a startled cry] Tewu (特务), literally “special service” or “special affairs.” In modern usage it is the ordinary word for a secret agent, and in both parties' propaganda it carried the menacing charge of the enemy's spies and secret police. The chapter's opening argument is that the word began as a neutral one; see the author's word-study below. Where he discusses it as a word it is kept as tewu; elsewhere it is rendered by sense.
- [Juntong agents had so fearsome a name] Juntong (军统), the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Nationalist Military Affairs Commission, was Chiang Kai-shek's military secret service under Dai Li; it and the party-run Zhongtong are the CCP's chief adversaries through this book. Both are treated at length in Chapter 2.
- [Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun!] Mao Zedong's most quoted dictum, spoken at the August 7 Conference of 1927. The standard English rendering is preserved here.
- [the August 7 Conference] An emergency conference of the CCP Central Committee on August 7, 1927, held after the collapse of the first Nationalist-Communist united front and the Nationalist massacres; it set the Party on the path of armed uprising.
- [three rooms above and three below] Shikumen (“stone-gate”) houses are the terraced townhouses of early-twentieth-century Shanghai and other treaty-port cities, built around a small courtyard behind a stone door-frame — the characteristic cover for underground work.
- [an "adventurers' paradise."] A stock epithet for interwar Shanghai, whose foreign concessions lay outside Chinese jurisdiction and drew spies, gangsters, and refugees alike.
- ["Three Heroes of Longtan"] Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di (龙潭三杰). Planted inside the Nationalist agent chief Xu Enzeng's own apparatus, their warning after Gu Shunzhang's 1931 defection allowed Zhou Enlai to move the CCP Center to safety — the episode this section recounts. Corroborated: the story is standard in both official and independent accounts of CCP intelligence.
- [Notice of Wu Hao and Others Leaving the Communist Party] The “Wu Hao Incident.” “Wu Hao” was a Zhou Enlai alias; the notice was a Nationalist forgery meant to suggest he had defected. It resurfaced decades later, during the Cultural Revolution, as material used against Zhou.
- [the "AB Corps,"] The “Anti-Bolshevik Corps,” an alleged counter-revolutionary network. The scholarly consensus, and now the CCP's own official verdict (quoted by the author later in this section), is that the organization was largely a fabrication and that the purge conducted in its name killed thousands of innocent Red Army members on confessions extracted under torture. Contradicted, as a real conspiracy of the scale charged.
- [the "Futian Incident"] The December 1930 mutiny at Futian by Red Army officers resisting the AB Corps purge. Mao Zedong's own role in initiating and driving the Jiangxi purge is documented and is more central than the author's emphasis on “devolved” authority suggests; independent historians treat the episode as a formative act of intra-Party terror. Partly corroborated / the author's framing is partisan.
- ["coerce, confess, believe,"] Biru gong xin (逼供信): to extract a confession by force and then believe it. The phrase became the standard shorthand for the method that drove the purges' self-multiplying “confessions.”
- [three foreign letters, "GBW."] The source prints the badge letters as “GBW,” then identifies them (below) with the Russian GPU. The letters as printed do not match a transliteration of ГПУ (GPU); rendered here as printed, with the discrepancy flagged. Either the badge or the author's reading of it is inexact.
- [the three provinces of Jiangxi, Fujian, and Zhejiang] As printed. Geographically this is doubtful: the Jinggangshan base straddled the Jiangxi-Hunan border, and the later Central Soviet Area lay chiefly in Jiangxi and Fujian; Zhejiang was not part of either, and the figures given describe the larger Central Soviet Area rather than the small Jinggangshan base. Contradicted by the geographic record; rendered as the source has it.
- [seized and strangled the Communist leader Li Dazhao] The source dates Zhang Zuolin's raid on the Soviet embassy and the execution of Li Dazhao to 1928. In fact both occurred in April 1927 (the raid on April 6, the execution on April 28). Contradicted; rendered as printed.
- [Red Star Over China] Edgar Snow's 1937 book, the first sympathetic Western account of the CCP, based on his 1936 visit to the Shaanxi base. The author's point is that Snow's changed view of the Communists owed something to his interviews with the security chief Zhou Xing.
- [the "sword and shield."] The paired emblem — sword for the offensive intelligence arm, shield for the protective security arm — echoes the insignia of the Soviet security services and is today that of China's Ministry of State Security.
- [the Xi'an Incident had broken out] On December 12, 1936, Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng detained Chiang Kai-shek at Xi'an to force him to halt the civil war and resist Japan. Its peaceful resolution, brokered in part by Zhou Enlai, produced the second Nationalist-Communist united front.
- [the Marco Polo Bridge Incident] The clash at Lugouqiao, southwest of Beijing, on the night of July 7, 1937, conventionally taken as the start of the full-scale War of Resistance against Japan (the Second Sino-Japanese War).
- [a Li Zicheng] Li Zicheng (1606–1645), the rebel leader from Mizhi in northern Shaanxi whose peasant army took Beijing in 1644 and ended the Ming dynasty — the author's portent for a movement rising out of the same poor country.
