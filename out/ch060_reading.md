### The Telephone

The telephone came in in the eighth year of the Guangxu reign of the Qing (a renwu year), fifty-odd years ago now. There was then an Englishman by the name of Pi Xiaopu who first set one working in the concession area, with two exchanges, a south and a north — the south office at Shiliupu, the north on Zhengfeng Street (that is, Canton Road). But there were no telephone instruments in those days, nor any ringing of a bell to call the number, and an automatic dial was further off still: if you wished to speak with someone, you went to the office in person, and for a fee of twelve cash could talk with your party. Later, as business fell slack and the takings would not cover the costs, it was given up. The next year — the guiwei year — a Catholic priest, a Frenchman named Neng Mugu, set it going afresh, put in proper telephone instruments, and ran a line from the Xujiahui church to the foreign firms of the two concessions, English and French, that the weather might be reported over it. Afterwards, as people came to know its convenience, they had it put in one after another, until now — beginning from a few dozen instruments and passing through several decades — the numbers have reached the tens of thousands.

In the old days you gave your number first, and an operator put you through before you could speak; but ever since it was all changed to the automatic dial four years ago, to make a call you need only know the other party's number and give it a dial yourself, and you are talking — handier by far than the old giving-of-the-number.

This handy instrument of communication was invented, they say, by a Westerner named Delüfeng, and so it is also called the delüfeng (for the three characters 德律風 see Huang Shiquan's Notes on Old Shanghai).

Jian Gong notes: By what this reporter knows, the telephone was invented by one Graham Bell, a native of the American province of Canada, and the three characters 德律風 (delüfeng) are a transliteration of the English "Telephone." Construed by the sense of the English, the first half, "Tele," means "long distance," and the latter half, "Phone," means "sound" — just as the gramophone is "Grama-phone" in English, and the sound picture has its "Vitaphone" (that is, weitaifeng). So when Huang Shiquan, in his Notes on Old Shanghai, will have it that the three characters 德律風 are the personal name of the telephone's inventor, it is rather too arbitrary, and comes near to pure invention. I shall, when occasion serves, translate out in full the "telephone" entry from an English encyclopaedia, for the reader's pleasure. The author adds: what my friend Mr. Jian Gong says has its reasons too, and so I set it down here as well; but how the matter truly stands — let the learned come and settle it by inquiry!
