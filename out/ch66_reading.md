## The Imperial Army Lands

When the government fleet and its convoy of transports appeared in the offing off Esashi, Toshizō was still in the bed, holding Oyuki's body in his arms.

Oyuki's topknot had come quite undone on the blanket.

(Very lustful, indeed.──)

Oyuki did not say it aloud, but she was taken aback.

The Toshizō of former days had been more mindful of appearances; even at Yūhigaoka in Osaka he had not been like this.

About the time the window began to whiten, the two of them, without noticing it, fell asleep.

But before even one koku had passed, Toshizō drew Oyuki's body close.

"Oyuki—this is a hard thing for you, I know,"

Toshizō, finding it comical even to himself, it seemed, chuckled.

"No. I am not to be pitied,"

"You're putting on a brave face. Your eyes are still half asleep, Oyuki,"

"Untrue. It is your eyes, Toshizō, that still seem to be in a dream,"

"In a dream, yes,"

In Toshizō a stale poetic phrase was welling up with an almost carnal reality.

That he should be here now, alone with Oyuki, just the two of them, in an upper room looking down on the harbor of Hakodate—was this not itself a dream?

(Life too—is it a thing like a dream within a dream?)

This too was a stale conceit; but seen from Toshizō's present state of mind it was exactly so. His thirty-five years of life had gone by like a dream.

The days by the Tama River in Bushū Tama, the Shieikan days in Edo, the enlistment in the Rōshigumi, the going up to the capital, the forming of the Shinsengumi, the countless sword-fights in the streets of Kyoto... those several scenes rose up only in a kind of false coloring, as though one were looking at a stage-flat or a picture-scroll.

A dream, the world of men too.

so Toshizō thought.

Toshizō now had nothing left but the self that looked back on it. For he meant, when the enemy landed, to fight while there was fighting to be done, and then to die.

For Toshizō there was, any longer, no future but death.

"I did it, Oyuki,"

{j} said Toshizō, all at once.

Oyuki looked up, startled. She was a woman with beautiful lashes.

"Did what, may I ask?"

"Oh, nothing. Only that I did it,"

he said in his broken way, and laughed. Had he any gift for eloquence, he would have wished to say, "I have lived to the full"—though it had been but thirty-five short years.

(My name will live on as an evil name. The names of those who went too far all live on among men as evil names.)

In Toshizō there was beginning to be born a certain ease, in which he observed himself no longer as his living self but as a figure in a play.

No—not ease; the Toshizō who now observed the past might be another person, newly born from within Toshizō.

"Oyuki.──"

{j} and he held her hard. He was pressing her body. Oyuki strove with all her might to receive it.

For Toshizō, there was no longer any way to feel that he was living now, save within Oyuki's body.

No—there was one thing more. It was to fight.

Outside of these, the whole of Toshizō's present world had vanished.

And Oyuki too—whether she felt in every part of her the groaning of Toshizō's life, this something striving to burst forth at the last—received him with a heart utterly dried of all grief.

The Oyuki on the blanket had become body alone. There was no head. A head was of no use whatever at such a time. The body alone was the one thing that took in all of Toshizō—his feeling and his past, his grief and his logic, his poesy and his remorse and his content, the whole of it. Oyuki moved her body in a rapture. Through that warm membrane she sought to draw Toshizō into herself. Oyuki kept her eyes shut in the rapture. Her lips were parted. She was smiling a little.

Presently Toshizō sank into sleep as though expiring.

Oyuki got softly down from the bed. She remembered that there was, surely, a mirror in the next room.

She meant to put her hair in order.

As she laid her hand on the knob of the door to the next room, she chanced to look at the window.

The sea lay below.

There were the warships of the Hakodate government.

That strange signal flags were flying at their mastheads, Oyuki of course did not notice.

Already the government forces had made an opposed landing at a fishing village called Otobe, fifteen ri from Hakodate, had driven off the thirty Hakodate-government troops stationed nearby, and were putting their order of advance in train. Word of it had come in haste to Goryōkaku and to Hakodate, and the proper signals had gone up on the warships in the harbor as well.

About the time Oyuki had mended her disordered hair, set her face to rights, and put on her kimono properly, Toshizō awoke.

Or perhaps he had only kept his eyes shut so as to let Oyuki set herself in order.

Toshizō pulled on his trousers.

Slinging the suspenders over his shoulders, he looked at the window.

A signal was up on the warships. Toshizō knew it for the signal calling on the foreigners resident in the town of Hakodate to take refuge.

"Oyuki, are you ready?"

"Yes,"

{j} and Oyuki came in. Toshizō's eyes widened. She had slipped back into the crisp, composed woman of before, so that one could almost doubt whether the one who had been on the bed just now had been the same person.

Toshizō sat down on the edge of the bed and raised a foot to pull on his heavy boots.

"It has come,"

"What has come?"

Oyuki, stooping, took up one of the boots and made to put it on him.

"The enemy,"

Oyuki caught her breath. But over her head Toshizō sniffed at his hand.

"Your scent is still on it,"

"You wretch,"

Oyuki could not help a rueful smile. Where, she wondered, had the enemy come?

Toshizō said nothing. Oyuki asked no further.

***

Toshizō went down to the reception room on the lower floor. He asked the servant to call at once for Yūjirō, the master of the house.

Yūjirō came in haste.

"Forgive my calling you out. An order to take refuge has gone out in the town of Hakodate, has it not?"

"It has this moment gone out. The talk in the town is that the government forces have landed at Otobe,"

"Hakodate will not become a place of battle at once. There are foreign trading houses here. There are foreign ships in the harbor. The government forces will hold back and not bombard it. You had best carry Kōnoike's trade on as before."

"I mean to carry it on, of course,"

"Good nerve. Like an Osaka merchant,"

Toshizō commended Oyuki to him over and over. For this man it was almost tediously repeated insistence, and he bowed his head a little over the table.

"I beg it of you,"

"Say no more of it. Once Kōnoike has taken it in hand, it is surer than any warrant of the government forces,"

"It is trading on your good will, I know,"

and so saying, Toshizō brought over the saddlebag he had set in the corner of the room and took out all the money in it. It was in nibu gold pieces, sixty ryō in all.

"On the British ship Oyuki is to go home by, I want you to take one more cabin. This is that person's passage. Whatever is left over, hand to him from your own hand as a parting gift. Yes—see him carried as far as Shinagawa; after that he will go wherever he will,"

"I will undertake it; but who, may I ask, is it?"

"Ichimura Tetsunosuke. He came forward at the last enlistment of troopers, at Fushimi. A retainer of the Ōgaki domain in Mino—and far too young in years for it all. He was fifteen..."

"………"

"I took him on because they said he was like Okita. He was glad of it himself, and through all the shifting fights of the Kantō, the Ōshū country, and Ezo, he came after me in his innocence. I do not want to take him with me any farther as a companion on the road."

Just then Ichimura came from Goryōkaku to bring the report of the enemy's landing at Otobe.

"Yūjirō—this is the man,"

{j} and he clapped Tetsunosuke on the shoulder.

Afterward, when Ichimura had heard how things stood, he wept and begged to remain, and went so far as to say he would cut open his belly.

What Toshizō said to Ichimura Tetsunosuke is set down in Ichimura's surviving account.

By it,

Going west from Edo along the Kōshū highway, one comes to a post-station called Hino. The headman of that station, Satō Hikogorō, is my brother-in-law. Throw yourself on him and take flight.

This is a duty laid on you. Tell that Satō Hikogorō in full detail the course of the fighting until now. As for how you are to dispose of yourself, Hikogorō will surely see to it as one of his own kin.

Ichimura refused to the last. At that the commander grew greatly angry, and declared that if I would not obey his command he would strike me dead on the spot. His look then was the same fearful fury as when he was always angered, so that, overborne despite myself, I took the duty upon me at last.

On the spot Toshizō got a sheet of hanshi paper from Yūjirō, drew out the kozuka knife and cut some two sun off its end to make a narrow "kogirigami" slip, and on it,

{j} "The bearer of this—I commend his welfare to your care. Yoshitoyo,"

{j} he wrote in a fine small hand.

Further, as what seemed meant for a keepsake to send to Satō Hikogorō, he added a photograph.

It showed him in Western dress with a short sword at his side, taken after his coming to Hakodate. This became the one photograph of Toshizō now in existence.

Last, he sent one thing more: his sword.

It was the Izumi-no-kami Kanesada, which since the Kyoto days had come through more scenes of carnage at Toshizō's side than could be counted.

"Tetsunosuke, I am counting on you. Unless it is told from your lips, the ends of Kondō and Okita and the rest will come, in the end, to be no more than the deaths of vagabonds."

Toshizō can hardly have feared the judgment of later ages so very much. Had he feared it, then, being a man of some literary gift, he would surely have left some writing behind.

It was only that he wished, even to his kin alone, to leave his keepsakes and the record of his conduct in life passed on.

In particular, his brother-in-law Satō Hikogorō was more than merely kin. In the days of the Shinsengumi's forming, when the accounts were still straitened, Kondō had often begged him and had money sent; he was, one might say, the paymaster of the founding days. To report the end of the Shinsengumi to its paymaster was a duty, if one chose to call it so.

There is a strange thing.

Toshizō, to the very last, said nothing to Ichimura Tetsunosuke of Oyuki, who was to sail on the same ship. He did not even introduce them. Perhaps he left it to nature, thinking that once aboard they would fall into talk of themselves. In any case, to the end he did not put off his nature, which would not have his own love-affairs known to others.

Oyuki too, all the while Toshizō was there, did not come down to the lower floor. As at Yūhigaoka, perhaps, she shrank from a leave-taking.

Toshizō mounted before the Kōnoike shop. When he had walked the horse some ten paces, its hooves ringing, he suddenly felt a gaze on his back and turned.

Oyuki had opened the upper window and was looking down at Toshizō without a blink.

Toshizō gave a slight nod.

That was all. At once he set his posture straight again, raised himself from the saddle and kicked the horse's flank. The horse, carrying a master of remarkably fine seat, broke into a run for the Goryōkaku at Kameda.

Back at Goryōkaku, Toshizō heard the state of the fighting from Enomoto, Matsudaira and Ōtori.

"Esashi too has fallen,"

{j} said Ōtori.

There was no help for it. The government force that had landed at Otobe was two thousand strong, and the garrison of thirty was crushed in the twinkling of an eye.

At Esashi, three ri beyond, their own side held a battery with 250 men. That the government fleet smashed with the fire of its ship's guns.

"Our troops number not above three thousand in all. They say a defending army needs several times the strength of the attacking one; at this rate, whether the whole island can be defended is a question,"

{j} said Enomoto Takeaki, with a grave, pained look.

For one thing, the troops were few, and for another they had scattered the garrisons too widely. In the main fortress of Goryōkaku, eight hundred men; Hakodate, three hundred; Matsumae, four hundred; Fukushima, 150; Muroran, 250; Washinoki, a hundred; and, besides, some tens of men each posted at Mori, Sawara, Kakkumi, Arikawa, Tōbetsu, Yafurai, Kikonai and the rest.

"The first thing is to gather our strength and deal the main body of the landing army a hard blow,"

{j} said Toshizō.

At once the concentration of the scattered forces was set in train. This alone ate up several days.

But before that full concentration was done, Toshizō and Ōtori each led a body of some five hundred men and set out, by separate roads.

Ōtori, to Kikonai.

Toshizō, to Futamataguchi.

In the meantime the Matsumae garrison, under the former shogunal retainer Iba Hachirō, head of the Shingyō-ittō-ryū, and others as its captains, marched on the government-held Esashi, met the government main body and greatly broke and routed it; and the enemy arms they carried off ran to three rifled guns of four kin, small ordnance, knapsacks, swords and spears, powder and shot, in great number.

Toshizō, holding the steep ground at Futamataguchi, waited for the enemy to come up in their advance.

"Let us lure the government forces in,"

{j} and Toshizō made a kind of defense in depth. He set his forwardmost line at Naka-Futamata, and then made Shimo-Futamata the position of his center.

But in these he planted only small numbers of troops.

"When the enemy comes, meet him with light blows and fall back little by little. When his march has stretched itself out to the full, we pour our troops out from the main position at Futamataguchi and wipe him out."

About three in the afternoon on the twelfth of the fourth month, six hundred of the government forces—troops of Satsuma, Chōshū, and Bingo-Fukuyama—appeared at Naka-Futamata, Toshizō's forwardmost line.

Up to Toshizō's position on the height a brisk sound of gunfire could be heard; but presently their own side, as planned, began to withdraw.

The center position too clashed with the enemy, and withdrew.

"Here they come,"

Toshizō, his eyes narrowed, was looking through a field-glass.

Above, breastworks had been thrown up in sixteen places, and the troopers waited, stroking their rifles.

At last they came.

Toshizō gave the order to fire. A furious rifle-fight began.

Toshizō, at the first breastwork, kept the red-and-white commander's standard flying high.

──So long as Hijikata-san is here, we win.

{j} Such was the faith there was in the Hakodate army.

The commander's standard was shot down three times, and three times Toshizō at once had a new one raised.

The fighting did not cease though it ran on into the dark of night, and at last dawn came, yet they fought with rifles more fiercely still.

In this one action the rifle-rounds Toshizō's corps fired came to 35,000, and the time of the fighting to sixteen hours: a record-long engagement such as there had never before been in the war-history of Japan.

At six in the morning the enemy broke at last.

"Wave the commander's standard!"

Toshizō gave the signal for the whole force to charge, had the standard-bearer shoulder the commander's standard, and slid at a rush from the top of the cliff down onto the road.

He drew his sword.

In a moment it became a hand-to-hand fight, and in some five minutes the enemy broke further and began to flee, tumbling as though down a slope.

They pursued that enemy for something over a ri, dealt him a blow all but of annihilation, and took rifles and ammunition in great number.

Their own side's loss was, astonishingly, but a single man killed—a wonder of a victory.

In the dispatch the government staff sent in haste some days after this to the military authorities of the mainland, the sense of it ran: "The enemy has many men seasoned in a hundred battles; they are no match for the enemy we faced in Ōshū. A swift success is very hard. We beg reinforcements with all speed."
