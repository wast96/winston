## Chapter 24

A winter night: the glow of the street lamps froze into frost on the road; there was no one, only a barbed wind, falling on me from the bare roadside trees. I pulled down my hat, turned up my collar, thrust my hands into my pockets, and walked on bleakly, having forgotten to plan where I ought to go. The cars had all been requisitioned, the trams long since stopped, Mei Yingzi's place was too far; so should I take a cab home? But these associations and notions only slid dimly across my mind, while my thoughts and consciousness were steeped wholly in Bai Ping's manner. Was it a surge of conscience in her, or had she found out that I knew her secret and flown into a rage of shame? Or was there, besides, some private grief she could tell no one, that set her reason and her feeling at war?

I mused in silence, my head low, walking slowly. I paid no heed to the street, but there seemed to be a black car along the road, its shadow lying aslant on the ground, and just as my steps trod upon that shadow, the car door suddenly opened, and a woman in black got out.

"You've had a hard time of it." A light laugh, and she stood before me.

"..." I was dumbstruck.

"Get in, my friend."

"Thank you!" With a scornful glance I lowered my head and stepped into the car like a prisoner.

"I should congratulate you on your success, I suppose?"

In the car's lamplight I saw the glinting eyes behind the black veil, and beneath the eyes a sweet smile; I began to catch that familiar fragrance.

Sure enough, it was Mei Yingzi. Suddenly she switched off the car's lamps, and the light from outside came in; out of the dimness I saw the fine white beads on the black veil, and, on the powder-white face, the jet-black pupils. It was a kind of threat. Quietly I fished the packet of documents out from inside my shirt and handed it to her flatly. I kept silent, and did not look at her.

"Regretting it?"

"Not at all," I said coldly. "Set your mind at rest."

"Home?" She started the car.

"As you please."

"Let me take you somewhere new to find a little pleasure."

"Thank you," I said.

She drove at a furious speed along the road; lost in a daze, I kept silent, paying no heed to the route, not looking out the window, not looking at her.

A good quarter of an hour must have passed before the car slowed, turning into a lane fenced with bamboo, from deep gray to pale gray and then to transparency, when I saw brilliant lights; the car drove in among them and stopped in a garden. Mei Yingzi opened the car door, and a burst of exciting jazz came crowding in; in this atmosphere of music I jumped down. I saw the neon word Standford.

How many lights gathered upon the girl in black; now I noticed Mei Yingzi's charm in her black-fox coat. She smiled, took my arm in hers, and we crossed the garden, where, among the withered flowers and trees on every side, the arbor-vitae showed a matchless splendor. Where the swing-door opened, the warmth of the steam heat pressed out and the exciting music suddenly rang loud; I went in beside Mei Yingzi, and together we checked our coats and hats at the cloakroom. Mei Yingzi now wore a blue jacket, the white silk turned-back collar baring her soft neck; a woolen skirt of pale yellow checked with red and blue did not hide the trim line of her calves. She smiled as she walked:

"This is your first time here, I take it."

I nodded; I had not yet said a single word.

Going in through layer upon layer of deep curtains, I saw light and I saw color, and amid the rich music and the bantering laughter I grew aware of the sin of the small-hours world.

When we had sat down, Mei Yingzi said to the waiter:

"Ginger wine." Then she asked me: "And you?"

"Always in your train," I said.

"Two glasses of ginger wine," she said.

I was silent, not listening, not looking; to all the stir of sound and color I made no response, until the wine came, when Mei Yingzi raised her glass and said:

"To your victory."

"The victory is yours."

"Won't you dance?"

I shook my head, lit a cigarette, and breathed out the dispersing smoke as if breathing out my faint sorrow.

When the music stopped, the lights flared up all at once, and countless young men and women came over to greet Mei Yingzi; I took no notice of them, and Mei Yingzi did not introduce me.

When the music struck up a second time, several men came before Mei Yingzi to ask her to dance, but she declined; afterward she said:

"The first dance tonight I keep always for our hero."

"I am only your slave," I said sarcastically, standing up and going before her. I said: "It seems I cannot let my lovely mistress break her word, nor let so many young men be disappointed."

On the dance floor I began to find that this was another world altogether; in the crowded press I did not see a single Chinese man, though there were no few Japanese. I said:

"What sort of world is this?"

"It is the crystallization of face-powder, sweet wine, and blood," she said.

Back at our seats, I fell silent again, and Mei Yingzi said in a low voice:

"Can you still not forget the tension of your work?"

"What do you mean?"

"A first campaign is often like this," she laughed. "Come now." She rose. "You need a stronger stimulant to forget your tension." She walked on, and I went beside her, giving her no answer.

She came to my side, pressed close against me, and looked to see there was no one around before saying softly:

"Let's have a great gamble. I'll come and find you at dawn; you should take Bai Ping's document back early."

Out through layer upon layer of deep curtains, along a winding passage, and in again through layer upon layer of deep curtains, we stepped into the gambling den, and Mei Yingzi took from her black-fox purse two bundles of banknotes and gave them to me.

"Let's go shares," she said.

As I sat down at the roulette table and the waiter handed me a cigarette, Mei Yingzi said:

"Then let me come back to you presently."

I watched her vanish like sunlight into the deep curtains, and idly, following the others, placed my bets on the wheel. But my heart was in a daze; I was conscious of nothing in particular, yet at every moment Bai Ping's anger, the wax-sealed document, Mei Yingzi's smile, and the notions of friendship, work, war, espionage, and the rest floated up and sank down in the sea of my mind, half-there and half-gone, now fast, now slow.

As my stakes were lost one after another, my heart began to draw back, and slowly I concentrated on the gambling; in the great coming and going of the chips I forgot at last the tangle of my recent troubles.

Life, perhaps, is just the intoxication of gambling. In that instant I did not think of the world, nor of the existence of Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping, nor of my meaning in the world; I did not even think of money. I reckoned only the rise and fall of the chips and the rhythm of the little ball, groping after my fate within a shallow, narrow compass.

I noticed the time was near five, but Mei Yingzi had still not come back; not wishing to gamble more, I cashed in my chips and slipped away to the ballroom. On the bandstand a lovely Japanese girl was now singing a Japanese song. I went close to listen, and amid the applause at the song's end, as everyone clamored for another, I wrote a note in English. I said:

"Young lady, this is Chinese soil and a Chinese night; sing a Chinese song. How about 'Sunset over the Huangpu'?"

My request did not fail after all; when she sang again, it was indeed "Sunset over the Huangpu," and I applauded, and the whole hall applauded. As she came down I went over and asked her to dance, and only on the floor did I say:

"Thank you for not refusing my request."

"Of course," she smiled. "You're a friend of Mei Yingzi's."

"No," I denied it. "I have no friends here."

"Then how sad," she laughed prettily. "Shall I be your friend?"

"Why? For my unexpected request, for the money in my pocket, or for the love in my heart?"

"For your giving me the first dance."

"What's strange in that? I'm a man with no dignity at all!"

"But Mei Yingzi kept the first dance to give to you, and you gave the first dance to me."

"Mei Yingzi again!" In surprise and resentment I said: "Do you really think yourself less than Mei Yingzi?"

"Do you think yourself above Mei Yingzi?"

I was silent; when the dance ended I came out alone, passed through layer upon layer of deep curtains, and, without my overcoat, walked out to the little garden.

From now on, was I to be Mei Yingzi's tool? I lit a cigarette and thought: for freedom, for love, for the nation, must I really work under Mei Yingzi's control? Could I not go to the interior and do anything at all? To be set opposite Bai Ping and forever circle in a cramped little ring, was this really the whole of what I was fit for?

Countless griefs and resentments welled up in me, and I resolved to break free of this bond; I would carry out Mei Yingzi's orders no more. On the moment I decided to go straight home and, after a sleep, to plan my future afresh. I walked briskly inside, meaning to fetch my coat and hat, but just as I came in at the door!

"Well? Where did you get to?" There right before me was Mei Yingzi; she seemed to have searched all through the gambling den and the ballroom, and spoke a little out of breath.

"Taking a walk," I said indifferently, seeing the purse in her hand and, behind it, a book wrapped in newspaper; the book was very thick, and I thought that Bai Ping's document was tucked inside it.

"Shall we go?"

"All right." So saying I went to get my coat and hat. With our overcoats on, we came out together; outside the sky was already faintly light. She handed me the paper packet and said:

"Do you need money?"

"Ah," I said, "I won at the tables; here's money." I took the thick wad of banknotes from my pocket and gave it to her.

"Keep it," she said. "After you've seen Bai Ping, meet me here again tonight."

"No," I said.

"Are you regretting it?"

"Not that."

"Then come to the Benner Inn."

"All right."

She walked with me into the garden, and before the black car we had come in, she handed me the car key and said:

"You may take this car."

I saw her red car standing beside it as well. I nodded, opened the car door, and she pondered a moment, then said gravely:

"You had best find an apartment and move out of your home."

"I can do that," I said, jumping into the car.

"Goodbye," I said.

"Dinner at the Benner Inn, waiting for you."

So saying, she turned from me and sprang into the red car.

I drove out of the bamboo-fenced lane and only then made out that this was a remote end of Columbia Road. Now it occurred to me that while I was in the gambling den, Mei Yingzi had never once gone out the main gate; for no car could come or go in the little garden without being seen, and her cloakroom token had been in my hand besides. Could she have gone out without her overcoat? Then she must have been inside, perhaps in some secret room; in any case, that this was an organ of their espionage there could be no doubt.

I drove southeast from Columbia Road, my heart filled with admiration, fear, and loathing for Mei Yingzi. The words of that Japanese singing-girl only reflected Mei Yingzi's bright brilliance. But for now I still had to work for her.

The sky was brighter now; I stopped the car before a Cantonese eating-house, chose a seat, and went to the lavatory, where I shut the door and opened the paper packet, meaning to see what was really inside the document. But the seal and the sealing-wax were as before, everything seeming untouched, so that I could not peek; I only did away with the wrapping and hid the document once more inside my shirt.

I went back to my seat and ordered, thinking to myself that Bai Ping was sure to sleep late this morning, and that while she was not yet up I would put the document back in its place in the study. So at eight o'clock I bought two boxes of Cantonese dim sum and drove straight to Route Prosper Paris.

To avoid waking Bai Ping, I did not ring the bell but knocked softly.

"Who is it?"

"Me."

The door opened, and Ah Mei said:

"Alone?"

"When have I ever brought anyone here?"

"Then you didn't meet Miss Bai Ping?"

"She's gone out?"

"She went to look for you at seven o'clock."

"What did she want with me?" I was deeply afraid the matter of the document had already been discovered, but I kept my voice from betraying alarm.

"I don't know," said Ah Mei. "But..."

"But what?"

"What time did you leave?"

"I didn't go back all night."

"Then she'll be back, I think."

"Did she say anything when she went out?" I said, walking into the study.

"She only said she was going to see you."

"Did she not sleep well last night?" I asked.

"I got up at two, and she was in her bedroom in a temper."

"Was she pacing about in her bedroom all the while?"

"I don't know," she said. "But when I got up in the morning, she was walking back and forth in here."

This really startled me; but I had to put the document back in its place first of all, so I said:

"Did she eat anything before she went out?"

"I asked whether she'd been up the whole night; she said nothing, only told me to make some coffee and toast."

"And so she ate and went out."

"Yes; she ate, bathed, changed her clothes, and then went out."

"Dressed very grandly, or very plainly?"

"Very grandly," she said.

I thought this was perhaps not the mood of one who had found a document missing. This was all I could learn from Ah Mei. I had to put the document safely back before she returned; as to whether she knew, I could only observe when I met her, and deal with her manner toward me as the moment required. So I said:

"I'll wait for her; could you give me a cup of coffee and some toast as well?"

"Of course," she said, and glancing at my face, asked: "Did you quarrel with her last night?"

"How could we?" I said.

"Forgive her a little," said Ah Mei. "She treats you well."

"Even if she killed me, I'd forgive her." In my mind I really did picture Bai Ping killing me when she found I had stolen the document. But Ah Mei misunderstood, and, almost choking back tears, said:

"She's a person with no father, no mother, no brothers, with only one friend like you; where she's in the wrong you must of course counsel her, but you must never, never give her pain."

"Yes, Ah Mei." I did not look at her, and spoke earnestly, but in my heart was a shame beyond words. If the leaking of this document were truly a danger to Bai Ping's life, how should I face myself? And so I began to regret it. Why had I not asked Mei Yingzi clearly just what its effect on Bai Ping would be? Otherwise I might tell Bai Ping that Mei Yingzi had already seen the document; but if doing so would harm the future course of history, then what was my conduct worth? So I could only leave things to their natural course, and pray that in today's meeting Bai Ping would tell me everything, and be willing to change her life. But for the present the most urgent thing was still to return the document...

At some point Ah Mei had gone out. I got up quickly, drew the door to, and at the bookshelf took the document from my breast, pulled out that copy of Faust again, and softly slipped the document back between page eighty-three; I had returned it to its place with ease.

At this my heart seemed a little calmer; I lit a cigarette and sat on the same sofa. Though the waves of conscience still surged in my heart, the tension of a day and a night, coming one upon another, slackened in an instant, and the weariness of many years seemed to rise up in me; it pressed down my heartbeat, my breathing, pressed down every ripple of my nerves, and I drifted off into a doze on the sofa.

But when Ah Mei brought the coffee in, I woke with a start at once; thinking it was Bai Ping come back, some feeling beyond words set my heart pounding wildly.

"Did I wake you?" said Ah Mei.

"How did I come to fall asleep?" I said. "Bai Ping still not back?"

"She'll be back soon, I think." So saying, Ah Mei went out, leaving me alone in the room. I drank the coffee, ate the toast, and smoked another cigarette. At last I lay back on the sofa and truly fell asleep.

No wind or rain, no sun; it seemed to be dusk, and I was climbing a mountain over white snow. There were no birds, no beasts, not a single footprint on the snow; I watched my feet sink into the snow and rise again, going up print by print, and looked back to see that on the whole mountain there were only my own footprints. Very pleased, I went on and on, but somehow, as though I had trodden into a trap, I suddenly fell into a deep pit; it seemed all the snow had turned to water and poured down over my head, and I lay at the bottom of the pit and let all the water pour over me. I thought that all the footprints I had left on the mountain must surely be wiped out now, but the water kept coming down, and I felt cold. Then I felt someone lay a blanket over me, a white one, white as the snow itself; was it a blanket woven of snow? I wondered, and looked closely, and came to my senses.

It was Bai Ping, laying a pure white woolen blanket over me. I found that my head, pillowed on the arm of the sofa, had slipped down, and that I was curled up on the sofa like a snail.

"Bai Ping!" I moved my head back up onto the arm of the sofa.

"Yes." A smile like a lily just opening. "I hurt you last night, didn't I?"

"No," I said. "It was I who hurt you."

Bai Ping sat down beside me, and from the look on her face I judged she had not discovered the document's disappearance; but my conscience was leaping within me, and a feeling of shame, gratitude, and desolation brought tears welling from my heart to my eyes. I said:

"Forgive me this once. If there is any danger, tell me at any time; I would gladly die for you."

"..." She lowered her head and wiped her glistening tears with a spotless handkerchief.

"Bai Ping, don't cling to Shanghai any longer." I took her hand, stroking the back of it and the palm, and said: "Come with me to the interior; let us pour out our passion in the bosom of the nation."

"..." She nodded.

"Truly, Bai Ping." I was roused.

"Of course," she said coolly.

"Then when shall we go?"

"I think, I think... ah, it seems impossible." She was composed and cool.

"Why?"

"Don't ask me," she said. "But perhaps you should go in first, and I may come in later."

"No," I said. "If we go, we go together."

"Then just wait for me," she said. "But it's a distant hope."

"Then, while I'm still in Shanghai, may we not meet and talk often?" I said.

"Of course we may." She stood up. "Now, sleep a while longer."

"No. You ought to rest too." I sprang up from the sofa and said: "Let me go home to sleep; I'll come and see you again tomorrow."
