## Turmoil in the Capital

The storming of the Ikedaya raised the Shinsengumi's name like a thunderclap, but it left grave marks on history as well.

It is commonly said that, because so many of the leading loyalist activists of the day were cut down or seized to death in that affair, the Meiji Restoration was set back by at least a year; but most likely the truth runs the other way.

It is nearer the mark to see the affair as having brought the Meiji Restoration on the sooner. Or rather, without it, that Satsuma-and-Chōshū-led Restoration might never have come at all. A revolution needs the violent military action of its revolutionary party, and none of the pro-Kyoto domains of that day had either the chance or the temper to make such a leap. Not one of the great domains' leaders so much as dreamed of raising a shield against the shogunate. It was the single powder-magazine of the Chōshū domain — three hundred and sixty thousand koku (with its light-industry policies in wax and paper and its opening of new paddy, Chōshū had the economic strength of a million koku) — that blew up.

The Kyoto Protector who governed the Shinsengumi — the Aizu domain — seems to have agonized too over whether the deed should be done at all. The present writer has never seen the original, but in a liaison-office document sent up two days after the affair from the Aizu headquarters in Kyoto, at Kurodani, to the Aizu residence in Edo, the agonizing that went before the deed is set down thus. To paraphrase:

"To leave the secret plottings of these Chōshū men, and of the persons outside the census registers — the rōnin — unchecked would not only make our lord's (Matsudaira Katamori's) charge, the office of Kyoto Protector, impossible to uphold; the calamity is pressing before our very eyes. Yet to move to suppression, we thought, would only make them harbor a deeper grudge, and our lord too was sunk in anxiety over it. But there being no other good expedient, and there being the fear that, should we lose the chance, we should ourselves be mastered by them instead, we had no choice."

So it reads. The stance of a government toward a revolutionary party, and its anguish, must be much alike in every country and every age.

This deliberation was still going forward while Kondō and Toshizō were mustering at the town meeting-hall before the gate of the Jitsujō-in at Gion, the staging-point of the attack. They deliberated on and on, and then, at last,

{j} "Unavoidable,"

{j} such was the Kyoto Protector's conclusion, and it was passed to the subordinate inspecting offices, the Kyoto Shoshidai and the town magistrates, and both gave their assent. Incidentally, the Kyoto Shoshidai of the day was Matsudaira Sadaaki, lord of the Kuwana domain in Ise, own younger brother to the Kyoto Protector Matsudaira Katamori; the two brothers, then, were charged with the peace of Kyoto between them. The understanding between the two ran with real speed.

But the deliberation ran too long. And because the mustering of the domain's troops was so sluggish, the hour of eight at night that the Shinsengumi had agreed on with the Aizu domain for the opening of the attack slipped by two whole hours. And so Kondō, without waiting on the official command, took it upon himself and struck the Ikedaya. For Kondō and Toshizō there was no such thing as political calculation. There was only the sword.

After the affair, a letter of commendation came down from the shogunate to the Kyoto Protector.

"The men of the Shinsengumi went out at once, struck down and took the villains, and did outstanding work"

{j} — so the text runs. At the same time a reward of money came down to the Shinsengumi. And further, from the shogunate council, there came a private intimation that the Shinsengumi's commander was to be raised to the rank of

{j} "senior yoriki."

So ran the intimation. But Toshizō,

{j} "Let it be,"

{j} he counselled Kondō.

"A yoriki — the thing is absurd."

And absurd it was. A yoriki was a direct retainer of the shogun, true enough, but by origin a local functionary, his post held for a single lifetime; a lower officer with no right of audience before the shogun, ranking no higher than a gokenin. On top of that he was a specialist in arrests, with no obligation of military service, and was despised in warrior society as a "defiling official." Think of him as no soldier but a pure policeman, and you will not be far off.

The shogunate saw the Shinsengumi as police. To Kondō it must have been galling.

Kondō held himself a loyalist activist. The final aim of the Shinsengumi, he maintained, lay in expelling the barbarian. His true heart aside, he had declared as much, over and over, within the corps and without. This was, so to say, a body of soldiers.

The greatest displeasure Kondō and Toshizō felt after the affair must have been just this: that the shogunate could see them only as police. The reckoning was too small.

"The thing is to wait,"

{j} said Toshizō. Wait, and the shogunate would come to rate them higher. It was not even beyond dreaming that they might be raised to daimyō.

Kondō had been dreaming of daimyō rank. The intimation of "senior yoriki" threw cold water on the dream, yet he was not cast down.

"My dream, you know,"

{j} Kondō said, and to Toshizō alone,

{j} "is to become an expel-the-barbarian daimyō."

That he went out of his way to add "expel-the-barbarian" came of the loyalist temper of the day; the ambition of becoming a daimyō and guarding Japan from the foreign enemy had, ever since his unheard-of feat at the Ikedaya, been swelling in Kondō's breast.

"Well enough,"

{j} said Toshizō. Setting aside all the talk of the barbarian, to ride the wind and the clouds up into daimyō rank, and, with luck, to seize the realm, was the way of warriors from of old. It was in no way unjust.

"I will help you to the last."

"I count on it."

Kondō declined the mean office of senior yoriki and went on contenting himself with the free station of a government-appointed captain of rōnin. In the shogunate council and in the Protector's offices, all were moved by Kondō's want of ambition.

But Kondō was not without ambition.

After the Ikedaya affair he bought a white horse, set a splendid saddle on it, used it for his patrols of the city, drew a train of spearmen behind him, and gave the townsfolk and the samurai the impression, for all the world, of a daimyō in his majesty. That a rōnin risen from the peasantry should parade the city in a daimyō's guise was a thing unthinkable in the shogunal order of a few years before.

Even when he went to attend at Nijō Castle, where the Protector had his seat, he went in mounted procession.

He was a daimyō now, and no mistake. To play the daimyō, and by the playing to build up a certain impression, was of a piece with the thick-skinned cunning of Kondō and Toshizō, so like the swordsmen risen from the potato-patch dōjō of Bushū that they were.

The Ikedaya affair fell on the fifth of the sixth month.

Not long after, after sunset on the twenty-sixth, the eerie effects of the raid had already begun to show.

It was at the Chōshū residence on Kawaramachi.

Since the Ikedaya affair this residence had fallen utterly silent. Within it there still remained a hundred and some Chōshū samurai and hot-blooded rōnin who had quit their own domains.

What they might be about to do was a matter of grave concern to the shogunate. All manner of spies came and went around the residence. Aizu spies, agents of the Shoshidai, informers of the Shinsengumi's inspectorate — the watch was never off its guard.

Late that night of the twenty-sixth. The night, like the night of the Ikedaya affair, was frightfully close and hot. Toshizō was woken by the inspector Yamazaki Susumu.

"What is it?"

He dressed in haste.

"The Chōshū residence on Kawaramachi has looked strange since dusk. Men are coming out."

By threes and fives, it seemed, they were slipping into the town so as not to be marked.

"In what direction?"

"When they leave by the side gate they scatter north and south, but by what the spies have traced, it seems they all bear west somewhere along the way."

"What lies to the west?"

"That we do not yet know."

"How many spies are out?"

"There are twenty-odd sown through the town, so we shall have the shape of it before long."

"Tell each squad leader to rouse his men. And send a runner to Kondō-sensei's lodging as well."

When Toshizō heard "bearing west," it flashed on him that they might mean to fall on Mibu here in the western reaches of the city. But no — it was otherwise.

Further west still. It was the Tenryū-ji at Saga, they said.

(This is going to grow large.)

So he thought each time a report came in.

The Tenryū-ji, the head temple of the Rinzai sect, where the Chōshū men of Kyoto were now gathering, is a great monastery in the western reaches. Its plaster walls rise high, and to hold it would be to hold a fortress outright.

By what came out later, a hundred and some Chōshū men had threatened the temple's steward at the point of the naked blade and simply settled in. To be sure, the Chōshū domain and the Tenryū-ji had had some slight tie two years before, in the second year of Bunkyū: when the Chōshū domain received the imperial charge to guard Kyoto and there was no place within the city to house a large force, the Tenryū-ji was made its camp, through the offices of Fukuda Rihei, a loyalist and country samurai of Shimo-Saga. But after the later withdrawal there had been no tie at all.

"Kondō-san, this time it is no such small stir as the Ikedaya,"

{j} said Toshizō, without expression.

"A raid on the Tenryū-ji?"

Kondō was already up on his mettle. It was as though Chōshū had gone out of its way to make him a chance at glory, he thought.

"I wonder. This may turn into a war."

"A war?"

"We shall need to make ready for it."

By making ready he meant the work of shifting the Shinsengumi from a police force into an army. For a start, cannon were needed.

The Shinsengumi had, from the time of its founding, an old-model gun on loan from the Aizu domain. It was a bronze, muzzle-loading field piece called a pompen gun — a long-shell thrower — that was loaded by heating an iron ball red-hot and rolling it down the muzzle, then fired with a match-cord. Its range was frightfully short; a single chō was as far as it was likely to carry.

(There should, I'm fairly sure, be a new-model gun at the Aizu headquarters, the kind made at Nirayama.)

It was not that Toshizō wanted cannon as fighting strength for the Shinsengumi. He wanted the guns, so to speak, in order to fit out a military establishment on the footing of a "daimyō."

Next morning, waiting for daybreak, Toshizō galloped his horse to the Aizu headquarters at Kurodani.

He met Toshima Kihee of the liaison office.

"Toshima-san, there is certainly going to be a war,"

{j} he said, to frighten him.

Because it was Hijikata Toshizō who had come, Toshima passed word to the senior officers as well. Before long the karō Jinbō Kuranosuke came out to the seat too. The whole company was extremely courteous. Since the Ikedaya affair the Shinsengumi's standing had risen by leaps, and toward Toshizō their manner was that of men receiving a senior officer of their own domain.

"Hijikata-sensei, should we attack the Tenryū-ji, we shall have to open a council of war without delay as to what strategy is to be used,"

{j} said the Aizu karō Jinbō Kuranosuke. It was half meant, no doubt, as a civility.

"Just so. But this time it will not answer, as at the Ikedaya, merely to draw our naked blades in a row and go over the temple gate. At Mibu, too, we need guns."

"You have one, I believe."

"No — it is not enough."

Toshizō explained. First he would break the earthen wall with the guns; through the breach he meant to send the men storming in — but with a single hole the losses would be heavy. He would line up five guns and break through at five places to storm in; five guns he must have, by all means, he pressed hard.

At this the Aizu side too was startled. Why, that would leave the Aizu domain with no guns at all.

"And I want those to be Nirayama guns,"

{j} he said. Of the Nirayama guns even Aizu owned but one.

"Impossible."

Toshima too had gone pale.

Toshizō said that the pompen gun now at Mibu had no more force than a wooden mallet.

"That thing is of no earthly use. Serizawa Kamo has already tried it out."

The dead commander Serizawa Kamo, when he had once gone to press money on the rich merchant Yamatoya Shōbee of Yoshiyamachi on Ichijō-dōri, and was refused, had a cannon hauled out from the barracks. He set the gun before the Yamatoya's shopfront, built a great fire beside it, kept pitching iron balls into the fire to heat them red-hot, and, loading them, shot away at the storehouse.

But the thick wall of the storehouse would not easily crumble, nor would it burn, and even Serizawa was at a loss. That was the "trying out" Toshizō meant.

"And yet,"

{j} the Aizu side said, and went on to explain that their own domain's firepower was very weak beside that of the Satsuma domain — in those days all but an ally of Aizu — and the rest,

"Hijikata-sensei, what say you? We shall take it up in time with the shogunate council too, and mean to meet your wishes as far as we can, so might you make shift, for the present, with just the one gun?"

{j} said Jinbō Kuranosuke. Toshizō had of course only been pitching high; one gun would do. And an old-model one at that. The whole point was merely to lend authority to his martial array.

"Well — let us make shift with it."

Putting them under obligation, he got one gun out of them. Old-model though it was, this made two Western guns. Two guns, and his martial array already outstripped that of a small domain of some fifty thousand koku.

He went straight back to the barracks at Mibu, but of the doings at the Tenryū-ji, the matter at hand, there was no particular intelligence.

For some days afterward nothing happened.

Then, sooner than the shogunate's own intelligence, a fearful rumor ran through the city of Kyoto. The domain's troops of Chōshū, it said, had divided into several forces, each setting sail from the trading port of Mitajiri in Suō, and were coming up eastward.

"To clear our wrong" — our innocence — "before the imperial gates"

{j} — such was the reason given for the sortie. In short: in the coup of the third year of Bunkyū, Chōshū's power had been swept out of Kyoto politics; and further, in the Ikedaya affair, many of the domain's samurai had been seized and killed like cats and dogs — to demand the reason for this and to make the domain's just cause plain was the ostensible ground; but the heart of it was to overpower Kyoto by military action, remove the Son of Heaven to Chōshū, and make good the work of expelling the barbarian and toppling the shogunate.

In their fright at the rumor, many of the townsfolk of Kyoto sent their household goods away for safety toward Tanba.

The rumor began to take on the color of truth once it was learned that Maki Izumi-no-kami and Kusaka Genzui, leading a rōnin band of three hundred of the Chōshū party, had landed at Osaka. The next day an armed force led by the Chōshū karō Fukuhara Echigo landed at Osaka likewise. And Chōshū ships still to follow were coming up eastward through the Inland Sea, it was said.

In the Aizu domain, charged with the guarding of Kyoto, senior-officer councils were held day after day. From the Shinsengumi, Kondō always attended.

At one such sitting, someone on the Aizu side put forward a strategy:

"Let us remove His Majesty, the Son of Heaven, for a time to Hikone Castle, and annihilate the Chōshū rebels at Yamazaki, at Fushimi, and in Kyoto."

However it leaked, this reached at once the ears of the expedition at the Chōshū residence in Osaka, and enraged them.

In short, the inner truth was that for Chōshū and shogunate alike the whole thing narrowed to one aim: to seize, or to guard, the Son of Heaven. That the side which held the Son of Heaven was the imperial army had, through the spread of the loyalist view of history in such works as the Dai Nihon-shi and the Nihon Gaishi, become the received law of that age.

Kondō, coming back to the barracks in high excitement, walked down the corridor calling out,

"Toshi, is Toshi here?"

{j} he shouted.

Toshizō was in his room. He sat at his desk, looking over this and that in the roster of the men, brooding on a reshaping of the corps. He was laboring to alter the Shinsengumi from an organization for policing the city into one fit, at a turn, for field war and the storming of walls. To Toshizō the political arguments of the court nobles and the domains and the loyalist activists were nothing at all.

"Toshi."

Kondō slid the paper door open. Toshizō turned around with a sour face.

"I hear you. 'Toshi, Toshi' — like a peddler, and shabby with it."

"The king."

{j} Kondō said it in a rush.

"The king?"

"That's it."

Kondō made the motion of moving a shōgi piece.

"This is the one we must not have taken. Let this be taken, and even the shogun turns rebel, if you please. This war is a different thing from the Ikedaya. Though we pile the Shinsengumi's corpses at the gate of the palace, the king we hold to the end. You understand?"

"Understood."

"Mind you do. Even if the Shinsengumi is wiped out in the tiger's jaws and only you and I are left, the Son of Heaven we hold to the end."

This was the good in Kondō, Toshizō thought. Two men risen from the peasantry of Tama meant to shoulder the Son of Heaven himself and guard him from the hands of Chōshū. The councils at Nijō Castle must have been full of abstractions and arguments over titles and just cause, but Kondō's head ran always to the concrete and the matter-of-fact.

Toshizō ran more matter-of-fact still. In this man's head there was nothing but the strengthening of the Shinsengumi.

Before long the Chōshū troops began pouring into Fushimi.

The commander Fukuhara Echigo, clad in armor, led his force at a mounted rush through the Kyōbashi gate of Fushimi, and when the Kishū troops guarding it barred the way,

"We men of Chōshū are ever prepared against the outer barbarian. Our armed dress is our common dress"

{j} — so he browbeat them, passed through, and for the time entered the Chōshū residence at Fushimi.

By the intelligence that reached the Shinsengumi, the Chōshū rōnin corps led by Maki Izumi-no-kami had taken up position on Tennōzan at Ōyamazaki, and at the Rikyū Hachimangū at its foot (in what is now Otokuni district, Kyoto Prefecture, near the national-railway Yamazaki station), and at the Dainen-ji and the Kannon-ji; and that as for the band at the Tenryū-ji in Saga, since there was no fit commander for it, Kijima Matabee, famed even in Chōshū for his valor, had hurried up to take its command.

The Chōshū troops at Tennōzan, at Saga, and at Fushimi deliberately built up a great number of watch-fires by night, laying a wordless menace upon the city of Kyoto, while against the court they opened a campaign of petitions.

Further, on the ninth of the seventh month of the first year of Genji, a force of eight hundred under the karō Kunishi Shinano — what might be called the main body of the Chōshū army — reached the camp at Ōyamazaki, and Kunishi himself entered the Tenryū-ji at Saga to take command of the whole force.

The Shinsengumi's post had already been settled. Together with the Aizu troops, they were to guard the Hamaguri Gate of the palace.

It was then, for the first time, that Toshizō put on armor.
