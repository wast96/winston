## 10

Zhongliang sold his bicycle and set up a cigarette stall on a street corner off Seymour Road, squatting there day after day like a weary, listless beggar. He soon learned to smoke, and smoked harder and harder — often a whole pack in a day, and better than half a pack more come evening. Xiufen watched him and never asked a word, only bent to her pillowcases.

One evening Zhongliang said to her, suddenly, “I want to join your assassination squad.”

Xiufen said, “You've never even killed a chicken.”

“You need couriers, and you need lookouts.” Zhongliang said, “I can't squat in the street like a dog all day long.”

Xiufen glanced at him and did not open her mouth again. Many things could not be told even to the closest of kin — that was the discipline of the organization. Each time, Xiufen received her orders from a quack-doctor's handbill on the utility pole at the mouth of the market, then went to a designated spot to draw ammunition and hand out the assignments. They divided the work among them, and when it was done they scattered in all directions. The members of the assassination squad communicated almost entirely by their eyes; there were times they would not exchange half a spoken sentence.

The day the International Settlement was renamed the First District of the Special Municipality of Shanghai was the hottest day of the year. The streets were hung all over with the Blue Sky, White Sun, and Wholly Red Earth flag, and everywhere were patrolling Japanese military police and plainclothes men of the garrison corps. Zhongliang had been driven off to a lane-mouth well away from the main street, and it was then that Su Lina appeared before him. Up from a pair of slender ankles Zhongliang slowly raised his eyes, and he saw that Su Lina's face, in the scorching sun, was dazzlingly white.

Zhongliang gave a small smile. “I've become a cigarette-seller now.”

Su Lina said nothing; she threw down a few reserve certificates, took a pack of Three Castles, and got into the rickshaw waiting to one side.

In the many days that followed, Su Lina would stop to buy a pack of cigarettes whenever she passed along Seymour Road. What she paid was sometimes little, sometimes much, but it was enough to keep Zhongliang's household going — and never once did she say a word.

One day Zhongliang finally spoke. Looking at the cars going by in the road, his face without expression, he said, “That's enough now. You needn't go on pitying me.”

Su Lina looked him over carefully, still said nothing, threw down the money, took the cigarettes, and left.

Two months passed, and Su Lina appeared no more at the Seymour Road corner — until one evening. Su Lina was suddenly standing before the cigarette stall again, and said she had in her hands the winter pacification plan Nanjing had just drawn up, aimed in full at the base areas of central Jiangsu. Zhongliang, a cigarette between his fingers, looked up at her. Su Lina said, “We can't let this intelligence rot in our hands.”

Zhongliang said, “Are we still intelligence agents?”

“This bears on the lives of tens of thousands.” Su Lina spoke as if giving an order. “You must find a way to send it out.”

“If I had a way, I wouldn't be squatting here.”

“You're not a child.” Su Lina bent down, took a pack of cigarettes from the stall, and looked into Zhongliang's eyes. “This little grievance of yours counts for nothing.”

That evening Zhongliang changed his clothes and went to St. Thérèse's Church. He knelt without moving before the altar until Father Kruger came out, and only then raised his head. “Help me this one time.”

“God helps every stray lamb.” Father Kruger said with a smile, “my child.”

“I have intelligence.” Zhongliang said, “about Jiangbei, north of the river.”

Father Kruger pondered a moment. “Then you've come to the wrong place.”

“I know you have channels. I want the intelligence sent out.”

“Do you still not understand?” Father Kruger said. “Your organization has abandoned you.”

“This bears on many lives.”

“It will also cost you your life.” Father Kruger crouched down and looked at him. “You know better than I: if there's anything wrong with your intelligence, your own organization will take my life for it too.”

“A man afraid to die isn't fit to be an intelligence agent.” With that Zhongliang stood up and made to leave.

But Father Kruger laughed, and watched him go all the way to the door before he called him back. His condition was that Zhongliang name the source of the intelligence; then he would consider whether to do him the favor. Zhongliang shook his head and, gazing at the holy image in the candlelight, said that even were this the military police of the Japanese, he would not name his source. Then he added, “You ought to know the rule of this trade.”

Father Kruger sighed and said the favor he could do, but Zhongliang must give him his promise: “You know, too, what line I'm in.” Father Kruger said, “I don't serve you for nothing.”

Zhongliang stared into his blue eyes. “Father — don't forget we have at least one enemy in common.”

Father Kruger laughed again, reached out and put an arm around Zhongliang's shoulders, and invited him up to the study on the floor above for a cup of coffee — for the one enemy they still had in common.

It was over the coffee that Father Kruger made his proposal: he hoped to work with Zhongliang. “I know you're not in it for money,” Father Kruger said, “and I won't ask again where the intelligence comes from. But for the sake of your country, and so we can win this war a day sooner, we all need to have friends.”

Zhongliang thought a moment. “Let me first confirm you've delivered the intelligence — then we'll talk.”

Father Kruger smiled. “You must trust me.”

Zhongliang was as though he had become a postman again. He carried the intelligence Su Lina drew from Qin Zhaokuan to St. Thérèse's Church, and Father Kruger sorted it and sent it by his various channels to the places it was meant to go. Zhongliang stressed one thing above all: that on every piece of intelligence passed along, both his and Su Lina's code names be marked. Zhongliang held firmly to the belief that one day the organization would come to make contact with them.

But then, all at once, things changed. One day Zhongliang came home to find on the table not only fish and meat but a whole white-cut chicken, cut and arranged, and he looked at Xiufen, baffled, and asked what day it was. Xiufen said nothing; her lips pressed together, she took a bottle of wine from the cupboard and filled both cups on the table. Xiufen, it turned out, was a woman who could hold her drink. Zhongliang had not swallowed a mouthful before she had tipped back her head and downed two cups. Zhongliang's face changed, and he asked what had happened. Xiufen did not answer, but smiled and laid a drumstick in his bowl. “I'm keeping the New Year early,” she said.

Not until the two of them had drunk the whole bottle dry did Zhongliang look at Xiufen again. “Tell me — what mission have they given you?” “A mission is a mission.” As Xiufen spoke, she rose and began to clear the table.

Zhongliang only watched her bustling about the room, and said not another word the whole evening.

After they had gone to bed, Xiufen spoke without warning. From under the quilt Xiufen said, “You know? The moment his head was struck off, I was already a dead woman.”

Zhongliang was startled, and by the time he understood, Xiufen had already pressed herself to him. Her body was scalding as fire, her breath reeking of wine, and yet her face was all ice.

The next morning, still not saying a word, Zhongliang watched as Xiufen dragged a brand-new canvas case out from under the bed, opened the cupboard, put his clothes into it one piece at a time, closed it, buckled the straps, and set it at his feet. From the drawer Xiufen took a wad of money, took his hand, put it in, and looked into his eyes. “Go now, at once. Leave Shanghai.” Zhongliang stood, and looked into her eyes the same way. Then, suddenly, Xiufen smiled. “As long as I'm alive, I'll come and find you.”

“And where will you find me?”

“Wherever you go, that's where I'll come to find you.”

With that, Xiufen bit her lip and let out not one more word. It was with her eyes that she pushed Zhongliang, step by step, out the door; she watched him all the way out through the shikumen gate before she leaned back against the doorframe, lifted her face, and gazed at the snowflakes drifting down out of the sky.

In truth, Xiufen did not know what mission she was to carry out. The afternoon before, when she had come to the rendezvous by the cipher on the notice, they were all there. Four people were gathered around a table; the superior was a middle-aged man with a wisp of a mustache, and when he drew a wad of money from his pocket, divided it into three shares, and set one before each of them, they all understood what was afoot.

One squad member, dressed as a dock worker, asked suddenly, “Why the three of us?”

“Four.” The mustache said. “Counting me.”

The man asked again, “Why the four of us?”

The mustache said, “Because we are all fighters who look on death as a homecoming.”

The man glanced at Xiufen and still pressed, “Why is there a woman comrade as well?”

“Why so many whys from you?” The mustache was growing a little impatient. “We're revolutionary fighters. Between men and women we are equal.”

The man opened his mouth no more; he lowered his head and gripped the money tight.

It was only just before they set out that they learned their mission: to assassinate Nakamura Nobuo. This intelligence expert, dubbed the “Eagle of East Asia” by the Japanese military command, was about to return home to report; and the general manager of the Dahua Trading Company was to give his old friend and colleague of many years a farewell dinner, on the tenth floor of the Cathay Hotel. That was the finest building in the Far East, and the pleasure-ground of Japanese secret agents and Nanjing's traitors, where amid the singing of orioles and dancing of swallows, cheek pressed to cheek, there often came the flash of knives and the shadow of swords.

Outside the hotel door lay Nanjing Road in the night. By now the snow had stopped and the wind had died, and all at once several cleaners came to shovel snow. In their pockets, besides pistols, each carried a small wax pill. As the mustache put the wax pills into their hands, he said, “Comrades, we do not fear to give our lives. Our sacrifice today is for tomorrow's victory.”

Out of the great glass revolving door of the Cathay Hotel a crowd suddenly came; the bellboy standing to one side took off his cap. This was the signal. Xiufen knew the moment they had waited for had come. She threw down the shovel in her hands, darted across the road, drew her pistol with one hand, and pushed the wax pill into her mouth with the other.

Nakamura Nobuo, in full military dress, had plainly eaten and drunk his fill; and just as he came down the steps and, together with his wife, bowed farewell to Qin Zhaokuan and Su Lina, the shots rang out. Bullets from four pistols, fired from three directions, struck Nakamura Nobuo and the Japanese embassy's military attaché standing to one side, and struck Qin Zhaokuan as well. The three men fell almost at once onto the snow, and only then did the bodyguards all around realize what was happening and pull their guns to fire.

Xiufen fired off all seven rounds in her magazine in one breath, then turned and ran. The route had been laid out beforehand; Xiufen had run only a few steps along the Nanjing Road pavement when her waist felt as though a fist had struck it, and she pitched headlong to the ground.

The gunfire still rang out, but Xiufen saw that her own blood, under the street lamp, was black. She bit down hard and burst the wax pill in her mouth, lay quietly in the snow, and quietly listened to the sound of the whole world receding.
