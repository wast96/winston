## Backstage

The Lehua was not far. Along Weixin Road south to Xihu Road, then a turn to the east, and one more block on, and there beneath the arcade hung the theater’s great signboard. Sure enough, Little Phoenix was on that evening’s bill — a scene from Ten Beauties Round King Xuan, the “Untying of the Red Silk,” with Little Phoenix in the very part of Su Jinding.

When darkness fell, Chen Qianli and Liang Shichao bought their tickets and went in early. It was not yet time for the play; the table seats in the middle stood empty, though a fair number of people had come to the seats along the two sides. The two had changed their clothes some while before — the one in a long gown and riding jacket, the other in a light Western suit, for all the world a pair of foreign-firm compradors. They did not sit down at once, but came round from behind the colonnade to below the stage; Chen Qianli signed to Liang Shichao to wait outside, pushed open the side door, and went in.

An odd-job man sat at the door to the backstage and was just about to speak, when Chen Qianli drew out a silver dollar, pressed it into his hand, and said straight out: “I’m here to see Little Phoenix.”

Rich patrons were forever coming backstage to ask for this or that performer by name, and the theater people thought nothing of it. The man took the money — never expecting a red packet before the play had even begun — and, well pleased, told Chen Qianli: “Little Phoenix is upstairs.”

On the stairs a smell of powder and rouge met him. The Qunfangyan was an all-women troupe, and the backstage was a flutter of orioles and swallows. A single passage ran along above, too narrow for two abreast; the doors on either side stood half open, and from within came a twittering of voices. Chen Qianli stood in the middle of the passage and said aloud, easily: “I’m looking for Little Phoenix.”

“Who wants me?”

A door opened from within. Her brows were bound up and the temple-pieces pasted on, the hairline blacked, but she had not yet put on the phoenix ornaments, though she already wore the gold-and-red Cantonese-embroidered costume. Uncle Li at the cigarette shop had said she was gorgeous as a will-o’-the-wisp, but there was no seeing it just now.

Chen Qianli walked over and said with a smile: “I do.”

Once through the door, he added: “Chen is my humble name.”

Little Phoenix looked at him doubtfully. A patron who came backstage was surely a regular at the theater, one she would long since have grown used to seeing from the stage. But this man was tall, his eyes bright and keen, not at all like the pampered wastrels she was used to; and something in her checked.

“The play not even begun, and Mr. Chen already wants to come and look at the players,” she returned, smiling too.

Chen Qianli took out a silver cigarette case, flicked it open, took one for himself, and held the case out to Little Phoenix. She reached for a cigarette — then, seeing all at once that they were Garrick, was checked a moment.

“I’ve come to see you on behalf of a friend.”

Little Phoenix shot him a wary glance. This visitor was not making love to her, the way the men who bribed their way backstage usually did. Those men generally came only after the play was over; a few would come between acts, while she had no part on stage, to pay her a call and show that hers was the only performance worth their watching.

To come in before the play began — in all these years, only one man had ever done that.

For at this hour the great men and officials were still at their banquets, or only just risen from them; they always came sauntering in long after the play had started, plumping themselves down grandly at the table seats in the middle of the house, with all the bustle of tea brought and hot towels passed.

“My friend — his name is Lu Zhongde. An officer at the Public Security Bureau.”

“He’s dead.” She turned to her own face in the dressing mirror, and answered fast.

“He is not dead, of course. And I know — and you know — that he is still alive.” Chen Qianli came back just as fast; the words ran like a tongue-twister, but his tone was very gentle.

“You’ve seen him?” she asked, doubtful.

“I’ve just come by boat from Shanghai.”

That woke her interest. Still she said nothing, though, keeping her guard against him.

“He goes by Yi now.” Chen Qianli chanced it. For all the makeup, the brows and eye-corners drawn up high, one could still see the flicker in Little Phoenix’s eyes. So she did know the man now went by the surname Yi.

“Who are you? What are you to him?” Little Phoenix asked, low and dim.

“He and I are colleagues, in Nanjing. Have you ever heard of Teacher Ye?” Chen Qianli followed his instinct, probing.

She relaxed a little. Perhaps they had threatened her; perhaps — perhaps they had even thought of killing her, and had in the end let her live. That last — had she herself ever come to hear of it?

“He’s run into some trouble now; he said there were things I could come to Guangzhou and ask you about.” Chen Qianli began to lay it on with an air of mystery — too much mystery, perhaps, for he found that Little Phoenix had fallen silent again.

Someone pushed the door open — the same odd-job man who had sat at the backstage entrance, a great flower basket in one hand, and in the other a flash of silver: a shield, near as big as the mirror on the dressing table. In the last year or two the fashion of Beiping and Shanghai — presenting silver shields to favored players — had spread to Guangzhou too. The shields were of solid silver, and their size was a measure of the giver’s means. This one was no small shield.

“A flower basket and a silver shield, from Young Master Wu.”

“Put it out on the stage.” Little Phoenix showed no interest; when the man had gone, she turned back to Chen Qianli. “Not a shadow of him for two months, and now he sends baskets and shields.”

“Little Phoenix is the toast of all Guangzhou; a man from out of town, once here, is bound to hear of her.”

“Mr. Chen makes his joke.” She spoke as though reciting lines on the stage, paused a moment, and her look turned somber. “Times are not what they were; folk would sooner see a film than a play. Once a single shout of praise from the pit would set the whole stage rocking — but those days have long since gone up in smoke. Outsiders see the bustle; we know for ourselves the warm and the cold of it. The gold plaques and silver shields at the stage-front, the banked flowers like brocade — all of it is cloud before the eyes. It’s as if, the moment he left, everything began to show its ruin.”

Chen Qianli found his instinct borne out, and made a bold guess: “He promised he’d be back for you within half a year — and then never came.” He said it low, half a sentence, with neither head nor tail to it.

He guessed that Ye Qinian had had, at the outset, no long-term plan to plant a man; he had merely brought off one coup in Guangzhou and wanted to bring off another in Shanghai — to send an agent under a dead man’s name into the Shanghai underground, and there break up one or two more Communist organs and take a few more Communists. It was only later, perhaps, that Ye Qinian slowly grasped that in Guangzhou the man the underground had meant to send to Shanghai was very likely known to no one. The organizational system had been smashed; these assignments had run on single-line contact from the first; some had died, some had turned, and there was no one left who knew the truth of it.

The transfer had been arranged before the Guangzhou underground was broken, and the Shanghai underground never knew that the man sent to them had been quietly switched, one man swapped for another; they could not tell the difference — might never be able to tell it. And so the agent’s time undercover was drawn out. He had supposed he need only hold on a few more months, and then he could take off the mask. He had very likely promised this woman before him that in half a year he would be back, and the two of them could live well ever after.

Chen Qianli had guessed right.

“At first he said three months,” Little Phoenix blurted, “and before he left he said half a year at the most. Now three whole years have gone by. What on earth is he doing in Shanghai? He can’t write, can’t wire, can’t tell a soul he’s still alive. But just three months — half a year at the most.”

“Do you know what sort of man he is?”

“I don’t. I never did know what he was about. He told me later that he’d first lost his heart to me at the Sincere Company’s rooftop garden; and afterward, when the troupe broke up and re-formed and I moved to the Lehua, he followed me there to hear me sing, sitting every day in his corner. It was at the Lehua that I slowly began to notice him. He was usually in plain clothes, though now and then he’d wear his uniform. I liked him in uniform — it gave him a fine dashing air. And unlike the others, when he watched the play he’d fix me with those eyes of his, till it set you trembling inside.”

A jumble of footsteps sounded along the passage, and from without came drifting the aria of King Xuan in his cups:

“This Zhaoyang — her face like a pink lotus risen from the water, her beauty out-vying the fairy maid in the moon; but the pity of it, she knows nothing of the arts of love, wooden as a block of wood; and as for charm and coquetry…”

It was as though a sluice had suddenly opened in Little Phoenix, and the words dammed up in her for years could pour out at last. At first she had thought she need only wait for him a few months; grudging as it was, she had agreed to it in the end. But then he had vanished, as if he had forgotten her. Let him forget, then — she had thought she could forget him too; and yet she could not, just like some of the women she played in the operas: the further from sight a person was, the less could she forget.

“A few months in, he suddenly came backstage. All that while I’d been wondering, when would he ever come in? But even when he did, he’d say nothing — he’d just sit in that chair of yours; only, unlike you, he’d pull the chair up closer, closer to the dressing table—”

Chen Qianli shifted his chair.

“Yes, just about there. He’d say nothing, only smoke — your brand of cigarette. He worked at the Public Security Bureau, not far from here, so he could come every day. He’d come in the afternoon, look in on me backstage first, then go and hear the play, and after the play come back again. A man of many thoughts, and he’d never tell you one of them. I never did understand what he was really about — plainly working at the Public Security Bureau, and then off duty, slipping away alone to listen to Communists making speeches, joining some study class or other.”

Little Phoenix looked at Chen Qianli. His eyes were lowered, and he listened quietly, like a knowing member of the audience following some low, winding passage of song. She wondered whether someday someone might take her own life’s story and sing it upon the stage.

“But which side he really stood on, I couldn’t make out. One night I saw a half-written letter on the table; it said that in the name of the worker and peasant masses the writer would shoot the man it was addressed to, for his shameless high-handedness, his running against all reason. On the envelope was written: Whampoa Military Academy. A few days later, coming home from the theater in the dead of night, I saw him in the back room with the floor all covered over — he’d pulled the flowers up by the roots, earth and all, and was burying a bomb under the flowerpots.”

Seeing that this Mr. Chen was rapt in her tale, Little Phoenix turned back to the mirror and set the temple-piece straight on her brow. “I was frightened out of my wits. He only laughed and told me it wouldn’t go off — it was to put a fright into someone. I asked him, a fright into whom? He wouldn’t say. I asked was he a Communist, was it that the Communists meant to assassinate someone; he said, never you fear, I could never be a Communist. I’m only playing the Communist to frighten a man — to turn him into a hater of Communists. A month or two after that, one day there was suddenly a rumor going round that the Zhongshan gunboat was going to mutiny, to sail up the Pearl River and shell Guangzhou. He came home in the highest spirits, and that night he was especially good to me.”

Little Phoenix paused a moment and looked down at the cigarette she had just lit in her hand; behind the makeup, no expression showed on her face.

“And then at last it came out in the papers — that he was a Communist, that he’d been shot. Even then I couldn’t make out whether he really was a Communist or not. For a whole week before that he’d been on edge, as though he were in the middle of something, afraid some slip might show. By that time we’d been living together more than two years.

“Before that, this Teacher Ye had come from Nanjing to see him. He came by airplane — the Dashatou airfield. In those days, whenever Teacher Ye came to Guangzhou to see him, it was always by airplane, hurrying in and out. A great personage — I’ve known few men who spend their days flying about by air.

“After Teacher Ye had gone back, he sat on the bed smoking and said to me, now at last it can all come clear. That night it poured with rain, and I had no need to go to the theater. He took a bath in the evening and opened a bottle of foreign wine. He said the work was done, the whole thing settled, and now he had only to wait to be rewarded for his merit.”

Chen Qianli said nothing, and made no move. The lead huadan of the Qunfangyan troupe, the celebrated Little Phoenix whom all Guangzhou knew, wore now the faintest smile: she had entered into the part, remembering those hopes and joys, though they had long since gone up in smoke.

“But just at that time, a man surnamed Long telephoned. I knew of him — he’d call now and then to reach him, and if he wasn’t in, would say to send him to the old place. It was only later, when it came out in the papers, that I understood he was a Communist, and that the old place they spoke of was most likely Moat-Bowstring Street. On the phone he told this man Long that he’d been trying to reach him for two days, that he had something urgent to tell him. He said, did you see the note? I think he must have left a note for the man Long somewhere, saying he wanted to reach him—”

She was born to make her living on the stage, Chen Qianli thought; not a single detail would she forget.

“At first he didn’t want to say it over the phone, but in the end he did. He told the man Long that someone — an Ouyang somebody — was in danger, that the Public Security Bureau meant to arrest him, and that someone had to go and warn him, but that he himself could not go, because this Ouyang didn’t know him. The man Long must have thought a while on the line; the two of them fell quiet a spell, neither speaking. Then the man Long began again — he’d decided, most likely, to go himself — and the two of them started to argue over the phone. He told the man Long, since you’ve already got out, you mustn’t be the one to go on this. But in the end he couldn’t prevail against him, and agreed to let the man Long go to that old place.

“But he put down the phone and told me he was going too. I wasn’t best pleased — hadn’t it been settled that the man Long would go? He said he couldn’t rest easy. That night he came home very late; I was already asleep. He got into bed with his hair dripping wet, his face white, and said he’d nearly been beaten to death. All night long he tossed and turned.”

Little Phoenix stopped all at once, and avoiding the visitor’s eyes, said, half to herself: “Why am I telling you all this?”

Chen Qianli understood now: Long Dong had indeed been ready to withdraw, but Lu Zhongde had used Ouyang Min as a lure to trick him to Moat-Bowstring Street. He looked up at Little Phoenix. She did not care a straw for Young Master Wu; it was Officer Lu she cherished in her heart. He had cut a fine dashing figure, and had promised her he would be back in half a year at the most, and that they would never part again. She simply could not understand at all why, three years gone, he still had not come back.

Just then came a soft knock at the door, and the troupe manager pushed it open and came in, making a bow with cupped hands to Little Phoenix and to Chen Qianli each in turn, and wearing an ingratiating smile: “When will the star take the stage? Their honors are already seated.”

“Let them wait!” said Little Phoenix sharply. The great stars never took the stage on time, and the manager was long used to it; he scratched his head and backed out.

Little Phoenix composed herself and looked at Chen Qianli.

“The next morning he was much better. He ate some congee and said he had done a great service, that good days were coming. He was happy for two days, buying me this and that every day. He always had money — more money than the other officers. On the day of the Dragon Boat Festival he gave me a set of jade head-ornaments, saying that on the stage it was the reds and greens that pleased the eye. But once the festival was past, he said he had to go — to leave Guangzhou for a time. He couldn’t write, wouldn’t wire me. On the day he left I saw the news in the paper, and it gave me such a fright that I hurried home to find him. He changed his tune again and said, half a year at the most, no longer. The news in the paper was false; if only everyone believed it, he would be safe.

“That evening, packing his case for him, I saw a travel-agent’s boarding list; the name on it wasn’t his — it was Yi. When I asked him about it, he frightened me, saying I must never tell a soul he was still alive. He was dead now; he would stay dead half a year, and after half a year he would come alive again, come back to Guangzhou, and we would never part again. But until then I mustn’t speak of my affairs to anyone, or not only would he be in danger, I would be in danger myself. He frightened me — said that because I knew he was still alive, there were people who meant to kill me, and that he had held them off.

“Every year the troupe disbands in the fifth month, and after the festival the retaining silver is paid out, so I had a little money in hand; afraid he’d not have enough for the road, I gave it all to him.” Little Phoenix steadied herself, seeming to have said all she had wanted to say.

Once Lu Zhongde was gone, she must have lived a long while in daily dread; only when she found that no one came to do her any harm did she slowly set her heart at rest, deciding she was surely in no danger after all, that she was safe. And then she had begun once more to dwell on Lu Zhongde’s kindnesses…

Chen Qianli rose to take his leave; he had just stepped out the door when Little Phoenix said suddenly behind him: “Mr. Chen — if you see him, carry him a word from me.”

Chen Qianli nodded.

“The rouge is all used up.” Little Phoenix closed the door.

Chen Qianli and Liang Shichao did not take table seats, but neither did they hurry away from the theater. They stood behind a pillar at the left of the back row and watched the stage a while.

On the stage the sovereign stared boldly at Su Jinding; she turned her back, and shyly undid the knot of red silk sent in tribute by the barbarian king — the riddle the whole court, civil and military, could not unravel, she unraveled.
