### The Traitor Gu Shunzhang's Shameful End

### A Rat Everyone Wants Dead

For years Gu Shunzhang had helped Zhou Enlai run the Party's intelligence and security work; he was the real head of the Central Special Section, a man of great power who held a mass of the Chinese Communist Party's core secrets. His betrayal did the revolution the gravest harm, posed an enormous threat to the safety of the Party's central organs and its leaders, and cost the Party's cause a heavy loss. True, Zhou Enlai's quick, decisive emergency measures had utterly wrecked the scheme Gu put to Chiang Kai-shek — to net the central organs and chief leaders all in one sudden haul — but the great manhunt the enemy now mounted in Shanghai still badly damaged the Party Center and the local Party organizations, and many Communists were arrested and killed. The British writer Dick Wilson, in his life of Zhou Enlai, says that Gu Shunzhang "gave away intelligence on the Communist leaders and organization, and as a result more than 800 Communists were arrested."

Because of Gu Shunzhang's betrayal, the whole set of secret working methods the Party had built up in Shanghai — for safety, security, and courier work — could no longer be used. New methods could not be set up at once, and from the Central Committee down the Party organizations at every level were brought to a standstill; in some places they were near collapse. Facing so grim a terror, the Party's leading cadres at every level could not only not resume their work but found even their own survival gravely threatened. At the same time, the intelligence network built up with such effort over more than three hard years was badly wrecked and the Special Section's strength gravely weakened; the heads and senior cadres of its several branches could no longer operate and could hardly remain in Shanghai, and were transferred one after another to other places. Among the men of the Third Branch, which Gu Shunzhang had directly led, some were arrested by the Zhongtong agents through his betrayal; most stood loyal and unbending and went calmly to their deaths, but a few — Chen Qingzhai (that is, Hu Dahai), Hu Hongtao, Wang Guodong, and others — fell under Gu Shunzhang's influence and became traitors to the revolution as well. And Tong Guozhong, Cai Fei (nicknamed Old Wrinkle-Face), and Chen Liansheng (nicknamed Little White-Face, aliases Zhang Alin, Zhang Wennong, and others) followed Gu Shunzhang in betraying the Party. So the Central Special Section faced disintegration, and the whole apparatus urgently needed to be adjusted, reorganized, and rebuilt.

Gu Shunzhang not only sold to the enemy such Central Committee leaders as Yun Daiying and Cai Hesen, so that they were killed by the Kuomintang reactionaries; he also schemed for the enemy, seizing Communists by every means. He used the family ties, the friendships, and the friends-of-friends of the members he knew, regularly posting his running dogs at their doors to arrest or tail any member they saw. He used every arrested renegade, spreading them through the streets — especially at the crossroads of important thoroughfares, at stations, even at the markets — to seize anyone important on the spot and tail anyone less so. He had his brother-in-law Zhang Changgeng and Zhang's cousin Ye Qicheng comb the streets for Communists, and at the door of the Laodafang shop by Jing'an Temple they caught Wang Shide (nicknamed the Old Gentleman) and handed him to the Kuomintang's Jing'an Temple police station. He set up rented rooms and pounced on any suspicious tenant; he planted his running dogs as waiters in hotels and aboard steamers to spy on the guests; he sent informers to lurk inside the Party's organs. After his betrayal Gu Shunzhang clung to the enemy with this same fixed devotion and, like a man possessed, wrecked the Party organizations in Shanghai and elsewhere. From a man at the very core of the Communist leadership he had turned into a soul-selling turncoat and the enemy's hired hound, and he set the whole Party trembling with shock and rage.

When Gu Shunzhang was taken to Nanjing after his betrayal, it was rumored at first that the Kuomintang meant to bring him to Shanghai to take part in the plan to wreck the Party Center. To punish this arch-traitor, Zhou Enlai instructed Chen Geng to deploy men and lay an ambush at the railway station, to catch Gu Shunzhang off guard the moment he stepped off the train and deal with him by open, lawful execution. But Gu Shunzhang, with a guilty conscience, did not dare come. Still he would not give up, and did all he could to send men to Shanghai to seek out the connections he could use, hoping through them to wreck our Party. The Central Committee took prompt, drastic measures and cut off every one of these ties that threatened the Party. As for Gu Shunzhang himself, on May 21, 1931, the Central Committee issued Notice No. 223, declaring Gu Shunzhang the most shameful of traitors, that the Center had decided to expel him from the Party forever, and calling on the whole Party to strengthen mass work and rise as one to destroy Gu Shunzhang, the enemy of China's worker and peasant masses, and every traitor to communism.

On December 1 that year Mao Zedong, chairman of the Council of People's Commissars of the Provisional Central Government of the Chinese Soviet Republic, "in the matter of the wanted revolutionary traitor Gu Shunzhang," signed in the red capital of Ruijin the Order of the Council of People's Commissars of the Provisional Central Government:

To the Soviet governments at every level — provincial, county, district, and township — of every Soviet area; to the Red Army and Red Guards everywhere and the great masses of toiling workers and peasants; to the workers, peasants, and all oppressed masses of the White-ruled areas:

The traitor to the worker and peasant masses, Gu Shunzhang (also called Li Ming, originally named Gu Fengming, once using the stage name the Magician Hua Guangqi; about twenty-seven or twenty-eight, a native of Wusong, Shanghai, extremely short, with bulging eyes and a flat nose), the moment he was caught by the Kuomintang counterrevolutionary detective corps in Hankou on April 25 this year, surrendered to the reactionary rulers. He betrayed to the enemy the Chinese Communist Party's courier station in Wuhan, the West Hubei United-Counties Soviet Government, and the Hankou office of the Second Group Army of the Red Army, so that all were seized whole and more than ten revolutionary fighters carried off, later all killed by the reactionary Wuhan government — down to a sympathetic worker aboard a river steamer whose station only Gu Shunzhang knew, whom he too gave up, to be flung into a counterrevolutionary prison. Having thus paid and pledged in blood, Gu Shunzhang wired Nanjing again and again to demand an audience with Chiang Kai-shek in person. Once in Nanjing, besides informing the Kuomintang counterrevolutionaries of the organization and activity of the Communist Party, the Soviet government, the Red Army, and every revolutionary body of the toiling workers and peasants, he pointed out from prison the Central Committee member and national youth leader Yun Daiying and others, already sentenced by the Nanjing government, so that the counterrevolutionaries shot them at once. At the same time he gave the counterrevolutionaries the five addresses, known to him from his Shanghai days, of the Central Committee members and responsible men Zhou Enlai, Qu Qiubai, Li Weihan, and others, and it was at once wired to the Shanghai Public Security Bureau to seize them by name together with the imperialists' concession police — though by good fortune these comrades had by then left Shanghai and so came to no harm. Then, on the leads he knew, Gu Shunzhang went on sending his kin and confidants to spy out in Shanghai the organs and responsible men of the Communist Party and every revolutionary body... From then on Gu Shunzhang rose to become an important man in Chiang Kai-shek's secret murder apparatus, the C.C. clique, a helper in Chiang Kai-shek's killing alongside such counterrevolutionary criminals as Chen Guofu, Chen Lifu, Xu Enzeng, and Yang Hu.

Of late, with the Red Army's victories across the Soviet areas, the founding of the Provisional Central Soviet Government, and the rising anti-Kuomintang, anti-imperialist struggle of the toiling workers and peasants of the White-ruled areas, the Chinese Communist Party and the revolutionary mass bodies, though under an extreme White Terror, have won the support of the great masses, so that their organizations have grown all the firmer and closer-knit and lead the toiling workers and peasants ever more resolutely to struggle around the Soviet banner. And so the traitor Gu Shunzhang's poisonous plot to wipe out the revolutionary organizations and the persons of the revolutionary leaders could not succeed; he changed his tack, and now, insolently branding our responsible revolutionary comrades and the Central Committee as murderers, tried by this trickery to wreck the faith the masses hold in the Communist Party and its leaders. Baser still, Gu Shunzhang himself put a wanted notice in the papers for the responsible Communist Zhou Enlai and others, claiming that since his own defection he had harmed no Communist but only shut himself away to read books. This shameless denial can never hide the bloody harm his cruel and murderous hand has done the revolution, as every revolutionary knows. As for the price on our heads: every revolutionary fighter, every toiling worker and peasant, the moment he casts his lot with the revolution, has already had his life entered in the counterrevolution's book of the wanted — what need to wait for the traitor Gu Shunzhang to advertise it?

Class hatred has come to a fight to the death. What the Kuomintang counterrevolution would proscribe is the very thing the great toiling masses would uphold; and, conversely, cruel traitors of Gu Shunzhang's kind, whom the Kuomintang counterrevolution shelters, are the very ones the great toiling masses would stamp out. The counterrevolution rests only on landlords, the bourgeoisie, the Kuomintang, the warlords, and the bureaucrats — men walking their own road to ruin. The revolution's makers and upholders are the vast body of workers, peasants, the urban poor, and all the oppressed, and the victorious Soviet government and the Chinese Communist Party are leading them to win the revolution across the whole country. As the revolutionary tide rises higher and higher, and Chiang Kai-shek's armies of two and three hundred thousand are beaten down, once, twice, three times, by the worker-peasant Red Army as by a gale through dead leaves, how can one traitor Gu Shunzhang and Chiang Kai-shek's murderous C.C. clique wipe out the revolution? On the contrary — they will drown at last in the great waves of revolution!

Therefore the Provisional Central Soviet Government hereby orders the Soviet governments at every level, the Red Army, and the Red Guards everywhere, and gives notice to the toiling workers and peasants of the whole country: guard strictly against the plots of the Kuomintang counterrevolution, and as one seize the traitor Gu Shunzhang. In the Soviet areas, should you meet this traitor, take him and hand him to a revolutionary tribunal for trial; in the White-ruled areas, should you meet this traitor, every revolutionary fighter, every poor worker and peasant, has the duty to stamp him out. To seize and stamp out the traitor Gu Shunzhang is the conscious, glorious duty of every revolutionary fighter and every worker and peasant. The bounty put up by the landlords, the capitalists, and the counterrevolution's running dogs cannot be used to defile us, the exploited and oppressed masses.

Let the great toiling masses of workers and peasants close their ranks and as one seize the traitor Gu Shunzhang!

Carry the revolutionary struggle forward, and wipe out root and branch Chiang Kai-shek's murderous C.C. clique!

Whether in the Party organizations of the White areas or among the people of the Soviet areas, Gu Shunzhang, this shameless traitor beyond all pardon, had become a rat in the street that everyone cries out to kill.

### Betrayal Worse Than Ever

When Gu Shunzhang first defected, his standing within the Communist Party made him, for the moment, a prize catch. Chiang Kai-shek summoned Xu Enzeng and heaped praise on him for it; the agents great and small who had worked the case, Cai Mengjian, Huang Kai, and the rest, were all promoted and ennobled; Cai Mengjian even won a "special citation" from the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters and was called in by Chiang Kai-shek for "training"; Huang Kai was moved up from Zhongtong special commissioner in Wuhan to special commissioner in Shanghai. The case was the making of Xu Enzeng's Zhongtong, and the capital on which it vied with Dai Li's Juntong for favor.

"Because of Gu Shunzhang's defection, Yun Daiying was killed, some of the Shanghai Communist organs were wrecked, and some Communists arrested. For this Chiang summoned Xu Enzeng in person, heaped rewards on him, and told him (in substance): where the Communists are, I am not; where I am, the Communists are not — you must go all out. So long as you get real results, nothing else matters. Now Chen Guofu and Chen Lifu too looked on Xu with new eyes and leaned on him as a right hand. From then on Xu Enzeng's leading place in the Zhongtong secret-service apparatus was fully established, and the Investigation Section he led loomed larger still within the whole C.C. clique. Xu Enzeng seized the chance to enlarge his tables of organization, fill out his ranks, and open training classes for secret agents. Then, at the Zhanyuan garden on Daoshu Street in Nanjing, he set up a General Secret-Service Headquarters, and step by step established anti-Communist commissioners and secret-service rooms in the provinces, cities, and railways of the Chiang-ruled area. From then on the Zhongtong secret service entered a new stage."

At first, prize as he was, the enemy did not trust Gu Shunzhang, and kept him confined in Nanjing all the while. Only after a long time was he let out of confinement (though still closely watched in secret). Xu Enzeng threw him a purse of reward-money and found him a woman named Zhang Ping. At the same time he was made head of the Zhongtong Investigation Section's action squad, and a courtyard house was rented for him on Xiliuxiang in Nanjing to serve as his office and private residence.

Once he had won the trust of the Kuomintang secret-service organization, Gu Shunzhang enjoyed a spell of "riding high." To show the loyalty of his "surrender," he sold the Party's secrets, hunted Communists like a madman, and cooked up wicked schemes for the Kuomintang, compiled lecture notes, ran training classes, and taught secret-service technique, greatly to Xu Enzeng's liking. The Memoirs of Xu Enzeng even praises Gu Shunzhang: "witty in talk, rich and seasoned in worldly experience, full of human feeling, good at reading people's minds, glad to draw close to others..." Among the Zhongtong agents one Meng Zhen called Gu Shunzhang the "master of secret-service work," saying that "on the civil side — disguise, conjuring, working and repairing machines, psychology, and the like — he was expert; on the military side — shooting with both hands, demolition, firing a pistol indoors so that nothing is heard outside, killing a man barehanded and leaving no trace, and so on — he was fit to be called a 'master,' with no one able to touch him." Lin Jinsheng, the bodyguard Xu Enzeng set at Gu Shunzhang's side, said too that Gu Shunzhang "was not highly educated, but he could talk a good line, and since he had long held responsible work in the Shanghai Special Section he really did have a knack for special-action work. Besides being able to do conjuring tricks, he had one gift I much admired — his skill at disguise was superb. Let him make a man up only a little, and you could hardly recognize him again. I remember he once fitted a set of false teeth into his mouth and his whole look changed. By his own account, in his Central Special Section days he had quite a reputation for appearing and vanishing like a spirit, mainly on the strength of this gift." He made it out to be almost miraculous — sheer boasting, blown clean out of proportion. It is said that for a time he became a prize over which the two great secret-service chiefs, Xu Enzeng of the Zhongtong and Dai Li of the Juntong, fought.

For such "favor" Gu Shunzhang was ready to give his all in repayment. He turned every trick he had to serve his masters, doing his utmost to wreck the Party organizations and hunt down Communists.

He offered Chiang Kai-shek and Xu Enzeng a plan, urging the Kuomintang to change its way of dealing with the Communists. In the past, Gu Shunzhang told them, the Kuomintang had either killed or jailed the Communists it caught — but they should understand that against Communists brutal repression was of little use. To wipe out the Communists, the best course was to attack the mind, to use Communists to subdue Communists. Arrested Communists should not all be shot; rather a policy of surrender should be applied, hard and soft together, to lure them into surrendering, and so break up the Communist organization. The method was to talk arrested Communists into betraying the Party and going over to the enemy — some by secret surrender, some by open — and to send some of them back into the Communist organization as spies. This poisonous scheme won high praise from Chiang and Xu, who put Gu Shunzhang in special charge of buying up Communist traitors and of the "education" of those who surrendered. The Kuomintang secret service duly changed its "beheading policy" toward Communists into a "surrender policy." They pushed the surrender policy across the country, and certain weak-willed men in our Party, men who clung to life and feared death, were drawn over by the Kuomintang and became shameless traitors. After Gu Shunzhang and the secret service had vetted them, these people were released, openly or in secret, and by various means planted back inside the Communist Party as spies, coming little by little to control our Party's activity, so that the Party's organs were wrecked again and again and the Party and the revolutionary cause suffered enormous harm. That our Party organizations in the White areas took such grievous losses between 1931 and 1935, and that the Central Committee was forced to move to the Soviet in 1933, was owing not only to the ultra-"Left" mistaken line within the Party but in no small part to this surrender policy that Gu Shunzhang had put to the enemy.

Huang Kai, the "big agent" who had taken part in Gu Shunzhang's arrest in Wuhan, was a favorite pupil of Zhang Chong; he had entered the Investigation Section back in March 1929, when Zhang Daofan, Wu Dajun, and others headed it, and was one of the Zhongtong's founding members. A conceited man, he would not deign to notice such juniors as Ye Xiufeng and Xu Enzeng. Yet after dealing with Gu Shunzhang, even Huang Kai had to admit that Gu "really did have his shrewd points in secret-service work." Huang Kai, mentioning Gu Shunzhang in an essay of his own, also much admired this stroke of his: "Gu Shunzhang came to Shanghai a while before me, and his work is said to have gone very smoothly; by his own account he had already planted some 'cells' (that is, spies) inside the Communist Party and drawn in some inside contacts, so that he knew part of what the Communists were doing. I had a poor opinion of him and kept no dealings with him, using a courier to pass word when something had to be arranged. But he was a threat to my own standing and reputation, for his cases came off, Dai Li and he were thick as thieves, and I could not help feeling put in the shade."

In the 1930s German and Italian fascism rose in western Europe. Chiang Kai-shek followed Hitler and Mussolini, pushed fascism in China, and ran a dictatorship. Gu Shunzhang too served Chiang Kai-shek's tightening of the fascist dictatorship, training action squads by turns for both secret services, the Zhongtong and the Juntong. The Zhongtong action squad trained two companies in its first course, about two hundred men. He also ran technical training classes, teaching agents in person how to disguise themselves, how to use chemicals, how to build an investigation network, how to set up courier stations, and so on. He joined the German advisers and the secret-service chiefs, too, in giving the trainees a fascist education. Gu Shunzhang laid down a creed for the agents: if an operation failed and they were caught, they were to kill themselves at once and leave no confession.

Gu Shunzhang also ran two secret-service training classes. The first had only four men, held at his residence in Shuangtangxiang inside the Nanjing city wall. Later he ran another on Changle Street in Nanjing with more than twenty. Lin Chengyin, who took the second class, recounted: "Before this, Gu Shunzhang had already run one class, of only four men, all graduates of the sixth class of the Whampoa Military Academy. I took the second, larger than the first, with about twenty men. Those whose names I can recall are Qian Danquan, Wu Shendao, Zhang Huaiqing, Zang Gonghui, Zhou Lian, Zheng Pu, Zhu Jiading, Tong Mingxin, Ji Chun'an, and others. Living in the class too were the secretary Wang Sicheng, the general-affairs man Wang Guodong, the accountant Fan Lan (Wang Sicheng's wife), my cousin's wife Hu Changyuan and her husband, Cai Fei, and Zhang Wennong. The place was on Changle Street inside Nanjing's Zhonghua Gate (I forget the number), an old-style house of three bays and seven courts, very roomy. The trainees ate and slept there, and two maidservants were hired to see to our daily needs.

"Once the class opened, Gu Shunzhang announced the rules to us: no trainee was to go out freely, and anyone who had special business outside had to get Qian Danquan's leave to go. The teaching was given by Gu Shunzhang himself, on The Theory and Practice of Secret Service Work — all such techniques as shooting, seizing and grappling, disguise, tailing, lying in wait. Gu Shunzhang was not highly educated, but at this trade he was richly experienced, an old hand indeed. Drawing on his own past, he talked away fluently and held one's attention. He was said to have trained in the Soviet 'Cheka'; but to me, a lad fresh from the countryside, all this felt so remote and strange, and I often shuddered at the cases he told, as at something out of the Arabian Nights. Later Zang Gonghui and Zhou Lian compiled his lectures into a booklet for the study of Zhongtong secret-service work, which even the training class the Juntong ran afterward took as a textbook; Gu Shunzhang and my cousin both went to Dai Li's house at Ji'e Alley and lectured to the Juntong's class, and during our training we went out twice with Gu Shunzhang for live-fire shooting. The three-month course soon ended, and on graduation day there was a great banquet at which Gu Shunzhang and his disciple Zhang Wennong performed a conjuring turn together. At the last Gu Shunzhang warned us again and again: once you take an assignment, you must never tell a soul, not even your own parents or wife and children, or you will face severe punishment."

Because Gu Shunzhang had trained in intelligence and security work in the Soviet Union, and had years of experience running the Central Special Section, he was quite a draw as a lecturer in the Kuomintang secret-service classes.

In these classes he set out the theory and practice of secret-service work very fully and minutely. To take one example: teaching "secret-service technique," he divided it into eleven topics — disguise, tailing, counter-detection, the weapons an agent uses, demolition, the art of talk, the art of secret speech, powers of observation, physiognomy, hypnosis, and photography. Under tailing he treated five aspects, and in the section "coping with various surroundings" he taught the techniques of tailing on foot, tailing by vehicle, tailing in a crowd, and special tailing. Of "tailing by vehicle" he said: "If the man you are tailing hails a rickshaw or a car, you must take a rickshaw or a car too and keep on his trail. But note: a rickshaw cannot tail a car; if a car is tailing a rickshaw it must go ahead of the rickshaw, get down at a suitable spot, and hire another rickshaw to tail. If there is no car or rickshaw to be had, you must at least note his vehicle's number, color, the time, place, and direction he rode, and check it later at the garage. In Shanghai the car plates are white on black in the concessions, blue on white in the Chinese city; and the numbers on hired and private cars also differ — all this must be kept clear. When tailing a car through a crossroads, the gap must not be too wide, or the change of the traffic light will break the link; at night the gap must be smaller still, lest the tailed car douse its number-lamp or throw out smoke and the car behind lose sight of it. In tailing a car one must watch too for the man firing in a tight spot, and be ready beforehand. In tailing on a tram, before boarding, watch the tailed man's manner, his movements, where he stands, whether he changes money and so on, to judge whether he means to board, and ready yourself accordingly. Once aboard, sit in the car matched to your station: in my ordinary clothes I can ride third class, in his Western suit he can ride first; each keeps it to himself and there is no need to greet the other. Aboard the tram, watch the ticket the tailed man buys — you need not see his ticket to know where he gets off, only how much money he counts out. When buying your own ticket, buy one for the far end, and watch the tailed man's every move and manner all the while, but do not stare at him woodenly, or not only he will grow wary of you but others too will take you for a pickpocket. Board and alight quickly, without hesitation, or the gate will close and you cannot. Board by jumping forward, right foot first, left foot after; alight by jumping with your motion, left foot first, right foot after." Of "special tailing" he said: "This means the long-distance tailing done on trains, on steamers, in hotels, and the like — work especially hard to do. If you can tail a man for a year without his noticing, then you have graduated in the art of tailing. It is in this work that a man's real ability shows. On a train sit behind him, and if you can, contrive to have his luggage set near where you sit, so that when he takes it he wakes you. Only when he is worn out and dozing may you sleep a little. Contrive to put some special mark on his luggage, but so that he does not notice it; if you lose him, you can search by the mark. Watch, too, in case he abandons even his luggage and slips away before you know it — then you must use the technique of socializing. If you can work the waiter and have him watch for you, the man cannot get away. On a steamer, watch every time she comes alongside a wharf. Once a student returning from Russia, passing through Vladivostok, boarded at night; the ship had sailed when he found an international detective on his tail, felt the danger, and at once leapt into the sea and fled. The next morning the international detective, seeing the tea in his teapot still hot, took it that he had truly just jumped into the sea and must certainly have drowned. He did not know that when the ship was not far out the student had leapt overboard alone and swum ashore, and that the tea was still hot because the waiter had kept it so for him. From this you can see this Russia-trained student's ability was a good deal higher than the international detective's."

Gu Shunzhang's craft caught the interest of Dai Li, the Juntong chief, too. Lin Jinsheng, the bodyguard Xu Enzeng had set at Gu's side, said Gu Shunzhang "was terribly afraid of being got at behind his back — I remember the first time Dai Li asked him to Ji'e Alley he insisted I go with him, and when he talked with Dai Li he insisted I sit at his side. Dai Li had asked him there to lecture to the Juntong's secret-service class." Around 1933, with Xu Enzeng's consent and Chiang Kai-shek's approval, Dai Li borrowed Gu Shunzhang from the Zhongtong to join the training work of the Juntong's class at Hongshan Temple in Nanjing, as its adviser and instructor. From then on Gu had direct dealings with Dai Li. Bent on outdoing Xu in merit and favor, Dai tried to turn the temporary loan into a permanent post, to pull Gu into the Juntong's ranks, courting him hard with promises of high place and large money. Since Gu, under Xu's charge, was neither trusted nor given power, he leaned more and more toward the Juntong.

Could Xu Enzeng brook such a thing?

In the essay by the Zhongtong stalwart Huang Kai already cited, there is an account of a Zhongtong high-cadre conference held in Nanjing in the summer of 1932, at which the sharpening quarrel between Juntong and Zhongtong over the seizing of Gu Shunzhang, and the intriguing within the Zhongtong itself, came into the open: "It was only when I heard the reports of Chen Lifu and Hong Lanyou that I learned Dai Li had come out on top: Gu Shunzhang was now the decisive figure on the Juntong side, and had made over to Dai Li every one of the 'cells' he had grown in the Communist Party. We had made a three-month tally of cases cracked, and the Zhongtong came to only a tenth of the Juntong; we showed results only in Nanjing, Jiujiang, Shanghai, and Hankou, while the Juntong's reached the whole country. Luckily we had cracked several stunning cases in Shanghai to keep up appearances, or it would have been more galling still. At the conference Ye Xiufeng's henchman Wu Xingbo said: 'We hand others the fruits of our hard work, and the Shanghai leaders must answer for it — for all day they smoke opium and go whoring, their private lives too foul, which drags down the morale of the lower ranks.' Zhang Guodong then said: 'If the Zhongtong falls flat, the Shanghai chief must answer for it. Now the Generalissimo has raised the Juntong's funds to eight times ours; we must learn from the Communists and consolidate our cadres with iron discipline — jail those who deserve jail, for merit and fault cannot cancel out.' Ye Xiufeng spoke up, first flattering me a while, then coming to the point: 'Comrade Huang has been in Shanghai more than half a year — what work has he arranged? Besides Gu Shunzhang, how many inside lines do you have in the Communist Party? Look over your record: it depends entirely on others bringing things to your door; were it not for the Military Council's attendants' office looking after you, you might go a whole year without cracking a case. If you slack any more, all the work will pass to that fellow Dai.' At this my hands went cold with anger, and I said: 'I cannot form lateral ties with you; for secrecy's sake there is no need to tell you. When I blew up the Yunjian and killed Japanese, was that a connection the Juntong handed me too?' Ye, thumping the table, said: 'Ask your own chief — surely he must know.' Xu Enzeng said awkwardly: 'The Shanghai work has indeed been passive, not active.' Ye Xiufeng said: 'We deal with matters, not men; to survive, we must sound out our organization. And you had better change your way of living.' Zhang Daofan protested sharply: 'A secret agent must be able to do everything, and know the bad things all the better — whoring and gambling can serve as cover; to strike at a good comrade who has been through life and death over such trifles won't do!' Zhang Chong seemed about to speak too, but Lifu stopped him, saying: 'His loose living is the organization's fault for harming him; I will answer for it — let him examine himself well.' Someone at the conference proposed merging Gu Shunzhang's and Huang Kai's two bodies of agents; Zhang Chong opposed it, and so did I. ...

"After the conference closed I returned to Shanghai with many others. By the resolution, a Shanghai Work Team was set up at once, with Ji Yuanpu as its head and concurrently head of the detective corps, and Wu Xingbo as head of the water-police corps, to take over Gu Shunzhang's work. We were back only a few days when my troubles began: Wang Bin, riding in a private rickshaw, reached the Great World amusement hall and was shot dead along with the puller; a few days later Cao Qingcheng too was assassinated at his own door by the West Gate; Lu Yuanhu was wounded as well. The men of the Work Team all turned into a funeral committee. By this time Gu Shunzhang had gone back to Nanjing, and Dai Li treated him as a prize."

In the few short years after his betrayal, Gu Shunzhang trained more than a thousand agents for the Kuomintang. These agents, their heads steeped in the reactionary spirit of fascism, became an important prop of Chiang Kai-shek's reactionary rule.

Gu Shunzhang also gathered up some traitors and surrendered men into a new action squad, made himself its head, trained them, and led them in person to Shanghai to wreck our Party's organizations at every level.

Among them, several traitors he had lured over from the Central Special Section — Tong Guozhong, Cai Fei, Chen Liansheng, and others — were Gu Shunzhang's confidants, cruel and merciless as he, like a pack of animals that had lost all humanity and feeling; and knowing certain of the Party's inner affairs, they wrecked the Party organizations again and again and cost the Party heavy losses.

### He Writes a Book to Curry Favor

Xu Enzeng seemed to set great store by Gu Shunzhang's lectures in those secret-service classes and by his work compiling training materials, and had detailed such trusted agents of the Investigation Section as Xu Zheng, Zhang Zhiren, and Zhu Qiubai to take down Gu Shunzhang's lectures and help him put them in order. In time these were made into six volumes of teaching material — Training Work, Intelligence Work, Reconnaissance Work, Action Work, Adjudication Work, and Organization Work — called the Secret-Agent Series. In the summer of 1933, while living on Anpin Street, Gu Shunzhang had someone ghostwrite for him, on the basis of these materials, a book for the Kuomintang's training of agents, The Theory and Practice of Secret Service Work. It was a book to curry favor with his masters, and one more piece of evidence of a soul sold after betraying the revolution.

By the account of Zhang Changgeng, Gu Shunzhang's brother-in-law, who lived with him at the time, the book's ghostwriter was Wang Yixin, a native of Changshu in Jiangsu. Wang had been a newspaper reporter and was himself a traitor who had surrendered after arrest, introduced to Gu by Gu Jianzhong. In his preface Gu Shunzhang says the book was "written with the help, in the writing, of Comrade Zang Gonghui."

Why did he write it? Gu Shunzhang meant, of course, to please the Kuomintang, to train more killer-agents for the enemy, and to pour out for the enemy what he knew of our Party's Central Special Section. Yet in his preface he protested his innocence:

My purpose in writing this book is to lay bare, and offer to the comrades who care about secret-service work as a reference, my own findings and views on secret-service work; and at the same time to ask my comrades for searching criticism and correction, as a guide to my continued study. I will say frankly that my motive is of the purest and most upright, with not the least selfish thought in it! I hope to make this book a guide in my "seeking teachers and finding friends," not a means of "fishing for fame."

The contents of this book rest entirely on the actual experience I have lived through in person, in all sorts of walks of society; they are neither cribbed from other works nor got by another's teaching. The great world of society is my school, hardship and adversity my teacher, all manner of real problems my textbooks and the stuff of my experiments. From the day I took up secret-service work until now, at every moment I have striven at everything in a spirit of creation and study, never idling, never growing proud or complacent.

The book had everything in it, the whole of his method for training agents. More than a hundred thousand characters, in eight chapters: an introduction, secret-service organization, the methods of secret-service work, the secret-service outlook, secrecy, secret-service technique, secret-service general knowledge, and the question of an agent's self-cultivation. In the chapter "secret-service general knowledge" there were "C.P. Secret-Service Work" and "Soviet Secret-Service Work." To tell the enemy, in the circumstances of the time, the top secrets of our Party's Central Special Section, and to publish them openly, was his continued selling of the Chinese Communist Party's secrets after his defection to the enemy.

The section on the Central Special Section was divided into nine parts — the historical development of C.P. secret-service work, its tasks and mission, organization, strategy, spirit, present state, what work it did, its methods, and "the defects found over the past few years" — laying out everything he knew. In the book he set out in detail the tasks and workings of the several branches of the Central Special Section, and drew five charts of its organization. He wrote:

"The First Branch of C.P. secret-service work does only general affairs, such as setting up important organs and arranging large meeting places. ..."

"The Second Branch does only counter-detection work, sending many people into all sorts of walks of society and into the enemy's own ranks. So the news they get on the country's politics, military affairs, economy, and international matters is very quick and very valuable. Above all, if their organs are to be wrecked, they know beforehand, for every day a bulletin called the 'Quick' is put out, so that their news is well-connected and they are hard to wreck. At the same time they contrive to wreck the enemy, and now and then put out contrary reports of every kind to sow discord within the enemy, setting him at odds with himself and scattering his strength."

"The Third Branch does only protection work. (The author's note: this has already been quoted in the first section of the chapter 'The Action Section and the "Red Squad"' in this book, and is omitted here.)"

"The Fourth Branch bears sole charge of secret special communications; they pass news to one another by radio. Forced by their circumstances, they carried out their own research, set up their own radio-manufacturing shop, and their people are all experts and their technique very fine; so their radios are made so exquisitely as to be hard to detect. Their operators are graduates of a crash course they run themselves. Besides stations placed across the country, they have stations for exchanging news with abroad. And they have all kinds of social couriers, using the sailors and waiters on the steamers, the drivers and peddlers on the trains, and so on, to bear the courier work."

"The Fifth Branch was in fact never established; it was to be given over to the hoodlum movement and to bear the charge of demolition work."

Of the Central Special Section's working methods he wrote in the book: "There are several principles of C.P. secret-service method that we must know: (1) This work must strike into the very heart of the enemy. (2) Everything of the enemy's must be turned to work for us. (3) It must be wholly socialized, for socialization can solve every difficulty in the work and ease it. (4) All our problems must be solved on the enemy's side and on society's side. (5) The work must be absolutely secret. To give a case in point: since the C.P. now knows the Kuomintang too runs secret-service work, it will contrive to get deep into the Kuomintang's secret-service organization, and to get deep into the general headquarters. So the policies the headquarters decides and the steps it takes will become known to the C.P., and they will set their own countermeasures by our strategy. Their so-called core members are the Party's most alert and loyal comrades, tried over a long time, whose effort and record in the work are better than the average. Again, take the First Branch's work: the man charged with commercial matters may enter into business, and now and then use a shop, which is better even than a shop set up at one's own cost. The man charged with arrangements looks for houses in which to hold meetings, and after a meeting, to avoid danger, gives up the house. Because houses had constantly to be found for meetings, and this might arouse suspicion, they set up an errand agency, which would run any and every kind of business for society, so that no one noticed or suspected — that is socialization. As for the Second Branch's method: it sends its own well-trained comrades to sit for the Shanghai concession police, so that when one is on point duty they can hold a meeting under his cover without danger; another lot, once trained, are set to run small businesses, using the rented rooms as secret courier stations that draw no suspicion. And they can be taught to make explosives and do demolition and assassination work."

Of the harm done to the Central Special Section's work by Gu Shunzhang's own betrayal in April 1931, he, as a traitor standing on the enemy's side, wrote shamelessly, in a tone of gloating: "From the twentieth year of the Republic [1931] until now, their secret-service work has gone bankrupt. Because a most unlucky event befell them, everything was broken. The wrecking of many of their organs over the past year is iron proof that they have lost the guarantee their secret-service work gave. The bankruptcy of C.P. secret-service work has cost the C.P. organization a great loss, for over the past few years they lived wholly on secret-service work, which had insensibly become a kind of insurance office on which all relied — even the more responsible comrades would ask the secret-service man before taking a road. So once secret-service work went bankrupt they lost their guarantee, and the whole was thrown into a state of upheaval."

Even the Central Special Section's whole record of achievement Gu Shunzhang seemed not to dare deny. He wrote in the book: "By the first month of 1928 — the seventeenth year of the Republic — they felt their surroundings extremely dangerous and vile, and so, to fend off the enemy's assault and protect their own safety, they had to model themselves on the organization of the Soviet GPU and formally establish secret-service work. Though they had no experience to speak of, with the right man in charge, and in a spirit of study and creation, they drew up a whole plan and pressed steadily forward by set steps. At first only one branch was set up; after six months the Second and Third Branches came into being one after another, and after another six months the Fourth Branch followed. In less than three years there was astonishing growth and singular success — and even from an objective standpoint we cannot but admire their fighting spirit! C.P. secret-service workers have a full grasp of politics and a full understanding of the Party and its doctrine, and their work is charged through with an active, quick, keen, creative spirit."

At the end of this section Gu Shunzhang added a note: "The C.P. secret-service work described above is much simplified; the details await a separate book." By his brother-in-law Zhang Changgeng's account, after finishing this book Gu Shunzhang had Wang Yixin help him write another, The Secret-Service Work of the Chinese Communist Party, betraying still further what he had known before and during his time in the Central Special Section. Zhang Changgeng said this second book was finished, but could not be published because Gu was executed; the manuscript was carried off by the Zhongtong agent Gu Jianzhong, who took it for his own and put it out after altering it. In the "Forthcoming Books" printed at the back of The Theory and Practice of Secret Service Work, brought out by the Jinghua Printing House of Nanjing in August 1933, there is a notice of Gu Shunzhang's The Secret-Service Work of the Chinese Communist Party: "This book, on the author's firsthand experience and in an objective spirit, sets down the facts, telling the secret-service work of the Chinese Communist Party from first to last, and offers it to the comrades who care about China's C.P. secret-service work, and to those who love detective novels or write detective film scripts, as an excellent reference."

And at the back of the book there is also a notice of a third book by Gu Shunzhang, A Study of "Man": "This book, on the revolutionary standpoint and on scientific analysis, and on the experience the author has gathered over several years of careful observation, makes a deep and precise study of the whole condition of every kind of person in society. In a culturally backward Chinese society, the thought and conduct of every kind of person often take a deformed course, giving rise to the present disorder. Thus the question of 'man,' in the present situation, is also an important one. All who live in society have need to attend to it. The author's motive in writing this book to offer society as a reference lies just here."

These works well reflect the mind of the traitor Gu Shunzhang — bored to the last degree, aping the cultivated — and his will to go on selling his soul and flattering his masters.

### Executed at Last by Xu Enzeng

As already told, after his arrest and betrayal Gu Shunzhang did indeed cut a smug, glorious figure for a while on the surface. But this "high-riding" spell did not last long, and in 1935, his use to them spent, he was executed by the Kuomintang authorities. Like every traitor who betrays the revolution and takes the thief for a father, though he had sold the revolution to the last drop and served his masters with fixed devotion, in the end he was killed and his corpse flung into the wilds, like a mangy dog with a broken back put down by its master. The Memoirs of Xu Enzeng, written by the very Zhongtong chief who ordered Gu Shunzhang's execution, in order to cover the quarrels within the Kuomintang secret service, cooks up a pack of lies about how they executed Gu Shunzhang, to fool the world. He writes: "What I regret is that this friend of special merit did not cooperate with me to the end. In the spring of 1935 he was executed for renewing his collusion with the enemy. By his restless nature, treat him as well as I might, in time he grew impatient and looked for a political way out. Finding none on our side, he went again to collude with the Communists, giving them reports on our inside personnel and business. Later it was found he had made ready to flee to the Jiangxi Soviet after carrying out an assassination plan, and I had no choice but to give him up. I handled no fewer than fifty or sixty cases of the surrender and turning of Communists as important as he, and Gu Shunzhang was the only one who, having turned, then wished to return to the enemy's embrace." That Xu Enzeng should say so is of course a monstrous lie, an absurdity. He lied because he wished to hide the fact that, once they had squeezed a traitor dry, they would "give him up" like a worn-out shoe thrown away. Every shameless traitor, however he cringes and fawns before the enemy and serves with fixed devotion, can seldom escape this shameful, pitiable end. So it was with the well-connected Gu Shunzhang; so with the middling-connected Zhang Mutao; and even the higher-connected Zhang Guotao was no exception, but was "given up" as well.

Though Gu Shunzhang sold to the enemy every inner secret of the Chinese Communist Party he knew, and committed monstrous crimes against it, Chiang Kai-shek, Chen Lifu, and Xu Enzeng never trusted him, kept a guard against him always, and watched him at every turn. When he was first taken to Nanjing, the enemy would not let him join the great manhunt in Shanghai, but held him under close watch in a large compound at Bailuzhou in Nanjing. Only after a long time was he let out of confinement and settled at Shuangtangxiang in Nanjing. Those who lived with him — Wang Guodong, Hu Hongtao, Li Zhiyuan — were all traitors. Xu Enzeng sent his confidant Wang Sicheng to be Gu Shunzhang's secretary and live with him, in fact to watch and control him; and from Jiang Xiaoxian's Third Gendarme Regiment he transferred a man named Lin Jinsheng (nominally an "investigator of the Central Party Headquarters Investigation Section") to be Gu's bodyguard. Here Gu ran a secret-service training class for the enemy and trained the first batch of agents. To win him over, Xu Enzeng had Wang Sicheng introduce a Nanjing woman named Zhang Ping to be his wife, and Gu moved to Xiliuxiang. Later he was made head of the Zhongtong secret-service action squad, but a Zhongtong stalwart, Gu Jianzhong, was made his deputy, and the real power of the squad lay in this deputy's hands. The quarrel between the two, Gu Shunzhang and Gu Jianzhong, ran deep, like fire and water.

Gu Shunzhang had a band of traitors about him, forming a little clique. When Xu Enzeng found that Gu Shunzhang meant to build a power of his own out of the surrendered men in the action squad, he found a pretext to strip him of his post. All of Gu Shunzhang's own men were transferred away, the Xiliuxiang office was at once given up, and he and his family were ordered to move to No. 71 Anpin Street.

How Gu Shunzhang was arrested and killed by the Kuomintang, and why, is told in different ways. Zhang Guodong (Zhang Wen), head of the first section of the Kuomintang Central Bureau of Investigation and Statistics, said: "After Gu Shunzhang betrayed the revolution, grave as the crimes he committed against the revolutionary cause were, Xu Enzeng did not trust him and secretly set trusted agents to watch him. In the years that followed, Gu Shunzhang only bore the work of training agents under Xu. He had Xu Zheng, Zhang Zhiren, Zhu Qiubai, and others help him put in order and compile a set of training materials for agents, the so-called Secret-Agent Series, six volumes in all — training work, intelligence work, reconnaissance work, action work, persuasion work, internal work. Around 1934, to train Juntong agent-cadres and step up its criminal anti-Communist, anti-people activity, Dai Li, with Xu Enzeng's consent and Chiang Kai-shek's approval, borrowed Gu Shunzhang from the Zhongtong to join the training work of the Juntong's class at Hongshan Temple in Nanjing, as adviser and instructor. From then on Gu had direct dealings with Dai Li. Bent on outdoing Xu in merit and favor, Dai tried to turn the temporary loan into a permanent post, to pull Gu into the Juntong's ranks, courting him hard with promises of high place and large money. Since Gu, under Xu's charge, was neither trusted nor given power, he leaned little by little toward the Juntong — a thing Xu could absolutely not tolerate. In 1934 Xu instructed Gu Jianzhong (then a section head at the General Secret-Service Headquarters) to charge Gu Shunzhang, at a work conference, with 'disobeying orders and scheming to raise his own banner,' and to draw a pistol and kill him then and there; but the matter was stopped by Pu Mengjiu, who chaired the conference (Pu then ranking just below Xu Enzeng and above Gu Jianzhong). After the conference Gu Shunzhang was put under house arrest, and before long sent to the Suzhou reformatory. In 1935 he was secretly put to death. All this Gu Jianzhong told me himself in 1940. Before long Xu Enzeng and Zhang Chong both became members of the Kuomintang Central Executive Committee, which shows Chiang Kai-shek's favor to this set of secret-service chiefs."

Lin Jinsheng, who had been the traitor Gu Shunzhang's bodyguard (calling himself an "investigator of the Investigation Section of the Organization Department of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters"), gives in an essay a wholly different account of how Gu Shunzhang was killed: "One day at the end of 1933, Gu Shunzhang said to me: 'You are a crack shot. I mean to assign you twelve men and set up a secret-service squad, with you as its head.' He said too: 'First we must do away with Chen Lifu and Xu Enzeng.' At his words I was very afraid. Was this not treason? Was it not asking to be finished? I knew the fellow was vicious in the extreme, and dared not oppose him to his face, still less show any displeasure. Only, from the night I heard this, I often could not sleep, forever fearing some mishap.

"As it chanced, only a few days later Gu Shunzhang handed me a letter to deliver at once to such-and-such a room in such-and-such a hotel. It was very cold that day, I remember; I was in an overcoat, took the letter, and stuffed it into my overcoat pocket. When I reached the hotel to hand it over, the letter had suddenly vanished; I turned out my whole person and found no trace of it. There was nothing for it but to report to Gu Shunzhang. He looked most displeased, but did not curse me; I knew he had come to suspect me and would sooner or later make his move. After turning it over and over, I decided to get in ahead of him. I found a pretext to leave Xiliuxiang, ran to Xu Enzeng, and informed on him. Xu Enzeng of course praised my action warmly, and told me to go back and keep quiet, to hold Gu Shunzhang steady. But how could I dare go back? I said to him: 'Mr. Xu, I cannot go back — even in Nanjing it is very dangerous.' Xu Enzeng thought a moment and said: 'Song Zhixian, chairman of the Shaanxi provincial party, happens to be asking me for a man; go there and take up security work.'

"After leaving Xu Enzeng, I took the night train that very evening back to my home in Danyang, meaning to see to a few things and then go to Xi'an. Afterward I learned that by not crossing the river that day to catch the train at Pukou for Xi'an, I had escaped a mortal snare. For Gu Shunzhang, seeing me still not back at Xiliuxiang after dark, knew I had turned; through his 'eyes' he learned I had been posted to Xi'an, and that very night sent his confidants to lie in wait at Pukou, meaning to do away with me and silence me.

"Xu Enzeng, on getting my information, did not act at once but, showing nothing, secretly made close arrangements and set men to watch Gu Shunzhang. Before long Gu Shunzhang was put under house arrest, and later, on the approval of Chen Lifu and Chiang Kai-shek, and once firm proof was in hand, Gu Shunzhang was taken to the Suzhou prison. In 1935 he was shot in the Suzhou prison. The man who carried out the sentence was named Lü Ruijing, whom I knew, and it was he who told me of it."

Why Gu Shunzhang was executed by Xu Enzeng is also told in different ways. Meng Zhen, a veteran agent under Xu Enzeng who had headed the fourth section of the Central Bureau of Investigation and Statistics, held that Xu Enzeng's account in his memoirs — that Gu Shunzhang "went again to collude with the Communists" and had "made ready to flee to the Jiangxi Soviet," so that Xu "had no choice but to give him up" — was wrong, and said Xu Enzeng's "explanation is self-contradictory, unreasonable, hard to accept." This man was an admirer of Gu Shunzhang, revered him as "master of secret-service work." Standing on the agent's ground, he spoke up hotly for Gu Shunzhang, calling his execution a "frame-up" within the Zhongtong secret service. He said the time from Gu Shunzhang's arrest and betrayal to his execution "was not long. And in that time Gu Shunzhang was busy helping crack cases, his days very full and pleasant — how could he feel impatient? Second, would the clever Gu Shunzhang not know that this trade of his had a limited market, that outside the Zhongtong, where he was in favor, he would be of no use elsewhere? Where was the thought of seeking another way out? Third, Gu Shunzhang was a Communist traitor; the evil he had done the Communists was unpardonable, and to seek another way out he would surely not rejoin the Communists. Fourth, even had Gu Shunzhang colluded anew with the Communists, the Communists would certainly have wanted him to be a second Qian Zhuangfei, and would never have wanted him to give intelligence or flee to the Soviet. Fifth, it says first that he 'renewed his collusion with the Communists' — meaning it was already done — but at the end that he 'wished to return to the enemy's embrace,' the added word 'wished' meaning he wished to collude but had not done so. Was Gu Shunzhang then guilty of a completed act or an attempted one? Here there is no telling — does this not give the game away? On the above analysis, it may be affirmed that Gu Shunzhang never returned to the Communists' embrace. This was a frame-up. Then how did Gu Shunzhang come to his death? There is a saying, 'Only a mediocrity draws no man's envy'; it was precisely because he was no mediocrity, and cut too sharp a figure, that he brought down on himself this mortal calamity."

Huang Kai, moved up to Zhongtong special commissioner in Shanghai for his "merit" in Gu Shunzhang's arrest in Hankou, said: "Gu Shunzhang was originally led in person by Xu Enzeng and Zhang Chong; at first Dai Li was forever going to him on the sly, and later Dai Li begged the Generalissimo for a directive to the Zhongtong chiefs, and for a time Gu assisted the Juntong's work. Gu Shunzhang used the quarrel between Zhongtong and Juntong to sow discord between them, so that Xu Enzeng and Dai Li were simply like enemies. In the two organizations then there was a saying that the big cases Gu Shunzhang handled went to the Juntong, the small ones to the Zhongtong. Sometimes Zhongtong and Juntong had a great row before the Generalissimo over the right to work a case.

"After more than a year of painstaking effort Gu Shunzhang had grasped a share of power in the secret-service system; the action squad he trained had a thousand men, and he had authority to move them. A surrendered man, Liu Ying, and others informed Xu Enzeng that Gu Shunzhang was recruiting men and horses, scheming to organize a new Chinese Communist Party, and plotting to use the thousand-man action squad as his armed base, to gather a crowd and rise in revolt and wipe out the party-government dignitaries, the Generalissimo among them. On the report Chen Lifu at once moved Zhongtong veterans and Gu Zhenglun's gendarmes to disarm Gu Shunzhang and jail him at the Zhenjiang detention house. Half a year later the Generalissimo approved Gu Shunzhang's execution, and Gu Jianzhong went to Zhenjiang to carry it out with his own hand."

Chen Weiru, a surrendered renegade who had worked at the secret printing house of the Central Committee's Red Flag in Shanghai and then followed Gu Shunzhang as an agent, said: "After 1934 Xu Enzeng tightened his watch on Gu Shunzhang (those charged with watching him were, of all people, Tong Guozhong and Zhang Wennong — that is, Chen Liansheng — 'confidants' who had followed him in betrayal from the Central Special Section's Red Squad). Gu Shunzhang was 'kept under house arrest at No. 41 Xiliuxiang (called outwardly a dormitory of the Central Investigation Section, but in fact a secret secret-service organ), and later moved to Mingwalang in Nanjing. At that time Gu Shunzhang did have secret dealings with Dai Li, kept up by Gu's later wife Zhang Yongqin. Watching over Gu Shunzhang were still his old right and left hands, Tong Guozhong and Zhang Wennong, who, seeing Gu fallen from power, watched him and his wife all the closer and reported to headquarters at every turn. Later the Zhongtong moved Gu Shunzhang's house arrest, jailing him in the Suzhou reformatory in 1934, and the next year he was secretly shot in Suzhou. (It is said that Gu Shunzhang knew conjuring and hypnosis, and that on the way under escort to the Suzhou reformatory, for fear he would work his arts and flee, they ran a rod through his shoulder-blades before setting out, to suppress his black magic.) In May 1935, not long after Gu Jianzhong came to the Shanghai district to announce Gu Shunzhang's case, Xu Enzeng too came to inspect the district's work. He said to everyone at district headquarters: our comrades must set their minds to their own work and not chop and change; the future is assured.'"

It would seem that the direct cause of Gu Shunzhang's execution was chiefly that his use to the enemy was already spent, while he "read the times ill," his ambition undying, "scheming to raise his own banner," and so offended his masters' deepest taboo. At the same time, Gu Shunzhang's refusal to sit quiet, his collusion with Dai Li, his "leaning to the Juntong" and being drawn into the Zhongtong-Juntong struggle, was indeed a thing Xu Enzeng "absolutely could not tolerate," and hastened all the more the day Xu Enzeng and his kind set to "give him up." But in sum, of course, that every utterly degraded, base, and shameless traitor is, sooner or later, given up by his masters in the end, is a law they cannot escape.
