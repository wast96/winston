## Chapter 24. The Wind and Rain Will Not Yet Give Up the Lingering Cold

“How do you do, Mr. Yang Muci. Let us make each other’s acquaintance. I am your attending physician, Xia Yuechun.” Kindly Xia Yuechun drew back the white curtain for Muci. “You mustn’t speak, or even try to speak—at the least, for a week I hope you can rest quietly, and keep absolutely still, so that the injury to the breastbone doesn’t split open again.”

Muci, who had scarcely ever suffered illness, this time truly learned what pain of body and heart could be. The hurt of the body was the lesser thing; what he could not bear was the grief of losing his comrades-in-arms.

Ronghua had passed through blood and fire to her rebirth.

He himself, in that same blood, had been given a new life.

The anguish within him came fiercer than the pain of his body; tears slid quietly onto the white pillow-cloth.

“The anaesthetic’s worn off; it will hurt a good deal.” Plainly Xia Yuechun took Muci’s tears for a show of enduring pain. “You’re young; you’ll pull through soon enough.”

Muci raised his hand to thank the doctor.

“No need to thank me. Your brother and I are old friends.” Xia Yuechun said it quite abruptly, and Muci’s gaze fastened on his face. A question, of course.

“The one who dragged you back from the gate of death was your own elder brother. If you would thank someone, save your strength to thank him. Do you know? Your blood type is Rh-negative type A, a rare type. Without his giving you blood in time, your body would long since have gone cold. I think you take my meaning.”

Muci said nothing.

“Your brother asked me to pass on a few words to you: The air outside these days is very cold and dank; a grieving wind fills the road, and the weather too has turned troubled and unquiet. It is a season of many troubles—take good care of yourself.”

Muci made a sudden move to speak; Xia Yuechun checked him. “He’ll come to see you in a few days; whatever you have to say, say it to him yourself. For now, you need absolute quiet.”

Muci respected the doctor’s advice, and by degrees calmed his heart.

“By the way, I forgot to tell you—your sister kept watch by you all last night, and this morning she went home to fetch you a change of clothes. Rest well, and remember: absolute quiet.”

At the doctor’s warm words Yang Muci closed his eyes. He truly wished he might sleep on just so—if he were never to wake again, might it buy back Ronghua’s bright and lovely smile?

If so, he would gladly take her place.

Ronghua’s cold body lay in the mortuary of the “Chunhe” Hospital.

Before Rong Sheng’s eyes all was black darkness. He had come out alone after taking the telephone call from Deputy Chief Han of the police bureau; he had told neither his mother nor the Third Madam.

He did not know how he had made his way to the hospital.

On the road to the hospital his whole mind was full of Ronghua as she had been as a child: sharp-eyed and clever-hearted, not fond of talk. She had loved to play with water, taking his leather shoes for little boats, setting them in the big bathtub to watch them rock and ferry from side to side. If a “boat” capsized, she would let out a most earnest shriek. She had loved to play with fire, taking his poem-drafts with the ink not yet dry and tossing them into the charcoal brazier, watching the flames curl the scented paper, laughing away foolishly all the while. The First Madam had said at the time: this girl is learning “Daiyu burns her manuscripts”; she’s sure to have some talent by and by. What no one reckoned was that she would have not only Daiyu’s gifts but Daiyu’s ill-starred fate as well.

Ronghua was by nature reserved, keeping herself hidden, not good at pleasing her elders or getting on with her sisters. It was easy for her to be met with coldness and forgetfulness in a great household. Fathers and elders will show their children some measure of unreasonable favouritism, just as the ten fingers stretched out are of unequal length.

Ronghua never got to study abroad, because her father was unwilling to cultivate a daughter; Ronghua never married, because her mother did not care to spend too much strength on a daughter born of a concubine; Ronghua seldom came home, because the family had never valued her—including Rong Sheng himself, who had never once truly cared for her.

He felt ashamed and desolate.

When Rong Sheng saw Ronghua’s disfigured corpse, he could not accept it. He would not accept this cruel fact; he wept aloud. Crouching down, he cried like a great child.

“Why? Why is this?” The sound of Rong Sheng’s weeping echoed through the empty mortuary.

“Why, then?” The same question Li Qinhong too was asking herself. Where had she gone wrong? A seamless plan had been torn apart out of nowhere; and the accident report drawn up by the police bureau gave, of all things, this analysis of the cause: situated in a district prone to traffic accidents, and owing to careless operation on one side, a tragedy ensued. Life is precious; the lesson must be earnestly learned. And so on.

It was simply a piece of official whitewash, all evasive wording.

Li Qinhong thought: if Ronghua’s crash was by chance, then was the fire at the Huamei Bookstore by chance too? In a single day, upon a single person, would two deadly “chances” befall?

Impossible.

Unless she deliberately manufactured one “chance,” so that the burning of the bookstore became a “certainty.”

A woman who uses her life to manufacture one “chance” collision must have a reason that made her collide at any cost. She was protecting her fellow Party members—or else, she was averting a crisis grave enough to be “drowning.”

In that case she must, before the Detective Division set out, have already got the intelligence she needed.

Nine fathoms of the mountain raised, and the work fails for one basketful short.

There was a “mole” in the Detective Division.

Everyone knew there was a “mole.”

No one knew who the “mole” was.

This “mole” was, even now, still at large.

Li Qinhong stood at the window of the Detective Division chief’s office, gazing at the scene outside; suddenly she saw that on a stretch of the red brick beneath the window-ledge one part was very clean, as though someone had once dropped down from this window. Such skill—there were not many in the Detective Division who had it. Just then Gao Lei and Xiong Zida came into the office, crestfallen.

They had been to the Settlement and the British police station to parley: on the day of the “crash,” the several Communist suspects they had arrested on Gordon Road had all been intercepted by the patrolmen of the British police station, who said they had no right to arrest offenders in the Settlement, and that if they wanted the men, very well, but let them first go through extradition proceedings.

“How did it go?” Li Qinhong asked.

“How did what go?” Xiong Zida furiously took off his cap and flung it on the desk. “Not a drop of water gets in.”

“The Communists have laboured in Shanghai many years; this time they took a desperate risk, gathering their best in one meeting—it was surely no rash, hasty affair.” said Li Qinhong. “Sir, let your vexation go. The pleasure of angling lies precisely in patient waiting—waiting for the instant the fish takes the hook.”

“The hook is in your hand; the chief and I can only stand by the pool and envy the fish.” Gao Lei blew a breath at the ceiling.

“What do you mean by that?”

“What do I mean? Every operation, you keep it secret beforehand, suspicious of every shadow. Isn’t it just that you fear we’ll steal your first credit? You’ve planted a mole among the Communists—why not give us word of it in advance?”

“Every man has his own road through. That you have no source of intelligence proves your incompetence.”

“Quite so, I’m incompetent and you’re the able one. Against wind and current you’ve sailed off with the only boat, and left the chief and me with no ferry across.”

“Don’t set yourself on a level with the chief.”

“Ah yes, I daren’t set myself on a level with the chief—not like you, always hoping to stand shoulder to shoulder with him, two peaks abreast, the stream forked in two.”

“You—?!”

“Enough!” Xiong Zida spoke up. “What’s all this quarrelling? Rather than vie for the last word here at leisure, go and catch me two or three Communists to look at.”

Li Qinhong caught the acid flavour in the chief’s words. She looked down on Xiong Zida because she reckoned her own ability and wit many times his; Xiong Zida ought to take orders from her, and she could not, for the life of her, bow her head meekly before him. In that brief silence the telephone rang.

Li Qinhong and Xiong Zida both reached for it without thinking; Xiong Zida’s hand came down on the back of Li Qinhong’s, and under Gao Lei’s slightly mocking gaze Li Qinhong drew her hand back in embarrassment.

Xiong Zida answered the telephone.

Li Qinhong spread her five fingers and made a show of admiring her own long nails.

Gao Lei hummed a little tune of the southland.

“Hello, you want Section Head Li?” Xiong Zida glanced at Li Qinhong; she signed to him to go on. “She’s not here. Whatever urgent matter you have, you can tell me straight. That’s right. I am Xiong Zida, chief of the Detective Division. … What? Say that again. … When? About what time? … If you were let hear him speak once more, could you recognize it? … Accuracy?”

“One hundred per cent.” said the other. “Telling voices apart over the telephone, my accuracy is one hundred per cent.”

“Good. I’ll arrange it. One by one through the sieve.” Grim-faced, Xiong Zida set down the telephone.

“What is it?” Li Qinhong asked.

“Your ‘Rivet’ says that within the hour before it all happened, he telephoned our Detective Division and gave, in plain terms, the door-number of the Communists’ gathering: No. 1141 Hengji Li.”

“Who took the call?” Gao Lei and Li Qinhong asked with one voice.

“A Communist.” said Xiong Zida. “Whoever took that call is the ‘Rivet’ buried in our midst. We must find this nail without fail, pull it out, wipe it out. Every member of the Detective Division is to assemble this afternoon. I mean to put them through a single telephone.” Xiong Zida picked up the receiver. “Make the White-Bone Demon show its true shape. Everyone, myself included, is to be screened and sifted one by one. I do not believe he can pass through unscathed this time.” He set the receiver down hard. “Pass on my order.”

Li Qinhong and Gao Lei came to attention.

“Put No. 1141 Hengji Li and No. 5 Plum Blossom Lane under close watch at once. Play out the line, and hook the big fish.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Reporting.” Adjutant Liu called at the door.

“Come in.”

“Sir, the police bureau just sent a man with a letter, saying a murder has occurred at No. 1141 Hengji Li—an old amah met a violent end. Since the time and place of the murder tally with the time and place of our hunt for the Communists, Chief Han is turning the case over to the Detective Division to handle; if we don’t take it up, he’ll send detectives to take it over.”

“Han Zhengqi did well.” As usual Li Qinhong took another’s task upon herself, reaching out to receive the document; then, all at once feeling she had breached decorum, she made a smart turn, came to attention, and handed the document respectfully to Xiong Zida. “Sir, I’d like to add more men to conduct a forced search around Hengji Li, to give the Communists the false impression that we are still blindly, aimlessly hunting for their organ. This way, first, it protects our ‘Rivet’ from drawing the Communists’ suspicion. Second, they may well bring No. 5 Plum Blossom Lane back into use as a liaison point. Third, the Communists’ Special Committee meeting never came off; they are bound to choose a new, reliable place to hold it. And then our ‘Rivet’ will bring us a real surprise.”

“Well spoken.” The reason Xiong Zida was willing to bear so much and yield to Li Qinhong again and again was that Li Qinhong had once been Du Luning’s underground lover, and he himself was Du Luning’s schoolmate; and, most important of all, the Detective Division needed a posture of “two armies facing off,” so that he could control the whole board at his ease. Besides, Li Qinhong was indeed a “doer.” She had gone through fire and death, and had won military credit for him too. Overbearing she might be, but a capable “Monkey King” was better than a useless “Sha Monk.”

“As for the men who go to survey the scene at No. 1141 Hengji Li, I suggest, sir, it’s more fitting to leave it to the police bureau. It can throw dust in the Communists’ eyes.” said Gao Lei.

“Do it that way.” said Xiong Zida.

“Only, sir, in the matter of the voice test over the telephone, there’s one more person who mustn’t be left out.” said Li Qinhong.

“Who?”

“Yang Muci—Adjutant Yang.”

“You can’t mean it.” Gao Lei protested Muci’s innocence. “Smashed up as he is, you still suspect him? If he really were a Communist, would that woman Communist have crashed into him so hard?”

“How did that woman Communist know the men riding in that disguised transport truck were Detective Division men?” Li Qinhong countered. “There is only one answer: she knew one of you—she knew this man was a Detective Division man, and of course she also knew this man’s true identity was Com-mu-nist.”

“Why not just come out and say I’m the Communist.”

“I don’t rule out the possibility.” Li Qinhong laughed complacently. “Perhaps you both are.”

“If you say Captain Gao is a Communist, I don’t believe it.” said Adjutant Liu.

“By your drift, then, Adjutant Yang is the likeliest suspect?” Xiong Zida asked.

“I didn’t say so.”

“I remember now—A-Ci once came back to fetch my document case for me.” Xiong Zida said, as if to himself.

A cryptic smile opened at the corner of Li Qinhong’s mouth; she had a foreboding that she was about to lay hold of the riddle’s answer. “We ought to go and look in on this little friend of ours…”

*No. 7 Plum Blossom Lane.*

Yashu, in a Chinese-collared cheongsam of embroidered silk with a little waistcoat, a handbag of novel design tucked under her arm, came out of the courtyard gate. Brilliant sun, a few idle clouds—they mirrored Yashu’s mood just now to a nicety.

Ever since the day the head steward of the “White Rose” ballroom had sought her out of his own accord and asked whether she would be willing to work at a securities exchange, at a generous wage, it was as though fate had opened for her another door of fortune long shut.

Yashu chose to believe this was a gospel bestowed by “Heaven.”

Her parents, harried by illness and burdened with debt, had died one after the other. An orphaned girl, in her helpless isolation, had chosen to sell her smiles and her skill at dancing.

She had thought once of going to Rong Sheng, but each time she reached the gate of the Rong house, her poor pride at once came to trouble her heart. Her pride told her: go to anyone you like, but you must not go begging to Rong Sheng—he will only look down on you the more; he will dole out money, but the look in his eyes will surely be one of contempt, of coldness.

The one man she dreamed of, in her dreams, was A-Chu. Still so sunlit, still so warmly smiling. Still that same feeling of near-yet-far…

She had never once thought of going to A-Chu—neither in waking life nor in dream.

In her new work Yashu was diligent, and much in the boss’s “favour”; not a week into the job, the boss had rented a house for her in Plum Blossom Lane, saying it was an empty house a friend had vacated, lent for her to stay in for the time being—first, it was near her place of work; second, she could keep an eye on the friend’s house for him.

Yashu felt she had unwittingly struck great luck; next year, who could say, better luck still might come—she might meet a match truly her own. Truly to fall in love, truly to build a fine home of her own. Not, for the sake of a good place to live, to covet a marriage of wealth and rank.

She walked in the sunlight, full of confidence and reverie.

Suddenly Yashu saw A-Chu.

A-Chu, in a smart Western suit, appeared before Yashu wholly made anew, and behind him a costly automobile. Yashu’s expression showed some surprise; and A-Chu, glancing back all at once, felt too that the Yashu of his eye and his heart was now very sunlit, while he himself, walking by night and on the knife’s edge, felt some little shame at his own unworthiness.

“How do you do, Miss Yashu.” A-Chu walked toward her into the sun.

Yashu returned a somewhat indistinct greeting. “How do you do, Doctor Chu.”

“It’s been a long while. Are you keeping well?” A-Chu’s manner was as respectful as ever, which did something to comfort Yashu’s heart; A-Chu, after all, had studied abroad and seen the world—not the sort of small man who runs wild the moment he gets the upper hand.

In the depths of Yashu’s heart she had always harboured a grievance against A-Chu’s status as a “house servant”; yet she had always been captivated, too, by his “gifts” and his looks.

They talked again a little of what had befallen each of them since parting—each, of course, hiding the parts they felt they must hide.

Knowing full well that Yashu’s “work history” was pure fancy, A-Chu still put on an air of “admiration,” of “appreciation”; he was himself astonished, and found it scarcely believable, at his own conduct.

Perhaps he had changed? A-Chu saw himself very clearly: his aim had already strayed from his first intent, shifting by degrees from the pursuit of feeling to a need of daily life.

Before A-Chu, Yashu kept always to reserve, gravity and decorum. This was the last lingering fragrance of her illustrious name.

“Are you off to work?” After the brief exchange of civilities A-Chu decided to take his leave for now; feeling, after all, must be nurtured, and nothing can be forced to a quick success.

“I’m off today; I’m going to market for groceries.”

“Would you like me to give you a lift on my way?”

“No need to trouble you. Aren’t you here waiting for your friend?” Yashu smiled her thanks.

“Another day, then.” said A-Chu. “Another day, I’ll stand Miss Yashu to tea.”

The two parted in the sunlight; Yashu went off with short, mincing steps, graceful and refined as ever.

No sooner had Yashu gone than A-Chu got into the car. He told Liu A-Si to drive to the “Chunhe Hospital.” The car cut through by another lane; so this meeting, it turned out, had been arranged on purpose.

*The Central Police Academy.*

The telephone in Du Luning’s office rang. Du Luning was reading a document; Yu Xiaojiang, standing in attendance at his side, took the call.

“Good day, whom do you want?”

“It’s Gao Lei; I want Teacher Du.”

Yu Xiaojiang covered the mouthpiece and said softly to Du Luning: “Gao Lei, for you.”

“Ask him what it’s about.” said Du Luning idly.

“The chief asks what it’s about.” Having heard Gao Lei out, Yu Xiaojiang said to Du Luning: “He says it’s urgent.”

“Hello, Du Luning here.” Du Luning listened quietly to the whole of Gao Lei’s account, said not a word, and hung up.

“Anything the matter?” Yu Xiaojiang asked.

“Suppose I told you Yang Muci was a Communist—would you believe it?” Du Luning asked back, unfathomable.

“I would.” Yu Xiaojiang answered without hesitation.

“Oh?” Du Luning’s words came a beat slow. “Why?”

“So long as there is proof enough of his true identity, I’d believe it.”

“And if someone told you I too was a Communist—would you believe that as well?”

“I would!” Yu Xiaojiang answered firmly. “Same as I said—so long as there’s proof enough, I’d believe it.”

“And if the proof were faked? Would that not be to slaughter the innocent?”

“The Generalissimo’s instruction: better to kill a thousand by mistake than let one slip through! Wrong a man rather than let him go free!”

“Good! Well said.” Du Luning laid the document aside and smiled coldly. “If Yang Muci is a Communist, I will execute him with my own hand. And if he is not a Communist, then let whoever picks a quarrel over him, and bullies my man, not blame me for a cruel and merciless heart.”

*The Chunhe Hospital.*

Yang Muci’s sickroom brimmed with a warm cosiness; the fresh flowers Yang Sitong had brought stood in a vase, giving off wave upon wave of quiet fragrance; the sky-blue curtain was rolled up, and A-Chu came strolling to the head of Muci’s bed.

“How is it?”

“Very good.”

“It doesn’t look so good to me.” said A-Chu.

Muci answered with an effort. “Thank you.”

“What?”

“I said thank you.”

“Thank me for what?”

“You saved my life.”

“How will you thank me?” The question was a barbed one.

“Do I owe you money too?” For some reason, once Muci had said it, he found it comical himself. And so a faint smile filled his cheeks.

“Is it funny? I don’t find it funny.” A-Chu said, straight-faced.

“It’s that very look of yours that’s funny.” Muci’s smile pulled at the pain of his wound; he frowned, and let out a groan.

“Why did you choose so violent, so extreme a way of taking your own life?” A-Chu truly could not understand it; this was the very first thing he most wanted to ask A-Ci.

“Because there was no other choice.” A-Ci’s voice was very low, but very certain.

“And if you chose again?”

“The ending would be the same.”

“For the sake of your future?”

“For the sake of the future of all the people of China—yours included.”

“And so you’d spend your lives, without grudging them, to die for your common ideal.”

“You may understand it so.” A-Ci said calmly.

“But a mother may not be able to understand it.” A-Chu murmured, low, to himself.

“You mean—”

“The second Miss Rong’s mother. She’ll break down over this.” A-Chu felt a heartfelt pity rise for the Third Madam. “I dare not imagine what utter grief will be hers in the instant she learns the dreadful news. The deaths of her two daughters both have to do with us two. Rongrong was blown up in my consulting room—wholly innocent, she died as my stand-in. And Ronghua went, crashing into your car and… and so was gone. Tell me, if we were to face their mother, how could our hearts be at peace?”

Muci did not take up the words.

“Has this so-called father of yours come to see you?” A-Chu abruptly changed the subject.

“Can we—can we avoid speaking of my parents?” No sooner were the words out than Muci met A-Chu’s stern, cutting gaze again. It baffled Muci; the relation between the two of them was very strange—at once close and repelling, at once opposed and in harmony. Before him A-Chu was always thus, austere and severe, laying an unseen pressure on him. Strangest of all, toward the man he felt a fear he could not name. Turn it over as he might, he came at last to this: he had received the man’s life-saving kindness, and it seemed he would have to watch that man’s face all his life long.

“Do you not think that what your parents have done runs against all common feeling and common sense?”

“Every family has its own way of living. You’ve come to visit me today—it can’t be only to reproach my parents, surely?”

“I’ve come today with two purposes.” A-Chu’s tone was very grave. “First: as to our relation as brothers, I should like, today, to establish it formally.”

“Merely by your having given me blood?” A-Ci was a very cool-headed man.

“Yes.”

“There are many people of the same blood type.”

“And of Rh-negative type A?” A-Chu pressed him sternly.

The plain gesture of “owning a younger brother,” conveyed through the hidden hint of the blood, was borne truly home to Yang Muci. Clearly A-Chu had come, this time, in “earnest.”

Yet A-Chu’s image in A-Ci’s heart was many-sided—at the least, “estranged.” If A-Chu meant, merely on the strength of one “transfusion,” of one life-prolonging kindness, to order him about, or to try to work him like a puppet, it was utterly out of the question.

A-Ci hoped A-Chu would understand this plainly too, and not go on trading on a kindness to wield authority.

“About the blood type, I’m very sorry. This so-called Rh-negative type A is only your one-sided word.”

“And the doctor’s word?”

“I trust no one at all.”

“We saved your life.” said A-Chu.

“Thank you.”

That one “thank you,” brimming with courtesy, knocked flat the first step by which A-Chu had meant to set up his authority before A-Ci. As for the second purpose, then—to speak of it, or not?

“Your second purpose—is it perhaps to tell me the secret about my family?” A shrewd, sly light glinted in the depths of Muci’s eyes.

“If I told you, would you believe it?” A-Chu decided not to speak. The time was not yet. Best to let the other want to hear it; he must not open his mouth and beg him to listen.

“If you don’t tell me, how am I to know whether I’ll believe it?”

So he did very much want to hear it. A-Chu gave a faint smile. “A pity your manner just now has disappointed me. When the audience won’t play along, the storyteller naturally loses interest.”

“And so?”

“And so I’ve decided to take my leave.” As A-Chu spoke the words, he saw plainly the flash of desolation that crossed Muci’s face for an instant.

“Mr. Yang.” Muci spoke up to stay A-Chu’s steps. “Once again, my heartfelt thanks for saving my life. If anything I said just now gave offence, please forgive me.”

“A-Chu!” The door was flung violently open. Xia Yuechun ran in with a look of alarm, and pulled the sickroom door tight shut behind him.

“What’s happened?” A-Chu asked.

“I don’t know.” Xia Yuechun’s breath was not yet steady. “But it’s very urgent.”

“Steady, keep steady.” A-Chu eased Xia Yuechun’s agitation.

Xia Yuechun glanced at A-Ci lying on the sickbed and said: “Just now the Detective Division telephoned again, asking after Mr. Yang’s condition; they mean to send an army surgeon over right away—they may be going to take him off.”

“Take him off? Where to?”

“I don’t know. Perhaps the Army General Hospital, or perhaps…”

“What?”

“Prison.”

A-Chu and A-Ci felt the shock at the same moment. A-Chu thought he had already cleaned it all up for the boy; A-Ci was thinking whether the Party Central’s Special Branch had got safely out of danger.

“Why do you read it that way?” A-Chu asked Yuechun.

“Since this morning the hospital gate has been reinforced with roving agent sentries, and the sickroom corridor has gained a good many so-called fellow patients who aren’t there to be treated. Detective Division or police bureau, every time they’ve taken a patient from my hospital there have been these same portents.”

“That’s the voice of experience.”

“It’s just because I have the experience that I make the judgment.” said Xia Yuechun. “Trust me. It won’t be wrong. And there’s one stranger thing still: at noon today two men calling themselves telecommunications-bureau workers came wanting to install, free of charge, a telephone for our critical-care ward.”

“Did they install it?”

“It’s installed already—right next door.”

Dimly A-Ci grasped the Detective Division’s intent: the enemy meant, through a telephone, to identify who he was; long premeditated, they had laid down the fragrant bait and were only waiting for the fish to take it. Today, it seemed, the omens for him were grim, the doom past escaping.

He struggled a little, but was so weak—unexpectedly so—that sweat broke out all over him.

A-Chu caught the subtle change in A-Ci’s eyes, and asked him: “You know what they mean to do—don’t you?”

“You can’t help me.” said A-Ci.

“Tell me—what do they mean to do?”

“This is a trap I’ve no way to flee.”

“Grant your assumption, for the moment. But a clever fox can find a way round the hunter’s snare. Tell me—what are they installing a telephone to do?”

“They mean to tell my voice apart.” said A-Ci. “There’s a man who once heard my voice; today, the moment he picks my voice out over the telephone, it’s enough to be the death of me. Do you understand? You can’t help me. If, this afternoon or this evening, anything should happen to me, please pass word to that Soviet friend of yours—if he’s still there, tell him this one sentence: The wind and the rain are both gone now; let him choose another road. Beware the thief within the house.”

A-Chu gazed at A-Ci a moment, then told him, clear and strong: “This life of yours now is one I gave you. If you would seek death, you must first have my consent.” He turned to Xia Yuechun: “Get him away at once.”

“No!” From somewhere A-Ci found the strength to reach out and catch A-Chu’s arm. “I won’t go. If I go, it’s as good as confessing without being pressed.”

“Stay, and your voice will sell you just the same.”

“I can have a try at it.” said A-Ci. “He may not, for certain, be able to pick out my voice.”

“A voiceprint is hard to change. Don’t underrate your opponent; be reasonable.” A-Chu’s words were gentle.

“Wait—there’s another way.” Xia Yuechun broke in. “We can make him lose his voice all of a sudden. A-Chu, this is our strong suit as doctors.”

“You mean make him—?”

“Hysterical aphonia—functional loss of voice, that is. How’s that?”

“No.” Once again A-Ci overturned the proposal. “If I don’t open my mouth to speak, it’s as good as opening my mouth to tell them I’m the—” A-Ci broke off. “In my view this thing is beyond your power.”

A-Chu looked at A-Ci without stirring his gaze; and suddenly a strange brilliance shot from his eyes.

“You’ve hit on a plan?” Xia Yuechun asked.

“Risky though it is… it’s worth a try.” said A-Chu.
