## Chapter 4. Beset on Three Sides, Ever Forward

### Fortress Firm, Meeting Challenge from Every Side

Winter in Shanghai, though not so cold as in Beiping or Tianjin, is yet not so warm as in Hong Kong or Hanoi. For us, in the matter of daily life, the hours and the seasons were forever turned about; to tell neither day from night nor one season from another had long since become a habit, and only in our work was not the breadth of a hair's error to be suffered.

Now it was just the first month of the twenty-ninth year, and, taking the occasion to reckon it up, I found I had been in Shanghai close on five months. Looking my own conduct over, I saw that, happily, the organization of the "Shanghai District" had been able to recover by degrees and all its workings to draw back toward the normal, so that the time and strength I had spent through these past months in forever answering "alarms" might well now be turned to deliberating how our work should be opened out. And so, with the District secretary, Brother Qi Qingbin, I held a long series of talks; that is to say, we spoke of it several times running. We reached our conclusions; yet these could not be counted as settled, for they awaited the approval of our superiors before they might be put into effect.

Before this we had drawn up a "plan for the readjustment of personnel," which had already been sanctioned and was at that moment in the course of execution. Later we found that this plan gave in too much to the state of things as they stood, that it came near to being a mere reform, and that it wanted thoroughness. It might answer for the pressing need of a moment, but it could not be made fully to fit the changes of circumstance we could foresee; and since the pressure from without grew ever the heavier, we ourselves had of necessity to be firm and strong. The substance of the "new plan" was not complicated, though it was not certain that our superiors would consent to it in the whole, for it touched upon a great deal. Its chief points were as follows:

(1) All comrades of the "Shanghai District" whose identities had been exposed, whatever their rank, high or low, were to be moved away entirely, an estimated one hundred and twenty-odd persons in all: more than double the number under the plan then in force. The spirit of it lay in "leaving not a single leak."

(2) Of those marked for transfer elsewhere, any who held a very good line of work would have his lines of contact shifted over to another by some means; where this was not possible, we would rather give them up.

(3) We asked that the authority of the "Shanghai District" be made single and undivided, meaning in the two matters of "accounts" and "wireless."

As to the accounts, only the gross of receipts and disbursements was to be reported upward; no detailed books were to be kept, and no vouchers preserved. For the procedure of the work, we asked that the District accountant be given full authority to handle it. As to the wireless, its personnel and its business were to be brought wholly under the direction of the District headquarters. These two points aimed not at gathering power into our hands, but wholly at safety.

(4) We asked that men be sent to make up our strength, taking for our ideal candidates the graduates of the training classes, the members of the Tianjin "Anti-Japanese Traitor-Killing Corps," and young men of a high political awareness; whether or not they had any local tie to Shanghai was not to be reckoned a necessary condition.

That it might the more easily win our superiors' consent, Brother Qingbin and I took thought together over the manner of it too, and this time, unlike the ordinary way, we did not mean to draw the matter up as a telegram to be sent up. We would take the occasion of the Bureau's holding of its enlarged "April First Congress" at Chongqing, and, as Brother Qi Qingbin was to attend it as the "Shanghai District's" representative, we would have him carry it up in person. In this way he could set it forth by word of mouth to Mr. Dai's face, could confer with the several offices concerned, and, should any obstruction arise, could yet explain it in detail and even argue it out on its merits.

This document, taking in as it did the heads of personnel, of funds, and of the wireless station, written upon paper and carried upon one's person, might—should any mishap befall—have led to who knew what disaster. For this reason we made, in point of technique, a most "clumsy" arrangement: one part Brother Qingbin was to keep in his head, one part to be written in secret ink and kept about him, and yet another part to be sent by wireless to Chongqing to be handed on to Brother Qi Qingbin.

Once he had reached Chongqing, he was then to draw it all together and write it up as a paper report, to be laid before Mr. Dai in person for his decision. Upon this business, besides the District secretary and myself, we set to work as well the two assistant secretaries of the two terms and the District accountant, and it was more than a month in the doing. It was well that this stretch of time lay in a settled state, with no fresh mishaps such as the arrest of our workers; for had there been any change, we should have had once more to add to it or strike from it.

Brother Qi Qingbin went to Chongqing in the third month, as the "Shanghai District's" representative to the "April First Congress" marking the eighth anniversary of the founding of the "Juntong Bureau," and returned to Shanghai about the middle of the fourth month. Mr. Dai himself and the Bureau headquarters agreed in principle to the "Shanghai District's" new proposals; only the finance department in charge of accounts (at that time it was not called the "chief accountant's room"; I recall it was the "accounts room") had its objections. First, that the cost was too great and could not for the moment be met, and that if it were to be remitted by installments, they asked us to name again a minimum figure for each. Second, that money spent could not but be entered in the books; that for large outlays, such as the "key money" for premises and the like, which ran readily into the thousands, vouchers of course had to be taken; and that as to how these vouchers were to be kept, that was a matter of technique, and they hoped we would work out some feasible conclusion. These two objections amounted, in effect, to a rejection of our proposals. Even as to the matter of "spending money without entering it in the books," Mr. Dai himself could not hand down such a ruling; he could only bid us work out some method of "keeping books that left no trace." The upshot was that the problem of the great transfer of personnel was solved, while the great chests upon chests of ledgers and vouchers remained as impossible to deal with as before. Of this matter the writer has already made mention three times in the present work, and there is more to come, for the lesson it taught us was too grievous.

In the third chapter above I set forth in fuller detail the substance of "New Group One"; next let me set down, of the "First Intelligence Group," what is worth the recording.

The "New Group One" and the "First Intelligence Group" were still two separate units: "New Group One" was newly formed after I took up my post, while the "First Group" was the one that had existed before. After the leader of the "First Group," comrade Zhu Xiaogu, could no longer carry on activity at Shanghai—the enemy and the puppets pressing their arrests hard—and had been transferred away, the post of its leader stood vacant, with as yet no fitting man for it, and so as a makeshift comrade Bi Gaokui, leader of "New Group One," was named to hold it concurrently. Leader Bi was of outstanding gifts, and he made the work of the "First Intelligence Group," no less than that of "New Group One," show forth with vividness and spirit.

The "First Intelligence Group" had a "group secretary" of its own, the post filled by comrade Zhang Xueli. Comrade Zhang was a graduate of the Linfeng training class; after group leader Zhu Xiaogu was transferred away, and before group leader Bi Gaokui took up the post concurrently, it was he who held it in an acting capacity. Comrade Zhang, though limited in his working experience and with as yet no great achievement to his name, was loyal and to be relied upon, close and careful in his thinking, and through his time as acting head he discharged his duty to the full.

Besides the gathering of intelligence, the "First Group" had one particular task laid upon it: that all our working connections within the two police forces of the Shanghai International Settlement and the French Concession should be handed over to that group to keep touch with and to guide.

On the side of the International Settlement police (the police of the Municipal Council of the International Settlement): among the higher ranks were the three men Liu Shaokui, Liu Junqing, and Liu ⅩⅩ; later we drew in six more, Dong Ren and others—Dong Ren being a false name, and the names of the other five reserved for the present. By Brother Bi Gaokui's showing, one among them, a Mr. Zhao Fengxing, once served in the Taiwan police and has now retired.

Brother Liu Shaokui joined the work as early as the twenty-seventh year; afterward, so that the several parts might support one another, Brother Shaokui in turn brought in Brother Liu Junqing.

The other Mr. Liu ⅩⅩ had no lateral tie with Brother Liu Shaokui, but was drawn in by comrade Liu ⅩⅩ, a member of the "New Group One." Now, at comrade Liu ⅩⅩ's charge to me, it were best that their names not be raised, and so I pass them over. This bespeaks a large and open heart that sought neither name nor gain; and it was also for the safety of those left behind on the mainland.

Brother Liu Shaokui had held in the International Settlement police such posts as detective sergeant, inspector, and chief inspector; Brother Liu Junqing had held the posts of detective sergeant and inspector. In view of the needs of the work, Brother Shaokui in the twenty-eighth year drew in two British police officers as well, to take formal part in the work. By "formal" is meant that they accepted the activity funds issued by the "Shanghai District," and understood at the same time that this was work done for the Chinese government. The names of the two were:

Lao Bosheng, chief of the political section of the police of the Municipal Council of the Shanghai International Settlement.

Kelaideng, chief of the eighth section of the police of the Municipal Council of the Shanghai International Settlement. (Charged with the coordination of anti-Japanese matters as between the Japanese side and the International Settlement.)

By the record in Brother Liu Shaokui's own "memoir," after the victory in the war Mr. Dai at Shanghai gave a banquet in honor of Lao Bosheng and Kelaideng, to thank them to their faces; and he invited as well, to keep them company, the two Britons, chief detective Gelehua and detective Pulaide, because these too had freely lent us aid in our work. The help that Brother Liu Shaokui and the others gave to the work of our "Shanghai District" was, in an environment such as that, exceedingly brave, and one may fairly praise it with a word: "no small thing"; for a hair's slip, and they themselves were finished as well. As each had his own charge, the help they gave the work took several forms: the furnishing of materials, the passing of word, the transport of arms, the covering of a withdrawal, the lightening of a sentence, the quiet release of a man, and the like. Do not, reading them there upon the page, take them for so light a matter; in the doing they were nothing so simple.

On the side of the French Concession police (the Director of Police under the Conseil d'Administration of the French Concession—the police department and the political-and-public-order department): there were the three men Jiang Futian, Li ⅩⅩ, and Yang Zhongfang. Brother Jiang Futian had served as detective sergeant and as inspector; Yang Zhongfang was a detective; Li ⅩⅩ had served as a deputy.

There were two Frenchmen, both of whom accepted our side's subsidy of activity funds: the one was Valontin, chief inspector of the police department, who went by the assumed name of Ma Long; the other was Sali, a deputy of the political-and-public-order department. The writer once met Ma Long, in his office; at the time I knew who he was, but he did not know that I was the man in charge at Shanghai, and when I had made it plain to him, he put a telephone call through for me, so giving me the chance to say a few words of the gravest bearing to Gui Difei, assistant secretary of the "Shanghai District"—this was the piece of help that left upon me the deepest impression.

While I was in Shanghai, the writer and Brother Jiang Futian were not acquainted; it was not until the fifty-first year of the Republic that I met him at Brother Bi Gaokui's house in Taipei. He has now been dead these many years.

The foregoing are the connections of personnel that the "Shanghai District" held within the two Concessions' police forces, and in those days they were of a truth of no small help to my work in Shanghai. And yet, for all that, we could never make out clearly the establishment of personnel in the two Concessions' police, the names of their several posts, and the like, so that in what is written above, as to the styling of the posts, there are here and there points that cannot but be left dim and uncertain. Some days ago I came upon a book, "The Question of the Shanghai Concessions," in the Zhengzhong Book Company's edition of the sixty-ninth year, which carries among its appendices a piece entitled "A Short History of the Two Municipal Police Forces, British and French, at Shanghai," noted as taken from "Materials for the Study of Shanghai." In these materials is a passage on "the present organization of the three police authorities," taking in the "International Settlement police," the "French Concession police," and the "Shanghai Municipal Bureau of Public Safety." I excerpt here the two portions on the Settlement police and the French police, to be set beside my own account for comparison.

### (1) The International Settlement Police

The Central Police Station of the International Settlement stood between Foochow Road and the space of Honan and Kiangse Roads, within a new building facing north with its back to the south, under the charge of the Secretariat of the Municipal Council. In sum the police officers numbered: Westerners, 513; Indians, 599; Japanese, 256; Chinese, three thousand six hundred and forty-five. Beneath it were divided thirteen police stations (the equivalent of precincts),

whose names were as follows:

Hongkou, Louza, Gordon, Sinza, Bubbling Well, Wayside, Kashing Road, Dixwell Road, Wayside, Yangtszepoo, Poutoo Road, Chengtu Road, Yülin Road, and the rest. As to posts and numbers: a chief of 1, deputy chiefs of 6, deputy chiefs of section of 8, chief inspectors of 20, assistant chief inspectors of 2, senior sub-inspectors of 15, chief detective sergeants of 6, sub-inspectors of 47, detective sergeants of 23, deputy detective sergeants of 64, sergeants of five hundred and nine, detectives of 226, probationary sergeants of 74, a probationary detective of 1, constables of 4,666, detective constables of 245.

The complement of officers and men set out above was distributed among the following ten units, whose names are: the management office (the police department); the arrest branch and the special branch (the head bureau for the investigation of crime, or the criminal section); the head offices of the several police districts (precincts); the district police stations (sub-stations); the armed reserve corps (the security corps); the traffic office (the traffic section); the court and lawyers' office (the court police); the store and transport office (the guard on duty); the mounted police (in former days there was a mounted force, and by this time there may still have remained a few horses for form's sake, since on the establishment there was but one senior sergeant and one sergeant). There was besides one special-branch office, with one sub-inspector and one constable, the charge of which I do not know.

The writer appends a note: what stands within the parentheses is added by the writer, and may not be altogether apt, but is for the easier understanding. The names in the original text were doubtless translated from the English, and even in the book itself there are places where they do not agree from one part to another. The Shanghai people commonly called the head of the Settlement police the "chief superintendent," which was probably just the "commissioner of police." And there is another term the Shanghai people forever have on their lips, "three-stripe head," which should be the sergeant, since upon his sleeve-badge there were three curved stripes, and so he was called "three-stripe head." Do not think the "three-stripe head" of no high standing; he had the most to do with the residents, and his power too was great. A few years at being a "three-stripe head," and most came away with a fortune of ten thousand strings of cash.

### (2) The French Concession Police

As to posts and numbers: the Director of Police and his subordinates numbered 1,794 in all; clerks and workmen, 212; judicial police, 36; the cigarette consolidated-tax office, 5; the dog pound, 4; the guard of parks and wharves, 15; the Concession defense corps, 127.

The system of organization: at the head was the "Director of Police"; beneath him it divided into the two departments of police and of political-and-public-order. The office units were the secretariat section and the court-police section.

Under the head of the police department were a deputy head for defense, a deputy head ⅩⅩ, and a chief of personnel.

Under the head of the political-and-public-order department were a chief of the political section, a chief of the public-order section, and a chief of the prosecution-and-interpretation section.

Of the foregoing, the portion on the International Settlement is the fuller, that on the French Concession very brief, and neither notes its date. The writer infers, from the wording about it, that this material may refer to the state of things between the twenty-fourth and twenty-fifth years of the Republic, and it is by now a relic of the past.

As for the puppet "Shanghai Municipal Police Bureau," we had working men lying hidden there too, kept in touch with by the "Fourth Intelligence Group," and having no connection with the "First Intelligence Group."

Besides this, the "First Group" was charged as well with keeping touch with several special working connections directly under the "Shanghai District." Among them was a man surnamed Yuan and named Shu, also called Xueyi, or Xiaoyi. Whether he was "handed over" to us by our superiors or "drawn in" on the spot is now past all tracing. This man came out of the newspaper world, his political ties exceedingly tangled; the intelligence he furnished was, for the most part, matter startling in its title and thin in its substance, of no value and, moreover, an added vexation—yet all at once there would come a piece of some most alluring stuff that made one feel he was a useful man of parts after all. By the time Li Shiqun had become chairman of Jiangsu Province under the Wang puppet regime, Yuan Xueyi appeared again in the guise of "director of the department of education," and yet he kept still with the "First Group" a tie neither near nor far, and went on collecting without fail the "activity funds" we issued him. Weighed up on the whole, he showed nothing out of the way in his work. Once the war was won there was no more news of him. About the thirty-ninth year I chanced to see his name in the newspapers of the central government—and there he was, a bureau chief of the puppet "Ministry of Education." By the Communists' going rate, a turncoat time-server is given at the most the empty title of some do-nothing commissioner; that he was made a full "bureau chief" is proof enough that Yuan Shu, Yuan Xueyi, had been from the first a member of the Communist Party.

The "First Group" had of old a "Korean cell," with Lu Jianhua as its leader and Shen Shen, Su Quan, and Yu Yi as its members (these three members' names being aliases). This cell's chief connection lay on the side of the Japanese navy. After Bi Gaokui took up the headship of the "First Group" concurrently, he judged that, since the men of this cell could keep a footing within the Japanese navy under Japanese identity, they might, over and above intelligence work, widen their usefulness and press on toward action and sabotage. And so that cell wrought a shining record of deeds, and bore too a heavy sacrifice; the facts of the matter I shall set down separately in a page of their own.

### "The Japanese Gendarmerie": Inhuman Cruelty

The "gendarmerie" was in its origin "the police of the army"; but through the years of the war of resistance the gendarme units belonging to the Japanese China Expeditionary Army changed utterly in their nature. They were an iron pincer and a yoke for the ruling and holding down of the occupied territory, and became too a devil's hand for the poisoning of our fellow countrymen.

From our Northeast down to the South of China, wherever the Japanese army held a district, there was a "gendarme unit" set up; the great cities had large "gendarme units" and their branch offices, the small towns and market villages had small ones, small down to a single "squad," or to but two or three men—so that one may say they followed like a shadow and were to be seen everywhere. All own it for an undeniable fact that the "Japanese gendarmerie" was against Heaven and against reason, out of its very mind; even the Japanese who once served as officers of the "gendarme units" dare not deny it. In the eight years of our war of resistance they lorded it and ran riot, worked every manner of evil, and slew who knows how many of our fellow countrymen besides. And of our comrades working behind the enemy's lines, who knows how many fell into the hands of the "Japanese gendarmes"? Though this be already the affair of forty-odd years ago, yet let the "Japanese gendarmerie" but be named, and there are, I am sure, still many in whom the rancor is not spent, the pain not stilled. Would that we, the sons of Yan and Huang, might for generation upon generation not forget this rare shame and great disgrace: we do not urge revenge, but at the least we ought to strive with all our might never again to suffer another's bullying.

The writer's understanding of the "Japanese gendarmes" is not yet a thorough one; but because it was, in China, our sworn foe in the work, and because we suffered no little at its hands, there has gathered, day upon day and month upon month, a fair acquaintance with it, and above all with the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie."

The "Japanese gendarmes" formed a system unto themselves. The gendarme units dispatched to China were under the control of the "General Headquarters of the (Shina) China Expeditionary Army," and were styled the "Gendarmerie Headquarters," stationed at Nanking. It was founded in the first month of the twenty-seventh year of the Republic, its first commanding officer Major-General Ōki Shigeru. Under the Nanking Japanese Gendarmerie Headquarters it divided into the two great parts of "Central China" and "North China." "Central China" was the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie"; "North China," the "Peking Japanese Gendarmerie."

Now let us speak of the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie." The "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" was founded in the first month of the twenty-seventh year of the Republic, at the same time as the Nanking Japanese Gendarmerie Headquarters, its unit headquarters on North Sichuan Road in the Hongkou district. From the twenty-seventh year until the Japanese surrender in the thirty-fourth, the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" changed its commander five times over, the first being Major-General Miura Saburō. His term ran from the twenty-seventh year to the twenty-ninth—which was just the time of the writer's own tenure as chief of the "Shanghai District." In those days the gendarme commander Miura appeared often in the intelligence materials; and among the intelligence the Japanese gendarmerie had gathered, they knew there was a "Blue Shirt Society," and knew too there was a "Shanghai District," but were not clear as to the "Shanghai District's" inner organization.

The headquarters of the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" was in Hongkou; and as to the greater Shanghai region, Hongkou was precisely the sphere of Japanese power. The common townsfolk regarded the "Japanese gendarmerie" with loathing and dread both together; and as for the whole body of our comrades, at the mere naming of the "Japanese gendarmerie" every man's teeth itched with hatred, for once a working comrade of ours had entered the "Japanese gendarmerie," he was foredoomed to a hair's breadth of death, and even could he come out alive, he would carry away some maiming or other—a lost arm, a shortened leg, a deafened ear, a blinded eye—as was seen again and again.

The matter of the Japanese gendarmes' abuse of cruel tortures I shall leave to be written of later on, and speak first of its establishment of personnel.

The inner organization of the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" was divided into two great departments. The First Section, also called the "police-affairs section," had charge of general administration, taking in personnel, finance, general affairs, and the like. The Second Section, also called the special-higher section, had charge of business, taking in espionage, stratagem, and action, and the like. What in the end the word "special-higher" is to be taken to mean I could never make out. In its nature it stood point-blank opposed to our own doings, but in point of character it was "low" past all mention. What they called "espionage" was chiefly social intelligence, worked through a pack of interpreters willing to play the jackal as their eyes and ears, who turned in their turn to the use of the local hooligans and ruffians of the place, pulling together this scrap and that in a tissue of nonsense—which was just to make a fine chance for laying hands here and there and preying upon the countryside. Speak of these interpreters, and they were of a truth the dregs of humankind, all conscience lost. Borrowing the tiger's dread, they worked every evil, and bitterly indeed did they harm the common folk of China. The Japanese took themselves for masters at the working of "stratagem," and at every mention of the two words "stratagem" they wore a look of no little self-satisfaction—which was proof enough of how full of wiles they were. Yet the "stratagem" the Japanese gendarmes came near was no more than some petty trick, some "devil's notion"; from true "policy" or "strategy" it was farther off than a hundred and eight thousand li. The "action" carried on under the special-higher section was, in fact, just "the taking of men" and "the working of men over," and that with no humanity in it at all.

As to the inner organization of the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie," besides the two sections set out above, after the affair of Pearl Harbor there was added a "foreign-affairs section," charged solely with the foreign residents remaining at Shanghai, though only the white men of Europe; the other colored races were looked upon as one with the Chinese.

Beneath the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" there were, moreover, many branch offices. Of second-order units there were:

the Shanghai North gendarme district—Hongkou, Chapei, Kiangwan; the Shanghai South gendarme district—the French Concession, Nanshih; the Shanghai West gendarme district—the International Settlement and the extra-settlement roads; the Shanghai East gendarme district—the whole of Pootung; the Water gendarme district—the Whangpoo River patrol; the district for railways and highways; the special-action squad, charged with matters bearing on the "Loyal and Patriotic Army"; the wireless-detection squad, controlling the interception of wireless dispatches.

Over and above those set out here, there were third-order units, and temporary formations, and the like; so that one may see how vast and how close-knit its organization was.

The regional "gendarme detachments" belonging to the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" had powers exceedingly great; their inner organization was of the same pattern as the headquarters, and, most of all, each was furnished with a "special-higher section," bent solely upon anti-Japanese activity.

Looking again at the range of jurisdiction and the manner of distribution of the several branch offices set out above, one may plainly discover how deep-laid was the pains the Japanese army of occupation took to hold "greater Shanghai" in its grip.

The gendarme unit had itself no "judicial power," but it held the power to detain a man without limit of time; that is to say, it could not "sentence," but it could "shut up," and could even torture to death. And so at the gendarme unit and at each detachment there was set up a "detention house." The "detention house" was a lockup, and for some luckless fellow countrymen to be shut up within a "detention house" for several years on end was a common thing; nor was it seldom heard that a man had died within the gendarme unit. The keeping of the gendarme unit's "detention house" set human nature at naught: over and above the harsh regulations, all went by the whim and the pleasure of the keepers—let one be out of humor and he might strike you a few blows or land you a few kicks to vent his spleen; let the fancy take him and he might toy with a man like a plaything, or starve you a few days, or punish you by not letting you lie down to sleep at night, so that a man's dignity was long since utterly lost.

It was said above that the gendarme unit had itself no "judicial power"; how then did it "close a case"? By what I understand, all whom the gendarme unit had marked for a sentence of death or of imprisonment were, without exception, sent over to the military-law organ of the highest headquarters of the local Japanese garrison to be dealt with—which, in fact, was but a form. For instance: the highest headquarters of the Japanese garrison in the Shanghai region was at first the "Seventieth Army" (code-named the "登 Force"), and afterward, on a change of guard, was altered to the "Thirteenth Army" (code-named the "枪 Force"); the man was then sent to that army's military-law unit for a judgment given for form's sake, while as to the facts of the case the army headquarters paid no heed at all. Those marked for imprisonment were committed to serve their term, some of them being turned over again to a prison under the puppet government to serve it; those marked for death, the army headquarters put to death in secret, and never made any open proclamation of it.

The Japanese gendarme unit's ways of dealing with its "prisoners," over and above the passing of them through the "judicial procedure" of form set out above, ran to many another sort of trick, of which, by what I actually understand, there were the following:

Take a man up by mistake, and he was taken up for nothing; he could but own his ill luck, and could he come away on bail it was already a blessing from Heaven. And yet among these there were some who, "gone bail," were then bled hard by the go-between.

The commonest was detention for inquiry. Once inquired into, it was a matter of some months; one questioning, and a layer of skin was flayed off; when the inquiry might be made clear, one could only lift one's head and ask of blue Heaven.

Next was the transfer to another jurisdiction for detention and inquiry. Nothing came to light in the one place, so they changed the place and inquired again; inquire here and inquire there, question once and question again, misery upon misery—how was it to be borne?

Long-term detention. There was a kind of case whose facts were tangled and never to be made clear, and so it was set aside outright and dragged on. This was a thing only the strongest of life could live through.

As for those done to death by the torture, that too was a matter seen every day; to them it counted for nothing at all, and never was the responsibility looked into. So it was that a great many men vanished without rhyme or reason, the most of them having set out upon this road to the Prince of Hell's own gate. In the end not even the bodies were known to have been cast away anywhere.

The Japanese gendarme unit had, in its dealing with our Chinese fellow countrymen, this trick of poison as well; the poison was compounded and furnished by yet another Japanese unit, and by the difference of the object was worked by a difference of means. The one was a slow poison: taken down, it gave at the time no particular feeling, nor any change to the outward form—all was as usual—and only after a day or two did it take effect; and since no one knew what the drug was, there was no saving a man at all. Wu Shibao, commander of the guard corps at "No. 76," was just such a one, who took down this poison and was then set free, and by the next day, when the drug worked, gave up the ghost; and the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" at the time flatly disowned it. (The writer notes: in the twelfth month of the seventieth year of the Republic I met at Taipei Mr. Ōtsuka Kiyoshi, who had once been chief of the special-higher section of the Gendarmerie Headquarters of the Nanking General Headquarters of the Japanese China Expeditionary Army, and later became a bureau director in the Japanese Army Ministry's military-affairs department; he owned to my face that the thing was indeed so. And he told me further: the drug used was furnished by the "Gyoku Unit belonging to the Tama Force." The "Tama Force" was the code name of a department given solely to the study of chemistry and biology.) The other was likewise a slow poison, much the same as the first, used not upon "prisoners" already arrested, but to "entertain" a guest. Such was Li Shiqun, the head man at "No. 76," who took down this poison at an evening banquet; and by that same night, once Li Shiqun had gone by train from Shanghai back to Soochow, the sweat broke out over his whole body and would not stop, and by dawn he died of collapse. In the interval a Japanese army doctor had been called to treat him, but could make out no manner of illness. And there was yet another, a swift poison, that might be taken by mouth or given by injection, and could kill in a minute or two. Those who died their cruel deaths under this crime were, I am sure, in great number, and among them our comrades were surely not few. Only, dying so swiftly, not one could be saved, and there would never be a living witness; and after death, of course, the body was destroyed and the traces wiped away, so that outsiders could never point out who had died and how.

Next let me set down the cruel tortures the Japanese gendarme unit worked upon our Chinese fellow countrymen. It was not the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" and its several detachments alone that were thus, but the gendarme units of every rank in all the occupied territory of the whole country, not one but was the same. To sum it in a word, they were a kind of "two-legged beast" with human nature wholly extinguished, caring nothing for humanity at all, and the lower officers above all had in them a fierce lust to torment, knowing still less that in the world of men there was such a thing as "human rights." I am sure these few words are fair and just, and by no means overdrawn. The gendarmerie's "tortures" were crueler by far than that set of "No. 76's"; were they to be described one by one, in full and in detail, they could not be told out even in twenty or thirty thousand words. Here I shall use for each but a single one-word verb by way of pointing, and make it clear in a phrase or two, so that, seen thus, the impression may perhaps go the deeper. Remember: this is the bitter lesson the weak received in a war between nations, and it is by no means to be lightly regarded.

Here, then, are the "tortures" of every kind and sort, past all imagining:

"Strike": using no implement, striking with the bare hand alone. The palm and the fist came together, over head and face all at once, striking till the eyes shot golden sparks and the mouth spat fresh blood; and of a sudden, one more blow aimed straight at the belly.

"Throw": to catch a man up, to hoist him upon the back, to swing him high and dash him down upon the ground, be it grass, or board floor, or floor laid of stone. One throw not enough, then a second; and were an arm or a leg thrown broken, what was that to him?

"Kick": the cowhide shoes the Japanese gendarmes wore were very hard and very heavy, as though for the kicking of a football, and had besides several iron studs upon the sole. It was in just such shoes that they kicked at the pit of the belly and at the shin-bone. Should a man think to shield himself with his hands, they would kick him wildly all over with a chain of kicks.

"Ram": to seize a man by the hair with one grip and ram his head hard against the wall as one pounds garlic, till there was not one but had his head broken and his blood flowing. Even had he sunk down past bearing it, still they would press him to the ground and ram him a few times more. All the above use no implement of torture, and may be called "empty-hand" work. There was besides the use of objects near to hand, which may be called "taking one's stuff where one stands," that is, in the very place of the questioning, using whatever there was. For example:

"Scald": burning coal, the cigarette hung at the lip, the fire-tongs upon the brazier, the iron poker of the coal stove—all might be taken up as they came to hand to scald any part of the body; and after the scalding, blisters and festering, in clusters and in a great spread.

"Kneel": to draw up the trouser-leg and make a man kneel upon sharp things—edged stones, broken glasses, and the like. Should one small piece sink into the flesh, that was bitterer still.

"Crush": a pencil to crush the fingers, a stool-leg to crush the toes, a man seated upon the chest bearing down with a jolt and a jolt, a rod set within the calf and pressure then put upon it—of these there was no end.

"Stab": the bayonet ready to hand, the sharpened pencil, the bamboo toothpick left from the eating—all were their instruments of torture, and let the fancy take them, they might stab at random. To stab one hole was nothing; to drive a toothpick into the quick of a fingernail pierced a man to the marrow, and truly he would rather not live. The above, too, can serve but as instances; the like tricks were many more. Below let me set down a few crueler and more venomous still:

"Bite": not man biting man, but the setting of a trained dog to bite a man. The dog was a wolfhound, its wildness not shed; at a single word of command it would bite in a mad fit, heedless of head or of face, heedless of rump, on until the word to stop. By that time a sound, whole man was already flayed and torn, the blood dripping down.

"Douse": this, again, was unlike that manner of dousing at "No. 76." They would draw a torn gunny-sack tight over the head, then turn a man upside down, two men holding him up by the two legs, and steep him in a swimming-pool; after a while draw him up, then steep him again; a few turns of it, and he was left neither dead nor alive.

"Shock": the gendarme units of somewhat higher rank had, near all of them, this apparatus. Once man and current were joined by this specially-made chair, there was, one may say, no spot upon the whole body, hair and all standing on end, that was not past bearing; and it would besides break down the tissues of the body and bring on many an after-affliction.

"Frighten": this was of the mind, a torment of the spirit, and what they were most given to using was the "accompanying to the ground." By "accompanying to the ground" is meant to be bound and led forth to the execution-ground alongside one who is to be put to death. Though it was another who was to be killed, yet the one who accompanied him could not but see it with his eyes and be frightened to the heart, his very soul flying from him for it.

The several sorts here set out are all the very experience, in their own persons, of our comrades: fact a hundred parts in the hundred, and only such as has been left out or passed over, with never a twig or leaf added, never a drop of oil or vinegar poured on. In our ordinary talk we are loath to speak of such things, for it is grievous to the heart to tell of them.

By the writer's own understanding and experience, and by the impression got from talking with Ōtsuka Kiyoshi, who was once chief of the special-higher section of the "Nanking Japanese Gendarmerie Headquarters," the gendarmes stationed in China through the war of resistance had, in different periods, a different way with them. To read this way as a "policy" would perhaps put it too strongly; and yet, in fact, in their dealings with our Chinese fellow countrymen, there were indeed many places where they differed from one time to another. This marked difference the writer, for the ease of explaining it, has divided into three stages:

The first stage, longest in time, began from the seven-seven war of resistance and ran on until before the "Pearl Harbor affair" of the twelfth month of the thirtieth year of the Republic. This stretch may be called the period of "mad savagery," when the fellow countrymen cruelly done to death were most in number.

The second stage was in the time from the twelfth month of the thirtieth year to the latter half of the thirty-third year; because the Japanese army's push to the south had left it in truth without the strength forcibly to hold the mainland of China, it was sunk in a plight it could not draw itself out of. And so it is named the period of "fierce without, hollow within," which showed itself in their way as a leaning toward "conciliation," the passion for slaughter being somewhat curbed.

The third stage, from the American counter-offensive to the Japanese surrender, was in the time from the latter half of the thirty-third year to the eighth month of the thirty-fourth—that is, to the victory in the war of resistance. By this time the Japanese gendarmes had come to the last period of "fierce of face and faint of heart." And yet they did not wholly cease their slaughter of the Chinese; only their hand had softened and their courage grown cold.

Though what is pointed at above is the Japanese gendarmes, it may in part reflect too the Japanese policy toward China.

Now, upon the materials at hand, let me set down in brief the chief personnel of the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie"—incomplete, yet already a thing hard to come by. The names, ranks, and terms of the successive commanders of the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" were, in the main, as follows:

The first, Major-General Miura Saburō, from the twentieth year to the twenty-ninth; the second, Major-General Nami Toshirō, from the twenty-ninth year to the thirty-third; the third, Major-General Kinoshita Eiichi, from the thirty-third year to the sixth month of the thirty-fourth; the fourth, Colonel Shikata Ryōji, from the sixth month of the thirty-fourth to the eighth month of the same year, thereafter raised to major-general.

The fifth, Colonel Yamazaki Naokichi, acting for Shikata Ryōji, and after the Japanese surrender handed over to the Third Front Army of the national forces for trial.

As for the men who crossed swords with us at close quarters in the work, they were the chiefs of the "special-higher section." Since the "special-higher section" had charge of "espionage, stratagem, and action," it was just the sworn foe of the "Shanghai District," and so we paid a particular heed to the men who filled this post. By what I have learned, the special-higher chiefs of the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" were, one after another: Lieutenant-Colonel Ōi Hideo; Lieutenant-Colonel Hayashi (the given name unknown); Lieutenant-Colonel Okamura (the given name unknown); Lieutenant-Colonel Nagamitsu Shōji, and the like—whether these four only, or whether there were yet others, I dare not say for certain. Each of these few men had a short story bound up with the "Shanghai District" or with the writer himself, which I shall bring forward hereafter along with the matter of the story.

The highest administrative organ of the Shanghai "International Settlement" was styled the "Municipal Council." The "Municipal Council" had a board of directors, and in the recess between its sittings a standing body, the "Secretariat," dealt with the general administration. Under the "Secretariat" were many units, and one of them was the "police department"; this "police department" was just what the Shanghai people were used to calling the "Settlement police," and what the rickshaw-pullers called again the "Great British police."

The head of the police department was styled not chief but "chief superintendent," a post filled by usage down the years by an Englishman. The deputy head was styled "deputy chief superintendent," of whom there was more than one, and among them one who was of Japanese nationality. What the state of things was before the war the writer took no pains to look into, and so passes it by; but after the war of resistance, one of the deputy chief superintendents of the International Settlement's police department was chosen and "recommended" by the "special-higher section of the Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie." Of these the name is known of but one man, Akagi Chikayuki. After the "Pearl Harbor affair," the police department of the Municipal Council of the Shanghai International Settlement was changed into a "police bureau," and the gendarmerie's special-higher section sent Lieutenant-Colonel Gotō Shigeru to set up a separate organ on Weihaiwei Road, becoming the real commander of the "police bureau." By this the Shanghai International Settlement lay wholly under the control of the Japanese gendarmes.

### The Notorious "No. 76"

"No. 76" is a house number, and in its origin nothing out of the way; it was only after being taken over by the Wang puppets' "Special Operations Headquarters" that its evil name came abroad.

"No. 76" was a great walled compound, taking up some eight mou of ground, standing on Jessfield Road in the western extra-settlement district of the Shanghai International Settlement; it was the private property of Chen Diaoyuan, an old soldier who had once been chairman of Anhwei and of Shantung provinces, and in the war of resistance was president of the Military Advisory Council. About the time of the National Revolutionary Army's Northern Expedition there was a bad fashion abroad: some of those who sat in high office and drew fat salaries, and above all the soldiers who had once held territory by force of arms and been men of the hour, were very fond of buying up land and houses within the "Concessions"—their own country's soil, yet under foreigners' governance—both to find an outlet for their surplus wealth, and to make ready a dwelling for their days of retirement; holding that in this way alone could they keep up their standing and style and the safety of their persons and their houses. Such not-very-seemly doings were seen again and again in Tianjin, Shanghai, Hankou, Xiamen—wherever there was a "Concession"—and most of all at Tianjin and Shanghai. It is not to say that every high official and grandee was thus; of those with empty sleeves and a clean life, keeping their own persons unstained, there were plenty still. General Wu Peifu, for instance, who all his life set up as his standard the "doctrine of the three nots," and could carry it out in his own person and his own strength, was a very good pattern of the kind.

Whence did the Wang puppets' "Special Operations Headquarters" come to be? One cannot say it rose out of thin air. By what is known, its full name was the "Special Operations Headquarters of the Chinese Kuomintang for the Rooting Out of Communists and the Saving of the Nation"; and, so seen, it belonged to the "Party." Only, the words "Chinese Kuomintang" here used were not the true "Chinese Kuomintang," but the name that Wang Jingwei falsely borrowed. For at the first setting up of the "Special Operations Headquarters," Wang Jingwei's puppet regime had as yet no footing, and, being in haste to open for business, he hung it therefore under the name of the "Party," and set up as his standard the "rooting out of Communists and the saving of the nation." This style of "rooting out Communists and saving the nation" sounds much like "opposing Communists and saving the nation," but is exceedingly awkward to look upon—"the one and only house of its kind," nowhere else to be seen.

The house number upon the gate of "No. 76" had long since gone; what hung on the outer wall beside the gate was a plaque of dark-brown wood, upon which were carved four great black characters, "Special Operations Headquarters." This quarter had of old been a residential district, and the sudden appearance of a few neither-fish-nor-fowl fellows standing sentry with guns showed all too glaringly. Whose notion it was, none knew, but they had gone so far as to sheathe the two leaves of the great gate in tin-plate and lay a coat of paint over it, black and glistening, the very image of terror and of crime.

To pass in and out at the great gate, all save a few of the head men had to have their credentials looked at. The credentials so called were not hung upon the breast, like a "waist-tally," but tucked into the bosom—wanting only the pinning to the waistband of the trousers.

The state of things above, anyone passing that way might see; what lay within the great gate was not so easily to be known by an outsider. For this pack wrought harm out of all measure, a hundredfold, a thousandfold beyond the common bandit; they made every manner of lawless, heart-stopping deed of terror—kidnappings, assassinations, bombings, open robberies—several a day, without cease, and that in the boldest, most barefaced way, striking of set purpose at the patriots and at the good common folk; till they had stirred over the lonely isle of Shanghai one reek of blood-wind and foul air, every man in dread of his life, blenching at the mere talk of it as at a tiger. And so the Shanghai people called this tract of the western Settlement the "Badlands," and called "No. 76" the "den of the Badlands."

Since they styled themselves the "Special Operations Headquarters," and their doings pointed already, dimly, at our working organizations in the fallen zone, they had long been set down as a target of our work, to be scouted out and made plain at all costs. Drawing together the reports from several different sources, we understood it thus:

Within the great gate of "No. 76," at the first turn to the right, was a passage some thirty meters long, and on either side, north and south facing, stood a row of one-storey houses each, partitioned off into small rooms one by one. In the row that faced north with its back to the south, the first room had within it a rude furnishing, no more than a wooden table and chairs, and this was the "interrogation room." Those "No. 76" seized were, by rule, first brought here for a preliminary "questioning," and a simple "deposition" taken down. In the row opposite, the first room on the inner side was the "office," reported to belong to the "action section of the First Section of the Special Operations Headquarters"—that is to say, this office was used by the "action section" to work its cases. Adjoining the "office" was a great open room, reported to be the place where the torture was worked, within it every manner of instrument of torture, its windows all covered over of set purpose, black as a pit, the very air within it stifling.

Facing the mouth of that passage where one came in was a small narrow door, reported to have been opened for the one man Wan Lilang alone. In the original building this had been but a brick wall; but because Wan Lilang had seized by force the house of the common folk next door for his "residence," a great hole was therefore cut in the wall and a door of boards fitted, that he might pass in and out and not be exposed to attack upon the street. This Wan Lilang here spoken of is the very Wan Lilang made mention of before, who had been deputy commander of the "Fourth Action Brigade of the Juntong Bureau's Shanghai District," and had now turned his coat and gone over to the puppets, become a traitor—made First-Section chief of "No. 76," giving out his orders within this one small courtyard: a very monkey crowned and gowned. Then, to the left of the great gate where the sentries stood, was a lane called "Hua Village"; this was no property of Chen Diaoyuan's, but had been taken in by "No. 76" within its bounds and made into quarters. Most to be marked was that, save by way of the great gate, there was no other passage through. And the people who dwelt within "Hua Village," every household of them, great and small, were on the register, and a "stranger" could by no means "slip" in.

Going farther in, there was a second gate, with no sentry to be seen at it, but "plainclothesmen" moving about nearby. Should armed guards suddenly appear, it meant that some high head man was about to come, or that a meeting was being held or made ready. These armed guards belonged to the puppet "guard corps of the Special Operations Headquarters," some two hundred and more in all. Some fifty paces from the second gate stood a great foreign-style building, which the men of "No. 76" called of themselves the "high foreign house." This building was just the office block of the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters." A high flight of stone steps, a dozen and more, and here again was a door, with a pair of sentries before it. Going in, there was to the right a great reception hall, arrayed in rich and splendid style—whether it had been so from the first, or was set in order again afterward, I do not know—and to the left a meeting room, at times, when guests were feasted, turned for the moment into a dining-hall. Between the two halls ran a passage, and at the passage's end a staircase, upon the staircase a red carpet laid; what lay farther up is not clear, for the reports did not tell of it, and our inside man had never gone in—we knew only that the one who came every day without fail was Fu Yewen, secretary-general of the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters."

Not far from the "high foreign house" were a few newly built one-storey houses, which they all called the "flat foreign houses," put up new for the Japanese. Our men could not get in, and did not know how it was within. It was said that the "Shanghai Japanese Gendarmerie" had sent several men to "stay on" here for the long term, at their head a warrant officer, and under him a sergeant-major, a sergeant, and a corporal, four or five men in all. Their standing at "No. 76" was a very singular one—to call it "supreme and sovereign" would not do, and to call it "adviser" would not do either. Yet that it bore some tincture of "surveillance" may be taken for certain. For, say what one will, this pack at "No. 76" were still Chinese, and, for all their harping on "peace" and "friendship with Japan," the Japanese side could not but keep a hand in reserve. The Japanese gendarmes stationed at "No. 76" commonly wore uniform, at times changing into plain clothes, but ever carried a pistol upon them, for that at any moment they might go out to arrest "anti-Japanese elements."

To the right of the second gate was a great stretch of buildings, and this was the "black gaol," reviled of men and setting the hair on end. Of our working comrades of the "Juntong Bureau" in the several places of the Southeast, those who here suffered lawless tortures and drank the cup of misery to the dregs, to the point of maiming or even of the loss of life, mounted up over five or six years (the twenty-eighth to the thirty-fourth) to, though there is no reckoning of it, at the lowest estimate two or three hundred. The whole area of the "black gaol" took up about one hundred and fifty square meters, walled off into a dozen and more small rooms, each with a wooden railing and iron lock besides, a plank bed laid on the ground on which twenty and more might lie crosswise; reckoning it up, at a full "house" it could hold above four hundred. Within it one room was set apart to hold the "women prisoners" alone. In the gaol there was no sanitary provision; twice a day the doors were opened—of a morning, one was let out singly by turns for ten minutes to wash, and of an afternoon, once, into the open ground for an "airing." In the keeping of it lenience and rigor were unequal, and whether the warder were good or bad each man took his chance—that is, to meet a somewhat better one was to suffer somewhat less torment, and to meet a bad one was but to own one's ill luck. What galled a man shut in the gaol the most, over and above the anxiety of his own "case," was the eating and the drinking of daily life; for the gaol-fare was hard to get down, the same the world over, and, thirsting, to wish for a mouthful of water was as hard as to climb to blue Heaven. From the end of the twenty-eighth year on, there were among our comrades men shut up within the "black gaol," some already exposed in their identity and become "prisoners awaiting the axe," some, though they had come through the cruel torture, yet still struggling on. Here it is worth the lifting of the pen to note: those valiant comrades, heedless of hardship and peril, putting aside a thousand difficulties, furnished our organization with true and exact intelligence. This indeed is the highest reach of a man's showing in the work; we do not sternly require of a working man that, once fallen into the enemy's hand, he must with his death make his fidelity whole.

The many men shut in the gaol of "No. 76" were not all anti-Japanese elements, or men with ties to the Chongqing side; among them were the nine streams and the ten trades, every sort of man, of every calling; ask the reason of it, and it ran to such things as revenge for a private grudge, kidnapping for ransom by a gang, an affair of the heart, or the utterly inexplicable—every strangeness was there. Such conduct, of course, brought on a foul name, and was cursed by all the world.

Now let me speak of the cruel tortures that "No. 76" laid upon a man's body. By what the comrades who came back set forth, those they suffered were four kinds in all, and each kind had besides its several variations. One of them was the "electric torture," which was not often used—not that this pack yet kept some scrap of humanity, or bore any pity in them, but that their level of skill was too poor and their hand not practiced enough, so that it would often go wrong; once the current was put through, it was either too light or too heavy, and though it would leave a living, whole, good man dizzied, retching, trembling, in convulsions, yet it could not force a "deposition" out of him, and once the strength was raised, all at once he swooned away—and so it was not often used. The other three were: the "leather whip," the "tiger bench," and the "dousing with cold water."

This "leather whip" was not that thin, round riding-whip made with a fold of soft leather looped at the tip; to look at its shape, it should rather be called a "leather paddle." It was made of cowhide, as broad as three fingers put together, over two feet long, over two-tenths of an inch thick, soft with a hardness in it; laid on the body without force, at once a red weal rose up; and were it whipped down with a will full upon the back, in an instant there was a print of blood, and after two or three lashes the blood and flesh of the back would cleave to the clothing, so that, strip as one would, it would not come away. The man who wielded it was nice in his "handling" too: were it laid on flat, meeting the flesh over the whole face of it, the hurt was lighter and the pain the easier borne; but were the whip tilted a little to the slant, the part struck bearing the force unevenly, then it hurt the more fiercely, sank the deeper into the flesh, and mended with great difficulty; and should the man wielding it turn spiteful, one lash down and then a hard drag with it could truly tear a piece of skin away. This was the meanest sort of "leather whip," and yet it was already more than enough to bear.

Everything whose name carries the word "tiger" carries with it the sense of a certain fierceness, as with the "tiger clamp," the "tigress," and so on. The "tiger bench" was one of the instruments of torture, and once a man lay upon it, four words alone can tell of it: "to die and come back to life." The "tiger bench" was a long bench made of wood, of a size and breadth to take a man lying upon it, made for the tormenting of men and nothing else. In the working of the torture, a sound, good man was forced flat upon the bench and then bound, man and bench together, with hempen rope, and bound fast, so that he could not stir at all. Now the questioning would begin; and if he could not answer what they wished to ask, the man wielding the torture would set a brick under the part of the thick sinew beneath both heels and lever it up from the knee down. The bricks were the common red or grey, about two inches thick; think of it—a thing of such thickness forced in beneath the heels, while the knee-joint must be strained back and bent the other way—what a taste that was, more than a mere aching and soreness. Nor was that the end: if still he would not speak, then one more brick was added, up to three at the most, and it truly pierced a man to the heart's core, the sinews of the foot near to snapping; and by this pitch of it near any man had long since fainted away. Afterward, it would leave a man maimed, even lamed in his walk.

The "dousing with cold water" was, of course, a cruel torture. First a man was bound upon the "tiger bench," and then the bench, man and all, was upended head-down and feet-up, which of itself put a man out of his natural state and was hard enough to bear; but crueler still, they used a great iron kettle full of cold water and poured the water without cease into the nostrils. The kettle's spout was very small and the stream it poured very fine, and thus the water would be sent into the nostrils. A man of strong lung-power could at the first still hold his breath full and blow it out, but the moment he drew breath the water came in again; and at this the coughing and the choking, the chest swelling up—let it be understood: what greatness and depth of spirit and of enmity toward the foe it took to hold on through this, to hold out against it! And crueler yet, they would mix chili powder into the water, and the taste of that agony is harder still to imagine; so that there were men doused till they spat blood without cease upon the spot.

Of the several cruel tortures set out above, many of our comrades taken prisoner had all of them tasted, and some, come through it by chance alive, carried their maiming through the years after, and grind their teeth even now in the telling of it.

The puppet "Special Operations Headquarters" was from the first a lawless robbers' den, that looked on a man's life as on an ant's, and would kill off a few men without its ever weighing on their hearts. And yet this reckoning, soon or late, must be settled.

This pack held to no "legal procedure" at all; whom to kill, whom to hack, how long to shut up, whether to release or no, went by no fixed rule—one may say they did as they pleased—and those who wielded this great power were, first, Wang Jingwei and Zhou Fohai, and afterward it fell into the hands of Ding Mocun and Li Shiqun; that is, Li Shiqun and the rest in turn set Wang and Zhou up as figureheads. Ding and Li fell out and came to blows, Ding losing and Li winning, and for a stretch of time Li Shiqun held the great power in his single hand, and that for the longer while, right up until he was poisoned to death by the Japanese gendarmes. Li Shiqun's accomplices were, at first, such underworld figures as Wu Shibao (Yunfu) and Zhang Guozhen, and afterward Fu Yewen, Hu Junhe, Wan Lilang, and the like.

The "Special Operations Headquarters of the Chinese Kuomintang for the Rooting Out of Communists and the Saving of the Nation" was directly under Wang Jingwei, who named Zhou Fohai to be at the head of its affairs. Zhou invited Ding Mocun to aid him, and Li Shiqun offered his service to Ding. Once Li Shiqun had shouldered Ding Mocun out, he drew near to Zhou Fohai in turn; before long, Zhou Fohai was a tail too big to wag, and Li Shiqun at a single throw reached to the top, climbing up to Wang Jingwei. Once Li Shiqun had come to power over the "Special Operations Headquarters," at the recommendation of Li's wife Ye Jiqing, Fu Yewen was made "secretary-general," to "mind the house" for Li. It was learned only later that Fu Yewen was, of all things, a member of the Communist Party. Under the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters" were set up two sections and a number of office units, and besides these one guard corps directly under it. The First Section was Wan Lilang's, the Second Section Hu Junhe's; the commander of the guard corps was at first Wu Shibao, and afterward a certain Xiao (Wan's brother-in-law) and Mr. Wang Xianhe. The division of their business was, in the main, that the First Section was set against the "Juntong Bureau," the Second against the "Zhongtong Bureau"; and though on the face of it they set up as anti-Communist, in fact it was nothing of the sort. Later still it was found that the Second Section chief Hu Junhe too was a member of the Communist Party.

The puppet "Special Operations Headquarters" had its field units set up as well, which seemed to have somewhat of the mimic in them, an attempt to confound black with white and pass the false for the true. Besides its two or three "action squads," it had "districts, stations, and groups" in the two cities of Nanking and Shanghai and the three provinces of Chekiang, Kiangsu, and Anhwei. In the other fallen territories the Japanese military would not suffer them any activity.

The above is no more than a rough sketch of "No. 76"; and its many manifold evildoings are of a truth too many for the bamboo to record. Upon the facts, and to sum the whole, they may be gathered under three great crimes:

First: the selling out of the nation's interests, the open setting of themselves against the government, and, in the war of a nation resisting invasion, the willing playing of the cat's-paw to the enemy and the puppets.

Second: the flaunting on the surface of "rooting out Communists and saving the nation," while at the bottom holding secret converse with the Communist Party, standing out plainly as the broker of the supply of materials, the screen for the doings of rebellion.

Third: the looking upon the mass of the people as meat upon the block, the making of a trade of the snatching of power and the scrambling for gain, the gathering into one of every crime, and the grievous ruin of China's own ancient tradition of morality.

### Striking the Devils with the Force of a Thunderbolt

The "Shanghai District," upon the need laid on it from above, had its work of sanctioning the traitors already going forward in full fury.

From the ninth month of the twenty-eighth year to the twenty-fifth of the twelfth month of that same year, over and above those already set down before, the more important were these several cases of sanction:

Cheng Haitao, chief inspector of the French police (an aider of the enemy in its cruelty); Chen Mingchu, chief of the "First Section of the Special Operations Headquarters" of the puppets; He Xingjian, commander of the "Second Route of the Anti-Communist National-Salvation Army" of the puppets;

and, besides Zhao Gangyi, commander of the "Third Action Brigade of the Special Operations Headquarters" of the puppets, there were yet Wang Yongkui, Liu Yong, Li Ruzhang, Liu Jiqing, Liu Bonian, Yan Yunyu, Shao Fanjiu, Gu Keqin, and others, eight cases in all. These are all upon the record, made two years after the event, on the Japanese side; but as to how each case stood, the writer in truth can no longer call it to mind in detail.

In the third chapter above, I made brief mention: "The particulars of the sanction of Yu Yefeng will have a separate record in the next chapter." Now let me tell it again from the beginning.

Yu Yefeng had long been marked in a case: before the writer had come to Shanghai to take up the post of District chief, the Bureau headquarters had already laid a sanction order upon him, and had once, on the twenty-fourth of the sixth month of the twenty-seventh year, carried it out in Room 407 of the Xinxin Hotel in the International Settlement—only, it did not accomplish the task, and Yu Yefeng came off safe and whole, having taken no more than a fright.

Upon this matter, page sixty-four of the first volume of Mr. Wan Molin's "Bygone Days at Shanghai" writes: "Yu Yefeng went every day to the Gengxin Stage to give his applause to Xin Yanqiu, and so gave the men of our action side a fine chance to lay hands on him. One day, Yu Yefeng was sitting in a box on the upper floor of the Gengxin Stage, in the company of Wu Laopu, a famous amateur of the Jiuji club and a singer of the painted-face roles, beating time in his delight, listening to Xin Yanqiu's opera. All at once the sudden crack of a submachine gun rose up; Yu Yefeng, quick-witted, fell forward on the instant, sprawling upon the floor, and so dodged that shower of bullets like rain. But Wu Laopu could not get away in time, his whole body shot through with not a few clear holes, and he fell of a sudden upon Yu Yefeng's body, the blood pouring out there and then, and gave up the ghost upon the spot."

Mr. Wan did not note the date, and the place is wrong too—what Mr. Wan tells of was in a box upon the upper floor of the Gengxin Stage; but that other record befell in Room 407 of the Xinxin Hotel, at the time the twenty-fourth of the sixth month of the twenty-seventh year, so that surely the two do not point to a single event. Yet two things may be held for certain. First: we of the "Shanghai District" never used a submachine gun to sanction a traitor, for it was too great in bulk and inconvenient to carry. Second: we have no record of ever having failed in the sanction of Yu Yefeng and killed Wu Laopu by mischance. That is to say, we would never have ordered our action men to carry a submachine gun into a box upon the upper floor of a playhouse and blaze away at random with a shower of bullets like rain. The common saying has it that a bullet has no eyes; would we, then, take no thought for the other spectators there "beating time in their delight"? By this it may be seen that this action at the Gengxin Stage was, if not a thing Mr. Wan misremembered, then not one that pointed at us, the "Shanghai District of the Juntong Bureau," at all.

But on page fifty-five of the first volume of "Bygone Days at Shanghai" there is again a passage that says: "Yu Yefeng did indeed die in the front row of the upper floor of the Gengxin Stage, where Xin Yanqiu was singing, and moreover he was, twice running, shot at by the men of our underground action work, led in person by Chen Mo." Looking at these two sentences, it plainly points at us, for comrade Chen Mo was one of the three cell leaders belonging to the "Second Action Brigade of the Shanghai District"; and moreover, of those doing action work at Shanghai in that day there was but the one unit of the "Shanghai District." That being so, then it must be that Mr. Wan misremembered. It is many years since, and any man may err; I do but append the mention here.

In the sixth month of the twenty-seventh year, the chief of the "Shanghai District" was Mr. Zhou Weilong, and the man in charge of the action work was Mr. Zhao Lijun, chief of the "Action Corps." Under the "Action Corps" was the "Second Action Brigade," its commander comrade Ji Zhencang, who went by the assumed name of Zhao Sheng. Under the "Second Action Brigade" were three detachments, which by our habit we called also cells; comrade Chen Mo had been a detachment leader, and was at times called too a cell leader. The shooting affair that befell at the Xinxin Hotel on the twenty-fourth of the sixth month of the twenty-seventh year may have been directed by Brother Zhao Lijun and carried out by the Second Brigade; but the original file was long ago destroyed, and its particulars are hard now to trace.

Yu Yefeng, a man of Hangchow in Chekiang, was sixty-one the year he died. His standing in Shanghai's "Chinese society," parasite though he was upon the "foreigners' Concession," ranked below the three men Huang, Du, and Zhang. When the war broke out, the three magnates parted ways; Zhang Xiaolin took the Japanese road, and Yu Yefeng followed after, having served as chairman of the puppet "General Chamber of Commerce" and got up the "New Asia Peace Promotion Association," buying up military stores for the Japanese army—a thing more to the purpose, indeed, than those who did but wave the flag and shout, singing an empty song of peace.

As to the second sanction of Yu Yefeng, the Second Brigade's commander Zhao Sheng had beforehand made a report of record. And sure enough, on the night of the fifteenth of the first month of the twenty-ninth year, past eight o'clock, at the Gengxin Stage on Newchwang Road in the International Settlement, Yu Yefeng was struck down grievously wounded in the balcony boxes, and, taken to the Renji Hospital, died there past all cure.

Afterward the Second Brigade of course made a full report, and the writer of course read it over with care; but as to what it held, I can recall only the general drift—that is, just such as has been set down above.

It is well that there are two pieces of material that may serve for corroboration, which I excerpt here severally as follows. Pages sixty-eight to sixty-nine of the first volume of "Bygone Days at Shanghai," written by Mr. Wan Molin, have this:

"On the fourteenth of the first month of the twenty-ninth year of the Republic, a Sunday, Chen Mo came again, and after we had talked over several matters, he said of set purpose, in a leisurely way, 'That parcel of goods, Yu Yefeng—it's about to be closed.' Hearing him, I . . . raised my head and asked him a word: 'What hour?' Chen Mo looked at me a while, and only then answered: 'If not this very day, then tomorrow.' (Four lines omitted.)

"From the night of the fourteenth to the early morning of the fifteenth, nothing stirred. On the night of the fifteenth, Chen Mo telephoned, and told me how the sanction of Yu Yefeng had gone: that action he had led in person, and the comrade who carried it out sat in the very seat next to Yu Yefeng, in the front row of the upper floor of the Gengxin Stage. Upon the stage Xin Yanqiu was singing 'Yutangchun,' the whole house rapt in the opera, not a sound to be heard. Yu Yefeng, more than the rest, had narrowed his eyes and was listening to the lingering song of his beloved; the comrade charged with it, his two hands folded upon his breast, drew out in secret the pistol hidden beneath his arm, took aim at the region of Yu Yefeng's heart, and with a crack fired a single shot. Upon the crack of the gun there was uproar above the stage and below; but the police and the plain-clothes detectives present looked on still with folded arms. Chen Mo and his brothers then mingled with the throng and withdrew at their ease, leaving toppled in the seat the corpse of that Yu Yefeng."

This passage is of a truth drawn with vividness and spirit.

By the great kindness of Mr. Wang Zhenhu, director of the Central Library, who spared no trouble, there was sent me a piece of news bearing on the case of Yu Yefeng, from page seven of the Shanghai Shenbao of the sixteenth of the first month of the twenty-ninth year of the Republic (a photostat)—come to hand this day, it is a thing most precious indeed. I copy it out here just as it stands; and though the news has become a story of the past, it loses none of its sense of the real for that:

"Bloody Affair Last Night at the Gengxin Stage; Yu Yefeng Falls to a Bullet.

"Last night at forty-five minutes past eight, upon the upper floor of the Gengxin Stage on Newchwang Road in the International Settlement, just as the evening play was coming on and the opera-loving ladies were thronging in one upon another, there suddenly befell a shooting affair. The man shot was one Yu Yefeng, a native of Chekiang, struck by two bullets, and, the wounds being grave, died at the Renji Hospital; a stray bullet wounded a local man, Zhang Xuebing. The particulars are here set down severally as follows:

"Yu Yefeng, a native of Hangchow, sixty-one years of age, had in the early years of the Republic served as commander of the water-borne anti-smuggling force at Kashing, and, laying down the post soon after, came to Shanghai to live as a man of leisure. He loved to hear the pihuang opera, and so the famous players of North and South were all glad to keep his acquaintance. At times he would write pieces upon the theatrical arts to pass the hours; and because he had stayed many years at Shanghai, those in every walk who knew him were not a few. In late years he had opened besides the Lin-ji timber yard on Avenue Edward VII, and held as well the post of manager of the Great Wall Gramophone Company. His friend Wang Dazhang is now manager of the Gengxin Stage; and because Yu was a lover of the Peking opera, and the Gengxin owed much to Yu's help in engaging the famous players of Beiping and Tianjin, he was often in and out of that theater.

"Last night, past eight o'clock, Yu Yefeng, the Gengxin Stage having newly engaged the actress Xin Yanqiu, come to Shanghai to show her art upon the red carpet, and the opera-lovers of the seaboard gathering all at once, went thither too in high good humor; before the gate the carriages ran like water, a great press of the hour, and just as the bright lamps dazzled the eye and the gongs and drums thundered, and upon the stage they were playing the piece of 'A Snowy Night, the Straying at Xiaoshang River, the Passing of Zaixing,' there suddenly rang out a gunshot.

"Yu's seat was at the mouth of the manager's room in the passage of the balcony boxes on the second floor, and at the time he had his eyes fixed on the stage; behind him the gunshots suddenly rose, the bullets like a string of beads, and Yu's chest was struck by one bullet, his left shoulder too struck by one. Beside him was Zhang Xuebing, a rehearsal hand of that theater, likewise struck in the right leg by a stray bullet. The seated guests of the whole house at first did not yet grasp the truth of it, but when Yu and Zhang, struck, ran with blood, the four quarters were startled; the theater's manager's room made hasty report to the police station, detectives were sent to the spot, and Yu and Zhang were carried to the Renji Hospital for treatment.

"Yu, being past his sixtieth year and his strength already worn, though, on reaching the hospital, urgent aid and cure were given him, yet, the bullet having struck a vital part, fell straightway into a swoon; his concubine and his son Yunjiu and the rest, hearing the alarm, drove at once to the hospital, but by then Yu could no longer speak, and, holding on to fifty-five minutes past nine, he gave up the ghost and died, to be laid in the coffin after report had been made to the court and the body examined.

"After the affair, the opera-loving ladies, having come in high spirits, had meant to hear the famed piece of Xin Yanqiu, 'The Tale of the Red Whisk-Bearer,' returned to the stage after long absence; but, the thing having befallen so abruptly, for caution's sake the playing was stopped there and then, so that the seated guests went off one after another in their disappointment; and when the police and the detectives had reached the theater, they searched high and low, but the assassin had already darted away into the throng.

"A year before this, he had escaped a peril and come off whole. Yu dwelt at No. 10 Yi'an Li, Lane 500, Avenue du Roi Albert, in the French Concession, kept a motorcar, and left behind one son, named Yunjiu, who had studied in Japan, and is now deputy headmaster of the Hongyi Middle School on Gordon Road and Bubbling Well Road. At the time of the affair Yu Yunjiu too was in the audience. Now, Yu had on the twenty-fourth of the sixth month of the twenty-seventh year, when the shooting affair befell at Room 407 of the Xinxin Hotel, been present likewise, and had by good fortune escaped the peril. This time he met the shooting again, and from the last peril it was about a year and a half."

Having read what "Bygone Days at Shanghai" wrote, and then read what the Shanghai "Shenbao" set forth on the very day of the affair, and set the two side by side, one finds that, though there are some discrepancies within them, yet, as to the case entire, one may in the main come to an understanding of it.

First, the writer would make a supplementary word: in the third chapter of this book, the date of Yu Yefeng's being struck is given as the fourteenth of the first month of the twenty-ninth year, whereas both "Bygone Days at Shanghai" and the "Shenbao" set it down clearly as the fifteenth of the first month; so that it would seem it ought to be altered to the fifteenth of the first month. Yet the reason it is set down as the fourteenth of the first month is that this follows the date recorded upon the "Statistical Table of Cases Committed by the Blue Shirt Society at Shanghai," published on page five of the Shanghai "Zhonghua Ribao" of the twenty-eighth of the eleventh month of the thirtieth year; and so I set them here side by side. As for our own Bureau, there is no longer any record of the case.

Having brought the record of this case to this point, what follows is all by way of a coda; the question is not one of disputing the true against the false, nor of arguing the right against the wrong, but is meant to take this for an instance, and to point out how hard it is either to read the news or to read history.

For example, "Bygone Days at Shanghai" says that Chen Mo, over the telephone, held forth at length upon the course of his action work. Were one to read the vivid passage described there in the spirit of enjoying a novel, that would of course be nothing to cavil at; but suppose one takes it from the standpoint of weighing intelligence, and makes a reasoned inference upon it—then it is full of holes, and hard to be believed. Chen Mo, too, was a man of the Du household, "one of their own" with Mr. Wan, that I believe; but Chen Mo was one who had been trained, and an old comrade besides, and he ought to have known the weight of keeping a secret close—how could he, over the telephone, hold forth so freely upon the matter of his work?

Then, setting "Bygone Days at Shanghai" beside the Shanghai "Shenbao" and looking at them together, one finds too many points where the two are at odds, such as:

"Bygone Days at Shanghai": one shot rang out.

The Shanghai "Shenbao": the bullets like a string of beads.

"Bygone Days at Shanghai": there were police and detectives present, looking on with folded arms.

The Shanghai "Shenbao": the detectives, getting word, arrived, and searched high and low.

"Bygone Days at Shanghai": died upon the spot in his seat.

The Shanghai "Shenbao": grievously wounded, taken to hospital, and died past cure. So seen, whom is one to believe in the right? This is hard indeed; yet the two have one point in common, and that is the proof that—the gun did sound, and no bullet went wide; the police and detectives seized no man, the men of high purpose had withdrawn at their ease; and Yu, in the end, was brought to justice.

Might there be, then, yet a third case of things? In this very affair there is a most curious instance in point:

"Bygone Days at Shanghai" says: "Upon the stage Xin Yanqiu was singing Yutangchun." The Shanghai "Shenbao" records: "Upon the stage they were playing 'A Snowy Night, the Straying at Xiaoshang River,' the passing of Yang Zaixing . . ."

And in the "Minzu Wanbao," in the "Great Wide World" page's column "Idle Gleanings," I have read a piece by Mr. Suibo entitled "Rooting Out Traitors and Killing the Foe: An Answer to Readers," whose last paragraph writes: "On the first night that Xin Yanqiu took the stage at the Gengxin Stage to open her run, I invited my old neighbor Brother Yuan Zixun, who happened to have come from Shansi to Shanghai on business, to be my fellow guest in the seats; and because the good seats down in front had all been booked up by her claque, we could but take the next best, and bought seats in the middle of the upper floor, second row. Just as the closing turn, Liang Huichao's 'Tiaohuache,' had come to the point where Gao Chong, hoisting the last of the carts in his run of hoisting, could no longer bear the strain and his horse stumbled, of a sudden there was heard behind the seats the sound of a gunshot; I saw a few wisps of light smoke rise up, and, hastily crouching down under the seat to dodge the stray bullets. Upon the stage Gao Chong had already darted into the wings and the gongs had stopped. Only after a spell of alarm and confusion did Xin Yanqiu, in her paint and powder, come on to go on with the play. By the next day's news report I learned that it was Yu Yefeng who had met the bullet and died—this is a fact I saw with my own eyes in the season of rooting out traitors at Shanghai, and I would that those who write the history hereafter may have wherewith to set it right."

This is the third case of things: when we see that in the one place, at the one time, what was played upon the stage was three utterly different operas—Yutangchun, Xiaoshang River, Tiaohuache—one can but laugh without a sound. And in fact there is no such thing possible; were one to make a choice, one ought of course to take Mr. Suibo's record for the standard, for it was seen with his own eyes, with proof and warrant, and he was besides a man who knew the opera, and so of course set it down clearly. The common saying has it that what is heard by the ear is empty, and what is seen by the eye is real; and this is no false word.

News and history hold their worth in being real; one may say too that only what reports the real is news, only what records the real counts as history. But as to how one may reach in fullness to the realm of the real, that is hard indeed; a word passed three times over goes all awry, and how much more the tangled interplay of the three—of person, of time, and of place! To speak plainly, to be able to draw closely to the real, to have seven or eight parts in ten of it, is already very well. (Here ends the fourth chapter; to be continued in the next issue.)

In the early days of the war of resistance, the "Juntong Bureau," under the leadership of Mr. Dai Yunong, once made a systematic and thoroughgoing review of the whole of its work. The summary report pointed out that the shortcomings of this period lay in: meager pay, straitened funds, a low political level, an unclear grasp of things, a want of instructors and of teaching materials, a generally low level of science and technology, being pushed aside within other organs, and every sort of basic work falling short of the ideal. Mr. Dai himself made his own self-examination too, and by what the writer knows, this was the first time in his life, and the only time. He owned of himself: "I am strong of character and heavy in my own opinions; the views of high and low cannot be wholly brought into accord; and in planning and oversight I have not been able to do all things well." And there was this besides: "For want of aides, whether in the handling of documents, the receiving of guests, or the meeting of daily affairs, I have had in all to take it upon myself in person, so that time has fallen short and many things have been delayed." Take the facts for proof, and what he said was all true.

The straitness of the "Juntong Bureau's" funds reflected the hardship of the national finances; at the same time it is not hard to see, too, that the "secret-service work" of this age had not yet received a fair regard from high and low, in the government and out of it. It is recorded in the materials that the whole of the "Juntong Bureau's" funds in the twenty-ninth year came to but 510,000-odd yuan of fabi a month; while the whole of its complement, taking in inner and outer service, and taking in the units public and secret, great and small, of the great rear, the war zones, and the fallen zones, was about 11,000-odd men and names in all. Think of it—what was the average to each man? And there were, besides, outlays running to several tens of heads. Yet for all that, Mr. Dai would by no means suffer his subordinates to raise funds of their own, and still less would he take a donation from any quarter whatsoever.

In the midst of the hardship of funds, he yet sanctioned the Shanghai District's plan for the expansion of action, and ordered arms to be bought on the spot. This move, again, was to add one more burden to the Bureau. And just at this juncture, a certain friend of the writer's, a good comrade trained in the same class, out of the bond of friendship generously made a gift of a batch of firearms and ten thousand rounds of ammunition; I understood full well that the strong-willed Mr. Dai would surely not take them, but, under the double reason of friendship and of need, I took it upon my own shoulders and, making bold, accepted them.

The former regularly furnished us with intelligence; while that "friend" of unclear background—his intelligence came to be the more prized by our superiors, who made many an inquiry after it. Besides answering truthfully according to the facts, I feared to stir up the earlier matter and let a fresh branch grow from the knot, and so kept his name hidden from those above—which again came near to a bold and willful acting on my own judgment. At the first, we had once suspected he was a member of the Communist Party; now, judging by the materials, he does not seem to be one after all, and so, in a weave of reason and of feeling, I am still scouting closely into the truth of him.
