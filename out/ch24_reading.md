## The Collusion and Rivalry of Shanghai's Three Big Bosses

{d} By Shi Jun

Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, and Zhang Xiaolin were known as the "three big bosses" among the gang hooligans of old Shanghai. Being birds of a feather, they colluded with one another; and because their interests clashed, they were forever at odds. Here, so far as the writer knows it, is a sketch of the whole.

### I. The Beginning of Their Coming Together

As a boy Huang Jinrong loafed about the Shiliupu district of Nanshi, and a crowd of ruffians from the docks and the brothel quarter, taken with his readiness for a fight, attached themselves to him. Among them were Hangzhou A-fa, Rice-Bucket A-San, Heaven-Storming Xu Fusheng, Coolie Liansheng, Qu Longlong of Hongzhen, and, from the British Concession, Ji Yunqing, Ma Siqian, Yang Shunquan, and others. Huang had once gone into the walled town to learn a trade at a picture-mounting shop, but being used to idling he soon drifted back to knocking about Shiliupu.

Shiliupu adjoined the French Concession, and the French police, finding that these ruffians were forever making disturbances along the boundary and upsetting order in the concession, called Huang Jinrong in to serve as a constable. He joined the force and put on the uniform; but the first day he went back to Shiliupu, some of the ruffians, catching sight of him, said he had become the police's running dog, come to lord it over his own brothers. He felt somewhat crestfallen, and the next day handed the uniform back to the French police. The inspector had a sudden inspiration on the spot: letting Huang go without the uniform, he reckoned, would serve just as well to keep this pack of little ruffians in check, and so he allowed Huang to work in plain clothes as a detective — a "baodating," in the slang, an ear-to-the-ground man. In this way Huang Jinrong found his footing in the French Concession police and rose to be a Chinese detective-head.

Once he was a baodating, Huang Jinrong felt he needed a base where his ruffian brothers could gather, and so at the Great Chiming Clock in the French Concession (today the site just west of the Huangpu District public-security substation on East Jinling Road) he opened the Jubaolou teahouse, and later a bathhouse, the Rixinchi, in the lane next door, turning the teahouse by night into a storytelling house for tanhuang opera. It was then that Du Yuesheng, introduced by Rice-Bucket A-San, became Huang's "crab-leg" — also called a "sanguang," a claw-and-fang and errand-runner. All this was in the last years of the Qing.

It was only after the 1911 Revolution that Zhang Xiaolin came to serve Huang Jinrong as a "sanguang." In the early years of the Republic, when a feud broke out among the Shanghai gangs, Ji Yunqing left Shanghai to lie low in Hangzhou. Zhang Xiaolin was then working the market fairs of the Hangzhou-Jiaxing-Huzhou country, gathering men to gamble, and Ji Yunqing, reaching Hangzhou, threw in with him. When the trouble had blown over, Ji went back to Shanghai and spoke of Zhang Xiaolin to Huang Jinrong, saying he had made something of a name in the Hangzhou-Jiaxing-Huzhou region; and Huang agreed to send for Zhang to serve as a "sanguang." This was the beginning of the coming-together of the three men, Huang, Du, and Zhang.

### II. Huang Jinrong Becomes a Notable of Both Concessions

After Huang, Du, and Zhang had joined forces, with the tacit leave of the French police chief Fiori and his senior officer Sa Weini, they set ruffians to running gambling dens and dealing opium in the French Concession, sitting back to split the takings. About this time a jailbreak-and-murder case arose in the British Concession. A man known as Guangdong Guilin, jailed in the Western Gaol for a crime, broke out together with one called Fusheng. The moment Guangdong Guilin got out he went to Hongzhen to see Qu Longlong, who kept a gambling table there, and Qu gave him a hundred dollars. With the money he went to bathe at the Wulongchi, first buying clothes at the Deshengchang, shoes at the Yuxinglong, and cloth stockings at the Hongmaochang — all famous Shanghai establishments of the day. This caught the eye of a British Concession plainclothesman (also called an "anchai"), who found something odd and out of place about the man and began to watch him. The British Concession had just then begun to employ plainclothes men, some of whom had themselves done time in the Western Gaol or served as its warders; and these, finding Guangdong Guilin's face somewhat familiar, moved to haul him into the station. Guangdong Guilin opened by asking them to "give him a break"; when the detectives refused, he drew a knife and wounded one of them. The British Concession police, mortified that their new plainclothes branch should stumble at the very outset — which would tell against their hiring of henchmen thereafter — asked the French police to help crack the case. The task fell to Huang Jinrong, who at once sent out some of his "sanguang" to hunt for leads and at last traced Fusheng, the man who had broken out with Guangdong Guilin; he contrived to bring him in and paid him three dollars a day to find Guangdong Guilin — three dollars a day being, in those times, an extraordinary reward. Before long Fusheng found Guangdong Guilin, and Huang Jinrong had thus rendered a great service. With the killer netted, Huang Jinrong became not only the darling of the French police but, from then on, a man valued by the British Concession police as well, and so a notable of both Shanghai concessions.

When the First World War came and some of the French working in the French Concession were called home to serve, leaving the police short of hawks and hounds, the French Consul reshuffled the force and gave Huang Jinrong the title of chief inspector; and Huang's friends Cheng Ziqing, Jin Jiulin, and others took inspectorships in the two sections, political and criminal-investigation. When there were dealings with Chinese officials and gentry, Huang was sometimes sent to make the contact; and such men as the Nanshi gentry-director Mao Zijian, the Shanghai Commissioner of Foreign Affairs Xu Yuan, and Liu Chunpu, chief of the judicial section of the police bureau, all became honored guests of the Huang house. When the European war ended, the French imperialists even gave Huang a medal.

### III. Clearing the Docks, Making Fortunes in Drugs

The imperialists, in their invasion of China, made the selling of opium one of their chief instruments, and Huang Jinrong naturally wanted a taste of this fat morsel. At that time the men who held the opium trade in the concessions were Guo Haishan, Shen Xingshan, and others; so Huang, with Du Yuesheng and Zhang Xiaolin, drove to the company rooms opened by Guo Haishan, Shen Xingshan, and Xie Baosheng at the Great Eastern Hotel in the British Concession, to "talk terms" (that is, to negotiate) with Guo and the rest. Stepping out of the lift, Huang ran straight into Shen Xingshan; and when Shen, catching sight of him, made to greet him, Huang, without a word, threw a sudden punch and knocked Shen flat on the spot. Guo Haishan came out at the noise, and seeing Huang and his men drawn up in full array, said: "Very well, Boss Jinrong, you go on home! Come to the Rixinchi tomorrow at two in the afternoon and I'll call on you!" Huang Jinrong answered: "Good — that's settled in a word!" The next afternoon Guo Haishan came to the Rixinchi single-handed, and when the two sides met they made their bows and sat down. Guo opened at once: "From now on, we go eighty-twenty." Then he added a further word: "Eighty-twenty in both the British and the French concession alike." As he was leaving, Guo said: "I've brought nothing to give you, so let me make you a present of my motorcar — and the driver will come over to wait on you as well." Huang Jinrong thereupon thanked him.

From then on Huang Jinrong had the opium and the money, and with money came power; he bought a house at Longmenkou, enlarged the Rixinchi (on the old site of today's Dazhong Theater), and ran the Gongwutai playhouse. Later, when Guo Haishan went back to his native Guanyun in northern Jiangsu, the opium trade passed into Huang's sole hands.

After this, foreign warships brought in opium in ever greater quantities. Under the protection of Fiori and the rest, Huang Jinrong formally set up a company, sold openly, and opened gambling houses. Of the profits, a portion was handed over to Fiori and the other chiefs of the French police, and even the French Consul in Shanghai and the officers of the French warships came in for a cut; when French fleet commanders visited China, the cost of entertaining such men as Admiral Mailan was likewise charged to the opium takings. In those days the district around Dongxinqiao, Zhengjia Wooden Bridge, and Baxianqiao in the French Concession became a teeming market of opium, gambling, and prostitution, joined in one with the opium-and-gambling dens of the neighboring British Concession, and for decades it was the imperialists' great sink of vice in Shanghai. These dens were opened and enlarged without cease; the most famous of the gambling houses were at Shengji Li on Xizang Road and at No. 181 Avenue Foch (today's Yan'an Middle Road).

### IV. Struggling for Power and Profit, Strife Within and Without

The heads of this opium-selling "Sanxin Company" were, in name, Du Yuesheng and Zhang Xiaolin; but it was in fact Du who ran it, with Huang Jinrong as the backer behind the scenes — though the biggest real boss of all was still Fiori. By this Du Yuesheng rose meteorically, his capable lieutenants being Gu Jiatang, Jin Tingsun, Ma Xiangsheng, Ye Zhuosan, and Ji Yunqing's disciple Little Ah Rong, among others. By now Du was often in touch with Fiori and with Fu's interpreter Li Yingsheng, and had become one of the "big bosses" of the French Concession, so that at times even Huang Jinrong had to defer to him, particularly where the drawing of the company's money was concerned.

In this Huang was thoroughly dissatisfied with Du; but the situation of a tail grown too big to wag had already formed, and there was nothing Huang could do. At the same time, because Du held all the power in the company, Zhang Xiaolin was no less dissatisfied — though he laid the blame on Huang for not giving him the same power as Du, and was out of humor with Huang too. On the other side, Du Yuesheng found Huang's constant demands for money hard to handle; and so a three-cornered friction arose among them. Added to this, the French police chiefs, high and low, Chinese and foreign, resented Huang and the company over the uneven division of the spoils, which tangled the relations further; and so all these currents met and swelled into a tide against Huang. Du and Zhang even slandered Huang Jinrong as no better than a "father-in-law who rakes the ashes" (meaning Huang's improper relations with his daughter-in-law), until the scandal was the talk of the town. Fiori, the boss behind the scenes, hard put to cope, thought of urging Huang Jinrong to step down so as to ease the strife. Huang, feeling then beset on every side, had no choice but to wire Ji Yunqing — who was off "beating the surround" around Harbin (gang cant for working the outside ports) — urging him back to Shanghai to help retrieve the wreck.

Just at this juncture came the Sun Meiyao train-hijacking at Lincheng in Shandong, and among the passengers carried off into the hills were the French Father Yao and the Italian lawyer Mu Ansu; through Huang's connection with Gao Shikui — a Green Gang elder of the "Da" generation, of Shandong mounted-bandit stock, and Yang Shunquan's "old man" — they were got out and freed. At this the imperialist adventurers in Shanghai again reckoned Huang had done them a service. Meanwhile Ji Yunqing had hurried back to Shanghai and talked Du and Zhang round, urging them to prize brotherhood, and telling them that the affair of the bedchamber was a murky thing, hard to make out, and not to be blabbed of at will. This at last steadied Huang's position in the police, and the storm among the three of them subsided for a time.

### V. The Contest Goes On, Recruiting Disciples Wholesale

The storm above having died down for the moment, the relations among them, though on the surface still a trinity, were in truth a fierce covert struggle. Du, among Huang's hangers-on and lackeys, worked constantly to sow division, meaning to leave Huang isolated. Huang for his part gathered a band of his own: the hooligans Zhu Jinfang and Cai Hongsheng, who after the closing of the gambling tables on Sixth Avenue in the British Concession had looked with envy on the opium and gambling of the French Concession; and he drew in as well certain old disciples — Tang Jiapeng, Chen Zegao, Xu Fubao, and the like — who had had nothing to do with the French Concession's opium and gambling but had some name among the hooligans and coveted that opium and gambling too, all to swell his following. At the same time both Huang and Du took in disciples and lackeys from the business and journalistic circles.

I was then a reporter at the Xinwenbao, and I joined the Huang gate. At Huang's instance, Tang Jiapeng, Chen Zegao, Qiu Zijia, Zhu Jinfang, Yuan Sanbao, and I swore brotherhood at Suzhou. There followed into the Huang gate Fang Juying, Jiang Jianhou, Jiang Zongyi, and Xu Chihen of the Xinwenbao; Xu Chengxu of the Shen Bao; Jiang Hongjiao of the Minguo Ribao; Hu Hanzhu of the Shishi Xinbao; Shi Jiqun of the tabloid Jingangzuan; Yu Daxiong of The Crystal; Wu Weiyu of Fu'ermosi; and Huang Guangyi and Feng Mengyun of the Xiao Ribao. Those who joined the Du gate were Kang Tongyi, Tang Shichang, and Zhao Junhao of the Shen Bao, and Chen Dazai of the Xinwenbao. Joining the Zhang gate were Yu Zhewen of the Xinwenbao (who later went over to the Du gate) and the tabloid man Huang Zhuantao. By comparison the Huang gate carried the greater weight, the tabloids especially standing wholly on Huang's side. Huang thereupon set the press to attacking Du. Du Yuesheng, a man of some cunning who knew the influence of the newspapers, no sooner saw the attacks begin than he made a show of throwing in the towel. In those days I used to attack Du Yuesheng's opium-and-gambling business in the press on Huang's behalf, in a planned way, winning Huang a victory in both prestige and money; and I got my own share of the good of it, the money Huang drew from Du being parceled out by me among the people concerned. Not until the time of the War of Resistance did I stand aside from it all.

In those days we formed a clique that we named the "Huang Nine Gentlemen," a byname for the "company room" that I and Huang Guangyi, Shi Jiqun, Jiang Hongjiao, Wu Weiyu, Feng Mengyun, Jiang Jianhou, and Xu Chihen shared at the Xin Huizhong Hotel. The room had first been taken as a place to rest by day, so that ordinary pressmen and others besides — Wu Tianweng, Chen Zegao, Yan Yinwu, and the like — were regulars there. As time went on, people outside were forever asking favors in the placing of news, and afterward there were gifts in return.

Meanwhile Du Yuesheng opened the Dafa Company near the New City God Temple and issued lottery tickets in the French Concession, put in the charge of three men, Zhu Jinfang, Yuan Sanbao, and Tang Jiapeng, the working staff being old hands of the Liji Lottery Firm run in earlier years by Huang Chujiu and Wang Hanxi; the draws were held at set times, and they swindled untold thousands. That the lottery could be floated at all was wholly the fruit of Du Yuesheng's running about to square things, high and low, in the French Concession, while Huang Jinrong sat back and reaped the benefit. This was another victory Huang won. Had Du not done so, Huang's men Zhu Jinfang, Tang Jiapeng, and the rest would have hijacked the opium out in the Huangpu River.

The opium handled by the company Du Yuesheng managed was of three sorts: "high-grade" goods, "north-pass" goods, and "foreign" goods. The high-grade goods came down from Yichang on the upper Yangtze and were taken off at the Longmao wharf, those brought from Hankou at the Daban wharf; the north-pass goods were taken off at the Ewo and Jinliyuan wharves, and the foreign goods on the East Beach at Gaoqiao. The steamers that carried this opium had specially built hidden compartments in the coal bunkers, and it was delivered on presentation of a chit and under armed guard. One spring, in 1931, Shao Wenbin — who, like Du Yuesheng, was of Chen Shichang's gate — went and did a thing that Huang's own champion Tang Jiapeng and the others had once wanted to do but had not dared. Commanding the Salt Administration's anti-smuggling gunboat, he intercepted Du's company's opium in the river off the Jinliyuan wharf. This consignment was under the armed protection of the Garrison Command, arranged by Gu Fukang (that is, Xiao Fujiang of the Eight-Gang); and after a gunfight in the river with another group guarding opium under the Wusong Fortress Command, the police water-patrol stepped in and unloaded the opium first at the Guanqiao at Shiliupu; then, through the overnight mediation of the police commissioner Chen Xizeng, it was settled that opium should thereafter be taken off by turns, a week at a time, among the three parties — the Salt Administration, the Garrison Command, and the Wusong Fortress Command — at a handling fee of forty-eight dollars a load (a hundred catties). This affair looked on the face of it like a quarrel between Shao and Du, but was in fact one more instance of the covert struggle between Huang and Du. Beforehand, Shao had gone to Huang's house at Junpeili, saying that "Ah-Sheng (that is, Du) looks down on people," meaning to have Huang mediate; little did he know that Huang's intimates, and Huang himself, would roundly curse Du Yuesheng as ungrateful, deep-laid, and sinister — and a few days later the thing above came to pass.

The above is a sample, so far as I know it, of the collusion and covert struggle among Huang, Du, and Zhang. Zhang was killed as a traitor during the War of Resistance, Du died in Hong Kong in 1951, and Huang in Shanghai in 1953; only then, one after another, did the entanglements among them come to an end.
