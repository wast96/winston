Chapter 10. Victory in the First Battle
In the late autumn of 1931, the Central Committee's underground courier line came to take Comrade He Chang out of Shanghai to the Jiangxi Soviet. In the days before he left, he would slip away every week to see me and ask how the work of rescuing Comrade Guan Xiangying was coming along; and whenever there was any progress I found a way to let him know at once. On the eve of his departure, just as he had come to say his goodbyes, Chen Zhigao happened to call on me as well. Up in my room I heard the horn and the engine of his sedan and knew at once that it was him. I told He Chang to duck into the bathroom next door for the moment, and I lay down on the bed with my clothes on, pretending to be resting. Chen Zhigao parked, came upstairs, and the moment he stepped in and saw me lying there he asked what was the matter. He had come to invite me to the pictures; the Grand Theatre, he said, was showing some very good film or other. I told him my old headache had flared up again and I could not go today. Hearing that I had a headache, he scolded me fondly: "I keep telling you not to smoke, and still you smoke; look, isn't this headache from smoking too much!" In truth I did not smoke heavily; but since he did not smoke himself he was quick to object whenever he saw me light one, though he was very fond of a drink. He asked at once whether he should buy me some medicine. I said, "I still have some of the medicine I keep by me; a little rest and I'll be fine. I'm truly sorry I can't come to the film with you today." Chen Zhigao was always courteous with me and never pressed me to do anything, so he said, "Never mind; I'll go with my brothers and sisters, then," and with that he took his leave.
After Chen Zhigao had gone, He Chang came out of the bathroom next door. I said to him, "Something big has just happened; I wonder if you've heard?" He said he had already heard the gist of it, but he wanted me to tell him the whole thing in detail.
I told him that a few days earlier Chen Zhigao had asked me out for coffee at the Donghua Café and had suggested that after dinner we go together to the Grand Theatre; he had already bought the tickets and meant to bring his sisters along too. I had already sensed that Chen Zhigao was deliberately trying to throw me together with his sisters, and so as not to interfere with their studies he always bought tickets for Saturday evenings. That afternoon, at about four o'clock, as the two of us sat chatting over coffee, we happened to run into Cao Bingsheng, a classmate and dormitory-mate of his from Xuhui College. Xuhui College was a strictly supervised Catholic mission school. After graduating, Cao had gone on studying French, and later passed the examination to become an interpreter at the Municipal Police of the French Concession. Seeing Cao Bingsheng come in, Chen Zhigao rose at once to greet him; the two old schoolfellows shook hands and clapped each other on the shoulder, laughing, altogether affectionate. I only nodded politely to Cao in greeting. The two of them then fell to chatting about this and that. Chen Zhigao asked, "Xiao Cao, any news lately at the police station?" Cao Bingsheng answered in a low voice, "There is! The Nanjing National Government sent men to arrest a Communist boss; a Hubei man, around sixty, with a mouth full of gold teeth, a brandy-nose, and only nine fingers. It took a reward of one hundred thousand dollars to catch him; a huge bounty!"
The speaker meant nothing by it, but the listener took it to heart. While the two of them talked away merrily, I stirred the coffee in my cup with my spoon without a flicker of expression, feigning complete indifference to such matters, not putting in a single question, only listening quietly. Cao Bingsheng went on, "Hah! The fellow was utterly useless; one turn in the electric chair and he couldn't take it, and confessed." After that the two of them talked of a few trivial things, and I no longer paid close attention, turning it over anxiously in my mind: a Communist boss, a Hubei man, who could it be? I began to run through them in my head, lining up one by one the Party's central leaders who came from Hubei and on whom the enemy would set a bounty of one hundred thousand yuan. Dong Biwu? No brandy-nose, and not nine fingers. Chen Tanqiu? No gold teeth; it didn't fit, and it wasn't possible either. Who on earth was it? All at once I thought of Xiang Zhongfa. Back when he was a dockworker in Wuhan he had loved his drink, and he had a brandy-nose. His mouth was full of gold teeth, and one of his fingers had been partly chopped off by someone, so that he seemed to have only nine fingers. After the Great Revolution failed, he had gone to the Soviet Union as the CCP's representative to the Communist International. At that time Qu Qiubai, the Party's General Secretary, had been dismissed from his post for the error of "Left" putschism. Before the Sixth Congress of the CCP convened in Moscow in the summer of 1928, the Communist International, in one-sidedly stressing worker origins when selecting cadres, had recommended Xiang Zhongfa. At the Sixth Congress he was elected Chairman of the Central Political Bureau and concurrently Chairman of its Standing Committee (within the Party still customarily called "General Secretary"). But Xiang Zhongfa was incapable of directing the work of the Central Committee: his cultural level was low, and he had taken on the ways of a lumpen-proletarian. Turning it over carefully, every particular fit, and I felt certain it was Xiang Zhongfa.
This was no small matter; I had to find a way to report it to the Central Committee at once. However frantic I was inside, I could let nothing show on my face, and put on an air of unconcern. The two friends rambled on for a while, and then Cao Bingsheng grew anxious to leave. Chen Zhigao said, "Didn't you just come off work? Why are you in such a hurry to go? Come, let's have a drink or two and a simple meal together, and after that we'll all go to the Grand Theatre. Look, I've already bought all these tickets; there's one here for you." Cao Bingsheng sighed, "Ah, how could I ever be as free and easy as a young master like you! I'm on the night shift tonight; we who eat the government's rice are not our own masters!" And with that he said goodbye and hurried off. I, in any case, had long been prepared: whenever I did not wish to go out socializing with Chen Zhigao, I would feign a headache. So I told Chen Zhigao at once that my headache had come on again and that I could go neither to dinner nor to the film. Chen Zhigao, a young gentleman of good family who truly knew how to treat people with courtesy, took me at my word and drove me home in his car, even stopping on the way to buy some aspirin tablets at a Western pharmacy.
As soon as I got home I telephoned Comrade Pan Hannian, who lived above a tobacco-and-paper shop (as Shanghai people call a general store) in Xujiahui, and told him I had something extremely urgent to tell him in person. Pan Hannian came quickly, and I repeated to him everything I had heard Cao Bingsheng say, adding, "Because I had asked Chen Zhigao to help rescue Guan Xiangying, he had just now asked Cao Bingsheng whether there was any news at the police station, meaning to ask after the progress of Guan Xiangying's case; and quite unexpectedly Cao Bingsheng let slip a great case like this. Count as I may, the only one who fits the description Cao Bingsheng gave of the man they arrested is Xiang Zhongfa." Pan Hannian said he too thought it must be Xiang Zhongfa. I went on, "From Cao Bingsheng's tone, Xiang Zhongfa broke under the electric chair and has very likely already turned traitor; there's no telling what worse may happen this very night!" and I urged him to go and report it to the Party organization at once, as an emergency. He hurried away.
After Gu Shunzhang's defection, the work of the Central Special Branch was in Kang Sheng's charge. Pan Hannian left my lodgings and made several turns through the streets; once he was sure there was no "tail" following him, he went straight to Kang Sheng. When Kang Sheng had heard him out, he reported at once to Zhou Enlai, who was in effect directing the Central Committee's day-to-day work, and Comrade Enlai told him to notify Li Fuchun, Cai Chang, and the others on the Central Committee immediately, arranging for them to move to the Metropole Hotel and lie low for a while. These central leaders generally kept no documents at home; any urgent papers they carried on them were burned at once, the ashes flushed down the toilet. By around eleven that night, these leaders, all in Western suits and got up as big businessmen, sauntered grandly into the Metropole Hotel, a French establishment, without arousing the least suspicion.
In those days Shanghai had a kind of pedlar who carried wonton on a shoulder-pole and hawked it up and down the streets, trading all night long, clacking a pair of bamboo clappers as he went. The moment people heard the "tick-tack, tick-tack" of the clappers they knew the wonton-seller was coming, and they would open their doors and come out to buy wonton for a midnight snack. Kang Sheng sent two comrades of the Special Branch disguised as pedlars, wonton-poles on their shoulders, to keep watch near the places, known to Xiang Zhongfa, where Enlai and Fuchun had lived before they withdrew. Sure enough, at about one in the morning, a man in handcuffs and leg-irons was seen leading men from the police station; he went straight up and opened the door of Enlai's home with a key and burst in. Naturally they found the place empty. There were only three keys to Enlai's home: one each for him and Elder Sister Deng, and a third given to Xiang Zhongfa (who was General Secretary), so that if neither of them was at home Xiang Zhongfa too could let himself in with ease. It was therefore plain that the traitor who had brought the police to arrest Enlai could be none other than Xiang Zhongfa. But once he saw that Enlai was not to be caught, and realized that the other central leaders had also caught wind of it and moved, he did not go on to search Li Fuchun's home; instead he made straight for the Party's underground printing house nearby, the Red Flag Press. This press hung out an open signboard, held a proper business licence, and did printing business with outside firms. The first floor was the (underground Party's) Red Flag Press, the second floor was the comrades' dormitory, and the third floor was the workshop where the magazine Red Flag was secretly printed. That night, the situation being so urgent, we had no time to warn the press, and Xiang Zhongfa led the police in and wrecked it, carrying off several comrades as well. After the Special Branch men disguised as wonton-pedlars had reported all this to Kang Sheng, the Central Committee confirmed that Xiang Zhongfa had turned traitor.
The next day Kang Sheng and Pan Hannian came for me in a car, saying that Comrade Enlai wished to see me. The moment I came in, Comrade Enlai clasped my hand tightly and said, "Mulan, Mulan, you've done splendidly! Let me hear your report." He questioned me in detail about all that had happened, and then, pointing to a heap of banknotes laid out on the table, said, "Might we, through Chen Zhigao, buy the record of Xiang Zhongfa's confession from the relevant people at the police station? We have already ordered every underground Party organ in Shanghai that Xiang Zhongfa knew of to move at once, but we still need to know exactly what he has confessed to the enemy, so that we can devise countermeasures." I said this would hardly be wise: I had never revealed my true identity to Chen Zhigao, and as a young lady from a landlord's family come to Shanghai to look for work, I had no business caring about a great Communist traitor like Xiang Zhongfa, still less paying a high price to buy his confession. Besides, where would I ever get such a heap of banknotes? If it really came to that, my identity would surely be exposed, and it would inevitably harm the work of rescuing Comrade Guan Xiangying. At that moment Comrade Enlai, perhaps too anxious, had not had time to think it through carefully. Hearing me out, he said at once, "You've reminded me; I can't let you do this. Even if we are to buy the confession, we must find another way, through some other channel." I said that I could pretend to be merely curious and, without leaving a trace, get Chen Zhigao to inquire after the progress and the outcome of Xiang Zhongfa's case. Comrade Enlai said, "That will do very well." Before I left, Enlai praised me again, saying that I was conscientious and responsible in my work and highly vigilant; that had I not noticed what Cao Bingsheng was saying, analysed and judged it carefully, and taken resolute measures, but had instead gone off with Chen Zhigao to dinner and a film, then the central Party leaders, himself among them, would long since have been arrested and made to suffer. He praised me again and again for keeping a cool head, for being quick and alert, for judging rightly, and said, "Old Leopard" (that is, He Chang) "did not recommend the wrong person. You and Pan Hannian, the two of you, have worked well together; the moment you took the field you won this great and extraordinary service for the Party!" He also charged me to conduct all open activity through Chen Zhigao and to keep myself out of sight as far as I could, acting only as his adviser behind the scenes, to be endlessly careful and cautious, and by every means to keep myself well concealed, to hold fast to Chen Zhigao, and to do my work well.
A few days later I thought I would find a chance to talk it over with Chen Zhigao. In those days he and I talked of a great many things; apart from my true identity and the Party's secrets, we spoke of almost everything. Seeing on the shelves in his study a great many works of Marxism-Leninism, along with Chinese translations of the Life of Marx and the Life of Engels, I brought up Marx and his wife Jenny, and mentioned that Marx too had once been a lawyer. Our conversations were usually about the books we had read and the news of the day in the papers, and so, talking on, I suddenly asked him in a curious tone, "Say, let me ask you: whatever became of that case your friend Cao Bingsheng was talking about the other day? I'm rather interested in how men like that end up." He said, "You want to know?" I said, "Of course! That day Cao Bingsheng seemed to tell only the beginning, and it quite piqued my curiosity; like someone listening to a storyteller who, hearing 'to learn what happened next, listen to the next instalment,' is longing to know how it all turned out." Chen Zhigao said that a telephone call to Xiao Cao would tell me.
Within a few days he had learned the particulars from Cao Bingsheng and told me at once: "Ah, the fellow was doomed to die. It turns out that the men who caught him wired Chiang Kai-shek, who was then at Mount Lu, to say they had taken Xiang Zhongfa, the Communists' General Secretary. Chiang wired straight back with the order, 'Execute him on the spot.' The men interrogating him then sent a second telegram, saying that Xiang Zhongfa had surrendered and come over, had helped smash the Communists' underground press, and had had some Communist elements arrested as well. But before Chiang's second reply came, the man who had received Chiang's first telegram, eager to be first to claim the credit and the reward, had him shot at once. Men like that deserve to die." Hearing this, I kept an unmoved face, feigning that once my curiosity was satisfied I had lost all further interest. Inwardly, though, I could not help being very glad; for now that Xiang Zhongfa had betrayed the Party and gone over to the enemy, unable to withstand the enemy's threats and inducements, he would surely go on selling the Party's secrets. He was a chief leader of the Central Committee and knew almost everything within the Party; against such a traitor one could never guard enough. Now that the enemy, in their scramble for credit, had killed him first, they had in fact helped us plug this great breach. Xiang Zhongfa was of worker origin, but after becoming a chief leader of the Party Centre his life grew ever more corrupt and dissolute, until in the end he turned traitor.
At the time I still did not understand the particulars of how it had come about. Later I heard from Pan Hannian that the organization had already investigated it thoroughly: because Xiang disliked his "country" first wife (who had even borne him his only son), he had taken up a disreputable, irregular cohabitation with another woman outside. That woman had originally been a prostitute; she too had friends in the police station, and so the police had put a tail on her and had secretly photographed her together with Xiang Zhongfa. After Gu Shunzhang's defection, when Nanjing sent men to hunt down Communists, one check against the photographs told them that this man was Xiang Zhongfa, the Communists' number one. For during the First KMT-CCP cooperation, in Wuhan, he had appeared on many public occasions together with Zhou Enlai, Li Lisan, and other well-known Communists, and the enemy could easily come by their photographs.
From the time we learned of Xiang Zhongfa's defection, the Central Committee made a great rearrangement of all the underground organizations in Shanghai, and many of the places that had been used before were given up and moved. For a long time the Centre never made the matter of Xiang Zhongfa's defection public. It was only in 1993, when Maomao spoke of it in her book My Father Deng Xiaoping, that my name and my part in it were made plain. In 1998, in a television drama marking a hundred years since Enlai's birth, Comrade Enlai, tidying his clothes-chest, says to Elder Sister Deng: "Huang Mulan has telephoned to say that Xue Gengxin of the French police has passed word that Xiang Zhongfa has been arrested and turned traitor; we must move at once." Why did the drama's author not mention Cao Bingsheng's name? Presumably because Cao had been assassinated in the early part of the War of Resistance, and few people now know of Cao Bingsheng and his wife. Xue Gengxin, the associate who succeeded to Cao's post, is still living; he is at present a research fellow of the Shanghai Research Institute of Culture and History and has written many memoirs. In those days, to commemorate Cao Bingsheng, Liu Yazi, Chen Zhigao, and others founded the Bingsheng Primary School, with Cao Bingsheng's widow Sun Xueying as its headmistress.
To return to He Chang: when he had heard my account he said, delighted, "Well now, I did not recommend the wrong pair when I put you and Pan Hannian forward to the Centre! The two of you have done rather well; barely started at the work, and already you've earned a great merit like this. This is truly a victory in the first battle!" I said, "Indeed! Enlai praised you too, as the one who recommended us." He charged me to be careful and cautious and, through Chen Zhigao, to keep constant watch on news from the police station. I also reported to He Chang how the rescue of Guan Xiangying was progressing: old Master Chen had already telephoned Judge Zhao to press for the outcome of the trial. The judge answered that he had in fact already decided on an acquittal and release, but that there was still some discrepancy between the deposition of the prisoner surnamed Li (the false surname Guan Xiangying had used when arrested) and that of another man in the same case, and the two were now being checked against each other. Old Master Chen, having learned that the clerk handling the case was surnamed Li and had a taste for opium, had Chen Zhigao send that clerk Li, in his own name, four taels of the finest "Yunnan earth" (the best-quality opium, produced in Yunnan). And so the clerk, out of regard for old Master Chen, altered the two men's depositions to agree completely. I said, "By the look of it, Comrade Guan Xiangying will soon be released without conditions." Comrade He Chang said, delighted, "Splendid; you're about to win another great and extraordinary service."
That evening, as we parted with the greatest reluctance, He Chang urged me again and again to be careful and to keep well hidden, and told me not to shed tears over our parting. He said, "For revolutionary companions, our every action must submit to the organization's decisions; to be transferred and parted is an ordinary thing, and we must keep a firm will. All the more so now, when the burden on your shoulders is no light one and you must strive to do your work well." And he went on, "We must be able to stand the tests of failure and setback; this is the loyalty to the Party and the faith in certain victory that a revolutionary ought to have. I trust you are equal to the Party's great charge. Once Guan Xiangying is snatched to safety, you must go on to rescue the other comrades who have been arrested. This post of yours is a very important one, and we must not fail the Party's hopes by fretting too much over a temporary separation. In the Soviet area I shall surely hear you send word of victory after victory, and when the underground courier lines improve I will have you brought to the Soviet area for a reunion. Having made my mistake, and caused the Party so great a loss, I feel I ought to go down to the grassroots and temper myself. Perhaps going to Jiangxi to wage guerrilla war with Mao Zedong and the others will make better use of what I am good at. I hope we may meet again soon in the Central Soviet area!" What he said was of course right, but our parting was heavy with feeling, and how could I hold back the tears that wet the front of my dress? He wiped my tears away and said, "A revolutionary soldier sheds blood, not tears. Take the greatest care; the work, the task, comes first. You rescue many comrades, and I will strive to send word of victory soon; though ten thousand li apart, our hearts are joined, striving for a common goal."
To keep body and mind healthy is the one condition for doing the work well, and the very ground of one's spiritual solace. He Chang's stirring words and high spirit were the hidden inner strength that sustained me in overcoming obstacle upon obstacle.
Some time after Wu Defeng had arranged for men to escort He Chang to the Soviet area, Longhua Prison at last notified Chen Zhigao's law office that Guan Xiangying could be released on bail. Chen Zhigao and I went in a car to fetch him. Because I had once been imprisoned at Longhua and many people there knew me, I was afraid that, even in disguise, I might be recognized and my identity exposed, and so I did not go in, but waited in the car. Chen Zhigao saw to the formalities, affixed his seal, and bailed Comrade Guan Xiangying out. Not knowing whether the driver could be trusted, I said nothing much in the car beyond introducing Chen Zhigao, the lawyer who had handled the case, to Guan. Comrade Guan Xiangying, who while in prison had already learned that the organization was arranging his rescue, said to Chen Zhigao over and over, "Thank you, thank you!" The car drove all the way to the Oriental Hotel, where a room had long since been booked, and the clothes, inner and outer, that Pan Hannian had bought for Xiangying were already laid out inside. Chen Zhigao did not get out; he parted from us at the door of the Oriental Hotel. After Guan Xiangying had bathed, had his hair cut, and dressed himself anew, I kept him company at lunch and settled the account with the hotel. After the meal I went with him to the Wing On department store on Nanjing Road to buy towels, a toothbrush, and other daily necessities, and there we deliberately wound up and down the floors a few times; once sure there was no "tail" behind us, we took a Johnson Company taxi and returned together to where I lived. Comrade Pan Hannian had already been waiting there a good while. We then told Guan Xiangying in full how the Central Committee had arranged his rescue, including that hair-raising episode of the discovery of Xiang Zhongfa's defection: how, luckily, the enemy in their haste to claim the credit had shot him first, so that he had only led them to wreck the Red Flag Press and had had no time to give up the true identities of Guan Xiangying and the other arrested comrades; otherwise it would have been perilous. Comrade Xiangying said, deeply moved, "Mulan, you are truly remarkable, remarkable! The moment you took the field you did a great service." He asked too after He Chang, and I said He Chang had already gone to the Soviet area. Xiangying said that he had long since applied to the Central Committee to go to the Soviet area to temper himself. After a long talk we shook hands and said goodbye, each bidding the other take the greatest care, till we should meet again!
Guan Xiangying was taken away by Pan Hannian to a secret guest-house of the underground Party, and before long Wu Defeng arranged for men to escort him to the Hunan-Hubei border Soviet area. He went on to do a great deal of work for the Party and made great contributions, above all on the Long March, when he served as deputy political commissar of the Second Front Red Army, firmly upholding the correct leadership of the Central Committee and Comrade Mao Zedong and opposing Zhang Guotao's plot to split the Party and split the Red Army. After the victorious junction with the Central Red Army in northern Shaanxi, he served in turn as political commissar of the 120th Division of the Eighth Route Army, secretary of the CCP Central Committee's Shanxi-Suiyuan Sub-bureau, alternate member of the Sixth Central Political Bureau, and member of the Seventh Central Committee. After the founding of New China, a lesson was once included in the primary-school Chinese readers describing how, when he lay ill in Yan'an, Mao Zedong went to the hospital to visit him. Comrade Wu Defeng told me that even in his illness Comrade Guan Xiangying still thought of me: knowing that I was still holding to the work in the White areas, he asked the organization, "When one day the revolution succeeds and Mulan comes back, I beg the organization to be sure to treat her well!" I was truly grateful to him. I knew that Guan Xiangying and He Chang had a deep revolutionary friendship, but I did not know much of his personal history; happily his great contributions to the Party's cause are set down in plain words in the Party's history, and there is no need for me to say more. A bronze statue of Comrade Guan Xiangying has been raised at Dalian, and it was Wang Zhen who went to take part in the ceremony.
These two events of 1931, the timely discovery and reporting of Xiang Zhongfa's defection, which safeguarded the central leaders, and the securing of Guan Xiangying's release from prison, are, both in my own history and in the Party's, very important and well worth recalling.
Though I had been praised by the Party's leaders and encouraged by He Chang, I did not on that account grow proud or claim the credit for myself. From then on I resolved to raise my abilities and the art of struggle still further, and to strive to become a loyal sentinel on the hidden front.
This first victory made me feel deeply that in deciding that I should not go to the Soviet area but remain at the important post of head of the Rescue Department, the organization and He Chang had placed great hopes in me and given me their full trust; and it made me feel too that in this special working environment I truly had a field for my talents and could contribute to the Party's work.
The White Terror in Shanghai was then at its most severe, and the traitors' selling of the Party's secrets had put our Party's work in a most difficult and dangerous position. As a Communist, in keeping with the Party's needs, I threw myself into completing the tasks the organization assigned me in the rescue work, using my wits and my stratagems, keeping my eyes open at every turn; to serve faithfully, under the Party's leadership, as an unexpected soldier on this special front was no more than my duty.
Looking at these two events of 1931, the Party organization made comprehensive use of its underground workers with leadership, with planning, with a division of labour, with coordination, and with review; and they show all the more clearly Comrade Enlai's brilliant foresight and the correctness of his instructions.
