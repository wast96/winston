## Clouds over Shijō Bridge

The fifth month of the first year of Keiō.

It may be called the watershed of the history of the Restoration.

The Shogun Iemochi entered Kyoto to take supreme command of the Second Chōshū Expedition. When the golden-fan horse-standard, borne since Ieyasu's day, passed into Nijō Castle, the townsfolk of Kyoto too

{j} "The shogunate's prestige rises high,"

{j} so they gossiped.

But in truth the shogunate no longer had either the military or the financial strength to chastise Chōshū.

And that was not all. Chōshū had already gone so far as to behead its three elders and make its submission; the shogunate had no just cause left to send an army against it a second time. To raise the army of chastisement, it had to force the pretext. This was the digging of the shogunate's own grave.

Against the chastisement of Chōshū, nearly all of them—the Tokugawa's related houses, its branch houses, its hereditary vassals, its outer lords—were opposed. The one power that pressed for it, and pressed hard, was the Aizu domain that guarded Kyoto, and the Shinsengumi under its command; or rather, one might say, Kondō himself.

"The best course now is to send troops into the two provinces of Suō and Nagato, wipe them out, strip the Mōri house of its three hundred and sixty thousand koku, and so cut the root of the shogunate's troubles."

{j} so Kondō, from about the New Year of Keiō 1 onward, urged again and again in council with the Aizu elders. That this simple call for chastisement was becoming the shogunate's death-blow, Kondō never so much as considered. In brains he was, after all, no more than a plain soldier.

"A weighty view,"

{j} and the Aizu side, too, had no objection.

The so-called Aizu debates, between the Aizu elders and Kondō Isami and his fellows, were rough stuff indeed, and, going round and about, they reached even the ears of the revere-the-Emperor man Matsudaira Yoshinaga of Echizen Fukui.

Rendered into plain speech, Yoshinaga's own memorandum runs:

As for the Second Chōshū Expedition, the shogunate seems full of confidence. Chōshū is a thing to be crushed like an egg, the great men of the shogunal council say. Now, by the rumor abroad, there are those who hold that the realm is thrown into such turmoil on account of the following domains; namely, Satsuma, Tosa, the Owari Tokugawa, the Echizen Matsudaira (Yoshinaga himself), the Higo Hosokawa, the Hizen Nabeshima, the Chikuzen Kuroda, and the Inshū Ikeda—the eight western domains. "These domains preach loyalty to the Emperor alone; they are fellows to be detested. When the chastisement of Chōshū is triumphantly over, we shall, in due course, destroy these domains as well." A certain man counseled me, "Outwardly the shogunate treats you well, but inwardly you must not let down your guard." It appears, indeed, to be the truth.

The above is one in substance with Kondō's opinion. It may even be that Kondō's playing the loyalist and discoursing on the realm and the state with the great men of Aizu had become the opinion of the shogunal council. Kondō himself was forever pouring his views into the rōjū's ears, and from Aizu, too, opinions of every sort were being sent up to Edo.

The great men of the shogunate in those days were such a pack of dullards that even the shogunal retainer Katsu Kaishū had thrown up his hands at them; so the opinions and readings of the situation from Aizu and the Shinsengumi, the shogunate's watch-post in Kyoto, they would, in the way of their office, have taken for reference material of the first importance.

On top of this, the shogunal men had suddenly stiffened their backs, and behind it lay the promise of backing from the French emperor Napoleon III, of which the French minister Léon Roches was forever pressing his counsel on the shogunate. But that this same French emperor was a man fated, a few years later, to fall from power, not one of the great men of the shogunate had any means even to guess.

When the shogun came up to the capital, Kondō, in high delight, caught hold of Toshizō and said:

"From here on it grows interesting."

Aizu upheld the shogun, the Shinsengumi became the core of Aizu, and its repute rose greatly.

"It is Aizu's realm now," said some; and a rumor of unknown origin arose, "Is Aizu to be granted a fief of a million koku?"

"Rejoice, but within measure,"

{j} said Toshizō, and showed him a lampoon that the inspectors had peeled down at the Sanjō great bridge.

{p} Cast off that fellow (Aizu),

{p} take a good wife instead,

{p} and I'd fain drink long (Chōshū) and deep.

{j} so it ran.

"Well made."

{j} Being a man of haiku, Toshizō cocked his head over it.

"Fool. Whoever heard of admiring the thing?"

"No, no—words don't fall so smoothly into line as this without some skill."

{j} He was chuckling.

"Tear it up,"

{j} he said. He was, by and large, a man with no taste for wit or drollery.

"The work of a spy, no doubt."

"That won't be all of it."

Among the several daimyō the Chōshū-sympathizing party was on the rise, and the common folk of Kyoto too were deep in sympathy for the crushed Chōshū. Though this owed something, to be sure, to the very lavish money Chōshū had spent about the town, in its heyday in Kyoto, to whip up its own popularity.

On the night before the shogun was to enter the capital, Kondō stayed at the barracks and, sitting up late, chanted aloud from his beloved book, the Nihon Gaishi.

"A fine voice,"

{j} and even Toshizō was impressed. Here and there Kondō misread, or could not carry a reading through, and, stuck fast, would clear his throat; but it was a splendid, carrying voice.

Kondō had come, almost with tears standing in his eyes, to the passage of the Kenmu Restoration.

It is the passage where the Emperor Go-Daigo, having destroyed the Hōjō of Kamakura, returns to the capital with Kusunoki Masashige for his vanguard.

Kondō thought of himself as a Kusunoki Masashige. The Emperor Go-Daigo, one supposes, would be the Shogun Iemochi. Were Masashige, the man from the grass roots, not to spend his loyalty, upon what could the wandering sovereign lean?—such was his frame of mind.

"Toshi, if I am Kusunoki Masashige, then you are cast for the part of Onji Sakon, is that it?"

"Well, maybe so."

{j} Toshizō gave him his assent.

"That crowd too were petty rural samurai, or mountain ascetics, or bandits, of Kongōsan in Kawachi—nameless fellows, at any rate; so our stock is not much different from theirs."

"It is not their stock I speak of. It is the part."

"It's all one to me; only, since the new organization's roster is drawn up, I'd have you call in Itō-kun as well and take counsel."

"Aye."

{j} Kondō called in Itō Kashitarō.

Itō came in with a trim summer haori of white ro-silk, its crest dyed in black, thrown over his shoulders. As ever, he was a handsome man, like an actor.

"So the new organization is settled?"

{j} he said, and sat.

(A strange fellow.)

To Toshizō, a man like Itō was past understanding.

Since joining, this man had never touched corps duties, but went out every day to meet with men of Satsuma, Echizen, Tosa, and the like—domains that stood in a critical posture toward the shogunate. (The Satsuma domain, at this time, still outwardly acted as a friendly domain to Aizu, out of its very hatred of Chōshū; yet it was no pure supporter of the shogunate either, and, as a domain that might strike out on its own at any moment, the shogunate both courted it hard and kept a wary eye on it.)

And besides,

—I should like to go about the provinces, and the Kyushu country in particular, on a speaking tour.

{j} so he had proposed to Kondō. That is: to probe the state of the western country, and, along with it, to consort with the so-called men of purpose, discourse on affairs of state, and explain the standing of the Shinsengumi as he went.

—A capital thing.

{j} and Kondō was delighted. As Toshizō saw it, Kondō, sad to say, was too fond by half of such men of learning, and of such learned-man's activity. Kondō himself was, indeed, of late in the manner of a full-blown controversialist, forever meeting the liaison offices of the great domains in Kyoto at Gion.

And that it was Kondō who talked most volubly at these gatherings—Toshizō had heard that too.

—Have a care of Itō.

{j} Toshizō told Kondō over and over; but Kondō, rather, held it against Toshizō—sworn brothers though they were—for saying so.

—The Shinsengumi's officers from now on must be men of the nation. Where there is argument, let it be laid open boldly before the realm, set out even before the shogun and the rōjū, and carry weight enough to move them; short of that, it will not do.

—Is that so, now.

{j} Toshizō was unconvinced. As he saw it, the Shinsengumi was in the end a band of swordsmen. To make it larger yet, into the greatest military organization of the shogunate, was the aim; to become a political society was surely not. The shogunate, rather, would find such a Shinsengumi a nuisance.

—Is that so, now.

{j} He would put on a sullen face; but Kondō was growing rather more discontented with such a Toshizō. As one arm of himself, the man of affairs, Toshizō would never do.

(If only this fellow had learning.)

The eye with which he looked at Toshizō had, at times, turned cold.

By just so much, Kondō leaned toward Itō Kashitarō.

—Itō-san.

{j} he would call, with respect in it. At times,

—Itō-sensei.

{j} he called. From the bare "Toshi," flung out without ceremony, it was a vast difference in usage.

Itō Kashitarō had a gift for verse. There was no savor in the poems, but they were textbook tanka, dutifully grounded in the tradition of the way of poetry since the Kokin and the Shin-Kokin.

When Itō left Edo to join the Shinsengumi and had come as far as Ōmori,

{p} Many are the leaves of words I leave behind me,

{p} yet parting without a word, on my sleeve the white dew.

As for a poem voicing his heart toward the times,

{p} If it be single-mindedly for the sake of my great lord,

{p} let me not scatter my heart to no purpose.

—such were the sort of thing.

"Ah, the Nihon Gaishi,"

{j} said Itō, peering at what Kondō held.

"Just so. I am fond of the Great Lord Kusunoki, you know."

"Ah. …"

{j} Itō smiled. Itō too, being of the Mito school, revered Kusunoki Masashige as something more than a god.

"That is Kondō-sensei for you."

(Blockhead. …)

{j} thought Toshizō. Kondō's Kusunoki Masashige upheld the Tokugawa shogun. From Itō Kashitarō, who bore the Emperor aloft, the kind of sacred palanquin was different.

"I too, when I went down to Osaka the other day, viewed the Settsu sea, and on the road turned in at the grove of Minatogawa in Hyōgo, and bowed low before the grave of the Great Lord Kusunoki. A poem came to me there on the moment—forgive me,"

{j} and, composing his bearing, he began to intone his own poem aloud.

{p} In the end

{p} thus shall it be with me as well—

{p} a moss-grown stone on the field of Minatogawa.

"Splendid. …"

{j} Kondō nodded, with the look of a man who understood. Toshizō had turned his face away.

"That reminds me, Toshizō-san. It was the roster of the new organization we were to talk over,"

{j} and Itō, with the look of a man come back to reality, turned his white face toward Toshizō.

Toshizō passed the draft that lay in Kondō's hands over to Itō Kashitarō.

—Staff Officer: Itō Kashitarō.

{j} so it read.

This was a matter already settled with Itō. The apportioning of the officers' seats to the rest of the Itō party, too, had all been drawn up in keeping with Itō's wishes.

In this new organization the name jokin (officer) was done away with, and, taking the shogunate's own infantry for a model, it was made into something like the French system of army organization.

"This is a splendid corps structure,"

{j} said Itō, and looked at Toshizō. It was the look of a man revising his opinion.

"Yes, Toshizō-kun has a talent for this sort of thing,"

{j} said Kondō, too, and looked pleased. In Toshizō's gift for building an organization, and that alone, Kondō rated him as having no equal under heaven.

The new organization ran as follows.

Commander: Kondō Isami Masayoshi

Vice-Commander: Hijikata Toshizō Yoshitoyo

Staff Officer: Itō Kashitarō Takeaki

Unit Captains:

First Unit: Okita Sōji

Second Unit: Nagakura Shinpachi

Third Unit: Saitō Hajime

Fourth Unit: Matsubara Chūji

Fifth Unit: Takeda Kanryūsai

Sixth Unit: Inoue Genzaburō

Seventh Unit: Tani Sanjūrō

Eighth Unit: Tōdō Heisuke

Ninth Unit: Suzuki Mikisaburō

Tenth Unit: Harada Sanosuke

Corporals:

Okuzawa Eisuke, Kawashima Katsuji, Shimada Kai, Hayashi Shintarō, Maeno Gorō, Abe Jūrō, Hashimoto Kaisuke, Ibaraki Tsukasa, Ohara Kōzō, Kondō Yoshisuke, Kanō Michinosuke, Nakanishi Noboru, Itō Tetsugorō, Kumebe Jūrō, Tomiyama Yahei, Nakamura Kosaburō, Ikeda Kotarō, Katsurayama Buhachirō

Inspectors:

Shinohara Tainoshin, Yoshimura Kan'ichirō, Yamazaki Susumu, Ogata Shuntarō, Ashiya Noboru, Arai Tadao

Of those on the roll, the ones in bold are men Itō brought from Edo. Besides these, of the Itō party, Hattori Takeo was given officer's standing as the corps's fencing-master, while Utsumi Jirō and Sano Shimenosuke were made plain troopers. But their skill was all of the first class, and it was understood that, as they grew used to corps duty, they would be raised to corporal.

"Very good,"

{j} said Itō, and, showing little interest, only added,

"For staff officer—that is a kindness I am grateful for."

{j} he said.

The post of staff officer, too, like the former general secretary of Yamanami Keisuke, was but an adviser to Kondō, with no power of command over the corps as the vice-commander had.

"By all means, for the Shinsengumi's sake, I mean to consort with the finest men of the realm and see that the corps takes no wrong direction."

"By all means I could wish it so,"

{j} and Kondō bowed his head.

"A poem has come to me,"

{j} and Itō drew out a sheet of pocket-paper and wrote, with a well-formed brush in the Shōren'in style, swiftly, on and on.

{p} A being of no account,

{p} I grudge not this body; in the autumn fields

{p} even this wandering, sleeping abroad,

{p} is only for my country's sake.

(His verse is not to be despised either.)

{j} Toshizō thought of the general secretary Yamanami Keisuke, who this second month had been made to cut his belly for the crime of desertion.

Yamanami's flight to Edo had, it seemed, been made after some understanding with Itō; and after his death Itō mourned him, and made four poems, and showed them to one and another of the troopers. That these poems were now spreading a subtle ripple among the troopers, Toshizō knew.

—Be even a guard to the Emperor: for I am a body that dies in an age gone awry, as the black hair falls in disorder.

—Drawn and lured by the spring wind, the mountain cherry scatters, and is mourned by men.

(A hateful fellow.)

{j} Toshizō thought.

But Itō Kashitarō's repute among the plain troopers grew higher by the day, and his revere-the-Emperor, expel-the-barbarian creed, which might almost be called a religion, was making converts among them.

Toshizō, seeing such men, would trump up some other breach of the rules and, one after another, order them to cut their bellies.

—To the Shinsengumi, thought is poison.

{j} Such was the resolute conviction Toshizō held.

Kondō was absorbed, more than in corps duties, in politics and thought.

Itō, for his part, frequented the mansions of revere-the-Emperor court nobles, Lord Ōhara of the Third Rank among them, and there discoursed on the affairs of the age.

Toshizō alone, as though left behind, threw himself into the duties of the corps. Of all the officers, he alone set up no place of rest outside the barracks, but lived within them, those peculiar eyes of his glittering, rolling about him.

The summer passed.

The order for the renewed expedition against Chōshū had gone out, but the times did not move, and there was a little stall. The shogun, having entered Osaka Castle, had fallen ill there, and had not yet, even now, ordered the troops to march. For one thing, no prospect had been found for raising the war-funds; for another, the daimyō's ranks would not fall into step. And in this while, quite unknown to the shogunate's side, something was going forward on the hidden side of the political scene. The Satsuma domain, until now a friendly domain to Aizu, had secretly turned its whole domain-policy about, resolved on toppling the shogunate and aiding Chōshū, and, with the Tosa Kaientai leader Sakamoto Ryōma for a go-between, was pressing on toward a secret Satsuma-Chōshū alliance. The sudden turn of the Restoration's history begins from here; but of course neither the shogunate nor its hands and feet, Aizu and the Shinsengumi, dreamed of it.

Autumn came, and still the shogunate handed down no order of attack, and in the eleventh month the shogunate went so far in its leisureliness as to dispatch an envoy of interrogation to Chōshū.

The chief envoy was the shogunate's Grand Inspector, Nagai Naomune, the Mondo-no-shō. The place, the Kokutai-ji of Hiroshima in Geishū.

Among the suite of this shogunal delegation there were, of all people, four men mixed in: Kondō Isami, Itō Kashitarō, Takeda Kanryūsai, and Ogata Shuntarō.

(A giddy business. What earthly use is it?)

{j} thought Toshizō, who had been ordered to stay behind and mind the barracks.

Of course Kondō and Itō and the rest did not go as shogunal envoys. Under the style of retainers of the shogunate's representative Nagai Mondo-no-shō, Kondō even changed his name, to Kondō Kuranosuke.

By that time the Chōshū side, through the good offices of Sakamoto Ryōma and his men, had bought a great quantity of new-model rifles from an English trading house in Nagasaki, and was making ready for the decisive battle.

The chief envoy who came to the Kokutai-ji of Hiroshima as Chōshū's representative was the elder Shishido Bingo-no-suke. But this was a downright lie; the truth was that it was Yamagata Hanzō—Shishido Tamaki, after the Restoration a viscount and member of the House of Peers—a glib-tongued third son of a middle-rank samurai, chosen for his ready speech. On him they had a domain-elder's house-name, Shishido, put for the occasion, and he appeared as an envoy got up on the spur of the moment. Chōshū, from the first, had no intention of coming to parley in earnest.

Toshizō, in Kyoto, minded the barracks.

In this while he cut down, almost daily, rōnin reckoned to be of the Chōshū line about the town; but a trace of loneliness he could not wipe away.

Taking Okita Sōji along, on the road to a Gion restaurant, up on Shijō Bridge he watched some several torn shreds of autumn cloud, dyed in the evening glow, hurrying steadily eastward.

"Sōji, look—clouds."

"Clouds, aren't they."

{j} Okita too stopped and looked up. From Okita's toothed clogs a long shadow stretched out over the bridge.

The samurai and townsfolk passing to and fro over the bridge went by as though giving the two a wide berth. Two men of the Shinsengumi—what, they must have wondered, were these two pondering?

"A verse has come to me,"

{j} said Toshizō. For Master Hōgyoku, it was a piece after a long while.

"A poor thing, no doubt,"

{j} Okita chuckled; but Toshizō paid no heed, and drew a verse-book from his breast and set it down.

Okita peered in.

{p} Hurrying toward home, the clouds of the Fifth Month.

"Why, it is the eleventh month now,"

"What of it—clouds of the Fifth Month are the brighter and gayer for it. With autumn or winter for the season-word, there's no help for the loneliness of it."

"I see,"

{j} Okita, saying nothing, began to walk.

This youth seemed to understand Toshizō's heart, to a degree that was almost frightening.
