## The Yangzhou Master

East of Hongkou Park lay the rifle range; from within its walls came the sound of gunfire, for first thing in the morning the Settlement Volunteers were at live-fire drill. Between the two walls ran a cinder path, and because it led to Chiai-li, where the Japanese residents were gathered, the path had come to be called Chiai Road. In Chiai-li was the Uchiyama Bookstore, where Chen Qianyuan often went; being a bookshop run by a Japanese, it stocked many books no one elsewhere dared sell. But today he had come too early, and the shop was not yet open. The houses along the road were little three-story buildings, with brick openwork walls around the courtyards before their doors, only waist-high. In the courtyards grew a good many cherry trees; ‘Chiai-li’ came from the English sound of the cherry blossom.

As soon as Dong Huiwen arrived, the two of them went north along the cinder path; they had arranged to go to the park.

This was the little path they liked best for walking, but Chen Qianyuan had first to ask:

“Did you shake off your tail?”

“Never mind them today,” Dong Huiwen answered. She meant to spend a proper day like any ordinary couple.

On the lakeshore in the park they found a bench and sat down; the chestnuts they had just bought were still a little warm.

“Yesterday afternoon he was busy again, still in the kitchen near midnight. I went down and forced him off to bed,” Dong Huiwen said.

“Shall I just call him Uncle Dong? If I called him Father, would he ask us on the spot when we're getting married?”

“Even if you don't call him Father, he'll still ask you.” Dong Huiwen gazed at the withered duckweed on the water. Some time before, Dong Huiwen had been taken into the Longhua detention house, and her father had had a terrible fright. When she came out she thought he had aged several years all at once; only after the New Year did his spirits pick up a little.

Chestnut shells fell into the water, the ripples spreading ring on ring, and several fish rose from the bottom.

Chen Qianyuan suddenly made up his mind: “When I see my brother, I'll tell him, and take the chance to ask our superiors' leave as well.”

The first time the two of them met had been at a secret organ of the underground Party, in a house beside a theater, its staircase shared with the theater's gallery. When she went, a newly written spoken-drama was being performed in the theater, an afternoon show. The play was already half over; the ticket-checker had gone off somewhere, and the door to the gallery stood wide open. On the stage Ma Zhenhua was pouring out her grief, and in every corner of the passage you could hear her sorrowing voice: she meant to finish writing that letter and then go drown herself in the river.

Beside the turn of the staircase was a door; she knocked, knocked hard, afraid that Ma Zhenhua's voice was too loud. The young man who opened it had very bright eyes and still looked like a student; he was glaring at her in a fury.

The room was very small, windowless, holding mops, a water bucket, and a heap of packing crates. On a crate lay an open book, and beside the crate stood a stool. She was taken aback — this was the organ? Only later did she learn that the organ was a floor up the stairs; you passed along the corridor before the entrance to the theater gallery, and there was the secret organ.

They spoke the recognition passwords, and she handed the documents to him; but his anger had not yet cooled, and he scolded her in a low voice:

“Why did you knock like that?

“Afraid people wouldn't notice you?

“This is a Party organ. Did you take it for your little sisters' dormitory?”

She had gone there three times, each time to deliver documents. Every time she received the assignment her heart was light, like a carrier pigeon flying from the sky over streets and lanes. She was already in love with him, only she did not know it then. Then, all at once, the errands stopped: for five months running, Old Fang gave her no documents to carry to that organ. She could not ask Old Fang why, and dared not ask. Perhaps the errand would never be given to her again. Had she done something wrong? Had he complained to the organization that she knocked too hard? But he was no longer angry — on the two assignments that followed he had been very gentle, pouring her water, talking with her.

One time it was summer, and he had even brought up half a watermelon from downstairs and let her sit by the crates and eat it. That time they talked a great deal; he told her the author of the Russian book was named Nekrasov, that he was translating his poems, that his brother and his brother's girlfriend both loved Nekrasov, and that afterward he had come to love him too. Could it have been because that time they talked too much, because she lingered too long at the contact point? She thought she would never go to that tiny secret room again, thought she would never see him again.

Later she did see him again — at a street rally the following May. He was speaking passionately, but a few minutes later the gathered listeners scattered, constables charged in, a baton struck him on the head, and she ran up to hold him. They ducked into a lane, turned into another street, and she took him to a safe place — and it turned out that her superior and his were both Old Fang.

The Scotto Cup was a great Settlement sporting event. On the park lawn, the second Western League First Division match of the afternoon had reached its second half. Chen Qianyuan liked watching football, but it was Dong Huiwen who had proposed their coming here to meet today. His brother had been away from Shanghai for many days with no word, and Chen Qianyuan was somewhat anxious. Tickets were sold to the public; on each side of the field stood three temporary long wooden stands, knee-high, holding five rows of benches, each stand seating two or three hundred. Ropes were strung around the stands, and those unwilling to buy a ticket could stand outside the ropes and watch too.

Dong Huiwen stood pressed close to Chen Qianyuan among the crowd watching from outside the ropes. The tall, big Englishmen of the foreign-merchant Lux team ran with a certain abandon, so that the turf boomed under them. In a scramble between the two sides the ball looked about to go over the goal line, when a lanky player of the Jinan team came sliding in to save it, kicking up a spray of earth and grass and dust.

The spectators outside the ropes cried out and clapped, nodding to one another in approval. One woman's voice in the crowd was especially shrill; Dong Huiwen thought it sounded familiar, and looking toward it saw Miss Tao, decked out in all her finery, squeezed into the throng, while the lanky player who had drawn the cheers kept blowing kisses her way.

Dong Huiwen tugged at Chen Qianyuan's sleeve and was about to leave, when she saw Miss Tao beckoning to her. She said in a low voice to Chen Qianyuan: “That Miss Tao.”

The words were scarcely out before Miss Tao came shoving toward them through the crowd.

“Miss Dong! Miss Dong!” Miss Tao, her face flushed and beaded with sweat, ran up to them, sizing up Chen Qianyuan while she fanned herself with a handkerchief. “You've come to watch the football too?”

Dong Huiwen said politely: “We were just passing by.”

Miss Tao saw that Dong Huiwen was being somewhat perfunctory: “Oh, oh — such fine weather, there's nothing better than coming out to stroll in the park. Though the football's well worth watching too, you know — look at those foreigners, legs like elephants, stamping the turf till it booms like a floor. Whoever lives downstairs from people like that, what rotten luck.”

Seeing that Dong Huiwen had no intention of introducing Chen Qianyuan, Miss Tao did not seem to mind: “And how is that Mrs. Ling? I ran into her once at the bank last time, but we were both in such a hurry we hardly said a word.”

Ling Wen had set off for Guangzhou many days ago with no word since, and at Miss Tao's sudden mention of her, Dong Huiwen felt a fresh flutter of unease: “Mrs. Ling is very well; she has spoken of running into you.”

“I really am ashamed of myself, honestly.” About that letter at Longhua, Miss Tao had at last found a chance to explain a little, but did not know what to say.

Seeing her somewhat embarrassed, Dong Huiwen said quickly: “Miss Tao, do watch the football, enjoy yourself. We have something to see to; we'll go on ahead.”

“It's quite all right — I can't follow the game anyway, I only came to look at people.” As she said it, Miss Tao winked at Dong Huiwen, as though she had already forgotten what she had meant to say before.

On the field just now, the Jinan University team led the foreign-merchant Lux team one to nil; the Jinan left winger broke through with the ball, and it came again near the goal line.

The spectators all around watched without blinking, but Chen Qianyuan caught sight of two men in the passage between two of the stands, looking over their way. The people watching the game, standing or sitting, all faced the field; but these two stood side-on, pointing and gesturing.

The player carrying the ball turned and let fly a long shot; the foreign-merchant goalkeeper gathered it in and was about to throw it out to a fullback when his hand slipped, and in the penalty area before the goal the Jinan forward, seeing his chance, rushed up and kicked — the ball came loose and dropped back into the net. All around, the spectators broke at once into a frenzy of cheers. Chen Qianyuan tugged at Dong Huiwen, and in the confusion the two of them quietly slipped away.

Dong Huiwen lived in Juyuan Fang by the Zhengjia wooden bridge; her father was a famous chef of Huaiyang cuisine, who had earned a whole household's fortune by the ladle in his hand. In his day the Yangzhou master had made his name overnight, with a single ordinary dish of braised whole pig's head, at the annual banquet of the Yangzhou South Gate Catering Guild. On that occasion several hundred proprietors and chefs from the city's restaurant trade had all gathered on the spot, and even the Huaiyang-style houses of Beiping and Shanghai had sent men to observe the ceremony.

That very evening people came bearing gifts of money and letters of engagement, inviting him to take charge of the stove. There were several bankers in Shanghai who, aping the foreign merchants of the Settlement, had followed suit and set up a private bankers' club of their own. Besides cards and conversation, the one indispensable thing was a good kitchen.

Master Dong had married and had a daughter only at forty, but sadly his wife took childbed fever and died two days after the birth. Master Dong raised his daughter alone; once Dong Huiwen had graduated and become a teacher, he passed sixty and retired. Retired though he was, he had trained up one or two apprentices in the kitchen, and there were still a number of eminences in politics and business who remembered his cooking, so that at festivals, or whenever there was some occasion calling for a banquet, they would still ask him to come and preside. Tomorrow was the Lantern Festival, and the previous month the Cheng family of Hart Road had sent to fix the date. Though the Chengs had failed at standard-gold speculation and gone bust in business, they meant to keep up appearances and to feast all their friends on the fifteenth of the first month. Master Dong had not the heart to refuse.

So he had told Dong Huiwen to invite Chen Qianyuan to a meal at the house, and it could only be arranged for the fourteenth — this very evening.

On the wall hung a photograph of Master Dong's late wife; in the middle stood a round tabletop, and, guests and host together numbering only three, three chairs had been set out. On the round table were several small dishes: oil-seared shrimp, bamboo-shoot tips, duck gizzards, ham, wine-steeped fish, and a dish of assorted vegetables. In the tossed pickled greens there were more than ten things — shiitake, wood-ear, bamboo shoots, dried tofu, celery, bean sprouts — all shredded fine and dressed with sesame oil. In the middle of the table stood a pot of warmed Shaoxing wine, but Master Dong was still at the back in the kitchen.

The day before, Chen Qianyuan and Dong Huiwen had gone out and bought a fur hat and a scarf, and boxed them up to bring today as a gift for Master Dong. Dong Huiwen took the box to the back, went into the kitchen, and came out announcing the menu: “Today Master Dong is treating you to the Three Heads Banquet.”

The words were scarcely out before the great Master Dong made his grand entrance. Because he had been working in the kitchen, he wore only a short black satin jacket, with matching trousers of the same fine cotton bound at the ankles; the cold had made him add a sheepskin waistcoat, and on his head sat, cocked to one side, a brand-new sable cap, with a camel-hair scarf slung over his shoulders — half of it in front, the other half hanging down behind, long in front and short behind, so that it was on the very point of slipping off, but Master Dong had no hand free. In both hands he bore a great platter, and enthroned on it sat a jujube-red pig's head, the face lifelike. The head had been boned and its bristles tweezed out, blanched three times, then set on a bamboo grate at the bottom of a great iron pot, over a bed of scallion and ginger, and, seasoned with rock sugar and soy sauce, simmered several hours over a low fire. Though it was served up as a whole head, it yielded five distinct textures: the eyeball soft, the ear crisp, the tongue tender, the cheek-meat succulent, the snout with a satisfying chew.

The next bowl was boned and stewed silver-carp head. Before the New Year, Master Dong's apprentice had made a trip back to Yangzhou and brought back several blood-silver-carp from the Three Rivers Mouth, which Master Dong had kept alive in a jar, waiting for just this day to feast his guest. Once the fish head was boned, it was placed in a woven bamboo net-bag and stewed with ham and bamboo-shoot slices into a rich, thick soup, then ladled into a bowl, the ‘clouds’ of flesh at the two gills opening like the petals of a flower.

Last came an earthenware pot holding four fist-sized lion's-head meatballs. Because just now not even a single hairy crab was to be found, Master Dong had used river catfish in its place, chopping the belly-rib pork together with the fish, kneading each ball loosely by hand, and setting them one by one into the pot to simmer clear over a low fire.

Ordinarily Master Dong had long since been brought to heel by his impertinent daughter, but now he put on his airs. Without ceremony he ordered Chen Qianyuan to kowtow to the late Mistress Dong. Beneath the photograph stood a side table, and on it two bowls of food, a plate of eight-treasure rice, and a plate of date cake. On the gray-brick floor Chen Qianyuan knelt down respectfully, and once the three kowtows were done, Master Dong took Chen Qianyuan for one of his own household's children.

It was only when Dong Huiwen was taken into the Longhua detention house that Master Dong realized there was a Communist in his own family too; even at the time of the Nationalist-Communist cooperation seven or eight years before, he had only heard of them from the newspapers. Though the scraps that reached the kitchen from the papers, or from the banquet table, had given him some vague impression of the Communists, that his own daughter had joined them gave Master Dong a faint, secret liking for them.

He did not mean to show what he felt in his heart. During those days his daughter was at Longhua, he had even gone to Mr. Chen of the bankers' club, knowing that Mr. Chen was acquainted with many people in Nanjing. But Mr. Chen had said to him, Old Dong, there's nothing I can do to help in this matter. Master Dong wished he could set out his views reasonably, so that the two young people would take note of his hopes — that they two would marry soon and have children. But he could put no reasoning to it; he only knew that a matter even Mr. Chen was unwilling to help with must offer very poor odds.

When two cups of the Shaoxing wine had been drunk, Master Dong asked, as if in passing: “Those days little Wen was at Longhua, were you together with her?”

Chen Qianyuan answered that he was, but that the men's cells and the women's were not together. At that Master Dong was again at a loss for words, and a silence fell over the table.

Dong Huiwen looked up at her father: “They arrested the wrong people.”

Oh, Master Dong nodded. He did not believe his daughter's account: the Communists were people the ordinary person could not meet even if he wished to, and if they had seized little Wen, then she was mixed up in it to some degree. But today was a good day, and one ought to be happy. He asked Chen Qianyuan again: “Were you two schoolmates?”

Today he said everything to Chen Qianyuan, asked every question of Chen Qianyuan; he was doing it on purpose.

“Of course we weren't schoolmates — he's three years older than me.”

“Then how did the two of you come to know each other?” Still he asked it of Chen Qianyuan.

“In the office at the theater.” As Chen Qianyuan said it, he glanced with a smile at Dong Huiwen.

“The nerve, calling it an office — it was a cat's hole.” Dong Huiwen turned to Master Dong. “He was reading on a wooden packing crate.”

“No wonder there was a cat outside battering at the door for all it was worth.”

“You're the cat — a wildcat with a filthy temper.”

Master Dong knew perfectly well that the two of them were flirting on purpose, to draw his attention elsewhere. With his chopsticks he separated out the pig's ears, giving one to Qianyuan and one to little Wen. He hoped that once the two of them had eaten the well-simmered pig's ears, ear-roots and all gone soft, they would be softer too about taking advice.

He raised his cup, meaning to drink a toast with Qianyuan —

Chen Qianyuan rose to his feet, cup in hand; he thought he really ought to toast the old man, for perhaps there would never be such a chance again.

Just then the door was slammed open, and it seemed a good many men had come into the passage by the kitchen; the three of them, cups and chopsticks in hand, turned their heads to look out the door.

It was You Tianxiao, with four or five of his detective-squad men.

He swaggered in and planted himself at the front of the parlor, running his eyes over the several people before him: “You two really can run. On the football field my men were just about to come up to you, and in the blink of an eye you'd run off here. Have you eaten your fill? Drink up that cup and come along with us.”

Master Dong made to rise, but two agents pressed him down by the shoulders, and You Tianxiao said to him: “Master Dong, the great chef. Our Section Chief Mu has eaten your cooking too — he couldn't stop praising your pig's-head meat.”

He looked over the table, reached out and grabbed a piece — a bit of snout from the pig's head — and stuffed it into his mouth, chewing as he spoke: “No need to go searching high and low, either. We've already collected up every cleaver and knife, big and small, on your chopping block.”
