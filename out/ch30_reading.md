## Chapter 30

"This is surely you! This is surely you!"

"Perhaps, but..."

"How could you, without my leave..."

"Only, you see..."

"Do you know how greatly this affects the work?"

It was the first time I had seen Mei Yingzi so agitated; she paced back and forth in the room, not looking at me, not listening to me, so that I could only wait in silence for her to calm down. But she suddenly came and stood before me and said:

"My cultivating of her is no easy thing, you know, and on the very day when she had to be used and could be used, you have utterly wrecked my plan!" She walked off in a temper and came back again, and said: "And besides, I had the concert all prepared. I had only to go and tell her."

"Can't it still be held?"

"What meaning is there in holding it now?"

"Your meaning is..."

"It's the work, the work!" she said.

"But why do you not tell her plainly, and simply have her work for the sake of the work?"

"That would be sure to come out; do you really not believe she is a person who can't keep her nerve?" Mei Yingzi walked off again, stood a while at the window, came back and sat down across from me, lit a cigarette, and said composedly:

"How, in the end, did you persuade her to change her life?"

"At the most I can only admit to having given her a little influence; I certainly did not persuade her, and as for the resigning, I too only learned of it afterward."

"You really did not tell her my purpose in finding her the post?"

"I've told you dozens of times, Mei Yingzi; can you not trust me even in this?" I said. "I long ago plainly declared that I did not approve of your using her in this way; if I really had let it out, why should I not own to it?"

"You know that this is the discipline of the work; whoever breaks it cannot escape punishment."

"But, Mei Yingzi," I said with a cold laugh, "if I held that to break the discipline was right, I would not fear punishment or death, and would have no need to deny it." I rose angrily from my seat and said: "I tell you once and for all, I did not let this out, but that was wholly out of my understanding of the work and my respect and love for you, and not because I feared punishment."

Mei Yingzi fell silent in thought, sitting there quite still without a movement; at last she said, in a very deep tone:

"Then how did you influence her, that her life should so change?"

"Just as you at first used vanity to influence her."

"So," she said with a cold laugh, "this is your private revenge on me, and that is why you must lead her back into the garden of philosophy." But she at once said gravely: "Yet what have you won? Your possession of her — and you have betrayed the work!"

"I have no thought whatever of possessing her; do not insult me," I said. "But standing on the ground of friendship, I naturally wish her to have a faithful life; and even on the ground of the work, I feel there is no reason to have her blindly serve as our tool. I feel that the human rights our democracies strive for — your methods are the destroying of human rights."

"And so you stop at nothing to wreck my plan."

"I knew nothing of your plan at all, so there can be no question of wrecking it."

Mei Yingzi fell silent again in thought. After a while she suddenly said coldly:

"Let us not talk of this now; the most important thing at present is to have her attend Umetake's evening party. Will you take on this work?"

"Yes..."

"That is, to find a way to have her attend Umetake's evening party," she said. "If she does not go, my work cannot be completed."

"You mean that at this party you have some great work to complete?"

"Yes," said Mei Yingzi firmly, but at once with a sorrowful tone: "But if she does not go, I have no way to complete it." Then, in an anxious tone again: "This really has taken me quite by surprise!"

"I will do everything in my power." I believed I had it in hand, and said: "But I hope this will be the last time she is used, the last time she is sent to socialize."

"Agreed," she said, opening her bright, blazing eyes wide, radiating excitement and comfort. "I promise you."

So saying she held out her narcissus-like hand and gripped mine tightly, and in her palm I felt her ardor, her faithfulness to the work, and her kindness toward me. An indescribable feeling welled up within me — as if a kind of regret, regret for my offending words to her just now; as if a kind of shame, that I had suspected myself of being influenced by Helen, and of harboring, unconsciously, a wish to take revenge on Mei Yingzi; as if a kind of gratitude, that in the clash and confrontation of our words just now, Mei Yingzi — my superior in the work, and a woman in her station — should yet have the breadth of heart to forgive me. My eyes grew moist; I did not know in what state of feeling she gripped my hand, her eyes lowered, and then with both hands smoothed her skirt lightly, as though smoothing her own feelings. It was the first time I had seen such an expression on Mei Yingzi, and without reason I felt it a most womanly tenderness; and so I said:

"Forgive me, Mei Yingzi; it is all my fault."

"It is not a question of right and wrong," she said. "So long as you really did not tell her the least of my using her."

"I did not, indeed I did not; believe me, Mei Yingzi!" I said. "But you know, in my conscience I truly do not approve of your using Helen in this way."

"You are in love with her!" she said, wholly in a breath.

"No," I said. "That is not the question. The question is Yamao's ugly behavior that day, and Helen's peril."

Of that matter I had meant to speak to Mei Yingzi last time, but because she took such an outcome as a thing foreseen, and even as a necessary stage, I had not wished to tell her; now, in a kind of sympathetic understanding, I began to tell her the details of that day one by one. But she listened quietly, neither surprised nor moved, and to my surprise, when I had finished, she said lightly:

"So this is what has stirred your deepest jealousy and love?"

"Perhaps," I said. "But not Helen alone; anyone who met with such a thing, I would pity."

"Then do you also pity the fate of those Chinese girls, in the many places that have fallen to the enemy?"

"Of course."

"And have you ever saved them, as you saved Helen?"

"..." I was silent a long while; she watched me all the time, waiting for my answer, and I said: "It is for this that I gave up my study of philosophy, to work with you..."

"And our work is battle, and battle is forever the exchange of a partial sacrifice for the whole victory, of a momentary sacrifice for the final victory."

Mei Yingzi's ice-cold gaze and firm tone sent a shudder through my soul. I fell silent, as though standing before a steep and towering mountain, not knowing how I should take my step; but she began to say, in a wholly forgiving tone:

"For the sake of your private love, Helen's mission shall end after Christmas."

She stood up, and like an empress commanded me:

"Now, you must find a way to have her attend Umetake's evening party."

"Yes," I said. "I think there will be no difficulty in it."

But the next day:

—

"I don't understand." Helen's brows shot up, and she answered me impatiently: "Why must I suddenly go and attend such a gathering again?"

"Because I hear they all know you are in Shanghai, and to refuse so would provoke them to shame and anger."

"I can say that I had meant to go, and then, because something came up, did not."

"Do you know that people will think I am the one keeping you from it?"

"What a joke!" she said. "The only ones who know we are friends are Mei Yingzi, Bai Ping, and Mrs. Stephen."

"But I hope you'll go, just this once; after this, even if you wished to go, I would come to dissuade you."

"You're too full of contradictions!" she laughed doubtfully. "Have you suddenly grown so timid, or are you acting on someone's orders?"

"Orders?" I said. "You mean to say..."

"I mean to say, could it be that your master has sent you as his spokesman?"

"Do you suppose I... that I count it an honor to be a guest at this party?" She fell silent, her brow knitted, her eyes gazing off beyond the sky; under the dark-gray banks of cloud a bleak fine rain was falling. Suddenly she turned and said resolutely:

"I won't go!"

"For what reason?"

"There is no reason; now that I have decided how I mean to live, I don't want to do meaningless things anymore."

Helen's words made me trust and respect her the more. In coming to call on her, I had, in the matter of the work, of course hoped she would consent; but in my ideals I hoped she would refuse. For only in refusing lay the mark of her noble character.

I was silent, not knowing whether I ought to urge her again to attend, or to give Mei Yingzi a negative answer here and now. Among these thoughts I had a beautiful feeling, willing not to trouble Helen further, but also a competitive impulse driving me to a second attempt at persuading Helen; I had my private feeling bidding me respect Helen, but also my work-feeling bidding me obey Mei Yingzi's words; at the last, I thought of the importance of Mei Yingzi's work, thought of the confident assurance I had had at the time, and thought too that the failure of a second attempt would be all the more an expression of the nobility and beauty of Helen's character; and so, rousing my already crushed feelings, I said:

"Helen, my attending is wholly on your account, for your difficulty."

"Yes, and for that I am grateful."

"Then, now that your way of life has changed, does our friendship still stand as well as before?"

"Of course."

"Then," I said, gently and bashfully, "you know, the reason I have come to beg you to attend is that I have a special difficulty of my own?"

"You?"

"Yes," I said. "But I hope you will not ask me the reason."

"But if you want me to go," she said, "I must know the reason for your difficulty."

"The reason has absolutely nothing to do with you," I said. "It is wholly a private difficulty of mine, and only your going can relieve it."

She said nothing, and after a good while, walking a few thoughtful steps, said with a cold laugh:

"You are not thoroughgoing enough!"

"What do you mean by that?"

"You have no reason still to keep up appearances with those people!"

"Helen, set your mind at ease; as for me, please trust me to the full and set your mind fully at rest," I said. "You know the Chinese saying, 'true gold fears no fire'?"

"You are true gold? Perhaps," she laughed. "But if you are true gold, what need is there to pass through the fire?"

"For love, for dreams, for ideals."

"Is that the faithful and brave life?"

"Of course."

"Then for the sake of the faithfulness and bravery of your own life, you bid me give up a faithful and brave life?"

"..."

"When your faithfulness hinders another's faithfulness, and your bravery hinders another's bravery, can you still call it faithful and brave?"

Her words, like a sharp sword, together with her sword-sharp gaze, pierced straight to my heart, and I felt all at once ashamed and wronged, and grew as weak as a child; I dared not look her in the eye, but bowed my head and watched the hem of Helen's dress glide past the gleaming chromium table. Just as I was about to summon the courage to say something, she said:

"I have decided; please do not waste any more words."

I swallowed my words, and like a wounded bird wished in that instant only to leave at once the sword-edge of Helen. Wearily I rose and said:

"Then, thank you; I'll go."

"Does that mean you'll blame me forever?"

"No," I said sorrowfully. "Helen, I shall forever respect your noble will today."

"Then sit a while longer."

"No." As I spoke I walked out into the corridor to get my hat and coat. The hat-and-coat rack stood deep in the corridor, and coming from the bright room it seemed decidedly too dark. Helen followed behind me, and suddenly switched on the light; I saw her in the mirror on the rack, and she too was startled at herself in the mirror. Our eyes met in the glass, but in an instant each of us looked away again, and it struck me all at once that under this lamp there seemed to have risen between Helen and me an unexpected, opaque barrier; an inexplicable sorrow drew tight the muscles of my face, and I put on my hat, tucked my coat under my arm, and came slowly out.

"You're going without shaking my hand?"

I did not answer, but turned back to shake her hand; still with my head bowed, I watched her put out her hand and lay it in mine, and I felt all at once a tenderness like that of our first dance together. She gripped my hand tightly, and in a low, trembling voice said:

"All right, I'll go."

"You'll attend?" I saw a light of sympathy shining in her eyes.

"Yes," she said. "Because it's the first time you have needed my help."

"No," I said. "You ought to live faithfully and bravely."

"But this is the faithfulness and bravery of my life." She still held my hand.

"Thank you, Helen." With a devout heart I bent and kissed the back of her hand.

"Then you'll come to fetch me?"

"That will be a rare honor for me."
