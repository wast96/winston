## Reunion

Toshizō was a fastidious man, and several times in the course of a day he would wipe down his riding boots.

His groom, Sawa Chūsuke,

{j} "Let me be the one to polish them, sir,"

{j} he would beg, but Toshizō would not hear of it.

A man polishes his own gear, he would say.

He seemed to regard his boots as "gear," a part of his arms.

That afternoon too he was wiping them carefully with a scrap of woolen cloth.

His page Ichimura came in and said, "Yamatoya Yūjirō is here to see you."

"Show him in."

Toshizō was working grease into the leather. Blood had soaked into it, and no amount of polishing would take it out.

This Yamatoya Yūjirō was a clerk of the wealthy Osaka merchant Kōnoike Zen'emon, and served as manager of the Kōnoike branch house on Tsukishima in Hakodate.

The tie between the house of Kōnoike and the Shinsengumi was a close one.

It went back to the early summer of Bunkyū 3, soon after the corps was formed, when a band of several rōnin styling themselves goyōtō, "requisition-robbers," broke into Kōnoike's Kyoto shop, and Kondō, Yamanami, Okita and the others, out on their patrol of the city, found them and cut them down in the street.

Afterward, when Toshizō went down to Osaka with Kondō and the rest, they were invited by Kōnoike and given a lavish entertainment.

On that occasion Kōnoike made the corps a gift of uniforms, and presented Kondō with a "Kotetsu."

There was even a wish, on Kōnoike's side,

{j} ──that the corps would recommend a man to manage the house.

{j} so far did it go. In those days of poor public order, Kōnoike no doubt looked to secure its own safety by keeping close to the Shinsengumi.

Even after Toshizō came to Hokkaidō, Kōnoike's good will did not change; word had come from Osaka to the Hakodate house that it was to "extend him every possible convenience."

Yūjirō came in.

In a crested kimono and Sendai-hira hakama, his topknot glossily bound up.

He was still only twenty-seven or twenty-eight, and had a little English.

"You haven't been by for a while, it seems,"

{j} said Toshizō, pulling on his boots and giving Yūjirō a chair.

"Yes, sir; I took the chance of a British steamer, and went down to Yokohama,"

"Oh? To Edo as well?"

"I saw how Tōkyō looks now, too. Daimyō mansions turned into government offices, hatamoto residences with new-government officials moved into them—the old shogunal days are coming to seem, little by little, like the far-off past. The world is moving with a tremendous force, it seems."

"Kōnoike's trade must be busy, then."

"Not at all. Unlike the Sumitomo and such, we had a great deal out on loan to the daimyō, you see. You will have heard that the three domains of Satsuma, Chōshū and Tosa have returned their domain registers. Put politely it is a 'return,' but in effect they have thrust their debts, and all, onto the new government—and the new government says it knows nothing of what was done in the old shogunal days. Among the wealthy merchants of Osaka, five or six houses may well go under."

Yūjirō brought news of the government forces as well. On his way back, when he boarded the British ship at Shinagawa, the government warship Chōyō was pouring out black smoke off Shinagawa. The talk in Yokohama was that the Chōyō was to carry the last body of land troops.

"Bound for Aomori, no doubt,"

{j} said Toshizō, with a sour look. At Aomori the government army was massing, one contingent after another.

"By the way,"

{j} said Yūjirō, without expression.

"I have brought a rare guest with me from Tōkyō. I have had her put up in the inner room of our shop. Her name, you ask? Yes—the lady Oyuki."

"Oyuki.──"

Toshizō shot to his feet with a clatter.

"You're lying. I don't know who you had that name from, but I've no taste for jokes of that kind."

For proof of his agitation, he put the boot-wiping cloth into the breast pocket of his waistcoat.

"Taste for it or no, it is the lady Oyuki and none other."

From the Goryōkaku headquarters to the town of Hakodate is something over a ri.

Pulling the visor of his French-style cap low, treading his stirrups level and holding himself up clear of the saddle, Toshizō walked his horse on alone.

(It cannot be believed,)

{j} he thought.

By Yūjirō's account, when he had called on Okita Sōji at his sickbed, he had heard of Oyuki from Sōji's own lips.

──From time to time, I should like you to look in on her, and see how she does.

{j} In that way, it seemed, Okita had entrusted Oyuki to this Yūjirō.

(Sōji, damn him, dying on me after such a strange piece of meddling,)

{j} and Toshizō, lowering the reins, looked up at the clouds.

They shone like white silver.

Okita Sōji's smiling face rose before him. But it vanished at once.

The nature of this country was too vast and rough-hewn to be a fit setting for remembering the dead.

Coming into the town, he dismounted before the Kōnoike house on Tsukishima and gave the reins to a servant of the house.

"Give him his feed."

The servant seemed to be of mixed Ainu blood. What he was thinking one could not tell; he had large, clear eyes.

Yūjirō met Toshizō at the entrance.

"So you have come, after all,"

...Toshizō looked at Yūjirō with bloodshot eyes.

His face showed that he had not slept the night before.

"On this matter, keep your words few."

A maid showed Toshizō to a separate wing. Built, perhaps, to lodge foreigners, this part alone was a two-story building in the Western style.

The maid withdrew.

Toshizō went to the window. Beyond it lay Hakodate harbor. Steamers, Japanese and foreign, rode at anchor.

Across the harbor mouth ropes had been strung to keep enemy ships from stealing in, and three vessels—the Kaiten, the Banryū and the Chiyodagata—took turns getting under way and circling round and round inside the harbor.

Toshizō felt the presence of someone behind him. He went on looking out the window.

For some reason, he could not bring himself to turn simply around.

"Oyuki?"

He meant to say it; but when the voice broke from him, it had become altogether different words.

"That's the Bentenzaki battery. Gunners man it day and night. When that falls, I suppose my life will end."

Behind him, all went still.

It was as if he could hear even the small beat of Oyuki's heart.

"Should I not have come?"

"………"

Toshizō turned around.

It was Oyuki beyond mistaking. Above her right eyebrow, no bigger than a thread of lint, was the old puckered scar of a burn.

It was the spot to which he had, more than once, pressed his lips.

When he had made sure of it, Toshizō, despite himself, let the tears fall in great drops.

"Oyuki. You came?"

He held her tight. He set his lips to the burn scar.

Oyuki shook her head, no, no. Toshizō remembered that once before she had made this very same gesture.

"I came, in spite of everything. Breaking my promise,"

{j} said Oyuki.

"Say nothing, for a little while.──"

{j} and Toshizō pressed his own lips to Oyuki's.

Oyuki received it in a rapture.

It was a caress the two of them had never shared before.

But in this foreign building, in this town full of foreigners, there was nothing in such an act that felt unnatural.

Presently Toshizō let Oyuki go.

At some point the door had opened. A maid with a childish face, who had brought tea, stood there holding her tray, at a loss what to do with the scene before her, dumbstruck.

"We've made things awkward for you,"

{j} said Toshizō, and with a grave face apologized to the maid.

"N-no, sir,"

{j} said the maid, seeming to come to herself for the first time, in great confusion.

"I'll set it down here."

"Thank you. But I've a favor to ask. Would you borrow me a yatate and a roll of letter paper?"

The maid brought them at once. Toshizō wrote off quickly a note to his page Ichimura Tetsunosuke, to let him know where he was.

"I am at the Kōnoike on Tsukishima. I shall be back at camp tomorrow afternoon,"

{j} it ran.

There was in the hand a certain elegance, like the man himself.

"Have someone carry it to the Goryōkaku at Kameda. Yes—the servant who saw to my horse just now will do. Has he Ezo blood in him?"

"So they say, sir."

The maid, with a frightened look, gave a stumbling sort of nod.

After the maid had gone, Toshizō soon found it hard to breathe.

Taking Oyuki with him, he went out into the town. They walked as far as the landing pier.

"Did you come on that ship?"

{j} said Toshizō, pointing to the offing. A three-masted paddle-steamer, the British flag hanging from her, crouched on the blue water.

"Yes. Kōnoike's Yūjirō pressed it on me so insistently. They said it was five hundred and thirty ri and more from Yokohama, so I nearly fainted at the thought; but it took only four days."

"When do you go back?"

"On the day that ship sails,"

{j} said Oyuki, with a deliberate brightness.

Just then a strangely shaped Ainu boat came by.

Some ten women, and only women, were plying the oars.

Their chant was unlike that of boatmen from the home islands. Sora-enya, sora-enya, it seemed to go.

"What can they be saying?"

"Who knows,"

{j} said Toshizō, cocking his head a little and listening; and then, rare for him, he made a joke.

"It sounds to me like 'Oyuki's regret,'"

"Oh—surely not..."

"Is it not so?"

"To me it sounds like 'Toshi the fool, Toshi the fool,'"

"Both, it seems, are true,"

{j} said Toshizō, and laughed aloud.

Oyuki held down her hem. A wind had come up.

"Shall we go back?"

{j} said Toshizō, urging her. Oyuki began to walk.

"When did your hair become so?"

{j} said Oyuki, looking up at him.

After coming to Hokkaidō Toshizō had cut off his topknot and combed his hair all back. Being thick, it suited him well.

"A topknot makes a cap hard to wear, so I changed to this. When it happened I don't remember. Since coming here, as each day passes I make myself forget that day. The past has no meaning for me any more."

"The past with me as well?"

"That past is different. In the country of that past live Oyuki, and Kondō, and Okita. That is a past beyond price to me. The past that came after is only the stringing-together of one day and the next."

"I don't understand. What is it you are saying?"

"The days in Hokkaidō seem to me to have had no meaning. They may have been so much surplus to my life. Here I have lived by nothing but today, today, today, one after another. Only the future, alone, stands before my eyes with a disagreeable sharpness."

"What kind of future?"

"War,"

Toshizō fell silent a moment.

"The government forces are the ones making my future for me. When they come, the consuls of the several nations are to be notified, and the foreigners each taken off to their own countries' warships in the harbor. After that—war. Ball and blood and powder-smoke. My future has its sounds and colors and smells, all complete, standing here before my eyes."

"On that British ship,"

{j} said Oyuki suddenly. No—not suddenly; the words were ones she must have turned over more than once. ──And yet

{j} "let us flee,"

{j} these words she could not say.

Yūjirō was waiting, and said that supper was ready in the Western-style wing.

The two of them sat down at the table where the meal was laid.

"I shall wait on you,"

{j} said Oyuki; and Toshizō laughed.

"In a Western sort of place like this, it seems men and women eat at the same time. On the ship they'll have fed you foreign food?"

"Yes. But—"

"The beef gave you trouble, I expect,"

"I didn't eat it. And you, Toshizō-san?"

{j} said Oyuki, calling him by his name.

"I don't eat it either. Speaking of beef—Okita hated the broth the doctor pressed on him. I remember that face of his even now."

"And so you won't eat it...?"

"Not quite that. I'm a man with a great many likes and dislikes in food, so new things won't do for me. Kondō, now—he was a good eater. He'd even eat pork. That one turn of mind of his I never could understand."

Toshizō seemed likely to talk on without end. He was himself surprised at his own talkativeness.

Come to think of it, since coming to Hokkaidō with Enomoto and Ōtori and the rest, he felt he had exchanged words with others only a countable few times a day.

"I'm talking a great deal,"

{j} and he hunched his shoulders.

"Ah—a ship,"

{j} said Oyuki, looking out the window.

The harbor had gone quite dark. On that black water a dark hull, its side-lamps lit, was moving.

"She's on watch. It would be awkward if a government warship came barging in on us of a sudden. Though we, too, went out and paid a call,"

"Miyako Bay?"

"You know a great deal,"

"In Yokohama they say it is the foreigners who know the more of it. It was in the newspapers, they tell me."

"But we bungled it. The feel of it is that the shogunate has used up three hundred years of its luck. Nothing we do comes right."

About the time a thin moon began to rise, Toshizō loosed the cords and the sash that bound Oyuki's body.

"No. Someone will come,"

"The door is locked."

The bedchamber was on the upper floor.

The bed and the lamp both seemed to be furnishings off a ship.

"I'll do it myself,"

{j} said Oyuki, struggling. Toshizō, saying nothing, went on with the work.

Presently all that Oyuki had worn lay scattered on the floor, and out of it her naked form was born.

Toshizō laid her over on her side and lifted her up in his arms.

"Tonight I'll not let you sleep,"

{j} said Toshizō, his face breaking into a broad smile.

But a tear fell on the nape of Oyuki's neck. At its coldness her skin took fright. Wide-eyed, she looked up at Toshizō.

(………?)

Oyuki was puzzled. Toshizō was not weeping.

And in the moment of the thought Oyuki's body turned in the air, and presently left Toshizō's arms and sank down buried on the bed.

That day the government fleet, packed with its landing force, sailed from Aomori and was bearing toward Hokkaidō.

The flagship was the Kōtetsu; the second ship the Kasuga; then the Yōshun, the Teibō No. 1, the Hiryū, the Hōan, the Shinpū. The land army had Chōshū troops for its main strength, with domain levies from Hirosaki, Fukuyama, Matsumae, Ōno and Tokuyama.
