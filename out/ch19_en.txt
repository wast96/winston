Chapter 19. A Righteous Rescue of the Worthies
In 1939, Liu Shaowen conveyed Lord Zhou’s instructions and persuaded Chen Zhigao to accept the work of the Central Relief Commission. Before Zhigao took up the post, Shaowen and we together prepared the working staff, proposing that Pan Da (Dacheng) be brought in as a commissioner, Wang Kuisheng as head of the Fujian office, my eldest younger brother Bopei (Zhangchuan) as head of the Qujiang office, Zhang Yilin as head of the cashier’s section, my brother’s wife Zhang Haoran as head of the accounting section, and Gao Boshi as head of general affairs. Pan Da and Gao Boshi were both old colleagues from the First International Shelter. Zhang Yilin had worked with us for seven years; he was prudent, honest, and devoted to his duty, thoroughly reliable. We also brought in Zhao Tianmin, who had been a servant in the household, as an odd-jobs man under Gao Boshi’s general-affairs section. Zhao Tianmin was loyal and diligent; he had taken part in delivering secret letters, and together with Chen Shaoxian, one of A Ying’s men, had packed eight big book-crates to send to the rear; he had been in my home for many years, and though the guests we received were many, he already knew that A Ying, Liu Shaowen, Yu Ling and the rest were important figures in resistance cooperation; moreover, A Ying valued him, and in transferring him to be a staff member we were also using each person according to his ability. Events proved that these staff members whom Shaowen and A Ying so appreciated could indeed share hardship and hard times, keep a tight mouth, and together weather the trials of 1942 to 1944.
In mid-January 1942, after I brought the children and followed Zhigao and his party to Qujiang, Wu Jufang, wife of Li Hanhun, chairman of the Guangdong provincial government, laid on a banquet at the Lizhi Society to welcome us and wash away the dust of the road.
After I reached Qujiang, I asked Zhigao about that “interpreter” who had told me to leave Hong Kong, and only then learned that after Hong Kong fell, the Japanese army had blockaded the sea traffic between Hong Kong and Kowloon and was frantically hunting down patriotic resisters, so that several hundred patriots of great influence in Hong Kong, together with their families, had fallen into danger; Liu Shaowen had been charged with doing all he could to rescue them, and Zhigao too had accepted the task. I told him about meeting, on the road, a warmhearted and helpful Old Madame Huang, whose two sons were East River guerrillas and had escorted us past the Japanese sentry posts so that we could pass safely through the enemy-occupied area. He said he had long been in contact with the East River guerrillas and was trying to find a way to send people into Hong Kong, so that with the help and protection of the East River guerrillas they could rescue the prominent resisters and patriots of every circle trapped there after the fall; to this end he had already wired Xu Shiying, chairman of the Central Relief Commission, saying that he was planning to rescue the well-known figures of every party and faction trapped in Hong Kong, headed by He Xiangning, and that he had trained a group of young cadres and was actively conferring over the arrangements. By that time the Central Relief Commission had already set up relief stations and reception stations in the various counties along the way from Qujiang into the Japanese-occupied areas. Ordinary refugees were seen to by the relief stations. Because Guangdong’s climate is warm, each relief station put up big sheds of bamboo as temporary lodgings for the refugees passing through. Each person was given two dollars a day for living expenses, to be paid again at the next station. The reception stations were responsible for receiving the special refugees of higher social standing whom the Commission sent people into Hong Kong to meet and escort to the great rear, and their conditions were naturally much better.
At this time Yang Huimin — who in Shanghai’s resistance had won fame under the name of the Boy Scouts’ Resistance Support Association when, on behalf of the several million people of the Shanghai concessions, she swam across Suzhou Creek to present a flag in tribute and comfort to General Xie Jinyuan and the Eight Hundred Heroes holding out in the Sihang Warehouse — had also fled to Qujiang, and came to ask to be assigned to resistance and salvation work. I recommended that she lead people secretly into Hong Kong to carry out the mission of rescuing prominent patriots. It happened that Shanghai banking circles also wanted to send in the commissioners Li Tongcun and Zhao Letian, to rush the large quantity of goods left in Hong Kong back to the great rear; they came to Qujiang to see Zhigao and ask for help. So Yang Huimin went along with them, taking a few strong and experienced Relief Commission staff to Hong Kong. When I saw Yang Huimin off, I told her in detail of my own flight from Hong Kong all the way to Qujiang, and I specially prepared a gift, charging her to pass it on to that Old Madame Huang who had had her sons escort us, asking them to go on carrying forward their spirit of loving country and countrymen and to support our special relief work; and I particularly urged her that the first to be rescued and escorted out must be the venerable elder He Xiangning. I told Yang that I recommended her for this heavy charge because of the bravery she had shown in Shanghai in those days, out of full trust in her patriotic spirit, believing that she could surely accomplish this glorious mission. She would carry with her a letter of introduction written by Zhigao in his capacity as special commissioner of the Central Relief Commission, and if she met comrades of the East River guerrillas along the way, she would surely have their support and cooperation. She promised readily, vowing that she would overcome every difficulty and firmly complete the rescue-and-escort mission, and asking me to set my mind at ease. And indeed, with the support and help of the East River guerrillas, they won victory in the very first engagement, bringing the venerable He Xiangning safely back from Hong Kong; they also sent someone to escort the martyr Lin Gengbai’s daughter and part of his manuscripts to Qujiang, and reported the sad news of Lin Gengbai’s death and the fact that his widow Lin Beili, her arm wounded, had been sent to hospital for treatment and would be escorted back to the rear once she had recovered. Only after the victory in the war, when Zhu Libo and her daughter and I met again and talked over old times, did I learn that on the night of December 19, Lin Gengbai had slipped quietly out the back door of my home, been taken for a “spy” by the Japanese soldiers garrisoning the observatory, and shot dead by the roadside — he was forty-five that year! In the haste a coffin could not be found, and his body could only be wrapped in a quilt and hastily buried in the vegetable plot across from my home, with a mark left so that it could later be identified for reburial. Lin Beili’s arm was wounded, and it was thanks to Zhu Libo’s help that she did not bleed too much. When this happened, I was on the flight toward Qujiang with my two daughters, and in Chongqing a false rumor had already spread that I too had died at the same time. Hearing the news, Luo Jianbing wrote a memorial article under a pen name and published it in the supplement of the *Xinhua Daily*. She later told me that Comrade Zhou Enlai had personally added a few strokes to the manuscript, correcting and supplementing it. That a person should learn, while still alive, that leaders and comrades-in-arms had written to mourn her so deeply — how could it not move one, be counted a rare honor? After the victory in the war, Lin Gengbai’s remains were moved for reburial to the International Cemetery in Shanghai, and Mr. Liu Yazi presided over the memorial ceremony; the newspapers called him a patriotic poet of the Southern Society, the glory of a life given for the nation.
He Xiangning was then over fifty, but her legs were ailing and she had trouble walking, and had long gone about leaning on a cane; on the road from Hong Kong to Qujiang, where there was a boat she went by boat, and where there was none, two bamboo poles were used to carry a rattan chair as a sedan, a journey of wind, dust, and weariness. When she reached Huiyang, Zhigao hurried in person to meet her. The moment the old lady stepped down from the chair she sighed: “Ah, I cannot spend the nation’s money to look after me; I must paint and hold a charity sale, and relieve our refugee countrymen together with you!” After reaching Qujiang, she rested only two days before taking up her brush to paint for a charity sale. I marked all her paintings at very high prices. Guangdong’s senior generals, such as Yu Hanmou, had all been students of Mr. Liao Zhongkai at the Whampoa Military Academy in those years, and so, for the paintings Madame Liao made for the charity sale, however high the price, they vied to buy them up — first to show respect and remembrance for Mr. Liao and his wife, and second to make some small gesture of feeling toward relieving the resistance refugees — so this charity sale had great influence and was a great success. He Xiangning also painted on the spot a picture of red plum blossoms and gave it to us, inscribed with the words “A memento for Mr. Zhigao and Madame Dinghui.” This was a very precious keepsake, kept for me all along by Comrade Zhang Yilin, and later brought back to my home in Shanghai, until it was lost during the “Cultural Revolution” — what a pity! After the charity sale, I made another special trip to escort He Xiangning from Qujiang to Guilin in Guangxi, where she was received by Li Zongren and his wife Guo Dejie. The venerable He later went on from Guilin to Chongqing.
In receiving at Qujiang the cultural figures passing from Shanghai through Hong Kong to the great rear, Zhigao gave special treatment to friends of the left-wing cultural world. When Tian Han, Cai Shuhou and their party came by way of the West River, they first sent Zhigao a telegram, and Zhigao at once wired them a sum for travel and wired back his regards. When Yu Ling was leaving Qujiang for the great rear, Zhigao prepared for him a small boat for his sole use, and had also bought twenty eiderdown quilts in Hengyang to comfort the prominent figures in flight; one of them was given to Yu Ling, along with travel money.
At Qujiang, enemy planes came to bomb every day. Early each morning every household would cook the day’s meals, and the moment the air-raid alarm sounded that enemy planes were coming, everyone would run out to the outskirts and hide in a small valley or a wood; some also took small boats and hid in the shade of the trees along the river. To receive these prominent figures, Zhigao had a rather large boat built and named it “Haige,” the Sea Pavilion. This wooden boat had two stories: the lower a bedroom, the upper a dining room that could seat two tables of guests. Many senior Nationalist-government officials passing through Qujiang were also received by Zhigao on this boat. Ordinarily I did not come out to entertain these people. Later some criticized him, saying he wasted the nation’s money. But in fact this way of receiving guests was truly both safer and more economical than putting people up in a hotel.
Among the old people who now come from Taiwan to Hong Kong and the mainland, some still remember that stretch of experience at Qujiang in those years; they say that all along the road from Hong Kong to Qujiang, Guilin, and Chongqing there were Relief Commission reception stations to look after them, and that of them all the care on this stretch, at Qujiang in Guangdong, was the best, with food, lodging, and travel all properly arranged. In three years of relief work at Qujiang, Zhigao was truly diligent, led by his own example, was earnest and responsible, and bore humiliation to carry his burden. He shared hardship with the staff; for compatriots whose homes were broken and families lost to the bombing, or who fled in flight, he saved the dying and healed the wounded. Whenever the air-raid alarm lifted, he would rush at once to the bombed site to take personal command, organizing rescue, and thereby set among the staff the example of leading by one’s own conduct and uniting everyone to work hard together. The distinguished guests he received all drew, as the rules provided, on a special reception fund, with absolutely no extravagance beyond the rules; and as for receiving great numbers of ordinary refugees, he always, again as the rules provided, arranged their board and lodging and gave them ample travel money, with never any skimming to line his own pockets — a thing many people saw with their own eyes at the time.
The Ninth War Zone Relief Commission recruited at Qujiang a group of out-of-school refugee youth and students and trained them to become staff at the refugee relief stations in various places. They were required to understand that serving refugee compatriots was also a contribution to supporting the motherland’s resistance, and that they must never hold a condescending attitude of charity. It was also made clear that in the course of receiving refugee compatriots they must not only do well the logistical service of providing board and lodging, but also urge the refugees to rely on their own efforts to return home and rebuild, so as to lift their spirits and ease the pain of their drifting displacement. The Relief Commission also gave each staff member a short course of military training and simple training in first aid and self-help wound care, to meet the needs of war readiness. These young people all had a higher-primary education or above; most came from poor families and had the sympathetic, mutually helping heart of “all the poor under heaven are one family”; their work attitude was very good, and they made the refugees in flight from every place feel the warmth of being at home. Though Qujiang lay behind enemy lines, the hardship of working and living here was no less than at the front. These young staff members at the Relief Commission’s relief stations gave play to their patriotic feeling, endured harsh tempering, and made this an unforgettable and important station on the road of their lives.
Only later, from documentary materials, did I learn that on the very day the Pacific War broke out, the Communist Party Central Committee separately wired Zhou Enlai, Liao Chengzhi, Pan Hannian, Liu Shaowen and others, instructing them to build a broad and sincere anti-fascist united front with Britain, the United States and other countries, and to evacuate the cultural figures and Party workers in Hong Kong. Zhou Enlai twice wired Liao Chengzhi with his views on evacuating the people in Hong Kong. Under Liao Chengzhi’s direction, it was studied and decided that Liu Shaowen would stay in Hong Kong to take charge of the transfer work. By the reckoning, from December of that year to May of the next, the progressive cultural figures and democratic personages evacuated from Hong Kong by four separate exits numbered as many as eight hundred, and more than 2,000 patriotic youths were also received back into the country to take part in resistance work. Qujiang, a strategic town in northern Guangdong, was the transport hub linking Guangdong with Guangxi, Hunan, and Jiangxi, and with Zhigao’s advantages in manpower and material resources, his strong organizing ability, and his sense of responsibility, a great many people, after leaving Hong Kong, passed through Qujiang and then went on separately to Guilin or Chongqing and elsewhere. The whole evacuation route ran ten thousand li, its footprints spread over eleven provinces and cities. Mao Dun called it “a rescue on a grand scale,” “the greatest work of rescue in the War of Resistance — one might say in all of history.” We had the good fortune, under Liu Shaowen’s leadership and in the name of the Relief Commission, to send work teams secretly into Japanese-occupied Hong Kong, and, with the strong support and help of the East River guerrillas, to escort safely to the great rear, again and again, many prominent patriots of every circle who had been trapped in Hong Kong — including quite a few figures of major influence in both the Kuomintang and the Communist Party, such as He Xiangning, Liu Yazi, Zou Taofen, Liang Shuming, Mao Dun, Tian Han, Xia Yan, Jin Shan, and Sun Ke’s birth mother, and also including the Hong Kong governor of the time and international figures such as American and Soviet pilots aiding China — doing all in our power to take part in this unprecedented work of rescuing the worthies.
When Qu Yingguang (styled Wenliu, a native of Linhai in Zhejiang, who in 1916 had served as acting military governor of Zhejiang), vice-chairman of the Central Relief Commission, came to Qujiang to inspect the work, he gave Zhigao’s work a very good evaluation. Qu also had a group photograph taken together with the Commission’s staff at Qujiang. My eldest younger brother and his wife Zhang Haoran were in the photograph too. During the “Cultural Revolution,” the Red Guard rebels discovered this photograph, and seeing the Kuomintang party emblem and flag in the background and the Commission’s staff all wearing Kuomintang military uniforms, they actually denounced Zhang Haoran as a “remnant scum of the Kuomintang reactionaries” and sent the whole family down to the countryside. Not until after the Party’s Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee was Zhang Haoran rehabilitated.
At this special post of ours in Qujiang, we did all we could to carry out the Party’s anti-Japanese national united front policy, cooperated very well with Li Hanhun, chairman of the Guangdong provincial government, and his wife, and the work went forward with great momentum. Wu Jufang founded at Qujiang a school for refugee children and a women’s production cooperative of the production-for-self-relief kind, which, hidden away in the forest to avoid the danger of the enemy planes that came to bomb from time to time, produced without cease towels, socks, undershirts and other military supplies for the front. When I went there to visit and saw how everything was organized in perfect order, brimming with a spirit of hard plain living and self-reliance, I understood at once that there must be comrades of our underground Party there playing the core leadership role. Only later did I learn that indeed it was Comrade Ou Mengjue who had played a great part there. Mengjue and I had met during the Northern Expedition, and not until after my wrongful case was reversed in the 1980s did I see her again. During the war I published three articles in a Guangdong women’s magazine: one commemorating International Working Women’s Day on the eighth of March; one mourning Zhu Wenyang, Cai Shuhou’s second wife, who had taken part in resistance and salvation activities as a democratic personage of the National Salvation Association and had done work in the Women’s Relief Association in Shanghai, and who wrote very well; and one recounting my impressions of visiting that Guangdong women’s production-for-self-relief cooperative, saying that the women of Guangdong had always been diligent and brave, able to labor shoulder to shoulder with men — carrying sedan chairs, working in the fields, and all kinds of heavy physical labor had women taking part — and now, in the flames of the resistance, they carried forward the spirit of production for self-relief, keeping up production without pause amid the sound of enemy bombing, both supporting the front and maintaining their own livelihood; this spirit is very much worth learning from and carrying forward.
