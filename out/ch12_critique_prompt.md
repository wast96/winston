CHAPTER UNDER REVIEW

Chapter 12. Light and Dark Change Places: A Powerful Apparatus Takes the Governing Stage

Storm the walls, seize the land! Cities, and great cities above all, are a standing temptation to an army. When the Hunan Army attacked Nanjing, held by the Taiping, Zeng Guofan's reward to his soldiers was three days' free plunder once the city fell. Foreign armies were crueler still: the Japanese army carried out the Nanjing Massacre. Yet Mao Zedong was in no hurry to enter the capital; before going in, he wanted his people to prepare first, by studying how a city is run.

But there was another "yet": the cities would not wait. Across every battlefront the towns, large and small, were dropping into the Communists' basket like ripe fruit, faster than anyone could count.

Into the cities, then. And the first task on entering a city was to keep public order, a task that fell to the Communists' security cadres. Were they ready to go in?

1. China's Public Security "Hundred-and-Eight Heroes"

In the second half of 1947, with the tide in northern Shaanxi already turning, Mao Zedong, lodged in Yangjiagou, a small village off any real road, had his eye on the great cities of the whole country. The Central Committee's organization drew up a set of Regulations on Punishing Counter-revolutionaries and began preparing a national conference on security work, meaning to forge the country's security work into a single complete system. The Central Committee sent notice to the Northwest, East China, and North China Bureaus and the Jin-Sui Sub-bureau to draw security cadres to the Central Social Affairs Department for concentrated public-security training, in readiness to take over the great cities across the country.

By the time the cadres had gathered, the Central Committee had reached Pingshan in Hebei. On September 17, 1948, the training class opened at Xihuangni, a small village in Pingshan next door to the Central Committee's seat at Xibaipo and the Social Affairs Department's seat at Donghuangni. The opening ceremony was a grand affair: Liu Shaoqi, Zhu De, and Ren Bishi of the Central Secretariat, and Li Kenong, head of the Central Social Affairs Department, all spoke. The final victory of the revolution, Liu Shaoqi said, was now beyond doubt; the enemy was moving step by step from the open into the secret, while we were moving into the open, and the question now was how to safeguard the victory. The study would center on urban work. Li Kenong stressed how hard urban public-security work would be, and asked them all to be "like the lotus that rises unstained from the mud, like the willow that takes root wherever it is planted."

Held in a country house of earth and timber, this training class was very likely the highest-ranking police academy in the world: its graduates would all go on to head public-security departments at the provincial level and above. The class director was Tan Zhengwen, deputy head of the Social Affairs Department; his deputies were Liu Yong and Long Qian, chiefs of the department's cadre section. The security chiefs of the several strategic regions, Zhao Cangbi, Yang Qiqing, Bu Shengguang, Xu Jianguo, and Yang Fan, led their people in. Students had to be security cadres of county or regiment rank and above, with at least a middle-school education. The course was planned to run two years, a systematic study of security, public-security, and intelligence work. Leaders and students alike set down their experience in earnest and prepared and gave lessons: Tan Zhengwen taught interrogation, Zhao Cangbi political investigation.

Intelligence and security cadres had always lived in secrecy; men in the same organ had never met one another. Now, out in the open at last, and with people from every corner of the country, it was a grand occasion. The roster ran: Xing Xiangsheng, Zhang Zihua, Li Jianping, Feng Ruchun, Yuan Ze, Wu Wenzao (f.), He Shenggao, Hui Xili, Bai Zhenwu, Zhu Jiyun, Gao Ke, Ren Chengyu, Bai Yushan, Yang Yaonan, Mu Fu, Ma Fu, He Ming, Wu Yu, Shi Lei, Feng Wenyao, Jia Zhencao, Chen Zhongkai, Li Yuelin, Yuan Zhenming, Xue Fei, Zhao Lian, Dong Zhongping, Liao Dicheng, Chen Shusen, Xu Xinsan, Zhu Wengang, Xu Du, Shan Zhaoxiang, Sun Qimin, Liu Qiguang, Miao Ruiqing, Zhang Baoshan, Yan Sheng, Liu Hanchen, Song Ding, Tang Guangli, Wang Zengtao, Li Yangyue, Tan Zhaoping, Li Yan (f.), Luo Tao, Tan Zhigang, Cai Qiu (f.), Lü Dai, Li Xuming, Zhang Dengrui, Yang Zhen, Li Huawen, Zhang Duo, Liu Fengqi, Yang Yongxuan, Xie Lizhi, Zhang Minglin, Li Yiting, Li Chao, Wang Pei, Chen Ligang, Ma Fang, Wang Ruonong, Guan Qun, Li Shoubin, Wang Xianxiang, Li Shouting, Tian Zhendong, Liu Jianzhong, and Zhang Chang; then Zhang Jie, Zhang Zhichen, Lü Zaolin, Zhao Xi, Lin Xiang, Duan Cunjian, Li Ruiqi, Wang Zhiguang, Niu Xinfa, Wang Jianbin, Cai Qijiao, Xu Jingci (f.), Xiao Yao, Hui Ping, Chen Tucheng, Wang Wei (f.), Yue Ming, Chen Yunhe, Yang Hao, Yan Jing (f.), Chang Ping (f.), Xiao Ying (f.), Tian You, Mai Lang, Kan Nianyi, Shen Tong (f.), Zeng Yu (f.), Tian Qiming, Li Ruoyu, and Yi Tong. One hundred and one students in all, who with the seven instructors were jokingly called "the Hundred-and-Eight Heroes."

China's public-security Hundred-and-Eight were full of themselves, every one. Consider: the Hundred-and-Eight heroes of Liangshan in the Song dynasty had held no more than a single marsh stronghold, while these Communist Hundred-and-Eight were to take over the police bureaus of every great city in China.

Security work never let you relax. This rare chance to sit down and study was one everyone took to heart. Barely a month in, word came suddenly that Fu Zuoyi was making a surprise strike at Shijiazhuang. Xibaipo had no combat troops on hand, so the Xihuangni class had to be pressed into the line. The students were no novices; they took up their weapons and dug in, digging trenches around Xibaipo, ready to fight a blocking action and cover the Central Committee's withdrawal. Within a few days Fu Zuoyi had lost his nerve and stayed away, and they set down their guns and went back to studying. A month or so more, and the situation came round again. With Tianjin and Beiping about to fall, the Central Committee decided to send the Xihuangni class's security cadres to take over the police forces of those two great cities.

A class meant to run two years graduated in a little over three months, not for want of will to study, but because victory came too fast. Tianjin was liberated on January 15, 1949, and Xu Jianguo, head of the North China Social Affairs Department, became its police chief, leading 815 cadres into the city to take control. Beiping was liberated on January 31, and Tan Zhengwen, deputy head of the Central Social Affairs Department, became its police chief, leading 539 cadres in.

Beiping was a complicated place, and the public-security burden immense. The Social Affairs Department mustered its intelligence chieftains from every quarter: Feng Jiping's Beiping-Tianjin station under the Central Social Affairs Department; Wang Xinghua's Baoman station under the North China Social Affairs Department; Liu Jingping's Pingxi station; Zhang Lie's Pingbao station under the Central Hebei public-security bureau; Li Ning's Pinghan station under the North Yue District Social Affairs Department; An Lin's Beiping intelligence committee for the East Hebei district; Ren Yuan's Ji-Re-Cha intelligence section under the Northeast Bureau's Social Affairs Department; Liu Maotian's Beiping group of the Beiping-Tianjin station under the Ji-Cha-Re-Liao intelligence office; and the East China Bureau's Liu Yunqi station, all gathered under the city bureau's command. The Beiping Municipal Public Security Bureau was strong and well-manned. Tan Zhengwen was its chief; the head of the First Section was Liu Yong, of the Second (investigation) Section Feng Jiping, of the Third (public order) Section Zhao Cangbi, of the Fourth Section Qu Jingxin, and Zhang Tingzhen commanded the public-security brigade; able cadres were sent out to take over the district sub-bureaus as well.

Once in control, the Beiping bureau at once posted notices across the city, opening the registration of reactionary party, corps, and agent organizations. The new regime's power was immense, and it struck fear into the men of the old one. On the morning of February 3 the Liberation Army held its entry parade; that afternoon Xu Zongyao, the major-general station chief of the Nationalist Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Beiping station, came to the bureau and gave himself up, handing over the station's personnel roster and 25 codebooks, and rounding up 312 agents to register. After a brief period of secret registration the bureau opened a public registration office, and by June 3,533 agents had registered in all.

The Beiping bureau's cadres worked at high pitch to receive the Central Committee's entry into the city. On March 25, 1949, Mao Zedong led the central organs into Beiping and reviewed the troops at Xiyuan airfield. He had left Yan'an on March 18, 1947, and crossed the Yellow River from northern Shaanxi into Hebei a year later, on March 22, 1948. Three Marches, and from Yan'an to Beijing Mao had taken only two years.

But on reaching Beijing, Mao did not move into the city proper; he settled instead at the quiet hill of Xiangshan. Security inside Beiping was still a problem.

Inside Beiping were more than 200,000 stray and scattered Nationalist soldiers, along with seven or eight thousand agents from 110 units of the Nationalists' eight great agent systems. Liberation Army military-control cadres were assassinated; the sedan of the Beiping mayor, Nie Rongzhen, was fired on. Nor could the city's hoodlums and bullies be taken lightly. Tianqiao had its Four Overlords of the four quarters, and from street performance to night-soil collection everything was in the grip of the underworld; among the 70,000 pedicab men hid puppet police, bandits, and agents; there were "monkey cabs" that steered clients to prostitutes and "gong cabs" that fleeced their fares; and in the economy there were "gold devils" who trafficked in currency and "grain tigers" who hoarded grain.

To take over Beiping was to take over the capital of a new China; to enter Beiping was to command the realm in the name of a state. In July the Central Committee decided to abolish the Central Social Affairs Department and split the intelligence and security work it had run in two, founding the Public Security Department of the Central Revolutionary Military Commission, and in November the Intelligence Department of the Central Revolutionary Military Commission, turning from the form of the Party to the form of the state, as a bridge to the corresponding government organs to be set up after the founding. Li Kenong became head of the Military Commission's Intelligence Department, Luo Ruiqing head of its Public Security Department.

Luo Ruiqing, head of the Military Commission's Public Security Department, had been head of the security bureau of the First Red Army Corps in 1933 and of the First Front Army in 1935, an old security hand himself. Taking up the post, he merged the Central Social Affairs Department's Second Office with the North China Social Affairs Department to build the Central Committee's public-security apparatus. In Beiping he convened a national public-security conference. Having reached the big city, of course a man wanted a taste of the good life: he treated the provincial public-security chiefs to the rinsed mutton of the Donglaishun. And for entertainment? Liu Jianfu, chief of the East City sub-bureau, took everyone to the Fenghuang Hall, where several dozen people were listening to the comic dialogue "The Opera Buff," with Hou Baolin poking fun at the Yiguandao, and they had a fine time of it. After Mao Zedong and Zhu De entered the city, the only entertainment Luo Ruiqing could produce, for the moment, was still comic dialogue. Hou Baolin's routines were an instant hit in Zhongnanhai and made his name throughout the national public-security system; every national public-security conference thereafter had to have its comic dialogue.

Communists with rich experience in intelligence and security took to urban public-security work quickly. Before long the public order of Beiping was wholly transformed. On October 1, 1949, the Central People's Government of the People's Republic of China held the founding ceremony of the state at Tiananmen Square in the capital, Beijing, a mass gathering of several hundred thousand people, safe and smooth.

The Central People's Government set up a Political and Legal Affairs Committee to lead the country's political-legal work, with Dong Biwu as chairman and Peng Zhen as vice-chairman. It set up a Ministry of Public Security with Luo Ruiqing as minister. Peng Zhen, who oversaw the country's political-legal work, also served as mayor of Beijing, and Luo Ruiqing, the national minister of public security, also served as chief of the Beijing Public Security Bureau; the two took the public-security work firmly in hand together. At year's end the Beijing bureau tallied its results for the year: 9,571 enemy agents and party and corps members arrested, put under training, or brought under control; 407 enemy radio sets and 1,559 firearms seized; 1,285 major cases cracked. The many public-security methods Beijing pioneered were relayed by the Central Committee to the whole country.

2. West! South!

The "country bumpkin" came to town: the deputy chief of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office had become head of the public-order section of the Beijing Public Security Bureau. Zhao Cangbi was not dazzled by the city's bright lights; before long he cracked a counterfeit-currency case, tracing the goods from Beijing to Inner Mongolia and back again until he seized the men who made the notes. Just as this lover of investigative work was about to make his mark in the capital, in May 1949 came a sudden order transferring him away, to Nanjing, as deputy chief of the public security bureau.

The great liberating armies won first in the three norths, the Northwest, the Northeast, and North China, then crossed the Yangtze southward to liberate the south of the country. In the southern campaign the armies of Liu Bocheng and Deng Xiaoping and of Chen Yi and Su Yu converged on Nanjing. Taking over Nanjing, the Nationalist capital, was not something the Communists dared treat lightly: Liu Bocheng, commander of the Second Field Army, took the post of Nanjing mayor himself. Deng Xiaoping wired the Central Committee to have Zhou Xing, chief of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office, specially transferred to be chief of the Nanjing Public Security Bureau. Zhou Xing wanted his old Border Security partner Zhao Cangbi along too; another deputy chief was Liu Binglin, security head of the Second Field Army.

Transferred to Nanjing, the investigation expert Zhao Cangbi found scope for his gifts as ever. Taking over a city generally meant several hard fights, registering reactionary party, corps, and agents, putting public order in hand, restoring economic order, but Nanjing had features of its own. The first thing set before Zhao Cangbi was a case of agents lying in wait. The "Bureau of Confidential Investigation's First Sub-station Hidden in the Capital" had a meticulous plan: its chief meant to work his way into the upper reaches of Beijing's cultural world, its signalers and cipher clerks were hidden among a theater troupe going north together, and even its orderlies and couriers had wormed into Liberation Army units. Yet for all the layered, many-threaded planting the Nationalist agent organs had done before withdrawing, these hidden agents were caught one by one by the Nanjing bureau. At counter-agent work Zhou Xing and Zhao Cangbi were both old hands. Back in Yan'an they had dealt with Juntong and Zhongtong and arrested the agents of Juntong's Hanzhong training class.

Another task was of a quite special kind: Nanjing held the embassies of many foreign states. When the Nationalist government withdrew to Canton, the Soviet and East European embassies withdrew with it, and a few South American states moved theirs to Shanghai; but the United States, Britain, France, India, and others, thirty-two states in all, kept their embassies in Nanjing, with 563 foreigners among them. These states had not yet established relations with the People's Republic, so the Nanjing military-control commission of course did not recognize the foreigners as having diplomatic standing. Still, they did live in China; so they were given the status of foreign residents and placed under the Nanjing bureau's aliens section. Some foreigners, long used to swaggering in China, took no notice of a public-security bureau run by Communists. The French military attaché, Lei Meng, drove drunk and speeding and injured an eight-year-old Chinese child. Stopped by the police, Lei Meng not only refused to get out for inspection but sat in his car brandishing a liquor bottle at them. For Chinese police to detain a foreign military attaché was a novel thing in Nanjing. The French embassy lodged a protest, and some Western embassies seized the chance to make a noise. The Nanjing bureau handled it coolly: the aliens section and the traffic section formed an accident team and brought Lei Meng in for questioning as a matter of course. The *New China Daily* ran the bureau's decision on its front page: Lei Meng was given a warning, ordered to pay all the Chinese child's medical costs, and made to apologize publicly.

The American ambassador, John Leighton Stuart, was a still more sensitive figure. After the Liberation Army occupied Nanjing, the diplomats of many countries hurried away, but Stuart insisted on staying. This former president of Yenching University was one of America's rare "China hands," and he hoped to establish some link with the newborn Chinese regime and so safeguard America's long-term interests in China. Beijing, for its part, sent Huang Hua, a Yenching graduate, to make contact with his "old president." Before long the U.S. State Department ordered Stuart home. At 8:30 on the morning of July 23, 1949, Stuart came to the aliens section of the Nanjing Public Security Bureau and applied to leave the country as an ordinary foreign resident. On August 2 the section chief led seven policemen to escort Stuart onto his plane. On August 18 Mao Zedong published his essay "Farewell, Leighton Stuart!"

Zhao Cangbi was fated to take over China's capitals: he had taken over Beijing, the Communist capital, then Nanjing, the Nationalist capital, and next would take over the wartime "auxiliary capital," Chongqing.

South! South! The liberating armies pressed on without pause, their long lances pointed straight at the nation's frontiers. South! South! Communist cadres bustled like figures on a revolving lantern, taking over cities large and small. Nanjing's police chief changed hands fast indeed: Zhou Xing, who had entered the city in April, left in October; Long Qian, security head of the Third Field Army, took over; Long Qian left again in December, and Chen Long, deputy head of the Northeast Social Affairs Department, took over.

The Communists' intelligence and security cadres went out from Yan'an to the whole country. Zhao Cangbi, who went early, took over three "capitals"; Zhou Xing, who went late, took over two; and Li Qiming, who had held on all along at the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office, at last got his chance at a "capital" too. Taking over the whole of the Border Security from Zhou Xing, Li Qiming led its cadres to set their sights on the Shaanxi provincial capital, Xi'an, the ancient capital of six dynasties.

Yan'an and Xi'an: for years the two cities had stood for the Communists and the Nationalists, and Yan'an's intelligence and security cadres had had their eyes on Xi'an not for a day or two, nor a year or two. From early in the War of Resistance the Central Social Affairs Department had kept a secret intelligence station in Xi'an; its chief was the underground Party member Wang Chaobei, and its deputy chief none other than Li Maotang, director of the Nationalists' Shaanxi Bureau of Investigation and Statistics. The Northwest Social Affairs Department built up an intelligence force of more than 130 people in Xi'an, penetrated the Nationalist Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Shaanxi station, and held partial control of Xi'an's Juntong organization.

As the great cities fell one after another and Nationalist troops lost the will to fight, the Nationalist garrison at Xi'an withdrew in haste, handing the city's defense to a citizens' self-defense corps. Intelligence men of the Central Intelligence Department's Xi'an station contrived to obtain the post of deputy commander of that corps. The Bureau of Confidential Investigation, meanwhile, ordered that no one be cut from the rolls, and used every kind of tie to go to ground. The Second Section of the Xi'an Pacification HQ deployed a "Huang Xueyu action team" of a dozen men, meaning to carry out assassinations and demolitions once the Liberation Army entered the city.

Xi'an was liberated on May 29, 1949, and Li Qiming, deputy head of the Northwest Social Affairs Department, together with Su Mingde and office director Yu Sang, entered the city and at once traced the enemy's dispositions. Won over through an intelligence connection, Gao Yao, chief of the Second Section's communications group, informed on the "Huang Xueyu action team" and on the Second Section's lie-in-wait groups across Shaanxi. The Northwest Social Affairs Department acted decisively, seizing them within a few days and giving the agents no chance to wreck anything.

Taking over was no one-time affair; the enemy agents were always trying to strike back. In mid-July, Wang Hongjun, chief of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Shaanxi station, and Wang Keyi, chief of its action section, fled from Xi'an to Hanzhong and, with a "Northwest special-skills group" as their core, put together a twelve-man "Xi'an action team." Through an inside line the Northwest Social Affairs Department got the roster and cracked the whole "Xi'an action team" within three months, keeping Xi'an safe. When Hu Zongnan and Ma Bufang plotted a counterstrike on Xi'an, the Northwest Social Affairs Department learned their battle plan in advance, and the Liberation Army laid a great trap for them along the route two weeks early. In the early days of taking over Xi'an, the Communists destroyed two provincial offices of the Nationalist Zhongtong, two stations of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation, 190 groups, 4,164 people, and 62 radio sets, and broke up lying-in-wait organizations of two stations, 49 groups, 273 people, and 21 radio sets.

The Yan'an cadres who had taken Xi'an were not content with the liberation of that one city. They split their forces two ways and struck out for the great Northwest and the great Southwest.

When the First Field Army liberated Lanzhou, liaison-department head Fan Ming hurried to find a Uyghur merchant named Aimaiti. The Comintern had handed this agent over to the Communists, and so, marching into Xinjiang, they had a reliable guide. The South Xinjiang intelligence station was set up at Kashgar, with Wang Yantang as chief and Yan Fu as deputy. The Qinghai intelligence station was set up at Xining, with Yu Kai as chief and Li Kunshan and Liu Xinghan as deputies. Yang Yindong and Chai Jiqun went all the way to Canton to set up a station.

The Qinghai station took the initiative in establishing contact with the Panchen Lama's establishment, handing the reply telegrams of Chairman Mao and Commander-in-Chief Zhu to Ji Jinmei, the establishment's chief of protocol. Ji Jinmei treasured them like a windfall and rode day and night to carry them to the Panchen's seat at Xiangride. From the Panchen's Khenpo council hall a telegram went to Chairman Mao and Commander-in-Chief Zhu, asking the Central Committee to "send a righteous army at once and liberate Tibet." In January 1950 the Qinghai station's Zhang Jingcheng and Xu Liping were the first to enter Tibet, bringing back the first official letter from the Tibetan Kashag government to the Central Government. In May three living Buddhas of a Qinghai peace-persuasion mission entered Tibet, and the Qinghai station sent a radio set along with them. The Border Security cadre Huang Bin, transferred to the Military Commission's Intelligence Department, set off in July 1951 with a group of Tibetan cadres and a radio set, going by way of India with Zhang Jingwu into Tibet to open telegraphic contact between the Central Committee and Tibet.

With Tibet and Xinjiang peacefully liberated, the South Xinjiang station and the Tibet office turned by degrees to foreign work. Huang Bin later became first secretary at the embassy in Burma. From Xinjiang, the couple Yan Fu and Huang Youqun later went to the Chinese embassy in Norway, Yan Fu as political counselor and Huang Youqun as first secretary.

As Hu Zongnan's forces fled south in haste, the Northwest Social Affairs Department ordered the intelligence force planted among them to keep on their trail. Wherever Hu Zongnan fled, they followed, until the enemy army was wiped out for good.

Liu Fengxian, a lieutenant-colonel inspecting officer at the headquarters of the Seventh Supply District of the Combined Logistics HQ, built a secret group and followed Hu Zongnan into Sichuan with a radio set. When Hu Zongnan fled on toward the Xikang region and the troops left to hold Chengdu lost heart, at the critical moment Liu Fengxian came out openly in the name of the Communist underground and took over Chengdu. On December 30, He Long, commander of the First Field Army, and Li Jingquan, its political commissar, led their troops into Chengdu, where more than two thousand Nationalist military-academy cadets lined up to welcome them.

Liu Bocheng and Deng Xiaoping led the Second Field Army up the river into the Southwest and pitched camp in Chongqing. Deng Xiaoping became secretary of the Communists' Southwest Bureau; Zhou Xing became head of its Social Affairs Department and chief of Southwest public security, with Zhao Cangbi as his deputy and Yu Sang as secretariat chief. The dispatch section's chief, Yu Bingran, had once lain hidden inside Juntong, and Cheng Yonghe inside Zhongtong, both old intelligence hands, only short of new blood. Just as they were short of people, underground Party members began reporting in to the newly hung-out shingle of the public-security bureau.

After the East Sichuan underground Party was wrecked, the organization gathered more than a dozen Party members of Sichuan origin from various places and sent them back to Sichuan to rebuild. Zhu Yongkang, of the southern branch of the Beiping underground, returned to his old home in Sichuan just in time for the liberation of the whole province. Zhao Cangbi examined Zhu Yongkang himself and set him the task of drawing a map of Chongqing; seeing that the young man had a real feel for intelligence, he assigned him to the dispatch section.

By the end-of-1949 tally, in the cities taken over across the Northeast, North China, East China, Central China, and the Northwest — Beijing, Tianjin, Shenyang, Changchun, Nanjing, Shanghai, Jinan, Wuhan, Taiyuan, Xi'an, and others — the Communists broke up 177 enemy-agent organizations, arrested 4,046 agents, and seized 666 radio sets.

From Yan'an to Beijing, from Beijing to the whole country, every great city had a police chief who had come from Yan'an.

The public-security cadres out of the yellow earth were of high enough quality, and the new regime found its footing in the great cities too.

3. Which Country Fought Terror First?

In the new situation of holding power nationwide, the Communists promptly adjusted the structure and staffing of their intelligence and security work.

Intelligence and security were separated. The Social Affairs and Intelligence Departments of the Central Committee and of the Party committees at every level were abolished; intelligence work was given to the Military Commission's Intelligence Department; security work was handed to the public-security departments of governments at every level; and the Military Commission's Public Security Department became the Ministry of Public Security of the Central People's Government. With this adjustment the Communists' intelligence and security work passed from the working form of the Party to the working form of the state, more in keeping with the rules of governing.

On October 15, 1949, half a month after the founding ceremony, the Ministry of Public Security convened a conference of senior public-security cadres in Beijing. This gathering, later called the First National Public Security Conference, drew the Central Committee's close attention: Mao Zedong received the delegates, and Zhou Enlai, Zhu De, Dong Biwu, and Nie Rongzhen took part in the discussions.

Zhu De put it to the conference that, once the Liberation Army had finished its bandit-suppression task, it would become a national-defense army, and a public-security force would have to be built to guard the peace and livelihood of the whole people.

Zhou Enlai stressed in his speech: "On public-security work hangs half the safety of the state. The army is kept ready but unused; you are needed every single day."

The conference studied Mao Zedong's instruction on public-security work: "We must learn both to struggle with the enemy in the open and to struggle with him in secret. If we do not attend to these problems, do not learn to wage these struggles against these enemies and win them, we will not be able to hold power, we will not be able to keep our footing, we will fail." And: "After the enemy with guns has been destroyed, the enemy without guns still remains, and he is bound to fight us to the death; we must on no account take these enemies lightly. If we do not pose and grasp the problem in this way, we will commit a very great error."

From then on these words became the motto of China's public-security system.

During the conference, the Government Administration Council approved the plan Luo Ruiqing put forward: a public-security department in each greater administrative region's people's government, a public-security bureau in each province's, a public-security bureau in each centrally administered city; a public-security office at the prefecture level, a public-security bureau at the county, a public-security assistant at the district, and a public-security officer in each village. Under the Ministry of Public Security were six bureaus and one office: the Political Security Bureau, the Economic Security Bureau, the Public Order Administration Bureau, the Frontier Defense Security Bureau, the Armed Security Bureau, the Personnel Bureau, and the General Office — 486 cadres in all. Wang Jinxiang held the Northeast, Li Qiming the Northwest, Zhou Xing the Southwest, Li Shiying East China, Bu Shengguang Central China, Tan Zhengwen South China, Yang Fan Shanghai, Chen Long Nanjing, Xu Jianguo Tianjin, and Luo Ruiqing Beijing concurrently. The youngest of the regional public-security chiefs, Li Qiming, was only thirty-six.

When the greater administrative regions were abolished in 1953, most of the regional public-security chiefs were transferred to work in Beijing, and the Ministry gained a set of deputy ministers of very solid standing — Yang Qiqing, Xu Jianguo, Wang Jinxiang, Zhou Xing, Wang Jinshan, Wang Dongxing, Liang Guobin, Li Tianhuan — jokingly called within the trade "the Eight Vajra Warriors." A Central Public Security Cadre School and local police schools were founded. As need arose, public-security departments were set up in the railway, transport, forestry, and civil-aviation systems. A People's Public Security Force was built as well, and its commander, Luo Ruiqing, was granted the rank of Senior General in 1955.

Mao Zedong, who by habit grasped the big things and left the small alone, among all the ministries of the Government Administration Council took foreign affairs, national defense, and public security regularly and directly in hand. From Yan'an to Beijing, the several public-security chiefs mostly came from the army, cadres Mao knew. From Shanghai to Yan'an to Beijing, the several intelligence chiefs mostly came from the old Special Branch, cadres Zhou Enlai knew well. On September 27, 1950, in his summary report to the First National Economic Security Work Conference, Mao Zedong wrote: "Security work must give special emphasis to the leading role of the Party, and must in practice be under the direct leadership of the Party committees; otherwise it is dangerous." Though in its working form public-security work had passed from the form of the Party to the form of the state, in essence it still emphasized the leadership of the Party.

The summing-up of intelligence work, by contrast, went on in quiet.

Li Kenong held both the headship of the Military Commission's Intelligence Department and a deputy ministership of foreign affairs in the Central People's Government, and within the Party served as secretary of the Central Intelligence Committee, with overall charge of the country's intelligence work. Li Kenong organized the drafting of *A Preliminary Summary of Twenty-two Years of the Communist Party's Intelligence Work*.

First, intelligence work must uphold the principle of the Party's absolute leadership. It must oppose any practice that abolished or weakened the Party's leadership over intelligence work, and equally oppose setting intelligence work above the Party's leadership. This principle plainly differed greatly from the principles of intelligence work in other countries. In capitalist states such as the United States, and in socialist states such as the Soviet Union alike, intelligence agencies held great privileges, often using the special nature of their work as a pretext to slip free of Party and government oversight, becoming the secret tool of some individual political leader, or growing into a tail too big to wag.

The report stressed further: second, intelligence work must uphold the principle of putting the political foundation first. With the political foundation first, the united front would draw intelligence along in its train. Peace and development were the common task of the peoples of the world; by winning the sympathy and support of the world's broadest masses, one could form a broad international united front. The report laid down expressly: never resort to bribery with money, seduction by beauty, or intimidation by the gun.

Seen now, this rule has all the more power to startle the deaf and wake the dull.

The nature of intelligence work is exceedingly special, and people generally suppose that to obtain intelligence one may use any means at all: buying "informers," making use of "beauties," committing "murder" are ordinary moves. In practice, the intelligence agencies of many countries, the United States and the Soviet Union among them, often do just this. And so intelligence work turns "dirty," departing from the moral norms current in society. A spy whose morals have collapsed can hardly keep faith; and so intelligence work loses the righteousness the state requires of it. This tendency, indulged by the politicians of many countries, at last swelled in the early twenty-first century into a tide of "terrorism" that swept the world. Only then did the international community reach a consensus: the whole world must oppose terrorism as one. And at this point people cannot help recalling: who was the first to oppose terrorism?

As early as 1950, China's intelligence world laid down expressly: no bribery with money, no seduction by sex, no assassination or intimidation. This was the earliest "counter-terror" rule anywhere in the world.

But if you do not practice terror, others will. The public-security organs of the new China now faced a fresh wave of it.

The Nationalists, beaten back to Taiwan and low in morale, found their agent organs stirring with excitement instead. "Before, the Communists were in the dark and we in the light; now that we are in the dark, we can turn from the passive to the active!" In the secret trade, security work was called passive, negative work, while intelligence, sabotage, and the like were called active work.

Just as Yang Jie, a delegate to the new Political Consultative Conference, was preparing to hurry to Beiping for the session, Ye Xiangzhi, action chief of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation, went out himself and had Yang Jie assassinated in Hong Kong. Taiwan's agents even slipped onto the mainland meaning to assassinate the mayor of Beiping, Nie Rongzhen, the mayor of Shanghai, Chen Yi, and the mayor of Canton, Ye Jianying; the cat-burglar Duan Yunpeng was given the task of killing Mao Zedong.

On June 25, 1950, the Korean War broke out, and that very night the United States rebuilt its intelligence cooperation with Chiang Kai-shek. "The Third World War has begun!" Nationalist forces lying dormant across the mainland rose up one after another; near Chengdu in Sichuan the bandits openly revolted, killing a senior Liberation Army cadre, Zhu Xiangli. This Zhu Xiangli was none other than the man who in earlier days had penetrated the Japanese army as chief of the Linfen intelligence station.

Light and dark had changed places, and China's public security faced a new challenge.

Under Mao Zedong's personal direction, the whole country launched the great Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries, beating down the enemy agents' arrogance. Drawing on a tradition of solid grassroots work, the cities large and small mounted a mass defense and mass control that left agents slipping in from abroad nowhere to stand.

The new China not only opposed terrorism in its policy line but created successful experience in fighting it.

4. Even the Police Chiefs Were Purged

On April 3, 1955, Pan Hannian, deputy mayor of Shanghai, was arrested on a charge of "treachery from within"; on April 12 Yang Fan, chief of the Shanghai Public Security Bureau, was arrested; the two cases together were called the "Pan-Yang case." Not until 1982 were the two men rehabilitated and cleared. People say the "Pan-Yang case" was the new China's first unjust, false, and wrongful case.

In its political and social impact, and in the rank of those involved, the "Pan-Yang case" may perhaps be called "the first"; but in the time of its occurrence, there was a case before it. In January 1951, Chen Bo and Chen Kun, deputy chiefs of the Canton Public Security Bureau, were arrested, shaking the whole public-security front; the case was called the "Two Chens case," and only in 1980 was it rehabilitated. Even if this "Two Chens case" cannot be pronounced the new China's very first unjust and false case, it can be judged the first in the public-security system after the founding of the state. The "Chen Bo" of these "Two Chens" was that same "Bu Lu" famous in Yan'an.

Bu Lu's real name was Chen Bo. Because he was a Cantonese, he had been specially transferred to the South China Sub-bureau under Ye Jianying to take part in taking over Canton. Back in his old Guangdong home, Bu Lu resumed the name Chen Bo and became chief of the Canton Public Security Bureau, with Chen Kun of the South China Sub-bureau as his deputy. Chen Kun was the intelligence chief of the Communists' South China and Hong Kong Sub-bureaus and held many local agent connections, including Liang Xia, who had penetrated Zhongtong, and Cheng Changqing, who had penetrated the independent brigade of the security police as its commander.

Canton, too, was the Nationalists' last "capital" on the mainland. Li Zongren had fled Nanjing with the National Government and made his final stand in Canton. As the Liberation Army marched into South China, Nationalist officials who had already fled to the ends of the earth had nowhere left to go: some followed Chiang Kai-shek to Taiwan, some followed Li Zongren into exile in Hong Kong, and some lingered in Canton. On October 14, 1949, the Liberation Army entered Canton, and Chen Bo went in with the Communists' South China Sub-bureau to take control. Canton bordered British-ruled Hong Kong, and one could pass freely between the two; Southeast Asian Chinese came and went constantly, Nationalist agents mixed among them, Hong Kong dollars and American notes circulated in the markets, and the local underworld disturbed the public order. Next to other cities, Canton was a place of stranger lights and shadows. Before long Tan Zhengwen was transferred south to be chief of South China public security and concurrently chief of the Canton Public Security Bureau, and the former chief Chen Bo was made his deputy. The public-security cadres of those first years after the founding feared no difficulty; they threw themselves into the work in high spirits. Cheng Changqing, the underground Communist who commanded the independent brigade of the security police, led his men over in revolt and helped the cadres coming south take over the Canton police smoothly. The two Chens put together a special work team, with the Guangdong cadres Zhang Qiang and Liu Tie as its chief and deputy, and in under a week seized Canton's notorious bandit chiefs Zhang Shu and Guan Song. This special work team got quick results, yet it also stirred some controversy: its makeup was rather mixed, and some men used their authority to extort the common people, and even killed their own by mistake.

In January 1951, the Ministry of Public Security decided to investigate Chen Bo's conduct in the takeover of Canton, and Chen Bo and Chen Kun were arrested for examination; the Two Chens case drew in no few Canton public-security cadres as well, and Cheng Changqing, Liang Xia, and others were held for examination. In August 1953 the Beijing military-control commission passed judgment on Chen Bo: "lost his revolutionary standing, shielded counter-revolutionaries, gravely broke the law and discipline, and caused the Party and the state grave losses." He was sentenced to ten years' imprisonment and five years' deprivation of political rights.

In the autumn of 1954, the Ministry of Public Security opened a case against the Shanghai Public Security Bureau for having "reused, shielded, and covered" agents and counter-revolutionaries, and arrested the "lurking Nationalist agent Hu Junhe." At year's end it detained the Shanghai bureau's chief, Yang Fan. Before this, Li Maotang of Xi'an had been transferred to Beijing to break an opium habit and had likewise lost his freedom. On the night of April 3, 1955, the Ministry arrested Pan Hannian. In 1961 the former Xi'an police chief Wang Chaobei was held for examination.

The "Two Chens case" and the "Pan-Yang case" were the two great cases of China's public-security system in the early years of the state, and both were rehabilitated in the 1980s.

Luo Ruiqing, the first minister of public security, once said in those early years that agent work was like a knife: used well, it could kill and wound the enemy; used badly, it could hurt oneself. Some old public-security men recalled that when a particular department in a particular place used enemies to investigate enemies, and the measures were improper or the procedures incomplete, faults and errors resulted, which others took for questions of enemy and self, making for years of unjust imprisonment. Some sighed that public-security work was like walking on a knife's edge, a high-risk trade. But many old hands held it more accurate to call it a high-difficulty trade: the three-word rule of public-security work was "steady, accurate, ruthless," and to do all three at once was truly hard, and called for a superb art of work.

High-risk or high-difficulty, the public-security work of the new China did in the end achieve superb results: efficient at catching agents, sound in public order, China became the safest great country in the world.

5. "Smash the Police, Procuratorate, and Courts!"

To speak of the importance of intelligence and security work, there is one more telling proof: the bad men fear security work.

Before the "Cultural Revolution" arose, Luo Ruiqing was brought down in December 1965 and Peng Zhen in May 1966. Toppling "Peng, Luo, Lu, and Yang" cleared the road for the movement. Lin Biao and Jiang Qing were deeply wary of political-legal work. The case of the anonymous letters written to Jiang Qing, the case of the anonymous letters written to Ye Qun, the case of the listening device planted at Mao Zedong's residence, all had been handled by public-security organs, and all had come up at last to Luo Ruiqing, the minister of public security, and Peng Zhen, head of the Central Political-Legal Small Group. On each of these cases Peng Zhen had acted in keeping with law and policy and had not made a great fuss. Forever hunting a chance to persecute people, Lin Biao and Jiang Qing had not got their way, and, afraid too that their own secrets might be seized, they hated the public-security system all the more. When the political movement rose again, they seized the moment and struck first. In his speech of May 18, 1966, Lin Biao held forth on the danger of a coup, alleging that Lu Dingyi held the pen, Luo Ruiqing the gun, and Yang Shangkun the Central Committee's secrets, and that Peng Zhen's hand reached further still: they meant a coup, and heads would roll!

In the "Cultural Revolution," Lin Biao, Jiang Qing, Xie Fuzhi, and others openly raised the cry: "Smash the police, procuratorate, and courts!" Peng Zhen, who oversaw political-legal work, and Luo Ruiqing, who had once overseen the Ministry of Public Security, were brought down; the Ministry bore the first brunt. Deputy ministers Xu Zirong, Yang Qiqing, Wang Jinxiang, Ling Yun, Yan Youmin, Yu Sang, and Liu Fuzhi, along with a batch of bureau chiefs and deputies, were thrown into prison one after another, and of more than a thousand cadres in the ministry only forty rebels were left. The cadres of the procuratorate and the courts were purged as well, and even their whole working systems were abolished. Kong Yuan, head of the Central Investigation Department, was imprisoned; his standing deputy Zou Dapeng was persecuted to death; and his successor Luo Qingchang was struggled against too. The first department head brought down in the Liberation Army's General Political Department was the head of its Security Department, Shi Jinqian; the department's deputy heads Gu De, Hao Su, and Li Ping were put on special-case files. The Beijing Public Security Bureau was branded a "counter-revolutionary clique," and more than a thousand of its cadres held in isolation for examination. Wang Jian, chief of the Shanghai Public Security Bureau, and Wang Fang, chief of the Zhejiang provincial public-security department, who had handled the case of the anonymous letters to Jiang Qing, were both thrown into prison. Across the country, 34,000 people in the public-security organs suffered attack and persecution; of them, 1,200 were hounded or beaten to death, more than 3,600 maimed, and more than 1,300 arrested and jailed.

The special nature of intelligence and security work became, instead, a crime laid to the cadres in charge of it. Peng Zhen, Feng Jiping, Liu Yong, and others, for having turned Nationalist agents to their own use, were branded a "spy-and-agent network." Zhao Cangbi, Li Qiming, and others who ran the public-security work of a whole province were, over the same turning question, raised to the plane of "contradictions between enemy and self." The counter-espionage work Ling Yun had built up over years at the Ministry was brought to a halt, and Wu Nanshan, who had rendered great service in cracking the case of Juntong's Hanzhong training class, was branded a Nationalist agent all over again.

Errors already corrected in the past came back to life: people purged in the Rescue Campaign, though vindicated at the time, were purged again in the "Cultural Revolution," the charge still the wrong verdict of those years. Wang Zunji, Zhao Xiaochen, Wang Huai'an, and others all went through this. Pan Hannian, Yang Fan, Bu Lu, and others, purged in the early years of the state, who should by then have served their terms and been released, were instead thrown into labor-reform farms.

The public-security and judicial fronts were paralyzed, public order slipped out of control, and grave, knotty cases came one after another. Tan Furen, chairman of the Yunnan Revolutionary Committee, was assassinated in his own residence, by, of all people, a section chief of the military-region security department; and in the course of the investigation the head of that security department killed himself. The old security hands Zhou Xing, Cai Shunli, and Zhao Cangbi gathered in Yunnan to crack the case, and Li Qiming was sent later, but with the movement's interference a full conclusion was hard to reach. Li Zhen, minister of public security, killed himself, and the case drew in his deputies Yu Sang and Liu Fuzhi, who were arrested, another strange affair.

Look back at the several great errors in this history: in the campaign to purge counter-revolutionaries, the security-bureau system had wielded privileges above the Party committees of its own rank and bore heavy responsibility for the errors of that purge; in the Rescue Campaign, the security system, as the executor, bore a share of working responsibility for the errors too; and in the "Cultural Revolution" even the public-security and security system itself was "smashed."

This line of development showed that China's intelligence, security, and public-security system was becoming more and more an obstacle to "Left" errors, more and more the object of bad men's hatred. And this, in reverse, proved that China's intelligence, security, safety, and public-security work was growing more and more mature, moving onto the track of the rule of law.

6. Excavating the Cultural Gene

The main matter of this book is the intelligence and security work before the founding of the state, and this is really a matter of excavating a cultural gene. It must be granted that, among all the trades, the Communists' intelligence and security work was rather outstanding, its record fine. In it, of course, lie both experience and hard lessons. The cultural gene formed in this period would carry on, and would shape the work of the years in power too.

The intelligence and security work after the Communists took power nationwide still has several great books in it waiting to be written.

In sending troops into Korea, Mao Zedong had felt out America's hand beforehand. The Volunteers won the First Campaign by surprise, while the American commander MacArthur still judged that the Chinese would not dare cross the Yalu. The head of China's intelligence line, General Li Kenong, directed the armistice talks at Panmunjom from behind the scenes, over two years and ten days, five times broken off and five times resumed, until an armistice was reached at last. Is the intelligence work of that war abroad not a new book in itself?

Chiang Kai-shek, withdrawn to Taiwan, schemed to strike back at the mainland and tried to build a "guerrilla corridor" along the southeast coast. From October 1962 to September 1965, thirteen armed Taiwanese agent teams slipped onto the mainland by air and by sea, all of them dropping into a net long since spread for them: 617 agents, not one escaping. Nationalist remnants withdrawn beyond the border lorded it over the Golden Triangle and probed constantly against Yunnan. The Liberation Army reconnoitered beyond the border and fought beyond it, driving the Nationalist troops back to Taiwan. This frontier-defense fighting had new methods of its own too.

The Taiwan agent organs, over the Spring Festival of 1962, the Spring Festival and May Day of 1963, and the National Day of 1964, plotted a "wrecking climax," ordering agents of every system to "use any means, fear no sacrifice, count no cost" and carry out large-scale demolition and sabotage. The Central Committee gave the instruction: no bomb was to reach Canton, none was to get past Shaoguan. The Ministry of Public Security and the Guangdong provincial department guarded closely and cracked case after case; on the busiest single day they caught agents in twelve separate cases. These counter-agent stories were more vivid than the movies.

When Zhou Enlai went abroad to the Asian-African Conference and Liu Shaoqi went abroad to visit Cambodia, both faced assassination attempts by the Nationalist agent organs. China's intelligence and public-security departments worked closely together, calling on intelligence connections at home and abroad, and safeguarded the leaders' safety. That story could rival any invented foreign blockbuster for suspense.

The Ministry of Public Security successfully remade Japanese war criminals, Nationalist war criminals, and Puyi, the last emperor of the Qing; the Investigation Department and the United Front Work Department won back the returning "acting president." This humane achievement of turning enemies into friends stood at the highest level in the world.

In 1964 China successfully exploded its first atomic bomb, a thunderclap that split the sky. Amid all the prying of foreign intelligence agencies, China's making of the bomb had somehow kept its secret. When top-secret drawings once went missing at the test base, Premier Zhou demanded the case be cracked in three days; case-cracking experts from the Ministry and the army flew in by special plane and cracked it the very next day.

Even during the "Cultural Revolution," when the public-security system suffered such vast wreckage, there was one successful counter-espionage case: the Soviet embassy's Li Hongshu spy case. Zhou Enlai directed it himself, and it came off to perfection.

After the "Cultural Revolution," the work of the whole Party, country, and army in implementing policy is well worth pondering carefully too.

The building of the rule of law is a thing still in progress. In the early years of the state Mao Zedong presided over the drafting of the 1954 Constitution; then came the "lawless and heaven-defying" years of the "Cultural Revolution"; after the Party's Third Plenum of the Eleventh Central Committee came the restoration of democracy and legal construction, the open trial of the Lin Biao and Jiang Qing counter-revolutionary cliques, the drafting of the 1982 Constitution, the invention of direct grassroots democracy, the taking shape of the policy of governing the country according to law...

Governing in the open did not do away with secret work; after the founding of the state the weight of intelligence work shifted abroad, to prevent the wrecking done by hostile foreign forces and to wage counter-espionage.

The greatest sound is faint, the greatest form has no shape, the greatest spy has no name. How many nameless heroes are still waiting for us to record...

Intelligence, espionage, security, counter-agent work, safety, public security, justice, the rule of law: these have always been the heart of writing history, and a treasure-house for art and letters as well.

Success or failure, it always stirs the heart. Experience or hard lesson, it always gives one deep pause.

The Communists' intelligence and security work deserves to be summed up and set down; China's national-security work needs the lessons of history.

Let those who dig for buried treasure not forget: China's most precious treasure-house is, in truth, its cultural gene.

- [The French military attaché, Lei Meng] The French military attaché's name survives here only in Chinese transliteration (雷蒙, Lei Meng); his name in French is not established, so the romanization is provisional.
- [Farewell, Leighton Stuart!] John Leighton Stuart (1876–1962), American missionary-educator, founding president of Yenching University, and U.S. ambassador to China from 1946 to 1949. Mao's essay “Farewell, Leighton Stuart!” (August 1949) mocked him as the embodiment of a failed American China policy; it long remained a set text in Chinese schools.
- [contact with the Panchen Lama's establishment] The tenth Panchen Lama (1938–1989), the second-ranking figure of Tibetan Buddhism after the Dalai Lama, whose seat was then at Xiangride in Qinghai. The xingyuan was his personal establishment or court; the Kashag was the governing council of the Tibetan government in Lhasa.
- [granted the rank of Senior General in 1955] In the People's Liberation Army ranks conferred in 1955, da jiang (“Senior General”) stood just below marshal; ten officers held it. It outranked shang jiang (“General”), the rank later cited for Li Kenong.
- ["the Eight Vajra Warriors."] Ba da jingang, the eight guardian-warrior kings of Buddhist iconography; used here half in jest for the ministry's eight formidable deputy ministers.
- [earliest "counter-terror" rule anywhere in the world] The author's claim, and a rhetorical one. No external scholarship treats a 1950 internal CCP intelligence guideline as the world's first counter-terrorism measure, and “counter-terrorism” in the modern sense is anachronistic here. UNCORROBORATED; the passage reflects the author's framing, written after the 2001 attacks, rather than a documented first.
- [Light and dark had changed places] The chapter's title, ming an yi wei: the “light” (the open, the state) and the “dark” (the hidden, the clandestine services) trade places, as the Party's once-secret apparatus comes into the open as the public-security organs of a ruling state and, in the campaigns that follow, is turned against its own.
- [the great Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries] The zhenfan campaign of 1950–1953, which the author presents here as a triumph of public order. Its scale was far bloodier than the text lets on: deputy public-security minister Xu Zirong's 1954 report counted about 712,000 executed, 1,290,000 imprisoned, and 1,200,000 placed under control; Mao himself later spoke of some 700,000 killed. Historians' estimates of the executions run from about 500,000–720,000 at the low end to two or three million. CORROBORATED as a mass campaign of execution; the book's framing omits the toll.
- [China became the safest great country in the world.] The author's partisan verdict. Low reported ordinary crime in the Mao years coexisted with waves of state-driven political violence — the very campaigns and purges this chapter goes on to describe — so “safest” measures only one kind of safety. The judgment is the author's, not a neutral finding.
- [the new China's first unjust, false, and wrongful case] On Pan Hannian, see the notes at Chapters 2 and 9. He and the Shanghai police chief Yang Fan were arrested in 1955 and both were posthumously rehabilitated in the 1980s. That the author — himself a sympathetic insider — flatly calls these “unjust, false, and wrongful cases” reflects the settled post-Mao verdict.
- [Toppling "Peng, Luo, Lu, and Yang"] Peng Zhen, Luo Ruiqing, Lu Dingyi, and Yang Shangkun, four senior officials purged together in 1965–66 as the “Peng-Luo-Lu-Yang anti-Party clique,” an opening move of the Cultural Revolution.
- [Smash the police, procuratorate, and courts!] The Cultural Revolution slogan za lan gong-jian-fa: smash the gong (public security, the police), the jian (the procuratorate), and the fa (the courts). Mao spoke of having the legal apparatus “beaten to a pulp”; from 1967 the procuratorate and courts effectively collapsed and the security leadership was purged wholesale. CORROBORATED as to the slogan and the wholesale purge; the casualty figures that follow are the public-security system's own post-Mao reckoning.
- [excavating a cultural gene] The book's closing figure, which answers the mining metaphor of its Preface: the “cultural gene” is the inherited character — the habits, methods, and instincts — that the author sees carried down through the Communists' intelligence tradition. The image is his own, meant as heritage rather than biology.
- [the Asian-African Conference] The 1955 Bandung Conference. En route, an Air India plane, the Kashmir Princess, chartered for the Chinese delegation, was destroyed by a bomb planted by a Nationalist agent; sixteen people died, but Zhou Enlai had changed his travel plans and was not aboard.
- [won back the returning "acting president."] Li Zongren (see above in this chapter), who had been the Republic of China's acting president in 1949, returned to the mainland from exile in the United States in 1965, a propaganda coup the CCP's united-front and intelligence organs worked to bring about.
- [the greatest spy has no name] The first two clauses adapt Laozi (Daodejing, ch. 41): “the greatest sound is faint of voice, the greatest form has no shape” (see the note at Chapter 6). “The greatest spy has no name” is the author's own coinage completing the parallel.
