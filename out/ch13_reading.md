## Chapter 13. The Pipa Weeps Blood, a Tearful Feud

Who am I?

A-Chu had asked himself the question a thousand times and more.

I am a foundling, a castaway child.

So A-Chu told himself, over and over, without wearying of it.

Cast away by whom?

In twenty years A-Chu had never once been able to strain from his heart the shadow of that “abandonment.” Who was it that had cast him off? His father? Or his mother? Was it done in helplessness past all remedy, or by deliberate design?

Twenty years, and not one single person had given him an answer to satisfy him. In blood-kinship, in the ties of family, he had utterly lost all faith; the pain hidden away so long had forced him to face reality, to give up entirely the suspicious search for his roots.

And yet—why? Today someone had gone to elaborate pains to arrange for him to come to a storytelling house, to sit and listen to a fragment of secret history, broken and incomplete. Every line of the chant, every word, was without exception a coded hint delivered sidelong.

A-Chu knew that someone had deliberately set the whole prologue in order for him, and waited only for him to step onstage in his paint and costume. Clumsy though this person’s stagecraft was—all of it the stalest, most jaded of tricks out of the Romance of the Three Kingdoms—still the “play” was performed to fine effect, and the one who set the scene had already attained her purpose: and that person was Fourth Madam Rong.

She meant to tell him a tale of the long-buried past—which was, perhaps, the riddle of his own unknown origins.

A-Chu felt his spirits suddenly weighed down; the nearer he drew to this so-called “truth,” the more he was seized by a nameless dread and an unbearable anxiety.

Within the storytelling house the pipa sounded again, and that beautiful storytelling woman began, as though weeping and pleading, to sing a passage of “Visiting Qingwen.” The man had already made his bow and left the stage; A-Chu felt that Fourth Madam was about to lay her cards on the table before him. Since it was so, then let him strike first himself—at the least he would not be led about by the nose.

A-Chu went out into the lobby of the Dongfang Hotel and asked the attendant, very civilly. “May I ask—has a Madam Rong reserved a room here?”

“One moment, please.” The attendant took the guest register from beneath the counter and searched it. “I am very sorry; there is no Madam Rong who has reserved a room here.”

“Then anyone named Rong? Is there any guest by the surname Rong?”

“A guest named Rong? There does seem to be one.” The attendant checked the register. “There is a Mr. Rong Chu who has reserved room 202.”

“Rong Chu?” A-Chu was utterly bewildered—this room was reserved by himself? He drew the register over to look, and sure enough, there in plain writing stood the name Rong Chu. Was there some mistake?

“Thank you.” A-Chu turned and made for the lift.

“Room 202.” A-Chu stepped into the lift and told the attendant.

The attendant nodded with a smile, drew the lift door tight, and bore his passenger up.

At the door of room 202 stood two men in short jackets; on seeing A-Chu they bowed low with great respect and invited him in, as though there were about A-Chu some soundless, formless power to overawe.

The door was pushed open, and the one who came to meet A-Chu was none other than the young storyteller of the house—a handsome, exceedingly dashing big boy.

“I had thought I might sit here withering to the end; I never dreamed you would truly come.” His words were modest, his manner courteous and respectful.

“How long have you waited?” A-Chu asked, going straight to the point.

“Neither long nor short—twenty years.”

“Twenty years?” A-Chu bore down on the stranger before him with a searching, scrutinizing eye, and said, in a tone of scorn: “Twenty years? Twenty years ago, how old were you? If you mean to stand before me spinning some preposterous tale, and come here to swindle by bluff and bluster, then you have chosen the wrong mark.”

The young man laughed.

“What is there to laugh at? I see nothing laughable in it.” A-Chu turned and cast his eye over the whole layout of the room. The chamber was plain—only two chairs and a table, and on the table lay a pipa and a small three-stringed sanxian; plainly it was a place where the players of the pear-garden troupe rested and practised. Besides this, he noticed that the suite had yet another door. “This room was reserved by you?”

“It was.”

“And your honourable surname?”

“Your humble nephew is Rong Chu.”

A-Chu gave a cold “Hah” of a snort. “You are Rong Chu; then who am I?”

“Who are you—can it be you have forgotten it all? Surely you must have some dim, blurred memory of it?”

“Do not go chancing your luck, thinking to cut open the scars within my heart and dig out some secret of my origins, and so make off with a haul of money—”

“You misunderstand me.” Rong Chu signalled to A-Chu not to work himself into a passion. “That I asked you here was, from the first, my mother’s wish. My mother is your own flesh and blood—can it be you have no wish to see her, to ask her for the truth of the matter?” Seeing A-Chu’s mood soften a little, he cupped his hands and said: “Please, take the seat of honour.”

“Host and guest are distinct. I am the guest, you are the host,” said A-Chu.

“Elder and younger have their order. The mountain, high as it is, is not higher than the sun—please, take the seat of honour.” Rong Chu spoke neither haughtily nor humbly, holding firm and yet courteous and mild.

“Very well.” A-Chu declined no further; being come, he would set himself at ease. “In the storytelling house just now I heard only the first half of the tale, broken and incomplete, and dark with gathering clouds; it would seem the latter half of the story . . .”

“The latter half of the story is, of course, thick with the air of slaughter. I wonder which passage you would care to hear?” Rong Chu, in a most refined manner, actually took up the pipa; his fingertips plucked lightly, the strings stirred, and there came a clear, bright note.

“I know what you mean to tell—no more than ‘aunt and uncle in adultery,’ ‘murdering the brother to take the sister-in-law,’ and, of course, ‘an orphaned star’s tears of blood’ thrown in besides. You know, those immoral, unlawful doings are the very things spectators take an interest in. But in them I have no interest at all. I want to see your mother; I want to hear her tell this tale thick with the air of slaughter.” A-Chu said. “And not to hear some apocryphal Hamlet.”

“Then—to obey is better than to defer.” Rong Chu called out in a loud voice: “Open the dragon gate!”

A door in the suite swung wide open.

The uncanny scene within it turned A-Chu’s hands and feet to ice.

White mourning-banners fluttered; plain white flowers were heaped upon the dust. Four men in heavy mourning stood bowed and solemn on either side, and in the very centre sat Fourth Madam, robed in sackcloth and mourning-white, a pipa in her arms, her expression one of grieving sorrow; and there too was that mysterious old nun, draped in black gauze, holding a black spirit-tablet, standing behind Fourth Madam.

It was too strange, too eerie.

A-Chu, without willing it, stepped across that mysterious threshold, his body and soul alike carried over into this hall of spirits where the ghosts made their dwelling. In the very moment of his wavering and hesitation, the door behind him was shut by Rong Chu—as though every road of retreat were gone.

Fourth Madam drew her slender fingers hard across the strings, and the pipa gave out a sound to cut gold and sever iron. Her tears streamed down; hoarse and cracked of voice, she began to sing. “Yang Mulian dons sackcloth and the deepest mourning! Pity me—my Yang family’s blood-feud runs mountain-high! For your sake I swallowed shame and disgrace to be a lowly concubine; for your sake I cast my own flesh and blood aside like grass by the roadside. I waited for you—waited for you to grow, waited twenty years—waited till the spring was spent and the blossoms fell in the slanting sun . . .”

Utterly at a loss, A-Chu asked: “What are you, in truth, to me?”

“I am your very own elder sister, born of one mother, of the same womb!” As Fourth Madam sang to this point, the string snapped and the note broke off. Her eyes swollen and red, she pointed to the spirit-tablet on the table and said, in a rasping voice: “Yang Muchu! The spirits of our father and mother are here! Down on your knees at once!”

For the first time—the first time anyone had called him out by his full name and surname; the first time he had known who he was. It was too sudden; it made A-Chu, on the contrary, unable to believe it. “It is not possible.” A-Chu wanted to flee. “You are making sport of me?”

“If this were sport, there is no crueller sport in all the human world.” Fourth Madam herself was the first to kneel, and gave a cry of weeping to the tablet. “Father, I have brought A-Chu back without so much as a hair of him harmed. Father! The wronged and bloody debt of these twenty years is about to be made plain before the whole world! Father! Let your spirit protect us—protect A-Chu, that he cut down the foe, restore the honour of our gate, and raise the Yang house up again!”

A-Chu knelt down facing the tablet, and saw plainly the writing upon it: “The spirit-tablet of our late father Yang Yubo.” He was thunderstruck—stricken to the heart! If his father was Yang Yubo, then who was the Yang Yubo who had once come to his consulting room to be treated?

The same name and surname? Sheer coincidence? The knot of riddles in A-Chu’s heart surged up like a churning tide.

“Tell me the truth, my . . . sister . . . I have lived twenty years in a blur and a daze, and you have hidden and dodged for twenty years. Why? Tell me. Tell me.”

To trace back a sorrowful past takes more than courage alone.

“Have you the courage to cut your own path and walk it alone? Have you the high, overbearing spirit that bows to none? Have you it, or have you not?” Fourth Madam asked.

“I do not know. But I do know that I have a right to know my own origins. I beg you, tell me; hide the truth no longer. What, in the end, is that truth?”

The truth?

Fourth Madam gave a wan, bitter smile.

“I will tell you all the truth,” said Fourth Madam, her voice soft and dark. “But do you know? Once the truth is laid bare, never again will you pass this life of yours in calm and grace. You will hate me, you will reproach me—I am a woman gone mad and lost to all feeling. To reach my end I would sacrifice everything; I have no choice, and neither have you any road of escape—for fate has held the mastery of our lives . . .”

Fourth Madam began to tell of a secret hidden away for twenty years.

Our father was a “red-capped merchant” of the late Qing, and our mother the only daughter of the chief of the “Golden Dragon Society,” the great overlord of old Shanghai. They met and fell in love in a seminary in France, and their union was full of drama and of every kind of romance. In those days father and mother loved each other dearly; our family carried on the fine traditions of our forebears—fond of studying every sort of new learning, in love with classical music, in love with life itself.

As a child I dearly loved every kind of Western doll; I would dress them in the most splendid little gowns and lavish evening dresses, and take them to the court balls.

The Empress Dowager was very fond of the foreigners’ dances; the eunuchs and palace maids, to suit the Old Buddha’s taste, all put on Western dress and performed the “waltz” for her—and the comical thing was that a long queue always trailed down over the collars of their Western coats, for all the world like a tail they could not shake off. Our father, being versed in foreign affairs, was much prized and favoured by the Empress Dowager, and so I was once granted the honour of dancing before her at the Golden Gate. In my sixteenth year, mother gave birth to a pair of twins—my dear little brothers, you and A-Ci . . . came into this world so full of light, and of hidden darkness too.

That same year, on a sudden whim, the Empress Dowager bestowed on father an eighteen-year-old palace maid to be his concubine; father could not refuse the Dowager’s kind, generous favour, and so brought this palace maid home to the Yang house, gave her the station of a secondary wife, and made her our new “aunt-mother.” When she first came she was very proper, and lively and open of nature too; being close in age, she and I soon became the dearest of friends. We shared father’s different affections between us, and shouldered together the care of our sickly mother.

Until one time, when father came home from doing business in Germany, he brought back for the aunt-mother a beautiful pair of crystal slippers. I was very jealous; several times I begged them from her, but she would not give them up—she said they were a gift of “love,” she said she would treasure them forever.

There was a rift now in our feeling and our friendship.

Mother died—of illness. Grieved as father was, he still had at his side an aunt-mother lovely as a flower. Not so with us: you and I felt utterly the loneliness and helplessness of it. And yet you were but two years old; the freshly opening bud does not yet know the sorrow of losing the flower’s calyx.

Mother, like a splendid shooting star, vanished forever from the world of our lives, and another sinister, treacherous wolf appeared—and that was our uncle, Yang Yuhua.

Yang Yuhua was a son of a concubine, and in the illustrious house of Yang he had no standing. In his youth his life was loose and unbridled, sunk in wine, women and every dissipation. Fearing he would go wholly to the bad, father spent a great sum to send him abroad to study; he used the money father gave him to travel the countries of the world, and in the end chose to enrol at an institute of finance and economics in Japan. It amazed me that such an idle good-for-nothing, a wastrel who neither studied nor practised any craft, could actually complete his studies without a hitch, and come home crowned with honour.

He came back like a child who had turned from evil and returned to the right, a very model of “the prodigal come home, worth more than gold.” He fawned upon father; the two brothers often sat knee to knee in long and intimate talk—from father’s management of the business to the whole story of how the Society was founded, from father’s friends to the important men who worked under father, he had it all off pat. He did not care to make a show of himself; he liked to wrap himself up and hide away at home. He never went to any social gathering, and avoided too much contact with outsiders. His one and only hobby was collecting Western paintings. At first I thought him merely skilled at protecting himself, careful not to flaunt his success. Only later did I understand that he did all this with a clear and deliberate purpose!

He was master of three languages, and his talk was full of wit and humour. His extraordinary eloquence drew us deeply to him—and just as easily captured the restless, stirring spring passions in the aunt-mother’s heart.

I have no wish to paint again the sordid tale of “Ximen Qing and Pan Jinlian” in their vile coupling—though it happened in our very own family. Uncle and aunt-mother tore off their masks of pretence; dry tinder to a blazing fire, they burned in the flames of lust.

The aunt-mother, to curry favour with me, actually gave me that pair of crystal slippers. She had forgotten everything she once loved. In casting off those shoes, she cast off too the “love” that father had given her.

Father was too good and kind. He believed that his own brother, his own beloved concubine, would never in this world betray him. He did not know that, besides betrayal, they were brewing an even greater, crueller, more monstrous plot! They meant to take his place! They would not shrink from soaking their hands in the blood of their own kin, if that was the way to seize riches and to make another’s life their own.

Father would not believe his daughter’s reasoning; he held that, having lost my mother, I was jealous of the aunt-mother. We began to quarrel, and were very unhappy with each other.

I began to loathe this family, and made up my mind to leave this house. The one who gave me the confidence and courage was the man I loved—his name was Han Zhengqi.

He was a man of military background, and a member of the “Golden Dragon Society” too; he stood high in the Society’s regard. He loved me dearly, but father would not consent, for he had once been a driver in the Yang house, and in father’s eyes he was no more than a lowly menial not worth a mention.

Our love was true and sincere, yet it could win no recognition from our elders—and could never, either, win their blessing.

We resolved to go and seek our own happiness—to leave home, and go in pursuit of the joyful life that should be ours.

On a spring night in the second year of Xuantong, the night was utterly still. There was no moon in the sky. Zhengqi and I had agreed to meet in the garden and settle the great plan of our flight. But there on the lawn I heard a most terrible sound of spades; it puzzled me greatly—the household gardener would surely never plant flowers and shrubs in the dead of night—and I made up my mind to go and see what it was.

And do you know what I saw?

I saw our father’s corpse, laid out in the cold, dank earth. Our uncle and the aunt-mother were burying their guilt beneath the pear tree!

They had killed our father!

They had made orphans of us!

Their hands dripped with father’s blood!

And their next step was to kill you and me!

For at that moment you had been set down in a cradle, less than ten metres from father’s body. So sweetly were you sleeping that for a long time afterward I never dared ask you: had you ever heard that terrible sound of the spades? They had meant to bury the two of you together. Their one miscalculation was that they had not reckoned on my coming.

It was Han Zhengqi who saved me! And saved you as well!

The very moment it happened, he rushed to my side, and with his strong arms stifled my terrified screams.

He told me the “Golden Dragon Society” was finished; on a quiet afternoon my grandfather and his Society had been wiped out by a band of Japanese of unknown identity. They were dead—like a shoal of fish that had swallowed poison, floating belly-up, taken and fried in the pan.

To keep alive, we fled, carrying you. While they were bent single-mindedly on digging the pit for father, we lifted you out of the cradle and carried you away. You were so good; in all that time of flight you never cried once. In that moment I had no time to search for A-Ci; as for him, I could only leave him to heaven’s mercy.

Not half an hour after we had gone, our dwelling was put to the torch. Beneath the broken beams and fallen pillars lay still the bones and unquiet ghosts of the servants who had not got away in time.

My nurse, A-Yue, my wet-nurse—she was one who lived through that great fire; but her face was ruined by the merciless, poisonous flames. With her own eyes she saw the aunt-mother carry off our little brother, A-Ci. For we could not all die; they needed a child to serve as their cover, they needed to build a whole and proper family. Only so could they make the reign of blood and terror die down again to calm as fast as could be.

He had it all planned from the moment he came back.

Everything was within the grasp of his hand.

They sent men to hunt us down and kill us; we had nowhere to hide, no road left to flee.

Han Zhengqi settled us in a small inn, and the nurse cared for you; you were very hungry, you were badly in need of nourishment. But we had no money.

And then Han Zhengqi vanished.

I do not know whether he was alive or dead—nor whether he had flinched and drawn back at the last moment—nor where in the end he was. He left not a single word behind, and vanished from before my eyes like a gust of wind.

At the very same time, the newspapers carried the report that the Yang house had burned—a mansion set ablaze through a servant’s careless use of fire. And they gave out that the Yangs’ second master, Yang Yuhua, together with the young lady and the eldest son of the house, had all perished in the fire. The master, Yang Yubo, in his boundless grief, had therefore taken his wife and his youngest son and gone traveling in Europe, hoping to free himself the sooner from the sorrow of losing his kin; the Yang family mansion, it was said, would be rebuilt within half a year, and so forth.

At last I saw it all clearly.

The whole course of the thing, from beginning to end.

Uncle and father were very alike in looks; the house having met with so great a catastrophe, the master’s face was bound to be withered with grief, and Yang Yuhua’s cheeks ran a little thin—the very thing to let the plum wither in the peach’s place. The whole family goes traveling in Europe, comes home again after a year or so, and Yang Yuhua passes himself off, openly and in broad day, as Yang Yubo.

And we had become the living dead!

Someone wanted us dead!

Time and again we were hunted to be killed, time and again we escaped from the very jaws of death. We had to go on living, and we needed money more still, needed it to keep your life safe.

When the nurse learned that the master of the Rong house in Shanghai was about to take a concubine, we set our minds on it: we needed a haven from the storm, we needed a good place to live. Otherwise we could not bear the heavy burdens of life. In those days I even thought of becoming a slave, a servant, a whore! I too would live on, and raise you to a grown man! I gave up all a person’s dignity, so that one day I might exact the debt of blood!

We passed ourselves off as a family come from Shandong to Shanghai to seek a livelihood, whose menfolk had by ill chance fallen sick on the road and died. There were left only the two of us, mother and daughter, alone and with none to lean on; all the money we carried was spent, and so we hoped to find the daughter a husband’s family then and there, and ask a bride-price, that we might scrape together the fare and bear the coffin home to be buried in our native place.

Lies and tears won us sympathy and help.

And so, according to plan, I married into the Rong house.

The nurse and you, having now a handsome sum for your keep, went into hiding for a time.

Three years later I gave birth to Rong Chu. So that you might enter the Rong house without a hitch, I laid a careful scheme. I had the nurse carry Rong Chu away, and feigned the grief of a mother who has lost her child, seeming out of my wits with it. And then, as a matter of course, I brought you in through the gate of the Rong house.

Why did I contrive it so painstakingly? Because I want vengeance! I want you to kill them with your own hands! To kill them with your own hands! I want to watch, together with you, as this pair of dogs, this man and this woman, turn to bubbles before our eyes, are ground into ash!

And the one condition for making you a successful avenger is . . . that you must receive a fine education. The Rong house could accomplish that—and, so far, has accomplished it very well.

The “Golden Dragon Society,” though it was dealt a crushing blow, still has its scattered soldiers and stray riders. At any moment, in any place, they wait upon your call. You are the true master of the Yang house; you are the Society’s new leader. Yang Muchu!

In these twenty years I have sacrificed every fair world that was mine. Twenty years I have waited for you to come—to wake the nightmare, to wash away the old shame in blood, to avenge the wrong and right the injustice.

Can you do it?
