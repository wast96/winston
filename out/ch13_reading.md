## Kiyokawa and Serizawa

The village of Mibu lies in the western outskirts of Kyoto—a single cluster of old temples, rural-samurai houses, and farms; but because in the age of the court it had been the heart of the Suzaku Avenue, it keeps somewhere about it a breath of antique grace.

Toshizō and the eight stalwarts of the Tennen Rishin-ryū line were quartered on the house of Yagi Gennojō in Mibu village.

"A splendid house,"

{j} Kondō was overjoyed.

Sure enough, the building was better than any grand house Toshizō knew in Bushū. Pillars, alcove-floor—choice, single-selected timbers had been used in plenty; and the front and inner gardens had an elegance to make a connoisseur tremble.

"Toshi, look—this is a famous garden,"

{j} the rough-hewn Kondō came out onto the veranda and gazed his fill. He would learn later that far from a famous garden, of gardens of this order Kyoto had enough to sweep away by the broomful, and

{j} "Kyoto is a fearful place,"

{j} would wear a complicated look; but for now he only stared wide-eyed.

"Say, Toshi,"

{j} Kondō turned. Toshizō, standing and looking out at the garden,

{j} "That 'Toshi'—hadn't we best give it up now?"

{j} he said. Come to Kyoto, Kondō somehow smelled of the soil. And soiled, he looked, unexpectedly, a smaller man.

"Then what am I to call you?"

"Have the goodness to call me 'Hijikata.' In return, I'll call you 'Kondō,' or 'Master Kondō.' It will feel a little awkward at first, but with these things the form is what matters. We are no longer country bumpkins of Bushū. I mean for us eight comrades, too, to build up a clean, ordered rank, measured by age and by ability."

"A good thing."

"You, of course, are the chief."

"So."

{j} His face said, naturally. From his days as a boy gang-leader Kondō had never once stood second.

"But in return, you must bear yourself with a chief's weight, unshakable."

"But, Toshi, I'm a plain trooper."

In fact he was. On setting out from Edo, Kiyokawa Hachirō, in consultation with Yamaoka Tetsutarō and the others sent by the shogunate as overseers, had fixed the corps' establishment and appointed from among the rōnin such officers as unit-heads and inspectors; but of Kondō's faction, from Kondō down, every man was a plain trooper.

Such is the sorrow of the unknown.

Among the officers was, as the most contemptible instance, Yūten Sennosuke. He had been a gambler. Having enlisted at the head of a great train of the hired guards and henchmen he kept, he naturally became corporal—unit-head—of the Fifth Unit.

Besides him, only the frivolous sort (so Toshizō thought)—Negishi Yūzan, Kuroda Tōmin, Niimi Nishiki, Ishizaka Shūzō, men who peddled hollow reputations among the expel-the-barbarian rōnin of Edo—took the officers' posts, and wore the master's face as though they had won the realm. Glib in their patriot's debating, but draw a sword and the likeliest end was that their legs would give under them.

(A foolish business.)

Even on the road up to Kyoto Toshizō had scarcely exchanged a word with such men, glaring them down now and then with the whites of his eyes, so that they found him uncanny.

(A mob of crows, this. Sooner or later the hoops will spring and it will fall to pieces.)

He would wait for that moment.

Toshizō's struggle had already begun. With the Tennen Rishin-ryū line of Bushū he must seize the power of this band.

(And how is that to be done?)

{j} Toshizō sat thinking, an ill-humored face on him all day long.

The corps was billeted about in scattered lodgings.

The Mibu houses had been requisitioned; the headquarters was the Shintoku-ji.

Beyond that they had taken over the temple-samurai Tanabe house, the rural-samurai Nakamura, Ide, Nanbu, Yagi, Hamasaki, and Maekawa houses, and the great farmers' houses besides, so that the narrow single village of Mibu seemed to overflow with eastland-accented rōnin.

That evening—

that is, the evening after their arrival on the twenty-third—a messenger came flying from the headquarters at the Shintoku-ji to the Yagi house where Toshizō and the rest lodged, and,

{j} "Master Kiyokawa will speak in the main hall of the Shintoku-ji. Assemble at once, if you please,"

{j} he called, and ran off.

"Hijikata, what would it be?"

{j} Okita stayed his chopsticks.

They were all eating in Kondō's room. Every tray bore a dish of pickled Mibu greens.

A vegetable unknown in the Kantō.

A variety of Kyoto greens (mizuna), deep green in color and coarse of leaf and stem, yet tender to a subtle crispness under the tooth.

—Good.

{j} The one who, again and again, ordered the Yagi housemaid to bring him more of it was Yamanami Keisuke. Toshizō despised him for it.

Not food alone; Yamanami praised anything whatever, so long as it was of Kyoto.

—The imperial city indeed. Coming here, I feel to the bone what rude eastland barbarians we are.

{j} he said, over and over. The Mibu greens Yamanami so extolled Toshizō pushed to the far side of his own tray and would not touch with his chopsticks.

(Rude barbarian, and welcome. Who could eat pickles as saltless as these?)

To be sure, at bottom it was no distaste for the food in Toshizō. It was distaste for Yamanami.

"What—Master Kiyokawa?"

{j} Yamanami stayed his chopsticks. This cultivated man, being cultivated himself, revered the learned and eloquent Kiyokawa Hachirō.

"Come, gentlemen, let us go."

"We're still at our meal,"

{j} said Toshizō.

"There's no call to hurry. Master Yamanami, Kiyokawa Hachirō is not our master. He is no more than an organizer. Let him wait."

"Hijikata,"

{j} Yamanami forced a smile.

"You too have come all this way to Kyoto. The speech of Kyoto does not stab at a man's heart. Better to learn such a way of considering."

"I'll go by my own school,"

{j} and Toshizō, pulling a sour, fierce face, tore at a dried fish. Okita, from the side, tittered.

"Hijikata, that's my dried fish. Yours is over there."

"I know it,"

{j} said Toshizō, saving face.

"Another man's tray always looks the tastier. I was only imitating Master Yamanami here, the Kyoto-smitten."

Kondō's faction, having taken at the last a bowl of rice in tea apiece, went out unhurried at the lodging's entrance. The Yagi manservant opened the gate. On its doors were driven heavy figure-eight fittings, as at a great lord's mansion. The gate was a warrior's long-house gate, but where it differed from the single-mindedly rough long-house gate of the Satō house in Hino, Bushū, was that its walls were painted with red ocher and its windows fitted with fine Kyoto lattice, giving it a strangely feminine feel.

Just outside ran the Bōjō avenue. Toshizō and the rest had only to cross the street, for the Shintoku-ji stood diagonally opposite the Yagi house.

The narrow main hall was already thronged with the whole company of rōnin. Toshizō and the rest had the lowest seats cleared for them and sat together there.

To the right of the main hall's altar-dais sat Yamaoka and the other shogunal retainers in a row, and beside them Kiyokawa, stroking his chin in vexation. About him sat Kiyokawa's confidants—Ishizaka Shūzō, Ikeda Tokutarō, Saitō Kumasaburō (Kiyokawa's own younger brother)—their faces strangely tense. Seeing it,

(Something is afoot,)

{j} Toshizō thought.

In the thirty-mat hall, but for some five candlestands set out, there was no light at all. In that dimness Ishizaka Shūzō of the Kiyokawa party rose.

"Gentlemen, silence, if you please. Master Kiyokawa will now speak."

Kiyokawa Hachirō rose. He was tall. A well-favored man.

Slowly he went to the front of the dais.

Fair-skinned, as a Dewa man will be, with clean-cut features—a face to charm even a man—and eyes keen, as befitted an adept of the Hokushin Ittō-ryū. His spirit brimming over, his bearing swallowing the whole hall, he had a truly dauntless air. Sure enough, there was cause for all the world's talk of him. One might set him down as a man of the first rank of the age.

"Gentlemen,"

{j} and Kiyokawa shifted his great sword to his left hand.

"This I would have you hear with your very souls. It concerns our own selves. It is the question of for what we are to shed our heart's blood. You are, each of you, men bold and ready to die. To spill blood you will not shirk. But spill it in a mistaken cause, and you take on the stain, unwashable to all posterity, of rebels and traitors. —And so."

Kiyokawa swept his eyes over the assembly.

Every man watched him with bated breath. And Kiyokawa said, at last, a thing beyond all expectation.

"That we swore our bond at the Denzū-in in Edo was to be the guard of the shogun (Iemochi), soon to come up to the capital. But that is a surface thing, and no more. In truth it is to guard the Emperor's Heaven and the Emperor's foundation, and to be the vanguard of Revere-the-Emperor and Expel-the-Barbarian."

(Ah—!)

{j} It was not the company alone who caught their breath. It was the shogunate's own sponsors, who had joined hands with Kiyokawa to work the council into founding the corps. Yamaoka Tetsutarō and the rest went white. Kiyokawa had told not even Yamaoka. (Yamaoka would within a few years grow into a man past recognizing; but at this time he was still young, and was much played upon by the strategist Kiyokawa's eloquence.)

"We came, it is true, at the shogunate's summons. But we eat none of the Tokugawa house's stipend. Our own coming and going is free. Therefore we shall work as soldiers of the Imperial Court. And should any officer of the shogunate henceforth (a senior councilor, say, or the Kyoto Deputy) turn against the Court and thwart the imperial command, we mean to cut him down without mercy."

The first man in all the history of the Restoration to raise, clear and plain, the banner of anti-shogunal action was this Kiyokawa Hachirō, in the Shintoku-ji at Mibu. Kiyokawa would make himself, for an emperor who had no soldiers, a hatamoto thrust upon him, and would establish at a single stroke a Kyoto government set above the Edo shogunate. It may be called the greatest bold stroke of theater in all the history of the Restoration.

"You will have no objection, I take it."

The assembly was swallowed up in Kiyokawa. Or rather—far from opposing him, scarce a man there had the cultivation even to follow his eloquence.

There Kiyokawa had their measure. Leave the brains to me, he reckoned; you are to be my fangs and claws, no more.

The company dispersed with never a word spoken.

From that night Kiyokawa set to work upon the Kyoto court nobles, campaigning to have the corps' intent laid before the Emperor. The court nobles were, in political grounding, as infants. And the reigning Son of Heaven (the Emperor Kōmei) was possessed of an extraordinary dread of white men, and was opposed to the shogunate's policy of opening the ports. And so,

{j} Kiyokawa's memorial—"to bear the will of Heaven and be the vanguard of the resolute expulsion of the barbarian"—greatly moved the palace, and word came leaking down to Kiyokawa and his men that "His Majesty's satisfaction was beyond the ordinary."

Kiyokawa was beside himself with joy.

Had the current of the times gone on favoring Kiyokawa so, it might well have come to pass that a mere rural samurai of Kiyokawa village in Dewa became the head of a new government in Kyoto.

"Toshi, what do we do?"

{j} it was Kondō who called Toshizō to his room that night. Kondō at this time still had no grasp of the times, and could not well make out even the cant of the so-called patriots' debates.

For Toshizō it was the same. Until only the other day he had been a man who did nothing but repeat country brawls with the Kōgen Ittō-ryū of Hachiōji, out in the Bushū Tama country.

But Toshizō had, where Kondō had not, an inborn intuition and a manly sense of honor.

"That fellow's a villain,"

{j} said Toshizō. That one word melted away Kondō's whole knot of doubt on the matter.

"Toshi, well said. Kiyokawa talked a great deal that was hard to follow, but in a word it's a turning of the coat, isn't it. Deck it out with all the Chinese phrases you like, the flesh inside is a man rotten to the bone. What's to be done?"

"There's nothing for it but to cut him down."

"Kill him?"

Kondō was a simple man. But Toshizō said that to strike Kiyokawa down alone would not settle the matter, and,

{j} "We must build a new party,"

{j} he said.

"A new party?"

"Mm. But we are only eight. With so few, cut Kiyokawa down though we might, we could do no more than be beaten to a pulp by the many and destroy ourselves. This wants a little contrivance."

"And for that, what do we do, Toshi?—"

"Have the goodness to call me 'Hijikata.'"

"Ah—so it was."

{j} Kondō set his face.

Toshizō listened a while, intent on the sounds from the next room, then brought out brush and paper, and

"Unless we draw this man in, the thing will not come off."

Serizawa Kamo was a rōnin who had left the Mito domain, and gave himself out to have been of the Tengu Party. A man of huge frame, with the strength, they said, of several men.

He held the full transmission of the Shintō Munen-ryū and had pupils of his own; but, worse luck, he was a kind of madman, capable of any violence when his temper was crossed.

"Serizawa, is it,"

{j} Kondō murmured low. This man he had met on the road with an unpleasant turn several times. To draw him into their company was no cheerful prospect.

Nor was it cheerful to Toshizō.

He hated that vicious man, it may be, even more deeply than Kondō did; but for the present, Toshizō urged, a once-only clasp of hands with Serizawa was needful for the leap to come.

"Why?"

"First, the man's numbers."

Serizawa's men were but five, yet every one a swordsman fit to be called a match for a thousand; all Mito men, and all of the Shintō Munen-ryū. Serizawa had joined the corps as their boss; but unlike Kondō's faction, from among his own band he had furnished two corps officers (Serizawa Kamo as an overseer, Niimi Nishiki as corporal of the Third Unit; the rest were Hirama Jūsuke, Noguchi Kenji, Hirayama Gorō).

"And besides,"

{j} Toshizō tapped a finger on the inked name "Serizawa Kamo,"

{j} "do you know this man's true name?"

"I do not."

"Kimura Tsugiji. And this man's elder brother, one Kimura Denzaemon, is posted at the Mito Tokugawa house's Kyoto residence. His office is liaison. You follow? Liaison—that would put him close to the liaison office of the Kyoto Protector, the Lieutenant-General Matsudaira."

"And so?"

"The Kyoto Protector, the Lieutenant-General Matsudaira Katamori—"

{j} Toshizō broke off and looked at Kondō. Kondō understood. The Kyoto Protector was the shogunate's chief organ in Kyoto.

"I have it,"

{j} Kondō looked pleased.

"That is to say—is it this? Through Serizawa, we get our plea for a new party worked upon the Kyoto Protector."

"Just so. Serizawa is a man like poison, but for this turn he'll be a sovereign remedy.—And, conveniently besides,"

{j} Toshizō said, crumpling the paper,

{j} "Serizawa's band of five happens to lodge with us."
