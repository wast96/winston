Creak——

The door of a wooden cage that had not been opened in a long while was wrenched bodily apart, its hinges grinding out a dry, rasping sound. Li Bi was shoved through it and nearly pitched to the ground. A string of iron fetters was clamped about his ankles, his hands were bound fast behind his back, and a cloth band had been drawn tight across his mouth to keep him from biting through his tongue and killing himself.

After he had been made to admire the “grand spectacle” of that fierce-fire thunder, the Pifu had brought him to a cellar near the courtyard. In it stood a huge wooden cage, one the master of the house had likely once used to keep some rare foreign bird or beast; a faint stench came off it from between the boards.

Li Bi stood bolt upright, keeping well clear of the bars on every side. He would not sit down or lie back; that was the way of a beast in a cage, and he held fast to the last shred of his dignity.

The whole cellar had but one small vent for air, so the atmosphere was thick and foul. The two guards, whether by design or not, both stood near the cellar door, where a flight of stone steps sloped up toward the surface and the breathing was a little easier.

The guards were easy in their manner; they had no fear that Li Bi would escape. Here was a frail, bookish scholar with no skill in fighting: even if he slipped his bonds, he was still shut in the cage; even if he broke from the cage, he was still shut in the cellar; and granting every allowance—even if he did somehow get out of the cellar, there were plenty more guards in the courtyard beyond, so escape was flatly impossible. Their one real duty down here was to keep Li Bi from doing himself harm.

Li Bi knew well enough that this time he was not likely to come off alive. His most pressing concern now was not to save his own skin, but to find some way to get word out—at the very least to let Zhang Xiaojing know what the Pifu’s method was.

Li Bi did not fear death; what he feared for was the Eastern Palace and all the common folk of the city.

Once more he looked all around, straining to find some slightest flaw. And once more Li Bi was disappointed: the watch here was too strict, and the pit ran too deep—never mind getting word out, he could not even see what was going on outside.

If Zhang Xiaojing were here, what would he do? Li Bi could not help wondering—but he truly could not picture it. A young man of high family, clad in silk and fed on delicacies since childhood, had no way to fathom the mind of an old soldier who had clawed his way out of death in the Western Regions.

Your Highness, this time I may have to break my word to you . . . A voice rose up within him that he could not, for all his trying, keep down.

Just then came a sound of footsteps at the cellar mouth. Li Bi raised his head and saw that Long Bo had, of all things, come back again. The man was still chewing on a mint leaf, his jaw working with unusual force, and a subtle smile hung on his face.

He came up to the cage. “Deputy Director Li, I have come on purpose to offer my congratulations.”

Li Bi said nothing. He knew there must be some fresh piece of bad news—yet how much worse could things possibly get?

“My men have just reported back to me: the Jing’an Bureau has already been rebuilt, so you, Deputy Director, may lay down that heavy burden of yours.” Long Bo fixed his eyes on Li Bi, watched his brows slowly knit together again, and was greatly pleased. A pity there was a cloth in Li Bi’s mouth; to have heard him answer would surely have been more gratifying still.

“I hear the man who has taken it over is a Palace Censor named Ji Wen. The very first thing the new officer did on taking up his post was to put out a citywide warrant for Zhang Xiaojing, naming him the traitor within. By now the Jing’an Bureau’s three-feather order has spread through the whole of Chang’an.”

There was no need to spell it out; Long Bo knew Li Bi could not fail to grasp what lay behind the news. The Right Minister had thrust himself forcefully into the matter, the Jing’an Bureau’s authority was wholly lost, and the last thread of hope for dealing with the Pifu was being cut through by his own side.

He had come down to the cellar on purpose to say this, meaning to deal the prisoner a final blow. Long Bo believed that this unlooked-for good news would make Li Bi give up all resistance for good. He looked over, beaming—and sure enough, the brows Li Bi had knit did not unknit again.

Long Bo lifted a finger, and the guards took the cloth from Li Bi’s mouth. Li Bi let out a long breath. He had not bitten through his tongue; matters being what they were, killing himself now would serve no purpose at all.

“So it is the Right Minister behind you Pifu, after all?” The question burst from Li Bi.

Long Bo laughed aloud. “You do us too much honor, Deputy Director; a personage that great is quite beyond our reach. Still, this new chief the Right Minister has sent over is no planted agent, yet he serves us better than one. Under his direction no one is hunting us now; every eye is on Zhang Xiaojing. We ought by rights to send him a plaque of thanks.”

Li Bi paid no heed to the mockery. “And Zhang Xiaojing? Has he been taken too?”

“Only a matter of time. If Zhang Xiaojing has any sense, he’ll have found a way out of the city by now,” said Long Bo, in high good humor.

Li Bi moved his lips but did not gainsay him. Zhang Xiaojing had lost the guarantee of his pardon, and had been stripped of the power to pursue the case; there was no longer any reason for him to hold his ground. Were he himself in Zhang Xiaojing’s place, he would choose the same.

That clean, handsome face took on a deep look of dejection, and the light went out of his eyes. This time he had lost utterly. Long Bo knew that the man had lost the will to resist, because he could no longer see the faintest hope.

“So you need cherish no more illusions, Deputy Director. Better to take a good rest, mutter your incantations, offer up your rites; who knows, before long you may truly sprout feathers and rise as an immortal, and have me to thank for making your fortune among the blessed.”

With that one remark Long Bo troubled himself no further over the former deputy director of the Jing’an Bureau, but turned and climbed, step by step, up out of the cellar mouth. When he reached the surface he looked about him, and sent his gaze into the dark corners the lantern-light could not reach. There a figure lay concealed—the one who, a moment ago, had passed him the latest news.

Before Long Bo could speak, the hoarse voice that was Yuchang’s alone came to his ear: “I am leaving.”

“Oh? The trail to the Shouzhuolang ought to be quite cold by now. Where else is there for you to go?” Long Bo was taken aback.

“I am going to kill Zhang Xiaojing.” The voice was as flat as ever, yet a heavy killing intent lay stored within it.

Long Bo knew that Yuchang had always been proud; this time he had nearly walked into Zhang Xiaojing’s trap and had lost an arm besides, and such a rare and bitter disgrace had to be washed clean. He frowned. “Zhang Xiaojing must be out of the city by now, surely? He’s not so stupid as that.”

“He is exactly that stupid. I saw him go back to the Jing’an Bureau. If I hadn’t had to come here to report, I’d have been on his tail already,” Yuchang answered stubbornly.

“The Jing’an Bureau?” The news astonished Long Bo beyond measure. “Is he walking straight into the net?”

There was no stir from the darkness; Yuchang himself did not know why Zhang Xiaojing should behave so contrary to all reason.

Long Bo glanced at the water-clock in the courtyard: it was now a little past the hour of the Pig, second half. He said to Yuchang, “Don’t let this man distract you. The last stage of the task is about to begin; you and I will go and see it done first. As for Zhang Xiaojing, let him be—he can’t be any threat to us now.”

“As you please. But I mean to do it with my own hand.”

Yuchang’s voice was gone; he had already left the courtyard. Long Bo stood a while where he was, reached into his belt and felt about, and found that he had chewed up all his mint leaves. He clicked his tongue in vexation, and ordered the man beside him to make ready a strong, sturdy mule.

Long Bo stood beneath the lamplight and murmured a few words that no one could hear.

Li Heng, the heir apparent, hearing a clamor outside, set down the yak-tail whisk in his hand and leaned out from the four-windowed carriage, and was just in time to see Tanqi clinging to the carriage’s rear crossbar, shouting herself hoarse.

In the darkness he could not make out the woman’s face, but her voice struck a chill into him:

“Your Highness! The Jing’an Bureau is in peril!”

Li Heng, in some alarm, glanced to left and right. Such words, shouted out in the open street where the whole retinue and all the common folk around could hear, what an uproar might they raise?

The guards reacted swiftly and were already upon her. Two or three of them seized Tanqi and dragged her roughly away from the carriage, while another raised his blade; at the same moment the driver shook the reins and quickened the pace. This was the usual response of an escort meeting with the unexpected. Li Heng scrambled to his feet and flung out his arm. “Stop! Stop!”

The driver, who had just been picking up speed, heard the order to halt and had no choice but to haul hard on the reins. Unhappily this was a four-horse carriage, and the four shaft-horses did not respond as one; such an abrupt burst of speed and then of braking threw the shafts at once into confusion. The rear horses checked while the front ones still ran on; with the four pulling unevenly, the carriage skewed off crookedly to the right, knocking over several pedestrians one after another, and swung its rear compartment about so violently that the fine carved-lacquer side scraped a long gash along the ward wall.

Consort Wei, the heir’s wife, who rode with him, caught at the front rail in some disorder and asked her husband in vexation what was the matter. Li Heng had no time to answer her, but shouted toward the rear, “Stay your hands—bring her here to me!”

The soldiers had been about to haul Tanqi away from the crowd, but at the heir’s word they had to turn about, and, gripping her by both arms, dragged her all the way up to the four-windowed carriage. To guard against a hidden blade, they ran their hands roughly over her body as well, tearing loose several of her silk sashes.

By the light of the lantern beside the carriage, Li Heng saw Tanqi’s face and recognized her as the household maidservant who attended Li Bi—Tanqi was the name, he thought? But unlike her usual grace and elegance, her coiled bun had been pulled loose, her long black hair hung down, and her dress was in disarray; she was in a wretched state indeed.

Under Consort Wei’s suspicious gaze, Li Heng stepped down from the four-windowed carriage. He did not approach Tanqi at once, but looked about him, then raised his hand and said to the soldiers, “Take her over there, clear the ground round about, and let no idlers come near.”

The place he pointed to was a tea-stall—a makeshift bamboo shed thrown up against the ward wall, its front hedged about with a few wooden crates and an awning-cloth to serve as a counter. Behind the counter stood a broad cart, and on the cart a small brazier-stove had been set up, where cheap broken tea-leaves were boiled together with ginger, salt, and roasted pepper. Lantern-viewers grown thirsty would come to beg a bowlful; the taste was thin, but it was handy enough.

At the heir’s order the guards went over at once, cleared out the stall-keeper and the tea-drinking customers, then raised screens to fence the tea-stall off into a space of quiet. Only when there was no one else within the screens did Li Heng ask Tanqi what had happened.

Tanqi, seeing on the heir’s face only astonishment and no anxiety, understood that he knew nothing at all of the attack on the Jing’an Bureau. Whether this was because Li Heng trusted Li Bi too completely, or because someone had deliberately kept the news from reaching the Eastern Palace, she could not tell . . .

Collecting herself, she gave a brief, plain account of all that had gone before. At the hearing of it Li Heng staggered back several steps to lean against the brazier-cart, his face as though he had been struck by lightning. After a moment he asked urgently, “Then . . . then what of Changyuan?”

Tanqi shook her head. She had not gone back to Guangde Ward herself and did not rightly know what had happened, but that some ill had befallen her young master was beyond all doubt. Li Heng paced back and forth a few steps, then called in one of his personal attendants in a loud voice and bade him hurry to Guangde Ward at once and find out, as quickly as he could, what had taken place there.

The attendant answered and went off at once. Just then Consort Wei came in with a worried face to ask what was the matter, but Li Heng, losing his composure, roared at her to get out. He himself pulled the screen down again, then turned the utility belt at his waist round and round with his fingers, tugging this way and that at the little things fastened to it—the reckoning-pouch, the knife, the whetstone—which was Li Heng’s habit when his mind was troubled.

The Jing’an Bureau was the labor of his heart, and Li Bi his most trusted man; neither of them could Li Heng bear to lose. Yet now that so grave a thing had happened, he had learned of it only because a maidservant had braved death to bring him word. That left Li Heng, besides his anger, with a faint, gnawing dread.

Tanqi watched in silence and sighed inwardly. This lord of the Eastern Palace had far too few trusted men to lean upon. With Li Bi gone, he could not lay hold of even the most basic intelligence.

Li Heng glanced at Tanqi and murmured, “Changyuan is so clever, no harm will come to him . . . isn’t that so?” It was less that he was comforting Tanqi than that he was screwing up his own courage. Tanqi took a step forward and said in a low voice, “Your Highness, the most urgent matter now is not my young master, but Zhang Xiaojing.”

“Zhang Xiaojing?” Li Heng had to search his memory before the name came back to him. It was over this convict that Li Bi and He Zhizhang had nearly come to a breach, and He Zhizhang lay senseless still.

“Commander Zhang is now the only hope of getting to the bottom of the Que-le Huo-duo, yet for some reason the Jing’an Bureau has put out a citywide warrant for him. Your Highness, you must find a way to set this right! Or else the whole city of Chang’an . . . and my young master too, are finished!”

But Li Heng said doubtfully, “Weren’t the Türks dealt with already?”

Tanqi grew anxious, and for the moment threw all thought of high and low to the winds; she stepped forward and said in a raised voice, “Your Highness, behind the Wolf Guards there is another hand at the head of it all. The danger to Chang’an is not yet lifted, and none but Commander Zhang can break this deadlock!”

Li Heng frowned. “Is the man truly so marvelous? Er, the pressing thing at present must be to find out about Changyuan . . . er, and what has befallen the Jing’an Bureau. Let us wait first for my attendant to report back.”

Tanqi felt that the heir was far too irresolute; there was no time to waste now, and still less could he afford to mistake what was grave for what was trifling. She was about to open her mouth to press him when Consort Wei lifted the screen a second time, sized Tanqi up with a suspicious eye, and then said to Li Heng:

“Your Highness, the spring banquet is about to begin.”

Only then did Li Heng remember, and a look of awkward difficulty came over his face.

This spring banquet was no ordinary banquet, but the Lantern Festival spring banquet given by the Son of Heaven in the Xingqing Palace. It began at the hour of the Rat, and all the imperial clansmen of the capital and the great ministers of the whole court would attend; the feast would last until the hour of the Ox, second half, when ruler and ministers, having eaten and drunk their fill, would gather together atop the Qinzheng Wuben Tower to watch the lantern-float pageants sent up from every region. So it had been at every Lantern Festival, year after year.

On so great an occasion the heir apparent could on no account be absent or late.

Li Heng said to Tanqi, “Come up into the carriage with me; we’ll go on to the Xingqing Palace first. When word comes back from over there, we can decide what to do.”

It having come to this, Tanqi could only walk out from behind the screens, resigned, and take her place beside Consort Wei in the guise of a serving-girl. Consort Wei, having just had a scolding from her husband, was in poor humor and showed her no kind face. But she had also seen for herself that there was no tender entanglement between this woman and her husband, and so lost interest in her.

The four-windowed carriage and its retinue set off once more, cutting through the steaming press of the crowd toward the Xingqing Palace, now not far off. The nearer they drew to the palace gate, the more dazzling the lights; and Tanqi could already see, on the great square before the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, a vast lantern-tower more than a hundred and fifty chi high, shaped like a gourd, draped with colored silks and studded with gold and silver, rising quietly up out of the darkness.

Tanqi had taken part in many a Lantern Festival’s lantern-viewing, but in all her memory there had never been a lantern-tower so huge; it all but eclipsed the Qinzheng Wuben Tower itself, and not even the Wild Goose Pagoda had such commanding presence.

The hour of the Ox, second half, had not yet come, and it had not yet been lit up all over with candlelight, but its heaven-reaching grandeur was already displayed to the full. Tanqi could scarcely imagine how resplendent it would be when the time came for it to blaze.

After Zhang Xiaojing and Yisi left Pingkang Ward, they made straight for Guangde Ward. From some shop or other Yisi had turned up a broad-brimmed, peaked hat of Persian style, which he jammed onto Zhang Xiaojing’s head, and with lamp-black he painted two rings around his eyes. Thus got up, Zhang Xiaojing became a mummer playing a Brahman farce, and the comical black paint served nicely to hide the mark of his single eye.

So arrayed, unless he were stopped and closely examined, there was no fear of his disguise being seen through.

By now the whole of Chang’an had given itself over utterly to revelry; every street, every turning was shoulder to shoulder, packed with people. They had finished their first round of lantern-viewing and were now turning their interest to the acrobatics, songs, and dances at every quarter. This made the flow of people surge tremendously, like scores of rivers rushing and crossing.

In such conditions a hardy mule was better to ride than a tall, high-headed horse. The two of them stole a pair of mules and rode across the city, threading every gap they found, keeping to the emptier ways. Sometimes they left the main roads altogether and went in at a ward gate to cut clean through the whole ward.

Thanks to Yisi’s fine work with the paint, they passed smoothly through seven or eight guarded crossings one after another. In such an extreme crush the Jing’an Bureau’s warrant could not be thoroughly enforced, and most of the constables gave no more than a cursory check. At one point only did a ward soldier, seeing Zhang Xiaojing got up as a player, call on him to perform some Brahman-farce jest. Zhang Xiaojing knew nothing of the sort; luckily Yisi smoothed it over, and they bluffed their way past.

The whole way Zhang Xiaojing rode on with his lips pressed tight, his gaze beneath the black paint glinting with a burning unease.

Over the two double-hours past, the changes at the Jing’an Bureau had been strange indeed, and the tidings the watchtowers relayed were vague and unclear. He felt he had to go back and see for himself before he could make out the true state of things.

Above all there was that one warning Yao Runeng had sent: “Do not come back. Do not come back. Do not come back.” How desperate must matters have grown, for that naive, stiff-necked young man—foolishly so—to send out a warning like that?

Just how bad had things at the Jing’an Bureau become?

Zhang Xiaojing was sick with worry. Besides Yao Runeng, there was no knowing how Xu Bin fared now; and there was Li Bi; and Tanqi, left behind in Pingkang Ward—where might she have gone? And more important still . . . there was Wen Ran. She was the last flesh and blood his comrade had left in this world; if any mishap befell her, how could he face Wen Wuji beneath the Nine Springs?

One by one, the people who had thrown all their strength into saving Chang’an were being swallowed up, each in turn, by this great dark city. Zhang Xiaojing felt the vines of despair twining about his ankles, and the darkness on every side bearing down on him like a toppling wall, with no light anywhere.

It was the same feeling as when he had stepped into the Wen Incense Shop last year. He had looked at the wreckage that filled the shop, seen Wen Ran weeping with bowed head, seen the document jointly sealed by the Forestry and Crafts Bureau and the commandant of Wannian County, seen Wen Wuji lying on the ground covered with a ragged cloth—and Zhang Xiaojing had sunk deep into a mire, without even the strength to take a single step or make a single sound.

Now, the farther forward he went, the more taut Zhang Xiaojing grew, not knowing what lay in wait for him ahead. Yet in the very next instant his single eye narrowed and shot out a fierce, dangerous light—the ferocity that bursts forth when a man has been pressed down to the utmost.

If it all truly came to nothing, then he might as well go mad one more time, he thought.

Yisi knew nothing of Zhang Xiaojing’s resolve; he kept peering about from his mule until he caught sight of the gate of Guangde Ward.

At the ward gate now stood several dozen soldiers, the watch strict and heavy. A great attack had just taken place here, so the level of alertness was far higher than elsewhere. Yisi volunteered, saying he would go and sound things out. Before long he came back crestfallen, reporting that all foreigners were now forbidden to enter.

Zhang Xiaojing was astonished. The order was far too crude; not only did it serve no practical purpose, it would set everyone suspecting everyone else. Only the laziest of officials would slice things off so bluntly.

Yisi could not get in, and neither could Zhang Xiaojing: his single eye was too conspicuous, and the guards would be sure to spot it. They were still turning over what to do when, as it chanced, a foreign petty clerk came walking out of the ward, his face downcast, a bundle clutched in his arms.

Zhang Xiaojing recognized him as one of the Jing’an Bureau’s men, but dared not show himself. Here was where Yisi’s worth showed. Handsome of face and lofty in his speech, he seemed to an outsider a man of great virtue and attainment. Yisi caught hold of the clerk and questioned him a little while, and without much trouble had the whole of it clear.

It emerged that the attackers of the Jing’an Bureau were an organization calling itself the “Pifu,” and that they had carried off Li Bi into the bargain. Then a censor named Ji Wen had taken over the whole Bureau. The warrant for Zhang Xiaojing and the order barring foreigners had both come down from him. The new Jing’an Bureau was now set up within the Jingzhao Prefecture and was being rebuilt—but that band of experienced foreign clerks who had survived had been driven out just so.

As for the whereabouts of Yao Runeng, Xu Bin, and Wen Ran, the clerk knew nothing at all.

Zhang Xiaojing’s face went taut. This turn of things went beyond the worst case he had reckoned on. The Pifu were of unknown origin, yet of enormous power; and the whole Jing’an Bureau, far from being any help, had become the most fearsome enemy of all.

To face two enemies at once—what a dreadful thing that was.

Zhang Xiaojing stood outside Guangde Ward, gazing at the black smoke that shot up into the night sky from deep within it. That, surely, was the burning great hall of the Jing’an Bureau? Never mind the hall—even the man who had first promised him his pardon and charged him with the task was gone. Zhang Xiaojing was now utterly alone, a solitary soul stripped of all legitimacy.

Matters being what they were, why should a condemned convict drive himself so hard?

If Zhang Xiaojing were to turn and walk away now, not a soul would reproach him for any failing of duty. Indeed, once this night was over, whether the city of Chang’an would ever have the chance to remember his name at all was more than anyone could say.

Yisi, standing beside him, was somewhat perplexed. He could feel the aura about Zhang Xiaojing shifting all the while, now strong, now faint, as though some struggle were going on within. Yisi did not dare disturb him, and could only trace a cross upon his breast and pray for him in silence.

After no long while, Zhang Xiaojing slowly raised his hand, brushed at his eye-socket out of habit, and, of all things, laughed:

“Deacon Yisi, earlier, listening to you talk with Tanqi, I heard you say that the Luminous Lord took pity on the sufferings of the world, came down to be born among men, and redeemed the multitude in their stead. Is that so?”

“Just so,” said Yisi, at a loss to know why he should bring this up all of a sudden.

“And I remember Tanqi said too that in Buddhism there is the bodhisattva Ksitigarbha, who took a great vow: that so long as the hells are not empty, he swears he will not become a Buddha. Luminous faith or Buddhist, these great and holy ones are all willing to answer for their own choices, to go down in the flesh into the hell of this foul world—how much more, then, should a mere man?”

As he said this the single eye brightened once more, clear all through, without a trace of confusion left: “That’s it—I had it wrong all along. Matters being what they are, the question for a condemned convict like me is not why I should drive myself so hard, but that I need not hold back at all.”

With that he laughed aloud, his laughter mounting up to affront the night sky, his high spirit piercing the clouds. Yisi, a little bewildered, narrowed his eyes, feeling only that the man was dazzling beyond words.

“Let’s go,” said Zhang Xiaojing, with a wave of his hand.

The two ward gates of Guangde Ward were plainly not to be gotten through. Leading their mules, the two of them went round to the side of the ward. Zhang Xiaojing remembered there was a water-channel here that ran straight into the rear garden of the Jing’an Bureau. But when they went over to look, they found the channel sealed off too, with a dozen or more soldiers standing on its bank, letting no one draw near.

From this spot the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau could be seen more clearly still. It was blazing yet, and the flanking halls to left and right were rolling with thick smoke, which made Zhang Xiaojing greatly afraid that the evidence from Changming Ward might already have gone up in flames.

The great watchtower still stood, and a few striking purple lanterns hung upon it—but they were strung out in disorder, plainly the work of some unpracticed hand. It seemed Yao Runeng was no longer up there.

“Let’s go over the wall!”

Yisi said it primly, rolling up his sleeves, itching to try. His fondness for such things as climbing walls and hopping roofs was second only to his love for the Luminous Lord. But Zhang Xiaojing shook his head: if the Jing’an Bureau had even the water-channel under guard, then every other place would be watched just as strictly, and to go rushing over would only be to beat the grass and startle the snake.

In his mind, this new Jing’an Bureau was an enemy too, to be guarded against at every moment.

Then Zhang Xiaojing suddenly recalled that between the thatched hut at the Cibei Temple and the Jing’an Bureau there ought to be a ladder still. So they drew back quietly from the water-channel and went round to a corner of the ward where the Cibei Temple pressed close against the ward wall.

Here grey bricks were laid up in courses to form an inward-leaning angle, and to bring out its Buddhist character a raised border of bodhi-leaf pattern had been added along the top edge—which both proclaimed the vastness of the Dharma and made for easy climbing. More to the point, no one guarded the outside of the wall, which showed that the Jing’an Bureau’s watch had not spread to the neighborhood of the Cibei Temple.

Yisi said, “Heavenly Father protect us,” spat twice into his palms, and was just about to climb the wall when Zhang Xiaojing suddenly laid a hand on his shoulder. “Deacon Yisi, it is enough that you help me up the wall. What awaits inside Guangde Ward, for good or ill, no one can say; there is no need for you to wade into this muddy water.”

He was carrying his wounds and could not easily exert himself, and needed Yisi to give him a pull up. But of the venture ahead Zhang Xiaojing himself was far from sure, and it was not worth dragging in Yisi, who had no stake in the matter.

Yisi said, aggrieved, “Can it be that the Commander scorns me as old and faded, no longer fit for great use?”

Zhang Xiaojing had no time to correct his choice of words, and only shook his head. “I am Commander no longer, only a condemned convict with a warrant out for him. Follow me, and far from clearing the name of the Nestorian temple, you’ll be dragged down with me.” Yisi put out two fingers and tapped his own gemlike eyes. “This pair of eyes of mine has been washed by the autumn floods; in all Chang’an there is nothing they cannot see through. And by their judgment, to stake all on following the Commander cannot be a mistake.”

Zhang Xiaojing could not quite tell where Yisi drew such confidence from. But the hour was already late and there could be no more delay, so he said mildly, “As long as you are willing to answer for your own choice, so be it,” and then he too began to climb the wall.

With some effort the two of them clambered over into the Cibei Temple. All within was quiet now, not so much as a single candle-flame to be seen. Zhang Xiaojing made his way warily through the meditation grove, skirted the pagoda, and came before the thatched hut.

The thatched hut was empty of all people now, though everywhere within were the marks of a search. Overturned on the ground lay a greasy wooden tray—the very one Tanqi had used, some double-hours ago, to serve the oil-fritters to him and Li Bi.

The searchers had evidently gone; there was no ambush laid about the hut. Zhang Xiaojing went over to the courtyard wall, and sure enough the ladder had already been taken down and carried off.

The people who knew of this thatched hut were but a handful. That it had been ransacked meant that either Yao Runeng or Xu Bin had fallen into enemy hands and been forced to give up the secret. Zhang Xiaojing crouched down beside the mercy-release pond and saw that a great hole had been broken in the ice, with scores of water-soaked footprints all around. There had likely been a struggle here as well, though with whom there was no telling.

Seeing these traces, Zhang Xiaojing felt that this rebuilt Jing’an Bureau was not merely incompetent but positively brimming with malice, bent with deliberate purpose on blackening and wiping out every arrangement made during Li Bi’s tenure.

The courtyard wall on the side of the hut nearest the Jing’an Bureau was no easy thing to climb. Happily there was a master of cave-running like Yisi, who used the cypress alongside to spring successfully up onto the top of the wall, then let down a rope and hauled Zhang Xiaojing up.

His feet touched down, stirring up a light cloud of dust, and Zhang Xiaojing had come back once more to the Jing’an Bureau.

The last time he had been at the Jing’an Bureau was at noon of this same day. Li Bi had just driven He Zhizhang off in a temper and taken sole command, and had sent him to the Pingkang Quarter to investigate. In those days the Bureau’s best were all on hand: the watchtower system, the Lüben Guards, the art of the Great Archive—all ran at high efficiency, and Zhang Xiaojing had wielded them as an arm wields a finger, as though a thousand men stood ready to help him.

In the space of a mere six double-hours, the place had fallen into a hell of fire and ruin, its things all changed, its people all gone. But Zhang Xiaojing had no time to mourn, and made straight for the evidence room.

The evidence room was set in a storehouse near the left flanking hall, where were kept all manner of possibly useful remains gathered from the scenes. It was here that Cao Poyan’s string of beads had been restrung. Zhang Xiaojing and Yisi moved carefully along the edge of the fire, forcing themselves to bear the scorching heat, passing by the side of the main hall and following a broken corridor to the left flanking hall.

The fire in the left flanking hall was hardly weaker than in the main hall. This was where documents and files were stored, and it burned with peculiar ferocity. If the left flanking hall too had caught fire, then the evidence room there could scarcely have been spared.

When Zhang Xiaojing and Yisi arrived, the fire had not yet died down, and the crackling never ceased. By its light they could just make out that the evidence room too was wrapped in thick smoke; what had become of the things stored inside needed no asking.

It seemed the Jing’an Bureau had given up all effort to put it out; not a soul had been left, and the flames were simply let burn. But Zhang Xiaojing would not give up. He looked about him, and suddenly noticed a corpse lying not far off to one side.

It was a piteous sight: the corpse wore fire-proof cloth and still gripped a fire-mop in its hand—no doubt one of the first constables to have rushed in to fight the blaze. From the footprints on his body, he had likely been trampled to death by the crowd of refugees swarming out.

Zhang Xiaojing took the fire-proof cloth off the corpse and threw it over himself, then picked up the fire-mop and gripped it in both hands. The fire-mop was in fact a long wooden pole with a great wad of coarse hemp rag bound at its tip, which could be dipped in water or mud and used to beat out flames.

Zhang Xiaojing charged Yisi: “If I don’t come back, withdraw the way we came and get out of the capital as fast as you can.” Yisi, not knowing what else to say, could only offer to pray for him. Amid the sound of the prayer, Zhang Xiaojing loosened his trouser-belt and pissed a great deal onto the head of the fire-mop, then drew the fire-proof cloth about him, took the fire-mop in hand, ducked his head, and charged into the burning ground.

Hereabouts even the ground was burning hot, and through the single layer of his leather boots the soles of Zhang Xiaojing’s feet felt as if he were treading on needle-points. He got his bearings for a moment and then charged straight for the evidence room.

The evidence room was an outer chamber at the corner of the left flanking hall, not connected with the interior, so Zhang Xiaojing need not risk plunging in where it might collapse—the best of a bad business, at least. He swung the fire-mop to drive off the scorching air and smoke, and where the fumes grew truly too thick to bear, he covered his mouth and nose with the urine-soaked hemp rag: the stink of piss was better than choking to death.

Having fought his way with difficulty to the doorway, Zhang Xiaojing saw the flames roaring up inside; the whole timber frame still stood, but was tottering on the verge of collapse. With the scant gear in his hands there was no chance of beating out a path through. He drew near several times, and each time the wave of heat forced him back.

Things made of bamboo take fire easily, and had likely been the first to turn to ash; even if he charged in, it would mean little. Zhang Xiaojing could only fall back, sullen, toward the place he had come from—and halfway there the left flanking hall suddenly let out a hair-raising shriek.

“Bad!” Zhang Xiaojing realized this was the sound of the main beam snapping, which meant the whole building was about to come down; when it did, burning timber would fly every which way and could strike anywhere at all—the most dangerous moment of all for a firefighter.

He glanced into the distance: it was more than thirty paces yet to a safe remove, and there was no covering it in an instant. Zhang Xiaojing made up his mind on the spot and simply threw himself down beside a flower-bed facing the left flanking hall, then stood the fire-mop up high, so that if some large object came flying his way, it might at least be knocked aside a little and not strike him square on.

No sooner had he finished this defensive move than the left flanking hall, having lost the upright ribs and slanting braces of its main beam, could no longer bear the weight of the great roof, and with a thunderous crash it split and collapsed amid the wailing of its timbers. Countless flaming pieces of wood flew off in all directions. Among them was a burning rafter, pressed up so that it flipped straight into the air and spun round several times like a fire-stick twirled by a Kucha entertainer, and came down right beside the flower-bed . . .

Zhang Luo was one of the recorders of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau, and tonight he had no way to roam and enjoy himself as his colleagues did, but had to keep close watch on the lanterns at every quarter.

The lanterns of Chang’an were, in general, put up by the various merchants themselves, but only building-craftsmen to whom the Forestry and Crafts Bureau had issued an artisan’s warrant were qualified to take part in the work. If any mishap befell a lantern, the craftsmen, together with the officer who had issued the warrant, would all be implicated.

Lanterns were not like other things: should any trouble arise, it would be before all eyes, with no hope of covering it up. On top of that the fashion in Chang’an ran to extravagance, and people loved to vie in lanterns, so that each household’s grew larger and larger, with more and more tricks of candle and flame—and the chance of an accident multiplied accordingly. Zhang Luo was on edge, and had made a point of sending ten-odd Bureau clerks on watch to patrol along the streets and forestall any mishap.

Nor did the pressure on him end there.

Besides the privately made lanterns, the imperial house too had to hang out lanterns and festoon its silks, and these must be stately and lavish enough that they could on no account be outshone by anything of the common people’s making, so as to show forth the grandeur of the imperial line.

The purchase and building of the imperial lanterns was managed by the palace treasury itself, but Zhang Luo had to see to the daily upkeep—the setting of candles, the adding of oil, and all such petty chores. In other words, these lanterns passed through no hand of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau, yet if anything went wrong the Bureau would be held to answer for it. Grumble inwardly though he might, Zhang Luo dared not say so aloud, and could only take double the care.

This year’s Lantern Festival above all: on whose bright idea there was no knowing, they had gone and raised a great lantern-tower a hundred and fifty chi high before the Xingqing Palace. Magnificent it was, no denying it—but the Son of Heaven had no notion how much toil it cost the men below to keep it. Setting all other troubles aside: at the fourth watch, when the “lantern-floats” were kindled, how many men would it take up on the tower to make sure that a lantern-tower so vast could be lit all over in a single instant!

The lighting of the great lantern-tower—from the allotment of supplies to the picking of the men to work it—was Zhang Luo’s charge entire. It was a thankless, back-breaking task: the director and vice-director of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau would only shift the blame onto others, and the recorders below with any wiles about them—Feng Dalun, for one—had wriggled out of it early on, so that in the end it fell upon the head of the luckless Zhang Luo, who had no backing at all.

He was standing just now at the crossroads of Anxing and Chongren wards, where an Arched-Moon Bridge spanned the water of the Longshou Canal that went purling beneath it. Standing atop the bridge with a hand on the rail, one had the nearby lanterns all in a single sweep of the eye. This Arched-Moon Bridge was a fine place for lantern-viewing, and besides Zhang Luo there were countless common folk trying to squeeze up onto it to seize a good spot.

So that his work might not be hampered, Zhang Luo had set three stout fellows about him, left and right, to fend off a ring of space by main force with wooden staves. But the press of people now was truly too great, everyone jostling and crushing together, and the bridge was a dark mass of heads. The three guards were of little use, and had been pushed back until they stood almost pressed against Zhang Luo himself.

Zhang Luo checked the time. By the plan, in another quarter-hour all the craftsmen, Bureau clerks, and black-clad serving-lads he had chosen himself would gather near the Xingqing Palace and then move together into the great lantern-tower to make ready for the final lighting. Seeing the people on the bridge grow ever more numerous, he resolved to leave a little early and go over the particulars of the lighting with his men once more.

Though they had rehearsed it many times over beforehand and ought to make no slip, Zhang Luo felt it never hurt to be careful.

He ordered the guards to open a lane and was just about to step off the bridge when a cry of alarm rose from the crowd and the sea of heads began to churn—it seemed someone was scattering coins. Zhang Luo’s eyes went wide. Scattering coins in a crush like this? Whoever was throwing the money ought to be seized and beaten to death with rods!

Soon the disturbance spread from the foot of the bridge up onto it. The people above did not know what was happening: some wanted to get down and snatch at the money, some wanted to get away as fast as they could, and others merely followed the crowd blindly, jostled along with no idea what was going on. In an instant the whole bridge was a boiling pot of confusion. Many tumbled off the bridge and came down on top of others, letting up a tremendous outcry. The three guards too were shoved apart, and Zhang Luo was crushed bodily against the carved-stone rail of the bridge, his upper body bent out over it, in a wretched plight.

He shouted himself hoarse, to no avail. Just then a hand reached out from the confusion, and Zhang Luo felt only a deft force pushing him up and over the rail, so that he toppled down toward the channel below.

There was a splash, and the water flew up. But not one of the common folk took any notice of the accident; they went on crushing and screaming. The three guards did note that their officer had fallen in, and they were alarmed, though not yet to the pitch of despairing terror. The Longshou Canal was not deep and could not drown a man; if only they hurried to the bank and pulled their officer out, the worst of it would be a few curses to endure.

Only Zhang Luo himself knew that he would never rise to swim again. At some moment he could not name, a wound had opened at his throat, and his body could do nothing but sink helplessly on down into the water, drifting who knew where along the current. His corpse would be fished up sooner or later—perhaps tomorrow, perhaps the day after—and then someone would discover that this was no mere accident of a fall from a bridge.

But not tonight.

“Quick! A man’s hurt!”

An anxious shout came from within the Jing’an Bureau, and the soldiers on duty nearby all looked over to see a Persian man supporting a burn-blackened casualty and dragging him out. The man’s face was smeared all over with soot, and he was draped in a piece of fire-proof cloth smoked past all recognition.

The soldiers were surprised: those who could get out should long since have gotten out—how was there still someone inside? And besides, with the order barring foreigners already given, how had a Persian sprung up now?

“Me, gaol, come out, this man still alive.” Yisi spoke in halting Tang speech, gesturing as he went. The soldiers more or less took his meaning: this fellow had been in the gaol, the door locked, so it had cost him some time to get out, and on the way he had happened to see this man still living and had dragged him out along with himself.

The soldiers on duty had all been drafted in for the occasion and had no idea who had been held in the Jing’an Bureau’s gaol to begin with; and besides, who would run into a burning building on purpose to tell such a lie? With Yisi’s handsome looks and earnest speech on top of that, they believed him at once.

The casualty was wrapped in fire-proof cloth, so plainly he had been among the first to plunge in and fight the fire; and the soldiers looked at Yisi with a new measure of admiration. This Persian prisoner, even in his own escape, had not forgotten to save another—truly one long steeped in the benevolent ways of the Central Plains.

Two soldiers stepped forward of their own accord and helped Yisi carry the casualty toward the reception hall of the Jingzhao Prefecture. It was there that all the injured were being treated.

As he walked, Yisi silently begged God’s pardon for the lie. Back at the flower-bed just now, Zhang Xiaojing had indeed been struck—luckily the fire-mop had knocked the thing aside a little, or that rafter would have taken his life. Even so, the flame at the rafter’s head had burned a patch of his back. This too was why the soldiers had no suspicion of any fakery.

By now the chaos outside the Jing’an Bureau had largely subsided; the rescue workers were mostly in place, each at his own task, and firebreaks, express lanes, and passageways had been marked out. The casualty and Yisi were soon brought into the Jingzhao Prefecture, where an apprentice from the infirmary saw to a first examination, then placed them according to the gravity of their hurts in a particular quarter of the reception hall, and called for a physician to attend them.

There were too many injured this night, and the apprentice was already run off his feet, with no time at all to study a patient’s face, still less to mind the Jingzhao Prefecture’s warrant. So when he saw Zhang Xiaojing, he only looked him over, front and back, without expression, and then tied a brown cloth band about his foot—the sign of a light injury. As for Yisi, he was given no band at all.

Zhang Xiaojing was helped into the reception hall, where the couches and tables had all been carried out, and scores of the wounded lay strewn every which way across the floor, their groans rising and falling one after another. A dozen or more physicians in green robes, with as many apprentices, threaded among them, every one of them drenched in sweat.

One physician came over and found the man strange: besides the burn on his back, there were many fresh knife-wounds on his body. He was about to question him closely when all at once he wrinkled his nose in disgust—he had caught the reek of piss on the man’s face—and at once put out any thought of pursuing it. Roughly he had Zhang Xiaojing lie face-down on a felt rug, cut open his upper garment to bare the patient’s back, poured raw rapeseed oil over the scalded part, smeared on a little atractylodes powder, then charged him with a curt “Lie still and let it air!” and hurried off.

Yisi, being unhurt, was allotted only a cup of honey-water to wet his throat.

It would be some while before the rapeseed oil had fully soaked into the skin, so Zhang Xiaojing had to lie still on the felt rug. Yisi, looking curiously this way and that, suddenly noticed that in one corner of the reception hall two mother-of-pearl-inlaid screens had been set to fence off a small private space. Outside the screens two guards stood as well; it seemed some person of consequence lay within, and he walked over.

Yisi had a born gift for winning trust, and after a few words the guards let down their guard. They said the one here was a traitor within the Jing’an Bureau, to be kept under strict watch. Using the chatter as his chance, Yisi peered through the crack between the screens: sure enough, a man lay inside. He made no further move, but withdrew quietly and described the man’s features to Zhang Xiaojing in a low voice.

“Youde . . .” Hearing that it was Xu Bin, Zhang Xiaojing let out a breath of relief; at least he was not dead. As for the charge of being a traitor within, it was likely from being dragged down on his own account. He gritted his teeth and made to rise, but Yisi pressed him back down.

“If the Commander goes over there now, his identity is laid bare. A plan has taken shape in my mind, though . . .”

Yisi whispered a few words to Zhang Xiaojing, then slipped over to another corner of the reception hall. There a group of menials were busy pounding out rapeseed oil on the spot in a long wooden trough, the underside of the trough wrapped in silk to strain the liquid clear, with a basin set beneath to catch it. Nearby were three or four small stoves, bubbling away with boiling water.

There were too many injured this night; even such crude medicine and hot water as this could not be supplied fast enough.

Everyone was bent to his own work, and no one heeded Yisi. Softly he made his way to the edge of the gallery at the corner outside the hall, stretched out his arms, and, using a gallery pillar and the carved rail, swung himself up onto a side beam. From inside his robe Yisi took a large bundle of rag-strips—the discarded bandaging he had quietly gathered a little before. He rolled the strips into a ball, tucked inside it a lump of live charcoal just scooped from one of the small stoves, and then dropped back down to the ground.

Before long a thick black smoke came drifting in from the corridor. The people in the reception hall had just lived through a great fire, and every one of them was a bird startled by the twang of the bow; at the sight of smoke rising, with no source of open flame to be seen, their first thought was that the blaze next door had spread over to them.

Seizing on the confusion, Yisi shouted out in pure, fluent Tang speech, “Fire! Fire!” At once the whole hall was in an uproar, and the guards rushed off toward the corridor to try to find the source of the smoke. The two guards watching Xu Bin could not sit still either; since Xu Bin was still senseless and could not possibly flee, they left their post to go and help.

Yisi, spying from one side, saw his chance the moment it came, and slipped in behind the screens.

Xu Bin still lay on the couch, eyes shut, saying nothing. Yisi went over, leaned down to his ear, and said softly, “An old friend from the Fuyuan asked me to bring you a word.” Xu Bin’s eyes abruptly moved beneath the lids; he reacted at once.

The Fuyuan was a wine-shop Xu Bin and Zhang Xiaojing often went to, known only to the two of them. At Yisi’s word, Xu Bin knew at once that this was a man sent by Zhang Xiaojing. Yisi said, “The situation is grave, and it is not convenient for the Commander to come over himself. He has asked me to inquire: the items left from Changming Ward—where else are they kept?”

Xu Bin opened his eyes and looked at him blankly, as though he had not yet come to himself. Yisi repeated it once more: “Chang’an hangs in a peril like a pile of eggs, and it is only a moment from disaster. The items left from Changming Ward—where else are they to be found?”

Xu Bin was silent a moment. Though he did not know who Yisi was, he trusted Zhang Xiaojing:

“The left flanking hall, the evidence room.”

“And where besides that?” Yisi glanced outside, growing anxious; the guards seemed already to have found the source of the smoke, and would likely be back before long.

This time Xu Bin was silent a good deal longer: “The Jingzhao Prefecture . . .”

Yisi’s eyes lit up: so there truly was another place where the Changming Ward evidence was kept. He pressed on, “Where in the Jingzhao Prefecture?” Xu Bin said, “The Deliberation Hall, in the right wing.”

The Jingzhao Prefecture had overall charge of the two counties of Wannian and Chang’an, and did not, as a rule, try cases directly. But cases the two counties could not settle were often referred up to the Prefecture for judgment. So within the Prefecture’s offices a hall was set aside expressly for the deliberating of cases.

The evidence the Jing’an Bureau had gathered from Changming Ward was too much for one place: besides the greater part kept in the evidence room, a portion had been handed over to the Jingzhao Prefecture. For one thing, the Prefecture was on holiday just then and had a great many empty rooms; for another, it could be counted a joint handling of the case by the two offices, so that the Prefecture would not feel it had been shoved aside.

All these petty matters of bureaucratic procedure had passed through Xu Bin’s hands, and even Li Bi might not have known of them.

Having gotten this news, Yisi hurried back out from behind the screens, and as he turned he ran squarely into the returning guards. Seeing that the Persian from before had, of all things, come nosing over again, they all showed a look of suspicion. Yisi hastened to stammer out an explanation: “Fire start, he not move, carry away, keep from burning.”

That cry of “Fire! Fire!” a moment ago had been in proper, practiced Tang tones, whereas this Persian monk could only jerk out one word at a time; so the guards had no suspicion at all that he had made the disturbance, and, taking him only for a kindly soul come to save a life, waved him away.

Yisi told Zhang Xiaojing how things stood, and Zhang Xiaojing, mastering the pain in his back, rolled over and got up. Anxious as he was about Xu Bin’s plight, he could not spare thought for it now; it was enough that the man was not dead.

From somewhere Yisi had gotten hold of a green physician’s gown stained all over with filth, which he pulled on over himself, and then, supporting Zhang Xiaojing, walked toward the door of the reception hall. Those they passed along the way all took it for a patient being moved, and did not so much as ask.

By now the Jingzhao Prefecture’s offices, save that the main hall and the treasury were sealed and barred, had all been thrown open and given over to the new Jing’an Bureau for its workplace. Clerks of every kind bustled to and fro, likely none too familiar even with one another, let alone able to pick out a stranger. The two of them passed through unhindered and soon learned where the Deliberation Hall lay.

But when they made their way over there, two grim-faced guardsmen barred the road. The guardsmen barked at them to say where they were going, and Yisi quickly explained that he was taking a patient to be treated. Without expression one of them pointed and said the reception hall was over that way, and here no one was allowed to come near. Yisi feigned incomprehension, saying the officer at the door just now had plainly told him to come here, and kept edging inward. Seeing him cling on so, the guardsman roared, “This is the seat of the Jing’an Bureau; any who enter without leave are to be cut down on the spot!”

The truth was that once Ji Wen had set the Jing’an Bureau up in the Jingzhao Prefecture, the first thing he wanted was a comfortable room of his own to work in. At the Censorate he was only a Palace Censor, sharing one room with seven or eight colleagues, and had long been out of patience with it. But in the Prefecture’s offices the main hall was sealed and the withdrawing-rooms were too small; pick and choose as he might, only the Deliberation Hall was both spacious and dignified—the best choice of all.

His vanity was satisfied—but it brought no end of trouble to Zhang Xiaojing and Yisi.

The two of them drew back for the moment to a corner. Yisi said to Zhang Xiaojing, “I observed carefully just now: there is a rockery of some size in the courtyard next door; from there one might climb up onto the eaves, then hang down head-first into the Deliberation Hall, and perhaps steal in.”

But Zhang Xiaojing shook his head. This was the Jingzhao Prefecture, no ordinary place; there were sure to be archers and crossbowmen posted on the eaves as well. If Yisi tried his cave-running here, he would likely be shot into a hedgehog.

Just then a man passed beside them, glanced their way in passing, and suddenly let out an “Eh?”—his gaze coming to rest on Zhang Xiaojing’s face and not shifting away for a long while. Seeing the danger, Yisi hastily stepped in front to block him. But by then the man had already cried out despite himself, “Zh—Zhang Xiaojing?”

Zhang Xiaojing sprang at him like a starving tiger, clapped a hand over his mouth, and forced him bodily into the corner. The man struggled frantically in his terror, and Zhang Xiaojing said in a low, savage voice, “Move again and I’ll kill you!”

“Mm-mm . . . it’s me . . .”

Zhang Xiaojing frowned, then quickly recognized the face—it was, of all people, Adjutant Zhao of the Right Xiao Guard. Two double-hours before, Tanqi and Yao Runeng had seized Adjutant Zhao and used him to break Zhang Xiaojing out of the Right Xiao Guard. Just before leaving, Adjutant Zhao had asked of his own accord to be knocked out cold, so as to escape any blame—and now, who would have thought, here they were meeting again so soon.

“How is it you’re here?”

Adjutant Zhao sighed. “After the Pifu attacked the Jing’an Bureau, scarcely one man in five was left. Deputy Director Ji is drawing men from every office to fill the gaps, and I have come to make up the numbers.”

The loss of Zhang Xiaojing had, in truth, begun with Adjutant Zhao. Even if Gan Shoucheng said nothing, Adjutant Zhao knew his superiors must be displeased, and so had asked of his own accord to come and help at the Jing’an Bureau—for one thing to atone for his fault by merit, for another to keep out of harm’s way—only to run smack into this bane of his life again.

“There’s a citywide warrant out for you now—how do you dare come back?” Adjutant Zhao stared at Zhang Xiaojing, and the back of his head began, despite himself, to ache faintly. Zhang Xiaojing had no wish to explain, and countered with a question of his own: “I need to find a way into the Deliberation Hall. What can you do for me?”

“That’s a hard one! Deputy Director Ji is working in the Deliberation Hall, the guard is strict and heavy, and to assassinate him won’t be so easy.”

“Who said anything about assassinating him?!” Zhang Xiaojing growled under his breath.

Adjutant Zhao goggled at him in astonishment. “You don’t? He’s put a warrant out on you, and it hasn’t roused the killing in you? That’s not like you at all!” Zhang Xiaojing grabbed him by the lapels. “Listen. I’m going to the Deliberation Hall not for any man’s life, nor for any goods, but only to fetch a trifling little thing. Since you now hold a post at the Jing’an Bureau, you might as well lend me a hand.”

Adjutant Zhao shuddered, his face going white with fright. “It won’t do, it won’t do—I’ve only the one head, you know.” Zhang Xiaojing said coldly, “Quite right, you’ve only the one head: either I take it now, or Ji Wen takes it presently.” Adjutant Zhao, in an extremity of terror, waved his plump hands and insisted over and over that he was a man of meager talents and a poor hand at play-acting.

As he talked on, his eyes suddenly lit up—he had thought of a splendid excuse. “You haven’t any hold over me, so the moment I get away I’m sure to report to my superior straight off, and that’ll be trouble for you too. What say we follow the old precedent and give my head a knock right here: I’ll do my swooning, you go about your business, and neither of us loses any time.”

Weighed down with cares though he was, Zhang Xiaojing could not help a smile; the man was candid, at least. Just then Yisi whispered a few words at his side, and Zhang Xiaojing nodded and said to Adjutant Zhao, “Here’s what we’ll do. You needn’t steal anything for us; just find some pretext or other to draw Ji Wen’s attention away—the space of one stick of incense will do.”

“The moment I set foot in the Deliberation Hall I’m sure to raise the alarm at the top of my voice, and that’ll go ill for you.” Adjutant Zhao said it with an ingratiating smile, sooner willing to be knocked out again than to go in there. Zhang Xiaojing pointed at Yisi. “Do you know who this is?”

Adjutant Zhao had long since noticed the Persian at Zhang Xiaojing’s side, handsome of feature, his eyes bright as gemstones. Zhang Xiaojing said, “This is a spell-caster I’ve brought from Persia, most skilled at drawing out a man’s very soul with his gaze. Should you dare to raise the alarm, within three days you’ll be haled away by that holy engine at his neck, never to be reborn through all eternity.”

This was not spun wholly out of nothing. In the wards of Chang’an it had long been rumored that the West abounded in strange adepts who often came to the Central Lands to work mischief, and every year a few people were arrested for spreading such devil-talk. Zhang Xiaojing had handled so many cases that he could pluck out a scrap of material to hand whenever he liked.

The corner of Yisi’s mouth twitched slightly: with a face as fine as his, to be called a magus of the poison-hex school of sorcery! He could not protest, and could only give a faint smile, turning those two eyes upon Adjutant Zhao—and sure enough, there was a sense, an illusion, of the man’s very wits being shaken.

Adjutant Zhao was duly frightened and had to agree. Still uneasy, he added a further charge: “If you do mean to make your move presently, be sure to kill—and kill thoroughly—or I too shall be dragged into it.”

“I didn’t fucking say I was going to kill him!” Zhang Xiaojing could have kicked him.

Before long Adjutant Zhao, quaking, went into the Deliberation Hall. Ji Wen was writing a dispatch to the Right Minister to claim credit for himself. He wrote and struck out, struck out and wrote, and had just, with great difficulty, hit upon a splendid turn of phrase when he was cut off by the sound of footsteps; he looked up to find Adjutant Zhao standing respectfully before him.

He was a little put out, but Adjutant Zhao was only a rank and two grades below him, and on loan from the Right Xiao Guard besides, so some show of courtesy was due. “What is it, Adjutant?”

Adjutant Zhao said, “There is a matter concerning Zhang Xiaojing, which I have come expressly to report.” At the name Ji Wen’s eyes lit up, and he laid down his brush. “Speak.” Adjutant Zhao glanced to left and right and said, as though in difficulty, “This matter touches on General Gan, and cannot well be spoken of openly; it can only be reported to you in private, Deputy Director.”

Hearing that Gan Shoucheng was involved, Ji Wen was at once seized with interest. He motioned Adjutant Zhao forward and leaned his head in close. Adjutant Zhao gathered his spirits and began to tell him of the Jing’an Bureau’s breaking into the gaol of the Right Xiao Guard.

Adjutant Zhao had lived through the affair himself, and with the embroidery he laid on besides, Ji Wen listened quite rapt, wholly absorbed for the moment.

At the same time a rope came slowly down from the roof-beam, drawing gradually near the floor. As he talked, Adjutant Zhao watched from the corner of his eye, and, seeing a shadow lowering itself down the rope, felt his heart abruptly quicken.

The shadow was Yisi. He had scouted it out just before: this Deliberation Hall was a half-hall, sharing one and the same roof-beam with the adjoining records store. The records store was used for keeping documents, and no one would come there. So Yisi had only to slip into the store, climb up onto the main beam, and could enter the Deliberation Hall without a sound.

In this way, so long as Adjutant Zhao held Ji Wen’s attention, Yisi could do as he pleased.

This was the most perilous and most thrilling cave-running of all. Yisi came down lightly, no more than seven paces from Ji Wen, not daring to draw a loud breath. Let Ji Wen but turn his head a little, and he would find one more person in the room.

Yisi looked about him: besides the writing-desk, the kneeling-rug, and the shelving, there was a heap of brocade-patterned wooden crates stacked in a corner, screened off. Likely the new officer had thought them untidy but had no good way to clear them out just then, and so had simply hidden the whole lot behind a screen. Yisi crept over on tiptoe, went round the screen, and opened one of them: sure enough, inside was a heap of oddments, no doubt the leavings from Changming Ward. But there was no bamboo in the crate, so he went on to open a second.

Outside, seeing that Yisi was still searching, Adjutant Zhao had no choice but to spin things out for all he was worth. Several times Ji Wen made to turn his head, and each time Adjutant Zhao, at the first sign of it, would raise his voice and force in some suspenseful episode that had never happened at all, to pull Ji Wen’s attention back. He groaned inwardly: he was fond enough of reading romances in the ordinary way, but had never thought that one day he would have to make one up himself.

Over there Yisi worked quickly and had already opened a third crate; after clearing away a heap of loose wood-scraps and broken timber, he found at the bottom a coarse cloth sack tied tightly shut. He undid the cord: inside was a handful of loose bamboo pieces. Overjoyed, Yisi reached in and scooped up the sack—but forgot to hold up the crate’s lid. The lid dropped down all at once; Yisi hastily thrust up a palm to cushion it and just caught it in time, but it still gave a soft “thud.”

The sound was not loud, but in the room it was plain enough. Ji Wen whipped his head round and looked over this way in puzzlement. Yisi quickly pressed himself in behind the screen and held his breath. Ji Wen raised a hand to signal Adjutant Zhao to wait a moment, and took a few steps toward the screen. The room was very bare and open, and the only place out of his line of sight was behind this screen; the sound had most likely come from there.

Only a single screen stood between Yisi and Ji Wen, and sweat beaded lightly at the tip of his nose. He was weighing whether to strike and pin Ji Wen down, and force his way out with him as hostage. Seeing the danger, Adjutant Zhao suddenly clutched his head, crouched down in pain, and wailed aloud, “That cursed Zhang Xiaojing, who knocked me senseless—the hurt of it has not left me to this day! Does it pain me? Pain me indeed it does!”

Ji Wen turned back and soothed him with kindly words. Seizing the moment, Yisi put out his everyday skill to its full twelve parts in ten, hauled himself up the rope onto the main beam in a single breath, and drew the rope up after him. Just then a rat happened to run past; Yisi caught it with a snatch of his hand and dropped it down below. The rat, landing, was dazed for only an instant, then sprang up and darted off toward the outside.

Ji Wen turned his head back just then, saw a rat go streaking past, and his face eased: he took it that the sound had come from the creature.

Yisi, gripping the sack, withdrew into the records store and rejoined Zhang Xiaojing outside. By then Adjutant Zhao too had come out, drenched in sweat: Ji Wen, having heard the tale to its end, found that the man was purely airing his grievances and had offered nothing of any present use, and so had given him a scolding and driven him out.

Yisi tugged at Zhang Xiaojing to go, but Zhang Xiaojing looked toward Adjutant Zhao. “Do you know where Yao Runeng is? The young man who broke me out.”

Adjutant Zhao, in charge of internal affairs at the new Jing’an Bureau, was well acquainted with such matters. “He was caught not long ago, and is being held now in the gaol of the Jingzhao Prefecture, on the charge of . . . colluding with you.”

Another piece of ill news confirmed. Zhang Xiaojing had no time to grieve, and asked again, “There is a girl called Wen Ran—do you know what has become of her?” Adjutant Zhao thought a long while, then shook his head. “I don’t know; never heard of her.”

At his side, Yisi heard Zhang Xiaojing give a very plain sigh. He asked in a low voice, “Shall we break into the gaol while we’re about it? Or get Recorder Xu out first?” Zhang Xiaojing shook his head firmly. “We have no time now; they will have to wait.”

Facing the great peril to Chang’an, Zhang Xiaojing could only pick and choose. His large hand tightened, despite himself, about the sack full of broken bamboo. All this night he had been making choices; as for whether they were right or wrong, he had no leisure to consider.

“I can look after them on your behalf,” Adjutant Zhao offered obligingly. “I can’t set them free, but at least they needn’t suffer any hardship.” Then he stole a glance at Yisi’s eyes and hurriedly looked away again.

Zhang Xiaojing lingered no longer; he let Adjutant Zhao go, and then walked with Yisi toward the gate of the Jingzhao Prefecture.

They truly had no time to spare, for now they had to go and find a key figure.

***

The Xingqing Palace lay just within the Chunming Gate at the northeast corner of Chang’an. It had once been called Xingqing Ward, and had been the Son of Heaven’s residence before he took the throne. After his accession he had marked off half of each of the three wards of Yongjia, Shengye, and Daoye and added it to Xingqing Ward, then greatly built up its palaces and halls, styling it “the Southern Interior,” to stand far off, facing the Taiji Palace and the Daming Palace. Over the course of a year the Son of Heaven spent the greater part of his time here, so that it was, to all appearances, the very heart of the city of Chang’an.

The Xingqing Palace differed utterly from an ordinary palace-city: to the north was a cluster of halls, to the south an imperial garden. Its most splendid part was the two towers in the southwest. One was called the Hua’e Xianghui Tower; the other, the Qinzheng Wuben Tower. The Lantern Festival spring banquet was held in the Qinzheng Wuben Tower.

The tower now blazed with lamplight, thrown back and forth by bronze mirrors. The guests’ cups and tallies passed to and fro, and the air was merry beyond compare. Painted serving-maids and attendants, bearing ewers and platters, moved among the seats like flowing water. Amid the drums and music, several dozen performers were dancing the Yellow Lion Dance—this was the Son of Heaven’s own dance, which no one else, save on this day, would ever have the chance to see. Some of the more high-spirited officials and foreign envoys even rose to dance along, drawing round after round of cheers from their fellows.

Li Heng, the heir apparent, gripped a rhinoceros-horn cup and strove to calm himself. But his faintly trembling wrist kept spilling the clear wine that brimmed in it, dotting the carpet with drop after drop. The look on his face was worlds apart from the joyous air all around him.

The attendant had already found out the full truth about the Jing’an Bureau and reported to the heir. Li Heng had not reckoned that things would be even worse than Tanqi had said: Li Bi carried off by the Pifu, the Jing’an Bureau seized by the Right Minister on the strength of it—and the root of all of it was Zhang Xiaojing’s collusion with the outland brigands.

Li Heng could not help falling to blaming Li Bi: it was he who had insisted on employing this condemned convict, and now look what a mess had been poked open. Li Heng cast a glance toward the head of the hall and hardly dared imagine what the outcome would be if all this reached his father the emperor’s ears.

Tanqi took up an ewer and came over as if to pour the wine, saying in a low voice to Li Heng, “Your Highness, at the very least find a way now to have the warrant withdrawn.”

Li Heng glanced toward the lower seats, where, at the very front of those several rows, sat the Right Minister Li Linfu, upright and composed. He shook his head helplessly. “Whether Zhang Xiaojing colluded with the outland brigands is not yet known for certain. To revoke it rashly would, I fear, only give the Right Minister more pretexts.”

On ordinary days, with He Zhizhang and Li Bi as his chief counselors, Li Heng had confidence enough to hold his own. Now that both were gone, in the face of the Right Minister’s assault the heir could only curl himself up into a ball like a hedgehog.

Tanqi said anxiously, “Commander Zhang was with me the whole while; he cannot possibly have colluded with any outland brigands!” Li Heng mistook her meaning and supposed there was some private attachment between the two, and gave her a cold look. “The whereabouts of your young master—that is what you ought to be concerning yourself with, is it not?”

Tanqi could not fail to catch the note beneath the words; her face flushed red, and she dropped at once to her knees. “It is not for his sake, nor for my young master’s, but out of care for the safety of Your Highness and the people of Chang’an. Ruffians such as the Pifu—none but Commander Zhang can stop them.”

“Hmph. Grant, for the sake of argument, that Zhang Xiaojing is innocent. Meeting with a thing like this, he has most likely fled long since. Withdraw the warrant or not, what would be the point?”

“No—Commander Zhang will not give up! All he asks is freedom to come and go, so that he may catch the villains.” Tanqi lifted her head and spoke with firm resolve.

Li Heng waved a hand. “A condemned convict, with a court warrant out on him, who still holds to his first purpose and does his utmost to pursue the case? Even I don’t believe such a thing—how am I to convince anyone else?” Here his tone softened. “I’ll go to the Right Minister presently; all I hope is that the Jing’an Bureau will find Changyuan as soon as it can. The rest I cannot trouble myself with—at the worst, I simply won’t be heir apparent.”

He felt himself sincere and heartfelt, but within Tanqi a ball of fire blazed up, and she longed to fling the wine in his face. Out there, others were racking their wits and risking their lives for Chang’an, while all the heir agonized over, round and round, was matters such as these.

“Those Pifu are still at large, beyond the reach of the law. The Que-le Huo-duo could destroy the whole city of Chang’an at any moment!” Tanqi’s voice rose a little, and the guests nearby all turned to look. Li Heng frowned. “Silence! What if others hear? I have the measure of this matter myself; you need trouble yourself with it no further.” With that he knocked the wine-cup down on the table and sat there swelling with pent-up temper.

To be pressed so hard by a household maidservant—the heir felt it a real loss of face. It was wholly for Li Bi’s sake that he did not order Tanqi hauled out.

Still on her knees, Tanqi shuffled back a few steps, her shoulders beginning to tremble. The heir seemed resolved to stand by with folded arms, and this left her at the utmost loss. Her station was too low; if the heir would not act, there was no other way left to her by which to turn the course of things.

Wait—there was one more way.

Appeal to the Sage face to face?

Tanqi was startled by her own thought—how mad a thing would that be? Yet she raised her neck and looked toward the head of the hall, past the heir. The Son of Heaven was not far off, up on the feasting dais, no more than a few tens of paces away. If she truly meant to rush before the emperor, now was the best chance of all. Tanqi knew that to charge the imperial seat was a grave crime, and that she might well be cut down on the spot by the guards—but at the least she could let the Son of Heaven know that the peril to Chang’an was, at this very hour, pressing hard upon them.

Do not fall back. Do not fall back. Do not fall back. The lantern-signal from the great watchtower lit up once more in her mind.

Tanqi’s breathing grew rapid. She had been an orphan; but for the Li family taking her in, she would long since have become a starved corpse. In this world, apart from her young master, there was nothing to hold her back—and so nothing to fear. Tanqi believed that her young master, meeting with such a thing, would make the same choice. As for that lecher . . . he too must be battling on somewhere in the dark, surely?

These two men had one thing in common: neither had ever treated Tanqi as a puppet with a beautiful shell, and both believed she could do things of more worth than waiting upon others.

Now was the very time to prove it.

Tanqi kowtowed to Li Heng to ask leave to withdraw, then set her back against the cloud-wall behind her.

All the walls here were sheathed in light gauze, embroidered with auspicious clouds in gold thread. When a breeze passed through the tower windows the gauze stirred and drifted, as though clouds were surging through the tower. Every palace serving-woman wore a rosy-cloud cape of the same stuff, and when idle would stand with her back to the cloud-wall, drifting and light as a heavenly maiden.

Keeping close to the cloud-wall, Tanqi edged forward without a change of expression. She lightly hitched up her sash so as to lift her skirt-hem a little higher, lest she trip on it when she came to run.

The Qinzheng Wuben Tower had been designed with the emperor’s feasting with his ministers in mind, and so its whole floor was not level but sloped very slightly. The imperial seat was at the top of the slope, and from there the whole scene could be taken in at a glance. On either side of this slope ran the ways by which the serving-women and attendants carried the dishes; guests going to change their robes or leaving the feast also went by these ways.

Today being a festival, the Son of Heaven attended in informal feast-dress to show that he made merry together with his ministers, and so there were no screens about him, only a hanging curtain of water-beads to set him a little apart. Tanqi went slowly up along this way, and above the glittering upper edge of the bead-curtain she could see that crown unique in all the world, the tongtian crown—she could even count clearly the twelve ridges upon it.

From this spot to the imperial seat there stood between them only one old eunuch and two guards of the imperial presence. She had only to put on a sudden burst of speed and she could reach him before they reacted—though she would have the chance to shout but a single sentence.

That one sentence was of the utmost importance. Tanqi turned it over in her mind, forced down her taut nerves, and made ready to step forward.

At that moment a slender jade hand came to rest on her shoulder. Tanqi started, and turned her head by instinct to see, standing behind her, a Daoist woman with a yellow cap on her head and a moon-white Daoist robe about her, a silk stole over the crook of her arm and a fly-whisk in her hand, looking at her with curiosity.

The Daoist woman was full-figured, her brows and eyes bewitching, with a dot of goose-yellow ornament between her brows: a radiant beauty indeed. The words burst from Tanqi:

“Sister Taizhen?”

The words were scarcely out when the watch-drums boomed outside: the hour of the Rat had come.

The strains of the Rainbow-Feather Dance rose up on cue, carrying the banquet’s mood to yet another height.
