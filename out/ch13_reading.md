## Chapter Thirteen

There was no lightning yet. Only the thunder, rumbling now and again like a heavy lorry driving past. The sky had drawn across itself a grey curtain without end, torn open only at the due-western corner, where a small patch of purple cloud showed through. Behind that purple cloud the flustered face of the setting sun was going down.

In the workshops of the Yuhua Silk Filature the electric lamps had long been lit. The work was at high pressure; the whole shop was one racing wheel. Even the lamps wore yellow faces in the thick steam, as though about to swoon. The women workers, their ears long deadened by the clatter of the reeling-frames, did not hear the thunder in the sky outside, but they heard the talk that passed among themselves; among them, too, a kind of thunder was gathering with a low, sullen roll. Their faces were flushed red, their mouths as busy as their hands. The overseers seemed to be "playing deaf," though they did not "play dumb"; now and then one would drop a word or two under her breath, and a burst of scornful laughter would break out among the women.

Suddenly the siren wailed out, its long hoot ringing through the whole mill. A stir ran through the shop; the noise of the reeling-frames sank lower and lower, and the sound of voices gained the upper hand. Carrying their empty lunch-baskets, the women crowded out of the workshop, submitted in a jostling mass to the search at the gate, and pressed on out through it. Only then did they learn that outside there was thunder, and a murk that goes before a storm, waiting for them!

The mill fell quiet; in the workshops the lamps were switched off. The light striking out from that row of buildings over on the management side seemed, by contrast, to have an added briskness. Tu Weiyue sat in his own room, his head bowed; above him hung a thirty-two-candlepower lamp, and by its light his face showed faintly green, as cold and still as a stone image. All at once the door opened, and Mo Gancheng's flustered face peered round it; he came in and called softly:

"Brother Tu! The Third Master has just telephoned again, asking whether that notice about docking the wages has been posted up! I told him it was your idea to hold it back till tomorrow, and the Third Master was most displeased! What is it exactly that you mean to do? Just now, at knocking-off time, the women were in an uproar; they have got wind again that we mean to post a notice docking their wages, and is that not the same as last time —"

"They're bound to know sooner or later — what is there to be afraid of!"

Tu Weiyue said it with a smile, threw Mo Gancheng a glance, and looked out of the window again.

"If the Third Master is angry tomorrow, that is no concern of mine!"

"Naturally!"

Tu Weiyue was thoroughly out of patience. Mo Gancheng's pair of rat's eyes fixed themselves a moment on Tu Weiyue's face; then he drew in his neck, put on an air of "in that case it is none of my affair," turned, and went out, slamming the door hard behind him. Tu Weiyue smiled and let it pass; but now he could no longer sit there cold and still as a stone image. He drew out his watch and looked at it, craned his head out of the window to gaze into the distance, and at last opened the door and went out. At that very moment two figures came hurrying up through the dusk and made straight for Tu Weiyue's room. Quick-eyed, Tu Weiyue had already seen them and turned back; he had just reached the door of his own room when someone came up behind him, gave him a light pat on the shoulder, and let out a little chuckle.

"A Zhen! Just now the two of us must keep things strictly to business!"

Tu Weiyue said it softly, turning his head, and stepped into the room; A Zhen followed him in.

Already in the room were Gui Changlin and pockmarked Li, who gave a single "Oh!" at the sight of Tu Weiyue and both began at once, each trying to speak first. But Tu Weiyue checked them with a look and, pointing to a long bench in the corner, made the two of them and A Zhen sit down, while he himself went and stood before the window with his back to it. All at once the thirty-two-candlepower lamp seemed to shrink its flame. The air in the room was uncommonly grave. Out in the sky the thunder rolled slowly over. A tinge of red came into Tu Weiyue's faintly green face; he ran his eye over the three of them and asked:

"Well! And where is Yao Jinfeng?"

"To keep from catching people's eyes, I did not call her! Whatever you want her to do, I will pass the word to her presently!"

A Zhen answered before the others, her smiling eyes fastened on Tu Weiyue's face; Tu Weiyue only gave a nod, without answering A Zhen and without answering that inviting look of hers. Then his face darkened of a sudden, and he raised his voice a little:

"Now everyone must be of one mind and get the thing done! Jealousy and rivalry, muttering and bickering among ourselves — none of that is allowed!"

A Zhen pulled a face and let out a "Pooh." Tu Weiyue behaved as though he had neither seen nor heard, and went on:

"Wang Jinzhen — I have sent her off on another errand, so she cannot be here; it will just be the four of us to talk it over. — The Third Master telephoned again just now to ask why I still have not put out the notice. This time the Third Master is in a great hurry, his temper is up! I have promised him it shall go out tomorrow without fail. The Third Master understands, too, that we need a little time to set things in order before we take the knife to them. He has made that plain enough! But our enemies are sure to undermine us in front of the Third Master. Three parts of our strength go to dealing with the workers, and seven parts, if you please, must go to dealing with our enemies! Changlin, the way you see it, the moment that notice is posted tomorrow there will be a row, eh?"

"There is sure to be a row! Qian Baosheng and his lot are only too eager for one — they mean to seize the chance to pull us down! The pack of curs, hmph!"

"Mr. Tu! Let us muster our men, and if they kick up a fuss tomorrow, we needn't stand on ceremony — we will 'treat' them to a taste of the Public Security Bureau; if we move fast enough, then however Qian Baosheng and his crew may be plotting their devilry, they will be too late for it!"

Seeing that Gui Changlin had put forward no plan, pockmarked Li hastened to get his own word in first, spreading out his two great palms with much complacency, spitting on them, rubbing them together, and then clenching them into two fists on his knees, striking the attitude of a man ready to fall to blows. Tu Weiyue took no notice of him; with a faint smile he turned to look at A Zhen and asked:

"A Zhen! Why don't you speak? What was the workshop like just now? We let out word of the wage-cut — what are the workers saying? Xue Baozhu, and that Second Sister Zhou — what rumours are they spreading? Speak up! Be quick!"

"I don't know! Send for Yao Jinfeng and ask her!"

A Zhen answered with her lips thrust out, and turned her face away to look at the corner of the wall. Tu Weiyue's expression changed all at once. Gui Changlin and pockmarked Li burst out laughing and pulled faces at A Zhen to shame her. Tu Weiyue's eyes were red enough to spurt fire; he stamped his foot and was on the point of flying into a rage, when A Zhen softened; she said, as if in a sulk:

"What are they saying? They are saying, 'Down with Tu the Chamber-pot!' And what do Xue Baozhu and Second Sister Zhou say? They say, 'It is all the Chamber-pot's doing!' — ever so many pretty things, more than I can repeat! — And you needn't laugh either, Changlin. In this 'Down with,' you have your share too!"

At this moment the first flash of lightning came outside the window. Two or three seconds later the thunder came rolling up from far off. A sudden gust of wild wind blew into the room, and all four of them shivered.

Tu Weiyue suddenly waved a hand, checking the angry bellow that had already risen to pockmarked Li's lips, and asked coldly:

"We now have hard proof that Qian Baosheng and his lot are deliberately out to make trouble for us — so what do we mean to do? Last night I told the Third Master I wanted to resign. The Third Master would not hear of it. So I have no choice but to carry on. That the union splits into cliques and factions is really no affair of mine; only I am one who cannot abide seeing a wrong done! To be honest, I see Changlin and his people too hard done by, and Qian Baosheng and his lot too high-handed! Old Li, tell me — am I right in this?"

"Right! Down with the fellow Qian!"

Pockmarked Li and Gui Changlin cried out with one voice, while A Zhen, off to one side, laughed behind her hand. Tu Weiyue threw out his chest, let out a breath, and went on:

"It is not that we are cleaning up the Third Master's mess for him; the truth is that Qian Baosheng and his lot use public business for private ends, laying hold of the workers to carve out ground for themselves. A single day that they are here is a day this place has no peace! That all of us should suffer for a little private greed of theirs — it is an outrage past all bearing! Tomorrow they mean to use the workers against us; very well, then, we will have it out with them! First we will drive off Qian's gang, then settle the strike — three days, three days at the very most!"

"But the way they stirred things up in the workshop today, a good many people have come to believe them."

A Zhen said it with her lips flattened. At once Gui Changlin knit his brows in heavy anxiety. He had never carried much weight among the workers himself; what little footing he had won of late was owing entirely to Tu Weiyue. Tu Weiyue took in the situation at a glance and gave a cold laugh, despising in his heart Gui Changlin's uselessness. He turned his eyes to pockmarked Li. This coarse, pock-faced fellow had his pair of eyes round and staring and his two fists clenched, showing plainly the mark of his kind: whoever hires him, for him he will spend his strength. Tu Weiyue felt well satisfied. He took a step forward, so that he stood right under the lamp, and spoke first to A Zhen:

"The workers believe them, do they? Do you mean to tell me that you, A Zhen, with that honeyed mouth of yours, are still no match for Xue Baozhu? That Yao Jinfeng is no match for their Second Sister Zhou? They can gull the workers — do you mean to say you cannot? The workers do not yet know that Second Sister Zhou is Qian's running dog — are the two words 'running dog' carved on your faces? Can't you strip the mask off Second Sister Zhou in front of the workers and let them all see her plain? Go! A Zhen! Go and tell Yao Jinfeng to join in the workers' clamour too! Against Qian Baosheng, Xue Baozhu, Second Sister Zhou! It is no matter if a strike comes off tomorrow! Go at once! Presently there will be others to back you up! Off with you! I will set this down as your first merit!"

"Who wants your reckoning of merits! Business is business, and that is enough."

A Zhen rose, deliberately gave Tu Weiyue a scornful roll of the eyes, and walked out. Tu Weiyue cocked his head and thought a moment, then took another step forward and, patting pockmarked Li on the shoulder, asked in a low voice:

"Old Li, can you muster twenty men together this evening?"

"Can do, can do! Never mind twenty — fifty would be easy!"

Pockmarked Li sprang up, his face reddening with delight, a spray of spittle flying from his mouth onto Tu Weiyue's face. Tu Weiyue gave a smile.

"Splendid, then! But tonight I only want twenty — to go strolling about that stretch of thatched huts where the workers live. — Old Li, you take my meaning? Just stroll about there. If you come on any quarrelling, let it be. But there are two people to be tailed: one is He Xiumei, the other is Zhang Axin — that flat-faced, big-bosomed Zhang Axin, you know the one? Early tomorrow these twenty of your brothers are to come to the mill as well. What they are to do we will settle tomorrow; for now go to Mr. Mo and draw a hundred dollars. There — off you go!"

Now only Tu Weiyue and Gui Changlin were left in the room, the two of them, and for a time neither spoke. The thunder wheeled about the sky, louder than before, but slack and lazy, like the night-soil carts of a morning. The lightning came round about every three minutes, and even so was but a brief glimpse. The wind, however, had grown stronger, and set the lamp in the room swinging. Outside the window the sky had gone wholly black. Tu Weiyue looked at his watch: it was just half past seven.

"Mr. Tu, if this strike drags on too many days, I am afraid we too shall come off the worse for it. Those three newcomers in the counting-house — the one surnamed Zeng, the one surnamed Ma, and Boss Wu's distant nephew — all speak ill of you behind your back. It looks as if they have thrown in with Qian Baosheng."

Gui Changlin said it slowly and low, and there was in his tone an undisguised pessimism. Tu Weiyue shrugged his shoulders and smiled. He feared nothing. Gui Changlin closed one small eye and, still in a low voice, went on:

"Just now you did not warn pockmarked Li against telling A Xiang how things stand with us — that was a false move. That A Xiang, I have always suspected he is the ear Qian Baosheng planted here among us! And pockmarked Li, for his part, is thick with him."

"Changlin, timid as you are, you will never do great things! This is the moment we must use our men, so we have no choice but to run a little risk! I have my ways of keeping A Xiang in check. The hard part still lies on the workers' side. Before Boss Wu I have thumped my chest and pledged to settle the strike within three days, to net every one of those rascals at a stroke, and to have no labour trouble for half a year or six months. So tomorrow I shall let them come out on strike — of course, even if we wished to forbid it we could not, but tomorrow I do not yet mean to use force. We will let them strike for two days, let them bring down Qian Baosheng's faction first, and then we will deal with them by violent means! So, Changlin, you must bestir yourself! Get the greater part of the workers into your hands."

"Am I to tell my people to come out against the wages being docked to eight-tenths as well?"

"Naturally! First we will deal with He Xiumei and her sort, and only then coax the workers to go back to work first and negotiate after. I am certain the two of them, He Xiumei and Zhang Axin, are up to something, but there are sure to be others besides, and we must ferret them out. Changlin, this business too I leave to you — bring me word tomorrow!"

As he spoke Tu Weiyue looked at his watch once more, dismissed Gui Changlin, and left his own room as well.

The lightning glanced across the wide sky, showing that the black clouds massed overhead were no longer one sheet of inky grey but were parted now into light and dark; here and there a dense one reared up sheer, like a mountain, blacker the nearer one came to its root. The thunder grew louder still. Tu Weiyue hurried across an open yard stacked with packing-cases and came to the outside of a room. It was the office where Wu Sunfu, on his visits to the mill, would summon the staff before him; ordinarily it stood empty, but now a faint light showed through the chinks of its tightly closed shutters. Tu Weiyue pushed the door open and went in, and the two people in the room both stood up. Tu Weiyue smiled and gestured to them to sit, and first put a question to Wang Jinzhen, the No. 2 frame overseer:

"Have you told her yet?"

"We have only just come ourselves. We were waiting for Mr. Tu to tell her himself."

Wang Jinzhen answered in an odd manner, gave Tu Weiyue a wink besides, and stood up as if to go. But Tu Weiyue pressed a hand down through the air, bidding Wang Jinzhen sit down again, while he turned his eyes on the young woman worker sitting there ill at ease. She was a bobbed-haired girl of about twenty, of middling height, very dark of skin, yet with a comeliness beneath the darkness, and a pair of eyes that were uncommonly quick. Under Tu Weiyue's boring gaze her face swelled to a purplish red.

Tu Weiyue looked at her a while, then said with a smile, very mildly:

"Zhu Guiying, you have been at the mill nearly two years now; your skill is none so bad, and you are a decent, well-behaved girl. I have spoken to the boss and mean to promote you to overseer. This is a jump over the heads of others, as I daresay you understand?"

Zhu Guiying flushed red and did not answer, her eyes on the ground. Her heart had begun to pound, her thoughts in a tangle. When Wang Jinzhen had come for her, she had said only that there was something to be spoken of in the counting-house, and Zhu Guiying had supposed that her show of opposition to the wage-cut before knocking-off had been reported by some running dog, and that the counting-house had sent for her to give her a scolding; now she heard the very opposite, and for a moment she was thrown into confusion. Besides, this Tu Weiyue, who held power on the mill's side, had always been fond of seizing the chance to trifle and flirt with her, so that this move now might well be his making a pass at her; and at that thought she was less able than ever to get out a word. Just then Wang Jinzhen chimed in from the side, beating the flanking drum:

"Truly there is no one fairer than Boss Wu, and Mr. Tu is willing to lend a hand too — but then that is because you are such a good sort yourself, Sister Guiying!"

"There is sense in what Wang Jinzhen says! Boss Wu is a fair man, and well able to feel for others. He is forever saying that, but for the drop in the price of filature silk and the loss he stands to make, he would gladly have granted that rice-allowance last time out of hand. And but for the price of filature silk falling again of late, he would never have turned his mind to docking the wages to eight-tenths! Yet, for all his losses, when Boss Wu sees a hand who is skilled and decent, he still gives her her due, and jumps her up a grade!"

Tu Weiyue was mild as before, his sharp eyes measuring Zhu Guiying up and down. Though she kept her head bowed, Zhu Guiying felt that gaze. At last she made up her mind, lifted her head, went pale, and said in a low voice but firmly:

"Thank you, Mr. Tu! I have no such good fortune!"

At this moment the lightning flashed outside, and all at once a crack of thunder came down overhead, so that the very room seemed to shake a little.

Tu Weiyue's expression changed too — perhaps at the thunderclap, but perhaps at Zhu Guiying's answer. He knit his brows and gave Wang Jinzhen a look. Wang Jinzhen nodded, pulled a face, and slipped quietly out. Zhu Guiying at once stood up as well. But Tu Weiyue barred her way.

"Mr. Tu! What is it you want?"

"Don't be alarmed — I have a few words to say to you —"

Zhu Guiying's face went red again, red as pig's liver. She had made up her mind that he was making a pass at her; in the old days, when Tu Weiyue was still a petty clerk, there had indeed been a time when she found the young fellow not unlikeable, but ever since Tu Weiyue had risen to be the most powerful man in the counting-house, she had felt a high mountain reared up between them, so that even to exchange a few extra words with him made her most uneasy; and now this Tu Weiyue had lured her here and was barring her way, and would not let her go!

"I do not want to hear it! Send for me to the counting-house tomorrow and say it there!"

Zhu Guiying answered with her eyes fixed on Tu Weiyue's face, and stood her ground. Tu Weiyue smiled coldly.

"Don't be alarmed! I keep things proper with a woman — I do not take liberties, I do not paw and grope! I only want to ask you this: why won't you take the rise to overseer? There is nothing troublesome I would set you to do; you need only lend us a hand and tell me which few people have dealings with the disreputable sort outside — with the Communists — and that will be enough! Nor will I let it out that you were the one to report them! You see, I have even sent Wang Jinzhen off out of the way!"

Tu Weiyue was courteous as ever, and his voice very low; but Zhu Guiying, hearing him, gave a start within, and her face turned wholly ashen. So it was not, after all, that he meant to make a pass at her — she simply hated this Tu Weiyue!

"That — that I do not know!"

With that Zhu Guiying darted past Tu Weiyue and ran straight off. She could still hear Wang Jinzhen calling behind her, and heard Tu Weiyue give a shout, seeming to call Wang Jinzhen back; but Zhu Guiying never turned her head, and in a flurry skirted the reeling-shop and ran for the mill gate.

Some four or five zhang from the mill gate stood the cocoon-house, a row of foreign-style buildings looming black. Zhu Guiying had just run up to it when a flash of lightning suddenly lit far and near as bright as day. A thunderbolt crashed down overhead, and in the roll of that thunder a man sprang out and seized her about the breast. Taken unawares, Zhu Guiying went limp in every limb, her heart pounding, unable to cry out. The man, holding her fast, had already carried her several steps.

"Help! You —"

Zhu Guiying struggled and cried out, taking him in her heart for Tu Weiyue. But the thunder went rolling and crashing about the sky, and her cry was of no avail. Suddenly another flash of lightning lit everything, far and near, snow-bright, and Zhu Guiying saw clearly that the man was not Tu Weiyue. At that very instant another man came toward them, a storm-lantern in his hand, and barred the way, demanding sharply:

"What is going on here?"

This was Tu Weiyue's voice. The man holding Zhu Guiying let go and made to slip away. With one hand Tu Weiyue caught him fast. Lifting the lantern to throw light on him, he knew it for Zeng Jiaju. Tu Weiyue's face went livid, and he fixed him with a stare. The thunder came too, slow now and trailing its tail. Tu Weiyue let Zeng Jiaju go, turned his face to Zhu Guiying, and smiled coldly.

"If you will not speak, no matter — but why run off! Walking out alone, would the gatekeeper at the mill gate ever let you through? Better go along with Wang Jinzhen!"

Tu Weiyue said it courteously as ever, called Wang Jinzhen over, and then went back.

By the time Zhu Guiying reached what she called "home," it had already begun to rain — great, scattered drops that beat noisily on the bamboo door of her "home." No lamp was lit in the thatched hut. But the light from the neighbours' came slanting through a hole in the broken mud wall, so that one could still dimly tell black from white. Zhu Guiying caught her breath a while before she heard someone groaning on the broken bamboo couch — it was her mother.

"What is it? Ma! Are you ill?"

Zhu Guiying went to her mother's side and laid a hand on the old woman's wrinkle-heaped brow. Seeing her daughter, the old woman seemed for a moment glad, yet could not hold back her sobs. The old woman was forever weeping, and Zhu Guiying paid it no mind; she only sighed, her thoughts going to the nightmare she had just come through, and then to the rumour that the mill meant to dock the wages to eight-tenths. Anxiety and hatred burned in her heart like fire. Her mother, choking, cried out again:

"A Ying, in times like these — for us poor folk — there is but one road, and that a dead one!"

Zhu Guiying gazed blankly at her mother and said nothing. A dead road? Zhu Guiying had long known that they were on a "dead road." But at nineteen, her courage tempered in a life of want, she would not so lightly let her thoughts run only to death; rather she thought that she ought to live as easily as other people did. Patting her mother on the breast, she asked, as though to soothe her:

"Ma! Business was bad today, I suppose?"

"Bad business? Ah, A Ying! Business has been hard — and not for one day only; don't I weep every day of my life? But today — go and look! Look at that rice-bowl of mine!"

The old woman flew suddenly into a passion, scrambled up all at once, and with her mouth drawn flat gave herself up wholly to her rancour.

Zhu Guiying picked up from the corner the wicker basket in which her mother hawked peanuts, the basket she carried on her arm every day, and looking at it closely saw that its handle-ring had been torn off and it could be used no more. The basket was empty. Zhu Guiying tossed it aside and, puffing out her cheeks, said:

"Ma, you have been quarrelling with someone, haven't you?"

"Quarrelling? Would I dare quarrel with anyone? The heaven-cursed robbers, the redcaps, came picking a fight for no reason at all! They snatched my peanuts and said they would haul me off to the station to answer for it in the courts!"

"How could they seize a body's goods for no rhyme or reason?"

"He said I was something-or-other — I cannot rightly remember! Look at that paper! He said this paper breaks the law!"

The more the old woman talked the more incensed she grew; she left off weeping, groped her way to the plank table, struck a match, and lit the kerosene lamp. At the bottom of the basket Zhu Guiying saw a few small square sheets of paper printed with some lines of red characters. It was wrapping-paper for the peanuts. She remembered that a dozen or so days before, Sixizi the rag-picker next door had somewhere scavenged a good thick wad of it, and her mother had traded a packet of peanuts for a share to use as wrapping — but did this paper break the law? Zhu Guiying took up a sheet to look closely, and in the midst of a line of large characters were three that seemed very familiar; she thought a moment and remembered — those three characters were "Communist Party." Those three characters were often to be seen on the wall by the mill gate and on the telegraph-poles along the road; her younger brother Xiao Sanzi had pointed them out and taught her to know them, and it was this very thing that Tu Weiyue had called her in to ask about just now.

"And I am not the only one to use such paper. Old Eight who sells cooked beef uses it too. And there is —"

The old woman, her lips trembling, cried out at the wrong done her and cursed. Zhu Guiying's shrewd heart had already seen through it: it was the ragamuffins who "scrounge a living" along the road, making the paper their pretext to squeeze a little out of her. She brushed the sheets aside, said no more to her mother, and picked up the basket again to see whether it could be mended and used. But all at once a graver care rose in her; the wicker basket fell from her hand to the mud floor again, and she cocked her ear to listen.

In the thatched huts to left and right — the dwellings of Zhu Guiying's sisters from the same mill — voices were raised in noisy dispute and bitter cursing. The sound of the rain beating on the bamboo doors was quicker and louder now, and the thunder rolled over the roofs of the huts; yet the human voices along that row of huts were fiercer than the rain, fiercer than the thunder. The bamboo doors creaked and cried out, and each cry was someone going in or coming out. The whole quarter of the silk-mill workers was astir as never before under the great rain and the racing thunder! Another kind of thunder was about to burst skyward from among these huts!

Zhu Guiying could sit still no longer; she leapt up all at once and was on the point of going out when the bamboo door of her own home creaked, and in burst a lean lad in a short jacket and trousers of blue cloth, bawling and cursing at the top of his throat:

"The mother-cursed dog of a boss! Money for whoring! Money for gambling! Money for putting up foreign houses! But for paying out wages, none! The beast, whelped of a cur of a father!"

This was Zhu Guiying's younger brother Xiao Sanzi, a worker at the match factory. Heedless of his mother's and sister's questions, he raged on:

"Six jiao a day for wages, and at the start of spring this year they cut it by one jiao; today the fellow Zhou has hung out a notice again, prating of heavy costs and losses, and means to cut another jiao!"

As he spoke he snatched up the box of matches on the broken table, slammed it down hard, and cursed again:

"A thing like this sells at two coppers a box, and still they cry a loss! — Sister, give me eight coppers to buy a flatcake. The people at our factory are holding a meeting tonight; I am going with Jin Heshang from next door! The mother-cursed fellow Zhou wants to cut our wages — well, your old man is going the hell out on strike!"

When the old woman made out that the factory where her son worked was again to cut wages, it was as though the sky had fallen. Xiao Sanzi had already gone. Zhu Guiying followed him out. The rain drove into her face, and she found it, on the contrary, refreshing; the fire of wrath in her heart leapt up ten thousand fathoms, and the rain, reaching her burning face, seemed as if it would dry.

Outside the bamboo door lay strewn the refuse the great rain had washed down. A flash of lightning lit that stretch of huts snow-bright, and by its light one saw people hurrying through the downpour. But the darkness after the flash was harder to bear. Zhu Guiying's goal, however, lay at the eastern end of the huts, four or five zhang off. She was bound for the "home" of Zhang Axin, a sister from the same mill; she meant to tell Zhang Axin how Tu Weiyue had summoned her, how he had tried to trick her, how he had sounded her out as to who had dealings with the Communists. Her heart was busier even than her feet. Yet just as she was nearing the hut before Zhang Axin's home, a figure sprang suddenly out of the dark and caught Zhu Guiying fast.

"Sister Guiying!"

That call at her ear steadied Zhu Guiying's wildly beating heart. She knew the voice: it was Jin Xiaomei, the basin-girl at the mill. A girl of thirteen, yet she understood grown people's affairs; she was the sister of Jin Heshang next door. Clinging to Zhu Guiying, Jin Xiaomei asked:

"Sister, where are you off to?"

"To Sister Axin's."

"No need to go there. They are all at Yao Jinfeng's house. Let us go together!"

So the two of them turned back and went off to the left. As they went, Jin Xiaomei told her a deal more "news"; Zhu Guiying grew hot all over as she listened, forgetting the rain, forgetting that her clothes were soaked through. — Yao Jinfeng was taking the lead this time too! So it was false after all, what Xue Baozhu had said last time, that she was the boss's running dog! And who else? Second Sister Zhou and Qian Qiaolin? Aiya! Isn't that Qian Baosheng's own sister from the union? This time she was in earnest too! Heaven, so the workers stand by the workers after all!

Before long they had run up close to Yao Jinfeng's house. It too was a thatched hut, but a tidier one, and it had a wooden door. The shouting could be heard from far off. Zhu Guiying's heart leapt for joy. Last time, during the "go-slow," there had been nothing so lively, so bold; last time it had all been plotted in secret and in whispers.

Jin Xiaomei sprang a step ahead to open the door, and no sooner had Zhu Guiying squeezed in than she felt a hot reek of sweat. The room was full of voices, full of heads. A single kerosene lamp lit only a space a few feet square, and within its ring of light was a plump, pale face with hooded eyelids — who should it be but Qian Qiaolin!

"It is Gui Changlin and Tu the Chamber-pot, the two of them, fawning on the boss! Strike! We strike tomorrow! Beat these two running dogs!"

Qian Qiaolin bawled it aloud, and from her hooded eyes fell a single tear.

"Strike! Strike! Over in Hongkou several mills have already come out!"

"We will go and make contact with them —"

"When they come tomorrow to rush the mill and block the way, we will shut down the frames and rush out to join them!"

Five or six voices said it, each cutting in ahead of the rest. Zhu Guiying made out clearly only the last speaker, one called Auntie Xu, a timid woman worker of thirty-odd.

"Tell Tu the Chamber-pot to clear off! Tell Gui Changlin to clear off!"

Beside Qian Qiaolin a head thrust out and cried aloud — it was that noted dumpy little woman, Second Sister Zhou. But at once someone else cried out too:

"Tell Qian Baosheng to get out as well! We will not have that cheating union of theirs! We want a union of our own!"

All at once the clamouring voices fell dead still. Many a sweat-grimed face turned this way and that in search of the one who had spoken. It was He Xiumei, her whole face flushed red, her eyes wide, glaring fixedly at Qian Qiaolin. But this tense silence broke again in an instant. Yao Jinfeng's small round face, pale and finely pock-pitted, flashed within the ring of lamplight, and she cried shrilly:

"That is right, tell Qian Baosheng to get out! And out with Qian Baosheng's running dogs too! Second Sister Zhou is Qian Baosheng's running dog!"

"You slut! It is you that is Tu the Chamber-pot's running dog!"

Second Sister Zhou screamed it as if gone mad, sprang up, and flung herself straight at Yao Jinfeng. The two of them grappled together. But the other women all took Yao Jinfeng's part, pulled the two of them apart in an instant, shoved Second Sister Zhou far off, and clamoured in a hubbub:

"Whoever strikes the first blow is in the wrong!"

"Sisters! I say Second Sister Zhou is Qian Baosheng's running dog, and I have proof of it! She has wormed her way in here to spy out our news!"

Yao Jinfeng said it breathlessly, her two darting glances rolling over the faces of the crowd, probing to see what effect her words had had.

A confused clamour arose in which no one could make out anyone else's words. Yet all of them knew that all were of one mind: Second Sister Zhou was no good sort! Amid the confusion one voice cried out louder than the rest — it was Zhang Axin:

"Qian Qiaolin has come to spy out our news too! Drive her out! Qian Baosheng's sister is no good sort either!"

"And she is carrying on with that fellow surnamed Zeng who is newly come to the mill! The fellow Zeng is some cousin or other of the boss's!"

Another voice cried out. And so the tumult began. At this point, had Qian Qiaolin and her sort made the least show of resistance, they would have caught a beating. But Qian Qiaolin and Second Sister Zhou, unguarded against this sudden onset, were of a moment at a loss. The two of them saw clearly in their hearts: better not take a loss right under one's nose. Watching their chance, they slipped away. Zhu Guiying seized the moment to squeeze in further, until she was almost at Zhang Axin's side.

"They have all run off! They are sure to go and inform on us — let us break up quickly!"

Timid Auntie Xu, squeezing along, strained her voice to a shout, meaning to make them all hear. They all heard her, but the answer was the opposite of what she wished.

"No, no! What is there to fear! We have not settled anything yet!"

"Tomorrow, once we have chosen our delegates in the workshop, we will rush out of the mill! Strike!"

"Then we will go on to rush Boss Wu's 'new mill,' and the other mills as well! We will sweep every mill in Zhabei clean at a rush!"

"Better first make good contact with those few mills in Hongkou that have come out; when they come rushing, we will shut down the frames and answer them!"

Another, who held for waiting for others to come and "rush" them, said it in a hurry — she stood right beside Zhu Guiying, and Zhu Guiying knew her for Lu Xiaobao.

"Pah — anyone who would wait for others to come and rush us is a running dog!"

He Xiumei cried in fury and spat full in Lu Xiaobao's face. Lu Xiaobao would not give way either. The two of them fell to trading curses.

Now the question had shifted to this: whether to wait for others to come and "rush the mill," or to rush out themselves and go on to "rush" the other mills. The seven or eight people in the room split into two parties. He Xiumei, Zhang Axin, and the rest, Zhu Guiying among them, held for rushing out themselves. Yao Jinfeng held the same. These seven or eight present each stood for two or three banks of frames, so that what they decided tonight could be carried out tomorrow. Auntie Xu again begged them all to take note:

"Be quick! They have gone to inform on us — someone is sure to come!"

Just at this moment Jin Xiaomei wormed in again through the press of people and said in a flurry that she had seen seven or eight "loafers" pacing back and forth nearby, as though looking for someone. Every face was startled. Only Yao Jinfeng understood in her heart: A Zhen had already told her everything; yet she too seized the chance to urge that they all disperse and settle matters in the workshop tomorrow. Her "task" was accomplished, and she was eager to meet A Zhen soon and report her success.

The rain had slackened a little; it was very cold outside, and the people coming out all shivered. Zhu Guiying and Zhang Axin, with another called Chen Yue'e, the three of them arm in arm, pressed close together and walked along the road. Chen Yue'e whispered in Zhang Axin's ear:

"It looks as if we are certain to come out tomorrow! Ma Jin is still there waiting for word from us."

"We will go at once! But it is terribly cold. Our clothes dry and then get soaked again!"

Zhang Axin answered in a whisper too. On Zhang Axin's left, Zhu Guiying heard their talk of "going" as well, and at once she thought of how Tu Weiyue had tried to lure her with the post of overseer. She was just about to speak when she suddenly caught sight of a man in a long black gown who had slipped out by the roadside and was following behind them. At once she nudged Zhang Axin's arm and jerked her chin to the rear. At this Chen Yue'e saw him too, and, pressing her elbow against Zhang Axin's waist, said loudly on purpose:

"Aiya! My goodness! It is terribly cold! Sister Axin, we must part ways here — see you tomorrow!"

The three of them unlinked their chained arms and went off by three separate ways.

When Chen Yue'e had gone a zhang or so, she deliberately turned a corner and, looking carefully, saw the man in the long black gown following close behind Zhang Axin. Chen Yue'e's heart gave a jump; she knew Zhang Axin was careless. She stopped at once and called out loudly:

"Sister Axin! You have left your handkerchief in my hand!"

Zhang Axin stopped, turned her head, and saw the man in the long black gown too; she answered, "Give it back tomorrow," and went straight home. The man in the black gown slipped out again from the roadside and followed close behind.

Chen Yue'e, seeing plainly that there was indeed no one tailing her, ran off quickly. She left the thatched-hut quarter of the workers and ran into a filthy back lane. At the rear door of the last house she knocked softly three times, and with a twist of her body slipped inside.

In the upstairs "front room" stood three broken beds, yet only one square table. Two bobbed-haired young women sat at the table with their heads bent, writing something by the dim electric light. Chen Yue'e's step was light, yet both writers had already heard it. The one of the two whose eyes were the more full of spirit lifted her head first, gave Chen Yue'e a greeting with her eyes, and then bent again to her writing.

As she wrote, she said:

"Cai Zhen, finish up quickly! Sister Yue is here, and it is getting late — let us get the meeting started at once!"

"Then it is not too late to write it after the meeting is over."

The woman called Cai Zhen stretched herself lazily and laid down her pen. She stood up and stretched once more. She was somewhat taller than Chen Yue'e, and wore a white cotton blouse cut short to the waist and wide-legged trousers of black cotton, like a silk-mill hand. But her cultivated face and bearing showed that she was, after all, one of the educated. Her eyes looked short of sleep, and her complexion was pale with a tinge of green.

The other laid down her pen too, and, her sharp, spirited eyes glancing first at Chen Yue'e, asked quickly:

"Sister Yue, how do things stand at your mill? If it can be set going tomorrow, then there is hope of a general strike of all the silk filatures in Zhabei."

And so, with much labour and in her plain sentences, Chen Yue'e set forth the state of the workshop that day and the meeting at Yao Jinfeng's house she had just come through; she forced in here and there a few newly learned "terms," saying over and over that "the fighting spirit is running high," and that, so long as there was "leadership," there would be no question of "setting it off" tomorrow. Her manner was excited; now and then in her report she stopped to catch her breath, and her brow was beaded all over with sweat.

"Much the same as on the Hongkou side! Tomorrow you are certain to come out first and then go rush the mills, and bring about a general strike of the silk filatures in Zhabei!"

Cai Zhen picked out the questions left unsettled in Chen Yue'e's report, and gave her conclusion roundly.

But Ma Jin, the woman whose eyes were so full of spirit, said nothing; she watched Chen Yue'e sharply and without blinking, as if to see whether there was any exaggeration in her "report." She felt, too, that the "report" held certain complicated problems within it; yet her mind had never been very quick, and for the moment she only felt it, and could not at once analyse it aright.

Outside the window the rain came whispering down again, and the lightning flashed once more. Within the window was a silent tension.

"Ma Jin, decide quickly! We have other business too!"

Cai Zhen urged her on impatiently, rapping the penholder on the table; to her the question was very simple: "the workers' fighting spirit is surging up," for at this very moment the "revolutionary high tide" had come, general throughout all China! For ever since March there had been the tramway strike in the International Settlement, the bus strike, the water-and-electricity strike in the French Concession, the ceaseless "spontaneous struggles" in every factory in Shanghai; and every "economic struggle," once begun, turned in an instant into a "political struggle," so that now it had already "risen to a revolutionary high tide" — all this she had heard again and again from Ke Zuofu, and it had by now become the very formula of her thinking.

And this "formula," to the ear, was very crisp and very "reasonable," and had been fixed fast in Chen Yue'e's memory just like the other "terms," and passed on by her in turn to Zhang Axin and He Xiumei; their simple heads and their heated feelings were, as it happened, the very soil best suited to such a "formula."

But Ma Jin was a little different; she felt that there was still something amiss in that "formula," yet, lacking as she was in both learning and experience, she felt it well enough but could not say it clearly. Nor did she dare to speak wildly. She was always minded to study more from the actual problems, and so over Chen Yue'e's present report she pondered and pondered again. Hearing Cai Zhen urge her on, she had no choice but to bring out, and not very completely, some of the opinions she had come to feel:

"Don't be so hasty! We must analyse it soberly. Sister Yue says that this time Yao Jinfeng's showing is better even than last time; but last time, didn't Yao Jinfeng waver? And another thing: with the two factions of the yellow union at each other's throats, perhaps Yao Jinfeng is that Gui Changlin's tool, worming her way in to seize the masses, to seize the leadership of the strike? All this we must first put into our reckoning!"

"Wrong! The question is quite plain: the revolutionary ardour of the masses has overcome Yao Jinfeng's wavering! What is more, you overlook the fighting spirit of the masses in the revolutionary high tide, you make light of the masses' revolutionary power of chastisement, and you still suppose that a tool of the yellow union can lead the masses — this of yours is a Right-deviationist view!"

Cai Zhen retorted at once, citing "formula" upon "formula," "term" upon "term"; and on her green-tinged pale face a flush of red came out too. Chen Yue'e, listening at her side, did not quite follow it, but felt that what Cai Zhen said was very fine.

Ma Jin's face flushed red too, and she shot back at once:

"How is mine a Right-deviationist view?"

"Because you doubt the great revolutionary strength of the masses, because you cannot see the surging up of the masses' fighting spirit!"

Without the least effort Cai Zhen cited yet another "formula." Ma Jin's face went pale again in a flash; she sprang up and said sternly:

"Mine is not a Right-deviationist view! I want to analyse the complicated facts — I hold that there is something behind Yao Jinfeng's Left-leaning show!"

"Then are we, for fear that Yao Jinfeng may seize the leadership, to hold off from setting things going? If this is not a Right-deviationist view, what is it?"

"I never said we should hold off from setting things going! What I hold is that we must first settle our tactics, and set things going after!"

"What tactics? You would have us settle tactics still! You have forgotten our general line! Right deviation!"

"Cai Zhen! I will not wrangle with you over Right deviation or no Right deviation! I only ask you this: the doings of the various factions of running dogs and blackleg-agents among the workers of the Yuhua Silk Filature — are we to think out no way of dealing with them?"

"A way of dealing with them? What! You mean to join with one faction to bring down the other? Why, that is opportunism! The correct way of dealing with them is to raise the revolutionary ardour of the masses to the utmost, and to give the masses' great revolutionary strength its correct leadership!"

"Ah, ah — do you suppose I do not know that much? These questions of theory we can discuss in the cell; right now let us speak only of the practical matter. Sister Yue has waited a long while. What I hold is that when we set the strike going tomorrow, we must have Yao Jinfeng take a definite stand —"

"It is enough to keep a strict watch on her with the strength of the masses!"

Cai Zhen said it lightly, as one lifting a heavy thing with ease, and smiled coldly. She had always admired Ma Jin; Ma Jin worked hard, bore hardship and toil, and her views were sound; but now she rather doubted Ma Jin, or at least thought that Ma Jin was flinching before the "revolutionary high tide."

"Truly there is no need to fear Yao Jinfeng is up to any tricks. The sisters have only to hear that someone is a running dog, and they will beat her! Yao Jinfeng will not dare play the running dog."

Chen Yue'e put in her word too. She was indeed a little out of patience, above all because she could not well follow all those "formulas" and "terms" of Cai Zhen's and the rest; but she was an ardent revolutionary working woman who strove to learn, and so, though she could not well follow it, she listened on patiently.

"I only fear she is a running dog already! — Enough, let us argue no more, but first settle all the arrangements for after the strike!"

Ma Jin, too, put aside the endless debate of "formula" against "formula," and from among the papers she had been writing she turned out one sheet and read off the programme set down on it. And so the talk came to centre wholly on matters of fact: how to organize the strike committee, and who was to be on it? What conditions were to be put forward? How were the striking mills of Zhabei to be linked as one? How was contact to be made with the mills of Hongkou? … Now they had no dispute, and Chen Yue'e no longer used her ears alone. Each of them had a great deal to say, and their faces were flushed with one accord.

Now outside the window the lightning, the crashing thunder, and the pelting rain displayed their might, gust upon fiercer gust. The very house seemed to shake perilously. But the three within knew nothing of it. Their whole hearts and minds were sunk in another kind of thunder, another kind of storm!
