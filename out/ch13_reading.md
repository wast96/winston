### Rescuing Ren Bishi and Guan Xiangying

After the counter-revolutionary coup of April 12, Shanghai stayed locked under a White Terror. Time and again the Party's central leaders were seized and murdered; revolutionary workers, peasants, and intellectuals were arrested and jailed in great numbers, and the Party's cause suffered badly for it. To block the enemy's manhunts, and to snatch arrested comrades back before they could be killed, became one of the Central Special Section's most vital tasks.

As related earlier, the Section did everything it could, through every kind of connection, to rescue comrades unlucky enough to be caught. The methods it used fell roughly into three. The first was money: buying off the Kuomintang officials who mattered, or the detective-inspectors of the concession police, or the enemy's own judges, to win a comrade a light sentence or none at all. The second was legal struggle: hiring a well-known lawyer to defend the case in open court. The third was to strike while a prisoner was being moved under escort and free him by armed force, a means held ready only for emergencies.

### Two Rescues of Ren Bishi

In 1928 and again in 1929, Ren Bishi, a member of the CCP Central Committee, was arrested twice running, once at Nanling in Anhui and once in Shanghai; both times the Central Special Section got him out of prison.

Ren Bishi was born Ren Peiguo, in 1904, into a poor schoolteacher's family in Xiangyin county, Hunan. At middle school in Changsha he was swept up in the May Fourth Movement and threw himself into its patriotic work. He joined the Communist Youth League in 1920, and in 1921 travelled with Liu Shaoqi and others to Soviet Russia to study at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow. Early in 1922 he transferred to full membership in the Chinese Communist Party. Back in China from 1924, he held a run of posts in the Youth League: head of the organization department of its central bureau, member of its central executive committee, acting general secretary, and finally general secretary. In May 1927, at the Party's Fifth National Congress, he was elected to the Central Committee. Against the conciliatory line that general secretary Chen Duxiu was taking toward the Kuomintang, he submitted written "Political Opinions" of criticism more than once; on one occasion Chen was so enraged that he tore the document up in Ren's face. At the August 7 Conference, the delegates were struck by how right and how firm his criticism of Chen Duxiu had been, and the twenty-three-year-old Ren Bishi was elected to the provisional Central Political Bureau, the youngest man ever to sit on it in our Party's history.

In March 1928 the provisional Central Political Bureau decided to bring Ren Bishi into the central apparatus. In May and June, Qu Qiubai and Zhou Enlai left for Moscow to prepare the Chinese Communist Party's Sixth National Congress; Xiang Zhongfa and Su Zhaozheng were already there, while Ren Bishi, Li Weihan, Deng Xiaoping, and others stayed behind to hold the Party Center. In July the Sixth Congress re-elected him to the Central Committee. The newly elected general secretary, Xiang Zhongfa, along with Political Bureau member Cai Hesen and alternate member Li Lisan, came home ahead of the rest to pass on the spirit of the congress and run the central apparatus. In mid-September, his caretaking done, the Center sent Ren Bishi to Anhui on an inspection tour. It was on this tour that he was first arrested.

Ren Bishi was arrested in October 1928, in Nanling county, Anhui. On September 18 that year he left Shanghai with Gong Deyuan, reaching Wuhu, the seat of the CCP Anhui provincial committee, on the 19th. On October 13 he left Wuhu with Lin Zhifu, secretary of the Youth League's provincial special committee, who went under the alias Xu Houchang, to inspect the work in Nanling county. They reached the county town at dawn on the 14th, and on the 15th went to Xiangyou Temple, an old monastery northwest of the town, for a meeting of the local Party and League cadres. Near the meeting-place stood the tomb of the wife of Zhou Yu, the Wu commander of Three Kingdoms times, a grave known as Xiaoqiao's Tomb. Around three in the afternoon Wang Defen, who led the county Party and League, brought Ren Bishi and Lin Zhifu to the tomb, where the notified members arrived one by one. That July the secretary of the CCP Nanling county committee had been arrested, the organization had been broken, and some had turned traitor. The Kuomintang county party bureau got wind of this gathering; traitors shadowed it, and now the county's Kuomintang self-defense corps suddenly threw a cordon around the whole area. Ren Bishi, Lin Zhifu, Wang Dehui, and Liu Xianzong were caught on the spot. Wang Defen was not there; he hurried to Wuhu that night to report the affair to the Party organization, and was himself arrested at Wuhu the next day.

The Nanling county court, sitting jointly with the Kuomintang county party bureau, tried Ren Bishi and the others on the night of the 16th, with a traitor present. Wang Dehui and Liu Xianzong admitted to being League members; they claimed not to know Ren Bishi and to have no idea who he was. Lin Zhifu admitted to Party membership but gave nothing away about Ren. Ren Bishi kept his head. Cool and steady, he took the temporary name Hu Shaofu and called himself an apprentice at the Weilun paper shop in Changsha, come to press for a debt and stopping at Xiaoqiao's Tomb along the way. All the enemy turned up on him was eighty yuan in cash, and nothing more. Unwilling to let it rest, they put him to savage torture, the "trampling bar" and the "squeezing rods," forcing him to confess; but he held to his story to the end and never let his identity slip. Treating them all as "suspected Communists," the enemy sent the group under escort to Anqing, the seat of the Kuomintang Anhui provincial government. On the boat Ren Bishi happened upon a fellow townsman, and while the escorting bailiffs were off guard he entrusted him with a letter to his uncle Ren Liqing, an engineer at the No. 1 Cotton Mill in Changsha. At Anqing he was locked in the detention house of the special criminal court.

In Shanghai, the moment Chen Congying received Ren Liqing's telegram she reported it to the Center. The Center at once sent someone to accompany her to Changsha to arrange a rescue. The Weilun paper shop in Changsha was run by Chen Congying's elder cousin Chen Yueyun. Posing as the shop's proprietress, she engaged He Weidao, a kinsman by marriage of the Ren family and one of Changsha's four great lawyers, to work the case. Reaching Anqing, He Weidao used his social connections to get the case moved from the special criminal court to the Anhui provincial court. In court he argued the defense, denouncing the Nanling county party bureau for arresting a man without cause, and demanded that the court verify Hu Shaofu's identity and acquit him. The court sent men to Changsha to check, and remanded Ren Bishi to the Yinmatang detention house. Meanwhile the Party Center worked several angles among the upper ranks of the Kuomintang, going so far as to buy off the officials concerned.

Held in the "Zhi" block along with Ren Bishi, besides the co-defendants arrested at Nanling, were Liu Shi and Dai Yingdong, taken at Wuhu. Liu Shi, also known as Liu Yifu, was secretary of the provisional provincial committee; Dai Yingdong was a district committee secretary of the Wuhu city party. In prison Ren Bishi was careful in word and deed, rarely talking with the other political prisoners, though at times he helped a fellow inmate think through his case and drew a lesson out of it for him. Liu Shi recalled that when Ren Bishi went over his case with him he warned: a young man's failing is impatience, the itch to break out of prison at once. To break out is well and good, but where plainly there is no chance of it, one must be ready to serve the term and use the time to study. He would not agree with those who let impatience get the better of them, losing their temper under interrogation and cursing the Kuomintang, which did no good at all: you deny you are a Communist, and one such outburst turns it into an admission that you are. Learn to argue with the enemy, he said, to reason the struggle out, to win a sentence short of death, and to get through prison life well. Dai Yingdong remembered that under Ren Bishi's guidance many comrades came to face prison life in the right spirit, and the appetite for study in the jail grew strong. Led by Liu Shi and others, they set up two study groups, one for the worker and peasant inmates to learn to read and one for the literate comrades to study politics, and little by little the mood among the prisoners steadied.

When the Kuomintang Anhui provincial court sent men to Changsha to look into Hu Shaofu's identity, Chen Congying was ready and waiting: she confirmed him as an apprentice of the Weilun paper shop and gave the court her surety. Out of moves, the enemy released Ren Bishi, and by the end of 1928 he was back in Shanghai. On February 30, 1929, Guan Xiangying reported to a meeting of the CCP Central Political Bureau that International Red Aid had allotted a special sum of 8,000 yuan for Ren Bishi's rescue.

### Ren Bishi's Second Arrest

On the morning of November 17, 1929, Ren Bishi went out to attend an enlarged meeting of the Jiangsu provincial committee of the Communist Youth League. Rain was coming down hard outside; as he left the house he told his wife, Chen Congying, that he would be back by twelve for lunch and had another meeting somewhere else in the afternoon. From noon on she waited; nightfall came and he had not returned. In a fever of worry she waited out the whole night, and still there was no sign of him. Certain now that something had happened, she hurried at first light to Luo Mai, that is Li Weihan, the Jiangsu provincial secretary, to ask for news.

The moment Luo Mai saw Chen Congying he confirmed it: Bishi had been arrested the day before. He comforted her too, saying the Party organization was already working to free him.

Once the news reached him through the Central Special Section, Chen Geng sent Yang Dengying straight off to the British police station to find out what he could.

As it turned out, the meeting-place Ren Bishi went to had long since been discovered by the enemy. The meeting was set for a two-story shikumen house in Jingye Lane off Huade Road in the International Settlement; the enemy had already broken up this office and posted plainclothes detectives inside, and before our comrades could give the warning signal Ren Bishi was already striding up the steps in the driving rain to knock. The door opened halfway, and he sensed something was wrong, for strangers were peering out inside. He was turning to go when the plainclothes police seized him by both arms, and struggling was no longer possible. The enemy pressed a woman comrade who lived at the office to name him. She looked him over once and said, "I don't know him." They did not believe her, but there was nothing they could do. Just then her small child, only four, caught sight of Ren Bishi and rushed to him. Without letting the woman explain, the enemy clapped irons on Ren Bishi and pushed him into the prison van. In that moment he slipped the secret document he had hidden into his mouth and swallowed it. The two who lived there, mother and child, were Li Canying, widow of the martyr Guo Liang, and his four-year-old orphan, Guo Zhicheng; Li Canying passed outwardly as a nursemaid minding other people's children, and that morning the two of them had been arrested. Ren Bishi was taken to the British imperialists' Huishan Road police station and thrown into a holding cell. All they found on him was a single tram pass, not a scrap of paper. He gave his name as Peng Desheng, an unemployed young man come from Jiangxi to Shanghai to look up relatives and find work, arrested by mistake because he had the wrong house number. The enemy had nothing else to go on. So they held him as a suspected Communist and later transferred him to Tilanqiao Prison.

The enemy put Ren Bishi to severe beatings, and even to the electric torture, but got no confession from him. Several foreign constables and officers took turns kicking him with their leather boots and lashing him with leather whips, trying to force him to talk. The interrogations had left him burning with fever, his body utterly spent, on the point of collapse. Yet he braced himself, and when they whipped him he threw up a hand to ward off the blow, and the whip came down across the head of the "judge" himself. This set the man roaring like a beast stripped of all humanity; he not only beat Ren more brutally still but turned to the inhuman electric torture. The yellow-haired judge knew that this cruelty could leave its victim half-swooning and half-numb, his body and mind gravely harmed, his nervous system in chaos, so that when he could bear no more he might blurt out the truth. And so, before he ordered the switch thrown, he pressed Ren Bishi hard to say just what he was. Ren Bishi, as before, breathed not a word. When the circuit closed, his heart raced with terrifying speed, his body felt pierced by a hail of arrows, his skull swelled, his eyes bulged, and all at once it was as if the earth had lost its balance, objects spinning, the room tilting away. Again and again the enemy used this torture, and again and again he fainted under it. Yet with a will beyond compare he held out against them. Even then they would not stop; with monstrous cruelty they kept raising the voltage, until the two electrodes had seared two fist-sized holes into Ren Bishi's back, giving off the stench of scorched flesh, holes that would not close for months. But beat him as they might, Ren Bishi kept a Communist's high integrity to the last, giving up not one thing, not a word of confession.

Zhou Punong, then head of the youth-workers department of the Shanghai General Labor Union, who after his arrest took the alias Zhou Akang, was arrested together with Ren Bishi; he had known him from the days when he was a district committee secretary in the League's Pudong district in Shanghai. That day he too had come to the enlarged meeting of the Jiangsu League committee, reaching the place ahead of Ren Bishi, and the enemy hauled him off with Li Canying and her child to the Huishan Road police station in the British Settlement. Neither he nor Ren Bishi let his identity show. In jail Ren Bishi told him: mind your cover, we must act as if we do not know each other; the enemy may suspect us, but they have no hard proof. That night the enemy moved them to the Minhang Road police station. He later wrote down his memory of the tortures the enemy used:

The enemy would not believe our statements, and put us to torture twice. The second time especially, they used the inhuman electric torture. When Comrade Bishi came back to the cell after the first session, he said to me: "Comrade Punong, we Communists are made of special stuff. We must be able to stand the test, ready at any moment to give our lives for our cause." Comrade Bishi's earnest words gave me enormous strength of spirit.

The moment Ren Bishi was arrested, the CCP Central Committee ordered the Central Special Section to press hard for his rescue. Zhou Enlai arranged the work of freeing Ren Bishi himself, and put Section One of the Central Special Section in specific charge of it. It later emerged that Ren Bishi and Zhou Punong had been caught because, in searching Zhang Yonghe, a Party and League secretary of the Shanghai Anti-Imperialist League and an overseas-Chinese youth who went by the alias Zhang Jingyun, the enemy had turned up a Jingye Lane address in the pocket of his Western suit and followed that thread to the raid. Since they held no other proof, they held Ren Bishi as a suspected Communist. On this footing the Central Special Section engaged Pan Zhenya, a well-known Shanghai lawyer, to defend him at the public hearing.

In the article cited above, Zhou Punong recalled:

On November 22 the Mixed Court of the Shanghai International Settlement, on North Zhejiang Road, opened the hearing. Comrade Bishi, Zhang Yonghe, and I were tried together in the one case. Before the hearing the Party had assigned Pan Zhenya and one other lawyer to defend us, and in the waiting room they took down Comrade Bishi's statement. In court we repeated the story we had made up at the Huishan police station, and then the lawyer Pan Zhenya rose to defend us. He pointed out that for imperialists to arrest innocent Chinese citizens wholesale on Chinese soil was an illegal act violating our nation's sovereignty. Pressed until he had no answer, the judge adjourned the court. At the second sitting the judge fell back on "endangering state security" to sentence Zhang Yonghe to two months, and Comrade Bishi and me to forty days each.

On December 25, while the foreigners in the Concessions kept their "Christmas," Ren Bishi and Zhou Punong were both "released with reduced sentences," and left Tilanqiao Prison together.

### Rescuing Guan Xiangying

In April 1931 the office of the Shanghai Federation of Trade Unions was broken up by the enemy after a traitor sold it out. Guan Xiangying, an alternate member of the Central Political Bureau who had come to work there only a short time before, was seized by the British police of the International Settlement, handed over to the Kuomintang government, and locked in the detention house of the Kuomintang's Songhu Garrison Command. Guan Xiangying had been arrested after Gu Shunzhang left Shanghai escorting Zhang Guotao; he went by the alias Li Shizhen and never once let his true identity show. But there was the danger that, should the already-turned Gu Shunzhang learn he was in prison, his life would be in peril. At the same time, when Guan Xiangying was caught the enemy carried off a whole trunk of his documents. Because these documents were so important, they would not only expose Guan Xiangying's identity but leak vital Party secrets. Zhou Enlai, hearing of it, was gravely alarmed, and ordered Chen Geng to find out at once how matters stood, draw up a rescue plan, and get Guan Xiangying out as fast as possible.

Guan Xiangying was born Guan Zhixiang, and used the aliases Li Shizhen, written two ways, Little Guan, and Qin Tao, among others. A Manchu, of the Gūwalgiya clan, he was born in 1902 to a peasant family at Daguanjiatun in Jin county, Liaoning. Poverty kept him from school until late, but he studied hard and did well. In April 1920 he passed into the commercial course at the Fushimidai public school in Dalian. Seeing with his own eyes, in the city, how the poor were ground down by both national and class oppression, his heart filled with pity for them and hatred for the invaders and the oppressors. At school he took part in a strike against a Japanese teacher who beat the students. After graduating in 1922 he went to work at the Nikka Kōgyō company, but out of national pride he quit within a few months. He then took a job as a workman at the Taidong Daily in Dalian. Japanese-run though this paper too was, it kept many books and periodicals a man could read, and Guan Xiangying, hungry for knowledge, took a keen interest. Toward the overbearing Japanese he held to a national dignity, once leading a crowd against a Japanese who had insulted Chinese workers and forcing the man to apologize. Here, through reading revolutionary papers like The Guide and New Youth, he advanced quickly in his thinking and took an active part in revolutionary work. In the spring of 1924 he joined the Communist Youth League, the first League member in the city of Dalian.

In May 1924 Guan Xiangying went to study at Shanghai University. That September the Party Center sent him to the Soviet Union to study at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow. In January 1925, in Moscow, Chen Qiaonian and Yu Shisong sponsored his transfer to full membership in the Chinese Communist Party.

In 1925 the May Thirtieth Movement broke out. The patriotic, anti-imperialist storm it raised swelled into the high tide of China's Great Revolution. The CCP Central Committee decided to pull a batch of cadres back from among those studying in the Soviet Union, and Guan Xiangying too was ordered home. Reaching Shanghai, he was assigned to the League's Hudong district committee. That September he went to Jinan as a League special commissioner. From then until April 1927 he served in turn as a chief officer of the League in Jinan, in Qingdao, and in the Shandong provincial organization. Before the April 12 coup he was transferred to Wuhan to work in the labor movement, and in mid-May he attended the Fourth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Youth League there and was elected to its Central Committee, and afterward to its central standing committee. That summer he was sent to work on the CCP Henan provincial committee. Early in 1928 he returned to Shanghai as head of the League's organization department. In July 1928 Guan Xiangying went to Moscow as a delegate of the Chinese Communist Youth League to attend the Chinese Communist Party's Sixth National Congress, where he was elected to the Central Committee, and at the First Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee to alternate membership of the Central Political Bureau, as well as membership of the Central Military Commission. In July he also attended the Fifth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Youth League, held in Moscow, and was again elected to its Central Committee, becoming secretary of its central executive bureau afterward. From July 17 to September 1 the Sixth Congress of the Comintern met in Moscow, and on July 28 Guan Xiangying spoke there, reporting on the work of the Chinese League. Early in 1927 Guan Xiangying returned to Shanghai to run the League's work.

In February 1930 Xiang Zhongfa, Luo Dengxian, Guan Xiangying, Ren Bishi, and Zhou Enlai reorganized the organization bureau; the military department was merged with the military commission under the Central Political Bureau, with Zhou Enlai, Guan Xiangying, and Zeng Zhongsheng as standing members of the commission. On March 3 the Center sent Zhou Enlai to Moscow to report to the Comintern; he returned in mid-August. After Zhou Enlai left Shanghai, Guan Xiangying served as secretary of the military commission. In September, at the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, he was elected to the Central Political Bureau. That winter he became secretary of the CCP Yangtze Bureau. In January 1931, at the Third Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, he was elected an alternate member of the Central Political Bureau.

The Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee was convened in Shanghai on Comintern instructions. The Comintern representative Pavel Mif attended, and worked the organizational levers without let-up to control its course. Wang Ming, twenty-six years old, not even a Central Committee member and lacking any real experience of struggle, was, thanks to Mif's backing, not only elected to the Central Committee but made a member of the Central Political Bureau. Wang Ming and his group in effect seized the leadership of the CCP Center, and from then on the "left" dogmatism they represented began a four-year rule over the Party's central leading organs. At this meeting, Li Lisan, Qu Qiubai, Li Weihan, He Chang, and others who had already corrected their errors or given up the mistaken line were subjected to merciless criticism and organizational reprisal, and Zhou Enlai too was sternly censured. Guan Xiangying would not go along with it; away from the sessions he, He Chang, and Ren Bishi would gather at the home of Gong Yinbing to talk over the abnormal way the Comintern had reached in to reshuffle the CCP Center, and above all he could not stomach Wang Ming's manner, a fox borrowing the tiger's dread. Displeased with him for all this, Wang Ming's group demoted him after the meeting to alternate member of the Central Political Bureau and moved him to the labor department of the Shanghai Center to work on the workers' movement.

After Gu Shunzhang was arrested and turned traitor in April 1931, he gave up a good many of the CCP Center's liaison offices in Shanghai. Because it all happened so suddenly, Guan Xiangying was not warned in time and carried on as usual. One day, going to a soy-sauce shop that served as a secret Party liaison post, he was seized on the spot by police detectives lying in wait there, and locked in the British police station of the International Settlement. When the enemy questioned him, Guan Xiangying saw that they still did not know who he really was; so he gave the name Li Shizhen, saying he had just come to Shanghai from out of town to make a living, that he had relatives and friends living near the soy-sauce shop, and that he had come to ask after where they lived. The enemy could get nothing out of him and held him at the station for the time being.

Once Guan Xiangying was arrested, how was he to be freed? Following Zhou Enlai's instructions, Chen Geng first sent Yang Dengying to the police station to feel things out. Yang Dengying soon learned that the first questioning had not established the prisoner's identity, and that the British were much taken with the whole trunk of documents seized from Guan Xiangying's quarters. But there were so many documents, and their Chinese was so poor, that they could not tell which mattered; they circled the trunk day after day and could think of nothing to do with it.

When Chen Geng reported all this to Zhou Enlai, Zhou Enlai said the rescue should start from these documents. On the one hand, the important ones had to be snatched out, or vital Party secrets would leak and the Party suffer; on the other, if the important documents could be carried off, leaving the enemy no way to judge who Guan Xiangying was, it would be all the easier to get him out. Through a counter-espionage asset concealed within the enemy's ranks, Chen Geng learned that the British police were fretting over how to deal with this batch of documents quickly, and that gave the Party organization a favorable opening to carry out the plan: rescue the documents first, then rescue Guan Xiangying. The British at the station, faced with so many documents and papers and unable to pick out the important ones themselves, circled the trunk day after day and felt the whole thing hopeless. On our side, meanwhile, we were just then anxious over the seizure of the documents. Chen Geng sought out Yang Dengying and told him to go to the British police station and find a way. Yang Dengying went. He came back to report that after Guan Xiangying's arrest the Kuomintang had been trying to get the documents away, that the British police would not agree and were arguing the point on the merits; the British said the case was the Concession's own to crack and they were unwilling to hand the documents to the Kuomintang. Chen Geng had Yang Dengying seek out Lampson, the Western detective-inspector of the British police station's political branch, and tell him the documents were important, and that he was willing to help the station sort them. Lampson, keen to pick out the important ones and keep them for himself, told Yang Dengying to take the sorting on. Yang Dengying begged off, saying he was busy and had no time, but offered to think of some other way. On Zhou Enlai's word, Chen Geng sent Liu Ding, deputy chief of the Central Special Section's intelligence section, to do the "sorting." Zhou Enlai gave the order that every handwritten and carbon-copied document was to be brought back, and the rest could be left. Chen Geng also charged Liu Ding pointedly: do not tell Yang Dengying the prisoner's real name and identity, lest knowing it should make him lose his nerve and spoil the rescue. So Yang Dengying introduced Liu Ding to Lampson as his "expert," took him to the station room where the documents were kept, and shut the door. Here Liu Ding went carefully through the lot, drew out the carbon copies and hid them on his person, and as he left picked up a few mimeographed sheets and told the station men, "I'll take these along to look over."

Afterward Chen Geng had Yang Dengying tell Lampson that the prisoner was a scholar and that these documents taken from his home were all academic research material. The result was that the British were fobbed off. Seeing that Guan Xiangying was no important "criminal" after all, Lampson turned him over to the Songhu Garrison Command at Longhua. Since Guan Xiangying had never let his identity show, and the Kuomintang had not got hold of the documents either, they too took him for no "wanted man" and paid him no heed. In time the Party found a way to get him out.

There was at the time a Communist named Zhang Ji'en, working at a CCP central office at No. 1141 Gordon Road, now Jiangning Road, in the Shanghai International Settlement, who had been arrested on June 23, 1931, handed over by the Gordon Road police station to the Songhu Garrison Command's detective squad at the Baiyunguan temple by Shanghai's West Gate, and moved on in July to the detention house of the Songhu Garrison Command at Longhua. In his memoir Zhang Ji'en set down what he saw of Guan Xiangying in prison: When he was brought under escort to Longhua, "in the Garrison Command detention house were also Guan Xiangying and Yu Changsheng—Yu had gone to the Soviet Union in 1928 as Xiang Zhongfa's secretary to attend the Sixth Congress, and in the spring of 1931 was again made Xiang's secretary—held there ahead of me. Things stood roughly thus: Guan Xiangying went by the alias Li Shizhen and Yu Changsheng by Li Jindi, both arrested that spring. Before I was moved from the detective squad to the Longhua Garrison Command detention house, Guan Xiangying and Yu Changsheng had long been held in Block 2 of the detention house, Yu Changsheng in cell 5 and Guan Xiangying in cell 1. I too was put into cell 5, in the same cell as Yu Changsheng. Before my arrest, Xiang Zhongfa, Zhou Enlai, and others had discussed at our office how to rescue Guan Xiangying and Yu Changsheng... Guan Xiangying and Yu Changsheng were both acquitted in the winter of 1931 and released on bail."

Another comrade, Li Moying, who worked at an office of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, the red trade unions, and was also arrested in the spring of 1931, recalled: "In Longhua prison we set up a Party branch too. The branch secretary on the men's side was Xie Xuanqu, with Chen Weiren and Liu Xiao as committee members. I was the branch secretary on the women's side. One heavy task of mine then was to write down the prisoners' real names, aliases, statements, and prison conduct on cotton paper, sew it into padded clothing to be carried out of the jail, and report to the organization. Guan Xiangying was in prison at that time too; he was a key figure for the branch to protect. We gave him no tasks at all, having him play the plodding nobody, so as to keep his cover."

At the end of 1931 Guan Xiangying was released from prison and sent by the CCP Central Committee, as its central representative, to work in the West Hunan–Hubei soviet.
