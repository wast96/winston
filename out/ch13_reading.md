## Chapter Thirteen. Twin Lotus on One Stem

### 1. Send Shen Wan

A child's character is fixed in the womb. Before I was born my mother was in great distress, restless day and night; she could eat nothing but iced oysters and champagne. If anyone asks me when I began to dance, I answer: "In the womb, already, because the oysters and champagne my mother ate are the food of Aphrodite, the goddess of love."

The girl still called Shen Wan, working at Yu Xijian's side and "helping him translate" The Autobiography of Isadora Duncan (1934), must have taken it down at his dictation while she quietly asked herself: Was my own character "fixed in the womb"?

Even if the answer was yes, she could hardly have foreseen that her later life would turn on going under, on "lying low," and that lying low would be bound to disguise. Character is destiny. But the destiny of "lying low" would work the other way, remaking her character: turning the simple complex, turning what had been clear as water into a "taste for disguise," until "disguising and protecting herself, at every moment and in every place" became a "professional instinct," not something she was born with, yet no longer easily shed. She was, after all, still a student at nineteen, a student at a shorthand school, spending her days among Bingxun shorthand marks. What else was there? There was "throwing off the shackles of feudalism," "taking her fate into her own hands"; there was "living by her own labor," "becoming an independent, free New Woman"; and there was, "like Wang Renmei or Li Lili," standing under the studio lamps to "make movies, be a movie star."

The day before I left the Nanyang Commercial School, Ye Luxi insisted on dragging me to a studio to have a movie-star portrait taken. I couldn't refuse, so I went along. Ye Luxi had herself photographed in every sort of pose; I had just the one taken. The studio helped design the hair; the dress and the necklace were the studio's too. That one portrait, full of youth and romance, I have kept by me ever since. Back then, deep down, I really did harbor a "movie-star dream." To be a movie star, how lovely that would be! (Shen Anna, 2016)

The same dream, as it happens, Ding Ling had dreamed too. She had had movie-star pictures taken as well. Her first work, Meng Ke (1927), is about "her own stretch of life as a would-be movie actress, and what she made of it and felt about it." (Jiang Zulin, 2016) What she felt was not good. She could not bear to "be looked over by all those disrespectful eyes." She "did not understand why she should so demean herself, it was like selling her body, even selling her soul."

Ding Ling said "no" to a "society of pure flesh."

So did Shen Wan; she refused this "grossly insulting" thing.

Ding Ling became a writer. "Her fame, her influence, her pull, for the literary youth of the day, could empty the streets and set the whole country wild." (Sun Li, 2002)

Shen Wan became an intelligence agent. She "carried too many secrets in one life, bore too many missions, lived through too many dangers." She was "made of special stuff," "an outstanding woman soldier of our Party's hidden front, one who kept a finger on Chiang Kai-shek's pulse." (Luo Yuan, 2010)

It was the strangest luck. In the winter of 1934, not long after I had finished helping Yu Xijian translate The Autobiography of Isadora Duncan, the Kuomintang's Zhejiang provincial government came to the Bingxun Chinese Shorthand School to recruit a stenographer. My class was still a month from graduating, so the headmaster, Yang Bingxun, decided to send me, one of the better students, and two of the boys to intern and sit the test; after a month, on the strength of grades and performance, one of the three would be hired. This was a job with the provincial government, and my classmates were falling over themselves for it, but I wasn't keen. I didn't want to sit an exam at a Kuomintang office. The way I saw it, even shooting progressive films would be easier on the spirit than waiting on bureaucrats in some Kuomintang office. (Shen Anna, 2016)

Shu Yuexin and Hua Mingzhi reported Shen Wan's "reluctance" to Wang Xuewen. Wang Xuewen said: "You must draw her out, and tell her to work hard and win the place."

The Nakanishi Ko Interrogation Record (1996) states that from "1934 on, Wang Xuewen took charge of the CCP Center's Shanghai work (intelligence work and the like), which was always inseparably bound up with the Japanese organizations in Shanghai."

The "CCP Center's Shanghai work (intelligence work and the like)" that Nakanishi meant was the Central Special Branch.

At the Special Branch, Wang Xuewen's superior was Wang Shiying.

Wang Shiying, courtesy name Zijie, was born in 1905 in Hongtong, Shanxi. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1925 and served in turn on the CCP's North Anhui Special Committee, as a member of the Bozhou branch committee, as a military-commission member of the Shanghai Central Executive Bureau, as acting secretary of the military commission of the Shanghai Provisional Central Bureau, and as head of the Central Special Branch, directing intelligence and liaison work.

Once Wang Shiying had agreed to send Shen Wan to work in Zhejiang, Wang Xuewen had Shu Yuexin, Hua Mingzhi, and Shen Min set about coaching her.

Hua Mingzhi, born Hua Jialiu, courtesy name Hongshen, was born in 1913 in Wuxi, Jiangsu, and joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1934.

Shu Yuexin, born Shu Yongzhi, was born in 1915 in Yixing, Jiangsu; he joined the Party in 1933 and married Shen Min in 1934.

Shen Min was Shen Wan's second elder sister, born in 1912 in Taixing, Jiangsu; she joined the Party in 1933.

On Wang Xuewen's instructions, Shu Yuexin and Hua Mingzhi settled their plan and then, with Shen Min along, came to talk to me.

The mood was grave, of course, and the place was the same as before, the little tingzijian of my sister and brother-in-law.

Shu Yuexin, his face stern, said to me: Didn't you ask to join the revolution? First tell me why you want to.

Without stopping to think, I said: I want to join the revolution to fight for women's liberation, to be free of arranged marriage, to have freedom, democracy, progress.

Shu Yuexin said: And what else?

I thought, and said: And not to be a slave of a conquered country! In the September 18 Incident the Kuomintang didn't resist the Japanese army; instead it suppressed patriots. I hate the Kuomintang reactionaries, and from what you've told me, only the Communist Party can save the country and the people.

Shu Yuexin said: Good! The Zhejiang provincial government is hiring a stenographer now, and the Party organization has decided to send you to sit the test.

The words "the Party organization" gave me a start. I had heard my sister say before that my brother-in-law was a revolutionary, and I had pushed to join the revolution myself, but nothing had come of it. Now I asked, testing them: You're Communists?

Shu Yuexin and Hua Mingzhi nodded gravely.

I was thunderstruck. Over the past year and more I had heard and seen no end of horrifying news of how the Kuomintang reactionary authorities were butchering Communists. In my mind, Communists were all high-minded revolutionaries who thought nothing of death. I had never dreamed my own brother-in-law and my schoolmate were Communists. The Communists I so admired were right beside me! It shook me to the core, and for a moment I didn't know what to say. I turned to my sister: Something this big, why didn't you tell me sooner?

Shen Min patted my hand and said: Just listen carefully while they give you your assignment!

Shu Yuexin went on: The Party badly needs a grip on the inside workings of the Kuomintang reactionary government. You must sit the test in earnest and win a regular appointment.

Hua Mingzhi added: To go and be a stenographer in the Zhejiang provincial government is to join the revolution.

I said happily: Really? If it's joining the revolution, I'll go!

To be a revolutionary, that was what I had dreamed of.

Shu Yuexin said: A stenographer inside a Kuomintang government office can learn the enemy's schemes in advance.

Hua Mingzhi went on: That way we can protect ourselves better and strike the enemy harder.

I was deeply stirred. My grasp of the Communist Party was shallow, and of intelligence work I knew nothing at all; but I knew my brother-in-law and my schoolmate, and I trusted them completely, and I felt I couldn't go wrong following them. So I said, firmly: I want the revolution, and I will do this well. (Shen Anna, 2016)

### 2. The Vanguard Column

Shen Wan was as good as her word. She not only "practiced over and over, day and night," but was "quick to react, with clearly stronger shorthand than the two boys." At the end of a one-month trial she was taken on as a stenographer in the Records Section of the secretariat of the Zhejiang provincial government, and she had already come away with several important pieces of intelligence.

Shen Wan didn't yet understand any of it. When Chiang Kai-shek's able lieutenant, the security commissioner Xuan Tiewu, gave a military briefing, spreading out army maps and pointing here and there, talking of "mopping up" the Communist Party and "mopping up" the Red Army, she paid the closest attention, took it all down, and quietly wrote it up. Before long a letter came from Shen Min telling her to come home, so she took the material she had secretly written up, along with some "original shorthand drafts of high-level meetings" she had filched, and secret documents printed too faintly and marked for destruction, and tucked the whole lot inside her clothes, "mixed in" among them in a small suitcase, and "rode the train back to Shanghai bold as brass." This gave Shen Min a terrible fright; her face changed on the spot. Shen Min called Shen Wan a "reckless fool" who "made no attempt to hide anything, just rolled up this pile of important intelligence in her clothes and carried it back." Seeing which way things were going, Shu Yuexin hurried to smooth it over, waving his hands frantically at the side, taking his young sister-in-law's part. Shu Yuexin said the fault wasn't all Anna's; if anyone was to blame it was them, for not teaching her well, since she was green and knew nothing.

"Anna" was the name Shu Yuexin gave Shen Wan, to mark her "resolve" to "join the revolution."

Shu Yuexin's words were: "A revolutionary Soviet Union is our China's future. I suggest you take the name of a Soviet girl. I think you should be called Anna."

Hua Mingzhi liked it at once: "Anna is a good name; it sounds well, and it suits Shen Wan's lively nature."

Shen Wan became "Anna," and Shen Min changed hers too, to "Yina." But Shu Yuexin stressed that in public they should keep their old names; it was safer.

Shu Yuexin thought of everything, and Wang Xuewen kept repeating that the first rule of intelligence work was to guarantee safety, and never to be rash or careless.

To keep me safe, Wang Xuewen also gave instructions: Anna is an intelligence agent, so she shouldn't be running as a courier; let Yina do the running. Yina made a few round trips, and then Wang Xuewen decided she really had too much to handle, and that a woman shuttling so often between Shanghai and Hangzhou was awkward besides; so he and Shu Yuexin decided that Hua Mingzhi would guide me and be my contact. (Shen Anna, 2016)

From then on Hua Mingzhi was often to be seen on the Shanghai-Hangzhou line, and more often still walking hand in hand with Shen Anna along the banks of West Lake, the two of them lingering as though they couldn't tear themselves away. To anyone watching, here was an ardent young man in hot pursuit of a gentle, innocent girl. But between them, in the understanding they shared, every superior's instruction passed on and every important report handed over deepened, day by day, an accord built wholly on a common faith and a common ideal.

The joint efforts of Hua Mingzhi and Shen Anna gave powerful support to the struggle of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army Vanguard Column.

That struggle began after the "Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army Anti-Japanese Vanguard, Bound North" was defeated at Mount Huaiyu.

On October 10, 1934, the 1st, 3rd, 5th, 8th, and 9th Red Army Corps, together with the Party Center, the Military Commission organs, and their directly attached units, more than 86,000 troops in all, set out from Ruijin toward western Hunan, beginning the strategic transfer. At the same time the CCP Center and the Central Revolutionary Military Commission authorized Xiang Ying and Chen Yi to form a CCP Central Sub-bureau and a Central Military Region to lead the southern Red Army in guerrilla war; and they wired orders to the 7th Red Army Corps, led by Xun Huaizhou, Yue Shaohua, Su Yu and others, to march east into Fujian and Zhejiang under the name of the "Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army Anti-Japanese Vanguard, Bound North." There it merged with the New 10th Red Army and newly upgraded local forces to form the 10th Red Army Corps, with the provincial Soviet chairman Fang Zhimin doubling as military-region commander, Zeng Hongyi as provincial Party secretary and military-region political commissar, Liu Chouxi as corps commander, Yue Shaohua as corps political commissar, and Su Yu as military-region chief of staff, and set out to build an Anhui-Zhejiang-Jiangxi Soviet area behind enemy lines, shifting from interior-line to exterior-line operations. By threatening the heartland of Kuomintang rule, it would draw off and pull away part of the forces "encircling" the Central Soviet Area, and so help the main body of the Central Red Army break out of the ring and make its strategic transfer.

But the 10th Red Army Corps fought and lost, lost and fought on, its casualties terrible, its life hanging by a thread.

On January 10, 1935, the 10th Red Army Corps held a meeting at Chashan in Sui'an, in western Zhejiang. Su Yu, Liu Ying (then director of the corps' political department and of the 19th Division's political department), and others made a last effort, urging strongly that the force split up for guerrilla warfare, shrink its profile, and preserve its strength. But Liu Chouxi would not be moved; he ordered the whole army to keep its formation and withdraw the way it had come, back home to rest and regroup, and in the end he led the entire 10th Red Army Corps, himself included, straight into a trap the enemy had long since laid.

On January 16, a vanguard of more than 800 men, led by Fang Zhimin, Yue Shaohua, Liu Ying, Su Yu and others, ran into a savage ambush at Gangtou village inside Jiangxi, sprung by a regiment of the enemy's Zhejiang Security 2nd Column and a still larger number of local pacification corps. Fang Zhimin sent a messenger urging Liu Chouxi, behind him, to keep close and lead the main force through the enemy's lines that same day to join up with the vanguard. But Liu Chouxi withdrew as he fought, taking his troops to Yanglin, near Mount Huaiyu in the northeast of the Kaihua-Wuyuan-Dexing country. This all but sealed the 10th Red Army Corps' fate: annihilation.

On August 6, Fang Zhimin, Liu Chouxi and the others met their deaths at Nanchang. The remnant of the 10th Red Army Corps, more than 400 men who had broken out of the Huaiyu encirclement, raised again, under their division commander Su Yu and political commissar Liu Ying, the banner of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army Vanguard Column, and set out from the Fujian-Zhejiang-Jiangxi base to push doggedly into Zhejiang and carry on the guerrilla war. They were a fire that would not go out. On March 25, 1935, they fought their first action on entering southwestern Zhejiang. They won at the first stroke, wiping out a squad's worth of landlord militia at Zhuxi in Longquan county, taking more than 30 prisoners and more than 40 rifles.

In early May 1935 the Vanguard Column entered the border country of the three counties of Longquan, Suichang, and Songyang, and at a meeting of division-level cadres Su Yu put forward the idea of the "two shifts": to move as fast as possible from a regular army to a guerrilla force, and from the whole division acting together in regular warfare to dispersing for a broad guerrilla war. The troops promptly scattered to rouse the people and gathered again to strike at small enemy detachments, smashing a string of Kuomintang district and township militias and putting down the worst of the township heads, town heads, and public-security chiefs. The Vanguard Column soon grew to nearly a thousand and was enlarged into five columns.

When word reached Hangzhou, the Zhejiang provincial chairman Huang Shaohong hastily buckled on his armor and took the field in person, mustering eight or nine security regiments to "encircle and suppress" them. Su Yu and Liu Ying turned this to account: dividing their force in two, they first slipped out of the ring and drew the fighting toward the Zhejiang-Jiangxi railway, then wheeled and struck back, wiping out two whole companies of Huang Shaohong's at a blow, and both companies belonged to Huang Shaohong's "Sword in the Breast" officer-cadet instruction regiment.

Huang Shaohong came away with his feathers plucked. The 2nd Column, under Zong Mengping (then secretary of the CCP Southwest Zhejiang Special Committee and, as the political commission's attached representative with the Vanguard Column, also secretary of its action committee), seized the moment to march into the borderland of Pucheng and Suichang, and at Caoxieling in Baoxi, Longquan, broke a battalion of the Kuomintang's 52nd Division.

In the end Zong Mengping's blood, too, was spilled on the soil of southwestern Zhejiang. At dawn on June 6, 1935, betrayed by a landlord's tip-off, his unit was ambushed and badly mauled by more than 200 men of the "Communist-suppression" volunteer corps of Jixia in Suichang; gravely wounded, Zong Mengping died a hero's death at Hengyuankeng in Suichang county.

In mid-June, at Xiaoji village in Songyang county, Su Yu and Liu Ying called on all units of the Vanguard Column to carry on Zong Mengping's work: to go deep in rousing the people, to carry out land revolution, to launch a drive to seize weapons and expand the Red Army, and by these deeds to mark the founding anniversary of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army.

The founding anniversary of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army dates from July 11, 1933, set by the Provisional Central Government of the Chinese Soviet Republic on the recommendation of the Central Revolutionary Military Commission of June 30. On June 15, 1949, the Chinese People's Revolutionary Military Commission issued an order making the two characters "August First" the chief emblem on the flag and insignia of the Chinese People's Liberation Army. After the founding of New China, the founding anniversary of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army was renamed Army Day of the Chinese People's Liberation Army.

The Vanguard Column's "August First" action fell like a bolt from a clear sky, blooming at many points at once, and the enemy in Quzhou, Jiangshan, and Xianju all cried out for help in turn. The Zhejiang provincial government hastily named Xuan Tiewu commander-in-chief of a "Southern Zhejiang Bandit-Suppression Headquarters" and sent him with his troops into Suichang.

But it never entered the enemy's dreams that their "southern Zhejiang bandit-suppression" plans were known to our army in good time. The Vanguard Column met each move with a countermove, worked with room to spare, and won all along the line.

Liu Ying said that because our army learned the enemy's "southern Zhejiang bandit-suppression" plans in time, it "smashed the enemy's attack" and won. In the summer of 1940, at the request of the CCP Southeast Bureau, Liu Ying spent more than a month at the New Fourth Army's headquarters in Jing County in southern Anhui writing a work of more than 70,000 characters, Bound North to Fight Japan, and Three Years' Struggle to Hold the Fujian-Zhejiang Border. In it, without naming names, he named them, dwelling in particular on "the matter of the connections." The "connections" Liu Ying meant were the "espionage network," that is, our Party's intelligence agents planted inside the enemy's own camp. As Liu Ying saw it, there was a chain of cause and effect: learning "half a month beforehand," through "the connections," that the enemy was about to "mount a large-scale, savage attack on the southwest-Zhejiang guerrilla base and the Red Army," led straight to the Vanguard Column's "deciding on countermeasures against the enemy's attack," "redeploying cadres and reorganizing units," and "fixing new tasks for each district and unit." Precisely because "before the enemy had made its dispositions, we had already scattered our forces among the several bases" and "set a central leadership in place in each district," when the enemy "massed a force of more than 26 regiments and mounted a large-scale, savage attack on our guerrilla bases and the Red Army," the result was still a total defeat.

At the time the lead story in the Southeast Daily reported: "Zhejiang has long been reckoned a place of peace. But since Su and Liu broke into Zhejiang, the banditry has spread through the whole province; as things stand, the Communist bandits of Zhejiang are no less than those of Sichuan or Jiangxi, and if the authorities cannot quickly stamp them out, the outlook is truly to be feared." In her heart Shen Anna felt a joy she couldn't put into words; but day by day, going to work and coming home, she stayed as she was, neither warm nor cold, neither startled nor stirred.

The news made me very happy. But I could only be glad in secret; I didn't dare talk about it with anyone, because that might rouse suspicion and give away my real political identity. I always kept in mind what the Party organization had told me: my identity was to be strictly guarded. (Shen Anna, 2016)

### 3. A Finger on Chiang Kai-shek's Pulse

In the autumn of 1935 Hua Mingzhi and Shen Anna were formally married, husband and wife. The quality of their intelligence rose a notch with it. In 2007, in Beijing, Shen Anna gave me an interview. Shen Anna said: "We were a married pair. Hua Mingzhi was my husband, and also my courier to the Party above us. My second sister, Shen Yina, and her husband, Shu Yuexin, were a married pair in Shanghai. Two married pairs, each independent of the other and yet connected. After Mingzhi and I married, our intelligence work grew more orderly still. He'd sort and boil a report down, and with very thin paper and a very fine pen shrink a whole big sheet into a small square, so it was easier to disguise and safer to pass along."

Later, when Wang Xuewen was transferred out of Shanghai, the new leader, going by the alias "Little Zhang," had Shen Yina write in code to call Shen Anna back to take up a new assignment. When Shen Anna hurried to an apartment in the French Concession, she learned that her sister had been moved to "keep house" with "Little Zhang," the two of them now a "sham married couple." Shen Anna said: "'Little Zhang' wasn't there at the time, and when I wanted to report to my sister, she said, better wait till he's back and report to him in person; he's our 'new leader.' She also said he was very young, and when I met him I could just call him 'big brother.' Later he came, and I reported to him and asked the 'new leader' for instructions. He said the situation was very grim, that he'd called me in precisely to tell me to be more on my guard and take care to stay hidden. As our Party's intelligence agents, however hard and bitter it got, we had to keep the secrets, obey the Party's orders absolutely, and always stay in touch with the Party."

It was a long time before Shen Anna learned that "Little Zhang's" real name was Qiu Jifu. In the autumn of 1935, on Wang Shiying's instructions, Wang Xuewen made his way to Hong Kong. Wang Shiying, then head of the Central Special Branch, led part of the group out of Shanghai to Tianjin. The intelligence work left in Shanghai fell to Qiu Jifu. Before long Qiu Jifu was arrested, and Xu Qiang took over the post.

On July 7, 1937, the Marco Polo Bridge Incident broke out, all-out war of resistance began, and Shen Anna made her way to Wuhan, where she chanced on Lu Zicheng and his wife, Hua Yunsan.

Lu Zicheng, courtesy name Mingsan, was born in 1893 in Shaoxing, Zhejiang. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1924 and the Central Special Branch in 1933. That same year he brought Shu Yuexin into the Party, and the next year Hua Mingzhi and Yao Zijian (born 1915, of Yixing, Jiangsu); he also brought Shu Yuexin, Hua Mingzhi, and Yao Zijian into the work of the Central Special Branch, under Wang Xuewen. Shen Anna and Hua Mingzhi both called Lu Zicheng "big brother," and said he was their "guide into the revolution and their first teacher."

Hua Yunsan, born Hua Manqian in 1914, was Hua Mingzhi's younger sister, of Wuxi, Jiangsu. She married Lu Zicheng in 1937 and joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1938, helping Lu Zicheng in the Party's secret work.

When Lu Zicheng heard from Shen Anna that she had lost touch with the Shanghai Party organization after "August Thirteenth," he directed her to the Eighth Route Army Office in Wuhan (the "Eighth Office" for short) to reconnect. Lu Zicheng said: "In the past, you and Mingzhi did secret intelligence work; I wasn't your leader then, but I knew your situation. Now, when you go to the Eighth Office, you mustn't casually let some ordinary receptionist there know that you do secret intelligence work. You must see a leading comrade like Dong Biwu; only then can you report on the work the two of you did in Shanghai and Hangzhou, ask to be assigned work, and ask to join the Party."

With the good fortune of Lu Zicheng's guidance, Shen Anna soon found the Eighth Route Army Office and met, one after another, Dong Biwu and Zhou Enlai. Dong Biwu said: "The Communist Party and the Kuomintang are cooperating again now, resisting Japan together. But we know very well that the reactionary nature of the Kuomintang diehards will not change. One must never drop one's guard. To get a clear read on the Kuomintang's intentions and moves inside would be a great help to the work of the Anti-Japanese National United Front. We know you did intelligence work before, as a stenographer in the Zhejiang provincial government, and that you know the provincial chairman, Zhu Jiahua. Zhu is now secretary-general of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters; you can use your standing as a former subordinate to go to him and ask for a place in the secretariat of Central Party Headquarters. If you can get into the Kuomintang's core apparatus and go on supplying us with intelligence, giving us early warning, then in our uniting-and-struggling we'll have more initiative and take better aim." Zhou Enlai said too: "This isn't the first day we've dealt with Chiang Kai-shek. We have learned in blood. Chiang was forced into resisting Japan, and he blows hot and cold; we have to keep a finger on his pulse, know which way he is moving, and not let him wreck the whole cause of the nation's resistance."

On Zhou Enlai and Dong Biwu's instructions, Shen Anna went the next day to No. 157 Jianghan Second Road, the secretariat of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters, to ask Zhu Jiahua to arrange work for her.

Zhu Jiahua asked: "You've come from Zhejiang?" Shen Anna answered: "I've come all this long way to Wuhan just to seek the chairman's patronage and serve the party and the state." Zhu Jiahua gave a chuckle, agreed readily, and even arranged a "special admission to the Party" for her, because people in the secretariat of Central Party Headquarters, and stenographers in the confidential section above all, had to be Kuomintang members. Shen Anna was not yet on the rolls, so one had to be seen to first.

A "special admission," or "direct admission," meant being jointly nominated and sponsored into the Party by three Kuomintang Central Committee members. This "flying over the sea" method was simple in procedure and quick to approve, and the membership number carried, in front of it, a specially conspicuous character: te, "special." That one "special" set the mind running easily to any number of things better sensed than said, a backer, a patron, a pedigree, a background. A "special-admission" member was especially well received inside the Kuomintang.

It was what others longed for in vain, and Shen Anna couldn't be glad of it at all. She "thought how she had gone to such trouble to come to Wuhan to find the Party, all in order to follow the Communist Party in revolution and resistance, and now she had to join the very Kuomintang she loathed with all her heart," and it was too galling, too "wrong"! Over having acted first and reported after, she "couldn't sleep for a long time." She was both "uneasy" and "on edge." She "felt as if she'd done something wrong." Falteringly she "reported" it to Dong Biwu, saying: "It came so suddenly that there was no time to ask for instructions, and yet I couldn't hesitate for a moment." Dong Biwu only "smiled" and said to her: "An intelligence agent has to be alert and nimble, able to adapt on the spot, and above all good at staying hidden. That is what intelligence work needs. Only with the standing of a Kuomintang member can you win trust and hold your ground for the long haul."

Hearing Dong Biwu say this, I felt a great weight lifted, as if a stone in my heart had come to rest.

But something still rankled: now that I had gone into a Kuomintang office, how would those patriotic, anti-Japanese young people see me?

"Do you still have misgivings?" Old Dong asked kindly, as if he had seen into my heart. "Remember, from now on you must learn to bear humiliation for the sake of the task. Bear humiliation for the task, do you understand?" Old Dong said, with weight on the words.

I nodded. From my heart I admired this wise elder. Later, when I was working at Central Party Headquarters, whenever I ran into a hard problem I would steady myself with Old Dong's charge, "bear humiliation for the task," and feel strength return. (Shen Anna, 2016)

### 4. Contact Lost

Bearing humiliation for the task, sunk deep in the heart of Chiang's party, Shen Anna obtained a great deal of important intelligence, among it the top-secret "anti-Communist" agenda of the Kuomintang's Ninth Plenary Session of its Fifth Central Committee.

From December 15 to 23, 1941, the Kuomintang Center held its Ninth Plenary Session of the Fifth Central Committee at Chongqing. Beforehand, the CCP Southern Bureau got wind of it and met at Hongyan village on October 10 to plan. Zhou Enlai told Dong Biwu, Kong Yuan, Deng Yingchao, and others: "Take care to collect material on the Ninth Plenary Session." At the time Shen Anna, though less than a month past childbirth, held to her post, seized the chance to get hold of waste documents in the printing group that were being held for destruction together, and passed them through Xu Zhonghang to the Southern Bureau. Among the material Shen Anna sent out were not only the "Resolution granting the Director-General full powers, in order to complete swiftly the victory of the resistance and the founding of the nation," which tightened Chiang Kai-shek's dictatorship further, but also He Yingqin's (then chief of staff of the Kuomintang Military Commission) Outline of a Report on the Communist Party Problem (Draft) and Xu Enzeng's (then deputy director of the Zhongtong Bureau) Proposal on Handling the Communist Party Problem (Draft), core Kuomintang secrets. These laid bare how, after the Southern Anhui Incident, the Kuomintang clung ever harder to its reactionary essence of "one party, one leader, one doctrine." Zhou Enlai attached the greatest importance to them, noting specially on the telegram sent to Yan'an: "Take care to protect the source." Mao Zedong likewise attached great importance to this batch of secret material, writing in his own hand: "The Ninth Plenary Session showed the Kuomintang's very great instability. This wavering seems unlikely to change until fascism is defeated; whether it then changes for better or worse will be decided by conditions at home and abroad."

The heavy intelligence work drained us without mercy, but we had no regrets. To do well the task the Party had given us was our greatest wish. (Shen Anna, 2016)

But Xu Zhonghang's sudden arrest, coming without warning, cut off Hua Mingzhi and Shen Anna entirely from the Party, so that however hard they tried, they could hardly complete their tasks.

In the autumn of 1942, Xu Zhonghang was secretly arrested by Kuomintang Zhongtong agents and shut up in the Wuyunshan concentration camp. The enemy's hounds fixed at once on Shen Anna, Zhu Jiahua's "trusted aide" and the "favorite" of Gan Naiguang (then deputy secretary-general of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters). They did not know that Xu Zhonghang was Shen Anna and Hua Mingzhi's handler, but they knew that Shen Anna had once had someone arrange a "special admission to the Party" for Xu Zhonghang, and they held in their hands a letter Shen Anna had written him, which said: "The other day the child was ill and I borrowed a little money from you for medicine; this month's salary still won't cover it, so I'll have to pay you back next month." Yan Mingfu (son of Yan Baohang, and later a secretary of the CCP Central Secretariat, head of the United Front Work Department, and vice-chairman of the National Committee of the CPPCC) has told this story. Shen Anna said: "In the autumn of 1939, on the 'special approval' of Bo Gu, head of the organization department of the CCP Central Southern Bureau, I was formally admitted and became a CCP member. Just when I was greatly heartened and meant to do more for the Party with Mingzhi, our direct leader, Xu Zhonghang, was arrested. Before that he had planted himself in the Kuomintang-run 'General Management Office of the Zhengzhong Book Company.' That job needed the cover of Kuomintang membership, so I'd got him a party card, asking two adjutants of the Central Party Headquarters secretaries to arrange a 'special admission' for him, but the card hadn't come through yet. This time he suddenly failed to make contact at the set time, and we didn't know where he lived, so we could only write, as colleagues, to ask after him. A few days later two men of unknown identity came to Central Party Headquarters with that letter, said Xu Zhonghang was a 'Communist,' and asked to speak with Miss Shen."

First the direct leader vanished; then came two uninvited guests wanting "to speak with Miss Shen." The whole thing was decidedly odd. Truly, no one who comes like this comes on a good errand. Truly, it was bad to talk and bad not to talk. Caught between the two, Shen Anna had to strike exactly the right note in the least possible time. The slightest slip could bring on consequences too grim to imagine. Shen Anna decided to meet motion with stillness, to answer a thousand changes by not changing at all. She said she did not know the two men and had the messenger send them away. But the "two men of unknown identity" simply refused to leave. They said they had been sent by higher-ups to see Miss Shen, and that if she would not see them, they couldn't go back and report. So Shen Anna rose and went downstairs to them. They pressed her: why had she borrowed money from Xu Zhonghang? And they added that he was a Communist and had already been arrested. Shen Anna said: "They looked to me like two small-time agents, and when I heard they were only asking about what was in the letter, I put on the airs of a Kuomintang official and, pretending to be angry, shot back: 'What proof do you have that the man's a Communist?' The two blurted out together: 'Xu Zhonghang's drawers are full of reactionary books.' I drew them out with a little goading, and hearing they'd only found a few progressive books, I brought out my 'big backer' to squash them: 'What does it matter if a petty clerk borrows a few dollars from a friend? If you have business, go report it to Secretary-General Zhu Jiahua.' With that I left the two of them standing and swept off."

Shen Anna had held off the agents' questioning for the moment, but she knew very well how serious it was. So the moment she got home she "moved the stove from the hallway into the room" and had Hua Mingzhi join her in a scorched-earth clearing, "one to go through the drawers, one through the trunk," and "stuffed straight into the stove and burned at once" the "intelligence hidden in the bamboo pole to be handed to Xu Zhonghang," along with every book and letter that "might cause trouble." Even though they had "always followed the rules of secret intelligence work, regularly clearing the house of things and destroying anything that might draw an agent's suspicion," there was still "mimeographed material and shorthand material" in the house, still "progressive books kept for years, even the works of Lu Xun and Guo Moruo and the like, and songbooks of the resistance."

We used the material we had to destroy to light the fire and cook porridge. In the rush I poured only a little water into the pot and actually forgot the rice. In no time the water boiled dry. Mingzhi found the bottom of the pot already scorched a dull red, and in his haste to lift it off he burned his fingers on the pot's ear and broke the pot. We got another pot and went on burning the material and cooking the porridge. (Shen Anna, 2016)

They were certain that once the enemy had fixed on Xu Zhonghang, suspecting him a CCP member lying low in Chongqing, they would stop at nothing; they had to pry his mouth open. Shen Anna and Hua Mingzhi judged rightly. In the enemy's interrogation room Xu Zhonghang suffered every kind of torture, was tormented within an inch of his life, yet never once gave up the Party's secrets. Some days later the chief of the finance section of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters, who was also chairman of the board of the Zhengzhong Book Company, talked with Shen Anna again. Shen Anna insisted, over and over, that she was not really close to Xu Zhonghang, only the ordinary give-and-take between acquaintances, a friend doing a favor. The section chief could only speak in generalities, telling her she was "young and didn't know better, helping people get a 'special admission'; don't do that sort of thing again." Shen Anna said: "Afterward we worked out why the enemy pursued it no further. For one thing, Xu Zhonghang had certainly held firm before them and had not given us up. For another, the enemy had long known I was Zhu Jiahua's 'old subordinate,' that Zhu had arranged my 'special admission' and placed me as a confidential stenographer in the confidential section of Central Party Headquarters, and they were afraid of overreaching and hurting themselves. But we didn't relax our guard for an instant, for who could say the enemy wasn't paying out a long line to hook a big fish?"

But the tie between Shen Anna and Hua Mingzhi and the Party was cut then and there, cut for three whole years.

For those three years they "had no taste for food and passed each day like a year." They knew perfectly well that "the danger hadn't passed, and it was no time to go on gathering intelligence, but they were still like a fast-running car that keeps a forward momentum even when the brake is pressed: the moment they saw a piece of valuable intelligence, they couldn't help wanting to collect it. Knowing there was nowhere to send it, they still kept at it in the dead of night, listening to the sound of the gendarmes next door flogging their prisoners on one side, and on the other looking at shorthand sheets full of intelligence, and letting the tears run down their faces."

We firmly believed: just wait a little longer, tomorrow or the day after, someone will come to make contact and take the intelligence away!

But day after day, several months slipped by in a flash, and still the organization sent no one to make contact.

Shorthand sheets with intelligence on them couldn't be kept long, so they had to be burned. To watch the intelligence we had obtained, piece after piece, burned away by our own hands, bit by bit, that was our bitterest pain. (Shen Anna, 2016)

Shen Anna and Hua Mingzhi, with the children in tow, held to their posts and flatly refused to move house.

Shen Anna "had worked at Central Party Headquarters for over five years, going from a couple of two to a family of four," and could no longer live in that broken-down room of under ten square meters at No. 75 Shangqingsi Street. The secretariat truly couldn't bear the sight of it, couldn't bear to have its "shorthand mainstay" go on "neighbor to hell," beside the "grim and terrifying gendarmerie," enduring year in and year out a "ceiling that dripped everywhere," a "floor pooled with rainwater," and "rats fighting and biting in the gaps between the boards," and "decided" to "move this shorthand mainstay to a better dormitory." But Shen Anna still would not budge, and gently declined her superiors' kindness.

In the end we made up our minds: we had already lived here five years, and even if we had to live here five more, we would hold on. When we first braved the dangers of war to go from Zhejiang to Wuhan to find the Party, what was it for? To find the Party organization. Now we knew where the Party organization was, and yet we couldn't go to it; we had to wait for the organization to find us. Now we had work, and a roof over our heads, and though life was hard, we could bear it. Whatever happened, we could not let the line to the Party be cut! (Shen Anna, 2016)

### 5. One Job Feeds You Three Years

After three years of straining their eyes in wait, at last a kinsman came from home.

One evening in October 1945, Shen Anna and Hua Mingzhi had just finished supper and were rinsing the bowls and chopsticks when they suddenly heard a knock at the door. "Tap, tap-tap, tap. Tap, tap-tap, tap."

Mingzhi and I looked at each other, half unable to believe our ears. Could it really be that the Party organization had sent someone to reconnect?

Holding down my pounding heart, I went to the door and asked softly: Who is it?

From outside came a voice that seemed half-familiar: Me.

On instinct I guessed it might be one of our own and quickly opened the door. The moment it opened a man slipped in and turned at once to shut it behind him, quick and deft.

Mingzhi and I looked hard and saw it was our old leader, Wu Kejian, unseen for years! (Shen Anna, 2016)

Wu Kejian, who had once gone by the name Wu Heicheng, was born in 1900 in Pingjiang, Hunan. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1924 and the work of the Central Special Branch in 1928, serving in turn as deputy secretary-general of the CCP Central Yangtze Bureau, standing member of the Southern Bureau, and editor-in-chief of the Xinhua Daily, among other posts.

Wu Kejian's excitement was every bit the equal of Shen Anna's and Hua Mingzhi's.

In his later years Wu Kejian recalled: "I was entrusted by Li Kenong of the Central Social Affairs Department to go to Shanghai and do underground work. Before I reached Shanghai, I found the Shen and Hua couple again in Chongqing. On the one hand I conveyed to them Zhou Enlai's commendation for their outstanding work; on the other I hoped they would go on supplying intelligence. And they supplied it in good time." (Shen Anna, 2016)

The next evening, the "intelligence" Shen Anna and Hua Mingzhi "supplied" to Wu Kejian was a full set of background material, prepared well in advance, on the Kuomintang's Sixth National Congress and the First Plenary Session of its Sixth Central Committee, among it the confidential Work Guidelines of Our Party's Comrades on the Communist Problem and the text of Chiang Kai-shek's speech on the evening of May 19, when he feasted the congress delegates at the Chongqing stadium.

Wu Kejian's heart leapt, and he sighed: "You really are three years without a sale, then one sale feeds you three years!"

"The position of our Party at present is that on the one hand it suffers the enemy's oppression and our allies' contempt, and on the other must guard against the reactionaries' vicious schemes; a moment's inattention, and we fall into their net and snare. Our Party's foundations are not yet firm, its strength not yet full; hemmed in on every side, we are in the gravest danger. All the more must we swallow the humiliation and endure to the end. Therefore we must all recognize that our Party's position is truly perilous in the extreme, and the responsibility it bears truly heavy in the extreme."

The "reactionaries' vicious schemes" spoken of in volume sixty of the Draft Chronicle were laid out nakedly, in the Work Guidelines of Our Party's Comrades on the Communist Problem, as this: "The Communists have consistently held to their armed separatism, using it to wreck the resistance, so that our Party's forbearance and its painstaking efforts at a political solution have to this day borne no fruit, while our Party's comrades, struggling bitterly in every quarter, are cruelly harmed by the Communists past all recording. To trace back over the past, can one but be indignant? And of late the Communists have gone further still, raising the slogan of a coalition government and plotting to manufacture their so-called 'people's congress of the liberated areas,' in an attempt to overthrow the government and endanger the state. Every comrade should raise his vigilance, give play to the revolutionary spirit, strive hard, put the army in order and cleanse the administration, strengthen our forces, and see our Party's policy of a political solution carried through." Set this naked statement beside Chiang Kai-shek's opening address of May 5, 1945, where he stressed again and again that "after constitutional government is put into effect, our Party's responsibility will not be lightened thereby, but rather made heavier"; set it beside the Kuomintang Center's setting up of a special committee expressly to "oppose the Communists"; set it beside the Resolution on the Communist Problem, "passed as reviewed"; set it beside the fifteenth session of the congress, which elected Chiang Kai-shek Director-General, the whole hall "rising as one to pass it, shouting slogans that rang to the skies," and then Mao Zedong's "Seventh Congress" prophecy is proved, once more, beyond all doubt: "Even if the Japanese invaders are beaten, China may still fall into civil war, dragged back into the old state of misery, not independent, not free, not democratic, not united, not prosperous." For "the anti-people clique inside the Kuomintang" clung to "fascist dictatorship."

"However winding and roundabout the road, the task of the Chinese people's independence and liberation must be accomplished in the end, and the time for it has now come. The great aspiration cherished by countless martyrs for more than a hundred years must be realized by our generation; whoever would stop it, in the end it cannot be stopped." (Mao Zedong, 1945)

On January 10, 1946, the first plenary session of the Political Consultative Conference opened in the auditorium of the National Government, and every evening the Kuomintang side held a "party-caucus meeting" to prepare for the next day's war of words with the Communist Party, and Shen Anna was in charge of the minutes. Shen Anna made the record in two copies: one to hand up, and one that Hua Mingzhi boiled down, abstracted, wrote in secret ink, and hid away, to be carried that same night to a prearranged meeting point and given to Wu Kejian, and passed on to Zhou Enlai, so that our Party kept a firm hold on the initiative at the negotiating table throughout.

At first Wu Kejian chose Niujiaotuo as the meeting point, not too quiet, not too busy, and close to where we lived, which saved time. For two nights running the two sides switched places, then kept the appointment at a set hour: whoever came first would hide in an empty corner or a dim spot, wait for the other to appear, and step forward to meet him. As the two brushed past each other, a touch of the hands, and Mingzhi's intelligence had passed into Wu Kejian's. The two would walk off back to back, circle round once or twice more, and each return his own way.

Wu Kejian later told us that in those days Zhou Enlai waited every night for our intelligence, and if it was even a little late he would ask his secretary: "Has the material come yet?" (Shen Anna, 2016)

In March and April 1946, Chiang Kai-shek called one small-group meeting after another, moving his troops and generals to seize the Northeast. The intelligence was again obtained by Shen Anna and reached Zhou Enlai's hands, and Zhou Enlai used it to expose Chiang Kai-shek's hypocrite's face in the open, which vexed Chiang Kai-shek greatly. He sternly ordered the secretary-general of Central Party Headquarters, Wu Tiecheng, to hunt down the leak; the result, naturally, was a tiger's head and a snake's tail, and it came to nothing.

In May 1946 Shen Anna went with the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters as it "returned the capital" to Nanjing, and worked at Dingjiaqiao.

On June 26, heedless of the whole nation's fierce opposition, the Kuomintang launched a large-scale assault on the liberated areas, beginning with the Central Plains Liberated Area centered on its siege of Xuanhuadian on the Hubei-Henan border, and spreading in turn to southern Shanxi, the Jiangsu-Anhui border, southwestern Shandong, both sides of the Jiaoji Railway, eastern Hebei, eastern Suiyuan, southern Chahar, Rehe, southern Liaoning, and elsewhere, and all-out civil war broke out.

Through the three years and more of the War of Liberation, besides going on guiding, helping, and coordinating Shen Anna's work, Hua Mingzhi, on the assignment of He Yiduan (born 1898, of Yingshan, Sichuan, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1924), kept in touch, one after another, with Ge Yiyuan (then a colonel section chief in the Police General Administration of the Interior Ministry), Wang Zhidao (then chief private secretary of the Changzhou-Zhenjiang purchasing and transport office of the Jiangsu provincial land-tax and grain administration), and Wang Lifu (then a colonel commissioner and concurrently office director at the Combined Service Forces General Headquarters of the Defense Ministry), gathering and handing up the intelligence they collected.

In the course of this, Shen Anna's second elder brother, Shen Qin, and Hua Mingzhi's sixth younger brother, Hua Zao, both joined Wu Kejian's intelligence system, so that the Shen family of the "Grand Historian's Mansion" in Taixing and the Hua household of the "Hua Charitable Estate" at Dangkou became, in the truest sense, an "intelligence dynasty."

Shen Qin, born in 1908, formally joined Wu Kejian's intelligence system in 1948, planting himself in the Transport Department of the Combined Service Forces General Headquarters of the reactionary Defense Ministry, and supplied intelligence such as the scheduling of military shipping through the port of Shanghai.

Hua Zao, born in 1920, joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1938, and from 1947 served full-time as courier between Shen Anna and Hua Mingzhi on one side and Wu Kejian and He Yiduan on the other, shuttling constantly between Nanjing and Shanghai.

One day in the autumn of 1948, I got word: Wu Kejian wanted to see me.

The word was passed by Hua Zao, the Shanghai-Nanjing courier who handled contact with Mingzhi and me. Wu Kejian asked me to come to Shanghai on the pretext of visiting my husband's family and report to him secretly, by word of mouth. After Hua Zao had scouted every angle, the meeting point was fixed at the "Four Sisters Ballroom" in the Pudong Building.

...

At eight o'clock sharp, with Hua Zao along, I reached the appointed place on time and met Wu Kejian.

Wu Kejian wore a Western suit and looked like a senior clerk at a foreign firm. I carried a lady's handbag and had dressed more smartly than usual.

Wu Kejian and I sat down in a corner of the ballroom, nibbling snacks and drinking coffee while we talked work in low voices, filling in what the written reports had left out. To avoid rousing suspicion, now and then we went down to the floor for a dance. Wu Kejian wasn't much of a dancer and only went through the motions. The two of us were much like the idlers who don't dance, what people call "minding a fortune-teller's stall," just eating, drinking, and chatting, and with the loud music it made good cover.

Hua Zao, in a felt hat, sat in a dim corner not far from Wu Kejian and me, going out now and then to check that all was quiet. He had pulled the brim low, and in the shifting light it was hard to make out his face.

...

When the meeting was over, Wu Kejian left first, with someone else waiting quietly to receive him a little way off. Hua Zao and I left after. Hua Zao saw me safely back to my Shanghai home, and the next day saw me onto the train back to Nanjing. (Shen Anna, 2016)

In Nanjing, Shen Anna held on all the way to April 20, 1949, the day the Nanjing authorities refused to sign the Agreement on Domestic Peace (Final Revised Text), the Kuomintang-Communist peace talks broke down for good, and our army broke through the enemy's Yangtze defense line along the stretch from Zongyang to Yuxikou in Anhui.

After the founding of New China, the Chinese Communist Party completed a historic transformation: it had gone from a party leading the people in the struggle to seize nationwide power to a party governing the whole country, and governing it for the long term. But the greater part of Shen Anna's later life, the greater part of her years, went on just as before. She still had to be one who "stands sentry without a gun, keeps watch without a tent; who watches the winds and clouds at every moment, marks the least detail in every place; whose battlefield, though it has no shape, keeps its soldiers and arms within the breast" (Xiao Ke, 1986). She was still an invisible person, unable to show herself as she truly was. She thought back to the start of the resistance, in Chongqing, at the "Fishing Village" at Zengjiayan, when Deng Yingchao said to her: "For the Party's secret work, you must be willing to be an unsung hero." Zhou Enlai had her keep in mind: "Our Party's cause needs a great host of unsung heroes." And she thought back to when she was learning shorthand, in Shanghai, at Yu Xijian's side, helping him translate The Autobiography of Isadora Duncan (1934), in which there was the line: "My dance is the expression of life."

Day and night I practiced dancing in the studio, seeking through the body's motion to give the human spirit a sacred expression. Sometimes I would sit still, both hands laid on my breast, over the heart, lost in thought for hours together. After some months of this, whenever I heard music, its every note and every wave seemed to pour into that central wellspring within me. And what it gave back was not the reflection of the reason, but the reflection of the soul.

Duncan put on her dancing shoes and could never stop again.

Shen Anna, at her battle station on the hidden front, could only "keep her mouth shut as a stoppered bottle, and guard her thoughts as a walled city"; could only "stand in awe of the divine, and be watchful when alone"; could only be as it is written: "The greatest white seems soiled, the greatest square has no corner, the greatest note is faint of sound, the greatest image has no form."

The English philosopher Pudeman said: "Sow an act, and you reap a habit; sow a habit, and you reap a character; sow a character, and you reap a destiny."

Precisely because Duncan lived by the dance, on nights when she "could not sleep" she would "go up quite alone to the Acropolis of Athens, into the Theater of Dionysus, and dance by herself." And in the wind-like whirling she heard "a sound of parting from the living and the dead."

Precisely because Shen Anna was utterly loyal to our Party's hidden front, to the very last moment of her bodily life, in her final hours, what she went on murmuring, in broken snatches, was still: "Am I exposed? They're making arrests, run out the back door..."
