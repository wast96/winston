## Chapter 3. Treacherous Tides, a Gathering Storm

### (1) A Special Personage Lends Secret Aid

In the first book of Nameless Heroes, Rooting Out Traitors in the North, I made frequent mention of Mr. Mao Wanli, who in occupied Beiping had, against all the rules, lent my work his powerful help; he died, to my grief, on the eighth of February in the seventy-first year, aged eighty. Old friends and good friends were never many, and now there is one fewer; but since sooner or later we must all go, and since Brother Wanli lived to a great age and died a good death, in the eyes of men like us this ought to be counted a happy event. So, whatever others might think, when I sent my condolence-gift I purposely chose a red envelope, meaning thereby to express my private feeling; and if it was a breach of etiquette, I could only beg the pardon of Mrs. Mao, Sister-in-law Yiqiu.

On the sixth of March, the day the public memorial was held, I met in the mourning hall the long-unseen Mr. He Zhiyuan. I had for some time wanted to go up the hill to call on him, but had truly been too lazy; to meet him thus was a piece of good fortune. Brother Zhiyuan is already past eighty, and though frail in body his ears are keen and his eyes clear; when he speaks he is orderly and unhurried, no less than in years gone by. I drew near to his seat and took the chance to ask him about that affair at the end of the twenty-seventh year, when Wang Jingwei left the country and fled.

Mr. He Zhiyuan is now a delegate to the National Assembly; at that time he served as chief of the Second Section of the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission, in charge of intelligence work. All the intelligence (the raw material) reported in by the several units under the Juntong—including the various war zones within the country, the Japanese-occupied areas, and posts overseas—passed through his section for processing and distribution, so that Brother Zhiyuan was able to see every item and to rule on its disposal. By Brother Zhiyuan's account: "Beforehand I never once saw any intelligence material whatever touching on Wang Jingwei's collusion with the enemy and betrayal of the country. On the very day Wang boarded the plane and left Chongqing—the eighteenth of December—it was indeed said he was going to the Central Military Academy at Chengdu to give an address, and no one suspected otherwise; as for the plane turning in the air and flying off to Kunming, and afterward reaching Hanoi, that was learned only in a report received later."

When the talk turned to the groundless rumor of "whether there had been a tacit understanding," Brother Zhiyuan too held it utterly without foundation. And so I gained one more powerful piece of ironclad proof.

The foregoing may count as a small supplement to what I have written before; now let me go on and tell again of the Hanoi affair.

Here a thing especially worth recording is the entrance of a special personage. His appearance in our work carried, however one puts it, a certain air of mystery. This gentleman was the very man whom Mr. Dai, before his departure, had designated for me to go and contact in person, forbidding any other working comrade to be sent to seek him out.

This gentleman's true name and his station in life, after careful consideration and after I had exchanged views with several close friends, I have decided to hold back a little, judging it better not to state them plainly. In truth there is nothing private in this at all; it is wholly out of regard for preserving a man's good name. Even so, all that he did was real and true, without the least fabrication. The reader need only pay a little attention, and clues enough will be found in the text.

This special personage—let us suppose his surname to be "Xu," and his given name may be omitted; hereafter, when I mention him again, I shall call him Mr. Xu.

"Mr. Xu" was a native of Jiangsu—if my memory does not fail me, of Wuxi—and was then about forty-odd years of age; he had studied in Europe and America, and I heard it was political economy. This gentleman was of short, stocky build, wore thick nearsighted spectacles, and spoke standard Mandarin. From his looks alone one could not tell what line of work he was in; in conversation his manner was bold and expansive, with not the faintest whiff of the study about him, so that it was hard to imagine he had ever been a man of letters.

But do not underestimate his value in public relations. Though he was not a man of Fujian or Guangdong, he was widely respected in the local overseas-Chinese community; indeed, for a great many matters large and small, there was no one to turn to but him. There was more: Mr. Xu was on close terms not only with the French officials stationed in Hanoi, but especially with the French chief of police, and from time to time he would give cocktail parties or dances to warm the friendship. Social connections of just this kind were precisely where we leaned on Mr. Xu.

The writer's first meeting with Mr. Xu was to establish contact according to Mr. Dai's instruction—in our working parlance this is called a "hand-over of liaison," meaning that in terms of organizational relations one is handed over to a certain unit or person for contact, not that there is any private tie between the two, nor that a given unit has recruited and developed the man on its own. As to how Mr. Xu came to build his working relationship with Mr. Dai, the writer never knew; and by our customary practice, such a question was not even permitted. I was curious too, and guessed in my heart that it was very likely Fang Bingxi who had introduced him to Mr. Dai, for he and Mr. Xu stood in the relation of near-townsmen; but why, then, was Fang Bingxi not told to make the contact with him? On this point I was always at a loss.

After Mr. Dai reached Chongqing he sent a further telegram inquiring about this matter, and charged me particularly: "In matters great and small alike, you may consult with him as you see fit; and in any need of the work, there is no harm in handling it in consultation with him." From this one may see how heavily Mr. Dai relied upon and trusted him.

Mr. Xu was affable and easy of approach, receiving one the moment one came, with not the least official airs; in his hospitality he was especially warm. To say no more than this: at our very first meeting he had his wife come out to receive me, and again and again pressed me to stay for a plain meal. In this way the distance between us was shortened, and it became easy to talk over problems.

Mr. Xu did not much mention Mr. Dai by word of mouth, nor did he make clear what his relations with Mr. Dai were; yet he was very fond of the two words "you people." His saying "you people" rather than "we" fully declared his standing—that is to say, he was aiding the Hanoi work in the position of a guest-retainer, and stood in no subordinate relation to our organization.

Mr. Xu said, frank and earnest: "You people, sent out to this place with neither the land nor its people familiar to you, will surely, as one can well imagine, meet with many inconveniences. Of course I am willing to help you—but only from behind the scenes. Should word ever get out that I had a part in it, that would be a disaster; so I must ask you to understand this point. In short, whatever I can do I shall surely do; be at ease on that." Mr. Xu spoke the plain truth, and I naturally understood, as I ought to. We talked it over and agreed together that henceforth, unless it were necessary, the fewer our meetings the better; that it would be best for Mr. Xu to designate or introduce someone to serve as our go-between, a messenger between us; and next, that whatever could be said over the telephone might be said by telephone, and only what had to be discussed face to face need be met over. At the same time Mr. Xu promised to take charge of finding a man to be his assistant in this business.

As for the matter of my seeing little of Mr. Xu, though this was what we said, we did not in fact keep to it so strictly. And the reason was not without cause. Wang Luqiao and I were both northerners; we could stomach neither Western food nor a steady diet of Cantonese cooking, and longed greatly for a meal of dumplings or steamed buns and such wheaten fare, as if only then could one count oneself truly fed. But there was no little northern eatery here, and we could only swallow our saliva a few times more. As luck would have it, Mrs. Xu was a native of Beiping, and the head cook she had trained could also turn out a little of the wheaten food northerners eat; it was for just this reason that Luqiao and I, contrary to our resolve, often went to the Xu residence as guests.

Mrs. Xu had no children at her knee, and beyond keeping house had no relatives or friends to visit; to pass the idle hours she would now and then, after a meal, invite us to play two or three hours of cards. And why not mahjong? For one thing there is the noise, and she feared the neighbors might hear and gossip; for another, mahjong can seat at most four, whereas at cards there are many ways to play, up to six or seven people, so that no one is left out in the cold and all may share the fun—was that not the better thing?

We played up in a little attic above the Xu residence, ostensibly keeping it from Mr. Xu; but in truth, how could Mr. Xu not have known? Only he was very tactful, and whenever such an occasion arose he would go off alone to hide in the study and rummage among his books. We would tease Mrs. Xu, asking, "Does Mr. Xu dislike card-playing?" Mrs. Xu said, "Not at all; it is only that he is too embarrassed to sit down. If one day he gave up his work, he might well be keener at it than any of you." We had our tasks, and naturally put the work first; but we were still young, and unless one puts on a show, I believe no one can escape the love of play—that is the honest side of it. Yet neither the larger circumstance nor our own small world would let us do as we pleased, and so it was kept to just Luqiao and me, the two of us playing off against each other, without even letting Fang Bingxi know. There were times, though, when I had to borrow a little money from Brother Bingxi to make things up; and as the times grew frequent and the sums grew large, Brother Bingxi could guess that we were either whoring or gambling. What was rare in him was his generosity: he still would not hurt our self-respect by saying it out. Whether he ever reported this not-quite-proper business to our superiors, that is hard to say.

Why have I told so much of this "idle talk"? Because too much play will always delay the serious business, and at the very least wear away a great deal of one's spirit. People all say that to be absorbed in trifles is to lose one's purpose; luckily we had not come to such a pass, though it did indeed have some ill effect. And I must add one line more: this is but a light and passing sketch; the fact is, there was worse than this.

Not long after making contact with Mr. Xu, he introduced, as we had agreed, a man to serve as go-between—a gentleman surnamed Zeng. He had a given name, but though I have racked my brains for several years I cannot recall the two characters. Most saddening of all, there is not even anyone left to ask, for those old comrades of those days are either dead and gone or cut off by mountains and rivers, all news of them severed. Mr. Zeng was a Fujianese, over thirty, a tall big fellow, lean and long, gentle in temper, slow and deliberate—the sort of man who does as he is bid.

Before Mr. Xu had introduced Mr. Zeng to me, Mr. Zeng and I were already acquainted, for we had gathered together on occasion. Mr. Zeng was not only a regular guest at the Xu residence but a man under Mr. Xu's hand. He had one great virtue: an exceedingly strong sense of responsibility, so that whatever one entrusted to him he was sure to see through. Of Mr. Zeng's help in the Hanoi work I made no report to my superiors, so there could be no record of Mr. Zeng in our personnel files. Happily his heart was not set on this line, so he never joined our working organization. Worth noting is that he would not even accept the travel money we offered him; he said, "This is doing work for Mr. Xu, and it is serving the country too."

Through Mr. Zeng I chanced, without seeking it, to make the acquaintance of a young fellow named Wei Chunfeng, only a little past twenty. His ancestral home was Fujian, but he had grown up in Annam; what his schooling was I never quite made out. This man was of surpassing cleverness, far beyond the common run. He was not very tall, and had grown up somewhat slight; but as for looks, he could be called clear-browed and bright-eyed, of a fine handsome bearing. He had the gift of four tongues: he was versed in the French language and could speak the local Annamese to the life; Fujian was his native place, though the writer could not tell Southern Min from the Fuzhou speech; and his own Chinese, in writing and in speech, would pass well enough. Because he had long dwelt in Hanoi as an overseas Chinese, he knew the general run of local society, and the ways and customs of the people, more thoroughly than one could wish; and so he became a man we sought to win over.

We could not well say just what we were about—the circumstances would not allow us to speak the plain truth, and neither the gravity of the task nor of the situation could be lightly told to a man not deeply understood. And Wei Chunfeng never asked such things; whenever we had a need that lay within his power, he did it. I remember most clearly that, right up to the close of the Hanoi Wang case, we never formally told him what our identity really was; and whether he heard anything of it over at Mr. Zeng's, I do not know—though even Mr. Zeng himself cannot have known much.

Writing to this point, the writer must again put in a few idle words. In our early work, in recruiting or winning over working comrades, during the period of testing or trial, this was more or less always the way of it: as if one were over-guarding oneself while neglecting the other man. That is to say, we wanted a man's participation and yet would not tell him what sort of organization it was, or what it did—which does indeed seem none too reasonable. And so some men, whose disposition and bent were not well suited, would in the end say they had been deceived at the outset, and cry out their regret. The writer, who has done outdoor field-work for many years, has for the most part met with just such situations, and it is much the same when I ask my colleagues and good friends. Told plainly, it is truly past belief: the Juntong, which began with the dozen-odd men of the Special Services Department, expanded by the close of the war, around the time of victory, to several hundred thousand. There must be a reason in this. Was it the "sense of mystery" that drew men in? Or was it "patriotic ardor" that spurred them on? Or could it be that the promise of "a ladder to heaven" enticed them? Let me stop here, and not run on too far.

From the time Wei Chunfeng informally joined the work, he became at once our indispensable guide in Hanoi, our steering-wheel and our map. For a stretch of time the writer, Brother Wang Luqiao, and Wei Chunfeng, the three of us, were together nearly every day; even our lodging, which served as the command center, was no longer kept secret from him. As the days of such closeness lengthened, we grew wholly unceremonious with one another.

We noticed that whenever Wei Chunfeng had lingered long at my place on some business, he would always find a pretext to slip away for a few hours; or he would be late even for an appointed time. Now why should this be? Naturally we had to get to the bottom of it before we could rest easy. So I asked Luqiao to look into the matter. Within a few days it was clear: he had a girlfriend.

Wei Chunfeng's sweetheart was an Annamese girl. We knew only that her surname was Nguyễn, and never could get out her given name; for though Wei Chunfeng did call her by it, we could not make it out. Miss Nguyễn was beautiful and demure. I do not know how to write it so as to convey her; and were I to borrow some ready-made phrase, it might not be apt, and by overstating I might affront Miss Nguyễn's purity.

Miss Nguyễn was tender and slender, eighteen or nineteen years of age, dressed in the true fashion of an Annamese maiden. She used no rouge or powder; her brows and eyes were as if painted, her charm all grace. On her head she wore a ponytail—that is, one long strand of jet-black hair let down over the shoulders behind, like the braid our country girls plait, but with no tie at the root and no knot at the tip. Having no name for it, shall we call it the "heavenly-maiden-scattering-flowers" style? She wore the long gown peculiar to Annamese women, rather like our cheongsam, but without buttoned loops; its greatest difference from the cheongsam was that the slits up either side were exceedingly high—and this was not Miss Nguyễn alone, but much the same everywhere one looked. Below she went barefoot without stockings, dragging a pair of high-heeled shoes with the heels bared, so that when she walked she was, sure enough, all swaying grace. Whether Miss Nguyễn and Wei Chunfeng stood shoulder to shoulder or walked arm in arm, look at them how you would, they were a matched pair of jade.

The writer asked Wei Chunfeng, half in earnest and half not: "How long have you and Miss Nguyễn known each other, and how far has it gone now? Are you just beginning to be friends, or has it advanced to courting—might it come to marriage?" Wei Chunfeng was frank enough; he said, "We have been together since childhood. If only I could have a steady livelihood, we should have married long ago." Let that pass; my reason for asking was not idle chatter about the household. It was to learn thereby the relation between the two, Wei and Miss Nguyễn, and to see just how much influence Wei Chunfeng had over her. For the Hanoi work, so urgently to be got under way, had great need of someone like Miss Nguyễn as a cover, to ease our movements; and now that I knew their relation was close enough, nothing could be better.

Afterward, though Miss Nguyễn never took part in our work, yet through Wei Chunfeng's hold on her and Luqiao's guidance, she did in truth give us much help in reconnaissance and cover of every kind.

This whole chain of personal connections was developed entirely out of Mr. Xu—and this only as far as the three or four persons with whom the writer had direct contact. Beyond these, Mr. Xu and Wei Chunfeng each had their own connections to work; who those men were, the writer cannot say. It is not that I have forgotten—I simply did not press to ask at the time.

There is one more thing to add here. After Wang Jingwei fled from Chongqing, every intelligence and public-security unit under our Party, government, and army was very likely subjected to one or several strict reviews. Owing to the sensitivity of my post, the intelligence dispositions on all sides were strengthened, and in fact did improve upon what had gone before. As to whether there was any coordinating body such as a "liaison center" or "joint briefing" to serve as an overall tie and division of cooperative labor, I do not know. But by the writer's surmise, there probably was not; and even if there was, it did not produce the effect one would have hoped for.

### (2) Ordered to Verify Whether Wang Was About to Leave Hanoi

When we first took up the task, we truly did not know just where Wang Jingwei was living. The intelligence material we obtained, for want of any basic grasp, gave us no ground for judgment. Some said he was living in seclusion on a nameless little island; others said he had taken rooms at the Railway Hotel or the Continental Hotel—but the great hotels of Hanoi were all marked with their names in a foreign tongue, and for a while there was no place to begin the search. There were besides some small and middling hotels run by overseas Chinese, all in the Chinese style—the Fozhaolou, or else the Hailutong. We searched every place; among the hotels with Chinese signboards there was not one called the "Railway" or the "Continental," and Wang would surely never be in such a place. Then it was heard that after Wang reached Hanoi he had lodged at a friend's house—but who this so-called friend was, no one could make clear; on further inquiry it was said to be one surnamed Zhu; by one account the former residence of the martyr Zhu Zhixin, by another the villa of his late friend Zhu Peide. With news of this kind, all head and no tail, how could one fix where Wang was really living? When one cannot even make out where a man lives, what talk can there be of "surveillance"?

Then as to "reconnaissance": to do mere surface work is of no use at all, and can at times lead one astray and spoil the whole affair. Reconnaissance prizes going deep; and going deep means, best of all, being able to "penetrate" into the other side's interior. If that cannot be done, one must at least have a reliable line—otherwise all becomes a cause of failure.

Just at this point, when I was at my wits' end, Mr. Dai sent a telegram from Chongqing. The wording I have long forgotten, but the gist was: "It is reported that Wang is about to leave Vietnam for Hong Kong and thence to Japan, or else to go straight to Europe. Whether there are such signs, verify and report at once; and it is hoped you will make proper arrangements." What was to be done! As I have just said, we had not so much as touched the edge of Wang, so how were we to verify and reply? The bitterness of it was that we could neither speak the plain truth to our superiors nor dare to answer loosely, and yet could not fail to answer at all—it was too hard a strait. The writer once called Fang Bingxi, Yu Lexing, Cen Jiazhuo, Wang Luqiao, and the others together to confer, but no one could put forward any concrete opinion to resolve this real problem—the more so as this was a matter that brooked no delay, an answer being wanted at once. We all understood that, unless there were already a ready working line to be followed and traced, there was no other road. Could so weighty a matter be answered on nothing but hearsay?

This was the first and greatest difficulty I had met since joining the work. In the past there had been situations of a like kind, but there had always been a way to overcome them; this once alone, in the midst of helplessness, I could only decide to make no reply for the time being. "For the time being" signified only a slight delay—not, that is, "at once." Among the principles by which we handled affairs, to every question there must be an answer; on no account was dawdling permitted, nor evasion on some pretext, nor shelving a thing and ignoring it. In a matter so urgent, how could it be left unsettled? Within my intended "for the time being," I secretly set myself a limit of forty-eight hours; if by then I still had no concrete answer, I would have no choice but to state the truth plainly.

Should Wang Jingwei leave Hanoi within this self-set limit, what consequence would follow? I reckoned in my heart: my superiors might make allowance for our being short-handed and our dispositions incomplete, and after a reprimand would decide the further steps of the work according to how things developed. If Wang had already gone to Hong Kong, then, apart from handing the main task over to the unit stationed there, perhaps one or two of us would be designated to trail him to Hong Kong; if he had gone to Europe, the matter would probably be brought to a close, laid aside for the time, and responsibility no longer pursued; but should Wang reach Japan, the point of watching his movements would be wholly lost, and then it would be different—we should be recalled to Chongqing and reviewed one by one. The heaviest responsibility would of course be the writer's, and in truth the writer ought to bear the whole of it. In my conjecture, I should then receive one of two punishments, far apart in weight: at the light end, a few words of blame and no more; at the heavy end, immediate detention, handed to the Third Section of the Bureau headquarters (which oversees military law) and put on trial for the crime of "botching a military mission." By our "house law," such a crime could be sentenced to twelve years, to life imprisonment, or to death.

Think of it—it truly made one shudder!

Let the reader note that this happened before the writer had yet made contact with Mr. Xu; that is, Mr. Dai's telegram inquiring whether Wang Jingwei showed signs of leaving came first, and my going in person to meet Mr. Xu as instructed came after. Had the order of time been reversed, I should not have had to fret and let my thoughts run wild.

Just when I was groping without a clue for how to report on Wang's movements, Mr. Dai sent yet another telegram pressing me about the contact with Mr. Xu. I took this for a hint—after so many years, how could I fail to catch it?—and so I hastened to see Mr. Xu, asking him face to face to find some way to get to the bottom of the matter. Mr. Xu answered crisply: "That is very easy; I will ask them, and we shall know." Mr. Xu wore a look of the utmost unconcern, as though he did not take the matter much to heart. Surely, I thought, he was not joking? He said, "I will ask them, and we shall know"—but whom would he ask? And who were "they"? Not Wang Jingwei and his circle, surely? I had put the question in a most solemn manner, and I was sure Mr. Xu would never be so careless; so this "they" of his must refer to someone else.

I had gone to see Mr. Xu in the morning, and that same day, not long past noon, he had a reply. First he sent Mr. Zeng to my lodging to answer my question; but fearing the message might have gone somehow astray, I went again together with Mr. Zeng to meet Mr. Xu, hoping he would tell me once with his own mouth, that I might rest easy.

By Mr. Xu's account: "Mr. Wang does indeed have it in mind to leave Hanoi, for he has already given notice to the authorities in charge here; but up to now Wang himself has not made a final decision—that is, he wishes to go, but has not fixed when. As for where he means to go, it is let out that he will first go to Saigon and then take ship for France. Whether he will go to Hong Kong or Japan, they do not know." Mr. Xu spoke clearly, and gave a full answer to the matters Mr. Dai had asked.

Just as I was about to put a question of my own to Mr. Xu, he asked me in turn: "Mr. Wang's movements—you people ought to know very well; and he is in constant touch with the Central authorities, and lately the Party and government have sent people several times, one after another. Do you people not know all this?"

How was I to answer this fitly? Mr. Xu did not know the position we were placed in; had he even a little understanding of it, he would not have spoken so. I told Mr. Xu earnestly: "I myself truly do not know. Mr. Dai's reason for inquiring into these matters must surely have its purpose. As I see it, he plainly wishes to verify whether Wang really means to leave Hanoi, and if so, all the more to know where he intends to go. This is all the plain truth, without the least falsehood; and there will be much more of this kind hereafter, so I must beg Mr. Xu to understand."

Having talked to this point, I had a question to ask—I had to ask it, for otherwise, even with a full answer, something would still be wanting: namely, the question of "source." Though Mr. Xu had given us answers to the matters we sought, I still needed a full grasp of where Mr. Xu had gotten them—including who had said it, what that speaker did, how he came to know, and what his relation to Mr. Xu was. Of course, how could I put the question to Mr. Xu in such a tone? So I had again to shift my manner and, in fairly roundabout wording, ask Mr. Xu to answer these questions one by one.

Mr. Xu was most cooperative, and told me the whole of what he understood, and the general course of his talks; it was all reasonable, and I was well satisfied. From this I could judge that, as to whether Wang Jingwei was about to leave for elsewhere, the truth and reliability of it were beyond doubt.

Only now did I understand that Mr. Xu's double-edged "they" referred all along to certain foreign friends—those, that is, who supplied the intelligence material and answered as to Wang Jingwei's movements.

I returned to my lodging, drafted the telegram, and replied to the incoming wire. In the Hanoi work this was but one small fragment; yet in other quarters at Chongqing it was a matter of much moment. As it appears in the record, I cite below several passages of the diary written by one under the pen name "Yongwu," in his "A Record of How Wang Jingwei Broke Away from Chongqing," for the reader's reference.

"The twenty-fourth of January (of the twenty-eighth year): heard that Mr. Wang will leave Hanoi for Europe. Luo Junqiang says: Mr. Chiang lately charged Song Ziwen to urge Mr. Wang to go to Europe, and further told Minister Wang of the Foreign Ministry (Chonghui) to wire the legations and consulates in Europe to look after him well—whether this is true I do not know."

"The twenty-ninth of January: Chen Shuren says that at the recent Fifth Plenary Session, Wang Liangchou (Chonghui) reported that Mr. Wang is about to go to Europe, that his passport has already been issued by the Foreign Ministry, and that his date of departure cannot be far off. Luo Junqiang's words of a few days ago seem thus to be confirmed."

"The thirty-first of January: Chen Gao, who handles affairs at the Wang residence, says that recently Madam Wang sent a telegram to Peng Xuepei (Vice-Minister of Communications), asking him to find a way to recover the firearms of the bodyguards at Mr. Wang's side (surrendered not long ago to the authorities concerned), and further hoping the authorities would permit a part of the bodyguard to go to Hong Kong—the bodyguards' travel money had been remitted a few days before. Chen Gao also said that Mr. Wang's passport for Europe was sent over by the Foreign Ministry, and did not come at Mr. Wang's request; seen thus, the news of Mr. Wang's going to Europe seems not to be trusted."

"The twenty-eighth of February: Brother Gu Zhengding came to my lodging for luncheon and told in detail of his going to Hanoi to see Mr. Wang. By his account, Mr. Wang holds firmly to his advocacy of a peace settlement, and is especially indignant that the Central authorities have expelled him from the Party and revoked his offices; he holds that the Central authorities ought first to have discussed his proposals, and only if these were unanimously rejected and he still would not submit, then to punish him—only so would it be fair. He blames, too, his comrades within the Party for lacking confidence in him, neither knowing to support his proposals nor able to advance and retreat together with him. In truth Mr. Wang, not asking whether the procedure of his proposal was proper, blames only the Central authorities' punishment as unjust—and that is hard to win a comrade's sympathy. Moreover, of his advocacy of a peace settlement, save for a few men like Chen Gongbo and Zhou Fohai, simply no one knew before he left Chongqing; so how can he force men to believe?"

What Mr. "Yongwu" here records the writer holds to be credible; assessed by the methods of handling intelligence, it is equally sound and reliable. From another angle, too, it is more objective than the official record.

On the basis of the several facts set out above—both what we learned in Hanoi and what was let out on all sides at Chongqing—it is not hard to see that the situation at this juncture (from mid-January to the end of February of the twenty-eighth year) was this: the Central authorities greatly wished Wang to leave Hanoi early and go on to Europe, and to drop his talk of a peace settlement, staying "quietly in his place" for a spell first, so as not to breed further trouble and harm the prospects of the resistance. On Wang Jingwei's side, though in his heart he was minded to accept the counsel and leave for elsewhere, by word of mouth he still bit down hard and held fast to his advocacy of a peace settlement; but in his actual conduct he was caught in hesitation and wandering, with much that he could neither say nor make plain. And why was this? The two chief factors were: one from Japan, and one the whispering at the pillow.

On the fourth of January of the twenty-eighth year, Japan's Konoe cabinet collapsed, and the succeeding premier was Hiranuma Kiichirō, a man who was head of the so-called Kokusui (national-essence) faction, and had no ties with the peace advocates of Wang's clique; in an instant Wang lost his prop. And the Japanese "peace-makers" who had been going to and fro among Japan, Hong Kong, and Hanoi—men like Kagesa Sadaaki—since word from home had grown silent, could not but return to their country for instructions. And so Wang Jingwei, left dangling at Hanoi, had much the feeling of one abandoned. Some said Japan's new cabinet was busy adjusting its internal and external policies; others said this was a device of the Japanese for dealing with Wang, deliberately taking no notice of him—which might be likened to "beating down the price" in a trade, or described as "letting him loose the better to catch him."

Having finished this passage, the writer is again moved to a reflection of his own; and after some consideration, I think it better to say it, for if I do not, I believe no one else will be able to. For one thing, an outsider would have no way to begin; for another, there is truly nothing to be said in dispute.

To tell it is somewhat shaming. In the section of the second part of this book, in that passage on how Wang Jingwei broke away and fled from Chongqing, I stated plainly that Wang's flight was by no means connived at by the government authorities; it was truly the result of no precaution being taken. And the reason no precaution was taken was that his standing in Party and state was too senior, his station too illustrious, so that no one ever reckoned he had secretly, and long since, made a compact with the enemy. I said above that this was a great losing move in intelligence work, a "blind spot"—and not the Juntong alone; I believe every other unit charged with gathering intelligence was the same, including the "Institute of International Affairs" run by Wang Fansheng, which specialized in international intelligence.

What was not mentioned before, I ought to add here in a line or two, for I think it necessary. In the early days of the resistance at Chongqing, all recognized the importance of unity; only by being of one heart and one mind, and helping each other across the same stream, could the hard straits of the time be met. So there was simply no thought of "guarding against a thief," and least of all against a "thief within the house." What this came to in fact was that, though the furtive doings of Wang's clique were none too well concealed, the intelligence and security units simply paid no heed to their activities—one might say the authorities never issued any instruction of the kind—and so he left Chongqing without the least hindrance. To put it more plainly: had anyone at the time watched his movements, none of the later trouble would have come to pass.

To speak again of the short period after we were assigned by Mr. Dai Yunong to work in Hanoi: though we were ordered to watch Wang's movements and to gather intelligence on the doings of his clique, we did neither of these two things thoroughly. Apart from what I have already said, the most laughable thing of all is that we did not even know who the men were that Chongqing had sent to negotiate with Wang; that among them was Mr. Gu Zhengding and the rest, I learned only from other materials. More absurd still: the men from Chongqing could find where Wang had put up, and meet and talk with him directly, whereas we, charged with a secret mission and ordered to watch him, did not know where he was—truly the greatest joke under heaven. Were the writer not to say it, who would believe it!

There is one more point which the writer did not understand in his early years, and only now, at this late day, comes to grasp a little. Someone asks: why, on the one hand, send men to watch him, and on the other send men to persuade him, while these two sides were kept apart, with no connection whatever between them? This is simple enough. Watching him belonged to intelligence work—plainly put, to secret-service work; persuading him may be reckoned political maneuver, and falls within what would now be called "political warfare." Each had its own charge, and it was for the higher command to hold the reins and bring all to a head. To explain further, there were within this gradations of high and low, open and hidden: those who did secret-service work were the base layer, the lower layer, the hidden; those engaged in political maneuver were of a higher grade, to be called the middle-and-upper layer, the open; and above these was the high layer that issued the orders.

Taking the matter as it stood, some affairs of that age were not yet reduced to system; the story I have just told comes near to each side going its own way. Still, Wang Jingwei's case had no precedent in history, and just how it ought to be handled, I believe no one had a settled notion; so it waited upon the turn of events. Seen thus, intelligence work showed itself all the more important.

The writer will say one word of hindsight. Had our intelligence at the time been able to grasp deeply that Wang was caught in the plight of one who can neither advance nor retreat, and had the various working units of differing character coordinated their measures aptly, the handling of the Wang affair might well, in the end, have come to a different result. But in fact, by the writer's reckoning, by the time Chongqing sent men to Hanoi to work upon Wang Jingwei, it was still not fully understood that Wang had already made a concrete compact with Japan, and had also made his decision to cast the nation's interest aside.

### (3) Lacking Inside Intelligence That Reached All the Way In

After making contact with Mr. Xu, the special personage stationed at Hanoi, the situation improved greatly. What we had wished to know before and had no way of knowing, we could now find a road to; one may say that, where the willows had darkened and no path seemed left, a way opened among the flowers, and out of a clueless muddle we found a thread.

Mr. Xu had originally not the least entanglement with the so-called Wang faction or the "Reorganizationists" and the like; and at present he had no private relation whatever with Wang's circle remaining in Hanoi. His being able to gather material on the doings of the Wang clique came, most reliably, from a "third party." By "third party" here is meant a subtle relation neither drawn into Wang's side nor belonging to ours; and this "third party" came to know the Wang clique's movements in Hanoi entirely on the strength of its administrative authority over the territory—that is, the doings of the Wang clique in Hanoi had by the rules to be reported to the authorities in charge here. Thus it was able to grasp Wang's comings and goings in Hanoi, and the activities of certain persons who had had contact with Wang, these being of two differing standings: those residing locally and those entering from abroad. All this was on the surface, tangible; so in intelligence parlance we call it "material on movements." As to what Wang and the men he met discussed, they had no idea; this kind of content, which cannot be grasped and yet urgently needs to be, we call "material on the static state."

Within the local administration and police command, who it was that let the news out to Mr. Xu—whether one man or two, or several; whether out of personal friendship or greed for reward—I did not intend to ask Mr. Xu, so as not to put him in a hard place, and the more so because Mr. Xu was not a working man in any subordinate relation. Still, by our operating procedure, this had to be made clear; and so I had to find a way to verify it from other angles, and only then could I judge on that basis how sound the material obtained was.

Though Mr. Xu supplied us material without cease, he never once wrote a single word in his own hand—perhaps for fear of leaving a trace, which might be inconvenient for him. Whenever he passed on news, the ordinary sort he had that Mr. Zeng relay; the more important, Mr. Xu would surely arrange to meet me and tell me by word of mouth. In fact, what Mr. Xu thought important seemed to me of no great value for reference or use; and what he took for quite ordinary was at times just what suited our need. From this it may be observed that Mr. Xu, though no utter layman in how to gather and handle intelligence, was still separated from it by a thin dividing line.

Next I should speak of Wei Chunfeng. Do not look down on his tender years, just stepped into society; at the work he was a sharp little imp, and had above all an uncommon interest in what we were doing—which was, of course, not mere curiosity. At times he would sit to one side listening to us chat and tell tales, and listen for half a day, and listen most raptly, as if he could never have his fill.

Did I not say before that he had an Annamese sweetheart? This Miss Nguyễn had a first cousin on the paternal side who served in the police as a plainclothes detective—a secret agent, one might say. What his rank was I do not know; at all events his station was not high. This man was of course surnamed Nguyễn; what his given name was, Wei Chunfeng told me, but I have forgotten, and there is no need now to make one up to fill the blank. As it happened, he was posted to patrol and guard the neighborhood of Wang's residence; by our judgment, this detail was very likely set up for Wang alone. There were three of them in all, taking turns on shift. Was this band sent because Wang had asked for protection? Or hired at his own cost, a guard posted for safety's sake? Or dispatched by the Hanoi administration, under the name of protection, on purpose to watch him? For the time we could not make it out. But however that might be, this man would be of great use to our work.

In view of this state of things, what step we would take—even if the writer does not write it out, I believe the reader will lay the plan for us; and sure enough, that is what we did. Anyone can grasp that, in this time and place, to win over a foreigner by "persuasion" was of course not easy, and that the most effective method was nothing other than "buying"—and if one added a little touch of the ties of kin, it would add several parts of trust and many conveniences. These two words, "buying," ill though they sound, are just the fact of it; and I still hope that Miss Nguyễn, who may perhaps yet be in the world, will pardon me.

Miss Nguyễn and Nguyễn So-and-so had the same grandfather; and though their parents had long since set up separate households, they were after all one family, so that the cousins were in the habit of visiting each other. It was through Wei Chunfeng's relation with Miss Nguyễn that we made contact with him; and at times Miss Nguyễn too could run an errand or two for us.

Having Nguyễn So-and-so, we were not content, and I talked it over with Wei Chunfeng: best of all would be to "buy" the head of this band—the detective sergeant or inspector—and the effect would be greater still. Wei Chunfeng promised to work at it, but nothing ever came of it; the crux was that Nguyễn So-and-so was unwilling to reach too far, fearing that if it went wrong he might smash his rice-bowl.

The above is the working connection we developed on the ground. This connection, though not ideal, was—to say it the other way—such that, without Mr. Xu's taking part, and relying on ourselves alone to grope, we could not have built up even this much. For instance, the most zealous Mr. Yu Lexing, on the strength of his many years in France and his fairly fluent French, and his acquaintance with not a few Frenchmen, busied himself for a good while and yet could not find a single familiar face, let alone build a working connection. One sees that some things are not so light and easy as saying them or singing them.

Broadly speaking, of the working connections we opened up in Hanoi, the upper level depended wholly on Mr. Xu, and the lower on this one line that Wei Chunfeng had opened; and on the strength of just these connections we were carrying out the task our superiors had laid upon us. Plainly this was nowhere near enough. If a strict and practical demand be made, what we felt most sorely the lack of was still "inside intelligence."

Save for events big enough to be truly striking—which, being deeply impressed, one can still remember—or for those with a record to consult, the ordinary run of things I have long forgotten as to the day and hour. Thus, for example, on just what day it was that we made sure Wang was living on Gao Street; or on just what day and hour we first found the traces of Wang Jingwei and Chen Bijun—of these I can truly remember only the rough outline. What follows is the preliminary grasp our Hanoi work gained of Wang, at a time around mid-February to early March of the twenty-eighth year; even if there be error, it will not be far wide.

First I must speak of Wang's address. After Wang and his party reached Hanoi, they moved their lodging several times; the earlier ones there is no way to verify, and it is of no importance, so I pass it by. His present address was No. 27 Gao Lang Street (in transliteration). The writer went in person to observe it, and many times—the pity being only that this was limited to observing from outside; I never went in. It was a Western-style house, three stories high, one side standing free and one side joined to the next building, its front facing the street, and behind it a small courtyard walled about with a low wall, with a back gate and a side gate; to go in or out by the rear, one had to pass two gates. Even the side that faced the sky, the rooftop, was looked over—though the writer had not the skill for that, and it fell to another, of which more below. As to the underground side, whether there was any cellar or the like, at the time I did not take note; perhaps this was one gap in our work.

The front gate faced the street—there was a main door, but it was not large, and stood shut from morning to night. The writer and Luqiao drove past the door many times, yet very seldom saw anyone go in or out. The gate did not set deep, and from the street one could clearly see the windows; had they not been screened by curtains, one might perhaps have seen the scene within.

At this point let me put in a piece of the ludicrous. The writer has read the book by Jin Xiongbai (pen name Zhu Zijia), The Opening and Closing of the Wang Regime; and in the fifth volume, on page forty-one, there is a passage that says: "They even rented a Western-style house beside the Wang residence, and daily from an upper floor watched the goings-on within it from afar." And again on page forty-seven he writes: "Next, outside the window of Zhongming's bedroom was a stretch of garden, and across the garden a house opposite, which is believed to have been rented early by the ruffians. For in this bedroom there was a round table, at which Wang would by day often talk with Zhou Fohai, Gao Zongwu, and the rest; and the ruffians, spying with binoculars, witnessed all…." Now, forty years having passed, the thing is long over and gone, and I have not the least wish to quibble; but I think this account may be a guess of the Wang household, or else an imagining of Mr. Jin's. In fact we never rented any house near Wang's residence, nor ever used binoculars—not that we would not, but that we had not so much as a pair of binoculars ready. These little trifling details are nothing much in themselves, and do not form the focus of the main content of the Wang case; still, if one is to be exact about it, best say what was so and only what was so, and not spin out fancies.

To speak further of the setting of Wang's residence: Gao Lang Street was not far from the busy quarter, but was very quiet and secluded, belonging to a high-class residential district. Before No. 27 was a stretch of lawn, lying between the footway and the carriageway, fit for children to play on or for a passer-by to rest a while—though ordinarily one rarely saw anyone resting or playing on it. The street was broad, and along it was planted a row of palms, with here and there a few tall royal palms towering up; so that looking across from the far side of the street, the house was screened away and nothing could be clearly seen. To left and right the neighbors were mostly foreigners, and of course Frenchmen chiefly. Nearly every house had its own motorcar, so that on each house's lower story, front or back, there was a garage. If from a road crossing Gao Lang Street one went into a narrow little lane, one could also come round to the back gate of No. 27; and looking up, each story had its windows, but on the bare wall there was not a single place to get a grip and climb. Following the lane on, it turned out that the winding path led to a hidden spot, twisting east and turning west, until it came out onto quite another street.

By a report relayed through Wei Chunfeng: the result of many days' observation was the discovery that a good many people lived in this house—men and women, old and young—but that, apart from Wang and his wife, one could not tell apart who were servants, who kinfolk, and who guests. On this point it was too vexing: from first to last we never made clear just who was who. This too was a weak point where our reconnaissance failed to go deep.

By a report from the same source: the one who went in and out of the Wang residence most often was Zeng Zhongming. Every day he came and went, at times as many as four or five times. By the common account, Zeng Zhongming lived on the third floor of this house; but by the result of our reconnaissance, he did not live here—he had taken a long-term room at the "Continental Hotel" on Paul Bert, the better to deal with the outside world on Wang's behalf. Anyone wishing to see Wang had first to arrange it with Zeng Zhongming, and Zeng Zhongming would then set a time for the meeting with Wang. Of course some, perhaps, could see only Zeng Zhongming, or were turned away and could not see Wang Jingwei at all. In other words, Zeng Zhongming's living alone at the "Continental Hotel" was Wang's one feeler put out to the world.

Besides these, the frequent visitors to the residence included Zhou Fohai, Gao Zongwu, and the Japanese Kagesa Sadaaki, and others; but the local detective who gave us intelligence, Nguyễn So-and-so, did not know these men's names or what they did, so that on receiving this report we knew, at most, the that of it and not the why. Over on Mr. Xu's side, though there was further material, we were vexed for lack of a third line to verify it by, and in the end still had only half-knowledge, with no way to make certain. There was yet another difficulty: the writer, for instance, knew only Zhou Fohai by sight, yet never once saw him appear here with my own eyes; as for Gao Zongwu, I simply did not know what he looked like, and even had I met him face to face, I should not have known who he was. Many little links of just this kind were among the basic factors that made our work fall short of the solid.

In sum, the "Hanoi work" under my direction, our subjective conditions being insufficient, held many inborn difficulties past overcoming, so that progress was slow and grew ever more thorny.

Just in this period, not yet fully abreast of the situation, Mr. Dai time and again (no—here I should rather use "with strict and repeated order") demanded that we make clear with all speed the manner of Wang Jingwei's collusion with the Japanese side, and the content of what was discussed, and report it soundly. This was too hard—but could I say "It cannot be done"? "One must find a way where there is no way": at first glance this sentence seems most illogical, but in truth it is one in meaning with there being no word "difficult" in Napoleon's dictionary—and this is just the special quality of our work. It is not to boast that we are all-powerful, but only to make plain the existence of the force of spirit.

For the present, our one road was to lean more and more on Mr. Xu. As already stated, Mr. Xu himself had no relation with the Wang clique, and he could only go once more to "trouble" his French friends. As one may well imagine, the news Mr. Xu brought back was neither concrete nor to the point, and could of course not satisfy our superiors' demand. To say nothing of any secret content—even a single Japanese man's name cost us no little effort, having to be turned and corrected through several languages before it could be rendered out; and how forced that was.

By what Mr. Xu inquired out from his French friends, it was said there were two officers representing the Japanese military lingering in Hanoi; it was known only that of these two officers one held the rank of colonel and one of lieutenant colonel, but their names could not be traced. It was only some years afterward, from a mass of materials, that we found these two officers might be Colonel Kagesa Sadaaki (his rank at the time) and Lieutenant Colonel Yagi.

Among those who had contact with Zeng Zhongming but did not necessarily meet Wang Jingwei were the Japanese consul-general in Hanoi, Suzuki, and the Hanoi correspondent of Japan's Dōmei agency, Ōya Kusuo, and others. And this Ōya may very well have had the double identity of newspaper correspondent and intelligence agent. There were not a few others coming and going, but all, alas, with head and no tail, incomplete; intelligence of this kind is perhaps worth even less than the news in a paper—what use is it?

It was not that we did not understand, still less that we would not put our minds and lay our plans to it; all of us conferred and discussed back and forth, and did what could be done, and tried what could be tried; but there was simply no way to plant an "inside line" at Wang's side.

### (4) More Comrades Arrive in Batches, Bearing Arms

Mr. Dai gave notice by telegram: "Shall come to Hanoi within a day or two for a meeting," and bade us arrange a place for him to stay, designating Brother Fang Bingxi to go and meet him. This time, as ever in his old manner, he by no means said why he was coming. Brother Bingxi met the plane on time, and waited until all the passengers had gone, yet saw no sign of Mr. Dai; he must, we thought, not be coming after all. By afternoon a telegram arrived, informing us the date was changed, and that when he would set out would be told in a further wire.

I greatly hoped Mr. Dai might come for a trip, stay a day or two, and then return; some of our difficulties could then be explained to his face and a way to solve them sought. Yet I also did not wish him to tarry too long, for that too would sit ill with me.

With telegrams thus going to and fro, and much bustle to no purpose over many days, in the end he could not get away and did not come. I cannot recall in which book or magazine there are these two lines: "From the end of February to the beginning of March in the twenty-eighth year, one head of the secret service, Dai Li, even went in person to Hanoi to make dispositions." In fact Mr. Dai, together with the writer and Luqiao, came from Hong Kong to Hanoi in January; he stayed two days and then returned to Chongqing. Thereafter, though he several times said he would come, each time he could not get free, and right up to the close of the Wang case I never again saw him in Hanoi. Thinking it over carefully, perhaps he did come again; and if he really did, then why was I not told? There are of course many possible reasons—and the more so as his movements were ever hard to fathom.

There are those, too, who ascribe it to Mr. Zheng Jiemin. Some years ago (about the sixty-eighth year), in the supplement of the United Daily News, there ran a long serialized historical novel, Painted Faces of a Spring and Autumn, a work by Mr. Gao Yang; on its very first day it said the Hanoi Wang case had been directed by Zheng Jiemin in person. And in Jin Xiongbai's The Opening and Closing of the Wang Regime there is likewise a line saying: "By news put out from Taiwan, the one who actually directed the assassination was Zheng Jiemin, leading Wang Luqiao to do it." These are of course all hearsay, and do not accord with the facts.

By what the writer understands, whether Mr. Zheng Jiemin ever went to Hanoi during the resistance I dare not say for certain; and if he did, it was for some other work. As to the sanction of Wang Jingwei, he never came even once, still less directed it on the spot. Whether he took part in Chongqing in the planning of the Hanoi work is hard to say; but reckoning by Mr. Dai's character, he would probably not have consulted Mr. Zheng Jiemin either. That Mr. Zheng Jiemin never directed the Hanoi work—of this the writer's memory is perfectly clear, and there can be no mistake.

Now, to speak again of how the Hanoi work went forward:

The working comrades already in Hanoi were, besides the writer, four men—Fang Bingxi, Wang Luqiao, Cen Jiazhuo, and Yu Lexing—and one other, a communications-and-cipher man; we were six in all. Then several batches more were sent in succession. The first batch to reach Hanoi were three men: Yu Jiansheng, Zhang Fengyi, and Tang Yingjie.

Yu Jiansheng, of the Hangzhou Police Academy, was perhaps a schoolmate of Wang Luqiao's; later the two became brothers-in-law. I had not known him before, but a short while after we met he gave me the best of impressions. He was of the inward sort, silent and sparing of speech, mild in temper, and of excellent bearing in his dealings with men, very easy to get on with; if a small group has such a man in it, he is best able to work a reconciling effect. He seldom offered an opinion, but once he took a stand he was fairly firm, and would not lightly change. I much like a man of this kind, and I especially respected his views.

Zhang Fengyi, a Shandong man of the seventh class of the Military Academy, has already appeared in Rooting Out Traitors in the North. When I was chief of the Beiping Station, he was a member of the Beiping Station Action Group; in that scene of my "desertion of post," he was transferred to the capital for training, reassigned to other work, and this time was drawn from a certain unit at Chongqing. I knew him all too well: brave and full of grit, courage all through him, better with a gun than with a pen; go along with him and all is well, but rouse him and it is hard to set right, for he has a stubborn streak and takes a very long while to cool.

Tang Yingjie, also called Zhang Xiaoliang, of Sichuan, may be counted a man of legend, though he lacked all grandeur. When I was chief of the Tianjin Station, he was once sent to Tianjin to help with action work, but not only made no showing—he stirred up no few laughingstocks. This time, as he came, I was truly a little wary; then again I thought, since he has come, I can only hope he will behave himself a bit, not slip up, and work in earnest. The writer introduced him in Rooting Out Traitors in the North; here let me sketch him again briefly: he was short of stature and unprepossessing, and by his looks would simply not do for polite company; yet one must not judge a man by his looks, for he had several real skills, and if his skills were turned to reconnaissance or action work, they would of course yield a doubled result for half the effort. So I still made honest and good use of him.

That our superiors sent these three men gave no instruction beforehand, and not one of them carried any proof of identity; even so, that they could come from afar, not shrinking a thousand miles, to a foreign land, and find me without the least difficulty—on this alone it was enough, and no token or credential was needed. But what had our superiors sent them to do? Could it be that force was again to be used?

Close on their heels came two more together, Chen Bangguo and Chen Buyun. Not only I, but Wang Luqiao and Yu Jiansheng too, knew none of them; after they introduced themselves and we had talked several times, we learned: Chen Bangguo, an Anhui man, seemingly risen from the ranks, though he did not say so himself. Big and tall of frame, gruff of voice and rough of grain—do not think, from his somewhat foolish look, that there was not in him a rock-steady composure past all shaking. He was skilled with a gun; it was said he could fire in bursts and single shots, enough to "shoot a flower"; and though I never saw it with my own eyes, I believe he must have been a master of the art.

Chen Buyun, of Xuzhou in Jiangsu, of what origins I do not know. This man was solid and sturdy, only he did not care to talk; at times when one greeted him, or drew him out to open his mouth, mostly he answered with no more than a smile. We had little contact, and were together but a short while, nor did I very carefully seek to understand him.

With so many coming one after another, Brother Bingxi was run off his feet; to arrange lodgings for each was, for the moment, beyond the funds to pay for. After we talked it over, I decided to put them all up at my own dwelling; and so we were seven in all, living together under one roof. These seven were: Wang Luqiao, Yu Jiansheng, Zhang Fengyi, Tang Yingjie, Chen Bangguo, Chen Buyun, and myself. Luqiao was designated to lead them and Jiansheng to assist.

So many grown men living together were bound, in their comings and goings, to catch the eye; happily the Hanoi public-security authorities of that time paid no heed to this, and we could take it calmly.

In the morning, as we sat together at our food, I ran a cold eye over the array, and was suddenly struck: look at them how one might, they were all men for "action"!

A day or two on, a certain Mr. Cao, through Fang Bingxi's liaison, wished to meet me, saying there was a secret matter of moment, and hoping I could come to where he lodged. I asked Brother Bingxi what it was about. Brother Bingxi said, "Mr. Dai sent him." I asked again, "Any instruction?" Brother Bingxi spread both hands and shook his head. Puzzled, I asked again, "Anything for proof?" Brother Bingxi said, "That is what he says; once you meet, you will of course understand." Since it was so, I drove there myself, asking Bingxi to go along with me to see him.

This matter was rather out of the way in the Hanoi work, and this Mr. Cao is also one of the participants now still living in the world. It is of course worth saying a few words more:

Cao Shi'ang, of Yiyang in Hunan, born in the first year of the Republic, a graduate of the French Special School of Military Aviation, had served as an instructor of the Thirty-fifth Squadron of the French Air Force and as a member of the Grand-Est Southern Aviation Committee of France, and other posts. On returning to China he had served as chief of the aviation section of the First Department of the General Staff, and during the resistance was liaison officer (or special commissioner) of the French Volunteer Air Squadron, flying French-made Dewoitine pursuit planes against the Japanese forces. This Volunteer Squadron was of the same character as the Fourteenth Air Force of the American General Chennault.

His coming to Hanoi this time was at Mr. Dai's invitation, sought out with the utmost pains as though seeking a treasure; for the working circumstance and character of the moment, he was too useful.

Brother Shi'ang, together with his French wife, had reached Hanoi from Kunming a few days before by a Eurasia Aviation plane. He drew out for me to see a great heap of identity papers, colorful and mixed Chinese and Western; I could not read them, but he was indeed truly named "Cao Shi'ang."

First he presented his wife: of medium height, plainly dressed, generous and dignified in bearing; though only twenty-odd years of age, she seemed fairly matured, and carried not a drop of Western hauteur, so that one found her very easy. She could speak a few simple Chinese words, only just enough to be understood; where it would not carry, Brother Shi'ang served for the moment as interpreter, and so the courtesies were got through. I was eager to talk with Shi'ang, but his wife showed no sign of withdrawing, and for a moment things were at a stand. Later Brother Shi'ang caught my meaning, and indicated I might say all I wished, for she knew everything—indeed she had promised to help.

On the eve of Brother Shi'ang's setting out, Mr. Dai gave a banquet to see him off; and at it the verbal instruction given him was this: "First make contact with Comrade Fang Bingxi, and then have Fang arrange for the man in charge, Brother Chen So-and-so, to meet you; thereafter, as to working matters, you may consult among yourselves. If you have views, best send a telegram—they will surely pass it on to me; and if there is need, please raise it at any time." And as they parted with a handshake when the banquet was over, Mr. Dai, holding his hand, charged him: "There is a package—inside are two pistols and a box of cartridges—please carry it to Hanoi and hand it in person to Brother Chen So-and-so; there must not be the least slip."

I asked, "Has Mr. Dai any other instruction?" Brother Shi'ang was taken aback for a moment, made as if to speak and stopped, then said, "Nothing more." Thereupon Brother Shi'ang had his wife take from a drawer a leather document case, set it on the table, and, pointing at it, said to me: "You surely know this is French territory; and to guard against the resistance movement of the Annamese people, they sentence anyone possessing or using arms, whoever he be, to heavy punishment. She and I, for this package, have been on tenterhooks the whole way; today, at last, I can discharge my errand." So saying, he opened the case to show me: two American-made revolvers, engraved above with the English letters SW, that is, what is commonly called a "Smith & Wesson"; by another term it is also called "armed peace"—a pistol of short range but great killing power. There was besides a small box of cartridges; hefting it, it had weight, but I did not tear off the wrapping to open it. Voicing my thanks again and again, I made to set it in the car and carry it back myself.

Afterward we grew very close, and got on very well; and having found him bold and open-hearted, I was much minded to make a friend of him—the pity being only that our time together was truly too short. I could sense he had one thing hidden from me, and that was his secret mission; had it not been that our superiors strictly forbade its disclosure, he would have told me long since.

The next day, receiving notice, I met also a Mr. Tan Tianqian (the character qian is properly pronounced qiàn, but he called himself Tianzhǎn), a man of the typical melancholy cast; the first impression he gave was as of one come with a bellyful of grievance and wrong. Once we had talked a little, I understood he had indeed just been released from Xifeng county in Guizhou—outsiders do not know that this place holds a certain destination, the very spot where our own working comrades go to "observe the law." Why he had come to be locked up there, of course I could not well ask.

If I remember rightly, Brother Tianqian too was a Hunan man, older than any of us, a returned student from France who had studied finance and economics; whether he held a degree I did not note. By his own account, after returning to China he had been an instructor at a certain training unit, and his coming this time was naturally in hope of atoning for his fault by merit and redeeming his past offense. Then why was he still knit-browed and long-faced the day through? There must be some hidden trouble hard to speak of.

Brother Tianqian had his own dwelling; meeting him there, I saw women's clothes and hats in his room, so he too, I supposed, had brought a companion; yet he never once had her come out to be introduced. Later I heard that this companion of his was a French girl, with no formal marriage between them—and there was some story in it besides, whether tied to the work or not I could not at the time make out. His working identity was also not quite the same as the other comrades'; our superiors' instruction was: "Keep in close touch, relay letters and telegrams for him; if there be any need of intelligence, you may ask him to handle it." I thought it would not be so simple; from the frequency of his telegrams to and fro, one could judge he must have some other important mission.

At the time, my working relation with Brother Tianqian was limited to this. Of his whereabouts over the forty-odd years after, I knew nothing—until, unexpectedly, in early March of the seventy-first year, I had news of him: he had come to a tragic end, and to a fate forever a riddle. Of this matter, the telling is long; when the Hanoi work has come to a point of rest, I shall write it.

Did I not mention twice before that, after Mr. Dai and the writer and Luqiao came from Hong Kong to Hanoi, he stayed only two days and returned to Chongqing? Thereafter I never again saw him in Hanoi; though he several times said he would come, each time he could not. Was it a hundred percent certain he never came? Had I not lately had the chance to meet Brother Cao Shi'ang once more, I should answer with certainty, "Absolutely not"; but now I dare not say so. Talking over old times with Brother Shi'ang, he said he had seen Mr. Dai in Hanoi. I asked him when; Brother Shi'ang said it was not long after his own arrival at Hanoi, though of course he could not recall the day. And not by memory alone: he offered two facts in proof, saying:

"Mr. Dai, in Hanoi, met Huang Qiang (Mujing), director of the Haiphong office of the Southwest Transport Administration, and warned him to his face that on no account was he to let it out."

"Mr. Dai went to Tan Tianqian's dwelling, saw women's shoes under the bed, and noticed the handle on the wardrobe was a little loose; without the least mercy he ordered Tan Tianqian to his face to bring out the woman hidden in the wardrobe, and there and then left him with nowhere to hide his face."

Hearing Brother Shi'ang tell it thus, with proof and evidence, was it not then made true? The friends of those days are all old and withered away, and few knew of this matter; so where am I to find counter-proof!

If Mr. Dai did indeed come to Hanoi, did he meet Brother Fang Bingxi? Did he meet Mr. Xu? These are all riddles; and most baffling of all, why was I not told? Could it be there was some yet more important mission? To these questions there will never be an answer.

Whether or not Mr. Dai came again, the Hanoi work went on in full vigor, and had reached the stage of "gongs and drums beating close." Within the past week or so, besides the five comrades Yu Jiansheng, Zhang Fengyi, Tang Yingjie, Chen Bangguo, and Chen Buyun arriving in batches, the two comrades Cao Shi'ang and Tan Tianqian had also come one after the other; not only were hands more plentiful, but Brother Shi'ang had brought two revolvers as well. Close on this, our superiors wired to tell us to go to a certain place at Haiphong and take delivery of three Browning pistols.

Haiphong is a port, a good few dozen minutes' drive from Hanoi; and of us seven not one was familiar with the way. This was not a thing to take lightly. Consult Wei Chunfeng? I feared he could not hold steady. There was nothing for it but to seek Mr. Zeng's counsel; and Mr. Zeng did not agree at once, saying he must ask whether it could be done. After a day's wait he answered us: he would send two men to go along with him.

The other party was a Miss Ji—this surname is not common, so I remember it well. She had, at another's charge, brought three new Browning pistols from Hong Kong, with twenty rounds attached, to hand to the man who came; her task then was done, and of all else she knew nothing. In fact she too was one of our working people, and thereupon, like a divine dragon that shows its head but not its tail, appeared no more.

And so the Hanoi work stepped into another stage—not only surveillance and reconnaissance, but the preparation before action. (End of this chapter.)

In Wang Jingwei's life, the revolutionary history of his earlier years was radiant and much praised, and for a time he became an idol worshipped by his countrymen. From middle age on—though by rights he too was one who professed the Three Principles of the People—he swayed left and right, his direction never held firm; in many of his acts he erred through impulse of feeling and willfulness, forever taking offense and giving way to temper, until it came to the point of caring only for persons and not for right and wrong. The one thing worth saying for him is that, luckily, he did not tread again the old rut of fawning on the Communists. Yet the pity is that during the resistance his will wavered anew, and in the face of danger he turned traitor; so that when the coffin was closed and the verdict passed, all held his late years flawed and judged him a criminal against the state, and in the end he became a failure of history. Some, for his surpassing talent, could not but lament that he had destroyed his own earlier revolutionary record; and of course there were more who, for his perverse conduct and his betrayal of Party and state, joined in the general chastisement. But say what one will, the great error was cast; right and wrong, merit and fault, lie plain in men's hearts—it is as the line has it, "Iron cast over the Nine Provinces makes a true blunder; from the annals there is no overturning the old game of chess." Even should the historians of after ages ransack the official books and old archives of some thousands of years, and weigh and appraise him anew, it would be hard to excuse him.

Yet, if one may be allowed to speak a fair word in charity, tracing his heart and forgiving his traces: his playing the tiger's cat's-paw, his colluding with the enemy and betraying the country, arose perhaps not from his first intent; it was only that, weak of will and dim of sight, and moreover overturned by the lust to lead, and having miscalculated both the enemy and himself, he betrayed Party and country, sinking deeper and deeper, unable to pull himself free. And looking back, from the facts, over all his acts and their effects, it is indeed hard for him to win sympathy.

This essay, then, taking Wang's family origin, his native temperament, his personal experience, and his states of mind before and during the resistance, makes a detailed analysis from various angles, that one may find out the chains of cause and effect within, and the tracks and traces of how the facts developed, as a reference for the reader in coming to a further understanding of this event of history.

### (1) Wang Jingwei and Chen Bijun: A Match Made for Each Other

Making use of this idle interval, while awaiting the order of sanction from above—and it falls, too, just at the point where "The Full Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi" is written half through—it is a fitting moment to introduce the leading figure of the book.

The older generation, men over fifty, may have some grasp of him, and most know who Wang Jingwei was; but the young generation, and especially the little friends born in Taiwan in the forties, unless they are often in touch with historical and literary materials, will very likely scarcely know who Wang Jingwei was, or who was Wang Jingwei.

Mr. Wang was one of China's famous political leaders. He was once elected Chairman of the National Government and Chairman of the Chinese Nationalist Party, and had served as President of the Executive Yuan; in the Northern Expedition and the bandit-suppression periods he stood in the leading rank of Party and state. In the early resistance he held the posts of Vice-Director-General of the Chinese Nationalist Party, chairman of the Central Political Council, and speaker of the People's Political Council. One sees how weighty and illustrious were his standing and his repute.

In the twenty-seventh year of the Republic, at the very fiercest of our country's war of resistance against Japan, he actually broke away from the wartime capital Chongqing and fled to Hanoi, answering the Three Principles of the Japanese premier Konoe Fumimaro, publishing the "Yan Telegram," and advocating peace. This was truly a startling act, and the whole nation seethed.

Wang's life could fill a book of great bulk; here I can give only a brief introduction:

Wang Jingwei's ancestral home was Wuyuan in Anhui; afterward the family moved to Shanyin in Zhejiang, dwelling there some generations, so that his registered native place was Shaoxing. Wang's father was named Shu, styled Shengzhai, a scholar who had failed of the examinations. Shaoxing is a place famed for producing "yamen secretaries"; and this Mr. Shengzhai, having no share in examination honors, turned instead to the secretarial trade, becoming versed in the affairs of penal cases and taxation. Later, having long practiced as a secretary in Guangdong, he registered his residence at Panyu. His first wife, née Lu, bore one son and three daughters; the son, named Zhaoyong (a Guangxu-era provincial graduate, who left behind such works as the Record of Painters of Lingnan), was Wang's eldest brother. Wang was born of a concubine, his own mother, née Wu, being a true native of Guangdong, who bore the sons Zhaoxuan and Zhaojun; Wang was the youngest, named Zhaoming. Being lowest in the birth order, he was styled Jixin (his intimates later mostly called him Master Ji), with a further style of Guixin. "Jingwei" was the pen name he used when writing articles for the Min Bao, founded by the Tongmenghui at Tokyo in the seventh year before the Republic (A.D. 1905). Wang was born on the twenty-eighth of March in the ninth year of Guangxu of the Qing (1883), at the county office of Sanshui in Guangdong, his father being then already sixty-three, and his mother thirty years younger than his father.

Wang's father Shengzhai, serving as a secretary in the county yamen, with many children and a life of straitened poverty, still kept the traditional notion of "a household of letters through the generations"; from Wang's fifth year he sent him to the family school, and of an evening would find time to oversee his lessons in person.

But good times did not last—as though it were fated that Wang's early years should be forlorn and bereft. At thirteen he lost his mother, at fourteen his father, and went on to be taught by his elder half-brother Zhaoyong. Zhaoyong, it is said, was cold by nature and exceedingly strict in his charge, never softening his words, so that Wang had truly the feeling of being faulted at every turn. Yet Wang's studies profited not a little thereby, and from this was laid the foundation of his learning. At the time, in Wang's own mind, though he knew his elder brother treated him so harshly because their parents were both dead—purely out of an elder brother's painstaking care to shield a younger, and not from any deliberate slight—yet, however meek his outward bearing toward his elder brother, as toward a stern father, in his heart he in fact bore a deep resentment, and could do nothing about it. That Wang came later to be outwardly mild and inwardly narrow, with an especially strong bent to revenge, bent single-mindedly on striving upward and outshining others, and that his contradictory character—two-faced and forever changeable—took shape, all had their root in the awkward circumstances of his early years.

Because his family was poor, at seventeen he began teaching to keep himself, and at the same time practiced calligraphy under his sister's husband, Yuan Yinbai. At nineteen he sat the examinations, placing first in both the county and the prefectural tests. By one account, in that seventeenth year of his he passed at the head in the counties of Panyu and Nanhai of Guangzhou prefecture, where the literary tide ran especially strong and the candidates numbered over ten thousand; that a young pupil of but seventeen should stand at the top of the list was indeed no small feat. This too proves his native quickness, and that his elder brother Zhaoyong's strict charge had not been spent in vain.

Before long his cousin Wang Zhaoxin recommended him as household tutor in the home of the Naval Commander Li Zhun, at a monthly stipend of sixteen taels of silver. Now a licentiate serving as tutor by the common rule of the time got only eight taels, and a provincial graduate sixteen; because Wang had placed first, the Li family made an exception and gave him sixteen, on the footing of a provincial graduate.

Wang's cousin Zhaoxin had meant to encourage him to go on and sit the provincial examination, so that—since his elder brother Zhaoyong was a provincial graduate—if Zhaoming too could pass, it would not only bring glory to the family but let him draw level with his elder brother Zhaoyong and win back a measure of pride. But Wang's own ambition did not lie there; he took no pride in provincial rank, still less wished to vie with his elder brother for a day's advantage. He was resolved to take in new knowledge and widen his view, and determined to go far off to Japan to study.

At twenty-one (the twenty-ninth year of Guangxu, that is, 1903) he passed at last as a government-funded student in the accelerated course of the Japanese Hōsei University, and crossed the sea to study. A year and a half later he graduated, and went on as a self-funded student into the specialist course of Hōsei University for deeper study. In this period his means of support came wholly from what he earned by translating books; so that his resolve and his diligent hardship were indeed things much to be praised.

In 1905 the Father of the Nation came to Tokyo to join with Huang Xing and founded the Tongmenghui. Wang, together with Zhu Zhixin (husband of Wang's eldest sister, then a fellow student at Hōsei), went to call on the Father of the Nation, and thereupon joined the Tongmenghui as a member. Before this, Wang had made a special trip to Yokohama to see the Royalist leader Liang Qichao, but did not find him in; only then did he turn instead to the Tongmenghui. One may say this was the greatest good fortune of his life, and from this his political career unfolded.

On the thirtieth of July that year, the Tongmenghui held its preparatory meeting at Tokyo, and Wang was charged with drafting its regulations. On the twentieth of August the Tongmenghui held its inaugural assembly, and by common acclaim the Father of the Nation was made Director, and Wang a councillor. At the same time, to strengthen revolutionary propaganda and to counter the Royalists' organ, the Xinmin Congbao, the Min Bao was specially founded, engaging Zhang Taiyan (Binglin) as editor-in-chief, with Wang among its writers. The pen name "Jingwei" began to be used at just this time.

Zhang Taiyan was a master of textual scholarship famed throughout the land, richest in national feeling, strict in the "distinction of Chinese and barbarian," urging with all his force "revolution and the expulsion of the Manchus" and the restoration of China; he had ever held in servile contempt the Royalists of Kang and Liang. In that day his learning and his utterances had great influence over patriots at home and abroad, and especially the educated. With him to hold the pen, the Min Bao might be called truly fortunate in its man; for he alone was fit to stand against Mr. Liang Rengong (Qichao)—one of the Royalist leaders, whose "pen-point ever carried feeling" and whose writing was most inflammatory—and the revolutionary propaganda of the Tongmenghui truly relied upon him. So that from the Father of the Nation down, men like Huang Kexing, Hu Hanmin, and Song Jiaoren all treated him with added courtesy and deferred to him with care. Wang alone did not: young and headstrong, and presuming on his being a core member of the Tongmenghui, much favored by the Father of the Nation and no mean writer himself, he actually harbored the ambition of being second to none, and coveted the post of editor-in-chief of the Min Bao, bent single-mindedly on ousting Zhang Taiyan and taking his place. So he elbowed Zhang in every way and crossed him again and again, until at last he drove off in a rage this Mr. Taiyan nicknamed "the Madman"—who later even went to the South Seas and, with Tao Chengzhang, set up a rival "Restoration Society," making trouble for the Tongmenghui at every turn. Beyond doubt this was a great loss to the Tongmenghui and a heavy wound to the revolutionary work! And Wang's base strain—disregarding the whole for a private ambition, drunk on power and position, willing, to satisfy his own desire and a little private pique, to breach his own Great Wall and split the revolutionary camp—here first began to show its edge.

The Qing court, seeing the revolutionary party's momentum grow daily, asked the Japanese government to suppress it severely; and the Japanese Saionji cabinet of the time actually complied, openly expelling the party members from the country. Wang, Hu, and the rest had no choice but to follow the Father of the Nation to Hanoi—and of course the Min Bao too was forced to suspend. In 1906, the revolutionary party's Qin-Lian uprising failed; the Qing court, well knowing its command center was at Hanoi, again asked Annam to expel them, and Wang again followed the Father of the Nation, fleeing to Singapore, to go on preaching revolution and raising funds from the overseas Chinese for the buying of arms and the moving of troops.

At Penang there was an overseas-Chinese club called the "Little Orchid Pavilion," whose head was a rich Chinese émigré of Panyu, Chen Gengji. Chen had a daughter named Bingru (later renamed Bijun), who often went with her father to the club to socialize and amuse herself; and Wang, being just then engaged in persuading the overseas Chinese to give funds, thus met Chen Bingru again at the "Little Orchid Pavilion." Wang was a famous handsome man, then in the flower of his youth and with a boundless future; Chen fell for him at first sight and pursued him by every device—and, as it happened, both being Panyu people, there was the added tie of a shared native place, so that in time affection grew between them and they came at last to be man and wife. In fact, before Wang went abroad across the sea, he had long since been betrothed. In those days Chen Chunxuan was governor-general of the two Guangs, and Wang's elder brother Zhaoyong was serving as a secretary in the governor-general's office; a colleague named Liu Zifan had a younger sister of marriageable age, gentle and comely, versed in letters and in propriety, and Zhaoyong took it on himself to arrange a betrothal with the Liu family on Wang's behalf. Of this Wang not only knew but had consented. Later, when Wang went to Japan and joined the revolutionary party, Zhaoyong, in fear of calamity and dreading to be implicated, on the one hand petitioned the governor-general's office to "expel my worthless younger brother and sever forever the ties of kin," and on the other found Liu Zifan and arranged to break the engagement. Of all this, Wang, being abroad and busy running about for the revolution, was naturally little aware; and the Liu girl too, waiting in her chamber to be wed, was kept in the dark. Only when the Xinhai Revolution succeeded, and Wang was released from prison at Beijing and preparing to marry Chen Bijun, but with the prior betrothal to the Liu girl the matter in a hard strait, did he consult in turn Mr. Hu Hanmin and Mr. Zhu Zhixin, and get no clear answer; in the end it was Mr. Zhixin who worked upon Zhaoyong, and Zhaoyong then sent for Wang and told him the Liu match was already broken off, so that he might join with Chen in wedlock.

Of Wang's marriage, the sacrifice of the Liu girl need hardly be told; and in fact his union with Chen Bijun may truly be reckoned a "karmic tie of sin." By "karmic tie" I do not mean there was anything amiss between the couple; in truth they were always deeply attached, and Wang especially bore toward Chen much the feeling of gratitude to one who knew him; for when Wang first threw himself into the revolution, Chen gave money and effort, braving danger and hardship, and aided him not a little, with no small merit worth the record. Yet later Chen's fierceness and domineering, her ingrained greed, and her jealous heart that avenged the smallest slight, spoiled a great many of his large affairs. Chen Bijun not only leaned on Wang to exalt herself but set herself up as a revolutionary elder; she treated all Wang's subordinates and kin and friends alike as "house-vassals," ordering them about, and was jealous of every revolutionary senior, and looked down all the more on the ordinary run of rising men of Party and state. Driven by the lust for power, she, like Wang, harbored deep prejudice even against our great Leader Chiang, forever taking it that he had risen over their heads, and grumbling and fretting in her injustice. This mean and grasping, self-inflated, ugly temper of hers had long been notorious. Add to this that Wang himself had long borne a grievance over his own political standing, and so showed toward her an extreme feebleness and a forced indulgence. This the more emboldened her to act without scruple as she pleased, until she fell to "the hen crowing at dawn." So that in matters great and small, public and private without distinction, she must thrust in a hand, or even usurp the office outright. On the other side she was forever at Wang's ear making mischief and fanning the flames—in a small way boycotting those she disliked, in a large way singing against the government authorities; and thus, hindering at every turn, she swayed Wang's will and his acts, until inner and outer grew estranged, all men looked askance, and she brought Wang at last to the plight of the "three blindnesses." The "three blindnesses" are: first, no self-knowledge; second, no knowledge of men; third, no clear sight of affairs. For Wang this was truly a great tragedy; for by rights, with Wang's talent and his past revolutionary record, and the lofty station he held in Party and state, he was much admired by his countrymen; but the pity is that, worked upon by Chen Bijun, in his late years he grew blind of eye and deaf of ear, and turned into a muddled man. He knew only to scramble for power, to make factions, to set up his own house; he knew only to weigh petty personal grudges, to act on feeling, and no longer to reckon consequences. To tell it, one may indeed lay it to objective factors; but it also fully lays bare his weakness of will and the greatest failing of his contradictory character.

Now let me go on to speak of Chen Bijun. In the twenty-seventh year of the Republic (1938), the winter of the second year of the resistance, Wang betrayed the country, left Chongqing and fled, and at Hanoi published the "Yan Telegram," openly answering the "Three Principles of Peace" put forward by the Japanese warlords for the destruction of our nation; and by March of the twenty-ninth year he went to Nanjing under Japanese occupation to organize a puppet regime, putting collaboration with Japan into practice, to stand against our government and the whole nation's soldiers and people. In all this, before and after, from head to tail, Chen Bijun took part in the planning and pressed it on the harder—some things even without Wang's knowledge. One may say she was the true "puppeteer" of this marionette show. By the account of Chen Gongbo, the number-two traitor-chief of the Nanjing puppet organization, who in March of the thirtieth year of the Republic told a friend of his at Hong Kong with his own mouth: "Mr. Wang was not keen to set up the government; the keenest was Chen Bijun." And he said further: "I myself had no wish to join Mr. Wang's government; it was Chen Bijun who dragged me in by a honey-trap." Chen Gongbo's lechery was a thing Chen Bijun deeply loathed; yet now, to drag him into the water, she did not scruple to go against her own nature and stoop to such base means to hook him in—so that of all the many other drawings-in and arrangings of men, one may the less imagine the reach. As for Wang, at the outset, when he had just left Chongqing and reached Hanoi and published the "Yan Telegram," because the people's feeling ran hot and the whole nation reviled him, and the responses on all sides were most unfavorable to him, he could not but be torn within and repent his rashness. At the same time he had first, at Kunming, failed to bring Long Yun round, and struck a great snag; then came the reshuffle of the Japanese cabinet, which at once set him coldly aside; and on our side the Central authorities, though aware his treasonous traces were now showing, still sent high officials again and again to urge him to leave the country early for Europe. In this period he was indeed sore troubled within, able neither to advance nor retreat. But he was at bottom a man soft of ear and weak of backbone; unable to withstand Chen Bijun's forceful goading, he was hoisted willy-nilly onto the tiger's back and left unable to get down. And once the "Yan Telegram" was out, and the whole nation seethed and reviled him together, he judged there was no room left to turn back, and cut himself off at last from Party and state, treading the extreme path.

To sum up: that Wang failed to keep his late integrity, and at the very crisis of the nation's life or death should kneel to the Japanese warlords in suit for peace, willing to be a puppet, doing as Liu Yu and Zhang Bangchang had done, fitly earned him the whole nation's rejection—"his person dead, mocked and laughed at by all under heaven." Such a lamentable ending was, for Wang, no more than his just deserts; but tracing the root of the disaster, six or seven parts in ten were spoilt at the hand of that woman Chen Bijun. Were Wang to know it in the underworld, he would surely repent that he had not, at the first, done otherwise.

One might say: Chen Bijun too was an old revolutionary, so how could she be so bereft of reason? Very simply: she had not even that little grounding in politics that Wang had, more or less. Her motive and aim went no further than these two points. The first was the venting of the lust for power. As all men know, Wang and Chen were both keen for power and position and exceedingly self-regarding, holding that, since the founding of the Republic nearly thirty years before, if one reckoned by Wang's learning, his history, and his repute, then who but he should lead the Party and lead the state? Yet in both these he ranked below others, having neither troops nor power, an empty name and no more, and so his years of pent grievance sat ill in his heart. She did not know that Wang lacked from the first the gifts to be a leader, and that since the founding of the Republic he had turned the clouds and rains, served Qin in the morning and Chu at night, with no record to speak of of blessing the country or profiting the people. She only fervently longed for Wang to grasp power, and would grasp power herself. Now that there was the chance to set up a "peace government" of their own, to lift their heads and let out their breath and see a long-held wish fulfilled, how could she not scheme with all her might and hasten it on? As to whether it was "true" or "false," or was cursed as treason—that was others' view; "to exist is to be right," let the mockery and abuse be what they might; at the least she would first taste the thrill of being "First Lady" and "underground chairwoman," and a spell of glory too was worth the having; the rest was not worth the reckoning. The second was the drive of a gambler's luck. Wang and Chen were both defeatists of the same stripe, long used to opportunism, and from the first lacking confidence in our national policy of long resistance. By October of the twenty-seventh year, after Guangzhou and Wuhan fell one after the other, they grew the more pessimistic, holding the resistance to have no future; rather than wait for defeat, and truly reach the day of the nation's ruin and the race's extinction, better to seek peace early—or even surrender—so as to keep some vital force for a future revival; that would be the wiser course. The more so as the national army was losing ground on every front, its lost land daily greater, the whole nation in dread, and it seemed the Japanese would soon sweep up the whole of China. At just such a juncture, Wang could set himself against the crowd and come forward to advocate peace; and he believed that a call from a height would draw a multitude of followers. If the peace-seeking succeeded, then Wang could straightway transform himself into a national hero, the savior of the state, fit to rank with "Gambetta" of the Franco-Prussian War and "Lenin" after the Communist revolution in Tsarist Russia—so why not do it? The success or failure to come could not be known, but there was no harm in staking a wager on it.

The above was Chen Bijun's state of mind at the time, with root and ground, and well known to those within the circle; it is no wronging of her at all. But the pity is that these "woman's notions," these one-sided longings, were truly laughable in their naïveté; and Wang's views ran in the selfsame rut as Chen's—another clear proof of his "three blindnesses."

### (2) A Presumptuous Man of His Age, Leaving Both Fragrance and Stench

When we have looked on the radiant page that Mr. Wang once had, we could never have reckoned that later he would do so muddled a thing; nothing under heaven could be more past belief.

In the second year of Xuantong of the Qing—that is, the twenty-first of February, two years before the founding of the Republic of China—came the affair of Wang Jingwei's attempt to assassinate the Prince Regent Zaifeng at Beijing, which failed and led to his arrest; and this, in the annals of the Republic's founding, plainly holds an honorable page too. Though the act was a failure, in stirring the revolutionary tide and rousing the people's hearts it yet had a positive effect of stimulus and encouragement.

That year Wang was twenty-seven. He and Chen Bijun, Huang Fusheng (originally named Shuzhong, who, taken and not dying, changed his name), Luo Shixun, and the rest, moved by Wu Yue's attempt to assassinate the five great ministers, held that to hasten the revolution one must shed blood and sacrifice. And so they went north together from Hong Kong, resolved to plot the blowing up of the Prince Regent. Before setting out, Wang left a letter of farewell to Mr. Sun Yat-sen, in which were such words as: "To hold the body together, the thing is to strive at the actual carrying-through of the work; then the disheartened will turn again to ardor, and the doubting turn again to faith." He wrote also a letter of farewell to the comrades of the South Seas, saying in brief: "Now I am about to go to Beijing; on this journey, whether the thing succeed or not, there can be no hope of returning alive, so I write this beforehand.… I shall do this thing, and my teachers and friends of a lifetime, knowing it, blame me, holding that to die easily is not so hard as to live hard. Yet in the work of revolution one should, according to what his nature is nearest to, choose one thing and give himself to it.… The dead are done; the living, as the revolutionary tide rises higher, will bear a heavier charge, and their toil and bitterness must be ten times that of today. I, unworthy, die before the rest of my comrades, and shall not share the hardships to come—of this I am truly ashamed! Though I shed my blood on the street of Caishi, still I shall open my eyes to watch the revolutionary army enter the capital gates."

The "Street of Caishi" spoken of at the letter's end is also called "Caishikou," outside the Xuanwu Gate in Beiping, the site of the execution-ground where the Qing court in those days put criminals to death; one sees that he went with a resolve to die. It is said that at the time Mr. Sun Yat-sen tried hard to dissuade him, and his good friend Mr. Hu Hanmin, weeping bitter tears, urged him not to do this useless thing; but he would heed none of it.

Sure enough, on the twenty-first of the second month of the gengxu year, the second of Xuantong, Wang, Huang, and the rest buried a bomb under the Yinding Bridge beside Shichahai, outside the Di'an Gate of Beijing, plotting to blow up the Prince Regent—for the spot was one the Prince Regent must pass daily on his way to court. Unexpectedly, before the thing was done, the plan leaked; Wang, Huang, and the rest were seized one after another and cast into the prison of the Board of Punishments, while Chen Bijun, being a woman and not on the scene at the time, got clear. This affair truly shook court and country; and Wang, judging he must die, wrote in prison those four widely-loved quatrains of "The knife brings one glad stroke," titled "Composed Extempore on Being Seized":

{p} Carrying stones till the folly is utter, over blue waves a sorrow ten thousand li wide; / flying alone I never tire, ashamed to drift after the gulls. / The riot of purple and crimson—now I know the dyeing is hard; / when one day the fair flowers open, mark well the flecks of blood! / With a full heart I sing in the market of Yan, calmly I play the captive of Chu; / the knife brings one glad stroke—I grudge not this young head! / Let the heart and soul remain; this ruined frame I give to the ashes of doom; / the blue corpse-fire's light shall not die, but light the Terrace of Yan night after night.

At the same time there was another poem, "Autumn Night," written for Chen Bijun and Hu Hanmin, which ran:

{p} Fallen leaves in the empty court, the night's faint sounds; old friends in dream, the two of us cleaving close. / The wind sighs over the Yi River—is today still yesterday? The soul crosses the maple wood—is it so or not? / Meeting underground I should feel no shame, but with no road to split the hills, where am I to go? / I recall how once at Xinting we shed our tears together—how can I let the streaks of weeping fill my robe again!

There was also "Random Feelings in Prison," which ran:

{p} West wind in the courtyard, the night sunk deep; the autumn sound fills my ears, past all bearing.
{p} A crouching steed still, the will to gallop a thousand li; the frost-struck tree, the heart of a hundred years.
{p} The southern cap unchanged, the ragged, broken form; the painted horn holding a note of fierce grief.
{p} My thanks to the blue corpse-fire that soothes my lonely sleep, still keeping me company through the failing night as I chant on in bitterness.

At the time Chen Bijun was still in Beijing, and secretly had a letter passed to Wang to console and hearten him. Wang was deeply moved, and, fearing besides that Chen's staying on in the capital would breed disaster, wrote in reply a "Song of the Golden Threads" to press her to leave the capital with all speed. This ci was made in the manner of Gu Zhenguan's "Song of the Golden Threads" consoling Wu Zhaoqian, and is likewise tender and moving. The ci ran:

{p} Are you well since we parted? Though we meet again, ten thousand things are desolate, past bearing to look back on! Country broken, home lost, a boundless grief—how has this life borne it?—and now the sorrow of parting is heaped ten thousand pecks more. Before my eyes, within my heart, it is as yesterday; telling my hopes, night after night we clasped hands; a whole heart's blood, I lay bare for you. / The tear-stains must have soaked the cloud-patterned paper through; leaning on the cold quilt I read it round and round again, the guttering lamp like a bean. What has this leftover life come to? For nothing it sets old friends fretting; I am ashamed to keep this head still on my shoulders as before. Trudging over passes and rivers is, I know, no easy thing; may my lonely soul guard the carriage, before and behind. The heart is already broken; the song can hardly be sung again.

These poems he had all secretly passed out by the jailer's hand from one to another. When they reached the revolutionary bureau then at Hong Kong, Mr. Hu Hanmin, Mr. Huang Kexing, and Mr. Zhao Sheng were all moved to weeping past all voice.

Reading his letters and poems, they are indeed most stirring, at once heroic and grieving; nor is it hard to glimpse in them a surging patriotic ardor and a revolutionary spirit that goes calmly to its death, leaping from the page. The Wang Jingwei of that day was in truth no unworthy revolutionary of ardent and mournful song, fit to make all under heaven pay him solemn respect. Yet, gentle readers, on no account overlook the most important point in all this: his willfulness and his impulse of feeling. Even this attempt on the Prince Regent, and his imprisonment, though it won praise—if one probes to the bottom of it—arose too from a momentary willfulness and impulse. Before he set out for Beijing, Mr. Sun Yat-sen tried to stop him, and Mr. Hu Hanmin too urged him hard "not to do this useless thing"; but he neither made allowance for, nor trusted in, Mr. Sun Yat-sen's surveying of the whole and his painstaking plans; nor did he consider that whether the thing succeeded or not, it was no help to the real work. He blamed only the party members for hanging back, holding them not united enough and wanting in the spirit of sacrifice. Young and headstrong, wishing to act alone, he rushed rashly into peril to do this "useless thing."

Wang was a man rich in feeling and poor in reason—a flaw of character, by no means to be changed by later cultivation. So whether he was loyal or traitorous, good or evil, hung on a single passing thought; and that "single passing thought" was a thing even he himself could neither foreknow nor hold in hand. If you doubt it, look again at his personal history, and it is fully proved.

After Wang was seized and cast into the prison of the Board of Punishments, Prince Su Shanqi, the Qing court's Minister of the Interior, examined him in person; and Wang wrote out his own deposition, some ten thousand words and more, the hand fine and the writing free and stirring, so that Shanqi was moved to a heart that cherished talent. Though the Board's Minister of Punishments, Tingjie, urged hard for beheading, Shanqi strove with all his might to get him off. It happened that the Qing court was just then preparing for a constitution, and judged that to kill a few revolutionaries would be of no avail, and it were better to take a soothing policy to show magnanimity. So on the twentieth of the third month there came at last the imperial decree: "Wang Zhaoming and Huang Fusheng are to be handed to the Board of Punishments for perpetual imprisonment." And Shanqi, reckoning Wang a man of talent, even went to the prison in person to "soothe him with a kindly face," and allowed him to read and write there. So Wang was able to chant poems and compose in prison. On the nineteenth of the eighth month of the third year of Xuantong, the Wuchang Uprising broke out; the provinces of the southeast declared independence one after another; the Qing court called Yuan Shikai back into use, and Wang too was released from prison. He was seen out as a hero: it is said the warden even made him three bows in parting, and the townsfolk of Beijing and the clerks of the several boards gathered at the great gate of the Board of Punishments, vying to see this "young head," till street and lane were choked in wild enthusiasm. Reckoning from Wang's entering prison in the second month of the second year of Xuantong to his release in the eighth month of the third year, the term of his imprisonment was just one year and a half.

Who could have thought that after Wang came out this time, he began to change.

With Yuan Shikai's return to power, he harbored a great ambition, planning first to make for himself a third party's place in the standoff between the Qing court and the revolutionaries. He marked Wang as a man to be used, meaning to use him as a bridge to seek cooperation with the revolutionaries, and then to go on and attain his own political ambition. Through many twists, and with all manner of drawing-in devices, and with Yuan's eldest son Yuan Keding accompanying Wang to Huanshang (Yuan's place of retirement in his home county of Xiangcheng) for repeated secret talks with Yuan, Wang did indeed fall into his snare, and from then on became the first man the revolutionaries dealt with Yuan through. Wang for his part meant of course to use Yuan as a tool to overthrow the Manchus root and branch; yet, drunk on power, he was content to be used by Yuan. The upshot was that, seeking gain, he got loss: when the Xinhai Revolution succeeded and the Republic was founded, Yuan not only usurped the reins of state, taking the Father of the Nation's presidency for himself, but staged the farce of the Hongxian enthronement.

In the eleventh month of the Xinhai year, the Father of the Nation returned to the country, and with Mr. Hu Hanmin came to Shanghai; on the thirteenth of the eleventh month he took office at Nanjing as provisional President, and, changing the calendar and the era, this was the first of January by the new calendar of the first year of the Republic. At this time Wang, charged by Yuan, went south with Yang Du to aid in the North-South peace talks; on the surface he stood on the revolutionaries' side, but at bottom he had already become Yuan's strategist and spy. Because he had a secret compact with Yuan beforehand, he strongly urged that "to realize a democratic republic, if we use Yuan, we shall do half the work for double the result." Then the Peace and Unification Conference was held at Shanghai, and Wang, mediating and negotiating within it, at last brought about North-South unification, so that the Father of the Nation had to yield the presidency to Yuan Shikai. After the Beijing government was unified and established, Wang left the capital for the south; and Yuan, well pleased, led all his cabinet in seeing Wang off, and on the day of departure presented Wang with the Second Order of Merit and a large sum of send-off money besides—all of which Wang gladly accepted.

Wang knew in his heart that Yuan's first step would be to disband the newly risen parties of the North, and his second step would surely be to make an enemy of the Tongmenghui, so as to make himself the one power to lord it over all. Unwilling to be caught in a hard place between the two, and wishing to sit and watch who would win, he resolved on a tour of Europe and America—and at the same time won himself the fair name of one who withdraws when the work is done.

Sure enough, when in the second year of the Republic the Tongmenghui was reorganized into the Nationalist Party, there followed the assassination of Song Jiaoren and the vile business of Yuan's "loan to suppress disorder" from the five-nation banking consortium. The Second Revolution failed, the Father of the Nation fled to Japan, and in the eleventh month the Nationalist Party was dissolved by Yuan. Wang, having founded the Sino-French University at Lyon in France to go on enlarging the revolutionary party's strength, was distrusted by Yuan, who bade Yuan Keding summon him home to take an important post. In the fourth year of the Republic, at the outbreak of the European war, he returned once to China. When in the fifth year the war to punish Yuan began, he at once slipped back to France to watch from the sidelines. Because the outcome of the struggle between Yuan and the revolutionaries was not yet known, he wished to keep himself apart and watch the turn of events quietly—truly an act of opportunism and of fishing for fame.

The May Fourth Movement of the eighth year of the Republic woke the nation from its dream, but was also the seed-bed for the transplanting of Communism into China. In the tenth year of the Republic the Chinese Communist Party was founded at Shanghai; not only were the people of North China deeply affected, but it spread by degrees to central and southern China. And at that time the warlord government of the North was strong and its momentum overbearing; caught between these two evil forces, old and new, the revolutionary road was plainly full of hardship. So the Russian Communists, from the tenth year on, sent successively Maring, Joffe, Borodin, and others as representatives, in name sympathizing with and aiding the national revolution, but in fact bent on actively fostering the growth and swift spread of the Chinese Communist Party, in hope of turning China red at an early day. In the eleventh year Chen Jiongming rebelled, and the Father of the Nation was forced to leave Guangdong; the next year he returned to Guangdong and resumed the office of Grand Marshal. By this time alliance with the Communists seemed already to have become the Nationalist Party's political decision, and the one who urged admitting the Communists most strongly was Wang Jingwei—for he had changed again, and was now deeply poisoned by leftist thought. In the first month of the thirteenth year the Nationalist Party was reorganized and held its First National Congress at Guangzhou; the Congress manifesto was drafted by Wang, and the policy of alliance with Russia and admission of the Communists was proclaimed. On the thirteenth of the eleventh month that year, on Wang's prior arrangement, the Father of the Nation, though ill, went north to confer on state affairs with the Duan Qirui government at Beijing; the Father of the Nation urged the convening of a national assembly, but Duan meant to gather all power to himself. Only then did the Father of the Nation wake to the truth that the Sun-Duan cooperation Wang had negotiated had trodden again the old rut of the Sun-Yuan cooperation of the first year of the Republic. This journey may be said to have been a warlord's trick—and a deceiving too by Wang. On the twelfth of the third month of the fourteenth year, when the Father of the Nation's illness worsened at the Peking Union Medical College and he was at the point of death, Wang and the other chief party members gathered round the bed to ask his last charge; the Father of the Nation, fixing his eyes on Wang, said: "After I die, the enemy will surely try to soften you; if you are not softened, they will surely do you harm; and if you seek to avoid the danger, you will in the end hardly escape being softened." This was as much as to say that he had long seen Wang for a compromiser and an opportunist, and so could not, even at his death, feel easy about him. Wang, on behalf of them all, answered on the spot: "We have followed you for some decades, and have never given a thought to danger, nor ever been softened by the enemy." The Father of the Nation's testament, too, was written by Wang's hand.

Because of the Nationalist Party's policy of alliance with Russia and admission of the Communists, a fine chance was given the Communists to "live as a parasite" within the Nationalist Party, and by degrees they came to outshout the host—forming the greatest hidden peril of the national revolution. And the Soviet adviser Borodin, in the posture of an overlord, acted arbitrarily and alone, secretly working the great power of the Nationalist Party. So the Nationalist Party split into two factions, left and right, and a fierce struggle began. In this Wang Jingwei was of one breath with the Communists, playing a double game, and became the chief figure of the left. In the sixth month of the fourteenth year Wang returned to Guangzhou; on the first of July the National Government was established, and Wang was pushed forward as its first Chairman. On the twenty-first of the third month of the fifteenth year came the Zhongshan Warship Incident, wholly the outcome of a political plot by the Communists in league with Wang's left to usurp the Chinese Nationalist Party and seize the leadership of the Chinese revolution. Happily the then-Commandant of the Military Academy saw through their treachery, and with a swift decision, in an action quick as thunder that leaves no time to cover the ears, put it down. Seeing the thing had failed, and stung with an inner guilt, Wang hid himself and would not be seen; then he resigned his several offices, held and concurrent—Chairman of the National Government, Chairman of the Military Affairs Commission, chief Party representative of the Military Academy and the revolutionary army—and once more drifted far off, going to France.

On the ninth of July that same year, the Commander-in-Chief of the National Revolutionary Army, the Duke Chiang, took office at Guangzhou and swore the army to the Northern Expedition. By the third month of the sixteenth year the revolutionary army was winning at every step, splitting bamboo, and had fought its way to the Yangzi. With the advance of the campaign, the Commander-in-Chief's headquarters moved to Nanchang. At this time the Communists and the left took the chance to launch a "welcome Wang back to office" movement, meaning to share in and usurp the fruits of the national revolution. On the first of April Wang did indeed return to the country and reach Shanghai.

Before this—that is, in the autumn of the fifteenth year—the Nationalist Party Central and the National Government had moved from Guangdong to Wuhan. But in fact this Nationalist Party Central and National Government were manipulated behind the scenes by the Soviet adviser Borodin and gripped by the Communists and the fellow-traveling leftists, so that it had already changed its nature and become an illegal regime plotting to turn China red. And at that time the Commander-in-Chief of the national revolution, the Duke Chiang, carrying on the Father of the Nation's will, holding faith with the Three Principles of the People, had raised the army in Northern Expedition, and had swiftly stretched the revolutionary power to the Yangzi basin, so that the unification of the whole nation seemed at hand; his achievement so high and his repute so great, he had naturally become the powerful leader of the orthodox Nationalist Party. This was just what the Russian Communists and the Chinese Communists under their wing most abhorred, and they took him for the greatest barrier to their reddening work, and used all manner of vile means to "topple Chiang." Meanwhile the Party-protecting members and the moderates of the Nationalist Party had also made ready, within a few days, to set up a National Government and a Nationalist Party Central at Nanjing (established on the tenth of April), to stand against the reactionary regime at Wuhan. This is the period called the "Ninghan Split."

To speak of Wang after his return on the first of April: the Duke Chiang met Wang at Shanghai, meaning to work together to reconcile the Party strife. Wang took it on himself, volunteering to go to Wuhan to mediate; the Duke Chiang counseled him: "On no account go to Wuhan; if you go, you will surely not get out, and then, though you would not be the Communists' tool, you will not be able to help it. If you truly act for our Party, then go to Nanjing." Wang would not heed; in fact, at the very time of his talk with the Duke Chiang, he had long had a compact with the Communist leaders Chen Duxiu, Zhou Enlai, and the rest. On leaving Shanghai he even issued a joint manifesto with Chen Duxiu, and gave the talk: "Let the revolutionary come to the left, and the unrevolutionary get out!" On the sixth of April he took ship from Shanghai and reached Hankou; and sure enough, as the Duke Chiang had foretold, this going of his not only did not return but made him the highest head of the Wuhan regime. At this time his titles were: Standing Member of the Nationalist Party Central, head of the Organization Department, chairman of the presidium of the Political Council, chairman of the presidium of the Military Affairs Commission, and Standing Committee member of the National Government (the office of Chairman of the National Government having been abolished). On the surface he gathered the great power of Party, government, and army in his one person, and might seem to have his lust for power much satisfied; but in fact he was at every turn at others' disposal, no more than a tool of Borodin and the Communists. Only when, on the first of August, the units of Ye Ting, He Long, and the rest of the Chinese Communists carried out their revolt at Nanchang did he wake to his being used; and in his shame and rage, on the sixth of August he published a piece, "Errors and Their Correction," and then resolved on "separating from the Communists." From then on he became once more a firm anti-Communist.

On the eighth of August Nanjing issued its "Qi" circular telegram, and on the tenth Hankou issued its "Zheng" circular telegram; for a while the talk of Ninghan cooperation rose to the clouds, but it did not come to pass. By early October, Wang left Wuhan at last and slipped back to Guangzhou—though he did not truly break away, and kept a broken-lotus-root connection with Wuhan; back at Guangzhou he took for himself the chairmanship of the Wuhan regime's Guangdong Political Sub-council, proposed convening the Fourth Plenary Session at Nanjing to resolve the Ninghan split, and on the eighteenth of the eleventh month reached Shanghai. On the second of the twelfth month a meeting was held at the Duke Chiang's residence, and after much back-and-forth there was fair prospect of agreement. Then, unexpectedly, on the eleventh of the twelfth month, the Communists Ye Ting and He Long launched the great Guangzhou uprising; Wang's repute was thereby gravely struck, and he was sternly condemned on all sides, called a double-dealing schemer, and even pointed to as the arch-culprit. With nowhere to hide his face, on the seventeenth he went abroad again with Zeng Zhongming.

In the several years after this, through a few more storms, until in the twenty-first year of the Republic he took the post of President of the Executive Yuan of the National Government and held the central power, he cooperated once more with the Generalissimo Chiang, jointly bearing the great charge of pacifying within and repelling without. In December of the twenty-fourth year he was shot at Nanjing and went abroad. After the Xi'an Incident of December of the twenty-fifth year, he returned to Nanjing as chairman of the Central Political Council. In the twenty-seventh year, when the full resistance entered its second year, he was elected Vice-Director-General of the Nationalist Party and speaker of the People's Political Council; yet he could not stand the test, and suddenly, at year's end, left the ranks of the resistance, fled from Chongqing, advocated peace, and at last threw himself into the arms of the Japanese warlords, going secretly to Nanjing to organize a puppet regime—ending with his person and name in ruins, dying in shame.

### (3) A Hundred Merits Cannot Hide This One Flaw

"Iron cast over the Nine Provinces makes a true blunder; from the annals there is no overturning the old game of chess." Wang Jingwei strove all his life, and when the coffin was closed and the verdict passed, because his late years were flawed he became a criminal against the state, a failure of history. Some lament him; some curse him as one whose death cannot atone. But say what one will, the great error was cast; right and wrong, merit and fault, lie clear before men's eyes, and even should the historians of after ages weigh and appraise him anew, they can absolutely never excuse him.

All his past waverings back and forth, his tangles of gratitude and grudge—whether out of pique or out of the scramble for power—may be left undebated. His greatest crime was simply that he ought not to have turned traitor and gone over to the enemy! And the factors that led to this crime were the sum total of many grave errors accumulated—a necessary result, that is. "One move wrong, and the whole board falls to pieces." When a man is bent on his own ruin, no one can save him.

Below I shall try to analyze the several grave errors he committed:

#### 1. The Roots of Defeatism

As I have said before, Wang's inborn character was rich in feeling and poor in reason. Therefore his mind wanted balance, and his willpower was especially brittle. Among the highest leaders of our country in that day, he was the one most apt to catch the "political sickness." Whenever he stirred up trouble or took a small setback, he would go off in a huff, and often had to be pressed again and again before he would come sauntering back. Thus he put on airs and grew used to opportunism, taking it that all hung on him, and yet was full of a sense of frustration; and defeatism took root in his consciousness. By Mr. Yongwu's diary, as early as the eleventh month of the twenty-sixth year, in the early days of the full resistance, Wang told Chen Bijun and his intimates at his residence at No. 207 West Cemetery-Park Road, Nanjing: "In the old days, when a city fell, all had to die with it to meet the highest standard of morality. Now the notion is different; I am still willing to keep this useful body to serve the country." No confidence at all in the resistance—the very words of a standard defeatist—and already he had let slip what he meant to do. When Nanjing fell on the thirteenth of December, he grew the more pessimistic. On the second of March of the twenty-seventh year, Mr. Yongwu wrote thus: "At six in the evening, I went with Zhuqiu to No. 9 Yide Road to see Mr. Wang. Not having seen him for nearly a month, he was gaunt and aged much, looking very tired, and had no strength even to talk.… Mr. Wang listened and shook his head, and at last sighed: 'The road ahead is a boundless blur; who knows what it will come to!' He seemed to have utterly lost faith in the resistance, and his pessimism and passivity had reached the last degree." By this time Wang had moved to Hankou, and our military reverses had made this defeatist not only waver in confidence but come near to collapse. By early August Wang had followed the government to Chongqing. On the eighteenth of August Mr. Yongwu wrote again: "This afternoon I went to see Mr. Wang in the new Western-style house on the little hill opposite Shangqingsi. It faces the Chuan river, and the view is very fine. Mr. Wang came from Hankou to Yichang, and thence by a small gunboat to Chongqing.…" And on the twenty-eighth he wrote: "At three in the afternoon I saw Mr. Wang, who suddenly gave me a large recent photograph, and even took up his pen and signed it—what he meant by it I do not grasp. In the evening Brother Pingqun and his betrothed, Miss Kang, gave a feast, and Mr. Wang joined it too, drinking most heartily, which likewise seemed somewhat out of the ordinary." One sees that Mr. Yongwu was after all a clever man, and was reckoned by Wang one of his outer intimates; yet even he could not make out that Wang had by then settled his dealings with the Japanese side, and was about to leave Chongqing and flee to Hanoi, openly to go over to the enemy. The diary of the twenty-seventh of December: "By Brother Naiguang's account, Chen Shuren says Mr. Wang's flight had two great causes: one the Communist question, the other the question of peace talks with Japan. How much Chen Shuren knew of the inside of Mr. Wang's flight is a question; but that Mr. Wang was pessimistic about the resistance and for peace talks with Japan was indeed of long standing. When the government was still at Nanjing, the Wang residence was already full of a pessimistic, defeatist air; Madam Wang and their children, in their talk, often took a mocking and sneering tone toward the resistance, and Mr. Wang seemed to show agreement with their words. For instance, when the war went ill and the papers, instead of 'defeat and retreat,' said 'shifting to advance,' this was matter for laughter in the Wang residence." By this it is proved that the whole Wang household, from Wang Jingwei and Chen Bijun down, were all defeatists; and this grave error of view and of temper had long since sunk past cure. Can he be pardoned? Not to take the road of going over to the enemy—where else were they to go!

#### 2. "Mr. Party-Discipline" Breaks Party Discipline

As far back as the sixteenth year of the Republic, Wang, on the plea of upholding Party discipline, opposed the purge of the Party, shielded the Communists, and breathed through one nostril with them. Thereafter, in all his stands on Party affairs, whatever was to his own disadvantage he warded off with "upholding Party discipline" as a talisman. And so he got the by-name of "Mr. Party-Discipline." Of course the dignity of Party discipline is sacred and not to be violated; but on Wang it had already changed its nature into a mere tool for his own ends.

In the early days of the resistance after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident, the Generalissimo Chiang cried out again and again: "Until the last extreme of sacrifice is reached, we will not lightly sacrifice; until peace is at the point of utter hopelessness, we will not give up peace!" This represented the peace-wish of our whole nation, and declared with grief the resolve to fight the full resistance to the end if need be, standing irreconcilable with the enemy. At the time the German ambassador to China, Trautmann, was busy mediating, and it was proposed to accept the mediation of the signatory powers of the Nine-Power Treaty; peace efforts of every kind dragged on a long while—which is to say, we did not refuse a settlement, and even down to before Wang left Chongqing had still not given it up. But the Japanese warlords, with a wolf's ambition, were bent on destroying our nation and seizing our land, and so we had no more room for a mid-course compromise. Had Wang truly advocated peace to save the country, then, with the dignity of his office as Vice-Director-General of the Nationalist Party, he might well have formally put forward this proposal to the Party's Central Standing Committee or the Supreme National Defense Council; and even were circumstances not to allow it, he might, after leaving the country's borders, have put the proposal to the Central authorities by letter or telegram. But this "Mr. Party-Discipline" did no such thing; he actually fled far off to Hanoi and openly published the "Yan Telegram" at Hong Kong, in answer to Japan's Konoe statement. This manner of proceeding was wholly illegal, and the gravest of great breaches of Party discipline. That "Mr. Party-Discipline" should be so self-contradictory, in the shameful fashion of the fox that buries and the fox that digs up, plainly shows he had another liver and lights within. So when some said afterward that the Party Central's expelling him from the Party was what drove him to extremes, that is incorrect.

#### 3. Defying What All the World Holds Right

From the fifth year of Guangxu of the Qing (1879), when Japan annexed our vassal state the Ryukyus, and especially from the "First Sino-Japanese War" (1894) on, Japan schemed against us ever more urgently. One may say that in the fifty years or so up to the victory in the resistance, Japan had become our nation's sworn foe—a thing all the people of China, down to women and children, well knew and gnashed their teeth over. Once the full resistance began, peace was utterly hopeless. The Japanese warlords were savage; of the soldiers and people at the front and the folk of the occupied areas, those cruelly slaughtered and trampled numbered in the millions, and even the eighty-year-old crone and the three-year-old child could not escape the fate of death—truly white bones propping up the sky, and blood flowing a thousand li. The fire of hatred blazed in every Chinese heart. Not at the call of the Generalissimo Chiang alone, but the whole nation, land without distinction of north or south, men without distinction of old or young, all resolved to perish together with the enemy. This was the manifestation of the Chinese nation's righteous spirit, and the one road to salvation from ruin, which no force could withstand. At just this juncture, Wang Jingwei suddenly betrayed Party and state, and alone ran off to a foreign land to advocate a settlement, and even accepted the nation-destroying terms the Japanese put forward, and moreover made a great to-do of it, saying such things as: "To speak one word of peace is at once to be called a traitor. Yet the people's hearts are not all dead, nor all their mouths gagged; I dare aver that within a few months a peace-and-national-salvation movement will surely rise in full vigor." But here again he was wrong; he supposed all Chinese as naïve and death-fearing as himself. In fact his foolish course was to defy what all the world holds right, betraying not only Party and state but the whole people. His fond reckoning had been that a call from a height would draw a myriad responses; the upshot was that every man cursed him as a traitor and spat on him like dog's dung. So his failure needed no milfoil or tortoise-shell to foretell.

#### 4. The Fish Sees the Bait, Not the Hook

Suppose that, as Wang Jingwei held, the terms of the "Three Principles" put forward by Japan's Konoe cabinet—"good-neighborliness, joint defense against Communism, and economic cooperation, with no territorial ambition and no demand for indemnity, no intent to violate China's sovereignty, and the return of the concessions and the abolition of extraterritoriality"—were spoken as they were meant. Then Wang was naïve and dull to a pitiable degree. The Japanese are world-famous for savagery, cunning, cruelty, and pettiness; such a "tiger telling its prayer-beads" would not deceive a child of three, let alone Wang Jingwei, an elder of Party and state and a "statesman." So some doubted where Wang's real intent lay. For my part I do not think so, for Wang was in earnest, and held that Japan was sincere. The defeatist fears most of all the loss of his country and his head; the man ruled by feeling loves most of all sweet words. Since the Japanese, in their military surplus of advantage, were willing to "leave one side of the net open" and treat for peace, and on terms so generous besides—not to be had for the asking—and since he cleverly left himself a back step, declaring openly to the Japanese, "If the Japanese side is not sincere and the settlement fails, I can still come back and go on resisting"—little did he know he was but a fish in a pond, that sees only the twitching of the bait and imagines its savor, longing to gulp it down at once and fill its belly, without ever weighing the treachery behind the hook. Once hooked, all is out of your hands; before you can struggle, your bones are picked and your marrow knocked out and you are cast in the pot, or hung up to dry into fish-jerky. The gain and loss of it all could see clearly; he alone, the man in the game, was blind, and still, with fine words, went on dreaming his dream of peace. This too was one of his grave errors.

#### 5. Fish-Eyes for Pearls, and Things That Have No Bearing on One Another

This heading, though it smacks a little too much of the literary, may serve for a brief and pointed illustration. First, "passing off fish-eyes for pearls" is an old Chinese proverb, hinting that the fish-eye resembles the pearl but is no pearl. It has neither the pearl's beauty nor its real worth, and counterfeit though it would be, it cannot carry the counterfeit off. Wang Jingwei, on the twenty-ninth of December of the twenty-seventh year, published the "Yan Telegram" in open concert, betraying Party and state, his guilt plain. On New Year's Day of the twenty-eighth year, the Central Party headquarters resolved to expel Wang from the Party and revoke all his offices. When things had come to this stage, Wang had no standing whatever on either the government or the Party side; his one standing was as a mere commoner. But he would not turn back from the lost road, and later set up all the same a "Central Party Headquarters of the Chinese Nationalist Party," and staged the marionette show of "the National Government's return to the capital." That the Chinese National Government and the Nationalist Party Central were at Chongqing all the world knew and with one voice acknowledged; yet he, under the enemy's wing, set up a "false organization," openly "passing fish-eyes for pearls"—which, save to be played with by the enemy and used to swindle and deceive, was truly meaningless. At the same time, Wang could represent no force whatever; his troupe, apart from a scant few like Chen Gongbo and Zhou Fohai, were, one may say, all dregs, who went along with the mind to fish in muddy waters and scrape up a few more dollars. By Mr. Yongwu's diary of the sixth of December of the twenty-eighth year: "When Wang was conferring on setting up the government with the North China and Nanjing traitor-chiefs Wang Kemin, Liang Hongzhi, and the rest, Wang Kemin asked Wang what men there would be in the future government, and was answered with Zhou Fohai, Mei Siping, and the like (Chen Gongbo being at the time still watching from Hong Kong); Wang Kemin said he had never so much as heard these men's names. Wang was for a long while silent." And by Mr. Gu Zhengding, after returning from seeing Wang at Hanoi in February of the twenty-eighth year: "Mr. Wang deeply blames his comrades within the Party for lacking confidence in him, neither knowing to uphold his proposals nor able to advance and retreat together with him." A bare pole, a single fish-eye, tumbled about in the enemy's hands—what could it come to? The answer needs no asking; one sees how far Wang overreckoned himself! Second, "the horse, the cow, and the wind" figures three things that have no bearing on one another; and to yoke them by force into one is to make an incongruous joke of it. Wang Jingwei, with the standing of a commoner and a fugitive under government warrant, actually had the face to treat for peace with Japan. First, he had no ground at all, nothing to rest on, not a leg to stand on anywhere; it was all buying and selling of empty air, and even talked, was of no effect. Second, when two parties, A and B, are at war, then—whatever the outcome—to lay down arms and treat for peace, it must be A and B who are each other's counterparts; from of old it was never heard as a wonder that peace was made with an unconcerned third party. For "the horse and the cow do not reach each other." That the Japanese warlords, fierce and wily, used Wang to split the resistance camp and strike at the people's morale and the soldiers' spirit—that they did; but to say they asked him to represent China in treating for peace is plainly a case of the donkey's lips not fitting the horse's mouth, a swindle of self-deceiving and other-deceiving. Pitiable Wang, having neither self-knowledge nor knowledge of men, and above all no clear sight of affairs, muddled and dim, took the painted stage in his makeup, played with by the enemy in the palm of the hand, all his fond and foolish hopes fallen through; yet he had boarded the pirate ship, and to repent was already too late. Truly, one slip of the foot becomes the lasting regret of a thousand years, and by the time one turns back one's body is a hundred years old!

#### 6. Bereft of Reason, and All the Nation Says He Deserves Death

In the early days after Wang left Chongqing and fled, there were still some, ignorant of the inside of it, who showed astonishment and gave him their sympathy. Only after he published the "Yan Telegram" and the article "To Give an Example" did they suddenly wake to the truth, and know for certain that he was about the business of colluding with the enemy and selling the country. And in his Hong Kong paper, the Nanhua Daily, he trumpeted his peace-talk advocacy the more, and denied the meaning of the full resistance; and at the same time all manner of traitor-theories came out of the oven together. On the twenty-fifth of May of the twenty-eighth year, Wang went from Shanghai to Tokyo and signed a "peace treaty" with Japan's Hiranuma cabinet (the former premier Konoe Fumimaro having stepped down); this act stirred the anger and the sighs of many, though our government did not take it seriously—only the Central Daily News of the time likened his act to "no less than a traitor's act of firing a signal in the midst of an air-raid warning."

Worse yet: as early as the beginning of April, Wang had proposed that Japan send planes to bomb Chongqing, Kunming, and other important cities, and send troops to attack Nanchang, Changsha, Xi'an, Nanning, and elsewhere, so as to shake the resistance morale and thereby attain the aim of peace talks. The papers were full of it, and readers' hairs stood on end! Whether the report was reliable or not, the facts proved that Japan had indeed begun to do just this. All understood that Wang's treasonous conduct had sunk deeper and deeper, to the pitch of one bereft of reason.

Thereafter Wang secretly sent his nephew Shen Song to Guangdong, to lobby the Guangdong commanders to lay down their arms and cease resisting, and planned besides such illegal doings as setting up a "Revival Army" and forming a new government at Guangzhou—none of which took effect. So on the twelfth of August he made a broadcast address in person, the gist being: "I counsel the resisting commanders of Guangdong to fight no more. I have already come to terms with the Japanese military; so long as the Chinese army does not fire, showing sincerity for peace, the Japanese army too will not attack. In this way, extending from Guangdong to the whole country, Sino-Japanese peace can be realized." Such a wild fancy, such a fool's dream—it took him to think of it, and to have the face to say it aloud! When the soldiers and people of the whole nation, at the front, in the rear, in the occupied areas, first heard this broadcast, they doubted their own ears; and once come to themselves, they simply all burst out together, never dreaming Wang Jingwei could be so naïve, so mad, a human head with a beast's bleat. At this his ugly mask of willing service to the enemy was at last torn wholly off by his own hand, and the little scrap of good image left of him in men's hearts was utterly shattered.

The several points set out above sketch in outline the causes of how Wang Jingwei cast his great error. "It is I who destroy myself; had I not brought it on myself, who could have destroyed me!" Wang strove all his life, and only because his last step went wrong, he wronged himself and wronged the country, not only pouring his whole earlier record of revolutionary glory into the flowing water, but leaving behind a name to be cursed through the ages. On the surface it may seem the doing of objective circumstance; yet, quietly marking his character and the track of his personal history, one may be sure it was a necessary result. A hundred merits cannot hide this one flaw—only because his crime was too great, and no man can strain to forgive him.

### (4) Willfulness and Impulse Made the Good and the Evil, the Loyal and the Traitorous

A man's character can shape his whole attitude in conduct and affairs, and bears directly on the gains and losses, the success and failure, of a lifetime's undertaking. As the common saying has it, "The hills are hard to move, and the nature hard to shift." Character is inborn, half by nature, one may say; the other half is formed by the circumstances of life from childhood into youth. It is like molten iron poured into a mold: once cold, it sets in fixed shape, and is hard to change again.

Wang's character was of the type rich in feeling and wanting in reason; and the man rich in feeling is easily ruled by feeling. So his weakness of will, his inner contradiction, his impulsiveness in affairs, his changeableness, all had their linked chain of cause and effect. I have lately come upon the monthly The Spirit of the Age, issued at Chongqing in August of the twenty-eighth year, in which a piece by Mr. Zheng Xuejia discussing Wang Jingwei says in one passage:

"Wang's life-history, though it had its most stirring, vigorous times, shows that he had no understanding of the whole course of the nation's historical development, so that all his acts were mere impulse; his praiseworthy acts of the past were impulse, and his going over to the enemy and selling the country now is also a kind of impulse."

This judgment is truly most apt. It is just as I said in the second section of this essay: "The Wang of that day was in truth no unworthy revolutionary of ardent and mournful song, fit to make all under heaven pay him solemn respect.… Yet on no account overlook the most important point in all this: his willfulness and his impulse of feeling." And again: "So whether he was loyal or traitorous, good or evil, hung on a single passing thought; and that 'single passing thought' was a thing even he himself could neither foreknow nor hold in hand." Set side by side, they tally like the two halves of a tally-stick—the "impulse" is one and the same.

Most delightful of all is a passage in the Young China Party's Guolun Weekly of April of the twenty-eighth year, criticizing Wang's character, which runs as follows:

"Wang is a thorough and genuine Chinese scholar of the old style; and Chinese scholars of the old style have the following faults. First, they often have a mood past all fixing, singing and weeping without cause, gloomy and glad by turns; though the company be all in high spirits, he can suddenly be overcome with a sense of drifting and falling. Second, the old-style scholar has, as a rule, a megalomania: though what he sees be common and again common, he always fancies he has some secret got of himself alone, and so looks down on all others, able to make others especially small and himself especially large; and therefore, at the least reverse, he often cannot master his own resentment. Third, the old-style scholar is least fit of all to do politics, yet loves politics most, because China's politics in the past were fundamentally romantic, and this best suits the scholar's palate. Fourth, Chinese literature has never regarded logic; and so the Chinese old-style scholar has only feelings, has ardor, has impulse, but is by no means good at thinking; his perception is fairly keen, and so he can stand no stimulus."

Look at this passage—light and offhand, lifting a heavy thing as though it were light, yet it digs into Wang Jingwei to the very marrow; and I believe the reader too will nod and say it is so, for it is put all too aptly.

At the same time, in an article criticizing Wang by another hand, a Japanese named Yoshioka Bunroku, he likens Wang to an earthworm, calling him a boneless flesh-worm. He says in brief:

"Mr. Chiang, at a single glance, gives one the feeling of the awe of a strong man; Hu Hanmin makes one feel a gravity, a gravity that leaves one unable to breathe; Wang Jingwei's character is soft, his voice as tender as a kitten's, and the characters he writes are just like a woman's hand; and the clique that runs with Wang, from Chen Gongbo down to the dead Tang Youren and Zeng Zhongming, are all men soft and feminine in the extreme."

One sees that this Japanese named Yoshioka Bunroku was well acquainted with certain figures of our political stage, and skilled besides at observation. His account of Wang has real insight, only it goes rather from jest into cruelty.

If it be true that the characters a man writes can really represent his nature, then Wang's writing was indeed somewhat feminine. As I see it, Wang's hand took after Zhao, but was closer still to Dong Qichang—like in look, but softer than he. The writing of the two, Zhao and Dong, is famed for its tender grace, so no wonder Wang was especially fond of following in their steps.

Again by the Guolun Weekly's words, "Wang is a thorough and genuine Chinese scholar of the old style": then Wang's pen and his tongue were truly a matchless pair. The pity is only that, able both to write and to speak, he yet "regarded no logic," and "had only feelings, ardor, impulse, but was by no means good at thinking." Wang was not only skilled in letters but an outstanding orator; among the notables of the Nationalist Party, for speech-making none could move men as he did, and even an address of no great content came off pleasing to the ear. The reason lay in his speaking with feeling. I remember, about May of the twenty-seventh year, hearing him speak at Changsha, the subject being, if I recall, "The Meaning of Saving the Nation from Ruin and Seeking Its Survival"; his wording was apt, his tones rising and falling, his manner impassioned, and when he came to the stirring passages his own eyes glistened with tears, so that some of the audience were moved to weep aloud there and then. I thought: with the country holding so remarkable a talent, able to work with the Generalissimo Chiang and lead the resistance, what had China to fear! And so I worshipped him the more. Who could have thought that in a scant half-year and more he would be heard of as gone over to the enemy? That a man could so change chilled the heart, and gave one besides a sense of having been deceived.

Indeed, Wang's bearing, his manner, his conversation were all first-rate; but outside and inside did not match, and men of this kind most easily deceive. So those who knew him well passed on, in private, a four-line verdict of him: "To your face, all courtesy; in talk, all sincerity; behind your back, all cursing; in the doing, all inconstancy." General Zhang Fakui, in a circular telegram, mocked him in like terms, saying in brief: "When he receives a guest, his manners are perfect; the moment he turns his face, the cursing follows hard upon."

Wang was just such a man; having neither the awe-inspiring rectitude to stand in the high hall nor the magnanimous and forgiving spirit, how could he but fail?

To speak again of that name of his, "Jingwei"—originally a pen name used in his early writing, which he yet kept his whole life long, while his real name "Zhaoming," save in official documents, was seldom seen elsewhere. We do not know what meaning he took in adopting the two characters "Jingwei" at the time. By the "Classic of the Northern Mountains" in the Classic of Mountains and Seas: "On the Fajiu Mountain there is a bird, named Jingwei; it calls its own name, and forever carries wood and stone from the Western Mountains to fill up the Eastern Sea." And by the Record of Strange Things: "The daughter of the Fiery Emperor drowned in the Eastern Sea and turned into the Jingwei bird, ever carrying wood and stone from the Western Mountains to fill the Eastern Sea." And by the Treatise on Broad Matters: "The Fiery Emperor's daughter drowned and became the Jingwei; she pairs with the sea-swallow, and bears young—the female called Jingwei, also named the wronged bird; the male called sea-swallow." This is of course an old myth of ours; that a tiny bird should carry wood and stone to fill the Eastern Sea is a plain labor in vain. So I have reason to believe that Wang, seeing how arduous was the road of China's revolution, took the name "Jingwei" to figure his own painstaking and dogged resolve to do what he knew could not be done; beyond this, there is no better explanation. But he ought to have thought too of "the sea of regret is hard to fill," and that within his consciousness there already lurked the seeds of pessimism and defeatism; and moreover the "Jingwei" is a female bird, transformed from the wronged soul of the Fiery Emperor's daughter—does this not, too, betray his feminine bent of nature? Chinese men of letters have always gone in for the notion of the "poem-omen" and the "word-omen"; and with "Jingwei, the wronged bird," he had indeed committed the omen of his own name. This may be talk beside the point, but was Wang's changefulness and his flawed late years a mere chance?

### (5) A Master Hand at Verse, Writing His Whole Inner Journey

To speak of Wang Jingwei's verse: overflowing with talent and of high attainment, he should be reckoned one of the masters since the founding of the Republic. Only the content and cadence of his verse ran more to the plaintive and clinging and less to the bright and open, so that some called it "the music of a doomed state," and Mr. Wu Zhihui even likened him to the Latter Ruler Li. Reading closely his Verses from the Double-Reflection Tower, and matching them against the several points argued in the Guolun Weekly, one feels these words not without reason: this was a man fit only to discuss poetry and letters, and never to meddle with politics.

The ancients said "poetry speaks the will"; the making of verse can often stand for the author's inmost voice.

Many of Wang's verses bear on his political life. In them one may glimpse too his thought, his temper, and the tracks and traces of his inner journey; these chantings all have value for reference. Here I select the chief of them and add a brief gloss:

In the first month of the twenty-sixth year Wang returned from Europe to join in the national ordeal. On the seventh of July that year the Marco Polo Bridge Incident duly broke out. In the fourth month of the twenty-seventh year, having business at Changsha, he wrote "The Azaleas in Full Bloom on the Nanyue Road, a Quatrain":

"Sure enough the fire-virtue lights the southern land, the mountain haze turned all at once to purple rose-cloud; the hearts of four hundred million are wholly crimson, and shall surely make the flowers of freedom bloom over all!" And also "Ascending Zhurong Peak," a poem in the old five-character style:

{p} …From of old this place has bred many men of resolve, and the nation's peril has never run so deep as now! Ah—the red of the mountain flowers is your patriot heart, the mottling of the Xiang bamboo your tears of grief for the country.

From these two poems, one sees that in the early resistance he was in mind full of patriotic ardor and hope of victory, holding that with the whole nation united, high and low of one heart, freedom and triumph must surely come.

Yet a bare three months later, on the boat into Sichuan, he wrote "On the Night of the Eighth of July, Moored at Mudong; Tomorrow I Should Reach Ba County," a poem in seven-character regulated verse, and his mood had already greatly changed. The poem ran:

{p} The gorge shuts its layered gates, untangled; mooring the boat, I still catch the slanting dusk-glow;
{p} the crescent moon's shadow steeps in the glassy water, the sun's rays melt into amber clouds.
{p} On the sandbanks the wild geese are just now gathering; in the sky the Herdboy and Weaving-Maid part again.
{p} The river runs east and the man goes west; forlorn, I hear the sound of the waves beside my pillow.

The mood shown in the last four lines of this poem is the very opposite of the two poems before: the former showed unity and striving, the winning of freedom for nation and people; the latter shows contradiction and rupture, the breaking-up and scattering of the flock—as if it were, four months in advance, a prophecy of his leaving the ranks of the resistance and stealing quietly away.

In early April of the twenty-seventh year, the Nationalist Party's Extraordinary National Congress was meeting at Hankou, electing the Director-General and Vice-Director-General, restoring the system of a single leader, and choosing the Duke Chiang as Director-General. Did this poem, written into Sichuan, express his discontent at the outcome of the election? But both "The Azaleas in Full Bloom" and "On the Boat into Sichuan" were written after the election was over, and ought not to be so contradictory, the one after the other. Moreover, from the end of April to the beginning of August the People's Political Council was meeting at Hankou, and all were pressing toward a new stage of unity and cooperation, with no serious dispute over the national policy of resistance;

yet so heavy a change came over his mood—it is truly hard to fathom. One can only call it the temperament of a man of letters, the flaw of his character: the singing and weeping without cause, the gloom and gladness by turns. So that Wang was throughout a tragic principal in the history of politics, and not without reason.

Not three months after his flight, his closest friend and his ablest aide, Zeng Zhongming, was sanctioned and killed at Hanoi. Then his nephew Shen Song (Cigao), also his faithful disciple, was slain at Hong Kong. The deaths of these two men wounded him deeply in spirit and struck a grave blow at his treasonous doings; and so his chantings mourning the two are especially many. Thereafter, too, all the grief and rage and sorrow of his puppet-political life he would often pour out under cover of chanting the memory of these two. In the twenty-eighth year he wrote three ci—"Song of the Golden Threads," "The Beautiful Lady Yu," and "The River All Red"—and in the Arbor-Day of the thirty-second year a poem in the old five-character style, all bearing on the mourning of the two, such as "Though the ashes of doom go cold ten thousand years, the feeling is warm still," and "With both hands I grasp the tree's branch, with both eyes the tears drip on its root; the old friend cannot be seen, but seeing the tree is as seeing the man," and "The old friend's hot blood was not shed in vain—I draw it into the River of Heaven, once to wash the sacred land." These too show his indignation and resolve. The ci being long, I do not set them down in full.

After the deaths of Zeng and Shen, Wang published the "Yan Telegram," and at once received from our Party Central the punishment of expulsion and the revoking of all offices; and the attack of national opinion grew fiercer by the day; and the Konoe cabinet that had answered him like drum to drumstick had also stepped down. At this the wandering, grieving Wang laid down his pen in desolation. Yet when, in the fifth month of the twenty-eighth year, he returned from signing the country-selling "peace treaty" with the Hiranuma cabinet at Tokyo, his mood brightened again. In the sixth month he wrote a seven-character regulated verse, "Night on the Boat," expressing just this state of mind. He wrote:

{p} Lying, I hear the bell tell out the deep of night; over sea and sky the fading dream is dim, past finding;
{p} the song leans in the deck-cabin, the wind still ill; the beacon-tower a faint blur, the moon half in shadow.
{p} Good friends fall away, gone with a thousand dooms; the sacred land I see again a hundred years sunk;
{p} in desolation I make no "lonely orphan's" sigh, but reckon up a lifetime's heart not fully spent.

This is to say that, with Japan's support, he could set up a puppet organization and realize his dream of "peace and national salvation"; he felt that, though the situation was perilous and good friends sacrificed, he must still do his utmost, and need not in desolation make the "lonely orphan's" sigh.

But from the sixth month of the twenty-eighth year down to the ninth month of the twenty-ninth—in this year and more—the results his "peace movement" produced were most unpromising. He counseled the Guangdong soldiers to resist Japan no more, with no result; his nephew Shen Song was slain;

he convened at Shanghai a so-called "Nationalist Party Congress," most coldly attended; he secretly sent men with money to Chongqing to invite certain persons to attend, and no one accepted; of the three important figures of Wang's clique, Gu Mengyu and Gan Naiguang paid him no heed at all, and Chen Gongbo, though he came to Hong Kong, was passive and would not go to Nanjing or Shanghai (it was in the end Chen Bijun who found a way to force him to go); in the first month of the twenty-ninth year, Gao Zongwu and another important peace figure suddenly exposed at Hong Kong the whole secret pact Wang had signed with Hiranuma; on the thirtieth of March that year his puppet government was, with difficulty, set up at Nanjing, but after two months and more Japan actually would not recognize it; in the fourth month Chen Gongbo made a public speech demanding that Japan respect its dignity; in the fifth month Chen Gongbo went to Japan to see the Emperor, who received him in the full-dress uniform of Grand Marshal of the army and navy, but Chen, loathing Japan deeply, deliberately wore plain clothes, to Japan's displeasure; at the same time Wang too made a broadcast address, especially raising the six words "integrity, courage, effort" to spur on the officials of his puppet organization—for all who joined that organization did nothing but seize the chance to make money and take their fill of pleasure, awaiting the punishment they must one day earn. Grieved that this greedy and corrupt air, this mood of "having today with no thought for tomorrow," would make his "peace movement" come at last to nothing, he wrote a seven-character regulated verse, "Reading History":

{p} The oil-stealing rat's greed knows no bound; the blood-gorged mosquito in the curtain, too heavy, cannot fly; through the ages, dying for wealth is one same rut—yet the burning navel still envies Duke Dong his fat.

Under the pressure of so vile a situation, Wang's ambition and desire alike faded, and optimism turned to pessimism; so in the Double-Ninth of the twenty-ninth year his ci "The Beautiful Lady Yu" carried the lament that follows:

{p} Autumn comes and carves away the green of the hills; I too have added white to my head; walking alone, forlorn, is grief enough—how much more with heaven all thorns and earth all brambles, and nowhere to return!
{p} Shutting my door, I make no plan to climb the height; yet I too pluck the dogwood and weep; who says the brave man does not come back alive?—look, the sound of the lute-mallet and the shadow of the strike fill the world of men.

A year before, that easy mood of "in desolation I make no lonely orphan's sigh" was swept clean away. Now it had become the lament "walking alone, forlorn, is grief enough—how much more with heaven all thorns and earth all brambles, and nowhere to return"—his wandering despair reached its last degree. Two months more, and he wrote a ci to the tune "Groping for Fish," its tone more mournful still. The original ran:

"On the first of November of the twenty-ninth year, at supper, the household suddenly pressed a cup of wine upon me; asking, I learned it was set for the day five years past, when I was cut down by the assassins and did not die; and so I composed this ci."

{p} I sigh how carelessly the springs and autumns have changed; before the lamp, the hair at both temples is not what it was. Through hardship I have kept this leftover life—only to learn that a leftover life is bitterer still. Do not trace it again; reckon that the wound cut to the bone is not yet the place of heart's-hurt. The wine done, and you and I—I ask, scratching my head, with a long sigh; chin in hand I sit in silence: what, after all, does it profit home and country?
{p} The wild goose's will—what golden pellet could daunt it? Fluttering, it has feathers left still; I swear to spend heart and strength to turn heaven and earth, nor feel the road long and barred. Try and count: how many old friends, their blood gone off as a river's flow! Alone in the courtyard I pace, and hear on the branch-tips the last leaves, the frost-wind battling alone, still as if calling the work-chant of men who haul.

This ci, though it too speaks bold and stirring words, is plainly the night-walker whistling—only to keep up a front for himself and stiffen his own courage; in the end it cannot cover the shadow of pessimism and loneliness, of pain past bearing, upon his heart. Of the affairs of home and country, and of his own political future, he had lost all confidence; the note of grief and choking sob had already come close on the heels of the Latter Ruler Li.

After the thirtieth year of the Republic, Wang's "peace movement" went daily downhill like a river in flood, and his misery and tedium grew by the day. In a poem in the old five-character style, "Inscribed on the Long Scroll Painted by Zishu," there are lines that show his state of mind:

{p} In youth I read Tao Yuanming's poems, and always thought of the hills and woods… At twenty I was caught in the world's upheavals, and this joy I dared not hope for; on the rough roads of the dust, every step was a snare; and if by chance I met fine hills and streams, only then were ear and eye set free for once. Dawdling toward sixty, the affairs of men grow the more clamorous; the climbing of heights I long ago gave up, and my dreams of return are but a daze; I dwell shut in and go not out my door, telling myself falsely it is because I am pressed with toil; on a night of wind and rain by the roof-beam, white-headed, I lift my face in vain…

To dwell shut in, not daring to go out the door, feeling at every step the net of a snare—is such a life not the very likeness of a prisoner's? An old man of sixty deceiving himself with the plea of public business, lifting his face to sigh in vain—how lonely and forlorn a thing that is!

In the sixth month of that year, at Tokyo in Japan, he wrote another "Song of the Golden Threads," in mourning of the seven comrades who, after the failure of the revolutionary army in the bingwu year, joined with him and Mr. Huang Kexing in planning a fresh rising:

{p} The old friends have each fulfilled their life's resolve, gone early to a cup of yellow earth at Yuelu, their hearts and souls leaning together; I ask, of those alive from that year, how many? A lone, solitary one, and no more—and his hair too gone thus to starry white! Broken waters and ruined hills fill the eyes, I sigh; though we meet, let no Xinting tears be shed; for the sake of the throwing-down of the pen, I sing till my finger is bitten through.

On the fourteenth of the same month there was a seven-character regulated verse, titled "On the Fourteenth of June, the Death-Anniversary of Elder Sister Fang Junying; Sitting Alone in the Boat, Grieved at Heart, and Mindful too of Younger Brother Zeng Zhongming":

{p} Once more toward the world's end this body is all that is left; the bright moon flying here—for what cause? Hung lonely, a broken brick, the shadow of rivers and hills; bitterly it lights the bleak and travel-worn wanderer. South-going, north-coming, all like a dream of dreams; parting in life and death comes all too often; year on year these tears are truly of no use—the road far, hard to turn back, the grave in early spring.

By the Mid-Autumn of that year he wrote another ci to the tune "Prelude to the Water Melody," sent to Chen Bijun, which ran:

{p} …Sated with the lone light, like ice and snow, in the lonely night; the silver shoals clear and shallow—how can they bear so much drifting duckweed? The wild geese come north and go again; the magpies fly south and halt once more; nowhere is not forlorn. Why grudge a thousand li of distance—let us share this one window's brightness.

These verses, lingering over things past and sighing at the scene before him, are full of the savor of the bleak and withered and falling—this is very easy to see.

Again, in the seventh month before Mid-Autumn, there was a seven-character regulated verse, "Composed by Chance in Early Autumn," whose last four lines ran:

{p} Setting my heart at large, I have forgotten what age this now is; showing my shadow, I know at last this body is all alone;
the nearer the dawn, the lonelier the man; the cock's crow far and faint, and I do not weary of its frequency." Around the eighth and ninth months there was again "Inscribed Offhand on the Painting 'Parting at the Yi River,' Two Long Verses," and the latter has the lines:

{p} Youth and vigor are now the frost at both temples; facing the painting again, I waver the more… A bounded land of hills and rivers, fit only for a fallen cauldron; no great flood of tears left to weep the lost sheep…

Of the real state of Wang's so-called "peace movement" in its last two years, something of the outline may be seen from these chantings. Lines like "walking alone, forlorn" appear again and again in his verse of these years, the natural welling-up of his loneliness and grief; and "the nearer the dawn, the lonelier the man; the cock's crow far and faint, and I do not weary of its frequency" is just like the rare footfall heard in an empty valley—much though one would wish to hear it, at the time his loneliness grew day by day, and it was as if, walking by night through a ruin, he wandered in fright and knew not what to do. And "a bounded land of hills and rivers, fit only for a fallen cauldron; no great flood of tears left to weep the lost sheep" is his utter despair of the "peace movement," with the deep feeling of one who repents too late. So his last ci, to the tune "Rite at the Court," appeared at the Double-Ninth of the thirty-second year:

{p} A hundred-foot city tower leans on the empty blue; on the geese's backs they fly just now low; fallen leaves rustle over all the ground, and the yellow flowers keep the slanting sun.
{p} I strike the railing aslant, the knots pent in my heart, the scene beneath my eyes; I ask the green hills and the blue waters, how many risings and fallings can they bear!

Beneath this ci he annotated himself: "On the Double-Ninth I climbed the Beiji Pavilion, and reading a ci of Yuan Yishan, came to the lines 'The rivers and hills of the old country are fair as a painting; drunk, I forget the risings and fallings,' and grief would not cease in my heart, so I too composed one."

With this near-frantic dirge he brought his literary life to a close—which was as good as declaring that his life on the political stage too had from then let down its curtain.

A clap of thunder: on the tenth of August of the thirty-fourth year of the Republic, Japan was defeated and declared its surrender, and our eight years of bitter resistance won at last the final victory. The Wang puppet organization at Nanjing, in a single instant, vanished like smoke. And Wang Jingwei himself had already, on the tenth of November of the thirty-third year, died of illness at Nagoya in Japan, and was straightway brought back and buried at Plum Blossom Hill in the outskirts of Nanjing. That this man—his late years a stench, his body laden with sin—could yet escape the open execution of the nation's law, may be called luck too. He was born, it is said, in the twenty-eighth year before the Republic, and lived sixty-one years in all.
