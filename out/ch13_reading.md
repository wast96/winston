## 13

Lin Nansheng waited in a hotel room until midnight, and already he had a foreboding of how it would end. All at once he collapsed onto the bed as though drained of his blood, and lay there, eyes open, staring without a movement until daybreak, his body curled feebly, like a dead man.

But when he walked into the Dangui Theater that afternoon, he had returned to himself, only that both his eyes were shot through with blood.

In a box on the upper floor Lin Nansheng waited until the play had begun on the stage, and only then, amid the crash and clamor of gongs and drums, did he see the tea-boy lift the door curtain. The man who came in was, of all people, General Rong of the Operations Department of the Ministry of National Defense in Nanjing. The two of them started at the same instant; they had met on many occasions, more than once. But Lin Nansheng understood in a flash: this was a meeting worth paying for with one's life.

General Rong slipped a hand into his trouser pocket, where there was, most likely, a pistol with a round in the chamber.

Lin Nansheng said evenly, "If this is a trap, killing me will do you no good."

General Rong's face was without expression. He drew up a chair, sat, and glanced at the newspaper spread on the table. It carried the news of Old Pan's death, and with it a large photograph of the scene.

Lin Nansheng said, "Old Pan is dead. I am the man taking his place."

General Rong said, "He should have known I trust no one."

"The reason he chose to meet his death in a public place was so that you would see the news of it in the papers." Lin Nansheng turned to look at him. "And you should know too: were he alive, he would never have allowed anyone to come to you."

General Rong said no more. He sat up straight and watched the performance on the stage below.

So Lin Nansheng, after a sip of tea, began with Old Pan's chance arrest and carried it through to the moment before he had left the Cathay Hotel. To get to see me once, he sold himself out; to make them believe he had turned, he did not even shrink from sacrificing two of his own subordinates. Reaching this point, Lin Nansheng thought all at once of Miss Lan. He looked at General Rong. "You must believe me. And I must carry out the task he set me."

General Rong said not a word the whole time, his eyes on the performance, smoking one cigarette after another.

"I must be blown by now," Lin Nansheng said, and after a pause added, "Our time is short."

General Rong stubbed out the butt, drew another cigarette from the pack, struck a match and lit it, took a deep draw, and, through the smoke he let out, fixed his eyes on Lin Nansheng. "Old Pan should have told you the one thing that matters most."

Lin Nansheng faltered, ran through in his mind everything Old Pan had said, and then answered, "You are from the same district. You served together in the Nineteenth Route Army, and resisted the Japanese together in Shanghai... His name back home was Liu Zongming."

General Rong shook his head. "What I think he would have told you is this: that even knowing he was dead, I could still appear in this box — and that alone proves the intelligence I have to pass is worth more than our lives."

With that General Rong stubbed out the cigarette, rose, and lifted the curtain and left without a backward glance.

Lin Nansheng sat on in the box, dazed, and a long moment passed before he came to himself; only when he rose to go and saw the pack of cigarettes and the matches General Rong had left on the table did he let out a long breath.

Toward evening, following the address written inside the matchbox, Lin Nansheng came to the mortuary of the Anfu charitable coffin-house, drew an oilpaper packet from the body of a corpse about to be cremated, and went straight to Zhu Yizhen's fallback home.

When the door opened, the color drained all at once from Zhu Yizhen's face. "You really are a ghost that won't be laid," she said.

"Once I've found you, I won't let you out of my sight again." Halfway through the words Lin Nansheng already regretted them. Once more he thought of Miss Lan; something seemed to stop up his chest, and he lowered his head at once. When Lin Nansheng raised it again to look at Zhu Yizhen, he said, "I need your wireless set, and the cipher."

"Dream on." Zhu Yizhen was making supper, an apron still tied about her. Without so much as turning her head she went into the kitchen.

Lin Nansheng followed to the kitchen doorway, drew the oilpaper packet from his pocket, and, watching the side of her face, said, "This is the Ministry of National Defense's just-approved deployment of forces in the Liao-Shen region, and the plan to reinforce Changchun."

Zhu Yizhen started, and turned. "Just what are you?"

"I am the man your people once schemed with all their might to make of me." Lin Nansheng crossed to her, held the oilpaper packet out before her, and went on, "For this, Old Pan is dead, and my wife's fate is now unknown. You have to send it out."

But Zhu Yizhen did not look at him, nor at the oilpaper packet. She slowly set down the knife she had been cutting vegetables with, untied the apron, laid it on the counter, and walked out of the kitchen in silence to the window, where she looked at the darkening sky. All at once she wanted so badly to weep, so badly to scream.

By the time Meng Annan got home the sky had gone wholly dark. Not the least surprise showed on his face. He sat at the table and finished the rice in his bowl, went on listening until Lin Nansheng had told it all, then went to the kitchen and rinsed his mouth a good while before coming out. "I believe all of this is true, but I have to report to the organization, and it has to be verified." Meng Annan looked at Lin Nansheng. "That is procedure."

Lin Nansheng nodded. "Then how long do I wait?"

"Until I come back." With that Meng Annan took up his bag and hurried out.

Lin Nansheng sat at that table until noon of the next day, and just as his whole body was beginning to stiffen, Meng Annan opened the door and came in. He glanced at Zhu Yizhen seated at the other end of the table and said to Lin Nansheng, "We could only confirm Index Finger's identity. In 1941 he withdrew to northern Jiangsu with the New Fourth Army office; in 1942 he went to study at the Anti-Japanese Military and Political University in Yan'an, after which a reassignment back to Shanghai can't be ruled out. But I can find nothing whatever about you. You'll have to give me time."

"It won't give us time." Lin Nansheng lifted the oilpaper packet in his hand. "You have to send it out."

Meng Annan looked again at Zhu Yizhen, gave a nod, and said, "Do as he says. Send it to the East China Bureau, for onward relay to Xibaipo."

"But..."

"No buts. The higher-ups will judge whether the intelligence is genuine." As he spoke Meng Annan took the oilpaper packet from Lin Nansheng's hand and passed it to Zhu Yizhen. "Go, quickly. That's an order."

When Zhu Yizhen had gone, Lin Nansheng let out a long breath and leaned back in his chair. He looked at Meng Annan as if to say something, but in the end did not speak.

Meng Annan smiled, glanced at the leftover food on the table, then turned and took half a bottle of foreign liquor from the cupboard. "Have a drink," he said. "Get some sleep."

Lin Nansheng nodded obediently, took the glass, drained it at a draught, and said, "Aren't you afraid this is a trap?"

"I only did what had to be done." Meng Annan gave a sudden smile and shook his head, looking at Lin Nansheng, though it was more as if he spoke to himself: "Trust is a strange thing sometimes."

Lin Nansheng started, and his bloodshot eyes went suddenly wide. He remembered: in that brothel run by the Italian, Gu Shenyan had once said the very same words.

Late one night not long before the Mid-Autumn Festival, Lin Nansheng at last left Shanghai. He had stayed a full half-month in the sitting room of Zhu Yizhen's home. This was on Meng Annan's repeated insistence: You are secretly wanted by the Baomiju; so long as you don't step out that door, you are safe in Shanghai. Lin Nansheng only smiled, knowing well that until those two pieces of intelligence were finally confirmed, he would be safe nowhere. He began to drink again — at first sending Zhu Yizhen out to buy it two bottles at a time, drinking by the window in the day, and at night, unable to sleep on the sitting-room floor, sitting cross-legged in the dark to drink. Later Zhu Yizhen simply had the boy from the sauce shop haul up a jar of Shaoxing wine. "We can't afford anything better," she said.

Lin Nansheng said, without lifting his head, "It doesn't matter."

But one day, not long after Zhu Yizhen had gone out, Lin Nansheng set down his glass and went into their room, and quickly searched the whole of it. At the bottom of a locked chest, at last, he found a plum-blossom-brand watch with no second hand.

The next day, when Zhu Yizhen came back from hanging the wash on the roof and had just sat down at her embroidery table, Lin Nansheng said suddenly, "The two of you are a sham married couple."

Zhu Yizhen faltered, straightened her back. "You no longer grieve for your wife?"

It was as though a needle had jabbed Lin Nansheng, but still he said, "Do you know what kind of man he is?"

Zhu Yizhen looked, without moving, at the sunlight on the windowsill. "Was she beautiful?"

There was much Lin Nansheng had long wanted to say, but in the end he let out not a single word. He kept his mouth shut tight, rose, ladled a cup of wine from the jar in the kitchen, came out, drank off half of it in one swallow, and went back to the kitchen to fill the cup again.

But before that jar of wine had been drunk to the bottom, the telegram confirming Lin Nansheng's identity arrived. In the instant she took the message down, Zhu Yizhen felt, of all things, her eyes brim with hot tears. She ran hurriedly upstairs, looked at Lin Nansheng, and it was a while before she got out a single sentence: "A wire from the old home... They're calling you back."

Lin Nansheng sat at the window without a sound, without lifting his eyes. He took the cup from the sill and slowly drank down the half-cup of Shaoxing wine in it.

Two days later, when Meng Annan drove him in a police car himself all the way to the Jiangsu line, the sky had already turned pale. He pulled the car over to the roadside, looked at his watch, and said, "We're early." Then he drew a pistol from inside his coat. "Take it. For your own protection on the way." And he added, "I only hope you won't need it the whole road."

Lin Nansheng took the pistol, checked the magazine with a practiced hand, racked a round into the chamber, and pressed it to Meng Annan's temple.

Meng Annan started. "There's a garrison up ahead. The shot will bring them down on us."

With his other hand Lin Nansheng fished the watch with no second hand from inside his coat. "You're a hawk Gu Shenyan set loose?"

Meng Annan nodded. "I know you were his student, but you didn't know I was his adopted son." He reached out, took the watch, looked at it, and went on, "Only, each of us chose his own road."

Then, under the muzzle, he told Lin Nansheng that since following Gu Shenyan from Vietnam to Hong Kong, he had been on Chinese soil now for nearly sixteen years, and all that while, under the name of a Juntong agent, had never once done a thing for them. On the contrary, what he did each day was the very work his parents had left unfinished.

Meng Annan's parents had both been followers of Ho Chi Minh; they had studied together in France, and it was there they came to know Gu Shenyan. But in the year Meng Annan turned ten, the two of them died together in a French prison in Saigon. In those days Meng Annan's name was Nguyen Chi Trung.

When he had said it, he turned his head so the muzzle came to rest at his forehead, and looked at Lin Nansheng. "When you reach the base area, you can go to the East China Bureau's political section. My file is there. My whole history is in it."

But in fact Lin Nansheng never reached the base area. Crossing the blockade line, the sampan he rode was sunk by fire from a blockhouse, and the courier escorting him was shot dead. Lin Nansheng swam in the water until his strength was spent, and when he woke he found himself lying on the deck of a cargo boat.

The man who saved him was a rice-shop owner down in the country collecting rents. He carried Lin Nansheng back to a small town on the outskirts of Shanghai, and, standing on the wharf where three rivers met, said, "Take a boat farther east and you're in greater Shanghai; south is Zhejiang province, and Jiangsu lies to the north."

Lin Nansheng said, "Then what place is this?"

The rice-shop owner said, "This place is called Xietang."
