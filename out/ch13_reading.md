## Chapter 13

"A solitary soul,

drifting away over the lake—

can it be the hills have changed,

that here you cannot stay?

A solitary soul,

—"

Mei Yingzi was humming a song with a Japanese lilt under her breath, toying with the water with the short oar in her hand; Bai Ping was gazing at the sky.

There were very few pleasure-boats on the lake, which deepened in me the loneliness of one returning jaded from his travels. The green water was very clear; the reflections of the blue hills held ten thousand moods of self-pity. The Su Causeway looked desolate, and the Bai Causeway too was forlorn to a dreadful degree, with a scattering of common folk and enemy troops walking upon it. I thought suddenly of my friends of the old days at the Art Academy—how they had laughed and raced and strolled there. Far off, Solitary Hill lay like a dream, where how many plum blossoms opened and fell of themselves. Niugong Mound was dim, the Three Pools Mirroring the Moon were forlorn; the old dreams all shattered, the old friends all scattered; and in the slanting sun I saw the bleakness of the human shadows on the water.

And who were these? A newly made American friend; a dissolute young lady I had only lately fallen in with; a mysterious dancing-girl met by chance, like duckweed drifting on the water. Why, then, was I among them, come to mourn amid this ruined scenery over the traces of my departed friends? And the old affections of yesterday's kin and companions—who among these people could understand this grief and this dream of mine? Who could enter into my present state of mind? A quiet pain, and an anxious tenderness, rose and fell in me with the flecks of golden ripple on the water.

We were all silent, letting the boatman drive the boat on. There was wind, and I felt the chill; so the dusk of early spring, it seemed, could hold the cold of late autumn too. Bai Ping seemed to shiver, and I took the overcoat she had laid before me and put it over her shoulders, saying:

"Desolate, isn't it?"

"…" She nodded.

The boat reached the bank at last, and we went to the hotel to rest. After dinner a cup of coffee and a cigarette were what it took to raise my spirits.

We talked for an hour, and played "bridge" for two. At ten o'clock I went back to my room to sleep; I had no book to hand, and not a sound was anywhere about; I turned off the light, and the moonlight came in at the window, and I felt the night pressing in, pressing in, from the open country. Everywhere, I felt, was night, everywhere was night; I huddled deep in the bedding, huddled into the corner of the quilt, but the night invaded my bed, invaded my quilt, soaked through my body, soaked through my heart, and at the last my soul dissolved away in that night.

When I woke I saw the room full of moonlight, and, tossing and turning, could sleep no more. There rose in my mind the mood of the lake that day: I thought of the human shadows, thought of Mei Yingzi, thought of Bai Ping, thought of what Bai Ping had said about Mei Yingzi in my rooms, and of what Mei Yingzi had said about Bai Ping on the hill. I began to perceive how mysterious they were, and how absurd and idle was this friendship of mine with them. And so I set to analysing myself: was it that they held some special power of attraction, or was it the discontent of my own life, that steeped me in this romantic diversion? Stephen was born to a romantic, adventurous nature—but I? I thought of Mrs. Stephen, of her serene and beautiful life, her love of art and her pursuit of beauty, and of what she had said to me. I felt I ought to give up the life I now led, give up my going about with Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping; I might go into the interior to do work for the war of resistance, or bury myself in scholarly work. But at once I thought of Mrs. Stephen's advice that I marry. Could it be that the contradiction of my life sprang from my very bachelorhood? Could it be that I truly needed the companionship of the other sex? And so I began to think of the dawn on the hill, of the stars and moon at the bottom of the sea; I thought of the lamp, thought of my own shadow beneath the lamplight, thought of Schumann's Reverie; I longed for my own room, as with homesickness, as with lovesickness; and I thought again of Mrs. Stephen's drawing-room, and all at once of her tea-party—Saturday—ah, Saturday; was not tomorrow Saturday? No—it was Saturday already, and I must go back. From this very day I would change my life—

Amid these wild fancies I fell asleep, and when I woke it was already half past eight. Outside the window the sunlight was brilliant, and the birds sang sweetly. Among the trees I saw Mei Yingzi standing, both hands breaking off a newly opened Chinese rose, and on her fingers flashed the diamond ring I knew so well—ah, then it was Bai Ping; no, it was Mei Yingzi, and Bai Ping's ring was on her finger.

After I had washed, several waverings settled me in the resolve of the night before. I found out from the waiter the times of the trains, and left a letter, in which I wrote:

"Today Stephen is host; but at four o'clock Mrs. Stephen too is a host of mine. First, that tea engagement came earlier; second, a lady's engagement is of course the weightier; third, I yearn for the air of that drawing-room. But I feared to shake your pleasure in the trip, and so I take my leave without a word—forgive my discourtesy. Amid these brilliant lakes and hills, where spring has come early for your visiting of it, I wish you a joyous outing."

In my pocket were two passes and tickets, one set of them Bai Ping's; these too I left inside the letter, wrote her name on it, and set it on the table. I slipped out unseen, jumped into a car, drove straight to the station, ate noodles at a little noodle-shop, and waited for the ten o'clock train.

At a quarter past ten I was sitting in a first-class carriage. The seats were all but empty; I had a compartment to myself, and there were many empty ones besides. I opened the tin of yellow-packet cigarettes I had just bought, put one to my lips and smoked it, and in the easiest of postures gazed out at the sunlit country beyond the window, feeling like a wanderer long absent and homeward bound, with home there before my eyes, a faint expectation and a serene gladness in me. I thought how Stephen and the others must by now have found me out in my flight, with nothing to be done; the three of them gone off up the hills, cursing me now and then—when suddenly a fresh red flower flew down from over my head and landed square on the table. Taking it for something someone had tossed amiss, I picked it up to return it to its owner. But no one before me had risen in expectation, nor was anyone behind me on the watch; I stood up and sat down again, and held the flower uneasily, waiting for someone to ask for it. Just then another flower, a white one, flew down over my head and landed square beside the cigarette-tin on the table; again I raised my head, but, looking before and behind, saw no one, and could only sit down and examine the flowers for any odd clue to go by, that might tell me their source and their purpose. Yet I gained nothing, save the sense that whoever had picked them understood the beauty of flowers—the stems were rather long, two or three leaves left on them, and the place of breaking well chosen. All at once I thought of Mei Yingzi: had she not been picking flowers in the garden outside my window as I rose? So the three of them had caught me up. I stood and looked behind me, but on the seats of the rear compartment there was no one to be seen; and so, waving the flowers in my hand, I turned to go out, and saw Bai Ping sitting aslant in the corner of the seat behind me, gazing at me with a faint smile. I sat quietly down in the seat across from her and said low:

"And the others?"

"Who?"

"Mei Yingzi and Stephen."

She sat up straight, the smile of a lily just opening rising to her face, and said quietly:

"If, when you boarded alone, someone had asked you that, how would you have answered?"

"I should have said, of course, that they were off enjoying themselves in the hills."

"That is just how I answer you."

"Then they have not come?"

"I do not know," she said.

"How do you not know?"

"While you supposed me off wandering the hills, did you know I was sitting behind you?"

"…" I could say nothing, but smiled and toyed with the flowers. Then I thought of the diamond ring I knew so well, and looked again at Bai Ping's fingers, and found that now it was on her hand once more; so the flower-picker of the morning must have been Bai Ping, and they had exchanged their rings back the night before. I said:

"Then it was you picking flowers in the garden this morning?"

"Yes," she said. "You took me for Mei Yingzi?"

"I saw you at the window; but then I thought, the ring was on Mei Yingzi's hand yesterday, and so I took you for Mei Yingzi."

"But a ring can be exchanged back at any time."

"When did you find my letter?"

"I picked the flowers, walked in the garden, and, crossing the passage, saw a waiter come out of your room. I asked whether you were up, and he said you had risen and just gone out. I meant to go in and wait for you to come back, but I found your letter; so I took the pass and the ticket, left a note, and came after you. On the platform I waited for you to board, waited for you to sit down, and only then got on myself."

"But why did you hurry after me?"

"And why did you?"

"Are my reasons not left in the letter?"

"My reasons too are in the note I left."

"A pity I never saw it."

"It is as well; the reasons in the letter are only on the surface," she said, smiling.

"You think my reasons too are only on the surface?"

"Of course."

"Then what is the true reason?"

"That is just what I would ask you," she said. "Did you not suddenly feel lonely?"

"What I felt…" I said, smiling.

"Could you feel lonely in Mei Yingzi's company?" she said. "What a rich soul there is for you to explore."

"I feel that only by leaving you all can I have a world of my own," I said firmly. "I am very fond of you, and of Mei Yingzi and Stephen; but how different your world is from mine. You have ten thousand kinds of radiance to make me covet and explore, but the end of it is that I leave my own world and press on toward your light. Exploring in your world, I believe I shall lose my way at last, and then, never able to grope my way back, I shall have no choice but to drift in your world as one of your good, obedient subjects."

"That is to say Mei Yingzi's radiance has shaken your bachelorhood, and you have taken fright." Bai Ping smiled, and her smile seemed to supply the name she had left out of her words—her own.

"I speak of my whole life," I said gravely. "It is the same with you and Stephen too; I mean, from today, no longer to be forever in your company. Will you respect this wish of mine, and help me to it?"

"Very well," said Bai Ping steadily. "I hope I can truly help you."

"Then,"—I grew excited with gratitude—"can you make Stephen understand and sympathize with this wish of mine?"

"Of course I can; and it shall certainly be done. Stephen has spoken to me already, saying that your friendship with him would go more happily as a friendship with his wife."

"Perhaps that is so," I said. "So I mean to go to Mrs. Stephen's tea-party; and little by little my mind will grow calm, and first of all I must finish a book on philosophy."

"What else can I do to help you?" said Bai Ping, bending her head and toying with the ring, in earnest. "I am always your friend."

"Yes. Bai Ping, let us be friends; when there is no one at your home I shall come to see you now and then, and you may come to see me at any time. But I shall enter no more dance-halls or gambling-dens, and give myself no more to amusement."

"Yes, you are right to do so," said Bai Ping.

A peaceful, serene silence began between us; then suddenly Bai Ping asked:

"And if Mei Yingzi comes to see you?"

"I shall receive her, but not go out with her—it is all one," I said. "Besides, her acquaintance is wide; she will forget me at once, and come to see me no more."

"But she is very fond of you."

"She has told you so?"

"Yes; we talked two whole nights," Bai Ping laughed. "And she is convinced that you love her a little."

"Do you believe it?"

"I cannot but believe it."

"You think her worth loving?"

"Worth it, of course," she said; "but it is a hazardous thing."

"You mean, to be made a fool of by her?"

"Or even to be brought to ruin by her," she said. "She is too mysterious; with a character like that, I do not believe she has any love in her."

"But she is very fond of you."

"She told you so?"

"Yes—that very day, on Geling," I said.

"I too love and honour her greatly," said Bai Ping with a sweet smile.

"I think the two of you might be very good friends."

"Perhaps," she said; "but most likely, too, rivals."

The train was going fast, the far hills and near rivers wheeling past the window, and my talk with Bai Ping filled my heart with a comfort and gladness past telling. I rose, went to my former seat to fetch the tin of cigarettes, and, lighting one on my return, asked:

"Did Mei Yingzi never speak to you of her own feelings?"

"When she said she was very fond of you, I asked her whether it was a little love. She laughed aloud, and said she had never yet given her love to anyone; that she was ready to give it, at any moment, to some man, but that no man had ever been worth her loving." Bai Ping bent her head and said, smiling: "She said, too, that she had a special understanding and reading of men; she said Stephen was a good friend and a good husband, but a dull lover; that you were the most delightful and engaging of friends, the most ideal of lovers, but the hardest of husbands to get on with. As for your bachelorhood, she said, Mrs. Stephen thinks it is that you cannot find an ideal partner, but she herself thinks it is only that you fear to discharge a husband's duties—a wish to run away."

"You think all this is right?"

"There is some truth in it, of course."

"But my bachelorhood, it may be, I shall soon give up."

"You mean, for Mei Yingzi's sake?"

"No; to speak truly, I do not love her," I said. "I only feel that Mrs. Stephen's advice to me was very right."

We were silent a while; then the waiter announced that the meal was served, and I went with Bai Ping to the dining-car. The food was dear and bad, but we passed the time very pleasantly all the same, for today Bai Ping made a happier impression on me; we talked of the past, of the future, of the city, of the country. At last I said:

"Bai Ping, will you cling forever to a life like this?"

"Not necessarily," she said. "But while there is no love, I shall spend my youth enjoying such a life."

"But youth does not last long," I said.

"And what is life? When youth is enjoyed to the full, one may as well die."

"Is it so simple?" I said. "Dying is not easy, either."

"Then marry some plain, honest, simple, older man," she said, half smiling and half not, "marry and settle down to wait for death."

Bai Ping's words left me with no answer; I grew aware of the greyness of the air, an oppressiveness hard to breathe in. After a long, long while, the train stopped at a small station, and we went back to the passenger carriage. I said:

"As soon as we reach Shanghai I'll see you home first, then take you with me to Mrs. Stephen's tea-party; afterwards we'll dine, and then everyone home."

"No," she said. "I shall not go to the tea-party."

"Why?" I asked. "Did she not invite you?"

"She told me she would be glad to have me go along with you."

"But you have no wish to."

"Somehow," she said, "today I very much want to rest."

"Then rest a while now—take a little nap, will you?"

"I'll try," she said with a smile, and, settling her posture, leaned into the inner corner and closed her eyes, the two rows of soft long lashes meeting, with a charm past telling.

I drew shut the half-open window, lit a cigarette, and sat across from her.

As one cigarette was near its end, I saw that she had fallen asleep; I took her overcoat and covered her with it, and, catching her faint breathing, saw the thin lips closed in harmony with the soft black lashes. Today she seemed to have put on no rouge, yet a natural flush showed through her face, doubling its loveliness. It was a sweet type of face, and I could not but keep my eyes on her; I drew a notebook from my pocket and tried to make a pencil sketch of her, but one after another—several of them—came out unlike; by the sixth I got at last a little of the flavour of it, and afterwards, having grasped what was distinctive in her, I drew one that I felt was quite good.

As the train was nearing its stop, I woke Bai Ping; she seemed still to crave sleep, but roused herself at once and said with a smile:

"What has come over me?"

"You are too tired."

"I talked too late with Mei Yingzi last night," she said, and, touching her brow with her hand, added with a faint smile:

"I do hope I'm not falling ill."

I began to see that the flush of her face was a sign of fever; I took her hand, and her fingers were very cold, but her palm burned dry and hot; she carried my hand to her brow. Truly, Bai Ping was ill.

Getting off the train, I saw her all the way to her lodgings, where a young, quick-witted maid in white came to the door. I had been to the door of her apartment many times, but had never been in her rooms; today was the first time, and I marvelled that in the past I had never thought to come in. It was so exquisite an apartment; her room was not large, but most delicately done. I began to discover her fondness for silver: the bedspread was silver, the sofa silver, the curtains silver; the pale-grey wall was half papered with silver silk; on the floor lay a silver carpet, and a whitish-grey fur rug was spread before the bed, on which stood a pair of silver bed-slippers.

"Sit down," said Bai Ping before one of the sofas, and went into the bathroom herself.

The lively, healthy maid brought in tea, gave me a cigarette, and went out. I lit it, sat down on a low little sofa, and idly took in the arrangement of the room. A tiny writing-desk with its chair stood beneath the window—drawers on one side, on the other two tiers of bookshelves crammed with books; on the desk too were some books and the like, and an elegant box for holding letter-paper and envelopes. Beside the bed was a low lamp-cabinet. On one side stood a wardrobe, and there were four sofas like the one I sat in; before them a low round brass tray, spread with white linen, held a dainty cigarette-box of Japanese make, an ash-tray and a lighter, and matches besides. I stubbed out my cigarette-end in the ash-tray, took another from the box, and tried that gleaming white lighter.

Bai Ping had changed out of her clothes and washed off all her powder and paint, and wore a loose grey cheongsam; the moment she came out she said:

"So I shall not go to the tea-party."

"Of course," I said. "Get to sleep quickly."

"I can sit a while." She sat down smiling beside me, and added: "Do you think my room fine?"

"It is Bai Ping's room, and no mistake."

"Thank you," she said, and, seeming a little tired, looked at her watch and said: "You should be off to the tea-party, and I must sleep."

"Very well," I said, rising. "I'll come to see you tomorrow."

As I went out at the door, she rose and seemed to make for the bed. Alone I came out into the street and walked toward the tram stop; passing a chemist's, I remembered that Bai Ping might do well to take a little aspirin before sleeping, and so I bought the medicine, bought some fruit as well, and went back to Bai Ping's lodgings.

Bai Ping was already lying in bed; I told the maid to pour some hot water, gave her the tablets to swallow, and said:

"What would you like to eat in the night?"

"I don't want anything at all."

"Very well," I said. "And even if you are hungry, be sure to eat little."

The maid drew the curtains to, and Bai Ping reached out and turned on the lamp on the stand. I said:

"Sleep well." She drew her hand back in, and I tucked the quilt close about her, and said:

"Now I shall go."

"Ah Mei—call a car for him," she said, seemingly to the maid.

"Very well," I said.

Ah Mei went to telephone in the passage, and Bai Ping said:

"When will you come to see me tomorrow?"

"In the morning."

"Take your meal here," she said, and gave a yawn.

"Very well," I said, and put out the lamp for her; she smiled at me and turned over. I stood up, and there rose in me all at once a strange sensation, as though a silver air had seeped into my breast, and I held myself in check a moment. A silver girl lay ill in a silver room; what manner of life was this, contending with its own youth against the passing of time? I could not tell whether it was tragedy or comedy; but today I began to know that silver, after all, is the emblem of a hidden desolation and a faint sorrow.

And with that hidden desolation and faint sorrow rippling in my heart, I sprang into the car.
