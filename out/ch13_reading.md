## The Water Dog

Back in the capital from Yoshino, Kuroami abruptly gave up the whetstone shop behind Hōkō-ji and, without so much as a pause, shut himself away in the ruined temple in Rakshasa Valley. One man, then another, patiently, he began calling in again the rappa he had once turned loose.

By the hour the city had gone quiet with sleep they would come stealing in, night after night, out of the dark, up through the space beneath the floor or in at a window, soundless as wind. Kuroami sat on the rotted boards before the altar.

"Name?"

He would call it out low. To whoever gave some name and came in he handed money and a task and sent him off at once. That night too, before he had well caught the faint stir beneath the floor and could put the question—

"Mimi."

"Eh?"

"Mimi of Nabari."

The man raised a floorboard and crawled out. He stood, slow and heavy, in the middle of the hall.

"Have you forgotten me?"

He pointed at his own small face. He would not yet have reached fifty, but the front teeth were gone, top and bottom, so that when he grinned his face turned fishlike. Rags hung from his shoulders; he had all the look of a beggar. Kuroami's gloomy face eased at last out of its suspicion.

"Mimi, is it. No, I've not forgotten. But I'd long taken you for dead. In the fighting of the Tenshō years — you were holed up in the Tsuge redoubt, were you not?"

"Ah, that fighting."

At the word the man covered his face with both hands. His endurance must have worn very thin, for the tears ran without stopping from his red, festered eyes. The savage memory of the Tenshō Iga rising must have come crowding back on him all at once.

"Of all who were in that redoubt with me, not one is left alive in the world now. My own jōnin, Lord Tsuge Yoshimune, was struck down by a stray ball."

"I'd thought you one of them."

"I barely got clear …"

"Thirteen years since. And what have you been at?"

"Begging."

The man sank down as though his legs had folded under him.

"With the world at peace like this, what other way is there for the likes of us rappa to keep body and soul together?"

"There's thieving."

"So you're a thief after all."

"No thief, but something near it."

For the first time Kuroami's dark face broke into a smile. As if to cling to that smile, the man went on:

"I was begging outside the temple at Saginomori in Kishū when I ran into an old comrade from the rappa, and he told me if I came to you there might be work with money in it. So here I am. I'm starving. If there's work, I'll do anything."

"But—"

Kuroami's look went hard again of a sudden, cold as a blade.

"There'll be nothing left in you now of the shadow-warrior's mettle."

"I couldn't say."

The man's face was baffled, as though he did not know himself.

"Try me the once."

"Then stitch this."

Kuroami drew a well-honed kozuka from his short sword and tossed it down by the man's knee.

"Right hand or left?"

"Whichever. As you please."

At the word the man took up the kozuka in his right hand, mouth shut tight, and held his left hand out steady.

A withered, leaf-like palm — the blackened skin barely clinging to the bone — trembled a little above the point of the blade. His face twisted; a sticky sweat ran from his brow. His breath came ragged, and he shut his eyes in his pain.

"What are you at?"

Kuroami's voice was low. There was contempt in his eyes. What sort of ninja Mimi of Nabari had once been, Kuroami had no real way of knowing: within the single province of Iga there was a difference of loyalty between the Tsuge faction and the Shimotsuge faction. But nothing was more hateful to him than to watch, with his own eyes, a ninja run to ruin. For this calling — a barefoot walk across the edge of a blade — it was not skill so much as the hardness of the spirit that upheld it that had always been held first. That the man had come down as far as beggary was no fall from grace. But that a slackness had crept into the sinews of his spirit — that was a fall past all bearing. Now the man had to stitch his own hand, with his own hand. By watching that act closely Kuroami meant to take the measure of this new man's worth as a ninja.

"Losing your nerve?"

Not the least use to me, Kuroami muttered, and stood up as if the whole thing had suddenly gone flat for him.

"Wait — wait."

The man clutched at Kuroami's hem, a desperate light in his festered eyes.

"I'll — I'll do it."

"………"

Kuroami looked down at him without a word. In the man's eyes was that desperation of one who knew that if he let this chance slip he would be back tomorrow to holding off his hunger. Terror of that hunger set the kozuka in his grip again. The point touched, just, the hollow of his palm, and quivered.

"Why shut your eyes?"

"Hyah—!"

As if driven by Kuroami's voice, the man's kozuka ground through the bone and pinned his palm clean through. The point, dripping blood, had passed out through the back of the hand and stood just clear; and while the man stared at it, glassy-eyed—

"Th-there. Is that enough?"

"Draw it out. Slowly."

"Like this?"

As he spoke the man writhed with the pain. The bone made a grinding sound, but Kuroami's face did not change.

"Too quick."

"H-have mercy."

Beside himself now, the man ripped the kozuka out in one pull. Without even wiping the blood that ran down his fingers he twisted his upper body toward Kuroami, pleading:

"There — now you'll take me on, won't you?"

"No."

Kuroami spat it out flat, without a shred of feeling.

"What—"

"In the little time it took to stitch one palm, how many times did you cry out? What use is a ninja with the marrow rotted out of him? Run a spear through the flank, hack off the hands and feet — an ordinary samurai I say nothing of, but an Iga man will not let so much as the sound of a breath escape him. That is the way of us."

Kuroami warped the beak-thin jut of his lips and laughed at him. Then he took a packet of dried rice from his breast and threw it down at the man's feet — thanks for your trouble, here, eat that — meaning, no doubt, to see him off no longer as a fellow rappa but as a plain beggar. The man snatched it up and, still on his knees, shuffled backward toward the window with a dry rustling, and—

"Kuroami. You'll remember this."

He sprang up at the window and vanished out of the dark hall. When Kuroami saw the faint smear of blood left on the wall below the sill, shaped like the flat of a hand—

"Beggar."

Kuroami took the kozuka up off the mat and scraped the bloodstain from the wall as though it were something filthy.

A ninja, in the end, is no samurai. He is despised by the common folk, who rank beneath the samurai — despised even by them; and yet to Kuroami's mind the one thing that let men of this calling live on in a separate, hidden world of the spirit, over against the rest of mankind, was the pride of a power that rose above the very machinery of human pain. Let a man step off that path and come back to being human, and how was he any different from a mere night-thief, a spy, a vagrant?

So it was that Kuroami picked out a dozen-odd rappa. He set these men to slip into Kyoto, Ōsaka, and Sakai, to go on thieving vigorously as before and to spread rumours; and at the same time, against the day of Jūzō's entry into the castle, he began looking closely into Hideyoshi's every movement.

Already, before this, in the eighth month of Bunroku 1, Hideyoshi had given orders to raise a castle on the hills of Fushimi, south of the capital. Even by the third month of this year the work on the main keep had begun. A man of many indulgences, Hideyoshi had a particular eye for building. Now and again he would come out to Fushimi to look the work over, and in high good humour would press them—

"The ridgepole's not up yet?"

— and the like. For his lodging at such times he used a corner of the Jurakudai, in Uchino in the capital, which he had already made over to his adopted son Hidetsugu. To assassinate Hideyoshi, slipping into Ōsaka Castle was hard indeed; but let him stop at this Jurakudai, and that, Jūzō and Kuroami both reckoned, would be the perfect chance.

Intelligence, disruption, thieving — Kuroami's rappa were parcelled out to their several duties and worked in good order. The chief of the reports on Hideyoshi's movements was carried, by one such rappa, from Rakshasa Valley to Jūzō's hermitage at Otogi Pass.

Kuroami went on with his night-thieving as before. Disruption might be the real aim, but the man plainly loved thieving for its own sake: once he had dropped down inside a wall in the dark his eyes shone like a beast's, his every motion melted into the night air, and his whole body took on such vigour it seemed to give off a phosphorescence.

One such night, having gone into the city on his work, Kuroami had loaded a subordinate with several stolen swords and got back as far as Ukiyo-bashi when a faint voice hailed him from behind. West of Ukiyo-bashi the townhouses stood in rows; to the east, the sub-temples of Sennyū-ji and the houses of the temple stewards stood scattered along both sides of an avenue of pines that ran out at last into the Sennyū-ji woods.

"Sounded like a voice, back there."

"Don't slacken your pace. Make as if you hadn't heard."

Kuroami went unarmed. He had already shed his ninja garb for his usual townsman's dress, but tucked against his breast he carried several shinobi throwing-blades, star-pointed and honed keen. He felt for them quietly against his bare skin, and—

"Listen. Don't lose your head and bolt for Rakshasa Valley — you'll give the hideout away."

"Understood."

"If it comes to the worst I'll run from behind Sennyū-ji up to the summit of Amidagamine, and if that fails, straight on into Ōmi. If it looks wrong, gather the men and set an ambush of them, hidden, at Amidagamine. What are you standing for? Quicken your step."

With a whisper of speed the man passed Kuroami and went on ahead. As luck had it there was no moon, and his shadow was swallowed at once into the dark. Kuroami, for his part, shortened his stride and waited for whatever might come from behind.

But behind him every trace had died away. A moment ago, true, he had felt no living presence — yet there had been a voice. Someone had called out to the two of them. Now, though, gather all his nerves into his back as Kuroami might and probe the dark behind him, there was only the vast, dim stir of the night air, and the rustle of the pine-tops carrying far off into the distance.

(This is …)

Something in him faltered. The one in the dark, he began to think, was no common enemy. The other had perhaps called out only to make sure this was indeed Kuroami. And by sending his companion on ahead the instant he was hailed, he had all but confessed his own true trade by the very act.

In any case, better to walk until the other showed himself — so Kuroami made up his mind. Back to Rakshasa Valley he would certainly not go. The other was watching from somewhere. It might be one man; it might be two. Laying his whole bearing open to their gaze, Kuroami walked on into the dark with a step to all appearances quite unstudied.

The white wall of Shuhō-in, a sub-temple of Sennyū-ji, ran along on his right. Where it broke off the road turned to enter the woods. Once in the woods there would be a boundless supply of pockets of dark for Kuroami to hide in.

(If he's to show himself, it will be about here.)

As though waiting for it Kuroami checked his stride a little, but all around lay the same deep, sunk dark as before.

Only, unlike a moment ago, one thing — some presence — grew thick about him. At times it brought on Kuroami the illusion that he could hear, close by his ear, the very breathing of a man.

(… I'll not be drawn.)

The other was plainly working to fret him. Even as he braced himself not to be caught by it, Kuroami's mind began, little by little, to chafe.

Kuroami's feet carried him on into the woods, and he set out along a path that ran the opposite way from Rakshasa Valley; but still nothing came at him. Nothing.

This path climbed toward a summit. Mountain it might be called, but it was no more than a shoulder of the Higashiyama range of the capital — nothing of any height. Only, the bare flank was so thick with scrub that in places he had to crawl beneath the leaves. Creeping under the branches, soundless as a rat, Kuroami came suddenly to himself and saw how badly he had handled the thing.

"Or else—"

— he thought. Was he not dancing here, all alone, in a phantom of his own making? Kuroami stopped moving hand and foot. It was then. As though it knew the very course of his thoughts, out of the mass of leaves behind him came a voice, low and crushed flat.

"Ho."

With a jolt Kuroami dropped low, as if he would sink into the ground.

"It's me. Don't you know? The man you once called beggar. A beggar I was, right enough. So I bear you no grudge, and I've no thought of paying you back. But don't be easy yet. Sorry to say it, but there's no mistaking I'm your enemy now. A rappa sides with the man who hires him — only natural, no? Your bad luck, that you would not hire me. … After you turned me off I heard word that others in the capital had lately wanted rappa. I cast about here and there, and found a band of Kōga men had moved in; I felt my way toward them, and there it was — Mari Dōgen of Kōga. Unlike you, he took me on at once. And what do you think — when I heard how the land lay, the man Dōgen has his eye on turns out to be none other than you. So of course I laid out the whereabouts of your Iga rappa's den, item by item. Tonight Lord Dōgen bade me guide him to that den, and I'd come as far as Ukiyo-bashi on the Sennyū-ji road when, luck would have it, there you were, out walking. That's the whole of it. — Ah, wait."

The man, catching the sign that Kuroami had slid a hand to his breast, said:

"Don't move. In Iga they called me Mimi of Nabari, and my keen ear was known well enough among our lot. However you move, I'll have it all by this ear. And moving does you no good — you're ringed about now with Kōga men."

"So you've sold out Iga."

"A man come down to beggary has neither Iga nor Kōga left in him. Unlike Iga, the Kōga folk are of an open humour. There's none of them treats a man as coldly as you did — a fine place to live, all told."

Kuroami slipped out of the mass of leaves, set himself sideways, and broke into a run down the mountain track without a sound. And still, close by his ear, Mimi of Nabari would not stop talking. Worse, by whatever trick he worked, the man who dogged him was nowhere to be seen. It was like hearing a voice out of the air, and Kuroami felt he would go mad.

Even as he ran, his breath began to fail. Since he had taken up the shinobi arts at three years old, such a thing had never once befallen him. To run a full ri with the face half wrapped in paper and never stir the paper with the breath — that was one of the trainings of the Iga men; yet tonight, for some reason, Kuroami's breath came heaving and wild, and the sound of it beat fretfully in his own ears.

"What ails you, Kuroami?"

"Ngh—"

The clinging voice of Mimi of Nabari came after him still. A thing of the mind, he knew — and yet, ninja though he was himself, Kuroami could not shake off this same ninja's cast spell. His spirit must be failing badly. Is it age, he thought, biting his lip as he ran; and the more he thought it, the deeper the spell that someone was working bored into his heart, until at the last—

"There. Right before you stands Mari Dōgen of Kōga."

The instant he heard it, Kuroami did see the shape of him, and with a cry he dropped to his knees and stared up at the empty air.

"Ha, ha. Hard at it, I see. Run like that and you'll do an old man's health a mischief."

Full and clear, Mari Dōgen of Kōga stood barring the way before him, and out of the corner of a mouth crooked with laughter he brought — yes, unmistakably — that thick, heavy voice.

"………"

Kuroami, seat set to the ground, shut his mouth with all his might and struggled to make no sound. He knew that to answer with even a word here would only let the threads of the spell bind him faster, until his heart went numb. Quietly he rose. And knotting the Samaya mudra of Samantabhadra, breathing his own breath into the mudra, he said within his mouth:

{p} Down with the demons, away with the foe; the seven calamities undone, the seven blessings born again—

— and, raising the middle and index fingers, cut the nine signs as though slashing them apart with a sword. The image before him thinned a little, and Kuroami set his foot forward and tried a step. Nothing met it. Where Dōgen had stood there was only the same vast, dim dark.

"Hah. Come close to this Kuroami, if you can."

Kuroami said it aloud, straining himself against the dark, past all his years. But the moment after, in the dark, he went pale enough to see his own colour. Should any of the Kōga men have caught that voice and learned his place, it would go very ill. As though flung off he began to run again, crabwise, along the mountain track. Whether the mantra had done him some good, the voice at his ear could no longer be heard.

He ran on toward the summit, out of his senses. There, as he had earlier ordered, several of his own Iga-line ninja should be lying hidden, waiting.

Crawling up onto the ridge, Kuroami started, and turned his eyes to the east. A faint light was breaking in the sky; the moon was on the point of rising.

(My luck's run out — mine too.)

The ridge ran north under a thin stand of pines, and beyond it Amidagamine heaved up like an overturned bowl. Even feeling his way by night's eye it was a good seven or eight chō off. Along that ridge, as the pale moonlight began to touch it, Kuroami ran for his life. His legs would not carry him as he willed, and even as he ran he could not put off the dark, damp thought that seeped up into his heart.

(It's the years. A ninja's work is good only to forty, at most. For all it's to fill the belly, I've kept too long in the shadow world.)

Ever since the Iga ninja bands had been wiped out by Nobunaga ten years before, the passing-on of the arts had broken off, and there were almost no young ninja left. Those who wandered the provinces and just barely kept the Iga line alive were most of them past forty by now. Kōga, on the other hand — the district of Kōga in Ōmi — had come through untouched, and so the men Dōgen had brought out to serve in the capital were picked from among strong fellows in their twenties. The Iga-line ninja who waited for Kuroami at Amidagamine, had they been Kōga men, would all have long since retired to a quiet life in the hills. And, as has been said, it was in the nature of the Iga man to be poor at working in a body; so that if the Kōga men ran them down at Amidagamine, Kuroami's men seemed to him to have no hope at all of winning.

(If only Lord Jūzō were here.)

Kuroami had gone so weak in the heart that complaints were near to breaking from him. It was then. As he was passing beneath a great pine, a black shape, as though it had dropped out of heaven, came down over his body with a rush and locked his arms fast.

"Ngh—"

Even with his breath being choked off, Kuroami caught the other's left hand in a reverse grip and bent the fingers back with a deft twist. There was the sickening sound of a bone breaking, and—

"Kōga, is it."

He took the sword of the man who had crumpled, and as he drove it through the chest, the way one skewers an insect, said:

"Old I may be, but a Kōga man will not down me so lightly as that."

— and groaned it out. Looking, he saw to his astonishment black Kōga shapes clinging here and there in the tops of the pines before him. From Kuroami's palm the shinobi throwing-blades whirled away like flying gears. As one bit into a man's flesh, without waiting to see the shadow drop to the ground, and letting out a voiceless cry of terror, Kuroami ran for Amidagamine.

By the time he reached Amidagamine the moon, risen in the eastern sky, had begun to light every tree and blade of the peak so that there was no hiding his body. Like a small hurt beast of the woods Kuroami ran this way and that.

"Anyone there? It's me. It's me, I say."

He looked less like a ninja tempered fifty years than some plain and ordinary old man.

Then the dwarf pines and the rocks and the grass-clumps on the height stirred all at once—

"Ōro Genzaemon, here."

"Ueno no Shishiji, at your service."

"Hirakawa no Tahyōe."

"Uezuka Dōgan."

"Kamitsuge no Sakichi. Here."

— voices came welling up from every side. Kuroami sprang on his feet, as if dancing, in the moonlight.

"Ah, you're here, you're here. Someone — the count."

"Twelve, near enough."

"Thirteen, then, with me."

"Just so."

It was Uezuka Dōgan, seemingly the eldest, who answered from behind the rocks.

"Dōgan — with this many, can we see one fight through?"

"And who is the enemy?"

"Kōga men — how many there are, I can't say. But that Mari Dōgen of Kōga is among them is past all doubt. Pay the rest no mind; strike down Dōgen, and only Dōgen."

"Ho. Dōgen—"

"Lost your nerve?"

"No. But where is Dōgen?"

At that moment a laugh rang out on the air and froze the blood of all who heard it. Slipped in among them who knew when, Dōgen rose slowly from behind a rock. He looked round with a smile at the faces of the Iga men, close enough to touch.

"Thirteen, by what I hear the Iga side numbers. Sorry for you, but there are fifty Kōga men lying hidden in these hills. Any who show me their edge, I cut down; but I'll waste no life to no purpose, so any who wish to run, run. All I want is the body of the man there called Kuroami — and the body of Kuroami's jōnin, Tsuzura Jūzō."

"………"

Whether the breath had gone out of them, no voice came from the Iga men for a while. Then Uezuka Dōgan, from behind the dwarf pines, letting his voice rasp low, said:

"Lord Dōgen."

— quietly.

"Ah, you're the Uezuka man. I know your face. In my young days I was in the Takeda's hire, and we worked a job together in Enshū, if I recall."

"The world in those days was nicely at odds with itself, and there was work in plenty for the men of Kōga and Iga both; our living was a fat one. But as the world settled, work for the likes of ninja and rappa dried up, and worse, the dens of the shadow-warriors of the eight villages of Iga were smashed by the Udaifu. In the early Genki and Tenshō years there was more work across the land than we could take, and Kōga and Iga shared it out between us in good fellowship; but come to these days, and even the scant work we do light on, we must fight each other for. If it is to live, there's no help for it. And yet, Lord Dōgen — just for that, we Iga men will not give this ground one step. Yield it, and word goes round that the Iga ninja lost to the Kōga; and in the end no lord will come to the Iga for his work, and our children's living will suffer for it. Fifty against thirteen — though we lie down dead to the last man in these hills, it is no shame to Iga. Come at us as hard as you like. Old we may all thirteen be — yet we'll show you the way of Iga."

"There's justice in what you say."

Dōgen gave a great nod.

"You're the Iga men, right enough — first among the realm at the shadow arts. And to settle whose craft is the better here in this moonlight, with no running and no hiding, is a rare sport. Come, then — draw and at me."

"You — alone?"

"Ay. Alone."

Dōgen opened his mouth wide and laughed.

"The Kōga men are still off somewhere prowling the foot of the hill, it seems. It's this Dōgen's head you're after. While they're not yet come, why not — the thirteen of you, edges all together — try the mettle of a Kōga man, here on the height?"

"Fool."

Dōgen's crowing must have galled one of them past bearing. An Iga man swung round behind and, out of nowhere, slashed backhand at Dōgen's trunk; there was a flash, and blood sprayed with a burst against the dwarf pines beside Dōgen and was flung across the white ground — but when they looked, wide-eyed, the one who had fallen was not Dōgen, nor the man who had struck, but another Iga man who had stood staring, dazed, at Dōgen's movement. As if the impetus of it carried him on, in that same instant Dōgen was already on his next quarry, springing like a starved tiger, and the spray of blood wetted the moon and fell to the ground.

(We lose.)

His face working, drawn and bloodless as paper, Kuroami shouted:

"Hai — the wheel! Take him with the wheel!"

The wheel meant three men, before and behind, forming a whirl and falling on the hemmed-in foe all at once. Once caught in the Iga wheel, it was said, no master of any school of arms could stand. Hemmed in the wheel, and pelted from one side with stones and throwing-blades thick as hail, not even Dōgen could get his hands free — and so:

"Now that's hard on me."

Dōgen stood on a bluff and began to slide down the slope on the Ōmi side as if gliding.

Where the slope ran out at the bottom there flowed a thin stream — a branch, most likely, of the Yamashina-gawa — called the Iba-gawa. Of the Iga men who had chased Dōgen this far to the bank, there were but nine. And even that — that they had chased him — was, strictly, not the case.

A little before this, at about the mid-slope of the descent, black-clad Kōga men, five of them, in shinobi garb dark as lacquer, had suddenly appeared at the Iga men's backs — neither to rescue the fleeing Dōgen nor to cut at the pursuing Iga, but only to press, softly, softly, close on the heels of the Iga men.

Each chō they went, their number swelled — five, then five more — exact as though timed to a clock. Dōgen ran at the head of the Iga men, and at the same pace, as if guarding their rear, the Kōga band ran behind. Not a footfall from them, not one man opened his mouth. Only the wind, keening over them, rose eerily high; and to the Iga men the wind cut the time into something more dreadful than any hell, like a kalpa-wind with a will of its own. No one spoke. There were those among them not without a mind to watch for the moon to be hidden and slip away into the hills, but the moon walked on unchanged into a clear mid-heaven, and it seemed it would go on lighting them plain, down to the roots of the grass, for another half-toki yet. All that was left them to do was to move their feet and go down the mountain.

At length, when they had broken through their terror and sunk into the still, forlorn frame of mind of some bird or beast that knows its hour has come, that here, this day, it will die — they saw, below, the thin flow of the Iba-gawa faintly lifting into view out of the dark.

(There, is it.)

The place the Kōga were luring them to would be that riverbed — so the nine Iga men, all as one, gazed at the stream.

"Dōgan. Make ready to die now."

Kuroami whispered it low toward Uezuka Dōgan, more as if telling it over to his own heart. There was a slight tremor in the voice. Yet the colour had already come back to his face, and there had begun to rise in it, for all his years, a warm, glowing blood like a young man's.

Dōgan gave a nod of assent.

"There's no help for it."

He said it slowly, again as if telling it over to himself, and the moment the words were out he dropped back to take the rear, while at the same instant Kuroami quickened his step to the front. The two had taken this order to show, to friend and foe alike, their resolve that the Iga band would, if anything, march willingly into the killing-ground the Kōga were silently leading them to.

When this strange column had come down as far as the riverbed, Dōgen, running ahead, slowed his feet for the first time and turned slowly round; and, as though it were the sign to his Kōga men, he lifted his sword to the sky.

"Now."

— he said, sharp and short. In an instant the Kōga column broke apart and, each man moving hard as if to dazzle the eye, ringed the nine Iga men round in a thick wall.

Within the ring, Uezuka Dōgan raised his old, cracked voice:

"There is such a thing as shame, even for Iga. And live on though you may, the world where a shadow-warrior can earn his bread will come no more. Die, every one of you, here. And for a parting gift to death, teach these raw Kōga striplings, to the full, what manner of thing the ninja of Iga is."

The words scarcely out, Dōgan flung himself in the posture of the praying-hands cut into the mass of the Kōga, and in a breath had struck down two or three; then—

"Did you see that!"

— and, so saying, with his own sword he swept off his own out-thrust head, and died.

One Iga man, cut at and covered in blood by two Kōga, sprang even so on one of them and locked him fast.

"This is the very height of the joy of killing a man."

— and, driving his blade in through the other's back so that the two were skewered together, he fell and died.

Every one of the Iga men was, in a manner of speaking, a death-troop; and so, for all their skill, the Kōga were for a while driven back from the riverbed to the water's edge and thrown into confusion — but by then the men on the Iga side who could still put up any real fight could be counted on the fingers, and these too went down one after another under the flailing Kōga blades, until at last Kuroami stood alone.

"Well, Kuroami?"

Dōgen, four or five ken off, drove his naked blade into the sandy ground and spoke. The Kōga men had ringed it about and stood watching, still.

Within the Kōga ring Kuroami was worn to the bone, and bore up, with a face full of suffering, against a body that at every moment threatened to swoon under him.

"Cut, and be quick."

"Had I meant to cut you, I'd have done it long since. You alone I spared. What do you say — will you not come under me? I'll give you land in the Kōga district, and make you a jōnin."

"Cut."

"No hurry. This is where a man weighs things well. You've only to tell me the whole of the plot that lies behind you. I'll see you come to no harm."

"Dōgen. That's wasted breath."

"How so?"

"Fifty years this Kuroami has followed the shadow way as a retainer of the house of Tsuzura in Iga, and never once has he betrayed a work of his own. A ninja stands, in the world's eye, on the one thing — that he never betrays the man who hired him. And though I keep my life now, at this age what span is left me? Do you think I'd betray my last work? I'm tired. Cut, and be quick."

"A man past understanding."

Dōgen gave his men the sign with his eyes, and bade them take Kuroami.

"Keep off."

Kuroami's left hand had drawn the armour-piercing dirk, ready to open his own throat at any moment, and held it to his neck; his right took a fresh grip on his long sword, and he tottered out toward Dōgen.

"Die, you too!"

He thrust straight for Dōgen's belly, but Dōgen parried it in a flash, and Kuroami's sword sprang ringing into the moonlit sky; and in the same breath Kuroami, leaving behind a laugh like some monstrous bird's, drew the dirk in his left hand across with all his strength. A spray of blood went up with a sound, and as Kuroami dropped, rolling, to the ground—

"My Lord Jūzō will avenge me, and soon."

— his breath went out of him.

"Dead, is he."

Vexed, Dōgen kicked Kuroami's corpse. It rolled loosely face-up, and the empty eyes looked at the moon. Dōgen turned his gaze from Kuroami and looked round at his men.

"Away, then."

"What's to be done with the bodies on this riverbed?"

"The Kōga among them, carry off and bury in the hills. As for the Iga—"

"The Iga?"

"Leave them. The crows will see to it in time. That is the last courtesy, so to speak, one pays the beaten."

Dōgen and the Kōga after him scattered to the four winds, as though they feared the coming of day.

It was scarcely one toki after this affair, in the dusk before dawn, that Tsuzura Jūzō, coming into the capital from Otogi Pass in Iga and making for the Shibutani pass, drew near the riverbed of the Iba-gawa. In the still-dim fields of Yamashina the lamps of the farmhouses, setting about the day's beginning, looked achingly dear to him, and here and there could be seen the shapes of farmers already out on the field-paths.

A thin mist lay over the face of the river. Picking a place to ford, wading through the mist, Jūzō for the first time caught sight of the strange thing there. Corpses of men clad all in black — some fallen on the sandbars, some half sunk in the shallows — it was for all the world like a crow's unlucky graveyard.

Jūzō stood in the shallows and did not go near, only gazing at the cluster of black corpses; and presently he groaned, without a sound.

(It's Kuroami.)

Right at the centre of the scattered dead lay the small corpse of Kuroami, in townsman's dress, the four limbs bent, clutching at the sky that had begun to pale. Gohei's work, was Jūzō's first thought. Or else Mari Dōgen of Kōga. It might be that band of Kōga men Kohagi commanded. Any of the three, no matter. There was no need, he thought, to go searching. Blood he had long forgotten, blood for the fight, welled seething up in the hollow of Jūzō's body, and in a giddy, reeling surge of it he settled on revenge.

First Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon had been killed by Mari Dōgen of Kōga; now Kuroami and his whole company had been wiped out. What bore in hard on Jūzō's breast was the Kōga's pitiless routing of the men of Iga. To restore the honour of Iga and lift the grudge from its dead, there was, it seemed, nothing left but an offering paid in blood.

(To kill Hideyoshi will do. But before that, I must make these Kōga men bleed, and the more of them the better.)

From the ground under Jūzō's feet a wind began to rise, and the mist began at last to draw off downstream. Behind him there was the sense of a man drawing near. Jūzō crouched, and while he retied the loosened cord of his sandal he waited for the presence to come up. It was a farmer.

"Beg pardon, samurai."

"Hm?"

Still crouched, Jūzō tipped up the brim of his woven hat.

"Th-those there — what are they?"

"Well, now."

Jūzō rose to his feet.

"Most likely the work of crows, or of some owl-goblin. Meddle carelessly with it and there'll be trouble later from the bailiff's office. Best look the other way here and get on to your fields."

As if the thought had just struck him he took several pieces of silver from his breast.

"That's for a requiem for those crows. Where the bailiff buries them I couldn't say, but if you learn of it, set out a stick of incense and a flower for them, quietly."

The farmer, looking from the silver in his palm to Jūzō's face and back, said:

"And who might you be, sir?"

"I? Say I'm one of the crows' own kin. Now be quick and go."

Jūzō did not look back. He crossed the shallows and went up into the mountain. Once over the crest, the far slope should run on, unbroken, into the streets of the capital.
