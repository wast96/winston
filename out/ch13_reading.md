## Chapter 13. The Voice of Justice

*To the tune "The Immortal by the River": Resistance and National Salvation*

*On the solitary island, sharing one hatred, we lit the torch; / resistance propaganda came in good time. / The whole people, bathed in blood, gave their common support. / Joining the army and going behind enemy lines— / women too showed their heroic mettle.*

*By the Fragrant River, unlooked-for, we met disaster together; / we raised funds to rescue talent and supplies. / Pure streams for ten thousand li admired each other's constancy. / Radiant, we upheld justice; / friendship forever graven in remembrance.*

In 1931, Japanese imperialism launched the "September Eighteenth" Incident, seizing China's three northeastern provinces by force of arms in the delusion of turning China into Japan's exclusive colony. The Communist Party called out that, with the nation in peril, the survival of the race must come first, and that all should unite as one to resist Japan. The Party's stand voiced the true feelings of patriotic people of justice, and the people all over the country raised a high tide of resistance and national salvation. World and China brought out a special issue on resistance and national salvation, and the very first article was written by Chen Zhigao. He dashed it off in one go in the apartment room where I was living, displaying his talent before me. I too wrote a short piece at the same time, titled "Students Should Take Part in the Patriotic Movement," signed Huang Shuyi. Its gist was that Chinese students, ever since the May Fourth Movement, had a glorious tradition of anti-imperialist patriotism; that "every common man bears responsibility for the rise and fall of all under Heaven"; that now, with the nation in peril, we could not stand by with folded arms and watch the country perish. Students must not read books to death or study themselves to death, indifferent to the survival of the nation and the race; they must care about the great affairs of state and actively take part in the patriotic movement of resistance and national salvation against the invasion of Japanese imperialism. This piece, together with the article Chen Zhigao wrote, appeared in that special issue of World and China; and after reading them, the famous Southern Society literati and Nationalist government legislators Liu Yazi and Lin Gengbai came calling of their own accord—and this was the beginning of our friendship across the generations and our cooperation in the cause of justice with the two of them, Liu and Lin.

Both Liu Yazi and Lin Gengbai were of the Nationalist Party's left wing. Liu Yazi was ten years older than Lin Gengbai, and Lin Gengbai in turn ten years older than I, yet the three of us formed a friendship across the generations. In 1925, during the first period of cooperation between the Nationalists and the Communists, Liu Yazi had served together with Wan Xiyan as a member of the Nationalist Party's Jiangsu provincial party headquarters. Once Liu Yazi learned that I was Wan Xiyan's widow, he supported our rescue work all the more actively and of his own initiative. Lin Gengbai was a legislator of the Nationalist government, and had served as secretary-general of the Extraordinary Parliament and as a member of the House of Representatives; he and Liu Yazi were both famous poets of the Southern Society. After the "September Eighteenth" Incident, as the Japanese invaders pressed forward step by step in North China, the pro-war and pro-peace factions within the Nationalist Party quarreled endlessly. Chiang Kai-shek willfully persisted in his own course, and under the name of "first pacify the interior before resisting the exterior," on the one hand intensified the "encirclement and suppression" of the Central Soviet area, and on the other banned resistance propaganda in the Nationalist-controlled areas, so that many patriots were persecuted as political prisoners for taking part in anti-Japanese activities. In rescuing political prisoners, it was not only our Party that was at work; the sympathy and support of the Nationalist left wing and of patriots was also widely won.

At the National Emergency Conference convened by the Nationalist government, Lin Gengbai joined with Nationalist elder statesmen and presidents of the "Five Yuan"—Sun Ke, Zhang Zhiben, Yu Youren, Tan Zhen and others who inclined toward Mr. Sun Yat-sen's Three Great Policies of "alliance with Russia, alliance with the Communists, and assistance to the peasants and workers"—and signed and passed a motion advocating that "now, with the national crisis grave, and for the sake of cherishing talent, all political prisoners already sentenced who behave well may have their sentences reduced by one-third and be released on bail..." He also, at the conference, directly opposed Chiang Kai-shek's fallacy of "first pacify the interior before resisting the exterior," boldly speaking out: the Nationalists and Communists were like brothers quarreling within the walls of their home, who ought together to ward off insult from without! After his motion was passed by the National Emergency Conference, it was partly carried out. For example, Yuan Puzhi, who had been sent to study in the Soviet Union at the same time as I in 1926, was arrested upon returning to China in 1928 and sentenced to eight years, but in 1933, before her term was up, she was released on bail. Some comrades in Longhua Prison were also released early thanks to Lin Gengbai's motion. This was the just support given us by patriotic and progressive people outside the Party!

In March 1933, the venerable He Xiangning wrote to the Nationalist Central Committee, proposing a general amnesty for the nation's political prisoners, whom she would lead north to engage in medical relief work for the anti-Japanese armies. She told reporters: "On this day of foreign aggression and encroachment, to deny political prisoners—who have the will to reform society and to cherish and protect the country—the chance to serve the nation, and instead to wear away their lives in prison, is indefensible! 'Sincere unity, joining together to meet the national crisis'—this the gentlemen of the Central Committee have for years sung to the skies; yet these political prisoners are in many cases the revolutionary comrades-in-arms and fellow revolutionaries of former days. Why, then, when there is sincerity, can there be no unity; when there is a national crisis, may they not join together to meet it? Let me ask the gentlemen of the Central Committee: how many of you were not political prisoners in those days? Anyone with the least ability to put himself in another's place ought of course to accept my proposal. Chen Jiongming betrayed the Premier, a thing intolerable to law and morality, yet in those days the Premier still permitted him to reform himself, and men of his former command now hold office in the government; the Northern Warlords opposed the revolution, yet may now be faithful comrades. Then there is really no reason why these political prisoners cannot be pardoned. Some criticize me as a humanitarian. If revolution is not for the way of humanity, is it then for the way of beasts? If my proposal be called humanitarianism, it is no more than the Premier's humanitarianism of 'revolution is the love of man,' the humanitarianism of Mr. Zhongkai in cherishing the young. My proposal is fair-minded, a revolutionary standpoint that rises above party and faction. I have always upheld the spirit of reorganization the Premier set forth in the thirteenth year of the Republic; I shall carry on Mr. Zhongkai's unfinished will, to overthrow imperialism, to relieve the sufferings of the people of China and of the whole world, and to realize a society of the People's Livelihood of the Three People's Principles. After the failure of the Great Revolution, I will join no party or faction. In recent years many young people have come asking me to form a party or a faction, but I dare not deceive people, and people cannot make a puppet of me, so I have declined them all. I have never had political ambition. I hope everyone will refrain from creating orphans and widows. If the aim of the Premier's national revolution could truly be carried through, it might well spare the tears of many an orphan and widow. But what of the present? I am not one who creates political prisoners; I appeal for the release of all the nation's political prisoners. Those who do create political prisoners ought also to ask their own conscience, and to endorse my proposal!"

To Ms. He Xiangning's righteous and forceful appeal, the Nationalist authorities not only paid no heed, but in the same month arrested Comrades Chen Geng and Liao Chengzhi one after another. In April, the League for the Protection of Civil Rights set up a Committee for the Rescue of Political Prisoners, and Shen Junru and others were elected as its officers. In May, all the executive and supervisory committee members of the Shanghai Bar Association formed a delegation and went to the Second Jiangsu Prison in Suzhou to look into the conditions of the political prisoners held there. Precisely because many of the political prisoners of those days were patriots and hot-blooded young people who propagandized resistance to Japan, the just cry for the release of political prisoners won wide support and response from patriots.

During the time I was on leave visiting my family, Chen Zhigao, Shen Junru and Yang Zhihao, three in all, as representatives of the Shanghai Bar Association, went to Qingdao to attend the Fifth Congress of the National Bar Association (which opened on 4 June 1933). Among the motions the Shanghai Bar Association put before the congress was the "Petition for the Legislative Yuan to Promulgate at Once a Law on Compensation for Wrongful Imprisonment," drafted by Chen Zhigao. Its content was: if the authorities concerned had caused a wrongful case, they must compensate the party for the loss of reputation and the family's living expenses during the wrongful case; if the party had unfortunately died, then a pension should be paid. As for those who had caused the wrongful case, besides pursuing the plaintiff and witnesses for the crimes of false accusation and perjury, the court that tried the case and its procurators and judges should each, according to circumstances, bear responsibility, and so on. The political intent of this draft was to use the legitimate means of a democratic legal system to punish the White Terror of the Nationalist reactionaries' arbitrary arrests and killings.

After this motion was read out by Mr. Shen Junru at the Fifth Congress of the National Bar Association and passed, it was forwarded to the government, but the Nationalist government shelved it. The progressive lawyers headed by Mr. Shen Junru, in a spirit of firm and indomitable perseverance, did their utmost to contend against the reactionary government through legal struggle. At the Sixth Congress of the National Bar Association, held in Guangzhou in 1934, it was resolved to establish a National Committee for the Movement to Compensate for Wrongful Imprisonment. After this national congress of lawyers, local bar branches in various places set up committees for the movement one after another.

On 5 May 1935, the National Committee for the Movement to Compensate for Wrongful Imprisonment held its first plenary meeting in Suzhou. A declaration was issued at the meeting, which said: "'One stroke of the vermilion brush in the hall above, a thousand drops of blood on the steps below'—that such a most piteous and painful state of affairs should linger on in the China of the twentieth century, what then is the misfortune of these people?" The declaration held that when the law is weak the state is weak, and that to establish a system of compensation for wrongful imprisonment, safeguarding the people's right to exist, was in fact to make sound the organization of our state. It therefore called on the lawyers of the whole country to concentrate their strength and act in unison, so as to meet the demand of social justice and urge the state to fulfill its legal and moral obligations. The meeting also decided that each year in June the bar associations should choose a day to hold a movement for compensation for wrongful imprisonment, in the hope that a nationwide unified appeal might rouse the deaf and awaken the unhearing, striving for the early realization of a national system of compensation for wrongful imprisonment. On 5 June that year, a Week for the Movement to Compensate for Wrongful Imprisonment was held simultaneously all over the country. The Shanghai Bar Association convened a mass meeting at the Chamber of Commerce in the Tianhou Temple, receiving the senior officials of the Party, government and judiciary and the representatives of various public bodies; Mr. Shen Junru reported on the meaning and course of the movement, in the hope that all sides would lend their support. After the meeting, the famous lawyers Shen Junru, Chen Tingrui, Lu Dingkui, Chen Zhigao and others went to the various radio stations to conduct large-scale propaganda. The movement to compensate for wrongful imprisonment won the wide sympathy and support of public opinion, and influential large newspapers such as Shen Bao, the News, and the China Times all published editorials on its behalf.

On 1 November the same year, the National Committee for the Movement to Compensate for Wrongful Imprisonment held its second meeting in Hangzhou. The meeting decided to select people to petition the Fifth Plenary Session of the Third National Congress of the Nationalist Party, requesting that legislative principles be settled at once and that the Nationalist government's Legislative Yuan be ordered to draft a law on compensation for wrongful imprisonment, so as to redress the people's wrongs and clean up the administration of officials; it also resolved to send telegrams urging the bar associations everywhere to petition in unison by telegram on the above matters. Before the meeting, the committee members went in a body to pay their respects at the tomb of Yue Fei. In the circumstances of the time, when "patriotism was a crime and wrongful imprisonments filled the land," to hold memorial rites for, and sing the praises of, the national heroes and loyal patriots of history, and to scourge traitors of the Qin Hui type, was clearly pointed and of real present-day significance. On the 16th, Shen Junru led the committee members Chen Zhigao, Liu Lumin, Chen Yaodong, Liu Zhe and others, carrying the Draft Law on Compensation for Wrongful Imprisonment, to petition together at the Nationalist Central Party Headquarters in Nanjing; at the same time they proposed that when the Legislative Yuan discussed the law on compensation for wrongful imprisonment, the committee should be notified to attend, and the bar association permitted to state its opinions.

In June 1936, the second national Week for the Movement to Compensate for Wrongful Imprisonment was held. On 7 June, the Shanghai Bar Association convened a mass meeting, and again Mr. Shen Junru delivered the report. In his report he appealed to all circles to act jointly and unite to push the government to complete the regulations on compensation for wrongful imprisonment, so that they might come into force on the same day as the Constitution. But in essence, the Nationalist authorities could not possibly redress wrongful imprisonments, nor could they possibly enact regulations on compensating for them. Just as the movement to compensate for wrongful imprisonment was deepening, in July 1935 the New Life Weekly affair broke out, and the patriotic democrat Du Zhongyuan was thrown into prison on a false charge; less than a year and a half later came the nationally shocking case of the "Seven Gentlemen," when even Mr. Shen Junru himself, for leading the national salvation movement, was clapped in irons on the charge of "endangering the Republic."

But under the influence of this vast and powerful movement to compensate for wrongful imprisonment, which lasted several years, some judges with a sense of justice came to recognize the movement; and even on ordinary judges it had a considerable restraining effect. At the same time, it linked the open, legal, ordinary rescue work with the special rescue activities of our Mutual Aid General Association and its branches everywhere. Many comrades held in prison could not only engage lawyers to defend them and win legal release on bail, but in places where a committee for the movement to compensate for wrongful imprisonment had been established, the Party organization could also have the families of arrested comrades come forward to ask the committee to right their wrongs, and to appeal and plead on their behalf.

In those years the close friend and confidant with whom Zhigao was on intimate terms was Mr. Lin Gengbai, the famous Southern Society poet. In 1933, when Zhigao drafted the law on compensation for wrongful imprisonment, he asked Mr. Liu Yazi, Mr. Lin Gengbai and Comrade Liu Bochui to review it, and they did not change a single character. Gengbai had a rare genius: he could argue and persuade with the best, astonishing a whole company with his words, and he was also rich in foresight and insight. He once cited the words of the Duke of Lu in the "Debate of Cao Gui" in the Zuo Commentary—"Though I cannot examine into every case great and small, I always judge them according to the true circumstances," to which Cao Gui replied, "This is a mark of loyalty; on this you may give battle"—to argue that, after the "September Eighteenth" Incident, with the national crisis grave, to show mercy to prisoners and reduce their sentences was benevolent government, a way to hearten the fighting spirit, and also a way to cherish talent. He pointed out that factional strife had existed since ancient times. After the ingenious plan of reducing sentences by one-third was carried out, he offered another scheme, proposing the establishment of a Three People's Principles Ideological Reflection Institute, to which political prisoners without confessions and without evidence would be transferred for so-called "Three People's Principles reform education." His proposal was, remarkably, passed as well. In this way the imprisoned could, as a first step, first obtain an improvement in their living conditions and be able to correspond with their families; as a second step, individual approaches could be made, with well-known figures coming forward to bail them out. The Nationalist Central Committee approved the establishment of reflection institutes at Beiping and Suzhou. But the Nationalist government's Ministry of Justice added a legal formality for those released: they were required to sign and seal the release document, which contained the sentence "once released, one must not engage in criminal activities to subvert the Republic of China." This was not Mr. Gengbai's idea; Yazi and Gengbai explained to us that to be sentenced without confession and without evidence was itself a wrongful imprisonment. If a suspect who was a CCP member had no confession and no evidence, that already proved he had not been exposed, had not surrendered, and had not turned traitor—and this could be verified from the files. A traitor, having already made a confession, was not qualified to enter a reflection institute, nor did he need rescuing. Gengbai said still more fervently: "We are friends bound by morality and duty; we devise these plans of our own accord, not for fame, not for gain, with a clear conscience." The Central Committee of our Party also considered this a clever stratagem of special rescue that could work. At a time when North China was in grave peril, if we did not actively rescue the comrades in prison, then once Beiping fell it would be hard to save them. The Central Committee agreed to have well-known figures stand guarantee and handle the bail procedures. Some underground Party members put up their own money to open a grocery shop as a shop-surety, and prepared red envelopes ready to deal with the court officials who came to verify. This was an effective method used in various places. Some had families come forward to contact well-known members of the relevant native-place associations to bail people out; some small industrialists and merchants who were themselves our own people, after rescuing arrested comrades and fearing trouble, simply closed their shops and escorted the comrades into the Soviet area. In 1933 and 1934, Chen Zhigao's eldest brother and Guo Yushang and others organized an exchange called the Yongchang Gold Exchange, and later opened a Taiji Money House. These financial exchange houses were all used by us to stand surety for released arrested comrades, and were of no small help; and through this connection I also gradually infiltrated our upper-level united-front work into Shanghai's financial world.

In the winter of 1936, to repay the noble friendship with which Liu Yazi and Lin Gengbai had supported our special rescue tasks, Zhigao ran about smoothing the way to win a correct appraisal of the Southern Society. At that time, working through Wu Kaixian, the head of the Bureau of Social Affairs, in Shanghai, and through Li Huanzhi in Nanjing, we finally succeeded in getting the Southern Society formally re-established. A celebration was held at the Guangdong Restaurant on North Sichuan Road, and I, Zhigao, and Chen's sister Baozhang all had the honor of attending as guests. In the small papers' report of the roll call of the "108 Generals," Mr. Yazi was of course Song Jiang.

Lin Gengbai's wife, Lin Beili, had a mother, Xu Yunhua, who in the early years of the Republic had followed her elder sister Xu Zihua and Wu Zhiying in running about and crying out to mourn and bury the martyr Qiu Jin—also a progressive and enlightened person of considerable social influence. Lin Gengbai and Lin Beili's father were friends of the same generation, but Lin Beili, admiring Lin Gengbai's talent and character, willingly married him, so that the couple were very far apart in age. The literary marriage-bond of Gengbai and the woman poet Beili was celebrated with a betrothal ceremony at the Overseas Chinese Hotel, presided over by Beili's mother. Liu Yazi and his wife and I, along with Chen's mother, the Elder Madame Pan, who led the whole Zhigao family, all attended the ceremony; Zhigao's younger brother Chen Shijun even served as Lin Gengbai's best man, and the two Yuan presidents, Tan Zhen and Zhang Zhiben, acted as introducers. A group photograph was taken as a memento at the time. In 1993, when Beili and I met again in Shanghai, I took out this precious and commemorative photograph and gave it to Beili to keep for herself. It was a treasure that survived the catastrophe—having passed through my investigation and escaped the disaster of the Red Guards—and it was moreover accompanied by several pieces of calligraphy left by Gengbai and Mr. Yazi. Beili published Gengbai's calligraphy and the memoir I wrote in the sixth issue of Southern Society Studies, edited by Mr. Yazi's son. Our friendship is precious and deep, and to this day Beili and I correspond often. People mistakenly took me for a member of the National Salvation Association or of the Southern Society—an honor all the deeper.
