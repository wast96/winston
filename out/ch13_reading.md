## What I Saw as Huang Jinrong's Steward

{d} Narrated by Cheng Xiwen; set down by Yang Zhancheng

### I. My Ties with Huang Jinrong

My ties with Huang Jinrong begin at the City God Temple. In the early years of the Republic my father kept a painting-mounting shop near the Shanghai City God Temple, and from boyhood I helped out in the shop. In those days there were five powerful "men about town" around the temple; because each of their names contained the character *sheng*, "born," they were known as the "Five-Sheng Party." One of them, Xue Liansheng, who had once been a lawyer and later kept an ivory shop, brought in one day a "Pines and Cranes" painting done by a pupil of Ren Bonian, and asked me to work a switch and change the pupil's name-seal to Ren Bonian's own seal. At first I would not, but I could not hold out against Xue Liansheng's repeated urging, and in the end I mounted it for him. Xue Liansheng took the picture off to give as a present, was forever praising my craftsmanship, and introduced me to Shi Jinxiu, telling me to bring some paintings and calligraphy to Shi Jinxiu's house to drum up trade.

Shi Jinxiu was the wife of the man about town Fan Kaitai. At that time Fan Kaitai kept an ebony business in Shanghai and was known as "Ebony Kaitai"; his younger brother Fan Huichun was a man about town too, and the two brothers were both well known in Shanghai, but Shi Jinxiu was even more famous than Fan Kaitai, called among the men about town "Bandit Jinxiu." She was a Changzhou woman, good at getting on with people, bold and open in temper and fierce in her methods, and among the women of that world in Shanghai she ranked as a person of consequence; in those days even Huang Jinrong stood a little in awe of her, for Huang's first wife, Lin Guisheng, was her sworn sister, and Shi Jinxiu was the elder of the two.

Fan Kaitai was a director of the City God Temple, and once I had come to know Shi Jinxiu, he and his wife had me help manage some of the temple's affairs. I was young then and eager to please, and on the first and the fifteenth of every month, when the temple filled with worshippers, Shi Jinxiu, before coming to burn incense, would always send someone ahead to tell me, so that I might get her incense and candles ready and see to sundry chores; she was well pleased. At first, when we met, I always addressed her as "old lady" and she called me "little brother," but as we saw more of each other she had me call her "godmother" and took me as her "godson." To help me earn a little more, she would have me pick out some good paintings and calligraphy and, with her going along beside me, take them to Huang Jinrong's house to sell to Huang and to his relatives and friends. So I was forever coming and going at the Huang house, and by spending a little money to get in with the Huang servants, high and low, I drew steadily closer to the family.

Around the fifteenth of the seventh lunar month in 1922, the crowds burning incense and watching the temple fair were unusually great. Huang Jinrong was president of the temple's incense-bearers' society and a firm believer in the City God; he came at the head of his trusted disciples to offer sacrifice and make his kowtows, and even escorted the City God's image out of the temple to the fair. The people in the street pressed in on both sides to watch, order broke down, and the worshippers flung their burning incense down outside the temple gate, so that a fire broke out and the temple's main hall was burned to the ground. The Daoist priest Kang and the others who lived off the temple's incense-money begged the society president, Huang Jinrong, and the director Fan Kaitai to rebuild the City God's hall. Huang Jinrong agreed at once, and put the two brothers Fan Kaitai and Fan Huichun in charge of raising the hall. Fan Kaitai was in the ebony trade and had timber in plenty; Fan Huichun was manager of the Jiading Bank, where money moved easily in and out; and Huang Jinrong by then had long controlled the Sanxin Company (the headquarters of the whole Shanghai opium trade). So Fan Huichun hit on a scheme: the large sum needed to rebuild the temple would be raised by adding a little to the price of every tael of opium the Sanxin Company sold, so that in all more than twenty thousand yuan was brought out. Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, Fan Huichun, Lu Jinlin (head of the temple's fire brigade), and others formed a board of directors to rebuild the temple, with Huang Jinrong pushed forward as chairman, the actual work in Fan Kaitai's hands, and I put in charge of materials coming in and going out. After more than a year the main hall of the City God Temple was rebuilt and made wholly new, and because I had managed the materials well I won the trust of Fan Kaitai and Huang Jinrong.

In 1931, when I was twenty-five, my father died and I took over the mounting shop. But in the course of the rebuilding of the Yu Garden, the land under my mounting shop was taken back, and with my livelihood gone, Fan Kaitai urged me to attach myself to Huang Jinrong. It happened just then that Huang Jinrong's adopted son Huang Yuantao was marrying, and Fan Kaitai had me go to help at the Huang house. It was a grand affair, and I saw to the arrangements; Huang Yuantao was greatly pleased, and asked me to send in my card and take his father as my "old man," so that he and I should become fellow disciples. So I made the three kneelings and nine kowtows before Huang Jinrong and took him as my "old man." Presenting his pupil's card at the same time as I did was Tian Tiefu, who under the puppet Wang Jingwei regime served as a puppet division commander in northern Jiangsu.

Even before I took Huang Jinrong as my "old man" I was often in and out of his house and already knew the whole household, old and young, men and women; after becoming his disciple I grew closer to him by the day. When he smoked opium I would pack his pipe for him at the couch; when he went to bathe at the Xiaoyao Baths I would go along. At the opium couch and on the bathhouse day-bed he would talk with me of almost anything. The affairs of the second floor where he lived, the meals of the kitchen downstairs, the receiving of guests, and the rest, he had me see to all of it, and I gradually became his head steward. Above all, for the "young mistress" Li Zhiqing, who held the family's financial power, I did a service that pleased her and Huang Jinrong greatly, and so won still more of their trust and made my place as steward secure. The way of it was this. Huang Jinrong's first wife, Lin Guisheng, had once kept a "flower house" (a lower sort of brothel) on Yizhichun Street in Nanshi, and had chosen from Suzhou a maidservant named Li Zhiqing, comely and quick-witted; Lin Guisheng could not bear to make her a prostitute, and kept her at her own side, treating her as a daughter. After Lin Guisheng married Huang Jinrong she made Li Zhiqing a child bride, matching her with Huang Jinrong's adopted son Fubao; Fubao died at seventeen, and so Li Zhiqing became the "young mistress" of the Huang house. This young mistress enjoyed Huang Jinrong's especial favor and ran all the inner affairs of the family, and there was a peculiar relationship between father-in-law and daughter-in-law that everyone in the Huang house understood well enough. At that time Huang Jinrong had a disciple named Zhang Fanggeng, who kept the Dacheng leather-goods company in the Dalu Emporium; after the victory in the War of Resistance it went bankrupt, with a deficit of more than 3.4 billion in puppet currency, and with creditors pressing on every side and no way to meet them, he begged Huang Jinrong's help. Huang put me in full charge; I negotiated with the several creditors, and since they all feared Huang Jinrong the matter was settled fairly smoothly. Just then the young mistress of the Huang house, having got wind that acting as a broker at the exchange was a money-making business, told me to rent premises and take out a license. I persuaded Zhang Fanggeng to give over the shopfront of the Dacheng leather-goods company in the Dalu Emporium for the young mistress's use, then went to Du Yuesheng to ask his help in obtaining a securities-exchange license — Li Zhiqing even telephoned Du Yuesheng herself — and in the end we got the license, named it the "Zhicheng" securities firm, and put Li Zhiqing's adopted son Huang Qiyu in as manager. This firm did a thriving trade with money pouring in, and by it I too won the favor of the young mistress of the Huang house.

Next, the young mistress and I together raised more than three hundred and sixty million yuan and opened the Little World Fuyou Bazaar, of eighty-four shopfronts, with Huang Jinrong making me its manager. In this way Huang Jinrong's disciple Ding Yongchang ran the Great World and I ran the Little World, and we both became his trusted right-hand men.

### II. Huang Jinrong's Ancestry and Family

Huang Jinrong was born in 1868 (the seventh year of the Qing Tongzhi reign). His father, Huang Bingquan, was a native of Suzhou, born at Caohejing in Shanghai; he had been a market-gardener and owned some land, but after it was seized by his aunt he moved into Shanghai and lived at Zhangjialong in Nanshi. At that time there lived in Zhangjialong men who had served as military officers and as constables, and being neighbors of Huang Bingquan they were the reason he later worked for a time as a constable himself. Huang Jinrong's mother, née Zou, was a Suzhou woman, and bore two sons and two daughters. The eldest, a daughter, Abao, married into the Zou family; Huang Jinrong was the second child; the third, a daughter named Zhaodi, married Xu Buzhou; and the youngest was a son, Mujin, whose whereabouts are unknown. Besides these, Huang Bingquan had a mistress who kept a rice-cake shop at the old drill-ground in Gaoqiao, called "Pockmarked Ah Jin," and she too bore a daughter, whose whereabouts are unknown. Huang Bingquan died when Huang Jinrong was in his teens, and a few years later the Zou woman died too.

When Huang Jinrong was past thirty he married Lin Guisheng. Though he was then a plainclothes detective in the French Concession police, he had not yet come up in the world, and he married into the Lin family, living at Junpeili at Baxianqiao. Because Lin Guisheng bore no children, they took in a son and a daughter. The son was named Fubao, also called Junpei; the daughter was named Baobao, and married a man surnamed Xu (whereabouts unknown). Fubao's wife was Li Zhiqing, who, as I have said, was a child bride and held the family's financial power; later she fell out with Huang, swept the Huang house clean of its gold and American banknotes and its jewels and ornaments, moved to Hong Kong, and afterwards died in Taiwan. She bore no children in the Huang house and took in two sons and a daughter: the elder son, Huang Qiyu, now in business in America; the second son, Huang Qiming, now in Shanghai; and the daughter, Huang Yueming, now in Hong Kong.

When Huang Jinrong was fifty-four he became chief inspector of the French Concession police, gaining both name and wealth; he had made his fortune. Because Lin Guisheng had borne him no children, he took a fancy to Lu Lanchun, a famous Peking-opera actress who played in *Hongbi yuan* at the Gongwutai theater he ran. Lu had once taken Huang Jinrong as her teacher; her singing of *Cursing Mao Yanshou* had been cut on gramophone records, and in her performances of reformed modern-dress Peking opera at the Gongwutai she was famous for a season, with the house often full. Seeing that Lu Lanchun was matchless in looks and art, Huang Jinrong, heedless of the tie between teacher and pupil, carried on hotly with her, and proposed to Lin Guisheng to take Lu Lanchun as a second wife. Lin Guisheng would not agree; the two fell out; and Lin Guisheng straightaway divorced Huang Jinrong and carried off his valuables — his diamond rings, his costly sable coat, and the rest — and lived on until she died in Shanghai in 1981.

When Lu Lanchun married Huang Jinrong she was only twenty-four, thirty years younger than Huang; she bore no children after the marriage, and her mother took in two sons for her, one called Huang Yuantao, milk-name Liandi, the other with the milk-name Gendi. Huang Yuantao is said to have been the son of a martial-role actor at the Dawutai theater, a man surnamed Li, a Shandong man whose given name is not known. Gendi was originally surnamed Mai, named Zhengxue, also called Yuanxun, a Cantonese; because his parents had been arrested for selling opium and had died in prison, the French Concession Mixed Court awarded him to Huang Jinrong as an adopted son, and at his marriage he took back his original name and left the Huang house. After three years of marriage to Huang Jinrong, Lu Lanchun asked to be let go; she is said to have first married a *laosheng*-role colleague, An Shuyuan, and later a man surnamed Xue, comprador of the Defu trading firm and a dealer in dyestuffs, and to have lived at the Lido Garden.

Huang Yuantao married at eighteen, taking as his wife Shen Jinghua, eldest daughter of Shen Asan, and had two daughters and a son. The elder daughter, Huang Beili, married the son of Lei Xianzhi — Huang Jinrong's disciple and owner of the Lei Yunshang pharmacy in Shanghai — and both are now in Shanghai; the second daughter, Huang Beilan, married a worker at the post and telegraph office (name unknown), and is also now in Shanghai. Huang Yuantao's son now works in the Northeast Aviation Brigade. In 1975 Huang Yuantao received a government special amnesty and was released; he went to Beijing to observe and study, and after returning to Shanghai was assigned in 1976 to the Wuxin Woodworking Factory at Gaoqiao in Pudong, and died of a heart attack in November 1982.

### III. Huang Jinrong's Childhood

In 1873, when Huang Jinrong was six, he moved with his parents from Suzhou to Shanghai and lived at Zhangjialong in Nanshi, and there he studied at a private school in the nearby temple of Mengjiangtang. From boyhood he was mischievous and would not study in earnest, but he was quick-witted and good at meeting things as they came. As I have said, there lived in Zhangjialong men who had been military officers and constables under the Qing, and Huang Jinrong's father, introduced by a neighbor, had served as a constable too. From his earliest years Huang Jinrong saw and heard the doings of these constables and stored up in his mind no few tricks of the underworld, and this had its effect on his later entering the French Concession police as a plainclothes detective, and on his making his name and fortune by rescuing the Frenchman Bishop Yao.

Huang Jinrong's milk-name was "Little Monk," not because he had ever been a monk; there is a story to it. In 1881, when Huang Jinrong was fourteen, his father Huang Bingquan died of illness, leaving his mother, the Zou woman, and four children, who could barely keep themselves by the mother's taking in washing. So the Zou woman sent Huang Jinrong to Mengjiangtang to do odd jobs and scratch a meal, living the life of a temple drudge like a little monk, and the local people took to calling him "Little Monk." Because his face was pockmarked, he later also had the nickname "Pockmarked Jinrong."

After doing the odd jobs of a little monk at Mengjiangtang, Huang Jinrong still could not get enough to eat, so his mother again got someone to send him to a painting-mounting shop at the City God Temple to serve his apprenticeship. This mounting shop stood below Huanlong Bridge on Yuyuan Road and was called the "Cuihuatang mounting shop," an old-established house. (Some say it was a mounting shop called the "Lingyunge," beside the Deyi Lou by the Nine-Bend Bridge at the City God Temple, but this is a mistake.) The Cuihuatang mounting shop's owner was called Huang Quanpu; Huang Jinrong took him as his master and served three years' apprenticeship, drawing only four hundred cash a month (forty coppers). After finishing his term he stood at the counter for two years, but his earnings were still small and his life still hard, with no question of supporting his mother and his brothers and sisters, and his mother went on keeping herself by taking in washing. Huang Jinrong spent five years in all at the Cuihuatang, and besides learning some proper mounting craft he also picked up a few tricks of passing off the false as true and working a switch.

### IV. How Huang Jinrong Entered the French Police and Made His Fortune

Huang Jinrong's entry into the French Concession police was chiefly owing to the good word of a neighbor, Grandma Tao. The Tao family too lived near Mengjiangtang at Zhangjialong, next door to the Huangs, and Huang Jinrong's mother often washed clothes for them; over time Grandma Tao, seeing the hard life of the Zou woman and her five, felt great sympathy, and the Zou woman asked her to find Huang Jinrong a place in the police. In 1900, when the French Concession was enlarging its territory and the police were openly recruiting Chinese constables, Huang Jinrong signed up to sit the examination. It happened that Grandma Tao's son had just graduated from the Shanghai Sino-French College and entered the French Concession police as an interpreter; Grandma Tao had her son put in a word inside the station, and so Huang Jinrong was taken on as a third-class Chinese constable. The station took on twenty Chinese constables that time, later reorganized into a detective squad (that is, plainclothes police), and the interpreter Tao recommended Huang Jinrong as their foreman; people of the day called them the "Twenty-Share Party."

In those days the real power in the French police lay in the hands of the French detectives, and Huang Jinrong was posted to keep order around the Shiliupu wharves. Because during his apprenticeship at the Cuihuatang he had often drunk tea at the Deyi Lou by the City God Temple, he had come to know some of the local ruffians and hoodlums and Green and Hong Gang men around the temple. Once he had become an "informer" (a plainclothes detective) for the French police, in order to keep order at the Shiliupu wharves and to break theft cases, he set about mixing with these men still more deliberately, and his ties on the dark side of society steadily grew. With small favors and small kindnesses he bought up a set of habitual thieves as his informers, manufacturing cases from within, using one set of thieves to catch another, and even playing the tricks of "the thief crying stop thief" and "acting a false play in earnest": on the one hand he would arrange for a band of small fry to agree to commit a robbery at such a place on such a day, and on the other have some other small fry go to the French Concession police to "inform" on them, and then inform in turn to the French detectives, so that he held the initiative in leading men to crack the case. When the appointed day and place came and the band of small fry he had set up were indeed carrying out the robbery, they would be caught in a single net by the detective squad, led in person by Huang Jinrong and lying in wait in disguise. After these thieves had been shut up in the station, Huang Jinrong would again pull strings inside and have them released one by one. Seeing Huang Jinrong break theft case after theft case, the French police authorities came to value him, and he gradually gained prestige.

Huang Jinrong's methods of squeezing and extorting the shop-owners and rich men of Shanghai were subtle and well hidden too. He would often set Green and Hong Gang men and local ruffians to make trouble in front of shops or in the busy quarters, even to stage fake fights and curse in the streets, hurting the shops' trade so that customers kept their distance and dared not go in to buy; and then Huang Jinrong, with constables and plainclothes detectives, would patrol the streets, and wherever he came the troublemaking little hoodlums would every one take to their heels, and sometimes Huang Jinrong would deliberately seize a few of them for punishment. So the shop-owners and rich men, feeling that he truly had a way with things, gave him money and gifts at every festival and even by the month, treating him as a guardian spirit; some sent in cards to take him as their "old man," some took him as "godfather," some as "teacher." As for the well-known rich, Huang used the method of kidnapping for ransom, extorting on a grand scale — as when he set his trusted disciple Ding Yongchang to kidnap Rong Desheng and extorted several hundred thousand US dollars at a single stroke. As for setting his lackeys to write threatening letters to the rich, that was commoner still. These victims, after being squeezed of great sums, had yet to ask Huang Jinrong to step in and settle the matter, to avoid worse to come. But what Huang Jinrong was proudest of was not the fake cases he cracked, but his cracking of one real case, the kidnapping of the French Bishop Yao, which made him the first-rank grand hoodlum and grand chief of Shanghai.

Bishop Yao was originally a French Catholic priest, on close terms with the French consul in Shanghai and the chief of the French police, and a man with real power behind the scenes in the Shanghai French Concession. To open up a mission base, he took the train from Shanghai in person, carrying several chests of silver dollars, meaning to go to Tianjin to found a church. When the train reached Lincheng in Shandong, it was stopped and robbed by the troops of the warlord Zhang Zongchang, who carried him off to the countryside near Lincheng and held him prisoner, meaning to extort a huge sum to ransom the "hostage."

After the affair, which caused a sensation at home and abroad, the French consul in Shanghai ordered the French police to crack the case with all speed and rescue Bishop Yao. The station mobilized all its detectives to inquire and search everywhere, but got no news at all, and had to fall back on offering a high reward: to anyone who gave word of Bishop Yao's whereabouts, three thousand silver dollars; to anyone who could find Bishop Yao, ten thousand. Huang Jinrong took the keenest interest in the affair, seeing it as a fine chance for promotion and profit, and sought clues by every means, even going himself to the City God Temple to burn incense and draw lots, praying the City God to protect him and let him get a clue, crack the case, win merit, and rise straight up in the world, and vowing that he would surely repair the City God's great hall and gild the City God's image anew; but still it was like a stone sunk in the sea, with no word at all. (This Huang Jinrong told me as he lay on the opium couch.)

As luck would have it, this great kidnapping case yielded its clue through a Shandong man who came to Shanghai and had a hundred yuan filched from him. There was in Lincheng, Shandong, a man named Han Rongpu, an adjutant under Wu Peifu, who took the train from Lincheng to Shanghai to buy things. When the train reached Shanghai and he made his way through the press of the crowd to register at a hotel near the station, he found that the hundred yuan tucked in his belly-pocket had vanished into thin air. In deep dismay, Han Rongpu remembered that a fellow townsman surnamed Sui served as a constable in the French Concession police, and clinging to a thread of hope he went to the French station to find the constable Sui; the constable Sui reported the theft for him and introduced him to Huang Jinrong. Hearing that the man had come from near Tianjin, Huang Jinrong would not let the chance slip, and questioned Han Rongpu about the stopping and robbing of the train bound from Shanghai to Tianjin and the kidnapping of the French priest. Since Han Rongpu was an adjutant under Wu Peifu, familiar with army matters, and since Wu Peifu's troops and Zhang Zongchang's troops were both stationed near Tianjin, so that each side knew of the other's misdeeds, Han Rongpu told Huang Jinrong what he had heard. Overjoyed to have a clue at last, Huang Jinrong at once paid Han Rongpu 150 yuan and told him to go back to Lincheng and find out exactly where the hostage was hidden, and, the moment he had it, to hurry to Shanghai with word, for which he would be given a further five hundred yuan in reward, and, if the case were cracked, a greater reward still. Moved by Huang Jinrong's open-handedness, Han Rongpu was won over, and sure enough, after returning to Lincheng, within a few days he made contact with the troops of Zhang Zongchang who had kidnapped Bishop Yao and found out where the bishop was held. He came to Shanghai a second time to meet Huang Jinrong and discuss the ransom. Huang Jinrong, full of schemes, told Han Rongpu not to go to the troop commanders to bargain a ransom, but rather to find a way to buy over the men guarding Bishop Yao. At the same time Huang Jinrong, consulting the interpreter Tao, first drew two thousand yuan from the station, gave Han Rongpu five hundred, and handed over another 1,000 for Han Rongpu to go at once and buy over the guards, promising that when Huang Jinrong reached the place where the bishop was held he would pay a further two thousand, and telling these guards to flee elsewhere. He also had the interpreter Tao write a note in French to tell Bishop Yao to set his mind at rest, for Huang Jinrong would come to Lincheng himself to save him from danger. After Han Rongpu had returned to Lincheng once more, Huang Jinrong, on the appointed day, led several dozen plainclothesmen in person, disguised as officers and men of Zhang Zongchang's troops, took the train from Shanghai to Lincheng, hurried by night to the countryside, rescued Bishop Yao, and returned safely to Shanghai.

By this method of taking the firewood from under the cauldron — not bargaining directly with Zhang Zongchang's troops, but spending only a few thousand yuan to buy over a handful of guards — Huang Jinrong rescued Bishop Yao from danger, and the French police gave him extraordinary advancement. Whereas the important posts in the French police had all been held by Frenchmen, they now promoted Huang Jinrong, for the first time ever, to chief inspector, and even assigned eight Annamese constables specially to guard his safety. With these eight Annamese constables coming and going about him, Huang Jinrong's power grew ever greater and his name ever louder, and from then on he served French imperialism the more faithfully.

Huang Jinrong held the post of Chinese-detective chief inspector in the French police for more than twenty years, and only after his sixtieth-birthday celebration did he give it up, retiring in name while pulling the strings from behind the scenes. Those who followed Huang Jinrong as Chinese-detective chief inspector were, in turn, Shen Defu and Ren Shuiyang, but because they would not truckle to Huang Jinrong's wishes they were squeezed out by the remnants of his party that he had planted in the station, and in the end the post went to a trusted man of Huang Jinrong's after all.

### V. Huang Jinrong and the Gangs

Though Huang Jinrong had constant dealings with gang men, he had never truly taken an "old man," and was neither of the "Da" generation nor of the "Tong" generation. By gang rule, anyone who had never entered a gang was called a "hollow one" and could not open a hall to take in disciples. But Huang Jinrong took no notice of all that: he called himself brother with the "Da"-generation men of the Green Gang — Zhang Jinghu, Cao Youshan, and the like — and gave himself out to others as of the "Tian" generation, one stroke more than the "Da." He held no incense-hall ceremony to take in disciples, but only had the disciple write out a card, headed "Master Huang Jinrong" and closed with "your pupil so-and-so, with respect." Huang Jinrong was one whose eyes lit up at the sight of money and who talked always of hard profit; so long as a man brought money and gifts and took him as master, he turned none away, the more the better, and so the disciples he took in were no fewer than two or three thousand. After becoming Chinese-detective chief inspector of the French police, he took in more than two hundred disciples at one time, of whom the important ones and those close to him were Jin Jiuling, Cheng Ziqing, Ding Yongchang, Lu Shaozhang, Lu Jinchen, Zeng Jiuru, Lu Ying, Shang Wu, Feng Zhiming, Wei Zhongxiu, Qiao Songsheng, Tang Jian, Ma Derong, Zhang Rong, Dong Mingde, Zhou Xiangcheng, Guan Baoquan, and others. This set of disciples were mostly petty chiefs or key men in the police of the French Concession and the International Settlement. There were besides Gu Zhuxuan, owner of the Tianchan Stage; Ma Hongkui, the "Night-soil King" of the French Concession; Zhu Shunlin of the gambling tables at the Liuguo Hotel; Yao Songru, owner of the Cangzhou Hotel; Tang Jiapeng, manager of the Great World, and "Fruit Rongsheng"; and men from trade and industry, such as Fu Liangjun, owner of the Xinguang underwear factory; Zheng Renye, owner of the Zhengtai Rubber Factory; Huang Zhenshi, manager of a fishery company; and Zhang Shankun, manager of the Xinhua Film Company; and still others from the worlds of arts and letters, the theater, the press, and medicine. The famous Peking-opera artist Qilintong (Mr. Zhou Xinfang), then being pressed and threatened by the owner of the Tianchan Stage, also once took Huang Jinrong as his master.

Besides the disciples Huang Jinrong took in himself, his adopted son Huang Yuantao took in two or three hundred, his grandson Zou Zhengzhi took in five or six hundred, and, with Gu Zhuxuan, Tang Jiapeng, Lu Ying, Huang Zhenshi, Ding Yongchang, and the rest all taking in no few disciples besides, Huang Jinrong's following, down the generations, was spread not only over the whole of Shanghai but, in the Jiangsu-Zhejiang region too, held his power.

### VI. Shanghai's Big Eight-Share Party, Little Eight-Share Party, and Thirty-Six-Share Party

Huang Jinrong had once sworn brotherhood with Du Yuesheng, Zhang Xiaolin, Yang Xiaotian, Wang Bailing, Sun Xiangfu, Chen Qun, and Chen Xizeng, gathering in gang men and hoodlums and ruffians; they were called the "Big Eight-Share Party" of Shanghai. In the order of their swearing at the time, the eldest was Huang Jinrong, the second Wang Bailing, the third Yang Xiaotian, the fourth Zhang Xiaolin, the fifth Du Yuesheng, the sixth Sun Xiangfu, the seventh Chen Xizeng, and the eighth Chen Qun.

Wang Bailing was a Yangzhou man, of a rich family, and during the Northern Expedition served as a Kuomintang army commander; after coming to know Huang Jinrong in Shanghai he used his troops to traffic in arms and in opium and drugs, and afterwards died at Yangzhou. Yang Xiaotian, named Yang Hu, once served as chief of the special-agents department of Chiang Kai-shek's commander-in-chief's headquarters and as commander of the Songhu Garrison. During the Northern Expedition he had been a subordinate of He Fenglin's and had served as a naval commander; in Shanghai he ran the Xingzhong Study Society and a Seamen's Club, joined the Hongmen, and took in no few brethren. Zhang Xiaolin had originally been a man about town in Hangzhou, where he kept a joss-paper shop; fleeing tax, he came to Shanghai to lie low, and there came to know Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, and the rest, and in the April 12 counter-revolutionary coup of 1927 he took an active part. In the enemy-and-puppet period he went over to the Japanese and joined a puppet body to farm the joss-paper tax; later, when the Wang Jingwei regime meant to make him governor of Zhejiang, Dai Li got wind of it and at once plotted with Du Yuesheng and had Zhang stabbed to death. Sun Xiangfu had once been an army commander in the Northern Expeditionary force and was partnered with Wang Bailing, and entrusted Huang Jinrong with buying arms for them in the French Concession. Huang Jinrong once spoke of Sun Xiangfu and Wang Bailing, on the opium couch, and said: "Those two are both lechers — the moment they spot a good-looking woman in the concession they get me to put my head to it for them, and if they can't lay hands on her, they'll even waylay her on the road and carry her off. Their lust is bold as brass." Chen Xizeng was a nephew of Chen Lifu, and had once been captain of Chiang Kai-shek's bodyguard and chief of the Nanshi police bureau. On the eve of the War of Resistance, when Huang Jinrong and the others organized an "Anti-Japanese Association," I served as an officer of it; and once, when the owner of the Baodaxiang silk-and-cloth store in Shanghai, a man surnamed Ding, was arrested by Chen Xizeng for selling Japanese printed cloth, he was released on bail after Ding got someone to plead with Huang Jinrong. I once received Chen Xizeng, who had been brought along and introduced to Huang Jinrong when Chen Guofu and Chen Lifu came to see him. When the two Chens wanted intelligence from the French Concession, Huang Jinrong would pass it to Chen Xizeng, and they kept each other informed. Chen Qun was the youngest of the "Big Eight-Share Party," but of the eight his learning was the highest, and he was full of resource and stratagem. During the Northern Expedition he served as director of the political department of the front-line command of the East Route Army, and at that time had his subordinates spread anti-Communist rumors, preparing opinion for Chiang Kai-shek's suppression of the workers' movement. During the War of Resistance he went over to the Japanese and served as puppet governor of Jiangsu; on the eve of the victory, in dread of punishment for his crimes, he killed himself.

Besides the "Big Eight-Share Party" there was also a "Little Eight-Share Party," all of them trusted, capable disciples of Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng, being the eight men Gao Xinbao, Ma Xiangsheng, Jin Tingsun, Ye Zhuoshan, Gu Jiatang, Yang Renqian, Fan Guobai, and Fan Hengde. These men too took in no few disciples, and lorded it over Shanghai.

Besides these, there was on the Shanghai wharves a set of hoodlums who went by the name of the "Thirty-Six-Share Party." Their chief was called Chen Fusheng, also named Chen Shichang, who had once taken Zhang Jinhu as his "old man." Zhang Jinhu had been garrison commander of Nantong, was of the "Da" generation of the Green Gang, and had taken in no few disciples. After taking Zhang Jinhu as his master, Chen Fusheng gradually gained standing in the Green Gang; when Du Yuesheng was selling fruit at the Shiliupu wharves, he took Chen Fusheng as his "old man," and after making his fortune he kept Chen Fusheng in comfort and even gave him a roomy house (on Rue Sieyès, present-day Yongjia Road, slantwise across from the Danjing Temple), to repay his benefactor. Chen Fusheng's nickname was "Stick-Sleeve Fusheng," because in earlier days he had cheated passers-by and travellers of their money on the wharves with the stick-sleeve gambling trick.

### VII. The Origin of the Sanxin Company

In old Shanghai the opium trade was originally in the hands mainly of the Cantonese gang; the big firms that made their fortunes selling opium (then called "black goods") were Zheng Qiaji, Guo Yuanmao, Tongchang, and others. In those days the hoodlums on the wharves were many, and among them was a set called the "water-tigers," whose chief was called Fan Gaotou. This set of hoodlums lived wholly by thieving in the water: whenever a British steamer carrying tens of thousands of chests of opium sailed into the port of Shanghai, then, since the Huangpu River is shallow and the steamer could not draw close to the wharf, small skiffs had to be used to lighter the cargo from the steamer, and when the opium was loaded chest by chest into the skiffs and rowed toward the wharf, capsizings would often occur and the chests of opium sink into the Huangpu — and this was the "water-tigers" at their mischief. They would upset the skiffs, then fish the sunken chests out of the water and set them on other skiffs and make off with the opium. Sometimes opium already carried onto the wharf would be stolen too. This thieving gave black-goods firms like Zheng Qiaji great trouble; and though the concession police assigned constables to patrol and guard by day and by night, it was of no use, and even the constables often went missing, their bodies found next day floating on the Huangpu.

On the land wharves too there was a set of hoodlums who did this business that needs no capital, the famous among them being Zhang Chunbao, Shan Ayun, Li Derong, and Rong Xiaoyun (nicknamed "Lame Ayun") of Shiliupu; Pu Zaiyuan of Hongkou; the "Rice-Cake Monk" of Jiangwan (a nickname); "Havoc-in-Heaven Xu Fusheng" of Tianhougong Bridge; and "Iron-Arm Chen Changfu," among others. One of them, a disciple of Fan Gaotou's nicknamed "Rubber Tiger," once threw a foreign constable into the Huangpu; the case was later cracked, and he was shot by Xu Guoliang, chief of the Nanshi police bureau, at Jiumudi.

Where there was an organization of hoodlums stealing opium, there was also a thieves' den for receiving and fencing the stolen goods, whose chief was another head of the Thirty-Six-Share Party, named Ma Dekuan. He kept no few disciples under him who did nothing but receive, harbor, and fence stolen goods, gathering at the "Jinsiniang Temple" near the Dongchang Road wharf on the Pudong side. The opium that Fan Gaotou and the other water-tigers stole from the water and from the wharves was all opened and taken in here, each chest of raw opium worth several thousand yuan.

Later, the head of the French Concession police, the Frenchman Shali, charged Huang Jinrong with finding a way to crack the cases; but since in fact it was all the doing of the followers of the "Big Eight-Share Party," Huang Jinrong could only open one eye and shut the other and put them off. But this was no lasting plan, so he talked it over with Du Yuesheng, Zhang Xiaolin, Fan Huichun, and the rest, and Fan Huichun proposed forming a company that would collect a premium as an insurance company does. Once opium was insured with the company, the company would issue a stamped certificate of insurance, and if the opium were stolen the company would make it good. The premium fixed was ten per cent of the opium's value, so that a chest worth several thousand yuan carried a premium of several hundred. These premiums brought in each month several hundred thousand to more than a million yuan, which the company paid out to the followers of Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, Zhang Xiaolin, and the rest, and to the hoodlums connected with the water and land wharves, as "monthly dues." This was the origin of the "Sanxin Company."

At that time Huang Jinrong, under the banner of the Sanxin Company, arranged for the station to send several hundred Annamese constables, who drove out in patrol wagons and cruised everywhere in great pomp, going openly onto the wharves to guard the opium and escort it into the warehouses. The cost of these several hundred Annamese constables Huang Jinrong met out of the premiums. At first the French Concession police nominally sent five hundred Annamese constables to guard the wharves, at a monthly cost of tens of thousands of yuan, though in fact, since the head of the French police, Shali, and the others all drew pay for phantom men, the number fell short of five hundred. Later, as more and more opium came in, the heads of the French police rose with the tide too, and the phantom billets grew from five hundred to a thousand, to two thousand, and the monthly outlay grew from tens of thousands to one or two hundred thousand yuan — but the wool comes off the sheep's own back: the premium the Sanxin Company took on the opium came out of the opium-selling merchants like Zheng Qiaji, and the premium the merchants paid came out of the men who smoked the opium. Of the premium income, Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, Zhang Xiaolin, Fan Huichun, and the rest sat and split the spoils, each taking tens of thousands a month, more or less. The one who did best out of it was Du Yuesheng, for since Huang Jinrong was then chief inspector in the French police and reckoned an official, and could not well appear openly, he had Du Yuesheng serve as manager of the Sanxin Company, with Zhang Xiaolin and Fan Huichun as deputy managers, so that the real power lay in Du Yuesheng's hands. Once Du Yuesheng was manager of the Sanxin Company, he held in his grip the hoodlum organizations, great and small, of the water and land wharves; Zhang Xiaolin was charged with dealing with the hoodlum organizations from outside Shanghai, and Fan Huichun with the money coming in and going out. Besides this, several hundred followers of Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, and Zhang Xiaolin were planted in the Sanxin Company as nominal inspectors, drawing from tens to hundreds of yuan a month.

The Sanxin Company won the opium merchants' trust, to be sure, because through it the frequent thefts of opium were quelled and the effect of "insurance" was got; but the main thing was that the opium trade turned from secret to open. At that time, when the Sanxin Company collected a premium, not only did the company affix its stamp, but there was a receipt issued by the French police, with its seal added. Besides collecting premiums on opium, this company cunningly invented pretexts and levied an opium-pipe tax, so that opium dens sprang up all over Shanghai; in the French Concession there was, in almost every lane, a selling-and-smoking place (commonly called a "swallows' nest"). The Sanxin Company collected pipe-license fees on the station's behalf, charging a few jiao a month for each pipe, later raised to a few dollars. In those days a den had at least a dozen pipes and a big den several dozen; in the French Concession alone there were ten or twenty thousand dens, so the monthly take ran to several hundred thousand yuan. Dens everywhere mostly under-reported their pipes, and the several hundred inspectors the Sanxin Company employed went specially to each den to check the pipe licenses and count the pipes; where they found under-reporting and concealment, they fined the offender in a light case, and in a heavy one revoked the license and closed the business.

Besides opium, that black drug, in the enemy-and-puppet period two more drugs, red and white, came into vogue: the red called "red seeds," the white called "white powder," also named heroin, that is, morphine. The "red seeds" were first manufactured in Shanghai by a Japanese rōnin named Teng Qusaburō, at the Kunshan Garden in Hongkou (near present-day Wuchang Road), and later manufactured in quantity by Gu Jiatang, Pu Zaiyuan, Yang Qingbang, and others at the Fahua Temple. This drug was cheaper than opium and easy to take — one could put it on a cigarette and smoke it — and so more harmful still. That the drug curse of old Shanghai spread so far is inseparable from the Sanxin Company's operations and protection.

### VIII. Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng Jointly Run a High-Class Club

On the eve of Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng's jointly founding a high-class club in Shanghai, in the Huang family's opium room and in the bathhouse cubicle at the Xiaoyao Baths, I heard Huang tell of how he and Du Yuesheng had together swindled the money of Yuan Shikai's son Yuan Kewen. At that time Yuan Kewen had newly come to Shanghai, a stranger to the place and its people; and among his attendants was a man named Han Rongpu, the very man who had passed word to Huang Jinrong in the affair of rescuing the French bishop, and so he introduced the young Yuan to call on Huang Jinrong. On meeting Huang Jinrong, Yuan Kewen at once presented ten gold coins as a first-meeting gift. These coins had been struck for Yuan Shikai by Britain after he became President, bore Yuan Shikai's head, and were quite precious. Seeing them, Du Yuesheng exclaimed his admiration again and again, and Huang Jinrong gave him three. So Huang and Du laid on great banquets and entertained the young Yuan in grand style; at every meal there were rich food and wine and fine Chinese and Western pastries; he wore costly Chinese and Western clothes, lived in an exquisite luxury apartment, and came and went with several Annamese constables to guard him. Yuan Kewen was greatly pleased at the assiduous entertainment of these two Shanghai "big shots." Huang and Du first took the young Yuan round all the famous sights of Shanghai, then led him to the "study-chambers" (high-class brothels) and the "long-three" houses of Huileli, and picked out several star courtesans to send to the apartment where Yuan Kewen lodged, to keep him company morning and night, singing and making merry, so that Yuan Kewen tumbled into the maze Huang and Du had laid for him. Of the four things in vogue among the Shanghai hoodlums — eating, dressing, whoring, and gambling — he took to three at a stroke, and hard on their heels came gambling. At first, when Yuan Kewen played mahjong together with Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, Jin Tingsun, and a fourth, Yuan always won and Huang and Du and the rest lost, so that Yuan Kewen's gambling-fever grew and grew, and a single stake ran from a thousand to more than ten thousand yuan. In less than ten days he had lost clean away the tens of thousands in silver notes he had brought, and had no choice but to slap his backside and go back to Beijing. Before he left, Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng even saw him off in person, and brought out five thousand yuan to give Yuan for his travelling costs.

Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng were no experts at the tricks of gambling, but among their disciples there was no lack of sharpers — men like Wang Maoting and Zhu Rushan — who all had shrewd methods at gambling. This time, keeping Yuan Kewen company at cards, Huang and Du grew interested in gambling; and hearing Huang's disciple Wang Maoting tell how much money the foreign hoodlums made running gambling tables, and of the dark secrets of roulette — Huang's disciple Ma Yuting could even make gambling gear, such as loading dice with lead — Huang and Du gathered Ma Xiangsheng, Gu Jiatang, Wang Maoting, Ma Yuting, Jin Tingsun, Fan Huichun, and others at the Sanxin Company to talk it over, and all agreed that running a gambling house was a fine business. Gu Jiatang proposed opening a club, Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng approved, and they fixed on No. 181 Avenue Foch (present-day Yan'an Middle Road), central in location and quiet in its surroundings, as the site.

Shanghai's earliest great gambling den was called the "Renhe Lodge," at the Lujiazhai Bridge by Xiaodongmen, that is, at the mouth of present-day Yizhichun Street. In those days many fish-brokers' houses were set up there and the fish market was concentrated there; the men who ran the "Renhe Lodge" were Chen Shichang, who lorded it over the Shiliupu wharves, together with Shen Jiafu, Fan Hengde, and others. Since the fish-houses opened for business early in the morning, the fishmongers, once they had sold their fresh fish, had money in hand, and Chen Shichang seized the chance to set up a gambling house and cheat the fishmongers of their money; but set beside No. 181 Avenue Foch, it was a small sorcerer before a great one.

No. 181 Avenue Foch was a fairly large garden villa. When it first opened, it served only as a club for members of the Sanxin Company; later it was extended to the families of employees connected with the Sanxin Company and to certain notables and rich men of Shanghai; and in the end anyone with money, or who knew any of the people connected with the club, could go in and gamble. Inside, gambling gear Chinese and Western was complete in every kind, with fine Chinese and Western dishes, high-grade tobacco and wine, and top-quality opium supplied free, and one could even ride home free in a motor-car. But once inside the club, winning money was no easy matter. The tables habitually used lead-loaded dice, and under the roulette gear lay magnets and the like, to cheat the players by sleight of hand. Even if a man won, once he stepped out the gate he would be shadowed, and by such methods as "frisking the mark" and "skinning the pig" his money would be seized. Sometimes they even staged a "surprise raid" to catch gamblers. One evening the writer saw with his own eyes: just as the players in the club were at the height of their excitement, crying their dice-calls, a band of Annamese constables and Chinese detectives suddenly swarmed in, herded the players string by string onto the police wagon, and shut them up in the French Concession station; in the end each was fined a few hundred yuan and released on bail — all of it, in truth, a trap set by collusion between the gambling-house owner and the station.

### IX. Huang Jinrong and Mr. Sun Yat-sen

Mr. Sun Yat-sen came to know Huang Jinrong through the introduction of Huang's disciple Xu Fusheng. Xu Fusheng, nicknamed "Havoc-in-Heaven Fusheng," had originally been a hoodlum at Tianhougong Bridge, with a good many disciples under him, throwing his weight about at Tianhougong Bridge and on the Suzhou Creek wharves; because he had seized the temple property of the Tianhou Temple, he got the nickname "Havoc in Heaven." This man followed Huang Jinrong in the opium trade and came to know some Cantonese, and through the Cantonese came to know Mr. Sun Yat-sen, and for a time followed him. In the early 1920s Mr. Sun came to Shanghai, meaning to use Huang Jinrong's power in the French Concession for revolutionary work, and once wrote a fan-leaf for Xu Fusheng; it was then the fifth or sixth month, and Xu Fusheng took this paper fan to see Huang Jinrong, saying that Mr. Sun was a revolutionary of high repute who, on coming to Shanghai, wished to see Mr. Huang as well. Huang Jinrong was greatly pleased, and fixed a day, telling Xu Fusheng to bring Mr. Sun to the Huang house. One day in the sixth month, Mr. Sun, accompanied by Xu Fusheng, arrived at Huang Jinrong's house. Huang Jinrong himself invited Mr. Sun up to the reception room on the upper floor. Mr. Sun said that he knew Mr. Huang had great resources in Shanghai and many connections in the French Concession, and asked that, from then on, when his comrades and friends came to Shanghai, Mr. Huang would give them all help and protection. Huang Jinrong agreed at once.

Later Mr. Sun wrote again to Huang Jinrong, the gist being of the situation of the time: he pointed out that the imperialists' backing of the warlords in fomenting civil war was the root of the people's ruin, that only revolution could end the melee among the feudal warlords and deliver the people from their misery, and that the revolution needed men of talent to take part and financial support besides, and he asked Mr. Huang to get in touch with like-minded friends and give more help in both men and money. Huang Jinrong, ordinarily no free spender, on receiving the letter actually brought out a thousand yuan at one go and gave it to Xu Fusheng to pass on to Mr. Sun, and also told Yu Qiaqing to give a sum in aid. On receiving it, Mr. Sun wrote back to Huang Jinrong to thank him. Because Xu Fusheng had bestirred himself and done his part, Mr. Sun wrote two more fan-leaves for him, one of which Xu Fusheng had specially mounted and framed, and hung in the very center of his parlor at home.

By Huang Jinrong's account to me, Mr. Sun Yat-sen wrote him several letters in all, but with several decades gone by their whereabouts are unknown. By another account, from Gong Tianjian, who was a secretary in Huang Jinrong's house, these letters had been seen in the keeping of Hang Shijun (Hang was the last manager of the Great World, now dead). In 1937, when the Japanese army occupied Shanghai, Huang Jinrong had his secretary and accountant sort out certain documents and letters kept in an iron chest, and Lu Zhenzhong told me that he had seen a letter from Mr. Sun Yat-sen — the envelope was moldy and old and the sheet stained with water, the writing blurred, but the signature "Sun Wen" could still be read clearly. Besides this, when Mr. Sun Yat-sen took office as Provisional President, Huang Jinrong had Hang Shijun write a letter of congratulation, which Hang Shijun himself carried to Nanjing and presented to Mr. Sun Yat-sen in person. After the victory of the Northern Expedition, Huang Jinrong would sometimes say to his disciples: "All my life I've stood by my honor and prized my friends; even in Mr. Sun Yat-sen's revolution, I lent a hand once. Whenever there's a difficulty, I'll never not help." These words I also heard him say, at his birthday celebration in 1941, to Li Jishen, who had come to offer his felicitations.

### X. Huang Jinrong and Chiang Kai-shek

Chiang Kai-shek had at first been a petty clerk "ruling lines" at the Shanghai Commodity Exchange, with small earnings and much discontent. Hearing of Huang Jinrong's power, he asked Yu Qiaqing to introduce him to take Huang Jinrong as his master. Huang Jinrong consenting, and with Xu Fusheng as the transmitting master, a formal master-taking ceremony was held and Chiang presented his pupil's card.

In 1921, when a slump swept the Shanghai exchange, the Hengtai brokerage run by Chiang Kai-shek, Chen Guofu, and others ran a great deficit, and Chiang himself owed several thousand yuan, which Huang Jinrong and Yu Qiaqing stepped in to settle for him. At that time Chiang wished to go to Guangzhou to join Mr. Sun Yat-sen, and Huang Jinrong, reckoning that he had done his part for Mr. Sun, joined Yu Qiaqing in providing travel money, and so set Chiang on the road of the "National Revolution."

Between 1926 and 1927, Chiang Kai-shek became commander-in-chief of the Northern Expeditionary army. Before he reached Shanghai, Huang Jinrong talked it over with Yu Qiaqing and decided to return the pupil's card. After Chiang Kai-shek arrived in Shanghai, Yu Qiaqing accompanied him on a visit to Huang Jinrong's house, and Huang, changing the old forms of master and pupil, said to Chiang: "It is an honor to me that the Commander-in-Chief should come to my house in person. That old tie between us is a thing of the past; I'll dig out that red card and give it to old Yu to return to you." Chiang Kai-shek said modestly: "A master is always a master; I'll not forget the help Mr. Huang and Mr. Yu gave me in the past." So saying, he took from his breast a gleaming gold pocket-watch and set it before Huang Jinrong, saying: "This is a keepsake I'm giving Mr. Huang, a small token of my regard." Huang Jinrong took the watch with thanks upon thanks. Huang Jinrong always set great store by this gold watch, and at every festival and grand occasion would bring it out to show off, and kept it until after his death, when Huang Yuantao took it away.

After the Northern Expeditionary army reached Shanghai, Huang Jinrong, Yu Qiaqing, and others called together men of trade and industry and social notables to give Chiang a welcome and their congratulations, and raised 100,000 yuan in "comfort money" for Chiang's troops; Chiang handed the 100,000 to Yu Qiaqing and Huang Jinrong, Yu Qiaqing donated 50,000 to charity, and Huang Jinrong gave his to Lu Bohong and others for church work.

When Chiang Kai-shek came to Shanghai on the Northern Expedition, his bodyguard force numbered a hundred-odd men, stationed near Dongjiadu in Nanshi, and Chiang himself decided to enter the French Concession in plain clothes with the captain of his guard. To protect Chiang Kai-shek's safety, the guard detailed more than sixty men, who set out from Dongjiadu in two military trucks, went by Waimalu, and turned onto Yizhichun Street, meaning to enter Avenue Edward VII in the French Concession. When the trucks reached the mouth of Yizhichun Street they were stopped by the French Concession's Annamese constables, who would not let this body of soldiers and police into the concession, and pulled the two guard-trucks into the French Concession station, detaining the guard sergeant as well. Learning of it, Huang Jinrong went himself to the French Concession station and, together with Cheng Ziqing, explained to the head of the French police that Commander-in-Chief Chiang was entering the concession on Huang's account, and that Huang, for safety's sake, asked that Chiang's guard be allowed to drive into the concession to guard the Huang house, in hopes of a peaceable settlement. The French police telephoned the French consulate for instructions; the French consul, feeling the matter grave, directed the station to let Huang Jinrong mediate, and Huang Jinrong proposed that the two detained guard-trucks first be let into the French Concession to drive around once in procession, and then driven to the Huang house at Junpeili, Baxianqiao. The French police agreed to Huang's proposal, and notified the French station at Yizhichun Street to let the guard-trucks into the French Concession, entering the mouth of Junpeili at Baxianqiao by way of Avenue Edward VII. Huang Jinrong's handling of this won him much of Chiang Kai-shek's approval.

In 1927, when Chiang Kai-shek launched the April 12 coup, Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, Zhang Xiaolin, and others organized the China Mutual Progress Society and served as shock-troops against the Communists; Chiang Kai-shek disbursed more than a hundred thousand yuan in expenses in all, and afterwards, in the name of the army commander-in-chief's headquarters, appointed Huang Jinrong a major-general adviser and Du Yuesheng and Zhang Xiaolin major-general counsellors, and later made Huang Jinrong an adviser to the Nanjing National Government and gave him a decoration. When the Huang family garden was completed, Chiang Kai-shek inscribed the four characters "learning, conduct, loyalty, faith," which Huang Jinrong treasured like a jewel and had carved on the hexagonal pavilion to the right of the Four Teachings Hall in the Huang garden (the Four Teachings Hall was named from these four characters Chiang inscribed); and when Huang's followers formed a society, they took "Loyalty and Faith" for its name.

The first day of the eleventh lunar month of 1947 was Huang Jinrong's eightieth birthday. As was the custom every year on Huang Jinrong's birthday, his followers, no fewer than three thousand-odd, all had to bring tribute and gifts, the least a hundred-odd yuan, the most over a thousand. At his sixtieth birthday, when he enlarged the Huang ancestral hall at Caohejing into the Huang family garden, it was by his followers' tribute and the gifts of the trade-and-industry world, without his spending a single cash himself, that he even raked in a few tens of thousands. Now, at his eightieth, it should have been another fine chance; but Huang Jinrong, considering that Chiang Kai-shek's armies were losing battle after battle on every front, said to Lu Jinchen and me: "This year let's not make too big a show of it; with the times against us, it's better to be sparing — we'll just set out a vegetarian feast at the Jade Buddha Temple and leave it at that." After I had talked it over with Lu Jinchen, Hang Shijun, Gong Tianjian, and the others, we asked Yang Hu and Du Yuesheng to preside, with Huang Zhenshi as general steward, and Lu Jinchen and I to receive the guests. Among those who came that day to offer their respects were Chiang Wei-kuo, the mayor of Shanghai Wu Guozhen, the head of the Social Affairs Bureau Wu Kaixian, and other bureau chiefs, received by Yang Hu and Du Yuesheng; later H. H. Kung came too, and was received by Du Yuesheng. Toward eleven o'clock Li Jishen came, and after eating a bowl of vegetarian noodles was accompanied by me to offer his respects to Huang Jinrong. Li called Huang his "big brother," and Huang Jinrong could not well follow Li Jishen's speech, so afterwards Yang Hu told it to Huang Jinrong in full, the gist being: Chiang Kai-shek was an ingrate — once he had won the realm he betrayed Sun Yat-sen, and now he had sent men to arrest me; a man so utterly without honor I simply will not answer to. Now Chiang has made a thorn in his eye of Xiaotian (Yang Hu) as well. Big brother, today I have come to wish you long life, and also to bid you farewell, for I am going to Hong Kong. Afterwards Li and Yang Hu talked privately with Huang Jinrong; I was not present, and do not know the particulars. As I understand it, in the past Yang Hu and Li Jishen had done arms business privately with Huang Jinrong, and had once had Huang Jinrong buy arms through the chief of the French Concession police, the business being handled by Huang Jinrong's disciple Cheng Ziqing. Before Li Jishen left the Jade Buddha Temple, Huang Jinrong invited him to have a photograph taken as a keepsake; Huang and Li had one taken of the two of them, and Huang Jinrong, Wang Xiaolai, Li Jishen, and I had one taken of the four of us.

Three days after Huang Jinrong's birthday, that is, on the afternoon of the fourth day of the eleventh lunar month of 1947, Chen Bulei telegraphed Huang Jinrong from Nanjing to say that Chiang Kai-shek would come to Shanghai the next day to offer his birthday respects at the Huang family garden. Overjoyed at the news, Huang Jinrong bade Lu Jinchen and me hurry to the Huang garden to make ready the Four Teachings Hall; and when Huang Jinrong's disciple Tao Xuesheng heard the news, he volunteered, calling up the local self-defense corps of Caohejing to guard the outside of the garden and posting trusted men to stand watch within. Huang Jinrong was very pleased to hear it, nodded his approval, and charged that the men outside might be more in number, while those standing guard inside the garden must be carefully picked for reliability, with no carelessness.

Early the next morning Tao Xuesheng posted five or six hundred of the self-defense corps to stand guard all along the road, before and behind, through Caohejing. Within the Huang garden, which covered nearly sixty *mu*, everything was swept spotless; before the Four Teachings Hall was set out a set of carved-stone Eight Immortals, in the center of the hall the Three Star-gods of Fortune, Rank, and Longevity were enshrined, and to left and right stood twelve great rosewood chairs (this rosewood furniture Huang Jinrong had specially had brought from Beijing and made of whole-root rosewood). In the afternoon Chiang Kai-shek came to the Huang garden, in a blue gown and black jacket and a bowler hat, and Huang Jinrong, at the head of Yang Hu, Hang Shijun, Gong Tianjian, Lu Jinchen, and me, met him before the garden. When they reached the Four Teachings Hall, Chiang Kai-shek said to Huang Jinrong: "I didn't come to the birthday feast — the Jade Buddha Temple was too crowded to be convenient, and I've been much taken up with official business; I beg your pardon." At once he set his own hand to moving a great rosewood chair; Chen Xizeng straightaway took it from him, and Chiang Kai-shek told him to set it down in the very center before the square table, then with his own hands took a cushion from one of the other rosewood chairs and helped Huang Jinrong into the central chair. Huang Jinrong said hastily: "I don't deserve it, I don't deserve it — a bow will do!" But Chiang Kai-shek had already knelt and made a kowtow to Huang Jinrong. As he kowtowed, Huang Jinrong, in his flurry, rose to raise Chiang Kai-shek up, and Chiang said: "I've come specially this time to wish you long life, to show my heart's regard; since the situation at the front is urgent, I must leave at once — please take care of your health, and may you have every blessing and long life." So saying, he took his leave of Huang Jinrong and hurried away; of the banquet Huang Jinrong had made ready, he ate not a mouthful, and did not so much as drink a cup of tea. Afterwards Huang Jinrong praised him without end: "President Chiang really is a great man, generous in courtesy and rich in honor. To be so respected by him is the glory of my life."

### XI. Huang Jinrong and Wang Jingwei

Wang Jingwei's dealings with Huang Jinrong began before the War of Resistance. In 1935, at the opening of the Kuomintang's Sixth Plenary Session of the Fourth Central Committee, Wang Jingwei was shot twice while a photograph was being taken, and came to Shanghai for treatment; Huang Jinrong found doctors for him, and from then on the two were on friendly terms. Before Wang Jingwei went over to the enemy and set about forming a puppet government, he came to Huang Jinrong's house and asked Huang to call together men of trade and industry and social notables for a meeting; at first Huang Jinrong meant to use the Great World as the place, but Wang Jingwei thought the Great World's hall too small and not compact enough, and later Huang Jinrong chose the Grand Theater cinema for the meeting-place, and had Yuan Lüdeng and his student Cheng Ziliang arrange the hall, with Yuan Lüdeng, Wang Xiaolai, and others notifying people of every circle. At the meeting Wang Jingwei put out his traitor's fallacy of "saving the nation through peace" and "saving the nation by a roundabout road," asking people of every circle to support "saving the nation through peace." Afterwards Wang Jingwei gave a banquet for Huang Jinrong at the Leshan Garden Hotel, run by the Japanese, and that day Gong Tianjian and I went along with him. At the banquet the Japanese officer Sato said to Gong Tianjian: "We warmly invite old Mr. Huang to be an adviser and help us set Shanghai's affairs in order; Mr. Huang's name in Shanghai is great, and a word from him carries weight." Gong Tianjian answered: "Boss Huang is old and in poor health, and retired long ago." Huang Jinrong then rose from his chair and said: "I'm old, I'm no use anymore." As he spoke he swayed and tottered, and I at once helped him from the Leshan Garden Hotel, and Gong Tianjian took his leave after paying his respects to Sato and Wang Jingwei.

Wang Jingwei next had Zhou Fohai come to Huang Jinrong's house, and Gong Tianjian and I received him and took him up to see Huang Jinrong. Zhou Fohai asked Huang Jinrong to be mayor of Shanghai, and Huang Jinrong, lying in a rattan chair, said to Zhou Fohai: "Thank you, Mr. Zhou, but my health is really no good, and besides I've no learning; these days, never mind standing up, I can't even sit for an hour." I chimed in: "Old master, let me help you to bed to rest — a few pipes will pick you up." Gong Tianjian then said: "Mr. Zhou, Boss Huang is unwell today; we're very sorry — another day." And he saw Zhou Fohai out.

Besides these, the puppet chief of the Huangpu sub-bureau, the Japanese Kobayashi, and other Japanese also came to call at Huang Jinrong's house, asking Huang Jinrong to be president of the Peace Maintenance Association, and Huang Jinrong again feigned illness and declined with soft words. There was also one Xu Tieshan, who is said to have once tutored the Japanese emperor and to have joined the Hongmen. The Japanese secret service, making use of Xu Tieshan's Hongmen connections, organized the "Yellow Way Society," and Xu Tieshan too came to Huang Jinrong's house to ask Huang's help in heading the Yellow Way Society, and Huang Jinrong again declined on the plea of illness.

The Japanese and puppets asked Huang to come out of retirement many times without achieving their aim, and in the end sent an agent named Tengqu, nominally to guard Huang Jinrong's safety but in fact to keep secret watch on him. This Tengqu was quartered in the Huang house for a long time, and left only just before the victory in the War of Resistance.

By Huang Jinrong's talk with Gong Tianjian, Hang Shijun, Lu Jinchen, and a few other trusted disciples and me, his feigning illness to decline puppet posts had several causes. The first was fear of death. After Fu Xiao'an was assassinated, Huang Jinrong said to Gong Tianjian and me: "Work for the Japanese and your name stinks and your life's not safe." The second was that Chiang Kai-shek in Chongqing was still talking of resistance; Huang's disciple Hu Shouzhi, who worked at the Shen Bao office, often came to the Huang house to tell of the political situation and of Chongqing, and Huang still had a fondness for Chiang, and once said: "Don't push a thing to the last; a smart man doesn't strike at ninety-nine — best leave a little room." The third was that Huang Jinrong loved to listen to *pingtan*; at the Huang house in Junpeili his disciples often brought in old *pingtan* performers to sing for him, and hearing the *Tale of Yue Fei* — how Yue Fei served the country with pure loyalty and won men's respect, while Qin Hui and his wife played the fawning traitors and left a stink for ten thousand years — stirred something in his mind. Once, after listening to the storyteller's *Tale of Yue Fei*, Huang Jinrong said to Gong Tianjian, Lu Jinchen, and a few other trusted disciples and me: "The land is China's; though the Japanese have fought their way in and seized Shanghai, they can't carry Shanghai off to Japan. That I could get power and climb up had to do with Chiang Kai-shek — when he did well, I did well. Now he's in Chongqing, and I can only help him; I can't have dealings with the Japanese and leave a stink for ten thousand years. I've got to think of my sons and grandsons to come." The fourth was that Huang Jinrong was an old hand in the ways of the world and habitually played the "smooth-on-both-sides" game; by his experience, by not putting himself forward and using his disciples and others to do things, he could sit back and enjoy the fruits. So he introduced his disciple Lu Ying to become puppet chief of the police bureau, and introduced the "Three Elders of Shanghai" — Wen Lanting, Yuan Lüdeng, and Lin Kanghou — to take puppet posts, and thereby controlled things from afar and gained advantage on every hand.

On Wang Jingwei's birthday, Huang Jinrong specially asked Wang Yiting, comprador of the Japanese Mitsubishi firm, to paint with care a "Long-Browed Arhat and Buddha of Longevity" scroll, and sent his secretary Gong Tianjian to Nanjing to give it to Wang Jingwei with his felicitations.

Huang Jinrong once got Zhou Fohai to do him a favor. The reason was that his disciple Shi Yuchun was then the head of the Shanghai sugar trade, and, together with the sugar merchants, had brought sugar from Taiwan and dodged the tax again and again, until the puppet tax bureau caught him out, detained Xi Delin, owner of the Shansheng sugar company on Fourth Avenue, seized two hundred bales of cane sugar worth six or seven hundred million in reserve notes, and meant besides to fine him five hundred million and prosecute Shi Yuchun. Shi Yuchun, in great alarm, went to Huang Jinrong for a way out; and since Shi Yuchun's wife was one of the ten sworn sisters of Huang Jinrong's daughter-in-law Li Zhiqing, she appealed to Li Zhiqing for help too. Huang Jinrong and Li Zhiqing sent me to go with Shi Yuchun's wife to the house of Zhou Fohai's concubine (on Rue Massenet), where we gave the concubine thirty thousand yuan in cash and five bars of gold. The next day Zhou Fohai telephoned Huang Jinrong, and Huang Jinrong had me go to the Zhou house again that evening; when I got there, Zhou Fohai said: "I'll send men with you to the warehouse — bring plenty of hands, and move the two hundred bales of sugar out this very night, and don't lose any time. I've already told the tax bureau: so long as the back tax is paid, there'll be no fine." I did as Zhou Fohai said, and the matter was settled smoothly.

Huang Jinrong also sent me to buy grain. In the Japanese-puppet period the control of grain was extremely strict; by then Wen Lanting, Yuan Lüdeng, and Lin Kanghou had gone over and joined the puppet body controlling finance, grain, and cotton cloth, and Huang Jinrong had me get in touch with Yuan Lüdeng, who let me buy a lot of rice at Suzhou and ship it back to Shanghai, to give out gruel at the Guandi Temple at Laoximen, the God-of-Wealth Temple on Wuding Road, and other places.

Between 1940 and 1945, Huang Jinrong was secretly in touch with Gu Zhutong of the Kuomintang Third War Zone in Chongqing. Gu Zhutong once sent his brother-in-law, a man surnamed Xu, to Junpeili to meet Huang, and Huang Jinrong sent his disciple Qin Xingyan to the offices the Third War Zone had set up in Nanjing, Zhejiang, and elsewhere; I heard from Qin Xingyan that he had gone to Gu Zhutong to ask him to issue a commission for underground work. Before the victory in 1945, Gu Zhutong appointed He Shangshi commissioner of the Shanghai liaison-commissioner's office, Qin Xingyan detachment commander, and Huang Jinrong's son Huang Yuantao brigade commander. He Shangshi swore brotherhood with Huang Yuantao and Qin Xingyan. He Shangshi is said to be now in Taiwan as a National Assembly delegate, and Qin Xingyan, after going to Taiwan, served as an alternate National Assembly delegate.

Huang Jinrong was also in touch with "Two-Gun Wang Bamei," then a guerrilla in the Lake Tai region. Around 1941, an American fighter plane, aiding Chongqing against Japan, was shot down by the Japanese in the Lake Tai region; the pilot, an American named Balaote (a transliteration), came down by parachute in the Lake Tai region, and was rescued by men under the guerrilla Wang Bamei. Wang Bamei sent word to Huang Jinrong, and Qin Xingyan, He Shangshi, Tao Xuesheng, and others contrived to escort Balaote to the rear.

### XII. Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng

Of the two, Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng, Huang was the elder and had made his fortune the earlier, and Du Yuesheng was one Huang Jinrong had raised up single-handed; the two had long carried on the opium-and-gambling business together, and their relations had originally been very close. Later Du Yuesheng came into connection with H. H. Kung, T. V. Soong, Dai Li, and the like; his disciple Lu Jingshi served as chief of the military-law department of the Songhu Garrison Command, his disciple Wang Zhaohuai as commander of the garrison command's detective brigade, and Lu Jingshi manipulated the Shanghai labor unions; Du Yuesheng's power and reach came to exceed Huang Jinrong's, and friction arose between the two. What angered Huang Jinrong most was that his favorite pupil, Chen Peide, who had been chairman of the union at the British-American Tobacco factory, meaning to stand for the chairmanship of the Shanghai General Labor Union, was actually charged by Lu Jingshi with being a suspected Communist and detained by the garrison command — a great humiliation to Huang Jinrong, who ground his teeth and said in fury: "Hmph! Without my lifting him up, how could he ever have scrambled so high? Now see how it is — he eats his fill and forgets the man who tilled the field. He forgets me; then I'll have no more of him." Huang Jinrong went straight to Yang Hu, commander of the Songhu Garrison, to demand Chen Peide's release on bail. When Chiang Kai-shek came to Shanghai and to the Huang garden, Huang Jinrong complained to Chiang Kai-shek of how his pupil had been struck at by Du Yuesheng, and Chiang directed Yang Hu to mediate, since Yang was on good terms with both Huang and Du. After Yang Hu's persuasion, Lu Jingshi and Chen Peide each served a spell as chairman of the General Labor Union, seemingly on equal terms, but Huang and Du kept up their secret scheming against each other.

In 1948, as the Kuomintang armies collapsed step by step on the battlefields and prices soared everywhere, Chiang Kai-shek appointed Yu Hongjun economic supervisor-commissioner for the Shanghai district and Chiang Ching-kuo deputy economic supervisor-commissioner, commanding the so-called "Suppress-the-Rebellion and Build-the-Nation Corps" and the "Greater Shanghai Youth Service Corps," meaning to make a great effort and steady the failing situation.

As soon as Chiang Ching-kuo reached Shanghai, Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng both invited him one after the other, preparing grand banquets. But Chiang paid no heed to Du Yuesheng's several invitations, so that Du felt matters were amiss. Huang Jinrong sent his trusted man Huang Zhenshi with a prepared invitation to invite Chiang Ching-kuo in person, for Huang Zhenshi had then been engaged as an assessment commissioner and could get close to Chiang. Chiang Ching-kuo at first put him off and would not go, and Huang Zhenshi said: "If it's not convenient to come to the Huang house or to Huang Jinrong's Rong Society, then please come to my 'Zhen Society'; I'm an assessment commissioner under the commissioner's office, and going to the Zhen Society can be called convening a work meeting — all quite proper and aboveboard." Chiang Ching-kuo agreed by word of mouth to come to the banquet the next day, and Huang Zhenshi was beside himself with delight. The next day he had the banquet laid, but who should come but Chiang's representatives Wu Shaoshu and Jiang Hengxiang — a bitter disappointment.

Now, when Huang Zhenshi invited Chiang Ching-kuo to the Zhen Society, I had got wind of it and reported to Huang Jinrong; hearing it, Huang changed his mind and said: "Better let the young mistress invite him — the young mistress has a good hand at these things, and young Mr. Chiang will listen to her." So saying, he gave a slight smile. I told Li Zhiqing, and she telephoned Chiang Ching-kuo herself to invite him to a banquet at the Huang house. The next day Chiang Ching-kuo came to the Huang house as agreed, and was received by Li Zhiqing; at the table Chiang declared his resolve to "beat the tigers," and asked Li Zhiqing about speculation and profiteering in the Shanghai exchange. Li Zhiqing's son Huang Qiyu was a broker at the securities exchange, and she herself was the power behind him, so she knew the exchange inside out; but wishing to use the chance to strike at Du Yuesheng on Huang Jinrong's behalf, she answered: "The exchange is in the charge of the Du family uncle" (meaning Du Yuesheng), "run by his third son, young Master Weiping. When my son wanted to apply for a broker's license, it cost him ten big bars, and even after pleading with the Du family uncle again and again, it still took five bars of gold." So Li Zhiqing laid an accusation against both Du Yuesheng and his son, and Huang Jinrong was greatly pleased. Later Chiang Ching-kuo arrested and imprisoned Du Weiping, which caused a stir in Shanghai and was applauded by the townspeople. Though this had no necessary direct connection with Li Zhiqing's accusation, Li Zhiqing's provocation fully reflected the discord and struggle between Huang and Du. Besides this, the open and hidden struggles of the two houses' disciples were many, and the Shanghai people of that day mostly knew of them, so I will say no more.

### XIII. Why Huang Jinrong Did Not Go to Hong Kong, and His Circumstances After Liberation

Before the liberation of Shanghai, Du Yuesheng was the first to leave for Hong Kong; then Huang Jinrong's daughter-in-law Li Zhiqing swept up Huang Jinrong's gold and silver and treasure and left Shanghai for Hong Kong, and afterwards went to Taiwan. At the time some urged Huang Jinrong to go to Hong Kong, but Huang Jinrong would not agree. He once said to Ding Yongchang, Gong Tianjian, and me: "Yang Hu says the Communist Party's leaders know about me and can let bygones be bygones; and they wrote out a note and gave it to Yang Hu to pass to me, which I can hand to whoever's in charge in Shanghai after liberation, and they won't arrest me." By Gong Tianjian's account, he and Huang Qiming (Huang Jinrong's grandson) had seen this note. This too may have been why Huang Jinrong did not go to Hong Kong.

In 1950, Huang Jinrong said to me: "I've bought 50,000 yuan of public bonds now, and I've no cash; go to Hong Kong and find Du Yuesheng, and ask him to get back for me a loan of 100,000 yuan — it's what Liu Yuzhang borrowed off me some years back." In February 1950 I went to Hong Kong to find Du Yuesheng, and met with a soft rebuff: he told me to go to the young mistress (that is, Li Zhiqing) for a way out, and I could only return to Shanghai and tell Huang Jinrong how things stood. Huang Jinrong had his secretary write a letter to Du Yuesheng, the gist being: I am now sending Cheng Xiwen to Hong Kong; as to recovering the 100,000 yuan owed by Liu Yuzhang, I must ask you to help settle it. I asked Huang Jinrong how Liu Yuzhang had come to owe this 100,000 yuan. Huang Jinrong said it was from an arms deal that he, Yang Hu, Cheng Ziqing, and others had done privately with the Frenchman Qiaobianshi, chief of the French Concession police, selling to Liu Yuzhang, who was an arms dealer and had never paid. Huang Jinrong had done arms business privately with Yang Hu and the others for many years, but he kept it close as a corked bottle, and even I had not known of it. Carrying Huang Jinrong's letter, I went to Hong Kong a second time to find Du Yuesheng, and Huang Jinrong sent Du several urgent telegrams besides, asking Du to find a way to recover the debt. In the end Du Yuesheng had his disciple Wan Molin remit 50,000 yuan to Huang Jinrong, and only then did I return to Shanghai, to keep Huang Jinrong company until he died of illness in 1953.
