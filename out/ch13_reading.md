## Afterword

For a book to be in print ten years, printed thirteen times over, and still coming out in new editions is a thrilling thing.

Yet I, its author, must admit that this is not because the first edition was well written, but because every edition has added important new material; not because the author can write, but because the subject is so good.

I have had the good fortune to interview, face to face, many who served on the hidden front themselves. Some foreign authors can make a book out of interviewing a single agent; I have interviewed several hundred.

The book is, one may say, the first reasonably complete monograph on China's hidden front. There is no lack of related works, but most write of a single person or a single case; only I was rash enough to want to write the whole history.

And yet this hidden front is a vast buried treasure, not to be dug out all at once, nor exhausted in ten years of writing.

This newest upgraded illustrated edition has added a great deal on intelligence work. It draws on the interviews I have done since the first edition, as the reader can see from the notes, where many an old intelligence hand appears in his own words.

It was not only that material was added; the treatment went deeper. No sooner had the first edition come out than it coincided with a lasting wave of spy-thriller enthusiasm, and I found myself riding a tiger I could not dismount. I seized every chance to interview old cadres and their families and to rescue the record. At the same time I lectured, gave television talks, took part in scholarly exchanges, and published such pieces as "Zhou Enlai's Little-Known Art of Secret War" and "Mao Zedong's Secret Methods of War." Arranging the material meant arranging my own thoughts, and my understanding of the Communists' hidden front deepened by degrees. I found that the Communists' intelligence and security work, of all the fields of modern China, was the earliest to reach world-class standing; it had a character of its own and a system of its own, and may well be called a cultural treasure-house.

In recent years, besides my *China's Secret War*, a number of works titled "The Secret War of So-and-so" have appeared, which shows that people care all the more about this secret war.

I fixed on the title "secret war," rather than "spy war" or "intelligence war," because this front is not easily bounded. In the Communists' language it was once called the hidden front. But society often equates the "hidden front" with the "underground Party" or the "second front." In fact the underground Party was the Party organization active in the White areas; the hidden front was a line run by the Party committees, and not confined to the White areas. The "second front" was the mass struggle and student movement in the Nationalist-ruled areas, while the hidden front lay deep behind the scenes.

Strictly speaking, the core of the hidden front is "intelligence work" and "security work." But this work radiates outward too; the united front carries intelligence with it, diplomacy gives it cover, and it can hardly be held within bounds. A concentric-circle division fits it best: "intelligence work," "security work," "agents," and "spies" belong to the core; a broader division adds "White-area work," "urban work," and the "second front," and further out "united-front work," "liaison work," and "diplomatic work." The concentric circles widen ring by ring, and there must be some name to cover them all, which can only be a name at once vague and yet compelling: "secret war."

The scope of "secret war" is relatively broad, but it cannot be blurred outward without limit. Abroad there is talk of the "organs of force," and those departments all seem bound up with this subject. On November 12, 2013, the Central Committee decided to set up a National Security Commission, and this opened a train of thought: the secret war I write of is precisely the domain of national security.

In its early days the Communist Party stood outside the law and could not survive a single day without security work, and so it valued political security to the utmost. The early organ for it was called the "State Political Security Bureau," and the First Bureau of the national Ministry of Public Security in the early years of the state was likewise the "Political Security Bureau." As the Party became a great nationwide party, and then the ruling party of the whole country, the security problems it faced grew more varied, broader, and more complex, spanning the political, territorial, military, economic, cultural, social, technological, informational, ecological, resource, and nuclear domains. The Communists' early leading organ for such matters, the "Special Committee," was also called the "Supreme Committee"; and the newly established National Security Commission has for its chairman the Party's general secretary in person, with the premier of the State Council and the chairman of the National People's Congress Standing Committee as its two vice-chairmen — a measure of the very high standing security work now holds.

New security problems call for a new security outlook; and with a new security outlook one comes to see history afresh. Looking back at history from an overall security outlook, one finds that the mission of the Communists' intelligence and security work turned always upon the core of security; that the history of the Communists' intelligence and security work may be called an overall security history; and that the Communists' overall security history has, in turn, a distinctly Chinese character.

The history of the Communists' intelligence and security work may be divided into periods: the founding period, the maturing period, the victory period, the construction period, the wrecking period, and the rule-of-law period.

The founding period runs from the creation of the Central Special Branch in 1927 and the founding of the State Political Security Bureau in 1931 to the outbreak of the War of Resistance in 1937. Before it lay the Party's founding period and the Great Revolution period, when intelligence and security work had not yet formed full-time organs or a specialized system. In this stage the Communists' intelligence and security work was raw and forceful, but under the great influence of the Soviet model too.

The maturing period runs from the founding of the Central Special Work Committee in 1937 and the Central Social Affairs Department in 1939 to the victory of the War of Resistance in 1945. In this period the Communists came fully into maturity, forming a character, a revolutionary road, a guiding ideology, and an intelligence-and-security system all their own.

The victory period runs from the outbreak of the civil war in 1946 to the founding of the state on September 30, 1949. In this period the Communists' intelligence and security work achieved its greatest results, serving effectively the seizure of nationwide power.

The construction period runs from the founding of the People's Republic of China and the establishment of the Ministry of Public Security and the Central Investigation Department to the eve of the "Cultural Revolution" in 1966. From a party of revolution to a party in power, the Communists' intelligence and security work passed from the form of the Party to the form of the state, and how to fit the new security situation was a new exploration.

The wrecking period runs from the launching of the "Cultural Revolution" and the smashing of the police, procuratorate, and courts in 1966 to the crushing of the "Gang of Four" and the restoration of the police, procuratorate, and courts in the autumn of 1976. This period was not only a national catastrophe but a great wrecking of the security system.

The rule-of-law period runs from the Third Plenum of the Eleventh Central Committee's decision at the end of 1978 to strengthen democracy and legal construction — the promulgation of the criminal law and the criminal-procedure law, the restoration of the people's procuratorates and people's courts, the founding of the Ministry of State Security, the drafting of the 1982 Constitution — up to the founding of the Central National Security Commission in 2013; China is strengthening the rule of law across the board.

History gives rise to books, and my *China's Secret War* should form a series too.

The intelligence and security work of the Second Revolutionary Civil War period (the Red Army period), briefly introduced in this book's first chapter, should be the first volume of that series; the intelligence and security work of the War of Resistance period, told in detail in chapters two through ten, may be the second; the intelligence and security work of the Third Revolutionary Civil War period (the War of Liberation period), sketched in chapter eleven, should be the third; and the intelligence and security work after the founding of the state, hinted at in chapter twelve, will be the fourth.

For now, the material I have gathered is enough for four substantial books, one each for the founding, maturing, victory, and construction periods. But my own time and energy are limited, and I cannot write so much all at once.

In the near future I will bring out two works. One is on the intelligence war against Japan, dissecting the top cases of the international contest for intelligence. The other is on the security problems of the Communist Central Committee in its Northwest period, the top cases of the struggle against agents and against espionage.

Alongside my documentary writing I also work as a screenwriter, with such spy-drama television series as *On the Eve of the Founding* and *Daughters of a Troubled Age*, such documentaries as *Secret War* and *Consultative Republic*, and such television-lecture series as *Zhou Enlai's Art of Secret War*, *Secret Warfare*, and *China's Secret War*.

In these years spy-drama film and television have been all the rage, but audiences always complain that the dramas feel too made-up. I have written a novel, *The Great Spy of the East*, on how the Communists won over Japanese to supply intelligence for the War of Resistance. This work carries real conviction and awaits adaptation for the screen.

The documentary series on China's secret front may be called a written record of China's intelligence history and China's public-security history, and may also be called an overall security history. It is thus bound to be a vast systematic undertaking.

I have already interviewed several hundred old cadres, including the leading authorities in the field; I have already gathered a great deal of written and audio-visual material, forming a small archive. But I cannot even organize it all on my own. This undertaking is not one I can complete alone; better that a body of young scholars work at it together.

I marvel to this day: why is the "study of the Communists' hidden front" not taken up by some university research unit? Is the value of such a research project not compelling enough?

Sadly, all I can promise is to keep working at it myself.
