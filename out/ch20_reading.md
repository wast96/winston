## The Xingchang Apothecary

In the east of Guangzhou, the road before Dashatou Station was short and broad, near enough to pass for a square, yet it was packed with motorcars of every size, and what little space remained was filled up entirely by rickshaws.

No sooner had Ling Wen and Yi Junnian come out of the station than the pullers closed in around them. The two had come over from Hong Kong, got up as a medicine merchant and his wife. Guangzhou was the provincial capital, and the rickshaw men who plied their trade at the railway station all knew a few words of Mandarin; they vied with one another to ask the two travelers where they were bound, but Yi Junnian answered in a broad, native Cantonese.

Xihaokou, on Taiping South Road—they were bound for the Nanhua Building. When he had wired ahead from Hong Kong to book the hotel, Yi Junnian had told Ling Wen that it was at the Xinya Hotel in the Nanhua Building that he had first truly learned the principles of revolution. After the Canton–Hong Kong Strike began, the Communist Party had opened a Labour College on the fourth floor of the Nanhua Building; Comrade Deng Zhongxia had lectured there, and there too he had once seen Comrade Shaoshan.

They were medicine merchants over from Hong Kong, without any great heap of trunks and cases, but Yi Junnian took two rickshaws all the same. Under Chen Jitang's rule of Guangdong, manners had turned conservative for a season, and a man and woman riding together would easily draw notice. The two rickshaws went up onto the East Iron Bridge one behind the other; along the banks of the Donghao Creek the boats were piled high with firewood, and the woodstacks on the shore, too, ran on farther than the eye could follow. Back in Guangzhou again, he thought.

Beyond the iron bridge lay the Bund, and along the Pearl River theaters and restaurants stood in rows. Yi Junnian told the pullers to slow their pace and to run the two rickshaws abreast, so that he could point out the sights of the place to Ling Wen. Back in Guangzhou all at once, he seemed a little exhilarated.

“Just as in Shanghai, here too there's an amusement park on the roof of the Sincere Company.” He was warming to his talk when all at once he fell silent. Perhaps, Ling Wen thought, he had remembered some scene out of the past.

The rickshaws went along the Pearl River at an unhurried pace, and in less than an hour they reached the Nanhua Building. After a while the two came out from under the hotel's arcade, turned into the Xidi, passed through a memorial archway, and walked on a stretch; then Yi Junnian, pointing off with his left hand toward the riverside, said to Ling Wen, “That is Shamian—all Guangzhou's imperialist consulates are there. In the great strike of the fourteenth year of the Republic, they set up machine guns on the far side and swept the crowd with fire; how many died! Look at Guangzhou now—it seems to have forgotten that scene.”

All the way Yi Junnian talked without cease, as though he were another man; but Ling Wen said little, with the look of one weighed down by her thoughts. These past years she had tried to keep herself from thinking of Long Dong; she could not even remember how the news of his death had reached her—who had told her, and when, and how she had come so easily to believe it. Perhaps because in these years too many comrades had died; perhaps because she believed in her heart that if Long Dong were alive he would surely find a way to tell her. She had asked comrades who came from Guangzhou, and they had all told her, with grief, that after the failure of the Guangzhou Uprising the underground Party organization there had been all but wholly destroyed, and countless comrades killed.

When Lin Shi told her Long Dong was still alive, she realized she had not believed the news at once; yet her hope had begun, little by little, to put out shoots. But now that she had come to Guangzhou, she found herself doubting again.

They did not keep on along Taiping Road but turned into a narrow lane in front of Jianglan Street, pressing in among a crowd still steeped in the leisured ease of the New Year. In this Xiguan district the streets and lanes were packed close with shops, ranged one against the next like fish-scales. Though it was winter, and near midday, the flagstones of the road were still somewhat damp.

The two of them walked and looked about, seeming to find an interest in everything. Today was the eighth of the first month, and people were gathered in the teahouses and taverns. Guangzhou was not like Shanghai: here the famous eating-houses would perversely open in the narrow lanes, and with the pressing of wine and the finger-games upstairs, the waiters bawling out orders, and the clash of ladle and wok from the kitchens, the din of voices fairly seethed, threaded now and then with strings and pipes—no wonder Old Yi should say the city of Guangzhou seemed to have forgotten that cruel massacre of years before.

A man came in from the mouth of the lane with huge bamboo baskets slung from a carrying-pole, live chickens and geese inside them; only when he had turned and gone into a tavern did Yi Junnian and Ling Wen pass on. Out of the Shiqifu lane, and there was Jianglan Street.

At the liaison station in Hong Kong they had been told that on past the Shiqifu lane, once you reached Yang Lane, you would see a building with a round white roof: that was the Tiannan Teahouse. Beside the teahouse ran a lane called Xirong, and the Xingchang Apothecary was inside Xirong Lane.

Jianglan Street was all apothecaries, stocked with every kind of medicinal herb, western and native; each shop dealt specially in a few sorts, and when a customer wanted goods it had not got, it could always fetch them from another shop—trading what one had for what one lacked, and sharing out the profit. It was for this convenience, too, that the underground Party organization had opened an apothecary here: on the one hand as a liaison station, from which people and supplies were passed on to the next station, one station linked to the next all the way to the Soviet areas; on the other, so that from here it could buy and ship the medicines the Soviet areas so badly needed.

The great druggists out on the main street had shops of generous frontage, most of them with an arcade before the door. The Xingchang lay deep in a lane, its premises what the Cantonese call a bamboo-tube house: two stories, the shopfront less than a rod and a half wide, but three bays deep. From the doorway one caught the smell of medicinal herbs; inside the shop a row of shelves stood on either side, bearing flat bamboo trays of amomum, pinellia, and schisandra—some dozens of herbs, set out for customers to look over. On the floor stood great baskets too, holding the hairy fig-root, Solomon's-seal, and Chinese yam that the Cantonese love to buy for their soups. During the New Year the apothecary did no trade, yet the local people often came to buy such things.

The shop was empty, but from the inner room came the sound of shuffling tiles; in a moment a shop-hand came out to greet the customers. Yi Junnian drew a letter from inside his coat and handed it to him, adding that a telegram had been sent to the shop the day before. The hand took the letter and called toward the inner room for the boss.

The apothecary's proprietor came out. Taking the letter to read even as he told the hand to play a round of mahjong in his place, he wished the customers a prosperous New Year. The man at the Hong Kong liaison station had told Yi Junnian that the proprietor's surname was Mo: “Comrade Mo Shaoqiu is a seasoned underground worker, with very good social connections in Guangzhou.”

Boss Mo already knew who his visitors were, but aloud he said only that the telegram had been received, and that he had not expected Boss Yi to hurry over during the holiday.

“It wasn't so very urgent,” Yi Junnian said. “With the New Year on us, I thought I'd bring my wife along and see a bit of Guangzhou while we were about it.”

“Aiya, had I known Mrs. Yi would be in Guangzhou for the New Year, I'd have asked you both over a few days sooner. The flower-market stroll on Jianglan Street, the New Year's-eve flower fair—now that's a lively thing to see.”

“The flower-market stroll—isn't that at Shuangmendi?” Ling Wen said. “‘At the Shuangmen flower fair the crowds throng to and fro; baskets brimful, the great boughs lush.’ I've long wanted to see the hanging-bell flowers of Lingnan.”

Ling Wen had always loved to grow flowers and tend plants, and remembered a good many lines of verse about them.

“These last few years,” Boss Mo said, “there's a flower market on Jianglan Street too.”

They went into the inner room, where a table of people sat at mahjong by the window. Boss Mo explained: “They're all neighbors from the shops next door. Would Boss Yi care to join a game?”

Yi Junnian shook his head with a smile, and the three of them went farther in; behind a door was a staircase, and they climbed to the second floor. The Guangzhou weather was not cold, and two Manchu windows stood half open, their glass of five colors, the sunlight falling through the etched patterns onto the flower-table beneath. Boss Mo pointed to a row of flowerpots: “These are the hanging-bell flowers.” There were great sprays of peach blossom too, and narcissus.

A shop-hand came up with hot water to make tea, and they took their seats. Mo Shaoqiu still wore the smile of a tradesman greeting his customers, but his words had changed: “Comrade Old Yi, Comrade Ling Wen—was the journey smooth?”

The Hong Kong liaison station had notified the apothecary in advance, and Mo Shaoqiu had made his preparations long since. He had Yi Junnian and Ling Wen sit a little while, saying that someone would come to see them presently.

“Have you been to Guangzhou before?” Mo Shaoqiu asked them.

“I worked in Guangzhou for a good many years, and only withdrew after the Guangzhou Uprising,” Yi Junnian answered. “Comrade Ling Wen has never been to Guangzhou, but her husband, Comrade Long Dong, worked here—and later gave his life.”

Ling Wen shot him a glance. Old Yi always liked to go about telling people of Long Dong's death; she could guess what was in his mind, and did not much care for it. The closeness to her that Old Yi displayed before other comrades went, she felt, beyond the closeness that actually was between them. And in speaking of Long Dong—though he had plainly never once met him—Old Yi always told how able Long Dong had been, how brave, spinning what he had heard at second hand into a legend of the revolution; whereas in truth, Ling Wen knew, the traces of Long Dong about her—the photographs, the old clothes, the odd fragment of a phrase she let fall—all made Old Yi somewhat uneasy. In this matter Yi Junnian's manner seemed less than sincere.

Besides, Lin Shi had told her that Long Dong had not died at all.

“Long Dong? I know of him—he didn't die.” So said Mrs. Mo. Their round of mahjong finished and the players dispersed, Mrs. Mo had come upstairs and caught just those words.

Boss Mo and Mrs. Mo—the Party organization had charged the two of them with this liaison station, and the couple had worked here for many years. At her words, they all turned their eyes to her.

“I oughtn't to be telling you this—I was moved off that line of work long ago, and by rights I shouldn't speak to anyone of those matters.” She sat down beside Boss Mo and said to Ling Wen, “But you're his wife, and I think I may tell you. Even a revolution must allow for the ties of kin, no?” And she turned her head to ask Boss Mo.

“You old gossip,” Mo Shaoqiu said, cursing her with a laugh.

“Comrade Long Dong—I know of him. After the uprising it grew very hard for the Party to survive in Guangzhou. That was—the seventeenth year of the Republic. The organization had me work as a courier, because I was a woman, you see, and a bit of a busybody besides, so that walking down the street I didn't easily rouse the enemy's suspicion.

“For a whole year the streets were too dangerous—at any moment a plainclothesman might stop you; sometimes one in a gauze shirt and a copper-basin hat would come swaying up to you and lay hold to search you then and there. And if your accent wasn't a Guangzhou one, like as not they'd haul you off.

“I worked as a courier a few months, and then was moved here to the liaison station, and became man and wife with you. I remember the day they had me carry a letter to Gaodi Street—it had to be there by first light. It had rained all night; the rain stopped as dawn drew near, and in the misty gloom the flagstones were slick underfoot one step and awash the next, till my shoes and trousers were soaked through.

“Not a soul in the whole street—only a night-soil cart going by, its wheels creaking, a bell shaken to call people out to empty their commodes, ding-a-ling, ding-a-ling, the bell ringing on and on in the lane alongside. I was almost there; the contact point was in a lane beside the People's Palace. The People's Palace—do you know it? Chen Jitang seized the opium boats and built the People's Palace with the fines he took in, said it was to shelter the homeless poor; but the poor were everywhere—how could it ever take them all in? A warlord—it was all just for show.

“Once into the lane, a few more steps and I'd have been there. At the corner where Gaodi Street turned into the lane, someone hiding under the arcade suddenly darted out and stopped me: ‘Don't go in.’ Why wouldn't he let me in? I didn't even know the man.

“I looked up at him—a big, tall man, and very handsome, in a raincoat with the hood turned up to hide his face; the sky was dark and there was the mist, but his eyes were so bright I felt almost shy. Then he said, ‘There are police inside—plainclothes.’ Seeing me pretend not to understand, he added, ‘That contact station's been found out by the enemy; there's an ambush laid, and I was afraid some comrade would go in and be taken, so I've been waiting here.’ That was how he saved me. I went back and reported to my leader—Secretary Ouyang, Ouyang Min. He thought a moment and said, that must have been Comrade Long Dong.

“Long Dong—I fixed the name in my memory. Secretary Ouyang also said that Long Dong was not of our line of work, that his work was more important than ours and more secret, and so he warned me not to speak of the matter to anyone. Secretary Ouyang was very glad that day, because the secret letter he'd had me carry was so important that had it fallen into enemy hands the consequences would have been grave.

“We owed Comrade Long Dong such thanks, he said; Comrade Long Dong was a talent the Party could ill do without—there was no secret of the Guangzhou warlord government he could not learn, and his intelligence network had reached deep inside the enemy. I thought he said too much, that he shouldn't have told me such things. For some reason I felt a secret fear for Comrade Long Dong: if so many within the underground Party knew of his secret work, then it was no great step to the enemy knowing too.

“Another year on, I read in the Guangzhou Republican Daily that the Nationalists had cracked a Communist intelligence network—shot on the spot Comrade Lu Zhongde, who had been planted in the public-security bureau, arrested the whole of that underground group, and taken the special-committee secretary, Ouyang Min, as well. The only one to get away was Comrade Long Dong; the enemy could not, it seems, lay hands on a photograph, and even had a likeness of him drawn and printed in the paper, saying that anyone who saw him and reported him to the local police might claim two hundred silver dollars. And after that there was never any word of him again.”

“Lu Zhongde—I remember that name; someone spoke of him during the Canton–Hong Kong Strike. A seaman, I think he was. Later he joined the workers' pickets—though whether it's the same man I couldn't say.” The recollection came to Yi Junnian suddenly, and he put in a word.

“That's very likely,” Mo Shaoqiu said. “After the great strike, the Party sought out workers of firm revolutionary will, quick and capable, and had them go underground; it even picked strong, sturdy comrades to work their way into the enemy's garrison command and public-security bureau. I went and sat the examination for the public-security bureau myself—that wretch of a police chief Zhu Huiri had them lined up to be picked over, and I, built like a stick of sugarcane, all skin and bones, got thrown out.”

“When was the paper?” Ling Wen asked. “You said the Guangzhou Republican Daily carried it—when was that?”

“I can't say exactly—the eighteenth year of the Republic, around the Dragon Boat Festival, as I recall.”

“Could a copy of that day's paper still be found?” Ling Wen went on.

“Round the turn from Jianglan Street is Guangfu Road—the newspaper street of Xiguan; someone there might know,” said Mrs. Mo.

They were still talking when the shop-hand called up from below, “Boss—a visitor.”

The newcomer had come the whole way from Ruijin, Changting, Yongding, Dapu, ten-odd days on the road. He said to Mo Shaoqiu, “At Qingxi I heard people had come over from Shanghai, and feared I'd not be in time.”

He was a messenger from the Soviet areas, bearing an important verbal message that had to be delivered to Lin Shi in person; he had not reckoned on Lin Shi's failing to appear in Guangzhou as planned. The messenger was taken aback. His instructions from above were perfectly clear: only on seeing Lin Shi might he speak the content of the message.

This was a thing unforeseen; Lin Shi himself could not have known in advance that an important message awaited him in Guangzhou, one he must hear with his own ears.

For the messenger it was a kind of test. He could break off the delivery and return the way he had come to report back—the safest course, safety first. But because it was a verbal message, he knew its content and understood how much hung upon it; if it could not be conveyed to Shanghai in time, the loss might be beyond all reckoning.

He was no ordinary messenger, but a high-ranking secret courier of the Central Committee's Liaison Bureau. Like Lin Shi, such men were given tasks of the utmost importance that yet very readily met the unexpected, so that they must decide by their own experience and loyalty.

By the ordinary practice of secret courier work, he need not even have met Yi Junnian and Ling Wen; but he decided to see them.

“When do you mean to leave Guangzhou?” he asked them.

“We've booked our return passage—we board at seven tomorrow evening, and the ship sails at twelve midnight.” Yi Junnian did not know who the newcomer was; Boss Mo had led him upstairs and said only that this was Old Xiao, come from the Soviet areas.

The workings of the Guangzhou liaison station Lin Shi had described to Yi Junnian. Boss Mo and Mrs. Mo were no sham married couple, and the shop-hand too was a comrade of the Party; the three of them were the station's permanent staff, with Mo Shaoqiu in charge. In ordinary times the apothecary had some common shop-hands as well, and when trade was brisk Boss Mo would take on a few more workers; these were honest, decent folk, no doubt, but the secret affairs of the underground organization still had to be kept from them. Lin Shi had not told Yi Junnian there would be an “Old Xiao, come from the Soviet areas.” This Old Xiao seemed to know Lin Shi well: “What's he playing at, not coming himself?” Did they work in the same system? Yi Junnian wondered. He told Old Xiao, “Comrade Lin Shi has been wounded, and the organization has decided that the two of us should carry out this task in his stead.”

“Wounded where?” Old Xiao showed a particular concern.

“In the leg.” Yi Junnian gestured at his own calf. “A bullet went clean through—not a grave wound, only it makes a long journey hard. The underground organization has its own doctor, and it's healing well.”

“What happened, over your way?”

So the news had not reached Ruijin, Yi Junnian thought; the damage to the underground organization was grave indeed. Of the central and local command systems some had withdrawn, some still held their ground. Lin Shi's superior department, it appeared, was in Ruijin—then from what unit had Chen Qianli been sent to Shanghai?

For all his years in the underground organization, it was the first time Yi Junnian had had occasion to grasp, on a larger scale, the command and liaison between its parts. Many groups, he knew, depended only on a single personal thread between one rank and the next, so that passing word was very hard and command ran far from smoothly. To keep up the struggle under such conditions, the cohesion of the organization could, much of the time, rest on nothing but the loyalty and will of each single person.

“Agents raided a secret meeting; our identities weren't exposed, and the organization got us out of prison,” Yi Junnian told Old Xiao.

“We knew nothing of this on our side.”

“We really ought to set up a wireless station as soon as we can,” Yi Junnian said.

Old Xiao gave Yi Junnian a glance. There had been a wireless station two years before, kept most secret. At first, the set being low-powered and its sending and receiving unsteady, contact from Shanghai to the southern Soviet areas had all been relayed through a secret Hong Kong station. Later the Hong Kong station had been broken up by the British police.

The next year the Red Army captured a high-powered set on the battlefield of the anti-“encirclement” campaign, and from then on wireless contact between Shanghai and Ruijin was joined up. But the distance was great, sending and receiving still unsteady, and the underground Party's wireless group in Shanghai was again and again hunted by the agents and the Concession police, several times all but exposed.

The “Hao cipher,” for all the brilliance of its design, was at bottom built by adding to and subtracting from a plain-code book; used too often—above all on matters the enemy might already know—it could let a pattern be found. And Old Xiao knew very well that the message he was to deliver to Lin Shi could be conveyed only by the most trustworthy courier, face to face; the wireless was neither secret enough nor, perhaps, fast enough. Even were it wired to the Shanghai wireless group, the various underground bodies had no lateral links between them: it would have to be passed up level by level, then relayed down again by a superior along another single thread, through several ranks before it reached the temporary action group. That, plainly, would not be safe enough either.

The matter was urgent, but he was only a messenger and could not decide on his own authority. He had thought of having Old Mo apply to his superiors for instructions, and contriving to get himself to Hong Kong to wire Ruijin for orders on the underground Party's secret set. But there were far too many uncertain factors in between. Would Old Mo's superiors agree to make contact by radio? How many links in the organization lay between? Would his deciding on his own to go to Hong Kong cause trouble? Would the transmitter's call raise Ruijin quickly? And once Ruijin's station had the message, would the deciphered text go at once to the right person, and thence to a leader who truly grasped the situation?

In fact he had only three choices: do nothing and go back to Ruijin for fresh instructions; deliver the message to one of these two comrades, and have him or her return to Shanghai and pass it to Lin Shi; or make the trip to Shanghai himself. Going back to Ruijin—there would surely not be time; going to Shanghai—he did not know whether he could book passage at once. And these two comrades—he knew neither of them well enough. But a choice he had to make.

“The two of you came to Guangzhou together—who's in charge?” Old Xiao asked.

“I am,” Ling Wen answered. Everyone was surprised, for to all appearances it was Yi Junnian who looked the leader of the pair of them. To tell the truth, Ling Wen herself had found the arrangement strange when Chen Qianli made it before they set out. Everyone knew that Yi Junnian had been the leader of the very underground group she belonged to. But so Chen Qianli had ruled, and no one knew why he had so decided: in everything the two of them did, they were to follow Ling Wen's direction, and Yi Junnian's chief task was to guard and cover her. Comrade Chen Qianli was a man full of surprises. In any case, for a task of this kind Ling Wen had little experience.

“Good, then—come to the back with me and let me have a word.” So Old Xiao said to Ling Wen.

The bamboo-tube house was like a length of bamboo—narrow at the mouth but deep within, and, like a bamboo stalk, made of many joints, each joint a room: the head room, the second hall, the third hall, the tail room, and behind the tail room a kitchen, joint linked to joint. Above the second story the tiled roofs, too, ran one behind another; but the third and fourth roofs were not joined, and between their gable walls two lengths of planking stood in the gap—only from within the upper room could one see clearly that it was a little terrace. Here Ling Wen and Old Xiao stood and talked.

“Do you know who is the highest man in charge of the ‘A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains’ plan?”

“Comrade Lin Shi never spoke of it.”

“It is Comrade Shaoshan. My coming to Guangzhou this time was at Comrade Shaoshan's direct charge—to find Lin Shi and deliver to him, by word of mouth, a secret order.”

“But Comrade Lin Shi did not come to Guangzhou—”

“The task given me was to speak the message to Lin Shi's face, and on no account to disclose it to any third person.”

Then why are you telling me? Ling Wen thought, but she said nothing.

“Perhaps I ought to go back to Ruijin for instructions.” Old Xiao had the feeling of a man who cannot settle his next move; this seasoned old courier, who over the years had handled no end of hard problems, had never once wavered as he did now. “But there isn't time. Comrade Yi Junnian is right—we really ought to put real effort into the wireless.”

But he soon shook off these feelings and said to Ling Wen, “Come here before twelve o'clock tomorrow, and I'll tell you my final decision. If I can get a ticket for the ship, I'll go back to Shanghai with you. If the arrangements don't fall out well, then it must rest on you to carry this message safely to Lin Shi. It is an utterly secret message, one that must on no account be revealed to anyone; if it has to be you who completes it, you must tell Lin Shi with your own lips.”

Ling Wen felt somewhat at a loss. She did not know this comrade, who had appeared before her out of nowhere and laid on her a task of the deepest secrecy.

“Comrade Ling Wen, if this matter must be left to you to complete, you must protect this secret message with your life, and see the task through though you die for it.”

Seeing the look on Ling Wen's face, he added that last.
