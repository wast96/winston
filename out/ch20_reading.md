## Afterword

I am a fan of Shiba Ryōtarō's historical fiction. Whether "fan" is quite the right word in this case I cannot say, but since I never fail to read this author's name whenever it turns up in a magazine, fan will do to begin with.

The book that first made an addict of me was this one, Owl's Castle. It came out in September 1959, and before that it had run as a serial in the Chūgai Nippō, a Buddhist newspaper.

The first thing to say about the novel is that its subject, the ninja, was still, at the time, something strikingly new and fresh. The ninja had been a favorite of the reading public ever since the early Taishō years, when the Tachikawa Bunko invented the character of Sarutobi Sasuke and sent the name of the "ninjutsu master" abroad through the land. Murayama Tomoyoshi's Shinobi no Mono is a well-known instance, and in the postwar vogue for historical fiction that set in around 1955, the ninja in his black costume was already turning up all over the place.

But when it comes to a full-length novel that puts a ninja squarely at its center as the hero, I believe Owl's Castle really was the first of the postwar years. At much the same time, Yamada Fūtarō wrote The Kōga Ninja Scrolls. These two books together set off an explosive boom in ninja fiction, and ninja novels appeared without number. The boom quickly spread into film and television, until there came a day when a full ninja outfit could be had at the toy counter of a department store. For a while Shiba Ryōtarō was known as a "master of ninja fiction."

Shiba Ryōtarō is an Osaka man. In 1955, at the age of thirty-two, he was awarded the eighth Kōdan Club Prize for a story called The Persian Sorcerer. Turning to account an unusual background — he had studied Mongolian at the Osaka School of Foreign Languages — he went on to write several more fantastical short stories drawn from Mongolia and the Western Regions, among them The Xiongnu of the Gobi.

His taste for the fantastical, one might say, became the ninja arts in Owl's Castle. This was the first full-length novel the author had written, and it won him the Naoki Prize for the second half of 1959, making him overnight a popular success.

But the freshness of Owl's Castle is not merely a matter of its ninja subject. (Shiba Ryōtarō, in fact, seems rather to have disliked being called a "master of ninja fiction.") What gave the novel its sense of freshness was its style and its way of drawing character.

The boom in historical fiction that arose around 1955 answers to the high-growth economic policy set in motion at just that time, and to the consumer boom that came with it. Weekly magazines sprang up one after another, like bamboo shoots after rain, and most of them carried a historical novel in serial. It was in 1956 that Ishihara Shintarō's Season of the Sun became a bestseller; and historical fiction too, responding to the temper of the age, took as its hallmarks a quick, dry prose and a profusion of erotic scenes.

The hero of a prewar historical novel — the protagonist of Yoshikawa Eiji's Miyamoto Musashi, say — is the ascetic seeker, a man who stands alone beneath heaven on his single sword. In the swordplay that came into fashion from this period onward, by contrast, the center of the stage is a nihilistic, hard-boiled swordsman, and the whole is studded with the naked bodies of beautiful women and scenes of cruel sword-work.

In the matter of quick prose and eroticism, Shiba Ryōtarō too is surely no exception to the temper of his time. This author is especially keen to portray, by preference, the calculating sort who is alert to profit and loss and moved by money. In Owl's Castle it is the secondary character Kazama Gohei who is such a man, and Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, who plays the double agent; in his next book, The Kamigata Samurai Code, he took the temperament of the Osaka man for his theme.

And yet one can by no means call the heroes he draws nihilists. Rather, they are men possessed by some conviction, idealists in a sense; and that, in an age enamored of nihilism, forms another of the great distinguishing marks of his fiction.

The Tsuzura Jūzō of Owl's Castle lives with a single aim — to kill Toyotomi Hideyoshi — out of his rancor at the massacre of his father, mother, and sister. To bring his life as a ninja to a beautiful completion: that is his heart's desire. In Burn, O Sword, likewise, Shiba draws a man who holds to a conviction, or rather a fixed idea, and carries his stubbornness through to the end. According to Shiba, the leaders of the Shinsengumi were for the most part peasants from the shogunal domains of Musashi Province, and it was precisely because they were peasants that they revered the house of Tokugawa, and precisely because they were peasants that they clung to an outdated warrior code more obstinately than any samurai. The iron rule of the Shinsengumi, "the warrior's code before all," turns out to have been, in a sense, the inverted expression of an inferiority complex.

In The Man of Demonic Cunning, too, the author writes the life of the rationalist genius Ōmura Masujirō, and that of another genius, Kawai Tsuginosuke, who loved the Nagaoka domain as though it were a work of art of his own making and, for that very reason, brought it to ruin. This historical biography, which portrays the extraordinary men of the last days of the shogunate, is to my mind the most finely distilled and accomplished of all Shiba Ryōtarō's work.

All of them are men of a masculine cast who have staked themselves on a single passion. Even with a piece of popular entertainment like Owl's Castle the point holds: the author's eye pursues the life of just such a man, and so his construction always takes a biographical form. The representative idealist-swordsman of the prewar years is Miyamoto Musashi; but one might argue that Tsuzura Jūzō is a Musashi of the modern type, freshly made over.

Around Yoshikawa's Musashi cling two women, the passionate Akemi and the chaste Otsū. In just the same way, around the Jūzō of Owl's Castle there are two: the wild Kisaru and the high-bred Kohagi. That Musashi refuses Otsū, and Jūzō refuses Kohagi, both for the sake of the "Way," is common to the two men; but whereas Musashi in the end never takes Otsū, the modern man and woman of Owl's Castle join their bodies at the very outset. The proud modern paragon of chastity (?), Kohagi, first appears as an enigmatic courtesan, gives herself to Jūzō, and thereafter is the one who makes the advances. (The man drives his blade into her white thigh and leaves.)

In this one can see, I think, the change in the very conception of love between two ages that the war divides.

It was just when the fashion for looking back to the Meiji Restoration was at its height that Shiba Ryōtarō began writing a series of novels set in the last days of the shogunate. And when corporate takeovers were in the news, he brought out a novel whose hero was Saitō Dōsan, the predatory warlord of the Warring States who seized his master's house. In this — as in his having been the one to set the ninja boom going — the quickness of his eye for a subject is quite exceptional, even among writers of historical fiction.

This has, in all likelihood, something to do with his having worked at a newspaper, the Sankei Shimbun.

Out of the ages of the past he has drawn the figures of men who lived by passion. A distinctive light is turned upon history. In Owl's Castle it is not yet so pronounced; but in the group of novels grounded in historical fact there is a real interest of historical interpretation. His Bakumatsu, a set of biographies of assassins, and the aforementioned The Man of Demonic Cunning, in that they portray the inner passions of men, call to mind a line that runs from the historical biographies of Kōda Rohan down to Sakaguchi Ango's The Second-Rate Man.

What deserves notice here is that, within these novels and biographies, the author holds himself toward his heroes' passion with a certain reserve. In The Man of Demonic Cunning, even as he draws the genius of Ōmura Masujirō, the author lets his eccentric figure surface with a touch of humor. Hijikata Toshizō, and below him the rank and file of the Shinsengumi, are likewise, in their out-of-season passion, faintly comic; and they themselves are dimly aware of their own absurdity.

With the hero of Owl's Castle it is the same. To take Yoshikawa Eiji's Miyamoto Musashi as an example once more: Musashi's idealistic passion is, just as it stands, the state of Yoshikawa's own heart at a certain period, and the two are inseparably bound together. The eye with which the author regards Musashi is solemn; there is not the least hint in it of laughter at his expense.

In Owl's Castle, by contrast, the author holds himself at a certain distance from his hero. The hero himself is more or less aware of the foolishness of his own single-minded passion. That awareness of foolishness reaches its peak at the moment when, having at last stolen into Hideyoshi's bedchamber, he comes face to face with the old man. A chill wind that declares the whole thing folly blows in through the ruined old body of Hideyoshi and puts out the last fire of the passion the hero has guarded to the end.

There is in this author a standpoint that regards every passion, and every idea, as merely relative. The weakness of the popular writers who turn out historical fiction lies, I have come to think, in the fact that so many of them are altogether too earnest, too solemn. The hard-boiled writers are of course another matter; but among the historical novelists who come out of the so-called popular-literature camp this tendency is marked, and it is what makes their novels, more often than not, hard to bear.

To put it in a word: Shiba Ryōtarō is an intellectual writer of historical fiction, one able to draw in not only the broad reading public but the hard-to-please intellectual as well. Within a field of vision that relativizes everything, he nevertheless brings forth, together with humor, the beautiful and valiant shape that passion can take. The intellectual, masculine cast of feature behind his novels is not to be overlooked. It is this that gives his fiction its strong tautness, its tension.
