## Author's Preface: Shanghai Behind-the-Lines Operations Against Japan

"Shanghai Behind-the-Lines Operations Against Japan" is the Third Part of the whole of my humble work Nameless Heroes.

The title first drawn up was Renown Won in a Hundred Battles. It was not without meaning; yet it did not show at a glance what the book was about, and so I changed it, plainly and directly, to Shanghai Behind-the-Lines Operations During the War of Resistance. On second thought, a title of ten characters was a touch too long, and seldom seen at that; and after weighing it several times over, I judged it better to use simply Shanghai Behind-the-Lines Operations, which came out the more resounding and forceful. But someone pointed out that, were the period not marked, it might be taken for something happening now; and it was for that reason that I settled at last on Shanghai Behind-the-Lines Operations Against Japan.

What this Part sets forth is a new page, glorious and heroic, of yet another stage; yet in content, in purport, and in spirit it still runs of a piece with the two Parts before it—Rooting Out Traitors in the North and The Whole Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi. Beyond this, the writer hopes above all to convey in full the true face and the actual traces of how, in those days, in the great fallen city of Shanghai, beset on three sides—by the Japanese invaders, by the Wang puppets, and by the foreign Concessions whose police and detectives stooped to serve as their hawks and hounds—the whole body of our working comrades, unyielding in bitter hardship, gave themselves in valor and in sacrifice!

The substance of the book is just the astonishing cases of slaying the foe and rooting out traitors carried out by the "Shanghai District" under the "Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Military Affairs Commission" in the years of the War of Resistance. In the "Prefatory Candour" of Nameless Heroes it was once mentioned: "The book of the Third Part chiefly recounts how, in the space of a little more than two years, from the eighth month of the twenty-eighth year to the tenth month of the thirtieth year, the comrades of the 'Shanghai District,' one and all devoted to their charge, brought off great deeds of the utmost foe-destroying power."

Why lop off head and tail and speak only of "the space of a little more than two years, from the eighth month of the twenty-eighth year to the tenth month of the thirtieth year"? Because this stage fell just at the critical juncture of the War of Resistance against Japan, and was moreover the very period in which the writer himself had charge of the "Shanghai District." Setting down these events in that capacity, he is naturally the most familiar with them, and the best able to keep them true.

The first thing that must be made plain is this: what the "Shanghai District" did was but one part of the "Bureau's" work in the greater Shanghai area as a whole. Which is to say, in its dispositions for this region the "Bureau" had, besides the "Shanghai District," still other working units at the same time. And to widen the scope further, then in the years of the War of Resistance the secret units that our Party, government, and military authorities kept in place, posted, or sent slipping into Shanghai one after another were many, many indeed. Each, of course, held to its own post, and each must surely have made its own contribution: the facts are all there, and need no laboring. Yet our "Shanghai District," over and above intelligence and subversion, had one further distinctive mark all its own—that it ceaselessly matched armed force against the enemy and the puppets, dealing them grievous blows time and again, and thereby raised up a national prestige that cowed the whole ugly crew, lifted the spirit of the people in the fallen zone, and steeled the will to resist.

The manner in which this book is told, too, is rather unlike the two Parts before it. In the past, besides planning and directing, the writer himself also took part in the actual actions; but once he had come to Shanghai to serve as District Chief, then—the organization being vast and the tiers of command grown many, and the safety of the whole having also to be kept in view—there was in fact no longer any such need, so that of the detailed course of many cases he could grasp only the outline, and that only when issuing orders or going over the work-reports; he scarcely even had much chance to hear the spoken accounts face to face. For this reason, many a fine detail, and the small moves of both sides, ours and the enemy's, on the very ground of action, simply cannot be written down. And so I have had no choice but to inquire on all sides, to call on people and gather what materials I could, that from the depths of memory I might hook up once more a spark or a fragment.

The "Shanghai District" was the largest of the hundred-odd field units, at home and abroad, under the "Bureau's" command; and set beside other units of its rank, or measured against the establishment and strength proper to a secret organization, it was indeed extraordinarily "large" and unreasonably "many." At the time this was, to be sure, to meet the pressing needs of the work; yet in the end it went against the basic principle that a secret organization thrives on being lean and nimble—and this was the chief cause that led, later on, to the grave reverse we suffered.

Just how large was the "Shanghai District"? A word of explanation is in order. The "Beiping Station" and "Tianjin Station" in which the writer served in earlier days, and even the makeshift grouping of the Hanoi work, ran at fewest to a dozen-odd men, at most to no more than twenty or thirty; but the "Shanghai District" was another matter altogether—at its fullest, as I recall, it rose to as many as a thousand souls, a thing truly beyond imagining. Its several ranks and divisions, within and without, were broadly as follows—

The duplex District Headquarters had two to three premises, shifting about in rotation, not fixed to a single office. These premises were occupied severally by the assistant secretary together with the couriers and cipher clerks assigned to him; while at the personal lodging of the Headquarters secretary the routine business was managed throughout.

The liaison (contact) stations and courier (relay) stations, within and without, came to twenty-two in all, cover-shops among them; each had its liaison men and its couriers, each charged with the business assigned to it. There were three standing wireless stations, permitted to signal with Chongqing alone and holding no lateral tie one to another. The ordinary signaling of each was overseen by a man the District Chief appointed for it; while for its technical questions of machinery an inspector was sent, direct from the Headquarters unit in charge, to see to it, beyond the "Shanghai District's" control. Besides these, one reserve station was set up as well, against any unforeseen need.

In the Shanghai area the Bureau Headquarters had further posted a "Chief Accountant" to take charge of finances, and the funds of the "Shanghai District" were by rule drawn from him, through the "District Accountant," and passed on. For strict secrecy the "District Accountant," though but a single man, kept a separate address and worked alone. What vexed him most was that he had to keep safe a great many vouchers that could not be destroyed—such as the assignment deeds for houses, the accounts of income and outlay, the receipts for monies drawn, the purchase invoices, and the like; and because these vouchers took in so many heads—items, personal names, names of goods, names of firms and companies, dates, addresses—there was, at every moment and in every place, the danger of a leak. Should they once fall into the enemy's hands, they were so much "proof of guilt," and became, moreover, a thread for the tracing, a chart by which to run us to ground. In our work behind enemy lines this was, in truth, a great taboo. Over this problem we deliberated back and forth a good while; but the accounting was a thing apart, and it insisted, moreover, that no outlay could be written off above without the original voucher to show for it—so that a conclusion was hard to come by to the last. For all our ceaseless labor to better the tricks of entering the names and the figures, still we could not wipe away those marks that woke suspicion and, the more they were covered over, the plainer they showed; and in the end these vouchers became, at last, the soul-summoning register in the Hall of the King of Hell.

To support the work of sabotage, we had to make and develop all manner of simple bombs, whether explosive or incendiary, and so a small technical research room could not be dispensed with either. And for fear of a mishap in a residential quarter, one store for demolition materials was set up as well, which in our jargon we called the "warehouse." All the foregoing were our "desk units"; ranked as "field units" were the ones that follow.

There was one newly formed composite unit for high-level intelligence-gathering and action work, its members all specially trained, not a few of them men who had studied abroad; its formal name was "New Group One," the whole body of comrades some forty in number. Of the intelligence-gathering units and the executors of every sort of "soft" work—inducing defection, counter-espionage, and the like—there were five groups, of unequal strength, the largest above thirty men; and each group, too, had its own distinct character, in the hope of drawing from it the freest and readiest effect.

Those given wholly to carrying out action and sabotage work were formed into eight brigades; each brigade was divided into two to four companies, and below the company into a number of cells, each cell made up of three to five men. Reckoned roughly, each brigade had on average a strength of thirty or forty men, and all were furnished with light arms over and above their number.

Besides these, not formally taken into the frame of the organization, there were still many patriotic young cadres of anti-Japanese bodies who lent their aid to the "Shanghai District's" work, and sundry persons passed down to us by our superiors—among them a good many foreign nationals; and these, though each had his own social standing and his own particular line, were yet the hardest sort of special-use personnel to handle. Here it must be pointed out: a secret working body as large as this, lying hidden behind enemy lines, was a thing seen but rarely—not in the history of our nation's intelligence work alone, but even in the First and Second World Wars. From now on, with the advance of science and the shifting of circumstance, an organization of such freakish growth will surely never come again. The tasks our superiors laid upon the "Shanghai District" were many under many heads; classed by line of business, there were intelligence, action, subversion, counter-espionage, psychological warfare, political warfare, youth work, labor work, technical research, liaison and communication, and so on. To carry out these many tasks to good effect, we followed on the one hand the principles our superiors laid down, and on the other, weighing by the "Shanghai District's" own working capacity what was grave and what light, what pressing and what could wait, we then fell to on the instant and went at it with all our might. The reason we spared no sacrifice and leaned toward action work lay in this: that, addressing the real state of the fallen zone in those days, we hoped to bring off an effect of overawing might—to strike down the arrogance of the enemy and the puppets, to rouse the spirit and morale of the whole nation, and to steel the faith that the War of Resistance must be won.

Action work is, of course, purely a matter of real blades and real guns, of matching wits and matching strength, of flesh pitched against flesh—one side waxing as the other wanes, the result plain the moment the pole is raised; it rests wholly on the investigation and planning done beforehand and on the disposition and the spirit of sacrifice shown on the spot, and can by no means trust to luck or harbor the least thought of chance good fortune. In the two years and more since the writer took charge of the "Shanghai District," to speak only of the sacrifice of individuals—those who gave their lives for the country, who were maimed under torture, or who fell into prison—the number came to above two hundred in all; and as for the blows dealt the enemy and the puppets, setting aside those whose effect cannot be told in any single figure—the workings of intelligence, the play of counter-espionage, the fruits of psychological warfare, and such, which cannot be reckoned up—then, by an incomplete count: traitors and running dogs great and small sanctioned to the number of a hundred-odd; the military installations of the Japanese invaders wrecked and their military stores burned to fifty-odd instances; and besides, what most struck terror into the enemy's troops, some forty Japanese armed officers on the active list killed outright. From this the grievous weight of our own sacrifice may likewise be imagined. When, at the end of October 1941, the writer himself met with disaster and was taken, the enemy and the puppets, on November 28, openly published the "case" in the newspapers, giving it a whole page, wherein were: a "Roster of the Five Groups of the Intelligence Apparatus" and a "Roster of the Eight Great Action Squads of the Shanghai District," with, appended, a "Table of Cases Committed by the Blue Shirt Society in Shanghai," in whose several columns were set out, one by one, the time, the place, the standing and names of the dead and wounded, and the action unit. Though the several particulars they alleged were not wholly correct, seen from today, more than forty years after the event, they must be reckoned the finest material, of the greatest value for reference; and the writer's old comrade-in-arms Mr. Huang Zhiyuan, who kept one sheet of the Xin Shen Bao and half a sheet of the Zhonghua Ribao from that day, will furnish them for this book to be plated and printed, that a glimpse of the whole may be caught.

Action and sabotage can never wholly escape burning and killing; even the writer himself felt, at times, that his heart could not bear it. Yet this was a policy our government adopted in the fallen zone during the War of Resistance against Japan, and we who took part in the actual work were, every one of us, executors of that policy, and made no decisions of our own. To speak truly, our working comrades were all just and reasonable men, by no means men who thirsted to kill; and the reason they could summon up that irresistible, self-forgetting valor was that they were, for the most part, governed and heartened by political faith and by a sense of nation—that, for the survival and continuance of the race and the independence and self-mastery of the country, with a mighty foe before them, they turned back for nothing: this is the plainest proof of it.

Our work knew no rest; our tasks came one upon another without a break.

In the first days of August 1939, the writer set out on duty once more, flying from Chongqing to Hong Kong and there transferring to a mail steamer that bore him to Shanghai. No sooner had he stepped onto the wharf than he learned that the situation had changed, and that the mission he bore was, he feared, already hard to accomplish.

While he was awaiting the next instructions, there came all at once a telegram from his superiors, appointing the writer District Chief of the "Shanghai District," bidding him take up the post that very day, urgently devise a plan to meet the changed case, and restore the working of the organization as soon as might be.

The "Shanghai District" was the largest regional unit under the "Bureau," and the most powerful secret organization our government had deployed within the enemy-occupied territory.

The desk and field staff under the "Shanghai District" lay hidden, each in its own sort of place—in the city proper of Shanghai, in the foreign Concessions, in the townships and near suburbs—and within the organs, at every level, of the enemy and the puppets.

In its make-up, the "Shanghai District," besides the duplex District Headquarters—one office at two addresses—was divided below into twenty-two courier-and-liaison stations, four wireless stations, one technical room, one accounting room, five intelligence groups, eight action brigades, one newly formed composite group, one labor cell, one anti-Japanese youth corps, and the rest, some fifty-odd units in all. There were also several score directly subordinate work-lines, foreign nationals not lacking among them. Its total strength was kept, as a rule, at about a thousand. And it was equipped with communications gear of every kind, demolition materials of many sorts, and arms and ammunition.

When the writer first took over, the District's work was in a state half at a standstill: the acting District Chief, a Mr. Zhao, hard pressed by the enemy's pursuit, was no longer able to discharge his duties, and all the business rested on the District secretary, Mr. Zheng Xiuyuan, who saw to inner and outer both, and bore it up single-handed. The cause of it was that the assistant secretary in charge of personnel, Chen Dirong, had gone wrong. It was through Chen Dirong's betrayal of the organization's secrets that the enemy-and-puppet gendarmes, together with the Concession police and detectives, had searched, all within one and the same span of time, fourteen of the "Shanghai District's" offices; and only because word had been got beforehand was no great loss suffered.

The "Shanghai District" had been long established, its organization close-knit, its foundation firm; a blow or two could not shake it at the root, and so it was able, in a short space, to raise its might up anew—and give the enemy back some color to reckon with!
