### Betrayal to the Last Scrap

### Gu Shunzhang Betrays Yun Daiying in a Nanjing Prison

On April 27, 1931, no sooner had Gu Shunzhang been brought under guard from Wuhan to Nanjing than he betrayed Yun Daiying, held in the Nanjing Central Military Prison. That day, on the road to Chiang Kai-shek's residence, Cai Mengjian — the Kuomintang agent who had arrested Gu Shunzhang in Hankou with his own hands — heard Gu say to him: by the Communist Party's rules a senior Central Committee member must join in grassroots work each year; Yun Daiying is a high Communist cadre, and he was doing grassroots work in Pudong under the alias Wang Zuolin, disguised as a worker handing out leaflets, when the police caught him. He gave himself out as an illiterate of the red masses, and so was sent to your Suzhou reformatory for reform. I spent several thousand yuan in influence money and have already got him approved for transfer within these few days to the judge advocate's office of the Nanjing headquarters, to be sentenced and released. You had best find out whether he has been let go — Yun is an important Communist talent.

Yun Daiying (1895–1931) was one of the earliest Marxists in China, and a leader beloved and a teacher revered by China's youth. Zhou Enlai once said of him: "His proletarian consciousness, his ardor in work, his firm will, his plain style, his spirit of sacrifice, his common touch, his moving power to persuade — these should forever be a model for the youth of China." In 1944, when Zhou Enlai learned in Yan'an of the death of Zou Taofen, he said: "Our country had two youth leaders. One was Yun Daiying, who died long ago; the other was Zou Taofen, who has now died as well. This is our greatest loss."

Yun Daiying, also called Yun Lianxuan, courtesy name Ziyi, went by the further names Tanyi, Tianyi, Yayi, FM, and others. His family came from Changzhou in Jiangsu, and he was born in 1895 into a scholarly household in Wuchang. As a boy in primary school his sharp mind, fresh views, and trenchant pen won him from teachers and classmates the name of "prodigy." Clever by nature, he read widely as a youth and had a keen political sense. Two months after the outbreak of the First World War, on October 1, 1914, at nineteen years of age, Yun Daiying published his first political essay, "On Duty," in the eleventh volume, fourth number, of the great magazine Eastern Miscellany, put out by the Commercial Press in Shanghai. The essay praised the Eastern morality of duty, whose core was self-dedication, service, and altruism, and attacked the Western civilization of rights, whose core — brought from Europe and America when the seas were first opened — was grasping, self-interest, and aggression, pointing sharply out that the outbreak of the world war was the very fruit of the rights-doctrine swollen to malignancy. Afterward he took active part in the struggle against Yuan Shikai, joined the surging New Culture Movement, made contact with Chen Duxiu, and published in Chen's New Youth philosophical writings upholding materialism against idealism. In 1917 Yun Daiying and his close friend Huang Fusheng founded the Mutual Aid Society, a study-and-cultivation body whose watchword was "pool wits and pool strength, help oneself and help others." It was the earliest youth body to advocate criticism and self-criticism to arise on the soil of a China still ruled by feudal thought. This vigorous society won the eager welcome of aspiring young people and grew steadily in the city of Wuchang like a rolling snowball. Under Yun Daiying's help, a whole set of cross-campus youth bodies spread through the colleges and all of Wuchang, and made ample preparation, in thought and in organization, for this region to greet the coming of the May Fourth movement.

When the May Fourth movement broke out, Yun Daiying, as a chief leader of the student movement in the Wuhan area, led the Mutual Aid Society and other youth bodies and students in supporting by real action the Beijing students' anti-imperialist and patriotic struggle, founding the weekly The Student, organizing demonstrations, and taking part in every kind of revolutionary struggle then rising.

After the May Fourth movement, Marxism-Leninism spread further in China. Yun Daiying founded in Wuchang a new-model bookstore, the Liqun Book Society — a book society whose aim was to spread the new culture and the new thought, and at the same time a revolutionary body for tempering the young in the cultivation of will. The store sold Marxist-Leninist classics and revolutionary periodicals, and its members used this base to try out a collective life of shared study, shared labor, and shared progress. When Mao Zedong led a band of young people north against the warlords and passed through Wuhan, he called at the Liqun Book Society, and with that began Yun Daiying's lifelong association with Mao Zedong. In the autumn of 1919 Yun Daiying joined the Young China Association. The next year he went to Beijing and came to know Li Dazhao and Deng Zhongxia. At the July 1921 annual meeting of the Young China Association, held in Nanjing on his own proposal, the Marxist faction and the nationalist faction joined fierce battle. This meeting gave Yun Daiying great inspiration and instruction, made him resolutely cast off the last residue of reformism and utopian socialism, and set him on the way to becoming a conscious Marxist. Not long after the Chinese Communist Party was founded in Shanghai, he joined it.

In the spring of 1922 Yun Daiying served as dean of studies and headmaster of the Sichuan Southern Normal School at Luzhou in Sichuan, setting up a Chinese Socialist Youth League organization, carrying through educational reform, and unfolding revolutionary activity. For this he was once detained by the local feudal power in league with the warlords, and was freed only through the efforts of Wu Yuzhang, headmaster of the Chengdu Higher Normal School, and the teachers and students of the Sichuan normal school. After 1923 he was elected to the Central Committee of the Chinese Socialist Youth League at its Second and Third Congresses, served as head of the League Central's propaganda department, and edited the League Central's organ China Youth. This journal nurtured and influenced a whole generation of the young, and he became an outstanding leader of the Chinese youth movement. In 1925 he helped lead the May Thirtieth anti-imperialist patriotic movement. In January 1926 he attended the Kuomintang's Second National Congress, was one of the Communist caucus's officers there, and was elected to the Kuomintang's Second Central Executive Committee. He then served as chief political instructor at the Whampoa Military Academy and as an officer of the Communist caucus, and taught as well at the Peasant Movement Training Institute in Guangzhou. At Whampoa he backed the Young Soldiers' League in its struggle against the Sun Yat-sen-ism Study Society that Chiang Kai-shek had fostered, making Chiang both hate and fear him, so that behind his back Chiang cursed him, Deng Yanda, Gao Yuhan, and Zhang Zhizhong as the "Four Fiends of Whampoa." In 1927 he was chief political instructor at the Central Military and Political School in Wuhan.

Around 1927, at the turning point of Chinese history, Yun Daiying on the one hand pressed within the Party, against Chen Duxiu's Right-opportunist errors of capitulation, the education on the importance of armed struggle; on the other he waged a tit-for-tat struggle against Chiang Kai-shek. After the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup, he led three hundred thousand of the military school's teachers and students and revolutionary masses in a mighty rally at Yuemachang in Wuchang to denounce Chiang Kai-shek. At the Fifth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party, Yun Daiying stood with Qu Qiubai, Cai Hesen, Mao Zedong, and others in criticizing Chen Duxiu's Right-deviationist errors, and was elected to the Central Committee. After Wang Jingwei's betrayal, Yun Daiying arranged step by step the concealment, withdrawal, and removal of the revolutionary forces of the central military school; then, on the orders of the Provisional Central of the Party, he hurried through the night to Jiujiang and Nanchang to join the August First Uprising that Zhou Enlai organized and led. After the uprising's army was defeated, he sailed from Jiazi harbor to Hong Kong. Two months later he took part in leading the Guangzhou Uprising with Zhang Tailei and others, serving as secretary-general of the Guangzhou Soviet government and seeing to the publication of the Party paper Red Flag Daily. After the Guangzhou Uprising failed he withdrew once more to Hong Kong, served as a member of the Party's Guangdong provincial committee, and went underground.

After the Party's Sixth National Congress in 1928, Yun Daiying was transferred back to Shanghai, serving in turn as secretary-general of the Party Central's organization department and of its propaganda department. At the Second Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee he was co-opted onto the Central Committee. In February 1930, as a central representative, he attended the Second Party Congress of Fujian province. After investigation and study, he published two articles in the Party paper Red Flag supporting Mao Zedong's strategic thought of encircling the cities from the countryside and finally seizing them, in effect criticizing Li Lisan's mistaken line of seizing power through urban insurrection. This greatly displeased Li Lisan. When Yun Daiying returned to Shanghai in early April, he again criticized Li Lisan's errors at a Party meeting. Not only did Li Lisan not accept his correct views; he clapped on him the labels of "conciliationism" and "Right opportunism," transferred him from Fujian back to Shanghai, drove him out of the Party Central, and "banished" him to the East Shanghai district as secretary of the action committee.

Yun Daiying was a well-known figure whom Chiang Kai-shek hated to the marrow and hunted with all his might, so that working on the front line carried enormous risk. He obeyed the organization's decision, took off his thick spectacles, disguised himself as a worker, and went to work in the factory district around Yangshupu. On the afternoon of May 6, 1930, as he waited before the gate of the old Ewo cotton mill in Yangshupu for someone to come and make contact for work, he suddenly ran into an enemy "shakedown search." Though he was got up as a worker and went by the alias "Wang Zuolin," the enemy found on him spectacles, a fountain pen, a watch, and forty yuan, and found near him a bundle of leaflets besides, and took him off to the police station. He was then handed over as a Communist suspect to the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal public security bureau, and thence to the Kuomintang's Longhua Songhu Garrison Command at Longhua. In February 1931 he was sent under guard to the Central Military Prison outside Jiangdongmen in Nanjing. At the moment of arrest he had cleverly clawed his own face raw, and, playing the worker, had changed his name. In prison he bore himself steadily, did not cut his hair or shave his beard, so altered his whole look that he held out for nearly a year without being seen through by the enemy. Though the enemy had not made him out to be Yun Daiying, they sentenced him nonetheless, on the trumped-up "crime" that "a worker who convenes a meeting on his own is guilty too," to five years' imprisonment.

"Yun Daiying hid his name, ruined his own face, disguised himself cunningly; and with the torments of prison life, even his student Huang Jingwen, who knew him well, could scarcely make him out. Yet as time went on, several fellow prisoners, students of the Whampoa Military Academy, knew their teacher again by his bearing, his speech, his voice. But these students were deeply moved by Yun Daiying's spirit and character. Though to betray Yun Daiying would have won them merit and reward and an early release, not one of them was willing to sell him out; they all resolved to keep the secret, letting not the slightest word of Yun Daiying's arrest leak out. Yun Daiying's hope of early release grew ever greater."

After the Spring Festival of 1931, Yun Daiying learned from the prison Party branch that Lin Yunan, Li Qiushi, He Mengxiong, and the League of Left-Wing Writers authors Hu Yepin and Rou Shi — twenty-four well-known Communists in all — had been massacred together by the enemy at the Longhua Prison in Shanghai on February 7. Yun Daiying, grieved and enraged, his heart stirred, wrote an immortal poem to mourn his fallen comrades and to voice his firm belief, unbending in prison and facing death as a homecoming:

{p} Roaming rivers and lakes, I count my old wanderings over; / old friends, in life and in death, each keeps his thousand autumns. / Long have I staked all, and hold grief and peril common things, / keeping my ardor whole, to play the captive of Chu.

Not long after Yun Daiying's arrest in 1930, Qu Qiubai and Zhou Enlai returned from the Soviet Union that August and took charge of the Central's work, convening the Third Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, which corrected Li Lisan's "Left" errors and criticized as well the improper work arrangement that had led to Yun Daiying's arrest and the feeble rescue Li Lisan mounted afterward. Zhou Enlai said: Comrade Yun Daiying is a comrade of very high prestige in our Party, who has made major contributions to the Party's cause. When he was chief political instructor at the Whampoa Military Academy, Chiang Kai-shek both wished to win him over and greatly feared him, and cursed him as one of the "Four Fiends of Whampoa." He took part in the Nanchang and Guangzhou uprisings, stood always at the very front of the struggle, and became an important figure on whom Chiang Kai-shek set a heavy price — how could he have been put in a post so easy to expose and wholly without safeguard?

Zhou Enlai directed that every means must be used, no price spared, to work through the enemy's various connections and rescue Yun Daiying. The Central decided that Zhou Enlai would head the rescue work and charge Gu Shunzhang, who ran the daily affairs of the Central Special Section, with carrying it out. Through the Central Special Section's efforts, by the spring of 1931 Chen Geng had, by way of the Party's underground tie with a judge of the Kuomintang Higher Court, arranged an early release, and the Party organization had already notified Yun Daiying to prepare to leave prison ahead of time:

In April, the prison Party branch, looking far ahead, had Xie Yunchao notify Yun Daiying to make ready to leave prison, a prison staff member named Ye Yaoming to take Yun Daiying out himself if need be. An underground Party member working in the puppet Higher Court told Chen Geng, then in charge of our Party's Special Section work: the Higher Court is already prepared to release "Wang Zuolin." Zhou Enlai therefore at once arranged for a comrade to go to the prison to receive Yun Daiying, charging him to bring ample travel money, and not to go by train or steamer lest he be spotted by the detectives, but rather to walk or take a civilian boat back.

Just as the Party organization was tensely making its preparations for Yun Daiying's release, there came the matter of Gu Shunzhang's defection and betrayal. Gu Shunzhang gave the enemy not only the alias Yun Daiying used in prison but the cell number where Yun Daiying was held in the Nanjing Central Military Prison — the "Star" ward. When Chiang Kai-shek learned this he was aghast, and on April 28 hurriedly ordered his judge-advocate-general Wang Zhennan to the prison that day to check. Wang Zhennan came into the prison with a photograph of Yun Daiying from the Whampoa Military Academy, and, once he had confirmed the man, pressed him to surrender. Yun Daiying answered the enemy sternly: "I am Yun Daiying!" On the spot he denounced Wang Zhennan's surrender-talk for the lie it was: "I am a Communist; I must make revolution against the Kuomintang reactionaries — that is my solemn task now." Wang Zhennan, beside himself, at once ordered Yun Daiying put in heavy irons and moved from the old "Star" ward to a solitary cell in the "Wisdom" ward. Chiang Kai-shek at once gave the order: shoot him on the spot at once.

The next day, April 29, 1931, the enemy put Yun Daiying to death on the prison drill ground. At the execution Yun Daiying refused to kneel; chest thrown out, he stood erect on the killing ground singing the "Internationale," and proclaimed the Party's cause aloud and denounced the crimes of the Kuomintang reactionaries. Wang Zhennan, in a panic, screamed "Fire!" The executioner's hands shook and could not draw the bolt. The execution captain then ordered another executioner to fire. Fearless in the face of death, several bullets in his body, Yun Daiying still cried out: "Down with Chiang Kai-shek!" "Long live the Chinese Communist Party!"

As the criminal shots rang out, the fellow prisoners at their midday meal set down their bowls and chopsticks and wept aloud. They cursed Gu Shunzhang, who had sold Yun Daiying out. One railed: "Have you ever seen a man worse, meaner than a maggot? This is a man who would eat his own mother's heart and liver, who would eat his own children's brains — he cannot be called a 'man,' yet he goes clad in a man's skin. His name is Gu Shunzhang!" Those few Whampoa students who had long since known Yun Daiying and had all along protected him were angrier still, and cursed the traitor Gu Shunzhang: "Heartless dead hooligan! Mangy dog with nothing human in you!"

Zhang Guodong, then serving under Xu Enzeng, said afterward: "The martyr Yun Daiying was arrested in Shanghai on May 6, 1930. Because Yun Daiying was brave and clever and dealt with things coolly, his true identity was never made out by the reactionaries, and he was held in the Nanjing prison only as a Communist suspect. In prison Yun Daiying went on with revolutionary activity, and through a charwoman who fetched the prisoners' meals and washed their clothes he passed messages and kept in touch with the Communist Party Central. So Gu Shunzhang knew very well the alias the martyr Yun Daiying used, his code designation, the cell number where he was held. After Gu Shunzhang told the truth about Yun Daiying, Xu Enzeng sent Gu Jianzhong, Li Xiyuan, and Wang Sicheng, three men, carrying a Whampoa fourth-class yearbook with Yun Daiying's photograph in it (Yun was a political instructor of the Whampoa fourth class), to the prison to identify him. Once it was verified that the Communist suspect was indeed Yun Daiying, Xu Enzeng was overjoyed, and at once reported it in person to Chen Guofu and Chen Lifu and passed it on to Chiang Kai-shek. At the same time Xu had Gu Jianzhong, Li Xiyuan, and Wang Sicheng put to Yun a whole series of threats and inducements, trying to force him to betray the revolution and to get all kinds of information about the Chinese Communists. But the martyr Yun Daiying was faithful to the last; though racked with every kind of cruel instrument of torture, he gave, beyond admitting that he was a member of the Chinese Communist Party, not a word of confession. The reactionaries, at a loss, had Chiang Kai-shek order him killed. Once, in a private talk with me, Xu Enzeng, speaking of how the martyr Yun Daiying showed no fear before his execution but was righteous and unflinching, crying the slogan 'Long live the Communist Party!' and singing the 'Internationale' in a ringing voice, fully displaying the lofty character of a Communist, said: 'The dreadfulness of the Communists lies just in this.'"

### Gu Shunzhang Goes to Hong Kong to Hunt Down Cai Hesen

In June 1931 Gu Shunzhang went again, this time to Hong Kong with Kuomintang agents, to seize Cai Hesen — an outstanding early leader of the Chinese Communist Party and a brilliant Marxist theorist.

Cai Hesen's family name was the compound Cai-Lin; his school name was Hesen. His original name was Cai Hexian (the 和 also written 龢), his courtesy name Runhuan, his sobriquet Zeying. He was born in 1895 into the family of a fallen minor official in Xiangxiang county, Hunan. In 1913 he passed into the Changsha First Provincial Normal School, and through reading such revolutionary books and papers as New Youth, which spread the doctrine of Marxism, he set himself a revolutionary aim. At this school he came to know his fellow student Mao Zedong; near in temper and one in aim, the two became close friends. Yang Changji, the school's ethics teacher, greatly admired the two students, and often said to people: "Mao Zedong and Cai Hesen are geniuses without equal, and will surely be pillars of the state one day." In 1918 Cai Hesen founded the New People's Study Society with Mao Zedong and put out the Xiang River Review. "Even long before the 'May Fourth' movement, the name of Mao and Cai was already spoken of highly among the general run of advanced youth in Hunan, and held up as a model." That June, on the New People's Study Society's charge, Cai Hesen went from Changsha to Beijing to make contact with Cai Yuanpei, initiator of the work-study-in-France movement for Hunan's youth. In August, with Mao Zedong and Luo Xuezan, come to the capital, he met Li Dazhao. In early 1919 they saw off the first group of New People's Study Society members going to work and study in France; and on October 25, Cai Hesen too left to work and study in France.

Cai Hesen went to France together with his mother and his sister. His mother, Ge Jianhao, came of a noble house and looked up to the woman revolutionary Qiu Jin. To let Cai Hesen study in Changsha she had sold her cherished dowry jewelry. After Cai Hesen went up to school in Changsha, he further urged his mother, near fifty, to go to the provincial capital and sit the examination for the women's teacher-training school. Ge Jianhao gladly brought her daughter and granddaughter to Changsha as well. When the school's teacher saw her come with her grandchildren, he would not let her enroll. Ge Jianhao thereupon took the matter to the county office. The magistrate wrote, "A rare and admirable spirit," and let her into the teacher-training school, her son and daughter into the school, her granddaughter into the kindergarten. Three generations going up to study in the provincial capital at once became a marvel on everyone's lips. The family of five lived in the provincial capital, their livelihood resting chiefly on the eight yuan a month the sister Cai Chang earned teaching at the Zhounan Girls' School, with the eldest sister Cai Qingxi helping out at times. Hard as life was, it did not check Cai Hesen's pursuit of knowledge. He studied hard on his own, read widely, and tirelessly sought the way to save the nation and the people.

Before long the work-study-in-France movement rose in the country's academic circles. Cai Hesen actively organized New People's Study Society members to take part, and by his urging his girlfriend Xiang Jingyu, his sister Cai Chang, and his mother resolved to go together. His mother, through marriage ties, borrowed six hundred silver dollars from the family of Zeng Guofan's son-in-law, and the whole family went off together to France in October 1919. Cai Hesen, Xiang Jingyu, and Cai Chang worked and studied part-time in France, and the mother did embroidery and learned French from the beginning, coming at last to read French newspapers. After returning home in 1921, the whole family took up revolutionary work.

After reaching France, Cai Hesen embraced scientific communism. From July 6 to 10, 1920, at a meeting of the France-based New People's Study Society he called at Montargis College, he put forward the proposal to found a Communist Party in China, and on August 13 wrote a long letter about it to Mao Zedong. In the letter he said that the party, the trade unions, the cooperatives, and the soviets were the four sharp weapons of the proletarian revolutionary movement. "I hold that we must first organize the party — the Communist Party. For it is the instigator, the propagandist, the vanguard, the fighting headquarters of the revolutionary movement. As things stand in China now, we must first organize it, and only then can the trade unions and cooperatives take on a strong form. Only so will the revolutionary movement and the labor movement have a nerve center. But is it to be urgent or gradual? I hold that we must prepare now." "I would have you prepare to make a Russian October Revolution." At the letter's close he wrote: "It is my view that within two years China must set up a party clear in doctrine, sound in method, and at one with Russia; this bears no small importance — mind it well." On September 16 that year Cai Hesen sent Mao Zedong another long letter, setting out in detail his views on the Chinese Communist Party, and putting plainly the call to "openly and formally establish a Chinese Communist Party." On January 21, 1921, Mao Zedong wrote back to Cai Hesen: "This letter of yours is exactly right in its views; there is not a word of it I do not agree with." In the letter Mao told him: "As for the party, Mr. Chen Zhongfu (note: 'Zhongfu' is Chen Duxiu's courtesy name) and others are already at work organizing it." Mao Zedong stressed that party-building must take Marxism as its theoretical guide: "The materialist view of history is the philosophical ground of our party."

In 1921 Cai Hesen organized a Chinese Socialist Youth League in France. He took part in Paris in two demonstrations against the loan and in the struggle to win entry to study at Lyon University, and in October was expelled from the country by the French authorities. On returning home Cai Hesen joined the Chinese Communist Party in Shanghai at year's end.

At the Second through Sixth National Congresses of the Chinese Communist Party, Cai Hesen was every time elected to the Central Committee. On September 13, 1922, the weekly The Guide was founded in Shanghai, with Cai Hesen as its editor-in-chief. The Guide was the Chinese Communist Party's first political organ, and one of the most famous and influential periodicals ever founded in our Party's history. Cai Hesen took part as well in the editing of The Pioneer, The Bolshevik, and other papers. The great body of political essays he published had a far-reaching influence in the world of thought and opinion. In 1925 he went to Moscow as head of the Chinese Communist delegation to the Comintern. Returning home in 1927 as head of the Central Propaganda Department, he was elected after the Party's Fifth National Congress to the Politburo, then to the Politburo Standing Committee, and served as acting secretary-general of the Party Central.

In 1927, at the critical moment of the Great Revolution's failure, Cai Hesen backed Mao Zedong's view and argued for independently unfolding armed struggle.

In early 1928 Cai Hesen's wife Xiang Jingyu was arrested in Wuhan. On May 1 Xiang Jingyu died a heroic death. This event brought Cai Hesen enormous grief, and spurred him in the hard years that followed to bear the revolution's heavy burden better still.

Afterward Cai Hesen worked long in both Shanghai and Moscow, and, for having opposed the "Left" policy, was stripped in 1928 of his Politburo membership and his post as head of the Central Propaganda Department, and went again to the Soviet Union. In the complex inner-Party struggles that followed he kept a clear head, and lodged no plea against the mistaken blows dealt him personally. He had criticized Qu Qiubai's "Left" putschist error and Li Lisan's "Left" adventurist error alike, but he resisted the cruel struggle and merciless blows against Qu Qiubai. When the Comintern convened a meeting in Moscow to discuss Li Lisan's errors, Cai Hesen at the meeting not only criticized Li Lisan's grave errors with warm fervor but made a self-criticism as well, so moving Li Lisan that he grievously examined his own former "improper expulsion of Comrade Hesen from the Politburo." Cai Hesen held the gravest vigilance toward the rule of Wang Ming's "Left" mistaken line within the Party.

In early 1931 Cai Hesen returned from the Soviet Union and asked to work in the Central Soviet. But no sooner had he reached Shanghai than he met the exclusion and blows of the new "Left" error headed by Wang Ming. They neither restored his Politburo membership nor let him go to the Central Soviet, but sent him instead to restore the Guangdong provincial committee, which the enemy had just wrecked, and to take charge of the work there. At the time the Guangdong provincial organization had suffered the gravest damage: the provincial secretary Deng Fa had been arrested, Guangdong lay under a White Terror, Guangzhou was hard to hold, and the provincial committee had for the time being set up in Hong Kong. Putting the Party's interest first, Cai Hesen never reckoned personal gain or loss. In March he obeyed the organization's decision and left Shanghai for Hong Kong. In Hong Kong he lived with his second wife Li Yichun above a foreign wine-and-canned-goods company, his public identity that of a clerk of the firm. To save the Party expense, he took no other office nearby, but each day walked a long way to his secretary's to hear reports and go over documents, his work of every kind extremely busy.

Not long after reaching Hong Kong, Cai Hesen learned of Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection. Through Gu Shunzhang's betrayal, Party organizations everywhere were being wrecked one after another, and Yun Daiying had been killed by the enemy. To cut the losses as far as he could, Zhou Enlai had directed the responsible people of the local Party organizations in the White areas to find ways to hide or move, to keep out of Gu Shunzhang's sight and pursuit. Zhou Enlai was especially anxious for Cai Hesen in Hong Kong, and stressed that he must leave Hong Kong swiftly. But Cai Hesen had long set life and death aside, and would not let his personal safety affect the Party's work in Guangdong. Before he came to Hong Kong, when people spoke of how perilous things were here and urged him to hold off, he had said: "To make revolution, one goes where one is needed; one cannot think only of personal safety." Of course he knew that he had worked long in the Communist Party Central's organs and that Gu Shunzhang knew him very well and knew the circumstances of his coming to Hong Kong. Yet he went on staying in Hong Kong day after day, leading the Party's Guangdong work, and left his lodging to go about as usual.

On June 10 that year the Hong Kong seamen held an important meeting. The Party's Guangdong provincial committee, reckoning that bad elements might slip into so mass a gathering, had not arranged for him to attend. But he thought the meeting important — "I can't rest easy not going." He decided to go a little late, and as he left about midday he said to his wife: "I'll be back before one this afternoon for certain; if I'm not back, then I've been arrested." Unhappily, the moment he entered the hall, the traitor Gu Shunzhang, crouching there already, arrested him with four plainclothes agents. Taken with him were Shi Huang and four others.

After Cai Hesen's arrest the Party organization at once took rescue measures. The Party member Li Shaoshi made contact through a social body with the British authorities in Hong Kong, who agreed to be paid a great sum to release Cai Hesen on bail. But when Li Shaoshi had the money in hand, Cai Hesen had already, two hours before, been handed over to the Guangdong warlord Chen Jitang. In the Guangzhou prison he suffered every kind of savage torture at the hands of the Kuomintang reactionaries. Cai Hesen met them with a cold and level brow, giving away nothing within the Party. Beaten to a bloody pulp, dragged back to the cell, he lay on the ground unable to stir. The fellow prisoners who saw it all wept in grief. But he heartened them to hold to the struggle, and said the final victory would surely be ours.

The enemy's savage torment did not weaken Cai Hesen's firm will in the slightest. At last they resorted to a stroke of the most inhuman kind: they dragged Cai Hesen to the prison wall, set several long iron spikes before him, and put him to one last threat and terror. Selfless and unafraid, Cai Hesen would sooner die than yield. The enemy barbarously nailed his four limbs to the wall with the spikes, then hacked his chest to a pulp with a bayonet. For the Party's cause, Cai Hesen thus died a heroic death, thirty-six years of age.

After Cai Hesen's death the Party and the people cherished his memory ever deeply. Mao Zedong said in a talk: "All that a Communist ought to do, Comrade Hesen did." Zhou Enlai too said: "Comrade Hesen is forever worthy of our remembrance."

### Betraying and Capturing Xiang Zhongfa

Gu Shunzhang also gave the enemy the address of Xiang Zhongfa, then general secretary of the Chinese Communist Party, and the mark of the missing half-joint on his right forefinger, so that Xiang was arrested in the French Concession.

Born in 1880, a worker by origin, Xiang Zhongfa was the man the Comintern had designated chairman of the Party Central's Politburo and general secretary of the Party at the Sixth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party. He had been a dock worker in the city of Wuhan; for taking active part in the Party-led workers' strikes, and showing himself keen, he joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1922, and in 1926 was elected chairman of the Hubei General Trade Union. In June 1927 he attended the Fourth National Labor Congress and was elected to the executive of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions; in August, at the "August 7" Conference the Party Central convened, he was elected to the Provisional Politburo, and in October went to the Soviet Union as the Chinese Communist representative to the Comintern. In 1928, at the Ninth Enlarged Plenum of the Comintern's Executive Committee, he was elected a member of the Comintern Executive and of its presidium. That June, Xiang Zhongfa attended the Chinese Communist "Sixth Congress," held in Moscow. During the congress the Comintern one-sidedly stressed making the personnel of the leading organs workers. When the Comintern secretary Bukharin gave his report at the meeting, he charged Qu Qiubai and others with being big intellectuals and demanded that worker cadres replace them. Pavel Mif of the Comintern's Eastern Department, present to guide the congress, extolled Xiang Zhongfa to the skies, because Xiang Zhongfa had supported Mif and Wang Ming on the question of opposing the "Jiangsu-Zhejiang Fellow-Provincials Society" at Sun Yat-sen University. Of the thirty-seven full and alternate Central Committee members elected at the congress, twenty-one were of worker origin. Xiang Zhongfa was elected to the Central Committee on July 10, to the Politburo and its Standing Committee on July 19, and on July 20, at the first Politburo meeting, was elected general secretary of the Party Central. Thus Xiang Zhongfa became, after Chen Duxiu and Qu Qiubai, the third general secretary of the Party Central. Though he held so high a place, his all-round level in politics and culture and his leading ability were low; he had little judgment of his own, and in three years as general secretary made little independent contribution, in truth playing no part as a chief leader of the Party. When Li Lisan's "Left" opportunism took shape, he "obeyed"; when the still graver "Left" opportunist Wang Ming came to power, he "followed close." But he used his office to pursue material enjoyment and privilege, and was morally corrupt. Past fifty, with a wife and children at home, he yet kept the prostitute Yang Xiuzhen as his mistress, to the vilest effect. As comrades who knew him well — Chen Congying, Huang Jieran, and others — said: "The 'Sixth Congress,' going by a mere doctrine of class origin, pushed him up to be the Party's general secretary; in fact he was a figurehead, could hardly read a few characters, could not so much as make out a document, and it was mainly Comrade Li Lisan who ran things. After Xiang Zhongfa joined the Party his way was all smooth sailing, one step up after another; but his thinking was never well remolded, and grew worse and worse instead. After he returned from the Soviet Union, his first thought was not for the Party's work but to use his office to make great play of privilege and pursue his own material enjoyment. He embezzled a great deal of the Party's activity funds, went in for eating, drinking, whoring, and gambling — all of it — and even kept a prostitute named Yang Xiuzhen carrying on at his lodging all day long."

This mistress was got for him by Gu Shunzhang's "pandering." In this the two men were a fine pair of scoundrels, both long since steeped in the ways of the gutter.

When word of Gu Shunzhang's defection came this time and Zhou Enlai was urgently arranging the removal of the central organs and leaders, the first he thought of was Xiang Zhongfa, whom he told to move at once into a certain house. On the pretext of "work needs," Xiang Zhongfa brought his mistress Yang Xiuzhen to live with him downstairs. To guard against mishap, Zhou Enlai had Ren Bishi's wife Chen Congying move in upstairs with her daughter, only a few months old. When the Politburo decided that Xiang Zhongfa was to move at once to the Central Soviet, Zhou Enlai further had Xiang Zhongfa move alone to Xiaoshadu Road (now Xikang Road) to live with him, and charged him not to go out carelessly. Yang Xiuzhen, escorted by Chen Congying and her daughter, went to lodge at a small hotel, and before the move dismissed the amah Gu Shunzhang had originally introduced. On the address Gu Shunzhang had given, Kuomintang agents failed to catch Xiang Zhongfa. Afterward, again on Gu Shunzhang's confession, they found that amah and set her to shadow Xiang Zhongfa. She learned that Yang Xiuzhen had not yet fetched clothes she had had made at a tailor's shop, and kept watch there every day. One day Yang Xiuzhen went to fetch the clothes, was tailed in secret, and followed all the way to her new lodging. Luckily the Party organization had already noticed this shadowing, and no harm came of it. To throw off this maid's pursuit, the organization moved Yang Xiuzhen again, to a newly built big hotel on Bubbling Well Road. Xiang Zhongfa was at this time still hidden at Zhou Enlai's lodging, the Central having already decided he was to move at once to the Central Soviet. When Xiang Zhongfa asked Zhou Enlai to let him go and see Yang Xiuzhen, Zhou Enlai sternly forbade it, urging him to think of the Party's interest and keep Party discipline, and charging him not to go out carelessly. On the night of June 21, Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao had to go out on business, and charged him again and again on no account to leave. But the moment Zhou and Deng were gone, he could not wait, and slipped off on his own, sneaking to the hotel to meet Yang Xiuzhen, and passed a night there. On Bubbling Well Road there was a British-owned auto firm, the "Delo," which the Party organization had often used as a connection. Xiang Zhongfa too often went there to call a car, and the men at the firm all knew him. The firm had an accountant named Ye Rongsheng, once a Communist Youth League member, who also knew Xiang Zhongfa. This was a shameless man who forgot right at the sight of gain; ever since Gu Shunzhang's defection, seeing the papers full of news of rewards for "meritorious" denunciation of Communists, he had colluded with the agents of the Kuomintang's Songhu Garrison Command, served as their informer, and made ready to sell Communists for a fortune. On the night of the 21st, Ye Rongsheng learned Xiang Zhongfa's whereabouts and at once informed the Songhu Garrison Command's agents. Early the next morning, as Xiang Zhongfa left the hotel to call a car, he was ringed in by agents lying in wait. They checked against Gu Shunzhang's confession that the right forefinger did indeed want a half-joint, and carried him off.

Word of Xiang Zhongfa's arrest was first heard, by chance, by Huang Mulan of the Central Special Section's Intelligence Section. On June 22, meeting the lawyer Chen Zhigao at the Donghai Café beside the Paris Cinema, she ran into Cao Bingsheng, who served as interpreter in the political department of the French Concession's Lujiawan police station. This man was well acquainted with the lawyer Chen, and told him the Songshan Road station had caught a big Communist chief, now moved to Lujiawan. He did not know the man's name, and said only: "This man's over fifty, a Hubei native, one hand with nine fingers, gold teeth; his 'looks' aren't bad, but he's got no backbone at all — before he ever sat in the electric chair he'd confessed the whole lot…" He said too: "Nanjing's about to send men over any moment, to extradite him…" Huang Mulan, aghast at this, hurriedly found a way to report it to Pan Hannian. Hearing her say "over fifty, a Hubei native, nine fingers, gold teeth," Pan Hannian judged it might be Xiang Zhongfa arrested, and at once reported it to Zhou Enlai through Zhao Rong (Kang Sheng), then head of the Central Organization Department.

Zhou Enlai, already anxious that Xiang Zhongfa had not come back all night and was nowhere to be found, on hearing the news of his arrest at once destroyed some secret documents kept at home, and then moved with Deng Yingchao into the Metropole Hotel, run by foreigners on Fourth Avenue (now the corner of Fuzhou Road and Jiangxi Road). Moving in with them were Li Fuchun and Cai Chang.

After his arrest Xiang Zhongfa first gave away Chen Congying. He told the enemy, "Mind that short one (meaning Chen Congying); the tall one (meaning Yang Xiuzhen) knows nothing." So the enemy at once drove to the hotel to arrest Chen Congying and Yang Xiuzhen. By Chen Congying's account, the two of them were at that moment waiting for the organization to come and move them (for this hotel was only a temporary stop); but the callers were three men in long gowns, who said the moment they saw them: "Xiang Zhongfa wants us to take you to where he is — come along at once." And so Chen Congying and Yang Xiuzhen were arrested too. They were first taken to the police station in the Shanghai French Concession, and there they saw Xiang Zhongfa. Xiang Zhongfa was all bows and scrapes before the enemy; the moment he saw Chen Congying brought in he pointed at her and told the enemy in his Hubei speech: "She's Ren Bishi's wife." The enemy, it seems, did not make out what he said, and paid it no mind. To fawn on the enemy, he turned and said to Chen Congying: "They know it all — you may as well tell them everything." The enemy came to threaten Chen Congying too. Chen Congying, holding her child, put on a much-wronged air and said: "I've only just come up from the country; I know nothing — what do you want me to say?" Because Chen Congying had twice taken part in the work of rescuing Comrade Ren Bishi, she had some experience of dealing with the enemy. The moment she was arrested she had her confession ready and had resolved never to change her story. So she dealt with the enemy very steadily. Now, seeing Xiang Zhongfa still talking nonsense, and afraid the enemy would keep pressing, she secretly gave her little daughter a hard pinch, and the child at once cried out loud and set the enemy on edge. Because the enemy were bent just then on seizing our Party's central leaders and wrecking the central organs, they made little of Comrade Chen Congying, a woman who really did look up from the country, and after a few questions with no result the "interrogation" of her was hastily ended. Xiang Zhongfa gave the enemy the central organ at No. 114 Hengjili, Gordon Road, besides, so that the workers there were arrested; he also gave away Zhou Enlai's lodging on Xiaoshadu Road, and that same deep night led agents to search the lodgings of Zhou Enlai and Qu Qiubai — luckily they had withdrawn long since, and the enemy got nothing. Still unwilling to give up, the enemy posted men to wait at Zhou Enlai's home.

On June 23 the Shen Bao carried in its "Local News" column a report put out by the Far Eastern Agency:

Xiang Zhongfa, a Communist chief, was arrested yesterday (the 22nd) at three-quarters past nine in the morning on Shanzhong Road, and taken to the Songshan Road police station. He was then transferred to the political department of the Lujiawan police station. It is learned that the garrison command, holding this prisoner to be a Communist chief, has now sent men to extradite him.

Xiang Zhongfa was that day handed over to the Kuomintang's Songhu Garrison Command; Zhou Enlai and the comrades concerned had for the time no way of knowing that he had already betrayed and gone over. Given the post Xiang Zhongfa then held in the Party, the Central reckoned he would be sent under guard to Nanjing for trial, and had the Central Special Section find out the time and route of the transfer, so as to send the Red Squad to seize the prison van and rescue him. Pan Hannian and the rest of the Special Section, all set by the Central's direction on rescuing him, tried by every means to sound out the particulars of the transfer to Nanjing.

Now the Songhu garrison commander Xiong Shihui, the moment he had Xiang Zhongfa in hand, wired Chiang Kai-shek, who was then on Lushan. The moment Chiang Kai-shek got the cable he wired Xiong Shihui back: shoot him in secret on the spot. But by the time he received Xiong Shihui's cable, after the trial, that Xiang Zhongfa had already turned traitor, Chiang Kai-shek at once changed his mind and ordered instead: "hold off the execution." Xiong Shihui, however, on the first cable from Chiang Kai-shek, had already shot Xiang Zhongfa in the small hours of the 24th. At the execution Xiang Zhongfa had knelt and begged for his life, and got no mercy from the enemy either.

On June 25 the Shen Bao carried another short item in its "Local News" column, saying "the Communist chief Xiang Zhongfa was executed yesterday (the 24th)."

The Central, learning that Xiang Zhongfa had been secretly executed within the Longhua Songhu Garrison Command, and getting word too from inside intelligence that Xiang Zhongfa had gone over to the enemy, wondered: since he had already betrayed, why was he executed?

Zhou Enlai's work was ever exact and meticulous, and on a great matter bearing on a comrade's political life he had to have solid proof before he would form a judgment. He therefore asked Pan Hannian to find some way to copy out Xiang Zhongfa's confession at his enemy trial.

Through the connection of Bao Wenwei, planted as a French interpreter in the Songhu Garrison Command, and of the Intelligence Section's Ouyang Xin, the Central Special Section had already bought the garrison command's judge-advocate-office clerk to supply the confession records of those arrested. But at this interrogation of Xiang Zhongfa, that clerk had not taken part. Through Wu Hanqi, a man under Wu Xingya, head of the Shanghai social affairs bureau, who had ties with the garrison command's Xu, our side found that clerk and, promising him a heavy sum, had him find a way to copy out Xiang Zhongfa's confession. To get a considerable reward, that clerk at last got through to another secretary who kept Xiang Zhongfa's confession, and secretly copied out the whole of it.

Reading this confession, Zhou Enlai at first doubted whether it might be forged by the enemy (who often took such means to muddy the truth). After his arrest in the French Concession, Xiang Zhongfa had been marched by the enemy under arms to search several secret organs, which, having got warning early and moved, took no loss. At the time it was thought Xiang Zhongfa had gone only because he knew these organs had already moved, to put the enemy off, and not necessarily that he meant to give the enemy secrets. But after Zhou Enlai studied the confession closely, many secrets known only to a few in the Central had all been laid bare in it, one by one, and this settled it that Xiang Zhongfa had indeed betrayed.

The enemy executed Xiang Zhongfa, but did not make his traitor's confession public. This reflected the contradictions within the enemy. The Songhu garrison commander Xiong Shihui and the Investigation Section chief Xu Enzeng were both staunchly anti-Communist in politics. But Xiong opposed the C.C. agents' running everything and grasping all power. This time, after Xiang Zhongfa was extradited, Xiong Shihui had the judge advocate's office interrogate him at once, and cabled Chiang Kai-shek in haste to claim the credit and ask how to deal with him, but did not mention the contents of Xiang Zhongfa's post-defection confession. On getting Chiang Kai-shek's reply — "shoot him in secret on the spot" — Xiong Shihui, without waiting for Xu Enzeng and the traitor Gu Shunzhang to have a say, had Xiang Zhongfa killed out of hand. This capture of Xiang Zhongfa had been the work of Xu Enzeng's Zhongtong agents and Gu Shunzhang. But at the three interrogations of Xiang Zhongfa, Xiong Shihui would not let them take part. Xiang Zhongfa's surrender confession, too, Xiong Shihui kept dark, lest Xu Enzeng stir up trouble before Chiang Kai-shek. And so, after Xiang Zhongfa was shot, Xu Enzeng's agents could not even use this confession for anti-Communist propaganda. After Xiang Zhongfa was killed, Xu Enzeng and his men still would not believe it true, and had Gu Shunzhang go specially to Shanghai to dig up the body and verify it; Gu Shunzhang found that the dead man's finger wanted a joint, which was Xiang Zhongfa's mark, and doubted no more.

The enemy had set great hopes on the Xiang Zhongfa case, thinking to use his defection to wreck the Communist Party's underground strength badly, and had not reckoned that the Party Central had already taken measures, leaving them to grope at empty air everywhere and fail of their aim; and so they lost interest even in the traitor Xiang Zhongfa. And Xiang Zhongfa, in the same frame of mind as Gu Shunzhang when he was arrested and betrayed the Party, had fondly supposed that by surrendering to the enemy, selling out the Party and his comrades, he could buy a shameful bare life; but the enemy put him swiftly to death all the same. He was arrested and turned traitor on June 22, and shot on the 24th — less than three days in all. This is the end no shameless traitor escapes.

In June 1966, receiving foreign guests in Hangzhou, Mao Zedong said: "After Xiang Zhongfa was arrested, he led the enemy to seize people; and once the enemy had seized them all, they killed him too."

In June 1972 Zhou Enlai said in a talk: "Xiang Zhongfa, this general secretary, got himself a fine house in Shanghai, and got himself a prostitute (meaning Yang Xiuzhen), and ate, drank, and made merry. When the enemy found out and made arrests, and caught the prostitute, she still would not admit Xiang Zhongfa was a Party member; but the moment Xiang Zhongfa was caught, he admitted at once that he himself was a Party member, and turned traitor — his integrity was not even a prostitute's."

### He Also Betrays Bao Junfu (Yang Dengying)

Gu Shunzhang also gave away Bao Junfu (Yang Dengying), the Central Special Section's most important intelligence connection in Shanghai. Bao Junfu was the first counter-espionage asset the Special Section had built, the earliest to be "pulled out" of the Kuomintang secret-service organs. Gu Shunzhang, who had run the daily affairs of the Central Special Section right up to his defection, knew his situation very well. In selling out Yun Daiying, Cai Hesen, and Xiang Zhongfa, he sold out Bao Junfu as well.

Just as word of Gu Shunzhang's defection first reached Shanghai, Chen Geng, on Zhou Enlai's direction, sent Chen Yangshan to see Bao Junfu, to let him be forewarned and give him what help he needed. Chen Yangshan said: "After Gu Shunzhang's defection, I was to see to Bao Junfu and his family. I could no longer go to Bao Junfu's house, and could only make contact by telephone, calling him out to talk; several times it was Bao's wife I met. Besides comforting her, I gave her a sum of money, and asked her what she needed and what troubles she had. I told her too to have Bao not run, and not be afraid even if he were arrested. I helped her think it through: this was the best course, nothing much would come of it, and Zhang Daofan and the others would help. And I passed word to Bao that we Communists would see his family's affairs through to the end. Afterward Bao Junfu was arrested, and I met his wife a few more times, had her give up the big house she rented and move to her mother's, and went once to see her. At the end of April 1931 Bao Junfu was arrested by the Kuomintang, but he said nothing; after the 'January 28' of 1932, the Kuomintang let him out. After 1934 he became deputy head of the reformatory of the Kuomintang government in Nanjing, and later took office under Wang Jingwei's puppet government. When we liberated Nanjing, he was keeping a little street stall, living very hard. In the 1951 campaign to suppress counter-revolutionaries he was seized; when Comrades Zhou Enlai and Chen Geng learned of it they had him released, and because he had no means of living, we gave him some allowance each month."

After the nationwide campaign to suppress counter-revolutionaries began in October 1950, the Nanjing public security bureau began registering members of reactionary parties and cliques and secret-service personnel; on the first day more than 1,500 people registered, and by the fifth day over 4,000. But a few reactionary party-and-clique members hung back and looked on, and Bao Junfu, who had been head of a reformatory of the Nationalist government, was one of them. Because Bao Junfu held a fairly high puppet post and did not register and come clean of his own accord, he was arrested by the Nanjing public security bureau and then sent on to the Nanjing People's Court for trial. Holding that he had served as deputy head of the Kuomintang reformatory and head of the reformatory of Wang Jingwei's puppet government, the court was prepared to impose a heavy sentence. But Bao Junfu cried out over and over: "In the Great Revolution I did no small service for the Communist Party!" and said he had saved Chen Geng, and asked that Chen Geng appear to testify. The Nanjing People's Court therefore wrote to Chen Geng, asking him to confirm whether what Bao Junfu said was true.

Chen Geng, after leading his troops to liberate Yunnan in 1950 and being appointed chairman of the Yunnan Provincial People's Government, was by this time leading his forces in the War to Resist America and Aid Korea as deputy commander of the Chinese People's Volunteers. The letter, sent to Yunnan, was forwarded thence to Korea. Reading it, Chen Geng wrote back to the Nanjing People's Court on November 24, 1951: "I have your official letter of October 29, No. 5237 (51) of the criminal division: as to whether the confession of Bao Junfu (alias Yang Dengying) is true, so far as I know, Bao did indeed have dealings with our Party from 1927, and was in touch with me at work before 1931, in which time he made no small contribution to the Party. When I was arrested I was in the same prison as he at the Nanjing gendarmerie headquarters, and his conduct then was still fair; afterward he served as head of the puppet Central reformatory, where, it is said, his conduct was poor — the other particulars I do not know… Still, it were best to keep watch and place him under control. This is my reply."

Bao Junfu made important contributions to the Central Special Section in the years 1928–1931, as related in detail earlier in this book. After Gu Shunzhang was arrested and turned traitor on April 25, 1931, he sold out Bao Junfu as well and had him arrested. After Bao Junfu was seized he did indeed say nothing of his dealings with the Central Special Section. When Chen Geng was hunted in Shanghai by agents Gu Shunzhang sent in 1933, he had at the same time been in the same prison as Bao at the Nanjing gendarmerie headquarters. After his release in 1937 Bao Junfu served as a section head and deputy head of a Kuomintang reformatory, and drew up letters of surrender for certain traitors. From 1938 to 1945 he was a high-ranking collaborator in Nanjing, head of the puppet Nanjing reformatory, though no fairly clear direct wrecking activity against the Communist Party has yet been found on his part. After Japan surrendered he was sentenced by the Kuomintang to eight years as a traitor to the nation. He was released from prison before the liberation of Nanjing.

In trying Bao Junfu, the Nanjing People's Court held that, though he had held fairly high reactionary posts under the Kuomintang and the puppet governments, he had no grave crime; taking his whole life in the round, his merit still outweighed his faults. On January 25, 1952, the Nanjing People's Court sentenced him to one year of control and released him on the spot (the control was lifted in 1953).

After his release Bao Junfu, having no source of income, was hard put to keep his household. In November 1952 Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan, learning of this, had Chen Yangshan — then vice-minister of justice — write to the Nanjing public security bureau proposing that Nanjing give Bao Junfu what care it could, though nothing came of it at the time. In 1955, again on Chen Geng's proposal and with the agreement of Li Kenong and Luo Ruiqing, the Nanjing public security bureau paid Bao Junfu a living allowance of one hundred yuan a month. In 1956 the Nanjing public security bureau raised his allowance by another fifty yuan. That March, with the agreement of the General Staff's general office, the Nanjing Military Region sent men to bring Bao Junfu to Beijing for three weeks of sightseeing. During his time in the capital, Chen Geng, Chen Yangshan, and others who had worked in the Central Special Section in those years — Li Kenong, An E, and the rest — all met him, and gave him some care besides. Afterward, whenever Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan passed through Nanjing, they went to see him.

During the "Cultural Revolution" Bao Junfu did not escape being struck at by the masses. Later, for the checking of materials, he was held in prison in Nanjing and Shanghai. In November 1968 he was brought under guard to Beijing, and Zhou Enlai had Li Qiang and Chen Yangshan work with the special case group to talk with him often. Unexpectedly, on December 19, 1969, he died suddenly of a heart attack.

Li Qiang, speaking once of Bao Junfu in 1981, said: "Bao Junfu was on very good terms with Chen Yangshan; Chen kept a single-line tie with him, and he did everything for us. This man was a 'veteran of four dynasties.' In the Kuomintang time he was the number-one man under Yang Jianhong in Shanghai, running the secret-service trade. After Gu Shunzhang's defection he was arrested. Later he went over to the Japanese, and became head of a reformatory under Zhou Fohai. Zhou Fohai's man, the Japanese's man, the Kuomintang's man, and still doing things for us — that is why he was called a 'veteran of four dynasties.' After Japan surrendered, the Kuomintang said he had been a collaborator and cast him off. With no way out, he kept a stall in the street. When Comrade Chen Geng learned of it, he had him given 150 yuan a month. The 'Cultural Revolution' shut him up again, and gave him a very hard time. When the Premier learned of it, he had him brought to Beijing and handed to Comrade Chen Yangshan and me, to talk with him twice a week. At the end of 1969 he died of a heart attack, outside the Qincheng Prison (there were a few rooms to live in there, with fairly free movement). His family and children in Nanjing were cared for by the government too."
