## Chapter 20

Late in the night of December 7, 1941, just as I had laid down my book and was making ready to eat a little and go to bed, I heard the sound of guns.

Could it be, then, that the Pacific war had broken out? I thought.

In all these days no few of the storm-clouds of the Pacific had drifted over Shanghai: first the withdrawal of the American garrison, then America's repeated recall of her nationals from Shanghai, and lately the halting of many ships, so that vessels already set out for Shanghai had turned back. Amid these scraps of storm and cloud I had now and then spoken of it with the Stephens; they never had any settled judgement, nor had they ever given any detailed opinion. Stephen was a military man, and seemed to feel that, beyond obeying orders from above, he need not forecast every change; Mrs. Stephen was a musician, with little interest in politics; so whenever it came up by chance, it never became the centre of our talk.

Yet now here were the guns! Whence, after all, did they come? In the concessions there were now no British or American garrison-troops; then naturally it was the British and American warships still lying here. But this was mere conjecture, that I could neither prove nor inquire into. I switched on the wireless, and only then knew that the Pacific war had indeed broken out, and that on the Huangpu the British ships and the Japanese forces were exchanging fire.

It was somewhat cold, and I was already very tired; I began to make ready for bed, thinking the next day's papers would surely have fuller news.

But the next day's papers had nothing at all; I went out to look, and the streets were all as usual. Afterwards, calling on a friend at a newspaper office, I learned that the guns of the small hours had indeed been a clash between the Japanese forces and a British ship; this British ship, unwilling to surrender her arms, had been sunk, and her whole crew had died at their posts. There was besides an American ship which, outmatched, had had her arms taken and her men made prisoners and sent to an internment camp.

This set me thinking of Stephen. By instinct I felt some alarm: was so dear a friend, then, to be seen no more forever? Looking back now, I first saw how few, indeed, had been our meetings of late; so I took up the telephone, in the full belief that Mrs. Stephen must be at home; but she had gone out early. I left word asking her to telephone me when she returned.

Coming out from the newspaper office, I went to a native bank to draw some money; the bank was fearfully crowded, and I waited a great while before I got it. Hurrying out, in a very uneasy frame of mind, I hired no car and had no destination, but walked alone to Nanjing Road. On Nanjing Road at that time were many Japanese military vehicles coming and going, with Japanese and Chinese aboard, scattering many absurd handbills and fearful prohibitions; the roadsides were all people, some standing to watch, some hurrying by, the streets in great confusion. I followed Nanjing Road on to Bubbling Well Road; in many places Japanese troops had already set up posts, and along the way were busy laying military telephone-lines; on the walls were only Japanese proclamations and nothing else at all. I much wished to go home and hear the news on the wireless, but the road from the British Concession to the French Concession was already sealed off; afterwards, hearing there was one road one might pass by, I went round about that way. Just then I thought of Bai Ping: in such confusion, how was Bai Ping ordering herself? I had not seen her in a long time; perhaps she could still tell me news of Stephen; so I took a car and went straight to Bai Ping's. Ah Mei came to open the door, and said:

"How is it you have not come all this long while?"

"That is why I have come today," I said. "Is Bai Ping at home?"

"She is at home."

But I still stood at the door; she laughed and said:

"Please come in."

"Is there a guest?" I asked.

"No." She smiled mockingly: "She is waiting only for you to come."

I said nothing, and went in. Bai Ping's door was shut; one could hear the sound of the wireless broadcasting in Japanese. After a moment's musing, I knocked.

"Come in."

I pushed open the door and went in. Bai Ping wore a long-sleeved grey cotton cheongsam, the sleeves rolled up to show two inches of white silk blouse; she sat very serenely on a low, small sofa, soft slippers on her feet, stretched out far, Jimi asleep beside her feet; on the right an electric stove was lit, on the tea-table to the left was a box of chocolates. Seeing me come in, she did not stir, but watched me with her eyes, and reaching back switched off the wireless, and showed that smile like a lily just opening, and said:

"Is it you?"

"Does it surprise you?"

"No," she said. "I thought you should be coming too."

I took off my overcoat and sat down facing her; she said:

"Come and sit over here; it is warmer."

I moved over; she took two pieces of chocolate and tossed one to me:

"Have a piece of chocolate."

"Thank you," I said.

For a good while she said nothing, but with a low, faint smile studied me. Then she said slowly: "Thinner still."

"You are too much at your ease," I said.

"What of it?"

"Outside is all this confusion, and you sit alone so serenely at home."

"What is to be done, if not so?"

"Have you news of Stephen?"

"I have not seen him this long while—how is he?"

"You have not seen him this long while?"

"He has not sought me out this long while," she said; "nor telephoned me."

"Do you know that the warship he belonged to had her arms taken yesterday?"

"Naturally I know."

"And he?"

"Sent to an internment camp, I should think." she said with a smile.

"Bai Ping!" I paused a good while, drew on a cigarette, and, my eyes cast down, said gravely: "It much surprises me that you are so. Stephen is your friend too, after all."

"Naturally."

"Then you are not the least anxious."

"How do you know I am not anxious?" she smiled mischievously.

"Your manner."

"Would you have me go crying it up and down the streets?" still she smiled mischievously.

"We are human, we have feeling, we have love," I said. But she took up my words mischievously:

"We ought to be anxious."

"And you sit serenely here!"

"And you?" she said mischievously. "You too sit serenely here."

"Do you know how many places I went to this forenoon?"

"Do you know how many places I went to from the moment the guns began?" she said, all the while mischievous and gentle, but of a sudden changing to a plain, true tone: "I have many things to be anxious about—my own plight, my own life, my own future; I have besides a still better friend in Hong Kong; ought I, then, to tear my hair and stamp and weep aloud before you?"

I bowed my head and was silent, and she said again:

"It is a rare thing to have you here; why come with a set face to quarrel with me? Friends—you have them, I have them—each goes to do his own duty, to spend his own love. Perhaps you have run about all the forenoon for Stephen; perhaps I shall weep a whole night for Stephen; but these are our feelings for Stephen, and you need not display yours to me, nor need I feign alarm to you."

"But we ought to take counsel and think of a way."

"Take counsel?" she said. "If, to rescue Stephen, I take counsel with the Japanese, is that not of more effect than taking counsel with you? But is even that of any effect? War, my friend, war! Do you understand?"

"…" I seemed to have something to say, but could bring out nothing.

"Do not be so; give me a little smile to look at," she said, laughing, and then called toward the outer room, "Ah Mei!"

Ah Mei appeared at the door, and Bai Ping said:

"Bring two glasses of grape wine."

As Ah Mei went to fetch the grape wine, Bai Ping switched on the wireless; she seemed to be hunting for something, and at last found some jazz music.

"It is a slow foxtrot," she said. "Very good; it is a long time since you danced with me; will you dance one with me?"

On the silver carpet I danced with her.

"Have I any change in me?" she asked.

"You are redder than before."

"And besides?"

"Deeper." I had indeed come to another understanding of her.

When the music ended, she raised her grape wine and said, with feeling:

"Let us drink a blessing on the Stephens."

We drained the wine, and she sat down, and looking at me, said calm and grave:

"It is not that I am deeper; it is that I am more seasoned."

I said nothing, but looked at her and waited for her to go on.

"I am a dancing-girl; I must hide all that is fearful and all my anxiety, all pain and all disquiet, and meet the world with a happy, serene smile, and order my own affairs and feelings with a calm, composed manner." she said, grey and grave: "So forgive me, then." And at last she called:

"Ah Mei, serve the meal."

At the table she said:

"Now you truly ought to be planning to go back to the rear."

"I thought as much on the road just now," I said. "And you, then?"

"Am I still worth the mention?" She laughed, dejected and cold: "Then let me live on in your heart."

After dinner she said:

"Stephen may perhaps get out, and perhaps not; but these are all things beyond your power."

She said again:

"Make ready early to go to the interior; if you need money, do not stand on ceremony—come to me for it."

And at last she said:

"Now go home; and hereafter do not come often to see me, unless I ask you."

I did not ask her the reason, but hurried out; Bai Ping grew, indeed, more and more mysterious, and there was in my heart seven parts disquiet and three parts dread.

I went straight home, and learned that Mrs. Stephen had not telephoned. From two o'clock till twelve at night I put through, first and last, twenty telephone calls at the least, and she never came home. The next day I went again to see her, but her maid said she had not come back at all; I asked the maid to telephone me when she returned, and left a note besides. What I feared now was not only Stephen, but Mrs. Stephen as well: could she too have been seized by the Japanese?—which was by no means an impossible thing.

On the morning of the eleventh there was still no word at all of Mrs. Stephen; but I had a letter from Helen. She said:

"Xu: I have telephoned several times and never found you in, and so must write you this letter.

The guns have destroyed my plans for singing, destroyed my future, destroyed my brightness and my dreams. What, after all, is life for? What, after all, is mankind about? I need a friend now; I need cool, clear thought.

On getting this letter please come at once to see me, and please bring me a few books to help my thinking. In the faint moonlight I hope all your hurry and toil may bear a brilliant harvest. I bless you.

Helen Manfield, night of December 10"

In a dark everyday dress, her golden hair loose about her back, her powder plain, her look grave and still—such was the Helen Manfield of about the time she wrote this letter. Two hours after reading it I was before her.

She showed a wan, faint smile and said:

"You are thinner."

"How is this?" I said. "Are you not well?"

"No." She lowered her brows and lashes, and said faintly.

"And your mother?"

"She has gone out."

I gave her the books; she did not open them, but took them and set them on the piano; on the piano was a flower-vase, whose flowers seemed not to have been changed for some days, and looked dim and withered. I glanced about me, and felt at once that the air of the room was wholly changed: all the liveliness had turned to disorder, all the pure quiet to loneliness—like a man after an illness, like a picture after the moth, like a plant beaten by wind and rain, like a garden trampled by cattle and sheep; for the storm-clouds of the Pacific had swept past this roof, and the guns of the Huangpu had shaken these walls! I felt fretted and oppressed, and went over and opened the window and drew a deep breath.

"So everything here is changed?" Helen asked in a low voice.

"How has it come about?" I said; but at once I felt this word had stirred her feeling; a faint frown gathered at her brows, and she showed a dim, faint smile; so I braced my own tone, forced out a lighter manner, and said, as I ran over to her:

"It must be that you have not sung this long while! Think—how much of your singing has this room drawn in? It lives by your singing, and is glorious by your singing. Your singing is this room's food, is this room's soul. But now it has run dry, as a flower loses the nurture of water, as grass loses the moistening of dew."

"…" Her lips quivered faintly, but she brought out nothing, and gazed at me witless and smiling; and when my eyes met hers, I asked tenderly:

"Is it that you have not sung this long while, Helen?"

"I shall never sing again!" she said with bitterness.

"Then," I said, "this room will wither, and wither, and at last fall down." I went to the piano and said:

"Look, the piano is all dust—dust!" I opened the piano for her. I went and begged her:

"Come, come, sing me a song. Sing a song you love."

"No, no." She refused me.

"Sing one, for me, only for me; it is a long time since I heard you sing."

"No, no," she knit her brows, and changed to a grave tone: "Do not force me so."

I saw there seemed to be in her heart some unspeakable oppression, and that she seemed on the point of anger; I could no longer press her, but sat down in silence, and unconsciously sighed a little, and lit a cigarette.

But she looked at me a moment, mused a little, and then, as though summoning much strength, rose slowly from the sofa, went slowly to the piano, and sat down; suddenly she touched the strings lightly, and rising by degrees, she began to sing.

It was so deep, so far-reaching; it called down the cry of the wild geese in the long sky, and called down too the nightingale under the moon; it leapt among short, urgent notes, and fled far off in long, deep strains, like a ship in a great storm, one moment soaring aloft, straight up to the clouds, the next drifting down and lost to sight; and at last it rose and sank in a trembling surge of sound, like a bird of prey in its struggle, wounded and fighting on, rousing itself toward life until, spent utterly, it gasped its piteous breath and moaned over and over, and with one last long cry fell sharply silent.

At first I watched with pleasure the heave of her back and neck; then by degrees it grew hard to bear, like a nurse attending a woman in a hard labour; so I closed my eyes and, leaning on the sofa, listened in silence, and felt my heartstrings wrench and my nerves shiver, the tears welling up in my eyes and at last streaming down my cheeks. I took out my handkerchief and wiped my eyes.

She closed the piano; I did not clap, but raised my eyes to her. She rose gravely, not a flicker of expression on her face, tears brimming in her eyes, and came slowly over, and sat down on the sofa where she had been before, and buried her face in her hands, and fell to sobbing. I had no words to comfort her; I ran over, bent to her ear, and said in the lowest of voices:

"You have indeed succeeded, Helen. Strive on! I look for you to strive on."

Still she lay against the arm of the sofa and sobbed.

"Strive on, Helen," I said. "I shall pray for you forever."

Still she lay against the arm of the sofa and sobbed. I rose, with a heaviness past telling in my heart; I did not know why she sobbed, nor had I words to comfort her, nor wished to offer her comfort. Her attainment in singing had gone beyond my expectation, and I felt of a sudden how very small I was; before a genius, as before some awe-inspiring great man ringed about with gleaming armed soldiers, I felt my own smallness; I wished to leave that place, and softly took up my overcoat and hat, and stole out.

But just at the instant of my going out I met Mrs. Manfield coming back. Her look was very hurried, her plumpness the same as ever, but her face very grey and dark; seeing me, she showed a faint, melancholy smile and said:

"Xu—how is this, making ready to go?" She held me back, and said again: "Dine here; I have just been meaning to have a talk with you."

I could only go back in with her; Helen leaned against the sofa with her eyes shut, and Mrs. Manfield said:

"Are you unwell, Helen?"

"No," a bitter smile on her face; she opened her moist eyes and said to her mother: "You are back?"

Helen may perhaps have noticed I had passed by, and perhaps not; she seemed not to care whether I was there; but Mrs. Manfield seemed to find the situation strange—she knew I was leaving, and saw too that Helen had been weeping, and so, with a questioning gaze, she looked at me and then at Helen, and said nothing; so I spoke first:

"Mrs. Manfield, Helen has indeed succeeded; her singing just now all but made me faint away."

"You have been singing?" Mrs. Manfield asked.

"Yes." said Helen. "I found I was truly singing for the first time."

"You mean…" Mrs. Manfield left off, half asking, half explaining.

"In the past my singing used only my throat; today I seem to have used my soul. I have forgotten my throat; I feel that my every nerve and every cell is singing."

"Would you sing another?" Mrs. Manfield asked.

"No, no." said Helen. "It can come but once; it was met by chance, met by chance—strange; I wonder at it myself."

I sat in silence to one side; Mrs. Manfield pressed her no more, but rose quietly and said to us:

"Have your talk." And leaving a dim smile, she went out.

Helen kept silent, but I noticed that the mood of a moment ago had subsided, and I said:

"Why is it you have not sung this long while, again?"

"What is it all for, indeed?"

"Was your singing, then, only for the Christmas concert?"

"No." she said. "But I have no interest in anything. What, after all, is life for? War, money, I…"

"Life is a blank sheet; fill it as you please."

"Must it be filled?"

"In fact you fill it every day—eating, sleeping, rising, sitting down, the mind thinking, the hand moving; to live is to fill the blank sheet of life. Only in death, only when you die, is the paper handed in."

"Then what is the meaning of life?"

"It lies in the filling of the blank sheet. The child takes the blank sheet and scrawls on it; the merchant writes his accounts on it; the painter paints on it; the musician sets down his notes on it; the architect draws his plans on it; the engineer draws his diagrams on it."

"And so you write philosophy on it."

"Well or ill, I fill in some meaning on the blank sheet God has handed me."

"Then I…"

"Sing, sing, sing—that is your meaning."

"…" She made no sound, and after a while asked suddenly:

"Have you met Mei Yingzi lately?"

"Mei Yingzi—have you not met her?"

"It has been a long time."

"Longer even than since I saw you," I said.

Mrs. Manfield came in and invited us to the dining-room. At table we spoke again of Mei Yingzi, and of Bai Ping; none of us had seen them in a long while. Then I spoke of Stephen's capture, and we all felt the desolation of human affairs and the terror of change; at last I said that there was no word of Mrs. Stephen, and that I feared some ill had befallen her.

"Mrs. Stephen?" said Mrs. Manfield. "I met her on the Bund."

"What did she say?"

"She did not greet me; she was sitting in a car, and did not, I suppose, see me."

"Was she alone?"

"There seemed to be two men besides."

This surprised me greatly; but if it were truly Mrs. Stephen, then it was proved at last that she had not been seized. I asked:

"You are sure you did not mistake her for someone else?"

"No; I am absolutely certain I did not."

My mind was much eased; I telephoned Mrs. Stephen's home at once. Mrs. Stephen was not at home; I meant to tell her maid to set her mind at rest, but the maid, knowing it was I, told me first that yesterday Mrs. Stephen had sent someone to fetch clothes and things, only without giving an address. Then Mrs. Stephen's safety was past doubt, and I hung up the telephone.

After dinner, in the sitting-room, Helen was not present, and Mrs. Manfield began to tell me how things stood in her house. It came to me that the foreign banks had all fallen into Japanese hands, and whether foreigners might draw money was a matter for which no procedure seemed yet to have been announced. And theirs was a household with no man and no very close kin or friends; and now the case of all foreigners was the same, so that they could hardly be looked after by anyone—then how did their finances stand? I saw all at once the reason for the dim air of this room, and said in the lowest voice, in the most earnest tone:

"Do you need money?"

"But…" she faltered, and looked at me shamefaced. I had at the time seven hundred-odd dollars in my pocket, and I gave her six hundred. I said:

"Take it and use it for now; I will bring more another day."

"No, no." As if ashamed, she would not take it.

"What does it matter, Mrs. Manfield? Are we not the best of friends?"

"…" Still she would not take it.

"War," I said. "Everyone has his difficulties. We ought to help one another; my case is a little better than yours; take it, by all means."

Mrs. Manfield looked at me with grateful eyes, and took the money and held it in her hand. I said:

"In a day or two I will bring you a little more; and hereafter, if it is not enough, tell me at any time."

"Thank you for your noble kindness."

"We are friends; in adversity we ought of course to help one another," I said. "But you must on no account tell Helen."

"Why?" she said. "Helen would be as grateful to you."

"No, no; you must by all means hear me," I said. "I know that with a nature so proud, so fond of standing on her own, as hers, she would never wish to let an outsider know her troubles."

"You are too good!" she said, showing a kindly, bright, grateful smile.

"Put the money away. It will not do for Helen to come in and see it."

Mrs. Manfield put the money away in her handbag; I heard the sound of Helen outside calling her mother.
