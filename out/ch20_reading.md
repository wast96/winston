## Chapter Twenty. The Hour of the Rabbit, First Half (5 a.m.)

A thick, pungent reek of blood hung over the whole polo ground, and those spirited fine horses of the Western Regions were all uneasy and restless, pawing without cease at the earth, kicking up patch upon patch of yellow dust.

Zhang Xiaojing stood in the middle of the field, breathing hard, his single eye red as a mad beast’s. Not far off, a long-handled modao lay cast upon the ground, and beside it a horse of huge and mighty frame lay toppled over, like a mountain of flesh. About its neck were tied colored ribbons, and its tail was bound with a plumed cord, marking it out as a beast of no common station; but alas, there was now a great gash across its belly, and the blood ran purling from its body, soaking into the yellow earth, and soon steeping the ground in a strange and eldritch vermilion.

His left hand, at this moment, had a death-grip on the top-knot of Prince Yong, Li Lin, holding this scion of the blood royal helpless to stir. Prince Yong kicked his legs in terror, crying out loud for rescue.

By now a great many people had gathered about the edges of the field: young gallants come to play at polo, the servants and guards of Prince Yong’s household, common folk from near the ground, and, but newly arrived, a great press of Wannian County buliang men. Yet, as one who would throw at a rat but dreads to smash the vase beside it, they all held back, and not one dared draw near. Who could give warrant that this madman would not do the Prince a hurt?

Zhang Xiaojing lowered his head and looked askance at this noble young lord: ‘When Wen Wuji died, was he too in so wretched a plight as this?’

‘I do not know! I never knew him!!’ Prince Yong screamed hysterically.

He had not yet recovered from the shock of a moment before. He had been happily at his polo when all at once a black shape came rushing onto the field, bearing a murderous will that reached to heaven, and with a huge modao cut down his own beloved mount, then pinned him fast to the ground. His fellow-players had tried to come to his aid, only for two of them to be cleanly and neatly killed, at which the rest took fright on the instant and scattered in a rout.

Prince Yong had never set eyes on this one-eyed man, and was utterly at a loss. Not until the one-eyed man uttered the name ‘Wen Wuji’ did he begin truly to be afraid.

Zhang Xiaojing’s blade gave a waver, his voice colder than a viper’s: ‘I am the buliang chief of Wannian, and there is nothing I am better versed in than pressing a case and putting men to the question. Since I have traced the matter as far as here, Your Highness of Yong had best tell no lies.’ Prince Yong was cowed by the threat; he could feel that this god of slaughter was capable of anything at all. He paused, then said hurriedly: ‘I truly do not know!’

Without expression Zhang Xiaojing drew from his breast a small bamboo tube, and forced its contents down into Prince Yong’s mouth. Prince Yong felt only a stream of intensely bitter liquid running down his throat into his stomach; and then Zhang Xiaojing clapped a square cloth tight over his mouth.

He gave muffled cries and struggled. Zhang Xiaojing drove a fist into Prince Yong’s ribs: ‘No need to fret—this is an emetic broth boiled up from fish-mint and baiwei root, kept in stock by any apothecary you please, a sovereign remedy for a man who has been poisoned. Hmm... though with something stopping up the mouth, it is a different matter.’

As though to prove the truth of Zhang Xiaojing’s words, Prince Yong suddenly arched his back and fell to retching violently. The gruel-like matter in his stomach surged back up the gullet to his lips, and was on the point of spewing forth—only to be blocked by the cloth before his mouth, and flow back down again; and a part of it passed into his windpipe, so that he choked in agony past all bearing.

On the one side his stomach cramped and heaved without cease; on the other, nothing could escape his mouth, and it drove back up into his nose. The two together left Prince Yong streaming with tears and snot, in a pass past all wretchedness, with even a stray fleck of vomit spurting now and then from his nostrils. Were it to go on so much longer, he might well be choked outright to death.

Zhang Xiaojing judged it near enough, and reached out to loosen the cloth. Prince Yong, as though granted a great amnesty, sprawled on the ground and vomited wildly for a spell before at last he grew quiet. Zhang Xiaojing said coldly: ‘This is called All Streams Return to the Source, a means of judicial torture invented in his day by Lai Junchen—the lightest of the Eight Methods of the house of Lai. Should Your Highness have the leisure and the fancy for it, we might try them over one by one.’

Did this fellow actually mean, before the eyes of the multitude, to put an imperial prince to the torture? Prince Yong was certain now: the man was an utter and thoroughgoing madman. Against a madman, neither power nor reason availed; one could only meekly yield.

‘I—I’ll talk...’ Prince Yong’s throat was raw and burning, and he could speak only in a hoarse whisper.

‘From the beginning.’

It fell out thus. On the seventh day of the seventh month of the second year of Tianbao, Prince Yong had chanced to pass by Dunyi Ward, and had happened to see Wen Ran setting out an incense-table in her courtyard, praying to heaven for skill. Seeing how her beauty stood out above the common, he was moved to certain thoughts. Back at his mansion, he spoke a few words of it to his confidants, then put the matter out of mind. A few days later one of these confidants came bustling in with news: that before long Wen Ran might be bought into the princely household as a bondservant—and only then did Prince Yong learn how large his people had made the thing grow.

‘That I coveted Wen Ran’s beauty is true enough, but I had no thought whatever of seizing her by force. It was only that the Bear Fire Gang, and those men under the Wannian County commandant, had a mind to curry favor, and gave their fancy free rein, and so brewed up this cruel disaster—it was none of my intending!’

Zhang Xiaojing understood the moment he heard it. Such things were all too common: a careless word above, and those below would throw ten times the force into pushing it through. Like enough the Bear Fire Gang had long had their eye on the situation of the Wen shop, and had this time borrowed Prince Yong’s name to work a trifling matter, by main force, into the ruin and undoing of a household.

‘I gave them a sound rating for it too—these men are forever stirring up trouble out of nothing!’

‘Stirring up trouble out of nothing?’ A muscle twitched at the corner of Zhang Xiaojing’s mouth. ‘And then you fined them three cups of wine for it, I suppose? In your eyes, I fear, these common folk are no better than so many mole-crickets and ants, mere aphids—is that the way of it?’ Only now did Prince Yong grasp that he had misspoken, and half in flattery he said: ‘Brave sir, if you are set on revenge, it is them you ought to seek out; I shall go along with you myself.’

‘Your Highness need not trouble himself—the Bear Fire Gang I have already washed clean through once over, and the honorable county commandant I have butchered as well,’ said Zhang Xiaojing lightly. A vein leapt in Prince Yong’s brow; he felt a dull ache stir again in his stomach, and knew that this day could come to no good end.

Zhang Xiaojing had been away on a case in the provinces, and had heard of this dreadful turn of events the moment he returned to Chang’an. Betraying nothing, he had set about a quiet investigation. With the arts of a buliang chief, it was the easiest thing in the world to trace out the several parties concerned. And so Zhang Xiaojing had first found himself a pretext, and led the buliang men to root out the Bear Fire Gang almost entire—though, worse luck, Feng Dalun had run fast and got away with his life.

The Wannian County commandant, getting word, hurried to the spot and made haste to call Zhang Xiaojing off. He and Zhang Xiaojing had worked together some years, and were on no bad terms, so that Zhang Xiaojing had meant at first to reason the thing out. But unlooked-for, the commandant, while feigning to soothe him on the face of it, had put poison in the wine, and lay a great many swordsmen in wait round about, to cut Zhang Xiaojing down on the spot. Happily a subordinate he was friendly with tipped him the wink; Zhang Xiaojing struck first, and there at the board ran the commandant through with a single thrust.

Zhang Xiaojing knew that for wiping out the Bear Fire Gang there was still some ground to stand on, but that to have killed his own superior would surely be pursued as a capital crime. So he simply rushed straight for the polo ground, to lay hold first of the last and chief author of the wrong, and settle the rest after.

Prince Yong lifted his head and tried to talk him round: ‘You have committed a crime that reaches to heaven, and are like to die for it. But I still have some weight with the Emperor my father, and might perhaps win you some measure of pardon.’ Instead Zhang Xiaojing stretched out his great hand, seized Prince Yong by the top-knot, hauled him up by the neck, and dragged him step by step off the field.

Prince Yong was terrified out of his wits, thinking the man was about to do him in. But that hand of Zhang Xiaojing’s was like a pair of iron pincers, and from it there was no breaking free at all.

‘Commandant Gan, Liu the Clerk, Song Sixteen, Du Poluo, Wang Hedong, Fan the Fourth...’ As he dragged him along, Zhang Xiaojing muttered over a string of men’s names. Prince Yong did not understand who these people were, nor what they had to do with the present business.

‘They are all dead—every one of them died in the Western Regions, killed by the Türk. Wen Wuji and I brought back the whole of their ashes, and set them in the Wen Incense Shop; so that the brothers of the Eighth Company, saving only that lad Xiao Gui, had all of them come to Chang’an at the last, for what it was worth...’ Zhang Xiaojing’s voice had been level enough, but of a sudden it turned brimful of killing intent. ‘And yet you people tore the Wen shop down about their ears; those urns of ashes were smashed to pieces too, scattered into the mud and the rubble, never to be found again.’

‘It was not I—it was them! Them!’ Prince Yong shouted till his voice cracked, feeling himself most cruelly wronged.

Zhang Xiaojing stamped hard upon the earth of the polo field: ‘From this day forth, the brothers of the Eighth Company are like this yellow sand beneath my feet—trampled every day under the heels of men and the hooves of horses.’

At such words a chill went climbing up Prince Yong’s spine. He was as one bitten by a venomous snake, his limbs all stiffened, and he let Zhang Xiaojing drag him where he would.

The buliang men and the Prince’s own attendants round about followed hard after them, yet none dared come near. The name of the Five Yamas weighed all too heavily with its terror in their hearts; they only drew up in formation at a distance, and watched from afar.

Prince Yong’s cries moved Zhang Xiaojing not a jot. Expressionless, he dragged this sixteenth imperial son all the way from the polo ground, and came to a Guanyin temple no more than a street away.

This Guanyin temple, which stood within Jinggong Ward, was of no great size; the most famous thing in it was a jade image of Guanyin set up for worship there. The temple had a deep bond with Prince Yong. At the time of his birth he had fallen gravely ill, and his mother, the Lady Guo, had come to this very temple in her own person to pray three days and three nights. Not long afterward the Lady Guo had died. Strange to tell, on the very day of her death Prince Yong had miraculously recovered. It was said throughout the palace that the Lady Guo had moved the bodhisattva to pity, and had bought one life with another. Her spirit-tablet, too, had been set up in the temple.

For the sake of this bond Prince Yong cared for the Guanyin temple with the utmost solicitude, making it frequent gifts, and coming at festivals and the turn of the year to offer incense—one bow to Guanyin, a second to his mother. His very interest in polo had come of the fact that a polo ground lay along the street from the Guanyin temple; each time he came to offer incense he would go and play a hand or two into the bargain, and had little by little become a fair master of the game.

Now, finding that Zhang Xiaojing was dragging him toward the Guanyin temple, his flesh crept, for he had no notion what this madman meant to do. Zhang Xiaojing kicked the temple gate open, drove off the resident monks with one fierce glare, and made straight for the Guanyin hall.

The image of the Water-Dripping Guanyin stood there in the midst of the hall, smooth and lucent, of no common excellence. Beside it stood a lotus-and-seven-jewels side-niche, and set upright within it a spirit-tablet—that of Prince Yong’s mother, the Lady Guo, as need hardly be said.

Zhang Xiaojing let go his hold, and with a kick sent Prince Yong sprawling to the ground, so that he knelt before the image of Guanyin. Looking up and seeing his own mother’s spirit-tablet, Prince Yong could not help but break into open weeping.

‘Swear me an oath before the bodhisattva and before your mother, and I will spare you your life,’ said Zhang Xiaojing lightly. Prince Yong could scarcely believe his ears: ‘Swear what oath?’

‘That from this day forth you will take no revenge upon, nor pursue any charge against, Wen Ran or the Wen Incense Shop; and should you break it, may the thunder of heaven rend you asunder.’

Prince Yong thought this all too easy—could it be some fresh trick to torment him with? He opened his mouth, but dared not lightly give his consent.

Zhang Xiaojing’s face was without expression, though within he gave a faint and bitter smile.

To kill off every last one of those concerned, root and branch, would be satisfying enough; but Wen Ran would surely then be made the target of reprisal. The ways of such men he knew all too well.

He himself stood alone in the world; if he died, why, he died, and there was an end of it. But Wen Ran was still young, with a long road of life yet before her. Were Wen Wuji’s soul aware in heaven, he would never suffer Zhang Xiaojing, for the sake of avenging him, to sacrifice his daughter’s happiness.

And so, mad though Zhang Xiaojing was, he could not but have a care for Wen Ran’s fate—she was, one might say, the one drop of flesh and blood that the whole Eighth Company had left behind in the land of the living.

In seizing Prince Yong, Zhang Xiaojing had never from the first meant to kill him, but to force from him a guarantee that he would not lay a hand on Wen Ran again. Zhang Xiaojing had made his inquiries: Prince Yong held this Guanyin temple in sincere and devout faith, and an oath sworn here he would surely take in earnest. So long as Prince Yong dared make no move, those under him would be bound to hold themselves in check, and Wen Ran might live out a quiet life.

Coming to this thought, Zhang Xiaojing aimed another kick at him, urging him to be quick. Prince Yong had no choice but to kneel unwillingly upon the ground, and wipe the filth from the corner of his mouth with his sleeve. He offered incense to Guanyin and made his prostrations, then offered incense to his own mother and made his prostrations, and then, pinching a stick of joss between his fingers, said with much squirming and reluctance: ‘From this day forth, all the score between me and the house of Wen is struck from the reckoning; there shall be no revenge and no pursuit of any charge; and should I break it, may the thunder of heaven rend me asunder!’

This said, Prince Yong made three reverent kowtows. Wayward and unruly as he was, before Guanyin and his mother he kept the forms with perfect deference throughout. This done, he snapped the joss-stick in two and held it out to Zhang Xiaojing: ‘Will that do, then?’

Zhang Xiaojing took the joss-stick and ground it between his fingers to a fine powder: ‘If you break your oath, then even should the bodhisattva Guanyin let it pass, I shall come seeking you.’ Prince Yong bowed his head, and dared not meet that terrible single eye.

Zhang Xiaojing let out a long breath, paid him no further heed, and turned and walked out of the Buddha-hall; with a shake of both arms he pushed open the temple gate and stepped forth. Outside the temple soldiers had already gathered in force, and at the sight of him one and all drew blade and spanned crossbow. Seeing Zhang Xiaojing come out with his hands clasped behind him, the first reaction of those buliang men was, of all things, to take a step backward all together.

‘Zhang Xiaojing, buliang chief of Wannian—come out to surrender and give himself up!’

Zhang Xiaojing reined in his killing air, lifted his head, and cried it aloud in the face of the crowd—startling from the great tree before the gate a whole nestful of jet-black old crows, that went flapping up into the air...

Several months had passed since then, and Zhang Xiaojing had never thought to see Prince Yong again—and in such a setting as this, no less.

Neither had Prince Yong thought to see Zhang Xiaojing again. Ever since that assault on the polo ground, he had been left with a chronic ailment: let Zhang Xiaojing but be named, and his stomach would seize with a cramp and turn to retching. And now, beholding the very man in the flesh, his face went by turns livid and red, his lips working open and shut, and with a ‘Bleagh!’ he spewed a whole floor’s worth of rare delicacies and fine wine. A sour and savage stench came rushing to the nostrils.

Xiao Gui laughed aloud: ‘Big-Head, before this you spared him his life for the sake of keeping Wen Ran safe. Now there is no more need for such scruples; this murderer of Wen Wuji I leave to you to deal with as you will!’

Zhang Xiaojing, silent, took a step forward. Prince Yong waved his right hand in a panic: ‘You gave your promise! I do not touch Wen Ran, and you do not kill me!’

‘The Bear Fire Gang carried off Wen Ran this day—did you know of it?’ Zhang Xiaojing asked.

‘Uh... uh... I knew nothing of it beforehand!’ Prince Yong’s face was overcast and clear by turns. He was not lying: Feng Dalun had reported it to him only after the fact, and had been given tacit consent. In Prince Yong’s own mind this did not count as breaking the oath—but the trouble was, the matter was not his to decide.

‘Big-Head, waste no words on him—cut out his heart and liver at a stroke, and make an offering of them to Wen Wuji,’ Xiao Gui called down from above.

The air in the great hall grew all at once taut with strain. Everyone knew how the Son of Heaven doted on this sixteenth son of his; and now these ruffians meant, before his very eyes, to cut Prince Yong open alive and carve out his heart—what was to be done?

Without expression Zhang Xiaojing hauled Prince Yong up by the front of his robe, then of a sudden threw out his arm and dealt him several savage slaps across the face. Prince Yong was struck dizzy and reeling, his cheeks swollen high. Xiao Gui, supposing he wished first to vent his spleen a little, did not press him, but waited with keen interest to see the moment he set to work.

Zhang Xiaojing spoke: ‘For a besotted princeling like this, cutting out the heart lets him off far too cheap. The Eight Methods of the house of Lai—we must give him each of them, one by one.’ He drew his lips apart in a grin, and there breathed from him a grim and venomous air. At the words Prince Yong shook all over like a sieve of chaff. The year before, All Streams Return to the Source had already tortured him till he wished himself dead—and that was but the lightest of the Eight Methods of the house of Lai...

Xiao Gui glanced at the firelight outside: ‘I’d not spoil your sport, Big-Head, but time is none too plentiful with us.’ Zhang Xiaojing kicked Prince Yong down with one foot, set his boot upon his chest, and said with a savage grin: ‘No matter—I have thought of a fine notion.’

And then, just as he had done some months before, he dragged Prince Yong by the top-knot, hauled him savagely to the broken bridge on the seventh floor, and thrust him outward. In an instant half of Prince Yong’s body hung out beyond the Qinzheng Wuben Tower. Xiao Gui watched with keen interest, looking forward to some fine piece of theater. The Son of Heaven stood at his side, unmoving, yet with anger showing plain in his eyes.

Prince Yong was by now frightened clean out of his soul, retching aloud, as though his nightmare had come again. Zhang Xiaojing gripped him by the front of his robe and said, low-voiced: ‘If you would live, do as I say.’

Prince Yong was still shrieking away to himself; Zhang Xiaojing dealt him a heavy slap: ‘I should dearly like to kill you here and now, but just now I still need you to do a certain thing.’ Prince Yong was taken aback, not grasping what this fiend could mean. Zhang Xiaojing said: ‘In a moment I am going to push you off the tower; you must listen closely...’

He said a few words softly in Prince Yong’s ear; Prince Yong first opened his eyes wide, then shook his head with all his might. But Zhang Xiaojing gave him no chance for it—with a hard shove, Prince Yong went plummeting straight down, screaming, from the broken bridge on the seventh floor. This place being called the Star-Plucking Hall, it stood of course exceedingly high above the ground, and a fall like this must surely turn a man to a smear of pulp.

The prince flung to his death, Zhang Xiaojing turned back into the great hall, cool and composed. Xiao Gui licked his lips, feeling somewhat unsatisfied: ‘Big-Head, you let him off as cheap as that?’ Zhang Xiaojing said lightly: ‘As you said yourself, time is short; better we go straight to the point.’ So saying, he let his gaze drift toward the Son of Heaven.

‘Enough! Whatever you have to say, say it to Us directly.’

The Son of Heaven, who had but now suffered the grief of losing a son, spoke at last. His brows were knit tight, yet his back was held bolt upright. Beside him a plump old eunuch, seeing it, dropped to his knees with a thud, and heedless of the Pifu’s threats, fell to weeping aloud. That weeping was as a signal: all the guests went down on their knees together in a rush. That these ruffians should have driven the Son of Heaven to such a pass—there was not one among the assembled ministers whose heart was not full of fear and trembling, and of unbearable shame.

The Pifu warily held their stout crossbows level: whoever dared put himself forward would take a bolt full in the head.

‘So Your Majesty has spoken at last.’ Xiao Gui’s look was between a smile and none.

When a moment ago they had burst in upon the seventh floor, the banquet hall had been all confusion, with wailing and howling on every hand; only this Son of Heaven had remained upon the imperial seat, refusing to stoop to remove himself. Even held hostage by the Pifu, he had not spoken a single word, keeping up a lofty, high-set disdain, laboring to preserve the last shred of his dignity.

The death of Prince Yong had made this veneer of reserve impossible to keep up any longer.

‘Who, then, are you?’ The Son of Heaven let his two broad sleeves of russet-yellow hang down at his sides, and inclined his head a little, as though putting a question to some minister of his court.

Ringed about by firelight, Xiao Gui closed his eyes in deep contentment, seeming to savor the sweetness of the moment. He put out a finger and tapped his own brow: ‘We are old soldiers of the Eighth Company of the Protectorate of the Western Regions. If Your Majesty’s memory does not fail him, nine years ago you handed down a decree in commendation of us.’

There was a faint blankness in the Son of Heaven’s eyes; plainly he remembered nothing of it at all. Xiao Gui went on: ‘Nine years ago, when Suluk Khagan violated the border and laid siege to Balhuan, the Eighth Company held the beacon-fort in a desperate defense above twenty days; at the last but three men were left alive, and of these two are present here today. Your Majesty, with ten thousand affairs of state to attend to each day, would naturally not keep so small a matter in mind.’

The Son of Heaven betrayed nothing: ‘Do you lay it to Our charge that We wore out Our armies in wanton warfare? Or that the reckoning of merit was unjust?’

‘No, no.’ Xiao Gui wagged a finger. ‘We were most honored to have had our part in it, to render Your Majesty our loyal service. To guard the border and defend the realm was but our bounden duty. With the rewards the court handed down we were well content. We are come here today not for those stale old matters, but for a remonstrance-in-arms.’

‘A remonstrance-in-arms?’ The Son of Heaven’s brows gave a twitch; he all but wished to laugh. Where under heaven was there ever such a ‘remonstrance-in-arms’ as this?

‘Your Majesty is the true dragon; we are but lowly aphids. Yet there are times when an aphid can see more clearly than the true dragon the strength and the weakness of these palace halls.’

He pointed offhand at one of the aphids: ‘This man is called Wu Guiyi, a man of Hejian. Year upon year of drought fell on his home, yet the rent and the labor-levy were not lessened, and his wife and children were scattered abroad. He left the camp and went home to his village, only to be falsely charged with desertion.’ Then he pointed at another of the aphids: ‘This one is called Mo Wa’er, a Hu of mixed blood from Jincheng. He took out a loan to raise a fine breed of trained camels, only to have the greater part of them driven off by an imperial commissioner; unable to repay the loan, he had no way but to give his own body in pledge, and all but died in prison of hardship.

‘Oh, and there is this one, Suo Fahui, a man of Henan County. He has some little connection with the Lantern Festival, too. Your Majesty loves the bustle and gaiety of the lantern fairs, and so the prefectures and counties of every region vie with one another to keep artisans and performers at great expense, contending for the name of the lantern-float red tally. Behind every troupe of lantern-floats that comes up to the capital stand some scores of others held in reserve, and the cost of it all falls upon the common folk of the county. Suo Fahui was by trade a skilled cartwright; drafted for corvée-labor by the authorities, worn out with toil, he was all but brought to ruin.’

At these words the whole company could not help but turn their heads together, fixing their gaze upon a girl in the crowd. She was this year’s holder of the lantern-float red tally; hearing that fierce man make mention of her, she could not help a change of countenance, and shrank back.

Happily, Xiao Gui did not press too hard upon this theme.

‘Every aphid upon this tower was once a veteran of the army, and behind each of them lies a story. Small stories, that would not catch the eye of you great officials, yet every one of them true and real. Such fates as these, scattered abroad among the people, are more numerous still, I fear. These little holes, gnawed out one by one by the aphids, stand plain to see upon the great roof-beams of Great Tang.’

‘And so you mean to have your revenge?’

‘How was it that Cao Gui put it? “Those who eat meat are of mean discernment, and cannot lay far-sighted plans.” Your Majesty, our Great Tang is already sick. To look at, it is all lush branches and thronging blossoms, a fair sight of a flourishing age—but the root of it is already rotten, rotten clean through, gnawed hollow; before our eyes it is about to come crashing down, just like this Qinzheng Wuben Tower. It wants a strong dose of fierce fire and fresh blood, to wake the world to its senses.’

It was likely many years since the Son of Heaven had last heard words so grating to the ear; he said in a heavy voice: ‘What is it, then, that you want?’

Xiao Gui said, weighing out each word: ‘Save by the burning of a great city, there is no way to startle the myriad multitude; save by the fall of the true dragon, there is no way to warn the black-haired people. What your humble servant desires is this: to have Your Majesty’s one life, here before a million commonfolk of this city of Chang’an.’

Though the company had long had a foreboding of what the Pifu were about, to hear him declare it so openly and brazenly still raised a stir among them.

The Son of Heaven betrayed nothing, but spread his two arms wide: ‘Our life is here. If you would have it, come and take it yourself. If such be the will of Heaven, We shall not flinch a step.’

But unlooked-for, Xiao Gui laughed again: ‘Your Majesty need not be in such haste. We aphids have laid our plan in two tiers. Were the lantern-tower able to blow Your Majesty to death before the eyes of the multitude, nothing could be better. But should Heaven not favor virtue, and the thing fall short of full success, then your humble servant would come up the tower in his own person to seek audience—and by that stage, why, it is best of all that Your Majesty should be living.’

He was smiling all the while, yet the malice in that smile grew ever thicker.

‘It is our hope that Your Majesty will for a time remove your dragon-steps, and deign to stoop so low as to come with your humble servant and look upon the world beyond Chang’an—to see with your own eyes the world of the aphids and the mole-crickets.’

Cries of shock and anger welled up from the crowd. This ruffian had gall enough and to spare: he meant to carry the Son of Heaven off out of the capital, and parade him from region to region besides, to shame him in public. Not even Emperor Yang of Sui had ever suffered such an indignity. Were it truly to come about, the face of Great Tang would be utterly and wholly lost—a thing more dreadful even than the Son of Heaven’s being killed on the spot.

At this demand the Son of Heaven’s face changed at last: ‘You may kill Us, but never think that We will go with you.’

Xiao Gui raised a hand, and with a whisk the Pifu lifted their short crossbows and leveled them at the throng of guests: ‘Has Your Majesty no pity, then, for these ministers and guests of his?’

The Son of Heaven said, his face set grim: ‘If the assembled ministers die for their honor, they may share in the sacrifices at the imperial tombs.’ His meaning was plain enough: though every man in the tower this day should die to the last, he would never depart in the train of these aphids.

‘When the sovereign is troubled, the minister toils; when the sovereign is shamed, the minister dies!’

A high, ringing voice rose from among the guests—it was a phrase out of the Discourses of Yue. That one cry, in an instant, kindled the anger that despair had held down in the guests. They fell to shouting one and all, and the crowd surged.

The twenty-odd aphids hastily raised their crossbows to hold them down, but the tumult only grew the fiercer; the guests seemed no longer to fear the threat of death. They had grasped at last that if the Son of Heaven were carried off or killed here, not one of them, likely, would come to any good end. Shouting and thronging close together, with numberless feet trampling over porcelain dishes and brocade, they came rushing toward the imperial seat.

Zhang Xiaojing quietly bent his knees, gathering his strength, thinking to wait for the scene to grow yet more disordered and then launch a sudden assault upon Xiao Gui. But at that very moment there came all at once the sound of a crossbow-string loosing, and then the official who had been first to cry out the slogan toppled stiffly over, a crossbow-bolt now standing in his forehead.

Xiao Gui lowered his crossbow, his face full of impatience. The shouting in the great hall fell silent in an instant; the spurting spray of blood brought home to them anew the terror of death. Here had been a great officer of the fourth rank, a man who by a stamp of his foot could set the capital trembling—and he had died just so, died like a dog.

When Prince Yong had fallen from the tower a moment before, they had heard only his scream; but this man had died here, real and undeniable, at their very side, and in a trice they were all struck dumb with awe.

Only one man was the exception.

A figure came rushing all at once upon Xiao Gui, and, while his crossbow was yet unspanned, launched an attack. Caught off his guard, Xiao Gui felt only his head struck by a jade flute. The jade flute shattered at the blow, but Xiao Gui too was left dazed for the space of an instant. The man seized the chance to close with him, and drove a fist into his lower belly.

Not until several finger-snaps later did those in the great hall see clearly that the dark figure was, of all people, the Son of Heaven himself. The aphids round about were struck aghast, and none dared loose a bolt, for fear of hitting their leader by mischance; they could only watch as the two men grappled and twisted together.

The Son of Heaven’s art of combat was of no mean order, and for the moment Xiao Gui was actually beaten down to the losing side.

The years of settled peace had lasted too long, and men seemed to have forgotten that this most exalted of sovereigns had in his youth been a master of the mounted bow, wont to gallop his horse in the chase of hawks and to bring down hares with the flying arrow. In the two palace coups of Tanglong and Xiantian, he had led his picked men in his own person, and fought hand to hand in the field—and only so had come the state of things that held to this day.

Though the Son of Heaven was now past sixty, the foundation of his youth was in him still. Everyone, Xiao Gui included, had taken him for an old man worn out with age; but the fierce temper born in his very bones was not to be lightly quenched by fine wine.

The two of them fought several bouts; Xiao Gui, being after all a seasoned soldier, little by little found his rhythm, and began gradually to turn the tide back. The Son of Heaven was panting hard, and soon enough was a spent bolt at the end of its flight. Xiao Gui was just on the point of delivering a mortal stroke when all of a sudden he stumbled.

The blast a moment ago had shaken the whole banquet hall, and the floor was littered everywhere with wreckage. Xiao Gui’s right foot chanced to come down into a half-open black-lacquered food-box, and his whole body lurched askew. The Son of Heaven spied out this one chance in a thousand, snatched up from the diexie belt at his waist a small and handy ivory-handled folding knife, and drove it savagely into Xiao Gui’s right eye.

Xiao Gui let out an agonized scream and fell back at speed. The Son of Heaven had struck too hastily, and had not even had time to loosen the tie-cord from the diexie belt, so that Xiao Gui, pulling against it, dragged him lurching forward. The two of them together overturned the imperial seat, and rolled down along the slope; the tongtian crown and the crossbow both went tumbling to the floor.

Zhang Xiaojing saw that his chance had come, and sprang up to lay hold of Xiao Gui. But the Son of Heaven had already scrambled to his feet; seeing him draw near, he was doubly wary, and caught up a spittoon and flung it at him. Zhang Xiaojing dodged it and said hastily, low-voiced: ‘Majesty, I am come to help you!’ But the Son of Heaven’s answer was to fling next a meat-carving fork at him. The floor being in any case all in a jumble, there was no want of things to snatch up.

The Son of Heaven was not to be blamed for it: Zhang Xiaojing had first struck Chen Xuanli senseless, and then killed Prince Yong, so that no one, likely, would take him for one of their own—only for a man come to help Xiao Gui.

Had Zhang Xiaojing been at the height of his powers, ten Sons of Heaven would have been no trouble to him. But he was now too weakened, his speed of reaction plainly fallen off, and he could only dodge and close the distance by turns. Zhang Xiaojing steeled his heart: if it came to the worst, he would have to knock the Son of Heaven senseless first.

Even as he thought it, that old eunuch at the side suddenly flung his arms wide and clung with all his might to Zhang Xiaojing’s legs. Zhang Xiaojing tried to pull free, but could by no means struggle loose. The Son of Heaven seized the chance to rush at him, and with that ivory-handled folding knife stabbed him in the throat.

The point of the knife had already broken the thin outer layer of skin; half a measure more of force, and it would slay this arch-leader who had assailed the palace city.

But before the Son of Heaven could put out his force, there rang through the great hall a woman’s scream. His face changed abruptly, his wrist trembled, and the knife, after all, did not go home.

Xiao Gui stood ten-odd paces off, his right eye streaming with blood, his left hand throttling savagely the slender neck of a woman clad in the robe of a female Daoist.

‘Taizhen!!!’ the Son of Heaven cried out in alarm.

***

Li Bi stood before the corpse of Xu Bin, and for a long while could find no word to say.

Xu Bin was a treasure he had picked up in the Ministry of Revenue. When he was setting up the Jing’an Bureau, he had drawn men from every quarter. Many of the offices had rendered mere lip-service, sending over only such clerks as were little regarded in the ordinary way, whose tempers and whose competence alike were painful past bearing. In a great rage Li Bi had asked for He Zhizhang’s warrant-tablet, and, standing on no ceremony, had sent the whole lot back.

The one man he kept was none other than Xu Bin, whom the Ministry of Revenue had sent.

The man was well on in years, yet knew nothing whatever of the ways of officialdom, and had fared very ill in the Ministry of Revenue—else he would never have been sent over. Li Bi found in him one merit: a prodigious memory. Whatever he had once read, and numbers above all, he never forgot at a glance. Just such a talent could serve as the very heart of the art of the Great Archive.

And so, under Li Bi’s careful fostering, Xu Bin soon became a member of no small weight in the Jing’an Bureau. The man was no ready speaker, but his bearing was most diligent; the records of all Chang’an were packed away in his head, to be called up at any moment, far faster than the searching of shelf and stack. That the Jing’an Bureau had the powers it had this day was bound up inseparably with Xu Bin. Li Bi knew that Xu Bin had an aged mother and young children at home, and had promised him with his own lips that, once this affair was over, he would have him doff the commoner’s coarse cloth and be raised to office.

But now all this had turned to drifting cloud.

Xu Bin now lay upon the couch, his head bent at a strange angle, his two eyes half-closed. He had been too timid a soul: even dying so wronged a death, he would not glare at any man, but had chosen rather to bow his head and close his eyes.

Li Bi closed his eyes; the wings of his nose gave a twitch, and he drew back into his nostrils the fluid that had been welling toward his eye-sockets, with a snuffling sound, and a faint drowning ache. He and Xu Bin had been no more than superior and subordinate, had not even been friends; yet he felt a grief beyond the common. And it was not for Xu Bin alone, but for all who had laid down their lives this day.

Mastering the turmoil within him, Li Bi reached out and set Xu Bin’s head straight, then folded his two hands crosswise upon his lower belly, so that he seemed as one in deep sleep. ‘I am sorry...’ Li Bi murmured within his heart.

He gently drew up the coverlet, meaning to cover Xu Bin’s face; but halfway through the motion his arm froze all at once. Li Bi’s eyes went wide: there was something strange about Xu Bin’s fingers. Leaning closer to look, he found Xu Bin’s fingernails all packed with pale-gray wall-plaster.

The Jingzhao Prefecture had charge of the capital’s confidential business, and so its walls were kept whitewashed; only, as the years of plastering wore on, the coating would turn to a faint gray render. Li Bi hastened round to the other side of the couch, and by the candlelight saw, on the side next the wall, a few scratch-marks scraped out by fingernails.

Li Bi had asked before, and learned that Xu Bin, his mind not yet wholly clear and his body unable to move, could still hold simple converse. So the likeliest thing was that the murderer had come in behind the screen and talked with Xu Bin. During their talk Xu Bin had sensed that something was amiss; but unable to raise the alarm or to flee, he could only leave marks with his fingernails, in secret, upon the wall—and had then been silenced for good.

Neither the Türk Wolf Guards nor the Pifu had any reason to kill Xu Bin. It seemed the murderer was someone Xu Bin knew—and, like as not, was that very mole who had all this while gone uncaught.

Li Bi crouched down and held the candlestand close against the wall. The wall of the office was very thick, the scratches too shallow, and the strokes hastily scrawled. Li Bi looked a long while, and could just make out that they were two characters: the first was the character si, ‘four’; the second seemed not to have been finished, and he could barely tell that it was the character ri, ‘sun’ or ‘day.’

Si-ri—‘the fourth day’? The fourth day of the first month? Or the fourth of some month last year? Had something happened on that day, perhaps, that might call the murderer to mind? Yet why had he not simply written the murderer’s name outright—would that not have been the readier way?

Numberless questions circled in his mind. Li Bi rose abruptly to his feet, and set the candlestand gently down to one side.

He withdrew from behind the screen, at once summoned the persons concerned, and issued two orders: ‘Take into custody the soldiers keeping guard here, and at the same time seal every gateway great and small, forbidding any man to come or go from the Jingzhao Prefecture.’ He paused a moment, found the second order not quite reasonable, and so amended it to: ‘Forbid any officer formerly of the Jing’an Bureau to come or go from the Jingzhao Prefecture.’

That mole must certainly have been, to begin with, a man of the Jing’an Bureau; and so the others need not fall under suspicion.

These two orders were swiftly carried out. The two soldiers who had guarded the screen were held fast by their own comrades and marched off to a quiet room to await questioning. At the same time more soldiers went to the entrances within and without the Jingzhao Prefecture, and took the place of the former guards.

This was a wholly necessary measure; the mole’s power to work havoc was all too great, and Li Bi had no wish, while he went about his business, to have a knife held all the while against his back. The Jingzhao Prefecture was now become a great jar into which not a drop could leak; as for how to fish the turtle up out of the water, that would be the test of his craft.

The questioning of the guard-soldiers went quickly forward. The two luckless troopers, at the mere news that Xu Bin had been killed, went green with fright, and made all haste to shake out everything they knew. By their account, in this while a great many people had gone in behind the screen—physicians, page-boys, and officials of every sort—and no record had been kept of them.

Li Bi asked further: who, in the end, had given them the order to guard Xu Bin?

The soldiers answered that the order had come from Yuan Zai, that Xu Bin was to be treated as an important suspect.

‘Who is Yuan Zai? By what authority does he do such a thing?’ Li Bi demanded sharply. One Ji Wen had been enough—how came there now to be a Yuan Zai sprung up as well? A recorder explained Yuan Zai’s antecedents in a low voice.

‘Where is he?’

‘A few shichen ago he went out with a party of Lüben Guards soldiers, and is not yet back.’

Li Bi gave a cold snort. Vexed as Yuan Zai’s doings made him, they at least cleared the man of any suspicion of being the mole.

‘Why should Yuan Zai have fixed on Xu Bin as a suspect? On what grounds?’ Li Bi asked.

The soldiers could not answer this. In the end it was Adjutant Zhao who stepped forward to reply. Short as his time here had been, he had felt out the inner truth of things pretty clearly: ‘Recorder Xu was found fainted in the rear garden. After the attack he was discovered and brought to the Jingzhao Prefecture for treatment. The Pifu stole into the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau by way of the water-channel from that very rear garden. Evaluator Yuan holds that it was Recorder Xu who opened the water-conduit and let the Pifu in, and then feigned a faint to escape suspicion.’

Li Bi fell silent, his long slender fingers tapping the tabletop. What Yuan Zai said was not wholly without reason. Xu Bin was of course no mole; but he must have chanced upon the very moment when the mole let the Pifu into the Jing’an Bureau. The mole had silenced him, it might be, from fear that Xu Bin had seen his face.

Thought over with care, this was the most reasonable conjecture of all.

This mole was cruel and bold indeed. At the mere thought that a venomous snake, its forked tongue flickering, lay coiled there at his very side, Li Bi could not help a chill along his spine. He rose to his feet, left a recorder to carry on the questioning, and set the guards to write down everyone who had come near Xu Bin, and then to match the list against the members of the Jing’an Bureau.

There was all too much for Li Bi to do next; he could not spend all his time here.

He walked out of the interrogation room, his hands clasped behind him, and let out a faint sigh. Now, at last, the weak point of the Jing’an Bureau was laid bare. It was a newly established office, wanting in depth, that had merely been set by main force above the two counties of the Jingzhao Prefecture, the Jinwu Guard, the patrol commissioners, and the gate guards. So long as some man of power sat in command above, the whole Jing’an Bureau moved like an arm moving its own fingers; but once disorder broke out, its men of talent were stretched thin as a threadbare coat.

‘Besides Xu Bin, whom else has Yuan Zai branded a mole?’ Li Bi asked of a sudden.

‘There is one Yao Runeng: he passed signals to the enemy up on the great watchtower, and was overpowered for it, and is now shut up in the gaol of the Jingzhao Prefecture.’ So answered Adjutant Zhao, standing by, with all deference. Fallen from favor in the Right Xiao Guard, he was hoping to get a grip on some other stout leg to lean on.

‘He? Passing word to the enemy?’

‘The particulars are none too clear, but it should have been to a man named Zhang Xiaojing that he was passing word.’ As Adjutant Zhao brought out the name, his face grew a little uneasy.

Li Bi’s face grew stern; his steps quickened somewhat, and he urged his attendants to left and right in a loud voice: ‘Take me there at once—Yao Runeng very likely knows who the mole is...’

***

In the instant that Xiao Gui seized that female Daoist as hostage, everyone, Zhang Xiaojing among them, let out a breath of relief.

So long as the Son of Heaven was free of the Pifu’s threat, the gravest danger was gone. Much as this woman of the Dao enjoyed the sovereign’s favor, in a pass like this her life plainly could not be weighed against the Son of Heaven’s; if she died, she died, and no one would think it a pity.

Only one man was the exception.

This time, once again, it was the Son of Heaven.

The Son of Heaven had by now got the better of Zhang Xiaojing, and with one stroke could have killed him. But at the sight of Taizhen held hostage by Xiao Gui, his movement stopped on the instant, and his eyes betrayed an extremity of terror.

‘You are not to harm her!’ the Son of Heaven roared in fury. Not even when Prince Yong had been pushed from the tower a moment ago had he shown such anger.

‘First let my brother go!’ Xiao Gui bellowed. His eye being hurt, the strength of his grip was ill-controlled, and Taizhen’s neck he throttled ever tighter, so that her breath came harder and harder; her fair pale cheeks flushed a deep red, and her full breast heaved up and down.

Without a word the Son of Heaven drew back the ivory-handled folding knife. The old man was panting too, after the fight just past; only the keen light in his eyes had not faded.

Zhang Xiaojing had not looked for the Son of Heaven to yield thus for the sake of a mere Daoist woman; but he had no strength left to show his surprise. His two knees simply gave way, and he sank down limp upon the ground, the muscles of his limbs beginning to cramp violently. The fierce struggle just past had drained the last of his strength.

‘Your Majesty—come here!’ Xiao Gui commanded, his grip still clamped on the woman’s neck.

‘Release Taizhen first, and I will go with you,’ said the Son of Heaven.

‘Your servant begs your pardon, but that command he cannot obey.’ Xiao Gui’s hand pressed a few degrees harder, and Taizhen’s soft body now grew softer yet.

The Son of Heaven did not hesitate the least degree, but with a shake of his robe-sleeves stepped forward. Two other aphids sprang over, kicked aside the old eunuch who tried to bar the way, and once more had the Son of Heaven fast in their hands. Another man raised Zhang Xiaojing up and came toward them as well.

Xiao Gui said with a savage grin: ‘Had I known Your Majesty was so tender-hearted a lover, why should I have wasted so much breath just now!’ But the Son of Heaven did not so much as look at him; he gazed anxiously at Taizhen, his eyes full of aching tenderness.

Xiao Gui loosened his hand a little, and Taizhen let out a long, drawn breath, her face streaming with tears.

The guests stood rooted where they were, feeling that all that hot-blooded outcry of a moment since—‘when the sovereign is shamed, the minister dies’—had turned into one great joke. That the Son of Heaven, for the sake of a woman, for the sake of a mere woman, should throw away so fine a chance of turning the tables—was it not too absurd? At the thought, no few of them privately grumbled in their hearts: this woman the Son of Heaven had snatched from the hands of his own son, and from a relation so preposterous it was no wonder if some further absurdity should spring.

The black smoke about the Qinzheng Wuben Tower spread ever thicker; after the lantern-tower’s fall, the fire had by degrees passed to the main body of the tower itself. From without could faintly be heard the clash of arms and armor and the sound of shouting: the relief of the imperial guards must by now be no great way off.

Xiao Gui knew the hour was near enough come. He gave a whistle, and the aphids, receiving their order, at once fell busily to work. First they dragged the Son of Heaven and Taizhen, and Zhang Xiaojing too, who had scarcely any strength left, to beneath the bronze crane in the southwest corner of the great hall; then, as one drives a flock of sheep, they herded the guests toward the center of the hall.

Just then Chen Xuanli came slowly round where he lay on the floor. His hands were bound behind him, but his mouth had not been stopped. Lifting his head, he cried aloud: ‘The palace guards are even now hastening in from every side; though you hold His Majesty hostage, where can you hope to flee?’

Xiao Gui cast a glance at Chen Xuanli, tore off offhand a strip of thin gauze from a cloud-screen, and wiped away the blood brimming from his eye-socket—yet the smile on his face did not change: ‘The General need not trouble himself over that! The aphids go up to heaven and down to earth; there is no crevice they cannot enter.’

The aphids had great faith in their leader, and showed not a trace of worry, but with torch and crossbow-bolt drove the guests, in perfect order, to gather at the center. The guests grasped that this was likely to make it easy to burn them all at one go; but with kindling-oil on their persons and bows and crossbows trained on them from without, none dared offer resistance.

All at once an envoy of some country or other, unable to bear the terror, gave a scream and, reckless of all, took to his heels and ran wildly outward. That aphid called Suo Fahui, without expression, lifted up a burning candlestick and flung it after him. A ball of candle-flame drew a precise curve through the air and struck the envoy full on, turning him in an instant into a man of fire. The man of fire shrieked piteously, his steps never halting, and rushed all the way to the floor’s edge, broke through the balustrade, and fell from the tower...

This ghastly little episode left a deep impression on the other guests, and they could only go on moving obediently toward the middle of the hall. The one act of resistance left them was to shift their steps a little more slowly.

Xiao Gui paid these things no further heed, but strolled at his ease to beneath the bronze crane in the southwest corner, where the Son of Heaven, Taizhen, Zhang Xiaojing, and the rest all stood.

Xiao Gui wound that blood-soaked strip of gauze about his hand, then slipped it over his head, to shut off the gore before his eyes. When he had bound it up properly, he gave Zhang Xiaojing a smile: ‘Big-Head, this time the two of us are alike.’ Zhang Xiaojing, his back against the bronze crane, was faint in every limb, and could only manage a slight nod.

At his side the Son of Heaven held Taizhen in his arms, his face all despair and solemnity—Zhang Xiaojing had even the illusion that this emperor seemed moved by his own choice, wholly drunk upon this act of resolute and mournful tragedy. It was rumored that he was besotted with watching plays in the Pear Garden; this blurring of the false and the true likely had its source in that.

Zhang Xiaojing was not so highly strung as the Son of Heaven. Weak past all telling as his body was, his mind was reckoning without cease what was to be done next.

The bad news was that he had never found a chance to subdue Xiao Gui or to rescue the Son of Heaven, and the chances to come were slighter still; the good news was that Xiao Gui still, to this hour, took him for one of his own, and his true allegiance had not been exposed.

The only plan for the present was to make use of this trust of Xiao Gui’s, and go on following along with them, taking it one step at a time.

But he was curious: how did Xiao Gui mean to withdraw? This was the seventh-floor Star-Plucking Hall, far too high above the ground to leap down from. And both stairways within the tower were unusable; even could they be used, one must face a countless host of imperial guards—a dead end, plain and simple.

Xiao Gui seemed to read Zhang Xiaojing’s misgiving, and put out a finger and wagged it: ‘Do you remember how Commandant Gan taught us in the Western Regions? In all things, forethought brings success, and want of forethought brings ruin. Beyond a Plan A, one must always have a Plan B besides. His teaching is not to be forgotten for a moment.’

At this, Xiao Gui turned his head and shouted toward the hall: ‘Faster, now—the enemy will be here any moment!’

At this urging the aphids one and all quickened their pace, kicking and beating those guests who deliberately dragged their feet, and driving them toward the middle of the hall. Smeared all over with oil, the crowd stumbled and lurched, their weeping and their cursing running together into one. The spot where they were gathered in the hall was the very mouth of the sky-reaching stair that led up all the way from the bottom floor—and the road the relief must of necessity take.

By now men at the side had their torches ready, to set the fire the moment the gathering was complete. These, a hundred and more noble-born human torches, would be enough to slow the pace of the relief, so that the aphids might withdraw at their leisure—if indeed there were truly any such line of retreat at all.

The guests were at last driven, every one, to the neighborhood of the sky-reaching stair, and ringed into a circle of despair. On the face of every aphid nearby rose a smile of excitement. All of them had known humiliation and oppression, and today at last it would be repaid—and in the most satisfying way of all.

As if by common accord, the aphids drew off to a good distance, lifted their torches or candles, and made ready to fling them all at once, sharing together in the grand event. For, mark, it was not every commoner who had the chance to burn so many high officials and famous princes to death at a stroke.

Just then the whole floor gave off a strange sound. It was fine-drawn and low, coming from none knew where, yet seeming to be everywhere at once. The aphids with the fire in their hands looked at one another blankly, not knowing whence the sound came.

Xiao Gui beside the bronze crane, and the Son of Heaven and Taizhen too, wore looks of astonishment, and searched all about for the source of the sound. Only Zhang Xiaojing kept his eyes shut; a thread of breath came slowly out from his slackened lungs, and his body edged quietly a few steps in Xiao Gui’s direction.

The sound held on a while, then began to spread from below upward. Fine dust came drifting down from the ceiling to settle on the tips of men’s noses. Every one of them felt that the costly cypress-veneered floor beneath their feet seemed to be faintly trembling, as in an earthquake.

Before long the floor and wall-corners on all four sides of the seventh floor gave out, all together, a clear creaking and cracking, as though into the music of a konghou there had of a sudden broken a high, shrill flute. Then noise upon noise joined in one after another, until it became a vast and utterly disordered chorus.

Before the company could react, the sudden upheaval came.

The floor of the seventh-floor hall first shuddered, then tore violently away from the walls on all four sides—first on one side, then ripping loose on two more—so that the whole floor tilted at one end and sank sharply downward, crashing all at once down into the sixth floor. This great movement wrenched the main structure to pieces; in a trice walls toppled and pillars broke, smoke and dust flew up, and guests, aphids, and banquet furnishings alike, all who stood in the hall, were thrown into utter confusion and went tumbling down into the sixth floor. The whole Star-Plucking Hall was thereby emptied, and even the roof above hung tottering on the verge of collapse.

The one thing spared was the ring of walkway round the Star-Plucking Hall, which bore up the main pillars at the four corners and was no part of the floor. The bronze crane chanced to stand at one corner of the southwest walkway. Seen from where the bronze crane stood, the center of the seventh floor had all at once caved into a great pit, the flooring sunk away, leaving a black and gaping hole ghastly to behold.

With that shock the people near the bronze crane were all sent staggering this way and that. In the reeling, Zhang Xiaojing suddenly altered his direction a little, and, his shoulder seeming drawn along by the tremor, brushed as if by chance against Xiao Gui’s back. Caught unprepared, Xiao Gui lurched and fell toward the edge of the hole.

But Xiao Gui’s reactions were quick indeed: in the instant his body toppled, he shot out a hand and caught fast at Taizhen’s plain black waist-sash. Taizhen gave a scream, and, hauled by him, was on the point of pitching out too. Luckily the Son of Heaven reacted swiftly, threw his arms about Taizhen, and pulled back with all his might. With this moment’s respite, Xiao Gui righted himself, got one hand on the broken edge of the floor, and several aphids hurried forward and, with many hands, hauled him up.

Zhang Xiaojing sighed inwardly: this Son of Heaven set great store by his feelings indeed; had he not held her back just then, Xiao Gui and Taizhen would both have fallen, and the whole situation would have been turned around. Having missed this last chance in a thousand years, there would likely be no chance left at all. He shook his head, and waited for Xiao Gui to come and call him to account.

But Xiao Gui did not suspect Zhang Xiaojing’s intent; the tremor just now had been too sudden, after all, and it was no wonder if a man staggered and lurched in any direction. He glared in a fury at the Son of Heaven: ‘What is the meaning of this?’

This unlooked-for turn had all but buried the greater part of the aphids and guests. Though there were six zhang between the floor of the seventh story and the sixth, yet, unless one’s luck were very bad, the fall would not kill. But the great force of the relief had by now climbed the tower, and there was no chance of leaving the aphids the leisure to set their fire.

His plan to burn the hundred officials to death had, in fact, already failed.

‘What is the meaning of this?’ Xiao Gui bellowed once again, blood seeping from his wounded eye through the gauze.

The Son of Heaven held Taizhen tight and shook his head. His expression, of all things, was even a shade more indignant than Xiao Gui’s. This was the Qinzheng Wuben Tower; from the twentieth year of Kaiyuan onward he had feasted here beyond all counting, yet had never known there was so grave a structural flaw in it. This—was this not rank treason itself?!

The only one who knew what had happened was Zhang Xiaojing.

The structure of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was wholly unlike that of the other palace halls. It was a tall timber building raised upon a stone rampart; that one might survey the prospect on every side, it could not, like an ordinary many-storied pavilion, be held up by great pillars and crossing rafters. The third-floor Wind-Wooing Hall and the seventh-floor Star-Plucking Hall in particular were open and unscreened, with the wind coming from all four sides; had there been a ring of standing gallery-pillars, it would have quite spoiled the view.

To make sure of both the view and safety at once, the Ministry of Works had sought out able men far and wide, and called in the two masters Mao Shun and Chao Fen to solve the difficulty; in the end it was Mao Shun’s conception that prevailed.

He pointed out that the crux lay in how to lessen the crushing weight of the upper four stories and the eaves-roof. By Mao Shun’s plan, from the fifth story up, the floor of each story was joined into a single whole by mortise-and-tenon work, resting not upon the four corner-pillars of the hall, but passing its load downward by means of close-set bracket-sets and attached turning-beams. In other words, it came to building within the Qinzheng Wuben Tower a separate load-bearing structure of the floors themselves.

In this way the main pillars bore no great load, and their number could be reduced; while at the same time the floor of each story had its own reliable and separate support, with no fear of collapse. Mao Shun had cunningly hidden this system of separate supports within the ornament of the stories, so that it jarred not at all, and a layman could never make it out. Mao Shun had even given it a name: ‘the tower within the tower.’

Chao Fen greatly admired it. Yet with his professional eye he pointed out one flaw in the design. Should anyone set out of set purpose to wreck it, he need not touch the main frame at all; he had only to destroy the close-set bracket-sets and attached turning-beams at a few key junctures, and the floors would then be unable to bear their own weight, and would collapse story upon story.

But the Ministry of Works thought little of this—who would be so bold as to come and pull a tower down at the Son of Heaven’s very feet? And so they appointed Mao Shun chief master-builder, in overall charge of the construction. When the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was finished, its broad prospect and its open, airy inner halls won it great favor with the Son of Heaven. Mao Shun’s worth rose with the tide thereby, and it laid the foundation for his winning, in time to come, the commission to build the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower...

Before Zhang Xiaojing had left, Chao Fen had told him too of this hidden danger. A while ago, down below in the tower, Zhang Xiaojing had marked that those several close-set bracket-sets and attached turning-beams outside the corner of the third-floor hall had all been damaged in greater or lesser degree. And so he had charged Tanqi to go and rally a band of surviving menials, and make ready to destroy all the ‘tower within the tower’ junctures between the third and sixth floors.

His strength was failing, but his experience remained, and he knew that if the Star-Plucking Hall fell into a deadlock, no single man’s strength could break it open. This plan of wrecking the ‘tower within the tower’ was the last means he could bring to bear once he found the thing past mending. To break craft by main force, to bring down the floor and make a great confusion, and so to seize advantage amid the chaos—that was the way of it.

As to whether it might cause the death or injury of the Son of Heaven and the assembled ministers, that Zhang Xiaojing had no way to guard against so completely.

His deliberately flinging Prince Yong down from the broken bridge had been the very key step of this plan. Below the broken bridge, at the sixth-floor spread of eaves, there jutted up aslant a long-necked beast’s head, with bulging eyes and a broad mouth, a scaled body and flying wings, called the makara. The spot from which Zhang Xiaojing pushed Prince Yong off the broken bridge had been carefully reckoned, so that he fell just upon the makara’s head, whence he could slide back down to the sixth floor.

Zhang Xiaojing had sent Prince Yong down the tower to carry word: to tell Tanqi that the situation above was past all saving, and to bid her set to work at once upon the plan agreed beforehand.

To judge by the result, Prince Yong had indeed gone meekly to carry the word, and Tanqi had carried out Zhang Xiaojing’s charge without the least slackness. The pity was that the floor had collapsed a little too slowly. Could it have come even twenty finger-snaps earlier, it would have swept up the whole net of aphids, Xiao Gui among them.

Xiao Gui craned his head out: the whole Star-Plucking Hall had wholly changed its aspect. Where once had been the broad and open banquet-floor, given over to unbridled revelry, there was now a great ragged hole, all gaps and jags. On the sixth floor below, firelight showed dimly, and one could just make out bodies, rubble, splintered timber, and litter heaped up together, with groans rising on every hand.

Xiao Gui apart, the aphids who had survived were no more than five, and every one of them wore a look of thankfulness. Had they but stood a little nearer the middle of the hall a moment ago, they would have met the same fate. Fierce men who feared not death, they were yet not therefore wholly without fear of an accident.

All at once Xiao Gui saw a half-ruined cypress board flung violently up, laying bare the curving handrail of the sky-reaching stair. Soldiers in full armor, stout crossbows in hand, came leaping out one after another from the stairwell. Though the dim light made their livery hard to see, by their vigorous movements they were beyond doubt the imperial guards. The moment they charged up onto the sixth floor, they caught sight of Xiao Gui looking down from the seventh, and seven or eight of them raised their crossbow-bolts high and shot fiercely upward.

Xiao Gui hastily drew his neck back and barely dodged them. Several crossbow-bolts struck the bronze crane, ringing out clear and sharp. But for the moment they had no way of climbing up.

‘Away, quick!’ Xiao Gui gave the order. To pursue now the question of why the floor had fallen was pointless; what mattered was to get these two precious hostages away as fast as might be.

Of the five aphids who had survived at the last, two held the Son of Heaven fast, two secured Taizhen, and one more bore Zhang Xiaojing upon his back. Treading the edge of the ring of walkway that had not yet fallen, they came swiftly to the southwest corner of the seventh floor of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower. Here they climbed over the balustrade and set foot upon the up-tilted black-tiled eaves. The pitch here was no small one, and everyone had to wedge his feet carefully into each ridge of tile to be sure of not sliding off.

This was now on the outer side of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, and set very high. The sky by now had grown deeper still: it was the darkest hour before the dawn. The night wind of those high airs blew keen and cutting, seeming somewhat stronger than the wind of the first half of the night. Clinging to the aphid’s back, Zhang Xiaojing lifted his head and looked out on every side. Great clouds of smoke rose curling up, but were soon torn to shreds by the night wind, and through the rents in the smoke the scene round about could still be taken in at a single sweep.

The city of Chang’an was still brilliant with lamplight at this hour, bright far and near. Yet, set against the gaiety of before, these lights showed a certain flurry of alarm. Zhang Xiaojing marked that the watchtowers, so long fallen silent, seemed to have resumed their working, their dense bean-like purple lamps flashing without cease. He read off a part of the message: it was giving notice to all the wards that the lantern fair was over, and the curfew begun.

‘This response is far too slow, surely,’ Zhang Xiaojing thought, and looked down again nearer at hand.

The upper half of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower had toppled and driven down into the Qinzheng Wuben Tower; the color of the flames burning over all its body traced out this stretch of wreckage into a weird and monstrous shape. In the nearby inner park of the Xingqing Palace, numberless burning fragments still leapt and scattered. The picture was as of a dying fire-dragon that had crashed head-on into a sky-propping pillar, its fiery blood spurting on every side.

And beyond the Xingqing Palace, the broken half of the lantern-tower still blazed fiercely, like a huge torch, lighting up the plaza before the Xingqing Palace. On the plaza lay fallen a great press of people, thick upon thick, covering the whole flagstone ground. By their garb, those who lay there were almost all common folk in white, come to view the lanterns, with here and there among them a few of the Longwu Army in black armor, and lantern-float performers. Numberless figures ran to and fro, and the sound of weeping shook the sky.

At the sight, Zhang Xiaojing’s heart sank. Weakened though the blast of the Que-le Huo-duo had been, it had still wrought grievous death and injury among the lantern-viewing folk. By the eye’s rough measure alone, the dead and hurt might well run to several thousand. Many had come to see the lanterns leading the old and carrying the young by the hand; whole families, it was to be feared, had died here together, cruelly wiped out to the last soul.

Zhang Xiaojing felt only a pent-up grief and anger gathering in his breast; heedless of whether the moment were fitting or no, he spoke: ‘Xiao Gui, do you see it? So many lives, gone—every one of them, because of us.’

Xiao Gui, standing on the straight ridge and gazing off in some one direction, heard Zhang Xiaojing’s sudden question and answered wholly unconcerned: ‘In doing great things, there are always some few sacrifices. So long as it is worth the while, one need not take it too much to heart.’

Zhang Xiaojing said in anger: ‘That is several thousand human lives! Common folk, the same as we are—and just so, they are gone. Have you not the least particle of remorse?’

‘But they served to hold the Longwu Army fast; else how could we so easily have got the Emperor into our hands? So they may be said to have died where they ought.’

‘Human life can never be weighed so!’

‘Human life is weighed exactly so!’ Xiao Gui thrust back at him hard. ‘The price of holding one beacon-fort is three hundred men; the price of beating down one tribe of the grasslands is a thousand; and the price of waking the whole of Great Tang to its senses is under ten thousand men—is that not a fine bargain?’

For the moment Zhang Xiaojing was struck dumb; this arithmetic was too cold-blooded, so cold-blooded that he knew not what to say.

‘It is not to wake Great Tang at all—that is merely a pretext. You wish only to vent your own hatred,’ he said.

Xiao Gui said coldly: ‘Big-Head, I saw it in you back when we held the beacon-fort. Everyone else was set with an iron will to hold to the death, and you alone would have had Wen Wuji and me withdraw first. For all your show of ferocity, at the very bottom you are the softest-hearted of us all. Only I never thought you would prove weak to such a degree as this.’

‘Having with your own hand made so many innocent wronged ghosts, do you not fear to fall into hell when you die?’

Xiao Gui turned his head; his blood-spotted face was full of savagery: ‘Hell? Big-Head, where do you suppose I have been living these nine years past? I have long been ready for it—and you?’ Zhang Xiaojing choked, and was about to say something. Xiao Gui raised a hand and forcibly stopped him: ‘Whatever you have to say, save it till we reach a safe place!’

Only now did Zhang Xiaojing recall that they were still a little band in flight, holding the Son of Heaven hostage. He had a mind to argue on, but at the thought that there was more important work yet to do, he could only hold his tongue and turn his head away, not to look on the ghastly sight upon the ground.

The Son of Heaven, standing on the other side, was also gazing down on the ruin of the Xingqing Palace. His face was heavy as still water, yet he betrayed nothing, and none could tell the mind of this sovereign. Taizhen, for her part, huddled trembling at his side; her one wish now was that the nightmare might end as soon as might be, so that she could go and soak, in perfect bliss, in the Huaqing Pool.

Xiao Gui made a sign, and went carefully forward along the straight ridge of the flying eaves, kicking a few black tiles loose now and then. Those behind followed one by one. Clinging to the aphid’s back, Zhang Xiaojing swayed and rocked, feeling that at any moment he might miss his footing and fall—a most wretched experience. Taizhen did worse than he: the place being so high and so steep, her two feet were weak beneath her, and much of the time she had to be held up by the arms between two aphids. Feeling sure that she must die, she could not help falling into little sobs.

The Son of Heaven halted his steps of a sudden: ‘You have Us in your hands already; she is of no more use to you.’

Xiao Gui said without so much as turning his head: ‘No—it is because we hold her that Your Majesty will do as he is bid.’

‘This is the eaves-roof of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, high in the air on all four sides; you are come to the end of your road,’ the Son of Heaven went on calmly. ‘Give over now, and We can promise you shall leave the capital alive.’

Xiao Gui gave a laugh of contempt. The party stumbled on some way, and came by degrees round to a ridge at the side-corner of a flying eave. Here was set a chiwen of fired clay, standing at the end of the main ridge, with a beast’s head and a fish’s tail, put there to ward off fire and draw down good fortune.

And beside the chiwen there was set, besides, a thing that could by no possibility have been there. At one sight of the object the Son of Heaven’s face changed on the instant.

‘This is our road,’ Xiao Gui said to the Son of Heaven in high triumph.
