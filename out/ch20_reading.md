## Afterword

The foregoing, Midnight in nineteen chapters, was begun in October 1931 and finished on the fifth of December 1932; in the interval, what with illness, with other affairs, with the Shanghai hostilities, and with the heat of the weather, the times I set it down and took it up again came, all told, to as much as eight months; so that even this was a book got together in haste, with no leisure to weigh it word by word.

But the time spent conceiving it was rather longer. At the turn of summer into autumn in 1930, I was afflicted all at once with nervous exhaustion, a stomach ailment, and an eye complaint, so that for a good half-year and more I could neither read nor write, and took to calling on kinsfolk and friends every day, muddling away the time among a set of busy men. It was just then that I formed the ambition of describing the phenomena of Chinese society on a large scale. Later, when my illness had somewhat mended, I often thought of realizing this "ambition." By October 1931 I had put in order the material I had gathered and began to write. So upon the conceiving of this book I may count myself to have spent some pains.

Now that it is written, I still find it, by my own view, full of gaps and oversights. But I am already weary, and the nervous exhaustion shows signs of returning; I have no leisure to reckon further over what is well or ill done, and so let it be published as it stands.

My original plan was a good deal larger than what has now been written. For instance, the economic conditions of the countryside, the ideology of the dwellers in the small market-towns (which is by no means so simple as a certain sort of people imagines it), and the "new Scholars of the Grove" of 1930 — all these I had meant to link into the overall structure of this present book; and again, several of the small structures already depicted in the book I had meant to develop more fully; but because this summer's fierce heat has harmed my health, I could only carelessly cut them away, and so this book has become what it now is — weighted toward the depiction of city life.

I must still thank the honesty of the doctors and the potency of the medicines, which allow me to be here today, still able to prate on!

Mao Dun

December 1932
