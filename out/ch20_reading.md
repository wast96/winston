## Du Yuesheng and the Heng Society

{d} By Guo Lanxin

Du Yuesheng was bound by a thousand threads to the political, financial, and industrial and commercial circles of the Kuomintang period. The Heng Society was an organization of the disciples and students of the House of Du, and Du Yuesheng was its chairman.

The Heng Society was founded in 1932. It was a secret-society organization dressed up in the outer garment of a civic association. The aims proclaimed in its charter were couched in twenty very respectable characters: "Cultivate virtue and pursue one's calling; revere the Way and honor righteousness; keep faith and help one another; serve society; be loyal to the nation." What it was in reality there is a settled verdict on, and no need to dissect it here. By its constitution the general membership meeting was to elect a number of directors and supervisors, and to choose one secretary-general and one or two deputy secretaries-general to handle the society's day-to-day affairs; below them were sections for general affairs, organization, finance, Peking opera, social relations, employment referral, and the like. But this was only the surface. In reality, though the Heng Society had a board of directors and supervisors, and though those directors and supervisors were produced through the forms of election, the candidates were always designated beforehand by Du Yuesheng in person; the general meeting merely read out the list, and everyone, as usual, raised their hands to pass it. The society's power lay neither in the general meeting nor in the board of directors, but in the single person of Du Yuesheng. Every matter of the society was first decided by him, and then put through the board or the general meeting to become "the decision of all."

The Heng Society was an organizational tool at Du Yuesheng's personal disposal. Among the secret-society bodies of that time that operated openly under the guise of an association, it was one of the earliest. Because it set the example of recruiting disciples and students and organizing them into a society, other leading gang figures followed suit one after another, and in Shanghai there appeared Huang Jinrong's Rong Society, Yang Hu's Xingzhong Study Society, Jin Tingsun's Ming Society, Wang Xiaolai's Sheng Society, Zheng Ziliang's Xiayi Society, and so forth. The bosses of the Rong Society, the Xingzhong Study Society and the rest, having from the outset close ties with Du Yuesheng of the Heng Society and drawing members of a similar type, still breathed the same air with one another even though each ran his own show. At times, for the sake of their separate interests, frictions and clashes among them could not be avoided.

The formation of the Heng Society matched Du Yuesheng's personal prestige and standing at the time. Around 1930, Du Yuesheng had already begun to rise beyond the hoodlum stage of life to which he had been confined in the French Concession — dealing in opium, running gambling dens, playing the gangster-idler, squeezing money by haggling — and was developing toward more complex forms. He made use of the chaotic conditions in the Concession, the tangled web of personal relations in Shanghai society, the contradiction that Kuomintang political power could not reach into the Concession, and other circumstances favorable to him, to make contact with every stratum of society on many sides. Thus he fished for a good name and cultivated his strength, and began to run "charitable" undertakings. There were many philanthropists in the Shanghai of that day, but of those truly willing to spend money, most preferred not to show their faces. People with money always feared trouble — as the old saying has it, "a man dreads fame as a pig dreads growing fat" — dreading both kidnapping and extortion. The darkness and crime of the Concession era were known to everyone, and no one then wished to spend money only to bring grief on himself. It was precisely because such a mentality was so widespread that a door was left open for another sort of so-called "philanthropist" to speculate his way to fortune, reaping both fame and profit. Du Yuesheng was at the time one of those suited to taking this road. He would not let the chance slip: he joined the ranks of Shanghai's prominent philanthropists, setting foot in such bodies as the Renji Charitable Hall and the Chinese Red Cross. In 1931 there was a severe and widespread flood at home, and he busied himself for a while in the flood-relief associations of the various provinces and in the Jiangsu Flood Relief Charity Association, thereby winning a change in his reputation. The political figures of the day — Xu Shiying, Wang Zhengting, Guan Jiongzhi and others — together with such men of industry and commerce as Yu Qiaqing, Wen Lanting, Wang Xiaolai, Yuan Lüdeng, Wang Yiting, and Huang Hanzhi, all took part in charitable work and enjoyed prestige; and from then on Du Yuesheng's name became linked with theirs, while advertisements thanking "the great philanthropist Du Yuesheng" appeared from time to time in large type in the newspapers. This was one of the chief reasons Du Yuesheng was able to build up his prestige at the time.

Besides this, another special circumstance now arose that likewise handed him openings for his activities: labor disputes were many, and industrial unrest surged from time to time, so that someone was needed to mediate. Not long after the great flood of 1931, in 1932 the January 28 Battle of Shanghai broke out, and Shanghai's industry and commerce were badly sapped; many factories could not keep up production, giving rise to labor disputes in which the struggle grew very sharp. The staff of the Kuomintang's Shanghai Municipal Party Headquarters and of the Social Affairs Bureau of the municipal government, whose business this was, all felt helpless and could find no way to mediate. Whereupon those among them who were used to frequenting Du Yuesheng's door resorted to the device of "a strange peak thrusting out to the side, a winding path leading to the same goal," and invited Du to come out and smooth things over. By what talent, by what method did Du Yuesheng resolve the disputes that the Party and government organs of the day could find no way to resolve? Told plainly, it was nothing rare: he simply advanced, out of his own pocket for the time being, the difference in the severance pay over which the two sides were at odds, paying it to the labor side so that both ceased their quarrel. Suppose a factory was to close, and the owners would put up only two months' severance while the workers insisted on nothing less than three: he would mediate between them, affirming each side's view in turn. To the owners he would say that, since the factory had reached the stage of closing, it of course had its unavoidable difficulties, and if they were willing to give two months' severance, then two months would do; and at the same time he would tell the workers that, taking their livelihood and unemployment into account, a demand for three months was reasonable too, and that the one month's difference he would in the end take up with the owners and pay himself. In this way the two sides at once settled the figure of the severance he was to pay, and he would then instruct his own counting-house to make out a check for the full amount and pay it accordingly, and the dispute was closed. Seeing him act thus, neither side, whatever else, could well refrain from expressing gratitude, and so large advertisements thanking Du Yuesheng would duly appear in the papers. Sizing up the situation, Du Yuesheng reckoned that acting this way would by no means leave him out of pocket, and later events indeed proved him right. Factory owners with real means, reflecting that to have a third party lay out money to settle their own shutdown dispute was, after all, not something they could square with reason and propriety — and that although this one factory was now closed, they themselves still had to go on making their way in Shanghai's industrial and commercial world, where Du Yuesheng was not a man to be offended lightly — thought it better to seize the chance to draw closer to him; and so they would return in full the portion of the severance Du had paid, and add a gift besides. To be sure, there were also isolated cases where a man truly could not repay the advance; but this did not run counter to Du's wishes, nor was it any loss to him. His fame grew greater, his social connections more numerous, and his avenues to wealth broader still.

Of the labor disputes Du Yuesheng mediated, the two whose waves ran highest and stood out most were, first, the 1930 unrest among the staff and workers of the French Tramway and Electric Light Company demanding better conditions, and second, the affair of the postal workers demanding a wage adjustment. As to the former, the negotiations between the two sides had reached a deadlock and the situation was tending to extremes, with the entire staff preparing for a general strike, and the senior figures specially deputed by the Kuomintang government to mediate had had no effect. Du Yuesheng had the year before been elected by the French Concession's Association of Chinese Ratepayers to serve as a Chinese member of the French Municipal Council, and it was in this capacity that he came forward to mediate. On the one hand he counseled the workers by word of mouth, while advancing some money to damp down the feelings of a portion of them; on the other he gave the Concession authorities his guarantee to prevent the unrest from spreading and causing incidents. In this way he eased the situation. Through this affair he raised his standing in the eyes of the Concession authorities and won goodwill in society as well. The two sides of the postal unrest were unlike those of an ordinary labor dispute: one side was a government organ, the Post Office, and the other its own staff. The highest administrative officer of the government's communications branch, the Minister of Communications, came in person to Shanghai and failed to reach terms; yet the postal workers' union invited the outsider Du Yuesheng to mediate, and an agreement was reached. This could not but be reckoned a striking event at the time, and it lent Du Yuesheng no small help in building up still greater prestige. From then on the men of the Nanjing Kuomintang government raised him higher still; and although there were a few individuals who took offense out of envy at the swelling of Du's power, there was after all nothing they could do about it. How was it that Du Yuesheng could mediate the dispute between the Post Office and the representatives of the postal union and bring it off? Once the source is explained, it is nothing strange: the leaders of the postal union at that time, Lu Jingshi and the rest, were favored protégés of Du Yuesheng, so that with inside and outside working together the problem was of course the more easily solved.

As Du Yuesheng's prestige waxed by the day, those who came to his door to acknowledge him as their "old man" grew more numerous, and at the same time more various and complex in their walks of life, no longer confined to certain earlier strata. By now he had begun to be selective in taking in followers, unwilling to enroll them indiscriminately. So it was that Kuomintang men, functionaries of government organs, people from industry and commerce, from the press, from the film and theater worlds, all came through his door, hoping to make him their backer; and he for his part needed such people to wave the flag and shout for him and to scheme and plot on his behalf, forming the backbone by which he built up his power. Once he had the men, he needed an organization suited to the times — and so the Heng Society appeared.

Du Yuesheng, who had never studied as a boy, had tasted every kind of life in the old society, undergone many trials, and accumulated a measure of shrewdness. By about 1932 he had evidently come to attend to a change in his appearance and manner, feeling that he ought to be a little more modest and courteous toward others. So he got himself up: over his short jacket and trousers he now put on a long gown and a riding jacket. His old crowd of fellow gangsters — men in short trousers and slippers and raw-silk shirts, loud and coarse, striding and barging about — seeing that he had changed his style, likewise took to long gowns and riding jackets and played at looking like men of the upper class; but their training was not up to it, and whenever they were carried away by high spirits their true colors would show through all the same. Little Ah Rong (Rui Qingrong), who had been with Du Yuesheng for years, would sometimes speak of this change and could not help laughing at himself. Du Yuesheng himself declared that, had he not set the example in his own person, this set of friends would never have accepted any restraint upon themselves; and so, even on the hottest day, in the reception room he himself never took off his long gown.

Du Yuesheng understood the relations of mutual exploitation that had bound man to man in the old society, and was therefore artful in turning them to use. He could deal fittingly with each different situation, each different sort of person, each different demand, so that whoever came with a request went away having gained something. He had, besides, one trait: in whatever he faced he could think it through with patience and care, and on any matter of importance or of moment he would be sure to weigh it deliberately from every side, and would not even scruple to seek others' counsel. Among men of his kind he drew unusual profit from this. After middle age he could read ordinary books and papers, but when he took up a brush he could only sign his name.

In the period around 1932, Du Yuesheng's brain-trust men were Chen Qun, Liu Chunpu and the like; later Qian Xinzhi became the policy-maker most closely bound to him. Those regularly in contact with him at the same time included Huang Jinrong, Zhang Xiaolin, Liu Zhilu, Jin Tingsun, Wang Xiaolai, and the inspectors and detective-chiefs of the French Concession police; his secretaries were Yang Du, Qiu Fangbai and Wang Youtang; his attendant bodyguards were Ma Xiangsheng, Ye Zhuoshan, Gao Xinbao, Huang Qingrong, Gu Jiatang and others. No. 266 Rue Wagner (today's Ninghai West Road) was in those days forever thronged with carriages and horses, its guests gathering like clouds and its gate as busy as a marketplace — to all appearances a magnate's mansion.

The name "Heng Society" was chosen by Chen Qun, who was then running the Zhengshi Middle School for Du Yuesheng; drawing on the allusion "unceasing in self-strengthening, constant (heng) as the sun and moon in their courses," he named the society with the character heng, and even wrote, as ghostwriter, an essay that appeared in the inaugural issue of the Heng Society Monthly.

The purpose and function of the Heng Society were to strengthen the ties among the men of the House of Du and to furnish a regular place of assembly, so as to make it easier to concentrate the mustering and direction of them. It was first set up behind Du Yuesheng's residence on Ninghai West Road, in the form of a club, its activities weighted toward the Peking-opera amateurs' clubroom, with gambling facilities as well. As for the center of the society's affairs and the consultation and decision over important matters, these were still conducted in front, at Du Yuesheng's home. A member's standing and role within the society were determined not by how he showed himself in the society's activities but by the degree of importance Du Yuesheng attached to him. For there were many members who, though they had likewise acknowledged him as their "old man," in fact had no regular contact with him and stood in no close relation of interest to him, so that he did not even come to know many of them. Only the core people — those who had rendered him service with results, whom he judged satisfactory and capable, and who came and went constantly at his mansion — could win his trust and esteem; and these were not the same as those who showed themselves busily active in the society. At the same time, the importance Du Yuesheng attached to a person had its span in time: even where certain men stood in a weightier relation of interest to him and were in contact with him from time to time, and had at one stage a place of importance in his mind, at another stage that importance would be lost, and with it the intimacy and the trust. Some men in the House of Du were for a time so favored they turned purple, only to pass later from purple to black, until they were not even allowed through the door, and their old connection was at an end.

The Heng Society had an emblem, round in form, with nineteen stars around the rim woven into a border, and in the center a large sheng, a slanting moon beside it — signifying the name "Yuesheng." The nineteen stars hinted at the number of the society's directors and betokened "the myriad stars paying court to the moon." The first-term directors of the Heng Society were Lu Jingshi, Zhu Xuefan, Tang Shichang, Xu Maotang, Hong Yanbin, Zhang Songjiao, Tang Zuanzhi, Zhu Dongshan, Xu Yefu, Wang Gang, Jiang Zhaoming and others — nineteen in all. Of these, Jiang Zhaoming was the very first disciple Du had taken when he "opened his gate," and was commonly called "Little Brother Jiang," a gambler. Du loved to gamble, and it was in the gambling house that he had recruited Jiang.

In the early days after the Heng Society's founding in 1932, the distribution of its principal members was roughly as follows. On the side of the Kuomintang's Shanghai Municipal Party Headquarters there were Chen Junyi, Wang Manyun, Lin Meixian, Huang Xianggu, Huang Zaoxiong and others; on the side of the municipal government's Social Affairs Bureau there were the section chief Zhang Binghui, Xu Yefu, and the director Wang Gang and others; in the press there were Tang Shichang, Zhao Junhao, Yan Fuzhou and others; in the postal workers' union there were Lu Jingshi, Zhu Xuefan, Fu Dewei, Zhang Kechang and others; in the film world there were Zhang Shichuan, Ren Jinping and others; at the fish market there were Tang Zuanzhi, Zhu Dongshan and others; at the French Tramway Company there was Shao Ziying and others; in the Peking-opera world there were Zhao Peixin, Sun Lanting, Wang Qijun and others; in the transport trade there was Zhou Xiangsheng and others. Besides those named above, there were people in other banks, enterprises, the Association of Chinese Ratepayers and other bodies as well; and in the police stations there were a number of detectives and constables besides — some open, some concealed — each doing his utmost at passing word and keeping in touch and turning the others to account. The ordinary members were of a still more mixed and various sort, spread broadly through every stratum of Shanghai society.

These many people joined the Heng Society not at one and the same time but earlier or later, being taken in continuously and without interruption from as early as after Du Yuesheng's debut down to before his death. All who were members submitted a card and acknowledged Du as their "old man"; but not all who submitted cards and acknowledged him joined the Heng Society, for there were also those who entered the House of Du in secret and never came out into the open — I recall that the legislator Jian Guansan was one of those who did not. Among those who acknowledged Du in H. H. Kung's mansion there was a batch who, for the convenience of their work in their own units, asked not to have their identities exposed, and so never came out into the open either. If one were to sort the members of the Heng Society into grades, they could roughly be divided into three types. First, the disciples of the early period: petty hoodlums, informers, constables, gamblers and the like — not many in number. Second, the sons of local rich households and great merchants, who in the Concession feared kidnapping and feared being bullied, and who at times also wished to profit by the connection; so they sought out the way, sent heavy gifts, and once inside the House of Du had their protection — these too were not very many. Third, those who had some capacity for activity of their own and some avenue for it, but who also met with obstacles and could not have their way, and found it hard to carry through their personal aims and designs; reckoning that Du Yuesheng's social standing and power were the very means by which their own name and fortune might be made — these were the most numerous, and of every sort and description. Shanghai in those days was a world of man eating man, a paradise for adventurers Chinese and foreign; there were people of every kind laying snares to harm others for their own gain, baring their fangs and brandishing their claws, and there were others who, though not necessarily meaning to devour anyone, were nonetheless on tenterhooks guarding against being devoured themselves. All these many people were anxious to find a backer and to bore their way in; and Du Yuesheng, having become the most resounding and influential figure of that time and place, naturally became the object that met their requirements for a backer.

To enter the House of Du one also had to find connections and do some dealing. First one had to find a person who had ties with Du and stood on terms with him to speak the matter over with Du; then, a step further, one still had to entrust some personage at Du's mansion who enjoyed Du's trust to look after the arrangements. In those days the manager of affairs at the Du residence was Wan Molin, who had begun as a small coppersmith from the Gaoqiao countryside and entered Du's mansion as a lowly errand-boy, and who gradually, by waiting on Du Yuesheng with care, won his trust and grew familiar with the formalities of coming in to acknowledge the "old man." In the early period, to come into the House of Du and acknowledge the "old man," Wan was a barrier: though a man had got through the upper layer of connections, he still had to smooth this road of Wan's. Of course, money will make the very devil turn the mill; but with him one always had, at the least, to size things up and arrange them properly. Only from the time of the August 13 war, when Du Yuesheng left Shanghai, were the methods of entry somewhat simplified, the preliminary formalities being transferred to the Heng Society and thence turned over to Du's mansion. For by then Wan Molin was in Shanghai doing "underground work" for the Kuomintang; having, through the good offices of Wu Kaixian, obtained a photograph of Chiang Kai-shek, he was greatly pleased with himself, and later even angled his way to becoming a "National Assembly delegate," so that he was not much inclined to meddle any longer in matters of entering the House of Du, being bent single-mindedly on following Du Yuesheng's old road and climbing upward.

The procedure for entering as a disciple was this: one first used a red card to set out one's own forebears of three generations and to write the words "willing to acknowledge myself your follower and obey your instruction," signed by the introducer and the man himself; then, on the appointed day, having got the gifts ready, one carried them to the Du house to perform the rite. At first one had to kneel and kowtow to Du; later this was changed to three bows, and if many acknowledged him at once, they could bow collectively. After the rite there was a photograph, and no other program besides. In the period when the formalities were handled by the Heng Society's board of directors, any applicant for membership was introduced by two members, filled out an application form, and — after it had been passed by the board, reported to the chairman, and approved by him — was on a set date put through the formalities, and then, accompanied by a standing director, the secretary-general and the like, went to the Du house to perform the rite. The gifts due on entry, apart from anything a man chose to present separately of his own will, were generally converted into a membership fee to be paid in.

As for the society's activities, in the early period there were regularly the spring and autumn general meetings, dress rehearsals, and communal feasts, and every year on the fifteenth of the seventh month gifts were sent to celebrate Du Yuesheng's birthday. When a child was born in the Du household, gifts of congratulation had likewise to be sent. Holding meetings of the board was, of course, a matter of course. During the gatherings there were gambling games as a daily thing, large and small, each finding his partners to make up a table, with the kitchen supplying the meals. There were also members who summoned sing-song girls to their tables and brought them along, giving themselves up to riotous pleasure. Every New Year, Wan Molin made use of the Heng Society to gamble heavily for several days, taking his cut and raking in a tidy sum on the side. Not a few gambling-lovers came to indulge their craving and try their luck, assured that none of the ills of other gambling houses would arise. Later, as people gradually came to feel that such dissipation — with its noise and clamor outside and its very bad name — was too much, in 1936 eating, summoning girls and gambling at the society were abolished, and there remained only the amateurs' clubroom and the library facilities, where one could only drink tea and eat refreshments.

The Heng Society had a publication, the Heng Society Monthly, whose contents, apart from the general reports on society affairs, included photographs and miscellaneous pieces, and on several occasions travel journals. After it had run to fifteen issues it could no longer come out on schedule; and after the outbreak of the war it was changed to an irregular publication, with special issues compiled and printed on the spot when there was a gathering. The society's earlier activities are recorded in a general way in the Monthly, but of any matter secret or of real importance no trace is to be found in its pages. Material of that kind was kept only in the minds of the core operatives at Du's mansion. When Du Yuesheng dispatched a disciple abroad on some errand, it did not pass through the hands of the Heng Society's board but was assigned and ordered directly and secretly; only the party concerned knew the content, and it had to be kept secret. The more a disciple or friend ran errands for Du in this obedient, do-as-bidden way, the more he was a favorite at the mansion of the day.

To be sure, there were also half-open instances among the affairs they handled. For example, in 1933, before T. V. Soong returned to Shanghai from the United States — where he had gone to negotiate the Cotton and Wheat Loan and to attend the World Economic Conference — he had sent a telegram to Du asking him to see to matters at the dock and guard against assassination; whereupon Du, half openly, mobilized all the force he could muster, including a portion of the Heng Society's people, to go and stand guard at the dock, and even sent a military band to play in welcome. Du Yuesheng established his friendship with Soong and got on terms with him earlier than with H. H. Kung.

At the time of the August 13 Incident of 1937, Du Yuesheng, serving as chairman of the Shanghai Local Association, gave strong support to the Kuomintang army's resistance against the enemy and defense against outrage. Some of the men of the Heng Society, on account of their tie with Du, took part in transport, comfort missions, medical work, recruitment and levy, relief and the like, and among them there were also those who took the chance to find themselves temporary employment through the connection. Another portion joined Dai Li's Juntong organization. Du and Dai had long been on terms; and now there was formed the Special Action Corps of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang Action Committee, comprising five detachments in all, each with its own command post and a commander at its head. The commanders of its second and third detachments, Lu Jingshi and others, were all men of the House of Du and the Heng Society. Under each detachment were three battalions, and under each battalion companies. Some of these battalion and company commanders were Heng Society members, the rest students or workers close to them. They flailed about for a while, but being not only untrained and ill-armed but also without military experience, in a little over a month, by November 12 when Shanghai fell, they scattered in a rout.

On the day Shanghai fell, the Concessions became a "Solitary Island," and on the 27th of that month Du Yuesheng, together with Yu Hongjun, then mayor of Shanghai, and with Qian Xinzhi, Wang Xiaolai and others, boarded a French warship and left Shanghai in secret for Hong Kong.

After Du's departure, the Heng Society lost its center of gravity in Shanghai and its members began to scatter: apart from a portion who stayed in Shanghai, some went to the interior, and some went off to other ports. The society's premises had by this time long since moved from behind Du's mansion to a building on Malang Road (today's Madang Road), just north of Avenue Joffre (today's Huaihai Middle Road), and its affairs came to a standstill of themselves. When the Pacific War broke out, even this establishment could not be kept, and the things inside it were dispersed; but the members who remained in Shanghai still kept up individual activities and contacts.

After Du Yuesheng reached Hong Kong, he rented a long room, No. 706, at the Gloucester Hotel to receive callers, and deputed his secretaries Xu Muxing and Weng Zuoqing, in succession, to the work of holding the fort — receiving and dispatching documents and keeping up outside liaison. At the same time, on Austin Road in Kowloon he rented a house, No. 110, where he settled his household, and began to lead the life of a gentleman of leisure in exile.

Not long after reaching Hong Kong, Du Yuesheng flew several times to Hankou with Qian Xinzhi and Wang Xiaolai; Nanjing had lately been abandoned, and the center of gravity of the Kuomintang government lay in Wuhan. To placate exiled figures of Du Yuesheng's sort, Chiang Kai-shek specially enlarged the Central Relief Commission, appointed Du a standing member, and put him in charge of the affairs of the Ninth Relief District. This district took in the three provinces of Guangdong, Guangxi and Fujian, with its head office set up in Hong Kong so as to facilitate Du's activities there.

At the same time, Du Yuesheng had originally been vice-chairman of the general association of the Chinese Red Cross; and since the association's chairman, Wang Zhengting, was then serving as ambassador to the United States, all the donated funds and goods relating to overseas Chinese were handled by Du in the name of vice-chairman, together with the standing directors Wang Xiaolai, Lin Kanghou and others. In this way Du Yuesheng was, on the surface, active in Hong Kong under the two names of the Relief Commission and the Red Cross; but in reality he became the transfer hub between Chiang Kai-shek and Shanghai and the other occupied areas, playing the part of go-between and support. The backbone Kuomintang men then in Shanghai all used their tie with Du Yuesheng as cover, and the relevant documents too were passed through Du to the Kuomintang government; men such as Jiang Bocheng, Wu Kaixian and Wu Shaoshu all made Du's organ in Shanghai and his residence in Hong Kong their liaison stations. Those doing the cover and liaison work for them in Shanghai at the time were Wan Molin, Zhu Wende, Zhang Kechang, Huang Guodong and a portion of the other Heng Society members. Later, some Heng Society members, such as Wang Manyun and Zhang Kechang, went over to the enemy and became traitors; while the yellow unions, originally controlled by the Kuomintang, were still schemed over and manipulated from within by Lu Jingshi and the rest, who from time to time passed secretly to and fro between Hong Kong and Chongqing, active under Du's direction.

In the spring of 1939, Wu Kaixian came from Chongqing to Hong Kong to confer with Du Yuesheng. Because the Kuomintang signboard could no longer operate in Shanghai, it was decided to change its guise and set up another organ to give unified leadership to the Kuomintang's activities on every front in Shanghai; this organ was named the "Shanghai Unification Committee." The persons then settled on to head it were: chairman Du Yuesheng, and standing members Yu Hongjun, Jiang Bocheng, Dai Li, and Wu Kaixian (concurrently secretary-general). On this committee were Du Yuesheng's protégés Lu Jingshi, Wu Shaoshu and others, while the Heng Society members taking part in the work in Shanghai were Tang Shichang, Ren Jinping and many more. Luo Qinghua, who had formerly been active in the Shanghai Chamber of Commerce, joined the Heng Society in Hong Kong on Lu Jingshi's recommendation, acknowledged Du as his "old man," and thereby became one of the mainstays of the Heng Society. I too was a Heng Society member, serving at that time as chief secretary at the Hong Kong head office of the Chinese Red Cross.

From 1938 on, the Heng Society members, apart from a portion who stayed in Shanghai, were of uncertain whereabouts; Hong Kong was where Du Yuesheng was, and when a larger number of members were in Hong Kong they would often hold communal feasts and take photographs to keep up the connection. Every year Du Yuesheng's birthday was all the more an occasion for the Heng Society members to gather, and many hurried in from Chongqing, Guilin, Shaoguan and other places to offer their birthday wishes. In coming to Hong Kong they of course also bore the task of reporting and seeking instructions. The Heng Society then had in Hong Kong the name of a branch, but no premises and no responsible person designated by Du, and simply took Du's mansion in Kowloon as its point of liaison and assembly.

The British authorities in Hong Kong were not without their guard against Du Yuesheng's activities. Du therefore could not bare his fangs and brandish his claws there as he had in Shanghai. He did take in a batch of followers in Hong Kong at that time, such as Qiu Tang, Tang Lai and others, but without any public fanfare. At the same time, figures of the Hong Kong secret societies, such as the Zhigongtang and the Three Dots Society, entered into liaison with Du Yuesheng, though there were also certain frictions.

After Shanghai, Wuhan and Canton fell, the Heng Society members gathering in the rear grew more numerous by the day. Around 1939, Du Yuesheng went several times to Chongqing over the affairs of the Relief Commission and the business of the traitors Tao Xisheng and Gao Zongwu. A branch of the Heng Society was thereupon established in Chongqing, at No. 19 Linjiang Road. Sichuan is the homeland of the Paoge, where the power of the secret societies is very great; and at that time, besides Du Yuesheng, the gang chiefs from various places — such as Yang Qingshan of Hankou and Yang Hu of Shanghai — had all come to Chongqing. Dai Li, seeking to link up with gang men on every side, set up the forerunner of the China New Social Enterprise Construction Association, the "People's Action Committee," and deputed the Juntong men Xu Weibin and Jin Banpo to the liaison work, with its address at Daijiaxiang. Du Yuesheng took part in the liaison among the upper-level figures, and further sent the Heng Society member Yu Songqiao, at various times, to keep in touch with these people, hold discussion meetings and communal feasts, and bring the Chongqing underworld under control.

After the Heng Society's branch was set up, Du Yuesheng's flag was hung out in Chongqing and drew the notice of every quarter. Each time Du came to Chongqing he took in a batch of disciples, quite a number of them, from among functionaries, industry and commerce, banking, and cultural circles. The core men of the House of Du in Chongqing at the time included Lu Jingshi, Tang Zuanzhi and others who had come from Shanghai; Bian Dingyuan and others, recruited in Nanjing, who were under H. H. Kung — Bian being director of the Public Debt Department of the Finance Ministry; and Shen Chubao and others from the Hankou steamship trade. At that time Lu Jingshi was director of the Organization and Training Department of the Ministry of Social Affairs. Bian Dingyuan was a department director in the Finance Ministry, a favorite at H. H. Kung's side who was in constant dealings with Kung's second daughter; and since Du Yuesheng and H. H. Kung were hand in glove, Du of course esteemed Bian the more. Thus the Heng Society in Chongqing came to form two forces. In terms of standing in the House of Du, Lu Jingshi's was of course the deeper; in terms of ties with the great families, Bian's was the stronger; each had his own knack, and they measured themselves against each other.

Among the members, some attached themselves to Lu Jingshi and some championed Bian Dingyuan. Du Yuesheng understood this state of affairs and used the contradiction between them to manipulate and control them. The Heng Society members in Chongqing were mostly men from the southeastern provinces; they carried even the round stage-curtain made in Shanghai, by roundabout ways, all the way to Chongqing, and put on several dress-rehearsed Peking-opera performances, which had a certain power of appeal to some strata of society. Among the famous actors and noted amateurs who had gone to Chongqing at the time — such as the Cheng-school qingyi Zhao Rongchen and the Mei-school qingyi Yang Wannong — both had taken part in the Heng Society.

In 1943, the Heng Society put on a performance to see off the Youth Army, and the venue was broken up by men — a striking incident in the Chongqing underworld. Why it was broken up, and who instigated it, was never made clear at the time, nor did the Heng Society men dare pursue the matter. Judging from the situation on the spot, it was very likely the doing of the Paoge. The Heng Society was throwing its weight about all over Chongqing, recruiting men and buying horses, which was bound to arouse the jealousy of the local snakes. That provocation was to give the Heng Society a taste of what was what. The Heng Society men at the time swallowed their anger and dared not make a noise about it. Du Yuesheng was then convalescing at his residence at Wangshan on the south bank of Chongqing, and his followers did not tell him; but Dai Li came to hear of it, and went in person to inspect the spot, though nothing further came of it afterward.

When the Pacific War broke out in 1941 and Hong Kong soon fell, Du Yuesheng had already, on November 16 of that year, flown to Chongqing with Wang Xiaolai and Qian Xinzhi on business, and so escaped the calamity. From then on Du settled for the time in Chongqing; but he suffered from severe asthma, and Chongqing's climate did not suit him at all, so that his old complaint flared up from time to time. His town residence was on the second floor of the Bank of Communications on Datong Street, his suburban residence at Wangshan on the south bank; the town was often bombed, while the suburb had the fresher air, and he lived at Wangshan the more. That little Western-style house had been specially built for Du by the Bank of Communications. Du was a director of the bank, and above all because of the friendship between Qian Xinzhi, the bank's great man, and Du, the bank had made the arrangements handsomely. After Wu Kaixian reached Chongqing, he too lived there for a time.

In October 1942, growing ever more dispirited in Chongqing and possessed by a feeling of being "a dragon stranded out of water," Du Yuesheng — stir-crazy to the last degree — accepted the invitation of the Northwest Kuomintang men and went to Xi'an to "inspect." After reaching the Northwest, he made preparations to set up a Xi'an branch of the Commercial Bank and put a hand into such enterprises as the Northwest woolen mill. But he did not stay long in Xi'an, and for reasons of health returned to Chongqing ahead of time.

He moved the head management office of the China Commercial Bank to Chongqing and set up a Chongqing branch, put in the charge of Luo Qinghua, Chen Guohua and others. On the enterprise side, he prepared to establish the Zhonghua Industrial Trust Company, with Yang Guanbei as general manager, Luo Qinghua and Shen Chubao as deputy managers, and Lu Zengfu as accountant — all of them Heng Society members. Part of the capital of the Zhonghua Industrial Trust Company was raised by Heng Society members, the rest put up by Du Yuesheng, the original plan being to build an economic foundation for the Heng Society. Chongqing was the chief venue for making fortunes out of the national calamity; and with this enterprise as a front, added to Du's power, one could of course build a fortune from nothing and unfold grand designs, so that the Heng Society would no longer need to rely on members' dues and extortion from every quarter as its main economic source. But because the forces of every side in Chongqing were so tangled and complex, Du then dared not be too unbridled, and mainly did only one smuggling deal at Kunming, from which he made a tidy fortune. The Zhonghua Company's address was No. 49 Linsen Road at Wanglongmen, a house bought with cash.

Besides this, the Tongji Company too was set up in 1943. This organ was planned by Dai Li and established through the tie with Du Yuesheng and Qian Xinzhi. Du and Qian served respectively as chairman and vice-chairman of the preparatory committee, with Zhu Huiqing, Yang Guanbei and others as standing members — Zhu taking part through his tie with Dai — and the preparatory office was set up on the upper floor of the Zhonghua Industrial Trust Company. During the preparatory period, Xu Ziwei, introduced by Qian Xinzhi, was made manager of the Jieshou branch office at Shangqiu, Henan; and in coordination with the Wartime Freight Control Bureau set up by the Chongqing government (the head of that bureau being Dai Li himself, and the transport battalion commander Wang Zhaohuai), a consignment of Shanghai yarn and cloth was rushed in. This yarn and cloth had been shipped up through enemy-and-puppet channels by the Minhua Company of Shanghai (a sister company of the Tongji Company). The head of the Shanghai Minhua Company, Xu Caicheng, was Du's agent in Shanghai; he was all along in collusion with the enemy and puppet regime, kept a wireless station, and through Du Yuesheng passed word back and forth between the enemy-and-puppet side and Chiang Kai-shek. This consignment of yarn and cloth was escorted by Xu Caicheng's son-in-law Lin Renmo and, accompanied the whole way by the Japanese-language interpreter Yuan Wenzhang, who smoothed the passages, was carried to Shangqiu, and thence taken on by Wang Zhaohuai to Xi'an and Chongqing, and handed to Dai Li's Wartime Freight Control Bureau, which transferred it to the Cotton, Yarn and Cloth Company as a bargaining chip for "stabilizing prices." This is one concrete instance of the mutual collusion, within the Chiang Kai-shek clique, of bureaucrats, politicians, secret agents, and the Japanese army and traitors. A second consignment of yarn and cloth shipped out of Shanghai was moved inland from Chun'an in Zhejiang, again handled by Xu Ziwei, who had wound up the organ at Shangqiu in Henan and hurried to Chun'an to manage it. This consignment was seized en route by Gu Zhutong, commander-in-chief of the Third War Zone, and put to another use, and did not reach Chongqing; over this matter Dai Li lodged a complaint all the way up to Chiang Kai-shek, but in the end it came to nothing.

When the Tongji Company's Xu Ziwei was taking delivery of the second consignment of yarn and cloth, he was moreover kidnapped near the Fuchun River by an irregular unit of unknown designation and carried off into the hills, and only ransomed for over 70,000 yuan — this too being a specimen of the black-eating-black within the ranks of the Kuomintang ruling class. The Heng Society member who went to Chun'an to take part in this business was Zhu Pinsan; and at the Tongji Company in Chongqing, the Juntong had sent in a chief auditor, Lin Fanmin.

In the summer of 1945, Du Yuesheng received Chiang Kai-shek's instruction to go to the southeastern war zone to assist the so-called "subversion" work and to coordinate with the so-called Allied landing — which, it was said, Dai Li had schemed from behind the scenes. On June 25 of that year, Du took his disciples Gu Jiatang, Ye Zhuoshan, Pang Jingzhou, Hu Xuwu, Yang Zhixiong and others and went by car from Chongqing to Guiyang, where he joined Dai Li and the American secret-service chief Miles (deputy director of the Sino-American Cooperative Organization); from there he flew to Dongjiang, then crossed into Fujian and reached Chun'an. After Japan's surrender, Du Yuesheng returned from there to Shanghai by way of Hangzhou.

During Du's time at Chun'an, a batch of Heng Society members in Shanghai hurried to Chun'an to greet and pay their respects to the "old man," among them Xu Caicheng and others. At the same time, the Heng Society's core member Lu Jingshi took his labor-movement cadres, Lu Keming and others, to be active in the southern Zhejiang region, making ready to enter Shanghai. The organization later set up in Shanghai, the "Workers' Welfare Association," had its seedling nurtured at that time.

After victory in the war, reactionary bureaucrats and politicians, warlords and secret agents, and hoodlums all regarded Shanghai as a fat piece of meat; from the skies and from underground they poured into Shanghai and unfolded a savage scramble to grab first — "the five sons passing the examinations, the six beasts taking wing" — lawless and heaven-defying, one great blackness. Du Yuesheng returned to Shanghai on September 3, and when he stepped off at Shanghai West Station he was given a rousing welcome by several thousand of his disciples and their disciples. But after this rousing scene, he realized that the Shanghai he had returned to after eight years' absence was no longer a place where he could do as he pleased as in former days. With the forces of the various Kuomintang factions tangled and complex and reaching deep into Shanghai, he found it hard to manipulate things at will; and it was above all the attitude toward him of his own protégé Wu Shaoshu that struck him very deeply.

Among the takeover grandees who had come flying down from the skies and boring up from underground, Wu Shaoshu — chairman of the Kuomintang municipal party headquarters, deputy mayor of Shanghai, head of the Three People's Principles Youth Corps and concurrently chief of the Social Affairs Bureau — had originally been a protégé of Du's, and was for a time immensely in favor; another protégé, Lu Jingshi, was the Ministry of Social Affairs' special commissioner for the Shanghai-Nanjing area; the mayor of Shanghai, Qian Dajun, was also an old friend of Du's; and the most powerful of the takeover grandees, the Juntong agent Dai Li, was again Du's old partner. By ordinary reckoning, Du had at that time linked up every side — party, government, army and secret service — and ought to have been able to have things as he wished. His vexation, however, arose over Wu Shaoshu. Before the war Wu had been a clerk in the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters, and had then formally acknowledged himself Du's disciple, forming a bond with Lu Jingshi, and had moreover, through Wan Molin's hands, received financial support from Du Yuesheng. During the war, Wu had further used his tie with Du to carry on so-called underground work in Shanghai, his liaison station there being set up in the former Du mansion. Later he leaned further on Chen Lifu, began to attack Du Yuesheng, and openly denied that he had ever acknowledged himself of the House of Du. In the latter part of the war, when Lu Jingshi wanted to canvass for a seat as delegate to the Kuomintang's national assembly and talked it over with Wu Shaoshu, hoping the Shanghai units would nominate him, he was unexpectedly refused by Wu Shaoshu — which created the first rift between Du and Wu. In the early period of the victory takeover, Wu held in his grasp the whole authority over party, government, corps, and public opinion in Shanghai (running the Zhengyan Bao), would not defer to Du Yuesheng, and made things difficult for Du at every turn; but by then Wu's plumage was full-grown, and it was hard for Du to deal with him.

During this period of vexation, Du was for a time brought together with Wu through the good offices of Hong Lanyou and Pan Gongzhan, meeting Wu at Gu Jiatang's house, where they ate and talked together and had a photograph taken as a keepsake; Wu Shaoshu even called Du "Master" to his face and admitted his past error in making things difficult for Du. But afterward Wu and his subordinates went on opposing Du as before. Later, Wu was fired at with a pistol in his car, but the shot did not pierce the safety glass, and he only got a fright. Everyone said this was Dai Li's doing, for at that time Dai and Du were cooperating very closely.

After Du returned to Shanghai, he revived the Heng Society's head society. E Sen once came forward to convene a discussion meeting at the Pudong Native-Place Association to work out the concrete problems of reviving it — the first being the question of premises, the second that of re-registration. This meeting was held in the autumn of 1945, attended by Tang Zuanzhi, Wang Runsheng, Wu Yingsu and twenty-odd others. Later, after several more gatherings, the question of premises was the first to be settled. For a Heng Society member, Xu Datong, had done a great trade in the paper business during the war and made a fortune, earning the name of "Paper King"; he had a club called the Lun Society on the sixth floor of a building at the north end of Jiangxi Road and Jiujiang Road, originally a place where he gathered his moneyed friends to eat, drink and make merry, taking up two whole floors of the building — quite spacious — and he volunteered to donate it to the Heng Society. With the premises settled, a general meeting was convened to re-elect the directors and supervisors; all were notified to attend except those who had been notorious for going over to the enemy in Shanghai. As for those who had gone over to the enemy, no expulsion procedure was carried out either, it being said that the secret societies set store by loyalty and that private dealings could still be kept up between individuals.

After the head society was revived, its activity was not lively, and the people who came regularly were not many. On looking into it, the cause was held to lie in the unsuitability of the premises; for the Heng Society could not be parted from the "old man," and its premises ought to adjoin the "old man's" residence so that members could constantly drop in at Du's mansion. So the Heng Society decided to build a new house of its own, and found a plot in front of today's Zhongnan New Village on Huaihai Middle Road, about two mu in size, which a certain gangster (whose name I cannot recall) had formerly given to Du Yuesheng. Du took out the land deed and handed it over to the Heng Society as the site, and building was begun. There were architectural and engineering talents in the Heng Society, who designed it gratis, planning a three-story building with a great assembly hall, a stage, a dining room, a guesthouse, a library and offices and so on — quite large in scale — and a wooden sign reading "Site of the Heng Society" was already raised on the ground. But by the time of Shanghai's liberation, work had still not begun, the reason being that the Heng Society members later did not put their energies into it. At the same time, Du Yuesheng, apart from staying a few days on Ninghai West Road when he first reached Shanghai, lived all along at Gu Jiatang's house and was constantly ill. The Heng Society's core man in charge of liaison work while at the Gu house was Tang Shichang. Du Yuesheng, being ill, had little contact with the members, and was continually depressed over the affair of Wu Shaoshu. And the members at this time were all mainly reckoning up their own interests, only a few caring about the Heng Society; so the Heng Society's new building was shelved indefinitely.

During this period, on Jianguo West Road (No. 344, as I recall) there was a house of the traitor Tao Xuesheng that was for sale (in reality an enemy-and-puppet property already sealed long before). Through Gu Jiatang's introduction and handling, the Heng Society was urged to buy it for premises; Du Yuesheng's consent was obtained, and so the Heng Society men launched a subscription and gathered 60 bars of gold. Du's eldest son Du Weifan (informally designated in advance as the heir to the chairmanship) and Tang Shichang handed this sum to Gu Jiatang to close the deal, and Gu turned over the title deeds to the Heng Society. It later came out that Tao Xuesheng got only 50 bars, Gu Jiatang having pocketed 10. Once the deal was done, the Heng Society moved in, and there was a spell of bustle; the furnishings and fittings inside were all got by plunder, quite enough to set the place up. But this house had a problem from the start: the Enemy-and-Puppet Property Liquidation Office had meant to seal it, and Tao Xuesheng and Gu Jiatang, knowing this well in advance, had wanted to get it off their hands early, while ordinary people would not and dared not take it on. It was Gu Jiatang who proposed passing it to the Heng Society, reckoning that under Du Yuesheng's signboard they need not fear sealing. In the event, the Central Trust came to seal it, learned it was already Heng Society property, and so pasted the seal-strip on the wall as a token that the procedure had been carried out, while the Heng Society went on using it as before, treating the seal as though it were invisible. Only when Shanghai was liberated in 1949 was this house taken over by the People's Government; by then the Heng Society's activities had already ceased, with a few members still living inside, who later moved out.

Du Yuesheng left Shanghai for Hong Kong in March 1949, and from then on the Heng Society dissolved of itself in Shanghai. From its founding in 1932 down to 1949, the society lasted seventeen years in all. On the eve of Shanghai's liberation, apart from a portion of members who stayed, no small number went off to Hong Kong and Taiwan. Those members appearing on the last roster numbered about 700; by a fairly reliable estimate — counting the dead, and the earlier and later periods, and including those who joined in secret and never came into the open — the Heng Society members numbered around 1,500. Some say it had tens of thousands of members, but that is going too far.

Du Yuesheng's favoring and estranging of his disciples marked the shifts in the Heng Society's center of gravity. In Du's eyes, only those who could open up a situation for themselves in society, in politics and in the economy — the capable and the well-placed, that is, those able to be of help to him — were his good disciples. In his own reckoning of the Heng Society's members, men of this sort were few; most were on terms of mutual use with him, and the great majority merely traded on his name for their activities.

From the Heng Society's founding down to Du Yuesheng's death in August 1951, the disciple Du Yuesheng held most capable, fit to be given heavy trust and charged with great affairs, was Lu Jingshi. In earlier years Chen Lifu had said to people that Lu Jingshi's loyalty to Du Yuesheng surpassed his loyalty to the Kuomintang — which likewise shows Lu's attitude toward and relation with Du. So Du did whatever Lu advised, and all the Heng Society's principal affairs and major decisions were taken with Lu's participation. It is said that before Du's death, Lu Jingshi, then in Taiwan, was summoned by telegram to come to Hong Kong for a parting interview. The only protégé he summoned by telegram at his end was this one man; whether they saw each other one last time is not known.

Among the Heng Society members, there were also several who took disciples of their own and so became Du's disciples-once-removed. Zhu Xuefan took the most students, and once formed a Qin Society, all of them workers, disbanded after liberation. Bian Dingyuan formed a Jing Society, with no few students too, mostly functionaries of government organs. Tang Shichang also had no few students, mostly staff of the Shen Bao office — Tang had been a deputy manager of the Shen Bao and was an active member of the Journalists' Guild. Zhang Kechang's disciples were mostly postal workers. Lu Jingshi took no students, but a portion of the Heng Society was under his direction.

After victory in the war, the Heng Society, under Du Yuesheng's direction and in collusion with Kuomintang bureaucrats, carried out the following main activities among others. (1) Raising funds for the so-called Northeast Refugees Relief Association: this was initiated by Song Meiling and was in the charge of H. H. Kung, Du Yuesheng and others; Du directed the Heng Society members' activities, raising funds through a benefit performance at the Tianchan Stage, with Mei Lanfang, Cheng Yanqiu, Mei's disciple Yang Wannong and Cheng's disciple Zhao Rongchen singing "Si-Wu Hua Dong" together, which caused a great sensation for a time. Afterward, H. H. Kung gave a Western-style dinner for the Heng Society's performance organizers on the fourteenth floor of the Park Hotel; Kung attended in person, and during the meal talked opera at great length with Ma Lianliang, Jin Suwen and others, in very high spirits. (2) Raising funds for the so-called North Jiangsu Refugees Association, initiated by Wu Zhihui, Gu Zhenggang, and Wang Maogong (then chairman of Jiangsu Province): the Heng Society sponsored this benefit performance, in which Du Yuesheng's wife Yao Yulan took part with a performance of "Mourning at the Golden Tortoise," drawing no few people to cheer it on. Besides this, there was also the electing of a so-called "Miss Shanghai" and the like — deceiving the public with gimmicks, squeezing out money for the reactionary bureaucrats and pulling strings; and where the money so gathered went, heaven alone knows.

Of the later activities of the Heng Society and Du Yuesheng, what I can recall for a start is roughly the above. Besides this, I have thought of one more thing. During the Chongqing period, when the Kuomintang was making a great to-do over the bogus National Assembly, Zhang Shizhao once put to Du Yuesheng the proposal of turning the Heng Society into a political-party organization; and for this a discussion meeting was once held at the "Liang Mansion" of the member Zhang Yuliang, with Du Yuesheng and Zhang Shizhao both attending in person. The upshot of the talk was that, apart from a few members who thought it might be worth a try, the majority held that the conditions were insufficient to establish a political party, and Du himself also thought the Heng Society's manpower and conditions inadequate, so nothing concrete was done. This too is an episode in the Heng Society's history worth setting down.

What I have related above is based on personal memory, with some subjective views added in between. Because the materials that might aid memory are very few, and my own level is very low, there must be no small number of errors and one-sided points; I hope the gentlemen who understand these matters will supplement and correct them.
