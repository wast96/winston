## Chapter 51

The car came before long, driven by an American woman. Mei Yingzi told her to take the other car back, and we all went down the stairs together.

It was a black 1939 Morris, an old model. I did not look closely; Bai Ping had already darted ahead to open the car door, meaning to get in, and Mei Yingzi rushed out to open it for her. Once Bai Ping was in, Mei Yingzi shut the door. I stepped over beside her and asked:

"Are you driving yourself?" But Mei Yingzi only ordered me:

"Sit beside me."

So Mei Yingzi and I sat in the front, and she switched off the interior light, deftly prized open the roof, and handed me a pistol. I saw only that it was a revolver, and just as I meant to look closer she said:

"Put it away."

I slipped the gun into my right pocket. No one said a word, nor did we so much as look at one another.

The car drove straight on, but I took in none of the street outside; there was an unspeakable unease in me, and I heard only my own heartbeat, my heartbeat. Now the road seemed long, now the car slow; now the road short, now the car fast. Anyone who has lived in Shanghai knows how far it is from Route Prosper Paris to Youheng Road; but that long road, in my uneasy state of mind, seemed to me like a circuit of the globe, and yet, as we neared the end of it, I marveled again at how small Shanghai was.

At last the car reached Youheng Road. Mei Yingzi slowed, and, like someone treading on tiptoe, crept gently forward. My heart beat all the faster. Suddenly a voice came from behind me and, for an instant, stilled my heartbeat; the blood in my whole body seemed to congeal, and I froze — but once I made it out to be Bai Ping's voice, my quick heartbeat came back. In a commanding tone, almost sharp, Bai Ping said:

"Stop."

The car stopped short. Mei Yingzi turned her head, and Bai Ping had already opened the door. She said:

"Wait for me ten feet on, ahead there." And with that she began to step down from the car. Mei Yingzi said quickly:

"Bai Ping, take care…"

I sat frozen; for an instant I wanted to speak, but Bai Ping had already cut off my words with the heavy sound of the door closing. She walked off toward the far side of the street, diagonally across.

Mei Yingzi, without a word, started the car slowly forward again. I watched Bai Ping's figure, and only then did I see how dim the sky was, how black the ground, how lightless the street lamps, how forlorn Bai Ping's figure! Slowly I saw her step up onto the footpath, and then I saw the houses, the lane beside them, the marker lamp above the lane, and under the lamp the faded gold characters, Juxian Li. I stared, and looked back, while the car crept forward, until I could see no more; but still I watched Bai Ping's figure. Mei Yingzi stopped the car and began to look back. She told me to move to the rear compartment, and I jumped down from the car and saw her shift over into my seat to keep watch. But just as I opened the rear door and was about to get in, I caught sight, all at once, of a white-clad maidservant coming out of the lane. I stepped up onto the running board and, leaning at the open window, watched them; without thinking, with a sweating hand I gripped the pistol in my pocket. Looking back on it now, from the moment I took the pistol my hand had never left it, and only when I got down to change seats did I loosen my hold — so that what came of it afterward was, of course, no accident.

I watched the two shapes over there, one black, one white, in talk; minute by minute went by, in what seemed courtesy, or a giving way; at last the black shape seemed about to turn back, when suddenly — a report, a flash, as though bearing down from above — I heard Bai Ping give a cry, and ah, she fell. It is strange that I was so calm then: no fear, no grief, no thought, no feeling; by reflex I drew out the gun and fired at the white shape that seemed just about to turn away. Yes — I heard clearly the cry of that white-clad maidservant, I saw clearly as she fell —

"Shut the door!" Mei Yingzi cried, commanding.

I obeyed at once. Mei Yingzi was already driving the car straight ahead, and I was thrown down onto the back seat. In a moment I heard a police whistle behind us, and then, far off, other whistles answering it.

The car raced on, and I could not tell what streets we passed; but after two turnings a searchlight seemed to throw its beam ahead of us, and Mei Yingzi suddenly gripped the wheel and the car slowed sharply. In a clipped tone she said:

"Get out. Wait for me by the roadside."

Without a word I opened the door, but at such a speed I still dared not jump. I said:

"A little slower."

Mei Yingzi did slow the car again, and without stopping to think, drawing on my boyhood knack for jumping trams, I slid down from it.

Seeing me slide down, Mei Yingzi jumped clear as well, even as she put on speed; the empty car ran on into the searchlight's beam, no more than a hundred paces off, when I heard a great roar — the car had blown to pieces. It had struck nothing; it seemed Mei Yingzi had set off the charge as she got out, though there was no time then to ask. Mei Yingzi hurried to my side and pulled me along. Just then I heard the roar of a police car ahead; Mei Yingzi turned into a side street and I followed her, and in the dark she suddenly slowed her steps and took me by the arm. I glanced back and saw a police car coming up the main road behind us too; we turned again, but just as we meant to go on, men who looked like peddlers and workmen came running toward us, and, barring their way with my body, I asked, as if asking nothing in particular:

"What's happened?"

"They've sealed it off again."

Sealing off a district was the means the Japanese then used in Shanghai to deal with every kind of incident, and Mei Yingzi seemed to have guessed as much long since; she drew me off again down a byway. I followed her in a daze, and at last found we had come to the riverbank at Fearon Road — but ahead of us a few more people were falling back.

For a moment even Mei Yingzi was at a loss. She stopped, leaned against a wall, and drew a breath, and I leaned beside her. In that quarter, at that hour, the few people about were either peddlers hurrying to the morning market, or workmen carting off rubbish, or coolies sleeping in the open, or else hard-worked boat people; we were the only ones in neat dress, and the moment a Japanese came by we would at once be marked for questioning. Mei Yingzi looked at every passerby, but she did not look at me. From her gaze I saw that what she was asking now was whether, among these coming and going, there might be some thatched hut where we could hide for a while.

Of those falling back, a few turned down toward the wharf, and I took them all for the children of boat people. Just then, by what feeling I cannot say, it came to me that perhaps some boatman would let us hide a while, and I said to Mei Yingzi:

"Wait for me a moment. I'll be right back."

Mei Yingzi seemed to know my purpose; she said nothing, but took my arm and crossed the road with me. On the far side was Suzhou Creek, and on both banks lay boats large and small, with only a narrow channel down the middle of the river left open — just as, on the streets of Chongqing where I write this, the crush of the crowds has turned both sides of the road into footways too, so that only a single line down the center is left for carriages and traffic.

Walking along the bank, every eight or ten paces brought a wharf; I wished to choose with some care, but there was no choosing, and at last, as though I had chosen and yet not, I went down at random from one of the wharves.

Ahead lay a dense mass of boats — at least several dozen moored bow and stern along this wharf — with their patched-together bamboo awnings, the furled sails drawn in about the masts, the mended-and-remended clothes hung at bow and stern, the ragged diapers, a woman's red jacket, a faded bellyband, all making up an intricate pattern. Far off were the outlines of the warehouses on the opposite bank, and the gray-blue sky; the east seemed by then to be whitening a little, but down below one could still make out anything only by the bank lamps, and of the boats some had a light burning and some had none. I wanted to find a more suitable boat and put my plea to it, but the boats nearby had no one aboard. On a boat somewhat farther off someone seemed to be coughing and stirring, but I could not call out across the distance; and just then, beyond five or six boats, I saw a man come out onto a bow. He glanced about him, paid me no heed it seemed, and then stood urinating over the bow. I was casting about for a way to speak to him when he suddenly coughed and said:

"Looks like rain today, I'm afraid." And having said it he went back inside.

Among the scattered boat lights I looked back and saw Mei Yingzi behind me, standing beside a boat at the far left corner. It was a small boat, without light or sound; but in the very instant I caught sight of it, a small flame suddenly flared up. It was like a lamp for one who has lost the way, and seemed to hold some hint for me, and without thinking I walked over to Mei Yingzi. Sure enough, someone put their head out of the boat — a girl with a braid, who seemed just to have woken, a water bucket in her hand. Mei Yingzi, who let no chance slip, went gently over and said in a low voice:

"Excuse me, little miss."

As Mei Yingzi said this, I saw for the first time that the other was a girl not yet twenty. She raised her head and looked blankly at Mei Yingzi; and Mei Yingzi now opened her handbag, took out several banknotes, and, still talking, held them out:

"There are bad men on the bank driving us on — let us hide with you a while?"

The girl seemed dazed and at a loss for what to say; she looked at the money in Mei Yingzi's hand, and then at Mei Yingzi's face, and gave a questioning smile.

"Tche San!" Just then someone suddenly called from inside the boat — a woman's voice: "What's the matter with you!…"

In the instant the girl put her head back inside, Mei Yingzi had already stepped aboard, and I followed her up. Entering the cabin, we saw a middle-aged woman with disheveled hair, who seemed also just to have risen; her hair was in a tangle, and at the sight of us she stared in great surprise. I left Mei Yingzi to talk with her and kept my attention on the girl behind me, afraid she would go ashore and tell others. I had not known how quick the hand becomes after one has killed a man: the moment things grew tense it closed on the gun. But the girl bore us no ill will; she stood beside me with a bucket of water in her hand.

"Excuse me," I heard Mei Yingzi say, "there are bad men on the bank driving us on, so we'd like to hide here a while; please save us, granny."

So saying, Mei Yingzi laid the banknotes in her hand on top of a wooden crate at the side, and then took a further wad of notes from her wallet and laid it on top too. She said:

"We'll thank you properly again later; please take this for now, and get us a little food."

It struck me as odd that Mei Yingzi called her granny, but she thought nothing of it, and, breaking into a smile, said:

"Stay here by all means — only it really is too filthy here. Oh, but I can't take the money; poor as we are, still…"

"There's no need to stand on ceremony," I said, going inside. "In saving us you are our benefactor; this little money is no thanks at all."

Inside was a rough plank table, with two bamboo chairs at its edge and two small bamboo stools by the side of the boat. I saw Mei Yingzi to a bamboo chair, and she gave a faint sigh and leaned on the table, burying her face in her hands, while I sat down first on a bamboo chair.

The girl was kindling a fire at the bow, glancing over at us now and then, and the middle-aged woman at the other end said:

"Teh-San, boil some water first, quick, and make tea for the guests."

Mei Yingzi now suddenly raised her head and looked at me; I paid her no heed, and she only said to the middle-aged woman:

"Softly — don't let the people outside hear, and learn there are strangers here."

"What's there to fear? What household here doesn't have a few well-off guests?"

"Say no more." I too called her granny now. "Granny, sit down, let us talk. Oh — and there's this." I rose and took the money from the crate and held it out. "Do please take this money first, whatever you say, and please believe we are by no means bad people."

I set the money on the plank table, and the old woman, a kindly smile on her face, awkwardly smoothed her hair with her hand, then felt about under the cabin bunk and brought out a candle-end stuck in a tin candlestick, lit it at the oil lamp in the stern, and carried it over.

Mei Yingzi gazed at me, and just then she suddenly said, in English:

"Didn't you notice, just now?"

"Notice what?" Thinking there was some disturbance outside the boat, I asked in alarm.

"That girl's name," said Mei Yingzi.

"Tche-San! Give my little teapot a good wash and make tea for the guests," said the old woman.

Teh-San, Tche-San — it came to me all at once that this was a name I knew, though I could not tell where I had heard it, nor call to mind which friend it belonged to. I looked at Mei Yingzi and said, as if asking and not asking:

"Very familiar — but…"

"Isn't it the girl you spoke of in the letters you sent, when you pretended to go off to the countryside?"

"…" It dawned on me all at once — the country girl Cishan I had invented in those letters — and for a moment I was so astonished that I seemed to have a great deal to say and yet could get none of it out.

Many strange chances there are in the world, but in the retelling even we ourselves cannot help doubting them. That girl too was called Cishan — not written so, of course — but among the thousands upon thousands of syllables, these two sounds were not the sort of words, like "Cui," "Bao," "Zhen," that are commonly used for girls' names; and to the very ends of the earth, that there should be such a coincidence! Even now, looking back, I cannot suppress a strange feeling. I did not, at the time, ask them how the girl's name was written; but from then on, until now, and even hereafter, whenever we think of the girl's name, whenever we think of those two sounds, the association they call up is "Cishan" — and so, in this record, I shall call her Cishan from here on.

I began to talk with the old woman. We called her granny, though in truth she was not old. By the light of the candle she had set on the plank table, I saw her dark-red skin, her bright eyes, her lightly wrinkled brow; and but for her thin, sparse hair, which let me guess her past fifty, she was a woman still under forty-five.

She told me she came from a village upriver on Suzhou Creek; they had been fishing folk by trade, but also carried goods for others on the side, and several times had been requisitioned by the Japanese to serve them. Her husband had been seized by the Japanese two months before and taken to Pudong for hard labor, and now she and her daughter lived off this one boat; luckily her husband had a younger brother who also had a boat and could look after them a little.

Hearing in her words how she hated the Japanese for their high-handedness, I told her we had gone to see a friend taken suddenly ill, and that on the way a drunken Japanese soldier had made to insult the young lady, so that I had quarreled with him; but the soldier drew a pistol, and we grappled together, and — who would have thought it — I wrenched the pistol from him, and somehow it went off and struck him in the chest —

"It served him right, served him right!" the old woman said, moved; but then, a little alarmed, she looked all about her, and suddenly blew out the candle and had us sit on her bunk. She said:

"Let us move the boat off a little."

So she went to the stern, to Cishan, said a few words, and the two of them set about moving the boat. It was hard work, for every way out was choked by other boats. They used no oars: the mother pushed off against the gunwales of the neighboring boats with her hands, while the daughter braced with a pole, and our boat worked its way in among the gaps between the others, squeezing and squeezing, until at last it came to rest. I heard the old woman say:

"This will do."

Seen from afar these boats seemed to lie in no order, jammed tight together, but in fact each had, at bow or stern, a little clear space that let people reach the water — where they washed their faces and clothes, rinsed rice and vegetables, and relieved themselves, all in that small patch of clearance. Though the water flowed at the riverbed, the filth of every kind floating on the surface between boat and boat stayed pooled where it was, and each time it was used one merely parted the muck aside — so that in the end it grew thicker and thicker.

Sitting on the old woman's bunk, we could see the little pool of water at the bow, and, across many boats, the far bank, and a corner of blue sky besides. Just then Cishan brought us tea, and she and Mei Yingzi spoke together for the first time. The sky had grown translucent, and the old woman blew out the oil lamp on the cabin wall — the very lamp that had shown us the way when we were lost. I watched the dying flame at its wick and waited for it to go out, and much stirred in me. By the daylight I could see Cishan's face, the face of a full, sturdy young girl, her dark-red cheeks a little chapped with cold, the fringe on her forehead hanging so that her brow seemed slightly knit, her hair black and thick, her single braid very coarse, her features clear and fine, her nose straight — only the nostrils showing a little, which one might count a flaw in the beauty — her lips thin, and a shy diffidence in her talk with Mei Yingzi. I do not know how she differed from the Cishan I had once written of in my letters, but I found Mei Yingzi excessively warm toward her, and this was certainly the imagining that the name had stirred up in her from before. A person's name, or a manner, an action, some slight play of expression, can often give another person a particular feeling. That feeling links up with what one associates with the first — remembered imaginings from the past, memories out of one's life, connections with characters from films, plays, or books — and makes a particular affinity, so that people take to each other at first sight, become friends for life, form all manner of strange bonds. So it was, in an instant, settled between Mei Yingzi and Cishan. With Mei Yingzi's adornment and beauty, her clever talk, she had a particular magic in befriending anyone at all; and it was naturally the easier for her to win over so plain and artless a child as Cishan.

But all of this was the handiwork of the god of fate — so tightly wrought, so ingenious — that in our recollection it seemed that, in a certain combination of things, not one of the smallest factors could be spared, or the outcome would have been wholly different.
