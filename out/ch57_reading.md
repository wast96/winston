## Chapter 57

My heart began to pound, and at once I thought of Mei Yingzi. But I made no sound; I turned to the front page, hiding my burning face, and at last stood up and lit a cigarette. I wanted to go on with Helen about the question of a moment before, but had no heart to talk. I was anxious for Mei Yingzi, hoping she would come to see me, or give me a meeting, or that a letter would come telling me how her success had gone and how things now stood with her. I worried for her, fretted for her, but most of all I wanted to bless her, to pay her my homage. I was ashamed too of the slighting sarcasm I had shown her at Dr. Philip's clinic; I wanted to pour out my remorse to her, but through the long, long day there was no word from her. I hungered for the evening papers, and the news in them was no different from the day's papers, and so I longed again for the night. — At night, alone in my room, I did not sleep, but sat on the sofa smoking and waiting in stillness. I had a strange sort of feeling, as if Mei Yingzi, did she not come tonight, would never come again, and yet as if she surely would come tonight; and so my heart was all the more anxious.

And sure enough, at half past eleven, someone knocked. Instinctively I thought of Mei Yingzi; I supposed Ah Mei had opened the outer door for her and she had come straight in.

"Come in," I said.

The door opened softly.

"Not asleep yet?"

It was Mrs. Manfield; I knew at once she had come to hear Helen's answer, and I said:

"Sit down, please!"

"Has she taken your advice yet?" Mrs. Manfield asked, her eyes wide with hope.

"Not yet," I said. "I thought I'd talk with her again another day."

"Do you think it possible?"

"That's very hard to say," I said. "But today I was out of spirits, and did not go on with it."

I think it was because, in my manner — for the unease in my heart over Mei Yingzi's affair — I had not been as earnest as I was that other night; and somehow this touched Mrs. Manfield, who, saying not a word, suddenly broke into sobbing.

This quite unmanned me, and I comforted her, saying:

"Mrs. Manfield, I'll surely do my best, rest easy," I said. "Happily there's time enough yet, and I've plenty of chances to persuade her."

Still she wept, and said not a word.

"Rest easy, Mrs. Manfield," I said. "It's early yet even to grieve; go and sleep now, and tomorrow I'll find a chance to speak again."

"But you…" She wept again.

"I what? I'm just as I told you last time." I strove to correct the coldness of my manner a moment before.

"It's not that I don't trust you," she said sorrowfully; "only I feel that the more you persuade her, the more, instead, you are persuaded by her."

These words seemed to lay bare what lay hidden under my own awareness, and made me see that my coldness today was not wholly for Mei Yingzi's affair, but that unawares I had been reflecting Helen's own hint. I felt shame and remorse, but I said:

"Believe me, I will never do anything to make you suffer, for I revere your great mother-love, and I too have a mother of my own."

She seemed to trust me a little, and, gazing at me with tearful eyes, said:

"Then persuade her tomorrow, and I'll come again at night to hear your answer."

"Good, so be it," I said. "Tomorrow I'll persuade her well."

And so Mrs. Manfield went quietly away. On her face was no longer last night's tearful smile, but a gloomy, deadening air. She let the flesh of her plump face hang slack, said "Good night" to me, and went out.

What a pitiable mother, spending herself so; in an instant I felt I must, for her sake, master myself.

Myself — yes; when Mrs. Manfield had gone out, buried in the sofa I thought first of her words: "…the more you persuade her, the more, instead, you are persuaded by her." I began to tremble. I felt that in talking with Helen today, from the very start, on the side of feeling I had been overcome by her; then I fell back within the bounds of reason and searched hard for arguments, but was at once broken there too, so that I became a captive waiting bound, with no power left to counterattack. Then tomorrow — tomorrow where should my words begin?

I had no way to answer, long, long no way to answer; and when a man is so hemmed in by his own question, the natural release is escape, and unawares I thought of Mei Yingzi again. It was already past twelve. Mei Yingzi would most likely not come now; by what force I know not — perhaps the heat kindled by all my pent-up gloom — it flashed on me in an instant that I ought to go and find Mei Yingzi.

But where to go and find her?

At once I thought of the "Standford."

For the moment I had no other thought; I took my scarf and hat and went out.

For some days I had not noticed that the green buds of the plane trees along the streets had turned to tender leaves, greener and fresher still under the street-lamps; when a breeze came, they trembled lightly, and their shadows on the ground were like dancing. There was not a soul in the street, and I walked treading on the leaf-shadows, hearing my own footsteps very clearly, and in an instant I seemed to have fled the plight of a moment before.

I walked past three streets before I met a rickshaw, and at a heavy fare had him pull me to Columbia Road.

Over this rather long road I felt the cold, and felt too the loneliness, and at last misgivings and fear leapt in my heart; several times I thought to get down, several times to turn back, and several times more to stop at a late-night eating house, but I said none of it.

"Can I really be so craven?" I asked myself.

"No!" I answered myself, and thought at once that, however I might change my course midway, once changed I should surely regret it again.

Reaching Columbia Road, my heart pounding, I directed the puller in through the bamboo-fence lane, and for an instant I was strained, keyed-up, and afraid. But at the sight of the brilliant lights and the neon of the Standford, my whole soul had but one strain in it.

Adventure is a thrill, and only a thrill can make one forget oneself.

So I sprang down, went in at the iron gate, crossed the court of red and green lights, went up the steps, and passed in at the door opened for me. I heard music, saw color, saw light, and smelled wave upon wave of fragrance. I checked my hat and scarf, and went in through the deep-hanging curtains. In an instant I felt released, the strain in my heart loosened.

This world still went on — the dim-colored lights, the sumptuous fittings. People, people, all people; the sound of people laughing, of people singing, of people talking, that seemed never, in all history, to have wearied!

I sat in the darkest, most out-of-the-way corner.

I did not go down to dance, but sat very composed, looking round about, hoping to find some trace of Miko.

When about three dance-tunes had passed, the lights on the music-stage came on, and someone announced Miss Miko's third number; there was thunderous applause, and I saw Miko come up from the right.

Just then I wrote a note and had a waiter carry it over!

"Is it the Sunset over the Huangpu?" so I wrote. "May I stand you a glass of wine at table sixty-three, lovely lady?"

I watched the waiter carry it over, watched Miko take it in her hand; she was just then singing a song in Japanese, and when the applause broke out at the song's end, I saw her read the note, and all at once a look of startled question came over her face, and with a floating glance she swept the direction where I sat; then, quite naturally, she said into the microphone:

"Someone has asked me for a Chinese song, 'Autumn Green amid the Cries of the Flower-Sellers' — I'll sing that first."

And so she sang; afterward she sang an English song too, and then, in the dark when the dancing was on, she came quietly up before me.

She had changed her clothes, and wore a very plain cheongsam; sitting sideways beside me, head tilted, she said:

"How have you come back?"

"Where is Mei…? I must see her."

"She's not here; she won't come."

"Where has she gone?"

"I don't know," she said. "They say a good many people are watching her, and she has had to keep out of the way for a time."

"Does anyone know where she is? — Mrs. Stephen? Dr. Philip?"

"Even if they knew, they wouldn't tell you; they don't wish to see you either. Haven't you left the work already? I thought you'd left Shanghai. Ah — you ought to leave soon too."

Miko, having said this much, went off, and I too paid at once. Through the color, through the fragrance, through the music and the laughter; I edged out past the soft velvet curtains, took my hat, and from the steps to the little court of red and green lights I saw, opposite, a row of gleaming motorcars.

This was my last farewell to the Standford; this was my last farewell to Miko.

At once I was adrift in a dark alley.

With a heart still as death I sat in the slow rickshaw and rode back to Route Prosper Paris.

When I reached the Manfields' door it was already twenty to five; Ah Mei opened for me, and asked in great surprise:

"Where have you been?"

"It's nothing," I said dejectedly. "They don't know — please say nothing of it."

Ah Mei looked at me with the deepest sympathy, and I stumbled into my bedroom.

Stephen and Bai Ping were long since parted from me by death; and now Mrs. Stephen — Mei Yingzi — was parted from me too by life. For work, for dreams, for love, for each one's standpoint and mission — meetings and partings, joys and sorrows; and in a world where no flower does not fade and no gathering does not break up, why do I still cling to the forms of this mortal life?

In this state of no-attachment I fell asleep, and woke to find it ten o'clock. I knew Mrs. Manfield had long since gone to work, and I mustered my courage and my words, ready, when I saw Helen, to give her the strongest persuasion.

But my heart was pounding, and as I went from the washroom to the sitting room I heard the sound of Helen's piano.

"Up late," said Helen, and the moment she heard me come in she rose from the piano-stool and turned her head to say it.

"Yes," I said. "I could not sleep last night."

In an instant I saw Helen: again in that yellow-and-brown-check cheongsam, her soft golden hair setting off a smile brimming with spirit, the light of her eyes flashing, as if she had already seen clean through my thoughts of a moment before. I bowed my head; I felt dizzy, and all the courage and words of a moment before had wholly vanished.

"— The more you persuade her, the more, instead, you are persuaded by her!" This line came to me at once, and not only did I not dare to raise the matter with her, I was in constant fear that she would raise it with me.

Just then what drew my eyes was the diamond ring on her hand, that diamond ring Bai Ping had sent expressly for her. I said:

"Will you play on for me?"

In the sound of the piano I felt deeply that, after the death-partings and the life-partings, I was like a ghost with nothing to lean on, a soul lost in the black night, a wretch cast down in the desert; I was like an abandoned infant, shivering with cold, crying with hunger; I needed something to lean on, I needed support, and Helen was my only light.

But in this sound of the piano, too, there grew in me a firmer resolve.
