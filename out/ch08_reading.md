## Chapter 8. Death Throes

In the eleventh month of that year, the most shocking thing of Tokugawa Ieyasu's whole life broke over his household.

It was the defection of Ishikawa Kazumasa.

Ishikawa Kazumasa stood with Sakai Tadatsugu as one of the two great elders of the Tokugawa house. He was a hereditary retainer of the old stamp. When Ieyasu was six years old and traveling to become a hostage of the Imagawa, the Oda snatched him on the road and carried him off to Atsuta; Kazumasa was at his side even then. Ever after he joined every battle Ieyasu fought, and never fought without earning distinction. He was the first of all the Tokugawa's meritorious men.

At Mikatagahara above all, the battle lost against Takeda Shingen, it was Kazumasa's own troops who knelt as one, planted their knees, made a hedge of their spears, and would not yield a step, and gave the Takeda force no end of grief.

So Ieyasu set him to hold Okazaki, the most important of all his castles, and left the vital hostages in his keeping.

And that same Kazumasa, on the thirteenth of the eleventh month, took his wife and children and household, took the hostages he was charged with, and suddenly fled. He went to Osaka and gave himself to Hideyoshi.

Why in the world did Ishikawa Kazumasa betray Ieyasu and run to Hideyoshi? Even in an age of warring states this was a dreadful thing, and it staggered the whole world.

I think the truth is that, over the long years, Kazumasa was little by little charmed away by Hideyoshi, that master seducer of men.

As Ieyasu's envoy Kazumasa met Hideyoshi again and again. When, in Tenshō 12, Ieyasu sent his second son Ogimaru to Hideyoshi as a hostage, it was Kazumasa who went with him, as this novel has already told. And each time Kazumasa came to him as Ieyasu's envoy, Hideyoshi worked at buying the man's goodwill.

When Ieyasu and Hideyoshi fought at Komaki, for instance, Hideyoshi took the trouble to send a messenger into the camp of the enemy Ishikawa Kazumasa. "Your golden horse-standard is a splendid sight," he proposed. "Might I have it, as a memento of our contest?"

"By all means, by all means," said Kazumasa, and had the standard carried over to Hideyoshi.

Then Hideyoshi, calling it a token of thanks, sent back a handsome sum of money. Kazumasa naturally hesitated, and asked Ieyasu what he should do. Ieyasu told him it was no matter, keep it; but Kazumasa, unwilling to have anyone think ill of it, returned the money to Hideyoshi.

Such things piled one on another. And in the many negotiations between Ieyasu and Hideyoshi it happened, again and again, that whatever Kazumasa advised turned out to favor Hideyoshi's side. Little by little the whole body of Ieyasu's chief retainers came to watch him with suspicion.

Hemmed in by suspicious eyes, he longed at last to breathe under a clear sky again; he could bear it no longer; and better, he thought, simply to run to Hideyoshi. So it seems he swallowed the bait Hideyoshi held out to him: come to me, and I will make you a daimyo of a hundred thousand koku.

At the time of the Kishū campaign Hideyoshi had said he would let Ogimaru watch the fighting, and why did Kazumasa not come along too? With Ieyasu's leave he had followed Ogimaru and looked on at the storming of the Negoro-ji and the water-attack on Ōta Castle. He had been treated with high honor, and struck by the imposing might of Hideyoshi's army; and afterward, as he watched the campaigns in Shikoku and the northern provinces unfold, he must have felt more and more surely that the first man under Heaven was Hideyoshi.

However it was, from Tenshō 12 into Tenshō 13 Kazumasa had been kept close at Hideyoshi's side while Hideyoshi made his soaring leap into the sky, and it is not altogether wrong to call the defection no wonder.

But this great gamble of Kazumasa's was, in the end, a great failure.

As Ieyasu's man he had held a place all his own. On Hideyoshi's side he was a raw newcomer, and however hard Hideyoshi favored him, the resistance around him ran strong. The world despised him too. On the gate-doors of his Kyoto mansion were pasted lampoons, several of them, like these:

{p} The old broom handed down in Ieyasu's house:
{p} fallen from it now, it sweeps beneath the tree.

{p} Ieyasu's cast-off broom, worn to the stump:
{p} come up to the capital, not worth a speck of dust.

They played on his title, Hōki-no-kami, for hōki is also the word for a broom.

Hideyoshi, having carried Kazumasa off, felt he must set Ieyasu's temper right again. He would marry his own younger sister to Ieyasu and tie the two of them as brothers-in-law. Ieyasu, who had been badly burned seven years before by the Tsukiyama affair, had gone on living a widower's life.

But the only sister Hideyoshi had was a half-sister by another father, Asahi-hime. And Asahi-hime was forty-three years old, and was moreover the wife of a country samurai of Owari, Saji Hyūga-no-kami.

None of which was enough to daunt Hideyoshi. He promptly ordered Hyūga-no-kami to divorce Asahi-hime, and to sweeten it raised the man's stipend by five hundred koku. Hyūga-no-kami said, "If it is a command, there is no help for it. To divorce my wife—"

"—but to draw a stipend for it would be the very depth of dishonor." And with that he killed himself.

In the fourth month of the next year, Tenshō 14, 1586, Ieyasu sent an envoy up to the capital and the betrothal gifts were exchanged. On the fourteenth of the fifth month Asahi-hime entered Hamamatsu in a splendid train of twelve palanquins and fifteen hanging litters. The bridegroom Ieyasu, at forty-five, was a year older than his bride.

Brothers-in-law they now were; but Ieyasu was in no hurry to go up to the capital. Go up rashly and he might be cut down in the dark. And short of that, he would be made to perform the ritual of submission before the eyes of every lord in the realm.

For Hideyoshi, who had the conquest of Kyūshū in mind, the pressing business now was to bring off one or the other, and be rid of all anxiety on his eastern flank. So in the eighth month he sent Ieyasu a letter. "I will send you my own birth-mother, the Ōmandokoro, whom I hold beyond all price, to Hamamatsu as a hostage, and without a single attendant to wait on her. Only come up to the capital, a short visit, to see me."

Pressed as far as that, Ieyasu had no choice, and agreed to go.

Hideyoshi sent the Ōmandokoro to Okazaki in Mikawa. On the eighteenth of the tenth month Ieyasu set Ii Naomasa, Okubo Tadayo, Honda Tadakatsu, Sakakibara Yasumasa, Honda Shigetsugu and others to guard Okazaki castle; and on the twentieth he himself went up west, taking Sakai Tadatsugu, Honda Tadakatsu and Sakakibara Yasumasa, and lodged at the Osaka mansion of Hideyoshi's brother Hidenaga. That night Hideyoshi came to Ieyasu's lodging, took him by the hands and wept. "How good of you to have come." And he begged him, most earnestly, to make the retainer's obeisance before him the next day, in front of all the assembled daimyo. Ieyasu knew this too for one of Hideyoshi's practiced tricks, and still could not refuse.

The next day, at Osaka castle, among the ranked daimyo, Ieyasu took the lower place and bowed, and Hideyoshi said grandly, "Tokugawa Mikawa-no-kami: a hard road up to the capital, and well traveled." After a stay of just six days Ieyasu withdrew.

At Okazaki, meanwhile, Ii Naomasa and Honda Shigetsugu had satisfied themselves that neither mother nor sister was a decoy. Even so they left nothing to chance. They kept the guard heavy, they piled firewood high around her lodging, and they made ready, should any harm come to Ieyasu in the Kamigata, to burn the Ōmandokoro alive on the instant. But on the eleventh of the eleventh month Ieyasu came home safe, and they sent the Ōmandokoro back to Osaka.

That same year, on the fourth of the fifth month, the Vice-Provincial of the Jesuits in Japan, Gaspar Coelho, came before Hideyoshi at Osaka castle. He brought with him four padres, four irmãos, fifteen dōjuku, and several seminary boys, more than thirty in all.

The occasion is described at length by that same Padre Luis Frois, who was in the party, in a report he sent, dated the seventeenth of the tenth month of that year by the Western calendar, to Valignano, the Superior of the Indies. It is a precious document, both for what Osaka castle was in those days and for Hideyoshi's bearing toward the Kirishitan, and I will pass on at least its main points.

Before entering the castle we waited a while at the mansion of Takayama Ukon. Beforehand we had made presents to the Kanpaku, to his consort, his mother, and several of his adopted sons; and while he looked the presents over we were kept waiting in a splendid building hung with tiger-skins and Chinese furs. Just then Maeda Toshiie, Hosokawa Tadaoki and other lords were in attendance, and the Kanpaku, wishing to show them how he received the padres, had them stay and sit by.

The Kanpaku was seated at the far end of a long room, facing us. As a secretary called each of us aloud by name, we took our places by the entrance door. His features we could not make out clearly. He bade us come forward into the room, and he called Takayama Ukon and told him, "You are a Kirishitan; go and sit by the padres." To Takayama, and to us, it was a signal favor. Fruit was served; and to the astonishment of us all the Kanpaku came down from his seat and settled himself not half a mat's width from the padres, and began to talk.

Several times over he said, "That the padres here in Japan give their minds to nothing but their preaching is greatly to be praised." Then he went on: "I too have conquered all Japan and come to the place I now hold, and I have gold and silver in plenty, so there is nothing I lack. All that is left me is to leave, after my death, a great name and the fame of my power. To that end I have made up my mind to hand Japan over to Hashiba Hidenaga and to cross the sea and conquer Korea and China. I mean to fell timber and build two thousand ships and carry my armies across. What I would ask of the padres is two great ships, fully rigged; and I want good crews for them. I will pay a fair price, of course—"

At so preposterous a thing the whole company took it, at first, for a jest or a piece of bluster; but seeing he was in earnest they held their breath and listened. "If I die on the campaign," he said, "it is no matter to me in the least. And if I win, I have no thought of holding the country and staying there; I mean only to bring it under my hand. And I wish to make half of Japan, or the most of it, Kirishitan." At these astonishing words the company was struck dumb.

Then, with his own hand as our guide, he showed us over the castle. Come along, he said, and I will show you; and he rose. When we had seen many fine rooms sheathed in gold, and gorgeous gardens, we came to the foot of the great keep. The Kanpaku himself opened the doors and windows and led us up as far as the eighth story, and where we had to stoop beneath a low beam to climb he warned each of us in turn to take care not to strike our heads on the beam above. As we mounted, he unlocked with his own hand every closed door and told us of the treasure stored inside. "In the room you see here are swords and dirks and other fine weapons. In that room yonder are gold, silver, raw silk, damask and the vessels of the tea ceremony, in abundance."

Passing one room, we saw ten or twenty new crimson cloaks of the European sort hung on a stretched cord; and he pointed out a great number of locked chests, immensely long. We looked at them and were amazed, for within stood two or three beds decked in gold and draped with woven covers far finer than the costly bed-hangings used in Europe, a thing we could not have imagined. In one of the rooms we passed, more than ten new scarlet cloaks hung from cords. In Japan there is no custom of sleeping in beds, yet here were two beds of the very costly European kind, with gold-embroidered quilts laid over them. He showed us, too, a room built entirely of gold, of the knock-down sort; it had stood assembled until the day before, but had just been taken apart and packed into its boxes, to our disappointment. (This was the famous Golden Tea Room.)

Every secret place he showed to us Kirishitan alone. On the topmost floor a gallery ran round the outside, and from it he bade us look out over the castle works and over a plain that stretched across four or five provinces. Then he went down another stair and led us into a still more private chamber, showed us where he commonly slept, and opened the doors of the closet where his everyday clothes were kept. There he sat a while, took up a wine-cup and gave it to a padre with his own hand, and had two more cups brought, and from each in turn he drank a little and poured the rest away into the rinsing-bowl, saying that by the custom of Japan this was the same as giving a cup to every man. So the cup went round among the padres and the irmãos. He took up morsels with his chopsticks, too, and gave them out by his own hand. This favor and this welcome ran to such an extreme that those who saw it said no king or prince had ever been shown a third as much—

So Hideyoshi treated the Kirishitan with high favor. That a bare year later he would turn his hand over and hand down an order forbidding it, not one soul so much as dreamed.

At the end of that year Hideyoshi was made Grand Minister of State, received the surname Toyotomi, and had climbed to a place above which there was no higher; and on the first of the third month he set out from Osaka for the conquest of Kyūshū. It was a war settled in his favor from the start, and for Hideyoshi the campaign was little more than a pleasure-outing. At the end of the third month he crossed the sea into Kyūshū; in the fourth he pushed from Kumamoto to Yatsushiro and Minamata; and when in the fifth month he came on into Satsuma, Shimazu Yoshihisa sued for surrender, shaved his head and became a monk. Hideyoshi pardoned him and gave him back, as before, Ōsumi, Satsuma and Hyūga for his domain.

Then he returned to Hakata. He had never seen the Portuguese ship that lay in at Hirado, and he sent word to ask whether it might sail round to Hakata for him. The Portuguese captain replied that to bring the ship round would be difficult, but that he would come to pay his respects, and so came up to Hakata. Hideyoshi, seeing a Portuguese for the first time, was greatly pleased. He guaranteed free trade with Portugal, and went further, and let it be known that he wished the ships henceforth to put in not at Nagasaki or Hirado, as before, but all the way in to Sakai. It tells us plainly that, having conquered Kyūshū, he meant now to keep the profits of the Portugal trade for himself alone.

And yet that very same night, wholly without warning, Hideyoshi proclaimed the ban on the Kirishitan.

"Japan is the Land of the Gods, and the false law is not to be suffered. To break the shrines and temples is an outrage past speaking of. The padres shall quit the country within twenty days from this day. Trading ships, being a matter of commerce, are another thing, and may come freely; and merchants, needless to say, and any who do not offend against the laws of Japan, may go and come from the Kirishitan lands as they will."

Why Hideyoshi should turn so suddenly no one could tell, and the missionaries were thunderstruck. In the reckoning of Frois, who had served as interpreter at the meeting with the Portuguese captain, it came to something like this. (From his report of 1587.)

"The Kanpaku, being now become lord of the realm of Japan, gave himself, like Nero, to the many vices he had until then kept hidden, and let his lust for women run without a check. It is said that within Osaka castle, besides his lawful wife, he keeps some three hundred concubines; that he keeps still other women at the many castles about the capital and about Osaka, against the times he visits them; and that, more than this, he sends men through every province of Japan to seek out girls of good looks, and, whatever their station, even the daughters of the great lords, forces them and has them carried to him. Of these brokers of hell the most notorious was a former monk of Mount Hiei, Maeda Tokuun, called also Seyakuin Zensō; who, having word that the women of the southern half of Hizen were beautiful, went there on his errand, and, when several Kirishitan girls he found and tried to lure firmly refused him and hid themselves away, fell into a great rage and denounced them, that they would not serve the lord under Heaven.

And he said, further, that Lord Takayama Ukon was gathering soldiers and sworn men about him and hatching some design of his own."

It was this Maeda Tokuun who, that night, when the Portuguese and the padres had gone and he sat drinking wine with Hideyoshi, poured all this into his ear; and Hideyoshi flew into a fury.

"Hideyoshi's lechery and misrule," Frois goes on, "are so gross that a man might think his palaces, scattered here and there, had every one turned into a brothel. No comely girl escapes him. The daughters of the leading nobles, too, he first takes as adopted daughters, and when they turn twelve makes them his concubines. This procuring of women is in the charge of Maeda Tokuun, a man near seventy years old, the foremost monk of Mount Hiei and a grave enemy of the Kirishitan. Even the daughters of the court kuge he summons through this Tokuun; and the traffic came in the end to be so shamelessly abused that the lords, every one of them, to hold on to their domains, took to offering up their own daughters unbidden. Not a single girl is spared." Organtino writes as much in his letters too.

The Kanpaku sent at once to Takayama Ukon, the foremost of the Kirishitan daimyo, and ordered him either to give up the faith or quit his lands on the instant. Ukon answered the envoy that give up the faith he could not; if for that His Highness would drive him out, he would take his banishment and hand back his domain. The envoy, and his friends as well, urged him to answer on the surface, "I renounce the Kirishitan faith," and keep his belief in his heart; but Ukon would not bend. "I mean to speak the plain truth," he said.

At this answer Hideyoshi grew angrier still, and put his questions to the padres at Hakata. Why do you pull down the shrines and temples, and persecute the monks, and refuse to live at peace with them? Why do you kill and eat the ox and the horse, that are of such use to men? And why do the Portuguese buy up so many Japanese and carry them off as slaves?

The padres answered courteously. As to the third charge, it was the Japanese themselves who sold their own people; the padres grieved over it, and had labored to stop it, and could not. Since it was the Japanese lords and heathen who did the selling, let His Highness forbid it outright and punish the sellers with heavy penalties, and it would cease.

Even from this it is certain that in those days great numbers of Japanese were sold abroad as slaves. That Kazue too might be among them, the fear of Yamatoya Tarōbei of Sakai, was a thing all too possible.

The Kanpaku's sudden edict against the faith threw the Kirishitan of all Japan into dismay. Ukon left his house with his family, wandered a while, and withdrew to the island of Shōdoshima in the Inland Sea. The seminary at Takatsuki, which came into this story earlier, was pulled down. The padres, each of them, went to ground.

The edict reached even the Shōfuku-ji, in the hamlet of Azōda on the outskirts of Ueno town in Iga, where Hattori Hanzō came now and then to worship. It was a temple in a backwater corner where a ruler's eye had no business reaching. But six years before, the image of the Maria there had worked its miracle, taking on its own back the shuriken and the grappling-rope meant for Hanzō, and bleeding in his stead; and ever since, its name had carried far, and many came to bow before "the blood-dyed cloth of the Holy Mother Mary"—which was in truth the blood that had run from Hanzō's earlobe.

At the start of the seventh month an old beggar-woman came to the Shōfuku-ji. Sister Kiara, who kept the temple—that is, Tamo, once a kunoichi—came out to look, and said, "A—"
h! The holy man Lourenço! What has become of you?"

Lourenço worked his dry lips. "A grave thing. The Kirishitan faith is proscribed. Not the European padres and irmãos only, but the Japanese irmãos as well, are all to leave Japan; and any who stay are to be killed. So the Kanpaku has commanded."

At that the two of them took the Maria image and the blood-dyed cloth and hid them, deep behind a hanging in the priests' quarters, in a hollow cut into the wall, and boarded it shut. In their place they dusted off the old Dainichi Nyorai and set it back on the pedestal. Lourenço took up again his lay name, Ryōsai, and Kiara took the name Tamo.

To old Ryōsai this blow told hard. That night he sat cradling the biwa he never put down, and in the moonlight he played—biin, biin, jararan—and even to Tamo's eyes he looked forlorn. His sight was almost gone now, even the right eye that was left to him, and he had to feel his way as he walked.

The next day Tamo left Ryōsai behind and went up to Kyoto. She wore a deep sedge-hat and carried a pack with a wooden pillow in it. On the fourth day she reached the capital.

Do you remember, reader? One evening in the third month, six years ago, a hideous woman-beggar, scarred by an old burn, came tottering out from under the Gojō Bridge and walked west from Kawaramachi toward Tako-Yakushi Street. (That was how this novel opened.)

That was Tamo. In the mountains south of Hōjiro in Iga she had fallen into the flames locked in the arms of her lover Kashii; and when she came to, Kashii had burned to death, and it was his body over hers that had saved her life. She had lost every hope she had, and sunk to a beggar-woman. Now, as she stepped into Tako-Yakushi Street, she heard again the bell she had heard that day. Unlike any other bell, it was light and high, a glad sound, a sound like laughter.

Tamo heard that strange bell again now. Drawn by it, she passed through a gate like a Chinese gate, crossed a forecourt walled with pale-green plaster, and, folded in the newly risen chorus of boys singing the Ave Maria, came to the three-storied tower that fronted her. Father Organtino had comforted her, and in time she had come to live there. It was the church whose true name was Our Lady of the Assumption, the one the people called the Nanban-ji.

She went to the spot where that dear building should have stood in view, and cried "Ah!" and ran to it.

It was an empty lot now, with nothing left standing, and the last laborer was just then hauling off, with a rattle of wheels, a cart heaped with broken tiles and earth. The Nanban-ji was gone.

Tamo let the tears well up and run as they would, and, leaning against the wall of the next house so no one would wonder at her, she offered a prayer under her breath. "Lord Jesus Christ, who to save all mankind did shed thy blood upon the cross, and Mary, thy Mother. Those who once sang thy praise, five days after are turned enemy, and instead
heap upon thee shame and contempt—yet thou, O Lord, standest ever firm. How wretched is man, whose heart is never fixed; even so. Because we, in the depths of this vale of tears, give ourselves to grief and lamentation, this is my fault, my own deep fault. Because we strive to hold our hearts ever firm, lend us thy strength and grant us thine aid. Grant us, we beseech thee, life everlasting in Heaven, amen—"

After lingering a while, loath to go, Tamo trudged back the way she had come, off toward Iga.

Her faith, it seemed, nothing on earth could shake now. She had caught hold of it at the very bottom of her desolation, when the will to live had drained out of her. And in that day it was the most advanced view of the world, the most advanced moral law, that there was.

Watching her go, you would find no trace of the fierce quickness that once, when she was twelve or thirteen, spun her four times through the air off a zelkova branch in the "four-legged practice" and set her down on the ground without a sound, and left Kashii lost for words. When she was burned in the hills of Iga she had surely done some harm to her insides as well; and her life since had been ill-fed. She will not live long. But she will end her days happy, and on that we may be easy. The unburned half of her face is swollen now. She gasps for breath, and painfully; and yet, when she draws her last, she will die believing that eternal bliss in the haraiso—Paradise—is waiting for her, that the Lady Mary reached out her arms to gather her to her breast. And though in truth there is nothing, and she goes back to dust, and Tamo lives on only in this story—still, she will be able to die thinking herself happy. In those days there was no hoping for more than that.

While Tamo was away in Kyoto, Hanzō came to the Shōfuku-ji for the first time in a long while. When he heard from Lourenço how they had swapped the temple's honzon, he said:

"Hmph. That baboon of a Kanpaku, the old monkey-lecher—balked of his concubine-hunting, he's blown his top, sure as anything. First man under Heaven, and not a wish of his crossed. No telling which way he'll run mad from here. If he doesn't drop dead soon, the whole realm and every soul in it is in for grief, and my own lord's in a tight place too. Still, a crazed old man's a crazed old man; this latest ban won't hold long either. And here in the mountains, least of all, there's nothing to fear. Lie low a while, keep your head down, and the storm will blow itself out."

He had them take off the wall-board, bowed before the Maria image, then bolted off to Chigachi. He ran into the house, shouting "Chika!—Chika!", took in his arms that body of hers, grown fuller than ever, had his will of her, and sprang out again. He went up into the pine wood on the hill behind and dropped down flat on the grass where the sun could not reach, and there, half-dozing, he gave himself to his thoughts. This was Hanzō's road, in body and in mind alike, for setting up the conditions in which some idea or other would float to the surface.

"My master, last tenth month, went down to Osaka at Hideyoshi's asking and made the retainer's bow before them all; but ever since he's kept himself level with Hideyoshi. And Hideyoshi, for his part,
grows more and more swollen-headed, and wallows in women; his hunger for power, for goods, for pleasure, breeds without end. Before Osaka castle is even finished he has begun raising the Jurakudai in Kyoto. That one he is building close by the palace, to live in himself; and while forty thousand men labor on Osaka castle, they say he has even more than that at work here. By Moriichirō's reports—I have him set to watch Hideyoshi—it is to be a mansion of a luxury never seen before. The daimyo given a share of the work must haul laborers and timber from far provinces. And not that alone: they are ordered to raise, in a ring about the Jurakudai, new mansions of their own, grander even than the ones they keep at Osaka. To squeeze out the cost the peasants of their lands are wrung dry, and the laborers are worked for nothing. And on top of all this the Kanpaku, at his camp in Kyūshū, let slip that he means to conquer Korea and the great Ming. Surely not, I tell myself. But an old man with the power of the realm in his fist—there is no knowing what he may take it into his head to do. My master need only bear it a little longer. The grand design has not changed—to wait, wrapped in the curses of all the land, for that man to drop dead. But now that a head-on war is out of the question, what must be seen to is the strengthening of the ninja corps. And the place to fix one's eye, for that, is Kōga."

"Kōga! It's Kōga!" Hanzō cried out before he knew it. He sat bolt upright and folded his arms. Kōga—Kōga—

Name the two great schools of ninjutsu and anyone will count off the Kōga school and the Iga school on his fingers. The feud between Kōga ninja and Iga ninja is a stock theme of most ninja fiction. From of old the two have been thought irreconcilable in every particular. Yet the researches of Okuse Heishichirō have made it plain that both the Kōga and the Iga schools take as their secret manual the same Bansenshūkai, in which their art was gathered up, and that their techniques, but for the most trifling points, are one and the same.

By geography too they are a single stretch of land, with no boundary river or ridge between. Kōga Saburō Kaneie, of the late Kamakura age—his grave stands in the precinct of the Aikuni Shrine in Kōga—held that whole region when it was not yet split into Kōga and Iga. Ninjutsu arose under one set of conditions, at one time, and only at the last stage of its birth divided in two.

Still, in the temper of the ninja, and in the make-up of ninja society, a difference between the two must be allowed. In Kōga there are mostly chūnin. In Iga there are jōnin, chūnin and genin, and of these the genin are the most numerous by far. And so with temper as well. In Kōga the chūnin houses, each keeping several dozen genin, held their own good-sized manors, call them forts, and fifty-three such families, local magnates all, stood side by side at their ease. In Iga, as we have seen, the genin were ranked strictly under the chūnin and the chūnin under the jōnin, so that a chain of subjection was built up in which a man's very right to live was held in another's hand; and at the top of it stood the two great jōnin houses of Momochi and Fujibayashi. Out of that a dark and pitiless character was formed.

The cause of the difference lies, first of all, in the lay of the land. Kōga slopes gently north from the hill country that runs on out of Iga, down into the Ōmi plain—in those days the richest granary in Japan—and dips at last into Lake Biwa, thick with fish. Across the heart of Kōga, east and west, runs the ancient highroad that ties the eastern provinces to the western. Along the east shore of Lake Biwa runs the road to the northern provinces, loud with the passage of men and horses; and on the lake itself boats ply in every direction.

All of it is very different from the shut-in, threadbare Iga hills. That the Iga ninja should be stubborn, obstinate, quick to suspect, while the Kōga ninja have a certain ease of mind and a shrewd, worldly turn, is only what one would expect. Most of their manors—forts, if you like—stand not deep in steep mountains, as Momochi's and Fujibayashi's do, but on open flats or gentle slopes, ringed with earthworks and moats, with woods and thickets. And where Iga was in name only the estate of temples and shrines, Kōga lay under a warrior power, the rule of the shugo of Ōmi. That bears on it too.

At Ryūhōshi in the district of Kōga stands the Mochizuki house, foremost of the fifty-three families, which claims descent from Kōga Saburō. Today it has become quite a sight, the "Ninjutsu House," and draws crowds of tourists, they say; but when I went, in 1960, it was still quiet. The present head runs the Ōmi Pharmaceutical Company and markets a cure-all called Ninjutsu-gan. On open ground a broad hedge of trees runs round, and six buildings stand about a courtyard. The main house looks to be a single story; step inside and you find it partitioned into three. There are any number of rooms so low you must stoop to enter, reached from within a closet; you can look out through a wire-netted window, and by pulling a clapper-cord send word up to the top floor. In the closet of the inner room on the ground floor, throw back a trapdoor and the mouth of a tunnel opens. The passage seems to have run under a stone gutter, out from beneath a workshop and clear away outside. In the storehouse of a separate wing were special doors, so contrived that to open the one was to lock the other.

And yet a ninja whose work took him out and abroad can have had small need to fit his own house with such contrivances. The truth is that the present building went up in the middle Tokugawa period, and the researchers hold that the Mochizuki house very likely turned Kirishitan for a time, and that all this was to secure a secret meeting-place. In the same class as Torii Moriichirō of Ōhara—the man who, under Hanzō, was spying out the doings of Hideyoshi and Goemon—there had been a grave, steady youth named Ukon, third son of Taki Shirobei of Aburahi in Kōga, who, as I told earlier, was sent to the seminary at Azuchi; and he, the hunter turned quarry, gave himself heart and soul to the Kirishitan faith. Long afterward his daughter married into the Mochizuki house. Perhaps the key to the riddle lies just there.

"Kōga!" Hanzō said it aloud once more, and, sitting bolt upright, went home to Hamamatsu without a backward look. With Ieyasu's leave he sent his own men into Kōga—ninja who went by such names as the Green-Leaf Coronet, the Waterwheel at the Rapids, the River-Wind that Draws the Waves—to spy out the chūnin of the fifty-three families. Then he went himself, in full ceremony, laden with gifts, again and again into Kōga.

All this while, the work on Osaka castle and the work on the Jurakudai were driven hard ahead. By Padre Organtino's report: "Forced to labor on the raising of the two castles, at Osaka and at Kyoto,
and to tell over the hurt that all of them bear, the toil of body and of spirit, the burden of costs beyond their station, would run on without end. The poor townsfolk are ground down under conscriptions and levies that fall on them thick and unheard-of. What this tyrant does is no thing a man does; it is baser than the beasts. Every one of them alike suffers his oppression, wrung dry to yield him gold and silver; and every day one hears fresh rumor of some new contrivance he has hit upon to bleed the people. That he sends men into exile for no cause at all, whatever their rank, comes of his having not a grain of love for his neighbor. The other day, they say, he ordered the charcoal-burners' hills seized. On those hills poor farmers and paupers had cut their firewood to live; and now, families and all, they are sunk into the very bottom of want."

Built on such sacrifice, the Jurakudai was finished in the summer of Tenshō 15, and in the ninth month Hideyoshi moved into it. How its splendor was carried to the last degree is dwelt on at length in the padres' reports of the day. "The outside of the building is faced with gold-leaf and silver-leaf and set over that with fine carving; on the bordered roof-tiles and along the ridge, figures of men and fish and birds and beasts are worked in gold; and in hall after gold-covered hall, on fragrant wood, hang paintings of every kind. Every room that is a room, every hall that is a hall, inside and out, above and below, down even to the kitchen—all is gilt with gold; and even to the shelves where the kitchen vessels stand and the meals are laid out, all is gilt with gold. How these dwellings are carried to the height of cleanliness and elegance, none can know but one who has seen them with his own eyes." (The writer of this report, Alessandro Valignano, having come over as envoy of the Viceroy of the Indies, was able in Tenshō 19 to go up to the Jurakudai in person and see it for himself.)

When Hideyoshi moved in, Toshiie's daughter Maa-hime, as one of his acknowledged concubines, was given a residence within the walls and came to be called Kaga-no-tsubone. And before long Ochacha too came to live there.

Ochacha you know well, reader. She was born to Nobunaga's younger sister Oichi and to Azai Nagamasa; but their castle, Odani, was stormed and taken by Nobunaga, and Nagamasa killed himself and burned in the flames, and she, with her mother Oichi and her two little sisters, Hatsu-hime and Gō-hime, was carried out alive. She was six then. Afterward Oichi took her three daughters and married Shibata Katsuie; and this time it was Hideyoshi who stormed them to their ruin, and Oichi and her husband killed themselves and perished in the flames of Kita-no-shō, and again she and her two sisters were carried out alive. She was seventeen then. So Ochacha and her two sisters passed under the power of life and death of the very Hideyoshi who had killed by war her father, her stepfather and her mother, and had run her little brother Manpukumaru through on a stake; and under his keeping they were lodged in Azuchi castle.

Ochacha's sister Gō-hime, four years the younger, was married in the autumn of Tenshō 14, at sixteen, by Hideyoshi's order, to Saji Yokurō, lord of Ōno castle in Owari and holder of sixty thousand koku—a young warrior of only eighteen, and her own cousin. And in the autumn of the next year, Tenshō 15, her sister Hatsu-hime, two years the younger, was married to Kyōgoku Takatsugu, lord of Ōmizo with ten thousand koku. So Ochacha was left alone in the Jurakudai.

In the spring of Tenshō 16, when the cherry that had decked all Kyoto had gone to leaf, the Jurakudai was thrown into a whirl by an Imperial progress. For five days the Emperor made merry there, and set the last seal of prestige on the house of Toyotomi. One night, after the whirl had died down, Ochacha was summoned to Hideyoshi's bedchamber, and without a breath of resistance became the concubine of the old lecher who should have been her mortal, unpardonable enemy.

About this time this old lecher kept, apart from the countless women called in for a single night, no fewer than sixteen acknowledged concubines. His lawful wife, Nene, had married him back when he was still a captain of foot under Nobunaga, before he had so much as the name Kinoshita Tōkichirō, perhaps: a shrewd woman, but childless. It was this Nene who, around Tenshō 4 or 5, in her jealousy, carried her complaint to Nobunaga; but in the end there was too much to be jealous of, and she gave it up.

Ten years on, in Keichō 3, 1598, the year Hideyoshi died, he held, five months before his death, a great blossom-viewing feast at the Sanbō-in in Daigo. The record-keeper Ōta Izumi-no-kami Gyūichi left an account of it. By it, the order of the palanquins of the women in Hideyoshi's train—that is, the ranking of wife and concubines—ran: first, of course, the Mandokoro-sama, his lawful wife Nene; second, Ochacha, then styled the Nishi-no-maru-sama; third, the Matsu-no-maru-sama; fourth, the Sannomaru-sama; fifth, the Kaga-sama. The Matsu-no-maru-sama was the elder sister of that Kyōgoku Takatsugu who married Lady Yodo's sister Hatsu-hime, and was famed for her beauty. The Sannomaru-sama was the fifth daughter of his onetime lord Oda Nobunaga; and the Kaga-sama was Maa-hime, daughter of Maeda Toshiie. Other well-known concubines were the Sanjō-sama, daughter of Gamō Katahide; the Himeji-sama, daughter of Nobunaga's brother Nobukane; the Nokai-hime, daughter of Hōjō Ujinaga; and the O-Tane-sama, daughter of a Fushimi country samurai, Takada Jirōuemon.

Every one of them, save O-Tane-sama, was the daughter of a former overlord or of a daimyo ranked above him. One reads in it the mark of his complex over the meanness of his birth: now that he was "lord of all under Heaven," he used his power to hunt out girls of just this kind. With O-Tane-sama alone he gave rank no thought at all—a strange contrast, for a man sprung of nobody, of stock that had scrubbed the bath-house floors.

In the autumn of Tenshō 16 Ochacha was already with child. Hideyoshi, at fifty-three, with a child of his own body for the first time, leapt for joy; and in the first month of the next year, Tenshō 17, he resolved to build a castle for her.

Last year, in February 1963, I was surveying the Yamashiro country to write the libretto for the opera The Yamashiro Uprising, commissioned by the Tokyo Workers' Music Council, and I took it into my head to go and see the place they call the site of Yodo castle. It lies southwest of Kyoto, in a plot of ground between the Uji River and the Katsura, just south of Yodo station on the Keihan Main Line. A stone marker reading "Site of Yodo Castle" stands there, and the plot, ringed by old stone walls and what little is left of the moat, is now the precinct of a shrine called the Yodo Shrine—the Yodo-hime Shrine, in common speech. I had taken it for the site of the Yodo castle Hideyoshi built. But a Mr. Sakai Yukimasa, a teacher at the Meishin Municipal Elementary School in Kyoto and a resident of Yodo, who had read my story "The Capture of Goemon" in the May number of Rekishi Dokuhon, was kind enough to write to me. By his account, the present Yodo-castle site is not the Yodo castle Hideyoshi built. That one the Tokugawa shogunate raised, under the Genna law of one castle to a province, as the castle of Yamashiro; and on the eve of the Meiji Restoration, in the first month of Keiō 4, at the battle of Toba-Fushimi,
when the beaten Shinsengumi tried to hole up in it, the rōjū Inaba Mino-no-kami, who held the castle, had already surrendered and refused to lend it to them, so they had no choice but to flee all the way to Osaka—that is the Yodo castle that stands now. After the domains were abolished it was pulled down, and came to look as it does today.

Hideyoshi's Yodo castle was not this one. It stood, they say, some four hundred meters to the north, near the precinct of the Myōkyō-ji in Nōso-chō. No trace of it is left, but the ground was opened up long ago. Mizutare, on the right bank of the Katsura, and Ōshimozu to its west were called Yodo-no-tsu in the age of the Heian court, and the regental house kept a manor-quarter there. By Mr. Sakai's research, a castle first appears here in the record in Eishō 1, 1504, when Yakushiji Yoichi, a vassal of the Kanrei Hosokawa Masamoto, revolted and holed up in it, and Masamoto stormed it and took it. It figures in the Heike Monogatari and the Taiheiki as well. In time the Nōso on the far bank—the name comes from a manor's tax-collection storehouse that once stood there—was opened up too, and came to be called Higashi-Yodo, the East Yodo, over against Mizutare, the West. After that, in 1559 the Kanrei Hosokawa Ujitsuna entered here; and in '65 one of the Miyoshi Triumvirate, Iwanari Chikara-no-suke Tomomichi, with Matsunaga Danjō and the rest, killed the shogun Yoshiteru and came in. In '73 that same Iwanari holed up here and was stormed out by Nagaoka Fujitaka; and in '82, at the battle of Yamazaki, the Akechi-side commander Ōi-no-suke holed up here, and the castle fell. Then, seven years on, came Hideyoshi's rebuilding.

Since the record says work began in the first month and was finished by the third, the rebuilding cannot have been large. But the first man under Heaven rebuilt it to house the favorite who was carrying his firstborn, and one may guess it was made as fine and fair as could be. On the north and west it ran along the Katsura; on the east and south a weed-choked open field stretched away to the Uji.

In those days a branch of the Uji flowed just south of Nōso and joined the Katsura, so one may picture Yodo castle ringed on three sides by water. If this was the castle Hideyoshi chose to house his favorite, there must have been some merit in it. Ringed on three sides by rivers, it would have made a cool place to live, out of stifling summer Kyoto. Otherwise there was no reason to choose so small a castle on the flat.

When I went to the Yodo-castle site in front of Yodo station there was no signboard to explain it, and I thought to ask at the shrine office; but the door was shut and no one seemed about. Then I saw, under the eaves, an old framed picture. I stopped to look, and it was an old map of Yodo castle.

The whole delta between the Uji and the Katsura is filled with castle. Both rivers run white-capped, and from their banks, a good five or six ken high, a stone rampart runs clear around. Within it are the honmaru, the ni-no-maru and the san-no-maru; and the ground the shrine now stands on seems to answer to the honmaru. In the foreground two waterwheels are drawn, monstrously large. That old trick of painters, I thought, of drawing what they want to show out of all proportion—and then I looked harder, and was startled all over again. From every spoke of the wheels a rod juts out sideways, and to each is fixed a thing like a pot. The pot dips up the river water, and when it comes round to the top and levels off, the water in it spills. And from the top of the castle wall a great flume reaches out to catch the spill, and the water in the flume is led into the inner moat of the castle and into the garden pond.

Judging by the height of the wall, the wheels must have stood a good seven or eight ken across—thirteen or fourteen meters. So that was how they raised the water, I thought, admiring.

The waterwheels of the Uji and the Yodo have been famous from of old. In the Kanginshū, a collection made in Eishō 15, 1518, of the short songs of that day, there is one: "The waterwheel in the shallows of the Uji—how it turns and turns in this sad world." Later this became "The waterwheel in the shallows of the Yodo—for whom does it wait, turning and turning?" and came to be sung as the monkey-song in the kyōgen play Utsubozaru. From all of which it seems that hereabouts, from old times, they used waterwheels to draw river water into the paddies and to grind their grain; and that, taking the notion from that, someone built great wheels to lift the river up into the castle is likely enough. The old map at the shrine office, by Mr. Sakai's account, was in fact drawn in the Meiji period, after an older one made in Kan'en 3, 1750, that is kept in an old family's house. So it is of course no picture of the castle Hideyoshi built; but to suppose that his too had its waterwheels is no idle fancy. That, to please Lady Yodo's eye, he made a pond within the castle and fed it by such a contrivance—is that not just like Hideyoshi?

The castle went up round the clock, and when it was done Ochacha carried her heavy body here from the Jurakudai. On the twenty-seventh of the fifth month she bore a boy. Hideyoshi, wild with joy, gave him the formal name Tsurumatsu; but from the old belief that a child named "Cast-away" would live long, he took to calling him Sute, the Cast-off. And from that time he took to calling Ochacha "the Yodo one," so that in common speech she became Lady Yodo.

Now Hideyoshi, spreading the sword-hunt and the land-survey across the whole country and wringing the peasants dry, and in his private life sunk in rank debauchery, was working steadily to complete his mastery of the realm. In the autumn of Tenshō 17 he resolved to crush the Hōjō, who held out in the Kantō and would not bow to his authority, and ordered the daimyo of all the land to make ready; and in the third month of the next year, Tenshō 18, he marched out of Kyoto. Ieyasu, whose provinces lay along the road, met Hideyoshi at Sunpu castle and welcomed him, and joined the eastern army as one of its captains. Hanzō's ninja corps went with him to a man, but for those scattered through the provinces on other work.

By the fourth month all the commanders had reached the outskirts of Odawara and had the castle wholly ringed. It was a war already won, so he pressed it at his ease, and in the fifth month sent for Lady Yodo. The castles of the Kantō—Matsuyama, Sano, Maebashi, Matsuida, Minowa, Edo, Tamanawa, Kawagoe, Iwatsuki, Tatebayashi, Nirayama, Hachigata, Hachiōji, Oshi—fell one after another, and at last, on the seventh of the seventh month, Odawara castle too opened its gates. For his part in the siege Ieyasu was given, entire, the vast old Hōjō domain—Izu, Sagami, Musashi, Kazusa, Shimōsa, Kōzuke, Shimotsuke. His holding rose by a million koku to two million four hundred thousand, and he stood a very great lord. But in exchange,
the five provinces that had been his until then—Suruga, Tōtōmi, Mikawa, Kai, Shinano—were taken from him.

Hideyoshi's reckoning ran like this: cut Ieyasu off from the old subjects who had long been loyal to him, move him to fresh ground, and friction would spring up between him and subjects who had been the Hōjō's; seize that moment, and finish the Tokugawa. But Ieyasu comforted himself with the thought that his new wealth would let him raise more troops—leave thirty thousand to hold the province, lead fifty thousand up to the Kamigata, and something could be managed.

The Edo of that day, though, was as the old account has it: "To the east, on the flat, tidewater reed-beds here and there, with no way to lay out so much as ten chō of townsmen's and samurai's plots; and away to the southwest, level pampas fields running on into the Musashino, no telling where they end." Tokyo Bay reached in as far as the present Tamachi and Hibiya and the whole Marunouchi quarter; Tsukiji and Ginza, of course, lay under the sea, or in marsh that showed and hid with the tides. From the close of the Heian age the Kantō had been thick with local magnates, the Seven Bands of Musashi and the Eight Taira of Kanmu; and one of the Eight Taira line, a man named Chichibu Shigetsugu, marked this inlet, raised a fort on a low hill above the sea, and changed his surname to Edo. The hill was a tableland that jutted like a peninsula into the inlet, about where the honmaru of the Imperial Palace stands today. On its east side ran a river, the Hirakawa, down from the Takadanobaba quarter; and its mouth, near the present Tokiwabashi, soon became a busy anchorage for cargo-boats. To the south, about the present Akasaka Tameike, lay a great marsh. In the middle of the fifteenth century, after the Edo clan had fallen, Ōta Dōkan built a castle on the site of that fort.

The castle, walls and roof alike of board, had a moat dug round it, twenty turrets and five gates. But by the eighth month of Tenshō 18, 1590, when Ieyasu entered it, it too had gone to ruin. "In those days Edo was the seat of Tōyama"—meaning the castle where the Hōjō retainer Tōyama had been posted—"a wretched, shabby place; the townhouses thatched, scarce a hundred of them, there or not there; the castle a castle in name only, with nothing castle-like about it." Nowhere on the outer works was there a run of stone, only earthen bank; and on the castle buildings the earth piled over the board roofs to turn cannon-fire had rotted the boards clear through, so that the rain came in, and the matting and the floors had rotted away. The entrance was a bare dirt floor, and for a step up two boat-planks were laid side by side.

The moment he entered the province Ieyasu set about parceling out his retainers' fiefs and their house-plots. The lesser hatamoto he placed close to Edo castle, the higher-stipended men out at a distance; the uplands west of the castle, the Yamanote, he made a quarter of warrior residences; and because the old provincial road that ran from the Hanzō gate through the Yotsuya-mitsuke toward Fuchū was both a key artery and a line along the castle's weak rear, he stationed there a body of hatamoto in the Tokugawa's own service, and settled the merchants and craftsmen on the reclaimed marsh to the southeast. When Ieyasu took the castle, Hattori Hanzō too came over to Edo with two hundred Iga men, and they spread out to live from the whole ground before the Hanzō gate out to Akasaka Hitotsugi, Kubo-chō and Harajuku. Their charge was the guarding of the castle within, and the watching of the daimyo of the provinces—above all, the doings of Kyoto and Osaka.

Edo castle came to its real building only after Sekigahara had made Ieyasu's supremacy sure, and was finished, through enlargement after enlargement, over the years from Keichō 8, 1603, to Kan'ei 13, 1636. That the inner-moat gate at the head of the road running from the castle's rear toward Fuchū should be named the Hanzō gate, after the Hattori Hanzō whose residence stood just outside it, must have come about very early.

Already from about the pacification of Kyūshū, Hideyoshi had let slip in letters and talk, again and again, that he would conquer Korea, and had startled people; and once the Kantō was subdued, he set to the work in earnest. Feeling that the wealth of Japan was too little to fill his bottomless greed, he fixed his eye on Korea and on Ming China. Trusting his arms too far, he meant to take it not by honest trade but by conquest. As a first step, through Sō Yoshitomo of Tsushima as go-between, he pressed Korea for tribute and Ming for trade.

The long-awaited Korean embassy came to the Jurakudai bearing their king's letter in the eleventh month of Tenshō 18. The letter congratulated Hideyoshi on the unifying of all Japan; but Hideyoshi took it for tribute, and wrote in his answer that he wished Korea to guide him so he might march into great Ming—at which the Koreans were aghast, and would have no more to do with him. His conceit and his ambition were not to be dashed by that. In year 19 he was talking of an expedition to India; he pressed the Philippines to send tribute; and by Bunroku 2, 1593, he had gone so far as to demand the surrender of Taiwan.

Hideyoshi went out to Yodo whenever he had the leisure. Ochacha lived in the finished castle with Tsurumatsu. But the child was frail from birth, forever on his sickbed, and at last, on the fifth of the eighth month of Tenshō 19, at just two years old, he died.

Hideyoshi was at the Tōfuku-ji that day. When the word reached him, he shut himself in a room, cut off his topknot, and went into mourning. Ieyasu and Terumoto, who hurried to condole, cut their topknots too, and it grew into a whole to-do of one man after another shearing his hair. This grief too may have had its part in what followed. In the eleventh month of that year Hideyoshi adopted Hidetsugu—born to his own elder sister Nisshū and to Miyoshi Musashi-no-kami Ichiro, and holding Owari and North Ise, a million koku—and on the twenty-eighth of the twelfth month yielded him the office of Kanpaku as well, and styled himself Taikō. With the new year he set to the preparations for the invasion of Korea in good earnest.

For the base of the march he chose the very tip of the Higashi-Matsuura peninsula, fronting Iki and Tsushima: north of Karatsu, a place long given to traffic with Korea, whose name itself had shifted from Kara-tsu, the Korea-port. By Frois's report the ground was "unpeopled, and quite unfit for men to dwell in; wanting not food only but every last thing needful to build a castle; all mountains, and on the one side a bog of mud. And yet, the Kanpaku's command being all-powerful, the sheer impossibility of the thing to human strength was set wholly aside, and every one of the lords took up his allotted share and flung in the labor of forty or fifty thousand men. Many died in the work, of weariness and pain; yet in a bare few months not only were the lords' splendid palace and castle for the Kanpaku raised in fine style, but on that waste plain a whole new city was built in that short span. And amid dread anxiety, toil, sickness, and want of food, they had at once to make ready to cross over to Korea again."

This November—1964—I am directing one piece in the Shinkokugeki's run at the Meijiza, and for the rehearsals I came yesterday, the sixteenth of October, to Kokura in Kyūshū. The company is playing at the Kokura Civic Hall, so we rehearse each morning from nine until noon, half an hour before the curtain. We were to have rehearsed late at night after the show, but no one would lend us a hall at that hour, and so it fell to the morning. The actor's is a hard trade. At this company's last production, with the curtain at eleven in the morning, the players were in their dressing rooms by ten, gave two shows a day of four and a half hours each, nine hours in all, and after that, in the dead of winter, in a fireless concrete hall, rehearsed deep into the night for the coming month—and the Shinkokugeki has a great deal of stage-fighting, so, two shows a day, they wiped off the paint and whipped their worn-out bodies on.

Now, I took the chance and, splurging on a hired car from Higashi-Karatsu, went to see the Nagoya castle site. (Meaning to catch the Chikuhi Line, I bought a paper at Hakata station and was handed an extra edition; it announced the fall of Khrushchev, and I felt it deeply.) Out beyond Karatsu town the car ran a while north along Karatsu Bay, then turned west into a country of winding hills. Paddies of wet rice, pines, cherries, palms, pampas swaying in the wind; and running among them a good forty minutes, until the driver looked up at a steep hill on the left, thick with old trees, and said, "That is the Nagoya castle site."

Up the car climbed, by the narrow road between the scraps of stone wall on either hand that must once have been the ōte, the great front gate. Looking down as we went on what seemed the traces of the baileys' moats and stone ramparts, we came to the top: a level of fifty or sixty meters square, with only a little old walling left, and two ungainly stone monuments. The larger bears, in the broad hand of Marshal Count Tōgō Heihachirō, "Site of Nagoya Castle." No doubt the aged Marshal, recalling how in that strait of Tsushima which opens out beyond he had destroyed the Russian Baltic Fleet in the war with Russia, took up his brush thinking, may that dream come once more. The other, in coarse, sprawling characters, is daubed by a man named Gettō: "The sea the Taikō glared upon—and now the haze upon it." What manner of man Gettō was I do not know; but the thing seems to be a hanger-on's cry of "Learn from the Taikō, kindle the ambition to overrun the continent."

The Taikōki writes of the place: "Now the ground of Nagoya is a spot of surpassing beauty, where to the west a broad sea heaves, and by the harbor the water bends and winds about for more than a hundred chō." Just so. Standing on the summit, eighty-nine meters above the sea, and looking out over the water to the northwest, one sees Iki and Tsushima lying far off. This is the nearest point to the Korean peninsula. I have crossed this Genkai Sea by ship many times, and by plane as well; and gazing out to the far edge of the sea, I found the land of the Chollima, the Thousand-League Horse, coming achingly close to me. To the right the sea runs inland in a long inlet, a first-rate harbor. Once a base for whaling ships, in the Greater East Asia War it was made a naval coaling station, and a base too for midget submarines
—it had been, they say, a training ground for them.

By the driver's account, the inlet is well enough, but come winter the Genkai Sea is forever in storm, and the north wind on this summit is so fierce there is no standing up in it. The Taikō named Kuroda Nagamasa, Konishi Yukinaga and Katō Kiyomasa the three chiefs of the building, portioned the work out among the daimyo, and drove it on. They had not only to level the top of this Katsuo-dake and raise a fortress with its honmaru, ni-no-maru and san-no-maru, but to build, on all the land about its foot, their own residences, as fine as they could contrive. That great labor they brought off in a bare five months, conjuring, on this all-but-empty waste, a whole castle-city about a splendid palace. By that same Frois's report: "All these dwellings are exceedingly large and sumptuous; and no sooner were the many of them gathered and finished in one place than the Kanpaku's palace was raised, a great thing, fine and clean and elegant, with many great rooms, called zashiki, gilt with gold and wrought by hand."

Ground down by the land-survey, and by the great building works that came one on another—Osaka, Kyoto, Nagoya—the common people of Japan sank to the very floor of misery. Frois, reporting on Higo and Nagasaki: "Parents who sold themselves and sold their children were in truth very many, and the old men said that never in all Japan's history had they heard of anything so pitiless and cruel." "And as the oppression and the anguish grew so heavy, one poor man, at the end of his rope, sold his eleven-year-old child to pay his tax. From the windows of the houses come only the sighs and the groans of these poor people." So they went about in droves, selling themselves for less than the price of an ox.

Even before this, the Japanese of the north Kyūshū country had often raided Korea. "The Japanese carried home great numbers of Koreans as captives and sold them off cheap. The Japanese about Nagasaki, to buy them and sell them on to the Portuguese, went ranging the country in search of Koreans; and to plunder the people they even crossed to Korea and cruelly killed many of them." The Taikō's invasion was, among much else, a scheme to do this on a far greater scale; and it heaped a far greater misery on the common people of Japan besides. Standing now on this Nagoya castle site, I could find nothing whatever to be proud of. I felt only the curses and the cries of a wretched people pressing in upon me, from the land and from the sea alike.

On the fifth of the first month of Bunroku 1, 1592, the Taikō handed down the order of mobilization for the Korean expedition, and, to secure his provisions, clamped down hard across the whole country on peasants leaving their villages and forbade the peasant to turn merchant or craftsman. In the third month, Mōri Terumoto, Kuroda Nagamasa, Katō Kiyomasa, Konishi Yukinaga and the rest went first to Tsushima and there waited their chance to cross. So rose the curtain on a war of aggression that would run seven years, to Keichō 3, 1598. In Japan it is called the Bunroku-Keichō War, the Taikō's Kōrai Campaign, the Kara Campaign, the Subjugation of Korea; in Korea, the Imjin and Jeongyu Japanese Disturbances; in Ming, the Wanli Korean Campaign. Of this war I mean to write in the sequel to this Goemon Kamairi, which I intend to go on to, following the doings of the Japanese ninja who crossed to the peninsula.

Hattori Hanzō was not called up. But Katō Kiyomasa asked for him by name, and Ieyasu—transferred to the Kantō and himself not mobilized for the Korean invasion—lent out fifty of the ninja band; and Hanzō led them across, and for a time set them to work here and there about the peninsula.
 The Green-Leaf Coronet had been operating over there; but Ieyasu had need of Hanzō, called him home, and set the Green-Leaf Coronet at the head in his place.

On the twenty-sixth of the third month the Taikō marched out of Kyoto. His lips were painted red, a great jet-black false beard hung from his ears, and he sat astride a horse harnessed in gold armor; a troop bearing golden swords and golden shields warded him round, and he rode at the head of an army of thirty thousand. The two concubines to be taken as far as Kyūshū—Ochacha and Kyōgoku-no-tsubone—came on two days behind. At six ri a day, the Taikō reached Nagoya castle a month later, on the twenty-fifth of the fourth month. By then Konishi Yukinaga's army, landed at Pusan, had driven up the western road as far as Sangju, and Katō Kiyomasa had come by the eastern road to Hikawa. The Taikō swelled with pride, and threw a drinking feast at each dispatch of victory from the peninsula; he sent for kyōgen players and entertainers from the provinces besides.

When word came, on the second of the fifth month, that Konishi's army had crossed the Han River into the capital, Seoul, and Kiyomasa's had entered close on its heels, Nagoya boiled over. But in the seventh month came word that the Ōmandokoro, the Taikō's mother, lay ill. He did not reach her in time, and, weeping, held her funeral at the Daitoku-ji in Kyoto, and at the end of the tenth month came back once more to Nagoya. Ochacha had found she was again with child. The Taikō leapt for joy. "Take care of your body. Guard yourself, guard yourself"—it was all he could say.

In the first month of the next year, Bunroku 2, Chacha—now called Lady Yodo by everyone—was carried, her heavy body tended like a treasure, from Nagoya back to Yodo castle, and on the third of the eighth month bore a boy. The word must have reached Nagoya at once. A letter of his survives, dated the ninth of the eighth month, to his wife Nene. "Matsuura"—meaning Matsuura Sanuki-no-kami Shigemasa, who was attached to the Ōoku—"sent a runner straight off, and I am well content. I should like you to give him my thanks. Since Matsuura 'picked up' the child and got me word so promptly, this child's name shall be Hiroi-ko, the Picked-up Child. Everyone, down to the meanest, must leave off the honorific O and call him plainly Hiroi. I shall be home from the field soon. Set your heart at rest. Again and again: the child's name is Hiroi. I leave here on the twenty-fifth. I shall come to you before long, and see you, and we shall talk of many things." And far from the twenty-fifth, he set out on the fifteenth, arrived on the twenty-fifth, and looked on his darling child. That he sent his letter of joy first to his lawful wife, and not to Chacha, shows well enough how carefully the Taikō handled his many women.

He had this one named Hiroi, the Foundling, just as he had had the first, Tsurumatsu, called Sute, the Cast-off: it went by the folk belief of that day that a foundling grows up strong. This Hiroi was the later Hideyori; and for all the love the Taikō poured on the child, a bare twenty-two years later he was set upon by Tokugawa Ieyasu, killed himself in Osaka castle, and his body burned with the castle—but that will come clear in the sequel.

Yodo castle was a castle raised in a fair country of waters; but for his own son Hiroi the Taikō would build,
 he decided, a splendid castle of his own. He chose a strong, commanding site: east of Kyoto, at the southern tip of Higashiyama, looking out over the whole Yamashiro plain, with the Ogura Pond below it to the south—reclaimed now to fields, but once a broad sheet of water—and set about raising Fushimi castle. At first he had not meant so great a thing; but by the next year, year 3, he took it into his head to make something splendid for receiving the envoys of Korea and Ming, gathered two hundred and fifty thousand laborers from the provinces, and had stone cut from Daigo, Yamashina and Kirarazaka, and timber from Kiso and Tosa. And before it was even finished he moved Lady Yodo to the ni-no-maru of Osaka castle, and lived himself in Fushimi.

Here is a letter he sent in the eleventh month of Bunroku 2 to Lady Yodo, then commonly called the Ni-no-maru-dono. "I have your letter of the first. I meant to answer, but was too busy and let it go. Is Ohiroi well since? Does he take the breast well? Again and again: nurse Hiroi well, and let him sleep by himself. And you must eat well yourself, so the milk comes freely. Do not fret over anything at all." Though he bids others call the boy Hiroi, he himself says Ohiroi. In another letter: "Is Ohiroi playing well? At all events, give strict order that they nurse him with all their care. If your own milk runs short his portion will be small, so contrive somehow to have him nursed in plenty." And in yet another: "Again and again: you are not to take moxa. I think it would do you good to give it—but to the young lord Ohiroi you must never, never apply moxa."

These letters are generally prized as brimming with the pure love of a world-bestriding hero for his child. To me they seem nothing but the flat, unreflecting egoism of a man who slaughtered other men's children without mercy, and was at that very hour killing women and children beyond counting in Korea.

While all this went on, in the sixth month of Bunroku 1, Kuroda Nagamasa's army took Pyongyang; but this one-sided run of victories was brought to a dead stop by the sea-fight off Hansan Island, and the war swung to a new turn. In those days the central government of Korea was sunk in factional strife driven by private greed; the mass of the people, bled to the bone, had been cast into the pit of want; and the numbers, the arming, the training of the army for the country's defense were wholly neglected. Yet even overwhelmed by the fighting on the defensive, the setting-in-order of arms began. Four hundred kilometers south of Pusan lies a harbor called Yeosu. There the Jeolla Left Naval Base was established, and the commander of the Jeolla Left navy was a great captain, Yi Sun-sin. He had seen from early on that such a day might come, and had drilled his fighting men and put his warships and all his weapons in order. He had a gift for the practical sciences besides, and had invented the famous turtle-ship: it roofed the whole upper part of the vessel over, just as a turtle wears its shell, and the entire hull was cased heavily in iron plate.
 It was stretched taut, so as to turn aside all the arrows and gunnery of the day; and its prow was of iron, so that it could ram an enemy ship and sink her. There is even a story that Yi Sun-sin had a submarine, though it is also said this was likely the turtle-ship magnified in the telling. But we have already seen, in an earlier part of this tale, at the siege of Ishiyama castle, a real submarine—the so-called Dragon-Palace ship of the Nojima pirates of the Inland Sea. One cannot believe those pirates had the skill to build such a thing themselves; most likely it was plundered from Korea. Which should make us allow that the turtle-ship, at least, could move about along a shallow bottom. The Korean army had made other new weapons besides: the hwacha, a kind of war-cart, and the bigyeok-jincheollae, a kind of timed bomb.

The very day after Seoul fell, the third of the fifth month, Yi Sun-sin sailed out of Yeosu, fought a string of actions—at Okpo on Geoje Island, at Happo in Ungcheon, at Jeokjinpo in Goseong—and shattered more than forty Japanese ships. It was Korea's first victory, by land or sea. From the close of the fifth month into the sixth he made a second sortie, broke seventy-two ships in the fights at Sacheon and at Dangpo and Danghangpo, hunted out the inlets where the Japanese fleet had fled and smashed them there, and won back many of the people who had been carried off. But on land the Japanese pressed ever harder, and on the fifteenth of the sixth month they took Pyongyang; so at the start of the seventh month Yi Sun-sin ventured a third sortie. On the eighth of the seventh month a great sea-battle broke off Hansan Island, between Goseong and Geoje. This time the Japanese fleet mustered its whole strength to drive west and north; but the Korean fleet met it head-on and shattered fifty-nine of its seventy-three ships. On the tenth they caught a second Japanese squadron, forty-two ships, pushing north, and wiped it out entire.

By this great feat of arms the Korean people took heart, and could stiffen their resistance on land as well; while the Japanese, their supply by sea now cut hard, began to think of terms. At Korea's plea, Ming gave Li Rusong a great host of forty thousand and sent him across the Yalu. On the eighth of the first month of Bunroku 2 Yukinaga was heavily beaten, gave up Pyongyang and fled south; and after several see-saw battles north of Seoul the Japanese at last, on the eighteenth of the fourth month, withdrew from the capital and held to the line of Pusan, Ungcheon and Jincheon. The peace talks dragged; Bunroku 2 wore out in a staring match; and the fight going out of them, the Japanese in Korea suffered for want of food and sickened of the long life abroad. The spring of Bunroku 3, 1594, passed as well, and the summer of that year drew on—the year that is to be the grand finale of this novel.

These ten years, what had Goemon been doing? All that while he had gone on farming, out in the village of Nagao. His hatred of Hideyoshi burned as high as ever; but for a time he bore the loneliness of losing Maki, and gave himself wholly to raising Goichi. The land Goemon held was all barley-field, and gave only a little rice. Even that little
 was mostly taken in tax; and the little that was left Goemon never touched a grain of, but gave it all to Goichi, who by now, even without a wet-nurse's borrowed milk, could take a thin rice-gruel.

Reared by a poor man's one pair of hands, Goichi throve wonderfully. Goemon himself stood five shaku four sun (one meter sixty-four): lean, slim enough that he had once slipped through that little hole under the platform of the Great Pagoda of the Negoro-ji. As a child too he had been a small, thin boy. But Goichi was round and chubby, the flesh creased sweetly at the joints; and the soft, doughy flesh, as the months went by, firmed up hard. His face grew more and more the living image of his mother Maki. Whether the child had come to look like Maki, or whether Maki had had, from the first, a face like a child's, Goemon could not have said. Maki had been defenseless, not in her face only but in her heart—wide open, leaning on Goemon like a child. And under that leaning his own sternly-shut heart had melted and run out of him; for the first time in his life he had learned the ease and the gladness of being open and unguarded. Goichi's eyes were like hers, and yet, when they met yours, they held a gravity that gave you a start. As wonderfully clear as hers, they had, for all their likeness—call it force of gaze—far more strength. And his eyebrows were very thick, thicker than Maki's, the right brow and the left all but meeting in the middle;

and by the time he was two, the down lay thick on his cheeks and about his lips, and when Goemon looked he found golden down growing thick between the boy's shoulders, on his chest, on his arms and legs. What a manly man he would grow to be—the thought put heart into Goemon. When he first found it, he grinned. "Here's a thing. He'll have women weeping by the dozen yet."

Goemon remembered his own youth. Those who have read The Stealthy Ones will recall it. His skin was pale, a blue-black shadow lay where his beard was shaved, and his eyes were sharply cut. Likely the continental people who settled this region in the old ages had kept, for a long while, from mingling with the Japanese, and so preserved that race's marks in the body. By the way this down of Goichi's grew, he too, come of age, would grow into a man heavy with the male marks—a blue shadow at the shaved jaw, black chest-hair thick—a man of a charm no woman could stand against.

Before Sandayū forced him to break his teeth and pull out his hair and turn himself into an unrecognizable ruin of a man, Goemon had known, over and over, how a young woman who saw his sharp-cut eyes, the blue of his shaved jaw, the thick chest-hair glimpsed at his collar, would go numb as though lightning-struck. It seemed to melt any woman's reason in a breath. Inone—he had brought that woman to ruin—and the years since had done nothing to ease his guilt toward her. Sandayū's virgin bride had been the same—poor woman—
 but the years had faded it, and what was left was only a wistful, lonely tenderness. If Goemon was black, there was nothing in Goichi of the dank, warped thing that was in Goemon. Goichi was crimson.

Goemon gave himself wholly to Goichi, and so the days passed and the years turned. At a full three years old the boy weighed five kan six hundred, twenty-one kilograms, and stood three shaku one sun four bu, ninety-five point two centimeters. In those days there was of course no national table of infant growth; but the 1958 average for a three-year-old is 88.5 centimeters and 12.73 kilograms, and a child does not reach twenty kilos until somewhere between eight and nine—so one may judge how far beyond the ordinary he was.

Goemon loved him past all bearing. He was forever wanting to stroke or touch some part of the little body, and Goichi too was always wanting to cling to him, always calling "Otchan, Otchan"—Daddy. His voice ran a note or two higher than an ordinary boy's, and the mismatch of it with that grave face and big frame only made him the more endearing.

When Goichi was five, word came that Hideyoshi had got a child of his own, Tsurumatsu, and was beside himself with joy. And when Goichi was seven, and Goemon heard that Tsurumatsu was dead, he gave a low growl. "Serves you right, Hideyoshi. Did you not kill this boy's mother, and all but kill the boy as well? There's your judgment from Heaven, plain as day! You have let your own child die, haven't you. Beaten, are you? Does it gall you? Damn you, Hideyoshi! Look here—my Goichi is growing up fine and strong!"

Goichi ate well. He was always thirsty, always hungry. At seven and eight he was already dropping a stool thicker than Goemon's own, a lusty thing near a shaku long. On his eighth birthday he weighed eleven kan six hundred, forty-three and a half kilograms. The next year Goemon heard that Hideyoshi's Hiroi had been born, and was unmoved: no child, he was sure, could be born to better Goichi.

But about then a worry started up. The boy was a dreadful coward. He was mortally afraid of the dark. Once night fell he would not set foot outside. And not that only: even by day, out walking, he would not go a step without a firm hold on Goemon's hand. When Goemon saw other children of that age squirming free of a parent's hand and leaping about on their own, he thought it strange. Goichi was fearfully wary. To jump down from a mere seven or eight sun he would peer over the edge, crouch, peer again, and not jump until he had worked himself up to a great resolve. The one-legged hopping game, chinchin-mogamo, he could not do to save his life.

By and by Goemon understood that the boy was badly nearsighted. On the paddy dike at the midday meal, a millet dumpling rolled from Goichi's hand and came to rest right in front of him, a bare two shaku off; and yet Goichi groped about with his hands—"Where? Where? Where?"

"It's right there, isn't it. Look properly, now. There—just in front of you, off to the right."
 Goichi cut his eyes sideways to the right, cried "Ah, there it is!", and snatched it up.

Watching closely, Goemon saw that to look at a thing the boy brought his eyes to within a shaku of it. "You—is it your eyes are short on you?" In this son he had thought better than common men in every inch of skin and hair, from crown to sole, a great flaw had shown itself.

Goemon took Goichi on his back at once, went to the house of Yamatoya Tarōbei in Sakai, got a doctor recommended, and had the boy looked at. "His fear of the dark is because he can't see well. Torime, night-blindness; yamōshō, in the hard word. Feed him plenty of nourishing food." Goemon was taken aback. All along he had fed the boy the richest food he could come by. And now to be told he had gone short of nourishment—it was on that very food that he had grown so fine. Goemon took endless pains, put together a handsome gift, and begged the treatment of a Nanban doctor who had come over with the Portuguese captain.

The doctor, golden-haired and blue-eyed, stripped Goichi and went over his whole body closely, then said, with a look of pain: "This appears, even in Europe, in about one person in a hundred thousand. It comes of a strain inherited from the forebears; and by that one strain alone it does not show, but only when it crosses with a certain other strain. It runs most in the heavy-bodied. First the eyes are struck; the field of sight narrows in from either side; less and less light is taken in, so that by night one cannot see; and in the end comes total blindness. I am sorry to say that no medicine and no operation will cure it. There are exceptions—but the life, as a rule, is not a long one."

"Cut out these eyes of mine! Put them in the boy's place!" So Goemon knelt before the Nanban doctor and pleaded; but "No such operation can be done, not by any Nanban medicine in the world," and he was waved off.

The doctors of that day had of course not yet come so far as to name it for what it is, an endocrine disease, congenital retinitis pigmentosa. But the disease exists to this day, and still no cure for it has been found. In 1962, of the causes of blindness among the pupils of all the schools for the blind in our country, 52.3 percent were of congenital origin, and in America the figure reaches 64.1 percent—and the greater part of it is this retinitis pigmentosa.

Was he to stand by with folded hands and watch this child—this keepsake of Maki, his one and only—go dark? When the light was gone, and the boy cried out weeping, "I can't see!", what was he to say to comfort him? Goemon made up his mind on the spot. "I will give my whole life for this poor child. To make his unlucky life happier by even a little, let my own self come to whatever end it will."

But things had only seemed to quiet a little; war might break out again at any hour. And as a peasant—one who, sound of body, could work and work and still not manage a bowl of rice-gruel—how was Goichi ever to live? As long as I live, Goemon thought, I will be a wall about him by whatever means, and keep him. But when I am dead, what then? He could only gaze and gaze at the profile of the boy, who, knowing nothing of his fate, played merrily or lent a hand at the work; gaze at those eyes still clear and grave; and sob inside his heart.

Some say that if a man is to lose his sight at all, better it go early, so he can school the inner sense; that to go blind grown is the harder suffering. But unless a man has looked on all the beautiful things of this world and known them beautiful and stored them in his breast—so that when the light is gone he has at least their memory, a thread for the imagination to hold—then the world is truly a darkness without light. And when the boy grew up and came to long for a woman, would any girl come forward to answer that wish? Shut in the dark, with little to turn his heart aside, he would long for others more than an ordinary man; and to meet a girl who would answer him was next to hopeless.

Yes—to keep Goichi alive was to keep him in pain. Before he woke to that pain, let me kill him. And let me die too.

Watching the boy's sleeping face as he snored his easy, innocent snore, Goemon thought so more than once. But—leave Hideyoshi be, and only he and his son go down to death? Ieyasu lived too, who had defiled Maki and lived on without a ripple. Was he to leave such men to go on in their pomp and splendor, and only he and his boy die? No—he could not let them live. Men like that he could not let live.

Toward the end of Bunroku 2, Goichi, at nine, went wholly blind at last. What sorrow, what suffering was in the boy's heart, Goemon could not even guess. To look at, the eyes were no different from before—the whites clear, the dark irises black as though they hid a keen, deep sight. Goichi only bore his fate, still and passive. Sparing of words from the first, he grew more silent yet, and only groped for his father's hand; and he seemed forever ashamed of his own clumsiness, of the tasks he could no longer do.

"Daddy will do anything in the world for you. Daddy's eyes are your eyes. Just think of Daddy as your own self."

One day, at the Shinjō-ji in Sakai, when they turned over the ground to raise a new bell-tower, a jar came to light, half-filled with treasure of every kind; and so it went into the storehouse the egōshū kept,
 he heard. The Shinjō-ji stands next to the Jōfuku-ji; and in the grounds of that Jōfuku-ji, thirteen years before, Goemon had buried a jar of treasure he had stolen in Kyoto and Osaka—only for someone to dig it up and carry it off before long. The kinds of treasure now said to be in the jar were every one of them graven in Goemon's memory.

For a long while he had given up thieving. But now he went to Sakai, slipped into the egōshū's storehouse, and lifted a good sum of gold and silver out of the jar he knew. For the blind Goichi's sake it was sorely needed; and that it was, stolen goods though it might be, what he himself had once gathered by his own hard toil, no doubt eased his conscience. He put it in his breast, and toward the end of the spring of the next year, Bunroku 3, he set out with Goichi for his home country of Iga.

He half wished to let the boy, who knew nothing of the world, see a little of it. But more than anything he wished to have Goichi tread, once, the soil of his father's birthplace. That birthplace held for him mostly hateful memories; and yet something drove him to it. They went as a well-to-do farmer and his son on a pilgrimage to Ise. Taking his time on the road, feeding the boy the local delicacies, telling him of all there was to be seen, he headed east. Goemon, who believed in no god and no buddha, and had more than once raided a shrine's treasury and made off with the altar-vessels, this time climbed Mount Kōya, trudged the road to the Oku-no-in, and bowed low before the Daishi's mausoleum. He went up the Kino River, out from Gojō, and by Hatsuse and Haibara and Sanbonmatsu came into Iga. The road runs along the Kuroda River. The look of the river was as it had always been.

When, going north on the Nabari highway, they drew near the village of Azōda, it was the evening of the eighth day out from Nagao. Then, from somewhere, came the sound of a biwa—jararan, boron, jararan. It was the temple called the Shōfuku-ji. Peering in through a break in the earthen wall, Goemon saw, on the veranda of the priests' quarters, a wretched-looking monk seated and striking a biwa. He saw at once that the monk was blind. Beside him crouched something black—an old nun, it would be, wrapped in a grey robe, the left half of her face covered by an ugly burn.

Why was Goemon so drawn to the two of them? For the biwa's sound, no doubt. And for the indescribably gentle, brimming happiness in both their faces. How raptly Goichi was listening, Goemon could tell from the small hands that gripped his own left hand and tightened hard, and from the entranced look on the boy's face as he leaned toward the sound. Goemon asked a night's lodging, and the two took them in gladly. Never, he thought, had he met people so warm and gentle to the very bottom of them as this blind monk, Ryōsai, and this nun, Tamo. That even the maimed could be so happy at heart, so full of joy, filled him with wonder. "Let me leave Goichi with these two; it will be the better for him." So thinking, Goemon set down money enough to keep the boy his whole life long, and begged the two of them
 to keep him. Without asking after his deeper reasons, the two gladly took the charge.

He stayed three days at the temple, and took Goichi to his own birthplace, the village of Ishikawa, and to Ōyamada, and to the ground about the Momochi fort. "Daddy has a piece of work he must do, come what may. Even while I'm about it, whenever I can find the time, I'll come to see you. Wait for me here till the work is done." Goichi nodded; and when they parted he ran both hands over Goemon's face, as though to grave it on his heart.

"Ah—now I have been able to let Goichi go. Now is the time! Now is when I must bring it off!" So Goemon thought. Now.

Up in Kyoto he found the Korean war at a truce and Hideyoshi at Osaka castle. All afire over the building of Fushimi, the Taikō would lodge in the unfinished castle to look it over, or come from Osaka again and again to inspect the work. When the sixth month came, and the heat that year ran uncommonly fierce, and the town grew too close for sleep, the Ni-no-maru-dono—Lady Yodo—took Hiroi and went off from Osaka to Yodo castle for the cool.

By the Taikō's own whim, a great waterwheel eight ken across had been set on the Katsura, and another on the stream drawn in from the Uji, one to each. From every spoke of a wheel a rod jutted sideways, and to each rod a pot was fixed. As the wheel turned, the pots dipped up the river water; and coming round to the top and leveling off, each poured what it held into a great flume. The flumes were carried over the castle wall and into the grounds—the Katsura's into the inner moat, the Uji's into the pond of the honmaru garden. The noise of the wheels and the running water never stopped, night or day, and blew up a coolness through the heat.

When he learned that Lady Yodo had moved to Yodo castle, Goemon felt sure the Taikō would come to see her, and set a close watch. By day, disguised as this or that, the man would surely come from Fushimi; so by night Goemon put on his shinobi garb and melted into the dark, and studied how he might slip into the castle. On the dark night of the third day he sank himself into the stream drawn from the Uji and swam quietly up to the wheel. Close to, the pots on the spokes were each a good two shaku five sun long. He caught hold of one, and the force of the water lifted him straight up into the air. In the slow turning his body rose and rose, and soon a great flume loomed in front of him. The pot tipped, and his body tipped with it—sideways in a moment, then clean upside down. He let go, and dropped with a splash into the flume. It was some three shaku wide and deep, with no cover; the water in it was shallow, not a shaku deep. He crawled ahead. The flume went over the stone rampart, past the san-no-maru, over the inner moat, through the ni-no-maru, and into the honmaru garden. A small pond, exquisitely wrought, loomed before him, and he flowed into it along with the water. Before him rose a keep of five stories that yet, with its deep-thrust eaves, had the air of an elegant mansion—one single structure,
 and from within it came a baby's crying. Beyond doubt this was where Lady Yodo and Hiroi lived.

Once he had made that sure, he ran back under the flume, climbed the wall where it crossed over, let himself into the flume again, crawled against the current to the wheel, caught hold of a pot, and rode it back down to the river.

On the evening of the seventh day of his watch, the Taikō came at last—on horseback, with an escort of some ten riders. Goemon watched him go into the castle, then went back to the ceiling-space of the nearby temple where he was quartered without leave, and lay down. For a long time now he had done no ninja's work. But he could not believe he would fail to slip into this little castle and take the sleeping old man's head. In his shinobi days he had almost never botched a dangerous task, and he had no feeling of failing at this one.

The Taikō seemed to be holding a feast. In the dead of night Goemon went by the way he had proved, slipped into the castle, and crawled beneath the floor of the single structure beside the keep. He tried to get up into the roof through a gable—the triangular space at the end of the roof—but every gable was sealed hard with plaster, and he could not. And under the floor of the living-quarters, save for the veranda parts round the rim, all was walled in with piled stone and iron bars, so that he could not get through.

From a spot he took for the kitchen he lifted a floorboard, and came up at last into the house. To his further astonishment the house had no ceiling: the sturdy roof-frame lay bare and open overhead. So his usual way—to hide in the ceiling and watch the room below—was closed to him. There was nothing for it but to go warily along the corridor, deeper and deeper in. The walls to either side were flat plaster, no pillar and no lintel standing out from them, so that he could find no hold for hand or foot, and could not climb.

"This is no ordinary house," Goemon marveled. Surely some man versed in ninjutsu had counseled its building, and it had been designed so that no ninja could break into it. But whatever its make, having come this far he could not draw back. He pressed on.

Around a turn, his dark-schooled eyes saw a corridor some six ken long that ended blind, and in the end wall a strange door. It seemed to be of copper, holding a heavy, sunken gleam, and on its face were carved arabesques and, in relief, a goat. He did not know it was a door carried from an old castle in Spain and presented as tribute; but he sensed that beyond it was the very bedchamber of the Taikō and Lady Yodo he was after. He stepped toward it—and sprang back with a gasp. The floor under his foot had sung out, kiki, kiku.

He stepped out again, warily. It sang out again. He shifted to the edge; that sang too. Setting his will, he made a single bound and came down on the floor some two ken ahead. The floor cried kikiki, kukuku, kiku, keke, for all the world like a greenfinch taking wing. There was nowhere left to set a foot. He stood frozen.

In that instant the walls to left and right swung round, and out sprang a crowd of samurai. Goemon leaped for the ceiling—but it too was smooth plaster, with nowhere to lay a hand, and as he dropped back down in vain, still more men piled on and bore him to the floor. A net like a fishing-net was flung over his head, and he was trussed hand and foot. Lamps were brought, and everything stood plain.

Then a few words struck his ear. "Lord Hattori Hanzō—a fine catch."

Goemon twisted his bound neck and looked round. Among the press of men stood Hattori Hanzō.

"This one's in shinobi black—who might he be?" said one, cutting the net away at his throat and tearing off the hood.

"Ha—Saeki Dennai!" cried Hanzō.

"Does my lord Hattori know this man?"

"I do indeed. An Iga ninja. For a time he served under me at Hamamatsu; but ten years ago he ran off, and his whereabouts were lost."

"Hm—and what would a fellow like that want with His Highness the Taikō—"

"That we shall have to ask the man himself. By your leave—" And with that Hanzō turned to face Goemon. "Dennai. It has been a long while."

"Hanzō—you—what are you doing here—" Goemon glared at him.

"Lord Ieyasu, you see, has come up to the capital for the feast in honor of Fushimi castle's completion, and lodges in the study-hall over yonder. And you crept in here not even knowing it? The design of this castle—my own ninja band's River-Wind that Draws the Waves had a hand in it. This corridor is a nightingale floor. Because we knew that you, out for His Highness's life, would come."

"You knew?"

"Your every move, you see, Moriichirō has been tracking. Where have you put Goichi? The blind Goichi?"

"What—? Goichi—" Goemon shuddered.

"A curious turn of fate, was it not. The Shōfuku-ji at Azōda is the very temple where I keep my faith."

"What—? Goichi—"

"Only twenty days ago I went to the Shōfuku-ji. And I found Goichi there. Ten years old now, he told me."

Goemon reeled, and crumpled where he lay.

Hanzō had heard from Moriichirō only that Goemon had set out east with the boy. By chance, at the Shōfuku-ji, he had marked a blind child there, and had it from Ryōsai and Tamo that the boy was left in their keeping by a well-off farmer on the Ise pilgrimage; but after a good deal of questioning he had guessed it was Goemon's son. And once Goemon had given the blind child up, it followed plainly that he would come up to the capital to go after Hideyoshi.

"You really are a luckless man. I opened the road for you, and what did you do but throw it away—why could you not have stayed quietly a samurai?"

A rage boiled up in Goemon, and he roared. "What! The brazen, barefaced gall of you!"

Hanzō gave a thick, deep laugh. "Ha ha ha—shall I tell you a good story? I gave Hatsuko to Lord Ieyasu. She is called the Ocha-no-tsubone now, and has all his love to herself. And I, when Lord Ieyasu entered Edo castle, was raised to a hatamoto of three thousand koku. Karasumaru too, and Etegi, are fine samurai these days. Had you but served quietly, you too, man—"

Just then the golden bell that hung before the door rang, karan, karan.

One of the night-watch samurai ran to the door and pressed both hands to the floor. The hoarse voice that came from within seemed to be the Taikō's. "What is this? Such a racket."

"We beg your pardon for troubling your rest. Just now a ninja, one Dennai or some such—"

Hanzō cut in. "His true name is Goemon, of Ishikawa."

The whole company was thunderstruck, and, forgetting they stood before the Taikō, burst out in a confusion of voices.

"What? Ishikawa Goemon?"

"That Goemon?"

"The one who in the early years of Tenshō—twenty years ago—set the whole capital in an uproar, the greatest thief of the age—"

Hanzō answered. "The very man."

"He was still alive?"

"He was."

"Where had he been lying hid?"

"In the village of Nagao in Kishū, farming."

The Taikō's voice came. "And what has so thrown this Goemon off his course that he comes forth now?"

Hanzō drew close to the door.

"Insolently, he meant to cut short the life of His Highness the Taikō—"

"The insolent fool, who fears neither Heaven nor earth!"

"Will you look upon him?"

"A defilement to the eyes. Away with him, and quickly! You will wake the young lord Ohiroi."

"At once."

They ran a pole through the net, hoisted Goemon up, and hurried out with him.

You will think this strange, reader. Ieyasu ought to have wished for nothing so much as the Taikō's earliest death; so why did Hanzō lay out the anti-ninja defenses for the Taikō, ready the net, thicken the night-watch, and take a hand himself? But Ieyasu's heart went deeper than that. Deep in Hideyoshi's trust, he had been set in the Kantō to hold down the newly-attached lords of the northeast, and was not called up for the army sent against Korea; so the longer the war ran on, the more strength he could lay by in the meanwhile. Thus Hideyoshi and the lords alike would wear themselves down; and if he only waited for Hideyoshi's allotted span to run out and the man to die, then, as the daimyo who had been most loyal to Hideyoshi and yet held the greatest wealth and the greatest force of arms, he would have the whole realm tumble entire into his palm. Far better, then, not to hand the lords a pretext to turn against him by some rash stroke like the murder of Hideyoshi.

Goemon was taken on the fifteenth of the sixth month, and until the twenty-fourth of the eighth month lay shut in a strong pit dug behind Fushimi castle. There he was examined; but as to why he had gone against the Taikō, he would say only, "Because he is the enemy of the peasants." This being judged too little, later ages heaped all manner of guesswork upon it. The Shinsho Taikōki above all, which never once saw that Goemon was a friend to the peasant class, could do no better than to imagine this.

When his one son Tsurumatsu died of sickness in the eighth month of Tenshō 19, Hideyoshi gave up hope of another and, in the eleventh month, made Hidetsugu his heir. Hidetsugu was the son of Hideyoshi's elder sister, the Zuiryū-in, and Miyoshi Musashi-no-kami—so Hideyoshi's nephew, and twenty-four years old at the time. But once Hiroi was born, Hidetsugu naturally became an obstacle, and Lady Yodo, most of all, was the spearhead of it, and pressed Hideyoshi hard. Hidetsugu, sensing he had gone cold in Hideyoshi's eyes, gave himself up for lost, sank the deeper into debauchery, and grew more and more savage. Among his retainers was one Kimura Hitachi-no-suke, who, seeing well enough where Hidetsugu was bound, thought it better to rise in revolt, kill Hideyoshi and seize the realm, and urged as much upon his lord. And here Goemon comes in. That is: Hitachi-no-suke picks out Goemon and commissions him to kill the Taikō. Goemon, with an eye to the reward, takes it on, slips into the Taikō's bedchamber, draws his blade, and in the very instant he would have run the neck through, the plover incense-burner set at the pillow cried out, so that he failed and was seized.

This plover incense-burner was a famed vessel handed down in the house of Imagawa Yoshimoto. After the Imagawa line failed it passed into Nobunaga's hands; at the Honnō-ji Incident it became the property of Akechi Mitsuhide's younger brother, Samanosuke Mitsuharu; and when Mitsuharu took his own life at Sakamoto castle, he made a present of it to Hideyoshi—or so the story runs. The Iga Ueno Municipal Library holds a manuscript called the Zokukin Hisei-dan, which by the kindness of Okuse Heishichirō I was able to borrow and read. It seems to be nothing more than this Taikōki passage spun out into a full-length novel of fifteen volumes. Some scholars hold that it was written in the Genroku era and comes before the Shinsho Taikōki; but to me—though I have no proof of it—it reads rather as a swelling-out of the Taikōki passage.

Now, about midday on the twenty-fourth of the eighth month, Goemon was dragged out of the pit, bound fast, and—for fear a master of ninjutsu might work himself free—covered over besides with a net, and roped again over that, and lashed like a bale of freight to the bare back of a horse, and paraded through the streets of Kyoto, and then led to the execution ground on the Sanjō riverbed. The Kamo River was far broader then than now, a spread of cobbles and pampas and reeds that seemed to run clear to the foot of Higashiyama. One spot there was fenced round with a stout bamboo palisade, and a great iron cauldron some four shaku across stood on a furnace built up of piled round stones. Over the cauldron four long, thick logs, a good fifteen shaku each, were set up in a fork, and from the center an iron chain and an iron hook hung down. All around rose a mountain of firewood.

The punishment called kama-yude—to bring water to a boil in a cauldron and drop a man in to be boiled to death—made even the people of that day, hardened as they were to cruelty, feel their flesh crawl. And when it went further, to filling the cauldron with oil and frying a man alive, it was a thing rare even by the measure of the age. The man to be fried, moreover, was the phantom thief who in the early years of Tenshō had plunged the streets of Kyoto to the very bottom of terror, and who now, twenty years on, had appeared out of nowhere and been taken while seeking the life of His Highness the Taikō. No wonder the ground beyond the palisade was a black mountain of onlookers.

A court noble of the time, Yamashina Tokitsugu, left a diary, and its entry for the twenty-fourth of the eighth month of Bunroku 3 reads: "Fair. A thief, and ten cutpurses, and one other man, fried in the cauldron. Nineteen of the same sort put to the stake. The high and the low thronged together," and so on. It gives no name for Goemon. But the shogunate's Confucian officer Hayashi Razan, at the command of the third shogun Iemitsu, compiled in Kan'ei 19, 1642—forty-nine years after Bunroku 3—the Toyotomi Hideyoshi-fu, and there sets down the robber's name plainly as Ishikawa Goemon. About a chō from this palisade, on the riverbed, was a second spot fenced with bamboo, where robbers and highwaymen and cutpurses were crucified that morning; and the two grounds were confused, so that Razan records Goemon as fried to death along with his mother and twenty of his fellows.

The twenty-fourth of the eighth month was an overcast, sultry day.
