## The Race Ticket

The film now showing was past its midpoint, and the audience for the next had not yet come; outside the Grand Theatre only two or three passersby hurried along in the cold wind. The film poster overhead had been half torn loose by the wind and flapped noisily in mid-air; the setting sun fell on the somewhat warped and distorted face of Marlene Dietrich, and that famous head of golden hair, worn down now by winter's wind, rain, and dust, had gone dull and lusterless. Chen Qianli made a circuit of the ticket office and came out again by another door. He walked on eastward a way, then turned left into Park Road.

He thought back carefully: there should have been nothing amiss. The day before, he had pasted a green slip on the telephone pole at the door of the painting-and-scroll shop. The slip was one Old Fang had given him before he died; the shop's owner had only to open his door to see the missing-person notice written on it, and would then go, at the prescribed hour, to the Carlton Theatre to meet the one who came. The painting-and-scroll shop had opened for business, and by night the second floor was lit as well. Chen Qianli had watched the neighborhood a long while: there was no one loitering idle at a street corner with nothing to do but smoke, and no cobbler clumsy with his tools.

These days an Italian opera company, the San Carlo, was giving Turandot at the Carlton. Beside the theater door, on a huge Expressionist-style poster, ran a line in both Chinese and Italian: In Turandot's homeland, the executioner is never idle. It was a line from the opening chorus, and there was no telling what the poster's maker meant by singling it out.

The owner of that painting-and-scroll shop was none other than Yi Junnian. Chen Qianli had watched him carefully at his shop door for two days, and picked out at a glance, from the crowd, Yi Junnian in his lined gown of gray satin.

Chen Qianli went forward with a small knot of people, drawing slowly nearer to Yi Junnian. He turned side-on and looked across the road, where the Four Banks' Savings Society building was going up, the winter sun striking the scaffolding that towered into the clouds. “A seller of old calligraphy and paintings—come to see an Italian opera, no less?”

“It tells a Chinese story, after all. And you are—?” Yi Junnian jerked his chin toward the poster, on which was a sharp-chinned Chinese woman, with the dim shapes of Chinese-style palaces in the background.

“The name is Chen.” Chen Qianli looked back at him.

Yi Junnian drew a cigarette case unhurriedly from his overcoat pocket, and under cover of offering a cigarette took the measure of the newcomer. Coming along with the flow of the crowd the man was almost unremarkable, but once he stood face to face he was like some figure stepped out of a film. Chen Qianli waved the case away; Yi Junnian took out a Garrick cigarette for himself and lit it. “Mr. Chen—not a local, I take it?”

“I've come from Xinjing, charged by another to look out for some fine things. They say that lately the wares of Beiping have all made their way to Shanghai—might Mr. Yi have a channel for such things?”

This overcoat of Chen Qianli's, bought from a secondhand shop in Vladivostok—lambskin-lined, fox-fur at the collar—declared at a glance that it came from a place of bitter cold. From the moment he stepped ashore he had let drop, in and out of his talk, that he was an antiques dealer with certain mysterious ties to the Japanese merchants adventuring on the mainland.

“Even the government means to ship Beiping's antiquities off to Nanjing—to say nothing of private hands.” Yi Junnian took up Chen Qianli's theme. Because the Japanese army was stirring restlessly along the line of the Great Wall, the National Government was said to be preparing to pick out a batch of treasures from the Palace Museum in Beiping and send them to Nanjing. For days the newspapers had been full of talk of nothing else.

The wind blew from the direction of the empty racecourse, scattering fallen plane-tree leaves everywhere. Yi Junnian tossed away the half-smoked cigarette and rubbed his hands. One before, one behind, as if merely by chance and of one accord, the two of them walked off toward the racecourse.

By the railing at the outer edge of the racecourse the passersby were few, and they stopped. The races were mostly held in spring and autumn, the two seasons when gamblers and tabloid reporters would crowd the trackside rails, each vying to gather and spread every sort of report, true and false. On ordinary days jockeys and grooms would now and then lead the horses a few turns around the track, showing them off before the crowd—feigning high spirits or low—to work upon the odds. But just now, on the racecourse wrapped in dusk, there were only a few foreign children scrambling for a leather ball.

“What is it that interests you, Mr. Chen? I know only a little of calligraphy and painting.”

“Then I've found the right man.”

The ball was kicked into the air and fell again on the gravel track, startling up a few sparrows. Chen Qianli said softly: “My one worry is buying a fake.”

“Buying a fake—that happens often enough; in Shanghai even a great collector like Mr. Jin is not spared being taken in.”

All at once a lone racehorse appeared by the rail, a striped blanket over its back, the groom following at a distance and calling out now and then. Horse and man, one each, circled the track in solitude.

“I should like to hear it in full—” To listen to a faintly comic tale in the bleak cold wind of a winter's dusk seemed much to Chen Qianli's taste.

“Mr. Jin loves the Four Masters of the Ming above all, and dreams of owning a ‘Qiu Ying’; everyone in the painting-and-calligraphy trade knows it.”

Yi Junnian lit another cigarette, his eyes on the band of small boys now climbing back over the rail to go home. “So one day, a ‘Qiu Ying’ came knocking at his door of its own accord. The man said he had a small ‘Qiu Ying’ painting in hand. Mr. Jin was overjoyed, set a day for him to bring it round to be seen, and expressly invited a Shanghai connoisseur of the painting-and-calligraphy world to come and appraise it with him that day.

“On the day, sure enough, the man came with a ‘Qiu Ying’ in hand; the connoisseur who had been invited studied it minutely for a good while, then said the painting was a fake—”

Yi Junnian paused, and drew on his cigarette.

“Being a fake, it was of course not something a man of Mr. Jin's standing would take. Mr. Jin said little; after the usual courtesies he saw the man politely to the door. The connoisseur too declined the evening's banquet and left along with him.

“Something struck Mr. Jin as odd, and, growing a little wary, he sent a servant out after them—who saw this very connoisseur, on the street outside the gate, stopping the man and insisting, come what may, on buying it. The servant came back and reported to Mr. Jin, and Mr. Jin flew into a rage: this was little short of daylight robbery. The next day he sent word to the connoisseur: either sell it to Mr. Jin—who would gladly double the price again—or keep the ‘Qiu Ying’ himself and never think to show his face in Shanghai again.”

“The painting was a fake,” said Chen Qianli.

“Just so.” Yi Junnian threw away the cigarette butt. “The connoisseur had painted it himself.”

Chen Qianli laughed suddenly. “A good story—but it sounds stolen out of the Xiaolin Guangji.”

From inside his overcoat he took a Guangyi Book Company edition of the Xiaolin Guangji and handed it to Yi Junnian. Yi Junnian took it and opened it: tucked in the book was half of a racecourse Grand Champagne ticket.

“This round's Grand Champagne has drawn a first prize of two hundred thousand.” As he spoke, Yi Junnian reached into his coat. “More and more people bet on the horses; the more slack the times, the livelier the racecourse.” He drew out his half of the ticket, printed with the words A charitable donation of one national dollar is deducted. He fitted the two halves together into one whole ticket.

Yi Junnian no longer needed to feign indifference, and reached, with some emotion, to grasp Chen Qianli's hand. He had just been through arrest and interrogation, and after his release had heard that Old Fang was dead; at every moment he feared losing touch with the organization for good. But Chen Qianli did not take the hand held out to him, and only smiled at him.

“My name is Chen Qianli,” he said.

At the name, Yi Junnian was taken aback.

“Comrade Chen Qianli,” he said, holding his feelings in check, though his tone still carried a grieved indignation, “Old Fang is dead.”

The sky had darkened and the street lamps were coming on one by one. Chen Qianli watched Yi Junnian closely. “Where did the news come from?”

“We have an inside line in the Municipal Police. They found Old Fang at his son's barbershop. The son was arrested too, and the enemy put him to cruel torture, wanting to know whom Old Fang had gone there to meet. Was it you?”

“Old Fang met me near the wharf; when he left there was nothing wrong.” Chen Qianli had reached no firm judgment, but he had resolved that some things he would watch a while before speaking of.

“By rights, you've only just come out of prison, and I ought not to be in haste to contact you.” Chen Qianli spoke very plainly. “But I must find ‘Old Kai.’”

Yi Junnian lit another cigarette, his face now bright, now dark. Chen Qianli felt he could not read the other's expression.

Yi Junnian let out a soft sigh, knowing Chen Qianli was right, much as he longed to win the man's trust at once and take up the work again straightaway.

“‘Old Kai’—that would be the comrade sent from above to convey the task?”

“Where is he now?”

“He had not yet declared himself when the enemy burst in. We had come into the room one after another, each laying his dominoes on the table. Once all twelve had gathered, the man chairing the meeting was to bring out a pair of dice, to prove he was the comrade sent from above to convey the task. But just then came the sound of gunfire, and then someone jumped from a window; everyone scattered in haste, but both passages were blocked, and we could only go back into the room. In the confusion I saw that the pair of dice was already on the table—which meant he had come. I realized we would be arrested at any moment, and that he, the one man who knew the secret of the meeting, must not be found out by the enemy. It was too urgent; without stopping to think, I quietly slipped the dice into my own pocket.”

Chen Qianli thought a moment, then asked: “Do you think the enemy knows ‘Old Kai’ is among these people?”

“My guess is the enemy may not know the alias at all; no one has ever mentioned it. Old Fang did not speak of it either when he notified us.”

“Can you recall how the interrogation went?”

Had they been standing here too long? Chen Qianli turned and leaned his back against the rail. The sky was nearly full dark; under the distant lamps a passerby or two appeared now and then, and on the main road newly installed neon lights traced the outlines of a building or two against the night. Were anyone to see them talking here so long, might it not seem strange? he thought.

“The second day after we were taken to Longhua, the questioning began very early in the morning. Mine came later—not until the afternoon. Someone heard that the women's cells too were called from the morning on. Afterward I heard from Comrade Ling Wen that they questioned Young Dong first—that is, Dong Huiwen—”

“Did you have a channel of contact in prison?”

“No, we hadn't—the women's cells were too far off. It was after we got out; after we got out, Ling Wen and I met.”

What is he shrinking from? thought Chen Qianli; in the dark he dimly sensed the other give an embarrassed smile before going on: “She was let out in the morning, and knowing we would all be released that day, she called two rickshaws, sending one empty to wait for me at the gate where the Garrison Command releases its prisoners, while she rode in the other and watched from a distance. We all got in touch; the six of us had agreed, on release, to keep in contact with one another, and whoever found the organization first would tell the rest. Ling Wen and I had worked together from the start—she was my subordinate. Our team dealt only in intelligence; she was inside staff and courier both, loyal and dependable, and her husband died a martyr some years ago. I trust her.

“Sometimes they took one out first, then another a while later, and now and then two or three would be questioned together. The first out would not necessarily be the first back. The interrogations were not held in one place, and everyone, back in his cell, would recall what he could, so as to tell the other comrades what to be wary of. I myself was taken out and questioned twice; You Tianxiao, the captain of the Judge Advocate's detective squad, came to both my questionings—only for a short while each time, not long, hurrying, I suppose, between several interrogation rooms. You Tianxiao did not believe our prepared story in the least; he was quite certain we were not gambling. How could people none of whom knew one another have come together?—he pressed the question again and again, and I think he had guessed we had some special task, and reckoned there would be a man to convey it. At the second questioning he brought up the dice, asking who it was that had produced them.”

“You mean that by the second questioning he knew about the dice? Then—someone must have leaked the secret to the enemy under interrogation?”

Yi Junnian gave it careful thought. “I think not. When You Tianxiao led his men into the meeting place, he himself brought out a pair of dice and set them on the table, putting on an air of knowing everything.”

“If that's so—” Chen Qianli seemed to weigh his words even as he strove to think it through. “—you suspect the organization has been penetrated by the enemy?”

“I believe—” Yi Junnian brought out with difficulty a thought he seemed long to have turned over, “—that there is very likely a traitor within.”

Chen Qianli shook his head, as if resolving to set the thought aside for now. “I must make contact with Comrade ‘Old Kai.’”

“The higher-ups didn't tell you, either, what the task actually is?”

Chen Qianli did not answer, but asked Yi Junnian in turn: “What means did you agree on for keeping in contact with one another after your release?”

Yi Junnian told him the others' means of contact. He found this comrade's memory remarkably good: every address and method he gave but once, and the man did not ask again.

As the two parted, Chen Qianli turned back again and asked Yi Junnian: “That Wei Dafu—is he the comrade charged with arranging safe houses?”

“The organization placed him in a house-letting agency; the moment a secret contact point or a safe house is needed, he can arrange it quickly—even at short notice it's no trouble to him. He has, too, some thoroughly reliable sureties in hand.”

Yi Junnian rolled up the Xiaolin Guangji and gripped it, hesitating a moment before he added: “But since the enemy wrecked the meeting at the market, and with some comrades arrested and imprisoned besides, he's grown somewhat downcast.”

“Then I'll take the chance to have a look at him.” Chen Qianli took off his hat and scratched his hair. Yi Junnian glanced at him. “Yes—the New Year's coming; I ought to go for a haircut too.”

Chen Qianli's retreating figure was soon lost in the dark of night. Yi Junnian pressed down his hat, drew his overcoat tight, and, hands clasped behind him, walked off toward the Racecourse Road intersection. Crossing the street, he tossed the Xiaolin Guangji, offhand, into a passing rubbish cart, whose draft mule plodded with drooping head, all unaware.

Yi Junnian stopped before the Morriss Building and lit another cigarette. In the cold wind the passersby walked at a slow pace, as though in no hurry to get home. Not until he had finished the cigarette and ground the butt out on his shoe-sole did he turn, along the dark-red brick wall, into a lane.
