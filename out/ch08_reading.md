## Chapter Eight

The rising wind on the government-bond market had crushed many a blind speculator. The three men Wu Sunfu saw that day in the dining-room of the Bankers' Guild were among those the speculation had ruined; and the one in the middle above all—Feng Yunqing, with his long narrow face and his crescent-moon whiskers, close on fifty—had taken a terrible fall.

Half a year before this, Feng Yunqing had still been sitting snug at home, enjoying his ease. A half-fledged juren of the former Qing, he could never quite push his way into the class of "country gentry" who ran the district; and so he, Feng Yunqing, had battened on the peasants by lending at usury, and had actually built himself up a fortune of a kind. The "village loans" he put out were never repaid in hard cash; nor did he care for hard cash, principal and interest matched, at the six-month term—what he was after was the land the peasants mortgaged with him. His whole art lay in this, that a debt of five or ten dollars put out could within two years be turned into five or ten mu of field! Such methods were common enough in the interior; but Feng Yunqing was a noted "smiling tiger," a noted usurer who "flew his kite on a long string," and his "net of usury" was laid out with the most rigorous care, exactly like a spider spreading its web to snare the flying insects. Let a peasant once fall into debt to him, though it were for a single dollar, and Feng Yunqing would in the end fleece him of house and home and make of him a tenant of the Feng estate—in plain fact a slave, an ox or a horse! By the year of the Qi-Lu war Feng Yunqing already held two or three thousand mu of land, all of it wrung and wrested from others three mu and five mu at a time, all of it steeped in the peasants' tears and their blood and sweat. It was thus, upon the bleached bones of poor peasants past counting, that Feng Yunqing raised up his warm, well-fed, dissolute life!

At the time of the Qi-Lu war several of the seasoned old "country gentry" had all taken refuge in the foreign concessions of Shanghai; when Sun Chuanfang's troops passed through the district there was scarcely a "somebody" to receive them, and it was Feng Yunqing who stepped boldly forward and waited on them with quite extraordinary attentiveness. By this he squeezed his way up onto the "political stage" of his native place, and his peasant-fleecing "net of usury" spread the faster for it, and grew the stronger; in less than two years his holdings had gained a thousand-odd mu more. But by now his newly taken darling, Old Ninth, was helping him run through money at a fine rate. And with such a young concubine at his side—a creature soft and spilling as a puddle of water—Feng Yunqing's vigour was much less than it had been; so that when of late the bandits swarmed up in the interior and the peasants rose in tumult, Feng Yunqing's boldness and composure fell far short of what they had been in the year of the Qi-Lu war, and he could only scrape together every dollar of ready cash he had and move the whole household to Shanghai—half for fear of the bandits and the peasants, and half to fall in with his concubine's own wishes.

Now that he was a "gentleman of leisure upon the seas" he could not simply eat into dead capital. He still had, it was true, some thousands of mu of land with rents to live on; but the times were no longer what they had been, when the safe four-and-six division of the rent came in of itself. The ready cash he had brought out came to seventy or eighty thousand; yet to put out daily-interest loans in a place like Shanghai, Feng Yunqing was not of standing enough; while to leave it in the bank drawing interest—the return was too thin. Reckoning in even the cost of his concubine's opium, Feng Yunqing's outgoings at his Shanghai residence ran to nearly a thousand dollars a month, and took no small contriving to meet. Happily the government was issuing government bonds in quantity, and—"thanks" to the unbroken years of civil war—the bond market was forever shifting; and so Feng Yunqing, with his seventy or eighty thousand in cash, walked into the bond market, and for half a year had had it all his own way, drawing off a good two and a half per cent in interest. He had all but set himself up for an "old hand at the bonds"—when, who could have foreseen it, this one tumble had left him reeling and giddy, half doubting he had dreamed the whole thing!

When the settlement came and he reckoned up his accounts, the loss was no small one indeed! Of fifty thousand in margin, not a copper came back to him, and within three days he must further make good more than thirty thousand—the broker Han Mengxiang had already been round to press him for it the day before. From the time he rose at half past eleven that morning Feng Yunqing had worked his abacus over who knew how many times, and now, when it was already two o'clock, he had forgotten to eat his breakfast and still could hit on no way out; and what baffled him worst of all was that he could not for the life of him see how a man was to "play" the bonds hereafter.

The sunlight, falling through that row of bamboo blinds, dyed the front half of the wing-room in stripes of black and white. Whenever there was the least breath of wind the blinds swayed gently, and the striped play of light and shade rippled like water over the furniture of the room, working itself into strange new patterns of black and white. Feng Yunqing sat by the square rosewood table at the window, a cigarette held between the fingers of his left hand, turning over his account-book with his right. The watery ripples of light flickered and flickered across the pages, until the very characters in the ledger seemed to be dancing there. All at once Feng Yunqing was seized with vexation; he slapped the account-book down with his right hand, rose, and paced into the back half of the wing-room, where he lay down on the rosewood couch that faced outward, shut his eyes, and heaved a sigh. Yesterday he had been a rich man at his ease; today he had turned into a pauper, and worse, was tens of thousands in the hole! Was it his own fault? That he would deny to the death!—"Bad luck!" he sighed again, and said it inwardly. Yet why should he, who for more than twenty years had run nothing but a lucky streak, be dealt so sudden a blow? Feng Yunqing knitted his brows and screwed up his eyes, and could make no sense of it at all. Suddenly a heavy thud fell on the floorboards over his head, and his whole body gave a start; in a flurry he sat up. Then he heard the electric bell in the maidservants' room behind the wing-room ring away for a good three minutes on end. It must be the concubine woken and calling for someone! Last night, again, she had not come home until daybreak. This was an old story, and had never used to trouble Feng Yunqing; but now, a ruined man from the failure of his bond speculation, he was of a sudden thoroughly out of sorts over it. And a thought stirred in him: it seemed to him that his own "bad luck" had something, in some measure, to do with the concubine's wantonness—for who ever saw a man in the green headscarf walk in good fortune?

Feng Yunqing shifted his feet and turned about, and the few crescent whiskers on his lip trembled and rustled. He had half a mind to go up and put on a stern face for the concubine to see. But he had gone no more than a step when he stopped again and fell to brooding. A concubine with all those sworn sisters was no one to provoke lightly!… Feng Yunqing swallowed down his temper and stared blankly at the framed "Maxims for the Ordering of a Household" of Mr. Zhu Bolu, penned in a close regular hand, that hung on the wall behind the couch. He sank, lost, into a brown study.

The clack of high-heeled shoes came clicking in from without and stopped short at the door of the wing-room. The door swung open, and in ran, light-footed, a girl of seventeen or eighteen; her face too was of the somewhat long and narrow cast, but the wholly charming red lips, the chin that was neither too pointed nor too round, and the long hair, waved and hanging loosely to the ears, made good that "fault" of narrowness entirely. She wore a long gown of pale-green figured voile, lined with deep-yellow silk and slit very high, so that as she moved it floated easily open and flashed a glimpse of a rounded, soft, full thigh—a most marked contrast to the tall, stiff collar, sealed close about her neck and propping up her chin. Seeing Feng Yunqing lost in gloom before the "Maxims for the Ordering of a Household," the girl too gave a slight start and halted at the door; but at once she broke into a giggle and, swaying her slender waist, ran up to Feng Yunqing and said in a coaxing voice:

"Papa! I want to buy a few things—"

Feng Yunqing turned his face round and, taken aback, opened his eyes wide.

"A few little things. A hundred dollars will just about do it. I'm going out directly."

The girl spoke again, twisting her waist to one side, her eyes on the floor. All at once she spun about and ran flying to the front half of the wing-room, darted to the square table, switched on the little electric fan with one hand, and with a turn of her body set her back to the fan, calling out again, all girlish and petulant:

"Ai, why haven't you got the fan on! Papa, your face is all sweat—come here! It's cool over here—a hundred dollars, Papa!"

Feng Yunqing shook his head with a rueful face, paced slowly up to his daughter, gazed at her a good while, and then, as though he had made up his mind, said:

"Ah Mei, you don't know yet—this time in the bonds I've taken a fall! I'm more than thirty thousand silver dollars in the hole! The day after tomorrow is the Dragon-Boat, and there's no meeting even the odd shop-bills. Just now I went over Old Ninth's account-slip, and this one item alone comes to over five hundred—"

"I've only had four or five dresses made, Papa!"

"Ai—but today you want a hundred dollars again; what's it to buy? Meiqing, your pocket-money runs to more than mine!"

"It's a great deal less than Auntie's!"

Meiqing pouted as she answered, and with a twist of her waist sat down on the nearest sofa-couch, watching her father's face. On that face there had now risen a look of helplessness and of dread together. Meiqing knew very well why her father was afraid, and added, of set purpose:

"Ai, if there's spending to be done, let everyone spend; why must I alone give way to her!"

"Now don't fret, you—Ah Mei! Let me give it you in a day or two, will that do?"

Feng Yunqing forced out a laugh as he spoke. But Meiqing did not answer; wringing a little figured silk handkerchief in her hands, she turned her face away to look at the scrolls and pictures on the wall. These too were a "marriage of Chinese and Western": beside a hanging scroll of a tiger by Zhang Daqian there hung, by way of complement, two rows of Western pictures printed in colour from copperplates, representing spring, summer, autumn and winter, each set in a gilt-edged frame. The sunlight coming through the bamboo blind struck the gilt edges of the frames and threw back a glittering sheen. Following his daughter's gaze, Feng Yunqing too looked at the prints, while he turned over in his mind how to get her to go; and all at once those four copperplate Western pictures of the seasons called up in him yet another matter of concern. These four Western pictures had been given him, when he first moved into the house, by one of the concubine's sworn sisters; the concubine had a great many sworn sisters, but the one who had made this gift was no ordinary person: her own "lord and master" was a man of great consequence, of very high standing in the Hongmen, and the safety of Feng Yunqing's person and property, sojourning as he was in Shanghai, depended much on the good offices of this powerful patron. Yet the day after tomorrow was the Dragon-Boat Festival, and Feng Yunqing had actually forgotten to send a handsome present to this man of influence, in thanks for the merciful eye his brethren turned upon him.

Feng Yunqing, thus suddenly recalling this weighty affair, felt that there was simply no way he could grant his daughter her request for a hundred dollars.

"Ah Mei, good child, the things you want to buy—wait till the festival's over and buy them then! Look, there are several important festival-presents I've still not sent; your papa really is very pinched just now—always this bad luck, the bonds went wrong. You're my one and only daughter; you don't think I'd harbour any partiality, do you, Ah Mei—"

At this point Feng Yunqing choked and could say no more; he lifted his face and stroked his crescent whiskers without ceasing.

A silence fell for a good while. Only the sound of the concubine clearing her throat, cough by cough, came drifting down from upstairs. Father and daughter, the two of them, each brooded on their own cares. Meiqing felt there was little hope now of getting a hundred dollars, and her heart was all gloom; the festive pleasures she had arranged so nicely were plainly become a bubble burst—and then, how was she to drag through these three days of holiday? Was she to skulk at home the whole day long reading Zhang Ziping's triangle-love romances? Besides, she had already made her engagement with someone—what was to be done! She saw, as in a haze, the face that other one would put on, disappointed and doubting and put out of countenance!

The electric bell rang out ting-a-ling; one, two, three. Feng Yunqing came startled out of his musings and, looking out of the window, saw that the rickshaw-man A Shun had already opened the front gate and was showing in a round-faced man of forty and more, in a glossy gauze skullcap. "Ah, it's He Shen'an come!"—said Feng Yunqing, as though to his daughter, and rose to go out and meet him. But the visitor was quick on his feet and had already come into the wing-room, crying "Venerable Yun!" and bobbing bow after bow, his two hands, clasped together, gripping a walking-stick between them. Meiqing made a bow of sixty degrees, mastering her laughter with all her might before she raised her head again. Every time she saw He Shen'an's skullcap and the air with which he cradled his stick and bowed, she could not keep from laughing.

"Ah Mei, tell the amah to pour Uncle He some tea."

So saying, Feng Yunqing settled He Shen'an on the outward-facing couch. He Shen'an followed with his eyes the retreating figure of Meiqing as she tripped out, and then, with a sudden movement of his brows, turned his face to Feng Yunqing and said gravely:

"Yunqing, I'm not flattering you idly—to have a daughter like that is real good fortune!"

Feng Yunqing gave a wry smile, taking this for a commonplace courtesy. He glanced at He Shen'an, secretly marvelling that this friend, who had likewise taken a fall in the bonds, should still be so "broad of mind and comfortable of girth"; then he looked at his own face as it was thrown back from the tall dressing-mirror in the corner opposite, and felt that in these few days he had aged at least ten years. He could not hold back a sigh, and said in a low voice:

"Yesterday when Han Mengxiang came dunning me for that sum, I simply had no way out at all. Come to think of it, old Han's not a bad friend to a man; that day when we saw him at lunch in the Bankers' Guild, didn't he urge us to cover quick? Had I heeded him then, I'd not have come this cropper. Ai—Shen'an, that day you too were a bit out in your reckoning; your Beiyang-clique friends wouldn't tell you the honest truth—"

"In a word, we all deserved it; the others rigged a trap and we walked into it! And you still say Han Mengxiang is a good friend—what sort of friend is that! He's Zhao Botao's trumpet; they'd rigged the trap beforehand, a great conspiracy, and I've ferreted out the whole of it!"

He Shen'an said this with a cold laugh, flung the stub of his cigarette hard into the spittoon, and took up his teacup for a sip.

"What? A great conspiracy?… You don't mean that winning or losing, too, was a trap laid in advance?"

"And why not! So it wasn't that our luck was bad—it was that we were too honest!"

Feng Yunqing rolled his eyes upward, broke into a cold sweat all over, and the few crescent whiskers rustled and trembled again. He could not but believe what He Shen'an said. He himself had always been in the habit of luring the peasants into his own traps; who could have thought that this time he was the one made to play out the trick of "inviting the gentleman to step into the jar"? Slowly getting his breath back, he tugged hard at his whiskers and said with a woeful face:

"Then—then—my half a lifetime's toil has all been spent playing the ox and horse for them! Shen'an, you've no idea how hard my bit of money came by! For the ins and outs of three mu of land, of five mu, the breath I spent was no little! Country folk are a sticky, dithering lot—they want the loan, and yet they cannot bear to part with the land; I had to go to endless trouble—had to stand them to the teahouse, and reason with them, and bring them to see that I sought only a return on my capital and was not seizing their fields by force;—Shen'an, I was no tyrant; when I went down to the country to collect rents, for instance, I'd not willingly come half a sheng or eight ge short, but I never took hired bruisers along—mine was the slow, patient, water-grinding way. That is how I gathered up my few thousand mu of land; not like you, who've been a magistrate and got your money without turning a hand—you skim a little extra off the land-levy, take a little more on the black goods, and your income for a single month matches mine for a whole year…"

Feng Yunqing paused, drew fiercely on his cigarette a few times, and was about to go on when He Shen'an, across from him, hastily cut him off:

"What's the use of chewing over these old tales! What I want to ask now is this: do you still mean to play the bonds again?"

"Play them again? To be honest, I'm rather afraid to! This morning it struck me that the market swings too violently, and I felt the bonds would be hard to play hereafter; and now that I know there are traps laid in the very middle of it, why, one simply cannot play them at all! Besides, after this utter rout I can no longer turn my capital over—but, Shen'an, what about you?"

"As for me, ten years of official pickings, all poured away east with the water! Yesterday I took a few curios to a tea-party and got a thousand-odd dollars for them, one way and another, so that this Dragon-Boat I can just about scrape through. But by my reckoning you're differently placed. You've your few thousand mu of land, and the rent-rice alone comes to a very tidy sum—"

He Shen'an was obliged to break off. For Feng Yunqing had all at once stood up and sat down again, his two eyeballs bulging, staring blankly, the whites all threaded with red, his face gone the grey of death, the muscles at the corners of his mouth twitching and jumping without pause. He Shen'an gaped in astonishment and put up a hand to scratch his scalp. After a while Feng Yunqing raised his hand with all his might and brought it down hard on the couch-table, and from between his teeth burst out a few words:

"Rent-rice? Who dares go down to the country in times like these to collect rent-rice! Else, with a fine five-courtyard mansion to live in, would I have come to Shanghai to keep up a residence, on tenterhooks the livelong day for fear of kidnappers?"

With that he lurched over onto his side and lay down, eyes shut, doing nothing but pant.

"That the country's unquiet, I know something of that too. But, Yunqing, are you to let those dog-heads off scot-free? You might very well take men down there with you!"

After a silence He Shen'an spoke at last, drawling, turning his glossy gauze skullcap over in his hands and examining it closely; having said his say, he blew a breath upon the cap, and at the last drew out his handkerchief and flicked at it a few times. On his oily round face there hovered a faint smile.

Feng Yunqing, lying there, answered only with a sigh. Not that he did not know the trick of going armed to the country to collect rents; but he knew still better that the peasants of today were no longer what they had been, and that if the armed men he took were a shade too few it was simply useless, while if they were many, then the cost to him, the landlord, ran very high, so that even were he to bring back a quantity of rent-rice, the gain would never cover the outlay: that lesson he had already had once. Being a "smiling tiger" and a nice calculator, he was making ready to let his tenants owe a year's rent, in hopes that next year would be "quiet" and he could bring out his old "smiling tiger" arts again and wring double payment out of the peasants!

He Shen'an lit a cigarette, drew on it a few times, and shifted the drift of the talk:

"Yunqing, let's put our heads together on how to win it back!"

"Win what back?"

Feng Yunqing sat bolt upright and asked it back in alarm. His mind at that moment was away in his native place, hovering over those lands of his; he seemed to see, bursting from the huddled thatch of the tenants' hovels, gust upon gust of resentment—the rancour of thousands of years of oppression and extortion, that now would have its revenge, that now like a volcano's eruption would burn up every fetter and chain. Yet of all this He Shen'an felt nothing; he answered with a slight smile:

"A man who has broken his arm three times becomes a good doctor! Where you took your loss, there you must go to win it back!"

"Oh—it's playing the bonds you're still talking of."

"Naturally! Or have you lost heart altogether?"

"It's not that I've lost heart—it's that my nerve's gone. Only think: they'd made the trap ready and were waiting for us to crawl in!"

As Feng Yunqing spoke he sighed again, and all but shed tears. But He Shen'an could not keep from laughing. He picked up the stick at his side, levelled it at Feng Yunqing, then drew a great circle in the air, and of a sudden reversed it and jabbed it against the floor with a queer loud rap, crying out at the same time:

"Come, come, Yunqing! I do believe that one fall has knocked you silly! How is it you don't see?—Just because the others make their traps to order, and because making money in the bonds turns on that one word 'contrive' and not at all on chancing your luck, we too have a fair few points in hand for winning it back…"

"Shen'an—"

"Don't interrupt—hear me out! The trap was laid by Zhao Botao and his lot; their arms are long, and on that ground we're no match for them, isn't that so? But suppose we could crawl through the dog-hole and spy out their secret—my dear fellow, would you still fear you couldn't win it back?"

At this He Shen'an, mightily pleased with himself, wagged his head, slapped both hands hard on his thighs, and stood up; he moved close in front of Feng Yunqing, narrowed his eyes to slits, and was just about to speak a word of moment when he saw Feng Yunqing ask, with knitted brows:

"And pray, this dog-hole—how is it to be crawled through? Zhao Botao is an old and cunning fox—"

"But old Zhao is one of those who say, 'I have a failing—I am fond of women'; and if we go at him with this snare of a woman, I'll warrant old Zhao shan't jump clear of it!"

He Shen'an put his mouth to Feng Yunqing's ear and said it low, and then burst out into loud laughter.

Feng Yunqing opened his eyes wide and gazed dazed at He Shen'an. His brows were still knit, and over his ashen face there spread a faint flush; he half suspected that what He Shen'an said was eight parts a joke, and it occurred to him that this business of his own concubine's never coming home of a night till daybreak must surely be known even to He Shen'an. But he could only feign stupidity, and lazily set about turning the talk aside:

"Tut, tut! A capital scheme! It would take a man aged and worn by ten years' bobbing in the sea of office to think it up. Venerable Shen, once the thing's brought off, you must let me share a little in the glow of it!"

"That's not the way of it. In this affair, Venerable Yun, it's your side that must do the work! I can only help you lay the plans."

He Shen'an answered with a wholly grave face, his voice dropped so low as to be scarce intelligible. But falling on Feng Yunqing's ear it was like a thunderclap out of a clear sky; his face changed of a sudden, and whether the leap of his heart was gladness or anger—or else it was fear—at all events it leapt with uncommon violence! He knew not how to answer, but only stared, his eyes fixed on that grinning, oily round face of He Shen'an's. And he saw this round face give a sudden shake or two, saw the wide mouth open and a scorched-yellow tongue-tip flick out and draw in again, as it went on softly with a further speech:

"People outside, praising old Zhao's expertness in this line, have a pair of sayings for it: with a gem, one glance and he knows the true from the false; with a woman, once he's had her he knows whether the goods are raw or no! And it's raw goods he loves to sport with. So long as she's an unwed girl, he'll take her up without distinction, black or white. In the room he keeps by the month at Such-and-Such Hotel, it's just this business he attends to. And he's forever going to make his rounds of Such-and-Such Roof-Garden, to pick out likely talent, too! To hook him is not the least bit hard, only—"

"Only—only what?"

Feng Yunqing asked hurriedly, and stood up at once, an air of keen interest showing itself now between his brows too.

"Only a clever, handsome, wholly trustworthy young lady—one just like your own honoured daughter."

He Shen'an answered without haste, faintly smiling; his words were still very low, but every word and phrase came clear.

A sound—"Uh"—came from Feng Yunqing's throat; his face turned of a sudden to the white of death, and without knowing it he sat down again, his eyes fixed on his friend's fat face. But He Shen'an, his expression unchanged, leaned a step nearer and said softly again:

"This is the one road left worth taking! Are you afraid it won't come off? Never fear! I'll go bail for it!—Yunqing, with a girl like that, a man's luck is no small thing…"

"Shen'an!—"

"And once this thing is well done, there's no end of further essays to be written on it; whether played the civil way or the military, the old style or the new, the choice is all yours. Rest easy—I, your counsellor, am to be relied on;—Yunqing, to tell the honest truth: at fleecing the peasants by the slow water-grinding method, I'm no match for you; but at crawling through dog-holes, at working the 'immortal's leap,' at 'loosing the pigeon,' there you're no match for me!"

Then, with a sudden giggle, He Shen'an took up his teacup and drank, and, hands clasped behind his back, turned to look at a family group photograph of Feng Yunqing's that hung on the wall, in the very midst of which stood the slim, lovely figure of Feng Meiqing. There He Shen'an stood looking a good long while, to give Feng Yunqing ample time to weigh the proposal. Just then the sunlight suddenly hid itself away, and the wing-room grew very dim. Women's little bursts of laughter came from upstairs, mixed with the gurgle of water running from the tap. And from the lane outside came the pedlars crying their barbecued-pork buns and their wonton noodles.

Only Feng Yunqing gave not the least sound.

He Shen'an turned his face aside to watch the tall dressing-mirror set obliquely opposite. This mirror, tucked in the corner, was like a doorway in which Feng Yunqing's wavering, irresolute face flickered and peered. Presently, on the lower part of that long narrow face, near the whiskers, a few creases gathered, and above them the pair of narrow eyes rolled round with a click, as though he had come to a resolve. He Shen'an could not keep from turning about, and just then Feng Yunqing let fall, as if to himself:

"Lay a trap for him too, to crawl into—eh?"

"Now that's the way to talk, Yunqing!"

He Shen'an hastened to take him up, and sat down facing Feng Yunqing. But Feng Yunqing, with a twist of his lips that was half a smile and half none, suddenly changed his tune again:

"Shen'an, let's talk sense after all. You say the rise and fall of the bonds hangs entirely on victory or defeat at the front, don't you? And yet not wholly so. The manipulation of the big operators counts for a great deal too; they can turn it about! Old Zhao—ai, how is one to spy out his secret? Shen'an, you're the man of resource and stratagem!"

He Shen'an did not answer, but shot up his brows and broke into loud laughter. He saw clean through Feng Yunqing—every word of it said back to front—and knew that his proposal had already stirred the old man's heart, only that for face's sake he could not own it openly. When he had laughed a while, he stood up and clapped Feng Yunqing on the shoulder, saying:

"My dear fellow, no need to stand on ceremony—are you so very far behind me? Weigh it and do as you see fit! Till later, then."

Here Feng Yunqing saw him to the front gate, and turning back stood a while abstracted in the courtyard, a zhang square, before a few pots of tender-red azaleas and a jar of goldfish, when all at once he could not help laughing to himself. But the laughter had barely ceased when down there suddenly pattered a few tears; he pressed two folded fingers to his eye-sockets, as though scarcely believing that what he shed was indeed tears. And at the same moment a vision rose before his moistened eyes: that tender red was no longer the azaleas, but his daughter's smiling face, and towering high beside it was a jar full of great silver ingots. He let out a soft breath, stepped quickly back into the wing-room, and let his body drop heavily onto the sofa.

He knitted his brows tight, meaning to think out carefully every pressing matter before him. But, strangely enough, only three things went rolling round and round in his head: his daughter was handsome, money was lovely, old Zhao was easily hooked. All at once he set his jaw, dealt himself a slap, and swore inwardly through clenched teeth: "Old turtle! Is this any way to go on?—He Shen'an came on purpose to make game of you! As a rule the men who've muddled through official life from the old Qing into the Republic are baser than dogs, the lot of them! You, Master Feng, are a landlord of standing, a house that hands down poetry and the rites; how is it that, hearing old He's one wild speech of nonsense, your heart should actually go tottering so?—The proper way is still to look to the land for a plan!" At this he felt his heart a shade lighter, and his backbone stiffened up a little straighter; but another queer thing came and stuck to the membranes of his brain and would not go: the peasants were in tumult, and those few thousand mu of good land were plainly no longer to be reckoned as bearing the surname Feng, yet it was still the Fengs who must pay the grain-tax and the levies upon it. With a rueful face he gave his head a shake, stood up and looked about him on every side; he could scarce believe he was still sitting in the comfortable wing-room, for he seemed dimly to hear an earth-rending, sky-splitting blast, and it came nearer and nearer, more and more distinct!

But he could not go on with these wild imaginings either. Someone was rapping the door-ring at the front gate with a queer loud noise. He started, paced out by instinct, and peering through the crack of the door made sure it was not, after all, Han Mengxiang come to press him over the bond debt, nor any other connection from the exchange; and only then did a little colour come back into his face.

The caller was Li Zhuangfei, a middle-aged man with a toothbrush moustache of the very newest cut, another of the fresh acquaintances Feng Yunqing had made in the bond market.

Feng Yunqing ushered in this new arrival with all ceremony, while he scanned closely the look of this fellow sufferer, who had likewise come a cropper in the bonds; and what set him wondering was that at the corners of Li Zhuangfei's mouth too there hovered a jaunty little smile, much as with He Shen'an a while before. Feng Yunqing's heart grew uneasy. With trepidation he wondered whether this Li Zhuangfei, who had once been a "revolutionary" magistrate, might not also have some outlandish scheme to set a man wavering and irresolute and plunge him into misery! Feng Yunqing, now getting on in years, felt that his startled and bewildered spirit could bear no fresh shock added to it.

But what startled Feng Yunqing the more was that Li Zhuangfei, no sooner seated, vented his own grievances:

"I say, old Feng, today I too can't help talking superstition: the year's stars are against me. Ever since New Year's Day this year, whatever I've schemed has gone awry! In March I got a magistracy into my hands, and before I'd been a month in the post the district was up with Communist bandits, and I lost the appointment; in one month, however hard you lay on the heavy hand and scrape the ground, you can't claw your capital back, now can you? Well! At least I'd seen the face of office! And last month, worse still! I laid out a full eighteen thousand dollars for a tax-bureau chief's post—a fat berth, they said; the chits handed down to me from above came to a good ten and more; hah, off I hurried in high spirits to take up the post, and I'd hardly been there two days when, damn it all, the fighting broke out! The enemy commissioned an adjutant of their own. If I hadn't rolled off quick, there might have been more trouble yet! Old Feng, you see how it is—in times like these, what savour is there left in holding office—"

"But you've not given up hope yet! Section-chief, secretary—you keep the whole lot of them about you; that room you take by the month at the hotel is nothing less than a county yamen!"

Feng Yunqing said it with a forced laugh. Forced, because this Li Zhuangfei not only used "revolutionary methods" constantly in his official business as magistrate, but was also adept at using "revolutionary methods" in money dealings among friends; whence his name of "revolutionary magistrate." Feng Yunqing, though he had not yet been favoured with such attentions, had long known the great name, and now, hearing him pour out his troubles, could not but lay by a few measures of wariness.

Li Zhuangfei followed with a laugh too, took a furtive glance round on every side, and then said in a low voice:

"No jesting now—those few, they're all 'hangers-on lending a hand,' and I can't but drag them along. But the outlay's the death of a man, truly. This round in the bonds I've dropped another stake.—Guess: how much am I still short of, before the festival?"

So it was that speech coming, sure enough! Feng Yunqing's heart gave a sudden leap, his face changed colour, and for the moment he could make no answer. Li Zhuangfei seemed to take the point too; his face fell, and his tone turned grave:

"Yunqing, don't mistake me! I know you've failed badly this time. But you'll hardly wash your hands of it for all that? I've hit on a way to win it back, and I've come specially to talk it over with you—only this way needs long capital to be of any avail."

But Feng Yunqing's face grew uglier still; this so-called "way to win it back," far from stirring him, only deepened his degree of flustered unease. He rolled up the whites of his eyes and stayed lost in thought, not half a word to say. Li Zhuangfei gave a cold laugh, eyed Feng Yunqing's face, and after a good while said loudly at last:

"A fine 'smiling tiger' you are, that can't stand the least wave or squall!—But no wonder, either. You're a country squire, used to a snug, safe life of collecting rents and lending money; the game of the modern speculative market, where today you're a few hundred thousand up and tomorrow a pauper again—that, I dare say, you've never so much as dreamed of. Well! Yunqing, let me play the volunteer schoolmaster for once: in bond speculation everything hangs on a one-word secret—splash! Say you go long, and buy one hundred thousand of 'troop-disbandment'; the settlement comes and you're wiped clean; good! You buy in another two hundred thousand—that's the way to roll it up and go on! Look, the government's own way of issuing bonds is just this rolling-up. In the first half of the year they put out two lots of seventy million; in the second half I'll warrant you there'll be four lots of seventy million flung onto the market, for without it the government's finances couldn't be carried on—"

"But what has that to do with our losing money in the bonds? The others are—"

Feng Yunqing could not help asking it back, and with a sigh between the words drew in the latter half of his speech. But Li Zhuangfei had already broken in again:

"There, there, you're at it again! That's just where the reason lies! Since the counters on the market are bound to keep on increasing, the swings of the market grow fiercer by the day; let there be but the least storm in the political situation, and the bond market feels it. And there's our profit, we who play the bonds. Go and ask the old hands at the bonds—which of them doesn't wish the warlords would fight a few more battles? If the political scene were peaceful, then when you lose money today it's a real loss, with no hope of winning it back tomorrow; but now, every single day, there's the hope of a great winning-back."

"I'd never have thought you welcomed their going to war—"

"Not necessarily. When I was a tax-bureau chief, I didn't welcome the fighting; now the tax post's gone and I've turned to the bonds, and naturally my view is different again. But there's another layer to it—we all deal in reorganization and troop-disbandment. The government issues these two loans, in name to find a way of quelling the war, but in fact this present war broke out over just this business. Once the war starts, the bandits of the interior multiply, the Communists' Red Army redoubles its doings, and the country squires all take their money and flee to Shanghai for shelter; the cash gathers in Shanghai, and that just lets the government put out yet more tens of millions in bonds. But where there's money there's fighting, and where there's fighting the interior falls into worse and worse confusion, and the worse the interior, the more the country squires flee with their money to Shanghai, and the more bonds the government can issue again—this is what's called the linked-rings trap of bond-issue and war. Old Feng, you understand now, don't you? Any other line of business runs to ruin when the fighting comes; the bonds alone are the exception. I'll warrant you a thousand years of war, and the bond trade will thrive and flourish a thousand years!"

"Zhuangfei, do you think the interior can never be at peace again?"

Feng Yunqing spat out the gob of thick phlegm he had held in his mouth a good long while, and asked in a flurry.

"Ha! You—old Feng, still dreaming that dream of ease! A year or two more, and you may hand your title-deeds to people as a gift and find none to thank you for them!"

So came the cold reply. Feng Yunqing gazed anxiously at Li Zhuangfei's full, keen-cut face, hoping there was more to come; and when he had made sure there was no more, and that Li Zhuangfei's look was so positive and unequivocal, there came a sudden buzzing cry at Feng Yunqing's ear and his wits went somewhat dim and blurred. A question he had turned over unresolved these several days had, in a manner, got its answer—but too grievous an answer! When with much ado he had steadied himself, he said through clenched teeth:

"Then the government has done us landowners a very great wrong!"

"Not wholly so. The government has, after all, issued bonds beyond all counting, and given you a road to wealth! And a great road to wealth it is, where to sweep in one hundred thousand, or two hundred thousand, at a single stroke is nothing out of the way!"

Whether in earnest or of set purpose, Li Zhuangfei answered, cool to the last degree, with a broad smile. But Feng Yunqing was already grieved almost to tears; and yet the doubtful crossroads that He Shen'an's visit had stirred up in him had found their solution: he, Feng Yunqing, had no choice but to stake his life on the bonds, to stake his all! He lifted his face and said in a quavering voice:

"I'm bankrupt! And you talk to me of making a fortune! But—Zhuangfei, what is this way of yours? You've still not brought it out, after all!"

Li Zhuangfei drew away at his cigarette, blowing the smoke into the air mouthful by mouthful, and gave no answer. He knew the old fox was subdued now and would not slip away again. When a full three minutes or so had passed, Li Zhuangfei suddenly asked:

"Yunqing, those lands of yours can surely still be mortgaged for a few coppers? Get them off your hands while there's time!"

Now it was Feng Yunqing who rolled his eyes and gave no answer, only nodding his head a little.

"Don't mistake me. That was kindly meant, my giving you the counsel.—As for the way to play the bonds, it's a simple word: you and I go partners in a company; when to buy in, when to sell short, you follow my ordering in it all; when we lose, the two of us bear it share and share alike, and when we make money, you give me over and above a three-tenths bonus. But there's one more layer to make clear first: on the margin too it's six-tenths yours, four-tenths mine—and that's my taking advantage of you. I've thirty thousand taels in native-bank drafts on hand at present; to discount them would be too great a loss, and it's come to hard words besides—and they don't fall due till the sixteenth of next month, by the solar calendar—"

"When it comes to ready cash, I'm even worse off than you."

Feng Yunqing hastened to put in; half in truth, and half because Li Zhuangfei's scheme sounded so bizarre to him that he had declared martial law in his heart. But Li Zhuangfei, rich in revolutionary methods, at once broke clean through Yunqing's cordon:

"There, there, you're at it again! No ready cash—but can't you mortgage the land? I'm acquainted with a certain division-commander, a fellow townsman of yours; to egg him on to set up some property in his native place, I flatter myself I've a fair grip on that. Just leave it to me. Even should you need three or five thousand before the festival, you've only to say so, and I'll contrive it for you, no security asked.—Only one layer: the exchange opens the day after tomorrow, and if you mean to act, you must be quick! To sell or to buy—he who strikes first prevails!"

"By your account, how ought it to be done?"

"Good! I'll tell you the whole of it at one go! Everyone knows it was Zhao Botao who ate his fill in this rising wind of the bonds; but within it there's also Wu the Third, Wu Sunfu, who is old Zhao's brains. He has a good friend fighting at the front, so his news comes uncommonly fast. I'm acquainted with a broker, Lu Kuangshi, who is kin to Wu Sunfu; most of Wu the Third's bond dealings pass through his hands. I've made a compact with Lu Kuangshi: he lets the inside word through, and we deal along with Wu Sunfu, and out of our winnings give him a little for his cut. There now—isn't that a good line to follow? Yunqing, hesitation is the mother of failure!"

His say said, Li Zhuangfei rose, stroking his toothbrush moustache with one hand while with the other he took up that Panama hat of his.

Just then there came all at once a sound of scolding and wrangling from upstairs, women on both sides, and Feng Yunqing knew at a hearing that it was his daughter and the concubine. At this his composure was utterly thrown into disorder; without knowing it he too stood up, cupped his hands to Li Zhuangfei, and said:

"I am much obliged, much obliged. My many thanks for everything. Before a true man one tells no lies: before the festival I'm still three or five thousand silver short, and since you, my dear fellow, have said you can help, I'll come to your hotel tomorrow and talk it over face to face!"

Li Zhuangfei agreed to it heartily, fixed the hour of their meeting besides, and went off in high feather. Thereupon Feng Yunqing, his heart full of a fluttering unrest, turned and went stumbling up the stairs, meaning to play the peacemaker as usual. He had just reached the door of his own bedroom when he heard a great splintering crash within, and the concubine's cold laughter, peal upon peal. Feng Yunqing's face went white all over; he stopped short at the door, cocked his head, and scratched his ear. But at once he made up his mind, gently lifted the door-curtain, and slipped in—to see only the concubine, her face all fury, seated on the opium couch, while the little maid Liu Bao knelt on the floor gathering up some broken bowls, and the pale-green white-flowered carpet before the couch was soaked in a great patch, all stained with bird's-nest congee. The hairdressing amah, Jin Ma, stood behind the concubine, toying with the wooden comb in her hand and smiling.

Seeing his daughter was not on the scene, Feng Yunqing felt his heart eased by half. Plainly the daughter, having taken the offensive against the concubine, had withdrawn of her own accord—the so-called "strengthen the walls and clear the fields"—so that the concubine had no one but the little maid Liu Bao on whom to vent her rage.

"Ai, so you've come: afraid you've come to the wrong room, though? You ought to go and look in on your precious young lady first. She's come off the loser!"

The concubine turned her face away, yet slanted her eyes round to watch Feng Yunqing as she said it, all pert and provoking.

Feng Yunqing bent his back with a rueful smile, and, taking his cue from the little maid Liu Bao, spoke up:

"Hah! This gets more outrageous by the minute. A thing you've carried a hundred times, and you can still let it topple over? Off with you and fetch another bowl, quick—what are you squatting here for?"

"Don't you go pointing at Zhang the Third to curse Li the Fourth!"

The concubine said it sharply, and suddenly turned her face round on Feng Yunqing, glaring balefully with her pair of small eyes. Seeing Feng Yunqing offer a limp, fawning smile, the concubine gave another cold laugh and went on:

"Even this chit of a girl grows insolent now. Bringing me a thing scalding hot! Meaning to scald me, is she? I don't suppose she'd dare—isn't it that someone's egging her on behind my back? Give me one clear, plain-tongued answer—"

"There, there; Old Ninth, it's not worth such anger. Take a pipe and calm your liver-fire. I'll cook the pellet for you. Jin Ma, be quick and do the concubine's hair. Tonight at nine there's the special race at Mingyuan. The Bai residence has already telephoned.—Old Ninth, the Fifth Concubine over there begs you to go and play twelve rounds of mahjong first and then on to Mingyuan. Look, the sun's already slanting—shouldn't you make haste? Why put yourself out over a trifle?"

As Feng Yunqing spoke he threw a meaning glance at Jin Ma behind the concubine; then, mustering his spirits, he gave a loud laugh, and only then lay down on the opium couch and took up the iron needle to cook the drug, though in his heart there was a queer, oppressive misery, as of a stone weighing on it.

"True enough. The young lady looks a grown woman, but she's a child after all, with no weight to her tongue. If the concubine has the spirit for it, give her a few words of correction; it's not worth making yourself ill with rage.—Ai, shall it be a horizontal 'S' again?"

Jin Ma too chimed in from the side, jesting and soothing, while with the nimblest of touches she began to do the concubine's hair. The concubine said nothing. Her thoughts had turned to the Fifth Concubine of the Bai residence—one of her sworn sisters. And that she could carry things with a high hand before Feng Yunqing was owing, in great part, to this Fifth Concubine of the House of Bai. When Feng Yunqing had first moved to Shanghai, he had once received a blackmailing threat from kidnappers, and it was the concubine who had found in the Fifth Concubine of the House of Bai the thread to follow, so that a word in the right quarter, carried through to the end, had after all kept everything safe and quiet. From that time on Feng Yunqing had come to know that a country squire like himself, in the snug nest of Shanghai, had nothing like the power of his widely connected concubine! And from that time on he had simply not dared to question her nightlife at all.

By now the little maid Liu Bao had cleared away the broken crockery and the grains of congee from the carpet and brought in afresh a bowl of bird's-nest congee, neither cold nor hot. Her work done, Jin Ma went off to the back wing-room to set the concubine's clothes in order. Feng Yunqing had by this filled a pipe; he laid down the opium-gun, shut his eyes, and thought again of He Shen'an's proposal and Li Zhuangfei's plan. His reckoning ran thus: if Li Zhuangfei's word could be trusted, would that not be better than He Shen'an's "crawling through the dog-hole"? To ply both together, of course, was the surest course of all; but then—"a house that hands down poetry and the rites," how could that be borne! And besides, the concubine, for her own special reason, already held the whip hand in the household; and if now a daughter too, for a "special reason," were to build up a special power, then, between the two great forces, his own footing as the old man of the house would be harder still to keep. Would that every single word of Li Zhuangfei's might prove faithful and sure!

And yet—

Here Feng Yunqing's train of thought was cut short by the concubine's voice. Sipping her bird's-nest congee, tapping the rim of the bowl with her silver spoon, the concubine said:

"The day after tomorrow is the Dragon-Boat Festival; you've got it all seen to, I suppose?"

"Ah—what?"

Feng Yunqing raised his head in a flurry and asked, a thread of spittle running straight down from the corner of his mouth and settling on the breast of his gown.

"What indeed? Pshaw! The festival presents, of course! The brethren yonder have given us the nod—do you suppose they wait on you for nothing!"

"Oh, oh—this—it's on my mind every hour of the day, but, Old Ninth, you know I've lost hideously in the bonds, both hands well-nigh empty now, and I'm still five or six thousand short. I was just about to talk it over with you, to see whether there's any way—"

"Oh—you'd have me go and borrow, is it? Ten thousand, then, or eight thousand? And what's to be put up for security? Those lands down in the country—folk aren't likely to take them, I fancy!"

"That's the very reason I want your counsel. There's a friend named Li who's promised—promised right enough, but I fear he's not to be relied on; there are but two or three days left, and to bungle the thing would be a disaster, wouldn't it?"

The concubine waited till Feng Yunqing had done, and only then took up the bowl of bird's-nest congee and drank it down at a draught; she twisted her neck and gave a soft little laugh, but made no answer. That her husband had lost in the bonds, she knew; but that he was pinched to such a pass, she was a little slow to believe. Would she handle the borrowing herself? To that she had no objection at all. For, since it would pass through her hands, she could hold back a portion for her own several uses over the Dragon-Boat Festival.

She picked up a toothpick and worked at her teeth a while, then said with a smile:

"A sum of a few thousand, without security, can of course be borrowed all the same; I've only to go to the Fifth Elder Sister at the Bai residence—surely she'll not grudge me that much face. Still, to take out a little security to show people is a matter of our face too. Isn't it?—The title-deeds are no use. I seem to remember there's still a passbook for ten thousand silver at the Yuanfeng native bank…"

"Ah—that, that—that mustn't be touched!"

Feng Yunqing sprang up with a start as he spoke, and all but knocked over the opium-lamp in the tray.

The concubine flattened her lips, gave a snort, sprawled crosswise on the opium couch, took up the pipe and drew at it with a whoosh.

"That deposit at the Yuanfeng bank cannot be touched. Ai, Old Ninth, that is Ah Mei's. In the beginning, after her mother's seven-times-seven mourning was done, it was settled by Ah Mei's uncles, on her mother's side and her father's, coming forward to arrange it, that these ten thousand dollars should be drawn and placed in the bank, never to be drawn upon, principal or interest, until the day Ah Mei marries, when the whole of it goes to her for her trousseau money!"

Feng Yunqing spoke with knitted brows, panting, and at the same time his mind went back to how, after his own wife's death, he had brought this Old Ninth into the house, and to the fierce wrangling of Ah Mei's uncles at the time. And from then on his luck had gone from bad to worse year by year, so that it truly bore out what Ah Mei's uncle had said—that "this newcomer with the flat round face has the look of the death-gate," and would not rest till she had brought the house to ruin.—Thinking thus, he could not hold back a sigh, and let his eyes steal round to the concubine. But the concubine's sharp gaze was upon him at that very moment, and the start it gave him was no small one; at once he shifted his eyes, cowed and shrinking, to the sizzling pipe-bowl, and forced a smiling face. He was in dread lest the thoughts of his heart should be seen clean through by the concubine.

Happily the concubine seemed not to have marked it; holding the pipe an inch or so from her lips, she blew two streams of thick smoke from her nostrils, and said, unexpectedly gentle and coquettish:

"Ai, so it's all setting your heart on being a father-in-law; the wedding, the trousseau money, everything got ready and waiting there, only for the daughter and son-in-law to come and kowtow. I've no such good fortune; but you, to think of it, seem to have—pshaw, when will you wake from this dream of yours?"

"Eh?—"

"Ai, do you truly not know, or are you shamming before me?"

The concubine said it with a sudden burst of giggling, plainly delighted rather than angry.

"I simply don't understand—"

"Just so; and I don't understand either why a fine precious young lady should not marry in proper style, nor want her ten thousand and more of silver for a trousseau—"

"Old Ninth!—"

Feng Yunqing cried out in alarm. At last he had caught that the drift was wrong, and that the concubine was full of a malicious relish; but the concubine, with two pipes of opium down her, was in finer spirits still, and her words came so fast that there was simply no room for Feng Yunqing to get a word in.

"What do you call me for? I, Old Ninth, am unlettered and don't understand the new ways. Your daughter's had her schooling, knows foreign writing, a modern miss; and she has her own style: she takes a fancy to a man, and up on her heels and off she slips! That's how a modern daughter shows filial duty to her parents—she marries without costing her mother and father a jot of trouble!"

So saying, the concubine laid down the pipe, and laughed no more; she shook her head again and again, as though she could not abide the sight of it.

"In earnest?"

Feng Yunqing forced out the two words with a struggle, his face wholly changed, the few sparse whiskers trembling again, the whites of his eyes turned yellow, staring fixedly at his Old Ninth, as though in doubt and as though in terror. This thought rapped hard at his nerves: freedom? Does freedom mean one must run away? But the concubine went on with her fearful answer:

"In earnest, indeed! Oh, it's I who spread the tale! Just wait and see for yourself! A low fellow of a student, an outsider, queer and outlandish—maybe he's one of these so-called Communists—I don't suppose you'd consent to have him for a son-in-law; but your not consenting is no use, their new style is just to grease off at the heel!"

Like a prisoner who has had his crime pronounced upon him, Feng Yunqing felt now there was no more dodging it; a sound gurgled in his throat, his eyes strained upward, his fingertips trembled and shook. He shut his eyes, and there before him rose He Shen'an's puffy round face and its queer smile; that smile, seen now, was full of mockery!—"Like enough this dog-head He Shen'an had heard long ago of Ah Mei's foul doings, and came on purpose to make sport of her old father!" Once more, of a sudden, such a thought flashed across Feng Yunqing's nerves, and there was added to his heart a strange new savour. He could not himself quite make out whether he was raging at his daughter's being "unworthy of her line," or grieving that the fine scheme He Shen'an had offered could not now be carried out; at all events, he felt that everything had failed, that the whole board was swept bare.

Just then a soft palm was gently kneading at the pit of his heart, and words softer still, warm and fragrant, were breathed into his ear:

"Tut, tut, it's not worth being so angry! It's I who ought not to have told you!"

Feng Yunqing gave his head a shake, and as he did so caught hold of that soft hand of the concubine's that was stroking his breast; after a while he said, faint and spiritless:

"The misfortune of my house—truly there's no guarding against it, guard as one may!—Who would have thought it. And yet, Ah Mei has never spent a night abroad; every evening she's home by eleven or twelve at the latest, and by day she's at school—she, she—I can't make out when it was she fell into anyone's snare?—"

His words, at the tail-end, changed their tune, and showed a meaning of not being easily convinced. At this the concubine's face changed; she suddenly drew back that hand of hers laid on Feng Yunqing's breast, spat full in Feng Yunqing's face, and cried out in a rage:

"Pah! You dead turtle! What talk is this! Say I do come home every day only at dawn—say I have a lover—then bring out your proof and show me!"

Feng Yunqing stared white-eyed and said nothing. A savour at once sour and burning shot straight up from his chest to the tip of his nose; the skin of his face flushed red, then turned on the instant to an iron grey; he could scarce hold himself in, and was just about to break out, when the concubine's second onslaught was already upon him:

"Carrying on with a lover, of course! The Fifth Concubine of the House of Bai and I are hand in glove. Go and ask for yourself!"

So saying, the concubine gave cold laugh upon cold laugh, tilted over onto her side, and lay on the opium couch cooking her own pellet. "The Fifth Concubine of the House of Bai!"—that phrase poured into Feng Yunqing's ear louder than thunder! It was like a halter slipped over Feng Yunqing's head, and with the concubine's lightest tug it hinted that, even were she carrying on with a lover abroad, she had her backing and nothing to fear. Now Feng Yunqing had no course left but to own himself in the wrong and smile it off.

Happily the concubine was in haste to keep her engagement, and so let it rest there for the time. Feng Yunqing bustled about on every side, and only when the concubine had changed her clothes, got into the motor-car fetched by telephone, and gone off without a backward glance, did he have leisure to turn over again the matter of his daughter. He paced a few steps about the room, his face pale, the corners of his mouth twitching; but by now his mood was no longer a simple resentment at his daughter's having spoiled the "family name," but carried a measure of complaint that she had not made good use of her precious body. This plea went to and fro across the membranes of his brain several times: "Since she has cheapened herself and lost her maidenhead none knows how, then, if I do but follow He Shen'an's scheme, I, as her father, shall have done her no wrong; nor any wrong to her dead mother, nor any wrong to my ancestors!" Little by little a complacent faint smile floated up on his face, but in one instant more he had knit his brows tight again. His careful thinking had suddenly hit on this: what if his daughter, who already had a lover, would not fall in with his fine scheme? Old Zhao was past forty, and though thick-set and sturdy of body, had not a particle of a handsome air about him!

A crow on the tiles outside the window cawed a few times. Feng Yunqing sprang up violently and said to himself through clenched teeth:

"If she truly won't consent, then she's an unfilial daughter indeed, an unfilial daughter!"

In a flurry he turned several circuits about the room, and looked at the electric clock, which pointed to just ten past six. A day, then, was gone by! He felt he could dally with the time no longer; he gave a few purposeful coughs and made up his mind to go and parley with his daughter.

Feng Meiqing was in her own room writing a letter, meaning to tell her friend why she could not keep their engagement and go out for a good, thorough spree. She was ashamed to say it was because her father would not give her money, yet a fitting pretext she could not think of; she wrote first in Chinese, and had got half through when, looking it over, she found it did not read very smoothly, and tore it up to write in English instead. But the vexing thing was that the very phrases she wanted now the teacher had never taught, nor were they to be found in her English reader; she wrote half a line and ran aground, propped her head on her left hand and racked her brains a while, then shifted to prop it on her right, the Parker fountain-pen held between her soft white middle and index fingers. Her two cheeks were flushed a sudden rosy red, her eyes swimming and moist, yet with a touch of weariness about them. At the last she gave over racking her brains, and drew mechanically upon the notepaper no end of little circles. Just then the turn-lock on the door of the room clicked once, and her father came in.

Not expecting it to be her father, Feng Meiqing gave a low cry of "Aiyo!" and threw head and arms alike down over the writing-desk, covering the notepaper she had scrawled out of all shape, and giggling. Feng Yunqing said nothing either, but flashed his narrow eyes about the room in a searching glance. There was nothing to draw particular notice. Music-books, handkerchiefs, a scent-bottle, a little powder-puff, a rouge-tube, lay scattered here and there about the room. At last he stood before Meiqing, turning over how to begin his first sentence.

Meiqing too raised her head, no longer laughing, her swimming eyes gazing at her father's face. Since that gaze seemed to carry a reproach in it, Feng Yunqing found it awkward to meet full on, and turned his head a little to the right—only to face, on Meiqing's half-turned upper body, the breasts that stood out so markedly there. A queer notion spread out then over Feng Yunqing's consciousness; it was as though he had verified on the spot that this daughter was already a woman grown, and at the same time he felt that this "giving without asking leave," this free act of his daughter's, did great injury to his paternal authority; his anger rose up, and so his opening sentence came out, unexpectedly, harsh:

"Ah Mei! You—you're no longer so young!—"

Here his daughter gave a laugh, all girlish and artless, which made it awkward again for Feng Yunqing to keep a stern face; he paused a moment, and his tone turned mild:

"You're seventeen this year, Ah Mei! Shanghai society is full of bad people; in keeping company one must always take care, and not let others deceive you—"

"Deceive me? Ai—and who has ever deceived me, pray?"

Meiqing stood up and asked it back, her long brows knitting just a little, though the rosy red on her cheeks faded a shade too. Feng Yunqing forced a smile, gave a little more ground in his tone, and at once amended the matter of his speech besides:

"Uh—deceive you of your money! Just think: how much do you spend in a month? Isn't it a hundred and fifty or sixty? You could never in the world spend so much by yourself! There must be someone helping you spend it there, mustn't there?—"

"Is Papa going to audit my accounts? Very well! I'll recite them for you."

"No need to recite them. Ai, I've a few serious words to say to you. This time on the exchange I've lost heavily, and there must be someone who's gained; Ah Mei, do you know who it is that's raked in a great haul?—It's a man named Zhao, who keeps a room by the month at Such-and-Such Hotel, and every afternoon goes to take a turn about Such-and-Such Roof-Garden, a man of forty or so, a great, imposing figure of a fellow. The gems and diamonds he's got by him! Only look at his two hands—"

Feng Yunqing suddenly stopped short, and though he brought out one "ai" after another, he could drag no more of it forth; his bewildered gaze only circled about his daughter's face. This was the crucial pass. For the moment he could not decide whether it were better to lay it all out plain and open, or to go round about and first stir his daughter's heart; and stranger still, beyond these two notions there lurked a third, which he could not himself quite make out, but which plainly stirred within him: he would gladly have had the heavens crack and the earth split just then, and destroy himself, his daughter, old Zhao, the bond market, and everything. He looked at his daughter's pair of bright, glittering eyes that seemed to smile, and at her bosom that seemed faintly to stir, and there rose at once before him the most unseemly of scenes. And hard upon it came another, no less unseemly, with himself in the leading part. It was as though someone spoke at his ear: "That other was no wedded wife of yours—let her play the wanton as she will; but this one is your own flesh and blood!" He could not help a cold shudder, his heart pounding, and all but shed tears. All this was the business of an instant—too swift to allow Feng Yunqing any weighing or deciding. And in that very instant Feng Meiqing gave a coquettish smile, gave her waist a wriggle, and blurted out:

"Oh—Papa, it's Zhao Botao you mean!"

"Ha—you!"

Feng Yunqing cried out in astonishment, and all his jumbled fancies at once scattered and fled, leaving only one emotion: wonder, and a secret joy. A question, like an arrow on the string, shot straight into Meiqing's face, and the voice had a tremor in it too:

"You know him? How did you come to know him?"

"A friend of mine—a girlfriend—knows this Zhao."

"Ai—the man named Zhao, Zhao Botao? The very Zhao Botao who's King of the Bonds, the famous great bull operator? The imposing, great tall fellow?—"

"That's the one. There's no mistaking it!"

Meiqing answered in a wayward tone, as though out of patience, dabbed twice at her lips with her handkerchief, and gave a soft tittering laugh. She did not understand why her father was so flustered and startled, yet she could plainly see that on hearing it was a girlfriend who knew that Zhao Botao he wore a look of some disappointment. Her father's questioning, however, was not yet at an end:

"Oh, your girlfriend? Ah Mei, is your girlfriend older than you, or younger?"

"Older by three or four years, I think."

"Then she'd be twenty-one or twenty-two. Where does she come from? Is she married or not?"

"Ai—she has been married. Her husband died last year."

"Then she's a widow. Strange! Wait, Ah Mei—what sort of person is she? Has she ever been to our house?"

"Papa!—what use is it, your asking all this?"

"Uh, it's of use to me; Ah Mei, it's of use to me. Tell it me plainly, and by and by I'll tell you what use it is. Quick, say: has she been here or not!"

But Meiqing did not answer at once; she sat down, watching her father with a smile, her little finger twining at her handkerchief, and then all at once, with a giggling, alluring laugh, she said:

"Been here she has not, but, Papa, you must certainly have seen her—perhaps you even know her!"

"Oh—"

"She often goes to the exchange. She's a little taller than I am, with a small round face, and a few fine white pockmarks beside the bridge of her nose that you'd not notice unless you looked. Her lips are done most beautifully. Her bosom is very high and her waist slender, and she walks like a Western woman. Papa, has it come back to you? She's often at the exchange—her name is Liu Yuying, and her father-in-law is that broker at the exchange, Lu Kuangshi—"

"Oh, oh, Lu Kuangshi! The Lu Kuangshi old Li was going on about today!"

Feng Yunqing suddenly cried out, looking much excited. He nodded his head without ceasing, as though he had luckily got clear a knotty question. After a while he turned his face and looked closely at his daughter, seeming to weigh the Liu Yuying of his imagining against his own daughter before him, the fair against the foul. At the last he let out a breath and asked, with trepidation:

"But is she anything of kin to Zhao Botao? Ai, I mean that girlfriend of yours, the one named Liu."

Feng Meiqing did not answer, but only gave a queer laugh, twisted her body aside and shook her loose long-haired head a shake or two, her eyes on the floor. Then suddenly she gave a snorting laugh again, raised her eyes to her father, and said:

"Kin or no kin, what does it matter! Anyhow it's—ai, Papa, how minutely you go into it all!"

Feng Yunqing laughed too; he had understood the whole of it now, and as he looked he took it that his daughter too was well used to it all; and so he felt that everything was nothing else than the will of Heaven, and that he had no choice but to act in accordance with Heaven. This notion having gained the mastery, he weighed his words a little and said to his daughter, straight out:

"Ah Mei, there's reason in my asking so closely. That Zhao Botao, when he plays the bonds, it's as though he had a demon to help him—every time he comes off winner. This time he's raked in a good million and more! This time our side lost at the front, and the bears were all looking for a fall, when who could have known it would suddenly turn about and the bulls have the advantage after all. Ah Mei, in one day's time your papa has turned into a pauper!—But you needn't fret, it can still be won back. Only there's one layer: I'm in the dark and the others in the light, and to go on like this will never, never do. There's but one way—to spy out Zhao Botao's secret! This man Zhao, sharp as he is, is wholly single-minded where women are concerned, and a woman's little finger can dig the very thoughts out of his belly! Ah Mei, you—your girlfriend is close with old Zhao, isn't she? This is Heaven's own granting of the chance, to let me win it back. I put the heavy burden into your hands now. You're clever, and you're pretty—ai, you understand of yourself, there's no need for me to say more."

Feng Yunqing let out a heavy breath, split his mouth open, and gave his daughter a forced, dry laugh. But suddenly there rose in his heart a contradiction he could scarce credit: on the one hand he dreaded lest his daughter should shake her head, and on the other he feared to see her nod her consent. But Meiqing's look was quite natural; with a faint smile, and no ado at all, she gave a nod. She had somewhat misunderstood her father's meaning, taking it that her father wished to make use of Liu Yuying to spy out old Zhao's secret.

Seeing his daughter had nodded, Feng Yunqing's heart gave a leap, but after that leap his whole body was strangely eased. He gave a faint sigh. The great matter was settled! Now there was no undoing it: a situation that could not be otherwise had at last set him on a road that could not be otherwise.

"And if, by some chance, Liu Yuying is unwilling?"

Suddenly Meiqing put this doubt. She said it in a low voice, and on her face there flew up a flush again; her eyes were cast down, she twisted her waist, and her two hands twined ceaselessly at her little handkerchief. Feng Yunqing, not guarding against such a question, was for the moment struck dumb. Now it was he who mistook his daughter's meaning. And out of that mistake he could not help thinking: a young girl after all, with no experience. Just then Meiqing too raised her head and looked at her father, her eyelids between a smile and no smile, as though she must have of her father a clear and plain answer. Feng Yunqing, having no help for it, could only say with a brazen face:

"Silly child! Must you ask even this! You must set your own sail by the wind! Besides, she's your good friend; surely you know how jealous she is? If it seems better not to keep it from her, then don't; otherwise, when you make your move, it's safer to keep clear of her eyes—"

"Oh-oh!"

Meiqing gave a low cry, leaned back against her chair, buried her face in both hands, and giggled without cease. At this Feng Yunqing withdrew and went off; he was in dread lest his daughter should ask another like question, for in any case there was something unseemly in a father's answering such things.

He had just got down to the wing-room and was not yet well seated when his daughter came too, carrying a snakeskin vanity case, with the air of one about to go straight out.

"Papa, the money? One can't go out to see a friend without taking money."

Meiqing said it standing at the door of the wing-room, tapping the threshold again and again with the heel of her high-heeled shoe, as though out of patience.

After a moment's hesitation Feng Yunqing gave her a hundred dollars. He felt there were still a few words of caution to add, but though he saw his daughter all the way to the front gate and watched her spring lightly into the rickshaw, in the end he did not bring them out. He stood blankly at the gate a good while, half in triumph and half in wretchedness. And just as he turned to go back in, his eye fell of a sudden on the whitewashed wall beside the gate, where someone had drawn in charcoal a most wretched turtle, and hard by this "national curse," written large in glossy black ink, were two slogans: "Join the May Thirtieth demonstration!" "Uphold the Soviets!" Feng Yunqing's breath jammed all at once in his gullet; on the instant his face changed, his fingertips went ice-cold and trembling again. With an effort he walked back into the wing-room and lay down on the couch, boundless rancour heaping up in his heart: he hated those peasants and Communists to the marrow! It was all, he felt, because of these people's turmoil that he had been driven to take shelter in Shanghai, driven to let the concubine run wild in her wanton nightlife; and because he was in Shanghai, that he had been driven to speculate in the bonds, driven to set his own daughter on to work the beauty-trap. All of this, to his mind, was strictly logical, and the one and only cause was that the peasants had risen in revolt and men's hearts were no longer what they were of old. He heaved a miserable sigh and said in his heart:

—So now, wife and daughter alike, I've handed them all out for others to hold in common! And the one who puts wife-sharing into practice is, of all places, here in Shanghai, and is, of all people, myself—where in the world is one to begin telling it? Where to begin!
