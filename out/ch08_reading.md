## Chapter Eight. A Nanjing Night, Deadly Urgent

### 1. Xu Enzeng Undone by Qian Zhuangfei

In August 1945 the Chinese people came at last to the final victory of the War of Resistance against Japan. Qian Jiang and his party traveled down from Yan'an to Chongqing and were reunited with his mother, Zhang Zhenhua. Zhou Enlai happened to be in Chongqing too, and he gave a dinner in honor of Zhang Zhenhua, Qian Jiang, and the others. Over the table the talk turned to the death of Qian Zhuangfei, killed on the Long March, on the bank of the Wu, where the waves beat against the shore.

Years later Qian Zhuangfei's figure surfaced again, in an essay by Xu Enzeng, "Recollections of My Battle with the Communist Party" (1992):

{v} As soon as Gu Shunzhang had signified his willingness to come over, he told me a piece of startling news. He said that one of my ablest assistants, the man who kept at my side and had charge of my confidential papers, was in fact a spy planted by the Communist Party. This astonished me greatly. The man had been taken on by open examination in the seventeenth year of the Republic, when I was in charge of setting up China's commercial wireless news service. In three years of watching him I had come to believe he was a capable young fellow, unafraid of hard work and faithful to his post, who kept his head down over his tasks, took no interest in outside matters, spoke little, and did everything quickly and well. That such a model clerk, a man so perfectly correct, should turn out to be a spy sent by the Communist Party—I simply could not believe my own ears. The telegram reporting Gu Shunzhang's arrest was deciphered by his own hand and handed to me in person. Even then I clung to a one-in-ten-thousand hope that the news was not true. But when I sent men to find him, sure enough, on the morning of the day before, he had quietly slipped away.

Xu Enzeng, courtesy name Kejun, was born in 1896, a native of Huzhou in Zhejiang.

From middle school to university, Xu Enzeng had been a classmate of Zou Taofen's the whole way.

Zou Taofen described Xu Enzeng as "a refined, gentle-mannered pale-faced scholar to look at; whether in Western dress or Chinese, always trim and neat, and at school his room was the most tidy and tasteful of all…" And yet it was precisely this genteel star pupil of Nanyang College who, to settle whether his "old schoolmate and old friend" really was a Communist, went so far as to set his men to shadow Zou Taofen "for seven years running."

At Nanyang College Xu Enzeng had read electrical engineering, and Zou Taofen remembered that "after graduating he went to study in America, and read electrical engineering there too; by rights he should have been an electrical engineer, but back home he burrowed into the secret-service line instead. He was kin to the CC, cousins by marriage, and that was perhaps what turned him to the new trade." In his own memoir Xu Enzeng says the same: that at the Carnegie Institute of Technology in America he had read business management, taking "the development of production and the building of transport" for his "lifelong aim of service," but that before coming home "a change occurred," and "on the urging of a schoolmate who had already joined the Kuomintang" he decided to join the Kuomintang and give himself to "reforming politics." In 1925 Xu Enzeng came home; in 1928 he was ordered to build the Kuomintang's first broadcasting station, and, under the National Government's Reconstruction Commission, took charge of setting up China's first commercial wireless station in Shanghai, and after that of founding an international commercial wireless station. Early in 1930 he returned to Nanjing, where his fellow Huzhou man Chen Lifu—then secretary-general of the Kuomintang Central—took a fancy to him and asked him to head, as its public face, the Party Affairs Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department. When he "grasped the real content of the task—that it lay in countering the Communist Party's underground work, and that this was a new line of business no one before had ever tried," he "at once felt the itch to try it" and agreed "on the spot." That one "yes" gave him the "Zhongtong" career that would "consume fourteen years of his life."

In his later years Chen Lifu recalled: "After the party purge in the sixteenth year, the Generalissimo wanted me, under the Organization Department of which he was himself the minister, to organize and set up an Investigation Section, whose chief task was to counter the activity of the Communist Party and to punish it." (Chen Lifu, 1994) On another occasion he added: "During the bandit-suppression in Jiangxi, our side's most important work was to steady the rear, so that the Communists' secret activity in the various provinces should be snuffed out as soon as it flared up. I was the man in charge of this work on our side, and the senior cadres who helped me were for the most part returned students from America, and most of them men who had studied the natural and applied sciences." (Chen Lifu, 1977)

It has been said that from the very first day the Kuomintang's secret-service work came into being, its purpose was to "counter the ever more rampant underground activity of the Communists" and to "root out the evil forces of Communism." (Xu Wenlong, 1996)

But I hold stubbornly to another conviction: "When the Investigation Section was first set up, it did not take the Communist Party for its main object of struggle; but after 1930, as the Party's activity across the country grew ever more frequent, the two Chens felt the need to strengthen the force ranged against it, and decided to add two groups within the Section. One was the 'Special Service Group'; apart from ordinary secret-service work, which stayed with the reconnaissance sub-section, all investigation and study of the Communist Party, all plotting and scheming, and everything held to be most secret—intelligence-gathering, direction of sabotage—was made this group's charge. The other was the 'Word-and-Letter Group,' whose main task was to collect the newspapers of the various provinces and cities, all manner of progressive periodicals, and Chinese-language publications from abroad. From politics, economics, military affairs, culture and education, and the rest, it clipped and sorted them by category, and sent them up each day to the section chief, and on to the minister for his perusal." (Yang Yingqi and Zhang Wandong, 1999) Zhang Guodong's account is borne out by Meng Zhen. Meng Zhen too was a Zhongtong old hand, having served as chief of the Zhongtong's Fourth Section, as its secretary, and as chief private secretary of the Internal Investigation Bureau. In *The Zhongtong and I* (1987) Meng Zhen says: "In February 1928 the Organization Department of the Central Executive Committee of the Chinese Kuomintang added an 'Investigation Section,' and this was the forerunner of the 'Zhongtong.' In those days the Kuomintang bristled with factions. The largest was the Reorganizationists, whose leader was Wang Jingwei, with Chen Gongbo, Gu Mengyu, Gan Naiguang and others as its chief members; its reach ran the length of the country. Because Wang's standing and seniority within the Kuomintang were higher than Chiang Kai-shek's, this faction's activity was a great threat to Chiang. There were many local factions besides—in Shandong the 'Grand Alliance' organized by Ding Weifen, in Hebei the 'Practice Society' led by Zhang Qingyuan and Jiao Shizhai—and these little organizations owed no allegiance to Chiang Kai-shek as the rightful line, so Chiang wanted an apparatus to keep a constant eye on their doings."

In fact, early in 1928, when Chen Lifu took up his post, he stated plainly at the Investigation Section's first full staff meeting that the Section's first task was to investigate the thinking and factional loyalties of Party members and to collect intelligence on the Kuomintang's own dissident cliques. And in an interview, speaking of "the origin and course of China's investigation-and-statistics apparatus," he set "the Kuomintang riven within by factions, at deep odds with itself," "Feng Yuxiang, Yan Xishan, Li Zongren and the rest, presuming on their merit and their strength, keeping their own armies and standing on their own ground, outwardly obedient to the Center but in fact each a law unto himself," "petty warlords everywhere, each on his own hilltop, still carving out his own domain," and "the Kuomintang's one-party rule, governing the country through the Party, proclaiming that outside the Party there was no party, so that the various parties and factions that had long carried on open activity were now proscribed and one after another went underground; while the remnant warlord scum, struck down during the Northern Expedition, unwilling to lie quiet, still schemed to blow the dead ashes back into flame"—all of these he ranked ahead of "the Nanjing party purge, the Wuhan split with the Communists, the Kuomintang and the Communists breaking openly and facing each other as enemies. The Communists, with the fostering support of Soviet Russia, seizing every gap and flaw, active everywhere, expanding hugely, would grow into a great calamity."

From 1928 to 1930, to break one by one the self-willed Li Zongren, Feng Yuxiang, Chen Mingshu, Yan Xishan, Han Fuju and Liu Xiang, Chen Lifu, Zhang Daofan, Wu Dajun, Ye Xiufeng and their like spared no expense, courting and buying up the greed-blinded politicians and staff men on a grand scale, and using the intelligence these men sold on the sly to feed Chiang Kai-shek's maneuvering and his sowing of discord with inside information.

From 1928 to 1930, to defeat one by one the untamable political enemies within the Party, and to smooth the road to "one doctrine (a fascism draped in the robes of the Three People's Principles), one leader (Chiang Kai-shek), one organization (the Chiang-brand Kuomintang)," Chen Lifu, Zhang Daofan, Wu Dajun, Ye Xiufeng and their like were forever spinning slander, fabricating charges, and spreading rumors; or else planting moles, keeping spies, hiring gunmen, and doing their utmost to defame, to smear, and even to destroy in the flesh Deng Yanda, Wang Jingwei, Hu Hanmin, Ding Weifen and the rest.

And of course, as the new warlord forces at home fell to pieces and the Kuomintang's dissident cliques were routed, and as the age of Xu Enzeng came on the faster, the Investigation Section—modern China's earliest, most complete, most vicious secret-service apparatus—took more and more for the "real content" of its "investigation-and-statistics" task the "countering of the Communist Party's underground activity."

Xu Enzeng set himself up as an "anti-Communist warrior," claiming to have "consumed fourteen years of his life" in "battle" with the Communist Party, not without "successes," not without "glory," not without "wonderfully subtle and ingenious combat experience"—yet he could not help admitting, downcast and in "bitter pain," that he had also had his "defeats" on the record. One of these was that, for "looking only at the surface" and finding "the change sprung from within his own armpit," he had been undone by Qian Zhuangfei.

### 2. The Iron Triangle

The first volume of the *Draft Chronicle* records that on April 18, 1927, "today the National Government fixed its capital at Nanjing, and the Duke attended the ceremony inaugurating the National Government and the citizens' mass rally in celebration."

By "the Duke" is meant Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang Kai-shek attended the inauguration of the Nanjing government at the former seat of the Jiangsu Provincial Provisional Assembly, that is, at No. 16 Dingjiaqiao. It was there, in December 1911, that Sun Yat-sen had been elected provisional president of the Republic of China by the delegates of seventeen provinces gathered on the spot.

No sooner was the Nanjing government founded than No. 16 Dingjiaqiao became the office of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters as well. In its early days the Party Affairs Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department numbered only seventeen or eighteen people, and they took up no more than a few rooms in the southwest corner of the second floor of the Central Party Headquarters building. But once Xu Enzeng moved in, he took separate quarters in a small building of mixed Chinese and Western style. It stood on the north side of the middle stretch of Zhongshan East Road, hard by the sumptuous Central Hotel. For all that it was plainly a secret-service organ, this little building took pains to throw people off the scent, hanging out over its front door the signboard of the "Zhengyuan Industrial Company."

The "Zhengyuan Industrial Company" was under Xu Enzeng's personal control, and Zhang Guodong went there only a handful of times. In those days Zhang Guodong was a raw beginner, a lowly assistant clerk of no account. Fresh out of the Central Party Affairs School, he had squeezed by luck into the ranks of the "most loyal and reliable" and so been posted to the Investigation Section as a trainee. Though he had crossed paths with Wang Sicheng, Li Xiyuan, Qian Zhuangfei and the other trusted men of Xu Enzeng's, he did not know them in the least.

Wang Sicheng became one of Xu Enzeng's trusted men because he was resourceful and full of schemes, seasoned and steady, acknowledged throughout the Section as its "party-faction expert"; the various "anti-Communist" documents were for the most part given their final revision by him, or came from his own pen.

Li Xiyuan became one of Xu Enzeng's trusted men because his record resembled Wang Sicheng's—he too had been through political-police training—and because he was uncommonly quick and sharp.

Qian Zhuangfei became one of Xu Enzeng's trusted men because he was both Xu's fellow townsman and his exact contemporary. He had been Xu's subordinate as far back as the time when Xu Enzeng, "under the National Government's Reconstruction Commission, took charge of setting up China's first commercial wireless station in Shanghai, and after that of founding an international commercial wireless station."

Qian Zhuangfei—milk-name Binsheng, original name Wangda, and also called Zhuangqiu, Nanfei, Xixi, and Qian Chao—was born in 1895, a native of Huzhou in Zhejiang. In 1908 he entered the Huzhou Prefectural Middle School (some say the Zhejiang Provincial Third Middle School). In 1914 he married a local girl surnamed Xu (given name Shuangying, whose father kept the Yongfeng cloth shop at Majin Lane), and they had two daughters one after the other, Qian Yeli and Qian Jiao. In 1915, after finishing middle school, Qian Zhuangfei passed into the Peking Medical Specialist School with the help of his kinsman Qian Xuantong, and there fell in love with Zhang Zhenhua, two years his senior. (Qian Xuantong was a champion and tireless practitioner of the "national-language movement" and a warm backer of Hu Shi and the others in their attempts at new poetry; it was at his urging that Lu Xun wrote his first story, *A Madman's Diary*, and published it in *New Youth*.) The two of them were fellow students there, the one reading surgery, the other obstetrics. The one was a descendant of the Wuyue kings Qian Liu and Qian Chu; the other was born of an eminent old family of Tongcheng in Anhui. Though Qian Zhuangfei's mother did not much care for Zhang Zhenhua, nor for her unbound natural feet, the two, being of one mind and one road, came together without a backward glance.

In 1919 Qian Zhuangfei and Zhang Zhenhua graduated from the medical school, and both hung out their shingles to heal the sick and save lives. Zhang Zhenhua was a doctor at the Tiantan Infectious Diseases Hospital and the Kemei Hospital; Qian Zhuangfei put up his own plate to practice on Changxing Street (the *Huzhou Gazetteer* says Chang'an Street), also took a second post at the hospital attached to the Peking–Suiyuan Railway, and taught at an art school, lecturing on human anatomy.

In this same period Qian Zhuangfei also acted, along with Hu Di, for the Guanghua Film Company.

Hu Di—original name Baichang, also called Beifeng, with the aliases Hu Ma, Qiutian, Yiyu and others—was born in 1905, a native of Shucheng in Anhui. In 1921 he skipped a grade to pass into the Hefei Provincial Second Middle School, and in 1923 skipped a grade again to pass into Peking's China University (originally called the Republic University, founded by Sun Yat-sen, established in 1913, renamed in 1917, and moved in 1925 from within Qianmen to the mansion of Prince Zheng at Damucang by the Xidan pailou). In 1924, introduced by his schoolmate Wu Luming, he met Zhang Zhenhua and her younger brother Zhang Xianzhong (an early member of the CCP) at the Anhui Guild Hall. Through the Zhang brother and sister, Hu Di and Qian Zhuangfei became fast friends, laughing and talking, sparring and horsing about together before the camera, and giving full play to their gift for disguise.

As everyone knows, espionage in human history can be traced all the way back to the pen of the blind minstrel Homer.

The story goes that the Trojans, overlords of the tribes of the East, and the mighty Greek tribes known collectively as the Achaeans, once fought a bitter ten-year war on the western shore of the Aegean. The Achaeans first assaulted the walls, then took the city by cunning: on a beachhead they gave up of their own accord they left a great wooden horse, and let the Trojans drag it into the city as a trophy to show off. As it turned out, the Achaeans hidden in the horse's belly, together with the fleet that came surging back over the sea, at last stormed the Trojans' royal city of Ilion in a single stroke.

The war for Ilion made "the Trojan horse" a household term, used to denote just such a feat of espionage—the enemy within joining hands with the force without, winning by surprise. And that feat of espionage—the enemy within meeting the force without, winning by surprise—is parsed in *Sun Tzu's Art of War* into the "local spy," the "inside spy," the "double spy," the "doomed spy," and the "surviving spy": making full use of the enemy's near neighbors and distant kin; making full use of the enemy's own officials; turning the enemy's agents nimbly against him; deliberately concocting and spreading false intelligence and passing it to the enemy through one's own spy planted in his camp, luring him into the trap; and striving to come back alive to report on the enemy. In short, going under, lying low, walking the road of the undercover man—under a particular kind of pressure, hiding one's true desires, profiting by disguise, including the theft of secrets and the filching of intelligence.

The truth is that Qian Zhuangfei and Hu Di alike had a gift for disguise long before. Before ever beginning their careers as agents, they had already tasted the art of changing face and passing the false for the true. According to Qian Zhuangfei's daughter, the late film star Li Lili, she and her father lived at the time at No. 13 Ping'anli, in a three-story little Western house. Near Ping'anli was a big red gate, and inside it a Guanghua Film Company and an open-air studio. Her father, and Hu Di, who had worked at Dan Duyu's film company, shot a martial-arts picture in that open-air studio, *The Knight-Errant of Yanshan*.

Cheng Jihua, chief editor of *A History of the Development of Chinese Cinema* (1963), once observed that in the mid-1920s the Chinese film market was in great disorder and martial-arts pictures a flood run wild, because "once the film speculators found that martial-arts pictures suited the taste of the broad mass of small townsfolk and were welcome to the Southeast Asian distributors as well, films of the martial-arts-and-sorcery kind supplanted the costume dramas that had had their day and grew wildly popular." Such martial-arts films, he said, "were sometimes shot on positive stock in place of negative; sometimes a south-facing room was taken at an inn, the actors brought in and made up, the light set, and shooting begun, with no scenery to build and no props to rent, the whole costing no more than three to five yuan; a single crank ran off two thousand feet; hire a few extras, shoot a little more on location, and in ten days or half a month a picture was done. Some even hired a few models to display a little of the beauty of the curve, threw in a spring ditty or a song-and-patter turn, plastered up big advertisements, and puffed the thing for all they were worth—every wonder and freak under heaven, nothing left out."

*The Knight-Errant of Yanshan* took a path of its own and did the opposite. Qian Zhuangfei brought the whole family into the cast. His wife, Zhang Zhenhua, played the mother, and Li Lili played the little sister. In one scene the father is driving the son out of the house; the mother will not have it for the life of her, clinging to him and weeping so she cannot speak. The little sister is the same—she is to grab hold of her big brother, not let him go, and cry as if her heart would break. But Li Lili could not cry; she laughed instead. It struck her as too funny that the one she was clutching was her papa while her mouth was to be calling him elder brother, and so the scene went round and round, over and over, and was shot many times without once passing muster. Zhang Zhenhua lost patience, lost her temper, and slapped Li Lili on the spot. That one slap, and Li Lili really did cry, and the scene was in the can. Everyone praised Li Lili again, so promising at such a tender age.

Of her father, Li Lili also remembered that he was "silent and sparing of speech, and looked very stern; apart from talking with friends who came and went, he seldom spoke to the children," but that he "loved to write, to paint, to carve seals and to decorate colored eggs. He had a wide-ranging interest in art, gave it his whole concentration, and had a creative streak. What stays with me most is that in writing couplets he used no ordinary brush, but gripped a wad of medicated cotton in a surgical clamp, dipped it in ink, and wrote with that; the strokes came out unlike those of a writing brush, and everyone liked them." (Li Lili, 2001)

In 1925 Zhang Xianzhong first brought his elder sister into the Party. In 1926 Zhang Xianzhong in turn brought in his brothers-in-law Qian Zhuangfei and Hu Di.

In the spring of 1928 Qian Zhuangfei went south with Hu Di, came to Shanghai, and looked for work and a living through the "situations vacant" printed in the papers. At the same time, on and off, they went to screen tests and stage tryouts, playing extras of every sort all day long, trailing after the big-name stars, taking whatever part they were given—until, in the studio of one film company, they fell in with a man who likewise loved to act, understood acting, and could play anything to the life: Li Kenong.

Li Kenong—styled Jiaxuan, and also Manzi, Xiagong, Tianchi, Zhenzhong, Zetian, Zhonghe—was born in 1899, a native of Chaoxian in Anhui, and had a middle-school education. In 1919 he edited the literary supplement of the Anqing *Guomin Daily*; in 1921 he was chief of the Second Section of the Lu'an County government; in 1926 he joined the Chinese Communist Party; in 1928 he came to Shanghai and, with Qian Xingcun (A Ying) and others, kept up his Party organizational life in the CCP Chunye Bookshop branch on North Sichuan Road (today's Sichuan North Road). That autumn he and Pan Hannian jointly founded a little paper, *The Armored Car*, and he went on to serve as propaganda commissioner of the CCP Central-Shanghai District Committee.

Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di took to one another at first sight, sorry only that they had not met sooner. This is set down exactly in Li Kenong's autobiography and in his talks during his lifetime. Li Kenong says: "In 1929 I met Qian Zhuangfei and then Hu Di. They were both members of the 'Nanshe' in Beiping, and had come to Shanghai to hide from the enemy's pursuit; at the time none of us had work, and seeing in the papers a notice that a wireless training class was recruiting, we reported to the organization to ask whether we might sit the examination. The Jiangsu Provincial Committee, the Special Branch, and Gu Shunzhang all judged this a good chance to plant ourselves inside the enemy and draw out intelligence, and told us to sit it. Qian went first; he came first in the writing test and was admitted with fine marks."

There is a further background to this. On December 31, 1927—the very last day of that year, when the Second Section (Intelligence Section) of the Central Special Branch had not yet formally come into being—the CCP Central Committee stated plainly in Central Circular No. 25, *On Work Toward the Kuomintang*: "By resolution of the Party organs, one or two utterly faithful comrades may be dispatched into the Kuomintang party organs and into certain reactionary institutions to do the work of detection and sabotage. But such dispatch must be confined to these two purposes alone." On May 25, 1928, the CCP Central Committee stressed again, in Central Circular No. 51, *Outline of Military Work*, that while "the various secret-service tasks bearing on military matters must be planned and carried forward by the Military Commission with constant attention," it especially required that "detection-and-investigation work too should be carried forward systematically and by plan; comrades should be sought out and sent into the reactionary military and civil organs to do work of every kind, so as to obtain secret documents and materials of every kind."

As a part of that "systematic and planned" "detection-and-investigation work," Qian Zhuangfei was the first to enter the Shanghai wireless training class.

Not long after Qian Zhuangfei had passed into the Shanghai wireless training class, he drew the notice of Xu Enzeng, then chief of the wireless administration office under the Reconstruction Commission. This very training class was Xu Enzeng's own venture. Xu Enzeng greatly admired Qian Zhuangfei's talents. And so, when Xu Enzeng was promoted to director of the Shanghai Wireless Administration Bureau, he took Qian Zhuangfei along and appointed him secretary of the Bureau.

In April 1929 Qian Zhuangfei went with Xu Enzeng to Hangzhou to run the West Lake Exposition.

Qian Zhuangfei's grandson Qian Hong says: "Grandfather was the designer and manager of the famous West Lake Exposition, which made a great stir at the time; it was a large exposition China had never held before, and of very high artistry too. The exposition drew Chen Lifu's attention, and it was through it that Grandfather won Xu Enzeng's trust."

From then on Xu Enzeng kept Qian Zhuangfei at his side. Qian Zhuangfei seized the chance to bring Li Kenong into the Shanghai Wireless Administration Bureau as an editor of broadcast news. Li Kenong's son Li Li says: "In those days examinations were held by open competition—you had to write an essay, and your handwriting had to be good, so my father practiced his brushwork. And for the essay you were tested on the Three People's Principles, on general science, and on classical prose too. My father shut himself in to prepare, bought books, and memorized some passages. As it happened he did well, and was admitted."

Speaking of his father, and of those nameless heroes on our Party's hidden front, Li Li's deepest sigh is this: "China's revolution runs long and deep, and the circumstances are very tangled. What I can tell is only a very, very small part of it. Some of it is still not declassified; some of it simply cannot be known. Even the 'Three Heroes of Longtan' are household names now. But we, Li Kenong's children, did not know it in the past. Father was closemouthed as a corked bottle, and Mother would not speak of it either. Later, when Father died and the memorial meeting was held, and Zhou Enlai revised the eulogy and added it in with his own hand, only then did we learn there had been such a chapter…"

After Li Kenong, Hu Di too, through Qian Zhuangfei's connection, entered the Shanghai Wireless Administration Bureau and transferred into the Investigation Section, becoming a mainstay of Xu Enzeng's "grave and mysterious political struggle," his whole-hearted "countering of the Communist Party's underground activity."

Xu Enzeng had Qian Zhuangfei hold Nanjing, and, under cover of his post as confidential secretary, take on as well the direction of the Zhengyuan Industrial Company's controlling organ, the Yangtze News Agency, and of its screening organ, the Minzhi News Agency.

Xu Enzeng further had Li Kenong stay behind in Shanghai to run an intelligence organ screened by the Wireless Administration Bureau's broadcast news.

And he had Hu Di go to Tianjin to serve as head of the Great Wall News Agency.

Xu Enzeng's fond scheme was to form a mutually answering "iron triangle," so that the Communists' "secret activity in the various provinces," as Chen Lifu put it, should be "snuffed out as soon as it flared up." Not in his wildest dreams did he imagine that Qian Zhuangfei's true identity was that of a CCP member. Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di were all important members of the Central Special Branch. They were not Xu Enzeng's "iron triangle" against the Communist Party; they were three sharp knives of the Communist Party against Xu Enzeng.

Li Kenong says: "I worked at this for over a year, mastered the whole of it, and won their trust. We learned that the organization the two Chens and Xu Enzeng had built up was the KK organization founded within the Kuomintang—that is, the CC organization. We reported the situation to the organization, and at the time there were two opinions: one, to plant ourselves deeper inside the CC and learn all its secrets; the other, not to go in. Zhou Enlai agreed that we should go in. He said, 'Take it over!' (meaning to gain control of this secret-service apparatus). To strengthen the leadership, the Central Special Branch put Chen Shouchang, Li Qiang, and Chen Geng in contact with us. In the spring of 1930 we were transferred to Special Branch work, under Gu Shunzhang's direct leadership, with Chen Geng as liaison. From then on the secrets of the CC organization came bit by bit into our hands.… At that time we were underground, in hiding, studying the enemy's situation all day, waging a tit-for-tat struggle against him, while the enemy was exposed. Besides, they were short of men, so we seized the chance to plant ourselves inside; the surveillance posts the enemy set up in Shanghai, Nanjing, and Tianjin were all under our control. One had to enter the mud and come out unstained. Sometimes the meeting place was an opium den. People generally supposed this was the last place a Communist would come, so we made a point of meeting in just such places. Or again, one might get a section chief or the like to come and gamble and take a cut. Running a gambling table had its uses: if such men chanced upon some business of ours in a place like that, we could also buy them off with money. The women comrades in those days had sometimes to play the mistress, sometimes the nursemaid. Comrade Enlai's mother-in-law, Granny Xia, my wife, and others all lived in our stations. Li Qiang and Liu Ding both played the young master, able to ride a bicycle and drive a car; sometimes for a meeting we rented a grand mansion or took an inn, and if the session ran a little long, the 'guests' stayed a few days more. The lodging was good enough, but the food was not; there were times when even plain rations were hard to come by."

Li Li remembers to this day how, in his childhood, whenever the pot at home could not be got open, his father had no choice but to strip the overcoat from his back and take it to the pawnshop. It was a middling, half-worn coat that fetched only a few coppers. But Li Kenong pawned and redeemed, redeemed and pawned, made those few coppers do in a pinch, and came back with a big basin of rice under gravy for the whole family to share.

### 3. Live Long Enough and You Will See Him Turn

When it came to the Communists' "secret activity in the various provinces," what most gave Xu Enzeng a headache in those days was the Central Special Branch—was Zhou Enlai and Gu Shunzhang.

Zhou Enlai was the founder and leader of our Party's struggle on the hidden front. It was under his direct leadership that the Central Special Branch met the two hands of counter-revolution with the two hands of revolution, and valiantly guarded the safety of the CCP Central Committee and its organizations at every level.

Gu Shunzhang was Zhou Enlai's deputy. In the words of Cai Mengjian—then the Kuomintang Central Organization Department Investigation Section's special agent in Wuhan, investigator for the two Hu provinces, and deputy chief of the detection-and-arrest office of the Wuhan field headquarters of the Army, Navy and Air Force Commander-in-Chief—"Gu was not merely a confidant of the bandit Zhou's own rank; he was, in effect, the bandit Zhou's second self." (Cai Mengjian, 1980)

Xu Enzeng too said that "the Communist Party's underground activity could not do without the support and protection of the red agents," and the "genius" among those "red agents" was Gu Shunzhang. Gu Shunzhang was "a cut above others in cleverness, alertness, and technique, and this it was that made for his surpassing skill in secret-service work."

Gu Shunzhang—original name Gu Fengming, also called Liming and Zhang Hua—was a native of Baoshan in Jiangsu (today's Baoshan, Shanghai) (Cai Mengjian says "a Pudong man of Jiangsu"), born in 1895 (one account says 1907), and joined the Party in 1924; "afterward he was sent to Guangzhou," and "there he became an 'action cadre' and served as Borodin's bodyguard." (Wakeman, 2011)

Wakeman, in *Policing Shanghai (1927–1937)* (2011), says: "Borodin arranged for Gu Shunzhang to go to Vladivostok (Haishenwai) in October 1926 and undergo systematic 'GPU' training in agent work and armed insurrection. In 1927 Gu Shunzhang served as commander-in-chief of the workers' pickets in the three Shanghai armed risings." Gu Shunzhang was elected by the Shanghai Citizens' Representative Assembly an executive committeeman and a member of the Shanghai municipal government, and afterward "went underground," "under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership," to provide, in the larger setting of "the open split between the CCP and the Kuomintang," "'political security work' for the Party's leadership."

Wakeman's account comes from Xu Enzeng. Xu Enzeng said: "After the Kuomintang purged the Communists, the Communist Party went underground, and Gu Shunzhang, under Zhou Enlai's direct command, organized the 'Red Squad,' charged with guarding the Communist Party's underground headquarters and punishing renegades." Wakeman too says that Gu Shunzhang "could move unseen and unheard under the very eyes of policemen by the dozen and of every nationality, a figure full of legend. His methods of assassination were spoken of far and near; it is said he could fire a gun without a sound being heard, and throttle a man without leaving the faintest trace. In those years, the mere mention of 'Little Gu,' who did the 'wet work,' was enough to make people blanch as at the tiger's name."

Zhang Guotao, in *My Memoirs* (1991), wrote: "Gu Shunzhang all along ran the CCP Central Committee's secret-service department, under Zhou Enlai's command, and was Zhou's able assistant. Gu Shunzhang had originally been a worker at the Nanyang Brothers Tobacco Company in Shanghai, an active man in the Green Gang, and one of the workers' leaders as early as the May Thirtieth Movement; in the three Shanghai workers' risings of 1927 he was head of the workers' pickets, and Zhou Enlai for a time served as Gu's deputy; thereafter Zhou was regularly Gu Shunzhang's superior."

Zhang Guotao also says that he had come to know Gu Shunzhang at the time of the "May Thirtieth," and much "admired" Gu Shunzhang's "ability." But he felt, too, that "his bearing and manner of speech had something a little Shanghai-slick about them."

Xu Enzeng's account of Gu Shunzhang is more concrete and more vivid. In his memoir essay "Recollections of My Battle with the Communist Party" (1992) he says that Gu Shunzhang was "about twenty-eight or twenty-nine, a Shanghai man of middle height, a stocky fellow of solid build, with a superb gift for performance; the magic he performed was deft in its handling and won loud applause from the audience. His other masterpiece was that lifelike art of disguise: each time he took the stage he made himself up as a high-nosed, small-mustached Western gentleman, and never once showed his true face to the crowd (a thing no one noticed at first, not knowing it was done on purpose, to guard against being recognized). Besides performing magic, he was skilled at society, spoke with great wit, and had a worldly experience out of all keeping with his years; he was warm and full of human feeling, good at reading the minds of others, and his manner toward people was always so gentle and sincere that they took pleasure in his company. He himself seldom went out, but his callers were many, of every description, of the nine streams and three teachings, nothing left out."

Gu Shunzhang was at the time an alternate member of the CCP Central Politburo and a member of the Central Special Affairs Committee (Central Special Committee), and had direct charge of the Central Special Branch. Gu Shunzhang had his firm and resolute side in the struggle against the enemy, but he had another side too—arrogant and overbearing, presuming on his services and full of himself. And so, though Chen Geng worked alongside Gu Shunzhang, doing the Party's political security work together with him, he "could not abide him at all." Chen Geng's son, Chen Zhijian (former deputy commander of the Chongqing Garrison District), once told me: "My father was a graduate of a regular military academy; after he joined the revolution he had been through the test many times, and his conviction and his ideals were very firm. Gu Shunzhang was not. He formed cliques and gangs, used a pack of thugs under his hand, and was always fond of pulling some extreme personal act of terror. This pack was cruel and ruthless, especially glad to mix with those gangster bands and with the Green and Red Gangs—not at all like Communists. And so my father said to comrades within the Party more than once: 'Gu Shunzhang's world outlook is wrong, and out of step with ours. As long as we don't die, we'll live to see him turn.'"

Chen Geng did not speak such harsh words for nothing. Gu Shunzhang was his superior, and a senior cadre of the Party besides; only, what he did was such that no one could truly respect him, look up to him, or hold him in honor.

The "Red Squad" was the Communist Party's armed force, yet Gu Shunzhang turned none away, accepting all who would surrender and offer themselves, and gathered in the desperadoes of the underworld on a grand scale.

Once, when Gu Zhuxuan, boss of the Tianchan Stage, came to blows with Chang Chunheng, boss of another theater, and asked Gu Shunzhang to step in, Gu Shunzhang duly led his men and helped Gu Zhuxuan beat Chang Chunheng to death.

More serious still: before the Central Special Branch had even formally come into being—on November 10, 1927, to be exact—the CCP Central Committee had already, in the *Resolution on the Labor Movement* discussed and passed at the enlarged meeting of the Provisional Central Politburo, issued a grave warning, sternly criticizing "a portion of the comrades of the Zhejiang and Shandong Provincial Committees, and a portion of the comrades in Shanghai," for their zeal for "military adventurism" and their "faith in the omnipotence of assassination," which "at bottom" was "an eruption of the lingering poison of opportunism." Were it not "purged clean," "then in the end our Party could never win deeply the confidence of the masses, and would be spurned by them." Gu Shunzhang, however, had a head full of burning, killing, and looting, and even thought to use several big trunks of explosives got off a Japanese warship to blow up the "Yipinxiang," the most famous restaurant in the Shanghai of those days.

Gu Shunzhang himself, moreover, was decadent and dissolute, whoring and taking drugs.

Zhou Enlai urged him to rein in at the cliff's edge, telling him that decadence and dissoluteness in one's private life ran wholly counter to the moral standard of a Communist. But Gu was two-faced, complying to the mouth and betraying in the heart, feigning obedience while defying in secret; behind Zhou Enlai's back he went on spending money like water, living drunk and dreaming his life away. His wanton conduct cut Zhou Enlai to the quick.

Gu Shunzhang went so far as to dare set himself above the Party. He held that it was the Party that needed him, not he who needed the Party. His pet phrase was: "No great matter of the Center can do without me, Gu Shunzhang."

His arrogance made it impossible for the organization not to act at once and shift its forces—reordering the personnel within the Central Special Branch on the one hand, and on the other sending Nie Rongzhen to Gu Shunzhang's side.

Nie Rongzhen says: "The aim in transferring me to the Special Branch was to strengthen the Special Branch politically. The Center had found that Gu Shunzhang did the lot—eating and drinking, whoring and gambling and smoking opium. He had turned these screening devices of Special Branch work into ends of personal indulgence, growing more decadent by the day, and it had put the Party on its guard. This Gu Shunzhang had performed magic in the past and once kept a magic shop in Shanghai; he was a lumpen-proletarian. Once he had gained a share of power within the Party, he grew swell-headed and ran riot. At the time no one yet thought he would turn traitor; it was only felt that, if he were let go on like this, trouble would come. I was brought in precisely to curb his dissolute ways." (Nie Rongzhen, 2007)

Gu Shunzhang sensed the organization's purpose in transferring Nie Rongzhen to Central Special Branch work, and was greatly displeased; he made things hard for Nie Rongzhen in every way, and gave him the tight shoe to wear. Knowing perfectly well that Nie Rongzhen had come back from abroad with no experience of underground struggle, and was unfamiliar with the temper and feeling of Shanghai, he insisted all the same on sending him out to root out renegades, so that he faced at every turn the danger of falling into the enemy's trap and being seized and killed by the concession detectives who secretly shielded the renegades, or by the Kuomintang agents.

Nie Li says: "Gu Shunzhang put my father in charge of running the operations just to see whether my father, an intellectual, could do it—whether he had the nerve to strike, to kill a renegade. That was a bloody stretch of history; it sounds very cruel, but under those particular conditions it was either you die or I live. If you didn't strike, the enemy would strike at you; you did it because you had to, there was no other way."

Nie Li is Nie Rongzhen's daughter, born in Shanghai in September 1930. In other words, she herself was born into just such a blood-thirsting age, such a time of terror. Looking back, she always says: "What I know of my childhood I learned after I grew up, from Mother, from Father, and from Uncle Zhou Enlai, Auntie Deng Yingchao, Uncle Liu Bocheng and the rest. I strung those threads together and tried to piece back its true face. What I saw was oppression and suffocation. For an underground Party member the White areas were hell itself, and they were people who dealt with devils."

Nie Li joined the Party in 1950 and the army in 1960; she served as leader of the engineering group of the Fifth Academy of the Ministry of National Defense, as head of the instrument department of the Thirteenth Institute of the Seventh Ministry of Machine Building, as deputy director and standing Party committee member of the Commission of Science, Technology and Industry for National Defense, and as deputy director and secretary-general of that Commission's science-and-technology committee and standing Party committee member, and holds the rank of lieutenant general in the Chinese People's Liberation Army.

Nie Li says: "Gu Shunzhang, too, was originally a man who dealt with devils. He should have led his comrades-in-arms to wipe out those devils who were the people's enemies. Who would have thought it would end in his wallowing in the same mire—that he himself would turn into an enemy of the people, turn into a devil."

### 4. Hua Guangqi

Before dawn on April 1, 1931, Zhang Guotao, Chen Changhao, Shen Zemin and others were escorted toward the Eyuwan Soviet by Gu Shunzhang, Dong Jianwu and others. Gu Shunzhang first had Zhang Guotao disguise himself as an ordinary merchant and Chen Changhao got up as Zhang Guotao's shop-hand, and then comrades of the Central Special Branch drove them by hired car straight to the Yangshupu wharf, where they boarded an unlicensed "wild-chicken boat" and left Shanghai without a soul the wiser.

On the afternoon of April 4, Zhang Guotao and his party reached Wuhan. As Gu Shunzhang had told them, they slipped ashore during the spies' mealtime.

Zhang Guotao says: "Chen Changhao and I each carrying our simple luggage, when we walked out of the wharf the sky was already near dusk. On the main road outside the wharf, a young man with a copy of that day's paper in his hand flashed us a signal—this was the man Gu Shunzhang had sent to meet us."

Toward eight in the morning of April 8, Zhang Guotao, Chen Changhao, Shen Zemin and the others left Wuhan for the Soviet, following the courier sent by the Eyuwan Soviet. By rights, Gu Shunzhang's escort task was done, and he should have gone straight back to Shanghai; but he suddenly found a pretext and lingered on, would not go. Dong Jianwu felt something was off and urged him: "We had better get back to Shanghai soon." Gu Shunzhang said: "No, we're going to stay in Wuhan and put on a magic show."

Of Gu Shunzhang's flair for magic, Xia Yan once observed in *Lazily Seeking Old Dreams* (2006) that Gu Shunzhang "in modern history really can be called a typical lumpen-proletarian, a figure out of legend. He had been a worker, had entered the Green Gang, and could 'work the tricks' (magic) too; for a while he was even billed, alongside the equally renowned Mo Wuqi, as 'the Great Magician Hua Guangqi,' and performed in Hankou and Shanghai."

Did Gu Shunzhang actually perform magic in Hankou?

In late April 1931, after Gu Shunzhang had seen off Zhang Guotao, Chen Changhao, Shen Zemin and the others, did he actually "put up at the Deming Inn in the French Concession near Dazhimen Station in Hankou, still under the stage name 'Hua Guangqi,' and perform magic openly at the New Market pleasure grounds," or did he not? (Wang Guangyuan, 1999)

Why did Gu Shunzhang really want to "use the Liming Magic Troupe as a screen, take the stage in Hankou, and perform magic for some days"?

Dong Jianwu's daughter Dong Huifang says: "Gu Shunzhang knew that when my father was a student at St. John's University he had learned magic from an American master magician named Butelin, so he wanted my father to be his supporting act and perform in Hankou. My father said, 'Once the task is done we go back and report; there's no fooling with those things.' Gu Shunzhang told him not to be so straitlaced, making a great to-do of nothing. My father said, 'How is this making a great to-do of nothing? You perform in the open, show your face in public—aren't you afraid something will go wrong?' Gu Shunzhang laughed and laughed, and paid my father's words no mind at all."

At the time Dong Huifang was past ninety, seeing out her old age in a home for the aged, and my interview with her too was conducted at the home. Such a graceful old lady is a rare sight. A sweet smile on her face, she sat across from me, kindly and serene, dignified and gentle. On the wall behind her hung one large yellowed photograph. It was of Song Qingling and herself. It was a matter of more than half a century ago. In those days she carried messages between her father and Song Qingling. She addressed Song Qingling respectfully as "Second Auntie," and Song Qingling called her fondly "Little Luxi."

Mu Xin says that Gu Shunzhang, "on the pretext of running a courier line, lingered in Hankou, lodging at the Deming Hotel in the French Concession not far from Dazhimen Station in Hankou, and carried on his personal doings. On his own authority he used the stage name 'the magician Hua Guangqi,' performed magic openly at the New Market pleasure grounds, and plastered advertisements about the streets to draw a crowd."

Gu Shunzhang, "by day performed magic under the alias Hua Guangqi at the New World pleasure grounds, and by night ran about with Miss Bai, the most famous socialite in Hankou."

Dong Jianwu said that he had seen with his own eyes that "modish young woman in white dress, white hat, and white high heels," "said to be a dance-hall girl he had met at the dance hall." "Afterward the Party organization sent people to Hankou to investigate, and only then learned that the woman in white was an agent."

Cai Mengjian, going by Gu Shunzhang's *Confession*, says that this "socialite" was merely one of the "several Hankou socialites" whom Gu Shunzhang "seduced" while he "put up at the Dazhi Inn at Dazhimen under the alias Hua Guangqi," "used the Liming Magic Troupe as a screen," and "took the stage to perform magic."

As for the "cause" of Gu Shunzhang's bringing "a performing troupe" to Wuhan for "performances" that "went on for several months," Wakeman, drawing on the Zhongtong archives at his command, holds firmly that it was "an attempt to assassinate Chiang Kai-shek."

Wakeman says: "After the anti-Communist 'bandit-suppression' began in Jiangxi in 1930, Chiang Kai-shek went to Wuhan. In December, Gu Shunzhang received an order from the CCP Central Committee headed by Li Lisan to lead a Red Squad to Wuhan and assassinate Chiang Kai-shek." Gu Shunzhang "wore a Western suit on the stage, got up with a big nose and a small mustache. The performances were a great success, and though they went on for several months, apart from performing he seldom left his room at the Pacific Hotel. Yet the callers who came each day were an unbroken stream, and among them were some Communist suspects and some senior Kuomintang men. These visitors drew the notice of Cai Mengjian, deputy chief of the detection-and-arrest office at the field headquarters of the Kuomintang Wuhan Pacification Commissioner's Office, who put Gu Shunzhang under watch as 'a man in contact with the Communist Party.' Cai Mengjian sent his photograph to the headquarters of the Special Investigation Bureau in Nanjing to see whether anyone knew him, and the answer came back that he was in fact the dreaded chief of the CCP Red Squad, Gu Shunzhang." (Wakeman, 2011)

### 5. Assassinate Chiang Kai-shek

From November 12 to 18, 1930, the Kuomintang's Third Central Executive Committee held its Fourth Plenary Session in Nanjing.

The session was convened after the Central Plains War among Chiang (Kai-shek), Yan (Xishan), and Feng (Yuxiang) had ended. On the grounds that "if the calamity of the Communist bandits is not removed, the people will have no time to flee even from death, social order will fall swiftly into ruin, and the whole nation will be left an irremediable disaster," the manifesto it passed "fixed" the "rooting out of the Communists and suppression of the bandits" as "an urgent task of government."

On December 7, Chiang Kai-shek, together with Lu Diping—governor of Jiangxi, concurrently director of the Nanchang field headquarters of the Army, Navy and Air Force Commander-in-Chief and commander-in-chief of the Ninth Route Army—and He Chengjun—governor of Hubei, concurrently director of the Wuhan field headquarters of the Army, Navy and Air Force Commander-in-Chief—reached Nanchang, and that very night, with He Yingqin, the National Government's minister of military administration, and Zhu Shaoliang, commander of the Second Army, concurrently commander of the Eighth Division and commander-in-chief of the Sixth Route Army, plotted to link up the provinces of Hunan, Hubei, Jiangxi, and Fujian to "advance by separate routes and strike in concert, driving straight in," and jointly "suppress the Communists."

The next day, on Chiang Kai-shek's order, the Jiangxi provincial government set up three "Communist-suppression" rural-pacification committees in the three counties of Yongfeng, Le'an, and Ji'an, with the three division commanders Tan Daoyuan, Zhang Huizan, and Luo Lin severally as chairman-committeemen, to carry out the "suppression of the Communists" with rigor and to check the household registers.

At the same time, the Ninth Route Army closed in on the Red Army's Donggu base at the meeting of the three counties of Yong, Ji, and Le; the Nineteenth Route Army came out from western Jiangxi into southern Jiangxi, and the Sixth Route Army cut out from eastern Jiangxi into southwestern Jiangxi, forming a great ring of encirclement around the Red Army units of Zhu (De), Mao (Zedong), Peng (Dehuai), and Huang (Gonglüe).

On December 9, Chiang Kai-shek convened a military conference in Nanchang to discuss the deployment of forces for the "encirclement and suppression" of the Jiangxi Red Army base, the liaison and the funds for "suppressing the Communists," the time limit for clearing them out, and the like, and set a limit of one month to recover the various cities and two months to clear out the Red Army: this was the beginning of the First "Encirclement Campaign."

That same day, Chiang Kai-shek further had the Nanchang field headquarters print several tens of thousands of copies of "Measures for Officers and Men Misled into the Communist Party Who Return in Battle," for the air force to carry over the Red areas and scatter, which read: "A soldier who comes over bearing a rifle shall be rewarded thirty yuan; one bearing a pike, fifteen yuan; one bearing a machine gun, five hundred yuan; one bearing a cannon, one thousand yuan; and a reward of fifty thousand yuan is set on the capture of Zhu, Mao, Peng, and Huang."

On December 15, Chiang Kai-shek convened another military conference on Mount Lu to discuss the "Communist-suppression" plan.

On December 16, Lu Diping, commander-in-chief of the "encirclement" forces, led the several "encirclement" columns in a general assault on the central district of the Jiangxi Red Army base, in one stroke seizing the line running from Wan'an and Taihe in western Jiangxi in the west to Jianning in Fujian in the east.

Beside himself with joy, Chiang Kai-shek decided to come down from Mount Lu and go straight to Wuhan, with Wu Zhihui, Zhou Fohai, Shao Lizi, Chen Bulei and others, to celebrate the "victory over the rebels."

Cai Mengjian says that Chiang Kai-shek "was scheduled to tour Wuhan, and the various circles of Wuhan were preparing to hold a mass rally for the victory over the rebels; unexpectedly the Communists seized the chance to infiltrate, worming into the preparatory committee's work to do harm. On a certain afternoon a few days before the Duke was to arrive in Wuhan, word suddenly came that an important Communist bandit, Xia Hua (or Hua Xia—it is now hard to recall—a Sichuan man) had been intercepted, and that under severe interrogation he had declared his willingness to surrender himself, insisting on seeing a senior man of ours in person to report an important matter. I sent for Xia Hua at once and spoke with him alone. He said: 'In a few days the soldiers and civilians of Wuhan will hold, at the Hankou General Chamber of Commerce (by Zhongshan Road, at that time a great building with a large auditorium attached, in Hankou), a mass rally to welcome the Commander-in-Chief for the victory over the rebels. The preparatory work for the rally is wholly in their hands, every arrangement under their sole authority. The staff are mostly Communist cadres, and the writing of the notices and the making and issue of the admission passes are all done by them (the Communist bandits). The Communists have already organized twenty-four groups, five to a group, all to take part carrying hand grenades, and it is planned that when the Commander-in-Chief arrives at the platform, the Communists below will fling their grenades all together up onto the platform. If you do not believe me, I will write out the names and addresses of the Communists who are helping to prepare the welcome; seize them for interrogation and the whole case can be cracked and the whereabouts of the bombs discovered.' This deposition was truly appalling to hear. I acted at once, followed the chart to find the game, and cracked the whole case without a remnant left."

Cai Mengjian says further that "among the important criminals interrogated for endangering our leader the Duke, there were seven (two of them women) who begged to redeem their crimes by service and firmly sought to surrender themselves," and You Chongxin was one of them. He had "first been of the bandit party's Jiangsu Provincial Committee, later transferred to the Hankou Municipal Committee," and in prison had "bitten open his finger and written a petition in blood." Cai Mengjian thereupon "sent men to rove the streets with You and go on pointing out the bandit Communists." In the end, "in front of the little golf course in the Hankou Special Third District (formerly the British Concession), they discovered him who had once followed the commander-in-chief of the Shanghai rising, Gu Shunzhang (thirty-one years old when arrested), talking on the street with another Communist; You confirmed it beyond doubt and cried out 'commander-in-chief of the rising!' Gu, knowing there was no denying it, let himself be taken calmly."

Cai Mengjian's recollection and Sheng Yue's differ little in the main.

Sheng Yue—original name Sheng Zongliang, also called Fajiao—studied and worked at Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow. He lived six years in the Soviet Union, returned to China in January 1933, and served as secretary of the CCP Shanghai Central Bureau.

Sheng Yue says that Kang Sheng told him "Gu's arrest was purely a matter of chance." "Two days before he was arrested, the Wuhan Garrison Command had caught a Communist and threatened him: unless he named a secret CCP agent or organ in Wuhan, they would have his life. But the underground CCP addresses this wretch gave had all been moved the moment he was arrested. So the garrison authorities, in the hope that he might just run into a few of his fellows, took him round the streets and lanes of Hankou. On one street they came upon Gu Shunzhang. The prisoner at once confirmed his identity as a CCP member, and the garrison officers arrested him on the spot."

But Xu Wenlong's *Communist Agents* (1996) tells us a different story.

Kuomintang soldiers with fixed bayonets charged in wave after wave, as many as fifty-odd at a rush. Every brick and tile of the little Huang house became a target of their assault.

Facing an enemy ten times, a hundred times their own number, the two brave men within, with ammunition enough, held out stubbornly.

Each time the enemy came howling on, what met them was a hail of grenades like sleet, and the two brave men grew bolder the longer they fought, until they were answering the enemy at last with clustered grenades. The blasts rose one after another among the enemy ranks, and the enemy's screams did not cease.

Gunfire and blasts rose and fell. The dwellings and buildings struck by bullets burned fiercely; all night the streets were bright with fire, as at high noon. The rolling smoke choked and stung; blood and flesh spattered the walls on every side, and enemy corpses lay slumped in the roadside ditches.

The two brave men within the house, Huang Yan and Jin Shijian, were by now gravely wounded, without even the strength left to throw a grenade.

Huang Yan crawled with difficulty to Jin Shijian's side and said: "Old Jin, we've more than paid our way. These few grenades that are left must never fall into the enemy's hands."

Jin Shijian nodded, still in that same way. "Rest easy—with our deaths today we'll wake those oppressed and toiling masses."

Outside the house the enemy launched another assault.

Rolling smoke, leaping flame. Even the moon, seared past bearing, slipped quietly behind the clouds.

The firelight lit up two faces no longer faces.

{p} Arise, ye slaves beset by cold and hunger,

{p} Arise, all you who suffer through the world,

{p} The blood within us has come to the boil,

{p} …

Two throats sang out as one.

Suddenly a deafening blast rang out; in that instant the Huang house crumbled to the ground, and all at once the fire shot up to the sky, and before long a great blaze was raging.

That great blast, that great fire, shook the people of Hankou to the depths, so that for many years afterward the memory of it was still fresh.

Huang You'nan "became, after Xia Hua, the second shameful renegade." "Huang You'nan's defection dealt the CCP Hubei Provincial Committee a heavy blow. The provincial secretary and his wife were both arrested by the enemy; the two of them stood staunch and unbending, and both were killed." "Cai Mengjian, together with the concession police station, arrested in the Hankou French Concession one of the organizers of this affair, You Chongxin. Under the many-sided threats and inducements of the agents and the renegades, You told all he knew of the Hankou underground Party organization." "Because the CCP's underground organization in Hubei was being broken up again and again, the Center charged the CCP Politburo member and Special Branch chief Gu Shunzhang to go to Hankou in April 1931 to get to the bottom of the matter." Gu Shunzhang reached Hankou and "did not set about getting to the bottom of the underground organization's destruction at all, but burrowed straight into the home of his mistress Cuihua."

On a spring night, in a guest room of the Dazhi Inn at Hankou, the doors and windows were shut fast. The night was already deep. Under a shaft of milk-white lamplight, cups and dishes lay in disorder on the table. The powerfully built Gu Shunzhang, half-drunk, peeled off the white shirt hanging open on him to bare a body all knotted muscle, the black chest hair standing up stiff. He stepped forward, caught his mistress Cuihua round the waist, and bit at her perfumed cheek. "Sweetheart!" he said, "I've missed you to death; now…"

The next day Gu Shunzhang took Cuihua to the Xiangtai gold shop, and when they came out there was a big glittering ring on Cuihua's finger. Cuihua said suddenly: "I'm a bit hungry, I fancy some bang-bang chicken." Gu Shunzhang said impatiently: "Where am I supposed to buy you bang-bang chicken, you little terror—just eat something nearby." "No, I want bang-bang chicken; there's some not far from here, right at the Jianghan Customs ferry wharf—buy me some!" "Why didn't you say so earlier? Come on then! Come on!" Gu Shunzhang, hauling Cuihua along, made straight for the Jianghan Customs ferry wharf.

Just then the renegade You Chongxin, mingling with the crowd around the wharf, wearing a battered felt hat pulled low, with seven or eight agents trailing behind him, was walking near the Jianghan Customs ferry wharf. Suddenly a woman's laughter reached him; he glanced over without thinking, and his eyes lit up.

What lit up his eyes was not that woman, but the burly fellow standing at her side. Was that not the CCP Politburo member and Special Branch chief, Gu Shunzhang? Now here was a big fish.

You Chongxin cried out in haste: "That's him!"

To catch the agents' attention, this renegade shouted again: "He's the commander-in-chief of the Shanghai rising!"

The agents rushed straight at Gu Shunzhang in the direction of You's pointing finger. Gu Shunzhang knew a little of fists and feet, and grappled with them. He had just knocked down two agents when a big fellow suddenly seized him round the waist and threw him; Gu drew a dagger from his waistband and gave the man a stab, and the fellow screamed and let go.

Before he went to Hankou, Gu Shunzhang had already learned from Zhou Enlai's own mouth of You Chongxin's "arrest and defection."

Zhou Enlai, it seemed, was still uneasy about Gu Shunzhang, and gravely told him that You Chongxin, former secretary of the West-Shanghai District Committee, had been arrested and had turned traitor soon after his transfer to Wuhan, and was leading the enemy's agents and police from door to door day after day, arresting Communists, and posing a great threat to the Wuhan Party organization. At the last he added a caution: "Comrade Shunzhang, You Chongxin knows you well; should you meet in a public place—"

"That's exactly the chance I want," Gu Shunzhang said with a cold laugh. "When the time comes, I'll show him the Horse King has three eyes!"

"No rash moves!" Zhou Enlai retorted almost by reflex, and then, in something close to an order, said, "You must remember: go fast and come back fast!"

"Yes!" Gu Shunzhang answered, with a good deal of feeling.

### 6. The Grand Plan Against the Communists

Gu Shunzhang's arrest came on the afternoon of April 24, 1931.

Cai Mengjian says that when Gu Shunzhang gave himself up without a struggle, "his manner was calm; taken to the Dazhi Inn where he was lodging to pack his things, he asked first of all to see Cai Mengjian, saying, 'I know Cai is the man in charge of countering the Communists in Wuhan.' I had Gu brought to me at once."

Gu Shunzhang was taken to the Wuhan field headquarters and saw Cai Mengjian.

Cai Mengjian said: "Though we have never met, I know you, and you must surely know me."

Gu Shunzhang said: "Not you alone—I know the chiefs of every secret-service organ across the country."

Cai Mengjian answered coldly: "Then let's put our cards on the table. What each of us is, we both know, and there's no need to say much of it. If you want to live, tell me all you know; otherwise, for a man in your position there's only the road to death."

Gu Shunzhang gave a faint smile, his face full of scorn.

Cai Mengjian handed him a cigarette, took out a lighter, and lit it for him. Gu tilted back his face and blew out a smoke ring, and did not so much as thank him. Cai Mengjian offered him tea as well, and still he said not a word.

The more silent Gu Shunzhang kept, the more Cai Mengjian betrayed nothing. He knew that with a big man like Gu Shunzhang one had to have patience enough.

At last Gu Shunzhang turned his face round and looked at Cai Mengjian afresh, and spoke again; but with this speech his manner grew more arrogant and overbearing than before.

Gu Shunzhang said: "I have a grand plan against the Communist Party. Arrange quickly for me to be received by the Commander-in-Chief, the Duke; I shall lay it out to him in person."

Cai Mengjian said: "I will bring you to see Director He of the field headquarters."

Gu Shunzhang said: "He would say no more, if he saw me, than the same thing."

Cai Mengjian much wanted to lose his temper, but held it in. He knew Gu Shunzhang was no hollow bluff, no empty show of strength. He had his advantages, he had his capital. His singular standing within the Communist Party meant that he could perfectly well haggle and set all manner of harsh conditions.

Cai Mengjian reported at once to He Chengjun. He Chengjun said he did not wish to see Gu Shunzhang. The two agreed to wire Nanjing separately that Gu Shunzhang had been arrested and had surrendered himself, and to ask that Chiang Kai-shek receive him.

He Chengjun further instructed Cai Mengjian that "that same night," besides his own "dispatch of several cadres," "a further squad of military police be sent aboard a special steamer to escort Gu Shunzhang to the capital."

Cai Mengjian passed He Chengjun's decision on to Gu Shunzhang. Gu Shunzhang thereupon called for paper and brush and wrote out on the spot several of our Party's secret contact points in Wuhan, as well as the Wuhan office of the Red Second Army Group.

Gu Shunzhang said: "I write these out for you as proof that I already have the sincere intent to submit to the government." But he added a special caution: "For the present, do not touch these organs; wait till I have reached Nanjing and seen the Commander-in-Chief Chiang, and then you may move on them."

To prove his sincerity further, Gu Shunzhang also gave up the whereabouts of Dong Jianwu.

It was Gu Shunzhang who had sent men to buy Dong Jianwu's boat ticket, and had his own disciple see him aboard the *Jianguo*. What he did not know was that, at the very last moment before the *Jianguo* left port, Dong Jianwu had changed to the *Luoyang*, which sailed two hours later.

Toward twenty past eleven that night, the *Luoyang* that Dong Jianwu had boarded drew near the Jiujiang wharf, all but catching up with the *Jianguo* that had left two hours ahead. Dong Jianwu threw on his clothes, rose, walked out of the cabin, went up onto the deck and gazed off over the rail—and saw at that very moment the wharf loud with police whistles, voices seething, a great body of soldiers and police, closing in as on a mighty foe, ringing round the just-berthed *Jianguo*.

Seeing the Jiujiang wharf in such utter confusion, and having no passengers of his own to put ashore midway, the captain of the *Luoyang* decided on the spur of the moment not to enter port after all, but to steer straight on down the Yangtze.

By sheer luck Dong Jianwu escaped the reactionary authorities' arrest, and did not become a victim of Gu Shunzhang's defection.

At almost the same moment, Gu Shunzhang was escorted by Cai Mengjian's men and a squad of military police from the Wuhan field headquarters aboard another cargo steamer bound straight for Nanjing.

Before he left, Gu Shunzhang cautioned again that on no account was Nanjing to be told of the matter beforehand.

But Cai Mengjian went his own way as ever, and sent one encrypted telegram after another—six of them—to Xu Enzeng, chief of the Party Affairs Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department. (Qian Jiang says it was seven telegrams. In *Seven Top-Secret Telegrams* he writes that the content of the sixth was "Communist elements lurk at Director Xu's side," and of the seventh, "have changed to escorting Gu Shunzhang to the capital by aircraft.")

### 7. A Cross Slashed on the Map

The encrypted telegrams reached Nanjing one after another; but Qian Zhuangfei, on duty for Xu Enzeng at the Zhengyuan Industrial Company at No. 5 Zhongshan East Road, had no way to deliver them promptly, as custom required, into his superior's hands.

Because his superior—minding at once the public business and the private—was not in Nanjing.

Xu Enzeng was in the habit, every Saturday, of going back to Shanghai to lose his soul in pleasure.

Qian Hong says: "Xu Enzeng was a lecher; every Saturday he went back to Shanghai to keep a tryst with his mistress, 'Miss Wang.'"

The "Miss Wang" in question was Wang Suqing. Wang Suqing was handsome of face and sturdy of build, but shrewish in temper and greedy for money. She had originally been the wife of a friend of Xu Enzeng's. At the turn of the summer and autumn of 1928, that friend went to study in England and entrusted his wife to Xu Enzeng's care. Xu Enzeng agreed with a will and bade the friend rest easy. Who could have known that no sooner was the friend abroad than Xu Enzeng, already drooling, took up with the restless Wang Suqing, and Wang Suqing bore four or five children, one a year. Later, when the friend came home and saw the shape into which his wife had been "cared for" by Xu Enzeng, he wanted to make a noise about it—but, cowed by Xu Enzeng's power, and afraid of losing both his wife and his standing into the bargain, he could only swallow the insult and hold his peace, and asked no more.

The friend, knowing the odds, withdrew; but Xu Enzeng's own first wife was no lamp that burned without oil.

Qian Hong says: "Xu Enzeng's first wife, née Zhang, was also a Huzhou woman, deeply superstitious, keeping to a vegetarian diet and telling her beads; she lived all along with Xu's mother, and got on badly with Xu. At first Xu merely ignored her, and later simply stopped coming home—or, if he did come home, no longer shared her bed; in fact they had parted as man and wife. But he did not dare keep 'Miss Wang' at his side either, for fear née Zhang would make a scene. So my grandfather helped Xu Enzeng settle 'Miss Wang' in Shanghai, hidden away in the house my grandmother rented. 'Miss Wang' lived in the front upper story of our house, my grandmother and the rest in the back upper story. Xu Enzeng went to our house for his trysts with 'Miss Wang.' That way, Xu Enzeng felt it was quite safe."

The "house my grandmother rented" of which Qian Hong speaks was, in those years, Qian Zhuangfei's home at No. 4 Xinxingshunli on Gansi East Road in Shanghai—today's No. 24, Lane 117, Jiashan Road. There, in just such a small building hidden from prying eyes, the incorrigibly philandering Xu Enzeng and his underground lover "Miss Wang" whispered their love and poured out their hearts to each other.

Before dawn on this day, Xu Enzeng, deep in the land of tenderness, had long since spent his rain and cloud and fallen sweetly asleep; but Qian Zhuangfei, in the Zhengyuan Industrial Company, found the long night short and all his five organs on fire.

Watching the encrypted telegrams pile higher and higher before his eyes, he could neither open them nor leave them.

Open them, and years of painstaking work would go for nothing, washed away downstream.

Leave them, and if he really missed some grave intelligence that must not be missed, the consequence was too dreadful to imagine.

Xu Enzeng said that Qian Zhuangfei was the confidential secretary, and that these telegrams were his to open in the first place. "The telegram reporting Gu Shunzhang's arrest was deciphered by his own hand," and "handed to me in person."

Zhang Guodong likewise says that "all Xu Enzeng's confidential correspondence and telegrams" passed through Qian Zhuangfei's hands to be "forwarded," and that this included "the several most urgent telegrams that Cai Mengjian sent to Xu Enzeng on April 25, to be relayed to Chen Lifu." Qian Zhuangfei first "took advantage of a moment when no one was about, and shrewdly deciphered and read the telegrams—that Gu Shunzhang had been arrested and had surrendered, would soon be sent under guard to Nanjing, and that Gu claimed that within a few days the CCP Central organs in Shanghai could be 'wholly cleared out'"; then he "sealed them up again," "handed them to Xu Enzeng in person," said he "wanted to go home and rest," "left the Zhengyuan Industrial Company unhurriedly," and "hurried by train to Shanghai to report Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection to the Party Center."

What is most baffling in this account is why Qian Zhuangfei was so "unhurried"—"unhurried" to the point that, having already learned that "Gu Shunzhang had been arrested and had surrendered," he not only failed to warn the Party at the first instant, but even dragged things out to the point of handing those "resealed" telegrams "to Xu Enzeng in person." Had he too much time, then, rather than too little? Once his true identity was wholly exposed, was there still any need for him to go on disguising himself?

Li Lun says: "My father, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di, the three of them, on the approval of the Party Center, planted themselves in the Kuomintang's secret-service command; their main task was to supply the Party Center with important information. For instance, that some comrade of ours had had his address exposed and the enemy might be going to seize him. Once they learned such a thing, they reported it quickly, and through Chen Geng it went to Zhou Enlai, who quickly arranged the move, so the enemy could not find him."

Li Lun is Li Li's younger brother, a lieutenant general in the Chinese People's Liberation Army and former deputy head of the General Logistics Department.

Qian Hong says his grandfather and the others had a mission that went far beyond "some comrade of ours had had his address exposed and the enemy might be going to seize him; once they learned of it, they reported it quickly." They "were able to reach secret intelligence directly, and at that time they took out the whole of the Kuomintang's plan for the encirclement and suppression of the Central Soviet, and passed it to the Center, which relayed it to the Soviet, so that the First and Second counter-encirclement campaigns both won through."

Seeing the long night at last near its end, the east faintly lightening, the belly of the sky going fish-white, Qian Zhuangfei broke his cauldrons and sank his boats, staked all on one throw, and made his final resolve. He was sure his judgment was not wrong. Something extraordinary and grave must have happened in Wuhan. From the crack in his drawer he took out the codebook he had hidden, and, checking against the *Collected Works of Zeng Wenzheng*, deciphered word by word and line by line those encrypted telegrams so conspicuously marked "for the Director's own eyes."

Qian Hong says he thinks this codebook was "absolutely" something his grandfather "got hold of." For he was Xu Enzeng's "personal secretary," and only he was so close to Xu Enzeng. Of course, "just how he got hold of it can no longer be said clearly now."

Li Li, for his part, says: "Xu Enzeng was a man of the deepest cunning; he kept the codebook on him at all times, and would not even let Qian Zhuangfei, whom he trusted most, look at it—the important codes he translated himself."

Li Li says further: "My father, too, knew that come what may the codebook had to be got hold of, or the core secrets would never be known. So once, when Xu Enzeng had gone off to Shanghai to play with women again—he had many women about him, not just one 'Miss Wang'—my father rented him a set of rooms, put every convenience his way, and close to where my father lived. Qian Zhuangfei and the others were there too. My father then told Xu Enzeng that a new woman had lately come, very pretty, and asked whether he was interested. At this Xu Enzeng was of course tempted, and wanted to go and take a look. My father said, You may go, but you have the codebook on you; do be careful—if it should be lost, and the Commander should hold you to account and lay the blame on you, that would be trouble. Xu Enzeng, hearing the sense in it, took out the codebook and handed it to Qian Zhuangfei, saying, You keep it for me, and give it back when I return. So off he went to play, and Qian Zhuangfei and my father quickly photographed the codebook."

Without that one moment of handing over, there would have been no deciphering of the six (or seven) encrypted telegrams. And without the deciphering of the six (or seven) encrypted telegrams, there would have been no such piece of "modern Chinese history" that, in the end, was never "rewritten" to Cai Mengjian's design.

Watching Gu Shunzhang's renegade face grow the more hideous, watching the CCP Central Committee, kept in the dark, in mortal peril, Qian Zhuangfei rose in a flash, went to the window, drew back the curtain, and gazed far off at the sky where the rising sun came bursting up.

Qian Hong says: "He felt it was grave, and he sent my grandaunt's husband—that is, his son-in-law, the husband of his daughter Qian Jiao, Liu Qifu—to Shanghai first, to pass the news on."

But Liu Qifu could not find Li Kenong in Shanghai, because it was not the appointed time to make contact. In that vast human sea he had no way even to begin the search.

Liu Qifu's fruitless return deepened Qian Zhuangfei's anxiety further. He realized the time had come for him to leave Nanjing. He laid the six (or seven) encrypted telegrams, all of which he had opened, out in a neat row on Xu Enzeng's desk, then went to the Yangtze News Agency on the fourth floor of the Central Hotel to warn the other comrades he had placed there to move in time.

Failing to find the men he sought in the deserted office, he took a small knife and slashed a great cross on the map on the wall, a signal to cut all ties and withdraw at once.

Qian Hong says: "Only when all the business in Nanjing was done did my grandfather leave. Reaching Shanghai, and afraid someone might be waiting to head him off at the terminus, he got off early, at Zhenru Station."

Qian Zhuangfei slipped into the city and, after many turns, at last found Li Kenong at the Phoenix Hotel behind the Sincere Company.

But then Li Kenong could not find Chen Geng. They too had a standing appointment, and could not meet just because they wished it.

Time slipped away, minute by minute, in such difficulty.

Danger mounted, moment by moment, in such helplessness.

And amid that slipping-away and that mounting, Gu Shunzhang reached Nanjing.

### 8. Breaking His Rule for a Cigarette

The moment Gu Shunzhang reached Nanjing, Chen Lifu, Xu Enzeng, and the Investigation Section's chief managers Gu Jianzhong, Zhang Chong and others all rode out in a small motor launch made ready beforehand to meet him in midstream, and then took him to the "Zhengyuan Industrial Company."

Xu Enzeng said their talk with Gu Shunzhang was wholly "in the manner of friends." They "treated him entirely as an ordinary friend, and not as a prisoner at all." When Gu Shunzhang entered his office, he too "saw that this was a building for ordinary office work and receiving guests, with no special fittings for detaining prisoners." All of this "took Gu Shunzhang wholly by surprise." As a result, his "psychological defenses" "collapsed at the first contact." He "asked" them to give him a time to "consider." They "agreed," and gave him two hours. In the end, "he agreed to 'come over.'"

No sooner had Gu Shunzhang agreed to "come over" than he told Xu Enzeng a piece of "startling news." Gu Shunzhang said that Qian Zhuangfei, who kept at Xu Enzeng's "side" and "had charge of his confidential papers," was a CCP agent, was "a spy sent by the Communist Party."

Xu Enzeng simply could not believe his own ears.

Xu Enzeng said he "still clung to a one-in-ten-thousand hope that the news was not true." But when he "sent men to find him, sure enough, on the morning of the day before, he had quietly slipped away."

Xu Enzeng "realized at once that things were somewhat amiss." Naturally, "the most anxious of all was Gu Shunzhang."

Cai Mengjian says that Gu Shunzhang, "learning of this, was beside himself with terror."

Gu Shunzhang said to Cai Mengjian: "If this Qian reports to Zhou Enlai and the other chief men that I have surrendered, then the heads of the various Communist organs will flee if they do not move, and the whole clearing-out plan will naturally come to nothing."

Cai Mengjian at once drew Zhang Daofan aside for a "whispered word," saying: "Gu has given up that the Central Investigation Section's secretary Qian Zhuangfei, and the Section's Shanghai station chief Yang Dengying, are both important bandit spies of the enemy's, and Qian has already fled to Shanghai."

Zhang Daofan thereupon "called together the Investigation Section's two chief managers, Zhang Chong and Gu Jianzhong," and with Cai Mengjian "conferred on how to use Gu to take action against the bandit party in Shanghai."

Gu Jianzhong was ordered to "take Gu Shunzhang to Shanghai and, through the Shanghai Municipal Police Bureau, get in touch with the British and French concession police, and search according to the blueprint of the bandit party's various organs and safe houses that Gu supplied"—only to find that the "various bandit-party organs" had all "emptied out, the birds flown."

For Qian Zhuangfei and Li Kenong had, after all, got in ahead of Gu Shunzhang, and through the Jiangsu Provincial Committee found Chen Yun.

*The Life of Chen Yun* (2005) writes:

{v} That day was not the day for Li Kenong to make contact with Chen Geng, chief of the Second Section of the Central Special Branch, and for a while there was no reaching the CCP Central Committee. Li Kenong thought at once of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee, with which he was often in touch. The Jiangsu Provincial Committee was in close contact with the CCP Central Committee, and to find the Jiangsu Provincial Committee was to be able to find the CCP Central Committee. By this time the secretary of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee was Chen Yun. Before this, the Jiangnan Provincial Committee, after being reorganized on January 17 into the Jiangsu Provincial Committee, had had Wang Ming as secretary. But Wang Ming's attention was on the CCP Central Committee, and he served as secretary of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee only a short time, about a month or so. By Kang Sheng's recollection: "As I remember, after Wang Ming came Comrade Chen Yun as secretary; Chen Yun left the provincial committee about June 1931, and after Chen Yun it was probably Wang Yuncheng." This was a moment of extreme peril. After Li Kenong found Chen Yun, Chen Yun reported to the CCP Central Committee at once, and the Center immediately entrusted Zhou Enlai with full powers to handle this emergency.

On hearing that Gu Shunzhang had defected to the enemy, Zhou Enlai—who never smoked—broke his rule and asked for a cigarette, and drew on it hard before Chen Yun's eyes. Later, at a meeting of the CCP Central Politburo, Zhou Enlai gravely pointed out: "The Special Committee's work had, it is true, many achievements, and gave the Party no little protection; but in the end, because of the defection of one man, Gu Shunzhang, the whole of the work was thrown into upheaval, and this cannot but be called the result of an error in the Special Committee's work itself."

Gu Shunzhang's defection meant the wholesale upheaval of the Special Committee's work.

Gu Shunzhang's defection meant that mass arrests might happen anywhere, at any moment.

With the help of Chen Yun, Chen Geng, Li Qiang and others, Zhou Enlai took resolute emergency measures: destroying great quantities of secret documents; moving the Party's chief leaders swiftly and putting tight security about them; moving swiftly to safe districts, or transferring out of Shanghai, every cadre who might become a target of Gu Shunzhang's investigation; cutting every important tie that Gu Shunzhang could make use of in Shanghai; and abolishing every secret working method that Gu Shunzhang knew.

On Zhou Enlai's instructions, that very night the organs of the CCP Central Committee and the Jiangsu Provincial Committee all moved to safety. Chen Yun even sent a man specially to find a printing house, paid four times the going rate, and within two hours had Gu Shunzhang's photograph blocked and reprinted in more than a hundred copies, to be distributed to the Party organizations of Shanghai at every level, with word to be on their guard.

On Zhou Enlai's instructions, Wu Defeng, then head of the Central Communications Bureau, likewise went about through the secret courier network raising the alarm, organizing his people to warn every comrade and organ linked to Gu Shunzhang and liable to be sold out by him to move swiftly, one and all, and to shift house before the enemy could act in Shanghai. Wu Defeng also sent people, by every means, to head off and bring back the several groups of comrades who had set out for the Soviet along the route Gu Shunzhang had traveled. At the same time he took emergency measures, effectively readjusting and redeploying the Yangtze-line courier links, gravely damaged and all but severed, so that the damage should not spread to the courier lines of other regions. Wu Defeng's many bold, out-of-the-ordinary turns of thought won Zhou Enlai's approval. For instance, the postbox numbers the Central Special Branch had set up at the post office had all been suspended because of Gu Shunzhang's defection; but once work returned to normal, he proposed reopening those very boxes. He held that an idle postbox the enemy had once sealed up was, if used with care, actually less likely to draw the enemy's notice, and so was safe, and moreover convenient for reconnecting ties that had once been broken.

Li Lun says his father was so taken up with "warning some of the Party Center's leaders to move" that he had no time to warn his own family, so that the nine-year-old Li Li and himself, four years old, drifted onto the streets with their mother, "with no money on them, and could only hide in the market, and got through two or three days."

Li Kenong, with no time for his own family, did not forget the safety of Qian Zhuangfei's household.

Qian Hong says that early that morning, "Li Kenong hurried to our house; he dared not call out loudly, and knocking on the door would not do either, for fear of drawing the neighbors' notice. In his urgency he remembered that my grandmother was in medicine, most particular about hygiene, and most disgusted by people relieving themselves anywhere; so he stood under our window and made water against the wall. My grandmother heard, pushed open the window, and was about to speak—then, seeing it was Li Kenong, hurried to open the door and let him in."

Qian Hong's account differs from Li Kenong's recollection. In December 1954, while convalescing, Li Kenong spoke with Huang Gang, and touched on the measures taken after Gu Shunzhang's defection. He said: "Qian Zhuangfei's wives were seen to. Word was sent to both of them. His later wife was on Avenue Edward VII; she had trained in medicine, an obstetrician. Xu Shuangying was on Chengdu Road. We ran to tell her: from tomorrow keep no one at home; the day after, go near the market, carry a basket, dress a bit cleaner, greet anyone who comes, and go along with them…"

At the time, Li Kenong also wired Hu Di, far off in Tianjin, saying "Ke Chao gravely ill."

This was a code the three of them had agreed on beforehand. "Ke" was Li Kenong; "Chao" was Qian Chao, that is, Qian Zhuangfei; "gravely ill" meant the situation was critical—get clear as fast as you can.

Of all this the Communist International, too, gave an objective assessment. The Special Work Department of the Comintern's Executive Committee, in its "Written Report on the State of Secret Work and Special-Service Work of the Communist Parties in the Far Eastern and Near Eastern Countries" (June 3, 1932), said: "China, before the middle of 1931, had a well-organized apparatus for opposing spies and traitors within the Party and for carrying on sabotage within the enemy's organizations and armies.… The CCP special-agent department had its own intelligence men at certain of the Kuomintang's operational posts, who gave advance word of the Kuomintang's plans of action against the Communist Party in the Soviet areas. The chief defect in that department's work was the over-concentration of the whole of the work in a single man; when Gu (Shunzhang) was arrested in Wuhan and gave up the content and methods of the Communist Party's work as he knew them, the harm of it was exposed. Only because the Party had an inside line in the Nanjing police was Gu's defection learned of, so that he was unable to destroy all the Party organizations and links of Party work that he knew."

### 9. A Great Conjuror Is Not a Master of Magic

We can scarcely imagine what disaster Gu Shunzhang's "coming over" would have brought upon our Party without the surprise stroke of Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di, their turning back of the raging tide.

We do not exalt the individual hero.

What we truly admire, from the heart, are those red agents lying low in the enemy's very heart; from the heart we admire their gift for disguise. They were worthy of the name "Three Heroes of Longtan," able even after the fact to leave their old enemies clutching at shadows, wholly at a loss.

Cai Mengjian insisted again and again: "There is solid material proving that after the mainland fell to the bandits, the bandit regime's minister of social affairs, Li Kenong, was in fact the bandit Qian Zhuangfei, Qian Zhuangfei being the false spy-name Li went by."

Sparring with each other, dealing with each other for the better half of a lifetime, in the end Cai Mengjian not only did not know who his opponent was, but even blew his own trumpet loud, prating of his "achievements" and "exploits" in "battling the Communist Party."

They were fit only to deal with the likes of Gu Shunzhang, able only to expose his tricks, but not to end them.

For a trickster is not a conjuror, and a great conjuror is not a master of magic.

Having changed his allegiance, Gu Shunzhang wanted to work one more great trick—to turn a whole crowd of living people into dead ones, to turn every one of his former comrades into a mangled, bloodied ghost.

But he botched the act; it came to grief in the hands of the "Three Heroes of Longtan," came to grief in the hands of Zhou Enlai. He was a great conjuror; Zhou Enlai was a master of magic. He could not crack Zhou Enlai's mystery, but Zhou Enlai could put an end to his tricks.

If "making a living person vanish" had once been Gu Shunzhang's specialty, then at most he could make one living person vanish before the audience's eyes—but Zhou Enlai could make the whole of the CCP Central Committee vanish under the very eyes of Chen Lifu, Zhang Daofan, Xu Enzeng, and Cai Mengjian. Living people by the hundred, by the thousand, right under their eyes, right under the eyes of the great conjuror Hua Guangqi, evaporated as one and vanished without a trace.

This is the difference between a master of magic and a great conjuror. This is the final parting of the ways between a great conjuror and a master of magic.

### 10. The Hero Dies; the Hero Lives On

In December 2001 a beautiful album came into the world in Beijing, appearing quietly within a not very large circle. Everyone who received the album was moved, as was the famous film and television actress Pan Hong, who wrote its preface, by the "tenacious will" of the album's author.

Pan Hong lavished praise on the author as "a participant in the early creation of Chinese cinema, one of the few living witnesses today." For the album's author had "left, on the Chinese screen of the 1930s and 1940s, countless bright images of youth in all their grace and color." She was Li Lili, who had played in one fine picture after another—*Little Toys*, *The Big Road*, *Blood on Wolf Mountain*, *Storm on the Border*, *Daybreak*.

Li Lili was Qian Hong's eldest aunt. Qian Hong's father was her younger brother—Qian Jiang, later a cinematographer at the Beijing Film Studio. And the father of her and Qian Jiang was Qian Zhuangfei, one of the "Three Heroes of Longtan."

In his lifetime Qian Jiang took his father's deeds for his material and created the documentary work *Seven Top-Secret Telegrams*, and then reworked *Seven Top-Secret Telegrams* into a thriller feature, *A Nanjing Night*.

The film does not say much of where Qian Zhuangfei went after warning the Party Center.

The living Qian Zhuangfei, as Li Lili put it, "moved to Ruijin in Jiangxi in the autumn of 1931."

Li Lili also said: "He set off in haste disguised as a vegetable seller. Which route he took, even Mother did not know; she told me only that Uncle Li (Kenong) came to our back door and said to my mother through the window: He's gone."

Li Lili said further: "After Father reached Ruijin, three letters came. In them he taught me: 'Use art well, and it is enough to save the country; misuse art, and it lures men into ruin.' I have always kept these words in mind, as the motto I hold to for life."

On April 1, 1935, as the Central Red Army crossed the Wu River southward, Qian Zhuangfei was separated from the main force, cruelly killed by local armed men, and died a martyr's death in the neighborhood of Houshan township, Shatu town, Jinsha county, Guizhou province.

{v} On March 31, 1935, our Red First, Third, and Fifth Army Groups, on the Military Commission's instructions, crossed the Jiaodu River southeastward along the Xifeng–Xiuwen line in Guizhou, so as to seek fresh room to maneuver. They kept part of their force watching the enemy in the belt north of Xifeng, and feigned the posture of our army going east into Hunan to join the Red Second and Sixth Army Groups, while the main force pressed by way of Xifeng and Zhazuo straight for Guiyang. On April 1 the Military Commission column, on the Commission's wired order, crossed the river smoothly at ten in the morning under the rearguard cover of the cadre regiment and moved into Niuchang to encamp. Once the troops were settled, I led the billeting command onto the road. A little past two in the afternoon, two Kuomintang reconnaissance planes came over, and half an hour later five bombers took turns bombing over Niuchang. After the enemy planes flew off, just as the billeting command was preparing to press on, Zhou Enlai's bodyguard Fan Jinbiao came galloping up and asked, gasping for breath: "Is Secretary-General Qian Zhuangfei with the billeting command?" I told him: "No." Fan Jinbiao went on: "The Vice-Chairman's orders are that if Secretary Qian is not with the billeting command, Commander Wang is to return to the Military Commission at once." I galloped back to Niuchang with Fan Jinbiao immediately. I saw Zhou Enlai standing under a great tree, his face stern and grave, in talk with the deputy director of the General Political Department, Li Fuchun; the deputy commander of the Military Commission column and head of the State Security Bureau, Deng Fa; the political commissar of the Military Commission guard regiment, Fang Qiang; the commander of the cadre regiment, Chen Geng; and its political commissar, Song Renqiong. Seeing me, he asked most anxiously: "Isn't Qian Zhuangfei with you?" When I had answered, he asked again: "Yesterday, was he with you?" I reported: "Yesterday we traveled together; today, after reaching Niuchang, he went back to the General Political Department." Zhou Enlai told me that after the enemy planes' bombing, Qian Zhuangfei could not be found. He ordered everyone present to organize the troops at once and search. We searched for close to three hours, and in a haystack at the southeast corner of Niuchang we found Qian Zhuangfei's body. His hands and feet were bound, there were more than ten knife wounds all over him, and he had long since stopped breathing, his heart still. Zhou Enlai stood a long, long while before Qian Zhuangfei's body, and shed tears. He decided that the Military Commission column would change its march plan and go on encamping at Niuchang, and ordered the Security Bureau and the guard regiment to investigate the case to the bottom and be sure to seize and punish the murderers, to avenge Qian Zhuangfei. I had another task and did not take part in cracking the case or seizing the murderers. Later, Chen Geng told me that Comrade Zhuangfei, while sheltering from the enemy planes' bombing, had run into a reactionary rural corps of Guizhou and been cruelly killed. The Military Commission guard regiment and the cadre regiment had already seized the whole of this rural corps, and had shot its leader. (Wang Zhitao, 2015)

Wang Zhitao later recalled that Zhou Enlai and Chen Geng afterward "neither of them much believed Qian Zhuangfei had been killed by blundering into a rural corps, and suspected that Chiang Kai-shek, hating Qian Zhuangfei to the bone, had sent agents to murder him."

Wang Zhitao's recollection was a wholly new version; before it, the usual account had Qian Zhuangfei "cruelly killed by the Kuomintang anti-Communist 'rural-pacification committeeman,' the district head Song Zizhen, and his henchmen Luo Shao'an and others," on "the ridge about one li to the right of Songjiazhai," in a "cave some fifty or sixty meters deep, called 'No-Conscience Pit.'" (Ye Bingnan, 1987)

Wang Zhitao—original name Wang Shouheng—was born in 1906, a native of Cangxian in Hebei. In 1931 he joined the Communist Party of the Soviet Union, and later transferred to the Chinese Communist Party; he served in turn as interpreter for Li De, as air-defense commander, as garrison-and-security commander, as head of the training department of the Kangda, as chief of staff of the East Hebei Military District, as commander of the training headquarters of the Northeast Field Army, as deputy commander of the East China Air Defense Command, and as deputy secretary-general of the Academy of Military Science.

Wang Zhitao said Qian Zhuangfei was ten years his senior. After he reached "the Central Soviet, he and Qian Zhuangfei were both at the Military Commission; they worked together in perfect understanding and got on well." Qian Zhuangfei "was a good comrade and good elder brother, of wide learning and considerable talent," and his "guidance" in "intelligence, enemy work, and mass work" left Wang Zhitao "much the richer for it." To the end of his days he grieved deeply over Qian Zhuangfei's death in the flower of his years.

But Qian Hong does not accept Wang Zhitao's account. For Ouyang Yi, then head of the Security Bureau of the political department of the Red Fifth Army Group, said that after Qian Zhuangfei went missing, "Mao Zedong took the matter very seriously; he knew not only that Qian held important secrets, but also that he was a cadre of many talents. So he gave orders that this man had to be found." The Red Fifth Army Group "gave the task" to Ouyang Yi. Ouyang Yi "at once took a security detachment and went back across the river to look for him," searching for fully "an hour and more, hunting and calling, and in the end found no trace of him."

Ouyang Yi, "returning later to Yan'an from the Western Route Army," learned that Qian Zhuangfei "had been kidnapped by bandits while sheltering from the planes in a wood. The bandits extorted money from him, and in his straits he had written to his wife in Shanghai to try to bring a ransom. But the way was long, and it was Kuomintang-ruled territory, and his wife had no means of coming to the rescue. In the end Qian Zhuangfei was killed by the bandits." (Ouyang Yi, 1998)
