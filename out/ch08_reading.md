### Chen Yangshan's Posthumous Writings
#### (1) On Bao Junfu
The Kuomintang set up its secret-service organ around March or April of 1928; it was called the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department. This was the forerunner of the Zhongtong, and the earliest of the Kuomintang's secret services. Its first section chief was Chen Lifu, its second Zhang Daofan.
Once the Investigation Section was formed it established its headquarters in Nanjing. Before long the headquarters sent Yang Jianhong to Shanghai to build a secret-service network. Yang Jianhong was a Cantonese who had led the foreign-trade workers' union at the time of the May Thirtieth movement and had since gone over to the Kuomintang right; his views were thoroughly reactionary. In the May Thirtieth days he came to know another Cantonese, Bao Junfu, and the two became close friends who kept nothing back from each other. Bao had studied in Japan and returned home at the time of the May Thirtieth movement; he had worked in a Japanese trading firm, moved in wide circles, and had dealings with the Japanese news agency, the concession police, and certain Kuomintang notables, and with comrades of our Party as well. He had once lived near Shanghai University and knew many people there; he had joined the Kuomintang left, and at the time of the April 12 coup the Kuomintang arrested him and then released him on bail through Kuomintang connections.
I first met Bao Junfu in the spring of 1925, introduced by a comrade, to learn Japanese from him. After the Spring Festival of 1928, when the peasant uprising I had organized in Zhejiang had failed and I had come back to Shanghai, I had nowhere to stay and lodged for a time at Bao's house. Yang Jianhong was a frequent caller there in those days, and Bao told me about him and asked me what he should do. I said it was a good thing to keep the connection up. Now and then Bao passed me news from the enemy's side. He knew I was a Communist, and when Yang came he had me keep out of the way.
Whenever Yang Jianhong meant to arrest someone he told Bao, and sometimes took Bao along; when Yang went to the garrison command, the police bureau, and the like, he often took Bao with him. Later, when Zhang Daofan and Chen Lifu came to Shanghai, Bao played host to them both, and the ties were close.
One day in April 1928 Bao told me that Yang Jianhong wanted him to do secret-service work for the Kuomintang. He wanted a Kuomintang post, but he feared that without the Communists' help he would get nothing done and might even be in danger of his life. He asked me to talk it over with the Communist Party and see what would be best, and he handed me a wanted list he was carrying, as a sign that he was willing to pass on to the Communists, and promptly, whatever he learned.
I was then under the Jiangsu provincial committee, and the committee had me write a report to the Center explaining that here was a connection that could be used. Before long Comrade Chen Geng came to talk it over with me; I set out Bao's situation in detail and judged that the man was on the whole inclined toward our Party and could be used. After Chen Geng reported to Comrade Zhou Enlai, it was soon settled that the connection would be used, that I would be moved to the Central Special Branch and cut off from every other department, and that Chen Geng would be my sole contact, responsible for the liaison with Bao Junfu. From then on Bao Junfu, under Chen Geng's direct leadership, became the first counter-espionage connection our Party planted inside the enemy's secret service.
Because Bao was forever coming and going with Zhang Daofan and Yang Jianhong at the police bureau and the concession stations, he needed a motorcar, and we met his request. We also gave him a bodyguard, one Lian Desheng, formerly a tram-company conductor who had shown great courage during the May Thirtieth movement; he had since been to the Soviet Union and, on his return, been posted to the Central Special Branch. Lian Desheng was a Party member, and so in effect the liaison between the Party organization and Bao. He went about with Bao every day, some matters were left to Lian Desheng to arrange, and I moved out of Bao Junfu's house.
In July or August 1928 Yang Jianhong, caught up in the embezzlement of a large sum, took his own life. Chiang Kai-shek formally named Bao Junfu the Shanghai special commissioner of the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, under the alias Yang Dengying, and he became the head of the Kuomintang's secret service in Shanghai.
Once Bao was special commissioner, we used his position on the one hand to plant our people inside the Kuomintang's police bureau, detective squads, and military police, and on the other did all we could to shore up Bao's standing in the Kuomintang. Certain traitors we had Bao denounce as false defectors still "in league" with the Communists, and so used the Kuomintang's own hand to be rid of them. Sometimes we deliberately set up dummy offices for the enemy to raid, and by such means won Bao the Kuomintang's trust.
Bao ranged widely. Lampson, the detective inspector of the British concession police, took him for an expert and was on excellent terms with him: he told Bao everything, consulted him on every knotty matter, let Bao see the British police files, and sometimes took him along when arrests were made. Bao reported all of it to us, and so we had, in the main, a grip on what the British police were doing.
Between 1928 and 1931 Bao Junfu did a good deal of work for our Party. When Ren Bishi was arrested in 1930, for instance, we worked through Bao to get him out: Bao told the British police that Ren Bishi was one of his own men, and Ren Bishi was soon released. When Guan Xiangying was arrested, a great many secret documents were seized with him; we had Liu Ding pose as an expert to judge whether the papers were genuine, and by Liu Ding's "appraisal" we recovered every important one. Guan Xiangying too was released before long.
After Gu Shunzhang's defection the enemy learned of Bao's tie to us, and at the end of April 1931 the Kuomintang arrested Bao Junfu; but he said nothing. In 1933, after the January 28 affair, the Kuomintang let Bao out, and from 1934 he served as deputy director of the Kuomintang government's Reflection Institute; later still he took a post in Wang Jingwei's puppet government.
When Nanjing was liberated Bao was keeping a street stall and living in hard straits. During the Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries in 1951 he was taken in; after Zhou Enlai and Chen Geng looked into the matter he was released, and, having no means of support, was given a monthly allowance by the Nanjing public security bureau. He died of illness early in 1970.
#### (2) A Letter to the Central Organization Department and the Ministry of State Security
To the Central Organization Department and the Ministry of State Security:
Since February 1979, when I brought to light how Kang Sheng had framed and killed four comrades who had once worked in the Shanghai Central Special Branch (there were more victims; I know of only four), the families of two of them have written to me. They report that some Party committees are now looking into and settling the cases, while others have made no inquiry at all to this day, let alone reached a finding. Ten years have passed since Kang Sheng's crimes were brought to light. For the sake of comrades already dead, a fair conclusion that squares with the facts must be reached: this is the dearest wish and the greatest comfort of the murdered comrades and their families, it is a duty the Party owes, and it is an important lesson for the young of today. A dead comrade's family cannot know the details of his work and activity; the revolutionary history of such a comrade should therefore be investigated jointly by the local county, prefectural, or provincial Party-history office and the family, and a formal finding written. Where a family is in real hardship, it should also be helped. For the local county, prefectural, or provincial Party-history office to undertake such an inquiry, the Central Organization Department must issue a notice; without one the local Party-history office is loath to take the task on. I ask that the above suggestions be given your consideration. I extend
my respects!
Chen Yangshan, June 14, 1988
Brief accounts of the four comrades, appended:
Wu Hujing (originally named Wu Huairang), from Meng county in Henan. He joined the Party very early, went to the Soviet Union to study in 1924, and after returning in 1928 held leading posts in Shandong, Tangshan, Tianjin, the Northeast, and elsewhere. In 1933 he directed the Central Special Branch under the Shanghai Central Bureau, and in the spring of 1935 he went to the Soviet Union with his wife, Hou Zhi.
On June 15, 1957, the Center posthumously recognized him as a revolutionary martyr, but issued no martyr's certificate.
He Changchi (He Changzhi), from Lianhua county in Jiangxi, worked in the Shanghai Central Special Branch in 1933, was one of the three members of the Provisional Central Bureau's group of three in the spring of 1935, and went to the Soviet Union in September 1935.
Xiao Shouhuang, from Dapu county in Guangdong, worked in the Central Special Branch from 1932 to 1935, and in September 1935 went to the Soviet Union with his wife, Su Mei (a younger sister of Cao Yi'ou).
Ouyang Xin, from Hubei, had studied artillery in the Soviet Union before 1929; after returning that year he worked in the Central Special Branch, and in the spring of 1935 went to the Soviet Union.

*The author's eldest son, Cheng Jianyu, appended this note.* In 1988 my father was preparing to write up his years of clandestine struggle in Wuhan, Shanghai, Tianjin, Chongqing, Chengdu, Xi'an, and other places. He drafted this Outline for a Memoir. As I understand it, items 1 to 17 were to cover his fighting years in the intelligence section of the Central Special Branch in Shanghai; 18 and 19, the great wrecking of the work in the White areas under Wang Ming's "Left" line; 20 to 23, his intelligence and united-front work in Chongqing and Chengdu; 24 to 30, his fighting years at the Xi'an intelligence station; 31 to 33, his work and study at the Central Department of Social Affairs and the Central Party School in Yan'an; and 34 to 36, his direction of intelligence and public-security work in the Jin-Sui and Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia areas during the War of Liberation. In the summer of 1989 his lung cancer suddenly worsened, the malignancy spread, and he died. He left only this outline, and could not, as he had wished, leave behind that piece of history.
#### (4) His Last Instructions to His Children
1. Heed what the leading comrades say, accept your assignment, and give your mind to the work.
2. In your work, talk things over with your comrades; do not cling stubbornly to your own view.
3. Take full responsibility for what you do; do not merely go through the motions; see the task done.
4. Do not quarrel with anyone or abuse them, and never strike them.
5. Do not look down on others, or talk of them idly, or mock them.
6. When you see another's strong point, learn from it.
7. When you see another's failing, look to yourself.
8. Keep strictly to the rules and regulations of the work, and never break them.
9. Keep discipline strictly, and do not take up smoking or drinking at home.
10. Do not meddle in others' affairs, but govern yourself well.
11. Be large-minded; do not measure your abilities against others' or fall to arguing.
12. In household matters too, act by agreement; do not decide everything on your own.
13. From now on the family must live in harmony, helping one another, forgiving one another, cherishing one another, with no more quarreling, no more abuse, no more blows.
