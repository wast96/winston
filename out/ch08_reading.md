## The Thief of the Capital
Dew had settled on the bush-clover of Makuzugahara.
Around the time such greetings were passing at the crossroads, the fire that sees the spirits off burned on the crown of Nyoigatake, and the autumn of Tenshō 19 came on.
Among the spirits sent away that year was Tsurumatsu, Hideyoshi's heir, who had entered the death-register at the end of summer. Because the boy was the one child he had got after passing fifty, Hideyoshi's face aged all at once. The moment Tsurumatsu breathed his last he had rushed to Tōfuku-ji and shorn off his own topknot there and then; and as he cut it, so they said, he wept aloud without shame before others. And since the assembled lords, great and small, could hardly stand by with folded hands and watch the Regent grieve, the moment Ishida Jibu-no-shōyū set the little blade of his kozuka to his own topknot they fell over one another to cut off theirs, until an untimely mound of shorn hair rose in the hall — or so the town told it, with a certain relish for the comedy of the thing.
Hideyoshi failed before one's eyes. The keen edge of the man who in Tenshō 10 had wheeled his army back from Himeji and staked his whole fortune on the field of Yamazaki was gone now, gone with Tsurumatsu. Even in the middle of talking with someone, if his mind came round to the dead boy his eyes would redden and brim on the instant.
Over Hideyoshi's grief it was the lords from Jibu-no-shōyū Mitsunari on down who put on the comic show of flattery; but the captain who fawned far more vilely than any topknot-cutting was Katō Higo-no-kami Kiyomasa. Granted audience at the Jurakudai, edging his big soldier's knees forward, he said:
"If your sorrow drags on and on, it'll do your body no good. There's nothing like war for driving grief out. Give the order for the crossing into Korea, and give it soon; it'll take Your Highness's mind off the matter, one way or another."
Kiyomasa knew that almost every captain under Hideyoshi secretly wanted no part of a war in Korea. These were men who had spent the long Warring-States years in nothing but fighting; that they should want, at last, to settle in peace into the rank and the domains they had finally come by was only human. Knowing it, he pressed the pointless game of fire on Hideyoshi all the same. Later, at Sekigahara, Kiyomasa would go over to the daimyō of the Tokugawa side and take his part in bringing the house of Toyotomi down; whether, before he died, he ever saw that the failure of the Korean war he had egged on here was the distant root of his old master's ruin, there is no telling.
After Tsurumatsu's death Hideyoshi flung himself into the preparations for the Korean expedition like a man possessed. As they went forward, the traffic of people between the capital and Ōsaka grew frantic, and even farmers and townsfolk with no stake in any war were swept into the general clamour. Such was the autumn of Tenshō 19.
Beneath the tiled roofs of a capital seething with war-preparations, Kuroami went about his own work, quietly and without pause.
His twenty rappa agents had grown by now to close on thirty, counting Kumobei and the rest. These men went disguised in every trade of the city — peddler, mendicant conjuror, showman, shop-clerk, beggar — and took what chances came to drive the talk of the streets against the Toyotomi government.
And that was not all.
To make a mockery of Hideyoshi's public peace, they had begun to work as thieves in the very shadow of the Jurakudai.
They slipped into the mansions of daimyō and rich merchants and carried off gold, blades, tea-vessels and the like. Kuroami had never been a covetous man; but whether it was simply that his nature was cut out for thieving, he threw a strange, fierce zeal into the work. The very act of breaking a guard and lifting the goods seemed to give him a tingling pleasure.
Among the thirty-eight yin-yang arts of the shinobi there is the art of thieving. The tools for it alone come to a great number — the large kunai, the toigaki, the shikoro saw, the knotted ladder, the floating bridge, the listening-iron, the caltrop, the underwater sounder, the underwater visor, the treading-float, the flying ladder, the cloud ladder, the hook ladder, the rolled ladder, the spider ladder, the probing hook, the nested hook, the pick-tweezers, the ganjiki, the burglar's nail-puller — though of course one never uses them all. How they are handled, according to the plan of the house entered and the state of its watch, is what tells the skilled hand from the poor one. At thieving, Kuroami's skill was of the first order.
"Hard at it, are you. Tonight as well?"
Jūzō, tapping polishing-powder along his sword-blade, looked at Kuroami with something close to disbelief. At his side Kuroami was busily setting his shinobi tools in order, getting himself ready to go out.
"It is the one pleasure left to me, you see."
"You overdo it a little, I think."
He said it looking at the great sheaf of swords heaped in the alcove. Carelessly stacked as they were, every one was the property of some daimyō, and more than a few were famous named blades known through all the land.
"I have grown old, you see. Allow an old man his small indulgence."
"It's no great indulgence. The daimyō mansions of the capital are badly frightened, they tell me. They keep it hushed and hidden, taking it for a warrior's disgrace, but sooner or later it's bound to reach the streets. When the heirlooms of great houses are stolen away one after another down the row, the Regent's authority of arms comes in for contempt as a matter of course. Indulge yourself all you please; it does no harm. Where are you bound tonight?"
"What of the Kaga mansion in Shimochōjamachi?"
"That house has a famous tea-bowl, has it not. What was it called?"
"'Withered Leaf,' sir. If I've it right, Hosokawa Etchū offered two thousand gold pieces for it and was refused."
"You are in rare good humour tonight, Master Jūzō. Did something pleasant come your way in the daytime?"
"That one I shall keep for myself. I've a mind to pour a little unstrained saké in it. The monk Rikyū said, so they tell me: a thousand-gold steed tethered at a thatched hut — there's true wabi. A tea-bowl does better in a rappa's hands than a daimyō's. It comes alive."
"…"
Kuroami tied to his waist the cord of the ring-shaped kurorogaki he used for opening the big padlocks, and rose with a face that had no more curiosity to ask. Nothing pleasant had happened, in truth.
And yet the reason Jūzō had turned a shade talkative may, oddly enough, have lain in this: in the daytime he had met Kisaru.
"Well, now. If it isn't Kisaru."
He had left the safe-house behind the Hōkō-ji and come near Yamato-kōji when Kisaru, got up as a townsman's daughter, stepped without warning from a bamboo thicket at the roadside.
"Oh — Master Jūzō."
"It has been since we parted at Kasagi."
He looked at her face. She turned her black eyes away. Faint shadows had gathered beneath them. Without asking, he felt he could read the kind of life the woman was living now.
"In there?"
Jūzō pointed at the thicket. Kisaru looked up at him from under her brows and shook her head. Afraid, perhaps, of having her lodging found out: she could not keep the alarm out of her eyes.
"Have you knifed Gohei?"
Jūzō laughed and bent close, as though to look into her eyes.
"N— no."
"And why not? Gohei's treachery is plain enough. He has made himself the magistrate's hound. That's the very man you swore to your own father, in so many words, that you'd kill."
"Mm."
"Well?"
"I can't."
"Why can you not?"
Kisaru opened her black eyes wide and said:
"If you want him knifed so badly, Master Jūzō, knife him yourself."
"Ha, ha — that's only a manner of speaking. From the look of things, have you and Gohei become man and wife?"
"…"
"Where are you living? Is Gohei good to you?"
Into the eyes she had raised to him the tears started all at once, and—
"Don't."
A hand shot out and struck Jūzō in the chest. Then she spun and turned her back on him. Feeling something at the end of its tether in her manner, Jūzō, hard as he was, let the smile go from his cheek.
Toward the master's daughter Jūzō felt the protective affection of an elder brother.
"You aren't being deceived, are you. By Gohei."
"Even so — … he said he would make me his wife."
"You may seem clever, but you're still a child in years. A Gohei who hides his own rappa birth — do you truly believe a man like that would take a rappa's daughter to wife? It's a trick, no more, to get his hands on your body. When the daughter of Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, the Iga rappa, lets a man gull her, there's no talking to her. Should you not be the one deceiving him, rather?"
Just then the face of Kohagi rose unbidden before Jūzō, and all at once he burst out laughing to himself. Here he was lecturing Kisaru, when he himself was being thoroughly fooled by Kohagi.
"What are you laughing at?"
"Nothing — a matter of my own. This Jūzō too is being fooled by a woman, one called Kohagi. There was a moment, a near thing, when she all but had my throat. You might take a lesson or two from her."
"Where is she — this woman Kohagi?"
Into Kisaru's eyes a jealousy flared all at once, with a blue light. There is no reading a woman's heart. Until now she had been sunk in doubt of Gohei's love; now she pressed at Jūzō as though to reproach him for a change of heart. Perhaps she wanted every man about her kept to herself alone.
(A woman quick to love.)
The thought was scarcely formed when Jūzō hit on a scheme: to set Kisaru's jealousy to work and so bring Kohagi's game out into the light.
"Komatsudani. Ask for the villa of the Ōkurakyō-Hōin of Sakai, and you'll find it."
"And what are you to her, Master Jūzō?"
"We have done the usual things a man and a woman do."
"I hate you!"
She spun her back on him, and the gaudy kosode, bright as flame, went running off into the thicket.
That was the whole of the meeting. And yet that much had left Jūzō's spirits oddly bright. Kuroami had teased him for a talkativeness that was not like him; but the sharp old man had perhaps already guessed at something.
When he had finished making ready,
"Well, then — I'm off."
He gave Jūzō a slight bow and quietly slid the fusuma open.
Out in the night streets, Kuroami did not take the road toward the Kaga mansion, but went straight north up Nishinotōin.
From the first he had had no intention of going to the Kaga mansion.
He meant to deal with Kazama Gohei.
Of this, Jūzō knew nothing.
And Kuroami thought it just as well he did not. Jūzō, as the jōnin, need only bend his mind to the one work of shortening Hideyoshi's life; the plots that served it, the killing of those who stood in the way, the arson and the thieving — all of that, Kuroami held, was work that he, the genin under him, must see done.
When he had come to the foot of the mud wall of Gohei's mansion, he pressed into the shadow of a rain-barrel to feel out what stirred within. The insects were shrill. Somewhere inside there was a small pond, perhaps, for he caught the voice of far-off frogs.
Still hidden, Kuroami picked apart the various insect-voices that welled up shrilling all around him, building a map of sound in his head, and tried to lift from that picture of sound the placement of the buildings within and the presence of any man.
A short half-hour went by.
And then Kuroami's face turned pale.
On the far side of the wall the insects had, all at once, fallen still. From some way off the insect-voices died stretch by stretch, and in that instant a figure settled, light as a feather, onto the roof of the wall right before Kuroami's eyes. A man in a crimson hood and crimson shinobi garb, a sword slung whole across his back as Kuroami wore his, swept the ground about him with a quick glance and dropped to the road. The moment he landed, he ran, like the wind.
Kuroami went after him. Both ran in that peculiar way, bodies turned sideways, scuttling like crabs. One look was enough to know the other for an adept of the Iga school.
(It's Gohei. There's no adept so fine as Gohei, save Gohei himself.)
When they had come near the mud wall of a certain daimyō's mansion, the shadow of Gohei running ahead sprang into the air with a force that seemed to crack the night, and was gone inside the grounds.
So fine was it that for a while Kuroami stood rooted, dumbstruck.
(Ah — such command of the body; even Master Jūzō couldn't match it.)
Kuroami slipped the sword from his back and hooked a toe against the guard. But he thought better of it. Better to keep to cover, outside.
(— And yet, why this house?)
Kuroami cocked his head.
The mansion was not especially grand. Gate, walls, the whole plan of it had a womanish air. Searching his memory, he placed it: the residence of Kanamori Hida-no-kami Yoshishige, a man known as a collector of tea-vessels.
(So that's it.)
His doubt cleared. He was not the only one prowling the capital and plundering daimyō. Like the fire set to the tea-pavilion at the Jurakudai, this was just the sort of contrivance one would look for from Kazama Gohei.
His guess was that Gohei meant, from the shadows, to help along the work of Jūzō's band, throw the world into thorough uproar, and then, waiting for the affair to swell, cast his net.
But there was nothing in it to be amazed at.
Within this house, he would turn Gohei into a corpse.
If a corpse in shinobi dress were found to be, in truth, a retainer of Maeda Gen'i, the Kyoto magistrate, the realm would stare aghast. The whole crime of the daimyō-robberies would come to rest on the Kyoto magistracy, and Kuroami's design of troubling the capital would grow more delicious still.
As Kuroami drew a short bow from his back and set an arrow to it, a cruel smile came to his gaunt cheek. In a straight passage of swords it would be he, rather, who was cut down by Gohei.
But the head of the arrow Kuroami had nocked was thickly smeared with poison, enough to end a man's life even where the vital point was missed.
Kuroami sprang across and hid himself in the four-legged gate of a temple that stood next to the Kanamori mansion, a lane's width away.
A shinobi never sets his line of retreat to windward. From some one point along the wall before Kuroami's eyes, Gohei was bound to show himself.
Kuroami drew the string a little, to test it.
A mist had begun to flow.
Over the top of the wall a mist, white even to the night-eye, began to waver. The line that parted wall from night sky was no longer clear.
Kuroami clicked his tongue.
(A lucky man.)
Straining his eyes, Kuroami fixed them on the dark sky a little above the wall. In the dark of night one seizes the outline of a thing more surely by dropping one's gaze to what lies around it than by looking at the thing itself.
The wind pushed the drifting mist right up to Kuroami's face.
There was a smell to it.
And now, at last, Kuroami understood. He had been caught off his guard. This mist — was it not signal-smoke, laid by a shinobi? A chill went through him, as though water had been dashed down his spine. He cast his eyes about him. Somewhere near, Gohei was.
Far off, at some distant crossroads, a dog barked.
Kuroami hunched his back and ran toward the sound.
(He has slipped me.)
In the dog's direction was Gohei.
Gohei, it seemed, had already known Kuroami lay in wait, had laid the smoke and stolen away under its cover. On the way, past all doubt, some stray dog had barked at him.
(Then shall I wait at Gohei's own house?)
Kuroami turned it over; but Gohei, most likely, would foresee that very move and keep away from home.
(A fearsome man.)
Kuroami felt a fresh shudder run through him at the sheer excellence of Gohei's art. Out on the streets now, he had changed back into a townsman's dress: the honest whetstone-master Iseya Kahei, tottering along, keeping to the shelter of the eaves.
But the contest with Gohei was not yet over.
By the time he reached the neighbourhood of the great bridge at Shijō, the night had lifted clean away. The clouds hung low over the downstream reach, heavy perhaps with rain, and this morning of all mornings the babble of the river came oddly loud. There at the foot of the bridge, in the fullness of the rapids' sound, a lone samurai stood with his arms folded.
"Kuroami."
It was Gohei.
He had lain in wait. He twisted his thin lips into something like a smile.
"A fine welcome you laid on for me, back there."
Kuroami braced himself. He could flee the spot. But Gohei had gone out of his way to choose the daylight. If Gohei had any thought of revenge in him — darkness would be one thing, but in this brightness it was plain he would be cut down before he had run half a chō.
Under his whetstone-master's disguise Kuroami swiftly loosed the cord of the sword-box he carried under one arm, and—
"You mean to have at it, Gohei. Here?"
"Need you ask? When a man's life is hunted by some petty genin, the anger won't sit still in his belly. Come — a little closer."
"As you see, I'm got up as a townsman. Kill me, and it stains your standing. The sparrows of the capital will be at it again — that a magistrate's man not only turns night-robber but earns his keep cutting men down at the crossroads besides."
"Be still."
Gohei stepped in and drew in one motion. Springing back, Kuroami let the sword-box fall. In his hand already was a shinobi blade, a full shaku and a half of it, bared.
"Gohei. This makes a poor picture. Two men of Iga — people will wonder what they can be quarrelling over."
"I'm no man of Iga."
"That stance — anyone with eyes to see would know it."
"You will be dead before they see."
"Heh. It won't go so easily. Come, then, as you please."
"Hah!"
Gohei sprang, uncannily high. In desperation Kuroami swept up the blade as it fell toward him. The shock of it broke his own body wretchedly out of true, and he pitched forward, tumbling several steps. Without righting his sprawl he fled on, low, licking the ground.
"Missed him, did I."
Nor did Gohei give chase. He had already caught sight of the figures gathering here and there at a distance to watch. Swiftly he sheathed the naked blade, and with an air of perfect innocence crossed the bridge to the east. To the onlookers' far-off eyes the whole of it must have been the business of a single instant.
"Master Jūzō."
"Mm."
Jūzō woke in the annex behind the Hōkō-ji. Kuroami was sitting at his pillow.
"Back, are you. Was the tea-bowl there, then?"
"I didn't go to the Kaga mansion."
"What were you about?"
"I spent the night after Master Kazama, meaning to finish him."
"Gohei?"
"He is beyond me. A shallow fellow, but his art was of a wholly different order from mine."
"Of course it was. That you came off with your life is luck enough and to spare. You took no hurt?"
"A little, at the wrist."
As he said it, Kuroami — a man of little expression — let slip a bashful smile, rare in him.
From his right sleeve showed a white bandage-cloth. Faintly the blood had seeped through it.
Turning his eyes from the blood, Jūzō spoke as though spitting the words out.
"Gohei I'll cut down myself. From now on, do a presumptuous thing like this and I won't forgive it."
He wanted to husband the years yet left to this Kuroami, who had served the house of Tsuzura since his father's day.
"You take my meaning?"
At Jūzō's pressing, Kuroami raised his voice a further pitch.
"That I can't grant. Nor is it a thing you're in any position to press on me. If it comes to that, why did you not cut him down long since? A rappa who keeps the common mercies of the world is no rappa at all. Gohei — every day he lives is a day of harm. Kohagi is the same. You, sir — ten whole years fiddling with the Buddha up on Otogi Pass, and the true rappa temper has gone soft in you, gone clean soft."
Day upon day he had watched Jūzō's lukewarm ways, and it must have galled him to the pit of his belly. He pressed on, with a biting, single-minded doggedness.
"Iga fell ten years ago. As the years pass, a man's grudges thin. But you, sir — you're a man who hadn't only his father but his mother, and his little sister, the Lady Hii, murdered as well. Let that grudge slip, and the dead will find no rest. True, the man who did it was Oda, the Minister of the Right, and he is dead now, gone from this world; but there's Hideyoshi, who took up the realm the Minister left, and the daimyō who follow Hideyoshi, and their creatures under them. As Nobunaga cut down the country-samurai of Iga the way a man mows grass, so you might kill off every last man who serves Hideyoshi's rule and no punishment would fall on you. Still less does the cutting-down of Kazama Gohei, who betrayed Iga and ran to Maeda Gen'i, call for a moment's delay."
"You have a tongue on you."
Jūzō gave a wry smile. In every particular he thought Kuroami right. When he had first set his hand to this work, there had still been in him the proper Iga hatred for the government seated in the capital. But once he had gone to ground in Kyoto, the sense that the age itself had turned came over him strongly. What upheld the Jūzō of now was not grudge but one thing alone: the tremendous ninja's stage of striking down the master of the realm — a stage granted to no man of Iga in all the hundreds of years before. That was why he left the ordinary rappa work of throwing the world into disorder to Kuroami alone and took no relish in it; and the punishing of some petty Iga turncoat had come to lie far indeed from the thing Jūzō now lived for. But knowing that to explain as much would only rouse the old man the more, he said only, offhandedly, as if to soothe him, in a low mutter:
"I'll cut him down. Only — lend me a few days."
"Lend or no lend, that's your affair, not mine. Only leave it be, and it may be you who is cut instead. He, unlike you, sir, is a rappa to the marrow. If he must, he cuts, and gives no quarter. Nor is it only Gohei. Kohagi is just the same."
"Kohagi — her I can't cut, not for a while yet."
He looked at Kuroami and laughed, teasing.
"There's love in it."
"On which side?"
"Hers, most likely. A woman who comes to you in love is hard to cut down."
"Feh. Mighty comfortable talk, that."
Kuroami, half in disgust, turned his face away.
(No help for it. Cut them, then?)
Even as he thought it, Jūzō could not bring his heavy hips to rise.
He was waiting on time. By time he meant the future's perfect moment, where heaven's season and earth's advantage and the surging fullness of his own spirit would cross. At that moment, and only then, he meant to drive in a single line into Hideyoshi's castle and stake the whole grace of his calling as a rappa on one motion. Everything else, all his ordinary days, had begun to feel a burden to him. Gohei, Kohagi — both had come to be no more than play within this time of waiting.
But Kuroami was another matter.
The old man lived within the spirit of the rappa of tradition. He saw human beings as no more than grasses and trees. Rank grass was to be cut without mercy. That very ruthlessness was, to Kuroami, what one might call the pure, unmixed soul of the man of Iga.
Jūzō understood it. If he had brought himself, barely, to make a move, it was because he felt he must set this old rappa's heart at ease.
(Once I've set myself to take Hideyoshi's life, a day for my own death is bound to come to me too.)
It was, so to speak, a kind of excuse of Jūzō's for sending Gohei and Kohagi to their deaths. To take another's life, a man needs so many words for it. And by just the weight of those words, as Kuroami said, the ten years on Otogi Pass had perhaps dulled the rappa's heart in Jūzō.
"I'm going out."
Jūzō picked up his sword and rose.
"Where to?"
"To Kisaru's lodging."
"Ah — to cut down Master Gohei, I take it."
"That I'll leave to the moment, if we meet. There's no hurry. On the turn of the moment, it may be I who am cut."
"Turned timid, have you?"
"Of Gohei, yes. …The man has a deft, quick make to him — reason enough that Master Jirōzaemon once marked him for a son-in-law. It's only natural I should flinch from him."
Out on the Matsubara road, a cool new-autumn wind curled about his feet. It came down off Higashiyama into the streets of the capital.
(This is the thicket, surely.)
As he made to set foot on the little path into the thicket, Jūzō faintly knit his brows.
A ninja of any discipline does not go into a thicket. He mislikes the sound the dead leaves make under his soles, for it rings off the bamboo and carries word of him far.
The path wound. He had come only about halfway when a voice welled up out of the thicket.
"Well, now. A new face today."
Feigning not to hear, Jūzō let the strength go out of his limbs — set so that he could spring free in any direction — and walked briskly on.
"Where are you going?"
He went on, paying no heed.
"Who are you? This is my temple, mind."
At that Jūzō slowed his step a little.
"Give your name. Have you no tongue?"
Still Jūzō walked on in silence.
Unable at last to bear it, the owner of the voice came out of the thicket and followed after him.
It was the Saint of the Bamboo.
Clad in bamboo leaves, he held his curiosity-bright eyes wide with a child's innocence. He seemed to love a visitor above all things.
Jūzō halted, and —
"Is there a house back in there?"
"There is. My temple."
"A temple? A woman should be living there, alone."
"Before that, suppose you gave your name."
"Call it Yamada Saemon, then."
"A lie."
"Is it, now."
"You are Tsuzura Jūzō, the Iga rappa. — Ah, wait."
At some point Jūzō had slipped the little kozuka blade, glinting, from his short-sword. The Saint waved both hands before him and put on a great, stagey show of alarm — yet his face, oddly, was not shaken at all.
"A dangerous fellow. Steady, now, and look well at this face. Have you forgotten it?"
He grinned. When he grinned, it seemed another face altogether looked out from within the first. The lips drew back off the bared teeth, and a chill menace came into his face. From the brow across the right cheek ran the pale-red mark of an old burn, and the scar had gathered the whole side into fine wrinkles that stirred by themselves, like a crawling insect.
"Oh—"
"You know me now? A-ha-ha-ha."
He laughed aloud, up at the sky. The two front teeth had been knocked out on purpose, and back among the teeth showed the wad of cotton he kept there to change his voice.
"Even Gohei didn't know me; small blame to you for taking me for another."
"It's you, Master."
"Just so. You raw young fool."
He lowered himself onto the moss at the roadside and, pointing to the place beside him, ordered Jūzō to sit as well. Jūzō did not move. Standing where he was, he said,
"Why do you carry on such a masquerade?"
"It is no masquerade."
By Jirōzaemon's account, ever since the band of Iga country-samurai was destroyed he had fled to the capital and passed himself off as the resident priest of this ruined temple. For all that, he had gone on living in the house at Shimotsuge in Iga as well. Once every ten days he would vanish from Shimotsuge and run to the capital. So that even his own household never once dreamed that the Iga ninja Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon and a wandering monk called Gyōzan — who lived in a ruined temple with neither temple-lands nor parishioners — were one and the same man; and as for the townsfolk of Matsubara in the capital, they had no way of guessing that this eccentric monk, hidden away in his bamboo grove and as wholly withdrawn from the world as an ascetic of Tenjiku, was in his origins one of the ninja who had fled Iga.
But in recent years, worn down by the crowding years, he had come only rarely into the capital.
"So I've not kept it up so well of late."
"Then why have you come this time?"
"It was Kisaru I was anxious for. Rappa though I am, one's own child is dear for all that. I left Iga a step ahead of her, and when I tugged her sleeve at the dry bed of the Shijō river she followed along without knowing me. Since then I've made this ruined temple her lodging."
"I see. — Now, is this a thing I had best not ask?"
"What is it?"
Watching Jirōzaemon in his splendid transformation, Jūzō put the weighty question with an air of the most complete unconcern.
"Since Iga fell, for what reason have you been lying hidden in the capital?"
"Leave it be."
"I should like to hear. And if you won't say — may I guess it for myself?"
"That's your own affair."
The Saint of the Bamboo laughed. Each time he did, the false face cracked and gave back the true face of Jirōzaemon.
When the Iga country-samurai were wiped out by Nobunaga and the Iga ninja were scattered through the provinces, many of them, hating Nobunaga and Hideyoshi, came into the capital to work mischief against the city's peace.
The work Jūzō was now about rode, for its part, on a proper, full-fledged organization of conspiracy; but the ninja who had prowled the capital through the few years around Tenshō 10 had for the most part no organization at all, and used the art of thieving only to keep themselves fed and to feed their grudge. Nearly all of them, it is said, used the art of the Seven Forms.
The Seven Forms is the art of changing into seven shapes — a monk, a yamabushi mountain-ascetic, a komusō flute-mendicant, a mendicant conjuror, a sarugaku player, a merchant, and the plain man of the street — and it went beyond taking on each one's craft and knowledge to the altering of the very features. When a shinobi master conferred his seal of mastery on a disciple, he seems to have prized this art of the Seven Forms above all the rest, and a tale called "the ferry test" has come down.
The river was likely the Iga. At its ferry-crossing there was an old man who had poled the boat a full fifty years, and whose eye for a passenger had naturally grown keen; a ninjutsu master of the neighbourhood would set his disciple to put on each of the Seven Forms in turn and ride the ferry, and let the old man try to see through him. To the disciple who crossed without being caught out, the master gave his seal.
Of the Seven Forms, Jirōzaemon was ablest at the monk. To have lived close on ten years in a temple of the capital not merely unmasked but positively cherished by the common people for his holy eccentricity — that was no ordinary feat.
(To what end?)
Had he worked as a night-thief, then, like the other Iga men in exile?
Jūzō turned it over.
Or perhaps there had been some separate, political reason. It might even be that ever since that last night-raid on Iga he had haunted the capital, stalking Nobunaga and Hideyoshi, and had grown old at last without ever bringing it off.
(A busy, painstaking old man. There's no telling what he may have been about.)
Clad in his bamboo leaves, this small Jirōzaemon perched so neatly before him — looking at him, Jūzō felt something like amusement rise up in him. A ninja, after all, may have a heart unlike an ordinary man's. Let him take a grudge, and he holds to it with the tenacity of some apparition. Since that night, had he too been crossing unseen between Iga and Yamashiro night after night, prowling the streets of the capital like a demon, as Kuroami did now?
And now he had come to be no more than an old father. To watch in secret over Kisaru, he had come out of Iga once again and taken up his life in this bamboo grove.
(And even that — where this man's true purpose lay, there was no telling.)
So he thought; but with an innocent face Jūzō said,
"Is Kisaru keeping well?"
"You met her the other day, on the Matsubara road on the far side of the thicket, did you not?"
Indeed, he knew all about it.
"Do you know Gohei?"
"I don't."
Jirōzaemon told a transparent lie.
"But he must come here often."
"I've never seen him."
"I hear your daughter and Gohei have become intimate."
"They are betrothed. Such things happen."
"They say Gohei has taken service under Maeda Gen'i. He has deserted Iga. Did you not know it?"
"This is the first I hear of it."
"Play the innocent if you like, but keep it within reason."
Jūzō raised his voice and made a threat of it. There was no telling what this old man was thinking, what he might be plotting. Even his hiding in this bamboo-grove temple to watch over the streets of the capital might not be the simple matter of "protecting Kisaru" that he gave out. And toward Gohei, too, he was oddly indulgent: knowing full well that Gohei was intimate with Kisaru, he would not so much as raise a fuss.
Come to think of it, a rappa could never have had any constancy to begin with. Jirōzaemon himself, who had sent Jūzō to the capital, might — if the wind shifted — be Jūzō's enemy by tomorrow. As things stood, this man Jirōzaemon was not one to be trusted an inch, to judge by his past doings.
(There's no end to it once one begins to doubt; but might not this fellow, of all people, have gone over to Gohei's side? At that Gohei is adept. There's no knowing what glib tongue he used to lure this greedy old man in.)
If that were so, then it was Jūzō himself who was the real dupe. The work he was now throwing himself into for Imai Sōkyū — trace it to its root, and was it not by Jirōzaemon's own command? By sending Jūzō from Iga to the capital, Jirōzaemon had already taken his fill of gold for it. But if he thought to take yet more on top of that, he had only to turn about and throw in with the side that would seize Jūzō and the whole party of Sōkyū. Surely not, Jūzō thought; and yet, if Jirōzaemon did go over to the enemy, he meant to cut the old man down without mercy. Jūzō himself, to see his present work through, would weigh no bond of duty against it. That too was the spirit of the man of Iga, taught him by this very Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon.
"Master. Let me tell you one thing."
"What is it?"
"Try anything strange, and your life is forfeit."
"What are you saying? If you've a reason, give it."
"The reason I don't know myself. I only serve you notice."
Jūzō left Jirōzaemon and went slowly into the depths of the thicket.
There was a temple. A hermitage, one might as well call it.
(This is the place.)
He opened the door of the small house that stood off by itself. There was a little earthen-floored entry, and then at once the boarded floor. The building had plainly seen many years; catching the light from the shōji, the boards floated an amber-coloured sheen up out of the half-dark. Stepping up and sliding one more shōji aside, he was in Kisaru's room.
Jūzō did not so much as ask leave. Without warning he slid the screen lightly open.
Kisaru sat bent demurely over a piece of sewing in her hands; she raised her round eyes.
"Oh — Master Jūzō."
"What are you sewing?"
"A kosode."
"Very fine."
Jūzō sat down. There was warmth in the round straw cushion. A faint smell of a man hung about the room. In the interval, no doubt, she had sent Gohei away. And if so—
(Fishier and fishier.)
— thought Jūzō, and said,
"Kisaru. Do you know there's a man in the thicket out there?"
"You mean the Saint of the Bamboo?"
Kisaru asked it back with a look one could only call the picture of innocence.
"Who would that be? I know of none."
"Come a little closer, here."
"Like this?"
As Kisaru shuffled forward on her knees to nestle beside him, Jūzō, without warning, seized her by the wrist and gripped it hard.
"It grieves me, but I am going to hurt you."
"…………"
Held fast, Kisaru watched him with a puzzled air. Jūzō's fingers, clamping the bones of her wrist, grew slowly stronger.
"Does it hurt?"
"It hurts."
"Then tell me. Keep no needless secrets from Jūzō. You know, surely — who that man is."
"What I don't know, I don't know. Why do you chastise me so? Has Kisaru done some wrong—ah, it hurts!"
"I don't know."
"Your hand will shatter."
"Ah—"
Kisaru writhed. Pinching her two wrists together and holding them so, Jūzō read her face with cold eyes. Under his stabbing gaze she strove with all her might to keep her look calm; but behind the effort it seemed plain that she knew the answer he was asking.
"Father and daughter, alike as two peas. If you'll play the innocent, I shall tell you myself. That man is your own father, Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon."
"…………"
"Surprised, are you? Why lie to this Jūzō?"
"…………"
"Gulled by Gohei, have you begun again — the two of you, father and daughter — to scheme together?"
Jūzō let go her hand. To keep from toppling, Kisaru rowed herself upright with both feet. In that moment he caught in her an unlooked-for scent of woman, and felt something falter, secretly, within him.
"One more thing I'll have of you. Gohei was here until just now. Why did you try to keep me from meeting him?"
"Master Jūzō."
"What is it?"
"Trouble yourself no further with our affairs. I beg it of you."
Kisaru said it coldly. Her look, too, seemed to be straining after a particular coldness.
"'Our' — whose affairs do you mean?"
"My father, and Master Gohei, and me."
"Hmm — so that's how it is."
Comrades of his though they were, the men of Iga were apparitions. There was no faith in them of any kind. They had no rule of action but their own gain. To these three there could be no scruple worth the name toward him. It was only that a new job had begun, and nothing more.
So thinking, Jūzō gave a low chuckle to himself.
Laughter was the one thing that could paper the moment over. It was nothing he had not foreseen; yet to hear the betrayal of the three from Kisaru's own lips struck him as a thing to make him wince at himself. Over Kisaru he had allowed himself a little vanity, and just for that, it was a thing to be laughed at. In that one instant Jūzō became a stranger to them. And at the same moment, an enemy. But more exactly — Jūzō had been turned into the fool of the piece.
"Kisaru, I don't say this to harp on it, but your father, too, has grown old. Gohei betrayed Iga out of hunger for the life of a mere thousand or two thousand koku; and your father sells a comrade to make his daughter the wife of a thousand-koku samurai. In either case the one sold is this Jūzō. That I'm sold, I don't mind. Only the price is a shabby one. For a man who is, after all, Tsuzura Jūzō, to be sold for a comrade's little patch of comfort — it'll sound ill among the rappa of every province."
"I beg you — flee the capital. If you throw up this work and vanish, Master Jūzō, the ugly business of selling and not selling is at an end."
"But then Gohei's cherished dream of a thousand koku bursts like a bubble. The master baited one disciple with another to make the first a thousand-koku man; that scheme comes to nothing too."
"Surely not — a scheme, you call it?"
"A scheme it is. True enough, he didn't set me to this work meaning to betray me from the first. His first thought was to profit through Jūzō. But somewhere along the way his mind changed. He judged that to take Jūzō was the better profit. It doubles his gain, besides. Of all the arts of deceit handed down in the land, there can be none so finely wrought as this."
"…………"
"I'm not blaming you. You think only of coming to a woman's happiness, as a woman should. And that's well."
"Flee—"
"A-ha-ha-ha. I won't flee. Tell your father, and Gohei too: Jūzō doesn't change his mind. Let them set about their work of betrayal with easy hearts. — In return—"
"What?"
"I'll show no more mercy. Which of us is the greater in the art — as ninja of Iga — that contest will be a pleasure."
"Master Jūzō."
"Mm?"
"Kisaru will have none of it. It was I who did wrong. I repent that I let myself dream a common woman's dream. At first I didn't know the Saint of the Bamboo was my father, but once I knew, I made my plea to him — about Master Gohei. I begged him to contrive it that I might be Gohei's wife. And that was what made my father sell you, Master Jūzō. The root of it all is Kisaru. I give up Master Gohei now."
"There's no need to give him up. Raised in a ninja house though you were, to give up a woman's happiness for the squabbles of the rappa is to go beyond a woman's place. Think of your own affairs, and none but yours."
"No. I'll go back to Iga now."
"So."
"But in return, I've a request. You said something to me once, Master Jūzō."
"Said what?"
"Take me — have me. Kisaru loves you, Master Jūzō. If I may have your love, Kisaru will wait quietly in Iga. — Master Jūzō."
"What is it?"
"Make Kisaru your wife."
Jūzō looked in silence into Kisaru's eyes. Where the woman's true heart lay, there was no telling. A skylight stood mirrored in the black pupils, and each time she blinked the little window was washed bright and clear again. The rappa's training lets the mind drift as it will and leaves it to answer as it may to whatever the outer world presents. Trained in that discipline from the age of three, Kisaru had likely come to a point where she could no longer grasp even her own true heart. She had become, one might say, a kind of guileless apparition.
"Still your heart and think it over well. Which do you love — Gohei, or Jūzō?"
"I love you, Master Jūzō."
"Then why will you not pledge with Jūzō, while with Gohei you'd — with Gohei you'd."
"…………"
"Or is even that distasteful to you?"
"Mm."
"A hard case."
Jūzō looked up at the woven-lattice ceiling and laughed, lightly. To go on being this apparition's partner, the day would run out. Leaving Kisaru behind in the room, he took up his sandals.
In the sky beyond the thicket silver clouds were welling up. Overhead came a ceaseless whir of wings. Small dragonflies swarmed, and the sky above their wings, brimming with a cold blue light, gave the feel of autumn's fickleness, its quickness to turn.
"I must hurry the work along a little."
With a faint fretfulness, Jūzō picked his way along the path through the thicket.
Where Jirōzaemon had gone there was no sign of him in the thicket.
As the days went by in the capital, everything around him had grown as clamorous as a hatching cloud of mosquitoes. Save for the genin Kuroami, there was no longer a soul he could call an ally.
The one piece of luck was that they had the rappa's cunning. While Jūzō's work was still unripe, they would not lay a hand on his person directly. Their plan, it seemed, was to wait for the very moment before he stole into Hideyoshi's castle, and then to cast their net. For Jūzō's part, just before they cast it he must cut every one of them down.
"A troublesome business."
This was what was in Jūzō's mind. His opponents were, so to speak, a company of goblins. Against an ordinary samurai there would be measures to take; but one was Jūzō's own master, and another was Kazama Gohei, whose art was nimbler than his own. Besides these there was Kisaru, and beyond her the one whose true self was still uncertain, Kohagi. Things being so, it seemed there was nothing for it but to spend his whole strength contesting art against art, and for the rest to await the will of heaven.
"Shall I throw up the capital and flee?"
It was not that the thought never came. But as an Iga country-samurai stripped of his lands, he had nowhere on earth to go. If the one hope of going on living that he had barely won was to kill Hideyoshi, then even should he fail there was nothing for it but to charge headlong at that.
Turning it over, Jūzō's feet carried him into the town of the capital, and as he wandered a sound of song and strings fell suddenly on his ear. Looking about him, he found he had come, without knowing it, to the mouth of Yanagimachi. A quarter of wine and women. As he strolled along with nowhere in particular to be, a voice hailed him down from the upper storey of a brothel. A courtesan, no doubt.
"You there — the samurai passing by."
Jūzō walked on, pretending not to hear.
"Can you not hear me? I say."
Jūzō stopped. He raised his eyes: a courtesan leaning out over the second-floor railing was looking down at him, her palm cupped at the corner of her mouth.
"You want something?"
"I do. One doesn't call out for nothing."
"Say it, then."
"I'll say it — so come up here."
"Say it from where you are."
"The want isn't mine. It's the guest here with me."
"Then let him come down."
"Jūzō."
A white-faced samurai thrust the courtesan aside and looked out, his chest bared. It was Gohei.
The thin lips moved:
"There's saké here. Let me pour you one."
"Mm."
A tension welled through Jūzō's whole frame. For a while he stood rooted; then, his mind made up at last, he bent his big body and went in under the eaves.
Just then two white dogs, tangled together, came racing past along the street.
"Ho-ho-ho, welcome to you."
A courtesan came pattering down the stairs and scattered her smiles toward the doorframe — but,
"Why—?"
The samurai who had surely come in under the eaves was gone.
"Did a man not come in here just now?"
"Who can say."
The two menservants in the entry looked at each other and cocked their heads. A moment before, the two dogs had gone by barking. In the brief span while their attention was on the dogs, Jūzō had slipped in, unhurried, tucked his sandals into his robe, and gone up the stairs. At all times, in every posture, the ninja lives within the art of vanishing. To move himself by seizing on some chance moment was second nature — not to Jūzō alone, but to the whole breed.
"Will you call a courtesan too?"
"No need."
They sat facing each other in the room. Jūzō gave his answer. Seeing it, the courtesan made to raise her voice; Gohei's palm checked her at the outset, and —
"Withdraw a while. I've business to talk over with this man."
The courtesan knit her brows with a suspicious air. Waiting for her footsteps to fade away down the corridor, Gohei said,
"First, drink."
"Drink it yourself."
"There's no poison in it."
"I know that."
"Jūzō. You met the master in that thicket, did you not."
"So you were watching."
"Mm. When I saw you go in, I thought it looked bad and left Kisaru's room. And while I was at it, I did a little eavesdropping in the thicket."
"Sharp as ever, you are. That old man has gone doddering ever since he lost his lands. He was never one to be gulled by the patter of a fellow like Gohei. Pitiful, to think of it."
"Heh. In the old days it was our side that was tricked, so we're quits. I've drawn the master and his daughter neatly over to me — and so, Jūzō."
"What is it?"
"Will you not come in too — onto my side?"
"What—?"
For all his composure, Jūzō looked at Gohei's face with a dubious air.
"It's nothing to be surprised at. Trace it to the root, and you and I were comrades of the same Iga. That we're now split into enemy and ally is, so to speak, a rappa's convenience. But as things stand, I'm cut down by you, or perhaps I cut you down. Do you not think it a needless business? And so — I've thought of a plan."
"…………"
"You go on with your work just as before, and take your gold from your employer. I too keep to the shape of things as they've been. I drive you to the very last inch, and you steal into Hideyoshi's castle. I give chase. At the last, just as it has all been laid out, I run you to the brink of breaking into Hideyoshi's castle — and take you. No — I take you, and round up your employer down to the last of his farthest kin. Well? A fine scheme, is it not? This way neither you nor I comes to any hurt, and the profit stays the same."
"The honour of Iga has fallen low indeed. In the old days, whatever befell, a man never betrayed his own charge."
"Driven from our province, gone to rootless grass, one can't afford such talk. Well? Will you not think it over? — Ah!"
Gohei's body sprang aside on a slant.
"Wh— what are you doing?"
A single kozuka had stitched through the left of Gohei's collar.
"Heh. Missed, did I."
"So this is your answer."
Gohei, his face gone pale, drew the little blade out.
"Well, let's call it that. But you're Gohei true enough — you didn't throw your life away so cheaply."
Jūzō made to draw up his knee and rise, but —
"Wait."
— Gohei checked him with a light hand and pressed himself flat, ear to the tatami. And it was no matter of one man or two. Below the stairs, at the end of the corridor, under the eaves — the breathing of men seemed to fill the house within and without.
"Jūzō — the constables. That woman just now must have grown suspicious and turned informer. She may have taken us for the gang of daimyō-mansion raiders that has the capital in a fright."
"No doubt of it."
"This is no time for laughing. I'm getting away."
"Stay and face them. Let it be known that a Kyoto magistrate's man is a thief, and high and low in the capital will lose their livers for fright."
"Prattle on."
Gohei loosed the sageo cord from his sword, slung the blade across his back, and swiftly wound a white cloth about his face. Jūzō watched it, still seated, and then —
"I'll hold them off here. Take out that ceiling board."
"Mm. — But I owe you no thanks for it."
"I'm only lending you time. Another day I'll have your life."
"If you can take it."
When Gohei put his strength into his hands, he slid smoothly up into the dark hole in the ceiling, and the board closed again as before.
"Sorry to keep you."
The one who came in, changing places with Gohei, was the woman from before. The constables had asked her, no doubt, to come and see how things stood. Seeing Jūzō sitting alone and upright, she said,
"And the other gentleman?"
"Gone to the privy."
"I see."
"Give me a cup."
"Yes."
Jūzō drained, with a sour face, what the woman poured into the cup, and then —
"Was it you who called the constables?"
"Eh?"
"No need to start. Let me have your name."
"Haruzemi."
"And your native province? Your father's name too, while you're about it."
"Kuze village, in Yamashiro. My father is called Jirōsaku. Why do you ask me such things?"
"I mean at least to send something toward the cost of your memorial rites."
"Memorial rites?"
"That you informed, I don't mind. Only, your ill fortune is that you've marked my face. — Now."
"Ah—"
In one motion Jūzō clamped the woman's mouth and pulled her down across his knee.
"The men of Iga are cruel. They count a life no more than a mosquito's. Resign yourself: it was your ill luck to meet me."
He drew his wakizashi, opened the woman's breast, and ran it through as one pins an insect. Making sure the breath had gone out of her, he took out the cloth and wrapped his own face.
Just then several forked catch-poles were thrust into the room. Jūzō kicked off the tatami, sprang up, and vanished from the attackers' field of sight. From his hand a small object fell. He was clinging to the ceiling lattice, gripping it with the fingers of all four limbs. Before the object reached the tatami it burst with a great report, and white smoke filled the room and the corridor. Through the smoke Jūzō, still fixed to the ceiling, glided along like a water-spider skating over water. With each move he made, smoke-balls burst one after another — on the stairs, in the storey below. By the time the fourth smoke-ball burst behind the folding screen of the room he had first been in, Jūzō had already left Yanagimachi and was walking at his ease toward the riverbank.
The first smoke-ball spat up a great gout of fire and licked at the fusuma. By good luck the fire only scorched the ceiling a little and went out; but the rumour spread through the whole city. The black shadow that had gone on terrorizing the mansions of the capital's lords, the Jurakudai first among them, now — with this affair that happened to break out in the town for its occasion — began of a sudden to loom before the citizens of the capital as an uncanny apparition.
"They say he works sorcery."
"And what is more, they say he bears a grudge against the Toyotomi order."
Hearing such rumours, the ones who secretly smiled to themselves were, of course, Gohei and Jūzō. Jūzō had only to set the world seething; Gohei's design was to stand above that and wait for the harvest to fatten. So the affair could be reckoned to be moving, for both of them, in the direction each desired.
The next morning, no sooner had Gohei reported for duty at the magistracy than he was called to the duty-room of the squad-leader, Maekawa Jūrōzaemon.
"Have you heard?"
Knitting the half-grey brows above what looked a testy temper, Jūrōzaemon opened with it straight off.
"Heard what, sir?"
He knew well enough what the man meant.
"The fire at Yanagimachi."
"Ah, that."
"Two men, they say — seemingly the ringleaders of the thieves who have been plundering the daimyō mansions — had the gall to go up into a brothel. Fujimoto Yasubei's squad gave chase, and the men vanished, they say, by some sorcery. It will be shinobi work. I take them for Iga men. What do you say?"
"It need not be Iga in particular. There are ninja in Kōga too. The rappa of Kishū, of Mino, of Kōfu can all manage a little stealth-work."
"It is Iga. None but those men could work an art so intricate in broad daylight. I have said as much to Lord Tokuzen'in, besides."
"And what did His Lordship say?"
"He said to summon you. Make ready at once. I will go with you."
Tokuzen'in was the tonsured Kyoto magistrate, Maeda Gen'i.
Among the lords Hideyoshi raised up, many were of no clear parentage, and this Minbukyō-Hōin was no exception. Only, he was no smith's son or cooper's son, as Kiyomasa and Masanori were: his earlier career had been as abbot of Komatsubara-ji in Owari, so he had at least the shadow of a lineage. He himself claimed descent from the Chinjufu general Toshihito and from the tail-end of the great Saitō house that had once flourished over all Mino — but in sum he was a man who had made his way in the world by one thing, an outstanding wit, and not by birth.
That his temple stood in Owari served the man well. Found early by Nobunaga, he was set to attend the heir, Nobutada; but by common report he had been on close terms with Hideyoshi too, from the days when both were men of small stipend. In those days, so it is said, Hideyoshi spun Gen'i this tall tale — how true it is, who can say. Gen'i went also by the sobriquet Hanmusai.
"Hanmusai."
"What is it? More idle talk?"
"Suppose — only suppose — that I took the realm. What would you want, your reverence? Gold? A temple? Or a domain? Whichever it is, I would grant it."
To say such a thing while Nobunaga still lived was treason, and a man as careful as Hideyoshi would surely never let fall so wild a word; but the teller of the popular tale has Gen'i answer as follows.
"Well now. Long ago, when I had left my province and was training on Mount Hiei, the men of the capital jeered at our country reek at every turn, and I recall it yet with hatred. I had always meant to open the eyes of the capital's arrogant sort one day. So — should you, my lord Chikuzen, by good fortune come to take the realm, will you make me Shoshidai of the capital?"
Strange to say, the jest came true. Hideyoshi became master of the realm, and Gen'i took the post of Kyoto magistrate. This anecdote survives. It would have been not long after he took office. Touring the town on horseback with a few attendants, Gen'i came near Tō-ji. An ox, yoked to a cart, stood blocking the road at its leisure. This man, ordinarily so still that no one could tell what was in his mind, glared at the ox and blazed up in fury.
"But it is only an ox, my lord."
"Do you defy my order?"
He was in such a temper that he looked ready to dismount and cut the ox down with his own hand, so the attendants, having no choice, ringed it round and stabbed it to death. The court nobles and the commoners of the capital were astonished, of course. To surround an ox in that fashion — they might well have set him down for a madman. A man who would cut down even an ox for disobeying: there was no telling what he might do to a human being. It was thanks to this cowering of high and low in the capital, they say, that through all the long turmoil of the Warring-States years the peace of the capital held without a hitch.
This man ate a fief of fifty thousand koku, and stood so high in Hideyoshi's favour as to rank among the Toyotomi Five Commissioners; yet how much loyalty he truly held is doubtful. For it was this same man who, nine years after this, on the very eve of the battle of Sekigahara, was the first to carry word to Ieyasu of the Ishida faction's rising.
Originally he had been a retainer of Nobunaga's heir Nobutada; and when Nobutada took his own life at Nijō castle before Akechi's rebel army, Gen'i had been expressly summoned and charged with what should come after — so deep was the bond. Escape this rout by whatever means; guard and rear the legitimate Oda heir, Sanbōshi, and set him to inherit the world and to avenge the wrong done his forebears — such was Nobutada's dying charge.
By the natural run of things, Maeda Gen'i was surely fated, after the fall of the Oda, to become the mainstay of the house, upholding its orphaned heir Sanbōshi; but his hunger to rise would not let him rest content in that lot. Hideyoshi must have seen through it. By satisfying Gen'i's ambition, he meant, no doubt, to seal in the worm of an Oda restoration. And Gen'i, for his part — though he felt his conscience twinge at the memory of his dead lord's charge — took joy in his own advancement.
When Hideyoshi died and Ieyasu's power began to rise, Gen'i for the first time laid bare his true heart, as though turning his hand over. Hearing that the young lord he was to have reared — Sanbōshi, that is, Oda Hidenobu, Chūnagon of Gifu — had answered Ishida Mitsunari's scheming and thrown in with the Ōsaka side, he sent an urgent messenger to Gifu castle and remonstrated with him thus:
"Be it that the descendants of the House of Oda, though many generations pass, must never turn against the House of Tokugawa. Let the weal and woe of Your House be cast in together with the House of Tokugawa."
This wording not only ignores, and splendidly, the Toyotomi house he had served so long with an outward show of obedience, but seems very nearly to revile Nobunaga himself as the usurper of the Oda. To Gen'i's mind, the rightful successor to the realm after Nobunaga's death was of course the legitimate line, Sanbōshi; and failing him, it was Ieyasu — the one everlasting ally of the Oda house — who ought to take it. That through the years of Hideyoshi's ascendancy he never showed the least sign of any such feeling was no different from the other daimyō beholden to the Oda; and one cannot lay the blame on Gen'i alone.
The tale has run ahead of itself. But this Gen'i, a man of so shrewd and tangled a character, now held the post of Kyoto magistrate — one might call it Superintendent-General of Police. And in the very capital under his own jurisdiction, a phantom thief had begun to run riot, bent on shaking the Toyotomi realm. Probe to the bottom of this man's heart, and it seems the Toyotomi world's coming to the brink of ruin might even have been a thing he would welcome.
Led by Jūrōzaemon, Gohei went up to Gen'i's principal mansion in Shimochōjamachi.
Gen'i, receiving Gohei, leaned wearily against his armrest, his stocky, greasy, blue-dark face set with a great nose whose nostrils flared strangely wide. The thin single-fold lids, cut in a level slit, scarcely moved, as if the man were half asleep.
The moment he saw the two led in, before ever they had come forward to their places, he waved a hand as though brushing off a fly.
"Jūrōza may go."
"Sir?"
"Withdraw."
He kept his gaze fixed through the side window on a wabisuke camellia branch shivering in the wind in the inner garden. Only when he had seen Jūrōzaemon withdraw and slide the fusuma shut did Gen'i draw the mulberry armrest toward him and let his body sag forward, listless.
Behind him a single page waited, and no more. Only, at Gen'i's back hung the vermilion tassel of a hidden guard-recess. In a hall of a hundred mats or so, but three men sat, each turned his own way; yet Gohei knew, by a sixth sense, that within those gold-washed painted partitions several guardsmen lay breathing quietly in wait.
"Shōbei."
"Yes."
Gero Shōbei Yasuji was the name Gohei had assumed on entering service.
"Where was it, now, that you were born?"
"Have you forgotten, my lord? Born in Tosa, a scion of the Ichijō house."
"You need not keep up the fiction."
"Sir?"
"Speak plainly. I will not reproach you for it. What is your native province?"
"It is Tosa."
"That I have heard. I am asking your true native province. Speak."
"It is Tosa."
"Do you take my eyes for knot-holes?"
"My lord—"
"When I took you into my service, I thought I had seen you somewhere. I may have been mistaken. At the least, I recall meeting once a man who carried a scent like yours. For a long while I could not think whom he resembled; but the other day, drinking tea alone and letting my eyes fall idly on the tea-garden path, it came to me of a sudden. It was His Lordship the Minister of the Right — Nobunaga."
"…………"
"The Minister kept a garden-hand. No stipend; only a handful pressed on him now and again. It was his custom to receive the man on the veranda of the tea-room. The fellow would kneel on the packed-earth floor and report all manner of espionage. I sat in on the scene twice and watched. Studying him, I thought: what strange creatures there are in the world."
"…………"
"His face looked to be a man of twenty. And then, in a single blink, it would suddenly cloud over with the age of fifty. It shifted and glittered like the heat-shimmer that burns over a field in early spring — now the kindly face of a Buddha, now the face of a shadow-demon, never still for an instant. After the man had left the garden-path, try as I might to call his features back, in the end I could not. I asked the Minister who he was. 'Do you not know?' he said, and spat it out: 'He is an Iga man.'"
"…………"
"You and that man are as like as two halves of a melon. You were born in Iga, were you not. With what aim did you enter this house as a spy? I will not reproach you, so speak plainly."
"I am overcome, my lord. This 'Gero Shōbei' is a flat lie."
"Who are you?"
"A rappa of Iga, by the name of Kazama Gohei."
Gohei smiled thinly and steeled himself. He had read that Maeda Gen'i seemed to hold some further design toward him. To speak honestly, he judged, would do him no harm; and yet at the same moment he did not forget to gauge the distance between Gen'i and his own place. Before the guards could burst from the hidden recess, he could set a blade at Gen'i's throat — so he reckoned, even as he said:
"But, my lord."
"What is it?"
"I did not steal into your house to spy. Were that so, I would not lay my identity bare so honestly. I have grown sick of the rappa life."
"Oh?"
"The lowliness of being received by one's master only at the edge of the garden became hateful to me past bearing. I wished to enter the samurai's world, where a man may make his name openly by a single feat of his own. That is why I changed my name and hid my origins. But now that you have seen through me, my lord, there is no help for it."
"How do you mean to deal with this Iga man?"
"I shall do nothing."
"Then, as before—"
"No — I shall raise your stipend to three hundred koku and make you a squad-leader. You will have no cause to complain."
"Ah — I am deeply grateful."
"It is nothing without precedent in the world. Lord Tokugawa, I hear, gives high place to an Iga man named Hattori Hanzō."
"The house of Kazama is a branch of the Hattori. Our line is of the Taira, so a rappa is not, when all is said, some root-and-branch churl sprung from the fork of a tree."
"Well said. His Highness the Kampaku himself, traced to his root, came out of a peasant's rush-thatched hovel in Nakamura, in Owari. It is an age of the single arm, the single deed. — But to change the subject—"
"You mean the matter of that thief, I take it."
"Ah, quick to grasp it. That thief is a man of Iga, the same as you. Am I not right?"
"Yes. But…"
"Don't hide it. You are now a proper retainer of the Maeda house. Do not forget that you are no longer of Iga. For you — who even changed your name to cut your ties with Iga — whether you sell your old comrades or not makes not the least difference. It even suits the road of advancement. Tell me whom you suspect."
"With respect, I cannot answer at once."
"As I thought — you cannot yet make yourself wholly a samurai. For the samurai, advancement comes first, by whatever means. Since the old days of the Genji and the Heike, the samurai has held it his first principle to sell his fellows, steal a march, grasp at merit, and guard his own single glory. The rappa is despised for the very reason that he prizes his comrades and does not set himself above them. To become a samurai, think only of your own renown. Well?"
"Yes. …"
"Then let me change the subject. Name me the Iga ninja whose fame has carried even to other provinces."
"There are none."
"What?"
"Do you make sport of me?"
"No, nothing of the kind. — With respect, a ninja of surpassing skill would never commit the blunder of letting his name become known in other provinces. Your common master of arms fights celebrated opponents and hawks his own name about the realm; but the ninja, on the contrary, holds it his first rule that neither his face nor his name be known to any. So the abler a man is at the art, the more surely he is a nobody to the outer provinces. Let name and face once show in the world, and the work of a shinobi becomes impossible."
"I see. The fault is in the way I asked. Who, then, are the men most skilled in the art?"
"Apart from the one they call Tateoka-no-Dōjun, it would be Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon. After them — the lay-priest Jōsai of Fukumori, Ōsu Magodayū, Momochi Den'emon, the lay-priest Shibata Suō; and among the genin, Kozaru of Momochi, Hidari of Ueno, Konan of Kanbe, Otowa-no-Kido, Kotarō of Shindō — number them off like this, and there is no end to it."
"Among them, the one now prowling the capital must, from his work, be a man of quite outstanding skill. Whom do you make him?"
"That is a hard riddle. In any case, among those I have just named there are eight who could kill a man from ten ken off—"
"Hmm."
"—and a hundred, I should say, who could make themselves vanish in a crowd by daylight. Work of that order is nothing to draw notice."
"And you?"
"In that I am a novice."
Gohei smiled a thin smile. That he pointedly left out the names of Tsuzura Jūzō and Kuroami was the doing of the shred of the Iga man still left in him. And besides, Gen'i's question was too green; to answer it in earnest would have been absurd. The ninja names he had reeled off were, for the most part, of men killed in battle, dead of sickness, or gone missing around the time of the Tenshō Iga Rebellion; few of them still lived in Iga. Gohei's honest thought was that, past masters aside, among living ninja who had reached the exquisite heights of the art there was none but himself and Tsuzura Jūzō.
"One more thing I will ask. Is it true that the shinobi of Iga bear a grudge against the reign of His Highness the Kampaku?"
"It is true."
"For what reason?"
"This, my lord, you can scarcely fail to know yourself. In Tenshō 9, when Lord Nobunaga fell upon Iga, he cursed the province, saying that apparitions dwelt there, and wiped out the Iga shinobi as a man cuts grass; and you, my lord, who were close at his side in those days, must know it full well. The grudge of Iga will not readily fade — no, not down to the very grass and trees."
"I do not follow. It is the Kampaku's age now, not his."
"A grudge needs its object. And the Kampaku, who took up Lord Nobunaga's world just as it stood, makes a fit object to vent it upon. Being of an apparition's nature, their tenacity runs deep."
"I hear the rappa long for a Tokugawa realm. Is that so?"
"The affairs of the realm are no concern of a ninja's. But among all the many daimyō of the age, the one the men of Iga revere with a single heart is none but Lord Ieyasu. In Kōji 3, long ago, when the young Lord Ieyasu attacked Uto castle in Nishigōri of Mikawa, seventy-eight Iga ninja, led by the sixteen-year-old Hattori Hanzō, won a hidden feat of arms and received Lord Ieyasu's especial favour; and this we have had told us by Lord Ieyasu himself, from our childhood, until our ears grew calluses with it. At the time of the Tenshō attack on Iga, too, word reached Iga that Lord Ieyasu alone had done his utmost to dissuade Nobunaga; and just after the Honnō-ji Incident, when Lord Ieyasu hurried back from Sakai to Mikawa, two hundred ninja came running from the hills and fields of Iga to guard him, and for that service the Iga ninja were granted a thousand kan. It is Lord Tokugawa alone, they say, who has never despised the ninja but treated them as men; and many of the Iga men store up in their hearts a wish to repay the Kantō's kindness, should trouble ever come."
"And you — what of you?"
"I am no Iga man. — And that, my lord, you said yourself a moment ago."
"Just so. But these thieves, this time — surely they have not behind them some plot to bring on a Tokugawa realm?"
"That I cannot know until I have probed it thoroughly."
"If it were so, it would be a grave matter. I leave the whole direction of it to you. Investigate, and quickly. Understood?"
"Am I to do no more than investigate?"
"A careful man. Do just as the words say."
Gen'i had said probe, but he had not said seize. Having pressed the point, Gohei inwardly wore a pale, cold smile. He had seen how unexpectedly tangled Gen'i's mind was.
(This man may, surprisingly, be wishing the Toyotomi world past as soon as it can be. Is he not secretly leaning toward the Tokugawa?)
It was precisely because Gohei faintly thought so that he had deliberately overstated, for Gen'i's ears, the Iga men's love of the Tokugawa and their wish to trouble the world; and this probe seemed, sure enough, to have struck Gen'i's mark.
"Very well, then."
"Sir."
Gen'i rose. Prostrate, Gohei was aware that the heart of his employer — departing with the rustle of hakama-silk — was now subtly wavering. He is afraid of seizing the thieves blindly. If Ieyasu held one end of the thread of conspiracy behind them, Gen'i's political footing would grow awkward in the time to come. This man foresaw, with a shrewd calculation, that once Hideyoshi's span ran out — or once he fell to an unnatural death — it would be Ieyasu who inherited the world. For now, at all events, from his post as magistrate, he must surely think it needful to keep a foot in either camp.
(This is turning out to be an amusing business.)
Gohei felt his heart leap in secret, and before he knew it a dank, sinister smile had risen to his lips.
