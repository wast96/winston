## Chapter 8. Racing North and South

From the autumn of 1929 to the spring of 1930, the secretary of the Southern Bureau was He Chang, and the person in charge of the Military Commission was Nie Rongzhen; the two of them were close comrades-in-arms who had taken part together in the August First Uprising at Nanchang. Transferred to the Southern Bureau, I was still secretary and confidential courier. My husband and I lived in the same apartment building as Nie Rongzhen and his wife, and we kept no maidservant. Among the people I came to know there was also a doctor named Ke Lin, a Guangdong man who held a proper license as a pediatric specialist and could also handle internal medicine on the side. In Shanghai, the clinic and home he ran were on the third floor, the first floor being an antique shop our underground Party had opened as a cover, and the second floor the place where the Central Political Bureau held its meetings; he often treated the leading comrades of the Central Committee. After being transferred to the Southern Bureau, he still used running a clinic as his professional cover, both treating us and using his clinic as our underground courier liaison station—of course only very few people knew his true identity. He Chang and I had already had him treat our illnesses in Shanghai and so were old acquaintances, which made our work contacts all the more convenient. Another with whom I had work contact was Comrade Li Qiang, who after the founding of New China served as minister of foreign trade; in those days he was in charge of the underground radio work at the Southern Bureau.

After we reached Hong Kong, seeing that I had been in prison and was pregnant and thin and frail, He Chang was especially considerate and caring toward me, doing his best to arrange lighter work for me. In November 1929, when Comrade Deng Xiaoping went, in the capacity of a representative of the CCP Central, to Guangxi to lead the Baise and Longzhou Uprisings, passing through Hong Kong on his way to Nanning, my husband and I and Nie Rongzhen and his wife had several pleasant meetings with him. In her book *My Father Deng Xiaoping*, Maomao gives a detailed account of how, as soon as he reached Hong Kong, Comrade Xiaoping made contact with the Southern Bureau secretary He Chang and the Guangdong Provincial Committee Military Commission secretary Nie Rongzhen. Maomao also mentions how Nie Rongzhen's wife Zhang Ruihua and I cooked supper for Comrade Xiaoping at the Happy Valley residence. When Guangxi held its Party congress, He Chang went to take part in the capacity of Southern Bureau secretary, together with Comrade Xiaoping. A few years ago, when the CCP Guangxi Regional Committee sent someone to interview me, they even checked over with me the draft of He Chang's speech at that Party congress in Guangxi and the alias he had used. Later He Chang worked together with Comrade Xiaoping again in the Central Soviet Area in Jiangxi, but by then I had been kept in Shanghai to work and could not be with He Chang in his work and life.

As it happened, Comrade Ruihua too was pregnant at the time, and we two big-bellied women even jokingly made a "betrothal in the womb," saying that if the two born were both boys (or girls) they would be brothers (or sisters), and if it was one boy and one girl, then let it be a betrothal in the womb. Later she had a daughter and I had a son. The talk of a betrothal in the womb was of course only a joke in fun, for after we parted in Hong Kong we never met again.

After the Great Revolution failed, our Party was in an underground state, and funds for its activities were extremely hard to come by. At that time the Chinese Communist Party was a branch of the Communist International, standing in a relation of superior and subordinate to it, and so at times it could also obtain some funding from the Comintern. In the winter of 1929, the Comintern happened to have a sum of money remitted through the Vietnam branch to the Southern Bureau, to be handed on to the Chinese branch. Because the CCP Central established in Shanghai had at that time not yet set up a financial institution that could receive foreign exchange, it fell to the Southern Bureau to collect it on their behalf in Hong Kong and then send someone to carry it to Shanghai. He Chang assigned this task—one that looked very easy but was in fact of grave importance—to me to accomplish. Big-bellied, I dressed up as a fine lady, put on an overcoat and a hat, and carried a camphorwood trunk, with a comrade to help me carry the trunk, playing my servant, and we boarded the steamship Empress of France. That camphorwood trunk was divided into two layers, the space beneath its false bottom stuffed full of banknotes—even I did not know just how many—while on top of the false bottom were placed some high-grade cosmetics and a Christian Bible, to cope with the opening of the trunk for inspection. But because we had comrades on the wharf to see to things, and I had bought a first-class ticket besides, the luggage I carried was not inspected at all. The comrade playing the servant could of course only travel in the ordinary cabin. That day happened to be December 25 by the solar calendar, the Christian Christmas, and in the evening the captain gave a banquet for all the first-class passengers to keep Christmas merrily; everyone drank champagne and danced together in the dining room. The captain toasted us, and the passengers, though strangers to one another, kept raising their glasses in toasts and good wishes. My own capacity for drink had always been good, and a few glasses of champagne could not make me drunk at all, so I drained each glass as it came, drinking down several in a row, gracious and perfectly composed, without losing my manner in the least, and rousing no one's suspicion. When some of the foreigners traveling to Shanghai came to invite me to dance, I would point at my swelling belly, shake my head, and say to them over and over: "Sorry! Sorry!"—the very picture of a serene fine lady—and so I actually carried it off successfully. When the ship reached the Shanghai wharf, I saw the small motorcar that had come to meet us; the comrade playing the servant loaded the trunk onto the car, we got in and drove to the appointed place, handed over the trunk, and the task could be counted a victorious success. At that time the person in charge of the Party Central's Finance and Economics Commission was Comrade Li Fuchun, and the one in charge of the accounts was Comrade Xiong Jinding.

Come to think of it, this was the only time in my whole life that I traveled first class in such luxury, and it was entirely out of the needs of the work. After I got back to Hong Kong, both the Party Central in Shanghai and the Southern Bureau secretary He Chang praised me for having accomplished this task well, saying that I could stand the test in the enemy's prison, and this time could also dress up as a fine lady and smoothly escort the funds to Shanghai and hand them over to the Central Committee—able to rise to the high and stoop to the low, truly first-rate!

On 3 March 1930, I gave birth in the hospital to my second son, whom I named He Ping. Before I entered the hospital, the Central Committee had already transferred He Chang to be secretary of the Northern Bureau, established in Tianjin, but because my confinement was near he had not mentioned the matter to me. His thinking was that during my pregnancy I had been in prison, had lacked nourishment to begin with, and still had to do the liaison work among the fellow sufferers, so that I had been under great mental strain the whole time and had contracted a severe stomach ailment, and after prison my health had remained very poor. The child too was congenitally weak, very thin and small at birth, and not fit to go with him on the long, hard journey to work at the Northern Bureau. But if he left the two of us, mother and son, in Hong Kong with no one to look after us, he could not set his mind at ease either. So he consulted with Ke Lin and with Lu Biao, who was taking over from him as Southern Bureau secretary. As it happened, Lu Biao and his wife had been married many years, were in their thirties, and had never had children, and He Chang took the initiative to propose giving He Ping to the couple to adopt as their son, which the Lu Biao couple of course accepted very gladly. Besides, there was the pediatric specialist Ke Lin who could help look after him. So when I left the hospital a week later, He Chang wrote the child's name down as Lu Ziping (later changed to Lu Ping). After making a careful consideration of all sides—my health, my future working conditions, and the healthy growth of the child—He Chang, without even time to consult me and ask my opinion, had settled the matter with Lu Biao and the others. At the time both Li Qiang and Ke Lin knew, and I alone was kept in the dark. It was not until we were about to leave Hong Kong, just before boarding the ship, that He Chang told me: "Think about it—your health is poor and you have no milk, so how could you raise the child? For now we're temporarily entrusting the child to others to adopt; besides, the Lu Biao couple are our own comrades and very much want a child, and are sure to treasure him all the more. Handing the child to them, I feel quite at ease, and I hope you can feel at ease too! To regard one's sons and daughters as one's own private property is a petty-bourgeois notion of private ownership. We all belong to the Party, belong to the people, and in the future when he grows up, this son will be both theirs and ours—I even hope he'll come to take over our revolutionary cause!" Things having come to this pass, my objecting was of no use. In my heart I thought: I bore my first son and he was sent back to Wan Xiyan's old home to be raised; now I have just borne my second child, and again he is given to others. Though I could hardly bear to part from the natural feeling between mother and son, I could not shed tears before He Chang, or he would again say I was too swayed by emotion. So although in my heart I could not bear it in the least, out of my mouth I could only say: "All right, since you've already arranged it, then it will just have to be this way!"

He Chang and I returned from Hong Kong to Shanghai and still lodged at the Mengyuan Hotel. Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao, Liu Shaoqi and He Baozhen all came to see us (Shaoqi and He Chang had once worked together at Anyuan, and He Baozhen and I had also once been colleagues in Wuhan). Elder Sister Deng even smilingly criticized He Chang: "Earth Leopard (tubaozi, "country bumpkin"—the nickname the comrades had given He Chang), you mustn't be a male chauvinist! You should look after Mulan more—how could you let her sail on a sea voyage with you before she's even a month out of childbed?" He Chang said: "It's precisely because she's weak after the birth and needs someone at her side to look after her that I waited until she'd had the child to set out together with her. Looking after her myself—isn't that better than having someone else look after her?" At which everyone laughed.

When we reached Tianjin, the seat of the Northern Bureau, I again did the work of secretary and confidential courier. In June 1930, I carried to Shanghai a report He Chang had written to the Party Central on the situation of the Northern Bureau, and lodged at the home of Gong Yinbing. He was one of the comrades in charge of the Party's underground courier work in Shanghai. He and his wife were both my fellow Hunanese, and lived behind the Wing On Company on Nanjing Road. By this time Comrade Enlai had already gone to the Soviet Union to report on the work to the Comintern, and Li Lisan, as head of the Central Propaganda Department and Central secretary-general, was actually presiding over the work of the Central Committee. Li Lisan came in person to Gong Yinbing's home and handed me a confidential document of the Party Central—the resolution of the Central Political Bureau meeting that he had drafted, "The New Revolutionary High Tide and Victory First in One or Several Provinces," which was the program of action for the second "Left" adventurist error that later came to be called the "Li Lisan line." The Party organizations all over the country almost all carried out this resolution. Under the guidance of this erroneous thinking, in a space of only three-odd months, the Party's organizations in various places suffered great losses.

After I carried this resolution drafted by Li Lisan back to Tianjin, I had grave doubts in my heart, feeling it seemed too much of an adventure. But the organizational discipline of the Party is that the lower level obeys the higher and the whole Party obeys the Central Committee; besides, He Chang had known Li Lisan well ever since Anyuan and rather trusted him, so he actively carried out this "Left" adventurist erroneous resolution of Lisan's, and, following the Central Committee's requirements, merged the leading organs of the Communist Party, the Youth League, the labor unions, the peasant associations, and the women's associations all into one "Action Committee," with He Chang as commander-in-chief, preparing to launch an armed insurrection. Unexpectedly, while still in the preparatory stage, it was spotted and discovered by the enemy's tails, and the insurrection thus miscarried. Not only in Tianjin, Beiping, and other places led by the Northern Bureau, but also in other big cities such as Shanghai, Wuhan, and Guangzhou, the armed insurrections that were being prepared all failed one after another. This was the bitter and painful price everyone paid for blind action under the guidance of the "Left" adventurist erroneous thinking, in disregard of the objective reality that the enemy was strong and we were weak.

With the insurrection's failure, the Party's organs were wrecked, and many leading comrades of the Northern Bureau—such as Tianjin municipal committee secretary Peng Zhen, Beiping municipal committee secretary Bo Yibo, and Northern Bureau propaganda department head Chen Fu—were arrested one after another. He Chang and I, on the other hand, had the good fortune to escape danger, because we had gone to Shanghai for a meeting in September 1930. Seeing so many close comrades-in-arms arrested because of his own erroneous leadership, He Chang was very grieved at heart. By this time Comrades Zhou Enlai and Qu Qiubai had returned to China from the Comintern, and they at once convened the enlarged Third Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee in Shanghai, which corrected Li Lisan's "Left" adventurist error. At the meeting Comrade Lisan admitted his own error and responsibility and made a pained self-criticism; He Chang and many other leading comrades from various places who took part in the meeting also all made serious and searching self-criticisms. Li Lisan was removed from his leading post in the Central Committee and transferred to the Soviet Union to study. He Chang too was removed from his post as Northern Bureau secretary and set to study and reflect further. By this time we had moved from Gong Yinbing's home to lodge on the floor above a pickle-and-sauce shop on Seymour Road (now North Shaanxi Road). We went in and out by the back door every day and had no dealings with the boss and clerks of the pickle shop below, so we drew no attention. In those days Comrades Guan Xiangying and Ren Bishi, who had formerly worked with He Chang at the League Central, often came to see He Chang; they too had made self-criticisms at the Third Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, but they were all good comrades of strong Party spirit, all determined to draw the lesson and correct their errors, and in consultation they decided together to request the Party Central to send them down to the Jiangxi Soviet Area to wage guerrilla warfare together with Mao Zedong, resolved to temper and test themselves further in the practice of revolutionary war. But all this I knew nothing of at the time; it was not until the Central Committee had approved that He Chang told me.

In January 1931, under the direct interference and support of Mif, deputy head of the Eastern Department of the Comintern, the "Left" dogmatists headed by Wang Ming, Bo Gu (Qin Bangxian), Luo Fu (Zhang Wentian), and Wang Jiaxiang, who had just returned from Moscow not long before, in an even more "Left" guise and under the banner of opposing "conciliationism," fiercely attacked the Party Central leadership after the Third Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee and forcibly convened the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee. Zhou Enlai, Qu Qiubai, and other Central leaders of real fighting experience and high prestige were severely censured at the meeting; while Wang Ming and others, who had not even been Central Committee members before and lacked practical fighting experience, at one stroke entered the Central Political Bureau, beginning the third "Left" erroneous rule, lasting four years, represented by Wang Ming. In those days I once heard He Chang, Guan Xiangying, and Ren Bishi discussing together, deeply dissatisfied with the erroneous practice of Wang Ming and others in inflicting excessively heavy blows within the Party. Hearing them, I too, blunt and outspoken, mocked Wang Ming with "part from a scholar three days, and you must look at him with fresh eyes." Though everyone felt the same, hemmed in by the Party organization's strict discipline they could only obey. Later Wang Ming opposed Mao Zedong's guerrilla strategy, stripped Mao Zedong of his post as general political commissar of the First Front Red Army, and deprived him of his military leadership; some comrades were also wrongly killed in the broadening of the "elimination of counter-revolutionaries," and Xiyan's cousin Wan Xixian died of injustice at just this time; not a few comrades were investigated and imprisoned, and Comrade Chen Yi too was once confined; still more comrades were pushed aside and struck down, given no suitable work for a very long time (such as Deng Xiaoping and Mao Zetan). Wang Ming's "Left" erroneous line in fact greatly weakened the Party's fighting strength, achieving the wrecking effect that the enemy wanted to bring about but could not. During the fifth counter-campaign against "encirclement and suppression," they again, in disregard of the vast disparity in strength between the enemy and ourselves, fiercely assaulted the enemy's blockhouses and fought the enemy head-on, causing the Red Army to suffer severe losses and be forced to begin the Long March. In the course of the Long March, the Zunyi Conference was held, which ended the rule of "Left" dogmatism in the Party Central and established Comrade Mao Zedong's leading position in the Party Central. The Zunyi Conference was a turning point of life and death in the Party's history, allowing the Red Army and the Communist Party to be preserved under extremely critical circumstances. The victory of the Long March astonished the world, and from then on opened a new phase in which one victory followed another, until at last the victory of liberating all of China and founding New China was won.
