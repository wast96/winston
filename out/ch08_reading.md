## Chapter 8

The car went first to Bai Ping's home. As she closed the door she made me promise to meet her at the Liti Café the next day, and with an incomparable brightness on her face, raised her hand to me.

The night was deep, the overcast sky seemed to hang low, and my car passed beneath the dim street lamps; only now did I feel how much Bai Ping's place at my side had come to mean.

A sharp spring chill and a heavy loneliness came at me from every side as I shut the car door again. Like a fugitive I ran into the house, ran into my own room, turned on the light, lit a cigarette, pulled out a book, and dropped onto the sofa to flee a desolation and oppression past telling. And so the night flowed by like water. Beyond the window the sky slowly brightened. I began to undress, and slipped into the weary bedding.

It was as though I had fallen into the very heart of the clouds. I saw light — the light of stars, the light of the moon — and light in layer upon layer, conjured into winding lines, light conjured into ordered circles, light conjured into a splendour of five colours; dazzled, I swooned, and began to pray, and prayed — darkness, darkness… and where, then, was my lamp?

"The lamp is here." I heard this voice, and then I saw a faint, soft radiance, and I followed it, followed it — out of the clouds, through the mist, to green thickets of trees. In secret joy I found the human world before me: this is our world, the world our forefathers wrought in bitter toil these thousands of years, and how many man-made lights were there to welcome my coming into it! Then I saw a myriad lamps light up all around. I laughed, I began to laugh — but in the midst of my laughter I found that I had stepped into the grave, and I came to see that the lights around me were all corpse-fires; I wanted to fly, I wanted to flee, but how much earth was pressing on me, pressing on me, and I gasped in the struggle.

"The sun is up," someone shouted.

And then I saw the dazzling sunlight.

"The sun is up." It was the voices of the household beyond the window; they were hanging out the washing to dry in the yard.

My watch said one in the afternoon. I threw on my clothes and got up, and just as I was washing, Stephen came.

"Only just up?" he said.

"Yes."

"Which girl was it, then, last night, with radiance enough to keep our bachelor philosopher awake?"

"It was Schelling," I said, pointing to the work of Schelling that I had pulled from the shelf the night before and tossed on the bed after reading.

"Don't tell tales, good friend."

"…" I did not answer him, but only said in a solemn tone: "'Good friend'? And all this while you never told me you were a married man."

"Because you said you were a confirmed bachelor, and I thought you would despise married men."

"Why should I?" I said. "That is each man's own freedom."

"Where in the world is the man, sure of his creed, who does not wish to fold all living souls into it?"

"No," I said. "I wish everyone had a wife as beautiful and worthy of love as yours, so that I might pass happy evenings like last night's again and again."

"I rather fear it was last night's young lady who made the evening happy for you."

"I'd rather not talk of that," I said. "You are a married man — why are you always seeking me out to go about with?"

"It is to prove to you that a married man may keep a bachelor's freedom too."

"Then I conclude you do not love your wife enough."

"Why, I love her to the very utmost," he said. "She has her own world, her own beautiful world; she loves classical music and poetry. I respect her."

"How different, then, she is from you."

"Why should we be the same?" he said, surprised. "I respect her pleasures, and she respects mine. We love each other, we are joined, we respect each other, and we live the happiest of lives."

"To me it is a riddle," I said.

"It is not to be known from a shelfful of philosophy. Only when you have the experience of marriage yourself will you be fit to speak of it."

"…" I made no answer.

"My wife thinks very highly of you," he said. "She hopes you'll come to us often. On Saturday evening dozens of friends are coming for tea, and she hopes you will be sure to join us."

"With great pleasure, of course." When I had changed, I remembered that the night before I had arranged to see Bai Ping today, and so I said:

"Care to come with me to the Liti Café?"

"Is it a first meeting with Mei Yingzi?"

"It is," I lied, with a laugh.

"Truly?" he said. "Then I've guessed it."

"You've guessed it?" I laughed.

"I guess that since yesterday you've become Mei Yingzi's satellite," he said. "But my wife would surely say you've become the satellite of some fixed star I don't know of."

"And who might that be?" I asked.

"She won't tell me; she only says, 'In time you will surely know.'" He said: "But today proves my guess was right."

Stephen's extraordinary good humour lifted my own spirits. We got into the car and drove straight to the Liti Café.

It was then a little past three, and as I had not yet eaten I ordered rather a lot. Stephen kept me company, smoking and drinking coffee, glancing now and then at the window, and suddenly he said:

"What time did you tell her?"

"Only 'the afternoon.'" It came to me suddenly that I had not in fact fixed a time with Bai Ping — but I trusted she would come before long.

But when I had eaten my fill and still Bai Ping had not come, I too began to fret, and had no more heart for chatting with Stephen, whose excitement had by now cooled a little. After a long silence, at about half past four, he suddenly broke into a pleased smile and said:

"Mei Yingzi is giving you a fine little setback."

"It's a trick any woman knows how to play."

"I don't think she'll come," he said. "Better ring Bai Ping."

"No," I said. "I won't do that. When a woman I have waited for has disappointed me, whoever I call in to take her place I insult by the calling."

"But let it be me who calls her, then."

"No," I said. "You are the same as I; and from now on, until I have your wife's leave, I will go about with you no more."

"That is no difficulty at all," he said.

A black car stopped outside the window, and Stephen said:

"Here she is."

When I looked round, sure enough a silver girl was stepping from the car; knowing it was Bai Ping, I turned back and smoked calmly, while Stephen kept his eyes on the door.

I kept my calm throughout, meaning to let Stephen be surprised when he saw it was Bai Ping.

But Stephen stood up, ran out, and said:

"Hello."

So I stood up too, quite sure I had played my joke on Stephen to the hilt, and made ready, delighted, to let Bai Ping in on the deception.

"He's been waiting for you a good while." I heard Stephen's voice; I looked up — it was Mei Yingzi!

I looked again — Mei Yingzi still.

So it really was Mei Yingzi. How could it be Mei Yingzi? Was Stephen playing a joke on me?

Mei Yingzi had come within a distance where I could not but greet her. I came out from my seat and said:

"How very unexpected, to see you here."

She sat down in Stephen's seat as though by appointment. I caught the rare scent I had caught the night before. She said with a smile:

"Did you foresee that I would come here? Or did I foresee that you would come?"

"Are you alone, Miss Mei Yingzi?" I said.

"Don't you see that I am alone?" she laughed.

"Can the sun exist without satellites?"

"Then does it not occur to you that wherever I go, the satellites are there before me?" Mei Yingzi laughed, showing between her lovely lips her pale, almond-tinted front teeth.

Stephen laughed with her.

"…" I had nothing to say, and only smiled faintly along with them.

A little abashed, I wanted a cigarette, but the pack on the table was empty, so I went to the counter, which was near the door. I had just bought a pack and was about to take one to smoke when a silver car stopped outside the window; hoping it was Bai Ping, I lit up slowly on purpose, gazing out the door, and when the car door opened it did not disappoint me — out came Bai Ping. I went to the door to open it for her, and said:

"Bai Ping!"

I brought her in, and she sat on the inner side of my seat. Stephen said gaily:

"Let me have a good night of it today."

"I don't agree," I said. "Not unless you can get your wife to come."

"So long as you can get her," Stephen said, laughing. But Bai Ping paid us no heed:

"I never expected so many of you."

"Had you two arranged this beforehand?" Stephen asked.

I nudged Bai Ping with my knee, and she took the hint and lied:

"I was just out shopping, and saw you all come in here; I couldn't find what I wanted, so I came to look for you." Then, turning her eyes to Mei Yingzi, she added: "Since Miss Mei is here — may I stand you dinner today?"

"Let me stand you all," Mei Yingzi said with a laugh, her glance sliding over the three of our faces. "The honour is mine, to have run into you. I know you are often together."

"That is the men's honour," I said. "I don't wish you ladies to rob us of it."

"But for me," said Bai Ping, "to stand Miss Mei a dinner is itself an honour — can't you men yield it to me?"

"No," said Stephen. "If you want to invite her, invite Mei Yingzi formally — don't snatch her at a table we've already claimed."

"Then," said Bai Ping, her smile a lily just opening, "my dear, will you allow me — when I invite you specially — to have you come?"

"Of course," said Mei Yingzi. "But allow me to invite you first."

"Say no more," Stephen broke in abruptly. "From today, let us plan four days of revelry, and take turns as host for the four days."

"Agreed," said everyone. But Bai Ping went on: "Today let me be host first."

"It's I," said Stephen.

"No — let fate decide the order of our turns," said Mei Yingzi. Showing her lovely almond-tinted teeth, she took out four matchsticks, wrote numbers on their stems with a pen, shuffled them and laid them flat on the table. Pressing them under one hand, she bade us each draw one.

Now I was caught by that lovely hand — the nails clipped very clean, without a speck of blemish, tinted with pale red lacquer. The slender fingers were like the leaves of a narcissus; not a single ring was on them, and they might have been the ivory work of some classical Indian sculptor. Looking up from the smooth, soft back of the hand, I saw on the wrist a plain gold bangle, and found in it an indescribable harmony with her light-blue dress, glittering with a charm all its own.

I picked one without thinking, but then felt Bai Ping's knee, on my right, touch mine, and understood that she wanted to exchange her matchstick for mine. So I put mine into her hand, while Bai Ping kept her eyes on Stephen and Mei Yingzi. They were both looking at the numbers on their own matchsticks; I saw that the one Bai Ping handed me was "three," and Bai Ping, looking at the number I had handed her, said:

"Whoever is host runs the whole night's programme."

"Very good," said Stephen.

Everyone laid theirs out. Bai Ping had "one," Mei Yingzi "two," I "three," and Stephen, naturally, was last; and so Bai Ping said, her smile a lily just opening:

"Then today's host is I."

"I say we should ask Mrs. Stephen," Mei Yingzi said suddenly, smiling, to Bai Ping. "Do you say we should ask her too?"

"Of course," said Bai Ping. "But for that we'll have to ask Miss Mei to telephone — it seems only you have quite the standing to invite her."

"A pity that today I have no standing," Mei Yingzi said, as if in jest.

"Why so?" asked Stephen.

"Because today's part of host has been snatched away by Miss Bai Ping," she said, arching her naturally lustrous brows.

"Then, Stephen," I said, "you go and ask her."

"Certainly I can," he said. "But my telephoning never has any effect."

"Then I'll telephone myself." Bai Ping stood up all at once, excited, and squeezed out from the seat.

"Let me do it," I said, rising, and asked Stephen: "What's the number?"

"Seven three eight two two," said Stephen.

"No," said Bai Ping, springing out of her seat. "I don't want you to make the call."

Bai Ping hurried to the counter; I stood where I was. Mei Yingzi and Stephen sat watching her.

We saw Bai Ping pick up the telephone at the counter; we did not hear her first few words, but then she suddenly raised her voice:

"The Liti Café, Bubbling Well Road — just at the corner of Medhurst Road." Then she added: "Good — good, then come at once."

She put down the telephone and came back lightly, sat down in her seat, and said:

"Now let us wait."

"You really got her to come out?" Stephen asked, astonished.

"Why not?" said Bai Ping.

"Today I want to see how our host means to entertain her guests," I asked.

"First I'll stand you dinner; after dinner I want you all to hear a concert; when the concert is over I'll take you dancing; and in the small hours we'll go to my home for tea and cakes."

It came to me suddenly that the Shanghai Municipal Orchestra was giving its symphony concert tonight; the Municipal Orchestra was still the finest orchestra in China. Its usual time was Saturday afternoon, but tonight's programme, having Beethoven's Ninth Symphony with its huge chorus — many of them drawn from outside the orchestra — had been moved to the evening, since by day, naturally, not everyone was free. I realized it was with this concert that Bai Ping had invited Mrs. Stephen, and I marvelled at her cleverness.

But just then a car came outside. Bai Ping rose to pay for the tea, saying as she did so:

"Now let us take this car and go to fetch Mrs. Stephen."

"…" Mei Yingzi laughed and rose; I laughed too, and helped her into her coat, saying low at her ear:

"A clever girl indeed, is she not?"

"…" Mei Yingzi only smiled. But Stephen said excitedly to Bai Ping:

"So your call was to the cab-stand?"

"…" Bai Ping said nothing, but took up her handbag and walked out, Stephen at her side.

And so I walked beside Mei Yingzi, and she said:

"To have such a sweetheart is an honour."

"Do you think she would be any man's sweetheart?"

"Can you really not love a girl like that?"

"No," I said. "I am a confirmed bachelor."

"I, for my part, already love her."
