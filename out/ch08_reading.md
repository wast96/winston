## Section 3. Tangled Roots, a Substitute Sacrifice

### 1. A Traitor Who Fans the Red Flames: All Say He May Be Killed

In the autumn of 1933 the "Beiping Station" was enlarged and reorganized: Bai Shiwei was made "chief of the Action Group," and two comrades of the seventh class of the Military Academy, Zhang Fengyi and Lu Songling, were taken on to the work; a proper action unit was formally established, and its arms and equipment made good.

Only now did the Beiping Station's action work begin to be reckoned a regular part of our business. Yet ever since Zhang Jingyao met the law at the Grand Hôtel des Wagons-Lits in the Legation Quarter of Beiping, the vile sort had lost their nerve and the petty skulkers gone to ground; that breed no longer dared come to Beiping to carry on. And so now, though we were better prepared than before, for want of a target we found ourselves instead with no field in which to show our mettle.

At that time the keeping of public order in Beiping, the watching of anti-government activity, and the suppression of unlawful doings were all the charge, severally, of the legally constituted military, police, and gendarme bodies—such units as the Beiping Municipal Police, the Third Regiment of the Central Gendarmerie, the Fourth Regiment of the Central Gendarmerie, and the Beiping Gendarme Command (formerly of the Northeastern Army). The "Beiping Station" was a secret working organization; it held no open, lawful standing, and beyond the tasks handed down to it from above it possessed no legal "authority" whatever. The sanctioning of traitors could be carried out only in districts the arm of the law could not reach, and in Beiping that meant only the one small patch of the Legation Quarter, the "quarter of the missions." Anywhere outside the Legation Quarter, the competent authorities could well arrest a man and bring him to book by law, and there was no call at all for us to take the field.

By reason of our organizational ties within the Lixingshe, Shiwei and I were both in touch with the Third and Fourth Gendarme Regiments; but our part was no more than to furnish intelligence bearing on unlawful activity. As for anything further—re-investigation, reconnaissance, arrest, legal disposal—that was left to the public-order bodies to handle.

Writing this, I am put in mind of an old affair.

At the time, by intelligence that Qi Nanpu had come by, there was said to be a band of men who, under the pretext of holding a birthday feast, had laid on wine and guests at a restaurant in Beixinqiao—when in truth it was a secret gathering, meaning to stir up trouble within the city of Beiping and along the eastern outskirts, and so disturb the public peace.

Having received this intelligence, the Beiping Station passed it for handling to Ding Chang, the regimental adjutant in charge of such matters in the Third Gendarme Regiment. Plainclothes men were sent to reconnoiter, and sure enough some dozens of men were gathered there—and among them not a single woman. Now, who ever heard of a birthday with no women guests? By that one token alone the thing plainly smelt of something amiss; and so, without waiting to sort matters out, the whole company was arrested and marched off.

Only when each had been questioned one by one did it come out that this was a pair of old men of the Green Gang—a certain Xu and a certain Fang, of the "twenty-second" and the "Tong" generation-ranks—"opening the incense hall" to take in disciples; it had not the least thing to do with the Japanese, still less any design to disturb the public peace.

This being so, the disposing of the detention and release of several dozen men proved no small trouble. In the end the greater part were let off lightly for "unlawful assembly," while a small number were recruited and turned to use as inside lines within the "secret societies."

The ringleader among them, the aforesaid Xu, volunteered to join the Beiping Station's work; this man was afterward Qi Nanpu's own father-in-law. Another, a lesser figure, Fang Yushu, likewise offered his unpaid help. There was besides one Tong Ronggong, shrewd and quick, who was sent up to Nanjing for training; after the victory in the War of Resistance he held such posts as colonel chief-inspector of the Beiping Investigation Office, and in 1949 was killed by the Communists at Qingdao. It was thanks to our having at that time got the true facts clear, and closed the case as we did, that we did not wrongfully fasten guilt upon men; otherwise, should we not have made a grave blunder, and how then could the heart have found peace? One sees that in work of this kind nothing may be treated carelessly; the cart ahead is a warning to the cart behind—dare one be anything but circumspect?

Let me add that, at the same time as the Beiping Station set up its "Action Group," the "Tianjin Station" too formed an "Action Group." Wang Tianmu served as its chief besides his other duties; its members numbered as many as seven or eight, of whose names I remember three—Ma Hetu, Yue Qingjiang, and Ding Baoling—for the reason that by 1940 these three did some notable work in Shanghai. The Tianjin action men had for the most part followed Elder Brother Wang, and were one and all natives of Henan province. In the quality of their makeup they differed markedly from the Beiping Station's: at the whole trade of handling a gun each of them was seasoned, whereas the Beiping action men ran deep in political consciousness.

At this juncture Elder Brother Wang's household was still in Beiping, while he himself shuttled between the two cities, Tianjin and Beiping; and a few of the action men would run to and fro with him.

So far as I know, the "Tianjin Station" completed several pieces of work in the Japanese concession at Tianjin; but as duties lay each within their own province, I had no part in the affair, and so pass it over.

It was just at this time that a startling event occurred in Beiping, of which I learned a little only because Mr. Dai Yunong came in person to Beiping to deal with it. Yet Mr. Dai himself never once spoke to me plainly of the inner truth of the matter.

The time was about the spring of 1934. Elder Brother Tianmu had been sent off to Zhangjiakou on business. The Tianjin Station's action men had brought down a great calamity in Beiping, raising such a storm that all the city buzzed of it, and it became the news most watched of all. Before long the Beiping Detective Squad declared the "case broken," the truth was announced "made clear," and a sensational marvel thus blew over and dispersed.

At the time I had no notion whatever that the affair had anything to do with the "Tianjin Station."

Afterward Mr. Dai came alone to Beiping. He had first sent me a telegram deciphered by his own hand, bidding me take a room for him and tell no one.

That evening I kept Mr. Dai company at the Central Hotel on East Chang'an Avenue, where we ate a little at random, then went back up to the fourth-floor room I had reserved to rest a while. The two of us sat facing each other with nothing to say; I made ready to take my leave, so that he might rest alone, but Mr. Dai kept pressing me to stay a little longer. I saw only that he went on musing silently to himself, until of a sudden he said, as though to himself: "For whom does one toil, for whom is one busy!" A moment later he rose and took two steps, sat down again, and again it was that same line—"for whom does one toil, for whom is one busy." And so, at intervals, he said it over a good many times.

This quite bewildered me; I was in a thick fog, and could get no purchase on it at all. I thought to myself: "When he speaks so, what in the world does he mean?"

Later Mr. Dai asked after Elder Brother Tianmu's recent doings; I said he had gone to Zhangjiakou and was not yet back, and that we had not met for more than half a month. Mr. Dai then asked, a word here and a word there, after the state of the Tianjin Station's work, and I told him all I knew. From his look and the tone of his voice, he seemed to take a particular "concern" for the goings-on of that batch of Tianjin action men.

We chatted a good while, mostly by side blows and glancing thrusts, as though he deliberately would not touch the main point. Mr. Dai too never let out his intention; he seemed to wish to charge me with doing something for him, yet would speak and then stop, unable to bring himself to decide.

When I was about to go, he said: "I mean to wait for Brother Tianmu to come back from Zhangjiakou; there is another matter I would talk over with him. Whenever I leave Beiping, I shall telephone to let you know."

Elder Brother Tianmu did indeed return the very next day; how they got word to each other I do not know. What matters they then talked over I likewise knew nothing of. That same evening Mr. Dai told me he was going back to Nanjing. I saw him onto the train, and only then found that traveling with Mr. Dai was Elder Brother Tianmu as well.

After Elder Brother Wang went off with Mr. Dai, he did not return to his post. The Tianjin Station chief's place was newly filled by Wang Zixiang; the Tianjin Station was reorganized throughout, and the greater part of its personnel changed about.

About a month later Mr. Dai sent a telegram, ordering the Beiping Station to "escort" to Nanjing, and there deliver up to justice, the Tianjin Station's clerk who remained in Beiping—that is, Brother Wang Tianmu's assistant. (This gentleman died some years ago in Taipei, a noted jurist and delegate to the National Assembly, a good man.)

At this the former Tianjin Station's "Action Group" fell apart of itself, and its action men too were severally placed elsewhere or disciplined.

As for how a criminal case in Beiping came to be tangled up with Elder Brother Wang and his men—years before, there had appeared in Beiping a realistic social novel, "The Case of the Corpse in the Trunk," which was precisely an oblique portrait of this affair. Only, what the Beiping Detective Squad "broke" was a false case.

Mr. Dai racked his brains sorely over the handling of this most thorny business of Brother Tianmu. In truth, had Mr. Dai not wished to shield him, the thing would have been easy to settle any way at all; but because Mr. Dai's intent was to preserve Wang Tianmu's life, while yet he had to have regard to the law of the land and to discipline, he was put in a hard place.

By the account of Mr. Dai's confidential secretary, Brother Mao Wanli—also a good friend of mine—Mr. Dai, in writing his report to Generalissimo Chiang, weighed the matter over and over; the whole came to only some hundreds of words, yet he wrote from evening until dawn, not merely turning each word this way and that in the wording, but taking endless pains even over the order in which he set out the three proposed methods of disposal.

The report Mr. Dai wrote out himself, in careful regular script with the writing-brush. First he set forth in brief the true facts of the event; then he listed Wang Tianmu's merits and abilities; and then he drew up three methods of disposal: first, the death penalty; second, imprisonment for life; third, to bear the guilt and redeem it by service. This last, to bear the guilt and redeem it, was in fact no real possibility, and so was placed last, its purpose being to dilute the first item, the death penalty—a mere foil. Mr. Dai's own mind lay with the middle course, "life": only let Wang Tianmu's life be saved first, and there would be days ahead for him to rise again.

The Generalissimo's ruling came down, and it was just the second of Mr. Dai's proposals for which he had hoped—"life."

What one may see from this affair is Mr. Dai's reasonableness and his mindfulness of old ties; and one may discover besides that there was in his nature a generous and forbearing side, in no way so stern and unfeeling as rumor abroad made him out.

Once Wang Tianmu's term was fixed, it was served in the Army Prison at Laohuqiao in Nanjing, of which a small part was set aside for the sole use of the "Juntong." Within the Juntong this place was called "Ground C," and there were confined those comrades who broke discipline over a long stretch.

Setting this affair aside, let me go on to introduce the newly appointed chief of the "Tianjin Station," that heroic figure Wang Zixiang.

Wang Zixiang was a graduate of the Peking Union Medical College; he held a license to practice at the works department of the British concession in Tianjin, a duly qualified Western-style physician. He was born in the eighth year before the Republic; he stood something over six feet, with clear brows and fine eyes, upright and handsome. Coming to know him, one felt the more how pure and how ardent he was. His family circumstances were very comfortable, and his own income from practice was considerable besides. His old father had retired to the shade of the woods, living at ease in a quiet little villa in Beiping, whiling away his days at leisure; Wang himself was single in Tianjin, with as yet no burden of household.

A young man of just such qualities was the very best sort that secret-service work seeks to draw in.

Wang Zixiang had a younger sister named Wang Yumei; the name was a touch common, but the girl was comely and gracious, and in those days too a person of note in Beiping, reckoned one of the "four vajra-warriors" among the reigning beauties and well-bred young ladies—why women should be called vajra-warriors I have not to this day understood.

I mentioned in an earlier section that Mr. Dai, after striking up a friendship with Wu Youquan, would now and then, through Wu, take part in the social doings of Beiping's "polite society"; and it was on just such an occasion that Mr. Dai came to know Wang Yumei.

Mr. Dai, moreover, had a mind to bring me out into the world, and would sometimes especially invite me to their little gatherings. And Wang Yumei's brother, Wang Zixiang, whenever he came up to Beiping for the weekend, would drop in as the mood took him; and so, one drawing in another, we were all strung together.

We never made any show of mystery, and Wang Zixiang naturally knew in rough outline what it was we did; he yearned after it beyond measure, and more than once declared that he greatly wished to serve without pay, that he might do somewhat for his country. Mr. Dai not only granted his request, but bestowed on him the post of Tianjin Station chief—doubtless with an eye to Wang Zixiang's loyalty and dependability, his learning and ability, and his professional standing, all such as one might rely upon.

This was how Dr. Wang Zixiang came to be a secret agent.

In 1934, our superiors handed down, one upon another, several sanction orders, among them ones for Shi Yousan, Ji Hongchang, and Zhang Bi. But these action targets all hid away within the Tianjin concessions, and did not dare come again to Beiping to carry on.

At this juncture the "Tianjin Station" was in a state of transition, the old failing before the new could take hold: its former action men had all been transferred away, and its new action unit was still in the building; and so this task of work fell yet again upon the shoulders of the "Beiping Station."

I, as chief of the "Beiping Station," ran to and fro between Beiping and Tianjin over the business.

Each time I came to Tianjin I would be sure to meet Dr. Wang Zixiang, for Mr. Dai had directed that we keep in close touch with the "Tianjin Station" and give it what guidance we could.

Dr. Wang Zixiang was most warm-hearted; more than once he declared that whatever I might need, and he could do, he was glad to lend all his strength to—and this quite apart from any direction from above that he should do so; even in private, as friend to friend, he was willing to help.

Dr. Wang was, of course, loyal and patriotic; only, in working experience and political understanding he was still somewhat shallow, and could not even always tell loyal from treacherous, being like the common run who look only at the surface and do not fathom the depths. On the very matter of our superiors' order to sanction Ji Hongchang, he raised a number of doubts.

He held: "Ji Hongchang is a general who resisted Japan; he fought the Japanese forces in the eastern Chahar country and recovered such places as Guyuan—the facts are all there. How can he be spoken of in the same breath as the likes of Zhang Jingyao?"

Dr. Wang Zixiang was not wrong; heard out, it was not without reason. But he did not grasp the whole situation, and unless the thing were laid out for him from root to branch, he would not understand; and if in his heart he remained unconvinced, how then could he be made to set to the work with a will?

And so I told over the whole course of the facts in detail, from beginning to end.

The "Tanggu Truce" was signed under the muzzles of the Japanese guns; its effect was to preserve North China for the time. Men of insight held it a makeshift born of necessity; but those who nursed other designs and are forever ready with cold, carping words charged the government authorities with selling out the country's sovereignty and shaming the nation. Cliques and individuals long discontented with the Central Government thereupon took it as a pretext to open anti-government activity, and Feng Yuxiang was one of them.

Feng Yuxiang, under the name of resisting Japan "single-handed," in fact busied himself building up armed force, his aim being to break away from the Central Government and raise a separate standard, and thence to seize political power. Nursing this ambition, he issued on the 26th of May, 1933, a circular telegram to the whole nation, organized the "Chahar People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army," took upon himself the office of commander-in-chief, and announced his assumption of the post at Zhangjiakou.

The purport of the circular was, in outline, as follows. It charged the Central Government: "Without resistance it abandoned the three eastern provinces; with sham resistance it lost Rehe; with a partial resistance that was not thoroughgoing it was worsted at Shanghai, Beiping, and Tianjin."

It reviled the government: "The forces used against Japan amount to but a tenth of the army; the navy and air force have not been put into the field at all; the outlay for resisting Japan comes to less than a twentieth of the nation's total revenue—and even the people's freewill contributions are withheld."

It set forth its own merits: "For months past, the people's bodies of every province and city have charged us with the great cause and urged us to resist Japan. Not daring to flee death and steal a coward's life, but leaning only upon the people's demand, I have led those of like mind and purpose to form an anti-Japanese battle-line, to defend Chahar in arms, and thence to recover the lost land, and to seek China's independence and freedom."

That his resistance to Japan too must be circular-telegraphed to the whole nation was of course to court the crowd's applause, to knit together the disaffected, and to set himself against the Central Government. But contrary to expectation, once this telegram went out, the response was cold, far short of what had first been reckoned.

The so-called "Chahar People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army" was of a mixed inner make, each part with its own designs, and it took in: the Volunteer Corps that had withdrawn into Chahar from the Rehe front, such as the units of Feng Zhanhai, Deng Wen, Li Haiqing, and Li Haishan; Chahar's own standing local forces under Zhang Lisheng; the unit of Tong Linge, of the Twenty-ninth Army, left in Chahar and reorganized by Feng Yuxiang; and puppet troops come over, and miscellaneous units such as Liu Guitang's, together with old subordinates whom Feng Yuxiang summoned from Shanxi—the units of Fang Zhenwu, Ji Hongchang, Sun Liangcheng, and the rest.

The "Anti-Japanese Allied Army" gave out its strength as one hundred and twenty thousand men, with eighty thousand rifles; the remainder were empty-handed.

Fang Zhenwu's unit styled itself the "Anti-Japanese National Salvation Army," and again went its own separate way from the "Anti-Japanese Allied Army."

Ji Hongchang's unit, though organized as the Second Army of the "Anti-Japanese Allied Army," yet in his words and deeds Ji Hongchang was plainly in concert with the Communist Party.

In the latter part of June 1933, Feng appointed Ji Hongchang front commander-in-chief, ordering him to recover the lost land of eastern Chahar; the beginning was full of color and sound, but the end was a tiger's head and a snake's tail.

In the months that followed, the Central Government counseled Feng Yuxiang: "Do not draw in the ringleaders of the Communist bandits to fan the red flames, and leave North China an endless plague." (See the joint telegram from the Center over the names of Chiang and Wang to Feng, dated the 28th.) This was for very dread of a new "tolerate-the-Communists-and-carve-out-a-domain" situation forming again.

The "ringleaders of the Communist bandits" spoken of in the text meant precisely Ji Hongchang and his sort.

From the materials set out below, the tie between Ji Hongchang and the Chinese Communists may be proved—or one may say, that Ji Hongchang was carrying out a task the Chinese Communists had laid upon him. The points that rest on firm and warranted evidence include these:

"In February 1933 Ji Hongchang went from Tianjin to Zhangjiakou to set on foot the 'People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army';

"The Chinese Communist bandit party, at the same time, secretly established at Zhangjiakou a 'Front Committee,' meaning to control the 'People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army';

"On the 26th of May, 1933, the 'People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army' was established at Zhangjiakou; Ji Hongchang served as commander of its Second Army and as front commander-in-chief, and demanded that Feng Yuxiang bestow on him the post of chief of the police department of the Chahar provincial government, together with the garrison command of Zhangjiakou—gathering both military and police power into one hand;

"On the 15th of June, the 'People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army' convened its First Representative Congress, and Ji Hongchang was elected a member, and a standing member, of the first Military Affairs Commission. This 'Military Affairs Commission' had plainly been infiltrated and worked upon by the Communist Party;

"On the 14th of August, Feng Yuxiang resigned the office of commander-in-chief of the 'People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army,' and Ji Hongchang and Fang Zhenwu thereupon issued a declaration, feigning to carry on the resistance to Japan. On the 16th, Fang Zhenwu circular-telegraphed his assumption of the acting commander-in-chief's post;

"Not many days after, Ji Hongchang, on the instruction of the Communists' 'Front Committee,' met and conferred with Fang Zhenwu at Laojuntang near Zhangjiakou, and it was decided that the 'People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army' be renamed the 'Anti-Japanese, Chiang-Chastising Army.'"

From this point on, Ji Hongchang not only did not resist Japan, but actually turned his gun-muzzles about to attack the Nationalist forces; the military movements that may be traced on record are as follow:

"On the 5th of September, 1933, Ji Hongchang led his unit to Dushikou. On the 17th, they broke through to Fengning in Rehe province. On the 20th, they took the county town of Huairou. Thereafter they pressed on and occupied the county town of Miyun in Hebei province. On the 23rd, they took Niulanshan in Shunyi county. On the 26th, they occupied Gaoliying, a bare sixty-odd li from the city of Beiping. On the 27th, they withdrew the force holding Gaoliying and turned to attack Xiaotangshan, fifty li from Beiping; failing there, they fell back that afternoon to Qincheng in Changping county. On the 28th, the units of Ji Hongchang and Fang Zhenwu, styling themselves the 'Anti-Japanese, Chiang-Chastising Army,' suddenly broke through again to the neighborhood of Nanzhuang and Beizhuang, near Nankou on the Beiping–Suiyuan railway. On the 29th, a small part of them slipped through to the Ming Tombs in Changping. On the 30th, from the Ming Tombs they turned to attack the county town of Changping. Ji's main force fell back and gathered in the hill country about Sandaoguan, to the northwest of Huairou."

From the 5th of September on, for more than twenty days, the units of Ji and Fang, who styled themselves the "Anti-Japanese, Chiang-Chastising Army," ranged within a hundred-li radius about the city of Beiping, raiding and harrying everywhere. Where was the "resisting of Japan" in this? It was nothing but betrayal of the Center, a plot to split the nation, setting themselves against the innocent common folk. At the time the charitable bodies of Beiping cried out again and again for a "peaceful settlement," yet the two traitors Ji and Fang turned to it a deaf ear.

If one really put to Ji Hongchang the single question, "Is this then how you resist Japan?"—I fancy that, against his own conscience, he could not have answered it.

Look again at the doings of the two, Ji and Fang, thereafter:

"On the 1st of October, the rebel troops of Ji and Fang seized the hill country about Shalingliang, to the northeast of Changping county. On the 2nd, they attacked the Changping county town once more, fighting the garrison through the whole night. On the 3rd, they attacked Changping again, and still not prevailing, fell back to the hills about Qincheng, northeast of Changping. On the 4th, they broke away eastward, and near Xiaotangshan and about Gaoliying had small brushes with the Nationalist forces. From the 5th to the 8th, the fighting fell quiet. On the 9th, the rebels attacked from Xiaotangshan toward Beiyuan and Shahe. On the 10th and 11th, the rebels attacked with their main force about Daxiaodongliu. On the 12th, the rebel main force massed in the Niulanshan district of Shunyi, meaning to fight a last stand with their backs to the wall. On the 13th, the units of Ji Hongchang and Fang Zhenwu, their dead and wounded grievous, their ammunition spent and their food gone, fell into encirclement."

By this point, thirty-eight days having passed all told, an end was thus put to Ji Hongchang's "mad and witless stirrings."

On the 16th of October, Ji Hongchang and Fang Zhenwu left Xinzhuang together and went to Majiaying, to the headquarters of the 142nd Division of the Nationalist Thirty-second Army, and there met Shang Zhen, commander of the Thirty-second Army.

This ought to be reckoned a surrender.

Having seen Shang Zhen, commander of the Thirty-second Army, Ji Hongchang, as a beaten general, put forward such demands as "a guarantee of the lives and safety of the officers and men of the anti-Japanese army, and proper medical care for the wounded." Commander Shang, though he answered that it might be considered, said he must first ask instructions of the Beiping Military Branch.

The Beiping Military Branch, on receiving Shang Zhen's telegram, ordered him to escort the two, Fang Zhenwu and Ji Hongchang, to Beiping to be delivered up for trial.

It is said that Shang Zhen used a certain artifice: on the plea that "the Acting Chairman of the Military Branch, He Yingqin, wished to confer with them face to face and seek some fitting means of mediation," two representatives of Beiping's "charitable bodies" "accompanied" Fang and Ji to Beiping to "negotiate."

At this the Beiping Military Branch put out an announcement: "The two, Fang Zhenwu and Ji Hongchang, raising troops in revolt and doing harm to the region, have of late raided again and again the outskirts of Beiping. Closely blocked and struck by our Nationalist forces, their strength spent and their power broken, they could not prevail, and today have signified their submission to our front-line troops. Fang and Ji have moreover left their units and gone elsewhere, and means are now being sought to dispose of the remnant that is left. He Yingqin, the 16th, executed under secret seal."

Setting out from Majiaying in Shunyi county, along the road while being escorted to Beiping—a distance of not a hundred li—the two, Fang Zhenwu and Ji Hongchang, did, one after the other, "escape." This is a marvel indeed; there must be some hidden matter behind it, and what in truth it was, one can hardly, I fear, get a correct answer now.

In a booklet the Communists put out, "General Ji Hongchang," there is a passage written in this wise, which the writer of these lines, even setting aside the bias he ought to have, still cannot credit; I set the original text down here, and do not know how the reader may feel on reading it:

"Hongchang saw through the enemy's snare at once, and said secretly to Fang Zhenwu: 'These five feet of us are still of use to the country; we cannot just meekly let them do us in.'

"They agreed to make their escape on the road.

"But when they left Majiaying, the motorcar was already thick with guards, weapons shouldered and loaded, the watch kept most strict. All along the way, Hongchang's mind was busy with how to get away. He thought: to get even one of them off first would be a good thing.

"When the car reached Sunhe, not far from Beiping, the sky grew slowly dark. Hongchang looked round at the lie of the land about them, well suited to concealment. He gave Fang Zhenwu a wink, and jerked his mouth toward a waterworks at the side. Fang Zhenwu asked the car to stop, feigning that he must go to the privy. The 'charitable body's' representative escorting the car, seeming somewhat out of patience, asked Hongchang whether he would go too. Hongchang, half-closing his eyes, lazily shook his head.

"The guards escorting them, though they had been deceived by their superiors and did not know that those they escorted were Ji Hongchang and Fang Zhenwu, yet all knew these were two men of consequence; and of the two, the burly, powerful Hongchang was the most 'dangerous.' Since he did not leave the car, they sent no one to follow Fang Zhenwu down. And so Fang Zhenwu, once out of the car, rounded the foot of a wall and strode off at speed and slipped away."

Should the reader be so minded, he may find in this passage two very plain great holes. Yet Fang Zhenwu did in fact escape; how then is this to be explained? There must surely be some other matter within it.

Fang Zhenwu, a fugitive under warrant, drifted thereafter down to Hong Kong. In December 1941 the Pacific War broke out; Hong Kong and Kowloon were seized by the Japanese army, and Fang Zhenwu, unable to lie hid, passed by way of Kowloon to Shenzhen. No sooner had he crossed into Guangdong territory than he was taken by our government troops and, by order, executed on the spot. From that time few have spoken of the man or his affairs.

To speak now of Ji Hongchang while under escort. By the account set down in that booklet "General Ji Hongchang":

"After waiting long and not seeing Fang Zhenwu return, the guards on the car fell into a huddle of confusion, and the guard-corporal grew the more like a leaping fury. At first they shook their fists at Hongchang and railed at him aloud, saying it was all his fault, that with his sly scheme he had let this important prisoner get away, and would land them in a great gaol. But after talking with him, and hearing him tell of the People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army's battle record against Japan, and his own principles of resisting Japan and saving the nation, they quieted down. The Thirty-second Army had also been an old unit of the Northwest Army; and when they learned that this was 'Ji the Bold,' the very Ji Hongchang who was a national hero, they all looked on him with eyes of reverence. In a flash he grew great in their esteem. Hongchang's putting others before himself, the broad and open breadth of his breast, moved even the 'charitable body's' representative deeply.

"The car drew nearer and nearer Beiping. The guards and the 'charitable body's' representative on the car all knew well that once Hongchang stepped down from the car, he would be seized by the Kuomintang's Beiping Military Branch. Hongchang's life was at that moment in their hands. After the guard-corporal and the 'charitable body's' representative had conferred in low voices, and having gone on a little farther, they looked about them and saw the place still and empty of men, and bade Hongchang get down from the car and flee. Hongchang turned his head and saw them all looking on him with eyes of reverence. Moved, he nodded to them, and with a single lift of his foot leapt down from the car. In a moment his massive form was lost in the fields under the low-hanging veil of night.

"Hongchang, having got down and walked a while, grew both thirsty and hungry, and turned aside toward a village by the road, meaning to ask for a little water. But there in the village was just then a party of Kuomintang troops pressing the villagers for timber. Alertly he slipped into a narrow lane, leapt over a stretch of surrounding wall, and dropped into a farmer's yard where firewood and straw were stored. Only when the clamoring enemy soldiers outside had gone did he crawl out from the straw-stack and find the old master of the farmhouse. The old farmer, hearing that this was a persecuted anti-Japanese soldier, felt uncommon pity for him, and brought out a few cornbread buns and boiling water, and found him besides a set of countryman's clothes to change the uniform off his back.

"Yielding to the old farmer and his son's counsel, Hongchang crouched two days in their straw-stack, until the rumor outside had died away; then, with gratitude, he took leave of the old farmer and his son, disguised as a countryman, and riding a donkey with a saddlebag across his back, made his way secretly back to Tianjin."

This piece of writing is far more of the storyteller's embroidery than of realism; what it leaves least accounted for is: how were that band of guards and the so-called "charitable body's" representatives to make good the sequel? And further: to report to the Thirty-second Army that they had escorted two important criminals who had betrayed the Center and raised troops in revolt—do they mean to say that not one officer was even sent, and that a single corporal leading a squad of brothers would serve? The doubtful points here are many, and the sharp-eyed will see at a glance that there is an inside story to it.

The doubtful points here need no more ink spent on them. In sum, one important criminal, Ji Hongchang, was thus let escape.

The fugitive Ji Hongchang slipped back to Tianjin, his ambition unquenched, and carried on still with his anti-government activity.

Touching Ji Hongchang's treasonous doings, the intelligence materials received on the side of the "Beiping Station" under my charge, gathered together, come to these points:

"About March 1934, Ji Hongchang went once to Shanghai in company with the Communist chief Xuan Xiafu, staying but a week; having received a new task laid on him by the Communists, he returned again to Tianjin. On the 10th of April, 1934, the Chinese Communist Central issued the 'Manifesto to the Whole People on Japanese Imperialism's Occupation of North China and Swallowing Up of China,' and called for the building of an 'anti-imperialist united front.' Ji Hongchang thereupon, under the direction of the Communist chief figures Xuan Xiafu and Nan Hanchen, secretly organized at Tianjin the 'Chinese People's Anti-Fascist Grand Alliance.' Upon the central committee of this Grand Alliance sat Feng Yuxiang and representatives of the Southwest. Ji Hongchang was pushed forward as chairman; Xuan Xiafu, Nan Hanchen, Ren Yingqi, and others were central committee members, Nan Hanchen serving besides as secretary-general. And Xuan Xiafu, Ji Hongchang, and the rest founded the magazine 'The National War-Banner' as the Grand Alliance's organ. After it came out it made neither sound nor scent, and was renamed again 'North China Beacon-Fires,' 'The Great Wall,' and the like, published at no fixed intervals, but still it did not thrive;

"Xuan Xiafu, a Zhejiang man, joined the 'Chinese Socialist Youth Corps' in 1922, and shortly rose into the Chinese Communist Party, becoming a Communist of the oldest standing. He had studied in the first class at Whampoa, and was expelled. In 1925, on Li Dazhao's introduction, he was sent to do political work in Feng Yuxiang's Guominjun. In 1927 he was chief of the propaganda department at the headquarters of the Allied National Armies. In the Northern Expedition he was director of the political department at the general headquarters of the Second Group Army of the National Revolutionary Army. In 1931 he joined the League of Left-Wing Writers. From 1933 to 1934, by the Communist Party's charge, he served as the behind-the-scenes master of Ji Hongchang;

"Nan Hanchen, a Shanxi man, joined the Communist Party as early as 1926; in 1927 he and Wei Yechou set on foot a peasant uprising in northern Anhui; in 1930, when Yang Hucheng was chairman of Shaanxi province, he served as secretary-general, and in 1932 was placed under warrant and left his post. At this time he was the number-two man arranging matters behind the scenes for Ji Hongchang;

"Through Ji Hongchang's connections, they drew in the former commander of the Twelfth Army, Ren Yingqi, then in hiding at Tianjin, to take part in the activity; and through Ren Yingqi's connections, they set afoot mutiny within the Nationalist forces;

"Ren Yingqi, a Henan man, was originally an old subordinate of Jin Yun'e of the Zhili clique; in the Northern Expedition his troops were reorganized as the Twelfth Army of the National Revolutionary Army, and he served as its commander. In 1930 he took part in the Yan–Feng revolt, and after its failure hid himself away at Tianjin;

"The conclusion reached by putting together the several differing intelligence materials: that Ji Hongchang, under Communist direction, would by no means give up armed rebellion, but would go on making 'resisting Japan' his rallying-cry, drawing together every force set against the Center, so as to stand against the government."

Such was Ji Hongchang's true face. I asked Dr. Wang Zixiang in turn: "A man of this kind—do you take him for a hero or a cur?"

### 2. Sprouted but Never Flowered: A Pillar-Beam Snapped Untimely

Among the several sanction orders our superiors had handed down, no order of precedence had been set: as occasion offered, any of them might be acted upon at any time and place. Because I was the commander on the ground, I urged that Ji Hongchang be put first of all—that is, that he be dealt with first. The present question was how to open our reconnaissance activity.

The task of reconnaissance, by the organizational system and by the actual case, naturally lay with the "Tianjin Station"; but because the newly installed Tianjin Station chief Wang Zixiang was not yet abreast of things, and to show his eager support and cooperation, he directed the "Intelligence Group" under him to stop all other activity at once and take sole charge of the business. And so the chief of the "Intelligence Group," Lü Yimin, was brought to meet me, and Group Leader Lü was told to his face to await my disposition.

Group Leader Lü Yimin spoke with a slight Tianjin accent, though one could tell he was not a Tianjin native. At our first meeting I did not yet know his origins or history; to look at, he seemed a few years my senior, guarded in his bearing, refined in his talk, rather like an honest, sincere man. He had a wife and children, and lived in the French concession; his home had no telephone installed, so that to reach him on any business one had only the one course of calling at his door. And call on him I must.

It was a stretch of old-style lane houses, one crowded against the next, all of the same pattern, so that but for the door-numbers set out plainly they would have been hard indeed to find. We had made no appointment beforehand, so he had no notion when I might come. Since I had come, he could not for shame refuse to let me in and be seated, but his face was all embarrassment.

The room was dark as a cave, plainly short of light, and with a set of hardwood furniture to set it off, darker still. Perhaps because there were children, a good many odds and ends lay heaped here and scattered there, set about in the utmost disorder, truly not fit to be seen—no wonder Group Leader Lü's face flushed red. But to me all this was of no moment. After a brief exchange of courtesies, we went straight to the main point of our talk.

First I again set out, over again, my task and my charge; then I conveyed to Group Leader Lü in brief what our superiors expected, that he might understand it. Then I laid out for him in short the general situation touching Ji Hongchang, of which I had spoken before with Station Chief Wang Zixiang, in the hope that Group Leader Lü too might have a correct grasp of it. The concrete items in which I asked Group Leader Lü's help were three:

First, to collect all intelligence bearing on any activity of Ji Hongchang's at Tianjin; and should anything be got, not to deal with it, but to hand the item received, in the original, to me, that I might weigh and judge it.

Second, to reconnoiter with all his might Ji Hongchang's personal movements, including where Ji lodged, the routes he traveled, the places he stopped, and so on.

Third, to take particular note of any person who had had contact with Ji Hongchang, and of every scrap of material concerning such persons.

These three points had bearing one on another; Lü Yimin of course understood. Only, by his account, up to the present he had got no news whatever of Ji Hongchang.

Lü Yimin took the reconnaissance task upon him.

I went back to Beiping to see to the Beiping Station's affairs.

In the summer of 1934 the "Beiping Station," in both its organizational system and its working procedure, underwent changes again.

Mr. Zheng Jiemin, who had before directed the work of the North China region, had been ordered back to Nanjing to another important task. The office of "special commissioner" he left behind was for the time held in acting capacity by Xing Shan (cover name Senzhou). Before long it was reassigned to Zhang Shi (courtesy name Xingshen). This belonged to the secret-service working system.

To the posts Mr. Zheng Jiemin had held on the side of the "Lixingshe" and the "Renaissance Society," Ruan Qi was sent to take charge. This belonged to the secret organization.

The upward relations of the "Beiping Station" and the "Tianjin Station"—that is, the command chain between the two stations of Beiping and Tianjin and the Bureau headquarters—were changed from a "single line" of transmission to a "double line" of exchange, so that things need not pass by way of the "special commissioner" of necessity. In other words, the two stations' standing in the work was raised.

As for the abolition of the "special commissioner" system, and its change into a "Beiping District" with Zhang Yanyuan (courtesy name Binghua) coming out as district chief—that was all a matter of later date.

From the first half of 1934 on, the "Beiping Station" took on, one lot after another, a good many hands, some sent down from above, some drawn in on the ground; counting inside and field staff, by units it came to five or six offices, and by heads to something over twenty men. For a secret unit this was already of considerable scale. The Beiping Station's old premises on North Chang Street no longer sufficed for use, and to give up the lease would have been a pity; so it was turned into a liaison post, and the "Beiping Station" moved its offices to a great gated mansion of four courtyards on Wofosi Street in the western city, where Nanpu, Shiwei, and I all lodged together.

Among the comrades newly come to the work was one man whose contribution to the work was of the very greatest, and whose influence upon me personally ran deepest of all—and yet, think as I may, I cannot recall how I came to know him. For a stretch at the outset my impression of him was not good: a hooked nose, round staring eyes, a low voice, small unhurried gestures; on his tight-drawn face not a trace of a smile was to be found, but rather it gave one a dank, cold, clammy feeling. Yet he always showed courage, and a readiness to run risks; and it was for just this that our acquaintance was carried on.

At our first meeting he gave his name as Wang Wen; after we had had dealings a few times, he told me his true name was Wang Wenhan, a man of Baodi county in Hebei province, who had trained at a certain cadre school of the Northwest Army and had been a junior officer. His parents were living, his brothers many; he was the eldest, with house and land, and had no need to earn money abroad to feed a family. Where his ambition lay was in finding some upright road forward; not that he sought to shed glory on his forebears, but that he might do some service to the country—having much of the spirit of "a man should make himself strong."

The more we saw of each other, the more I revised my view of him: he was a solid, wholly genuine man; whether his heart was good or bad I could not yet make out, but in his talk a sense of justice fully showed itself. And there was one small point besides: the reason he spoke so slowly was that he stammered a little, and by speaking slowly he kept it from showing.

After I had applied for his appointment and it was approved above, Wang Wen became a regular working member of the "Beiping Station"; I assigned him to the "Action Group," under Brother Shiwei's command.

Shiwei, Wang Wen, and I three went to Tianjin together; and Wang Wen and I two went to Tianjin as well—running to and fro, all over the business of sanctioning Ji Hongchang. But the city of Tianjin was wide, and split besides into several different concessions; its people numbered two million and more; and without some dependable lead it was fishing a needle from the sea—where was one to look? A stroke of luck like the last, with a lucky star shining down, may be met with but not sought for; how could such befall time and again?

Once, chatting on the train, we chatted out something to the purpose beyond expectation. At first, when Wang Wen and I were talking business, I heard him only say that in his Northwest Army days he had seen Ji Hongchang, and should he meet him again would still be able to know him. This time, ranging over one thing and another, Wang Wen mentioned besides that he knew Shi Yousan too, and that right before us there was a very close schoolfellow, a fellow townsman as well, then serving as an adjutant at Shi Yousan's side.

This was too fine for words—yet another good seed not to be had for the seeking. But here we were on the train, with many eyes and ears about, not the place to say much; I did not press him further, meaning to find an occasion to go into the matter more fully by and by.

For the sake of the new lead unwittingly turned up in Wang Wen, I turned it over in my mind too: might the two pieces of work, Ji Hongchang and Shi Yousan, be carried on separately at the same time, and both handled at once? For to do one is to be at work, and to do two is likewise to be at work. But reckoning it over more closely, I feared it would not do; our own strength was still too thin, and by the look of it, the surer course was to gather our manpower and do them one at a time.

We came to Tianjin, and the first man we touched was the Tianjin Station's chief, Dr. Wang Zixiang. He always showed a hearty welcome. So long as we of the "Beiping Station" were in Tianjin, however busy he was, he would spare a little time to drive us about in his own car, that we might learn the roads. He was orderly and methodical: nearly every time, he would first drive up and down the main roads once or twice, then wind about along the side lanes, and wherever a place could not be freely passed through, he would point it out to us clearly besides. By the look of it, he had some few parts of a natural gift for secret-service work.

There were times, too, when Wang Zixiang was so busy he truly could not get away; then he would entrust an old comrade of the "Tianjin Station," Wu Ping, to look after us in his stead. And this Comrade Wu Ping had grown up in Tianjin from a child, and knew "Tianjin Wei" as well as the palm of his hand; with him for guide he took us to a great many places, and, as the saying goes, strange the first trip and familiar the second, we came little by little to be no longer wholly strangers to that great, all-embracing city.

Dr. Wang Zixiang more than once would have kept me lodging at his clinic, so that I need not take a hotel every time; but I always felt it somewhat inconvenient. He then advised me: why not rent a room to live in at "Xiaobailou"?

"Xiaobailou" is a place-name, in the First Special District of the city of Tianjin. Speaking of the "First Special District"—it too is a brand left seared on us by China's suffering of foreign wrong; this patch of ground was the old site of the German concession, taken back after the First World War and thereupon renamed the present "First Special District." Though it fell under the Tianjin municipal government, it was not quite the same as the other administrative districts: besides keeping certain of its former forms, such as its transport and public works, its most marked difference was that stateless foreigners were especially many there. In this quarter there were apartment-style small rooms to let, at a monthly rent of twenty to twenty-five dollars, far cheaper than a hotel at three to five dollars the day; and if one boarded, it was reckoned by the meal, with a servant besides to see to tea and water. Yet in those days not many could afford to live in such rooms; and those who liked to live in a foreigner's household were fewer still among the few.

For us, apart from the cheapness of the cost, there was a greater convenience: one need register one's name with no one—Zhang, Wang, Li, or Zhao as you pleased—so long as the rent was paid in advance, one came and went at will, and no one troubled who you were or what you did.

On moving in, one stated it was for a single person's lodging; and if now and then an extra person passed the night, there would be no interference, so long as one showed the servant some little regard—he would even lay you on an extra blanket.

Those who ran this sort of rented-room trade were either Jews or White Russians. They rented a house for their own living, hired a Chinese servant to do odd jobs and cook, and let out the rooms they did not need for the extra income; the board they gave their guests too was portioned out of their own meals—all close reckoning. To rent one room to me was more of a bargain still, for I was away two days out of three, and there was not even a lamp to be lit.

When I was in Tianjin, Dr. Wang Zixiang often had me over to his home—which was in fact his clinic, since at any rate he lived a bachelor there alone. The parlor where guests were received became, when a patient came for treatment, the consulting-room of course. In the parlor were several glass cabinets, and in them, ranged all neat and trim, glass bottles great and small of every kind; no need to ask—the bottles surely all held drugs.

The sight of the drugs stirred a thought in me, and quite of itself the talk turned to poisons. To this question I am most sensitive, and my interest in it is keen, for one day such things are sure to come in useful. And here, be it said, it was not I who guided him, but I who sought his instruction.

Everyone knows a little of the surface of it: the most "ideal" poison has these properties—odorless, colorless, tiny in dose, water-soluble; that is, it must be without taste and without color, a mere trace, that dropped into soup, tea, or drink dissolves at once and leaves no dreg, and once swallowed sends a man off in an instant—that sort of thing.

Talking to this point, we all laughed: to this day the whole world has no such drug. Yet by Dr. Wang's account, he knew of a certain white powder, an "X-frost," that came very near this standard; its one fault was that it carried a faint almond scent. Given the chance, he said, one might well try it once on a rabbit or a cat or dog—the effect was very good. I seized the occasion to ask Dr. Wang for a small vial of it.

Dr. Wang Zixiang would often try things on himself, alone; I thought this rather a hazardous thing, but he took it with perfect composure. Some, not understanding, wrongly supposed he was dosing himself with morphine; hearing such idle talk, he made no defense, but went his own way as before, trying things as ever.

Brother Wang Zixiang and I, living long in each other's company, formed an attachment; I felt he was truly a man of pure heart, and so we said whatever we would to each other, come to the point of setting all forms aside.

Because I had lingered too long in Tianjin, my mind was much on edge, and I asked Brother Zixiang to press Lü Yimin, and see how his work was going. But when Lü Yimin was fetched and questioned, still there was not even a shadow, to my great disappointment. To sanction Ji Hongchang, though no time-limit was set, yet counting from the day the order was received, it had already been a good many months; in point of my charge, it was hard indeed to render account—and the more so as Ji Hongchang grew daily more arrogant, so that the sooner he was destroyed the better, to be rid of the after-plague.

And so a notion stirred in me: better break with old usage and ask several of the comrades concerned in the work to come together, and let all pool their wits—perhaps some good scheme might be talked out. I put this thought to Brother Zixiang, and he agreed of course. In fact those with a part in the affair were but five: Zixiang, Wang Wen, Wu Ping, Lü Yimin, and myself.

All were indeed most keen, and though their spirits ran high, the pity was that there was no thread to lay hold of. The opinion Wang Wen put forward I thought fitted the case best. Wang Wen said: "Ji Hongchang's sphere of activity cannot leave the Northwest Army; we need only work down along that line, and I am sure there will be findings. For instance, among our own kin and old friends there may just be a present connection." All heard it and thought it likely enough; yet no one said the word, "I have a connection."

I thought to myself: since Wang Wen speaks so, perhaps he has a lead already; but then, he has run to and fro with me so long—why have I never heard him mention it?

Group Leader Lü Yimin put forward, too, that he had once been in contact with a line of this sort: they were two men who had held high military posts in the past—one of Baoding academy origin, one an old ranker—both fairly conservative in their thinking. At first he had thought to use them to collect intelligence, but they had held it beneath them. Though they spoke again and again of their many acquaintances in military and political circles, they could put forward no concrete fact; and so it had been laid aside. By the two men's origins and careers, they had no roots in the Northwest Army; but one of them, a certain Zheng, was a Henan fellow-townsman of Ji Hongchang, and might well be acquainted with him. Lü Yimin meant to seek them out and treat with them again, and perhaps thus break the present deadlock.

The conclusion reached at this gathering of us five was no more than that. Because the social connections at our disposal were not wide enough, we felt in the opening-up of the work rather powerless.

After the gathering broke up, I asked Wang Wen alone: "You say that, tracing down along this Northwest Army line, one can always find a thread; is it that among your own kin and friends you already have such a connection?"

Wang Wen answered: "Some days ago, when we were chatting on the train, I mentioned that there was a good friend of mine at Shi Yousan's side, serving as his adjutant; at the time I saw you took no notice, and so I did not think it fitting to go on."

I asked, not understanding: "But what was there unfitting in that?"

"Because my friend is in Shi Yousan's employ, and his standing differs from mine; were I to press the matter too far, I feared to draw down a misunderstanding."

Hearing him say this, I was for a moment much moved: at work of our kind, to keep so strict a measure and be careful at every turn is truly a great taxing of the wits.

In fact I had not thought so far; it was only that on the train there had been no chance to speak, and I had meant to find some occasion to discuss the matter fully, and simply never found it.

I told Wang Wen that I was a plain, straightforward sort; that henceforth, working together, it were best we each hold out our whole sincerity, with no need of any reserve, nor any need of taboos—only when all are solid and true can there be "cooperation."

Wang Wen assented, and yet again. Still I asked him to set out the full particulars of this connection, that I might take it into the compass of my thinking.

Wang Wen told me: "There is a good friend who has been my schoolfellow from childhood, and a fellow-townsman besides; his family's house and mine are not half a li apart. This man's surname is Xian—the 'xian' of 'xiansheng,' a very rare surname, easy to remember. He is Shi Yousan's attendant adjutant, and from 1930, from Kaifeng to Dalian, and again from Dalian to Tianjin, he has followed Shi Yousan throughout. Now he lodges in Shi Yousan's household in the Japanese concession at Tianjin.

"The last time I saw him was still two months ago, when we met visiting kinsfolk at our old home in Baodi county." I asked then: "If you would find him, how would you go about it?"

Wang Wen paused a moment and answered: "One way is to go straight to the Shi residence in the Japanese concession and seek him; another is to have his family carry word to him, and bid him come out and find me."

The means of contact Wang Wen named seemed to me neither of them quite fit: to go straight and seek him left a trace; to trust his family to carry word—there was no telling what day one might wait for. I asked Wang Wen: "Could one not telephone and call him out?"

Wang Wen said, as though with much assurance: "Only decide to win him over, and this matter you may safely leave to me; as for the question of how to make contact, I have a way to settle it. Once I have got hold of him, I shall report to you at once."

Good; the matter of winning over Xian Hongxia was to wait upon Wang Wen's report, and be reckoned then. Only, this did not yet join up with the sanctioning of Ji Hongchang.

I could not tarry over-long in Tianjin; there were many things to see to in Beiping, and I decided to go back once more. At the same time I hoped to gather in Beiping some intelligence of Ji Hongchang's doings.

Back in Beiping, I went to find the directly-attached communicator Fan Xing. As I mentioned in the second section, Fan Xing was the pillar of the Beiping Station's intelligence; this man had boundless resource and a touch of the inspired, and truly had ways of his own. Within three to five days he had, one report after another, come back with returns. Put together, the points that might serve for reference and offer a lead were several:

First, Ji Hongchang's aim in his activity was to unite all the forces set against the government into a single "united front," taking in even the traitors under Japanese support. This was what the Communists call the "united front." Those directing it, besides Ji Hongchang himself, were Communist elements such as Xuan Xiafu, Nan Hanchen, and Zhang Mutao.

Second, through Ji Hongchang's personal connections, they were by every device inciting, deluding, and lobbying within our own military and government departments.

Third, they did not, for the breaking-up of the units under Ji Hongchang, give over the rebuilding of armed force; and so they were still taking in and reorganizing small bands of scattered stragglers in the provinces of Hebei, Shandong, Henan, Chahar, and Suiyuan.

Fourth, at present they were opening every kind of activity from a base at the distributing-office of "The National War-Banner" in the French concession of Tianjin. This place bore the name of the "Great Red Building."

For our present need, of course it was this fourth point—"a base at the distributing-office of The National War-Banner in the French concession, called the Great Red Building"—that was of the most use. Setting aside for the moment just where in the French concession this "Great Red Building" might be, it did at least furnish a most concrete lead. If one traced down along "The National War-Banner," that openly circulated magazine, one might very likely come upon Ji Hongchang's tracks.

Not a week had passed since my return from Tianjin, and I had barely got a thread of a lead and was making ready to go to Tianjin again, when of a sudden I received from Mr. Dai in Nanjing a telegram marked "Most Urgent"—a very bolt from the blue, a thing never dreamt of, that had yet come to pass. The wording ran: "It has just come to hand that Brother Wang Zixiang of the Tianjin Station has died in strange circumstances. Ascertain the truth with all speed and report back fully, as is essential." Where was one to make head or tail of this! With no time to inform Shiwei and Wang Wen in person, I left a note and set off at once for Tianjin.

Through those two hours on the train I thought of nothing, my whole mind bound up in this matter of Wang Zixiang's sudden death. Had the news come from any other quarter, I should surely not have believed it true; but now that it was a "Most Urgent" telegram sent by Mr. Dai himself, there was not the least room for doubt. How could such a thing befall? A man like him—how could he come to such an end?

The moment I got off the train I hailed a car and drove straight to Wang Zixiang's clinic in the British concession. Going in and mounting straight to the second floor, the one who rose to come out and meet me was none other than Wu Ping—and it was he too whom I had come to see. I saw only that Brother Wu Ping wore a band of black crape about the sleeve of his Western suit, his whole face a picture of grief; before I was well seated, he burst into weeping, snot and tears, past speech. Seeing Wu Ping's bitter weeping, my own heart was sore too; yet I held myself in check, and urged him to stay his grief, for there was the sequel to see to. Wu Ping wiped his face, and told me the whole course of it, point by point:

"It happened yesterday toward evening, about half past five. I came in from outside and saw Elder Brother Zixiang lying alone on the sofa-bed. At first I thought he was resting, and called him low; but there was no stir. Going over—the light was dim and I could not see well—I switched on the lamp and looked again closely: he lay on his side, face to the wall, both eyes tight shut, his face waxen white, not a trace of blood in it. I felt his hand—ice-cold; I felt for his pulse—there was still one, but so faint one could scarce make it out. I called 'Elder Brother' twice more in his ear, hoping he might open his eyes and look, but still there was no response.

"My heart was in a panic—this was dreadful! What had happened? What was to be done? I raised my head and looked about, and found on the little stool at the bed-head several small vials, and beside them a glass syringe for injections. In that instant a doubt rose in me: had he given himself an injection?

"The more I thought the more afraid I grew, and the more anxious in my heart; I called aloud for old Guo the cook to come up at once. I swept up the little vials and the syringe and stuffed them into my pocket; and old Guo and I, one holding Elder Brother Zixiang's head from behind and one lifting his two legs in front, with no time even to say anything to old Guo, went straight down the steps toward where the car was parked. Old Guo and I laid Elder Brother Zixiang flat in the car; then I had old Guo get in first and move the head onto his knees with his hands, while I myself drove, racing to the nearest, the Mackenzie Hospital, for emergency aid.

"At first the hospital would not take him in; only after much pleading, and my mentioning too that the 'patient' was himself a practicing doctor, was he wheeled into the emergency ward.

"I scribbled a telegram draft and had old Guo hurry it to the telegraph office to send, to let old Master Wang and the Second Miss in Beiping know first, and see how the treatment went before deciding further.

"Having sent old Guo off, I squeezed into the emergency ward too, to hear the news. Watching the doctors and nurses bustle in confusion a while, and by their looks and by Elder Brother Zixiang's still lying without stir, I feared he could not be brought back.

"I asked the doctor, but he only shook his head again and again without answer; I held my patience and waited a while more, until I saw the doctor draw up the white sheet over Elder Brother Zixiang's head—and only then was it certain that he was past saving.

"I had meant to take the things from my pocket and show the doctor, that the fatal cause might be traced; but then I thought again: since Elder Brother Zixiang was past saving, those few vials and the syringe ought properly to be kept for our superiors to deal with."

At this point in Brother Wu Ping's telling, I broke in anxiously with a word: "Did the hospital inquire into anything?" He went on:

"The doctor judged that if it were not suicide, it was drug poisoning; though the possibility of murder could not wholly be ruled out either.

"And so the hospital had already reported the case to the works department. It was said that they must wait for the forensic examination to be passed before deciding what came next. Whether there would be trouble, none could yet say.

"I think Elder Brother Zixiang would never take his own life, nor could he have fallen to another's plot; still it is most likely drug poisoning."

When I had heard out Brother Wu Ping's account, my heart was full of sorrow past bearing. Having been comrade to Brother Zixiang, and got on with him most congenially, and having Mr. Dai's charge besides, I felt I ought, come what might, to go to the hospital mortuary and see him a last time. But Brother Wu Ping earnestly dissuaded me: I must on no account go; he held it better to wait until the questions from the police had been got past, and then to make an offering before the spirit-tablet and weep him a long, free weeping—would that not be the fitter course?

I asked Wu Ping: "Has old Master Wang come?" He said: "The Second Miss, Yumei [Wang Zixiang's younger sister], came ahead alone, keeping it from the old master; and when she learned that Elder Brother Zixiang was already dead, she gave me a great scolding, saying it was I who had delayed matters. The Second Miss is now staying at the Lishunde Hotel; shall we go and see her or not?"

"We ought to go and call on her—first to talk over how to see to the funeral, and also to offer condolence on behalf of our organization," I said.

The Lishunde Hotel is the first name in Tianjin; only those who go in for style and show would stay in such a place, the price frightful, and no comfort at all in living there, yet its custom held ever at a certain level.

Wu Ping went with me to the foot of the Lishunde; the desk having first asked up to the Second Miss, we went up together by the lift to the fifth floor. A boy led us into the room.

Miss Wang Yumei, plainly dressed, held an unspent reproach in her face. I offered her my "sorrow"; she did not so much as rise, but only waved a hand to bid me sit, while Wu Ping stood to one side and dared not take a seat. Miss Yumei opened at once: "Zixiang has died an unaccountable death, and one that cannot even be noised abroad—and this all for the sake of your work. You should tell your Mr. Dai that this is the Wang family showing itself sensible of the larger things. As for the trouble with the works department, I shall go once and it will be settled; there is no need for you to trouble yourselves. And Zixiang's funeral I shall see to myself, and want not a cash of your money. When all is set in order, I mean to go to Nanjing or Hangzhou to find your Mr. Dai; he must get to the very bottom of it, so that my brother Zixiang may die with his eyes closed."

I seemed to be receiving a dressing-down, and it was not for me to say much; yet in my heart I understood: Brother Zixiang's death was, eight parts in ten, from some carelessness in compounding and trying his poisons, that had gone wrong. If it were indeed so, then truly it was a case of "though I did not kill Boren." It was afterward confirmed that the remnants in those few little vials and in the syringe were all poisonous.

By this it was judged: that Station Chief Wang Zixiang had "tried a drug upon his own body" and met an accident, and that he ought too to be listed as "fallen in the line of duty."

Musing on it, I here confess, with a guilt I can never wipe away, my fault to Brother Zixiang: your loyalty and valor, your ardor, are graven on my heart; you were the forerunner of our organization, and the nameless hero I revere the most!

Afterward this Second Miss Yumei did indeed go to Hangzhou; what she said before Mr. Dai I do not know, but Mr. Dai had Wu Ping summoned to Nanjing and confined for a month—a most cruel wrong.

### 3. The Search for Ji's Traces: A Lead at Last

For the "Tianjin Station" to bring forth in one year two affairs so sapping to its vital strength was misfortune of the extreme. After Brother Wang Tianmu and his men slipped up, only half a year apart, his successor as station chief, Dr. Wang Zixiang, died suddenly in an accident too, which was more singular still. Though our superiors strove to nurse it back and make it whole, they could not for the time muster and dispose men enough; and so the post of Tianjin Station chief hung empty a long while, with no fit man to be sent to fill it.

So far as I know, apart from the one "Intelligence Group" and the two "directly-attached members" belonging under the "Tianjin Station" being transferred to the "Beiping Station" for the time to keep in touch, the rest were led directly by the Bureau headquarters in Nanjing—this too a makeshift of the moment. As for the appointment of Wu Gengshu as Tianjin Station chief, that was a matter of much later date.

Even so, the several pieces of action work I had in hand by order were not on this account broken off. The Intelligence Group of Lü Yimin, whom the late Tianjin Station chief Wang Zixiang had assigned to work with me, was likewise ordered attached to the "Beiping Station," and for the time under my direct command.

At this juncture the "Juntong" system set up an "inspectorate system" throughout. To Beiping was sent Wang Pingyi as inspector, his standing parallel to a "station," but in fact set up to supervise the "Beiping Station."

Wang Pingyi, a Shandong man, had studied politics in Russia, and had taken part in the "Investigation Section" of the Nanchang Field Headquarters that my senior schoolfellow Deng Wenyi directed. After the "Investigation Section" was merged into the "Juntong" and came under Mr. Dai's leadership—Wang was short and wiry, and though he had a touch of a "stammer," it did not hide his talent.

I was, at bottom, opposed to this system; yet this did not mean I would not cooperate with Wang Pingyi, and in fact I was not suffered to oppose the setting up of an inspector in Beiping. My reason for opposing was simple: I held that this inspectorate system merely bred inner vexation without profit to the work, for to spur on the brave man to advance, and yet lay ropes of one's own to bind and trip him, is a rather self-contradictory measure. Of course, those who upheld the system had a whole fair-sounding case of their own to make. But I stubbornly opposed it still, for some decades, right down to my days in Taiwan. Even now, out of office as I am, should anyone ask my opinion, I should still answer flatly, "Opposed"—"Opposed."

Oppose as I might, that was my own affair; and the "inspectorate system" existed for decades all the same. Since it could hold unchanged for decades, there must be some reason in it; and what that reason is—it would stray too far from the subject to tell, and this is not the place to say much of it.

In Beiping I received a telegram forwarded from Nanjing by Lü Yimin of the Tianjin "Intelligence Group," inviting me to Tianjin with all speed to meet him. This signified that his charge of the reconnaissance of Ji Hongchang might have made progress—doubtless it touched the renewed contact with the two men, Zheng and Fu.

This was still the matter, decided at the gathering of us five before Dr. Wang Zixiang's death, that Lü Yimin was to carry forward. Though the persons concerned were two, the business treated of was one. Of the two, the elder, and the more forthcoming, Mr. Zheng Enpu, a Henan man, had in early years joined Fan Zhongxiu's "Nation-Founding Henan Army," and in 1930 had been commander of the newly formed Third Army. The other, Mr. Fu Danchi, a Hebei man, had held such posts as defense commissioner of Xiangfan. To say they were then at leisure would not be amiss; only they were in no haste to seek a post, or to find a bowl of rice.

Zheng and Fu had served in military and civil office many years, most of that time in Henan. It was said they still had many old subordinates about Henan, scattered through various units and among the greenwood. In the past it had been the way, taking one's still-existing subordinates as personal capital for advancement, and these two were no exception, often making much in their talk of how many they could call up. I write this indeed as the fact, with no thought of slighting the two men; what followed will make it plain. By Group Leader Lü Yimin's own reading, the two men's real wish was still to find a way to serve the Center. On the earlier occasion, Group Leader Lü had talked with Zheng and Fu twice, but because he could get no purchase, it had cooled off. Of late he had renewed the contact, to see whether a lead on Ji Hongchang might not be found through the two of them. And this stroke was, as it happened, hit right on the mark: in talk, Zheng and Fu let out that they had once met Ji Hongchang, and that their impression of him was, moreover, fairly good.

This was the sole reason Group Leader Lü had asked me to Tianjin; and when I learned that the reconnaissance of Ji Hongchang's whereabouts had at last got a thread, I was overjoyed indeed. Group Leader Lü told me the whole course of the thing was this: in the talk between the two, Zheng and Fu, and Lü Yimin, Mr. Zheng Enpu let fall unawares a word—"A few days ago I met and talked with Ji Hongchang once at a hotel." Zheng Enpu's impression of Ji Hongchang differed from ours; he said: "Ji Hongchang recovered lost land in eastern Chahar, and grappled with the Japanese devils for months on end—he may fairly be called an anti-Japanese hero. A man of this kind the Center ought not to bear old grudges against, but should give a chance to make himself anew, and entrust with heavy charge; why instead disarm him and detain him and put him to the law?"

Lü Yimin asked Zheng Enpu in turn: "Is Ji Hongchang truly resisting Japan? Or has he some other design? Do you know that he takes his orders from the Communist Party?"

Zheng answered beside the question: "Ji Hongchang holds that the Center does not resist Japan, but blindly pursues compromise, and that this is why the 'Anti-Japanese Allied Army' has fought on alone, answering the great people's demand. Ji declares that let the Central Government but once launch the war of resistance, and he will be the first to step forth and ask leave to lead the van." These two sentences were, to the letter, of Communist teaching. So Lü Yimin nailed him with a word: "Whose orders, then, does Ji Hongchang take at present?"

Zheng replied: "Ji has declared again and again that what he means to do now is to unite all anti-Japanese strength and patriotic elements to withstand the foreign foe together." Hearing this, it seemed the two, Zheng and Fu, might be over-honest, and had been drugged by Ji Hongchang's sugar-coated poison. How were they to know that Ji Hongchang was even then engaged in rebellion under the Communists' secret direction and design?

I felt that, matters standing thus, to win the two gentlemen Zheng and Fu to a sincere cooperation must take much more labor yet. I then talked it over with Group Leader Lü: might he find some occasion to introduce me to Zheng and Fu for a meeting? There was no need to scruple over anything: whether they believed it or not, let me first tell them plainly that I was sent by the Center. I should see how they reacted, and then decide the further steps. By now I had made up my mind to win them over by frontal persuasion.

Mr. Zheng Enpu was single in Tianjin; Mr. Fu Danchi had his family with him; both lived in the French concession, their two homes not far apart. Through Lü Yimin's arrangement, a meeting was fixed at Mr. Fu Danchi's residence. No sooner had I exchanged a few words with Messrs. Zheng and Fu than I first revised my view of them as "honest men."

Mr. Zheng Enpu was scrupulous in the extreme, of a lofty and pure moral cultivation; every sentence, every word of his came out only after thought, choice, and care. This did not stand for "depth" in him, or a hiding of what he had; it should rather be read as "steadiness," which comes nearer the mark.

Mr. Fu Danchi was seasoned in the ways of the world, of the sort well weathered by wind and frost, not so shallow as we who had not gone deep into the world showed ourselves.

For one so little seasoned in the world as I to deal with these two, I must own I had some years of tempering yet before me; and apart from being solid and true, sincere and earnest, there was no other means to bring the two of them to a glad and willing accord.

On such an occasion, were I still to bring out my cover identity—that I was head of the "Beiping branch of the Nanjing Military Magazine Society," or a special correspondent of the Nanjing "China Daily," and the like—it would carry too little weight, nor would they be sure to credit it; and so I made my true identity known. This was, by the working code, not permitted; but to accomplish the task, I acted for expedience.

Zheng and Fu, though they had long had word of our organization, could by no means make out just what the "Juntong," the "Special Services Department," the "Renaissance Society," and so on really were. But at a first meeting one could not of course speak too baldly; it was enough if they could believe me no counterfeit. What else, perhaps, won the two men's trust was that I was a "Whampoa student."

Once we had, from declaring identity, each set out his standing, and gone so far as to profess mutual respect, we turned by degrees to the main point that answered my own subjective wish.

Mr. Zheng Enpu and Ji Hongchang were fellow-townsmen of Henan; though they had never served together, they were old acquaintances. How the most recent meeting had been arranged, Mr. Zheng passed over lightly; and I, fearing to rouse Zheng's suspicions, did not think it fit to press. From Mr. Zheng Enpu's tone, he cherished a sympathy for Ji Hongchang throughout; and if this were not corrected now, it would greatly hinder our future cooperation.

For this reason I gravely declared my attitude: "However Ji Hongchang may be as a man, or whatever abilities he may have—even granting one might pity the 'hard road' by which he, of common soldier's beginnings, rose to be a provincial chairman and a commander-in-chief—we ought all to stand firm on our own ground, and set the nation's interest, and the honor or shame of the race, before all else." And I asked the two, Zheng and Fu, frankly: "Should I give a brief account of what Ji Hongchang has done these last years, would the two of you have any interest in hearing it?" Zheng and Fu together: "We should be glad to." I put a few of the past facts into some order and said: "In 1930 Ji Hongchang took part in the Yan–Feng revolt; after its failure his troops accepted reorganization by the Center, yet he would not obey the order to suppress the Communists, and actually withdrew his troops and ceased fighting. The Center, in leniency, did not call him to account, but sent him abroad to study.

"After Ji Hongchang returned home, instead of serving the country to redeem his past faults, he dared slip off to Hubei to set his old subordinates to mutiny; the matter failed and he fled, and only then did the government issue a warrant for his arrest.

"Feng Yuxiang formed the 'People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army,' with Ji as its chief contriver; when Feng resigned, Ji Hongchang and Fang Zhenwu raised a separate standard, renamed it the 'Anti-Japanese, Chiang-Chastising Army,' and, far from resisting Japan, turned their gun-muzzles about to fire on their own countrymen.

"I ask you two gentlemen: are such doings the proper deeds of a so-called 'anti-Japanese hero'? And if I say he is a member of the Communist Party, will you two credit it?"

Zheng Enpu and Fu Danchi were silent a while, and did not answer my question; I guessed they would by no means believe Ji Hongchang a Communist.

I went straight on and pointed out plainly: "Ji Hongchang and his sort, under the banner of a united anti-Japanese front, are in fact splitting the whole nation's unity and setting themselves against the Center—surely this is a fact plain for all eyes to see?"

At these words the two looked at each other and gave a slight nod, as though they had taken the point; yet they showed no attitude. I did not wish to press too hard, and so stopped there, leaving a space that the two of them might have time to exchange views.

In a fairly grave air the talk ended. When we had fixed the next meeting, I purposely left a hook: I asked the two of them to keep up their contact with Ji, and see just what game he was playing.

After Lü Yimin and I had taken our leave, I asked Yimin on the road: "Do you think Zheng and Fu really met Ji Hongchang? For you have only their word for it, and we can prove it no way; so we ought to think how to make sure. I think, before I see them a second time, you might go once more—first to hear their impressions after their talk with me, and, more important, to learn just how things stand between them and Ji."

In the week that followed, though I went back and forth between Beiping and Tianjin often, I met with the two, Zheng and Fu, four times, and the distance between us closed. They formally declared themselves willing to take part in the work; and I promised to recommend to my superiors at once that a "Military Group" be set up in Tianjin, of which Zheng and Fu should have joint charge. Within a few days Mr. Dai wired back, approving my proposal, and consigning it to the direction and supervision of the "Beiping Station," its personnel and funds too to pass through the "Beiping Station."

The paperwork and working procedure of our kind of work differ greatly from those of an ordinary office; and this was all shaped by the difference of the actual circumstances and mode of activity, in no way any deliberate parade of novelty. In fact, our "requests for instruction" and "replies" rested wholly on a single sheet of "telegraph text." The so-called "telegraph text" was but the "cipher" copied down off the wireless set, all to the eye a string of Arabic numerals, which had then to be turned by the "cipher book" into plain script before the content of the words would show. Ordinarily we merely copied it out onto a small slip of paper—and this was the "official document," so recognized by ourselves, that touched the great affairs of the nation and men's lives. Were one to hand this little slip to someone who had never taken part in the actual work, would he believe it held force equal to a document sealed with an official stamp? Strange as it is to say, over how many years, I never once met a man who raised any doubt of this "document"!

Touching the matter of Zheng Enpu's and Fu Danchi's being approved to take part in the work, I informed the two of them; and though the "Military Group" stood equal in rank with the "Intelligence Group," because I could not stay always in Tianjin, it was still consigned to Lü Yimin to keep liaison as my representative—that is, the state of things held as before, the difference being only that a formal working relation was now set up.

That a working relation should be reckoned established on a single word, the two men Zheng and Fu accepted quite naturally, and raised no other question of any kind. I of course kept my respect for the two of them as before.

Only, I had one reserve: I could not for the present tell them plainly that I bore the mission of sanctioning Ji Hongchang. For one thing, I dreaded lest they take alarm; for another, a whole mutual trust was not yet built up between us.

Mr. Zheng Enpu was a man of great "openhandedness," respected on all sides among his fellows, so that many friends called him "Third Master Zheng" to mark their esteem. Especially in a place like Tianjin, whatever a man's trade—scholar, farmer, artisan, merchant—when two met and exchanged courtesies, the first word out was "Master": say, "Master Zhang," "Master Li"; and this one calling out a single "Master," the other would return three "Masters, Masters, Masters"—this too a social custom. For which reason I too changed my address and called Zheng Enpu "Third Master Zheng," down until, after the victory in the eight-year War of Resistance, when we met again in Beiping, we changed once more to "Elder Brother." But that is a later tale.

For the present, the matter of laying a work-task on Zheng Enpu and Fu Danchi rather set me hesitating. What we urgently needed just then was any intelligence bearing on Ji Hongchang; yet in Zheng Enpu's notion, to do intelligence was no dignified affair. It was over Group Leader Lü Yimin's asking him to collect intelligence, at the first, that things had turned so unpleasant they nearly came to a break. That I now proposed to our superiors a "Military Group," a thing there had never been before, for the two men Zheng and Fu, was all too for the sake of accommodating the fact. As for the "Military Group's" basic tasks—such as setting puppet troops to come over, and liaison with popular armed forces—these were no matter of the utmost urgency; in the order of execution, the reconnaissance of Ji Hongchang's doings should still be listed first. As things now stood, apart from avoiding such terms of art as "collecting intelligence," what was in fact still required was a full grasp of Ji Hongchang's movements and activity. After I had set out to Zheng Enpu and Fu Danchi the considered wording I had prepared, the two, Zheng and Fu, at once nodded assent.

Third Master Zheng was, as fitted him, a man good as gold at his word; and so of Ji Hongchang's whereabouts we now had some little knowledge. Though we could not yet act on it, it was a great stride forward from before. From what Zheng and Fu supplied us to understand, we learned that Ji's chief purpose in meeting Zheng and Fu from time to time was to set Mr. Zheng to go back to western Henan, and Mr. Fu to southern Hebei, each to work upon his old subordinates, and upon what small armed forces had already taken shape, to carry forward the so-called "united anti-Japanese front"—which was in fact no more than to serve as the vanguard clearing the Communist Party's road.

Just as we were closely watching Ji Hongchang's doings, Third Master Zheng suddenly told me: "Tomorrow at three in the afternoon, Ji has asked me and Brother Danchi to meet at the Jiaotong Hotel in the French concession, saying he wishes to introduce us to two new friends, and that we may all confer over all the questions bearing on the setting-out and after."

I asked: "Who are the new friends?" Zheng said: "I do not know."

I asked again: "Third Master, did they say which room?" Zheng said: "The man who came told us it was No. 37 on the fifth floor, and charged me particularly to remember the number '437' and it would do."

No need to ask more; this was enough. I too fixed the room-number "437" fast in mind; and by Third Master Zheng's look, he had not yet perceived what we were about to do.

### 4. What Is Commonly Called Meeting the Moment as It Comes

This Mr. Zheng Enpu told me with his own lips; he said: "Ji Hongchang has sent a man to ask me and Danchi to meet tomorrow, saying it is to introduce two new friends." My judgment was: the so-called new friends must be "cadres" the Communist Party had dispatched, very likely the men who were to accompany Zheng and Fu severally to the northern-Henan and southern-Hebei country to carry on armed activity.

I fully believed Mr. Zheng would never speak me false, and so this was truly a rare good chance, one we had looked for long. Having the chance, of course we must grip it fast, and on no account let it slip lightly. Once I had the news from Third Master Zheng's side, I at once called together Lü Yimin, Wu Ping, Wang Wen, and the rest to meet, hoping to settle a plan of execution simple and easy to carry out.

First I set out in brief, by way of an analysis-report, the situation as already understood:

The source of the news was the two men we had spoken of before, Zheng Enpu and Fu Danchi, both now approved to take part in our work; the lead they now furnished, and Ji Hongchang's stopping-place, were, by my own reckoning, of a high dependability, past all doubt. What needs particular note is that of this business now toward, our sanctioning of Ji, the two men Zheng and Fu knew nothing.

By expectation, at the moment we made our move, the two men Zheng and Fu would very likely be together with Ji; and so we must tell friend from foe clearly, and be answerable for guarding the safety of the two of them, without the least slip.

Our adversary was not one wily old warlord alone; behind him was besides a pack of Communist elements full of crooked schemes. Facing a strong foe, we of course had nothing to fear, but neither might we take it lightly.

I had one assumption of my own: that the other side (Ji and those about him) would not carry weapons. My reason was very simple: since they dared appoint a meeting in a public place, it proved that in their minds they had set up no "guard" at all.

The place was in a bustling, thriving quarter, the time just in the afternoon when ten thousand people are astir; the general setting was as though heaven-arranged, most favorable to us.

Our action target was one man only, Ji Hongchang; this man's treason was warranted, he was under warrant on record, and he was even now engaged in anti-government activity at the Communists' instigation. His crime went far beyond an ordinary traitor's.

Putting together all the points above, the whole situation lay within my grasp; and if each did his part, we of course had every prospect of success.

When my analysis-report was done, I asked next for each man's view, and bade them raise questions without regard to form.

Wang Wen held the thing was as easy as turning the hand: only let the target be found, and he could "take him at a touch." Wu Ping too held: the hardest was the finding of a sure lead, and now that even an inside line was to hand, all that followed would of course go smoothly and hopefully. To hear Wang Wen and Wu Ping talk, there was much of the newborn calf's not fearing the tiger.

Lü Yimin voiced no particular opinion; he only stated the thing that fell within his part. He said: "I have already appointed a working comrade of the group, Miss Yang, to keep liaison with Zheng Enpu and the rest, and news can be passed at any time."

At this point in our talk, I asked the three comrades present once more to raise questions; all were silent a while, and signified there was nothing more to put forward. And so I gravely allotted each man his task in this action. Though the case was different, this had a like effect to a troop-order handed down in battle. The points I set forth were as follow:

Sanction target: Ji Hongchang;

Place of execution: the Jiaotong Hotel in the French concession and its vicinity;

Time appointed: about three in the afternoon of the 9th of November;

Executioner: Comrade Wang Wen;

To take up and cover: Comrade Wu Ping;

Reconnaissance on the ground: to be the several charge of the two Comrades Wang Wen and Wu Ping;

I myself would coordinate Comrade Lü Yimin and the intelligence source, and would choose a fixed spot near the Jiaotong Hotel, keeping in touch at any moment with Comrades Wang Wen and Wu Ping, so as to meet any sudden change; the task done, each would withdraw of himself, and, save some special circumstance arising, we four should meet again at the same spot at eight o'clock that evening. The point to note particularly was that the safety of the two men Zheng and Fu must be guarded; should there be any slip in it, one would be reported to our superiors and dealt with for disobedience of orders; but if it was the safeguarding of Zheng and Fu that hindered the work's going forward, the fault lay not with the comrades, and I would bear the whole of it myself. The allotment of the work done, all raised no objection, and it was taken thus as the final decision, to be set in train at once, each severally by the several rules laid down.

There was one matter bearing directly on the work that had to be settled at once, yet could not be laid down hard-and-fast by me in my capacity as commander; there being no help for it, I had to talk it over privately with Wu Ping. For Wang Wen had come from Beiping and carried no weapon, and to go back and fetch one—though there was still time enough—yet should some mishap befall, would it not spoil the great affair? I knew Wu Ping had always been fond of playing with guns, of many kinds, and had shown them off before me a good many times; so I meant to borrow one of his to use. Wu Ping was most openhanded: he readily agreed to take Wang Wen to his lodging at once to choose one, and, should only the thing succeed, to make Wang Wen a present of the gun for a keepsake. In two or three words a great problem was solved.

Our gathering of four settled no few questions, and thereupon we set out each his own way, each to his own charge: Lü Yimin went first, back to his lodging to await liaison; Wang Wen and I took Wu Ping's car to his home to fetch a gun. Wang Wen was used to a "Mauser"; this pistol, in the Beiping–Tianjin country, they all call the "box," and some the "wood-shell," the German-made being the best. Wang Wen chose a No. 3; besides the rounds in the chamber, he took an extra clip, filled up to hold ten in reserve. By Wang Wen's mind, it were best to carry it on him now, to save more running about. I was rather against it in my heart, but for shame could not check his high spirits, and only let him tuck it into his waistband. Luckily it was cold weather, and one wore many clothes, so that unless one looked particularly it did not show.

Coming out from Wu Ping's lodging, we made ready to go together to the Jiaotong Hotel in the French concession, and take a room first, to grow familiar with the actual ground that might become the scene.

Wu Ping parked the car in the alley behind the "Quanyechang"; without going far, crossing the main road, we reached the Jiaotong Hotel. Led by Wu Ping, we went by lift straight up to the fifth floor, and asking at the desk, were told there was not one empty room on the whole floor, and bidden ask again on the fourth. Going down one story to the fourth floor, Wu Ping alone dealt with the desk; I did not catch clearly what few words they muttered, but saw only the boy take a key and go ahead to open a door for us.

At the push of the door, it was black as pitch, nothing to be seen; switching on the lamp, the light was still dim—truly a place the day never came into. Brother Wu Ping told us: "This hotel is much fallen off from what it was—the third and fourth floors are all of them flashy, gaudy doings; those who really mean to rest and stretch their legs, or pass a night, are too few indeed. Just now I said we too had come for a lark, and that is why we got the room."

My temper is quick; I greatly wished Wang Wen and Wu Ping first to reconnoiter the ways up and down, and then go to No. "437" on the fifth floor to look—the room where they were to meet—so that when the time came all might be done at ease. But Wu Ping's and Wang Wen's mind was that this was too simple a matter, they could do it at any moment. They proposed that I go back, and leave the business here to them; nothing would go awry, and I might set my mind wholly at rest.

In fact they were quite right; these things truly did not need three men to come together over. And so I fell in with their view, and decided to go back first alone: if I could, in cool blood, plot the whole out a little more, perhaps I might make the general design more thorough.

The next forenoon, Wu and Wang came to my temporary lodging at Xiaobailou, to lay their reconnaissance report before me in person, and to set out some of the arrangements already made. Wu Ping said: "Room No. 437 on the fifth floor of the Jiaotong Hotel—all last night there was not a stir; the lamp in the room did not light either, as though no one lodged within. This room, it may be, was never let out at all, or someone booked it and had not yet moved in.

"There may be things besides we have not thought of; but for fear of rousing suspicion, one had only to ask at the desk and it would be plain.

"We tried it—from the ground floor by lift up to the fifth, out of the lift to the door of No. 437 is but a dozen-odd paces; and from the door of No. 437 to the head of the downward stair is likewise a dozen-odd paces; these two ways in and out are about equal in distance from No. 437.

"Up and down there are two lifts by turns, and those that work the lifts are all men-servants in uniform.

"Coming down the stairs from the fifth floor, there are eight flights, seventy-four steps in all, each flight of nine steps, only the lowest flight being eleven. Going down fast, one can reach the ground in a minute. The thing to be careful of is that the light is too dim, and one misstep and you risk pitching headlong down.

"The ground floor is not large; and every afternoon there is an unbroken coming and going of people up and down by lift. Off the stairs, three to five paces bring one to the main door. Out of the main door, at this hour of three in the afternoon, the whole street is full of people; whichever way one goes, or across the road to the other side, in the blink of an eye one is lost from view.

"The constable on point-duty in the road is busy directing traffic; the nearest post to the Jiaotong Hotel is fifty feet and more away. Should there be a gunshot on the fifth floor within the hotel, then, reckoning by the din of voices and the clang of the trams, very likely it would not be heard, or one could not tell what the sound was.

"Should the constable on post find an incident within the hotel and come running at once, it would take him a minute and more at the fastest, for he must part a dense, thronging crowd.

"As for the patrolmen one sees now and then, two to a beat—at what time they patrol to what place, that is hard indeed to foretell."

Wu Ping summed up all the points thus understood: "This is the harvest of our reconnaissance, the two of us; I believe we have done it thoroughly and solidly."

Wang Wen then added a word: "The room we took yesterday on the fourth floor we have given up; afterward, giving the boy two dollars more for a drink, we took a room on the fifth, No. 422, which we still hold now. This room is kitty-corner across from No. 437, not far off; if one push open the door and look through the crack toward No. 437, one can see it clear as clear."

I listened carefully to Wu Ping and Wang Wen through to the end; and on what they had said, adding what my own thinking could reach to, put forward a few opinions for them to weigh well. I said:

"The first is the question of at what time to act. If one act before three o'clock, launching while the two, Zheng and Fu, have not yet arrived, one can indeed safeguard Zheng and Fu; but at that moment there are many things not yet certain—for instance, by what means to get the door opened? Is the action target Ji Hongchang surely inside? These must all be weighed. Better wait until Zheng and Fu have gone in, talked, come out, and gone downstairs; then we shall have the chance, through this whole course, to observe solidly a great many circumstances, and at that time may pick the fittest moment to launch.

"There is a kind of chance that falls out by hap, which of course may be met with but not readily sought—say, when you two are going up, you chance to be in the very lift as Ji Hongchang; then what is there to waver over? Naturally one decides on the instant.

"There is besides the question of withdrawing afterward; the principle is safety first. I think the lift not so good as the stairs, for the lift must be waited for, its timing cannot be controlled, and in the lift one is at another's mercy; the stairs one can take of one's own will, and in time it is not slower than the lift, and should there be some hindrance, there is still room to parry.

"As for coming out of the Jiaotong Hotel and whether one might run into a patrolman—though the likelihood is small, one cannot 'trust to luck' either; so I mean to talk it over with Lü Yimin, and ask him to pick a man of stout build to move about near the Jiaotong Hotel from half past two to half past four this afternoon, charged solely with watching the patrolmen's movements, against all chance."

Besides this, there were many things not to be foreseen, that might arise at any moment and place; and all that must rest on the executing comrades' meeting the moment as it came.

The two comrades Wu Ping and Wang Wen held the opinions I had furnished all within reason, and gladly accepted them.

I looked at the time; it was now eleven-thirty in the forenoon. They asked me to go and eat lunch together; but I thought to use the interval to make a trip to Lü Yimin's home—first to hear whether there was any fresh news from Third Master Zheng's side, and also to ask Lü Yimin to lend a man's strength, so as to make full-proof provision against the patrolmen. Before we three parted, we fixed to meet at half past two at the Zizhulin coffee-room.

I came to Lü Yimin's, where Third Master Zheng had no fresh news. For the man I asked him to lend to help, he meant to introduce a young fellow named Chen Guorui, and asked me to decide. Lü said: "Chen Guorui is the younger brother of our group's liaison, Miss Yang Yushan; he does not like study, has a touch of the wild in him, and has long wished to join our work; the pity is that he can do nothing yet, but given the chance to be trained, he is good stuff." I asked him to call the youth for me to see; and one look—tall, dark, and strapping, all muscle from head to foot. I asked whether he knew Tianjin, and his whole mouthful was Tianjin talk. There was no time then for much talk; I asked him first to help this once, and afterward to recommend him to our superiors. The thing was settled thus. Yet I still wondered in my heart: his sister was surnamed Yang—why was he surnamed Chen?

I drafted two telegrams and took them myself to the wireless set to send. Then I went on to Zizhulin to meet Wu and Wang. When I got there they had already eaten and were waiting for me; I ordered a sandwich to stay my hunger. Since no fresh circumstance had arisen, and what needed preparing had all been done, and what needed saying all said, they rose to go back to the Jiaotong Hotel, while I stayed on here as the hub of liaison.

This Zizhulin coffee-room was on the same street as the Jiaotong Hotel, a bare dozen storefronts apart; sitting within and looking out, one could see the passers-by come and go in a stream—and sure enough, among the passers-by was the temporary helper Chen Guorui.

The time was not yet three, and my heart had begun already to tighten; at this moment, save to wait still and quiet, there was not a whit of strength one could put to use. Five past three—not a stir; naturally it would not be so soon. Eight past three—again I saw Chen Guorui pacing slowly along the sidewalk. Twelve past three—the need to pass water pressed hard; I thought to force it down—no, but then I feared just at that moment something might happen. Quarter past three—back from the privy to my seat, I ordered another cup of hot coffee, telling the shop to brew it strong, the bitterer the more bracing. I guessed Wu Ping and the rest might just then be waiting their chance.

A few more minutes went by; my heart was ill at ease. I tipped a box of matches out on the table and counted them odd and even—not to divine good luck or ill, but to steady my nerves by it.

Two minutes short of half past three, more and more on edge, I could not go on even counting the matches. Just at this pass, Wu Ping alone pushed open the door and came in, a look of blank dismay on him—could something have gone amiss? Wu Ping sat down and said low: "The room has been empty all along." I asked: "And Wang Wen?" Wu Ping answered: "He has stayed up there to await the next instruction; and he is unwilling to give it up besides, and would wait and see a while more."

The state of things had changed; anxiety turned to vexation, but there was no help but to steady down and meet the difficulties of the moment. I asked Wu Ping to tell me first how things had gone just now—how had they learned the room was empty? Wu Ping said: "From two fifty-five we watched No. 37 through the door-crack, the two of us looking by turns, and never once saw a stir. By all reckoning, even were there no one in the room, Zheng and Fu ought to have arrived about three; yet clear on to twenty past three neither of them came. The host not there, the invited 'guests' not come—there was of course something odd in it. We two talked it over, and there was no help but to go straight at it and ask the boy."

Wu Ping went on: "We called the boy, and on the plea that two men to a room was inconvenient, thought to take another room; since that No. 37 was empty of lodgers, let us have it. The boy answered: 'That room, though no one lodges in it, the desk has already taken a man's deposit; if you would use it, I shall talk it over with my mates, and we can spare it you for an hour or two, sir; longer, I fear, would be hard to manage.'

"I knew the boy was after a bit of extra; but it also confirmed that that No. 437 was indeed booked by someone who had not come—and as to why it was booked and left empty, that was the very crux of the question. I fooled the boy that I was going out to meet someone, and would settle it when I had met him and come back. So I asked Wang Wen to stay on in the hotel, while I came down first to report and take instruction."

I could truly not fathom the reason of it; and Third Master Zheng was by no means a man to make a mystery for its own sake—yet surely he ought to keep an appointment? Just at the knot where I could not undo this puzzle, Lü Yimin came in together with Miss Yang Yushan. Miss Yang led by the hand besides a little girl of five or six.

This was just the hour of afternoon tea; we ordered a few kinds of refreshment, and, especially for the little sister brought along, a cup of cocoa. To look at, was this not a family out strolling and taking refreshment? Who would have made out that we were the very executors charged with a heavy duty, to root out evil? Only, were someone really to sit to one side and watch our looks with cold eyes, that would be another matter: save the child, we several all wore grave faces, tense in look and color.

I fixed my eyes on Lü Yimin, waiting for him to say quickly just what had gone amiss; but he let Yang Yushan speak first. Miss Yang then lowered her voice and explained the cause, saying: "I was charged by Mr. Lü to keep liaison with Zheng and Fu; today after noon, before one o'clock, I took my nephew and went to the Fu house; the two of them met there at the Fu house, and at half past two in the afternoon went to keep the appointment, and came back only at five minutes to four.

"After the two, Messrs. Zheng and Fu, came back, they were themselves at a loss over this sudden change of the meeting-place; Mr. Zheng said: 'It was fixed for the Jiaotong, and then changed at the last to the Guomin—one really cannot make out what game is being played.'"

I waited for Miss Yang to go on, and she continued: "I asked Mr. Zheng, from the Jiaotong to the Guomin, then who informed you of it? Mr. Zheng described the scene at the time: 'About two fifty we reached the Jiaotong Hotel, and were just about to get into the lift, when suddenly a man slipped out from one side; we did not know him, yet he knew our names. This man, apologizing all the while, and waving his hand to have us follow him outward, murmured low, "Might I trouble you two gentlemen for a few more steps; Mr. Ji and the others await you respectfully on the second floor opposite." And so, following him, we came instead to the Guomin Hotel.'"

I thought in my heart: the rascal! To shift ground at the last—this crafty trick is a thing only the Communist Party is most practiced at playing. This being so, it might be he had already turned the tables, host for guest, and that our action had perhaps fallen under their arranging.

At this point in Miss Yang's telling, I unwittingly caught sight of Chen Guorui outside the glass window, peering in at us; I quickly asked Lü Yimin to call him in to rest a while and eat a little—and perhaps there was some weightier matter I should want his help in.

Plotting in my heart the while, I asked Miss Yang to go on. Miss Yang, still relaying Mr. Zheng's words, said: "Danchi and I came to the second floor of the Guomin Hotel, to a room right facing the head of the stairs, the number seeming to be 138; the man who had brought us up pushed open the door and let us in—and, ah! the room was full of people. Besides Ji Hongchang, I knew one more, Ren Yingqi. Ji did not even introduce us, but showed us into the inner room; and in came after us a lean short man of some forty years, and Ji Hongchang introduced this as the new friend we had spoken of."

Hearing to here, I could not hold back from breaking in with a word to Miss Yang: "The room-number was 138—you would not have it wrong?" She said: "I heard it very clearly, it was 138; if it be not so, then it must be that Mr. Zheng himself remembered it wrong."

I looked at my watch; it was now four forty-five in the afternoon, more than an hour past the time of their meeting, and there was no knowing whether Ji Hongchang was still in the hotel. Thinking of this I reproached myself inwardly, deep in fault: for in the disposition of the work this was a great hole—I ought at the outset to have set a man close on the heels of the two, Zheng and Fu, and then we should not have lost this chance. As things now stood, there was nothing for it but to mend the fold after the sheep is lost, and shift ground at once and press hard after. No sooner said than done: I ran my eye over those at the neighboring tables, who seemed to pay no heed to our doings, and, to save the moment, taking no thought for much, there in that coffee-shop made the following urgent dispositions:

I asked Comrade Lü Yimin to hold the two, Zheng Enpu and Fu Danchi, fast at once, and on no account to part from them until word was received, for the safety of all. I asked Miss Yang Yushan, together with Chen Guorui and Yang's little niece, to make up the show of a small family, and buying near at hand some carry-along oddments, to move at all speed into the Guomin Hotel, and taking "138" for their mark, to set about reconnaissance forthwith.

Lü Yimin, Miss Yang, and Chen Guorui might go out together; Lü Yimin was charged to explain to the Yang brother and sister Ji Hongchang's build and features and marks, that they might tell him.

I asked Comrade Wu Ping to go first back to the Jiaotong Hotel, and call Comrade Wang Wen here to meet me; then to go to the alley behind the Quanyechang, drive the car out, and park it opposite here—that is, at the roadside outside the main door of the Guomin Hotel.

Comrade Wu Ping's task thereafter was to receive the signal Chen Guorui should convey from the Guomin Hotel, and pass this signal on to Wang Wen, who would be with me.

Once Comrade Wang Wen had entered the Guomin Hotel, Comrade Wu Ping was still to stay in the car at his post, ready at any moment to take him up.

Miss Yang's reconnaissance on the spot was the hardest; the situation being unclear, I could not lay down rules rashly, but must ask Miss Yang to use her wits and act for expedience. If it were proven Ji had not yet left, Chen Guorui was to convey it with all speed to Wu Ping at the roadside; and Miss Yang might then withdraw of herself with her little niece.

The allotment done, I signed to them all to go, to carry out their tasks severally or together. About five or six minutes later Wang Wen came. He is always silent, and what should not be said he never adds a word to; but this once he showed himself out of patience, and said: "Third Master Zheng told it as large as life, and now how is there not even a shadow of it? Can there be some trick within?" This was small blame to Wang Wen: from the day before, in high glee, rubbing his fists and whetting his palms, he had made ready to show his mettle, and to have a change come at the last was in truth a whole breast of hot blood doused with a basin of cold water—let it be who it might, he would feel it a great vexation.

I comforted Wang Wen, and roused his spirit besides, saying: "The chance is still there, right before us; whether it can be had will show in a moment. Brother Wu Ping will drive the car to the opposite side of the road; we watch the sign he makes, and shall know whether the man is there or not. For our object has now shifted to the Guomin Hotel opposite, and so we have disposed anew, and all are gone severally to their tasks. Charged to enter the Guomin Hotel and reconnoiter the truth are Yang Yushan and her brother; if Ji is still within, the chance is not lost, and the odds of success are high still—it will rest wholly on your showing. If he has already gone, our thread of work remains, and there will be great chances yet hereafter."

Even as I spoke, Wu Ping's car was already drawn up on the far side of the road, but twenty or thirty meters from the Guomin Hotel's main door. He wedged the car into an open space, keeping a fair distance fore and aft—at a glance a safety measure, that when he moved off the way out should not be blocked. Having parked, he took out a chamois and wiped the windscreen: the sign that he was ready.

Wang Wen and I, the two of us, kept anxious watch in the coffee-room; Wu Ping sat pent alone in his car; and so, far apart, we faced each other, longing for the signal to come from the Guomin Hotel side.

At such a moment, what is a man's mood, what does he feel? Only one who has stood in the place can know it. And each man, by the difference of where he stands, differs again in his taste of it. Someone once asked me what it was like for me then; I truly cannot paint it, and remember only that I drank a great deal of water and still felt my mouth go dry, and yet had, again and again, to make water.

### 5. Lost by a Hair, yet Recouped at Sunset

In the spoken report Lü Yimin and Yang Yushan made to me afterward, they described in detail the course of things then. Set down in brief, the general shape of it was this:

When they left the Zizhulin coffee-room together, it was already four fifty-seven in the afternoon. On the road Lü Yimin described Ji Hongchang's looks and marks to Yang Yushan, pointing out over and over that the tall, big-framed man with a face full of side-whiskers was the one.

His charge given, Lü Yimin went off to Fu Danchi's home; he meant to call Zheng Enpu out and take the two of them to eat at some small restaurant, and so hold them fast—there being no more natural pretext he could think of otherwise.

Yang Yushan and Chen Guorui, leading the child, first went up to the second floor of the Quanyechang and bought a canvas hand-bag, then bought half a pound of wool and some odds and ends to carry in hand; passing a toy stall, the child clamored for a rubber ball, and one was bought for her. Coming out onto the main street and walking a few steps, they felt the hand-bag too light and flimsy to look right, and went up again to buy some old books to stuff into it; and now, weighty and solid, it looked the part. And so they walked to the Guomin Hotel, not far off, went in, and took a room.

The brother and sister played a young married pair, the niece serving for a little daughter; to look at, they were truly a family. They said they had come to Tianjin from the town of Gegu down the coast to visit kin, their bedding and baggage left for the time at the wharf, and many gifts for their kin besides, which they would fetch once the room was settled. In the end they took a room on the second floor—no chance, this, for this hotel let rooms to travelers and merchants only on that one second-floor story, some dozens of rooms; higher up, on the third and fourth floors, the greater part were engaged wholesale by the madams who kept singing-girls, and what was left over was kept for the guests to use as "balconies."

Up on the second floor, at the turn to the left, was the No. 130 they had taken; Yang Yushan, reckoning by the run of the numbering, made out that No. 138 ought to be kitty-corner to the right of the stair-head. Waiting till the boy who saw to the tea had gone, the brother and sister talked it over: the first step of reconnaissance ought to be, by what means to get open the door of No. 138 and see what was within, so as to make certain whether Ji Hongchang was still there or not. To ask the boy straight was scarcely fit; to feign knocking at the wrong door would startle the grass and alarm the snake. What then to do? Yang Yushan, tilting her head up and looking at the ceiling while she cast about in her mind, caught at a glance a transom standing open above the door-frame, and a thought struck her—Yang Yushan had her way.

To make sure, Yang Yushan opened her own door and looked at the rooms on both sides, to left and right: not only this one had a transom—every room, it turned out, was the same, the difference being only that some were shut and some already propped open.

Just at this pass, she saw only a knot of three men, a pair of two, turn into the corridor opposite; but which room they went to she could not see. Yang Yushan quickly had Chen Guorui go round from this end to meet them, and see just which room they entered.

Now Yang Yushan watched No. 138 again: the door shut tight, not a stir. Chen Guorui came back from his round and told his sister: "That lot just now were all of No. 145; there seem to be people in it still." The brother and sister, leading the niece, went back into their room, and watched No. 138's door intently to see whether anyone went in or out; some seven or eight minutes passed, and still not a stir.

Yang Yushan was silent a while, and then made her decision: she charged Chen Guorui at once to begin marking her movements. Yushan said to Chen Guorui: "I shall now go first and probe out the truth of No. 138; if there are people within, I shall find a way to get the door open and see whether there is any trace of that man [Ji Hongchang]; but if within there is not a stir, then either he has gone, or he has changed rooms. And if he has really changed rooms, No. 145 is the most to be suspected. So I must go to No. 145 too and get to the bottom of it. When I have reconnoitered it solid, and he is really still in the room, I shall take the child downstairs and go back first to Lü Yimin's home to await our superiors' instruction; you, seeing me go downstairs, follow me down, and do not come up to ask anything—with all speed, by the appointed signal, sign to Mr. Wu waiting in the car at the roadside, and that will do."

Chen Guorui asked Yang Yushan: "And if neither No. 138 nor No. 145 is the right one?" Yushan said: "I shall come back to our room and think again."

Yang Yushan did her work most solidly; fearing Chen Guorui had no seasoning and might be a little rash, she asked it over once more—which was as good as checking it a second time; and when Chen Guorui showed he wholly understood, she took the little niece by the hand, and with the small rubber ball just bought at the Quanyechang, coaxed her to play in the corridor. Yushan led the child on purpose to bounce it forward, nearer and nearer to No. 138's door, and, cocking an ear, heard within the room not the cheep of a bird; she bounced the ball against the door-panel a few times on purpose, and still there was not the least response. By this, Yang Yushan could be all but certain there was no longer anyone in No. 138.

Now and then a boy or a guest passed in the corridor; but seeing a woman coaxing a child at ball, none took it to heart.

Yang Yushan led the child on toward No. 145; and suddenly she heard from within the room the clack and clatter of mahjong tiles. How strange—could someone be at play? Was it the very party we sought? Or was there some mistake?

Miss Yang was strong of will; she thought: "Come what may, I must make them open the door, and let me see clear, or I fail my charge." She looked up at the transom over the door: it was just then open, a gap above of some sixty or seventy centimeters long and thirty centimeters wide. She glanced left and right—no one—and quickly took the small ball from the child's hand and pitched it up at the transom; wide of the mark, the ball did not go in but fell back, luckily without alarming those in the room. Then, steadying her breath, she pitched the ball up again most carefully—there: the ball was bounced right into the room.

She did not wait for a response from within, but without the least hesitation knocked twice softly at the door. After a little pause, she saw the door drawn slowly ajar a crack, and more than half a head thrust out from within; perhaps because he saw only a young woman with a little girl, his guard was blunted, and he opened half the door and threw out the small ball. A burly man stood there in a huff, glaring straight at Yang Yushan; by the look of him he might have meant to let fly a few words, but seeing Miss Yang all wreathed in smiles and unceasing in apology, he softened and let it pass, and at once drew the door to.

In that instant of pushing and drawing the door, Miss Yang saw the room bright with lamplight, and four men indeed at play; the one who had just opened the door and thrown out the ball was a fifth.

The gaming-table was set aslant, none seated square on; save the one opposite, whose face could be seen more than half, the other three showed only half a face, or a small part of it. The one seated at the left of the man opposite stood out markedly: seated though he was, he was half a head above the other three. This man had fine brows and single-lidded eyes, a square face and a large chin, a pair of moustaches over his lip, and his whole jaw blue with the roots of a beard—an ill-favored sight to look on. Miss Yang judged in her heart: these were the very men Third Master Zheng had met; and the big fellow among them, half a head taller than the rest—who could he be but Ji Hongchang?

Yang Yushan bade the child pick up the ball, and led her by the little hand toward the stairs, coaxing her that they were going to the open yard to play. Walking, she turned her head to glance at Chen Guorui, who stood at a distance. Chen Guorui, seeing his sister lead the child downstairs, and carrying nothing, hurried a few steps and followed close down the stairs. Out of the Guomin Hotel's great hall, a small turn to the left, ten-odd steps, was the iron grille outside the hotel—that is, the main gate; this gate stood always open. He saw Miss Yang stoop and take up the child, and step off calmly northward, though ever and again turning her head to look back. Chen Guorui, once out of the gate, turned instead to the south, and hastening down the sidewalk came to where Wu Ping's car was parked.

Wu Ping wound down the car window and beckoned Chen Guorui near, asking: "Good news?" Chen Guorui said: "The man is still there; he has moved from No. 138 to No. 145. Come quick with me; I shall wait for you at the head of the stairs in the great hall." And having said it, without waiting for Wu Ping's answer, he turned and went back into the Guomin Hotel—meaning, no doubt, to nail that party fast, for fear they should slip away.

Wu Ping got down from the car and made a show of examining the tires fore and aft. Wang Wen and I, seated within the great glass window of the Zizhulin coffee-room, saw it all clearly. Wang Wen rose to his feet, tightened his belt, and as if by chance felt at the pistol thrust in his waist. He looked at me, to see whether I had any further word to charge him with; I said nothing, but with a smile bowed slightly and wished him success in his going!

I watched Wang Wen thread through the crowd across the road and, without a backward glance, walk in at the main door of the Guomin Hotel. At that instant my heart beat hard; I sat down, steadied myself, and only then called the waiter, settled the reckoning, paid, and walked out of the Zizhulin coffee-room, to stand beneath the show-window of a Western-clothes shop, feigning to look at the samples, my whole mind on waiting for word.

Chen Guorui, waiting in the great hall of the Guomin Hotel, saw Wang Wen come in from outside, his whole face flushed red, and felt an excitement past words. With a step near to bounding he led the way up the stairs; Wang Wen, gathering up his long gown, his hand on the pistol-butt, followed behind. Reaching the second floor, Chen Guorui made straight for the door of No. 145, planted his feet firm, and seeing Wang Wen too come up to the door, put out his hand and softly turned the door-knob; the knob did not budge a hair. He drew back half a step, raised his right foot, and with all his strength kicked—kicked the door hard open. Chen Guorui stepped half aside, flattening himself against the wall to clear the way; Wang Wen, seizing the moment, darted in at the door, and halting some three meters short of the mahjong-table, cried out at the top of his voice, "Commander-in-Chief Ji!" That tall big fellow jerked up his head and was just rising to speak, when Wang Wen leveled his gun and fired, three reports in a row; and one saw two men slither limp and boneless down under the table. The others too were struck dumb with fright, at a loss what to do.

Reckoning it done, Wang Wen turned and made for the stairs; and just then, out of the corner of his eye, he caught someone in the corridor already opening a door and craning to look out. Taking no thought for much, gun in hand, he flew down the stairs, and in two steps and three was in the Guomin Hotel's great hall; a turn, and, with nothing to bar him, smoothly and easily he got clear of the scene.

Out on the street, Wang Wen still saw the back of Chen Guorui; but the passers-by being then too many, in a trice he was lost to sight. Wang Wen did not go south, meaning it seems not to take Wu Ping's car; and in no long while he too pressed into the crowd and was gone.

I, on the far side of the road, watched the two of them come out one after the other, both walking off northward; then looked again at the Guomin Hotel's door—all as usual, not a thing astir. Much puzzled, I wondered what this could mean. Could it be that Wang Wen and the rest had not "moved"? Just as I was full of doubt, before I was aware of it Wang Wen appeared at my side, all smiles, and, without a word, walked straight off south. Ah—I understood: he was drawing me away from this spot, to talk with me after. He walked ahead, I followed behind; when we had gone a hundred meters and more, and the passers-by on the road had thinned a little, Wang Wen slowed his pace, drew near, and said low: "It is done; three reports rang out, two men went down, and one of them I knew for the fellow himself." I asked him: "Why did you not take the car, and get away from here sooner?" He said: "I was mindful lest someone nail me and note down the car's number—would not that leave a trace?" Right! He had thought more thoroughly than I.

By now the whole street was ablaze with lamps; I looked at the time—it was eight-forty at night.

As we walked, we seemed to hear from across the road the not-too-loud "toot, toot, toot, toot" of a car's horn; turning our heads to look, unlooked-for, it was Wu Ping, driving the car and following slowly along—doubtless he had been minding us all the while. And so Wang Wen and I crossed the road, got in the car, and drove on ahead; past two street-crossings, we were already come to the British concession.

This was one great feature of the Tianjin concessions in those years. Because the several nations' concessions adjoined one another, each with its own laws and rules, one had only to cross a single road to change one's whole surroundings. This small convenience we too turned to use.

To be the more careful, we made a wide circuit through the streets, and only then drove to Wu Ping's lodging. They first stowed away the guns they carried, then washed their faces, and thought to rest a while; but my mind was that Wu Ping should make one more trip—to inform Lü Yimin first, and have Lü Yimin with all speed pass word to Third Master Zheng and the rest, to find a place to lie low at once, lest they be dragged in.

Wu Ping agreed to go at once; Wang Wen meant to lodge a night at a grain-and-provisions shop on Hebei Main Street kept by a fellow-townsman, and go back to Beiping first thing next morning. I too was anxious to get back to Beiping, but had to wait for a sure result before I could set off. That night I went back after all to Xiaobailou and passed the night, leaving all to be decided next day.

At this juncture what I longed to know was the outcome of the night's action—how, in the end, had it gone? Had Brother Wang Zixiang not died, he had many acquaintances in Tianjin, and friends too at the works department of the French concession, and a single telephone-call would have got sure news. As things were, the "Tianjin Station's" work-web was not yet wide enough, and one's personal social connections most limited; and in such a case there was nothing for it but to wait for the newspapers next morning.

I recall that in those years no broadcasting station or such system of spreading news had yet been set up, and the general news reporting too was not so swift as today's; the thing having happened at night, the evening papers were of course too late to carry it, and one had to wait for next day's morning papers—this too there was no help for. Waking early the next morning, I opened the paper, and my heart sank halfway: the dead was but one man, and not the action target Ji Hongchang, but a stranger by the name of Liu Shaorang.

Liu Shaorang—who was he? Of him we had known nothing beforehand, nor ever dreamt that we should hang the wrong man on such a one; not to speak of his inner history, even his name I saw for the first time in the newspaper. Afterward, through much checking on many sides, it was fixed that his standing was as a representative of the "Southwest Executive Department of the Central Executive Committee of the Chinese Kuomintang," come to take part in the "Chinese People's Anti-Fascist Grand Alliance." Here again one may see how venomous the Communists' splitting activity was. In truth, even had we known beforehand that he was a representative of the "Southwest," we should not have harmed a hair of him; or to put it plainer still, we had had no such order at all—though they too were engaged in splitting activity against the Center.

Reckoning it up, this gentleman's death was most unworthy; though it came near being the woe of the pond-fish, one still felt it something of a wrong. And who could know that the affairs of this world would fall out so by chance?—six years later, on a Shanghai street, I met a friend, a Mr. He, who worked in the "Institute for International Affairs" that Wang Pengsheng directed; we had known each other in Chongqing, and meeting now again in a strange place, we had of course to talk a good while, and found a place to drink coffee and speak of our long parting. Little did I think—we talked, and only then learned that the Mr. Liu Shaorang shot dead in Tianjin was Mr. He's own father-in-law. How mortifying this was! It was thanks to Mr. He's understanding and largeness of mind that he said nothing much; had it been a man easily roused, the thing does not bear thinking of.

Awkward affairs of this sort I met with not this once alone; and though one may console oneself with the two phrases, "wholly for the work, in no way a personal grudge," yet in the depths of the heart, how is one ever to be at peace over it?

But let this pass; at the moment, what we cared for most was still Ji Hongchang's life or death, and where he was.

By our intelligence put together, the general state of things was this: about ten minutes after the event, the police-house of the French concession, having the Guomin Hotel's report, sent a French police officer leading armed patrolmen and plainclothes detectives to reach the scene in several parties, and forthwith took the whole company in Room No. 145 back to the police-house for questioning. And an ambulance was called to bear away the corpse of the dead man, Liu Shaorang.

Those taken back to the French police-house were either four or five. Among them, besides Ji Hongchang and Ren Yingqi, the rest were of names unknown. Whether the Communist elements Xuan Xiafu, Nan Hanchen, Zhang Mutao, and so on were among them under false names, there was no telling either.

One report pointed out that Ji Hongchang had indeed been wounded, and had been sent on by the French police-house to the Catholic hospital for treatment. On checking again, the report proved true; Ji Hongchang was indeed under treatment at the Catholic hospital.

The French Catholic hospital lay behind the terminus of the green-plate tramline in the French concession, a charity hospital attached to the Catholic church. In ordinary days visiting the sick, the coming and going was quite free; but now police detectives had been posted to guard it, and one ward was forbidden to be approached—plainly there was some special reason. We judged it was precisely because Ji Hongchang lay within.

How grave was Ji Hongchang's wound? This became again a thing we were anxious to know. It was said the wound was in the shoulder, and that he had not been struck directly by the gun, but that a spent, rebounding bullet had grazed off a patch of skin. Borne out by the fact that, the day after the event, the works department of the French concession summoned for questioning the kin and friends of those held, and Ji Hongchang himself was among those brought up for examination—from this one point, Ji's wound must have been slight past mention.

To turn to the news reporting: by the Tianjin Dagongbao of the 10th of November, 1934, the headline was "Strange Shooting Case at the Guomin Hotel Last Evening," the substance being in outline: "A little past eight, of a sudden several loud reports were heard within a room; thereupon a man rushed out, a Browning pistol in hand, and fled outward in haste. As he went he cried aloud, 'There are bandits within!' Then a second man ran out, and the two fled together toward the main gate. Because the passers-by near the main gate were many, and rickshaw-men were massed before the door besides, the constable on post came forward to bar them; whereupon they fired several shots in a string, and made off toward Road No. 32, flinging the pistol down at the door of the telegraph office, and going away to the southeast." Besides this, the same paper had a further short passage: "It is heard that the six men within the room were all of a military cast; report abroad varies, some saying it sprang from a feud, some that it came of a joint dealing in narcotics. What the truth may be is not yet to be known."

There was besides, in the Shanghai Shenbao of the 11th of November, a "special telegraphic report from Tianjin, the 10th": "The tragedy at the Guomin Hotel in the French concession was, at half past eight on the evening of the 9th, in Room No. 45, where four men were playing mahjong and one stood by looking on. The boy outside the door suddenly heard several gunshots within, whereupon two men forced the door and came downstairs, running and crying that there were bandits; reaching the front gate, seeing the hotel full of people, they turned back and fled out by a side door, turned into Road No. 30, and were barred by a constable on the street, while people from within the hotel came in pursuit besides. The two thereupon fired two shots, turned into Road No. 25, and made off by way of Road No. 32; passing before the door of the telegraph office, they flung away their pistol, in which were four rounds, taken up by a constable." And so on.

Besides this, the English-language "Peking & Tientsin Times" published in Tianjin also had a brief record, its point of difference from the rest being this one sentence: "It is believed that the shooting was of a political motive."

In sum, whether by the intelligence materials or by the news reports, this piece of sanction work aimed at Ji Hongchang was, viewed by the plain standard of the task, of course to be reckoned a failure; but viewed politically, it produced a certain effect—not only dealing the Communists a severe blow, but shattering a Communist-fomented anti-government plot.

I may add, in passing, a word here on the reliability of the news reporting of this case. By the actual course of the case, on our side, as set forth above, there is no falsehood whatever; in certain small points there may be omissions, and, the years being long past, some small errors too perhaps; but there is no need of any mystifying or making of riddles. When we have shouldered even the blame of the work's failure, what need is there to lie?

The newspaper said: "Barred by the constable, they fired several shots in a string on the street." No such thing; Wang Wen and Chen Guorui met no barring, and of course did not fire; and the more so as Chen Guorui carried no gun on him at all. The papers said again: "flung the pistol down at the door of the telegraph office," or "passing before the door of the telegraph office, flung away their pistol." The gun Wang Wen used was brought back, and never thrown away. If the constable really picked up a pistol, then it was not ours. From this one may see how the truth of a thing often stands far off from the fact—and thence what a discount a whole history of human doings must go at.

To ask further, why did this case come to such a result? The part that miscarried—how in truth was it made up? We too made our own review of it. By common reckoning, the executor, Comrade Wang Wen, had known Ji Hongchang to begin with; when he had leapt into No. 45 and planted his feet, he cried out loud, "Commander-in-Chief Ji," which held the double effect of "overawing" and "identifying," and at the time Ji Hongchang had at once reacted, and was therefore making to rise and strike back—so there could be no mistaking the man.

Second, Ji Hongchang and Liu Shaorang differed greatly too in build and features, so that still less could there be error from any illusion of the eye.

What is possible is this: at the gaming-table, the four men's heads and chests were but a hand's-breadth apart, and each sat with a slight "inward lean," so that heads and chests were nearer still. In such a case, the slightest deflection of the shooter, or the least small swing of the muzzle from an unsteady grip, would strike the men to either side of the target; and if one at the gaming-table chanced "just then" to sway a little, he too might meet the flying bullet. Beyond this, no account that stands to reason can be offered. If any blame the executor Wang Wen that his aim was untrue or that he lost his head at the pinch, there is no answering it—let me bear the fault for him.

On this point, in a "General Ji Hongchang" the Communists published at Beiping in 1979, there is indeed a passage of description, but still with a deliberate warping of the facts, and matters of mere surmise; I here copy out one passage after the original text (the simplified characters have all been corrected):

"Hongchang, back at the Guomin Hotel, handed the passbook to the man the organization had sent, and began again to pass the time at the gaming-table, awaiting another comrade come to make contact. At this time the secret-service men were making ready to do him harm, and had already taken another room across from No. 38. He perceived that the party opposite were not to be trusted, and at the last moved to lodge in No. 45.

"They changed rooms at dusk when the lamps were lit; and the door of No. 45 was at that time marked with a cross in chalk by the secret-service men. Very soon it was noticed that people passed to and fro without cease in the corridor outside the room. At this Lü Yimin hit upon the plan of buying a rubber ball and pitching it into No. 45, and sending the woman secret-service agent Yang Yushan, on the pretext of fetching the ball, into the room to reconnoiter Hongchang's seat. The ball was fetched out, Hongchang's position made clear; and then, with Lü Yimin and the rest keeping watch, Wang Wen, gun in hand, burst into the room.

"When the woman secret-service agent first went in to look, Hongchang was sitting near the steam-pipes. Being too hot, he had taken off his padded gown and wore only a white singlet. As chance would have it, when the secret-service men outside decided to do the deed, within, they had just finished four rounds at the table and were changing dealer and seats, and Hongchang had moved to sit opposite. Being now far from the steam-pipes, he put his padded gown on again. And another, a representative just come from Guangxi to Tianjin, moved to the very seat Hongchang had left. This Guangxi representative too had taken off his padded coat, and, as chance would have it, likewise wore a white singlet.

"The secret-service assassin burst in at the door and fired several shots straight at the man in the white singlet, killing him on the instant, so that he fell to the ground; because the bullets were fired at very short range, with great force, one passed through the dead man's body and bounded back from the cement floor, grazing Hongchang's shoulder; Ren Yingqi too was wounded in the hand. Hongchang, heedless of the pain of his wound, gave a great shout, sprang up and seized the assassin; the assassin, finding he had struck the wrong man, in his panic raised his gun again, but Hongchang with a crack of his fist had already knocked it flying. The other secret-service man who had burst in, seeing this, and fearing to be caught, ducked his head in fright and, dragging the first assassin, turned and fled in confusion. Fearing the assassin might fire again, they did not go out of the room to pursue. The assassins ran out of the Guomin Hotel by the door of the upstairs Western-restaurant. The people then dining in the restaurant all saw a man in long gown and mandarin jacket and a man in a Western suit, one before the other, run hurrying past; but none dared bar them.

"In a trice the whole hotel was thrown into confusion, all running out to trace the source of the shots. The boy pushed open the door and came in, and saw Hongchang sitting on the sofa, the fresh blood running straight down his neck. Yet he was as though nothing were amiss, and said with a laugh: 'This was meant for me.' At this many people, heedless of danger, ran into Hongchang's room to see him, and some pressed forward to help bind his wound. Hongchang hastily charged the boy: 'Have everyone get quickly away from this place of trouble!'"

Looking at this passage of writing, one may divide it into two parts for comment. First, as to why the wrong man was killed: by their account, it was because, at mahjong, seats were changed; and because, being near the hot-water pipes, he had taken off his padded gown and wore only a white singlet, whence a coincidence that caused the target to be misread. As for the true, real cause—that, I fear, is hard indeed to come by an answer to.

Second, as to the book's exaggeration and wild talk: we can very easily point out that several places are all sheer lies:

First, Lü Yimin was not on the scene at all.

Second, the rubber ball the child played with was bought before Yang Yushan and the rest went into the hotel, the first thought being to buy it for the child to play with; there was no foreseeing it would come to use.

Third, who chalked a cross on the door of No. 45, we do not know that there was any such thing, nor do we pass judgment on it; at the least, we should not do such a thing. Wang Wen and Chen Guorui too noticed no chalked cross on the door. Still less do we understand the point of chalking a cross—was it for fear of finding the wrong room?

Fourth, that Ji Hongchang with one blow of his fist knocked the "assassin's" gun flying—whereas in fact the gun Wang Wen used was most certainly brought back. Had Wang Wen's gun been knocked flying, and not flown up into the sky, then when the detectives came they ought to have picked up this gun.

This is the trick the Communist Party is ever wont to use—making something out of nothing, standing right and wrong on their heads.

At this point the case, taken purely on the ground of the work, had come to a stage; but there were yet many developments in the affair worth the setting down.

### 6. A Man Possessed by a Devil, Born Under a Baleful Star

To speak truly, our "Beiping Station" and "Tianjin Station," joined together, put out a good many men, and spent no little contrivance to reconnoiter Ji Hongchang's whereabouts and doings; yet, save for the one line Lü Yimin had developed through Zheng Enpu, we never once even learned Ji Hongchang's address. There is no denying it—we of those days were not only limited in social connections, but not deep enough in them either; in working technique we were green in the extreme; and as for scientific equipment and its use, that was further still below the mark.

Whether it was that Ji Hongchang saw ghosts in broad day, or was a case of mistaken names and misplaced feelings, he would have it flatly that he was matching wits with the "Blue Shirt Society"—which is truly a great joke. In this book "General Ji Hongchang" there are two whole pages given over to this very matter, and it mentions besides, with a plausible show of half-truth, the "Juntong," the "Tianjin Station," the "Beiping Station," the "Action Group," and how Mr. Dai Li, Mr. Zheng Jiemin, and the writer of these lines himself planned and disposed things, and so forth. The original text of this passage runs thus:

"He harried the secret-service men grievously, so that no few of the shadowing agents came to fear him. But the hunt of the Kuomintang secret-service organs pressed a day tighter each day, and at the last they decided to compass his death by the shameless means of assassination. The Juntong secret-service chief Dai Li planned it in person, and dispatched Zheng Jiemin and Chen Gongshu especially to Tianjin, bringing a great pack of talons and fangs to lay the assassination work. Those who took part in the murder work included the Juntong North China Action Group's Bai Shiwei and Huang Siqin, the Juntong Tianjin Station's action-group chief Wang Wen, the Juntong directly-attached communicator Lü Yimin, the Juntong Tianjin–Tangshan sub-group chief Lü Wenyou (Lü Yimin's nephew), and the Juntong Beiping Station and Tianjin Station intelligence agent Yang Yushan (Lü Yimin's mistress), and the rest; they kept a direct shadowing watch on Hongchang, biding their time to do the deed."

By the account above, the Communists did indeed know not a little, though it was not wholly correct—yet at least there was some shadow of it. That the Communists had such knowledge was, I suppose, because the man who wrote the book, one Mu Xin, in some years after the event pieced it together out of many documents and materials; or else it was heard from the mouths of Juntong men who, after the bandit-puppets usurped the mainland, fell into their hands as captives. In the year 1934 itself, it was past all knowing.

Below, I point out again, upon the facts, the errors and inversions in it:

First: Dai Yunong (Li) was the actual head of the Juntong, and the supreme commander who gave the orders. In this case, he only handed down the order, and did not plan it in person.

Second: Mr. Zheng Jiemin had at that time already been ordered back to Nanjing to office, and was not in charge in North China. This has been set out in detail above.

Third: I, the writer of these lines, by order in my capacity as chief of the "Beiping Station," bore the sole charge of the planning and command on the ground at Tianjin. The reason for it lay in

Fourth: The chief of the "Beiping Station Action Group," Bai Shiwei, was not the "Bai Shiwei of the North China Action Group." He first took part in the planning, and afterward acted in my stead in my post at Beiping, and did not come to Tianjin. As for who Huang Siqin was, I cannot for all my thinking make out, nor do I know who the man was.

Fifth: Wang Wen was at the time a newly come action man of the "Beiping Station," not the "Tianjin Station's action-group chief." This has all been set out above.

Sixth: Lü Yimin was the "Tianjin Station's" intelligence-group chief; the so-called "Juntong directly-attached communicator" is wrong too.

Seventh: Lü Wenyou was a directly-attached communicator of the "Tianjin Station," posted at Tanggu, and took no part in the sanction work.

Eighth: Yang Yushan was a liaison agent of the "Intelligence Group" under the "Tianjin Station." She afterward made a happy marriage with Mr. Zheng Enpu.

In the passage of two hundred-odd words copied out above, there are thus eight points not true. One may see that what they understood was no more than a rough outline. Now let me speak again of Ji Hongchang and his men's start-at-shadows suspicions, which, pricked through on both sides, are very droll besides.

Ji Hongchang had taken a long-term room at the Huizhong Hotel, to serve as an outward liaison post. Of this we knew nothing whatever; we were still racking every device to reconnoiter Ji's whereabouts. The book says: "The secret-service men at the Huizhong Hotel grew suddenly more numerous; on his way home from the hotel he often found secret-service men shadowing him." I do not know where the "secret-service men" here spoken of came from. If they were not sent by the French police-house, then they were the "every bush and tree a soldier" of his own hallucination. For we did not so much as know where his home was—how then was this "shadow" to be dogged?

The book says again:

"One day, coming home at dusk, he said with a laugh to his wife: 'Today I gave that fellow a start. The moment I came out of the hotel, I saw him following me. I went on purpose to a tobacco stall to buy something, and he hid over there pretending to look at an advertisement, all the while watching me with his eye. I thought, let me have a joke with him. So I went over and asked him: Here, friend, what do you keep following me for? How much do you make a month? It gave the fellow such a fright that he turned tail and ran.'

"His wife asked him, 'Who was that man?' 'Who else—the Blue Shirt Society, of course!'"

If these two sentences were really spoken by Ji Hongchang, then it is truly his dog's foul wind. Had we found him out, what should we want with shadowing him?—we should long since have made an end of him, to be rid of the after-plague. The Communist Party is ever wont to chase the wind and clutch at shadows, to fashion lies, and to gild its own face with all its might; it is not worth a laugh to tell.

Below I speak again of the unlooked-for developments of the Ji case:

On the 14th of November, 1934—that is, the fifth day after the shooting at the Guomin Hotel had failed of Ji Hongchang—the French concession authorities "extradited" the two held prisoners, Ji Hongchang and Ren Yingqi, to our national government. Once this got abroad, it was greatly beyond the common expectation. Just how the negotiation was brought off, and by what unit it was managed, and who bore the charge of it—of all this the "Beiping Station" and the "Tianjin Station" under the Juntong knew nothing whatever. Searching the archives decades later, there was not a drop of record either. By conjecture, it was very likely carried on in secret by the Beiping Military Branch.

On the surface, the works department of the French concession acted at the request of the Hebei provincial government; but in fact the thing could of course not be so simple. By materials the writer has looked into, the course of the extradition was this:

About ten in the forenoon of the 14th of November, the French concession authorities, in an armored car (a prison-van shelled in iron plate), escorted the two, Ji Hongchang and Ren Yingqi, to the Public Security Bureau of the city of Tianjin, where they were forthwith taken into custody. At five in the afternoon, the Public Security Bureau delivered them to the headquarters of the Fifty-first Army for examination, and soon they were shut into the Army Prison at Caijia Huayuan. The commander of the Fifty-first Army at that time, Yu Xuezhong, held the chairmanship of the Hebei provincial government besides.

Ji Hongchang and Ren Yingqi were held in the Tianjin Army Prison nine days; on the 22nd, Yu Xuezhong received an order from the Beiping Military Branch to escort the two criminals Ji and Ren to Beiping to undergo a "military court-martial." They arrived at Beiping at five that afternoon, and were forthwith held in the Army Prison in Paoju Hutong within Dongzhimen.

Brought up for trial on the 23rd, Ji Hongchang admitted that he was a Communist, and admitted the several crimes of his treason. His deposition said: "Yes, I am a member of the Chinese Communist Party. Through the Party's teaching, I broke free of the old warlord's life and came over into the camp of the toiling worker-and-peasant masses. That I could join the ranks of revolution, could become one of the Communist Party, could strive for our Party's cause, for the liberation of all mankind—this is the greatest glory of my whole life, and this is what sets me apart from the common ruck of Chinese soldiers."

That Ji Hongchang should say he had broken free of the old warlord's life, and was set apart from the common ruck of Chinese soldiers—how grand and fair-sounding the words are. In fact, one may see in the very "General Ji Hongchang" the Communists themselves concocted: Ji Hongchang had in Tianjin a piece of house-property each in both the British and the French concessions, owned a motorcar, had deposits in the bank of over ten thousand, and often took rooms at hotels to play cards. How is this, again, to be explained?

After the military court-martial, Ji Hongchang and Ren Yingqi were forthwith sentenced to death, and at half past one on the afternoon of the 24th the two were shot together. The Beiping Military Branch openly proclaimed the crimes of Ji Hongchang and Ren Yingqi, and posted a notice for general knowledge. The notice ran, in part: "Whereas the Fifty-first Army has delivered up to this court the two important criminals Ji Hongchang and Ren Yingqi, long under warrant and repeatedly raising troops in disorder, and joined besides to the Communist Party; and whereas on examination they have confessed to the design of disturbing public order, and to having joined the Communist Party, as proven; we do therefore, in accordance with Article One of the Emergency Law for the Punishment of Crimes Endangering the Republic, sentence the two criminals Ji and Ren to death…"

I myself feel that the phrase in the notice, "the two important Communist criminals Ji Hongchang and Ren Yingqi," is a little loose; for Ren Yingqi was no member of the Communist Party, but only an accessory made use of by the Communists.

It is said that before his execution Ji Hongchang wrote a poem on the ground; whether the thing be true I leave aside, but that five-word poem ran: "I grieve I did not die resisting Japan, / and am left to be this day's shame; / the land thus broken, and still it comes to this— / what should I grudge this head of mine!" On reading it, do some find it heroic? My guess is that this was fitted onto him by the Communists after Ji Hongchang's death, to make him fair; whether so I cannot say.

But Ren Yingqi they cast into an ugly light; it is said that Ren Yingqi, the moment he entered the execution-ground, was all a face of tears, and could not so much as walk. By contrast, does it not make Ji Hongchang out the more of a "brave fellow"?

Ji Hongchang is no more; he was but thirty-nine at his death. Had he not strayed into the wrong road, a man of his parts and his willingness to press upward should have done great things. The pity is only that he met with a devil's possession, joined the Communist Party, and came in the end to ruin of body and name, made in the end the Communist Party's forerunning pawn, and even at his death still drunk and unwaked in his delusion. Had Ji lived three years more, and passed through the full war of resistance, he might perhaps have changed; had he lived longer still, and watched the Communists' perverse doings, turning all fair China into a hopeless mess, plunging its hundreds of millions into fire and flood—he would surely have come to rue his beginnings.

Of this man Ji Hongchang's past, from his entering the ranks at nineteen, his whole career was but twenty years. Yet these twenty years of his were not without note; let me set them out in brief below:

Ji Hongchang, courtesy name Shiwu—doubtless taken from the sense of "may five generations of his line prosper"—was born in 1895 to a farming family of Fugou county, Henan.

At fourteen he was apprentice silversmith at a jeweler's shop. At sixteen he was a little clerk in a general store. At eighteen, when Feng Yuxiang was raising troops at Yancheng in Henan, he entered Feng's unit as a private in the Second Regiment of the left wing of the left-route reserve army. At nineteen he was picked out and moved into the cadet-soldier company.

At twenty-two he was a company commander; at twenty-seven he rose to battalion commander; at thirty he was cavalry regiment commander in the Suiyuan military governor's office; at thirty-one he was brigade commander of the 36th Brigade of the 18th Division, and in Gansu fought the "Zhili clique" of Wu Peifu.

In 1926, when the National Revolutionary Army reached Wuhan, Feng Yuxiang issued at Wuyuan in Suiyuan his oath-of-arms manifesto, and declared his joining the revolution. The 36th Brigade was enlarged into the 19th Division, and Ji Hongchang served as its commander.

Before this, Ji Hongchang was but a soldier without political thought.

In 1928 Feng Yuxiang reorganized the Northwest Army; Ji's 19th Division was broken up, and afterward reorganized as the 30th Division, of which he was made commander.

In 1929 Ji Hongchang was commander of the 30th Army; in July he drove out Men Zhizhong at Ningxia and took upon himself the chairmanship of Ningxia province. This was already a warlord's way of doing.

In 1930, when Yan and Feng revolted, Ji Hongchang still took his orders from Feng, was made instead commander of the Ninth Army, and fought the Central forces about eastern Henan.

When Yan and Feng failed, and Feng withdrew, and the Northwest Army was taken in and reorganized by the Center, Ji's unit was reorganized as the Twenty-second Route, and he was made its commander-in-chief, stationed at Huangchuan in Henan.

In the winter of 1930, Ji's unit was ordered to attack the Chinese Communist bandit forces in the Henan–Hubei–Anhui border, but secretly held friendly commerce with the bandit forces.

In 1931 Ji Hongchang, feigning illness, went to Shanghai for treatment, and had some contact with the Communist chiefs.

Beguiled by the Communist elements' honeyed words, Ji actually disguised himself and slipped off to "tour" the Henan–Hubei–Anhui bandit area.

At this his thinking greatly changed—though to say he had by now a political understanding is perhaps not so.

After Ji Hongchang returned to his defense-post, he actually withdrew his troops and ceased fighting, refusing to suppress the Communists. In August he was relieved of his post, but not called to account.

In September 1931 the government sent him abroad to "study." On his return he wrote a book, "Notes on a Tour Round the Globe."

At the end of February 1932 he returned to Shanghai, and soon went off to Tianjin; before long, again from Tianjin to Shanghai, to take part in the work of the "Northeast Anti-Japanese National Salvation Support Association."

In August of the same year, at the Communists' instigation, he slipped off to Songbu in Hubei to set his old subordinates, the officers and men of the 30th Division, to mutiny; it failed utterly, and when being hunted and taken he actually escaped. The government issued a warrant for Ji Hongchang's arrest.

Ji Hongchang, by way of the Dabie Mountains, slipped back to Shanghai; of his figure at this time he has a picture: "When I showed myself at a friend's house, I wore an ill-fitting ragged gown, torn cloth shoes on my feet, my face a mass of side-whiskers, my hair long and unkempt." To look at, he was of course like some little Shanghai vagabond.

By this time Ji Hongchang was already drugged by the sweet-coated poison. He said: "Though this uprising failed, yet it opened a crack in my skull. There were many things I had thought I already understood, but in that understanding there was still muddle. Though the resolve to make revolution had long been taken, in the doing I still thought only of my own troops, and forgot the strength of the people; only this time did I truly understand, and come to know the true road of saving the country: only by following the Communist Party meekly and obediently can the oppressed poor lift their heads, and China be saved; individual heroism alone will not do. Hereafter I shall begin over from the beginning, and give my whole life's strength to the work."

This does not sound like Ji Hongchang, for he was by now bewitched.

Just at this time the Communists found him a place of concealment on Bubbling Well Road, where he met daily with the men the Communists sent. This, I think, was training of the "brainwashing" kind—of which I have given full account above.

After the whole formality of "application," "examination," and "approval," Ji Hongchang formally became a member of the Communist Party.

The time was about September 1932.

At that time we knew nothing of the affair. Soon the Communists sent him back to Tianjin to make contact with old subordinates, and to carry on the activity of a sham resistance to Japan and a real opposition to Chiang.

As Ji Hongchang's train passed through Shandong on the Tianjin–Pukou railway, he slipped off to Mount Tai to talk Feng Yuxiang round to "unite and resist Japan."

In October 1932, Feng Yuxiang came down from the mountain, and indeed came to Zhangjiakou.

In February 1933, Ji Hongchang went from Tianjin to Zhangjiakou, and with Feng Yuxiang plotted the setting-on-foot of the "Chahar People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army." Feng Yuxiang, of course, understood well what game Ji Hongchang was playing; yet still, as though his wits were bewitched, he was content to play the puppet. From this one may see that between Feng Yuxiang and the Communist Party there was surely reached some measure of understanding, and it is very possible that certain conditions were even struck.

Such is a stretch of Ji Hongchang's career.

Some years later, while handling materials on the bandit situation on the mainland, I came again, here and there in fragments, upon some pieces touching Ji Hongchang before his death and after.

It turns out Ji Hongchang had two pieces of house-property in Tianjin: one at No. 1 Garden Road in the French concession, a three-story building of red brick walls, which they themselves called the "Red Building," with a double meaning in it. The Beiping Station's Fan Xing had once furnished the intelligence that "Ji uses the Great Red Building as a base," and I am sorry to say we did not at the time make out its meaning. The other was at No. 3 Oxford Villa, Road No. 40, in the British concession. Ji Hongchang lodged first in the "Red Building" in the French concession, and only later moved to Oxford Villa in the British concession—at the time we knew nothing of it whatever.

He had deposits in the bank; how much in all is not to be known, but he had many times supplied them to the Communists as activity funds, and so he said he "ruined his family to succor the country's straits."

When Ji Hongchang, after the extradition, was held in the Tianjin Army Prison, Ji's wife Hu Hongxia ran everywhere to seek his rescue, and even made a special trip to Mount Tai in Shandong to see Feng Yuxiang. But Feng Yuxiang could do nothing either.

Ji, at Beiping, learning of the death sentence, wrote three letters, severally to his wife, his brother-in-law, and his friends, as parting words. In the letter to his wife was this: "Your husband now dies; it is a sacrifice for the age. Men must all die; at my death you need not grieve too sorely, for there are yet the children awaiting your care."

On the 27th of November, 1934, the Beiping Military Branch granted the petition of Ji's wife Hu Hongxia, and allowed her to take out Ji Hongchang's body, to lie for the time in the Changchun Temple on South Chang Street in Beiping. A year later it was borne back to his native place for burial.

As the common saying goes, "into the earth, and at peace." The Communist Party holds by none of this; not only do they make sport of the living, but they order the dead about besides. It is learned that on the 5th of April, 1964, the Communists again moved Ji Hongchang's remains from Fugou county in Henan to the "Martyrs' Cemetery" at Zhengzhou.

Had Ji Hongchang truly been able to sleep long thus in the earth, and serve as a piece of the Communists' furniture, well and good. But who could know that, a "baleful star" in his fate, even in the earth he should find no peace: in the years 1967 to '68, a great riot of "Red Guards" broke out at Zhengzhou. In the faction-fighting, the "Red Guards" of the Mao hag Jiang Qing and the field troops under Lin Biao fought a confused battle in this very "Martyrs' Cemetery," for several days and nights on end. In the end not only were more than four thousand "Red Guards" killed, but the whole cemetery was smashed to pieces and ruin, turned into a stretch of disordered graves. And Ji Hongchang's tomb, of course, could not escape the calamity either.

This was an action-case of utter failure, and we took from it a bitter and painful lesson!

The thing happened in the winter of 1934, just a little after the Ji Hongchang case. The place was the Japanese concession at Tianjin; the sanction target was Shi Yousan, a played-out petty warlord, fickle and forever shifting his allegiance, veering with every wind.

The carrying-out of this case, from an over-eager craving for merit and a rashness of haste, met with defeat.

Its outcome: the action target Shi Yousan came off safe and sound; while the two who carried out the deed, Xian Hongxia and old Chu, both came to grief on the spot, and were delivered to the Japanese gendarmerie for interrogation under torture. From that going there was no return; they must long since have perished. Another man of honor, Shi Dachuan, by his wit got clear of the cage, but then, over a misunderstanding beyond all common sense, left in anger, leaving us a regret past all mending, for all time.

Touching the planning and execution of this case, there was much amiss in it, and the writer truly cannot shirk his blame. Toward the sacrificed Xian Hongxia and old Chu, and toward the praiseworthy Shi Dachuan and the rest, there is no denying I bear at the least a moral responsibility that ought to have been discharged and was not. The bitterest grief of all is that, through the writer's ignorance of working procedure, I neither reported to my superiors beforehand nor could produce complete proofs afterward; and by this one failing, the two loyal martyrs Xian Hongxia and old Chu, who gave their lives for the country, came in the end to have no case on record, and no name in the annals.

And when it came to seeing to the aftermath of the case, fresh trouble sprang up from the side, with many a blunder; and so the working foundation built up with such toil was dragged down too.

As for that Shi Yousan, lucky for a while, through the repeated pressure that the two successive commanders of the Japanese garrison army at Tianjin, Tada Hayao and Tashiro Kan'ichirō, brought to bear on our North China authorities, in a subtlety past comprehension the warrant against him was actually canceled, and he was bestowed with the high government post of "Peace-Preservation Commander of the Northern Hebei Border Region."

By rights, Shi Yousan ought to have repented his former faults to the marrow, and sworn himself in loyalty to the war of resistance, so as to requite the country—that were the proper thing. But who could foresee that, crafty by nature, he would slip back into his old ways; and in the early period of the eight-year War of Resistance, he was, for the crime of rebellion, openly put to the law, and ate the fruit of his own evil.
