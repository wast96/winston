## 8

Chongqing summers were a brutal, breathless heat, and the Juntong drill ground at the foot of Mount Gele was one vast steaming basket. Here Lin Nansheng taught the trainees contact and communications, the analysis and vetting of intelligence, the craft of field work; now and then he interpreted for the American instructors. He was the only Chinese instructor in the training class, yet he wore an American uniform, and at night, at the foreigners' guesthouse, he drank and danced with the American officers and sang American country songs in English.

Lin Nansheng seemed to have grown carefree, to have forgotten even the bullet lodged in his spine, the one that could take his life at any moment.

One day Director Hu, of the headquarters inspectorate, came out to the drill ground without warning and drove Lin Nansheng to a ferry landing on the Jialing River. The two of them climbed a long flight of stone steps to the upper floor of an ordinary house. Director Hu pushed open a window, pointed to a small courtyard across the street, and asked, "Do you know what this place is?"

Lin Nansheng knew, of course. Not long after Gu Shenyan reached Chongqing he had been placed under house arrest here. Some said it was his punishment for burning down the Xianghu Building. Others said he was merely the Juntong's scapegoat, a way to paper over the disaster at Shanghai. But most held that he had come to this because he had crossed the will of Boss Dai.

"He was your teacher," Director Hu went on. "Why have you never been to see him?"

"I have to avoid the appearance of it," Lin Nansheng said. "This is Chongqing."

Director Hu laughed. "Mr. Gu's peaches and plums fill the world. Even Boss Dai once sat in his classroom. What appearance is there for you to avoid?"

Lin Nansheng answered gravely. "If Director Hu has instructions for me, he need only say so plainly."

"You were once teacher and student. You are to visit him often, take an interest in him — and study him. Analyze him." As Director Hu spoke, the smile drained from his face, and both eyes fixed on Lin Nansheng through the lenses of his glasses. Then he turned the subject to the underground intelligence network Gu Shenyan had rebuilt, and to the files Gu had submitted to headquarters for the record. On examination, most of the personnel rosters, the organizational codes, the methods of contact those files gave turned out not to exist at all. Director Hu looked hard at Lin Nansheng again. "We need to know what he is after. And where all those living, breathing people have gone."

Still, Lin Nansheng did not believe this was headquarters' true reason for recalling him to Chongqing. He chose a weekend afternoon to call on Gu Shenyan. Rain was coming on; a black weight of cloud had gathered over the Jialing River, and the air felt too thick to draw. Gu Shenyan sat at a go board under the eaves, working through a game record, a Song-dynasty printing of the Wangyou Qingle Ji in one hand, a stone in the other. When the old servant showed Lin Nansheng in, a smile came to his face, as though he had been waiting a long while, and he gestured at the board. "Black moves first." All afternoon, to the sound of the rain, Lin Nansheng played go with Gu Shenyan, game after game, until the light began to fail. Then Gu Shenyan tossed his white stones back into their jar, stood, and said to the old servant waiting at his side, "Go and find an umbrella. See Instructor Lin off."

With that, and without a backward glance, he went inside.

By the time Lin Nansheng got back to the drill ground at Mount Gele, umbrella or no, he was soaked through. The next day Director Hu sent a car to bring him to the headquarters inspectorate, and greeted him at once. "I waited here for you all yesterday evening."

"I assumed the old servant would come and report to you," Lin Nansheng said.

Director Hu started, then broke into a smile. "What else did you notice?"

Lin Nansheng had seen long since that this little building was a fixed watch post, that a roving lookout stood at each end of the street, that several action-squad men lay hidden in the grocery. It was the Juntong's highest grade of surveillance, reserved in Chongqing for the Eighth Route Army office at No. 50 Zengjiayan. But after a moment's thought, what he said was: "I believe that if he means to leave, no place on earth will hold him."

Director Hu said nothing. He took off his glasses and polished them with a handkerchief, slowly, for a long time.

By the time Lin Nansheng next went to Gu Shenyan's house, the old servant had learned tact: he always found some pretext to slip away — out for vegetables, or off to tidy a room — and left the two of them alone. Only, teacher and student alike kept off the subject of Shanghai, and off the times, and off intelligence. They were like two men easing into old age. Whenever Lin Nansheng came, he sat with Gu Shenyan under the eaves or in the shade of a courtyard tree, often bent over the go board for half a day at a stretch. Sometimes he simply stayed to supper, as in the days of the old training class. But the moment he stepped out through the courtyard gate, a plainclothesman would take him into the little building opposite, strip him naked in front of everyone, wait until every garment had been searched, and only then let him dress. After that he was put in another room, the door shut, and made to sit before a tape recorder and repeat onto the tape every sentence Gu Shenyan had spoken — and to set down, with it, his own reading of what those sentences meant.

One day, his eyes on the board, Lin Nansheng said abruptly, "Sir, if you want to leave this place, I will be waiting outside to bring you out."

Gu Shenyan laughed. He drew deeply on his cigar and spoke through the smoke as it drifted out. "If you help me leave, you betray the Party and the state. It doesn't frighten me. I'm a man who could die at any moment." Lin Nansheng smiled too, and looked up at him. "There are some things I have no choice but to do."

"Doesn't it strike you that this is a test of you as well?" Gu Shenyan's face turned hard in an instant — then, just as quickly, he smiled and pointed at a window in the little building across the way. "If I'm not mistaken, there's a lip-reader standing at that window, watching your mouth through a pair of field glasses this very moment."

Lin Nansheng gave nothing away. He only held his gaze on those unfathomable eyes, held it until Gu Shenyan let out a long breath of smoke and said not one word more.

But between the two of them, the battle on the go board turned harrowing for the first time.

On 23 August 1943, fifty-four Japanese aircraft flew out of Wuhan to bomb Chongqing for the last time. By the time the diving planes had dropped their loads and turned away through the fire and the smoke, the old servant found that Gu Shenyan had long since vanished. Toward evening Lin Nansheng was summoned to the courtyard. Director Hu was already waiting when he came through the gate. Neither man spoke. Led by several plainclothesmen, they went over the house and grounds in silence, then stood together on the steps.

Director Hu looked at Lin Nansheng. "If he's gone to Yan'an, or to Nanjing, we're both finished."

"I doubt he's gone anywhere at all." Lin Nansheng's eyes stayed on the unfinished game set out on the board. As he spoke he picked up the copy of the Wangyou Qingle Ji lying on the chair, turned to a page, and studied it against the board for a good while. Then he turned to the old servant. "This manual runs to three volumes. Go and find the other two."

The old servant did not dare move. He looked up and waited until Director Hu gave the sign, then hurried inside.

Director Hu plainly knew nothing of go, still less how to read a game record. He took the copy of the Wangyou Qingle Ji from Lin Nansheng's hand. "What is this? The mother text of a cipher?"

His eyes on the black and white stones, Lin Nansheng said, "It should be Morse, lightly enciphered against the game records."

With that he pulled up a chair, sat, took a handful of black stones, and began laying them out on the board.

Two mornings later, but for the broken walls and the rubble, there was no sign anywhere in Chongqing that it had been bombed at all. Lin Nansheng walked down to the Chaotianmen wharf, pressed into the crowd and looked about him for a while, then turned and went into a busy teahouse.

In a private room over the river sat Gu Shenyan, in a clean white silk gown. Seeing Lin Nansheng come in, he smiled, turned one of the teacups upright on the table, poured tea into it, drew a small silver box from inside his gown, opened it, took out a pill, and swallowed it down with the tea. Then he pushed back his sleeve, glanced at the watch on his wrist, and said, "We have about half an hour."

Lin Nansheng nodded and sat down across from him.

Then Gu Shenyan smiled again. "It seems I'm still good for something. I'm not so old yet that I need you to spirit me out."

He picked up the cigar resting on the ashtray, drew on it with pleasure, and turned to the river beyond the window, as though surveying the whole of his life; and soon the smile drew back into the depths of his eyes.

At twenty, Gu Shenyan had crossed the oceans to study in France, and there he joined the Chinese Youth Communist Party in Europe. He came home to enroll at the Whampoa Military Academy, and served in both Eastern Expeditions and in the Northern Expedition. During the party purge of 1927 he made, in Shanghai, the most consequential choice of his life: he broke with the Chinese Communists, and afterward followed Dai Li into the Lixingshe. All of this stood recorded in the files of the Bureau. What was not on record was that, passing through Guangxi, he had gone to the prison at Nanning to visit a man he had no business visiting. The Vietnamese there had been his classmate in France; he had gone under the names Nguyen Ai Quoc, Li Duan, Hu Guang, and Qiu Weng, and now called himself Ho Chi Minh. The first thing Gu Shenyan did on returning to Chongqing was to have this intelligence relayed to No. 50 Zengjiayan. Boss Dai flew into a fury, and for the first time dressed him down to his face in the office. "This is treason against the Party and the state."

"I only wanted him back in his own country as soon as could be, to organize Vietnam's counterattack against the Japanese — to tie down the Japanese army with forces of his own, and so ease the pressure on our Expeditionary Force in the Burma-India theater." When he had said it, Gu Shenyan raised his wrist and looked at the watch again, then carefully stubbed out the cigar. He looked at Lin Nansheng, and all at once he smiled. "Mine has been a life of disappointment."

Lin Nansheng was silent a long while. He looked at him. "Then you could choose again."

Gu Shenyan shook his head and pressed his lips together. He nudged a volume lying on the table — Selected Poems of Baudelaire — gently across to Lin Nansheng, and patted it once with his hand. "Perhaps it can free you from your present bind. But who is there to help those of us buried behind enemy lines?"

A bitter smile crossed Gu Shenyan's face. He reached to unfasten the watch at his wrist, but his fingers would no longer obey him. Lin Nansheng rose quickly and unfastened it for him.

Gu Shenyan looked at the watch — a plum-blossom brand, with no second hand. "I had meant to leave it to you," he said. "Now I see it clearly. I must give him a way out."

By the time he had said it, Gu Shenyan was spent, but still he forced his arm out through the window and dropped the watch into the river; then, like a man who had seen his last wish done, he sank back into the chair and let out a long breath. Blood ran from his nostrils in that same moment, dripping onto the white front of his gown, and he seemed not to feel it at all, letting it spread across his chest, red as flowers in the full bloom of spring. Something came back to Lin Nansheng. His eyes went wide. "You still haven't told me — where did the other coffin, the one that left the Renji Hospital, actually go?"

But Gu Shenyan could speak no more. The pill in its sugar coating had already taken his life.

When at last Director Hu lost patience waiting below and led his men in through the broken door, Lin Nansheng was still sitting across from Gu Shenyan, gripping his teacup, not moving at all.

Two weeks later, working from the markings in the Selected Poems of Baudelaire and using the Wangyou Qingle Ji as his key, Lin Nansheng decrypted the roster and the contact methods of the whole Shanghai network, and headquarters commended him for it. In truth these had never left the safe in the Juntong's records room: the rosters had been cunningly hidden between the lines of the very files Gu Shenyan had submitted for the record. Handing the decrypted documents to Director Hu, Lin Nansheng said, "Every extra person who knows puts these people in that much more danger."

Director Hu shook his head. "The greatest danger is betrayal."
