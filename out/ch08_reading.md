## Shanghai Gang Figures I Have Known

{d} Xue Gengshen

Before liberation I worked in the French Concession police for more than twenty years and had dealings with a good many gang people and with hoodlums great and small; here I set down some recollections, choosing the essentials.

### I. A Few Famous Gang Chiefs

The highest generation-rank in the modern Green Gang is the "Da" generation. In the Shanghai area there were only a few such men, most of low social standing and unknown, the one exception being Zhang Renkui, styled Jinhu, who in the days of the Northern warlords had served as garrison commander of Tonghai in Jiangsu and took in more than thirty thousand disciples over the years, of every sort—scholars, farmers, workers, merchants, and students. Anyone who took Zhang as his "old man" thereby held the standing of the "Tong" generation. In his last years Zhang lived at the Wanyuan garden on Avenue Haig in Shanghai (present-day Huashan Road). Huang Jinrong, Chen Shichang (Du Yuesheng's "old man"), Xia Qifeng (who once served as foreign minister), and others were all his favored disciples.

While Zhang Jinhu was at Nantong he was on close terms with the late-Qing top graduate Zhang Jian (styled Jizhi). In those years, when the top graduate Zhang asked old Yu Qisheng to commission my late father, Xue Zhongjiang, to buy cotton-mill machinery from England, a celebration banquet was held at Nantong, to which my father, Yu Qisheng, and Zhang Jinhu were invited. Later, learning that I was Xue Zhongjiang's son, Zhang Jinhu invited me, along with the senior International Settlement police officers Liu Shaokui and Zhang Yuanqing and the managers of the three big department stores (Wing On, Sincere, Sun Sun), to dine at the Wanyuan garden. After the meal he said to me privately: You are the son of an old friend of mine, and it gladdens me to see you rise so high (I was then a chief inspector of the French Concession police). But in these troubled war years I have one word of advice for you—a man dreads fame as a pig dreads getting fat; remember it well, remember it well. I have always thought this a piece of counsel worth pondering, and worth taking as a motto.

The Hong Gang man of higher standing in the modern Shanghai area was Xu Langxi of Shaanxi (a fellow provincial and close friend of the Kuomintang elder Yu Youren), who then lived in a small French-style villa on Rue Marco Polo (present-day Madang Road) at Observatory Road in the French Concession.

The Hong Gang's sphere had originally been the middle Yangtze, especially the region of Yichang, Hankou, and the Dabie Mountains. The first password when the brothers met was "We drink together the water of the Yangtze." Later, when the warlords of the southwestern provinces trafficked in opium (particularly the Sichuan and Yunnan grades), they shipped the raw opium by steamer from Chongqing to Shanghai, hiring Hong Gang bosses to guard it along the way; when the ships reached the Taikoo wharf at Xinkaihe in the French Concession, it was handed over to the Shanghai Green Gang combine of Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng to be taken on. The stewards on the ships and the little foremen who moved the cargo on the docks were nearly all Hong Gang members, and because their interests were bound together and they depended on one another, the Green and Hong gangs merged imperceptibly into one.

I came to know Xu Langxi through the introduction of Yao Zhubo (that is, Yao Shu, also a gang elder brother), deputy chief of the Special Higher Section of the International Settlement police, and we saw each other often. Old Xu had contributed to the revolutionary cause led by the Chinese Communist Party, and so after liberation he was treated leniently, which shows that the Communists do not forget an old friend.

### II. The Origins of the "Little Punks" of Zhengjia Wooden Bridge

When the foreign settlements of Shanghai were first laid out, the French chose the tract running west from the Bund outside the Small East Gate, along the city moat, reckoning that being close to the walled city of Shanghai county it had a livelier commercial life, while to the north lay the moat that marked the boundary with the British Settlement. This moat, also called the "Yangjingbang," was later filled in with rubbish to make Avenue Édouard VII, today's Yan'an East Road. In those days there was a bridge over the moat outside the Old North Gate, called the "Zhengjia Wooden Bridge." South of the bridge was Old North Gate Street in the French Concession; north of it was Fujian Road in the British Settlement, also called Stone Road. Because the longan and sandalwood shops the Fujianese kept were mostly clustered here, it was named Fujian Road.

Farmers from Nanhui, Fengxian, and other counties of Pudong would row little wooden boats loaded with bean stalks and rice straw to sell by the bridge, and a number of "little punks" (the common name for loafing petty hoodlums) gathered at the bridge approach; when they came upon an honest farmer or boatman, they would pick a quarrel and extort money. If the victim cried out and a British Settlement constable came to interfere, they fled to the south of the bridge; if a French Concession constable came to interfere, they fled to the north. Because the two ends of the bridge belonged to the British and French settlements, like two separate countries—an offense committed in the British Settlement was no concern once one crossed into the French, and the reverse held too—the bad elements found their loophole in this; and so a saying went round in old Shanghai society, "Watch out for the little punks of Zhengjia Wooden Bridge," which became the common folk's byword for guarding against being fleeced.

Some small traders who had to row past the place every day would, to spare themselves trouble, take the initiative of bribing the stronger among the punks to come forward and protect them, and then went a step further and took the strongest of the punks as their "old man"; so a local force for evil gradually took shape, some of them, backed by the plainclothes detectives of the two settlements' police, becoming the chiefs of gangster rings.

### III. Huang Jinrong and the French Police

Huang Jinrong got his start mounting paintings in a Suzhou painting-mounting shop, had a higher-primary-school education, and came to Shanghai around 1900. When he first arrived, he drifted about the Zhengjia Wooden Bridge area as a little punk. The first men he came to know were Ding Shunhua and Cheng Ziqing, sons of Nanhui-county farmers who rowed firewood boats. Ding was good at boxing; Cheng, nicknamed "Blackskin Qing," was a Zhenjiang man who had started as an apprentice in a rice shop and had three years of tutoring in a private school—from boyhood he had heaved rice sacks and had a very strong grip—and later entered the French police as a constable and finally rose to chief inspector of the political section, all told over a span of thirty-seven years. The three of them, being birds of a feather, swore brotherhood in the Green Gang: Huang the eldest, Ding the second, Cheng the third.

To deal with this crowd of little punks at the Zhengjia Wooden Bridge, the French Concession authorities used the method of fighting poison with poison and hired Huang Jinrong as a plainclothes detective of the criminal section (colloquially a "pao dating"). Huang took most of the little punks as his disciples and kept them in hand, using them as his capital for scoring merit with the French police. Through Huang's connections, Cheng and Ding both entered the French police as constables one after another. Turning these advantages to account, Huang did in fact solve a number of theft cases and was gradually promoted to detective sergeant—and in the Shanghai settlements of those days, under foreign rule, the position of detective sergeant was no small thing.

Leaning on the power of the criminal-section chief Chazel in the French Concession, Huang began to have a name in Shanghai society. In the days of the Northern warlords he struck up ties with the Zhejiang military governor Lu Yongxiang and the Shanghai defense commissioner He Fenglin (Lu's trusted man) and trafficked in opium in partnership with them. Through Zhang Yishu, a France-returned man in Shanghai, Huang colluded with the French police's chief inspector, Captain Fiori, and at first sold opium half-openly at Baoyu Li in Dongxinqiao, the supply coming up from Hankou aboard the naval vessels at Gaochangmiao under He Fenglin's command. Later Huang, through his blood brother Chen Shichang, hooked up with Ma Xiangsheng, Jin Tingsun, Gao Xinbao, and others to ship it straight from Hankou to the Taikoo wharf in Shanghai, and openly ran opium and gambling businesses in the French Concession. He set up opium dens at Baoyu Li in Dongxinqiao and at Baokang Li on Songshan Road off Avenue Joffre, and gambling houses at 181 Avenue Foch (today the Luwan District gynecology hospital on Central Yan'an Road), at Shenji Li by Chujiaqiao on Rue Montauban (today's South Xizang Road), and in the first lane of Baoyu Li in Dongxinqiao. As Huang rose meteorically, the power of the Shanghai Green Gang rose with him, and he became Shanghai's number-one "notable." Cheng Ziqing told me that Yang Hu and Mei Lanfang were both counted among his disciples, and that he took in tens of thousands of disciples north and south. At his sixtieth-birthday celebration, his disciples raised a great sum to build a Huang Family Garden at Caohejing in his honor, which after liberation was renamed Guilin Park.

Huang Jinrong and Wei Tingrong fell out over the stage beauty Lü Meiyu, and in the end Lü married Wei. Wei was then a Chinese member of the French Municipal Council and commander of the French Concession's Chinese Volunteer Corps, and his father-in-law Zhu Baosan was a great comprador-merchant and head of the Shanghai Chamber of Commerce, on whom the authorities of both the British and French settlements relied. With all these connections, Wei naturally would not defer to Huang, and not only carried off the beauty but lodged charges with the French Foreign Ministry over Huang's various misdeeds, forcing Huang to resign his post as detective sergeant of the French police. As misfortunes never come singly, Huang also clashed with Lu Yongxiang's son Lu Xiaojia over the famous actress Lu Lanchun and took a blow from it; this, along with other factors, gradually weakened Huang's position in Shanghai, and Du Yuesheng came up behind and surpassed him.

The Wei-Huang quarrel later drew in the open and hidden struggle between the gentry-councilor faction and the big gangsters of the French Concession. In point of strength Huang's side outdid Wei Tingrong in the French Concession, but a force for evil makes too many enemies and rouses public anger, and the upper ranks of the Shanghai Catholic community in particular all supported Wei. At the same time the France-educated Xu Wencai, and Zhu Kongxiang—younger brother of Zhu Kongjia (a Chinese member of the French Municipal Council and an important director of the Nanshi Water and Electric Company)—came forward through the French resident Destes and put out a French-language daily called La Vérité, concentrating their fire in attacks on the opium and gambling openly run in the French Concession, aiming at the three gangster notables Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, and Zhang Xiaolin, and pointing out especially that Huang had made his start by relying on his French Concession connections. The moment this paper appeared it caused a sensation and drew the notice of the French Foreign Ministry. Just then Wei also charged Huang before the French government several times with "taking in disciples wholesale, swindling under false pretenses, running opium and gambling, and harming the residents," pointing out especially that on the open ground outside the Fazang Temple on Rue Ratard (today's Jinan Road) he had thrown a great banquet for the sixtieth birthday of the French police criminal-section chief Chazel, collecting birthday gifts from more than ten thousand merchants and residents and stirring up trouble by the show of it. Under all these charges, the Paris authorities recalled the Shanghai consul-general, Koeclin, to ask about the gangsters and the opium and gambling in the settlement.

The charges being verified by the French government, the police commissioner, Captain Fiori, was removed and transferred back to France, replaced by Lieutenant Colonel L. Fabrer, and the professional diplomat Jobez was made deputy commissioner, with opium and gambling strictly banned and the gangsters' arrogance sharply cut down. From then on Huang drew in his edge. It is said that on the eve of liberation, on Mei Lanfang's advice, he sheltered some revolutionary democrats and so was able to earn merit and redeem himself; and so in the campaign to suppress counterrevolutionaries he was treated leniently by the People's Government, kept under control at home for life and dying a peaceful death.

### IV. Huang Jinrong's Relationship with Du Yuesheng

In 1939, after I was promoted to special-grade chief inspector with the treatment of a foreign national, I was invited through Cheng Ziqing's good offices at least once a week to bathe at the Xiaoyaochi Baths at the corner of Minguo Road (today's Renmin Road) in Dongxinqiao in the French Concession. Huang would have the head bath-boy, Ah Wu, set out special Henry-brand cigars, Garrick cigarettes, and specially made Chinese and Western pastries. In the private room at the Xiaoyaochi, Huang spoke to me many times about his relationship with Du Yuesheng. He said: "On the recommendation of my Suzhou townsman Chen Shichang, I took Du Yuesheng into the opium and gambling businesses I ran in the French Concession—everyone knows that. In the beginning he called me Uncle Huang. When the Northern Expedition army came victorious to Shanghai, not long before the April 12 party purge, Chiang Kai-shek came to Shanghai, and at Longhua airfield I personally introduced Du Yuesheng to Chiang Kai-shek (Du had gotten to know me back when he was at the Shanghai exchange, through Yu Qiaqing's introduction). Present at the time were the garrison commander Yang Hu, his headquarters secretary-general Chen Qun, and Cheng Ziqing of the French police. Chiang Kai-shek said to Du and me, 'There'll be important work ahead, and I'll be counting on you two!' After the purge, my disciple Chen Peide and Du's disciples Lu Jingshi, Zhou Xuexiang, and others became bosses of the Shanghai labor unions. But Chen was nowhere near Lu and the rest in ability, and Du also played his part in pushing the sale of the National Government's bonds, so his standing came to far outstrip mine. After that, on the telephone Du stopped calling me Uncle Huang and switched to 'Brother Jinrong'—and I must say it left a bad taste in my mouth."

But Huang often had Cheng Ziqing carry word to me, asking me to look after Du's own master, Chen Shichang (who lived at the corner of Rue Père Robert and Route des Sœurs in the French Concession), and I thought him a man who set great store by the loyalty of the brotherhood. In short, it was through Huang's connection that Du could rise from a fruit peddler to a boss of opium and gambling, and then hook up with the Kuomintang and climb straight up—and that he came to respect Huang less than before is also a fact. As a saying among the Shanghai gangsters has it: "Between brothers you can share hardship, but not riches!"

### V. My Dealings with Du Yuesheng

In 1930, more than a thousand workers of the machinery department of the French Electric and Water Company in Shanghai demanded a suitable raise, and the management refused. Under the leadership of Xu Amei, the workers launched a strike that lasted more than a month, and the management's attempt to buy off Xu Amei was rebuffed. The French police's chief inspector then told Du Yuesheng that if he could not help settle the labor trouble, he would order a ban on all opium and gambling, and consider lifting it again only after the trouble had died down. Du at once sought out the strike leader Xu Amei, offering to take him as a disciple and to give him a monthly allowance of three hundred yuan. When Xu would not agree, Du bought off, at heavy cost, the French detective-head of the French police political department, Marmorat, the interpreter Lu Kuaidong (who later changed his name to Lu Zhaohua), the Chinese detective-head Fan Guangzhen (a Juntong plant), and Zhao Zibo (a student of Yu Youren), and together they laid a scheme: Fan first had several hundred forged Communist leaflets printed with inflammatory contents (Fan had them printed at the Gaosheng Da paper shop on Rue du Consulat, today's East Jinling Road), and one night Marmorat led a party of more than thirty men—Cheng Ziqing, Zhu Liangbi, Fan Guangzhen, and others—to search Xu Amei's house, forced the leaflets into Xu's bedroom, and marched Xu off to the Third Branch of the Jiangsu High Court in the French Concession, prosecuting him under the National Government's promulgated "Emergency Statute for Crimes Endangering the Republic."

The French Concession authorities had always recognized only the Nanjing government's "Criminal Code of the Republic of China" and not the special criminal statutes, but this time they broke the rule and invoked one, and the court, treating it as a case of inciting a strike, sentenced Xu to ten years' imprisonment. Afterward Zhang Fubao, Shi Quanfu, and others took Du as their master and led the French Company's union; and so a stirring strike struggle was suppressed.

Later, when the French police commissioner Fabrer ordered a ban on all opium and gambling businesses, Du Yuesheng tried to drag Fabrer in through the France-trained engineer Zhang Yishu, and sent someone to present him with a "golden table service" (a full set of banquet tableware, made of two thousand taels of gold). Fabrer not only flatly refused it but demanded that Du run a notice in the two great Shanghai papers, the Shen Bao and the Xinwen Bao, admitting the error of the gift and guaranteeing not to do the like again, on pain of being driven out of the French Concession.

This thunderbolt of an affair bore on the future of the big gangsters, so Du used his disciples Zhao Zhiying (a meter-reading team leader at the French Electric and Water Company) and Shen Jinggeng to stir up a strike of the two thousand men of the traffic department; it made a mighty show, lasting more than two months, all the trams in the Shanghai French Concession stopped running, and the traffic and public order of the French Concession were gravely disrupted.

When the police held a security meeting, Cao Bingsheng and I both took part. I put it this way at the time: "Since Lieutenant Colonel Fabrer took office and abolished the two great evils of opium and gambling, he has won the praise of all Shanghai's Chinese residents and is spoken well of by everyone. But ever since Shanghai was opened as a treaty port by the Anglo-Chinese Opium War, Chinese and foreigners have lived mixed together, a mixed lot good and bad, and the settlement situation especially is very complicated, with gangsters and local bullies running rampant. If the methods used against them are too severe, it may rouse resentment—they may even send their disciples about to throw grenades and do harm, disrupting public order; and when Paris sees this, it damages the prestige of the police. For this reason I propose that Du Yuesheng first be given twenty-four hours to write a formal apology to you, admitting his fault and pledging not to do the like again; if Du complies, the commissioner's prestige will stand higher and the matter can be settled to everyone's satisfaction." Fabrer accepted my proposal. I told Du, and he gladly agreed and at once notified Zhao Zhiying to end the strike; and from that time he gave me three hundred silver dollars a month, later raised to five hundred.

Afterward Zhao and Shen, the two of them, were dismissed by the French management and taken into Du's household. When I went to Hong Kong to visit Du in 1940, Du assigned Zhao to receive me on his behalf.

In 1934, Song Ziliang took up with a dance-hall girl surnamed Zhang at the Vienna Ballroom in Shanghai, and she became pregnant; the woman demanded of Song an allowance of 100,000 yuan, Song would not agree, and the two sides wrangled without end. Song asked Du to handle it. Du summoned the dance-hall girl to the Zhonghui Bank building on Old North Gate Street in the French Concession, and the moment she arrived she was seized by four bodyguards, taken by car out beyond the mouth of the Wusong, and drowned in the sea, unborn child and all—what is colloquially called "planting lotus."

In 1936, when the French police's deputy commissioner Jobez was reopening the case of Gu Zhuxuan's killing of Tang Jiapeng, the manager of the Great World, he got wind of this dance-hall girl's murder and investigated it himself, learning the whole of what had happened. He told me what his investigation had found, and asked me to keep it strictly secret and on no account let it out, for it touched the affairs of the Soong family, important figures in the Kuomintang government, and the French authorities would have to consult the Paris government before they could decide how to proceed at law. Just at that time the Sino-Japanese war broke out, and soon France was crushingly defeated in Europe and Jobez and the others all went off to Africa to join General de Gaulle's Free French forces; and so the matter came to nothing.

### VI. Zhang Xiaolin's End

Zhang Xiaolin, a Hangzhou man of the "Tong" generation of the Zhejiang Green Gang, after coming to Shanghai struck up ties with Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng and built a residence together with Du at 216 Rue Wagner in the French Concession. Because the Shanghai defense commissioner He Fenglin came under the Zhejiang military governor Lu Yongxiang, and Zhang was a Zhejiang man, in the combine formed by Huang, Du, and Zhang—the "Three Big Bosses"—Zhang was specially in charge of keeping up the connection with Lu Yongxiang and He Fenglin, colluding with them to traffic raw opium, while Huang and Du dealt specially with the French Concession authorities.

In the early part of the war, the Kuomintang side once urged Zhang to leave Shanghai, but he refused on a pretext, and was later shot dead at the window of his Rue Wagner residence. The chief reason he was killed, it is understood, was that he had accepted an appointment from the Japanese-puppet side and was preparing to become governor of Zhejiang. Three days before his death, he received the letter of appointment at the home of Cui Deguang at 54 Qishan Village on Dianyuan Road, and was killed soon after. The assassin was his own bodyguard, and also the son of the wet nurse of Zhang's eldest son, Fadang (a France-returned student); his name was Lin Huaibu (nicknamed "Monk Wang"). The whole plan was carried out under Chen Mo, the Shanghai action-group head sent by Zhou Weilong of the Juntong. The killer was a fine shot: that day, because the bodyguards and the chauffeur were quarreling in the courtyard, Zhang leaned out of an upstairs window to look down, and the bullet entered through his mouth and passed out through the back of his skull. The quarrel had been staged on purpose, to draw Zhang into craning his head out the window so he could be shot. A secondary reason was that Zhang had meant to use the power of the Japanese puppets to take Du Yuesheng's place in Shanghai while Du was away; so Du, conferring with Dai Li, used the pretext of Zhang's coming defection to the enemy to "eliminate a traitor" in good and proper form.

After the event, Zhao Zhiyou got in touch with the French authorities and put it that the killer had all along borne a grudge against Zhang Xiaolin for being small-minded and hot-tempered, and had killed him to vent his resentment and take revenge. Through this account, the French police's deputy commissioner Jobez judged the murder an ordinary criminal case, not touching on politics, and so the Japanese puppets could find no way to meddle in it. Zhao Zhiyou's French wife also spoke to me about the inside of the affair. The killer was later released and sent to the interior.

### VII. Other Gang Bosses Connected with the French Concession

Besides Huang, Du, and Zhang above, there were also two "big bosses of Jiangbei," Jin Jiuling and Gu Zhuxuan. Gu, also called Gu Si, and Jin both took Huang Jinrong as their master; Gu ran the Tianchan Stage on Fuzhou Road in the International Settlement and was a bosom friend of Yu Qiaqing. This was a murder-demon famous throughout Shanghai: by the account of his trusted disciple Wang Xinggao, Gu had murdered at least seven men of note, two of them lawyers. Because he commanded a force for evil, he could go on unpunished, and he took in more than ten thousand disciples, including the plainclothes detectives of the two settlements and the rickshaw bosses. Gu and Jin were both leading men of the Subei Native-Place Association in Shanghai.

Gu Zhuxuan and Tang Jiapeng were both disciples of Huang Jinrong, and to seize the manager's post of the Great World amusement hall that Huang had opened, Gu incited his disciple Wang Xinggao to kill Tang. Wang was afterward arrested and sentenced to ten years. It had been agreed at first that Gu would bear all the expenses of Wang's family, but after three years Gu failed to pay on time, plainly meaning to go back on the deal. Taking the chance when Jobez was touring the Rue Massenet prison, Wang secretly informed Jobez of the inside of the case (Jobez was fluent in Chinese) and exposed Gu as the instigating principal. The investigation bore it out, but Gu had vast resources, so Jobez conferred personally with the International Settlement's chief inspector and, keeping it from the plainclothes detectives of the two settlements' police (since most of them were disciples of Huang and Gu), had Gu arrested directly and handed over to the Second Special District Court of Shanghai for trial. Though Gu's family spent many gold bars, under the French police's watch he could not but be sentenced to ten years, held in the Rue Massenet prison. Only when the war began, on Gu Zhutong's coming forward to bail him out, did he regain his freedom.

During the war Gu did some work useful to the revolution over the medical supplies and other goods shipped through Xinkaihe and the Taikoo wharf in the French Concession to the New Fourth Army areas in Subei, and after liberation was treated leniently by the government and kept under control for life.

### VIII. The Various Criminal Organizations and Illegal Activities

Under the direction, protection, and control of the gang bosses, old Shanghai society had many criminal organizations engaged in illegal activities.

(i) The pickpocketing organization. This organization was a deep-rooted secret ring whose details none but its own chiefs knew. In the spring of 1934 the International Settlement police found a list in a resident's house of criminal-section police staff who had ties to the pickpockets and drew a daily allowance from them—sixty-five in the International Settlement and twenty-seven in the French Concession—and all ninety-two were dismissed by the two forces.

In fact the Chinese detective-heads and detective sergeants Lu Liankui, Liu Shaokui, Jin Jiuling, Lü Zuolin, Rao Laosi, and others of both the British and French settlements were all connected with the organization, and were the "lines" the settlement's Chinese detective-heads and sergeants put out. The organization had more than five hundred members, divided into "coarse" and "fine" thieves. The "fine" thieves were the top-grade pickpockets, trained from the age of seven, in two groups of boys and girls, the very ablest of whom were called "little young masters."

Anyone who could rise to the rank of "little young master" had to go regularly "on assignment," transferred to Nanjing, Beijing, Taiyuan, Suzhou, Hangzhou, Guangzhou, Hankou, Hong Kong, and other places to lead the work of the secret trade.

The pickpockets' places of activity in Shanghai were chiefly stations, theaters, public conveyances, big department stores, jewelry shops, silk-and-cloth shops, and the like, and each member had his specialty; goods that had been picked had to be held for three days before they could be disposed of. The daily meeting point was Huang Jinrong's tavern, north-facing, at the west end of the Baxianqiao market in the French Concession, from eight to ten in the evening. A man with the right connections could, with the help of police staff, go to that tavern to reclaim his stolen things.

It is said this pickpocketing trade honored Shi Qian of Water Margin as its founding patriarch. Its internal organization was extremely strict: once you joined you could not leave, and anyone who did was killed. There were single-line contacts among the districts and lines, the internal ranks were rigidly graded, and before the war there was even a national head. This organization differed from the Green and Hong gangs, its historical roots reaching back still further, and along with "kidnapping" and "snatching" it belonged to the low, unranked special trades.

(ii) The human traffickers. The hoodlums who trafficked in people had a long history in old China; those who dealt specially in boys called it, in their cant, "moving stones," and those who dealt specially in girls called it "picking mulberry leaves."

The backer of the boy-trade was Shang Wu, criminal-section detective sergeant of the Hongkou police at the former Haining Road at Whashing. This man belonged to both the Green and Hong gangs, had more than two thousand disciples, and colluded with part of the Guangdong Chaozhou-clique Three Harmonies Society on Wusong Road in Hongkou to traffic in people. In those days some rich merchants of Guangdong, including overseas Chinese, would—besides the sons they had of their own—adopt some boys and give them an education, meaning later to hand the running of the business to whichever proved clever and capable; so the great majority of the boys were trafficked to Guangdong. Shang Wu served half a year as criminal detective sergeant when I was head of the Huangpu subprecinct, and after liberation was executed—such is the end of a man wholly lost to conscience.

The backer of the girl-trade was You Agen, criminal-section detective sergeant of the former International Settlement Laozha police, also a man of both the Green and Hong gangs, with more than five thousand disciples. Most of the girls they abducted in Shanghai they sold to the northern regions. Having grown rich by shielding the trafficking of people, You Agen bribed the foreigners and rose to be chief detective. In 1939 this man helped the Japanese gendarmerie seize and kill ten Miao anti-Japanese fighters.

These ten Miao men of resolve, brave and unyielding, were tortured to the point of maiming and gave their lives for the country; the Japanese army headquarters honored them as the "Ten Brave Men of China," set up a monument at Jiangwan, and sent a squad of soldiers to pay respects before their graves. For this You Agen was killed by Chen Mo of the Juntong at the door of the Yangtze Hotel on Hankou Road.

The chief head of the people-trafficking in the French Concession was Ren Wenzhen, chief inspector of the field section of the French police criminal section and a disciple of Du Yuesheng. Ren's backer was Yolenti, who had started as a plainclothesman in the Paris police, nicknamed "Medicine-jar Head"—the sense being that anything at all could be settled with money—and whose wife was a Chinese from Guangdong. Because these men served as protectors in the settlements, the buying and selling of the girls in the brothels was something no one dared interfere with.

Besides this, there was a disguised form of trafficking people abroad, called "international passport dealing." Old China was then beset by natural and man-made disasters, with the warlords fighting year after year and the people unable to make a living. Around Qingtian in Zhejiang province many young farmers were skilled in stone-carving, and the women bound their feet; they were lured by the "yellow-croaker" method, through Portuguese hoodlums in Macao, to Portugal and then on to France—the men forced into stone-carving labor, the women taken to Marseille, Nice, and other places to perform the "bound-foot dance." Marseille and Nice, the two of them, are famous scenic spots in the south of France, thick with villas, a summering and sightseeing resort for European and American capitalists. It was called "yellow-croaker dealing" because Chinese have yellow skin and are of the yellow race, and because on the passage to Portugal, should one die aboard ship he was thrown into the sea like a fish; and so the trafficking of Chinese was called "yellow-croaker dealing."

There was at the time one Chen Zhiming, a Wenzhou man and a disciple of Du Yuesheng, who colluded with the Qingtian county government, the passport section of the Shanghai Nanshi police headquarters, and the compradors of the steamship lines of the two countries, France and Italy, and abroad with the people-trafficking hoodlums of Portugal and France, in league with the degenerates in China's legations to those two countries, to lure Chinese youths abroad and profit from it, gravely damaging the nation's dignity. Their method was this: each person taken in was given a Western suit but had to pay Chen Zhiming a travel fee of four hundred fifty to five hundred yuan in fabi (worth several taels of gold at the time), all handled by Chen; in fact he did not buy a ship ticket at all, but colluded with the ship's crew to hide the people in the cargo hold, at most a little over twenty per ship each trip.

When the ship reached the port of Marseille, they were received by French hoodlums, who contrived to get them by train to the Portuguese capital, Lisbon, where they were received by the relevant people of the Chinese legation; after resting a while they signed a "certificate of travel to France," which allowed them to settle in France for three months. On reaching France, the men were promoted and introduced by local hoodlums, carving seals or figures for people out of Qingtian stone, at five to twenty-five francs apiece; over the three months, after all simple living expenses, each could clear on average 5,000 francs. The women performed the bound-foot dance and in three months could clear about 8,000 to 10,000 francs. At the end of the three months they returned to Lisbon and each paid 1,500 francs to the Chinese legation, which was called "flowers blooming on the tree." So the coming and going went round and round without end, one lot after another going to France; and after five or six years some managed to bring home a French wife. The staff of the Chinese legation in Portugal, whose government was forever behind in paying the legation's expenses, also lived off this trade.

These farmers, to pay the travel fee, often sold their homes and land, and even rented out their wives; the women had to undergo pornographic training and were forced to peddle photographs of Chinese classical-costume erotica besides. Moreover, since no ticket was bought once they were aboard—the fare pocketed entirely by Chen Zhiming—they hid in the coal piles after boarding, and when customs men searched the cargo ship too long, they often suffocated to death, their bodies then buried in the bellies of fish. At the time Japanese reporters made a point of photographing Chinese bound-foot women dancing and printed the pictures in Japanese magazines to insult China.

Mr. Shi Liangcai of the Shen Bao once exposed and denounced this matter in an editorial, drawing the notice of the French authorities. The Shanghai French Concession police were ordered to make a thorough investigation, and with the Nanshi Shanghai police chief Wen Hong'en helping in person, they arrested Chen Zhiming at the Wangjia wharf in Nanshi and handed him over to the Shanghai Second Special District Court in the French Concession for trial, where he was sentenced to five years. Chen's organizations in Shanghai were all broken up, the compradors of the Italian and French steamship lines were sternly warned, the relevant staff of the Qingtian county government and the passport section of the Shanghai police were dismissed and investigated, and by 1936 the case was at last brought to a close. Xi Neng (a Frenchman), who handled the case for the French Concession, and I were commended for it. For this the French consul-general also wrote an official letter to Wu Tiecheng, mayor of Greater Shanghai, thanking Director Wen for helping in person to crack the case.

According to what the comprador of the Canadian Empress steamship line, a Mr. Xia, disclosed at the time, Chen Zhiming confessed after his arrest that the hoodlums of the Guangdong Shantou clique had, through the Three Harmonies Society, trafficked more than fifty thousand young men and women between 1930 and 1936 to Paraguay and Uruguay in South America to open up virgin wasteland, all expenses borne by the two countries' governments, which paid the Shantou hoodlums one hundred U.S. dollars for every laborer they lured out. I passed this material on behalf of the French police to Director Wen Hong'en to forward to the National Government in Nanjing, but heard nothing of it afterward.

(iii) The smugglers. The Yangtze "diving ghosts" all belonged to the Hong Gang, their organization huge and very complex. In those days, near the Bund in the French Concession, on Avenue Édouard VII (today's Yan'an East Road), diagonally across from the Peace Goddess statue (the First World War memorial), there was a "Guanji Motor Transport Company" that stirred little by day; before four in the morning, more than ten little wooden boats would unload duty-evading imported goods from the foreign steamers at the Peace Goddess wharf, and the company's trucks would carry them to the foreign firms' warehouses—as many as five or six truckloads a day—with both the settlement's protection and the foreign firms' protection, truly "double insurance." The company's manager, Wu Zhilong, a Pudong man, took Huang Jinrong as his master and joined the Hong Gang at the same time, and had close ties with the Yangtze-clique diving ghosts, relying on the divers to bring up onto the little boats the goods dropped to the bottom by the foreign ships. During the war, when some foreign residents were forcibly interned by the Japanese army, Wu regularly mailed parcels to the foreigners connected with his smuggling. After the victory, recommended by these foreign merchants, Wu became comprador of the British egg firm at Yangshupu. From then on the smuggling was carried on by his son Wu Xiaolong. Wu Zhilong died during the ten years of turmoil; his son Wu Xiaolong was sentenced to twenty years in the campaign to suppress counterrevolutionaries.

Besides, before the war, smuggled goods coming in along the Zhejiang coast, apart from being sold on the spot, also entered Shanghai on boats carrying fruit, mountain products, and seafood, the chief goods being Western medicines, high-grade cosmetics, and luxury articles. In fact, in old China the biggest smuggling combine was the anti-smuggling apparatus that the Four Big Families, in league with the Juntong, controlled, which monopolized this business. In wartime such imported smuggled goods could be bought even in the Shanghai French Concession. Though the smuggling's market was nationwide, the Shanghai French Concession remained one of the chief distribution points. Some Kuomintang military and government notables used their authority to carry on large-scale smuggling—by air and by warship, or under the name of military supplies—and some did it by taking shares or entrusting others to trade; and even customs inspectors, coming upon smuggled goods of this kind, would let them pass unquestioned.

(iv) Opium and narcotics. The narcotics current in old Shanghai were of three kinds—opium, heroin powder, and red pills—the first two chief among them. Opium was of four grades: "Indian earth," also called "big earth," shipped from India, of the best quality and dearest price, beyond the reach of ordinary people; "Yunnan earth," produced in Yunnan, the top grade of domestic opium, which the Yunnan warlords forced the farmers to plant and which spread its poison over the whole country through smuggling; "Sichuan earth," produced mainly in the Sichuan-Xikang region, the economic mainstay of the Sichuan warlords; and "Mongol earth," produced in the Inner Mongolia region. Mongol earth was in turn of two kinds—one, Rehe earth, of finer quality, which Tang Yulin made his chief source of income while governing Rehe, and the other, Suiyuan earth, of poorer quality but suited to making heroin powder.

Heroin powder was used to make heroin and red pills, and was mostly brought into Shanghai by Japanese rōnin aboard warships and passed on through degenerate Koreans as middlemen. These men, protected by Japanese consular jurisdiction, could go about unharmed. The Japanese warlords used heroin powder and red pills on the one hand to poison the Chinese people and on the other as funds for the Black Dragon Society, the China-destroying apparatus led by Toyama Mitsuru.

In doing the opium business in the Shanghai French Concession by leaning on Huang Jinrong and Zhang Xiaolin, Du Yuesheng had two able helpers, Jin Tingsun and Xu Caicheng. Jin made a great fortune by it, built a mansion at the corner of Xinle Road and South Xiangyang Road (now the office of a transport company), and served besides as manager of the Huangjin Grand Theatre. Before the war, for a time, the Nanjing side appointed him manager of the Shanghai Aviation Lottery Company, housed in the Zhonghui Building on Old North Gate Street in the French Concession. Xu Caicheng was Du Yuesheng's confidential secretary; during the war, when Shanghai became an isolated island, Xu was named the Chongqing government's direct correspondent in Shanghai, and using his ties with Sakata of the Japanese Embassy's Kōain, played a great "two-man act," setting up a secret radio at his residence on Route Cohen to keep in communication with the Chongqing military and government organs.

Jin's disciple Lou Yinchuan and Gao Xinbao's disciple Wu Shibao were in charge, before 1930, of taking on the opium brought by river steamer from Hankou and Yichang to Xinkaihe in Shanghai at the Shiliupu wharf, then carrying it by car to the "earth shops" run by the Chaozhou Dapu clique at Baoyu Li in Dongxinqiao and Baokang Li off Avenue Joffre, whence it was distributed everywhere.

During the war, Sheng You'an, a descendant of the Qing official Sheng Xuanhuai, lived in a large garden villa at the corner of Rue Père Robert and Rue Corneille in the French Concession (today the corner of South Ruijin Road and Yongjia Road) and shipped and sold Mongolian-frontier opium under the name of the "Hongji Benevolent Hall." In fact he was controlled by the headquarters of the Japanese expeditionary army, which used Japanese planes to ship Inner Mongolian opium in bulk to Nanjing, handing it over to the "Hongji Benevolent Hall" to be shipped and sold throughout the Jiangnan region. Sheng's residence in the French Concession was guarded by police the Japanese side had asked the settlement authorities to post. He turned the business of pushing opium in the Jiangnan region over to Lan Bisu. Lan, a Dapu man of Guangdong who had always been in the opium trade, was a disciple of Du Yuesheng's disciple Ren Wenzhen (chief inspector of the field section of the French police), and sold chiefly in the Nanjing, Suzhou, Hangzhou, and Shaoxing region; after the victory he fled to Hong Kong. His large residence at 217 Yueyang Road was occupied in the early days after the victory by the gendarmerie captain Jiang Gongmei. Apart from the regions above, Lan Bisu also opened "dens" and "earth shops" around Jiumudi in Nanshi for retail and wholesale. In 1942 (France having by then been defeated in Europe), he colluded with the Japanese gendarmerie captain for southern Shanghai, Lieutenant Colonel Sugihara, to ship opium into the French Concession for sale each day from eleven in the morning to three in the afternoon. There were also Yan Chuntang, a Shanghai man and a disciple of Huang Jinrong, and Yan Chaosheng (nicknamed "White-Horse Chaosheng"), a Pudong man and a disciple of Chen Shichang, who both took part in these activities.

In the enemy-and-puppet period, the biggest heroin-powder dealers in Shanghai were Wang Yongkang, Li Zhengbao, Xu Amao, and others. Wang Yongkang, who took Chu Minyi as his adoptive father and had Ding Mocun for a backer, thus won the contract for Shanghai's night soil at the time and dealt in heroin powder besides. Li Zhengbao was a disciple of the puppet police chief Lu Ying. Xu Amao, nicknamed "Pickle Amao," took Du Yuesheng as his master; his narcotics-selling apparatus was very cleverly disguised, set inside the bath faucet of a large apartment—two pipes, made in Japan, one giving water for bathing, the other loaded with heroin powder that sprayed out at the press of a hidden button, not easily seen through by an outsider. He was on very close terms with Lu Liankui and Liu Shaokui, chief inspectors of the International Settlement police criminal section, and with Jin Jiuling and Lü Zhulin of the French Concession.

(v) Gambling. On the pretext of "aiding and developing Chinese education," the Shanghai settlement authorities, around the nineteen-twenties, held horse races each spring and autumn in the International Settlement and set up the "Mingyuan greyhound track," and in the French Concession founded the "Yiyuan greyhound track" and a "jai alai fronton"—all gambling in disguise, and many middle-class families were gambled into ruin. The Yiyuan greyhound track is today Culture Square, and the jai alai fronton has become the Luwan District Gymnasium—turning dens that harmed people into useful places, which shows the difference between the social systems of past and present.

In the mid-nineteen-twenties, Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng too, on the same pretext, colluded with the settlement's Chinese and foreign chiefs to set up a "club" openly in the French Concession—in fact a gambling house in disguise. The go-between was the France-educated Zhang Yishu (a student of Li Shizeng and a Chinese member of the French Municipal Council). The most famous gambling houses of the day were at 181 Avenue Foch, at Shenji Li by Chujiaqiao on South Xizang Road, and at Baoyu Li in Dongxinqiao on Zhejiang Road. The general manager of the three was Gao Xinbao, who paid the staff of the French police and the Mixed Court a special monthly allowance of forty to five hundred yuan, graded by rank. There was at the time a Chaozhou-clique Three Harmonies Society boss, Zheng Ziliang, of the Hong Gang, whose members all spoke Chaozhou dialect; he had approached Huang and Du to demand a monthly allowance of five thousand yuan and been refused, and so he often sent men to the above places to throw homemade cigarette-cake grenades, causing casualties. Sometimes they were seized by the gambling houses' bodyguards and made to vanish by the "planting lotus" method, thrown into the sea beyond the mouth of the Wusong. A dozen or more died such wretched deaths every year, and no one dared say a word. The French police too looked the other way, and the Shanghai papers great and small kept as silent as cicadas in the cold, all seemingly bought off.

Besides this, there was Du Yuesheng's fellow gang brother Ma Xiangsheng, who lived at 10 Route Dollfus in the French Concession and colluded with the hoodlum "Red-Foot Fat Ah" and others of the Ximen area of Nanshi to set up "flower-lottery booths" all over the two settlements, using the twelve zodiac animals as counters, a winner turning one yuan into thirty-six. Many working people, taken in, were left worse off in life, or driven into family quarrels, or even to suicide—no small harm.

After Shanghai fell in the war, the area west of Jing'an Temple was extra-Settlement road land, where the roadways had been under settlement police keeping the traffic and the residents on either side under Chinese authority. When Shanghai fell and the Chinese authorities withdrew with the army, no one governed it, and the foreign papers called it "Bad Land." Great numbers of traitors and desperadoes gathered there, opening in turn such big gambling houses as the "Six Nations Hotel" and the "Lübao Club," protected by the western-Shanghai big gangsters Pan Sansheng, Zhu Shunlin, Li Xiaobao, Wang Yongkang, and others. Later they moved again to Jiumudi in Nanshi. The bosses of these gambling tables were all of the Guangdong Chaozhou clique, and the gambling and the rake-off went on for as long as four years.

(vi) The wharf despots. After the settlements were laid out at Shanghai, many wharves were built along the Huangpu for the loading and unloading of ocean, coastal, and inland steamers. The labor hired on the wharves did chiefly carrying work, commonly called "wharf coolies."

Most of these wharves were run by foreign firms, which, for ease of management, appointed certain men as labor-contracting foremen. The contracting foremen were themselves hoodlums, mostly of the Hong Gang. These wharf contracting foremen were the so-called wharf despots. For each piece of cargo carried from ship to wharf or into a warehouse—two hundred jin at the lightest, over six hundred at the heaviest—the freight charge ran from ten to twenty-five cents in fabi per piece. They took sixty percent of it without lifting a finger, and the porters who really did the hard labor got only forty percent. These coolies were all hired by the day, the labor was heavy and the strain on the body great, and toiling in all weathers, rain or shine, hot or cold, they very easily fell ill, or met with injury or a fall into the river; their lives and health had no protection whatever.

A single wharf, when busy, would hire one or two hundred men, and the foreman's income was quite considerable. For ease of management they also hired little foremen, exploiting and oppressing layer upon layer. These foremen mostly belonged to the gangs, and had foreigners and their "second devils" to back them, while the coolies were a sheet of loose sand, unable to stand against them.

Nominally the wharf workers could get forty percent of the pay for their labor, but in fact they still had to bear all sorts of unreasonable outlays: at the festivals, or at the weddings and funerals of a foreman or a little contractor or their kin, the workers had to chip in gifts. These wharf despots all belonged to the gangs and kept in touch with one another, so that once one gang despot would not employ a man, he could hardly get a footing at any other wharf either.

Besides cruelly exploiting the workers, these foremen also lorded it lawlessly over the wharf and swindled honest travelers. The wharf, for example, was floored with wooden planks, one or two of which they would secretly change into movable boards. Such movable boards were of two kinds, the springing and the sliding: when a traveler came off the boat and set his luggage on the wharf, at the least carelessness his luggage would in a moment vanish into thin air—for a thief lay hidden beneath the wharf floor, seizing the chance to steal it away. These thieves were all disciples of the wharf despots, and afterward they split the loot on the spot. Having been head of the Huangpu subprecinct, I knew the inside of it well. I remember that at the Xinkaihe wharf there were two aggrieved parties, one surnamed Chen and one Liu, whom I had held through Huang Jinrong; a great deal of stolen goods was recovered, and in the end they gave a written pledge to reform. After liberation, in the campaign to suppress counterrevolutionaries, on my confirming that they had truly shown reform, their death sentences were commuted to life imprisonment.

As far as I know, before liberation the three greatest of the wharf despots each took in several thousand disciples. The first was Shen Guansheng, nicknamed "Broadsword Guan the Sage," a fellow gang brother of Du Yuesheng who had also joined the Hong Gang (from the nineteen-twenties on, the Green and Hong gangs mixed, and one could belong to both), and who held a great deal of real estate at Dingjia Street on Rue de Sœurs in the French Concession and was a sworn brother of the CC-clique intelligence man Liu Huai, the wharf-group head. The second was Li Maoling, who took Gu Zhuxuan as his master, made his start as a wharf despot, and in the days of the Japanese-puppet occupation was the traitor foreman of the coal yard of the Japanese firm Mitsui in Pudong, growing very rich, with such real estate as "Maoling Villa" on today's West Jianguo Road, "Maoling New Village" on Yueyang Road, and "Maoling Village" on Tianping Road in the French Concession. When Li Maoling was building the thirty three-story Western houses of "Maoling New Village" on Yueyang Road, since the site was in a first-class residential district, the regulations required first-class building materials too; through my help, on the pretext of the wartime shortage of materials, he was allowed to use third-grade materials instead, and to thank me, Li sold me the house at No. 21, Lane 79 Yueyang Road, in that new village, cheaply for thirty thousand yuan in puppet currency to live in for twenty-seven years. But times have changed, and I have enjoyed only seven years of it, with twenty years' rights still owing—which, for lack of proof, seems hard to settle. The third was Zhang Chunbao, nicknamed "Wharf Chunbao," despot of the inland wharves on Suzhou Creek, who died of illness long ago.

(vii) The Night-soil King. In old Shanghai most residents used chamber pots, and every morning from four to eight someone would pull a night-soil cart through the streets and lanes, calling out "Empty the pots." Each pot was charged twenty cents a month for emptying. The night-soil cart the scavenging workers used belonged to the night-soil foreman; at the night-soil wharf the foreman would add a cart of water to each cart of night soil, one becoming two, and sell it to the night-soil-boat farmers as fertilizer at one yuan a cart.

The International Settlement's night-soil wharf was by Suzhou Creek at Caojiadu, and the French Concession's on Rue Xujiahui at Dapuqiao (today the corner of South Ruijin Road). Both settlements had contracting night-soil foremen: for the International Settlement it was Wang Yongkang, and for the French Concession it was Ma Honggen, son of Huang Jinrong's concubine Sister Lu Gui. Take the French Concession: the yearly contract fee was 8,000 yuan, and there were four hundred night-soil carts in all—this was the foreman's initial cost. He paid each scavenging worker eight yuan a month and gave the relevant staff of the French police and the sanitation department 6,000 yuan, clearing about 10,000 to 12,000 yuan a month; apart from sending one man each morning to the wharf to register the number of night-soil carts sold to the farmers, he did no labor whatever and simply sat back and reaped the profit.

Ma Honggen, nicknamed "Ma the Third," was a disciple of Yang Hu and on very close personal terms with me. His bookkeeper, Zhang Quansheng, held all the power in his hands, owned a great deal of real estate on Rue Ratard in the French Concession, and ran the Rixinchi Baths.

### IX. The Yellow Way Society and the Green Gang Traitor Chang Yuqing

In the early nineteen-thirties the Japanese militarists sent Kawashima Yoshiko to Shanghai and Nanjing to carry on secret-agent work, on the one hand gathering intelligence on the inner workings of the Kuomintang, and on the other inciting Japanese monks to set fire on Xijiang Road to the Sanyou Industrial Company, which had been effective in the boycott of Japanese goods—so touching off the January 28 Shanghai resistance war. At this time Chang Yuqing, a Subei man of the "Tong" generation of the Zhabei Green Gang, acting on Kawashima Yoshiko's orders, sent men to Yancheng and other places in Subei to recruit fifty boys of about ten years old, and sent them through Hongkou in Shanghai to Japan for training by the Black Dragon Society. In early 1937 these children were all brought back to Hongkou in the guise of Japanese nationals to investigate the deployment of Chinese troops and the coastal defenses along the Jiangsu and Zhejiang coasts. After the August Thirteenth fighting broke out between China and Japan, these children, disguised as popsicle sellers, went to the points where Chinese troops were stationed in Nanshi, Pudong, and Zhabei to gather all sorts of information. The collecting point for the Nanshi area was set in an east-facing cold-drinks shop at the corner of Dongxinqiao Street and Avenue Édouard VII in the former French Concession, its keeper surnamed Wu, childhood name Ah Mao, a former worker at the Rihua Cotton Mill in Lujiazui, Pudong. Every night at ten, once the children had put together the information they had gathered, Wu would take a Yunfei taxi to deliver it to a Japanese living at 117 Avenue Road (today's West Beijing Road) in the International Settlement.

One day in late August 1937, at the iron gate on Minguo Road in Dongxinqiao in the French Concession, while residents going in and out were being searched, three thirteen-year-old popsicle-selling children were found with a great deal of Japanese silver on them; the Huangpu subprecinct sent the three children to the political department for investigation, and under my interrogation they told the whole of the above.

Afterward the French Concession authorities sent the boss Wu and all the children to the handover office at the Minguo Road iron gate, and turned them over to Liu Huai, the political-section chief of the Nanshi Public Security Bureau, and the detective Xue Shilin, whom I had arranged with beforehand. Liu later told me that two weeks before the Kuomintang army withdrew, they shot Wu Amao before the Chen Yingshi Memorial Tower at Ximen and sent all the children back to Subei.

After the August Thirteenth Incident, when the Japanese army occupied Zhabei, Chang Yuqing served as head of the maintenance committee for the Zhabei area, and under the direction of the Japanese special-service chief Colonel Kusumoto organized the Yellow Way Society and made himself its head. The setting up of this society was an attempt to reach the goal of destroying China under the false banner of promoting the morality of the yellow race.

Chang Yuqing's Yellow Way Society came directly under Colonel Kusumoto, head of the Black Dragon Society's Shanghai branch, and from 1939 its power gradually reached into the Shanghai International Settlement. Among those Chang bought over were the chief inspector Pan Shaoliang, a St. John's University graduate and senior officer for external liaison of the International Settlement police political department; Lu Liankui, chief inspector of the International Settlement police criminal section; and Lu Bohong, general manager of the Nanshi Water and Electric Company (who had served as head of the Nanshi district maintenance committee). All three of these were later killed by Chen Mo, head of the Juntong's Shanghai group.

I myself was also pestered many times by men Chang sent, but was not moved. At the time some of the plainclothes detectives of the two settlements' police were bought over by Chang, but because they had no real power the effect was small, and especially under the watch of the great mass of patriots they could do nothing. After the victory Chang Yuqing was executed.
