## Chapter Eight. The Hour of the Rooster, First Half (5 p.m.)

Her lovely eyes turned; she rose and went out, and in a little while carried back a plate of the Cibei Temple's plain-oil fritters, with a few flatcakes laid underneath. The fritters were fried in plain oil, and filling enough.

The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Rooster, first half.

Chang'an; Chang'an County; Guangde Ward.

Outside, the city of Chang'an had grown so merry it seemed near to melting; but this one room in Guangde Ward stayed cold and grim as ever.

It was a low brick house, roofed with no tiles but only two layers of blackened thatch. It stood squarely between the offices of the Jingzhao Prefecture and the Cibei Temple, with the Yong'an Canal running just alongside. It had once been the Prefecture's morgue, kept for the coroners to do their examinations in. A canal beside it to carry off the filth; a temple at its flank to see the dark souls across. By the common tale, it was for just this—that a body might be examined at any hour and his art of medicine honed on it—that Sun Simiao had chosen to live in Guangde Ward in his day.

Cao Poyan lay on a rough plank of elm, his chest faintly rising and falling, the blood from his belly seeping slowly into the wood and making its dark-red grain more savage still. He was not yet a corpse—but he would be one soon. The room was heavy with the breath of death; he could feel it, the cold gnawing fast at the little life that was left in him.

In Changming Ward, Zhang Xiaojing's blade-point had run him through the belly, and he had toppled to the ground. His years as a Wolf Guard had made his frame formidably tough, so that even a mortal wound would not stop his breath at once. When the Lüben Guards soldiers were clearing the scene and found there was still breath in Cao Poyan, they carried him straight back to the Jing'an Bureau.

At that moment Ma Ge'er and the rest were driving their cart in a headlong dash, and all the Bureau's attention was fixed there. So whoever took Cao Poyan in only gave his condition a cursory look, judged him not worth interrogating, and dumped him straight into this morgue. It was only by luck that one Lüben Guards soldier who had been in on the West Market roundup recognized him and entered his name in the record; otherwise Xu Bin might never have known of the matter at all.

The wooden door creaked open, and Zhang Xiaojing came alone into the death-room. He trod step by step across the uneven bluestone, holding a white lantern high in his left hand and a lacquered food-box in his right. The candle in the lantern swayed, its light and shadow shifting, and it threw his one-eyed face into a savagery all its own—like Yama come into the world.

At the sting of the light, Cao Poyan's eyes rolled once.

Candles call up spirits too readily, so no candlestand was ever set in a morgue; they used pine-torches instead. Without a word Zhang Xiaojing lit the four torches on the wall one by one, so the room grew a little brighter, then blew out his lantern and lifted from the food-box a bowl of yellow-brown broth to hold the life in a dying man.

He propped Cao Poyan's upper body up and wedged a block of wood behind his back to hold him. Then Zhang Xiaojing took a coroner's hook, hooked the man's mouth roughly open, gave a hard twist to pry the jaws apart, and forced the bowl of broth down his throat.

As the hot broth went into him, the color of Cao Poyan's face seemed to ease a little.

Zhang Xiaojing moved round to the head of the plank, bent down, and said in a low voice: "We meet again."

Cao Poyan kept his eyes shut and did not stir, but for one instant the muscles of his cheek twitched, betraying that he had indeed heard, and understood. A man on the edge of death has far less command of his body than before.

Zhang Xiaojing gave a low laugh and went on in fluent Türk: "Wolf Guards of the steppe—I have killed a good many, and you are the hardest of them all. A worthy adversary."

Cao Poyan was silent still.

"I know your kind, you Wolf Guards. Loyalty is your blood, honor is your soul. You live your lives for nothing but the words in the khagan's mouth." Zhang Xiaojing paced slowly around the plank bed, in no apparent hurry to come to the point. He reached out and stroked the bald patch shaved into the crown of Cao Poyan's head. "I am curious. A Wolf Guard as loyal as you, one who does not fear even death—how does he come to have his crown-hair shaved away?"

To shave a man's crown-hair meant that his soul had been claimed before its time—an extremely dishonorable thing to do to him. Sure enough, the moment Zhang Xiaojing raised it, Cao Poyan's breathing turned suddenly heavy, with a trace of humiliation in it, and of grievance unwilling to yield.

"The reason I can more or less guess. No sooner had you entered Chang'an than the Jing'an Bureau ambushed you, with heavy losses, and so your crown-hair was shaved off as a punishment. Oh—and by the way, I forgot to mention: your plan has already failed, or I should not be standing here now."

Zhang Xiaojing's voice was low and slow, as though chatting with an old friend: "The only ones with the standing to punish a Wolf Guard are the nobles of the Ashina house. Which is to say, above you there is at least one more man in charge, directing the whole of the Wolf Guards' operation. You lie here at your last gasp, while he is still at large and free."

Cao Poyan rolled his eyes a few times in contempt, as if mocking how clumsy Zhang Xiaojing's provocations were. But Zhang Xiaojing wagged a finger and clicked his tongue: "No, no—I am not tempting you to betray anyone. I know that would be useless with a Wolf Guard. I only mean to share a few things with you, so that you are not so lonely at your dying."

Zhang Xiaojing leaned against a pillar and began from his own conscription into the Jing'an Bureau, recounting the whole course of the pursuit in detail. His tone was easy, as though the man lying before him were a friend of many years and the two of them were warming green-ant wine over a red clay stove, drinking and talking as they went.

He told it frankly and minutely, threading in sharp remarks along the way—"hanging a smoke pellet inside the door showed real imagination," "the Tang court is a good deal stupider than you." Only, within these accounts, Zhang Xiaojing left out certain details, whether by design or not, and laid the colors on thick over others. This was an unfair duel: he had to handle every sentence with the utmost care, circling in toward his mark, while his opponent had only to shut his mouth and die to win.

"…Abducting Wang Yunxiu was a blunder. Yes, she is Wang Zhongsi's daughter—but how much sway can one woman have over the great affairs of war and state? If you meant to destroy Chang'an, you should have concentrated every resource on the one target."

"Why did you not get the ward-maps from the foreign merchants at the outset? That would plainly have been safer than going through Cui Liulang."

"The safe-house and the depot could both be found. Why was it only on the very day of the operation that you were hurried into the city?"

Like a cunning hunter, Zhang Xiaojing kept posing his counter-questions, luring the talk step by step onto the ground he had chosen. These questions were fated to go unanswered, but they let him govern the rhythm of the exchange. He had interrogated too many prisoners not to know the moment for the deadliest stroke.

Through it all Cao Poyan kept his eyes tightly shut, only his rising and falling chest showing that he still lived.

"…It may well be that you Türk Wolf Guards were being used by another party, to draw the Jing'an Bureau's eyes. And that party seized the chance to haul the fierce-fire thunder away for some purpose of their own. All these sacrifices you have made—you have only sewn a wedding-gown for someone else to wear."

This was his first assault: Zhang Xiaojing threw out his conjecture, then shut his mouth and left Cao Poyan to digest it for himself.

Cao Poyan opened his eyes and looked at the thatch of the ceiling. The thatch was thin, and through it one could see the changing light of the sky outside. He kept his silence, but Zhang Xiaojing could read his meaning: "And what of it? So long as Chang'an is destroyed, that is enough."

Whether it was the Türk Wolf Guards who did this thing or some other, Cao Poyan did not care. Zhang Xiaojing saw that attacking from this angle would not do, and shifted his line of assault in time.

"That's right—and what of it?" Zhang Xiaojing said with a broad grin. "The Tang realm is so vast. Chang'an gone, there is still Luoyang; there is Yangzhou, Jiangling, Chengdu. The empire has fifteen circuits and their governing seats, and more than three hundred prefectures—can you blow them all up? And how many are you Türks? Once the wrath of Tang burns its way to the steppe, your tribe will be torn out by the roots; your kin and your khagan will be reduced to the lowest, meanest herd-slaves."

Cao Poyan clenched his fists so hard that fresh blood welled again from his belly. Zhang Xiaojing lost no time in swinging the keen modao of his words:

"See, then—even if this plan succeeds, it is certain to bring down the full vengeance of Tang, and the ones who suffer worst will be the Türks themselves. You put in the most, you come off the most ruined, and you gain the least. When Ozmish Khagan was laying this attack, did he ever truly weigh the consequences? Was it for a moment's satisfaction—or because someone bewitched him?"

At these words Zhang Xiaojing noticed Cao Poyan's fingers give a sudden twitch. He knew he had struck the joint square this time.

"This whole affair—I fear that from the start someone with a design cozened your khagan into pushing the Türks out front to take the risk. A fine reckoning, that. Tang suffers grievous losses, the Türks are wiped out to the last of the clan, and that party? Not a hair harmed, and their bowls and jars brimming with profit."

Cao Poyan still made no sound, but the look on his face was not what it had been a moment before.

"To use the Türks, that party would have had to find a man inside the Türks to work with them. This man would need influence enough to talk the khagan round, authority enough to move the Wolf Guards—and he would have to be inside Chang'an itself to hold the situation in his own hands…"

Zhang Xiaojing slowed his speech, and Cao Poyan's chest began to heave fast.

"All of this—only that noble commander of yours could bring it off, could he not? He has betrayed Ozmish Khagan, sold out every Türk Wolf Guard, and cast the steppe into ruin past all redemption. Every effort and sacrifice of yours has become his gift to a new master—and this traitor is the very one who shaved the crown-hair of loyal men."

Before the words were out, Cao Poyan reared his head up and let out a cry like a wolf's howl: "The Right Shad!!!" The thatch of the roof shivered a few times at the sudden shout. Zhang Xiaojing caught the word keenly, and it startled him: the Türks had actually sent a noble of such high rank into Chang'an.

He laid a hand on Cao Poyan's chest and patted it as if to soothe him: "Every man must answer for his own choices. The shame of having your crown-hair shaved off by a traitor—only by killing him can the honor of a Wolf Guard be restored…"

Before Zhang Xiaojing had finished, Cao Poyan roared once more at the roof: "The Right Shad!!!"

Those two furious cries seemed to spend the last of the life left in him, and Cao Poyan's whole body began to convulse violently. Zhang Xiaojing had to hold his shoulders down and pour another mouthful of the broth into him. But this time no turn for the better came; the brown medicine ran out from the corner of his mouth, and the luster on Cao Poyan's face dimmed swiftly away.

Zhang Xiaojing bent close in haste and shouted in his ear: "Quick—say it! Where is the Right Shad?"

But Cao Poyan gave no answer; his whole being was flooded now with despair and fury. A Wolf Guard never fears death—but a Wolf Guard fears a death worth nothing. When he found that everything he had fought for was a lie, the collapse within him was enough to crush the very will to live.

Zhang Xiaojing had not looked for so great a reaction. He slapped at Cao Poyan's cheeks with all his might—if the man died here and now, the last thread of a lead would be severed for good. Seeing the light in the other's eyes fade fast, he snatched from his breast a string of colored-stone beads and shook it before his face.

Under Li Bi's drilling, the Lüben Guards had formed a good habit: everything of note from the Changming Ward depot they had gathered up and brought back, be it a splinter of cask-wood or a scrap of stray bamboo—nothing too great or too small, all collected and none let slip—and stored in the room beside the left side-hall. Going through it, Zhang Xiaojing had found the few scattered colored stones and remembered at once: these had hung at Cao Poyan's neck, cut loose by a single stroke of the blade. So he had asked Tanqi to string them again and had carried them into the morgue.

Strange to tell, the moment he saw the string of colored stones a little color came back into Cao Poyan's eyes. He grew calm and made a sound of no clear meaning, as though murmuring a name. Zhang Xiaojing pressed the necklace into his palm and leaned to his ear: "I, Zhang Xiaojing, swear to Heaven that I will send this string of beads and your soul back together to the steppe."

Cao Poyan's crown-hair had been shaved off by the Right Shad, which meant that only when the Right Shad was dead could his soul truly be free again.

Cao Poyan turned his face aside and, for the first time, looked of his own will at Zhang Xiaojing. Zhang Xiaojing gripped his shoulders and asked once more: "Where is the Right Shad? For your honor, for your Türk khagan, so that the one who made this string of beads may grow up safe—answer me. Where is the Right Shad?"

Cao Poyan worked his mouth and gave out a few blurred syllables. Zhang Xiaojing turned his ear and listened closely, and could just make them out: "the cross-and-lotus."

"The cross-and-lotus? What does that mean?"

Zhang Xiaojing would have pressed on with his questions, but Cao Poyan breathed out his last breath, closed his eyes, and slumped limply down. His look was contorted no longer; his cold, stern brows and eyes grew peaceful for the first time, and the string of beads was clenched fast in his hand.

Zhang Xiaojing was about to let the body go when his nostrils suddenly quivered and his single eye narrowed, and he did a strange thing: he took hold of the dead man's shoulders again, kept him half-raised, and pressed his head close to the slowly cooling chest, holding there a long while.

The night wind blew in through the gaps in the thatch, and the pine-torches guttered, throwing the two men into one weird huddle of shadow. Only after a span of a dozen-odd finger-snaps did Zhang Xiaojing lay the dead man slowly flat, a look of gladness on his face.

With Gan Shoucheng's ban in force, Zhang Xiaojing had no way back into the Jing'an Bureau's great hall and had to go on instead to the thatched hut in the Cibei Temple. Luckily Xu Bin had sent a few quick-handed clerks to set two wooden ladders against the wall between the hut and the hall, which made the coming and going far easier. This time he truly had become that wall-climbing rake Tanqi called him.

"The cross-and-lotus?"

Hearing out Zhang Xiaojing's report, Li Bi knit his brows. He tried hard to think what the thing might be, and what it had to do with the Right Shad lying hidden in Chang'an. But no thread would come to him at once, so he waved a hand and passed the word on to the great hall of the Jing'an Bureau, into the keeping of the old clerks under Xu Bin.

Before the art of the Great Archive, Li Bi trusted, this would be no great problem.

"By the way," Zhang Xiaojing went on, "I think I may know where Wang Yunxiu is." Li Bi's brows lifted. The safety of Wang Zhongsi's daughter was the second priority, next only to finding the noble Right Shad; yet there had never been the least clue to it—and Zhang Xiaojing had wrung even this out of his prisoner?

"Cao Poyan confessed that as well?"

"No—he died the moment he'd said 'the cross-and-lotus,'" Zhang Xiaojing explained. "But when I laid the body flat, I caught a scent at his chest: spirit-summoning rue incense. That is the incense the young lady of the Wang house is fond of burning."

Li Bi gave an "mm" and bade him go on. Zhang Xiaojing said: "When the Türk Wolf Guards fell back from Xiuzheng Ward to Changming Ward, they took a woman with them, and Cao Poyan had been waiting all the while in Changming Ward, with the scent of spirit-summoning rue on him. That means Wang Yunxiu's last stopping-place must have been in Changming Ward. We have to go and look, and quickly."

Having laid it all out, he could not help pressing his lips together.

In this matter Zhang Xiaojing harbored a private motive. He did not care in the least what became of Wang Yunxiu; he wanted only to get Wen Ran out. He knew that only by misleading the Jing'an Bureau—by letting them think it was Wang Yunxiu the Türks had carried off—would these men put their strength into the search.

The lie would not hamper the main line of the inquiry, but Zhang Xiaojing was not sure it could get past Li Bi—the man's eye was far too shrewd, and he was not so easily fooled.

"How do you come to know this is the incense Wang Yunxiu favors?" Li Bi asked back, suspicious. Sure enough, he had seized the crux at once; but Zhang Xiaojing had his answer ready: "A friend of mine keeps an incense shop, and has long supplied this made-to-order scent to the Wang mansion."

Li Bi gave the report in his hand a shake: "But the Lüben Guards have already searched Changming Ward carefully, and found nothing."

"I can take a sighthound and go once more," Zhang Xiaojing insisted, and there was, of all things, a faint note of pleading in his voice. It rather surprised Li Bi, and he could not help giving him another look. Why should this fellow care so much about Wang Yunxiu?

He pondered a moment, then granted the request. She was Wang Zhongsi's daughter after all; if only as a gesture to the Wang house, the search had to be made. But Li Bi would not let Zhang Xiaojing go in person. The most vital strength must be kept for the most vital business, and the Bureau's chief concern now was not Wang Yunxiu but the noble Right Shad.

Seeing this, Yao Runeng promptly volunteered. He had watched Zhang Xiaojing work a hound before, so he had a little experience. Li Bi nodded his leave. Just before setting off, Zhang Xiaojing caught Yao Runeng's arm and pressed a few earnest pointers on him about how to use a sighthound's nose—truly like a master schooling a pupil. At this even Yao Runeng felt something was amiss, and wondered whether, back when Zhang Xiaojing was a buliang chief, something had passed between him and this Wang Yunxiu.

After Yao Runeng had gone, the hut was soon left to Li Bi, Zhang Xiaojing, and Tanqi alone. Xu Bin was still inside the Bureau working the Great Archive, and no result had yet come of it. In this rare moment of leisure the three of them looked at one another, and for a time truly did not know what to say.

Li Bi gave his fly-whisk a flick. "Let us go back over the movements of the Türk Wolf Guards once more—" But Zhang Xiaojing reached out and caught the whisk's strands, his face grave. "How long has it been, Deputy Director Li, since you rested?"

"No more than two days. I keep the grain-fast the year round; I can hold out yet."

Li Bi made to draw the whisk back, and found to his surprise that Zhang Xiaojing's grip was so strong he could not pull it free. Feeling that such a tug-of-war was beneath his dignity, he gave a cold snort and simply let go. Zhang Xiaojing wrested the whisk away and tossed it aside. "Deputy Director Li, I advise you to snatch a little sleep. Strung this tight all the while, you will crack sooner or later."

Tanqi threw Zhang Xiaojing a grateful look and stepped forward, meaning to help her young master to a seat. But Li Bi waved her off and said with self-mockery: "No use—I simply can't sleep. These past days, the moment I shut my eyes I am afraid some great thing will happen while I sleep and I shall not be in time to deal with it." Zhang Xiaojing rebuked him without ceremony: "With a mind so busy fretting over gain and loss, and you would cultivate the Way?"

Li Bi let out a long sigh. "The Daoist heart stands alone and apart; it prizes letting nothing whatever cling to one's mind. But these hundreds of thousands of lives lie in my hand—how can I truly set them aside and pay them no heed? Heaven and earth are not benevolent; they treat the myriad things as straw dogs. But I cannot cultivate my way up to that height."

"Then why cultivate any Way at all? Would it not be better to be, plainly and solidly, a chief minister?" Zhang Xiaojing shot back.

Li Bi curled his lip, with a look that said what would a coarse fellow like you understand? Unwilling to be drawn further on the subject, he asked back: "That wound on your hand—how did you come by it?"

Zhang Xiaojing had crawled and scrapped his way through this whole day—racked under Ma Ge'er's cruel torture, locked in a life-and-death struggle with Cao Poyan, put through the pincer of fire and water and the abuse of the Right Xiao Guard—and was covered in wounds. But his most conspicuous hurt was the severed finger of his left hand. One look told Li Bi that this cut-off finger was wholly unlike the other injuries, and that there was surely a reason behind it.

Zhang Xiaojing had nothing worth hiding, and gave a rough account of the business with Old Ge. Li Bi had already heard Yao Runeng's report, but much of the detail had been unclear, and only now did he learn what had truly happened in the shack in Pingkang Ward.

The color changed on Tanqi's face several times; she had never known that this untamed, unruly man, who kept no rule of any kind, should hold a promise so dear. Li Bi laced his ten fingers together, but showed little reaction. As he saw it, to sell out a planted informer was a flaw in a small matter, but for the sake of the larger design there had been no help for it. He and Zhang Xiaojing were at bottom the same sort of man: both would kill an innocent without hesitation to keep the great ship from going over.

But that Zhang Xiaojing should cut off one of his own fingers to atone—that took Li Bi greatly by surprise.

"Affectation," Li Bi remarked, two cold words. "Were it I who met with such a case, you should simply strike and be done; no need to fuss and fret over guilt or the like. The whole is what matters. Where is the crime in it?"

Zhang Xiaojing shut his mouth and narrowed his eyes, plainly unwilling to linger long on the subject.

Both men were of the sort who say one thing and keep ten hidden, and neither had any thought of sharing his life with the other, so the mood of the talk melted away like smoke. The hut fell for a while into an awkward silence; they looked at each other a long moment, and each half regretted it—better, they thought, to have talked of work after all.

These two might be the best of partners, but friends they would certainly never be.

Tanqi looked from her young master on the one side to the rake on the other, and smelled the thick reek of awkwardness in the air. Her lovely eyes turned; she rose and went out, and in a little while carried back a plate of the Cibei Temple's plain-oil fritters, with a few flatcakes laid underneath. The fritters were fried in plain oil, and filling enough. These two had eaten nothing since noon, and there was no telling how long the next stretch would run; they had best use this scrap of leisure to eat their fill.

With food to break the deadlock, the scene grew at last a little less strained. Li Bi and Zhang Xiaojing each dragged over a rush cushion and settled on the steps outside the hut. Tanqi set the plate down between the two of them.

Li Bi would not squat down carelessly, but knelt bolt upright with scrupulous decorum; Zhang Xiaojing, for his part, lounged sideways against a wooden post of the hut and stretched his legs out with easy insolence. Reaching into the plate for the fritters and washing them down with cold, clear well-water, they looked out beyond.

The Cibei Temple stood on low ground, and from this angle not a single lantern could be seen. But the night-sky, half of it reddened by their glow, proclaimed that all Chang'an had fallen into a joyous revel. Set against each other, the two only made the chill and stillness here the plainer.

So the two lonely guardians stayed there in the dark, eating cold food and cold water, gazing in silence out at the fair hour and fine scene unfolding before them.

The time left them to rest was not long. The fritters on the plate were but half eaten when word came from Xu Bin in the great hall: they had traced the source of the cross-and-lotus—it was the Persian Nestorian church.

The Nestorian faith, with Manichaeism and Zoroastrianism, was one of the Three Foreign Religions. The faith in truth came from Daqin, and had reached the Central Lands as early as the Zhenguan years. In official documents it was called the Persian Temple. Its scale was somewhat smaller than the Zoroastrians', and it spread only quietly through the western city, so that even Zhang Xiaojing had not known where the cross-and-lotus came from.

As it happened there was one Nestorian believer in the Jing'an Bureau, and the moment he heard the words "the cross-and-lotus" he pointed out at once that in a Nestorian temple the most prominent emblem was just this: a cross hung above, borne up on a lotus below.

The Luminous stands for the great light; the lotus for great purity; the cross for great redemption. The doctrine had a character all its own.

Since Cao Poyan had spoken of the cross-and-lotus, plainly this noble Right Shad must be hiding within a Nestorian temple. Earlier, Long Bo had lurked among the Zoroastrians; it seemed the Türks were fond of using innocent congregations for cover.

But Zhang Xiaojing and Li Bi wore no look of gladness. Within Chang'an there were a dozen or more Nestorian temples of any size, and the Nestorian monks numbered above a thousand. To hunt the Right Shad on the strength of this one phrase alone was like fishing a man out of the sea.

"Could we not check the Nestorian temples' ordination certificates, the way we checked the Zoroastrians before?" Zhang Xiaojing asked.

Li Bi shook his head. When they had looked into the Zoroastrian temple before, it had been confined to the single ward of Huaiyuan; to check the Nestorian ordination certificates across the whole of Chang'an, there was simply no time.

Tanqi gave a soft little cough at their side. Before Li Bi could say anything, Zhang Xiaojing looked up first and said with a smile: "It seems the young lady has a thought?" Tanqi had meant only to hint quietly to her young master, and finding herself dragged into the open by this rake, she could not help shooting him a glare of vexed embarrassment.

But Li Bi had no attention to spare for such trifles: "There are no idle ears here, Tanqi; you need not scruple. If you have something to say, say it plainly."

Only then did Tanqi make bold to speak: "It is an old matter I recalled. When our Jing'an Bureau was first being founded, its site was changed several times, and settled at last on Guangde Ward. Here the Jingzhao Prefecture shares the ward, which makes the archives easy to draw upon; to the west it neighbors the West Market, so the foreign merchants can be watched; to the north it borders the imperial city, keeping it in constant touch with the palace; to the east it joins the Vermilion Bird Avenue, so troops can be moved with ease. Only by sitting in command here can my young master grasp the whole and direct affairs as they arise… I should think the Right Shad reasons just the same, does he not?"

She put it delicately, but Li Bi's eyes lit up; he rose from the rush cushion and wiped the grease from his hands on a flatcake: "Bring the ward-map!"

There was no sand-table here, but a Bureau draughtsman had run up a map on bamboo paper. The brushwork was rough, yet every mark that ought to be on it was there. Tanqi turned at once to fetch it, spread it out on the ground, and Li Bi and Zhang Xiaojing bent close over it to study.

Tanqi was keen indeed; in a stroke she had found the perfect way in. The noble Right Shad had not come to Chang'an for a holiday but to command and coordinate. On the one hand he had to control the Wolf Guards; on the other he had to be able at any moment to reach the mysterious power that had bought him—so his need for communication was very high. But he had no watchtower system, and must choose a place open on every side to make his base.

Zhang Xiaojing took up a small wolf's-hair brush and drew a black line on the map, running from the Jinguang Gate to the West Market, on to Changming Ward, and back again to Guangde Ward. Midway it threw off a dotted line linking to Xiuzheng Ward in the east. The Wolf Guards' course through Chang'an soon lay plain at a glance. Beside him Li Bi took up a small wolf's-hair brush of his own, but dipped it in cinnabar, and marked out every Nestorian temple within two wards of that black line.

All the faiths of Chang'an were administered by the Bureau of Sacrifices. Xu Bin did his work most conscientiously, and just now, in sending word to the hut, had made a point of drawing from the Bureau of Sacrifices the register of Nestorian temples, ready for reference.

The two of them traced and dotted, black line and red points, and in a little while the map was a mess. To an outsider it looked like a child's scrawl, but in their eyes it was a net closing steadily in. As place after place was ruled out, the enemy's hiding-place grew ever clearer.

At last their eyes converged on one spot of the map; together they looked up, and smiled at each other.

The place was called Yining Ward, at the far northwest corner of Chang'an, right beside the Kaiyuan Gate. In the ninth year of Zhenguan the Nestorian monk Alopen had come to Chang'an from Persia, and Emperor Taizong had granted him leave to raise a Persian foreign temple in Yining Ward—which could be reckoned the mother-temple of the Nestorian faith in the Central Lands. The Bureau of Sacrifices' register showed the temple held some two hundred Nestorian monks.

On the face of it the ward lay in the northwest of Chang'an, in an out-of-the-way spot. But look again more closely: to its northwest was the Kaiyuan Gate, to its southwest the Jinguang Gate, and due south the West Market—all of them thoroughfares by which the foreign merchants came and went, so that any stir at all could be spied from a height. Along the ward's northern face ran a crossway, one of Chang'an's six great avenues, sweeping straight past the imperial city and crossing the Vermilion Bird Avenue to form the great cross running the length of Chang'an. For traffic it was extremely convenient.

Whether for concealment or for communication, the Nestorian temple of Yining Ward was the inevitable choice for the Right Shad.

"I'll go and look into it myself, at once." Zhang Xiaojing rose quickly. Li Bi stopped him: "Even if you get inside the temple, with several hundred monks before you—how will you find him?"

"The Right Shad's rank among the Türks is high," said Zhang Xiaojing. "He cannot have lain hidden in Chang'an for long. Just ask which of the Nestorian monks came lately, and we shan't be far off." Li Bi thought the sieve still too coarse, but with so little intelligence to hand there was nothing for it but to let it stand. The particulars would have to be left to Zhang Xiaojing to meet as they came, on the spot.

The wax had incense blended into it, and scented oil filled the base, so that where the lights burned thickest there often hung a rich, oily fragrance of candle-scent. Countless commonfolk pressed in beneath the lantern-frames, every one with face upturned—eyes on the lanterns, nose to the fragrance, and a grain of coarse salt held besides beneath the tongue. This was a custom long in vogue in Chang'an: salt, yan, is "to prolong"; the candle, zhu, is "long life." Breathe in the fragrance of a whole candle, and you may add a year to your span, and take an omen of good luck by it—the thing was called "drawing in candle-long-life."

It was just because of this tradition that Chang'an's Lantern Festival was not, at its start, so very crowded. But luckily Tanqi was a house-born bondmaid of Li Bi's, raised in the Li household from childhood, and no one would be fool enough to suspect her.

Others, though, would not necessarily be granted such treatment.

The Tang had never marked high from low by blood, and there were officials aplenty, civil and military, not of Central-Lands birth. Among the Bureau's subordinate clerks the foreigners were not few, the ratio of Han to foreign being about five to one.

If word of a traitor got out now, the foreign clerks would likely go in fear each for himself, and this easy, tolerant air would likely be no more. Xu Bin could more or less understand, then, why the Deputy Director had had to say it to him only at the foot of the wall.

No helper, no one to consult, nothing made public—yet the traitor must be dug out, and quickly. It was truly a harsh problem set before Xu Bin. At the thought he heaved a vexed sigh and paced the great hall with his hands behind his back, tilting his head aside now and again to observe every man in it.

Unluckily his sight was poor, and without meaning to he would draw as close as he could. Often before he had seen clearly the other had already grown aware of it, and would look back in astonishment at this odd-mannered recorder. Circling the hall a few times thus to no purpose, Xu Bin suddenly noticed a man standing beside the coiled-dragon water-clock in the corner of the hall. He squinted, trying to see clearly, drew unknowingly very near, and lifting his head all at once, found their four eyes meeting.

"Eh, eh?"

This man—it was Cui Qi!

This traitor to the Jing'an Bureau had actually come brazenly back?

Cui Qi's face was full of awkwardness; before Xu Bin could open his mouth to ask, he showed his new waist-tally first: "By General Gan's order, I oversee the Jing'an Bureau's affairs here on patrol."

By the agreement Li Bi and Gan Shoucheng had reached: the Right Xiao Guard would no longer hunt Zhang Xiaojing, but would not permit him to appear at the Jing'an Bureau. To make sure the agreement held, the Right Xiao Guard would naturally send a man to the Bureau to supervise. But that General Gan should send Cui Qi, of all people, was plainly to spite Li Bi on purpose—whether Cui Qi himself might find it galling was no part of Gan Shoucheng's reckoning at all.

Since coming back to the Jing'an Bureau, Cui Qi had kept to a corner the whole time, uttering not a sound. So long as Zhang Xiaojing did not appear, the rest was no concern of his. It was only now that Xu Bin had discovered he was there at all.

Whether on public grounds or private, Xu Bin had not the least good feeling for Cui Qi. He gave the traitor a cold glance, made no bow, and simply turned his head and walked off.

The corner of Cui Qi's mouth twitched. The fellow was only an unranked old clerk, yet dared be so rude to a full Vice-Commandant of the Proclaiming-Fidelity grade. In ordinary times he would long since have knocked him flying with his scabbard; but now the whole Jing'an Bureau was his enemy… Only this morning, rising from his bed, he had been in high, ardent spirits, meaning to win a great merit together with his elder brother—how had it come to this pass?

"Elder brother—perhaps you should never have brought me over from the Long Mountains."

Cui Qi looked at the brightly lit hall, sighed a deep sigh, stepped back, and hid himself again in the dark.

This was the road he had chosen, and he must bear its consequences.

Xu Bin neither knew nor cared for Cui Qi's troubles; he was circling the hall like a headless fly, his mind in a tangle. How to find this traitor had truly stumped him.

Reciting figures was no trouble at all to Xu Bin, but the guessing of men's hearts was a far harder thing. Hands behind his back, he came back to his own desk, and suddenly saw before him a small bamboo knife used for cutting paper-scrolls.

It came to him of a sudden: just looking them over one by one like this, what year would he ever be done? He had been very dull indeed. A workman who would do his work well must first sharpen his tools; he must have a "method." So Xu Bin simply knelt down again in his seat and set in order, one by one, the four treasures of the study on his desk. This was a habit of Xu Bin's, a way of turning a line of thought over.

When everything on the desk had been sorted each to its own kind, all neat and in order, Xu Bin had, sure enough, hit upon an idea. He shook a bronze bell and had a servant fetch at once a detailed plan of the Jing'an Bureau, then took up a crystal lens, held it to the plan, and studied it closely.

The whole establishment fell into three parts: the main hall, the left and right side-halls, and the rear hall. The main hall was for business, the side-halls for the storing of files and documents, the rear hall the prison where captives were held. Behind the whole complex was a great garden, taking up a broad stretch of ground, with a scatter of separate little buildings about it—a retiring-room, a watchtower, a kitchen, a privy, a wellhead, a falcon-perch, a canal, and the like. Around the outermost edge ran a ring of high courtyard walls, planted along the top with thorns.

The whole Jing'an Bureau had only two ways out—the main gate of the main hall, opening onto the ward's crossroads, and a corner gate opening to the east, which connected directly with the neighboring offices of the Prefect of Jingzhao. Oh—and now there was, besides, the wall-ladder leading over to the thatched hut in the Cibei Temple.

Xu Bin's line of thought was simple: whoever this traitor was, he must face one problem—how to pass the intelligence out. And from the speed of those few leaks, the channel had to be an especially fast one. From the plan, there were only the two gates to choose from.

And there was the question of the source of the intelligence.

As to the Bureau's news, there were clear rules for which might be made public to all, which was told only to the several recorders, and which only the Deputy Director and the Jing'an Commissioner might break the seal on. The Wolf Guards' movements in the West Market, for instance, were open to the whole staff; but the news of Wang Yunxiu's abduction, at first, only Li Bi had known.

The Bureau's two leaks of intelligence—one at the Xifu, one at Changming Ward—were neither of a very high grade. It could be seen, then, that this traitor could not reach the higher matters.

Soon Xu Bin had sketched out the traitor's basic case: first, he could pass freely through the main gate and the corner gate; second, he had access to the Bureau's latest doings, but only up to a middling level. That would sieve out a great crowd of the lesser clerks, and leave only a few of recorder and registrar rank.

Coming to this, Xu Bin lifted his head and glanced again at the corner of the hall. Cui Qi had deliberately hidden his form in the dark, not easily to be spotted. The irony was that just now he was the one man in this hall who could be certainly ruled out as the traitor.

Wait—might Cui Qi know who the traitor was? His own betrayal, after all, must have had a go-between. But Xu Bin quickly ruled this guess out too. Whoever had drawn Cui Qi into defecting must have been one of the Right Minister's open men, to carry conviction. The go-between saw to the winning-over, the traitor to the passing of intelligence—these were two separate lines, each apart from the other.

And besides, even if Cui Qi knew, he would never tell the Jing'an Bureau.

It seemed he must look for a way from some other quarter.

Xu Bin ran his eye over the plan again, and suddenly had a splendid idea. But the idea still wanted an occasion, and he had for the present to wait patiently.

The water-clock had not run out a quarter-hour when a stir suddenly rose from outside the hall. With hurried footsteps, Yao Runeng came in supporting Wen Ran. A light blanket was draped over Wen Ran, and wary of the strange surroundings, she let the man beside her urge her forward as he would.

The great mass of clerks lifted their heads to look at her, their eyes full of complicated feeling. This must be Wang Zhongsi's daughter? Found at last! It was this very woman who had kept them working late till now, unable to join the Lantern Festival.

Yao Runeng brought Wen Ran up before Li Bi, and before Li Bi could speak, Yao Runeng stepped over first and said in a low voice: "This lady is not Wang Yunxiu; her name is Wen Ran."

Li Bi was taken aback at the words; he had thought this matter was at last accounted for—how had it sprouted a new branch off the side? He said coldly: "Who is Wen Ran?"

Yao Runeng said: "I've asked it all out on the way. She is the keeper of the Wen Incense Shop in Dunyi Ward. By her own account, she was set upon by the Bear Fire Gang and went to Wang Yunxiu for help; they went out together in a Xi cart, and then were attacked by the villains and dragged all the way to Changming Ward—so it may be… ah, that we had it wrong from the very start."

It was a pitiable misunderstanding. The one the Wolf Guards had seized all along, it turned out, was Wen Ran.

"Then where is Wang Yunxiu?" Li Bi glared at her.

Wen Ran thought this man fierce and shrank quickly back behind Yao Runeng, shaking her head. From the moment of the cart-mishap, the things around her had grown stranger one than the next; she could not keep up with any of it, still less mark where Wang Yunxiu had gone.

Li Bi lost interest in her, and told Yao Runeng to keep the woman for a few questions and, if nothing was in doubt, to let her go. Yao Runeng was helping Wen Ran away when Li Bi suddenly recalled something and called them back: "Do you know Zhang Xiaojing?"

Hearing the familiar name, a gleam of gladness showed in Wen Ran's eyes: "He is my benefactor."

A look of sudden understanding came into Li Bi's eyes; he gave his fly-whisk a flick and said with a cold laugh to Xu Bin: "No wonder Zhang Xiaojing insisted on searching again—the one he wanted to find was not Wang Yunxiu at all, but this Wen Ran!"

Zhang Xiaojing's earlier fixation on searching Changming Ward again had all along struck Li Bi as strange. Now, seeing that the one found was Wen Ran, Li Bi seized keenly at once on the subtle connection in it. Looking back now, Zhang Xiaojing's flat insistence in Xiuzheng Ward that it was Wang Yunxiu who had been carried off had, he feared, been a deliberate misdirection from the very first.

Li Bi was at once angered and disappointed. True, Zhang Xiaojing had indeed risked his life to stop the Türks, and this misdirection had held up no real business. But the small maneuver had shattered Li Bi's unconditional trust: had he other concealments besides? Would there be more such doings in the future? It bred a whole train of problems and hidden dangers.

"Take her and confine her in the rear-hall cells for me, and question her clearly on what her relation to Zhang Xiaojing is!"

Li Bi sternly revised his order. Yao Runeng thought he had misheard; to keep and to confine were two words of wholly different natures.

Seeing him hesitate, Li Bi brought the fly-whisk down hard on the desk with a thud. Yao Runeng could only take hold of Wen Ran and, with a touch of guilt, draw her toward the back.

Wen Ran, knowing nothing of what lay behind it, could only grip Yao Runeng's arm fast—the one person in the whole hall who made her feel safe.

After they had gone, Li Bi closed his eyes, his mind already made up. The moment there was progress at the Nestorian temple in Yining Ward, he would recall Zhang Xiaojing at once. In the action to come, he was not sure he could go on trusting that man.

Xu Bin, off to one side, did not know that his superior's attitude toward their collaborator had subtly changed; he was writing away with all his mind on nothing else.

For the occasion he had been waiting for had come.

The Jing'an Bureau had in all two doors to the outside world, one main gate and one corner gate, both guarded by Lüben Guards soldiers. Everyone passing in or out had to show his bamboo pass; to enter without one was reckoned the same as breaking into the palace precincts, and the soldiers might cut the intruder down on the spot.

From the hour of the Snake today, great numbers of people had been going in and out of the two gates without a break, all on business that brooked not a moment's delay. This press kept up right through to the hour of the Monkey, when the bright candles hung high and the soldiers, long since worn out, had grown perfunctory in their checking of the bamboo passes.

A long-faced official came out of the Bureau's corner gate, a bamboo pass in his hand. One look at his face and the gate-soldier recognized Registrar Pang. He was forever going through this corner gate between the Jingzhao Prefecture offices and the Jing'an Bureau, charged with drawing out files of all kinds. Today alone he had made no fewer than a dozen trips. So the soldier could not be bothered to check the bamboo pass, gave it a cursory pass through his hands, and waved him on.

Registrar Pang stepped over the threshold into the Jingzhao Prefecture. He glanced left and right, and instead of going straight to the office of the Recording Adjutant, took a turn and slipped into the horse-hoof passage between the main hall and the outer wall. This alley was very narrow, wide enough only for one horse to set down its hoof, whence it was called the horse-hoof passage. All manner of odds and ends were piled up here, and few people came at ordinary times.

He walked to the middle of the horse-hoof passage, bent down, and drew a rolled wad of paper from his breast. Suddenly a gong sounded, a row of lanterns blazed up along the wall, and the whole alley was in that instant bright with light. Xu Bin stood at the other end of the passage, hands behind his back, looking at him with regret.

"Old Pang—I never thought it would be you…"

Registrar Pang said in a fright: "I—I only came to relieve myself." Xu Bin shook his head with a wry smile: "Ah, ah, don't cozen me; are there not pits enough in the Jing'an Bureau's privy?" He walked over, snatched the paper-roll from Registrar Pang's hand, and opening it, found it was—of all things—a list of victuals.

Registrar Pang forced a smile: "Old Xu, you know me. The privy back at the Bureau is too filthy, so I came here to ease myself. This paper-roll is softer for the backside than a privy-stick—with the Ordinance to Cherish Written Characters in force, a man has to do this sort of thing out of sight, doesn't he?"

The court had promulgated the Ordinance to Cherish Written Characters, requiring that paper and the written word be revered, and strictly forbidding the use of written-on paper in the privy. For Registrar Pang to wipe himself with a list of victuals was, strictly speaking, a beating-offense too.

Xu Bin said: "Ah, ah, Old Pang, you fret too much; the law is strict, but human feeling remains—who would arrest a man over one wretched sheet of paper?" And he handed the roll back to him. Registrar Pang let out a breath and was about to clap him on the shoulder in a show of warmth, but Xu Bin dodged lightly aside, his face turning grave: "If there's an arrest, it will be for the—the leaking of military intelligence."

He was an honest man, and such aggressive words, coming out with a stammer, lost all their force. Registrar Pang's face turned displeased at hearing them: "Old Xu, you can't slander a colleague so. That I used the paper to ease myself is a fault, but this charge of yours goes too far, surely?"

Xu Bin flinched, then sighed, finding his own presence truly ill-suited to catching a traitor. He stepped his body aside and revealed a man behind him. By the candlelight Registrar Pang saw that it was the guard who watched the corner gate, already trussed hand and foot, and he began to tremble.

The passage was hushed and still, in exact contrast to the clamor outside. Only Xu Bin's voice sounded, faint and small:

"I knew there was a spy in the Bureau, but I had to wait for an occasion. Just now Wang Yunxiu came back to the hall—only to be found to be a different woman. I deliberately had that news copied round to every officer. It was too important; the traitor was bound to send it out as fast as he could. Whoever left his seat and went out just at that moment, ah, was bound to be the most suspect."

Xu Bin earnestly explained the trap he had laid, for fear Registrar Pang should not follow it.

"I kept thinking how the Bureau's traitor was to get through the main gate or the corner gate, ah, ah. And then I found I had fallen into a false way of it. The man need not be one who passes through the gate; he might also be… mm, one who guards the gate." Coming to this, Xu Bin gathered up a breath, and his tone grew more confident. "I have just seen it: you walk through the corner gate, and while your bamboo pass is being checked you hand the news to the gate-soldier, and leave white and clean; the gate-soldier then passes it to a man outside, and goes on guarding the gate white and clean. A fine method—look at either of you singly and each is spotless. Only put together does the trick of it show."

Registrar Pang went down with a thud, slumping to sit in the passage. Xu Bin bade the buliang men to either side go and take him, and Registrar Pang hurriedly lifted his face and pleaded: "I—I do this for the Phoenix Pavilion…"

The Phoenix Pavilion was the Secretariat. He confessed of his own accord to being the Right Minister's man, hoping Xu Bin would go easy on him. But slow as Xu Bin was, even he knew that the Right Minister could never admit to such a thing, still less shield him; Registrar Pang's official career was finished.

Registrar Pang realized this too, and clutched at Xu Bin's sleeve: "I must see Deputy Director Li! I only passed news along—I never once held up the Jing'an Bureau's business!"

Hearing this, Xu Bin grew a little heated: "Hai! Still won't own it—if you hadn't been in secret collusion with the Phoenix Pavilion, could the fire at the Yuanlai Trading Post have started? Could Cui Qi have defected?" Registrar Pang was stunned at the words, then cried out: "The matter of Commandant Cui, true, I passed to the Phoenix Pavilion—but the Yuanlai Trading Post I never passed at all!"

"Hm?"

"To work for the Türks—that's a head-chopping offense! And no profit in it besides." Registrar Pang was filled with righteous indignation.

Reminded thus, Xu Bin saw that the two leaks were in fact wholly different in nature. The chance fire at the Yuanlai Trading Post had profited the Türk Wolf Guards who were stealing the map at the Xifu; the winning-over and defection of Cui Qi had profited the Right Minister.

Shameless as Registrar Pang was, he would not have taken the pay of both houses at once.

"Do you mean to say… there are actually two traitors?" Standing in the passage, Xu Bin could not help a shudder. When had the Jing'an Bureau become a sieve, that any silt and sand at all could seep through?

He stared hard at Registrar Pang, till the other's flesh crept. But Registrar Pang soon saw that Xu Bin's short-sighted gaze was in fact fixed on the roll of blank paper meant for wiping. He held it out gingerly: "If you'd like to use it…"

Xu Bin suddenly sprang up, turned, and ran out of the passage. Remarkably nimble for a man past middle age, in a moment he had vanished at the passage's end, leaving Registrar Pang, the gate-guard, and the few buliang men holding them to look at one another in blank surprise.

Xu Bin panted for breath, but his mind was near bursting. He had just thought: within this Jing'an Bureau there was another, better channel of transmission still!

The four streets about Guangde Ward were all dazzling with lantern-light. Those huge lantern-frames cast out ten thousand rays of golden light and lit up half the sky.

To the sightseers this was a splendid sight rarely to be seen, but to the watchtowers the Jing'an Bureau had set in the several wards it was the most maddening of interferences. Ten thousand candles ablaze, clamor the whole night through—whether the watchtowers beat their drums or raised their fires, it was near to useless.

For this the constables on the watchtowers had to hood their lanterns with two layers of purple paper, to tell them apart from those huge lights. Were an immortal to look down over Chang'an, he would see a shimmering golden sea of light spread over the city district, and only by careful discernment make out the many faint purple points scattered within it—like a stingy shopkeeper who had sprinkled the merest few sesame grains on a bilo cake.

Just then, on one of the watchtowers near Guangde Ward, a purple light went suddenly out. But set beside these colored lanterns bright as sun and moon, this little rotten-firefly glimmer was far too slight to notice, and no one marked it at all.

Soon the light on a second watchtower went out as well.

A third, a fourth, a fifth… within the span of a few dozen finger-snaps, the purple points of the watchtowers ringing Guangde Ward all dimmed away, like a dark band of cord drawing slowly tight about Guangde Ward's neck.

Yao Runeng shut Wen Ran in the rear-hall cell, came out, and stood in the courtyard, letting out a long breath. Wen Ran had been unwilling to go back into that cold, dark place, and kept asking Yao Runeng what all this was about. It was only with much coaxing this way and that that he had soothed her feelings.

This ordinary girl had gone through so much torment today; she was truly pitiable. That the Deputy Director had just now ordered her locked up like a prisoner rather rankled with Yao Runeng.

He gave a word to the jailer who kept the cell, and had set inside it an extra candlestand and a bronze basin brimming with clean water—Wen Ran's hair-knot and face were filthy past all telling, and wanted a good wash.

Arranged so, it would at least be some sort of accounting to give Commander Zhang when he came back, Yao Runeng thought.

This woman called Zhang Xiaojing her benefactor; who knew what story lay between the two of them. Yao Runeng was now full of curiosity about Zhang Xiaojing's life; he was eager to see the man clearly, and Wen Ran ought to be an excellent way to learn of him.

Yao Runeng left Wen Ran to wash herself, and used the time to take the air in the courtyard, to clear his line of thought before going back to interrogate Wen Ran—mm, not interrogate, question, he corrected his own choice of word.

The rear-court cell of the Jing'an Bureau joined onto the left side-hall, and midway between the two was a little courtyard, where its former owner had set an artificial rockery all overgrown with creeping vines, a very picture of a secluded mountain scene. Yao Runeng strolled into this little court, head bowed in thought, when he suddenly saw what seemed a figure stirring behind the rockery. He narrowed his eyes and, on his guard, laid a hand on the iron ruler at his waist: "Who's there?"

"It's me, Cui Qi."

The figure stepped out, and Yao Runeng's eyes went wide; this was unexpected indeed.

"Oh, if it isn't the Right Xiao Guard's General Cui?" Yao Runeng laid a mocking stress on the two words "General." He had thought never to have to see this face again in his life, and had not looked for the man to come brazenly back to the Jing'an Bureau.

Cui Qi kept his face black and lifeless: "I've come about something." Yao Runeng went on mocking: "To haul me back? A pity General Gan only barred Commander Zhang, and made no mention of a nameless little nobody like me."

Cui Qi said in a low voice through gritted teeth: "It's not that. I tell you, the Jing'an Bureau may be in danger!"

Yao Runeng nearly laughed; the fellow talked more absurdly than a jester playing the adjutant-farce. The Jing'an Bureau planned the capital's defense—it, in danger? Its whole work was to find out danger, for goodness' sake!

"No—hear me out. I've no real proof just now, but I have a strong foreboding that something is wrong."

There was an edge of impatience in Cui Qi's voice. He had soldiered in the Long Mountains and had a natural instinct for danger. Some while ago he had suddenly felt he could neither sit nor stand at ease. The footfalls of the men in the hall, the flow of the wind, the clamor outside, the frequency of the dispatches—something felt wrong, and yet he could not say what.

"Of course you'd hope the Jing'an Bureau came to grief," Yao Runeng curled his lip.

"You little whelp, is that any way to talk?" Cui Qi flew into a rage and slapped the rockery hard with his palm. "Yes! I'm a traitor! I fawn on the powerful—but what would I gain by inventing a lie like this?"

Yao Runeng looked at his face, and his own expression slowly grew grave. The man might be cowardly, might be base, but he was no good at dissembling. Just now he seemed truly anxious.

"If you're so full of good will, why not go straight to Deputy Director Li and Recorder Xu and tell them?" Yao Runeng asked, suspicious.

"They won't believe a traitor's word," Cui Qi answered with a bitter smile. "But if you, young Yao, sound the warning, that's another matter. Listen—I'm not doing this for the Jing'an Bureau, I'm doing it for myself. If something really does happen to the Bureau, I shan't get off unscathed either."

This was the plain truth. Had it been possible he would have fled long ago, but with Gan Shoucheng's military order upon him he could only stand guard here where he was.

Yao Runeng said: "Then you'll have to say clearly what is going to happen; a feeling alone won't do—how is the Jing'an Bureau to guard against it?"

Cui Qi said urgently: "Call in a few companies of the Lüben Guards first—that can't be wrong!"

No sooner had the words fallen than the two of them heard hurried footsteps at once. They looked toward the sound, and found it came from the rear garden further off.

Xu Bin ran in one breath from the Jingzhao Prefecture back to the Jing'an Bureau, and from the Bureau on to the back of the compound. Here was a very large garden, broad and open, with only a retiring-room, a privy, a falcon-perch, and the like; the Bureau's watchtower too was set at the garden's center, ringed about by a wall of high gables.

By rights this was a dead end, with no way out at all. But Xu Bin had suddenly recalled that there was, in fact, another passage in this garden.

The canal.

Why was the position of Guangde Ward so important? Because the three canals along Chang'an's western side—the Guangtong, the Qingming, and the Yong'an—came together at just this point, and then flowed on into the imperial city.

The three canals entering the ward made the waterways within Guangde Ward both broad and deep. This rear garden of the Jing'an Bureau had a water-gate in each of its east and west walls. From the east wall it drew in the water of the main canal, which bent midway into a bow-shape, curving just half round Li Bi's retiring-room, and drained out again into the main canal through the west wall. So the garden had a living stream, and as long as one of the three canals did not run dry, clear water forever circulated here—excellent in its geomancy.

Seeing the paper-roll in Registrar Pang's hand, it had struck Xu Bin all at once: that traitor need not go through the two gates at all. He had only to make the excuse of the privy, run to the rear garden, drop an oiled sheet of paper into the canal, and arrange for a man outside the west wall to scoop it up with a strainer. The current would complete the passing of the intelligence—reliable, swift, and utterly safe.

The trick, once exposed, was worth nothing at all, but it was more practicable than Registrar Pang's method.

Xu Bin's deliberate release of the news that Wang Yunxiu was Wen Ran was, to that other traitor, likewise intelligence to be sent out at once. In other words, by rushing over in such haste, Xu Bin might just corner him beside the canal—or at the very worst catch the man scooping up the intelligence by the west wall, and choke this passage off.

Five buliang men followed at his back. Xu Bin had the two strongest of them go over the wall from the far side as fast as they could, to block the other side first, while he and the remaining three fanned out and closed in toward the canal.

Xu Bin had not exerted himself so in a long while. His lungs burned and stung, and he gulped for breath in great gasps, yet his feet did not dare pause for an instant. With Registrar Pang taken, that traitor might well go to ground now; this was the one chance there might be to seize him.

They ran into the rear garden and went swiftly along the gravel path, and soon saw the shadow of the retiring-room standing in the dark. There was no lamp here, so they could see no more clearly, and could only hear the gurgling rush of water in the canal.

Eh? How could there be no lamp?

The Jing'an Bureau's great watchtower was set nearby; because it had to receive news from every quarter of Chang'an, it was twice the size of other watchtowers, and could hold eight constables on top. After nightfall there ought to hang sixteen purple lanterns up there.

Xu Bin lifted his head, and found the great watchtower all in pitch darkness, not a single lamp upon it.

This was bad!

A most ominous foreboding, like a viper springing up out of the shadows, bit down hard on Xu Bin's heart.

From the far side of the wall came two screams of agony—the two buliang men who had just gone over. Xu Bin's face changed abruptly and he craned his neck to look in haste, but his sight was helpless in the dark, and his foot caught, and he pitched flat on the ground.

At the same moment a shadow rose up out of the canal; the buliang men started and drew the iron rulers from their belts. Then one after another a dozen or more shadows broke the surface and climbed up the canal bank, for all the world like demons welling up out of the water.

They wore black water-suits, and stood in a row with short crossbows in hand, keeping a terrible silence. At the foot of the west wall not far off, the water-grating had been torn away; these men must have swum through from there. One black figure stood at the edge of the west wall, gazing calmly this way, toying with a straight-handled horse-tooth rasp in his hand.

The remaining three buliang men halted their steps in fear and made to run back. A few of the short crossbows moved, and two were struck down on the instant. The last man hurried to cry out a warning, but a crossbow bolt suddenly flew at the crown of his head and drove in through the top of his skull.

A black figure poked its head out from the great watchtower, confirmed the targets dead, and then made a sign with a gesture of the hand.

The black figures stripped off their water-suits, re-strung their short crossbows, then split into several parties and rushed swiftly toward the great hall of the Jing'an Bureau…
