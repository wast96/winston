### Fengtian Military Academy Instructor — Zhao Weigang

### Joining the Communist Party Two Months After the "April 12" Coup

In this period the intelligence network the Central Special Section directed reached into every great city of the country, out to the far frontier towns and even Hong Kong. Red fighters in different places and at different posts, in their struggles with enemies of every kind, wrote many heroic deeds that move one to song and to tears. In the chart of the Central Special Section's intelligence work that Gu Shunzhang drew, after his arrest and defection, in that book he wrote to curry favor with the enemy, The Theory and Practice of Secret Service Work, there was a "Manchuria intelligence network" headed by the "Manchuria Pockmark," and it included an "instructor at the Fengtian Military Academy," a "Japanese organization and consulate," and so on. The first of these was Comrade Zhao Weigang. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in Osaka, Japan, in June 1927, and returned home that September after graduating from the Japanese Military Academy. He joined the Chinese Communist Party two months after the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup, just when the Chinese revolution was sinking to its low ebb and the White Terror was at its worst — a mark of his loyal love for the Party and his firm faith in the victory of the Chinese revolution. On his return he entered the Fengtian Military Academy and its Advanced Military Studies Class, and under cover of teaching gathered military and political intelligence for the Party over many years — a model of "breaking into" the enemy's military organs. Zhao Weigang (1905–1999), originally named Zhao Shiyu, also used the names Shi Jimin, Zhao Guoyuan, Wang Lin, and Zakharov. A Manchu, he was from Shenyang in Liaoning. In 1925 he went on a government scholarship to the infantry course of the Chiba Military Academy in Japan, and while in Japan joined the Chinese Communist Party. Of how he came to study in Japan and to join the Party, Zhao Weigang recalled:

In 1925, after finishing middle school, I obtained through the connection of a schoolmate's father the support of Yang Yuting — chief of staff of the Northeastern Army and superintendent of the arsenal — and the Northeastern Arsenal advanced my tuition to study in Japan (to be repaid by working at the arsenal after graduation). So I set off for Japan with four classmates who had graduated in my year, and entered the Military Academy in Chiba prefecture. During my studies in Japan I came to know Li Renyi (Li Guochen, an underground Communist), the mentor who awakened my thinking. Under his guidance I read a good deal of progressive material, including The Communist Manifesto, A Popular Exposition of Capital, Twelve Lectures on Leninism, and journals and books the Chinese Communist Party published, such as New Youth and The Guide. He took me to meetings and to hear progressive talks, such as those against the May Thirtieth massacre the imperialists had made. My awareness rose bit by bit, and I came gradually to understand the relation of exploiter and exploited in the old society, the relation of Japanese imperialism and the Fengtian militarists, the difference between the Kuomintang and the Communist Party, the difference between the Kuomintang's left and right wings, and why there had to be Nationalist-Communist cooperation, what extraterritoriality was, tariff autonomy, and such questions and ideas. In those days the Japanese translated new Soviet books very fast, a new book done inside a month. But at the crucial, important passages they would often put dots, dots, dots in place of the text, so an ordinary reader could not follow; so I often studied together with Li Renyi, who could help me understand. The times, at home and abroad, were turbulent then — the great waves washing out the sand, mud and grit tumbling down together — and every kind of thought and view was current; the students argued endlessly. After the October Revolution the Soviet Union, in 1924, declared void all the unequal treaties China had suffered under the tsars, and this shook China's progressives and young students greatly. In 1925 Feng Yuxiang backed Guo Songling's revolt against the old militarist Zhang Zuolin. All this made me see that I could never again take the old militarist road. In December 1925 I wrote an article, "Against Japanese Imperialism's Invasion of China," and through my schoolmate's father's connection had it printed in the Fengtian Citizens' Daily. That was the vow with which I resolved to take the revolutionary road. On June 10, 1927, in Japan, introduced by Li Renyi and Old Yu (name unknown), I joined the Chinese Communist Party, and worked under the Osaka branch of the Party in Japan.

In September 1927 Zhao Weigang graduated from the Japanese Military Academy and returned to take a post as a major on the staff of the Northeastern Army's arsenal. Because his arsenal income, after deducting the repayment of his tuition, left him very little, he took on several concurrent posts: one as a major instructor at the Fengtian Military Academy; another as a Japanese-language interpreter and instructor in the model corps of Zhang Xueliang's Third and Fourth Front Army (a unit that took in middle-school students and trained them as junior officers and NCOs); and soon he also held a post as instructor in the Advanced Military Studies Class attached to the Fengtian Military Academy. He held these posts until the "September 18" Incident.

It was at this time that Zhao Weigang began his intelligence work. At first he came directly under the Central Special Section of the Party Center in Shanghai; at the end of 1928, after the Manchuria Special Committee (Secret Service Department) was set up in Fengtian, the Central Special Section made Cai Boxiang its chairman and Zhao Weigang its secretary-general, under the dual leadership of the Central Special Section and the Manchuria Provincial Committee. In 1932 he joined the intelligence work of the headquarters of the Soviet Far Eastern Red Banner Army. From 1933 to 1934 he studied at the Soviet Far Eastern Artillery School at Khabarovsk. From 1935 to 1936 he studied at the Moscow Mechanized Forces School. In 1937 the Moscow General Staff sent him to Xinjiang on a survey. From 1938 to 1940 he was director of the Dushanzi oil refinery in Xinjiang. In 1940 he went to the Moscow General Staff as a staff officer. In 1941 he taught at the Yan'an Military Academy; in 1943 he was a senior staff officer of the Central Military Commission; in 1945, director of studies at the Northeastern Artillery School; in 1948, chief of the training section of the Northeastern Military Region headquarters. In 1951 he worked in the training department of the Central Military Commission; in October 1952 he became head of the artillery department of the Harbin Military Engineering Institute; and in October 1959 he was transferred to the Ministry of the Chemical Industry. I came to know Zhao Weigang while working at the Harbin Military Engineering Institute.

In the years 1928 to 1931, when Zhao Weigang did intelligence work in the Manchuria Special Committee (Secret Service Department) under the Central Special Section, Chen Geng was chief of the Central Special Section's intelligence branch. When Chen Geng went to inspect intelligence work in the Northeast, he met Zhao Weigang several times. Not long after war broke out between China and the Soviet Union over the Chinese Eastern Railway on July 26, 1929, Chen Geng went to Shenyang. Introduced by the "Manchuria Pockmark," whom Chen Geng had sent to take charge of the "Manchuria intelligence network," Zhao Weigang and Chen Geng met for the first time in the back yard of the Fengtian mortar factory. Beforehand the Pockmark had had Zhao Weigang ready a passport and travel money for Chen Geng, and afterward he sent Zhao, in uniform and in the guise of a translator-instructor of Zhang Xueliang's Advanced Military Studies Class, to accompany and protect Chen Geng for many days of work at Qiqihar, Hailar, and elsewhere. Around April and May 1930, Chen Geng came to the Northeast together with Qian Zhuangfei and Hu Di, ostensibly for the Kuomintang but in fact to gather intelligence on the Northeastern Army for our Party and to build an intelligence organization there, and Zhao Weigang took part in this too. When Zhao Weigang later came to work at the Military Engineering Institute, Chen Geng was the institute's president and political commissar. Before and after the smaller meetings, and in the intervals, I saw the two of them speak of those old intelligence days. So in the late 1970s, when I was preparing to write a biography of General Chen Geng and gathering material on his work in the Central Special Section, I very much wanted to interview Zhao Weigang. But we had lost touch for years, and it was long before I got word of him. Only in the summer of 1997, when he was ninety-two, did we meet again. One day, out visiting a friend, I got a telephone call from home saying, "A ninety-two-year-old old comrade-in-arms has come to see you; his name is Zhao Weigang." I hurried home, and our meeting was full of emotion and joy. He said he had already seen my General Chen Geng, published in 1985, and was interested in its chapter "The Tiger's Den," which tells of Chen Geng's years in the Central Special Section. Sadly, being now advanced in years and having suffered greatly through the ten years of turmoil, his memory was badly impaired, and he could not speak of the past very fluently. Fortunately, as early as March 1983 he had written a memoir of his intelligence work in the Manchuria Special Committee (Secret Service Department), finished two years before General Chen Geng was even published. He had specially brought the final draft of this memoir to give me, for reference, and in the hope that it might one day be made public.

Zhao Weigang lived then in a residential block on Fuxingmenwai Avenue, not far from my own home on Chegongzhuang Avenue, and we saw each other often afterward. Old as he was, he always came and went alone, wanting no one to accompany him, and when a car was found to see him home he would decline again and again. From our talks I learned that after joining the Ministry of the Chemical Industry in 1959 he had served in turn as deputy dean of the ministry's First Design Institute, director of its foreign-liaison department, an inspector, and an adviser to its planning institute, retiring in 1982. In the "Cultural Revolution" the rebels called his history "complicated," framed and persecuted him, laid all manner of "trumped-up" charges on him, and hauled him through endless struggle sessions that gravely harmed him body and mind. Yet he was loyal and loving toward the Party throughout, showed a broad and generous spirit, kept a vigorous revolutionary temper, held to the Four Cardinal Principles, and supported reform and opening. He grieved deeply over the tendency in theoretical circles to abandon the basic principles of Marxism, and over the spread of passive corruption and lawbreaking within the Party; each time he spoke of it he was greatly stirred, and sometimes could not hold back his tears. His anxiety for the Party and the country was moving. But his faith in communism was strong, and he often quoted a passage of Chen Geng's on his hopes for the young: "The fighters of the older generation have won the country, but the older generation will not stay in the world forever; we are all bound to die. We hope you young people will grow up quickly and take over well from us. The great task of building communism is for you to shoulder! Do not forget the past; carry on the will of the revolutionary martyrs, and realize the great ideal of communism." Zhao Weigang said he cherished Comrade Chen Geng's charge to the younger generation; it was also his own lifelong lofty aim, and the force that spurred him to overcome every difficulty and struggle on without rest. In his last years he fell gravely ill, and fought the illness stubbornly with the iron will of an old Communist. Sadly, medicine could not save him, and at 8:45 on the evening of April 10, 1999, he quietly passed away, aged ninety-four. "A Life of Comrade Zhao Weigang," issued by the Ministry of the Chemical Industry's planning institute, states: "Comrade Zhao Weigang was a veteran member of our Party. His was a life of revolution, a life of struggle. In more than fifty years of revolutionary life — whether in the war years amid the flames of battle, or in the extraordinarily hard and complex struggle of his years in the Soviet Red Army, or in our country's war of liberation and its socialist construction — he was boundlessly loyal to the Party, full of the certainty of the revolution's victory, and gave his whole life to the Soviet revolution and to China's revolution and construction. He loved the Party, loved the socialist motherland. Through his long revolutionary work he studied Marxism-Leninism and Mao Zedong Thought in earnest, carried out the Party's line, principles, and policies in earnest, firm in faith, upright and unbending, with a high sense of duty and responsibility, careful and thorough, diligent and painstaking, meticulous, rigorous and practical, selfless in his devotion, striving hard to do every task well." Here I set down, in full, this memoir of Zhao Weigang's, detailed in its account, vivid in content, and plain in style:

### At the Fengtian Military Academy and the Advanced Military Studies Class

In November 1927, on the instructions of the Osaka branch of the Party in Japan, I went straight from Osaka to Shanghai to make contact with the underground Party there; but I drew a blank, and could only go back to Shenyang to see my old schoolmate Zhu Shiqin. He told me the four of us had all been assigned to the Shenyang arsenal (then called the Northeastern Arsenal). I wrote to the Osaka branch, reported this, and asked them to link me up with Shanghai. I reported to the arsenal and was assigned to the machine-gun department as a major on its staff. (Once I had also made a working link with Shanghai, I took on, besides, the job of interpreter for the lectures of Hamamoto, the Japanese adviser to the model corps, once a fortnight; I held a major's instructorship at the Military Academy [though I never actually took a class]; I translated the Japanese Field Service Regulations at home; and a little later I went to work in the Advanced Military Studies Class as well.) So I worked on, one part of me waiting for word from Osaka. Before long Osaka wrote back that they were making the connection and told me not to fret, to keep at my work for the time being (meaning the open, public occupation). Some time later, around New Year's Day 1928, I sent another letter (a telegram) to the secretary of the Osaka branch, saying I wanted to go to Osaka, or straight to Shanghai, to find the organization. He wrote back again saying not to fret, that once the link was made he would tell me at once, that sooner or later it would come. Another two or three months on, one day Liu Bogang came to my home and said Shanghai had sent someone, that I was to go to the Yuelai Inn at the Japan Station to make contact. He led me up into that inn, a building of four or five stories, and after introducing me to the man, left.

The man was surnamed Liao (that is, Liao Ruyuan, then secretary-general of the Manchuria Provincial Committee); he seemed a Northeasterner, a little stout, round-faced, close to me in age and height. Afterward I met him again at the Pockmark's home, where they said he was the East Manchuria special commissioner; and from then on I put the weight of my underground work on gathering military intelligence and political information.

I remember our first meeting was around three in the afternoon. The moment he spoke he called me Comrade Zhao, and he introduced himself, saying he was surnamed Liao and had been sent by the Shanghai organization to make contact with me. I saw his room was very small and plain: a small bed with a sheet spread on it, an old novel — Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio, or something of the sort — and a nightstand beside the bed. He said, "You've come; I was just waiting to eat with you!" It was through what he passed on that I first learned the spirit of the August 7 Conference and some of the news at home. He also told me that we had committed the error of right opportunism, that our country's revolution was at a low ebb and had suffered great damage, that the revolutionary tasks ahead would be harder still, and so on. Of course the level then was not what it is now, and he could only speak in general terms. Besides this, Old Liao told me my task was to gather intelligence. He gave me a mailing address, the name of a woman at the East Asia Hotel on Sichuan Road in Shanghai — something like Zhen or Su — seemingly an accountant or bookkeeper. The method of communication was very simple: one way was a letter written in chemical ink, another was to write the secret message between the lines in rice water, which, when held to a flame, would bring the words out — that was the method everyone used then. I used the first. I gave him the address of the Xinyuanhao confectionery. I also asked him what to do if I lost contact with Shanghai again — could I turn to Liu Bogang? He said I could not, that Liu was not yet a Party member. I asked him then whom I should turn to. He said I should write a report to Shanghai once a month, and that if by the third month there was still no reply, something was wrong and I should stop writing to Shanghai; then I could get in touch with so-and-so (I forget the name) at the YMCA in the city of Shenyang.

After I met Old Liao I sent a letter to the Osaka branch telling them I had linked up with the Shanghai organization. In this way I kept up my correspondence with Shanghai by the means above, until, at the end of 1928, before the Pockmark came, a middle-aged man came to my home looking for me. I was out at the time, and he left a note asking me to make out a housing guarantee for him under a false name; I saw to it and sent it off.

At the end of 1928, near the lunar New Year, the Pockmark (that is, Cai Boxiang, also named Shao Fumin) came. I went to the station to meet him. The Northeast has such cold weather, and he came in a Western overcoat — a very plain mark, so conspicuous that I said, you needn't have signaled, I'd have known you anyway.

Because I had made arrangements beforehand, the moment he arrived I put him up in the shop I knew, the very place that was my mailing address; this confectionery was next to the Korean Bank in Xiaoxiguan. The shopkeepers wanted to use me to get a telephone and build a lavatory installed; I threw my weight around and used my position to sort it out for them, and they were very grateful. I had something to ask of them too: to let Old Cai stay there a while.

There was a restaurant at the Japan Station, and he told me the manager and his family lived above it. The manager was well off, the room finely furnished, all antique elegance; he was a relative of Cai's, a Shandong man.

Old Cai had come this time to learn how my work stood and to instruct me what else, besides intelligence, I was to do from now on. I reported to him. I also told him my experience was not enough, and asked whether he could come and lead the work, or help me for a time and guide me, and then go back. He said he could not decide it himself, and would write to inform the family — the organization — of my view. Before long he told me a reply had come, that the organization agreed to have him help me in my work. But he had to go back to Shanghai once first, and then return.

He said he had studied medicine and wanted to open a clinic or small hospital as cover. Good enough — but he was not a graduate of a Northeastern medical school, had no Northeastern certificate, and to practice he would have to go through formalities and sit an examination; an out-of-province diploma would not do on its own. I got my old schoolmate Feng Xilian and others to help, claiming Old Cai was a Soochow University classmate of my elder brother's. They agreed at once, and even took me around to look at where the hospital had best be placed.

Inside the Xiaoxi city gate of Shenyang there was an Anlijiao, a shop that dealt in fresh goods, well known. Its head manager, Dong Qingyu, was my uncle-in-law, and I asked him to find me a small building. This little building had a small room outside and, inside, a room with a stair leading up; patients would wait in the outer room. The furnishings at the entrance looked the part, too, and we settled on the place.

The first thing we had to do was to get hold of the lower floor: to spend money and give gifts and move out the family living downstairs. It took no small face and no small pull — in the end the pulling reached all the way to Ji Jinchun, the military governor of Chahar. Ji's son Ji Shaogang was captain of the students' company, a returned student of the sixteenth class of the Military Academy, and Ji Shaogang traded on his father's name. We named the hospital the Shoushan Hospital. The hospital's very signboard was written by Ji Shaogang's father, Ji Jinchun — no small thing, that! It could not have been done otherwise. In those days it was all backdoor dealing: without connections you got nowhere; with money but no connections you could not even spend it!

In the spring of 1929 the Pockmark brought his wife to Fengtian, staying at that hotel by the Japan Station; before long his mother came too. This time the house I found for him was behind the mortar factory. The mortar factory's director, Li Yichun, was the son of a certain Northeastern brigade commander. Seeing that I had ties to Yang Yuting, and seeing that the arsenal's chief manager, Zang Shiyi, and its deputy manager, Weng Zhilin, both wished to draw me to their side and regarded me with special favor, he settled the house for me. Two families shared this little courtyard; in the outer part was some section chief Li Yichun had brought in, and the three inner rooms went to the Pockmark. After Cai's wife died at the Japanese hotel, he moved here and lived with his mother.

At the Shoushan Hospital the Pockmark also kept a medical assistant. When the Pockmark went home each evening, this man looked after the hospital for him, treated minor ailments, and did some nursing work. Cai's dealings with society all went on at the Shoushan Hospital, while his dealings with people inside the Party were at his home. He was chairman of the Special Committee, I its secretary-general; the two of us made a pair. The work we did was to scout the enemy's situation, protect the Party, and eliminate traitors; we also built a courier line — a courier line from Shenyang to Shanghai. How that line was used I do not know, but it was one we built later, and from then on I had my most direct superior. The medical assistant surnamed Li was a comrade Old Cai had brought from Shandong, probably a Communist; I had no particular contact with him, but I knew he did some medical work.

All along, our contact with the Shanghai organization went through the East Asia Hotel. Whether Old Cai had any other address for reaching Shanghai I do not know.

When Old Cai first came to the work, I introduced to him some people of fairly good thinking who could be of some use in the work — my social ties were fairly wide. Ordinarily, if there was anything at all pressing, I would go to Old Cai's home in person; otherwise I would seek him at the hospital; but whether to the hospital or to his home, I always telephoned first. At the time we were under the dual leadership of the Provincial Committee and the Central Special Section: we reported our situation to the Center, and did every task the Provincial Committee entrusted to us, and reported important intelligence to the Provincial Committee too; but generally speaking, the Provincial Committee had little to say about how the Special Committee's work should be done. Because the Center was in charge, the Provincial Committee seems never to have issued us work directives; from the Provincial Committee we could see such publications as the Manchurian Red Flag.

Our people were few. The work, though, was much, and so much work leaned mainly on the masses, on connections. In those days, even a comrade who did well in the work the Special Committee would not let join the organization. Back then, if you had no friendship with all sorts of odd people, then even with several men of your own you could get nowhere; without wide social ties you could not do the work well. After I came back from Japan I relied first on my fellow Military Academy students, and then, in the Military Academy, the arsenal, the model corps, and above all the Advanced Military Studies Class, I did the Party's work chiefly by relying on old acquaintances. I stayed four terms in the Advanced Military Studies Class, and even after the "September 18" Incident, when the class moved to Beijing, I still got things done through some of the people there — finding out how Zhang Xueliang would react to the Japanese invasion, what action he meant to take. The ones I relied on were mainly the middling officers whose standing could go up or down; these people were of the greatest use to me in getting things done, and they had real power. I noted down in a little book the names, posts, addresses, and telephone numbers of those useful to my Special Committee work, and their social ties, and ordinarily kept close to them, standing them meals, chatting and socializing; when the time came that I needed some man to settle a problem, I would look him up in the little book and ask him to see to it. Everywhere I kept a few such men, which made my movements easy. During the rectification study at Yan'an I wrote a set of source materials on the Northeastern Army (about 100,000 characters; the secretary-general of the Military Commission read it and offered revisions, and it was placed in the Military Commission library at Wangjiaping. I had no time to revise it — Japan surrendered, and I left Yan'an).

I introduced a few people to Old Cai as well, for his use in his work. These had ties of every kind — the Military Academy's, the model corps', the superintendent's office. He cultivated these men especially, and treated their illnesses with special zeal; sometimes he would take a parcel and go to them on his own, saying, "Here! No charge, it's nothing." These people came to him too, and little by little they became his friends. This also consolidated his social standing and covered his work.

It was precisely because of these special working ties that we could do some work for the Party smoothly, in that White Terror of an "army under siege."

Old Cai was transferred to work in Qingdao in the spring of 1931. He was bold and careful, and well experienced in underground work. The two of us always got on well and worked together in fine harmony. Once, in a private talk with Comrade Chen Geng, he told me Cai had been falsely accused of being a traitor, framed, and wrongly killed by the Party; before he died he cried out "Long live the Chinese Communist Party!" which drew the Party's notice, and only in the end was the truth of the matter cleared up.

Wu Baoxiang was a Party member; in 1928, doing underground work in Harbin, he was arrested and jailed, and gave himself out as a Kuomintang member. When Zhang Xueliang joined with Nanjing and raised the blue-sky-white-sun flag, he was released, and afterward went to the Soviet Union for treatment of tuberculosis. In the autumn or winter of 1930 he was posted to the Special Committee; his younger sister came with him and stayed at the Shoushan Hospital; when he went to Qingdao she went along too, and after Cai left, Wu succeeded him. After the September 18 Incident Wu's illness recurred, and later his cousin escorted him to Qingdao to recuperate; I too left the Chinese Party at that time.

In those days I was mainly in the Advanced Military Studies Class; besides that I did a spell of side duty at the Military Academy and the model corps, and I also held a nominal post at the arsenal. I had several schoolmates at the arsenal, and sometimes I would drop in and show my face; there was much talent there. Yang Yuting had brought in skilled workers from Shanghai as its backbone, and had gathered and kept there university graduates and specialists of every field who had studied abroad in various countries, to build up his own strength and stand against Zhang Xueliang — this too was a concrete sign of the rift between them. Among the men Yang Yuting had gathered were even the governor and chief manager Zang Shiyi and the deputy manager Weng Zhilin. These were all graduates of the Military Academy. He cultivated these men, and I too made a point of drawing close to them, especially to Weng Zhilin, whom I called "teacher" before others and behind his back — though what teacher of mine was he really? It was only that we wanted to make the tie close, to have something to lean on, so that our movements could be free and privileged. If anyone asked where we were going, we would say we had gone to see the deputy manager, and so we could slip past the director's level and quietly get a little business done, and those below dared say nothing. We often used such pretexts to drop in and tour this factory or that, gathering intelligence and collecting materials — very easily. In those days I truly could get whatever material I wanted, obtain whatever intelligence I wanted. So even before Old Cai came, I had already gathered a great pile of things. This material was all on military matters — military technology, military production plans, product output and kinds, sources of raw material, army organization and manning, military equipment, and all sorts of inside information, everything one could ask for, all built up over a long time. The moment Old Cai came, I handed it all over to him. There was a great deal of it, and he was very pleased to see it. He had a quick mind and was very decisive; he said at once, "I must get this material out right away." I said doubtfully, could it be gotten out? So much of it — how? He said, would it do not to get it out? He set to at once and made a large case with a false compartment, stuffing the material in bundle by bundle, and told me exultantly, "It can certainly be delivered."

On his first visit to Shenyang, Old Cai stayed less than two weeks in all.

In the first half of 1929 this happened. The younger brother of a certain Jiang, Chiang Kai-shek's Northwest field-headquarters director stationed in Lanzhou, then about twenty, was sent under my name to the first company of the students' corps of the Northeastern Military Academy as a cadet. The company captain was Ji Shaogang, son of Ji Jinchun, the military governor of Chahar (a returned student of the sixteenth class of the Japanese Military Academy). Jiang's brother read our Party documents and, when he had finished, put them under the blanket on his bed. They were found during a sanitation inspection and turned in to the captain, Ji Shaogang. People said, wasn't he brought in by Old Zhao? — they meant to arrest him. Back in his quarters, the brother found the documents gone and panicked; he wanted to run but could not, the sentries watching close. The next day, on duty, he was called in and asked who had recommended him. He was dumbstruck, could not answer. "You don't even know how you got in? Was it Zhao Shiyu who brought you in?" He did not know who this Zhao Shiyu was, and how I had recommended him I did not know either. I had the master list; I usually kept it in the wall. There was a loose brick in my wall, the list stuffed behind it, and when the time came I would quietly take it out for a look and then hurry it back in. When the trouble broke, Ji Shaogang telephoned and said, "Old Zhao, that treasure you sent has landed in trouble." I said, is it so serious? He said, come quickly! I looked and said, what is this thing? A Communist leaflet! How did it get here? They said, how did it get here? You brought him in! Ask whom? Half joking, I said: if that's how it is, then send me off to the gendarmerie headquarters! They all laughed and said, look, how would you settle this case? I said, how am I to put it? I'm not you — do I have the say? If you must have me say it, I'd say you are the military governor's eldest son; who could suspect you of being a Communist? Isn't it settled with one word from you? Just get a guarantor, that's all; or you can take him into custody and send him off, and say on a bend of the road you weren't watching and he got away — isn't that that? What's so hard about it? Laughing all the while, they said, would that work? I said it would. Ji Shaogang had been a student of the sixteenth class of the Japanese Military Academy and got on well with me as a fellow student; and since doing a favor would cost him nothing, he let Jiang go and then said Jiang had escaped. So the problem was settled. But before long the mother came, went straight to my home, sat on the kang demanding her son, and would not leave without him. Hard to manage, that — told to go she would not, she just stuck to you, and you could not slight her. Say what you would she would not listen; she just wanted her son! But there was not the least clue to the boy's whereabouts, and she stayed several months. Only when a letter came at last from her son did she leave.

### Rescuing the Arrested Liu Bogang

One day in the spring of 1930 Old Cai came to me to work out the matter of Liu Bogang's work, and asked whether I could arrange for Liu Bogang to do work among the soldiers. I said he was a university professor, had studied economics at Tokyo Imperial University, walked in a mild and cultivated way, and could not even get standing at attention and at ease right — how could he be a military officer? He knew nothing of military matters, so how could he do work among the soldiers? But Old Cai had him do it all the same. I could only put him up at my own place in the Military Academy, his open occupation being to organize some instructors to learn Japanese, his cover being that he was a friend of Yang Bingsen — the leader of the machine-gun, mortar, and flat-gun training class (that is, a class in machine guns, mortars, and flat-trajectory guns). His targets were the students (NCOs) studying in that training class.

Yang Bingsen, also named Yang Fengzhang, was a good friend of a colleague of mine, fairly progressive, and the leader of this training class. Appearing as his friend made it easier to get close to the class's soldiers. From then on Liu Bogang did work among the soldiers here, talking with the class's officers and men on holidays. Jia Tao, one of the class's three squad leaders, had a room to himself and was fairly free, and Liu Bogang often went to chat with him about the international and domestic situation and such, which did much to advance his thinking.

Besides this, Liu Bogang often went to the East Tombs forest garrison to chat with the officers, and this unexpectedly aroused their suspicion. That July or August, Liu Bogang went down to the Hun River to bathe, and someone secretly felt in the pocket of the clothes he had left on the bank and found a copy of the Manchurian Red Flag; and so he was seized. After they had asked him a few simple questions, they drew up a warrant and marched him out of the East Tombs. As they came to the gate of the Military Academy, the sentries and messengers, who all knew him, saw him bound with several armed soldiers behind him, and rushed back to report to Yang Bingsen. Yang Bingsen, so flustered he had not even put on his cap or done up his tunic, ran out shouting "Halt!" The forest garrison men making the arrest, seeing he was a lieutenant colonel, had to halt, and reported that they had caught a Communist and had proof. Yang had no way to save him, could only have him bound a little looser, and then came at once to find me. We hurried to the Northeastern Rural Pacification Committee, but they had already held the hearing and gone home for the day. At the hearing the secretary-general, the assessors, and the forest garrison had all taken part. Yang Bingsen, then the leader of the machine-gun, mortar, and flat-gun training class, did not know Liu Bogang was a Communist. From what he ordinarily understood, he thought Liu Bogang a learned man, sound in all his talk and reasoning; Liu had probably done a good deal of work on them while teaching them Japanese, and so Yang was much concerned at his arrest.

At the time Yang Bingsen and I reckoned together how to save Liu Bogang. I said he might be a Communist, or he might be a Kuomintang man — I could not make it out; nowadays there were many progressive people, and he had studied abroad besides; among friends there were Communists and Kuomintang men both, and it might be that he had seen someone's document and idly picked it up for a look and so seen the trouble in it — we could not for that reason stand by and watch him die! Let's spend a few dollars and get him out! What do you say? He said all right, it could be done. I said I would take charge of the money — first get him out, then talk. Seeing they had knocked off for the day, we went to find the judge too. Seeing us in officers' uniforms, he kept saying "Sir, sir," went to find the record, then found the clerk, and even offered a plan: Liu Bogang also had a Military Academy badge; have him say he had picked it up off the ground, and that would turn the trouble simple; whether he could shake off the document depended on his own skill.

As for where he was to be sent, the judge said he had to be sent up — first to the prison, then to the court. Argue as we might, they simply dared not release him; in the end they agreed not to pursue the matter of the Military Academy badge Liu wore (the one I had given him), accepting that Liu had picked it up, and by this we cut the tie — but they still kept Liu imprisoned.

At this point I had still not given up. I thought of how I knew Bao Wenyue, the Military Academy's director of studies, and the corps commander Dai Shihan, and Wang Jingxiu, and such men, who had all been at the Japanese infantry school; I could go to them, put up a bit more money for the man and give a large gift, and see whether Liu Bogang could be gotten out. So I went to Old Cai and told him how things stood. Old Cai said, how sure are you? One thing is that the money won't be small — but that's a minor matter; the most important thing is that there isn't much certainty, and if it goes wrong it will expose you too, and if you're exposed, how will you do the work afterward? Frankly, planting yourself as you have is not easy! Are you to give up your work then? You would have to leave the Northeast; and though leaving the Northeast isn't the end of working, the whole organization would lose out. And how, again, are you to put it? Say he's a Communist? Say he isn't? But he has a document of his own, so leave this matter alone for now; we must wait and see.

At the time, by my standing and position I could have gone forward and asked for him myself, but I feared it might fail at one stroke. We had not been back in the country long, and my ties with the director of studies and the corps commander were not deep — only acquaintance, no great friendship; how could they, on your say-so, testify that he was not a Communist? If it went wrong I might land myself in it, and once they used you they would put a question mark against you, and in future, if some little thing went amiss, it would be awkward. In Japan I had shown no flaw; to stir up gossip here would not be worth it. He said he would send in a report and ask for instructions. I said, what am I to do now? I'll go visit the prison — at the very least I have to bring him his luggage! He said, besides the luggage, bring out the leads to the work too.

Liu Bogang was in the First Model Prison. I went and saw him wearing leg-irons. He told me the names of the four Party members he had developed in the training class, and these were changed over to my contact. Old Cai asked me whether I knew a man named Tong Yingxuan; I said I did — he was a teacher at the Pingdan Middle School; whether he was a Party member at the time I did not know, but I knew he was progressive and did work for the Red Relief Society. Cai said to transfer Liu's contacts over to him. I found Tong and brought him Liu's luggage, and told him: take the luggage in; the contacts are transferred to you. He agreed. Later Yang Bingsen also went to see Old Liu. The luggage, and the little quilted jacket I had made for Liu, he received them all.

After this I went back and forth into the military school doing work among the soldiers, taking Old Cai along (he could not go in) to the messengers' room to call out the soldiers' names and make contact; and so we took on the work among the soldiers as well, carrying it on for several months, until the chairman of the Manchuria Military Commission, Hu, and its secretary-general, Huang, and others came, and we turned this line of work over to them.

When the Northeast was liberated in 1948, I ran into Liu Bogang in Harbin. He said he had been let out under the general amnesty at Puyi's enthronement. When I met him he was working in a commercial department, and that department's political section had once written to me to learn the circumstances of Liu's arrest. I went to our military-control organ to find out how Liu had behaved in prison, and I read the prison files too; they proved Liu had no problem in his conduct in prison. At the time a schoolmate of mine was head of the prison's medical office, a man named Zhou Deyu, and he had looked after Liu all along. Liu had said the document was one he picked up; people did not believe him, but he always said so, and there was no hard proof, no one pressed him, and no other bad sort went to trip him up. On top of that Zhou Deyu always spoke well of him, ran about for him, treated his ailments, looked after him. The enemy could not but weigh that connection, so his case stayed at "suspected Communist," and inside he suffered no torture of any kind. Zhou Deyu had once approached Zhushi, a Japanese teacher at the South Manchuria Middle School. Zhushi's condition was that Liu turn himself in, give up his faith in communism, and say that communism would not work in China: write something and I'll get you out. Liu Bogang refused, and did not fall for this bait. All this was learned through the prison's military representative in his inquiry.

### Fresh Setbacks in Work Among the Troops

Some four or five months after we took over the work among the soldiers, at the end of 1930, the Manchuria Provincial Committee sent two comrades to handle military matters. One, surnamed Hu (returned from the Soviet Union, later murdered; the papers, in reporting it, called him Han Yuanbo), was secretary of the Manchuria Provincial Committee's military commission; the other, surnamed Huang (a graduate of the first class of the Whampoa Military Academy), was its secretary-general. I made the connections for both of them and rented houses on their guarantees.

This was just the time when Li Lisan's "left" adventurism was in full swing, with armed uprisings being got up all over the country. After they came, I turned the work among the soldiers of the machine-gun, mortar, and flat-gun training class over to them. Four Party members of that class, Lu Touyun and others, had been developed by Liu Bogang. They were soldiers of the thirty-eighth regiment of the fifteenth brigade of the Northeastern Army, come from Boketu in North Manchuria; during their studies they too came under the influence of the Li Lisan line, and after returning to their original unit set about preparing an armed uprising in earnest. The Manchuria Provincial Committee's military commission also ran a military training class in Shenyang; I was put in charge of getting hold of a rifle, a Mauser pistol, and a hand grenade, and I taught the military training course while Old Huang taught the political one.

The trainees were five worker Party members brought over from Fushun, and the place of study was chosen at the home of Huang, the Provincial Committee military commission's secretary-general; the subject was marksmanship and grenade-throwing. Later, Central documents came quickly, criticizing the Li Lisan "left" line and halting the plan for armed uprisings across the country, and this marksmanship-and-grenade class disbanded, the five worker Party members returning to their original posts. Old Hu (that is, Han Yuanbo), secretary of the Manchuria Provincial Committee military commission, took the passport I had made out for him and set off at once for North Manchuria (the Boketu area) to transmit the Central document, giving notice to stop the armed uprising. On the very day Old Hu arrived — the evening of January 13, 1931 — a meeting was in session when all the more than ten present were arrested, and five were later killed. Because Old Hu's house in Shenyang had been rented on my guarantee, once the trouble broke the Manchuria Provincial Committee at once had me clear out Old Hu's lodgings.

### A Struggle to Save the Documents

Another of my tasks was to make out shop guarantees for comrades — a shop guarantee being a certification that someone was a "good citizen."

I had many social ties, but had to keep developing more, because a guarantee could not always be sought from one place; always seeking one place was not safe. So the Military Academy, the Advanced Military Studies Class, the arsenal, the Guangji Hospital, the Anlijiao, the Xinyuanchang, the Guixiangzhai, and the Feng Xilian clinic were all "hooked up"; this guarantee here, the next one there. Whenever there was trouble, Old Cai and I would read the papers and check the name lists, asking everywhere who had been taken in, whose guarantee it was, who had handled it at the time, what problems might arise, and so on. The Party then was rather immature, the organization somewhat loose, some Party members without organization or discipline and rather lacking in experience of struggle against the enemy, so trouble kept breaking out.

After the Manchuria Provincial Committee was broken up in 1930, this happened.

At the time the Korean comrades had no independent party of their own; the Korean Communists all joined our branch and did underground work alongside us, all under our Northeastern Party's leadership. Once, for the Provincial Committee's safety, it was decided to entrust some of the committee's important documents to a young Korean couple for safekeeping. This couple I never saw, but the Provincial Committee made the guarantee through me, and the house was one I rented, the wing rooms of a certain brigade commander at Xiezhen. This brigade commander, though he had already stepped down, still had a telephone, a house, and servants, all his privileges as before. The house was very trim and imposing, a separate compound with its own gate. But this brigade commander was very sly, and he wanted to investigate the tenant. After receiving the guarantee slip he at once went in person to verify it, demanding another seal be stamped, and asking: what was the relation between this tenant and your guarantor, the Guangji Hospital? Who was this man who had asked you to make out the tenant's guarantee — his name, his occupation? My schoolmate Zhang Haitang at the Guangji Hospital gave him my cover. This brigade commander said, tell him the couple you guaranteed have a problem; asked what the problem was, he only said it was serious, serious, and left. As for the young Korean couple, seeing how grave the situation was, they took fright and dared not go back. The brigade commander, seeing the door of the wing rooms always locked, grew more suspicious, pried open the window, and found the other things inside very plain — but there were several big wicker cases; what was this, had the people fled?

I heard from Zhang Haitang that the brigade commander had gone in person to verify the guarantee and pressed further to learn who had had it made out. Once we learned this, we felt the matter was very grave, a threat to the safety of the Provincial Committee and the Special Committee. What was to be done? Old Cai was a very experienced underground worker; I went at once to the Pockmark, and he too found the thing a headache — first, where had the people gone? Had the gendarmerie headquarters taken them off, or had they fled? Second, what of the documents? Had someone gotten them open, or what had become of them? It all had to be made clear; he told me not to be afraid, to keep steady. But even the county committee could find no clue where the couple had fled. And the brigade commander had already had the wicker cases pried open and found them full of documents, which made the thing graver still, for the matter now touched much — it touched the Provincial Committee, our Special Committee, and the guarantors too. I was very anxious, but the Pockmark told me to go to the central street and buy a few gifts to give the brigade commander; at the same time he told me that if the man would not deal on friendly terms, we would strike; the other course was to be ready to set a fire. We hired a car, found one of our own men to drive it, brought a few more men over from the arsenal, and I went and got hold of guns. If the man tried to telephone out, we would cut his wire; if he tried to send someone out with a message, we would shoot. We also took along a barrel of gasoline: if by then the documents could not be gotten out, we would pour it on his wing rooms and set them alight. We arranged, too, who would stand where, who would light the fire, who would shoot, who would do what. We haven't come to burn your house — we've come for these documents; can't get them out, can we!

I went as well. Cai the Pockmark's handling of the work made a deep impression on me: he was bold and careful, resourceful, thorough in his arrangements, very experienced. He went in first, and I kept watch at the outer gate against any mishap. There was a telephone in the brigade commander's messengers' room; I was ready, the moment anyone came to use it, to pin him down, gag his mouth, tie him up, then cut the line and rush in. We had agreed beforehand: if it succeeded, Old Cai would come out and turn his cap around; and for fear the electric lights might go out and we could not see, each of us wound a white towel around his neck.

But once inside, the brigade commander, seeing there were gifts and money, softened his manner somewhat. On top of that the Pockmark said the officer who had helped guarantee the tenant for you could not come today and had entrusted me to come see you — putting on great airs, as if to say, my rank may be no higher than yours, but I have standing — and spun out a whole line. Cai the Pockmark explained further that he did not know these two young people either; he was a Military Academy graduate making his way, so how would he have any tie with these two youngsters? Was it not a matter of a friend asking a friend? We are all military men, so let's make friends! With all this laid on, the brigade commander, seeing there were so many gifts and money, and being himself a retired officer — well water not troubling river water — thought making a friend no bad thing either! In future he too might have something to trouble people with; brigade commander though I am, I've no real power today, while these men are Military Academy graduates with friendships to this or that high official; so he could only give way and say, take those wicker cases away! And tell that friend of yours to take a bit more care next time! It's a complicated business! We thanked him again and again, and had the things all carried out to the car, and so the matter was settled to perfection, without recourse to arms. We were all overjoyed. From this one sees that Old Cai's handling of problems was no simple thing, that he had real skill; and there were many other matters, too, that he handled very well!

### Learning a Month Before "September 18" That Japan Would Strike

In those days our daily task was to scout the enemy's situation and pass along intelligence. On the eve of the "September 18" Incident in 1931, we noticed something abnormal at the South Manchuria Railway station in Shenyang: the station's great warehouse, already very large, had now been enlarged a great deal more; where it had been fenced with barbed wire, it was now boarded up to keep outsiders from seeing in; around it many temporary military tents had been pitched, and no few bunkers dug. Looking in from a height, we found many Japanese youths inside undergoing military training. For this I made a special trip to Siping, and found the same sort of thing there, with Japanese sentries added at the railway's water towers. It looked as if the Japanese were going to strike. A month before the incident we reported to the higher Party organization. Two weeks before it we reported again to Zang Shiyi, the governor of Liaoning province, telling him the Japanese were going to strike.

Another matter was that Cai the Pockmark had me look into two questions about the enemy: first, how in the end the thing would be fought, and what the next step would be after the fighting — would they go on riding roughshod? And second, what further activity Japanese imperialism had in store. At the time Chiang Kai-shek wanted to use Zhang Xueliang and the Soviet Union to provoke a war, to use Soviet military strength to hold Zhang Xueliang in check.

On the whole, because our social base was so solid then, we felt Special Section work no great strain. But to carry the work in to the Japanese was hard — one could not easily break in. To break in through such people as those who fetched and delivered clothes at the laundries was of limited use, too. The Japanese used Chinese servants, but as a rule Chinese women did not live in Japanese homes; they went, finished the work, and came home in the afternoon; and when the Japanese talked among themselves they guarded closely against the Chinese — you could not hope to overhear. So through such people, too, we could gather little. There was another sort, the quasi-Japanese, the plainclothes traffic police for the Japanese. At first we worked desperately on such people, but later found little came of it, because they too had no way to get at the "core secrets." As for the movements, strength, equipment, garrisoning, and freight-train comings and goings of their army, we could all find ways to learn; ordinarily, using the convenience of the work, we gathered military intelligence and materials, and they never noticed. But on the most secret things we did not succeed — for instance, we wanted at the time to learn about Japan's newest gas-protection gear, and worked at it a long while, but never got it out.

Still, looking back, we did complete our tasks. Whatever work the Provincial Committee had, we backed it at once, and always with results: making out shop guarantees, issuing passes, finding housing, hiding underground workers; money when money was wanted, work when work was wanted, and, when trouble arose, pulling strings and mounting a rescue — in all these we were of use to them. Our division of labor was this: whatever the Provincial Committee had, they came to Old Cai; and I? Old Cai told me to make a guarantee, I made a guarantee; to issue a pass, I issued a pass; to scout something, I scouted it; and when someone was arrested I would go to the gendarmerie headquarters to find Zhang Jiazhen, son of Zhang Xinya (of the nineteenth class of the Military Academy), and learn who had been taken, what the charge was, how it was being handled. The everyday work was making connections, getting cozy, socializing, joining in the fun: giving a gift on the Zhang family's birthday, offering congratulations when the Li family had a child, idle chatter here, loafing there, so that when something came up it would be easy. Whoever was useful, I stood him more meals and gave more gifts; whoever had solid connections, I edged up toward and bored in. In my heart I was vexed then, disliking it, but for the sake of the tasks there was no help for it. Our working this way had one good result: right up to the time Old Cai left, none of the four of us ran into any danger, and the tasks the Provincial Committee and the Center gave us were all completed.

After the "September 18" Incident this post was closed down, and I left the Northeast for the Soviet Union; but I left all the leads and ties to Wu Baoxiang. Whether he opened up the work afterward I do not know. I know only that later, at the Northeast's flag-changing, he gave himself out as Kuomintang, was set free, and after his release went off to the Soviet Union for treatment and study. Old Cai left in the first half of 1931, in the spring perhaps; I reached Beijing two days after "September 18," and the Northeastern Special Section work was then at an end.
