# China's King of Assassins — a reliability map

*A reader's guide to what in Dou Yingtai's book is documented, what is
disputed, and what did not happen. Compiled from chapter-by-chapter checking
against Chinese, Japanese, Korean and English sources.*

---

## The short version

The book is not hogwash and it is not history. It is a novel built on an
accurate set.

Almost everything you could look up will check out: the streets, the buildings,
the ships, the institutions, the public events and their dates, and most of the
real people who walk through it. What does not check out is the one thing the
book exists to assert — that Wang Yaqiao was the hidden author of the era's
famous violence.

And that failure is not random. It has a shape.

---

## The finding: two Wang Yaqiaos

Lay the canonical operations out chronologically and the pattern is unmistakable.

| # | Operation | Date | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | **Xu Guoliang**, Songhu police chief | Nov 1923 | **DOCUMENTED** |
| b | **Zhao Tieqiao**, China Merchants Co. | 24 July 1930 | **DOCUMENTED** |
| c | **T.V. Soong**, Shanghai North Station | 23 July 1931 | **DOCUMENTED** |
| d | **Lushan attempt on Chiang Kai-shek** | June 1931 | **DOCUMENTED** |
| e | **Hongkou Park bombing** | 29 April 1932 | **REFUTED as his** |
| f | **The plot against Lord Lytton** | Nov 1932 | **REFUTED — impossible** |
| g | **Yang Xingfo** | 18 June 1933 | **REFUTED — no link at all** |
| h | **Wang Jingwei shooting** | 1 Nov 1935 | **DISPUTED** |

The four that hold up are all **1923 to 1931**, and all **mercenary or
factional** — killings with a paying principal, where a contemporary
investigation identified who commissioned them or where the political
consequence left a documentary trace. Xu Guoliang was bought by Lu Xiaojia.
Zhao Tieqiao was bought by Li Guojie, who drew eight years for it. The Soong
attempt was Canton faction money, and Wang's authorship was exposed by an
informer of his own. Lushan is corroborated down to the guns smuggled up the
mountain inside Jinhua hams.

The three that collapse are all **1932 to 1933**, and all **patriotic** — the
ones that make him a national hero rather than a hired gun.

**So: the documented Wang Yaqiao is a professional assassin for hire. The
patriotic Wang Yaqiao is largely a construction of the 1980s.** This book is a
late node in that construction.

## How the three refuted ones fail

**Hongkou Park (April 1932).** The bombing is fully documented — Yun Bong-gil,
acting for Kim Ku's Korean Patriotic Corps, killed a Japanese general and cost
the Japanese minister to China a leg. Wang Yaqiao appears in **no Korean,
Japanese or English source**. The attribution traces to a **1983 article by
Wang's own younger brother**; Chinese Wikipedia carries it with an explicit
note that no solid source supports it, and a named Chinese scholar rejects it.
The diagnostic detail is the weapon: the book uses a *timed* bomb hidden in a
thermos flask, and the real device was **thrown**.

What is real, and more interesting, is that Wang armed the *other* Korean
faction — the anarchists of the Anti-Japanese National Salvation League, whose
operation was the March 1933 attempt on the minister Ariyoshi. The book took a
genuine relationship with one group and transplanted it onto the famous
operation of a rival.

**Lytton (November 1932).** Impossible on the book's own printed dates. It sets
the plot between 2 and 13 November 1932. The commission had left China on **4
September**. On **2 November** — the day the book opens the plot — **Lord Lytton
was speaking in the House of Lords in London.** No record exists of any attempt
on any commissioner, in any language.

**Yang Xingfo (June 1933).** He was shot by the Nationalist secret service
outside the Academia Sinica publications office, his fifteen-year-old son in
the car. Searched in both directions: **no connection of any kind** between Wang
Yaqiao and Yang Xingfo. The popular literature does not even make this claim;
it is the book's own construction.

**And the disputed one.** For the Wang Jingwei shooting, the event is fully
documented and the organizer was **Hua Kezhi**, running a cover news agency in
Nanjing. Wang Yaqiao was in Hong Kong that day. The attribution to him
originates with Dai Li's investigation and Nanjing's own announcement — and was
**denied at the time by Hua Kezhi himself**, in a public letter written while
Wang was still alive, which claimed the operation for his own group and named
Chiang as the intended target. At least three mutually exclusive attributions
circulate, which is itself the strongest sign that none is documented. Wang's
financial or material association is plausible and widely asserted; his
authorship is not attested.

## Why the sources feel more solid than they are

The single most important thing to understand about this literature: **the
Chinese popular corpus on Wang Yaqiao is one body of story wearing many hats.**

It recycles a small set of narratives and leans ultimately on two streams: the
memoirs of **Shen Zui**, a Juntong officer who wrote after 1949 in PRC custody
as a reformed war criminal, and the **文史资料** recollections gathered decades
after the events. Finding a claim in a dozen articles across a dozen respectable
outlets — including *People's Daily* magazine pieces — is not corroboration. It
has been copied twelve times.

**And there is a decisive negative.** The claim that Wang was behind the Hongkou
Park bombing first surfaces in **1983**, in 文史资料 written by his own younger
brother and nephew. Nothing before 1949 connects him to it. More tellingly,
**Shen Zui's memoirs — which discuss Wang Yaqiao at length — say nothing of
it.** A Juntong officer writing from the other side, with every reason to
inventory the man's operations, does not have the most famous one in his
account. That is the strongest single indication that the patriotic Wang Yaqiao
was assembled after the fact, by his own family, in the 1980s.

Note too what the mainstream-looking reprints actually are. Pieces carrying
*People's Daily* or *Guangming Daily* mastheads turn out, on inspection, to be
**书刊博览 popular-book excerpt columns** — reprints of trade paperbacks, not
archival reporting. The masthead is not a citation.

This is why useful checking had to leave Chinese-language sources entirely:
Japanese naval records for the *Izumo*, Japanese Wikipedia and postwar testimony
for the Nichiren monk affair, Korean national reference works for Yun Bong-gil,
and Hansard for Lytton's whereabouts.

There is essentially **no Western monographic scholarship on Wang Yaqiao**. He
appears in Frederic Wakeman's *Spymaster* only in passing — as the labour
organizer and hitman who mentored Dai Li until 1923, which independently
supports the early, mercenary picture and nothing later.

Even the epithet is suspect. **暗杀大王, "king of assassins," appears to be a
retrospective coinage** — no period newspaper page reproducing it could be
found, and the whole cluster of titles reads as modern popular-history branding.
The book's own title participates in it.

## The book's three habits

**1. Relocation.** Taking a real event and moving it. Chapter seven's murder of
a Japanese monk at Longhua Temple never happened — but Tanaka Ryūkichi really
did arrange an attack on Japanese Nichiren followers by the Sanyou towel works
on 18 January 1932, one of whom died, and the riots that followed ran directly
into the Japanese assault of 28 January. Right instigator, right sect, right
method, right consequence, wrong temple, wrong victim, wrong date.

**2. Retrojection.** Pulling later events and institutions backward so Wang can
be present. Chapter two folds 1929 and 1930 into a March 1928 frame. Chapter six
gives Dai Li the Renaissance Society's Special Services Section a year before it
existed, puts Sun Ke in an office he would not hold for six months, and stations
Wu Tiecheng as mayor of Shanghai eighteen months early. Chapter six also puts
Sun Fengming on the North Station platform when he was still in the northeast.

**3. Annexation.** The main event — attaching other people's operations to Wang.
See the scorecard.

## What is reliably accurate

Genuinely a lot, and it is why the book reads as authoritative:

- **Physical Shanghai.** Sassoon House and Victor Sassoon, the Cathay Hotel,
  Route Père Robert and Guangci Hospital on it, Shiliupu wharf, the Waibaidu
  Bridge, Jing'an Temple, the Great World.
- **Public events and their dates.** The North Station shooting, Mukden, the
  January 28 attack, the Songhu ceasefire, Hongkou Park, Yang Xingfo's murder,
  the Fujian Incident.
- **Real people, correctly placed.** Dai Li's Shanghai station officers all
  resolve to documented men. Shen Junru. Zhao Lijun. Shigemitsu, Tanaka, Nomura,
  Ueda. Cai Tingkai and Jiang Guangnai.
- **Occasional details better than expected.** The Jinhua-ham gun smuggling at
  Lushan is in the record. The *Izumo* mine attempt really happened on the night
  of 1 March 1932, almost exactly as described, and Japanese naval sources
  confirm the current defeated it.
- **A remarkable true coincidence.** T.V. Soong's mother died at Qingdao on 23
  July 1931 — the same morning her son was shot at. That is real. The further
  story that she died of shock at the news is legend welded onto it.

## What is reliably invented

- **All dialogue and interior thought**, by genre convention. The book does not
  pretend otherwise.
- **Minor personnel.** 45 of 216 names in the glossary are unattested. Several
  central to their chapters return literally zero hits anywhere — Wei Yiming,
  the informer chapter ten turns on; Inamura Kagorō and Tada Ichirō in chapter
  six; Wei Xiuyan, the secretary who leaks Soong's train.
- **At least one flat contradiction of record.** Li Guojie, murdered in Suzhou
  Creek at the end of chapter four, was not murdered. He was tried, jailed,
  recovered control of the China Merchants company in 1932, and was shot by Dai
  Li's service in February 1939.

## How to read it

Trust the set, not the plot. Treat every claim that Wang Yaqiao was behind a
famous event as unproven until checked, and treat the pre-1932 mercenary
killings as the solid ground. Read the dialogue as a novelist's.

The most useful single question to carry: *does this operation have a paying
client?* If it does, the book is probably on firm ground. If it is patriotic and
famous, it probably belongs to somebody else.

## A closing judgement

Two things deserve saying at the end that no single chapter shows.

**The book is at its most honest where it had most to gain by lying.** Chapter
thirteen puts Wang Yaqiao in Hong Kong on 1 November 1935, learning of the Wang
Jingwei shooting from a three-day-old newspaper. That is exactly where he was.
An author bent purely on inflation would have had him in Nanjing.

**And its most consequential invention is the quietest.** Chapters twelve and
fourteen contain no famous event and so invite no checking, and between them
they install what chapter fifteen needs: a romance between Wang and Yu Wanjun,
and a mechanism for turning her. Neither is attested. The documented betrayal
is a woman, a sum of money, and a promise about a husband in prison. The book
supplies it with a broken heart.

That is the book in one movement — accurate where you would check, invented
where you would feel.
