## Chapter 43

But though I had left the hospital, I could not stretch out both arms lively and free, for at that time my left arm could be raised only to some sixty-seven or sixty-eight degrees in front, and but ten degrees behind; later it gained a little, but the doctor said that anything above eighty degrees would be forever impossible.

This did not greatly hinder any of my daily movements, but dressing each day was a little awkward: I had to draw on the left sleeve slowly first before I could put on the right, which kept me forever conscious of my maiming; and even now, wholly used to it as I am, that consciousness is still with me. As for large, forceful actions such as lifting weights, those of course I could no longer have the good fortune to do.

There are two kinds of people in the world: one that, meeting a thing, thinks first to the good of it, and one that thinks first to the bad; and the former, I think, suffers the more. My maiming was not, at the time, discovered by any outsider, but among the few who knew of it these two attitudes appeared. Bai Ping, for one, liked to look on the better side: had the bullet caught the arm as well, she said, you would not have even this small awkwardness—only a few inches out; how unlucky it was! While Mei Yingzi liked to look to the worse: luckily, she said, the first wound had bent you double, or it would have caught you full in the chest—only a few inches out; how lucky it was!

For my own part I felt this shooting a wrong in itself; and the one thing that consoled me was that I had won it as the price of an honor.

That very night I left the hospital, Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi told me of a plan of work; and the first step of it had already borne fruit.

It was this: they had egged Umetake into holding once more a purely masked evening ball. For at the last one the goodwill between China and Japan had been, on the Chinese side, no more than a courteous show, with no true warmth of feeling reached; so this time all would go masked, and all in Western evening dress. Under cover of it they meant to steal a certain important document. It was only now that I learned Umetake was not merely a naval staff officer but an intelligence officer of a special kind. Umetake had already agreed to the masked ball, and it was fixed for the thirteenth of March.

Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi were as excited as schoolgirl athletes on the eve of a match. In the planning, each pressed to be the one to carry out the theft. Bai Ping said:

"I have done a great deal of this kind of work, so I am the surer hand at it."

But Mei Yingzi said:

"Last time it was my affair, and in the end you snatched it from me; so this time, whatever happens, let me be the one to do it."

The matter was never settled, and each time we met and it came up, the same dispute arose. In truth I too very much wished to take on this work, but because of the awkwardness of my arm I never spoke of it. Now we met almost daily, always at Bai Ping's place, though we were seldom out together; each kept to the old round of life, so as not to draw notice. Mei Yingzi's visits to Bai Ping's were usually by night, and she seldom used her red car—sometimes she did not take a car at all, and sometimes she stayed the night at Bai Ping's.

Once, when I called at Bai Ping's, Mei Yingzi was already there before me. Again they went from the plan of the work to the one who should carry it out, and, when the two could not agree, each hoped for some support from me for her side; and my own longing to take on that work could bear no more restraint, so I said:

"Since neither of you will yield it to the other, then let me be the one to do it."

"You!" Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi both laughed.

"You know this is not a problem in philosophy," said Bai Ping.

"But I have stolen something from your own hand before."

"That was because I counted you a friend," said Bai Ping, "and because you know all my ways."

"And your arm is a little crippled now," Mei Yingzi added, by way of reason.

"But this is a thing that needs the fingers, not the arm." I felt Mei Yingzi's words an insult, and so I spoke very gravely.

"And that room, that air—you do not know them as we do," said Bai Ping.

"No," I said. "You have only to tell me, and shall I not know them at once?"

"And besides, this is work fit for a woman to do," said Mei Yingzi.

"Sewing and cooking are said to be women's work too," I said; "but the famous tailors and cooks of the world are still men."

"But none of them are philosophers," said Bai Ping.

The dispute went on a long while, and still no decision. On the one hand I felt I must, for once, play the leading part; in the second place, their calling me a philosopher, a bookish scholar, and a cripple all rankled in me, and at last I said:

"I am a man; and for a man, standing among you, to be spared the dangerous task is a disgrace! Besides, my life is a thing to spare—if this time your shot had gone a hair's breadth awry, should I not be dead already?"

"But my own life has had far more escapes than yours," said Bai Ping.

"This is not a question of the worth of a life," said Mei Yingzi; "it is a question of the work's efficiency. What we want is victory, not sacrifice!"

Mei Yingzi's words silenced Bai Ping and me, and then she went on:

"In this whole work we cannot so much as speak of failure. Such a failure would be no private affair, nor even the affair of us three. We may hold our own selves cheap; but standing on the ground of the work, to cherish the work is to cherish ourselves."

"Yes," I said. "Standing on the ground of the work, you are both more important than I, and so I…"

"No," said Bai Ping; "but beyond the work you have a philosophical life besides."

"I think this dispute will never end," said Mei Yingzi. "We had better settle it by drawing lots."

This put me in mind of the drawing of lots before our last trip to Hangzhou. But that time, though it was for pleasure, each heart was set against the others; now it was for work, and our hearts were in accord. Bai Ping was the first to approve, and I raised no objection either.

On Bai Ping's table stood an automatic cigarette-case, a little toy that flicked one out at a press; inside were State Express 555s, and she took a Lucky Strike from my packet on the table and drew one out and put it in. After shuffling them she said:

"Now each of us will press it once in turn. Whoever draws that Lucky Strike shall be the one, by rule, to take on this work."

It was a most amusing way of drawing lots. Mei Yingzi pressed one next, saw it was not the Lucky Strike, and lit it; the second was I; the third, Bai Ping. So it went round, and on the third round I pressed out that Lucky Strike after all. The cigarette had been mine to begin with, so that it should fall to me let the two of them raise no objection. We had settled the matter at last.

Time slipped quietly by, and our life was one of excitement, gladness, and strain. Each day I ate well and slept much, and my health came back day by day, like a boxer resting up beforehand. Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi were like my managers, forever watchful over my life; they felt that only with full spirits and a sound body could one be calm under strain and careful in peril.

It was during this time that Helen came back from Qingdao. Mei Yingzi was the first to know, and after she told me, the very next day I went to call on Helen.

I rang at her door, and it was Helen herself who opened it. She was not only much younger but plainly heartier; her skin seemed browner than before, which made her hair look yellower and her eyes bluer, and the freckles on the bridge of her nose seemed to have faded. Light and quick in body, in a nimble blue everyday dress, she welcomed me with a fresh smile innocent of any powder or paint.

Her mother seemed already to have told her the story of my wounding, and she said: "If this came of my not going to the ball that night, then it is wholly my fault; but it was not a thing I could have foreseen."

"If it came of my invitation—sending you off the sooner to Qingdao, where you met the encouragement of music, gained an open heart, and won back the health you had lost," I said, "then God has set my wound's price high enough already."

"Thank you," she said, and smiled shyly.

Then she talked with me of her life in Qingdao, of Stoyevsky, and brought out photographs taken there to show me: some of herself, some of Stoyevsky, and some of the two of them together. By the photographs Stoyevsky was a man of dashing spirit and a sense of humor, and beside Helen he made her look the younger and more childlike still. That Mei Yingzi should mean to draw such a child into a dangerous struggle made me shudder now to think of it; and I too, that last time, had come with a purpose of my own to invite her to the ball, so that a kind of shame rose in my heart, and I said:

"Helen, shall we go out for a walk? I hope I may take you to dinner."

Helen smiled, nodded, and gathered up the photographs, but left two behind—one of herself standing alone by the sea, one with Stoyevsky at the piano—and gave them to me, saying:

"Will you keep them?—in memory of my new life."

"Thank you." I put the photographs away, and she said:

"Let me leave a note for my mother." She wrote it quickly and said:

"Shall we go?"

"Yes." She took up the handbag from the table and walked out of the door; I followed behind, and watched her take her coat from the rack and throw it on, without so much as a glance at the mirror on the stand, and follow me out.

The Helen of now had neither her first melancholy nor her later affectation, and she had washed off, too, the fashion-chasing of the days before Qingdao; measured against the Helen of our first meeting, she seemed to have lost some shyness and gained in vigor. With her one felt this was not at all the Helen of before. She talked and laughed naturally, and walked in health. On the tram, her loose hair nestled against my neck, and two or three times the wind carried it to my cheek, and I felt again that very tenderness she had made me feel at our first meeting, and seemed for the first time to draw a happiness from it.

We saw the half-past-five film at the Cathay, and dined at chez Rovere; and at the table I began:

"Has the plan to go to Beiping won your mother's consent yet?"

"Yes," she said; "but it is only a consent of the reason. In her feelings she does not want me to leave her…"

"That is natural," I said. "Then how have you decided?"

"Of course I mean to go."

"And when?"

"She has told me to wait until after Easter."

"…" I said nothing. She said suddenly:

"You do not approve?"

"…" I smiled, and said: "I only hope you will not change your purpose."

"Set your mind at rest," she said. "Think—my mother has not seen me for so long; she wants me to stay a few more days…"

"Of course, that is all quite right," I said. "I hope that once you have fixed a day you will not change it again."

"So long as you keep encouraging me."

"Is my encouragement of any use to you?"

"If you would come with me to Beiping…" she asked, watching me.

"I?"

"Why should you not?" she said. "What meaning has Shanghai for you?"

"…" It was as though several answers came to my lips at once, and for a moment I could bring out none of them.

"Beiping, I am sure, would suit your study of philosophy better than Shanghai," she said.

"…" I nodded.

"Then what is it that holds you here? Did not Bai Ping urge you to leave Shanghai?"

"To leave Shanghai would mean going to the interior."

"If you go to the interior," she said, with a smile that might have been earnest or in jest, "I will go with you."

"You?"

"What of it?"

"You forget your music again!"

"If you will not go to the interior," she said, "then come with me to Beiping."

"After Easter."

"Truly?"

"Yes," I said. "Helen, I will go with you to Beiping."

At the time I did not know from what inspiration I said those words. Only later did I see in it a kind of weariness, below my own consciousness, with the work of that time, and a yearning, out of Helen's world, back toward a world of my own. Everyone, I think, has a world of his own, just as he has a native place: easy to leave, easy to forget, but when you grow weary and worn and old in some other world, you cannot help thinking that the quietest and sweetest thing of all is still your native place—which for Helen was music, and for me philosophy.

"Truly?" Helen grew excited then. "You are not joking with me?"

"Of course not." Helen fell silent, a radiant smile on her face, and put out her hand to grip mine hard, saying:

"Now you can no longer blame me for putting it off. I wait only for you to give me the day to start—and remember, if you do not go, then perhaps I shall not go either."

"I will make it as early as I can," I said. And at that moment I was clearly conscious in my heart that, once this work of mine with Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping had been carried to victory, I would surely go back, alone, into the world of my philosophy.

After dinner, in the cold air, I walked Helen home on foot. My heart was very open and light, and it set me thinking that, since Helen and I had become friends, the two of us had been together not a few times—from the days of her infatuation with philosophy, through the season of her turning to vanity, and then the season of her busy social round, down to her dejected awakening and our chance meeting before Stephen's grave—yet today was the first time we had been together in a mood so glad and serene, with no entanglement, no barrier, no knotty trouble stored up in the heart.

I saw Helen to the door of the Bakou Apartments, and at parting I remembered Mrs. Stephen's wish to see her, and told her of it earnestly. She said:

"Do you know what it is about?"

"Perhaps she only wishes to see you," I said. "She too is much in favor of your going to Beiping."

"Now I shall tell her that you are going with me," she said, smiling.

"Till another day," I said, shaking her hand.

"Won't you come in and sit awhile?" She gave me her hand.

"No. I'll come to see you again in a day or two."

"Then goodbye," she said. "I am always waiting for you to come."
