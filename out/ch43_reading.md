## Editors' Postscript

Comrade Chen Geng once said to my second elder brother, "Mulan's life is a reflection of the twists and turns of the Chinese revolution's development." For the revolution she rose up to resist an arranged marriage, and then was parted by life and death from three later husbands; three times arrested, she remained steadfast and unyielding. Under the White Terror she was brave and quick-witted, rescuing comrades and protecting leaders. She cleverly used her standing as a banker to raise funds for the underground party, brilliantly carrying out arduous tasks beyond what ordinary people could imagine. After the founding of the People's Republic of China, Rao Shushi rudely refused to recognize her Party membership and standing; she resolved to begin again from grassroots work, and then had the misfortune to be implicated in the unjust case of Pan Hannian, losing her freedom for seventeen years in all. Yet she never once complained or regretted, and her Communist faith never wavered.

Her idealism is the greatest spiritual treasure of us, her children.

From 1963, when she first came home from Qincheng, she petitioned ceaselessly, including writing autobiographical materials many times. After her rehabilitation, her old comrades-in-arms — Mao Dun, Yuan Puzhi, and others — repeatedly urged her to write an autobiography and memoirs. She held that her own work had been part of the Party's underground work, bound by discipline and not fit for public release; and for the sake of the safety of our father and of some old friends of many years' cooperation, it was indeed not suitable at the time to make it public. But she also held that to set down truthfully the revolutionary years she had lived through was a duty she could not shirk. So she related fragments here and there, and some journalists wrote reports on that basis, but these articles often carried the writers' subjective guesses and were not wholly accurate. Only after the dear old lady settled in Hangzhou in 1993, as times and circumstances changed, did she resolve to set about writing her autobiography herself. Each day she dictated one cassette tape, and after she had finished speaking, work-study students from Hangzhou University transcribed the recordings into text. In 1996 she asked Comrade Ye Bingnan, former deputy director of the Committee on Literary and Historical Materials of the Zhejiang People's Political Consultative Conference, to begin editing on the basis of that proofread transcript. In the course of this she was hospitalized many times, and Ye's own work was heavy, so progress was slow; and the old lady, dissatisfied with her own transcript, set about rewriting it. As Ye Bingnan put it: "The life the venerable Huang recalls rose and fell with great dramatic turns, rich in the stuff of legend, spanning nearly a century in time, its cast touching many well-known figures from every walk of society, and the relations among them intricate and complex." Only in the summer of 2000 was the first draft completed. Afterward our elder cousin Qian Lin, former Party group secretary of the Anhui Daily, worked it into a second draft. After Yunzhong's daughter Hongshen read the second draft carefully, she suggested that, for the sake of young readers, some introduction of the people and of the background of the times should be added. We two sisters, having consulted materials on some of Mother's contemporaries and accepted the young people's advice, added a little text describing the background of the times, and so formed the present third draft. The whole book has the names of some four hundred and more people with whom she had working relations; it was impossible to annotate them all, and since she wrote only of her contacts with these people through work, it was impossible to give a full account of each one's life.

The Party's underground work was carried on in perilous conditions, and much of it through single-line contact; what our mother has recorded is what she herself experienced, and cannot be complete. For example, the book mentions many friends who cooperated during the War of Resistance; some of them, like her, had hidden their true identities and acted openly as people who had left the Party or under other identities, without one another's knowledge, and such things have only come to light gradually in recent years. Or again, Mr. Guo Moruo, in his *Song of the Great Waves*, writes that he already knew the date his ship would leave Shanghai but could not tell Yu Liqun; and that he met He Xiangning and Zou Taofen aboard the liner Empress, calling it a chance encounter. How could there have been so many "chance encounters"? Even those who lived through them did not necessarily know how the Party arranged them, how many people were specifically in charge, or by what means they were accomplished. And so, in their article commemorating Comrade Liu Shaowen, "A Nameless Hero, a Model for a Thousand Ages," Comrades Luo Qingchang and Chai Chengwen write: it was precisely because of the selfless devotion of a great host of nameless heroes that the Party's underground work could achieve such results. The Monument to the Nameless Heroes standing in Tiananmen Square is a tribute to, and a remembrance of, all the soldiers amid the fires of war and on the invisible front.

We know that this third draft is still imperfect: for one, we sisters are not equal to the task; for a second, our mother is already at the great age of ninety-seven. We hope that the publication of this book will draw readers' attention and bring us more supplementary material. If possible, we will take it in and incorporate it into a revised edition, as a gift for our mother's hundredth birthday.

We are deeply grateful to Uncle Mei Yi for his support of Mother. It was precisely with the support and encouragement of that generation that Mother, with great willpower, overcame illness and completed her autobiography. Uncle Mei Yi also gave special attention to the book's publication, doing his utmost to arrange for the Encyclopedia of China Publishing House to take on the editing and publishing, personally recommending an editor to us and agreeing that, after the press's second reading, he would read it through once more and write a preface for the book. Unexpectedly, this September Uncle Mei suddenly left us; here, with reverence and gratitude, we offer our deep mourning.

We thank the editor in charge, Liang Yunfu, and the art editor Tong Xingkan, whose careful sorting and editing of the original manuscript of more than four hundred thousand characters and more than a hundred photographs were a vital link in ensuring the quality of the book's publication.

We also thank the General Office of the Zhejiang People's Political Consultative Conference for its support; Comrade Shen Yingying typed up the first draft of the whole book in her spare time, and this was the basis for our repeated revisions. Many others, too, supported the publication and distribution of this book in various ways, and to all of them we here express our deep thanks.

Chen Yunzhong and Chen Dazhong

20 December 2003
