## Afterword: Closing Remarks

Leaving aside how well or ill "this book" has been written—for my own part, after ten years from first to last, I have, all in all, and according to my original conception, finished writing these five books. Though the whole of the matter is not yet brought to a close, here it must nonetheless stop. (Editor's note: what the author calls "this book" comprises the five books: 1, Rooting Out Traitors in the North; 2, The Full Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi; 3, Behind-the-Lines Operations at Shanghai in the War of Resistance against Japan; 4, Counter-Agent Work in the Latter Period of the War of Resistance; and 5, Pacification of the Beiping-Tianjin Region.)

To speak truly, the writer himself is not satisfied with this book. I should have liked to take the original material, add to it and cut from it, and write the whole through once more; but my strength is no longer equal to my will.

In the "Prefatory Candour" at the head of the whole book, the writer once said: "I do not wish to write a memoir." What I meant was this: that this book is not the writer's personal "autobiography"; the place I occupy in it can serve only as the nail that stitches the book together, the thread that runs it through. It is precisely for this reason that the book dares to bear the name "Nameless Heroes"—which is to say, the writer is unworthy to be a hero, while the true heroes are, for their part, nameless.

The past several decades were an age of great upheaval, and above all we lived through the "Eight-Year War of Resistance" and the "all-out Suppression of Rebellion." In that time there were many deeds worth the setting down; and those I know most intimately lean toward the loyal courage and sacrifice of my comrades in the work, and their contribution to the nation and the race.

Deeds should be the outward showing of a man's proper conduct; and those that carry some measure of worth may also be called historical material. The greatest distinction of this book lies in this: that it gives a somewhat vivid account of "secret-agent activity" or "intelligence activity," which has been very seldom made public.

In times past, books that set down "secret-agent activity" or "intelligence activity" at any length were very rarely to be seen—indeed one may as well say there were none at all. The reason is this: apart from the necessary "keeping of secrets," it is, I suppose, that of the men who do this kind of work, very few are able to enjoy their remaining years in peace and are also willing to write it out and make it public. The writer, though without talent, has yet been granted this one piece of luck; and it is with this that I have made good my want of skill, and so been able to make the attempt.

Before the first book, Rooting Out Traitors in the North, had yet appeared, the authorities in charge of intelligence and public order had real misgivings as to what, in the end, the contents of this book might hold, and by roundabout means sought once to dissuade me. But once it had come out in Biographical Literature, apart from feeling a slight displeasure at a phrase or two in the "Prefatory Candour," they set their minds much at ease. Thereafter the second, third, fourth, and fifth books were serialized one after another, over a span of fully seven years, and never once was there any interference. This one thing alone is proof enough that the measure of our political enlightenment has come a considerable way. In earlier years, it could by no means have been done.

The book has yet another feature: that here and there I have woven in some of the folk customs of this or that region of the Chinese mainland. Though on the face of it these bear no connection to the matter of the book, the writer holds that there is in them still a savor of "history"—for they are "old," they are "traditional," and they are the things men will forever "hold in fond remembrance." If I did not take this chance to make some mention of them, I fear they would little by little sink away into oblivion.

When it comes to publishing one's writing openly over a long stretch of time, besides the many conditions that must be met, one needs as well a fixed plot of ground; and for this I must thank Mr. Liu Shaotang of Biographical Literature for the strong support he has given me.

I would not, of course, unduly belittle myself; yet neither may I do violence to good letters. The truth is that the writer's store of learning is not wide, and in the text there could hardly fail to appear such faults as miswritten characters, wrong characters, misused set phrases, and ill-matched, unbecoming comparisons; and had it not been for the sharp eye of Brother Liu Yuanshen, who proofread the whole book and set these right as they came, no end of laughing-stock would have sprung from it.

For all five books of the whole work, I troubled my old workmate Brother Luo Jing to write the cover and inscribe the title-page of each. This was not only because his hand is fine—full of practiced strength and of beauty—but carried a larger meaning: that in the years of the War of Resistance, in the indiscriminate bombing by the Japanese army, Brother Luo Jing was gravely wounded and left crippled in body, so that it stands, too, as a remembrance of the pain that wounding left behind.

To help me bring this book to completion, friends and colleagues furnished me with much precious material bearing upon it—whether told me face to face or shown me in writing—and to all of them I here respectfully render my thanks.

From the time the first part, Rooting Out Traitors in the North, appeared in Biographical Literature, readers have written without cease to instruct and encourage me, or to put questions and inquiries; and if I have not been able to answer each and every one, it is truly because I am old and my mind will not hold to one thing—a matter that floats up into the mind today sinks tomorrow back into the depths of memory. Though I did jot them down on slips of paper, I fell into the fault of laying things down heedlessly wherever I chanced to be, so that when I wished to find them again I could not. Beyond this there is scarcely any other reason; for which I here own my fault and beg your pardon.

The writer has done "intelligence work" his whole life long; steeped in it by ear and eye, at it morning and evening, I came, as a matter of course, to have my own understanding of it. Add to this the on-the-ground experience piled up over several decades, and though I fall short of an expert, I have at least become an old hand. On the strength of this one qualification, and in a wholly objective spirit, to raise a few substantive questions bearing upon "national security and intelligence activity," and thence to take stock of the past and look ahead to what is to come—this, surely, is no empty and idle talk.

Yet here I can only set out, for a beginning, a few rough opinions; as for putting them in good order and system, that must wait upon the instruction of wiser heads.

The intelligence organizations and intelligence activity that history, Chinese and foreign, has recorded, have existed for some thousands of years already; yet in every case they were the tools by which a few ambitious men and rulers won an empire and kept a realm, and were not necessarily of any good to the nation or to the people. Coming to modern times, everything has advanced—even the way men talk is more pleasing to the ear than before—and so the opportunist and the trickster, too, will often tie intelligence activity to the national interest, and make a banner of it.

In truth, of these things of every sort and kind, the greater part are used as means to some private design of their own; and from the national interest they are not merely far out of reach, but run flat counter to it. Down to the present, of all the intelligence activity in the world—forever hitting on new tricks, in who knows how many patterns—the writer still cannot cite so much as a single instance that truly binds itself to the national interest. On this one point alone, let no one set a superstitious faith in any so-called "democratically advanced" nation: hold it up to the searching glass, and its ugliness stands wholly revealed, its features hideous in the extreme. This is no idle word; the writer has seen it with his own eyes.

In our own country this is a question never yet openly discussed; and those in power, I should guess, do not necessarily see it as a question at all. In fact the question has been with us long, and may well now be brought out for all to talk over. The writer's own opinion is this: that at present, under the premise that "national security stands above all," a policy ought to be laid down, in the two matters of "intelligence activity" and "public order and security protection," to reform them on a sweeping scale. What that policy contains is very simple; it is this—that the national-security departments be brought within the administrative system.

That the intelligence organization be made one and unified.

That the security-protection system be regularized.

That no party, body, or combination whatever be permitted to use it as a tool of political strife.

That all its workings and activities be carried on openly and aboveboard; and that, in spirit, they must uphold the moral norms and the cultural tradition of the Chinese nation. So far as the writer knows, the regulations and the like now in force in our country, though at some distance from the principles I have here conceived, are yet not too far off; a little altering, a little opening up, and it would be near enough. What matters, further, is the setting-right of our notions: it should be held settled that to safeguard national security is a thing as fixed as the way of heaven and earth, with nothing "special" about it whatever—and the so-called "secret-agent work" of former days, let it be cast off! Then there is the making-general of its practice: military affairs and diplomacy are important, to be sure, but there is no branch of administration besides that does not bear upon it; and so one ought to have a whole and rounded understanding of the thing.

Watching coolly from the side, I think that those in power may even now be quietly moving in this very direction; or, if not, then—government issuing from many quarters, and things being ranked by what is weighty or slight, urgent or slow—it may be that for the moment they have not the leisure to come to this. There is yet another possibility: that though they are alive to it, they still have certain misgivings of a technical kind. Say what one will, the writer's view is this: it is the trend of the times, and sooner or later it must be done!

Be it as it may whether the intelligence system that bears on national security has already been reformed or no, looking out from the present state of things toward the foreseeable future, it must in any case be not wholly the same as the past. So far as my narrow view reaches, let me try to set the matter forth as follows. The idea of safeguarding national security will pass from the complex to the simple, and grow ever more single. Within: to secure the stability of society, and prevent upheaval from arising. Without: to understand others, and protect oneself—and it is for the protecting of oneself that one comes to understand others. The "others" here meant are not necessarily the enemy.

The word "secret agent," abused by men in the past and carrying a note of scorn, all have now cast aside and left unused; at present it seems to have turned into a byname, in political struggle, for charging one's opponent with stopping at nothing. And so "secret agent" and "intelligence worker" have plainly come to be distinguished, and are no longer taken as words of one meaning.

The air of mystery about intelligence activity, too, grows thinner and thinner. That it was once made so mysterious ought to be laid to the charge of deliberate mystification. Hereafter, it is my contention that within an intelligence office, save only for the drawers where documents are kept, which may not be opened at anyone's will, all the rest might well be thrown open to view.

Within our own country, in the history of intelligence activity, of Mr. Dai Yunong (Li), who has been cast into an idol's figure, there will be no second hereafter. I do not here speak of his merits or his faults; a figure so wrapped in legend as that could only have been brought forth amid the tangled circumstances of that age. In a country running on its proper rails, how should such a thing ever come to be again?

To engage in the guarding of national security is a public duty like engaging in any other work; there is no need to make much of its sacredness, nor ought one to sneer at it as something furtive. Let it be carried out with an upright and proper bearing, and a frank and open breast, and that is enough. To judge by the way things stand at present, this state will very soon be reached.

At present there is a certain region of the world where intelligence activity that stops at nothing is still much in vogue; and this not only lowers the worth of the intelligence worker, but does harm besides to the good name of legitimate activity. It has to do with the degree of culture and the standard of morals of a nation or a people, and does not necessarily stand in direct proportion to democracy or to wealth and strength. The writer has come to understand, too, that the spirit of revenge bred out of clashes of religious faith and the inherited hatreds of race is likewise a weighty factor in it. Our own country has its own foundation as a state, and a deep and excellent tradition; and in the intelligence activity of its foreign relations it will be a nation that keeps most strictly to its proper part. Where interests are touched, our country has yet never had any ambition of aggression abroad.

Hereafter, under no circumstances whatever, will the work of sanction that takes an individual for the object of action ever occur again. Looking back over what is past, it was stopped and carried out no more as early as after the victory in the War of Resistance. To speak of the matter plainly: where a person offends and errs, the government may of course bring him to book by law; and this measure of sanction outside the law is by no means necessary. And all the more since history makes plain that this is not the best way to the solving of a problem, and runs counter besides to the spirit of the open and aboveboard, of the great, the central, and the utterly upright. Yet the thing called "assassination," in the private dealings of man with man, is, I fear, still not wholly to be avoided.

Last of all, the writer looks forward, in all sincerity of heart, to this: that once our country has brought the national-security departments within the administrative system, made its intelligence organization one and unified, and regularized its security-protection system, and has laid firm hold of the spirit of the great, the central, and the utterly upright, the prospect before us must surely be one flood of light.
