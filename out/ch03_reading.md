## Intelligence Chief "Wang Yong" — Chen Geng

### Chen Geng's Legendary Career

Section Two of the Central Special Section was the intelligence branch, set up to gather intelligence, keep track of the enemy, and take the offensive against the enemy's own detective organs. In a Shanghai held down by the imperialists and by the Kuomintang's army, police, gendarmes, and secret services, there was no protecting yourself without going on the attack. The most important side of the Special Section's intelligence work lay in two methods—planting agents inside the enemy and winning enemy people over—to reach deep into his vital departments and break up his schemes in time.

The intelligence branch was headed, under the alias "Wang Yong," by Chen Geng—later a general who would storm across the revolutionary battlefields and win fame at home and abroad. His military life had begun as a private, second class, in a warlord's army.

Chen Geng, styled Shukang, was born in 1903 in Xiangxiang County, Hunan. As a boy he listened to the many battle stories his grandfather told, a man who had soldiered in his own youth; over time they left the lively, restless Chen Geng "forever thinking of throwing over his books for the army." In the end, with the marriage his parents had arranged for him as the spark, he ran away from home.

In the winter of 1916, at thirteen, Chen Geng left home—wanting to live on his own and set on making something of himself—and became a private, second class, in Lu Diping's Hunan Army.

As army life wore on, the darkness of the warlord units slowly broke the boy's simple dream. In 1921 Chen Geng at last threw down his bolt-action rifle and found a clerk's post with the Hunan bureau of the Canton–Hankou Railway in Changsha.

Earning his living, he also studied at a supplementary school and a night school and joined progressive mass bodies such as the Youth Salvation Association, throwing himself into anti-imperialist, national-salvation work. In this time, guided by He Shuheng and others, he joined the Chinese Communist Party in December 1922. On the Party organization's decision, at the end of the following year Chen Geng secretly sat the examinations for the Army Military Academy that Sun Yat-sen had founded in Guangzhou. In May 1924 he passed into the Whampoa Military Academy, in its first class.

Seven months later, on his fine soldierly qualities, Chen Geng graduated and was kept on at the academy as a company commander over the second class of cadets and deputy squad leader of the third regular class. Then, in the first Eastern Expedition against Chen Jiongming, in putting down the mutiny of Yang Ximin's Yunnan troops and Liu Zhenhuan's Guangxi troops, in supporting the Canton–Hong Kong strike, and in hunting the assassins of Liao Zhongkai, he showed a wit and courage beyond the common run.

On October 1, 1925, the Guangdong Revolutionary Army launched its second Eastern Expedition. Chiang Kai-shek was commander-in-chief of the expedition and commander of the First Army; Zhou Enlai was head of its General Political Department and Party representative of the First Division of the First Army; and Chen Geng was a company commander in the Fourth Regiment of the Second Division. In the fight to take Huizhou, Chen Geng charged at the head of his men; when an enemy bullet struck his left foot, he pulled out the slug and fought on, leading his unit to be the first onto the wall. His company was then moved to the expedition's headquarters as its guard.

On October 21, Zhou Enlai and the division commander He Yingqin led the First Division in taking Haifeng. But the Third Division, moving near headquarters, pushed rashly toward Huayang on the 27th, ran into the enemy, and broke in full retreat. Chiang Kai-shek hurried up to oversee the fighting, saw at a glance that things were going badly, and quickly sent Chen Geng to the front to carry an order to the Third Division's commander, Tan Shuqing—"no retreat"—and to have Tan shoot anyone who fled before the enemy. Seeing Chiang's order, the division tried to counterattack, and failed again. By the time Chiang reached the place, one flank blow from the enemy had shattered Tan's division along the whole line. Chiang told Chen Geng to take over as acting commander of the Third Division and go gather the scattered men. But it was too late; the situation was past saving. "When an army breaks, it breaks like a landslide"—the division's own officers were turning to flee, the enemy pressing closer step by step, until even the men and horses of headquarters scattered every which way. Chiang, only just hailed at Huizhou as "commander-in-chief," swaggering and beyond all restraint, now felt his dignity slipping. As things grew more and more desperate, he struck a pose and cried: "I must die a martyr's death here—I have no face to go back and meet the elders of Jiangdong." Chen Geng, watching this playacting, found it faintly comic, yet still soothed him: "Because you are the commander-in-chief, what you do bears on the whole campaign. All that's lost this time is a single division. Get away from here quickly, and we can still put the troops in order and come back to fight." Coaxed and pleaded with by Chen Geng, and seeing a way to climb down, Chiang gave up the act. With the enemy closing in, his nerve failed and his legs went weak, until he could not walk at all. Chen Geng half-dragged, half-carried him at a run for several li, all the way to a riverbank, got him onto a boat, and only then were they out of danger.

At first Chiang Kai-shek did not know Chen Geng was a Communist. Deeply grateful for having his life saved, and using it to draw the young man closer, he made Chen Geng his personal staff officer, free to come and go from his quarters. After the expedition took Shantou, headquarters was set up at the Qilu villa; Chiang lived on the first floor, Chen Geng and Zhou Enlai on the second. One day Chen Geng found on Chiang's desk a register of Whampoa cadets and officers at every level; opening it, he saw a red circle above every Communist's name, and beside his own an annotation: "This man is a Communist; he must not be given troops to command." Chen Geng at once reported it to Zhou Enlai and asked what to do. Zhou Enlai said: "Write him a note resigning your post, and see how he handles it." The next day Chen Geng took leave on the pretext that his mother was ill and went home. Chiang, having read the note, asked slyly: "That's not the real reason, is it? You've seen something of mine, haven't you?" Chen Geng made no sound. After a night had passed, Chiang sent over a boat ticket, travel money, and a "commission" appointing Chen Geng a lieutenant-colonel squad leader at the Army Officers' Academy; and Chen Geng left Shantou at once and returned to Guangzhou.

In May 1926 Chiang Kai-shek put his "Party Affairs Reorganization Resolution" to the Second Plenum of the Second Central Committee of the Kuomintang, forcing the Communists among Whampoa's Kuomintang members out of the Communist Party. Even the opportunists within the Communist Party went so far as to urge Communists to withdraw. At just this moment Chen Geng, without a moment's wavering, made his Communist membership public and declared that he was quitting the Kuomintang.

In September 1926, on Zhou Enlai's arrangement, Chen Geng slipped out of Guangzhou to report to the Party Central in Shanghai. Chen Duxiu received him and told him the center had decided to send him, Gu Shunzhang, and Lu Liu—three men—to the Soviet Union to study its experience in political-security work. Li Qiang, then working in the Party Central's organs, found a sampan and took Chen Geng, Gu Shunzhang, and Lu Liu aboard, rowing them out to a Soviet ship. From Shanghai they sailed to Vladivostok, and among the Red Army they studied political-security work and the experience of armed mass insurrection. By the time he returned to Shanghai in February 1927, the Northern Expeditionary Army had already taken the Yangtze valley. In March he went to Nanchang, just as Chiang Kai-shek was beginning to betray the revolution, savaging the revolutionary forces and hunting down and killing Communists. He turned then to Wuhan, where the Party organization made him commander of the special-service battalion in Tang Shengzhi's Second Front Army of the Northern Expedition; and that April he attended the Fifth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party, held in Wuhan. After Chiang Kai-shek and Wang Jingwei openly turned traitor, Chen Duxiu's Right-opportunist line forced Chen Geng to give up the special-service battalion. Chen Geng left the unit alone, fell back to Wuchang, and went in secret by boat with Zhou Enlai to Jiujiang, then on to Nanchang. On August 1 he took part in the Nanchang Uprising. When the insurgent army withdrew from Nanchang, he served in the Third Division of He Long's Twentieth Army and marched south with it. On August 24, in fighting against reactionary troops in the Huichang area, Chen Geng was gravely wounded: three bullets took his left leg, breaking the shinbone and fibula clean through. His aide Lu Dongsheng then went with him by way of Changting and, moving painfully, they reached Chaozhou and Shantou. After the insurgent army's defeat they boarded a Butterfield & Swire steamer and reached Hong Kong.

This was the first boat out of Chaozhou to Hong Kong after our army's defeat there, and many of our comrades were aboard it. On reaching Hong Kong the checks were especially strict; the other comrades slipped away quickly once ashore, but Chen Geng, badly wounded, stayed on the boat, and only by spinning a "story" in his broken Cantonese was he let through. Ashore, he could not find a single familiar face, and he and Lu Dongsheng sat by the roadside, where the constables drove them off with clubs and would not let them linger in the street. They put up at an inn for a while, but so many people came crowding to gawk at the "Red Army man who had fled from Shantou" that, afraid the story he had told the inspector would not hold, he and Lu Dongsheng hurried out. Lu Dongsheng supported him along the pavement, but they could find no place to rest. They went to a hospital, and the hospital would not take them. To pass some time somewhere before trying to find people he knew, they took a number at a Chinese doctor's. When they had sat and talked a while, the doctor realized Chen Geng's injury came from fighting and turned him out too. Out on the road, the constables drove them off again with clubs. There was a public latrine at the roadside, so he had Lu Dongsheng carry him in to sit for half an hour's rest. But he was very hungry, and looking up he saw a Western restaurant not far off and joked to Lu Dongsheng that a Western meal would be just the thing. Honest soul that he was, Lu Dongsheng went and fetched one. The waiter followed him back to the latrine, gave them no food, and cursed them for their trouble. Chen Geng had twenty yuan on him and wanted to go to Shanghai; he had Lu Dongsheng ask around, and it happened there was a boat for Shanghai an hour later. On the motor-launch the crew, seeing his wound was from fighting, gouged five yuan out of him. Once aboard the steamer they made trouble again, and it was thanks to a worker who stepped in that the fix was smoothed over; the worker found a camp-bed, and he and Lu Dongsheng carried Chen Geng into the hold.

When the steamer left Hong Kong, it had to detour and put in at Shantou to take on passengers and cargo. The very boat Chen Geng was on turned out to be the first from Hong Kong to Shantou after our army's defeat. Shantou was still under martial law, a place seething with menace under the reactionaries' rule. Having only just escaped and now doubled back here, he was very tense. No sooner had the boat docked than many comrades of the insurgent army came aboard. Among them was the Third Division's commander, Zhou Yiqun, who had been taken prisoner by Kuomintang troops, gone unrecognized, and escaped; a charitable body in Shantou had given him a boat ticket. He came aboard, a tattered mat tucked under his arm, found his way to the hold, saw only one man there, and—heedless of everything—furtively lay down and fell asleep. Chen Geng was there reading a newspaper, and, delighted to see him come in, played a joke on him: hiding his face behind the paper, he said, "The news in this paper is quick indeed—Zhou Yiqun hadn't even boarded, and here it is in print already." It gave Zhou Yiqun a start; he pricked up his ears, placed the voice at once as Chen Geng's, and the two had a good laugh. Later they went together to Shanghai and found the Party.

### Trouble at the Niu Huilin Hospital

Chen Geng, Zhou Yiqun, and Lu Dongsheng left Shantou on the same boat and, through countless dangers, reached Shanghai. It was now mid-October 1927. That they found the Party in Shanghai was owing to the help of Chen Geng's wife, Wang Genying. Wang Genying was born in 1906 into a poor peasant family on the outskirts of Shanghai, and at eleven went to work as a child laborer in the Japanese-owned Hengfeng Cotton Mill (and later moved to the British-owned Ewo Mill). She joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1925 and was an active figure in the May Thirtieth Movement. In the mill she long led the women workers in struggle and became a mass leader whom women workers inside the mill and out held dear. She took an active part in all three of the armed uprisings the Shanghai workers mounted between October 1926 and March 1927. In March 1927, after the third armed uprising succeeded and the Provisional Government of the Shanghai Special Municipality was proclaimed, she was elected a member of the city's people's council. After Chiang Kai-shek launched the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup, Wang Genying was hunted by Kuomintang detectives and concession constables and forced underground. That April, as a delegate of the Shanghai Party, she went to Wuhan for the Fifth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party. She had already come to know Chen Geng in Shanghai; the two had long admired one another, and they married in Hankou. After the Fifth Congress, as a worker delegate sent by Shanghai, she attended the Fourth National Labor Congress held in Hankou in June; and while in Hankou, as a representative of the Chinese working class, she attended the Pacific Labor Conference. That July, Wang Genying returned to Shanghai for secret work and served as a courier in the Party Central. After parting from Chen Geng in Wuhan, she had waited constantly for word of him. When she read in the newspaper of the Nanchang Uprising of August 1, she was overjoyed; soon afterward came word that the insurgent forces had met defeat on their march south to Chaozhou and Shantou, and her worry and longing grew stronger by the day. Now, suddenly seeing Chen Geng come back to Shanghai dragging his gravely wounded leg, through every kind of danger, she was overjoyed, settled him at home, cared for him with all her heart, and at once helped him make contact with the organization. Many years later, on May 24, 1983, Li Qiang still said of it: "Wang Genying was brave in her underground work in Shanghai. Without her, Chen Geng would not have found the Party so quickly."

Knowing Chen Geng's wound was serious, the Party organization contrived to get him into the Niu Huilin Orthopedic Hospital, at the corner of Avenue Edward VII (now Central Yan'an Road) and Chengdu Road, for treatment. Niu Huilin had studied in England and was a fine orthopedic surgeon of progressive leanings. Because his skill was superb, he had a great name in Shanghai, and many Kuomintang "grandees of the party-state" and rich men came to him for treatment, so that senior Kuomintang officers were forever coming and going. When Chen Geng first went in, Dr. Niu Huilin suspected he was a robber, thinking he had hurt his leg in the course of a burglary, and was not very willing to treat him. Repeated explanations could not dispel the doctor's doubts. Seeing that the doctor was an upright, honest man, Chen Geng judged it better to show his true face than to be taken for a robber, and told him the truth. Hearing Chen Geng out, the doctor was deeply moved and became most friendly. He asked Chen Geng why he had not said so earlier—for the Niu brothers were first cousins of Song Qingling's, had always sympathized with the revolution, and had wholeheartedly supported her revolutionary work. Song Qingling was not in Shanghai just then—she had left for the Soviet Union late that August and was in Moscow—but Dr. Niu Huilin knew that she had always cherished Whampoa's progressive teachers and students, and that she was a member, and a member of the presidium, of the Revolutionary Committee of the Chinese Kuomintang formed at the time of the Nanchang Uprising. Learning that Chen Geng was a man wounded in the uprising who had come to Shanghai for treatment through every hardship, he judged that he ought to give him his fullest care. From then on Dr. Niu Huilin warmed to Chen Geng all the more, resolved to cure his leg, and treated him with great seriousness. The two became close friends.

Chen Geng's left leg, though set on the march, had been set crooked, and after more than two months of wandering and hard traveling the wound had grown worse. By the state of it, the usual course would have been to amputate and be done; but Chen Geng flatly refused amputation. Dr. Niu Huilin met his wish, set the leg afresh, and saved this leg that had been wounded in so many places. Had it not been for the advanced facilities of Shanghai and Niu Huilin's superb skill, the thing would truly have been past imagining. Chen Geng was deeply grateful to Niu Huilin, and long afterward, whenever he spoke of them, his praise never ran dry.

When Chen Geng first went into the hospital, Lu Dongsheng went on caring for him. In early November, Zhou Enlai also came to Shanghai. Then He Long and others came by way of Hong Kong as well, and Zhou Enlai sent Lu Dongsheng to bring He Long to the Party Central's organs. The center had originally decided to send He Long, together with Liu Bocheng and Lin Boqu, to the Soviet Union, fixed for departure on November 17; but on the 16th the organs were suddenly broken up, the enemy already knew He Long had reached Shanghai, and they were hunting him everywhere. He Long missed the sailing and could not get away. Later he talked it over with Zhou Yiqun and resolved to go back to western Hunan-Hubei to rouse the masses, organize armed struggle, and build a revolutionary base. Zhou Yiqun wholly agreed with He Long's view and was willing to go with him to western Hunan-Hubei, and he reported the matter to Zhou Enlai. The center soon approved their request. In the middle of January the next year, Zhou Enlai again sent Lu Dongsheng to escort He Long and Zhou Yiqun out of Shanghai and off to western Hunan-Hubei. Tempered in the revolutionary war and helped along by Chen Geng's teaching, Lu Dongsheng had taken up the Communist Party's political stand, and his outlook had made a further leap. On December 7, 1927, in Shanghai, he joined the Chinese Communist Party, sponsored by Chen Geng and Wang Genying. Lu Dongsheng was loyal and true to the revolution, honest and forthright by nature, and warmly attentive to his comrades; and Chen Geng carried always in his heart, and could not forget, the whole-hearted care Lu Dongsheng had given him along the road since the Nanchang Uprising. Whenever Chen Geng spoke of the shared trials of the two of them, he was deeply moved. All through Chen Geng's stay in the hospital, the Party organization showed him every care, and Zhou Enlai often sent people to visit; Li Qiang came most often of all. Xu Yixin, after escaping the peril of Chaozhou and Shantou, came to Shanghai by way of Hong Kong a little later, and when he heard that Chen Geng lay wounded in the hospital, he too often came to visit and talk.

Soon after his operation Chen Geng discovered that quite a few officers who had graduated from Whampoa were staying in the hospital; the head men among them in Shanghai even came to visit them there. Afraid of being recognized, Chen Geng hid in his ward and kept out of sight. But just as his leg was nearly healed, a Kuomintang regimental commander happened to move into the hospital—into the very ward next to Chen Geng's. This man too was a Whampoa graduate and knew Chen Geng. One day his groom recognized Chen Geng, and then the man himself came over to chat, asking how his leg had come to be so badly hurt. He did not believe the "story" Chen Geng put together on the spot, and pressed him with many questions. Chen Geng knew the man was reactionary in his thinking and sensed the danger in staying on in the hospital. The moment the man was gone, Chen Geng decided on the instant: with no time even to say a word to Dr. Niu, he had a comrade who was also staying in the hospital carry him quickly away. A few days later Dr. Niu Huilin met Chen Geng on the road, at once stopped his car, sprang out, gripped Chen Geng's hand tight, and asked what had happened. When Chen Geng told him why he had fled the hospital, Dr. Niu Huilin said, with a most regretful air: "I never dreamed of it! If only I had found you a better room earlier."

### The Ever-Present "Mr. Wang"

It was while Chen Geng lay treating his wound in the Niu Huilin Hospital that, on Zhou Enlai's proposal, the Party Central was already planning in Shanghai to form the Central Special Section, to safeguard its own security. Chen Geng came out of Niu Huilin's hospital, convalesced at home for a time, and in April 1928, his wounded leg not yet fully healed and his walk still a limp, joined the work of the Central Special Section in Shanghai in keeping with the Party Central's decision.

In a Shanghai shrouded in a heavy White Terror, grasping the enemy's movements accurately and promptly was of the utmost importance to the safety of the Party's organs and to the sound conduct of every struggle. To strengthen the new work of taking the offensive against the enemy's detective organs, the Central Special Section set up its intelligence branch (Section Two) in April 1928. From the very first it appointed Chen Geng its chief. The branch's work was carried on under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership.

Chen Geng took up the post of the Special Section's intelligence chief, and at the same time the Party organization made him deputy to Gu Shunzhang, who directed the Special Section's daily work; whenever Gu Shunzhang was away, Chen Geng acted in his place (later, when Gu Shunzhang was transferred out of the Special Section, Chen Geng came to direct its daily work under Zhou Enlai's immediate leadership). From that time on, Chen Geng struggled day and night in Shanghai against Kuomintang agents and Party traitors.

Through the joint efforts of Chen Geng and those who worked in the intelligence branch at one time or another—Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di, Chen Shouchang, Chen Yangshan, Liu Ding, Ke Lin, Ouyang Xin, and others—and under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership, the Central Special Section's intelligence work opened up quickly. Soon it had built secret-work stations throughout Shanghai and the Party's various areas of activity; it changed the way the Party operated in the White areas; it established and firmed up the system and discipline of secret work; at the same time it picked out fine Communists to go deep into the tiger's den, plant themselves in the enemy's confidential departments, and win intelligence straight from the enemy's hands; and it built counter-espionage assets inside the enemy's specialized anti-Communist organs, and so opened the way to penetrating the enemy's vital departments and controlling Shanghai's detective agencies.

As Chen Geng and the other Special Section comrades steadily widened their gains from ground already won, everywhere—in the Kuomintang's secret services, police stations, garrison commands, gendarmerie, and party offices, and among the gangs, local bullies, and hoodlums of society, and the plainclothes men and constables of the concessions—there were our own people, or sympathizers, or connections we could turn to account, ready at any moment to furnish the Party with intelligence of great timeliness and value on political, military, economic, and international matters. With so many connections, our Party was not only well-informed but often able to do things scarcely to be imagined. Once a comrade lost, unawares, an important document from his person on a tram; using a connection among the hoodlums to hunt for it, he had the document back within two days. In this period, through this web of connections, Chen Geng brought off many tasks the Party Central set him, some of them superbly. This owed something to factors on every side, but it was inseparable from his single-minded devotion, his conscientious sense of responsibility, and his gift for holding comrades together—above all from his principle of "trusting the men he used": he gave full trust to his subordinates and let them work boldly with a free hand; when they met difficulty, he talked it over and solved it with them; and when mistakes cropped up in the work, he took the responsibility himself. So it was that in those tangled, perilous surroundings he moved like a fish in water, riding the wind and cutting the waves, forever pressing on. In the summer of 1930, when the "Li Lisan line" was preparing an armed uprising in Shanghai, it ordered Chen Geng to lead the intelligence-branch comrades in surveying the imperialists' and the Kuomintang's military, political, and economic situation in Shanghai. The task was heavy, the time pressing, and hands too few, so he led in person the newly returned Liu Ding, together with Liu Dahan (Ouyang Xin, also called "Big Hunk") and Chen Yangshan, to do the reconnaissance; and later, when surveying the concessions' garrison forces called for people with foreign languages, the Party sent a woman comrade surnamed Gong and a man surnamed Wei to help them. To survey the imperialists' warships, they longed to climb aboard every one and count the gun positions, but the enemy forbade any craft near the warships. Then, hearing that long-distance passenger boats could pass close by, they contrived to get a large boat, disguised it as a long-distance craft, and the comrades themselves sailed it up to the warships to reconnoiter, and so got the whole picture clear, one ship at a time. They also managed to obtain a map of the Wusong forts. Not knowing how many men were in the foreign barracks, they set up a stall at the barracks gate, watched the daily comings and goings and the goods carried in, kept a running tally of how much grain and vegetables went in each day, and then, weighing it all up, reckoned the garrison's rough strength; not knowing how many prisoners the jail held, they quietly climbed onto the roof of a church nearby, sketched all the jail's roofs, worked out the floor area from the roofline, and, from the density of the cells, estimated the number held. In surveying Shanghai's streets and buildings, they broke the items down very fine—the number of stories in a building, its structure and quality, how the streets crossed, how tall each big block on the Bund was, how far apart the buildings stood—and made precise measurements of it all, furnishing every kind of data for street fighting and artillery fire. So that after the uprising we could control Shanghai's economy and money market, Chen Yangshan bought the address-and-telephone books of the city's banks and big firms. They made detailed surveys of the major banks and firms and gathered a great deal of important material.

The "Liu Dahan" who took part in this survey was, it is said, surnamed Liu by birth, went under the alias Ouyang Xin, and was a Hubei man. His English was fluent, and, operating under the name of a special correspondent of the Xinxin News Agency, he could enter the foreign barracks stationed in Shanghai. To learn the foreign garrisons' weapons and equipment, he contrived to survey their arms depots. In gathering Kuomintang military intelligence, too, he worked as a journalist, using connections on every side and every kind of method to get close to Kuomintang military organs and to the military and civil officials moving between Shanghai and Nanjing, making friends with certain reactionary officers, drawing them into a relationship, and getting a good deal of intelligence. In this way, in a very short time, the real strength of Shanghai's foreign garrisons and the Kuomintang forces was fairly clearly grasped, a major contribution to the survey.

They combined what they had learned of the Kuomintang's garrison, police, and secret-service organs in Shanghai with the various intelligence gathered in this reconnaissance into one thick volume of material and sent it to Liu Bocheng, then chief of staff of the Central Military Commission, furnishing the Party with a great deal of reliable intelligence for the coming armed uprising. Long afterward, when the country had been founded, Liu Bocheng once met Chen Geng and Liu Ding in Beijing, and, talking with them of the errors and harm of the Li Lisan line, still said: "All the same, our Communists were very brave. Take that set of material you prepared in Shanghai for the Li Lisan uprising—that really was no small thing."

The practice of the Central Special Section in this period proved that intelligence work is an important weapon of class struggle: without it, the Party loses its eyes and ears. In this period the Party's intelligence work drew fully on the factors in the situation that favored us; it gathered intelligence of every kind by open and secret means, combined legal and illegal forms to learn the enemy's schemes, and relied on the Party and every kind of force to wage a covert struggle against the enemy—so that the work of safeguarding the Party Central's safety won major successes, and laid a rich store of experience and a good foundation for the Party's intelligence work afterward. In this period Chen Geng carried on his work under conditions of unusual danger. He needed a household for cover, and rented a room on Rue Vallon (now Nanchang Road), where Wang Genying moved in to live with him. The landlord was Lu Liankui, a detective chief of the British concession police. Chen Geng said to Wang Genying: "Renting from a man like this lessens the enemy's suspicion—let Lu Liankui play bodyguard too." The next year a boy was born to them, named Zhifei. To throw off the pursuit of enemy detectives and agents, they moved house often and changed their names; by the rules of secret work, each place had to be one with many ways out, easy to slip from if trouble came. In the two years from 1929 to 1931 they moved five times, living in turn on Avenue Joffre, Rue Lafayette, Baoding Road, and elsewhere. All the while, Genying covered Chen Geng with all her heart and strength, kept him safe, and let him work in safety. Shanghai-born and bred, she knew the place intimately. Day to day she watched, carefully and cleverly, all that stirred before and behind the house, marking every suspicious-looking passerby, and forever turning over how to meet a sudden crisis. Whether facing the questions of police and constables or the probing of plainclothes agents, she could deal with it all by her wits. When little Zhifei had grown enough to talk and run, she would not let the child learn the street and door number of their home, and never spoke before him of Chen Geng's real name or of his work outside. Chen Geng sometimes brought a gun home and, sleeping, tucked it under his pillow. When Zhifei was three, he slipped out to play in the street one day alone, saw the gun slung on a "Red-Turbaned Number Three" (that is, an Indian constable of the British concession), and, suddenly thinking of the pistol his father had hidden by the bed a few days before, said innocently: "The Red-Turbaned Number Three's gun isn't as good as my daddy's." At this the constable fixed his eyes on little Zhifei, followed him close to the door of the house, and there met Genying, who had come out to look for the child. The constable asked her, "Have you a gun in your house?" Guessing at once that Zhifei might have let a secret slip, she answered smartly: "Yes—there's a gun, the toy gun his daddy bought him at the Wing On Company." And because she spoke so calmly and naturally, she sent the Indian constable on his way.

By the requirements of secret work, Chen Geng not only changed his address often but had to keep changing his identity and his appearance. Luckily he had a ready tongue from boyhood, could speak many dialects, had acted in school plays, and had played all sorts of parts. In these years of work in Shanghai he was now in Western suit and leather shoes, like the foreign-styled dandies one saw in the concessions; now in full uniform, a senior Kuomintang general; sometimes in a short jacket and peaked cap, playing a "crab's-foot" runner for the enemy's detective house; sometimes in long gown and riding jacket, felt hat and cloth shoes, the picture of a great merchant. Chen Geng loved most to wear a long gown; and sometimes, working in a factory, he changed again into a worker's clothes…

At that time Chen Geng was constantly meeting people of every sort, and left a deep impression on them all. General Zhang Kexia—chief of staff of Zhang Zizhong's division in Feng Yuxiang's Northwest Army, who later, in the Huaihai Campaign, led his troops with He Jifeng and others to declare an uprising in the Tai'erzhuang area—had met Chen Geng in Shanghai in this time. Zhang Kexia had entered the Northwest Army early; in the spring of 1927 he had gone to Soviet Russia to study at Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow, and returned home in the summer of 1928. His studies had raised his political awareness, and he was resolved to join the Chinese Communist Party and fight for the communist cause to the end of his life. In July 1929, Zhang Kexia reached Shanghai, found the organization through Dong Jianwu, who had ties to the Central Special Section's intelligence branch, and applied to join the Party; soon, with the center's approval, he became a special (secret) Party member of the Chinese Communist Party, with someone sent by the Party to keep single-line contact with him. On the Party's instructions he returned to the Northwest Army, was appointed chief of staff of Zhang Zizhong's division, took active part in the struggle against Chiang Kai-shek, and sent along whatever intelligence he could to the Party organization. In October 1930, after the war of Feng Yuxiang and Yan Xishan against Chiang Kai-shek had failed, Zhang Kexia came again to Shanghai to report to the Party organization and ask for instructions on his future work. Chen Geng went then to his lodging to see him and talked warmly with him. Half a century later he recalled his impression of that meeting with Chen Geng:

During my time in Shanghai the Party sent many comrades to my lodging to see me and talk with me, telling me many vivid tales of the revolution. Among them, Comrade Chen Geng left a deep impression. Comrade Chen Geng came in disguise; he was dressed in a blue satin jacket and trousers, the trouser legs bound tight with black satin ties, very much in the style of a Shanghai "young master." He called out loudly the moment he came in, and greeted me like an old friend, most warmly. He was full of dash and a lively, witty talker. I had already heard he was wounded in the leg at the Nanchang Uprising, and I asked with concern whether the wound was serious and whether it had healed. He gave a demonstration on the spot: now leaping freely, now squatting and bending the leg, to show that his wounded leg was quite healed. We talked over what each of us had seen and felt, like the closest of friends; and this one meeting stays fresh in my memory to this day and is hard to forget.

Set down in the tiger's den, moving openly among detectives and agents through the tangled, sharp, and cruel struggle in the shadows, Chen Geng was so steady and quick that the enemy not only never suspected him but took him for one of their own, greeting him warmly as "Mr. Wang" or "Old Wang." In those days, whether it was a place where Kuomintang agents gathered or the concession detective house of the foreign invaders, Chen Geng could come and go at need. Once, at a meeting in a theater in the French Concession attended by many Communists, concession constables and Kuomintang agents suddenly surrounded the place, and the situation was desperate. Chen Geng was there too. To cover the comrades' safe withdrawal, he thought quickly, stepped forward, and at once "cooperated" with the detectives who had come to make the arrests: playing the "crab's-foot" runner, he guarded one exit alone and quietly let a number of Party members slip away. Under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership, Chen Geng, relying on the Party organization and working through connections of every kind, had a grip on the concession detective house and on certain Kuomintang detective chiefs and agents, and even on a few men at Chiang Kai-shek's own side. In his dealings with the enemy, on the Party's instructions, Chen Geng would often, single-handed and at great risk, go deep into the enemy's heart to deal sternly with the traitors who had betrayed the Party and imperiled the revolution. His work struck fear into the enemy: Kuomintang agents and concession constables both, at the mere name of Chen Geng, hated him and dreaded him and tried by every means to catch and kill him, and a huge price was set on his head—yet they could never find his trace. In truth he moved often among the very Kuomintang agents; but the enemy never discovered that his surname was Chen, never knew that he was the Communist they schemed against day and night.

### A Close Call with Qian Dajun on the Train

In this time Chen Geng had many a legendary brush with danger that came to nothing. Thirty years later, a month before he died, Chen Geng told his family and the staff around him of one such time, when he had met peril on a train during his Central Special Section years.

At the Spring Festival of 1961, not long after his second heart attack, Chen Geng traveled by special train to Shanghai for treatment. Once the train had pulled out of Beijing Station, he first asked the children: "Do you know what this kind of carriage used to be called?" None of them knew. He said: "This is a saloon car, for the rich and the great to ride in. In those days all I could afford was an ordinary carriage—a third-class coach."

In 1928, while directing the Central Special Section's intelligence work, he once went from Shanghai to Tianjin on a mission. When the train stopped at Nanjing, Chen Geng stepped down and strolled on the platform. Suddenly he saw that a special saloon car had been hitched, for the moment, to the rear of his train, and, looking up, he saw several senior Kuomintang generals walking along the platform. Afraid of being recognized, Chen Geng hurried back into his carriage, sat down with his hat pulled low, and stole a careful look out the window—and got a great shock, saying to himself despite himself: "Trouble!" For the man the officers were crowding onto the saloon car was none other than someone who knew him: Qian Dajun, formerly the Kuomintang's Songhu garrison commander and by then transferred to command the Kuomintang's Jiangnan "bandit-suppression" forces.

Qian Dajun had known Chen Geng as far back as Whampoa. When Sun Yat-sen ordered Chiang Kai-shek to prepare the founding of the Whampoa Military Academy in 1924, Qian Dajun was on the preparatory committee. After the academy opened, he was a lieutenant-colonel instructor in weaponry in the teaching department under Ye Jianying, then acting colonel chief instructor, rose to be chief of the staff office of the academy headquarters, and later served as acting dean and acting principal. When Chen Geng was a cadet in Whampoa's first class, Qian Dajun had taught them their weaponry course. On graduating, Chen Geng, for his outstanding record, was kept on to serve as deputy squad leader and squad leader of the second, third, and fourth classes in turn, and was a well-known left-wing firebrand of the academy. He and Jiang Xianyun and He Zhonghan—three Hunanese—were together called the "Three Heroes of Whampoa," and were much valued by the principal, Chiang Kai-shek; Qian Dajun of course knew him well. From March to September 1928, Qian Dajun served as Songhu garrison commander and was a mortal foe of the Central Special Section. Though by now transferred to command the Kuomintang's "bandit-suppression" forces, he still held the Nanjing–Shanghai area within the sphere of his troops.

When the train left Nanjing that day, Chen Geng had thought he could keep clear of the man and not be found out. But the train had not gone far when Qian Dajun's aide suddenly came to Chen Geng's carriage and said to him: "Mr. Chen, the commander requests you." Chen Geng's heart sank—he had been seen after all. But he said coolly: "My name is not Chen; I'm a businessman. How would I know your commander? You've mistaken your man." The aide said: "There's no mistake. Please come over there for a talk." Chen Geng thought: since it had come to this, he had no choice but to go. He walked into the saloon car, and Qian Dajun, seeing him, said with a sly smile: "I saw you the moment I came into the station, and had my aide follow you." Chen Geng looked at him and said nothing. When Chen Geng had sat down, Qian asked again: "What have you been up to lately?" Chen Geng answered: "Nothing to do—I'm looking about for work everywhere." Qian Dajun was hardly likely to believe it, and, watching him, questioned him a few times more. Seeing that Qian was not in earnest, Chen Geng found ways to fend him off. Chen Geng said, too, that he would get off partway to see a friend and look for something to do. After chatting on like this a while, Chen Geng slipped away. When the train reached a station, Chen Geng got off quickly, and as it began to move again he swiftly climbed aboard another carriage and sat down, hat still pulled low. But he had already been marked by Qian Dajun's men, and soon after the train started, the aide came looking again. He came with his lips pressed and a faintly furtive air, and said: "Mr. Chen, you didn't get off after all. The commander requests you." Chen Geng screwed up his courage and went once more; and because Qian Dajun had taught their course at Whampoa, there was at least a little of the "bond of teacher and pupil" between them, and Chen Geng spoke to Qian Dajun of the old Whampoa days and appealed to the larger cause. Since Chen Geng had saved Chiang Kai-shek's life once and left a deep impression among the Whampoa men, arresting him would be to take up a "hot potato" and bring trouble on himself. So Qian Dajun was glad enough to turn a blind eye and do him the favor. Besides, once the train passed Xuzhou it left the territory of his own Jiangnan "bandit-suppression" command. So after chatting a while he let Chen Geng go. Qian Dajun made no more trouble for him after that, and Chen Geng passed safely through Xuzhou and went on to Tianjin.

His account done, Chen Geng told the children: "Victory does not come easily! For the revolution you must train yourself to handle every kind of hard situation, and deal with a cunning enemy by your wits. Only by seizing on every condition you can use can you turn defeat into victory and finish the task."
