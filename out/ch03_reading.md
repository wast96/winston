## Chapter 3. Surface and Underside

She was drunk, and the self-styled woman ninja went over onto the bedding in a heap no one could bear to look at.

Hanzō spared her not a glance. Over nothing but a loincloth he pulled on his black shinobi clothes, and over those the robes of a mountain ascetic. He undid his topknot and let the hair fall loose, set the little pilgrim's cap on his head, and knotted its cord under his chin. Then he took up his pack and his staff.

"I'll be gone four days. Understood?"

And with that he shot out into the night.

North he ran, up the eastern shore of Lake Hamana, fast as the wind. A fearsome eagerness carried him. All that big talk over the cups with Goemon had worked him up, no doubt, until he was half in love with his own greatness.

He was tearing along like a scud of black cloud when a pitch-black shape came running the other way. Pitch-black too, but ragged, half-coming-apart. They flashed past each other. In that instant both of them overran their own feet, caught themselves, stopped, and turned.

"M-Master Hattori—" the other gasped, out of breath.

"You're Moriichirō," said Hanzō. "What's become of you, all this while?"

The same black figure, but on the man coming toward him the black was the black of mud, head to foot. At the sound of Hanzō's voice the man called Moriichirō crumpled where he stood, as though some strung-tight strength had drained out of him all at once.

Hanzō caught him by the sash and dragged him, scraping over the ground, off the road and into the grass at the verge.

"It's two months now since Yamazaki. Where have you been? What have you been doing?"

Moriichirō was the heir of Torii Kihachirō of Ōhara, a pupil at the Azuchi seminary, and, on top of that, the runner who carried word for Oyu, the kunoichi Hanzō had planted in Nobunaga's household. He only sank his hollow eyes deeper and heaved for breath, his shoulders working.

"Pull yourself together! Out with it!" Hanzō roughened his voice on purpose and barked at the spent youth, to shock him back to his senses.

"Lady Oyu—" said Moriichirō. "Yesterday— for Hideyoshi's sake— they killed her—"

"What? Killed?"

Hanzō was startled.

Moriichirō's story was this.

Oyu had done as Hanzō ordered and worked to turn Nobunaga's suspicion onto Mitsuhide. That was surely not the whole of it, but the scheme took, and took cleanly: Nobunaga stripped Mitsuhide of the duty of feasting Ieyasu, and Mitsuhide, telling himself the end had come, at last crossed over into revolt. By her doing Nobunaga died; then Mitsuhide was destroyed in his turn; and now the realm had come tumbling into Hideyoshi's lap. To Hideyoshi, Oyu was a benefactor.

On the night of the Honnō-ji, Oyu had already read from Moriichirō's messages that the blow was coming. Near dawn she slipped from her bed and, the warm wind on her, crept along under the eaves. Behind her the Honnō-ji she had only just come out of was going up in flame. She stole away toward Kawaramachi.

Then, without warning, a roar of voices and the crack of guns.

The startled townspeople opened their shutters a crack and peered out. An uprising of the Ōmi packhorse-men, perhaps; perhaps only robbers.

Oyu stood rooted, trembling, as the thing she herself had made showed itself to her, stark and plain.

Just then a monstrous shape slipped past her and ran, lurching, toward the Honnō-ji. Oyu shrieked. But it never turned its head. It ran with a strange, inhuman gait, its hair writhing and glinting about it like a knot of snakes, rounded the corner, and was gone.

That it was Tamo, you will already have guessed.

Only when it had turned the corner did the other thought strike her. What of Soda Yoichiro? The Yomoda Tajima-no-kami he served held Fukuchiyama castle in Tanba and was a captain of weight under Mitsuhide; this dawn he would surely be in the front rank of the storming of the Honnō-ji.

(For Oyu could never have known Tamo. The two had never met; she could not have told Moriichirō of her at all.) Let me pause here and trace, from what the Shinsho Taikōki hands down, how Yoichiro's lord, Yomoda Tajima-no-kami Masataka, bore himself that night.

The Shinsho Taikōki is a vast work: twelve series, three hundred and sixty volumes, from the hand of Kurihara Ryūan. Ryūan was born in Edo in 1794 and died in 1870. It never set out to chase historical truth. It was written as popular entertainment. So one must read it braced: it makes a hero of Hideyoshi past all reason, and every one of its battle-scenes is packed with invention and flourish.

That night, it seems, Yomoda Tajima-no-kami was held in reserve for the assault on the Honnō-ji.

At the temple, the account runs, a stalwart named Yomoda Matabei charged in and made for Mori Ranmaru where he stood on the veranda, working his cross-bladed spear. "Ranmaru's dress that day: a chestnut-and-maroon Echigo weave, strewn large and small with crane-roundels in white; wide trousers of glossed red-plum silk, hitched high at the side; a sword of some three shaku at his waist; his age eighteen years, his skin fair, his stature tall." A war-cry went up near Nobunaga's bedchamber, and Ranmaru flung Matabei off and ran for it.

His younger brother Rikimaru, sixteen, took the fight in his place. "Back, base fellow!" and, swinging a great sword of three shaku two sun, he and Matabei "crossed blades, striking fire from the points." Matabei was "galled to have let Ranmaru slip, yet mindful that Rikimaru too was a branch of the Mori tree, and not to be let go"; he pressed in, and when Rikimaru stumbled he drove forward, ran him through, pinned him, and took his head.

The eldest brother, Bōmaru, seventeen, sprang up onto the veranda. "You are Akechi's man. Not my lord's enemy alone, but my brother's slayer now, and before my own eyes. You shall not escape." He rushed in, and Bōmaru too was cut down at the last.

Yomoda, with Kimura Jirō-uemon and Murai Matabei, vaulted lightly onto the veranda. "Lord Oda, look well: we come unbidden—"

"You dogs, get back! Who do you take me for? I am the Right Minister, Nobunaga!" The light of his eyes was lightning, his voice the roar of a lion. Murai, struck by an arrow from Nobunaga's own bow, dropped from the veranda with a crash. Kimura took a shaft in the shoulder, fainted, and fell.

Then several Akechi samurai came running, and at the very brink of it: "just then a lady of the household came out from within, stripped the sheath from a sickle-cross spear, and offered it up. Nobunaga, beholding her, praised her aloud: 'The bearing of Lady Shizuka at the Horikawa night-attack — a very tale of old! Splendid, this presence of mind: fair in form and bold of heart, a matchless deed; the true sister of Manabe no Rokurō-dayū!' The Akechi soldiery cried, 'A woman warrior — here's a rarity! I'll have her down,' and closed on her; but the woman bound up her sleeves with a double cord, tied on a red-plum headband, whirled her long-sword like a water-wheel, and came at them running. Her kimono-hem in ribbons, she fought like fury, and beneath her hand fourteen or fifteen men were slain." But this woman warrior, too, took a stray arrow in the shoulder; and, resolved not to fall into enemy hands — "now this is the end" she ran into the inner rooms, set a dagger of nine sun five bu to her breast, and, pierced through, died.

After much more of the like, it comes to a duel between one Yasuda Sakubei, a boar of a fighter, and Ranmaru, come back again. Nobunaga fled behind the paper screens. "He'll get away—" and Yasuda sprang after and "thrust with a lunging stab, and the stroke told true"; but Ranmaru leapt in between. So Ranmaru pinned Yasuda against the high rail of the veranda and, with a bellow, drove to run him through at a single thrust. Yasuda seemed to draw back and turn the point aside; then, springing clear, he vaulted the balustrade, meaning surely to drop into the little garden; but as ill luck would have it he missed his footing on the flagstones set at the eaves-drip and pitched over, flat on his back, face up, into the deep drain.

Ranmaru looked down from above and cursed him. "Wretch, to raise your hand against the minister's house — learn now the judgment of Heaven!" He shifted his grip, and with a shout to crush hide and flesh together brought the cross-spear stabbing down, willing it to pierce clean through to the last. But though he stabbed, the iron of the cuirass held, and the point slid off and drove in between the thighs, half severing the man's member. Yasuda's spirit flashed clear. "Got you!" he cried, and seized the spear-haft, and Ranmaru jerked the spear back. Pulled up by it, Yasuda came to his feet, sprang onto the veranda, drew his sword, and struck one-handed: the flash of that blade, and Ranmaru's shins were both shorn off, and he toppled down with a thud onto his haunches. There Yomoda came running up and took Ranmaru's head.

This scene, in the author's boyhood, was an old familiar from the big color prints in the shopfronts of the picture-book sellers.

Now, this popular novel, written a hundred and some years ago, runs on further in its telling. Though to call it flatly "popular fiction" is not quite fair. Bakin's Satomi Hakkenden, written at almost the same time, was composed from the first as a novel; the Shinsho Taikōki was composed as history. My own father and mother read it believing it history. And even taken as fiction, this Edo-period work drew its matter from what people took to be fact. If the sharebon is the popular novel, then the yomihon is the artistic one, and by that reckoning the Hakkenden belongs to the latter kind.

"The Right Minister — pierced through the thigh by Yasuda's spear-point thrust through the screen — must have judged the end had come. When he had cut himself open, they drew the sliding screens shut over him and set fire; and the attendants, each as he chose, slashed their bellies and leapt into the flames, and so were gone."

All this was the work of Akechi's man, Yomoda Matabei. Yomoda Tajima-no-kami, meanwhile (whether the two were kin, no one knows), the moment the Honnō-ji burned, bore straight down on the Nijō palace, where Nobunaga's son Nobutada was.

"'The front gate will draw heavy resistance; you push against the back gate,' Mitsuhide told him, and gave him the very haori off his own shoulders." So, three hundred men at his back, Yomoda threw himself in with a will.

"The attacker was Yomoda, a warrior who had won a name among the Seven Bands of Musashi; the defenders were the young lord of the realm, the honor-guard of his lordship the Jō-no-suke, Third-Rank Middle Captain. Charged, they were driven out; driven out, they turned and came again — like great waves rolling back on themselves, like the welling of the tide." And so the besiegers pressed at last into the castle. Kajiwara Matauemon, whirling a white-hafted long-sword that Nobutada had given him, clashed with Yomoda. "'Ah, a worthy foe! Let me strike him down and make of him a gift for the court of Enma!' — and no sooner said than they closed; but for all the fire of his spirit, how should he beat the Tajima-no-kami? A while they seemed to grapple; then the Tajima-no-kami caught Matauemon one-handed, flung him better than three ken, and, as he struggled to rise, ran him through and pinned him with a single thrust." And so Nobutada too made his end by his own hand.

The retainer of so brave a captain, the young warrior Soda Yoichiro can hardly have played the coward. He must have fought like a lion at bay. The author of the Shinsho Taikōki passed him over; but the cry within his heart I fancy I can hear in my own ears: "Lady Oyu, look! Look at what Yoichiro is doing! Lady Oyu!" Yet Oyu was a waiting-woman in the service of the very enemy he was storming. He must have tried, somehow, to pull her out to safety. But in the end he never reached her.

Having written this far, I murmur to myself.

"Something is wrong. Round and round I have gone, and now I have written up the Honnō-ji all over again — three times now. Have I been walking in a circle, back to where I began? It must be the heat. (The third straight day of thunder since the rains broke, and by noon already thirty-four degrees.) Has it addled my head?"

So I turn over the clippings I have gathered and look through them. The first time, I told it from the side of the Christian fathers at the Nanban-ji. The second time, out of Ishikawa Goemon's own living of it. The third time I have followed the pen of an Edo teller of popular pseudo-history, a hundred and some years back. That is the shape of it.

Which set my mind at ease. The truth of a thing is never to be had from one side alone. It comes clear only as you press in on it from one position after another; so this going in circles is a thing there is no helping.

Now, I have taken my leisure in the middle of that hurried roadside talk between Hanzō and Moriichirō. Here is what Moriichirō told.

That dawn, Oyu had wanted to know what would become of Yoichiro. She drew near the Honnō-ji, then edged in close to the Nijō palace; and there, taken for a suspicious woman, she was seized by Akechi's men. The rumor reached Yomoda's camp, and Yoichiro hurried over at once and pleaded for all he was worth: she had been Nobunaga's waiting-woman, true, but she was in sympathy with Lord Akechi, and time and again she had carried Nobunaga's mind to him. Pressed for why she should favor Mitsuhide, though, he could give no account that held together. In the end they settled it that she had simply lost her heart to Yoichiro, and let the matter drop.

But this was an hour when no one knew when Hideyoshi's army, then drowning Takamatsu castle under water, might catch the news and come wheeling back; and the victors were flushed and giddy with having taken the realm. So Oyu was handed down to Yoichiro like a spoil of war. One girl was not worth the fuss. The two of them should now have been free to be together in the open light of day.

And more: Yoichiro's—

Setting this down, I cannot get past a small vexation. Yoichiro, Moriichirō, why must the names be so maddeningly alike? They were of much an age, born into the same turn of the world, and yet, top to toe, two wholly different men. Why saddle them with such names? For my part I cannot help laying the blame on Moriichirō's father, the Kōga middle-rank shinobi Torii Kihachirō of Ōhara. As for the Soda side, the Shinsho Taikōki ignores him and his father's name is lost, so there is no one left to hold to account.

At all events, Yoichiro's lord, Yomoda Tajima-no-kami, went out at Mitsuhide's order as far as Harima, seventy men behind him, every one schooled in the scout's art, to find out whether Hideyoshi might come turning back from Takamatsu in Bitchū. The Taikōki calls them "masters of the kamari, seventy-odd men." Look up kamari in the Kōjirin and you get: "a stealth-scout, a reconnoiterer." Masters of that craft, so there were surely ninja among them.

These seventy-odd men, heads bound in white cloth, short sleeves rolled back, leggings of plaited cattail-leaf on their black-haired shins; some shouldering hoes, some with a rush hat and a straw cape on their backs, a whetstone at the hip and a sickle in the right hand, got up as peasants, gathered into band after band, lay in wait, tidying the road as they went.

Hideyoshi had made his sudden peace with the Mōri, and once past Himeji he put his horse at a gallop out in front, only the few of his escort who could keep the pace strung out behind him, and flew east, and east again. This was the ninth day of the sixth month.

Past Nishinomiya he came up on Amagasaki, and ran headlong into peasants tidying the road. They fouled his galloping, and he shouted them down.

"Whose bidding has you sweeping the road? Into the paddy and pull weeds!"

At which one of the peasants: "By the jitō's order, sir. A great guest passes today, we're told, so sweep the road. And who might my lord be?"

"I'm Hashiba Hideyoshi. I ride in haste to crush Akechi. Peace under Heaven's close at hand now. Put your backs into it." And he was past and gone.

The scout posted farthest out was quick to run the word to Akashi Gitayū, who lay hidden in the shade of the trees. Gitayū's men scrambled up and flung themselves across the road before Hideyoshi's horse. In the same instant the men who had passed for peasants snatched up the spears and swords they had hidden along the paddy-dykes, and ringed him round.

A glance aside showed a single thread of a path running off along the dykes of the paddy to the south. Heaven's own gift: "flying, leaping, quick as a lightning-flash, in the space of one drawn breath he had ridden four or five chō clear."

But the narrow path ran to a dead end at a paddy, and on the left stood a temple, the Kōtoku-ji. A rat in a bag now, thought Yomoda, and came on without haste, chuckling to himself. Hideyoshi swung down from the saddle, turned the horse about, and laid two sword-cuts across its rump. The maddened beast bolted straight for Yomoda; Yomoda spread his arms, caught its forelegs fast, heaved it up, and pitched it into the deep mud of the paddy. In that same breath Hideyoshi ran into the main hall of the Kōtoku-ji and looked wildly round for a place to hide. There was a room hung with a tablet that read Bath. He counted it his luck, stripped bare, dropped into the tub, and, casting about him, found a razor. He borrowed it, shaved off his side-locks and his hair, pulled on a white robe someone had left there, went through to the kitchen, and set to grinding miso.

Yomoda ran the whole temple through, and found none but monks. And who should come pounding up behind him but none other than Katō Toranosuke.

"Cutting the kamari down, cutting and cutting them into a rout, whirling his great blade that ran with blood, he raised a huge shout: 'There stands Yomoda Tajima-no-kami — be halved!' and cut at him. Kiyomasa was that year twenty-two, Yomoda thirty-eight. 'Got you!' — parrying and turning the stroke, beating aside the next when it drove in, tiger-wild and dragon-leaping, shadow against shade, each spending to the last the secret art he had mastered, they fought above a hundred passes." Then they threw their swords aside and closed like wrestlers, and at long last Kiyomasa cut through Yomoda Tajima-no-kami's neck. So the story goes.

Even this far, something is off. That there was time for all of it: to climb into the bath, shave his head to the skin, dress in a monk's robe, cross to the kitchen, and grind miso. That I cannot for the life of me believe.

But according to the Onna Taikōki, which Sawa Juji, head of the women's desk at the Yomiuri, ran as a newspaper serial eight years ago, this temple, the Kōtoku-ji, stands at Number Eight, Teramachi, in Amagasaki, and burned down in the late war, so that nothing of its old face remains. Hideyoshi granted it a vermilion-seal charter of five hundred and fifty koku, which endured, they say, down to the Man'en years; and the very mortar he is supposed to have ground his miso in that day is kept at the temple still, so anyone living out Amagasaki way is welcome to go and hunt for it.

Even this the Shinsho Taikōki found too tame, and piles one last great reversal on top.

To the spot where Kiyomasa had cut down Yomoda came Kuroda Kanbei, Nakagawa Sebei, Takayama Ukon, Fukushima Ichimatsu and the rest, at a run. They scattered Akashi Gitayū and went hunting for Hideyoshi, and the monk who had been grinding miso in the kitchen was somehow standing bolt upright in the main hall. Look close: it was Hideyoshi.

"Tight as the corner was," said Nakagawa, "to make yourself a shaven monk — it's a poor sight." Fukushima fired back: "It's just because he is a lord that he could snatch at such a ruse in an instant." They were on the edge of a quarrel when a foot-soldier came up, leading the Chikuzen-no-kami's relief horse by its rope. He tied the horse, strode into the hall, and praised the monk: "Asano Hachirōzaemon — well toiled, well toiled!" Whereupon the monk they had all taken for Hideyoshi went flat to the floor and, in fear and trembling, made his report: "Somehow I have just brought it off." The whole company stood dumbstruck and looked from the one to the other. The foot-soldier's face, too, was Hideyoshi to the life.

"This man who played the shaven monk," said the foot-soldier Hideyoshi, "is one Asano Hachirōzaemon. His face is the very image of mine, and I had long meant to keep him for a double: I put him in my armor and sent him spurring ahead from Himeji. But that he had the wit to turn himself into a monk on top of it — that I never dreamed. Well done! Well done!"

Twist an invented tale this far, make Hideyoshi a man to shame a ninja, and that carefully kept mortar at the Kōtoku-ji comes to be no more than "the mortar Hideyoshi's stand-in ground his miso in." Does it not?

Now, Yoichiro was most likely one of those kamari, and by the look of it he was cut down by Kiyomasa or another of Hideyoshi's captains. The next day came the battle of Yamazaki; the day after, Mitsuhide's death in the field. Akechi's whole reign was over in eleven days. And Oyu, at Hanzō's order, set about working her way close to Hideyoshi.

Yoichiro's death was as good as certain; and even had he lived, a surviving man of the traitor Akechi could never again show his face in the world.

It was not that Moriichirō loved Oyu. But short of a woman being downright hideous, any woman at all would do for him. And every man some woman loved, he hated. In a man of this stamp, of all the feelings he owns jealousy is the strongest; whatever he touches, it is jealousy that stirs first. So of late his heart had been light. Oyu, for her part, was torn a thousand ways, wondering whether Yoichiro might somehow yet be alive.

He summoned her, and came on high and hard: Hanzō's orders, get in close to Hideyoshi now; leave the openings to me.

"Well — do you understand?"

But Oyu only stared off at nothing with bloodshot eyes. Then, without warning, her body would jerk in a spasm.

"Are you deaf to me? — Here!" Moriichirō caught her by the wrist and wrenched.

Off her guard, Oyu pitched against his chest. His arms clamped around her, brutal and unprovoked.

For Moriichirō there was no such thing as love with any weight of meaning in it. Only an animal appetite. His heart was sly, and cruel; his coldness could freeze another's heart at a glance.

Small wonder, then, that Tamo, the kitchen-woman at the seminary after its move from Azuchi to Takatsuki, had feared him, and shunned him, above all others.

The place was a stand of trees behind the Gion shrine in Kyoto. The sun had been down an hour.

A kunoichi cannot refuse a summons from her handler, however she may loathe it. Moriichirō, with no prelude at all, threw her down and came down on top of her. For him, plainly, neither the ninja's code nor the Christian's commandments were worth a second thought. A woman was something to try your luck at; refused, you let it go. What answer she gave, and how her refusal would bend under his pushing, there was the thrill, past all words; the kinds of women were nothing but that one difference. Such was the whole of Moriichirō's idea of women.

A ninja, meet what danger he may, will not raise his voice to beg for rescue. A kunoichi is no ninja, but she too will never cry for help. Moriichirō knew it. So all that was left was to let his strength bear hers down; till then he had only to labor on, tireless, unflagging.

Her clothes were in wild disorder now, her power to resist all but spent. In a popular novel, at this very instant some figure appears out of nowhere, takes Moriichirō by the scruff, and flings him crashing onto his back. In life it does not go so.

Her kimono was dragged open, and where her upper body was laid bare, pale even in the dark, a length of white cotton was bound about her belly. The sash had long since fallen loose and been cast off. In those days no woman wound a cloth band about her stomach unless she was carrying a child. Oyu had taken it up of her own invention, because it seemed to draw her tight and ready, and it was this that saved her.

"Ei — what a bother!" Rough-handed, Moriichirō felt for the end of the cotton and began to roll her body over and over on the ground, to strip it away. As he did, a slender dagger wound into the band dropped to the earth before her eyes. In a daze she snatched it up, flung off the sheath, and gripped it reversed, and Moriichirō never saw. When he lowered himself over her once more, the dagger drove into his side.

And here the half-trained Moriichirō, a stripling of nineteen, cried out despite himself.

"Aaagh!" he shrieked.

Just then five or six of the town-patrol, men under Katō Toranosuke, one of Hideyoshi's retainers, then quartered in Kyoto, came running up.

Moriichirō's heart went black and wild. Now I'm about to die, and for nothing at all, and the cause of it is this filthy bitch, curse you, curse you, And so, wringing the words out through his pain, he spoke.

"Th-this woman is the sweetheart of Soda Yoichiro, retainer to Yomoda Tajima-no-kami, a captain under Akechi Mitsuhide. She wormed her way in as a waiting-woman at Lord Nobunaga's very side, and it was she who guided the Akechi men into the Honnō-ji."

The startled patrol sprang on the naked Oyu and bound her fast, arms wrenched high behind her. The two of them were hauled off to Katō Toranosuke's camp-quarters at the Sennyū-ji, in Higashiyama.

Moriichirō's wound, taken just above the sash, came to little. They washed it with shōchū and lashed it tight with white cloth. Oyu, before the eyes of the rough soldiers, had her bonds loosened and was at last let to dress.

"What has become of Soda Yoichiro?"

She had the look of a woman who would say not one word more until she was answered.

"Hm. Yomoda Tajima-no-kami? Our lord cut him down at the Kōtoku-ji in Amagasaki. And all seventy-odd of the Tajima-no-kami's men were killed to the last one, so your fine fellow was surely among them — no doubt of it."

At that, Oyu went white in a breath, and the tears came streaming.

Hideyoshi had been able to seize the realm because Mitsuhide killed Nobunaga for him. Had Nobunaga not been struck down then (and Nobunaga was only forty-nine), Hideyoshi, younger by a bare two years, might in the end never have taken the realm at all. Mitsuhide, you might say, was Hideyoshi's benefactor. But that was the underside of it. On the surface he stayed, first to last, the arch-traitor who had murdered his lord, and the hunt for his surviving men was pitiless.

And there was this besides: for all the spy-craft Hideyoshi worked, he loathed the spies themselves as men. So when the report on Oyu reached him, he curdled with distaste and turned his face away. The messenger read it as: A defilement even to hear of. Cut her down. What Hideyoshi had meant was only, I cannot be troubled with such a creature; do as you please.

So Oyu was led straight out the back gate of the Sennyū-ji, cut down in the shadow of the hill, and buried where she fell.

As for Moriichirō, the inquiry to Padre Organtino confirmed him a pupil of the Takatsuki seminary, under the protection of Takayama Ukon, who had distinguished himself at Yamazaki; and so he was let go. Setting out to carry his report to Hamamatsu, he ran headlong, on the east shore of Lake Hamana, into Hanzō.

"And the wound in your flank?"

"It's closed over already, sir."

"Good. Then go to my house and rest. I have an errand of my own." So saying, Hanzō was gone in a rush, like a scudding mass of black cloud.

Now, this place is the village of Yono, in the province of Iga. It lies in the hills two ri south of the town of Ueno, along the road that runs from Ueno through Yagyū to Nara; but it is a hidden hamlet, shut round by mountains. Above its terraced fields stands a manor, ringed on all four sides by a shallow moat and walled in packed earth. This is the seat of the Chigachi, a house long powerful in these parts.

The manor is gone now. There is only a low rise, remembered by name as the site of the Chigachi fort. I went to see it myself, a few years back. At the foot of the climb to the fort-site stands a shrine, the Hanagaki, ancient to look at. It was a cold day in the second month, the shrine hushed and empty. Word had it that its ridge-plaque, the plaque a patron inscribes with his own name and dedicates when a shrine is built or rebuilt, fixed up at the raising of the ridgepole; still carries, to this day, the name Chigachi Hanzō Taira no Masashige; and I meant to see it. But knock as I would, no one answered, and the old woman who came out at last could make nothing of my question, so I gave it up and left.

And now, back to a matter of three hundred and eighty-two years ago.

The head of that Chigachi house was away the year round, and came home only now and then: five or six times a year, no more. This had begun with the previous head. Before that, the master had lived there about half the time. And down to the last generation, when he was at the manor in the Hattori village just north of Ueno, he took the name Hattori; at the Chigachi manor, the name Chigachi.

Earlier in this story we met an astonishing secret of the ninja's craft: that Momochi Sandayū and Fujibayashi Nagato-no-kami, two men utterly unlike in face and in nature, taken for sworn enemies besides, proved, once they were dead, to have been one and the same man. This was a different thing.

The Hattori, as I have said before, were of Chinese descent, but they came into the world's eye in the medieval age. Hattori Ienaga served Taira no Tomomori, styled himself Iga Heinai-zaemon-no-jō, and won a name in arms. When a contest was held before the throne at the archery ground of the Seiryōden, to try the art of the bow, Ienaga entered among Tomomori's men and made an outstanding showing; and the retired emperor of that day, the Rokujō-in, gave him a hand-cart heaped with a thousand true-feathered arrows. From that time, they say, the house took the arrow-nock wheel for its crest. There is an old book that names one Iga Heinai-zaemon-no-jō Hidetsugu as chief of the ninjutsu men of Iga.

At the time of the Ōnin War the house entered the service of the Ashikaga. In those unresting politics a man had need, at times, to drop out of sight. So they raised a fort at Yono, and from then kept two dwellings, this one and the Hattori, bearing the name Hattori at Hattori and the name Chigachi at Yono. It was not that they lived out two wholly separate men, as Sandayū and Nagato-no-kami had; face, form, and character stayed the same, and only the surname was turned now one way, now the other. Why they kept it up even after the Ōnin War was done, through two generations, father and son, that has long puzzled the historians.

But of late I have come to see how it was, and the long-standing puzzle melted like spring ice. That, though, I will explain later.

Hanzō's father, Hattori Yasunaga, was most likely squeezed out of Iga by the rising power of the two upper-ninja masters, Momochi and Fujibayashi, and took service with Ieyasu. After that he showed his face at the Hattori manor almost never, and came home instead to Yono a few times a year, a habit Hanzō too had kept.

And so it was that Hanzō, in his mountain-ascetic's dress, was to be found, by the evening of the day after he met Moriichirō on the east shore of Lake Hamana, at the Chigachi manor in Yono.

There was a woman there of thirty-two or three, named Chika, not quite a concubine, not quite a caretaker. She was full of energy, the sort of woman Hanzō liked: big-boned, well-fleshed, the healthy blood running with a thud, a thud, under her rosy skin. Poke her anywhere, and the flesh was so firm it sprang back. Her hair was black and thick.

Yet for all that body, Chika's whole tempo was slow, and unhurried. Not that she was dull, or too soft. Only, to put it this way. In bed she answered readily enough when Hanzō wanted her, but leave her untouched however long, and she felt no particular want. That she was a woman of this kind fitted well the place life had put her in.

No, even that is easy to misread. What I mean is that to mirror other people's moods at every turn, to fret over each small thing, was to her simply a bother. She was, a little, a lazy one.

Were she otherwise, she could never have kept this manor for a man who came home so seldom, run it, and led the forty peasants of the Chigachi house, eight of them ninja. There was no steward set over the place, as Goemon had once been at Sandayū's manor at Ryūko. Chika showed not a flicker of curiosity about how Hanzō lived and moved when he was away from Yono. At first, it seems, she did; but somewhere along the way she had learned that curiosity got her nowhere. She had taken the true measure of her own boundary and mastered the art of living wholly inside it, the fruit, no doubt, of having been born and raised the daughter of a fairly well-off temple hereabouts.

Home here, Hanzō too turned idle. He would wander the fields, go into the woods and, as the season gave, gather bamboo shoots, hatsutake, shimeji. It made a health-cure of a kind, surely: a place to rest the nerves he held stretched and raw at Hamamatsu.

You might take it for the same thing as a politician who spends his weekdays in the heart of the city, sunk to the neck in plots and graft-grubbing and faction-fights, and passes the weekend at his villa in Hakone. But in Hanzō's case there was one further purpose. It was to make his devotions at a temple called the Shōfuku-ji, an Esoteric-sect temple in the village of Azōda, at the southern edge of the town of Ueno.

The Shōfuku-ji was not the Hattori family temple. Yet every time Hanzō came home to Chigachi, he went there without fail to worship.

It lay a good way southwest of the house where Tamo had been born, but it too stood close by a quarter of poor folk who dealt in hides and the like.

In this region, by an old custom, the place where the dead are buried and the place where the gravestones stand are two separate places. A graveyard, in Korea and such lands, is laid out on a sunny hillside, and the family carry box-lunches up and picnic there and pass a whole happy day. On Shōdoshima, too, in the Inland Sea, at Bon the families set out with their lunches to meet again, and be easy with, the spirits of the forebears come home. And even where it is less bright than that, the sight of the moss-grown stone pagodas that loving, reverent descendants raised with all their hearts is a clean thing, and a solemn one.

But in country where custom keeps the grave and the corpse-ground apart, the place where the body alone is buried, they call it the sanmai, is a desolation past words, and its ghastliness bears down on the heart. Most often there is no fence, no wall, to mark off any bounded ground: only the barren earth, pocked here and there with the fresh scars of new digging and filling, not one living blade or leaf, and crows wheeling over it with their sickening cries. It makes you feel, in truth, the emptiness of life.

This Shōfuku-ji, too, is an old temple, no one knows how old, its grounds cramped, and just such a sanmai lies hard beside it. On rainy days the phosphorus that leaches from the rotting bodies under the earth meets the air and gives off a faint blue glow that sways and wavers. And the work of carting the dead there and burying them, dumping them, rather, fell, too, to the poor of that quarter.

Asano Nagamasa, who later married the younger sister of Hideyoshi's consort, the Kōdai-in, and rose to be one of the Five Commissioners, is a man of whom Michael Steichen's Christian Daimyō says both that he was "a great friend of the Fathers of the Society of Jesus," and that "Nagamasa, the Taikō's brother-in-law and a favored retainer, though more than once he was bound to enforce stern edicts against the Christian missionaries, is well known already to have borne himself kindly toward them." In the Tenshō Iga War, at Nobunaga's order, he led ten thousand men into Iga from the southeast, by the Hase pass, and came through Nabari to Ueno. Azōda lay square in his line of march. By good fortune no Iga band had forted up in the temple, so it was spared the soldiers' torches and passed by.

In Asano Nagamasa's close service was a small man, hideous to look at. He stood under five shaku; his left eye was ruined, and the right, by the look of it, very weak, so that to make a thing out he had to bring his eye up almost to touching.

He never lifted the brown hood from his head. What he wore was a filthy, baggy sack of black cotton. It was, of course, that former biwa-priest, the holy man Lourenço.

For some months past Asano Nagamasa had given his whole heart to this holy man and studied the doctrine from him; and when Lourenço begged to be taken along on this Iga campaign, Asano had brought him. Even after the army withdrew, Lourenço stayed on four months at the Shōfuku-ji. The abbot there was past seventy and gone senile, and the temple was too poor to find anyone to succeed him. So it happened that a temple of the Esoteric sect on the surface had become, underneath, a Christian one. Fallen to ruin as it was, and the conqueror Asano's word being law, they took Lourenço in whether they would or no; and the Ikkō faith, the religion of the poor and the weak, had not yet reached the soil of Iga.

Only the wife of Koichi of Higashi-Yubune, thinking of her Koichi not yet home, chanted the Namu Amida Butsu alone. Just before Ishiyama surrendered, Koichi had loaded the founder's own sacred likeness onto a woman's palanquin, given out that the lady's waiting-woman rode within, and slipped cleverly away to Washi-no-mori; then he doubled back to the castle, and when surrender was at last resolved, sooner than yield the place to Nobunaga, the enemy of the faith, he set it alight and burned it, and went straight home to Iga. But finding that Sandayū's anger had not cooled, he left again for Saiga.

Now, the new doctrine that Lourenço, practiced of tongue, began to unfold sank at once into the hearts of the downtrodden poor of that place.

That this world was made by the one Lord Deus, and stands beneath His rule. That this God made heaven and earth and all that is in them, and made mankind besides; but that mankind, overborne by the tempting of the evil fiend, Adan and Eva, were cast out of the world of ease, and entered into this sea-and-mountain of suffering, and, brought here to the end of all going forward or back, took the devil's part, and so became fit to suffer the torments of the Inferno.

But Deus, letting fall His mercy, sent down His Son, Ezusu Kirishito, from Heaven, to be the mediator that should atone for our sins. He became at the last our substitute: over His whole body not a space wide enough to set a needle, so did He take His wounds; He shed His precious blood, made Himself one with us sinners, bore out the labors of thirty years and more, washed our defilement with His tears, and with His blood made amends for our sins. That we must fall down before Deus, and render thanks for this grace—

And the downtrodden poor who were bathed in this compassion, this boundless mercy never heard of before, were the very ones laid bare to the layered cruelty of the masters bearing down on them, ground day and night, without cease, by poverty and sickness. In their hearts was no comfort and no hope, and tengu and demons and monsters set spurs to their real and present griefs.

Into such hearts, this was the first astonishing revelation ever to open. The universe rose before them charged with a wholly new meaning. No longer a universe that trampled them and kicked them from its path as it went, but a universe made ready, and set in order, for their very sake.

For the first time, they were not beings wholly cast away, but were watched over on every side by the most high God, by His Mother the Virgin Santa Maria, by His Son Ezusu Kirishito. Which is to say, in the word of today: they were loved.

What those words, mercy, compassion, set forth is, in a word of today, love. Love, without which the human heart can find no strength to bear the living of a life. And now, for the first time, that love was poured out over them.

The teaching told them something more astonishing still. Before Deus, all men alike are sinners. All alike must cling to the mercy of Deus, or fall into the Inferno. Lord and slave, it is one. Man and woman, one. Rich man and poor man, one. Nay, sooner the grieving, sooner the poor, stand near to the road of salvation.

That the downtrodden, hearing this new teaching, were struck with wonder, dumbfounded, gladdened, wept, and were swept away, was the most natural thing in the world. Before long even landholding peasants, village headmen, the townsfolk of Ueno, were gathering at the rumor of it.

Even after Lourenço left, the great Dainichi Nyorai in the main hall was gone, and in its place stood a Santa Maria some four shaku high; and one after another, unremarked, padres came and went.

The Holy Mother, blue-eyed and golden-haired, holds the infant Kirishito to her breast, and pours down on him a gaze brimming with tenderness. This was the very image Goemon had seen at the house of Yazaemon, the Sakai image-maker, and gaped at in astonishment. From Takayama Ukon's Christian temple at Takatsuki, at Lourenço's asking, it had been handed on to this place.

The Japanese are a people desperately weak before the new and the rare. When they first came up against the culture of the western continent and the peninsula, the Japanese of that age gave themselves to it entire, and scrambled after it hand and foot. Clothing, food, language, buildings, government, there was nothing it did not touch; and the notion that continental culture was the ideal ran on through the medieval and the early-modern ages, clear to the fall of the shogunate. The Nanban culture that began to arrive about the time of the Renaissance had a narrow point of contact and kept to the upper class, but it bred, even so, a good many modish rulers, Nobunaga and Hideyoshi first among them. The wave of "civilization and enlightenment" that came in with the Meiji Restoration was so thorough that one half-wondered whether it would remake the Japanese to their roots; and the Americanizing since the Second World War runs on yet, so that there is not a day one is not put out by it in some fashion.

It was not the truly poor in heart, the broken in soul, alone who drowned themselves in the Christians' revolutionary doctrine; those who merely liked a novelty were pulled in hard as well.

Why did Hanzō go to worship at the Shōfuku-ji each time he came home?

He chose an hour after the evening meal, not the set time of worship, and passed under the temple gate. He raised the black curtain that hung dead ahead, and to the lamp-stand there he struck flint, clack, clack, and lit the lamp. Out of the dark the image of the Holy Mother swam up dimly. Hanzō knelt, raised his eyes to her face, and pressed his palms together. He murmured some words of prayer under his breath; and before long a liquid welled from both his eyes, ran down his cheeks, and fell, drip, drip, to the floor.

A liquid that runs from the eyes ought to be tears. But that Hattori Hanzō, Hanzō of the Spear, Hanzō the Devil, should weep as he prayed was a sight no one who had not seen it could believe.

When he had prayed a good half-hour, he wiped his tears clean and went through toward the priest's quarters.

"It's Hanzō. Is the holy man within?"

Lourenço's voice came from inside. "Ah, Hanzō. Come in, come in."

For close on two hours Hanzō listened, rapt, to Lourenço's discourse. By the look of it, Lourenço trusted him deeply too.

"Then does Deus see clean through me — not only what I do, but the very things I think in my heart?"

"He does. Not the least thing has any way to escape the eye of Deus."

"Then if a man does not confess plainly the evil he has done, or has thought in his heart, does he stand before Deus a sinner for it?"

"That goes without saying."

"Then confession is — truly — truly a hard thing." Hanzō said it with a sigh.

"Man, since our first parents Adan and Eva, was made so that he cannot live without sinning. So unless he confesses each sin and clings to the mercy of Deus, he becomes in the end a fiend and a demon. Yet, as you say, confession is a hard thing indeed. It is for that hardness that we weep and cry aloud in this valley of tears. Deus knows well how hard it is. And so He sent His only Son, Kirishito, down into the world, and let Him be nailed to the cross in our sins' stead. He pities our weakness; He stands between us and Deus, and grieves in His heart without cease to save us. Think on that, and however hard it may be, to confess with all your strength is surely a man's plain duty."

"Just so. Just so." And Hanzō, weeping once more, nodded deeply, deeply.

"We who have Deus's trust and hear confession serve only as go-betweens of penitence before Deus, and never let a word slip to any man. So whatever it may be, keep nothing back: split open the very bottom of your belly, and confess."

Hanzō went pale, his lips trembling, as though he would bring up something from the very floor of his heart; and presently an oily sweat broke out on his face. In a strained, brooding voice he said:

"To deceive another — is it never, in any case, permitted?"

This time it was Lourenço who sweated. He knew, near enough, both what Hanzō was and what work he did under Ieyasu. Blinking his one half-open right eye fast: "That is a truly hard — a truly hard question." And, fingering and fingering the sleeve of his baggy robe, he spoke in the rusty voice that years of biwa-song had trained.

"To deceive a man for your own lone profit is, of course, a great sin. But — but — for a warrior born into these warring times, a vassal, besides, who cannot cross his lord's command — for the realm's sake, for his lord's sake, there will be times he must deceive the enemy."

"It is truly hard. Truly hard."

At that Hanzō let slip, in spite of himself, a breath of relief, and wiped the sweat from his brow. From inside his robe he drew a few grains of gold and twisted them into a screw of paper.

"An offering." He held it out, made his farewells, and went flying down the night road, back to the Chigachi manor.

The instant he was home: "Chika! — Chika! — Chika!—"

He roared it, and the woman who came out with her eyes still thick with sleep he caught up and fell upon with a savage force, the two of them for all the world like a pair killing each other. And how, in heaven's name, did this hang together with the man who, only moments before, had wept and prayed before the image of the Holy Mother, listened in humility to Lourenço's sermon, and trembled at his own sin? That such a man, the very incarnation of the selfish and the scheming, enough to turn even Goemon's stomach, should have given himself to the Christian faith: what on earth was one to make of it?

But we shall not try to crack the riddle here and now. As we follow Hanzō's doings from this point on, it will come clear, little by little. A Hanzō of this kind exists; therefore there is a cause, a reason, that he is so. And by watching, with patience, what he does, we shall surely uncover the secret hidden on the underside of this seemingly impossible fact.

Now, Hanzō slept deep and sound, and rising early the next morning went up into the pine woods on the hill behind the house. At one spot, where the leaf-cover broke, the clean blue sky showed through, and a kite wheeled slowly up, and down. He threw himself down on the shaded grass. And chewing over, with pleasure, his tussle with Chika the night before, and setting Hatsuko's body against Chika's to weigh them anew—

"The Christian faith allows a man but one wife, and strictly forbids concubines. But in my case neither Hatsuko nor Chika is a wife in due form. So it can't be said that I keep concubines on the side. And more: I am a man who serves his lord not with the arts of war alone but with ninjutsu. For that, my movements must be secret always; and the first step of secrecy is to keep several houses. Keep houses, and you must set a woman in each. Hatsuko at Hamamatsu, Chika at Chigachi — this is a thing there is no helping, done in my lord's service."

Having hit on his defense before Deus, Hanzō was content, and slept sweetly the better part of two hours. When he woke he felt his strength filled up to the last drop. A Hanzō in that state was past all handling. A man who reads everything to his own advantage, who holds it for certain that he is the ablest and most gifted man under Heaven, the very model of a man, whose every least act falls square with the order of heaven and earth, such a man is not to be handled at all.

But why should a man so full, so satisfied, have given himself to a faith like the Christians', which preaches that all men are sinners, saved only by the mercy of God?

Wedged in among Europe, Asia, and Africa, the people called the Israelites were forever cruelly used by the nations around them. Their tearful prayers for their people's deliverance went unanswered; the prophets' words of cheer — "A savior shall come at the last and take your vengeance for you; endure" faded, one after another, to nothing; and in the end they were made a colony of the mighty Roman Empire and ruled by the officials it sent out, and cast down into the condition of slaves. This oppressed people at last let go all hope of deliverance and independence in this world, and set their longing instead on a freedom and a happiness in the next that would go on forever; and hardening that inward wish into a stone, they came at length to believe it an outward truth. That is the Christian faith.

What has an oppressed people's religion of resignation to do with a Hanzō sated to the brim? Had he, full already in this world, grown greedy for eternal bliss in the next as well? But a man like Hanzō, in his prime and his health, does not think so far ahead as what lies past death.

The truth is simpler. A man of no fixed view of the world, and very greedy, was bewitched, wholly bewitched, by the splendor of a new conception of the universe, one that had come over the sea along with gunpowder and firearms and a hundred strange and curious wares, and with tales, heard for the first time, of a vast and open world. And because the spell lay so deep on him, he was brought to the pass of accepting even moral laws that went badly against his own grain.

To be fair, the Christian teaching preached in Japan in those days carried no such commandments of absolute nonresistance, no "Love your enemies," no "Blessed are they who are persecuted and suffer in this world, for so much the greater is the grace they shall have in the next," no "When they strike your left cheek, turn the right to them as well" nor the promised reward that props such commandments up. For Hanzō, the one rule that gave trouble was that he must confess, and confess, and confess again, and that he must let no woman but his wife draw his heart, and that he must deceive no one. Those alone.

As I have said, Hanzō's art of managing a master lies in making the master feel a resistance. Had the faith not put a certain resistance in his way, he might never have taken it in as a new truth at all.

Hanzō sprang up, and, roaring "Woah! — Woah!", threw both arms overhead, wrenched his hips, worked his spine long and short, sprang on his feet, and broke into a wild, freakish dance. Had there been in those days such a thing as the Twist, or the tamure, Hanzō would have danced them; but in that age, more's the pity, there was nothing for it but this, a dance at once simple and frenzied, a made-up, outlandish flailing, and no help for it.

From there he went round, house by house as they came, among his lower ninja and his tenant farmers.

When it grew dark he went once more to the Shōfuku-ji, knelt before the Maria, and gazed and gazed at it. He seemed, somehow, unnaturally fixed on that image. Unnaturally, that is, from something more than piety. You could read it in his eyes as he looked at it.

It was the very image whose likeness to Maki had once startled Goemon. Which means, of course, that it resembled Hatsuko too. But Hanzō had not yet caught that. This Maria's face was like Hatsuko's, and yet it had nothing of what was Hatsuko's own, the quick, brimming life, the look of one lying in wait for some mischief, the touch of the savage. That, no doubt, is why, close as the likeness ran, Hanzō saw in it only the face of his ideal woman, and never tied it to Hatsuko.

Just then he started, and tilted his head to the right. A star-shuriken, flung from behind, sliced his earlobe and struck with a thud into the tip of the Maria's right foot, where it peeped from under the hem of her long robe. Had he not caught, in that instant, the air-splitting hiss of it and canted his head, it would have gone true into his neck.

Feeling the blood run from his ear, he caught the feet of the Maria before him in both hands, heaved the image up, slung it head-down across his back, and dropped into a crouch. A shuriken is never thrown one at a time: five, ten, faster than the eye can follow, that is how they come. And sure enough, the back of the Maria on Hanzō's shoulders bristled with thirteen star-shuriken, like a hedgehog. The intruder's stock had been, it seemed, fourteen, so there came a small gap; and in it Hanzō rolled hard to the right, and in the same motion sent the Maria image rolling away, while his own body spun twice the other way. Faster than the eye could follow, a black rope went snaking out and wound about the Maria's neck. It was a cord plaited from a hundred and eight strands of woman's hair, two jō eight shaku long, a sickle at the one end and a weight at the other, strong enough to bear any strain.

Toward the hand that had paid the rope out, Hanzō, still flat where he had fallen, flung a ten-point shuriken. By the time he had the Maria on his back the votive lamp had already tipped and gone out, and it was all a fight in the dark now.

Fish in muddy rivers and dark seas, they say, know an enemy not by the eye but by the scent that reaches them through the water; and Hanzō now, in the black, knew by the smell of blood that he had cut his man. To shed blood in the dark is to hand the enemy your position by it, so the one who has bled stands eight parts of the way to defeat. But now both of them were bleeding, and it came out even.

Hanzō thought as he fought. To ambush me here, the man must know me for Hanzō. Who wants me dead? None but Nobunaga's party comes to mind, and yet not even Nobunaga has any direct grudge against me. And in the Tokugawa house there can be no one who hates me.

Then it struck him. Might this be one of the remnant Iga ninja who had tried, and failed, to fall on the column at Lord Ieyasu's crossing of Iga? The Iga ninja whom Nobunaga's army had broken so utterly in the Tenshō Iga War, a good many of the remnant must be holed up in the Iga hills, turned bandit by now. Such men curse every warlord alive, and mean, in time, to raise an independent band of ninja once more, as Momochi Sandayū and Fujibayashi Nagato-no-kami had, and to make Iga a country to do with as they pleased. And how would such men see me? As a degenerate who had let the daimyo Ieyasu keep him and lost the ninja's independence, a traitor to the ninja world. That, surely, was how. They must be lying low, biding their time, dreaming of a day like this. And if so, the enemies stalking me are no mere one or two.

And sure enough, from somewhere a second shuriken flew, and slit Hanzō's right earlobe. This has turned grave, he shuddered. A second enemy foretold a third. Of late Hanzō had given all his interest to intrigue, and was no longer a man who drilled the body's craft day and night. He rolled twice to the left, fetched up against the fallen Maria, and heaved it up to make a shield of it, and with a hard haul on the rope still wound about her neck, he whipped it once and swung it, right to left. The hook at its end sank into a mass of flesh, and from there came a strange, snuffling gasp — "Kku—"

On the instant the shuriken came flying like rain, one after another, and struck into the front of the Maria: thd, thd, thd. Hanzō slipped the rope from her neck, took the two-jō-eight-shaku cord at its middle, and began to whirl it like a water-wheel. Several shuriken met it and dropped to the floor with a clatter.

Had there been a light, and had anyone watched from the front, he would have seen the Holy Mother, her face brimming with tender love, cradling an infant in two hands and, with two more, whirling a black hooked rope and striking men to the ground.

There were three of them, for certain. When their shuriken were spent, needles came, blown from pipes. Hanzō, the Maria for a shield, shifted his ground inch by inch and spoiled their aim. But let time run on like this, and he had no hope of winning.

Then, by luck, from the priest's quarters came the sign of someone waking. Lourenço, who for all his gentleness had lived his life through hardship and peril, had caught the faint stir in the main hall.

"Kakunen! — Seishin!" He called to the two monks; and there came the sound of flint struck, clack, clack.

"Hanzō!" A muffled voice came out of the dark. Hanzō, of course, said nothing.

"You are the traitor to the Iga ninja!"

Then another voice took it up. "The ninja is a man who should stand alone in the world. Ieyasu is no different from Nobunaga — one more daimyo grasping at the realm, and nothing else. And you would lure the ninja into being kept, caged, Ieyasu's tame retainers."

And a third, in its turn: "Drawing a stipend, keeping a mansion, taking a wife — where is the ninja's honor in that?"

"Ninjutsu began, at its root, with En no Gyōja Ozunu; it is the true-born child of the Shingon mysteries. And you give yourself to the foreigners' Christian faith."

"Should you ever come back to Iga, know this: we will be at your heels for good."

And with that, in one breath, every trace of a human presence was gone.

When Lourenço and the two monks came in, they gaped. There lay the Blessed Maria, toppled and rolled aside, her whole body stuck through with shuriken and needles beyond counting.

"Oh! Alas, alas!" Lourenço broke into sobs. "What in the world — what has happened here!"

Hanzō spoke, in a voice pitched to the last degree of feeling.

"It is this. Just now, as I gathered my whole heart in prayer before the Blessed Maria, someone — I could not tell who — flung shuriken at me from three sides at once, out of the dark, and both my earlobes were slashed through. Three villains there seemed to be, coming at me from three sides; and just as, at the next throw, in the space between one breath and the next, my life looked to be at its end, this very image of the Maria came gliding down and hid me behind her own body, and took the shuriken and the needles into herself, and with her own neck caught and held this hooked rope. Ah — what mercy! What grace beyond all deserving!"

At this the three of them, in the fullness of their hearts, flung both arms toward heaven.

"O Mother of boundless compassion, Santa Maria! You who are the Mother of the Son of God — the God who offered up himself in sacrifice to save the children of sin — with your own sacred body you take upon yourself, and willingly, torments such as these. Wounds beyond counting on your body; your own blood shed. And all of it for our sinfulness. All of it for us — for our sake!"

And Lourenço, the tears streaming down in floods, fell weeping to the floor. "Alas, poor Lady! And — oh, alas!"

Lamenting so, they drew the shuriken and the needles out of her, one by one, and wiped the blood away. That blood was in truth the spatter from Hanzō's own slashed ear; but the white cloth it had clung to was revered ever after as a treasure of the temple, the "Blood-dyed Cloth of the Blessed Virgin Maria."

The tale ran through all Iga in no time, told and retold as the miracle of the Maria of the Shōfuku-ji, and crowds came flooding to see the wounded image and the blood-dyed cloth.

And by the same stroke, Hanzō himself gained greatly. For he had set himself forward, front and center, as a man worth enough that the Blessed Maria would offer up her own body, deliberately, as a sacrifice in his place. Every voice raised to praise the Maria was a voice raised to exalt Hanzō. For him it was a harvest cheap at the cost of two unsightly slit earlobes.

He had learned, too, that Iga held ninja who marked him for a traitor. So at once he set to work on the body-craft that had gone a little to rust. In this he was a man of real decision: he never put a thing off, never let himself go slack. A man already at a fair level, once he girded his loins and threw himself in, came on by leaps and bounds; and above all his arts of leaping and of vanishing reached a pitch that astonished even himself.

And just as this sense of his own fullness had swelled to bursting, Hanzō was ordered, without warning, by Ieyasu, to go secretly up to Kyoto.

After the Honnō-ji, the struggle over who should follow Nobunaga as master of the realm was at full swirl. While Ieyasu passed his seemingly untroubled days at Hamamatsu, the political world was moving fast and hard at Kiyosu and in Kyoto.

Ieyasu knew it was not yet his place to push out into the thick of it, and held himself still, and waited.

First to take the lead was Shibata Katsuie, the senior man among Nobunaga's surviving retainers, oldest in years and in service, and the most respected. On the twenty-seventh of the sixth month, fourteen days after Mitsuhide's death, he opened a council at Kiyosu castle in Owari. There, Sanbōshi-maru, the son of Nobunaga's eldest son, Nobutada, had been brought from Gifu to live.

Those who gathered were Hashiba Chikuzen-no-kami Hideyoshi, Ikeda Shōnyūsai, Niwa Gorōzaemon-no-jō, Hachiya Dewa-no-kami, Takayama Ukon, Tsutsui Junkei, Nakagawa Sebei, Sakuma Genba, Maeda Matazaemon, Sassa Kuranosuke, Mori Katsuzō, Mōri Kawachi-no-kami, and others. None could read another's mind; they only traded glances, and not a man would open his mouth.

At length Shibata spoke. Young Nobutaka, Sanshichi Nobutaka, was concubine-born, true; but he was of a fit age, four-and-twenty, and quick-witted, and seemed to Shibata the best choice.

Hideyoshi answered him. Nobunaga's heir Nobutada had died by his own hand at the Nijō castle; but Nobutada's son, Sanbōshi-maru, was alive and well. Uphold the true line, or the submission of all men could not be counted on. So he pressed it, and the two opinions met head-on.

Then Niwa Nagahide put in his word. Even had Nobutada left no young lord, if there were a daughter, she should be raised up as heir; and if any of Nobutada's women went with child, they should wait upon the birth, and set the boy up as heir if a boy it proved. How much less, then, was there any question at all, with a true-born grandson-heir like Lord Sanbōshi ready to hand. And so he threw his weight behind Hideyoshi.

Niwa Nagahide was lord of Sawayama castle in Gōshū, forty-eight, a year younger than Nobunaga, an elder among all the Oda captains, and in prestige second only to Shibata. The alliance of Hideyoshi and Nagahide was a strong one indeed.

And Nagahide went on to add:

"Lord Shibata, there is no need to take it ill that I go against you. When our lord met his bitter end at Mitsuhide's hand, Hashiba Chikuzen-no-kami was away in Bitchū, in the Chūgoku, locked with that great enemy Mōri Terumoto. Yet he struck a deft peace, came back at a speed no man would credit, and in a single stroke cut Mitsuhide down. And you, Lord Shibata — you bestirred yourself and marched out of Kita-no-shō in Echizen only the day after Mitsuhide had fallen. Weighed so, and with Lord Hashiba's counsel squaring besides with the Five Constant Virtues — well, there it is."

His argument trampled flat before the whole assembly, Shibata was in a great rage, and set about a plot to have Hideyoshi killed. But Niwa caught wind of it and warned him; and Hideyoshi, feigning sickness, gave out that he was off to take the cure at the Arima hot springs, and went home.

Now, when his eldest son Nobutada died, Nobunaga had two sons left standing: Nobukatsu and Nobutaka. Four more came below them. Nobukatsu was of the same mother as Nobutada; Nobutaka was born of a low mother, and though he had in fact come into the world twenty days ahead of Nobukatsu, the report of his birth arrived late, and so he was counted the third son. And Nobukatsu being a dull stick, the ambitious Nobutaka set his own heart on succeeding Nobunaga, and had thrown in with Shibata.

The upshot of the council at Kiyosu was this. Nobutada's orphaned son Sanbōshi was made the successor; until a temporary residence could be raised at Azuchi he was kept at Gifu, given three hundred thousand koku of Ōmi for his fief; and the four (Shibata, Hashiba, Niwa, Ikeda) each set an official of his own in Kyoto to rule on the common business, and all bound themselves by written oath to stand together in concord and uphold the young lord; and so they went home to their provinces.

But Hideyoshi stayed on in Kyoto and would not budge. And so, in the nature of things, power gathered into Hideyoshi's hands.

When the tenth month came, to make that mastery firmer still, he took it upon his own initiative to hold Nobunaga's funeral. This was the famous seventeen-day rite at the Daitoku-ji in Murasakino, one grand demonstration, in a word, of "I, and no other, am Nobunaga's heir." Hideyoshi reckoned that the captains would each have business at home they could not leave, and that even without it not one would come bowing and scraping to a summons of his; and so he sent his invitations out. Pray attend.

When one reached Hamamatsu, Ieyasu, wanting at the least to have the whole of it looked into closely, summoned Hanzō and gave the order, and Hanzō passed it to Goemon.

It was an easy journey with nothing to weigh on him, and Goemon set out for Kyoto taking his pleasure in the autumn along the road. Everywhere the maples were beautiful. Hills and fields, to the farthest eye, lay under a brocade of crimson and gold.

Coming into Kyoto by the Yamashina road, he went to look at the burned ruins of the Honnō-ji. A hundred and twenty-four days had passed since that day, and still, without so much as a fence about them, the pillars and the stone terrace stood there, burned black and blistered, bared to the autumn wind. At the place that must have been Nobunaga's bedchamber, Goemon stood with his arms folded.

And into his ears came all the sounds of that time. Nobunaga's death-agony face rose up before him, vivid. The face at the instant when Goemon's blade had laid it open aslant, from above the wing of the nose, so that the lower half hung slack, and the two eyes, all that was left whole of it, turned to a look piteous and tearful, begging for mercy. And the next instant, into those two eyes, this same Goemon's own hand had driven a spike-shuriken, one and then the other. And Nobunaga, Right Minister of the realm, whom none in power could match, became a single dying beast and went down into the blazing pit.

Ah, this loneliness, this solitude: what a hateful thing it was. A cold wind came blowing into Goemon's breast, a wind that hollowed it out and froze his heart through with a desolation beyond all bearing. At such a time a man loses, utterly, the strength to live.

Yes. Ever since he was born, again and again this hateful feeling had come down on Goemon. A human being, all unknowing, is at every instant making the effort to live; the inborn instinct, whatever the case, strains toward living. And when this loneliness, this solitude, comes down on him, a man loses the strength to live, and is no longer a man, but a thing.

Now I have lost the very instinct that makes me a man. And that awareness itself unmanned him further—

Since he had won Maki, this cold wind had never once come to Goemon. Struck by it now, out of nowhere, he was thrown into confusion.

"Serves you right! You mad dog! No man — a dog. No, not even a dog. A monster! A fiend out of hell!" By whipping up his old rage against Nobunaga he tried to drive the cold wind off. But, a thing to marvel at, there was no rage against Nobunaga left in his breast at all. Only a vague, shapeless pity, and nothing else.

When he was cut to pieces by this hand of mine, and blinded, and went down from this floor into the sea of flame below, what heat did he feel? It wrapped his whole body in an instant and burned it to a cinder; but in that little while, what agony must he have tasted? Goemon felt it now as though in his own flesh, and gooseflesh rose in a shudder over the whole of him.

A bare thirteen years after this, Goemon himself would be put to a far crueler death, boiled alive in the cauldron. Did some presentiment whisper it to him? No. There could be no such thing. Seen so, it was only one more sign that Goemon had never quite made himself into a true ninja, and that now, more than ever, he was sliding down the ninja's downward line.

And so, buffeted by that cold wind, Goemon left the burned ruins of the Honnō-ji behind him in haste, and turned north up the Horikawa.

For Nobunaga's funeral, to open the next day, they had chosen the Daitoku-ji, the great Zen monastery at the far north edge of the capital.

As he came near, carriages, ox-carts, and hand-carts wove back and forth thick as a loom, and the sheer scale of it knocked the breath from him. Three chō short of the main gate a temporary guard-post had gone up, where foot-soldiers stood watch with oaken staves seven shaku five sun long. Goemon made a full circuit: the posts ringed the whole Daitoku-ji, and no one without a pass got through. Patrols tramped their heavy rounds through the streets.

He withdrew to his inn in Higashiyama, and listened to the talk.

"Lord Hashiba's glory — it's a grand thing, no? While the rest of the captains dither, he holds the funeral off his own single strength."

"Neither Lord Nobukatsu nor Lord Nobutaka can hold his head up in front of Lord Chikuzen."

"The master of the realm's Lord Hashiba, right enough."

Nothing but voices of that kind.

That night Goemon dressed himself in shinobi black and stole into the Daitoku-ji. A wind was blowing; torn clouds ran across the sky, and the moon went behind them again and again. On such a night the art of ten-ton, the heaven-hiding art, works best of all. It is the art of using every sign of the heavens to slip the enemy's eye: the light of sun, moon, and stars, of course, but wind, thunder, rain, snow, cloud, and mist all make powerful instruments too. An untrained eye, when light and dark trade places fast, is dazzled for an instant and loses its sight, and it is that instant one seizes.

Face the wind, and the eyes narrow of their own accord; the field of sight shrinks by that much, and the sight grows weaker. So it stands to reason. And an eye that has stared long into a strong light is bound to tire. To reckon all this finely, and turn each condition to use, that is what a trained man makes of the art of ten-ton.

Goemon used the instants when the running clouds shuttered the moonlight and let it through again, and ran on: out of the light into the dark, out of the dark into the light. Heedless of the crowd of guards, he crossed the main gate boldly from the Ōmiya-dōri side and passed beneath the mountain gate.

The Buddha-hall at the front was a great structure, five ken square; but he had heard the rite would be held, from the next day, in the lecture-hall beyond it. So Goemon made his way to the lecture-hall, a two-storied building, hip-and-gable roofed, seven ken by six, climbed lightly to the roof, broke through the gable, and slipped into the space above the ceiling. This lecture-hall had been rebuilt about a hundred years before by the priest Ikkyū: a great building, austere and splendid. (The one that stands today was rebuilt again some fifty-odd years after that.)

In one corner of the great hall's ceiling he bored a hole with his kunai and looked down. Deep in the night though it was, great candles were burning, and a dozen and more people were up and busy with the next day's preparations. Knowing this was the very place he was after, Goemon set his mind at ease and lay down to sleep on a beam.

When morning came and he looked down again, Goemon caught his breath.

The floor was laid throughout with square black tiles in the Chinese fashion, and countless thick round pillars held up the ceiling. At the front stood a high dais, railed all about, climbed by railed stairs on either hand. Full before the dais, on a great table of rosewood, wrapped in gold brocade and studded with gold and silver, stood a splendid coffin.

Search the burned ruins of the Honnō-ji as they might, not one bone had come to light, so what, Goemon wondered, biting back a laugh, could be lying inside that magnificent coffin? An old book, the Koretō Taiji-ki, says with a perfectly straight face, in words that would have set Goemon roaring, that, the honored bones being wanting, a Buddha-image was carved from aloeswood and laid reverently within the coffin. The Shinsho Taikōki says nothing of the coffin at all. Before the sacred image, it writes, they set out rare delicacies, dragon's liver, phoenix marrow, and the flowers and fruits of hill and field; they raised lotuses of gold and sal-trees of silver; in lion-shaped censers of gold they burned the finest sinking-grade aloeswood; and on a cloud-footed table of rosewood they spread Shokkō brocade and set the mortuary tablet upon it. Of the trappings, and the trappings only, it tells.

The vast hall was already packed tight with Hideyoshi's close captains and samurai, gathered, it seemed, before the day had broken. Goemon put his head out through the gable to see how things stood without. Even the broad precincts of the Daitoku-ji lay buried under people, thick as cloud and mist.

Some thirty thousand guardsmen, by the look of them, ringed the precinct in a solid wall, bows and guns in hand; and beyond them again swarmed the crowd, high and low together.

Having seen this much, Goemon drew his head in from the gable and returned to the hole in the ceiling-boards. The hall was filling more and more. Every man of them alike wore the eboshi cap and, on his body, the fujigoromo, a mourning robe of kudzu-cloth.

Which one is Hideyoshi? Goemon made his eyes wide as saucers. He had never once, to this day, set eyes on Hideyoshi, the man who would be his enemy in years to come, the man for whose sake he would lose his own life. He knew only, by hearsay, that he was a short man with a face like a monkey's.

Nobunaga, now shrunk to a mortuary tablet, had, five or six years before, sent this letter to Hideyoshi's wife, Nene:

{v} It was good of you to come all the way to Azuchi. And I thank you for the many fine gifts. Your looks and your carriage have grown twice as handsome as when I saw you last, and I was quite struck by it. Tōkichirō, they tell me, has his complaints — an outrage; a shameful thing altogether. Search where he might, that bald rat could never find himself a wife to match you. So from this day take your ease; carry yourself grandly, as a lady should, and do not fly into little fits of jealousy. And show this letter to Hashiba too, and give him your mind on it.

The letter carries the vermilion seal and passes for a sound historical source; and by it, Nobunaga called the forty-year-old Chikuzen-no-kami, lord of Nagahama and its hundred and twenty thousand koku, "the bald rat."

Look at the portraits of Hideyoshi that have come down to us: the narrow brow, the sunken eyes, the hollow cheeks, the short pointed chin with its thin, straggling beard. More rat than monkey, past doubt. And when he went darting and scuttling about, he was nearer a rat than ever.

Presently the incense-smoke rose in rolling clouds, the great bell boomed and boomed, the monks' voices lifted in the reading of the sutras, and the memorial rite began. When the time came to offer incense, first of all a lean little man came out, squaring his shoulders and cocking his elbows to make a show of dignity, in short scurrying steps. This, then, is Hideyoshi! And Goemon fixed his eyes on him.

The whole hall of mourners followed the man's every motion with eyes of deep reverence. His face and figure were mean, his movements light and quick; and yet, for all that, the vital force that brimmed from his whole body, the taut ferocity of his presence, pressed hard, up even into the breast of Goemon, watching from beyond the small hole in that high ceiling, where an ink-drawn dragon lay coiled full within a great circle.

"What a fellow!" Goemon marveled, and his tongue curled in his head.

This day's show of strength was the plain, blunt, single-minded overflow of one ambition: to drive it home to the whole realm that, with Nobunaga gone, the realm was Hashiba Chikuzen-no-kami Hideyoshi's; and to hold down Nobunaga's own surviving sons, and Shibata and the other veteran captains with them. Through the whole long order of the rite, Goemon watched no one but Hideyoshi.

That Nobunaga, before the assembled captains, would call him "Monkey, Monkey," and that he for his part would rather gladly bow to the beck and call with "Aye, aye" this much Goemon had heard. But of the "bald rat" he had not known; and gazing hard now, he had marveled to himself: The fellow is a rat to the life.

Goemon had had little to do with monkeys. Among the ninja there were men skilled in the handling of beasts, who worked with monkeys, Kashigi, the father of Kashii, was one. But since Goemon spent his life hidden above ceilings, it was rats he met with, day in and day out; monkeys, by comparison, he scarcely knew. Small wonder his thoughts ran to the one and not the other.

If a prime minister of Japan today were to feel that, having come this far, the highest wish he was born into the world to reach had been reached, and to plume himself besides as the very model of the highest a man can be, it would be an absurd thing, a thing to laugh at. But in the Japan of the warring age, scientific truth, philosophical thought, a view of the world such as we know had not yet been born; so that "taking the realm," "becoming the first man under Heaven," should be a Japanese's highest ideal seems, in that day, far more reasonable than it would seem now.

And more: Goemon himself had no wish beyond living happily with Maki. So he did not look on this spectacle of Hideyoshi's seething ambition and find it childish and endearing. Only, the sheer force of one man made so pure, so plain, so wholly bent upon a single end, his whole spirit strung taut toward it, bore down on Goemon head-on.

When the long rite was over at last and the people had all dispersed, Goemon went back to his inn and made the rounds with his ear open to the talk, working to gather it as exactly as he could, from the great monks, the famous captains, the wealthy merchants.

By what he heard, Hideyoshi had spent an enormous sum on the rite, gifts to the imperial court and all; in incense-money alone he had laid out fifty pieces of gold, three hundred of silver, ten thousand kan of copper coin, a thousand bales of polished rice. He had raised the Sōken-in within the Daitoku-ji, put up eleven hundred pieces of silver toward its upkeep, made over fifty koku of temple land, and given a thousand kan besides as sword-money.

A single piece of gold, around Tenshō 10, was reckoned worth thirty-five koku of rice; so fifty pieces came to one thousand seven hundred and fifty koku. And that, in the Kyoto measure of the late Tokugawa years when the Shinsho Taikōki was written, was one thousand six hundred and eighty koku, four thousand two hundred bales at four to the bale, so they say. I am poor at figures myself, and had rather someone with a head for them worked it into present-day prices.

At all events it was, past doubt, a funeral on a grand scale, and for a while Kyoto, and all Japan with it, could speak of nothing else.

The Shinsho Taikōki makes the whole funeral over into a novel. It sets Nobukatsu and Nobutaka, Shibata, Niwa, Takigawa, Maeda, every one of them, in attendance, and rigs up a quarrel over the order of offering incense. By Shibata Katsuie's urging, it says, the first offering should fall to Nobukatsu. But since Nobutaka was Nobukatsu's elder, the two should offer together. And as Nobukatsu stepped forward and laid his hand on the incense-packet, Takigawa Shōgen put in that, Nobutaka being the elder brother, the two should take it side by side; and just as they reached to take up the packet, from beyond the curtain, someone shouted, "Hold! Hold the incense a while!"

The whole company was struck dumb, and as they cried out in alarm, what is this?, the curtain was quietly drawn aside, and Hideyoshi came forth, bearing in his arms the young Lord Sanbōshi, now seven years old. And here Hideyoshi lets fly a mighty harangue.

"Lord Nobukatsu — soft by nature — you marched out to avenge your own father, Lord Nobunaga, and turned back halfway: what conduct do you call that? You made no search for Lord Nobunaga's body; you never so much as thought of a funeral: what unfilial creature is this? And you, Lord Nobutaka, though you lay nearer to Akechi than ever I did, would not give battle, but waited for Hideyoshi to come marching up and then trailed at his heels: what cowardice is that? To have thought of no memorial for the dead — there you are Lord Nobukatsu's twin. Step up before the mortuary tablet now, and your dead father will take no joy of it. Keep back a while, behind the men who earned their place!"

At this Katsuie flew into a great rage. "To fling such insolence at the two young lords — it passes all bounds! I'll throttle him!" And he rose to his feet.

The whole company held its breath, what would come of it?, when the young Lord Sanbōshi, his eyes flashing wide, You hateful old man, laid his small hand on the hilt of his sword. And even Katsuie, in spite of himself, gave ground; overawed, he held his peace, and the great lords and the small trembled their tongues in awe. A martial bearing sat on the child of itself, the very image of a captain of the realm. And here Hideyoshi, pressing his advantage, made to rise.

At that instant, far off, a great crash of massed gunfire rolled and rolled, dadadaan.

What is it? The people ran out from the hall and looked up. The gunsmoke was as red and white dragons running through the clouds; over Kyoto within the walls and without, over the peaks of Higashiyama and Nishiyama, great banners and small streamed and snapped in the wind; north and south the conch-horns and the bells rang out fearfully; and a host of hidden soldiers sprang up and ringed the Daitoku-ji round, till there was not a chink for an ant to creep out by. Katsuie too caught his breath with an "Ah!" and rolled his eyes white.

"Lord Shibata is an old man, and has come the whole long way up from Echizen; his shoulders and his loins must ache sorely. Chikuzen would knead them for him, but today he is the young lord's guardian. Katō, Fukushima — rub him down, and thoroughly."

At the word, Katō Toranosuke, Fukushima Ichimatsu, Katagiri Sukesaku and the rest of the strong-arms answered "Aye!" and came running, crowding in: "We'll rub you, we'll rub you." Kneaded by devils like these, a man's flesh and bone would come apart. The lowest seat, anywhere, it makes no matter, only let me offer my incense, and Shibata took the lowest seat, made his offering in haste and anyhow, and "like a wet black rat, scurried off home; a fine and satisfying thing to see" so the tale runs.

Hideyoshi wore a black robe, and on his head an ornament of twill shaped like an opened fan; a fearsomely long sword of black lacquer hung at his hip; and, holding the small Sanbōshi-maru, dressed all in white twill and white hakama, he stood firm on a place raised a step above the rest, and Shibata and the rest, in their tall eboshi caps, throwing up their hands: Look! Look! Pictures of this scene I remember well from my boyhood, a great many of them, in the boys' magazines of the day, in the picture-history books, and even, I think, in the schoolbooks. But at the time, Nobukatsu, Nobutaka, Shibata and the rest were each in his own province, and not one of them came to Kyoto. So the whole thing was a fabrication. It is a fiction got up to flatter the common feeling, the feeling that cannot rest until the favorite is made ever grander and the villain ever blacker, and it is Shibata Katsuie who comes off worst by it.

Now, Goemon, having watched Nobunaga's funeral and gathered the run of the talk about it, went back to Hamamatsu and made his report to Hanzō. And after that, somehow, Hideyoshi would not leave his head.

Why should the face of that little rat of a man, and his quick, scuttling movements, keep flickering before his eyes? Between that man and me there's no clash of interest at all. And yet why does his phantom flicker so? Whether the fellow takes the realm or not is nothing to me. It's the first time I ever laid eyes on him.

That face and figure, and the great thing his taut vital force was bent upon, and those movements, they did not balance. An unbalanced man like that he had surely never seen before; and the imbalance was of a kind that, at a glance, sinks into the heart and stays.

But was that enough, in itself, to brand itself into the head of a man like Goemon, a man with no political ambition of his own, and so no curiosity, no yearning, after the famous? Seen so, it must have been the man's single-minded straining of his whole spirit, its very pathos, its gallantry, that took Goemon's heart. Both Goemon and I are materialists, and realists; so I will not have it that the fate by which, in years to come, the two of them would play for each other's lives had bitten into his heart already as a "presentiment."

Of this first meeting of Hideyoshi and Goemon, Namiki Gohei wrote, a hundred and ninety-seven years later, in a kabuki play: the famous Kinmon Gosan no Kiri, in whose second act, second scene, the two are made to meet at the mountain gate of the Nanzen-ji. The scene was so great a hit that it was later cut loose to stand on its own, its title changed to Sanmon Gosan no Kiri, and has been played over and over. The late Enjaku made it the crowning role of his life; and this very month, September of 1963, at the Shin-Kabuki-za in Osaka, the third Enjaku plays it in the art his father handed down.

As the gate-tower is pushed out from the back of the stage, Goemon, a padded gown thrown over his chain-mail, a hundred-day wig on his head, sits drawing at a long pipe and gazing out at the view, and speaks the famous soliloquy: "To call the prospect of spring worth a thousand in gold is to price it too cheap. For Goemon it is worth ten thousand ryō. The sun leans westward now, and the cherry of a spring evening is lovelier, and lovelier yet. Ah — what a soft and radiant prospect it is."

(It was only later that the line became "What a view! What a view!") Then, to a drum-roll, dorodoro, a hawk comes flying in and settles on the railing. The dorodoro is the stock sound for the coming of a ghost or an uncanny thing, made by brushing the great drum with the stick. In the old days nearly all the sound-effects came from that one great drum: rain, snow, waterfall, the running of a river, all of them rendered by changing only the manner of the stroke; and the audience knew the code, and took it at once, that beat, and it means this.

"Now, what's this?" Goemon catches the hawk and looks: on the white markings of its wing-feathers, letters are written. He reads them, and, a thing to wonder at, is it not a secret writing in the hand of his own vanished father?

The father's writing runs:

{v} I am one Sō Sokei, a subject of Shinsō, the twelfth emperor of the great Ming. I crossed to Japan carrying a plot to overthrow this country and make it Ming land; but the labor of long years has come to nothing, and this very day the plot stands exposed. Though I perish to no purpose in a foreign land, my son, whom I left behind in that country, has followed me over to Japan, they say, in longing for me — and yet I have not been able to meet him. But this son of mine was born fierce and fearless. So, by the token of the incense-wood called ranjaboku that I left with him for a keepsake, seek him out, I beg of you, and tell him of my bitter grief, and let him strike down Hideyoshi for me—

So it reads.

Reading it, Goemon is thunderstruck. For Goemon is the son of that same Sō Sokei of Ming. When he was a child his father had crossed to Japan, and he, longing for him, had crossed after; by chance he fell into the care of Akechi Mitsuhide, grew up, and was raised to be a captain. But Mitsuhide, to whom he owed so deep a debt, had been destroyed by Hideyoshi at the battle of Yamazaki; and so Goemon, to avenge him, had turned thief and gathered a band about him.

And now Goemon grinds his teeth. "Villain Hideyoshi! For my father's bitter grief, for Mitsuhide's grudge — though the whole design come to light, though this body be boiled in oil, the flesh melt from it and the bones be crushed to powder, shall I leave this bitterness unavenged?"

He hooks his sword over the railing, throws down the pipe, plants his right foot on the rail, half-draws the blade, and pulls his left eye up toward the bridge of his nose, up until the pupil is all but hidden, and draws the right pupil in hard toward the nose as well; slowly he rolls his head around, and, snap, strikes the grand mie.

The clappers sound, chon, chon, chon, and the gate-tower rises, higher and higher, until the lower part of it comes into view. There, on the stone steps directly beneath the gate, stands Hideyoshi, got up as a pilgrim, a pilgrim's over-cloth on his back, a sedge-hat in his left hand, and in his right a staff with a brush lashed to its tip, scrawling on the gate-pillar. He finishes, and as he reaches the edge of the hanamichi, Goemon, with a great show of "Now, what is this?", looks down. Hideyoshi turns, and reads out the verse he has written on the pillar.

"Ishikawa — though the sands of the beach be spent—"

Goemon: "Ha — what's this?"

Hideyoshi: "—the seed of thieves in the world shall never run dry."

One glaring up, the other down: face meets face. "So — it is you," and Goemon throws a shuriken. Hideyoshi catches it with a smack on the ladle in his right hand: "Alms for the pilgrim." Both strike the mie on the instant, and, chon, chon, chon, chon, the curtain falls. So runs the scene: hugely old-fashioned, heavy with meaning, symbolic, and very short.

It is a long way from any "presentiment": the two act in full knowledge of each other's birth and of all that is to come. Staged in a kabuki where such unnaturalness is the very rule of the form, it has gone on living now a hundred and eighty-five years. Why has this one scene lived so long, since it was first put on in the fourth month of An'ei 7, in the day of the tenth shogun, Ieharu, at the Kado theater in Osaka, the present Dōtonbori Kadoza? Because the wildly fanciful conceit satisfies the curiosity of the house: the great thief declaiming atop the grand gate, the cherries in full bloom below him, face to face with Hideyoshi, the man who would go on to take the realm, and to put Goemon to the cruel death of the cauldron.

The stage this time was the lecture-hall of the Daitoku-ji, no less grand than the mountain gate of the Nanzen-ji; and there, in pitch-black shinobi garb, Goemon lay flattened in the dust above the ceiling. But in truth it was nothing so gaudy. In the thick of Nobunaga's funeral though it was, the rite that had stirred the eyes and ears of the whole realm, Goemon —

And what is more, Hideyoshi did not so much as know that Goemon existed.

If we are to mean, by their "first meeting," an occasion when Hideyoshi looked on Goemon and knew him for what he was, then we must wait another thirteen years: to the night, in the innermost bedchamber of Yodo castle, when Hideyoshi, roused from the Western bed the Nanban traders had brought him by the sense of someone near, sprang up in the dark and, peering through the keyhole of the great bronze door that Spain had made him a gift of, caught one glimpse of Goemon, trussed hand and foot. For Hideyoshi, that is to say, the first meeting was the last; and each of them, moreover, had spied the other through a hole.

Now, Hideyoshi, never dreaming that his image had burned itself into Goemon's breast, was wholly given over to shoring up his own camp.

Under his banner stood Ikeda Shōnyū; Hosokawa Fujitaka and his son Tadaoki; Gamō Katahide and his son Ujisato; Tsutsui Junkei; Niwa Nagahide; and the rest. On the other side, the anti-Hideyoshi power drew together about Shibata Katsuie, with Nobutaka at its head, and Takigawa Kazumasu, Sassa Narimasa, Maeda Toshiie, Sakuma Morimasa rallying to it. But these lay up in the northern country while Hideyoshi held fast in Kyoto; and, fret as they might, with winter closing in it was hard to march troops down out of the north. So, taking Takigawa Kazumasu's counsel, they resolved to patch up a peace with Hideyoshi for the while, and to wait for spring and force the matter to a decision.

But Nobutaka held Sanbōshi captive at Gifu; and there was this besides. Nobunaga's sister, the Lady Oichi, had been a widow ever since Hideyoshi, nine years before, at Nobunaga's command, had stormed and destroyed Azai Nagamasa at Odani castle in Ōmi, the Lady Yodo is her daughter, and now, at his earnest asking, gave her in marriage to Katsuie, and set steadily about a plan to make Katsuie the master of the realm and pit him against Hideyoshi.

Hideyoshi, for his part, on the seventh of the twelfth month marched out of Kyoto at the head of thirty thousand men, brought Shibata Katsutoyo, Katsuie's adopted son, to heel at Nagahama, entered Mino by the eighteenth, and bore down on Gifu. Nobutaka lost his nerve, handed over his mother, his daughter, and others as hostages, and sued for peace. Hideyoshi's people carried the hostages, with Sanbōshi, off to Azuchi, set Nobukatsu up as guardian, and on the twenty-ninth returned in triumph.

All this doing of Hideyoshi's, Ieyasu watched, a face of perfect unconcern on the surface, and underneath a mounting impatience.

The report on the Daitoku-ji funeral had been full and to the point; so this time, thinking the absence might run a little long, Ieyasu gave Goemon his order: go and see the reckoning between Hideyoshi and Katsuie through to its end. To watch Hideyoshi's doings at close range held a strong pull for him; and so, hating though he did to be parted from Maki, Goemon bowed to it without a word, and in the early morning of the thirtieth of the twelfth month, the year at its very close, set out for Kyoto. There was no saying he might not be caught up in the whirl of the realm-deciding battle, so he furnished himself fully with his shinobi gear.

At Azuchi he learned that Hideyoshi was at the Takara-dera castle in Yamazaki. The next day would be New Year's Eve. In the guise of a mountain ascetic he made his way there, and put up at an inn along the highroad, a bare three ri south of Kyoto. When he woke in the morning, Goemon strolled out of doors.

Two hundred days before, it was here that Hideyoshi's army and Mitsuhide's had fought. When Goemon killed Nobunaga at the Honnō-ji he had run straight on and away, so of the battle here he had only the rumor.

Now Goemon stood on the Saikoku road, the Yodo river at his back. Before him rose Mount Tennō, two hundred and seventy meters above the sea, looming inky black; behind him, across the Yodo, hunched Otokoyama, a hundred and forty. Between the two mountains the Yodo runs through a narrow defile of a bare ten chō. It is, precisely, the gate between the plain of Kyoto and Yamashiro and the plain of Osaka. But a barrier like this is a stronghold only for the moment, in the military sense; it is no ground on which to raise a lasting capital. So the Takara-dera castle, thrown up on the mid-slope of Mount Tennō, was no more than a temple that had stood there already, turned in haste, for the time, into a fort.

Hideyoshi's army had taken Mount Tennō and the Takara-dera castle first, and from there fell upon Mitsuhide's army, encamped at the eastern foot of the hill. Mitsuhide's eighteen thousand and Hideyoshi's twenty-six thousand crashed together from about four in the afternoon, and by seven the matter was decided. More than six thousand dead, of the two armies together, dyed the earth of that place crimson.
