## A Few Notes on the Shanghai Workers' Movement and the Gangs

{d} Zhu Xuefan

### I. The Gangs Were a Major Problem in Labor Work in Old Shanghai

Looking back on the labor work he took up in Shanghai in 1924, Li Lisan once said that the biggest problem in working among Shanghai's workers was the gangs. The first time he organized, he ignored the Green Gang, and the capitalists used it to break the workers' struggle at the Rihua Cotton Mill in Pudong. The second time, Li Qihan joined the Green Gang, but he trusted it too far, the gang bosses sold him out, and that failed too. So Li Lisan studied the history of the Shanghai labor movement and turned on the Green Gang the same methods he had used against the Hong Gang at Anyuan; and in the May Thirtieth Movement, the men who protected him were Green Gang disciples. He arrived in Shanghai the same year I passed into the Shanghai Post Office as a junior clerk. The Green Gang had no foothold in the post office yet, and I knew nothing about it. Only later did I join, set up a branch of my own, and use the gang to work among the men; and I came to understand fairly well how the gangs and the labor movement fit together, and to feel the truth of what Li Lisan had said. In my later dealings with trade unions abroad, too, I was struck by how the gang problem that dogged the Chinese labor movement, tangled as it was, had almost no parallel anywhere else in the world.

Of all the cities in China before liberation, Shanghai had the most workers in jobs, the most out of work, and the most who had joined the gangs. After 1932, as the world depression bit, many of the city's factories closed, unemployment was severe, and working people struggled desperately to stay alive. The Shanghai Post Office once held a hiring exam for letter carriers: there were only 68 openings, but more than 2,400 men applied. In the winter of 1936, 3,000 sat the exam for junior postal-clerk posts and only about 50 were taken. People called a customs job a "golden rice bowl," a bank job a "silver rice bowl," and a post-office job an "iron rice bowl," and the last drew no shortage of graduates—from primary school, middle school, and college alike—who had dropped out, lost their jobs, or were still looking for work. But holding on to that rice bowl, even inside the post office, was not easy, and the carriers above all could be fired at any time: a foreign firm had only to find fault with a carrier's delivery and write to the foreign postmaster, and an "iron rice bowl" was smashed. So many postal staff joined the gangs, and the gang factions there ran so deep, for one big reason beyond the factions' own scramble for power and turf: the men joined to get a backer who would protect their livelihoods. The outdoor carriers, who delivered letters and hauled the mail sacks down to the docks, had their own safety to worry about as well, and needed a protector all the more. And the carriers' work varied so much in ease that some men joined the gang just to have someone speak up for them—to be handed lighter duties, or to move up a pay grade a little faster. (The post office raised wages on a fixed schedule, and the pace of promotion could differ by half a year among a postal assistant, a postal cadet, and a postal clerk.) A few, finally, joined simply to skim a little on the side.

Employees of the British Tramways Company joined the gangs in fair numbers too, and for the same main reason: to keep a rice bowl and stave off unemployment. A tram job did not rank with one in the customs or the post office, but when a man landed a place with the British tram company, his friends would congratulate him warmly, since such a spot was hard to come by. Like the customs and the post office, the tram company had a retirement fund, a kind of old-age pension. At the post office, an employee with 25 years' service could retire on a pension of 25 months' pay, figured on his last month's wages, with another half-month for every year past 25. The tram company's pension was smaller: a man who had served 15 years and reached the age of 55 retired on half a month's pay for each year served, and at 20 years' service, a full month for each year. But tram workers, like customs and postal staff, found that pension hard to actually collect, since a man who was fired got nothing—and the tram company would sack a worker over the smallest thing. In the five short months of 1937, from the Battle of August Thirteenth to the year's end, more than a hundred were let go; and that winter the company fired union delegates and union stalwarts without cause and, leaning on the patrol house, had them run out of the city. It handed out punishments of every kind at will. A conductor might be hauled into the office several times a month, sometimes a dozen, to be dressed down by a foreign clerk or fined anywhere from a few dimes to a dollar or two—a penalty known in the men's slang as "eating a big dinner" or "seeing the King of Hell." To get such a fine eased, a conductor had to have someone plead with a senior clerk or an inspector afterward, and send gifts. Another punishment was called "docking a man's shift": he was ordered to stop work and his pay stopped with it. Let a conductor be suspected of pocketing a fare, waving one through, or ringing up the wrong one, or let a foreign passenger write in to complain, and, right or wrong, he lost several shifts—no pay, and made to report to the company every day and wait, three or four days or a dozen, until he was put back on. Too many dockings in a year and he was fired. Drivers, mechanics, and inspectors all lived under the same threat of punishment, even dismissal.

This insecurity, this being pushed around and squeezed at the whim of capitalists Chinese and foreign, ran through every trade; in some, a man had no guarantee even of his own safety and could be beaten and trampled. So people had to find some source of solidarity and mutual aid to protect themselves, and joining a gang was one form it took.

Gangs go back a long way in China. Under the old feudal order they were a primitive kind of secret popular society, bound together under a patriarchal head into groups of many different names, some of them armed. People used them for mutual help in the business of living, and at times to rise against the officials and landlords who oppressed them. By modern times their membership had widened from mostly ruined peasants, out-of-work craftsmen, and drifters to take in industrial workers, intellectuals, and even small and middling Chinese businessmen; and their standing shifted, step by step, from a secret existence toward the open and the legal. That change in their makeup and their standing gave them new life and made them a force of their own in society.

By one estimate, about 20 percent of the post office's staff had joined the gangs, and the share among the city's workers as a whole was probably higher still. Add to the gang members the brotherhoods, sisterhoods, and Guandi societies that workers formed on their own in every trade and workplace, along with the native-place cliques—the Cantonese, Ningbo, Shandong, Hubei, and Subei gangs—and the share of the city's whole workforce grows larger yet. In these groups, large and small and of every name, plenty of people had ties to the gangs and looked to them for backing. The brotherhoods, sisterhoods, Guandi societies, and native-place cliques were all bodies of mutual aid for working people in their daily lives and their economic struggles. This was no accident; it grew out of all the economic and political conditions of China's semi-feudal, semi-colonial history—working people needed to band together and help one another in order to survive and protect their interests. They could not, of course, find any real way out through brotherhoods, sisterhoods, Guandi societies, or gangs. When they were unwilling to attach themselves to the Kuomintang but had not yet come far enough to draw close to the Communist Party, they took up these lawful, traditional bodies as a way to protect themselves for the time being. And because the gangs were backward and narrow by nature, and in some real tension even with the interests of the working people who joined them, they were plainly a transitional thing in the course of the workers' united struggle, and might become one of the objects of the united front.

There was also, within the gangs, a strain set against the interests of working people—what Li Lisan called the class struggle inside the gangs. It is a point the study of the unions ought to keep in mind.

### II. How I Used My Ties to Du Yuesheng to Work Among the Workers

#### (i) How I Came into Du's Circle

Once the Postal Workers' Union had become one of Shanghai's seven big yellow unions (the other six were the unions of the Commercial Press printing plant, the Commercial Press distribution office, the newspaper trade, the Nanyang Tobacco factory, the British-American Tobacco factory, and the China General Electric Company), Lu Jingshi—to tighten his grip on the postal union and make a name for himself among the seven—picked eleven men (twelve counting himself) from the Kuomintang government's Ministry of Communications, the Directorate General of Posts, and the Shanghai Post Office. In 1931, through the introduction of Chen Junyi—an executive committeeman of the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office, chairman of its Committee on Staff and Workers' Affairs, and a disciple of Du Yuesheng—they took Du as their master. The first group was Lu and myself, both postal cadets. The second was the postal cadets Zhao Shusheng, Zhang Kechang, and Xiao Qingsong; the postal clerk Fu Dewei; Yang Ketian of the Ministry of Communications; Miao Hongjun, an inspector at the Directorate General of Posts; Liu Xinquan and Yu Songqiao, postal assistants at the Shanghai Post Office; the carrier Xu Ziyi; and the office boy Yang Deling. Du took it as a mark of prestige that post-office men should come under his door, for the post office was run by the Chinese government, its director was a foreigner, and it stood well in society.

Soon after we joined Du's circle, the ancestral hall he had built at Gaoqiao was finished, and every notable of the party and government, from Chiang Kai-shek down, sent gifts and congratulations. Lu Jingshi and I could not afford a rich present, so we hit on a way to spend little and still do Du honor: we cut a commemorative postmark for the opening of the Du ancestral hall, to be used only at the Gaoqiao postal sub-office. It was a novel touch for the time, people found it clever, and Du Yuesheng, taking it that the post office too was paying him respect, was pleased. Cutting a commemorative postmark privately was against post-office rules, but with the party and government dignitaries all crowding to congratulate Du, and the whole thing the talk of Shanghai, the postal authorities saw which way the wind was blowing and let it pass.

Soon after entering Du's door, I helped found the Heng Society. The name was Zhang Shizhao's, taken from the phrase "constant as the moon in its fullness," in praise of Du Yuesheng. Lu Jingshi laid down that every Heng Society member had to have a certain social standing—not every disciple who came under Du's door would be made a member. The society had a round emblem: a bronze bell in the center, and on the face of the bell, dead center, a new moon. The bell stands for the source of sound; "sound" (sheng) is a homophone of the sheng in Du's name, and with the moon (yue) added it makes "Yuesheng." A council ran the society's affairs, and Lu Jingshi and I were both standing members.

#### (ii) Founding the Dagong News Agency

From the 1920s into the early 1930s, Shanghai had not one news agency devoted to the labor movement. In 1932 I became chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union. It was just after the January Twenty-Eighth fighting; the war had driven Shanghai's industry and commerce deeper into slump, and capitalists Chinese and foreign were trying to shift all their losses onto the workers—longer hours, lower wages, closed plants and suspended business, dismissals and layoffs—until the unemployed ran into the hundreds of thousands. To survive, the workers rose in struggle one after another; strikes broke out trade by trade and plant by plant, but with little to show for it, and most failed. Beyond the owners' stubbornness and the crackdown by Kuomintang troops and police, a big reason was that the strikes were mostly spontaneous and scattered, without mutual support, and so had little weight.

Coming out of the post office, I knew how much getting the word out mattered, so I set up the Dagong News Agency to gather strike news and put out daily dispatches on the important doings of the labor movement. It was housed at Huayifang on Jiujiang Road. I was its head, Shao Xubai its editor, and Shi Xiangya ran general affairs; there were two reporters at first, Lu Muqin and Yu Yaoqiu, and later Shao Jicu. The General Labor Union had its office in the Puye Guild hall opposite the Little World in Nanshi, and every evening I went on to the news agency—straight from the union or from wherever I happened to be, but every night without fail—to go over the dispatches with Shao Xubai. Through Heng Society brothers at the main papers—Yu Kongwo at the *Shen Bao*, Tang Shichang at the *Xinwen Bao*, Jiang Xiangqing at the *Shishi Xinbao*, and the brothers Ge Zhongyi and Jiang Zhongdao at the *Shi Bao*—our copy was picked up, and the strike news that made the papers grew steadily, the *Shen Bao* running the most. When a small plant struck, the workers would come to the Dagong agency to report it, or phone it in. The agency had two sources of money: the fees the papers paid for our copy, and the earnings of a cooperative print shop I ran with Zhou Xuexiang, Ye Xiangju, Li Mengnan (of the Five Continents Guben Soap Works union), Shao Xubai, and others. The shop was at Jinjiafang by Fangbang Bridge in Nanshi, managed by Ye Xiangju, with Peng Wenmei (also called Xi Xiangmei) in charge of the printing. It took orders from the unions and workers of every plant and firm, and the strike leaflets other shops were afraid to touch came there to be printed; so business was good.

That the Dagong agency came to matter owed much to the "Movement to Strengthen the Postal Foundation," launched jointly on May 1, 1932, by the National Federation of Postal Workers and the Postal Staff Union. The postal workers demanded, first, that the Savings and Remittance Bureau be abolished and merged away; second, an end to all subsidies to the airlines; third, that every rule of postal employment be upheld; fourth, that postal revenue go to the post alone. On May 12 the Shanghai postal workers struck, and by the 23rd the postal workers of Beiping, Tianjin, Kaifeng, Taiyuan, Jinan, Nanjing, Anqing, Fuzhou, Zhengzhou, Hangzhou, Luoyang, Qingdao, Yantai, Suzhou, Changzhou, Wuxi, Zhenjiang, Xuzhou, Ningbo, Songjiang, and other cities had all walked out—a show of force that shook the whole country. Wang Jingwei removed Qian Chunqi, director of the Directorate General of Posts, and Long Dafu, head of the postal department in the Ministry of Communications, and ordered the mayor of Shanghai, Wu Tiecheng, to press for an immediate return to work. Seven leading men of Shanghai society—Yu Qiaqing, Wang Xiaolai, Shi Liangcai, Lin Kanghou, Zhang Gongquan, Du Yuesheng, and Pan Gongzhan—came forward to mediate, and eight accepted mediation on the workers' side: Lu Jingshi, Zhang Kechang, Qi Jiamou, Fu Dewei, Shi Yitang, Lao Jieming, Lin Zhuowu, and myself. These fifteen formed a mediation committee, put terms of settlement, and on May 26 everyone went back to work. The so-called Movement to Strengthen the Postal Foundation was really a faction fight inside the Kuomintang, dressed up as something else. From first stirrings to strike, the postal workers won little for themselves—only a $2 cost-of-living allowance a month per man, with some improvement in how year-end bonuses were figured and in holidays. But because the strike touched the whole country and caught the public eye, and because the news came out through the Dagong agency alone and was taken up by every paper, the agency's standing shot up, and the papers began to shed their old indifference to labor news.

#### (iii) Organizing the Yi Society

I came into Du's circle to secure and strengthen my hold on the Postal Workers' Union; I organized the Yi Society to secure my place in the Shanghai General Labor Union and widen my reach among the unions of every trade in the concessions.

From 1932 I served as chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union of that time. On October 2, 1933, the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office told the union to file provisionally for the record; the city government's Social Affairs Bureau, torn by its own feuds, refused to recognize it, and the concession authorities ignored it altogether. Since most of Shanghai's main plants and firms lay in the concessions and the extra-settlement roads district, the union carried little weight with the workers. Only in September 1934, when it received an order from the Kuomintang government's Ministry of Industry—citing Section 2, Item 8 of the Revised Plan for Organizing People's Bodies and Item 2 of the Revised Plan for Guiding Mass Movements, which allowed the unions within a county or municipal district to combine into a general body, and noting that the Ministry had drawn up model rules for such county and municipal general unions—did the Shanghai General Labor Union begin to gain legal standing.

The union's core, besides the chairman, was five standing committeemen, all gang men. I was of Du's door; the five—Zhou Xuexiang, Shao Xubai, Ye Xiangju, Li Mengnan, and Long Peiyun—had come under Jin Tingsun's. Early in 1934 I began taking in "students" within the union itself, the first group being Fang Rusheng, Wan Banghe, Shen Biao, and others; then within the Shanghai Post Office, the first group being Wang Dongbai, Xiao Qingsong, Li Kesu, and a few more; and after that I spread out widely. In the concessions' main firms—the British Tramways Company, the bus company, the power company, the waterworks, the Yunfei and Xiangsheng taxi companies, the cotton mills, rubber plants, machine shops, and the tugboat trade—I took in "students" everywhere. Outside the concessions, my main targets were the China General Electric Company and the Zhabei Water and Power Plant.

In the autumn of 1934, some of my "students" felt there should be a body to bring everyone together, and thought of forming one to be called an "Academic Study Society"; they drafted a charter aimed at building fellowship and friendship, with specific subjects for study. It was talked about for a few months and never formally founded. Early in 1935, at a dinner, it came up again, and someone remarked that the gang bodies of the day generally went by the name of some society or other—Du Yuesheng's Heng Society, Jin Tingsun's Ming Society, and so on. So, borrowing the sense of the phrase "perseverance is the root of a man's standing," and following on the Heng Society, we called ours the Yi Society. And on the model of the Heng Society, which had Du Yuesheng as its patron, the Yi Society had no chairman but asked me to be its patron, with a board of directors and supervisors to run its affairs. Its address was the Puye Occupational Union at No. 117 Fuyou Road in Nanshi; with no room of its own to meet in, it amounted to a mailing address. In 1936, when the Pudong Building on Avenue Edward VII (now East Yan'an Road) was finished—a building run by the Pudong Native-Place Association, which Du Yuesheng headed—I rented two rooms in it through my tie to Du, and only then had a place to meet and could hang out the Yi Society's sign for real. We designed an emblem, too: a round red ground, an open book at the center, and a ring of chain around the book. The red stood for the word "Zhu," the book for "Xue," and the chain for "Fan"—together a sign of solidarity and mutual aid, and together my own name. Made into badges, it was given to some members to wear. The Yi Society's watchword was "I for everyone, everyone for me," and it preached mutual help and cooperation. By 1936 the working people in the Yi Society came to over 1,000 across the whole of Shanghai, scattered through the plants and firms of many trades.

The Yi Society's quick, broad growth caught the notice of the gang factions and stirred up frictions of various kinds, which could be smoothed over only because Du Yuesheng backed me. Du's disciples were prominent men in every trade, and on the union side it was mainly through me that his influence spread. Since Lu Jingshi had by then become head of the military-law section at the Shanghai Garrison Command and an executive committeeman of the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office, with no time for union affairs, Du turned all the labor world's business over to me and trusted me completely. So the Yi Society gradually came out on top among the many factions in the plants and firms of many trades, and its core people there moved into the leadership of the unions. They in turn, through sworn brotherhoods and sisterhoods, bound crowds of workers to them and widened their reach. Through my 1,000-odd "students" and the mass of workers they drew in, I built ties with the unions of every trade and their grassroots branches, broadened the General Labor Union's base, and secured and strengthened my own place in it. But what really made the union count in society, and let it open a way in the concession districts, was Du Yuesheng's coming forward to mediate labor disputes.

#### (iv) Du Yuesheng Mediating Labor Disputes

After 1932 the national crisis deepened by the day, and with the world depression and the dumping of Japanese goods, Shanghai's industry and commerce sank a little further each year and the workers' lot grew harder. To make a living, Shanghai's workers took up struggle in every form. By the Kuomintang's own count, in 1932 the city saw 82 strikes, with more than 71,000 strikers, and 253 labor disputes touching more than 1,400 plants and firms. In 1933 the Chinese-owned cotton mills cut operations by 23 percent from April, the tobacco plants ran three or four days a week, the rubber plants mostly shut through the summer, and in October two-thirds of the city's silk filatures closed as the silk price collapsed; there were 88 strikes, more than 74,000 strikers, and 301 disputes touching more than 700 plants and firms. In 1934, with American cotton, American wheat, and flour flooding in and Japanese goods smuggled and dumped besides, most flour mills stopped and the cotton mills cut back across the board; workers were thrown out in droves, and the city saw 73 strikes, more than 31,000 strikers, and 233 disputes touching more than 900 plants and firms. In 1935 there were 94 strikes, more than 78,000 strikers, and 221 disputes touching some 400-odd plants and firms. In 1936 the December Ninth student movement in Beiping raised a nationwide tide of resistance and salvation, and Shanghai's labor movement saw 110 strikes; above all, the November victory of the anti-Japanese general strike by more than 40,000 workers at the city's Japanese cotton mills carried the resistance movement to a new height.

In these years the Kuomintang, while stepping up its "encirclement" of the Soviet areas, was also cracking down on the labor movement—again and again forbidding strikes outright and readily using troops and military law to threaten strikers. So of the strikes in these years, only a few ended in the workers' outright victory: 18 percent in 1932, twenty-odd percent in 1933, 28 percent in 1934, 18 percent in 1935. That the workers, fighting in every form under the harshest conditions, could win even part of what they wanted came chiefly from the courage of the great mass of them; another factor was that once a struggle reached a certain point, they took mediation and stopped while they were ahead. In settling the disputes and strikes at firms of real social weight and larger plants, Du Yuesheng's mediation played its part. Here are a few cases I saw myself.

On January 7, 1932, the roughly 1,000 crewmen of the China Merchants Steam Navigation Company sent delegates to petition the company for the same treatment as its office staff and for a double year-end payment, and were turned down. From the 8th the crews struck, and the China Merchants ships that reached Shanghai afterward struck in turn—17 in all. The company brushed them off, warning that "no ship may be laid up at will in a time of national crisis." In the end Du Yuesheng mediated, and three points were agreed: first, the crews would get half the double year-end payment (half in the first month, the rest in March); second, no crewman would be dismissed without cause; third, if the company recognized its staff union, it would recognize the crews' federation as well. And so the ships began sailing again from January 12.

On April 2, 1932, the management of the *Shishi Xinbao*, seizing on the jump in unemployment after the January Twenty-Eighth fighting, forced its workers to print the *Da Wanbao* too; when there were not enough hands, it used that as an excuse to fire more than 80 men and hire the unemployed in their place. Because the management had powerful men in the Kuomintang government behind it, the dispute dragged on for more than a month. On June 1 more than 1,000 workers at the *Shen Bao*, the *Xinwen Bao*, and the *Shi Bao* struck in sympathy (every big Shanghai paper had Du Yuesheng's "students"), demanding that the *Shishi Xinbao* take the ousted men back and pay their wages for the time they had been out. The management only bore down harder, setting the Kuomintang police to arrest more than 20 *Shishi Xinbao* workers. Under the sheer weight of the mass struggle, Du Yuesheng stepped in; all the arrested men were released, and it was agreed by word of mouth that the dismissed workers would get severance.

At the end of August 1933, the management of the British-owned Wheelock Company, pleading slack business, fired more than 100 workers. Mediation by the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office and the Social Affairs Bureau got nowhere. On September 19 the whole workforce of more than 500 struck, all 102 cargo lighters stopped, and the merchants pressed the company with complaints and demands for their losses. Du Yuesheng mediated and an agreement was reached: first, the 20 dismissed workmen would get retirement pay; second, the other 80-odd would all be reinstated; third, wages would be paid in full for the days of the strike.

On January 19, 1934, the outworking silversmiths of the jewelers' trade laid down their tools, protesting that the owners were paying them at 80 percent and demanding a return to the "thirty-six split" (the worker to get 36 percent of the price of each piece) and the clearing of back pay. The owners, pleading slack business, refused, and mediation by the Kuomintang's party and government offices failed. On March 3, 17 of the men still out went together to the jewelers' guild hall on Huayuan Street outside the Great East Gate and began a hunger strike. The General Labor Union sent people to comfort them, pressed them hard to drink milk, and asked Du Yuesheng to mediate. In the end it was settled at a "twenty-seven split" (the worker to get 27 percent of each piece), with the wages owed from the previous year's Mid-Autumn Festival to year's end paid off at 23 percent. On March 13 all the workers went back.

More than 1,400 workers at the old and new plants of the American-owned Shanghai Power Company struck from September 29 to November 21, 1934, against the owners' wholesale layoffs and dismissals. The union put forward seven terms, chief among them: first, no worker to be dismissed without cause, and temporary hands with half a year's service to count as permanent; second, the order dismissing the workers' delegates to be withdrawn; third, the union to be recognized and given $300 a month for expenses; fourth, wages to be paid during the strike. The American bosses held firm, refused the terms, and colluded with the patrol house to force the old hands back and take on 360 White Russians as temporary workers to threaten the strikers. At the start the office staff would not act with the workers, so some Yi Society members among the strikers grabbed a leading staff man, drove him by car to Gongwu Road in the Chinese district, pushed him out, and fired two shots in the air; the man, all "yes, yes," went back and rallied the staff to join the strike, and so they did. The strike hung in the balance for 54 days, and in the end Du Yuesheng mediated: the owners would advance a month's wages during the strike, to be deducted later. It was really a defeat; Du's mediation won the strikers back only a little face, and gave the thing some sort of ending.

On July 4, 1935, the Council of the French Concession put out a notice ordering the more than 40,000 rickshaw men to register by the end of the month at five dimes a head, and the rickshaw men rose against it. On the morning of August 1 the Council sent the patrol to register them by force in the streets and impounded several hundred rickshaws. That afternoon the men met at the South Wharf and sent delegates to petition the Kuomintang party office, the Social Affairs Bureau, and the city government, but mediation by the party and government offices came to nothing. On the 6th the whole body struck, chose more than 1,500 delegates, and marched from the Great South Gate to petition the party office, the French Concession's Ratepayers' Association of Chinese, and the Council. When the column reached Xieqiao it was blocked by the French Concession patrol; a clash broke out, and on the spot 12 workers were beaten and hurt, 3 arrested, and 1 lost. Du Yuesheng then mediated: the impounded rickshaws were returned, registration was made free for all, and the deadline was pushed back two months.

In November 1936 more than 40,000 workers at the city's Japanese cotton mills held an anti-Japanese general strike, putting five terms: first, a 10 percent raise; second, the machines stopped an hour at meals; third, no worker to be dismissed; fourth, no worker to be struck; fifth, no forced extra hours on Sundays. The strike was organized and set going by the underground Communists Zhang Weizhen, Han Nianlong, Zhou Lin, Chen Zhiyi, Guo Guangzhou, and others; it drew support of every kind from the Shanghai National Salvation Federation and from the various societies and schools, and became, under the banner of the anti-Japanese national united front the Communist Party had raised, a historic act of anti-Japanese unity by the working, student, and commercial people of Shanghai, the salvation groups, and people of every walk of life. To tighten the crackdown the Japanese capitalists brought in marines by the boatload and spread spies and patrolmen through every plant to arrest workers. But because the workers held firm and the whole city stood behind them, Funatsu Tatsuichirō, head of the Japanese cotton-textile association, had no choice but to ask Du Yuesheng to mediate. On November 25 Du called me and more than 20 striker delegates, along with delegates from the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office, the Social Affairs Bureau, and the police bureau, to the Zhonghui Building, and there settled seven terms: first, a 5 percent raise; second, the monthly bonus scheme changed to a reward scheme, the deserving raised a grade and paid more as merited; third, no worker dismissed without cause; fourth, no beating or cursing of workers; fifth, twelve hours' work a day and fourteen on Sundays, the extra two Sunday hours paid separately; sixth, the machines stopped 30 minutes at meals; seventh, all the plants' workers back on the morning of the 26th. Before this I had already arranged to bail the arrested workers out of the Longhua Garrison Command. After the strike I backed the Toyoda mill workers in forming a workers' welfare society.

Before 1932 Du Yuesheng had mediated labor disputes now and then, but seldom, and mostly in the French Concession. After I became chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union, the times he came forward grew steadily, and their range spread over all of Shanghai. This was because, after the January Twenty-Eighth fighting, the national crisis and the slump deepened, disputes and strikes multiplied, and the union ran into no end of trouble handling them. For one thing, though the union of that day answered to the Kuomintang party office and the Social Affairs Bureau, the two sides often disagreed on how to deal with a dispute. The party office and the bureau always started from so-called keeping public order and blocking Communist activity, sided with the owners, met strikes with suppression, wanted them quelled fast, and would even set the police to arrest strikers. The union held that the thing to do was to conciliate, to settle some of the workers' real hardships, not to reach for hard measures, and least of all to arrest workers at will. So the party office and the bureau kept pressing the union to make me fall in line. When that happened, I turned to Du Yuesheng. The city government, the party office, and the other organs were full of his "students"—Wu Xingya, who handled local political matters under Wu Tiecheng; Lu Jingshi, who ran labor affairs for the party office and headed the military-law section at the Garrison Command; the executive committeeman Chen Junyi; and more—and once Du came forward to mediate, of course they all had to give him face. Because he ordinarily stayed out of the labor world's affairs, and because a dispute was such a tangled thing, here he trusted me completely and spoke for me. There was another reason: Du Yuesheng's rise to being a Shanghai "notable" had a lot to do with his mediating one dispute after another. Through the Dagong agency I always played up his part in a settlement, and after each dispute the parties involved would run notices in the big papers thanking him for it, so that he felt much honored. He mediated not for money, and whenever I asked him to take the field he never turned me down. My second difficulty in handling disputes was the clash with capitalists Chinese and foreign. I stood for cooperation between labor and capital, but I also held to bettering the workers' lives, and among the workers of many trades my "students" often stood against the owners in a dispute. The owners not only joined with the party and government organs against the workers and even had workers arrested, but at times played on the frictions among the gang factions, pulling one in to lean on another (many of the foremen and inspectors in the trades belonged to one faction or another), to threaten and beat the strikers. When I ran into that, I went to Du Yuesheng for help against the owners and the rival factions. That I could bail workers out of the patrol house, the Garrison Command, and the police bureau rested on my tie to Du: he put in a word, and I went and did the paperwork.

Du Yuesheng's way of mediating did not quite match the stance of the Kuomintang's party and government organs. He set great store by face, thought himself an arbiter of fairness, and wanted everyone to take his word and heed it. So I would often have the workers give a little first, to save Du's face, and then push him to lean on the owners and squeeze a bigger concession out of them. Sometimes he simply stepped over the workers' heads and answered their demands for the owners. He had a pet line: if such-and-such a boss won't put up the money, I will, and my word is good. As a rule it held true. But even Du fell short at times: once, mediating a dispute at the French Tramways Company, the workers came around to his terms but the owners would not, and he had to put up a few thousand dollars of his own to fob the men off.

#### (v) Organizing Gang Armed Units

When the July Seventh war of resistance began in 1937, I was on my way home by mail steamer out of Marseille, and heard Chiang Kai-shek's speech at Lushan aboard ship. I reached Shanghai on August 7 and went straight to Du Yuesheng to put my view that the workers should take up arms against Japan. He told me that on July 22 the various circles of Shanghai had already formed the Shanghai General Association for the Support of Armed Resistance; that he sat on its presidium with Wang Xiaolai, Qian Xinzhi, Pan Gongzhan, Zhang Shoushan, Tong Xingbai, Huang Yanpei, Lu Jingshi, Ke Ganchen, and others; and that he also headed its fund-raising committee, in charge of raising all the money to support the anti-Japanese forces, with the Heng Society's Luo Qinghua as one of the deputy heads. As for arming Shanghai's workers, the fund-raising committee could put up the money and he would put up the guns.

When the war of August Thirteenth broke out, Du Yuesheng told me he was making me commander of the Third Detachment of the Special Operations Corps, with an establishment of 1,500 men. I picked more than 400 from the Yongjin Corps, rounded up a further batch from the workers of various trades, gathered them at Longhua Temple, and held a founding assembly, where I spoke. The Third Detachment's headquarters was in the Star Film Company at Fenglin Bridge; my adjutant Wang Zhenbai and my clerk Wang Baochang were both my "students." The detachment had three battalions: one commander was Shui Xiangyun, another was Han Jingxian, a drill instructor from the old Yongjin Corps, and the third's name I no longer recall. Yi Lirong brought in Professor He Hanwei to give the detachment political lessons. In a post-office car I brought a case of pistols over from Du Yuesheng and handed them out to the officers; the rifles that came over were all old, and there were not even enough for one apiece.

The corps' commander-in-chief was Liu Zhilu, once an officer of the Northeastern Army, who often called at Du Yuesheng's mansion. The corps had five detachments. The First Detachment's commander was He Xingjian, a "student" of Du's; its ranks held both Green Gang and Hongmen men, and the Hongmen's Xiang Songpo sent in a batch to join. The Second Detachment's commander was Lu Jingshi, the Third was mine, the Fourth was Zhang Ye's, and the Fifth was Tao Yiguan's—he ran military training at the Garrison Command and put the shop clerks, apprentices, and young workers under training into his detachment. Liu Zhilu once called the detachment commanders to a meeting somewhere on Hongqiao Road and handed out a flag; after that he called no more meetings. He issued one document, appointing me commander of the Third Detachment of the Special Operations Corps of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang Action Committee, with the rank of colonel. When I asked Du Yuesheng whose charge this Jiangsu-Zhejiang Action Committee came under, he said it had to do with Dai Li—which was how the guns had been come by.

The Shanghai resistance of August Thirteenth held out into mid-November, and the Third Detachment stood by the whole time, never reaching the front. Three days before the Kuomintang armies pulled back to the west, Liu Zhilu ordered the Third Detachment to turn all its rifles over to the Second Detachment in Pudong. On the night the Kuomintang armies left Shanghai I was quartered at detachment headquarters; at first light the sentry reported that the police had withdrawn and two truckloads of Japanese troops were heading for Fenglin Bridge. It struck me as very strange that both the troops and the police should have pulled out without a word to me, and it left me with no time to prepare. With only pistols in hand and no way to resist, we slipped out the back gate; but the iron gate of the French Concession was already shut, so we crossed Yongjiabang into the concession instead. The next day I went to Du Yuesheng for disbandment money and paid off the whole Third Detachment, officers and men. From the fund-raising committee of the Shanghai General Association for the Support of Armed Resistance, Du put up several thousand dollars—six for each officer, three for each soldier. I gave the pistols back to Du, keeping 25, which I hid at the home of Wu Shuhe, dean of studies at Minli Middle School (I had been the school's secretary-general and knew him from then). Those pistols were later used to kill the traitors Lu Liankui (former chief inspector of the International Settlement patrol house) and Lu Bohong (head of a maintenance association).

After the Kuomintang armies withdrew west, Du Yuesheng's next move was a matter of concern on all sides, the enemy and the puppets included. Yang Guanbei, Lu Jingshi, and I pressed him hard to leave Shanghai, while Xu Caicheng and others of the Heng Society wanted him to stay. Du took the advice of the few of us and agreed to go to Hong Kong at once. I saw him to the customs wharf and watched him board a launch and go up onto the ship before I turned back. A few days later I too reached Hong Kong, and a month after that Lu Jingshi came as well, and met with Du Yuesheng and me.

### III. Changes in the Gangs After the CCP Proposed the Anti-Japanese National United Front

Around 1936 the Shanghai gangs, Green Gang and Hongmen (the Hong Gang) alike, were fairly active, throwing their gates open and taking in disciples in crowds. The Yi Society I had built up flourished in this same stretch, drawing in great numbers of working people, and this had everything to do with the times. As the resistance and salvation movement rose, working people grew more caught up in public affairs, and felt that to fend off the outsider's insults and resist the pressure of capitalists Chinese and foreign, the brotherhoods and sisterhoods they already had were too weak; they wanted urgently to combine on a larger scale and to join the salvation movement and other progressive work. The tide of the movement battered the gangs' old frame, and some of their upper figures, pushed by the rank and file and moved by the nationwide resistance, came around to sympathy and support for it and joined in activity of every kind.

Du Yuesheng took part in the city-wide resistance and salvation work from the January Twenty-Eighth resistance of 1932 to the August Thirteenth resistance of 1937. The Hongmen's Xiang Songpo (Xiang Haiqian), too, made uniting to resist Japan his watchword and worked among the men. Before the July Seventh war his lieutenants gathered workers from Shenxin Mills Five, Six, and Seven, Jardine's, the Heping firm, and other plants to a dinner at the Da Zhonghua Restaurant, held forth on standing together against Japan, and called on the brothers to hold not only to the loyalty of the Hongmen but to the loyalty of Chinese.

After Shanghai became a solitary island, the gangs changed greatly within: some leaders joined the resistance, and some turned their coats and went over to the enemy and the puppets. Du Yuesheng left for Hong Kong and then Chongqing; one of his agents in Shanghai, Yao Huiquan, stayed in touch with the city's underground Communists, helped raise money for education and culture, and promised that if anything came up on the patrol-house side, he could find a way to smooth it over. Once the enemy and puppet forces pushed into the concessions, working people, to fend off the Japanese bandits' abuse, took the still-lawful gangs as their shield, and so the gangs' strength grew somewhat.

The gangs inside the Shanghai Post Office changed greatly by this time too. The post office had had many gang societies: Du Yuesheng, Jin Tingsun, Wang Xiaolai, Yang Hu, Ma Xiangsheng, and Peng Baiwei all took in disciples among the postal staff, and I, along with Fu Dewei, Shi Yitang, Zhang Kechang, Liu Xinquan, Lu Keming, and others inside the post office, took in disciples too, each of us setting up his own door. More than 300 of the postal staff had joined the Yi Society and more than 200 Zhang Kechang's Chang Society, the two largest bodies. I upheld the anti-Japanese national united front the Chinese Communist Party had put forward, and after leaving Shanghai I went on, from Guangzhou to Wuhan, with the work of uniting the country's workers in resistance. Zhang Kechang went over to the puppets and in December 1938 set up a traitors' union in Shanghai; most of the Chang Society, grasping the larger cause, would not follow the enemy, though a small part were dragged along. Just then the comrades of the Workers' Movement Committee of the CCP's Jiangsu Provincial Committee, who led the underground work in the post office, got some of the Chang Society's members to put out, in the name of "the hot-blooded of the Chang Society," a *Letter to the Brothers of the Chang Society*. It is short, and I copy it out here just as it was:

{v} Brothers of the Chang Society, chivalrous and righteous, faithful and steadfast: from the first day we joined this society we swore to be filial to our families and loyal to our country, and to draw the knife at the sight of wrong; our loyalty as brothers reaches to heaven, and blessings and burdens we share alike. Every disciple of this society should be a filial son at home and a good, law-abiding subject in the land; should seek out worthy masters and honest friends, mend bridges and repair roads, care for the old and help the poor. Because our aims are upright and our loyalty runs deep and long, we brothers respect and love one another, and the society's affairs have grown with it. Yet now there are unworthy men who trade on this society's name to swindle and deceive—men who, under cover of a dinner at the Huibin Lou, brought in the Korean Lin Zijiong, secretary of the puppet Chinese Workers' Welfare Association, to preach surrender, hoping to blind the brothers, to injure the sovereignty of the post, to harm the nation and drag the brothers into dishonor. Mindful that our society has always been clean, and will not now sink to treason and a stench that lasts ten thousand years, we should rouse ourselves at once and make our stand plain: "loyalty, filial duty, integrity, and honor—no turning traitor." As for the one named Shen and the one named Dai, who scheme with the enemy and take the bandit for a father, we sound the drum against them, and we will never let our upright society harbor such shameless men. Let every son of the Yellow Emperor, every child of the Chinese nation, seize them and put them to death. This we declare.

The open letter shook the Chang Society, and through the underground Communists who had worked their way in, the members were brought around: most of the Chang Society came over to the stand of "loyalty to the country, no turning traitor," the tiny handful of turncoats were isolated, and the traitors' scheme took a blow.

In March 1940, when the puppet Wang Jingwei government was set up in Nanjing, Zhang Kechang became a deputy minister of social affairs in it. By then the Chang Society's numbers in the post office had grown somewhat, and underground Communists were at work among them, doing their best to lead the society's rank and file both to use the lawful shell to guard their own interests and to break out of the gangs' narrow circle and stand under the banner of the anti-Japanese national united front, doing some useful work for resistance and salvation.
