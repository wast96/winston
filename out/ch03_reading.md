## The White Hōin

But that case had been a little different, Jūzō thought. Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, his master, would surely have bedded the woman and then cut her down without mercy the moment he knew her for a rappa.

The Iga man shunned women. Almost every disgrace that overtook an Iga man turned on one. Let a tenderness for a woman wake in him in the thick of the work, and the cold instinct of his trade lost its magic at last.

As for his not having killed her, what Jūzō feared was less the woman than the tilt of his own heart. In cutting her down he ought to have cut away that much of himself as well. So he told himself.

(Over that woman, with not one thing to recommend her—)

That he should be so caught up in her—Jūzō caught himself in a wry smile.

(Next time we meet, I kill her, and no mistake.)

Red rose into Jūzō's face as he crossed Tobuhino. Whether it was the dawn sun beginning to climb the hill of Byakugō-ji, or a pity for that woman that had begun, somewhere in the living flesh of him, to take root—Jūzō himself did not know.

In Tenshō eleven, when Hideyoshi raised his castle at Ōsaka, he ordered the townsmen of Sakai to remove to Ōsaka and had the moats filled in—the moats that had held their blue water along three sides of the town. Sakai has gone eight years now, stripped of its defenses.

Even so, at every block a massive gate reared up, black and strong, and spoke of the town's wealth and of a pride that would not readily bow to the armed power of the provinces.

The afternoon sun burned on the ranked roofs of the merchant houses and set tens of thousands of tiles shining, each one, to silver.

Jūzō passed under the gate of Minamihanchō.

(Has a China ship come in?)

So Jūzō thought. And in fact, of the people swimming through the crowd, more wore foreign dress than not.

"Which way is the bathhouse quarter?"

"Straight on here, and go east."

A man who looked like a shop clerk threw the answer at Jūzō's question and hurried off, all business.

Jūzō went on at his leisure. He passed under nine of the town gates, and when he came out onto the Ōshōji road the wind blowing across from the north of the street carried the smell of the sea.

"Here. Where's the bathhouse quarter?"

Playing the country samurai come to gawk at Sakai, Jūzō called out to a passerby in a good, idle drawl.

A loafer of thirty-four or -five stopped, opened his red mouth, and said:

"Mind your tongue."

"Why, how's this? Have I given some offense?"

"I'm telling you to mind that high-and-mighty tongue. Elsewhere it may be otherwise, but here in Sakai a two-sworded samurai doesn't rank with the day-laborers."

"A painful blunder, that. Let me ask you once again. Which way lies the bathhouse quarter?"

Jūzō drew right up under the short loafer's chin and, all smiles, made him a little bow.

"You've a house you favor?"

"I'd like a drink in the bathhouse quarter."

"As you see, I'm a country fellow in Sakai for the first time."

"You've got money?"

"Enough to make me shiver with it. If you've a house you know, come along and let me stand you a cup."

The loafer broke suddenly into a mean, thin-lipped grin, and—

"Well, here's the very god of the hour. My throat's just been dry and begging."

The loafer hitched his hem a little and led the way.

Jūzō followed, an easy smile on his face.

In the bathhouse quarter, though the sun still stood high, the sound of strings and singing rippled out.

"Why, it's the fellow from the Daimoku Hall—"

"That's the one—Sōgorō. Today I've brought along a gentleman with deep pockets."

They went into a house whose sign read Ryūkyū-ya, and women came welling out, several of them, and took Sōgorō and Jūzō each by both arms.

"Oh—what a big man."

"He's like Ken Ikkan of the Ming."

"And yet his face isn't so fierce for all that."

"His mouth is the very image of Master Sōkun's."

"Sōkun?"

Jūzō asked it while they held his hands.

"Sōkun—that would be the son of Imai Sōkyū, if I recall."

"You know his lordship Tatewaki Saemon—Sōkun, that is?"

From this detached room a courtyard showed, and to the right of the garden ran a Chinese-style gallery built to mimic an arched bridge. Sōgorō of the Daimoku Hall, the moment he took his seat, said this, working his hands together before his knees and looking up at Jūzō. Jūzō said:

"Well, now. I've somehow got the name by heart, at least. But it's a strange thing all the same. Here in Sakai, does the mere naming of Sōkyū and Sōkun—townsmen, when all's said—set even a loafer squaring his knees like that?"

"Don't talk such rot. Just where are you from, sir?"

"A gōshi of Kanbara in Echigo. Aida Gen'emon by name."

"Ah, that's a thick country burr, right enough. It's because you've crawled out of some back-of-beyond we've never so much as heard tell of that you say such daft things. Here in Sakai—"

"Girl. Pour this gentleman a drink, won't you."

"Yes."

"In Sakai—what?"

"Aye—in Sakai it's money that talks, I'll tell you. Even a provincial lord, when he comes to this place, strips off his armor outside the moat and enters the town bare: that's the rule here. In this land the master is not the bow and arrow. The master is money. And the men who hold the money are the Tsuda, the Imai, the Konishi, the Miyake…"

"You there—Daimoku Hall, or whatever your name is—"

"What is it?"

"Your drink's going cold."

"I'll drink when I please. For the likes of us, who've lived off the spillings of Master Sōkyū's house since our fathers' time, it's no clean thing to be treated by a man who doesn't know the majesty of the Imai. Just now—you said mere townsmen, didn't you."

"I did."

"They're no townsmen."

"What are they, then?"

"Ōkurakyō-Hōin—aye, Master Sōkyū holds a proper court rank, granted from within the Palace itself."

"A grand thing indeed."

"And more—merchant though he is, he's had from the Kampaku a fief of two thousand two hundred koku, the villages of Abiko, Sugimoto, and Oriono in Settsu together."

"Excuse me. Master Sōgorō."

A new woman had come in, changing places with the one before; and the moment she plumped herself down beside Sōgorō—

—she took up a glass sake-vessel, heavy with the lead in it, and nudged his knee.

"There's no profit in reckoning up another man's stipend. What matters, sir, is that here you get drunk on the fine stipend inside your cup. Come—drain it."

"Fair enough. I've talked far too much and gone dry in the throat."

Sōgorō clapped his forehead with a clown's flourish, cradled the cup in the fingers of both hands, brought his mouth to the rim, and worked his throat up and down with a gulp.

From the moment the woman had changed places in the room, Jūzō had been watching her profile without a break.

Wiping with a cloth the drops of sake spilled on Sōgorō's knee, her profile thick with paint, the woman took Jūzō's stabbing gaze without a flicker.

It was Kohagi.

By what contrivance there was no telling, she had swapped with the woman on duty and taken over the serving of the party.

Kohagi did not look at Jūzō; she kept her hand on Sōgorō's knee and poured, and poured.

Watching the way Kohagi carried herself, Jūzō felt, at the measure of his own foolishness in being outdone by this woman twice over, something nearer to amusement; a wish to laugh aloud welled up from the back of his throat. He hardly knew what to do with the strange brightness of the feeling, and it left him flustered with himself.

The woman, quick to feel it perhaps, did not turn toward Jūzō; only a smile flickered for an instant across one white cheek.

Then Kohagi rose, smoothly, all at once. She stood tall enough that her hair, wound high in a Chinese coil, brushed the low pierced-carving of the lintel. Looking down at Jūzō as she stood, her face held no smile.

Kohagi turned her back with a spin and was gone from the room.

Right after, the sliding door opened. To the Ryūkyū-ya woman of before, who came in bringing the lukewarm air from under the eaves, Jūzō said:

"That woman just now—what's her name?"

"The woman just now?"

The woman tilted her head, puzzled.

"You know. The tall one."

Sōgorō put in from the side. A weaker head for drink than you'd think—he could already barely hold himself upright.

"Hmm."

The woman, tilting her head, said:

"Today, with the overflow from the fine-timber fair, so many guests came in that I'm the only one attending you gentlemen, I'm afraid."

"I see. Sakai's a bustling port whose name has carried clear to the Great Ming and to Luzon, right enough—so the foxes come out as early as the fourth hour, do they?"

"Oh, you're teasing me."

Laughing, the woman let it pass, edged closer, and poured for Jūzō.

(That woman—she has come all the way from Nara to Sakai on purpose, it seems, on my trail.)

Seeing Jūzō go into the Ryūkyū-ya, she had disguised herself and slipped in among the women of the house, he judged; but even so—

(To what end—)

—and there even Jūzō had no answer.

(—That she is watching my movements, that much I understand. She shows herself at each crucial break in what I do, half in menace, to let me know I am watched—no doubt of it.

—It may even be that the woman knows my very object, through and through.

—And who, in the end, stands behind her?)

(No matter…)

Jūzō set the cup he had been barely tasting back among the dishes, and thought:

(As for what she really is—that will come clear as the thing goes forward.)

Jūzō stood up.

"Where are you off to?"

"To the privy."

Tossing that to Sōgorō, he settled the reckoning and went out into the night town.

From the Jōraku-ji, past the front of the silk-guild hall and out to the Myōkoku-ji, there was no gate.

Sakai's day is short. This town, that by daylight thronged so from the harbor along the main street, emptied of foot traffic once the sun was down. Every shop had its great shutters lowered.

Jūzō, who had stopped before the mountain gate of the Jōraku-ji, sat on the stone steps and rested there aimlessly, meaning perhaps to kill a little time; presently he rose and this time turned his feet north.

One lane, a second, and at the third a long high wall of packed earth ran on for the better part of a chō. Here and there over the top of it, huge sago palms thrust out their fronds.

It was the Imai mansion.

(Why, it's the size of a small country castle.)

Walking slowly along the wall, Jūzō untied the cord of his scabbard. He tucked the long cord's end into his sash and drew the sword, scabbard and all, from his belt.

He walked on just so.

Judging the spot, he leaned the sword against the wall, set the big toe of his right foot on the guard, and sprang lightly up onto the coping. From the top he hauled the sword up by its cord, and—

(Fewer trees in the garden than I'd have thought.)

—dropped soft onto the grass. It was the work of a single instant.

Jūzō went forward, picking his way from tree to tree.

All at once the ground before him went pale. Faint starlight caught the raked sand and set it glittering, streaming. Boring their dark shadows into the sand this way and that, great stones lay scattered, large and small.

A tea-house stood in view.

Crouching against one of the stones, Jūzō studied the lay of the place.

The dark closed over Jūzō's body; and as the pores of his stilled frame began quietly to breathe the cold air of the grounds, a strange rapture ran from deep in the man's body to the very tips of his fingers.

This might be called the very physiology of the ninja, a creature that lives in the dark. When he stole into another man's house or an enemy castle and let his body sink into the clinging dark of the grounds, another living thing inside him seemed to wake, bright and alert, and stretch its limbs.

(Where does Sōkyū sleep?)

Fixing on a certain building, Jūzō spent a full watch in the shadow of a stone, and when the mansion had sunk to sleep he moved, quietly.

He lifted a paper screen from the kitchen, went in, slid without a sound down the corridor, and poured oil into the sill-groove of the screen of the room that seemed to be the private chamber of Ōkurakyō-Hōin Sōkyū.

The screen slid open without a sound.

Then a heavy sliding door, washed with gold, came open.

The room he slipped into was dark. A cold smell of incense hung in it.

"Lord Hōin—"

"…Mm?"

The master of the room was plainly a very light sleeper; a low voice came resonating out of the mosquito net.

"A night-thief?"

"Something of the kind."

To set the man at ease, perhaps, a laugh came into Jūzō's voice.

"Light a hand-candle. I'll wait in the next room."

"I'll call the watch."

"No use in that. I've bound the two of them and quieted them a while."

"Whoever you are, you've come well prepared."

There was a stir of movement inside the net.

Jūzō went into the next room, lit the paper lamp, and waited.

Jūzō was not in ninja dress; he sat with his appearance ordered as befit a man paying a call at another's house.

Sōkyū came in.

He carried not an inch of steel.

Over a white damask sleeping-robe he wore a black sleeveless coat.

"Thief. I am Sōkyū."

"No thief. One Tsuzura Jūzō, of Iga."

"Of Iga?"

"Just so. I have come at the prompting of one Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon."

"Shimotsuge—. I don't know the name."

"No doubt."

Jūzō folded his arms, the faint smile still on him.

He was a frail wisp of an old man.

His double-lidded eyes were lightly shut. For so small a body the face was strangely large, and its features were as gentle as a child's in a palace doll. In Iga there was a lore of its own for reading the bones of a man's face: in a childlike face grown old, it was said, one now and then found a great villain.

(This old man is no easy mark.)

Jūzō felt a wry smile inside. It was not to be thought that Sōkyū would lightly lay bare his schemes to the likes of a rappa.

That the old man had risen from the son of a masterless samurai in Imai-no-shō in Yamato and, in a single lifetime, built up a vast fortune, was owed, they say, to his approaching power early, ahead of the other Sakai men.

By the account Sōkyū put about, his grandfather was Imai Nobutsune, lord of Imaichi Castle in the Takashima district of Ōmi; his father, Dewa-no-kami Muneyoshi, drifted for some reason down into Yamato. Sōkyū was the third son.

In his youth he sojourned in Sakai and studied tea under Takeno Jōō; a merchant of the same school, one Naya Sōji, took him for a promising man, made him his son-in-law, and handed over to him the whole of the Naya house's goods and tea-vessels. Yet before the eleventh year of Eiroku this merchant was next to unknown. What little his name was known at all, among the tea-connoisseurs of the day, rested on the single fact that he owned the famed vessel Matsushima, the shoulder-caddy passed to him by Naya Sōji.

It was on the second day of the tenth month of that eleventh year of Eiroku that he called on Oda Nobunaga—come up to Kyoto to command the realm—at his field camp at Akutagawa, in the Nishinari district of Settsu.

What Sōkyū used, so to speak, in place of a calling-card, was—so the story goes—the Matsushima caddy itself.

When Nobunaga's men stopped this small, tea-master's-looking man, come along the embankment from the direction of Ibaraki with a few clerks at his heel, he answered with the smooth, practiced courtesy of one deep-versed in the way of tea:

"One who would offer up the tea-caddy Matsushima for the diversion of your camp."

"Your name."

"The third son of Imai Dewa-no-kami Muneyoshi, of a far branch of the Sasaki Genji of Ōmi; now dwelling in Sakai in Izumi, and called Sōkyū."

"The Matsushima caddy has come, has it?"

Nobunaga, hearing the name of that shoulder-caddy that was hidden from no one in the land, came up onto one knee before he knew it, and spoke. His passion for tea-things was already showing itself by about this time.

"What manner of man is he?"

That he had announced himself by such a pedigree was, no doubt, because a lineage went down more easily in samurai ears.

"Sir. But the man who has come is Sōkyū, of Sakai."

"Bring him here, and be quick about it."

Nobunaga, granting Sōkyū audience, was in an uncommon good humor. What set this lover of tea-things rejoicing was not the fist-sized caddy alone that Sōkyū set before him. It fell in a delicate season, when Nobunaga's name was not yet fixed in the world. He had set up the Ashikaga shogun Yoshiaki and marched into Kyoto ahead of the other great houses; and yet—let alone the distant provinces—even the samurai of the home region were slow to reckon at its true worth the strength of this warlord of Owari, who had only just brought Suruga and Mino to heel.

This Nobunaga was burning, young and hot, with the ambition of Tenka Fubu—to cover the realm in arms. It was a season when he would rejoice at any man who came drawn by his name, be the man only a Sakai merchant. Sōkyū may be called one who struck shrewdly at Nobunaga's heart just then.

"So this is the Matsushima."

He said it, his brows spreading open.

"Sōkyū."

Gripping it on his knee, holding the delicate sheen of the black glaze up to the light, he said:

"It is—"

"Why do you give me this caddy?"

A smile at his mouth, Sōkyū quietly raised his face.

"Because it is beyond my station."

"Your station?"

"Yes. This shoulder-caddy is called the first famed vessel under Heaven. And the first vessel under Heaven should come to rest in the palm of the first man under Heaven. It has no business in the hand of a mean fellow like myself."

"You knead your flattery well. So I am this first man under Heaven, am I?"

"I bow to you as the one who, before many days, will master the realm."

"Flattery, and I know it—yet in camp you've spoken a lucky word. Is there something you want?"

"Unworthy as I am, I should like, with what my station allows, to lend my strength to one corner of your work of conquest."

From this beginning Sōkyū took in hand the whole supply of the matchlocks and powder the Oda armies used, built a colossal fortune, and—granted a fief of one thousand two hundred koku—served at Nobunaga's side as one of his intimates.

Shrewd as ever, Sōkyū borrowed Nobunaga's authority to move founders and casters into Abiko, his fief village on the outskirts of Sakai, bringing them from Tannan in Kawachi and from Minato in Izumi, and raised up a huge settlement of foundries.

It was through the guns, too, that he grew close to Nobunaga's lieutenant, the Tōkichirō Hideyoshi.

In the sixth month of the first year of Genki, when Nobunaga sent Hideyoshi and the rest against Azai Nagamasa in Odani Castle in Ōmi, the gun-fighting proved bloodier than looked for, and the powder ran short.

"Gunpowder—of the very best—thirty kin, to be conveyed to Ōmi in all haste."

So Hideyoshi wrote in a hurried dispatch; and into it he had added, besides, a fine and thoughtful care:

"The rice for your porters is stored at Ōsaka; use that. I will hold back men and horses to keep the road into northern Ōmi clear, so convey it with an easy mind."

And so Sōkyū's fortune swelled prodigiously as Nobunaga's designs went forward.

That Nobunaga died.

The Matsushima caddy, the seed of his bond with Nobunaga, was consumed in the flames of the soldiery at the Honnō-ji; but Sōkyū, before ever that happened, had already worked his way into Hideyoshi's favor.

At the battle of Yamazaki in Yamashiro he staked everything on Hideyoshi's winning. He made Hideyoshi—wheeling his great army about and driving north from the Chūgoku road—a gift of a thousand matchlocks, and firearms overwhelmingly the better of the Akechi army's swept the hills and fields of Yamazaki and brought in the age of Hideyoshi. In the tenth month of the tenth year of Tenshō, after Hideyoshi held Nobunaga's funeral at the Daitoku-ji in Murasakino, he summoned Sōkyū and said:

"As in the days of the Udaifu—Lord Nobunaga—I shall show you no less honor."

And with that he added a famed tea-vessel and a further one thousand koku of fief. It was meant, one supposes, as a token that the alliance with Sakai would go on.

…And nine years have gone by since.

Now that same Sōkyū sits face to face with a single Iga man. His heavy lids drooping, a face strangely young for his years floated pale in the shadow of the lamp. That face, as though sunk in sleep, would not so much as move its lips.

Winding his clinging gaze about the old man's mouth, Jūzō was secretly weighing the place this man held in the ninth year of Hideyoshi's rule.

(—Very likely, but for that huge quantity of guns Sōkyū supplied by his brilliant merchant's cunning, neither Nobunaga nor Hideyoshi could have become master of the realm.)

The Hōin Sōkyū believed this, somewhere down in his belly. And that belief, Jūzō thought, was this clever man's one patch of darkness—and had become his present misfortune besides.

Nobunaga, whenever he made ready for a battle, had leaned for all his supplies on Sōkyū alone.

Hideyoshi inherited Nobunaga's domain. But Hideyoshi did not lean on his dead lord's merchant—did not lean on Sōkyū alone.

True, to Sōkyū he confirmed the right to levy the salt and salt-goods of the Five Estates of Sakai, and in the building of the Great Buddha of the Hōkō-ji, in the fourteenth year of Tenshō, gave him such lucrative charges as the supply of vast quantities of lacquer. But his treatment stopped there.

For Hideyoshi, apart from him, was raising up in Sakai a new merchant more to his own taste.

One Izumi-Hōgen Konishi Ryūsa. Of his birth nothing is known.

This merchant, risen from a face-powder shop, who would go down nine years after this in the ruin of the Western Army at Sekigahara, was so favored that, a merchant himself, he was made Magistrate of Sakai, while his second son, Yakurō Yukinaga, was named Settsu-no-kami and climbed to be a daimyō with a fief of two hundred and forty thousand koku.

With Hideyoshi's authority at his back, Ryūsa cornered the real profits of the trade that reached from Korea to the Inland Sea; his son Settsu-no-kami Yukinaga, based on Shiwaku and Shōdoshima, stood in for Hideyoshi's sea-power; and Ryūsa himself, from the twelfth year of Tenshō on, became the real manager of Hideyoshi's treasure and revenue, while his wife, as secretary to the Kita-no-mandokoro, held in her hand the power that moved the great inner chambers of Ōsaka Castle.

Against the Imai Sōkyū of Nobunaga's day, their positions were exactly reversed.

Sōkyū, if anything, had fallen away, and badly.

In the fifteenth year of Tenshō, when Hideyoshi marched his armies into Kyūshū, the supply of provisions and munitions was put into the hands of Ryūsa and three other Sakai merchants; and to the profit of that colossal procurement—the food alone rations for two hundred thousand men, fodder for twenty thousand horses—Sōkyū fell to the place of a man who could only look on and suck his finger.

—And now, on top of all this, Hideyoshi has proclaimed his design to attack Korea.

For Imai Sōkyū—shut out of the profits of war, and barely keeping his breath by trade with Korea and the Ming—Hideyoshi's design was as good as forcing his own ruin upon him.

(If only Hideyoshi were gone…)

If this man had set his mind on such a thing, it was, near enough, out of just those circumstances—so Jūzō judged.

Of course the old man had not thought out so dangerous a scheme for the bare act of killing Hideyoshi. Someone had promised him the vast profit that would come of it. …And that someone was, perhaps—

(Ieyasu?)

The thought came to Jūzō of a sudden. If not Ieyasu himself, then someone among the Tokugawa house's retainers was surely working the threads of this plot—so it seemed to him.

Jūzō's thoughts ran on without end. But the shaven-headed old man before him only sat, the thick flesh of his throat bared, his eyes rigidly shut.

(Is this not a wasted labor…?)

It was not that he thought Sōkyū's scheme a wasted labor. It was only that Jūzō half wanted to laugh at himself, at how doggedly he had begun to take an interest in the whole picture behind the man in front of him.

(I have only to take my money.)

On that, and that alone, the Iga man exists. However the world might be moved by his shadow-arts, it is no concern of an Iga man's. So he told himself; and yet, strangely, he could raise no will to dam the interest welling up in his chest. Rather, an urge took hold of him to make the Hōin open his mouth here and now.

"Lord Hōin."

Jūzō swallowed the spit that had gathered in his mouth, so the Hōin would not mark it, and slowly opened his lips.

"Will you not talk a little? That I came by dark of night, like a thief, was out of care for others' eyes and ears. Take it, if you will, for the way of Iga. Or do you tell me this Iga man is not to be trusted?"

"…In Nara, did you meet a man named Matsukura Kurando?"

"I met him."

"Then that will serve. You need not have troubled to come to me at all. The business is settled well enough."

"The man Matsukura told me nothing."

"Why not?"

"The dead have no mouths."

"Oh? So that man is dead."

"I cut him down."

"…………"

As though seeing him for the first time, Sōkyū peered into Jūzō's eyes with a fresh curiosity.

"You cut him down."

There was no particular surprise in him at it. Rocking his body gently, his small eyes brimming with a bottomless shadow, Sōkyū laughed aloud.

"And that too—one of the ways of Iga?"

From behind his smile he watched Jūzō steadily.

"You may take it so."

"Hm."

The smile was gone from Sōkyū's face by now. Only his eyes held fast on Jūzō's.

A small body, a great face. All at once Jūzō was seized by the illusion that Sōkyū's face, floating there in the lamp-shadow, was swelling up before his eyes into something monstrous.

(This man—)

Within his belly, Sōkyū opened his mouth.

(He'll do.)

So he thought.

It was no more than instinct. Why Jūzō had killed Kurando, Sōkyū of course had no means to know. And yet he felt himself drawn to the dark, strange flame burning in the man's eyes—such as he had seen in no kind of man he had ever met.

(So this is a rappa's eye…)

These eyes held, he was sure, no ideal and no hope for a life of their own. Holding none, they hid nonetheless, far down, a strange cast of spirit that could stake life—coldly—on the single trade of the shadow-arts and nothing else. And that strange cast of spirit lit in the man's eyes a phosphorescence there was no naming. …

As the thought came, a natural warmth began to flow in Sōkyū's breast, like ice melting. That he should feel it safe—wish it, even—to open his great secret to this man was strange to Sōkyū himself.

Sōkyū rose, came over to Jūzō, and clapped him lightly on the thick shoulder.

"Will you take some tea?"

"At this hour of the night?"

"The way of tea is to be ready to receive a guest even in haste, even in ruin. And you, Jūzō—in the tea of this Imai Sōkyū, who is named one of the three masters of the age beside Sen no Rikyū and Tsuda Sōgyū, you'll find little wanting."

"Heh. That's grand tea, then."

With no great eagerness in his face, Jūzō picked up his sword and followed at Sōkyū's back.

Along the roofed gallery a while, and the corridor had somewhere turned into an earthen bridge. From under it came the sound of a clear stream running.

Leading the way, Sōkyū held up a hand-candle and set garden clogs on Jūzō's feet. As they went, the path threaded a little wood, and then narrowed into a tea-garden walk.

When the order had gone out there was no telling, but the window of the tea-room—which served also as a study—was lit. Even the quiet seething of the kettle came through the paper screen.

Jūzō, taking the guest's place, said:

"There could be no greater luxury than this,"

and laughed.

"You mean this tea-room?"

"No. A garden, a tea-room—with money there's nothing to it. The luxury is to sit down to tea with no ceremony at all, and one of the realm's great tea-masters for your host."

"If you can see the joke of that, you've reached the heart of tea without ever a lesson."

Sōkyū, laughing too, took up the tea-scoop.

Over the hearth, a strangely shaped kettle of Nanban iron breathed out a sound like wind in the pines.

"…………"

Jūzō stared at the kettle and strained his ears—not for the sound of the water, but to catch the scent of some other presence moving under this roof.

"Lord Hōin."

"Mm?"

"There is someone in the next room."

"That is nothing to trouble over."

"Who is it?"

"They will come here by and by."

Sōkyū set the tea-bowl before Jūzō's knee. Jūzō, for a wonder, kept to the forms and drank.

"Well—faintly right, your manner. So even a ninja drinks tea?"

"Not to speak of. I only ape what I've watched."

"Your bearing much resembles the Udaifu's. He would bury the forms within him and enjoy his tea, for all that, in a large, free way. …The Kampaku could never do so."

"…………"

"Because his birth is low, I suppose."

"Just so."

"The Kampaku looks all boldness, but once he sits down to tea he's timid about the forms—more than you'd think."

"…………"

Sōkyū had answered Jūzō's leading remark as if echoing it, and there was hatred in his tone. He went on:

"And more—that lord of the realm likes to gather about him none but men of no known birth."

"Konishi Ryūsa would be one such."

"…………"

"And you, Lord Hōin, would like to change that lord of the realm for another."

"Does it look so?"

"It does. But the Kampaku is old. And his one son, Tsurumatsu, is dead. Hidetsugu, who follows him, has no man's regard. Without forcing anything, the Toyotomi age can have little time left to run. You have only to wait for that day, Lord Hōin."

"I cannot wait."

Sōkyū said it, and quietly took the tea-bowl in his hand.

"Thanks to that lord's madness, before many days the road of my trade with Korea and the Ming will be cut clean off. To sit and wait is to watch my own ruin."

"Kill him, then?"

Jūzō said it as if mocking him. At the careless words Jūzō tossed out, Sōkyū—composed as he was—caught his breath and swallowed his answer. He held his mouth shut, peering, from within the lamp-shadow, straight into Jūzō's face.

(There's a timid streak in this man, more than one would think.)

Jūzō was satisfied to find that the tone he had thrown out had come back to him with an unlooked-for probing power.

He carried on:

"Kill that man, and war will break out across the land over where the power falls next. It would throw everything back to what it was before the Genki years."

"That—"

Sōkyū's words broke off. He looked at Jūzō. Under that look, Jūzō's eyes smiled.

(These eyes can be trusted.)

Yes—and once Sōkyū had made sure, inwardly, of his own reassurance, he said in a voice like something pressed flat:

"That is exactly what I want. Once war begins, my trade will go well, all of it."

"That is not the whole of it, surely."

"…………"

"And the one who joins hands with you, Lord Hōin, to make a new realm—who is he?"

"Man of Iga—"

"What is it?"

"Keep to your bounds. You press too far into the matter. What I have asked, you do—that is all that is wanted."

"Fair enough."

Jūzō laughed.

"Still—once, for the sake of my dead parents and sister, I set out to kill Nobunaga, and lost my chance. The bitterness of it dragged me down to where I would have thrown the world away. But Hideyoshi may be called Nobunaga's heir. For me, there is not nothing to be won in the killing."

"And so, for no cause at all, I've taken more idle interest in this business than I ought. Let me have my tea."

"…………"

"But if by any chance,"

said Sōkyū,

"the thing should come to light and you be taken—you will not give my name?"

"You harbor a foolish worry."

Jūzō turned aside and spat it out in distaste:

"I am Tsuzura Jūzō. Among the rappa of the provinces the name means something."

Just then the sliding door at his left sleeve creaked.

Jūzō looked toward it. At once he brought his eyes back to the matting, took up the bowl before his knee, and said:

"I've been asking for this a while now."

"Rather than tea…"

Sōkyū took the bowl back and, wiping it, said:

"Would sake not suit you better? As it happens, a beauty well fit for the pouring has just come."

"That would be Kohagi."

Jūzō fixed his eyes on the door.

A woman knelt there, her head bowed low.

It was Kohagi.

"You knew her, then?"

"Knew her—and more. On the way here she made sport of me more than once. —What is this woman to you, Lord Hōin?"

"My adopted daughter."

"Adopted daughter—?"

An unlooked-for expression crossed Jūzō's face. But he kept his heavy brows drawn, and would not let his suspicion go.

"By birth she was the daughter of a certain noble house—one that is already fallen and gone."

"A lady of rank, then. And your adopted daughter besides, Lord Hōin. …And yet—"

Jūzō gave a small laugh.

"Whatever she is, I'll treat you as a courtesan. As with any courtesan—if I have a use for your body, I'll come and ask for it myself. Is that understood?"

Jūzō said it pointedly, in a tone that menaced at every word. Kohagi raised her face.

In her narrow, single-lidded eyes a smoky smile trembled, as it always did. Those eyes looked straight at Jūzō, and:

"As you please."

"Heh."

Jūzō meant to let out a wry laugh, but it would not come to a laugh; only his cheek twisted, and he knew it himself. There was a sense of defeat in him.

(Am I in love with this woman…?)

Something in him faltered.

And Kohagi, for her part, with a face whose core did not give way in the least, quietly saw straight through this working of Jūzō's heart.

Jūzō was angry—not at Kohagi, but at the giving-way of his own guard.

(By nature I am weak where the wanting of a woman is concerned. One day it will be my undoing.)

"Tsuzura Jūzō—"

Sōkyū spoke the name formally.

"From now on you will take my orders through Kohagi."

"Is that so."

"The money, too—Kohagi, or a messenger of hers, will bring it to you each time."

"Hm."

"It's warm."

Sōkyū crossed his legs of a sudden and, little as it became a tea-man, pinched his collar and bared the skin of his chest.

Kohagi opened a small fan in her palm, edged near, and fanned air in at his collar. There was a fine, clean fold to the movement, without a trace of loosened coquetry.

(A strange woman.)

Jūzō found himself half lost in gazing at her.

By Kohagi's hand the sake had already been made ready.

Kohagi put the fan away, dealt out cups to Jūzō and to Sōkyū, and took up a silver vessel. Holding it by the handle, she looked at Jūzō.

"Will you have some?"

Jūzō waved a flat, curt hand.

"None. The Iga man lives for the work and nothing else."

Without meaning to, he had said a thing better left unsaid.

"Oh—no head for drink, then?"

said Sōkyū.

"For tonight I'll take my leave. Woman—no doubt we'll meet again somewhere. I am going up to the capital."

Jūzō rose.

"There's a good while yet till morning. Why not sleep here in the house?"

"You are kind. But as for sleep, I can sleep anywhere. A ninja has no business staying long under any roof."

"Come, don't be in such haste—stay the night…"

Kohagi, still kneeling, looked up at Jūzō. Wearing that same smile, she said:

"…Kohagi is a courtesan, after all—there would be nothing amiss in her keeping you company for the night."

"Fool."

Jūzō set his hand to the window. Wary, he did not leave the tea-room by the crawl-door but opened the window and dropped, light as a leaf, into the dark. Picking his way through the shadows of the trees, he felt an aching hunger for the flesh of Kohagi—once known, and now remembered—stir and wake deep within him.
