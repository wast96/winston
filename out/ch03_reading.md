### 1. The Xi'an Incident: A Triumph on the Hidden Front
The Xi'an Incident, which broke out on December 12, 1936, was a turning point in the history of the Chinese revolution and bore deeply on the whole of modern Chinese history.
Through 1936 Japanese imperialism kept widening its aggression against China, while Chiang Kai-shek clung to his policy of nonresistance, shouting that "to resist the foreign foe we must first pacify within," and pressed on with civil war. Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng, who commanded the Northeastern Army and the Seventeenth Route Army, were moved by the Chinese Communist Party's policy of an anti-Japanese national united front and by the people's resistance movement; they saw that "exterminating the Communists" led nowhere, made a truce with the Red Army, and pressed Chiang to join the Communists against Japan. Chiang not only refused their demands but moved his own loyal divisions to the Henan-Shaanxi border, forcing Zhang and Yang to go on attacking the Red Army. In early December Chiang went to Xi'an to direct the fighting in person and had local anti-Japanese youths killed; on the twelfth Zhang and Yang launched the Xi'an Incident and seized him at the Huaqing Pool at Lintong.
Before and after the Xi'an Incident, the secret work of the nameless heroes on our Party's hidden front never let up. What is set down here is Li Kenong's secret meeting with Zhang Xueliang, and also Wang Shiying's secret meeting with Yang Hucheng, and Chen Yangshan's secret contact with Song Qiyun, Yang Hucheng's secretary.
The commanders and fighters on the hidden front were then hard at work on united-front liaison and intelligence toward the Northeastern Army. The ground on which our Party formed a united front with General Zhang Xueliang was this. In several battles in northern Shaanxi, at Laoshan and Yulinqiao and elsewhere, the Northeastern Army had attacked us and been badly beaten, which shook the army and Zhang himself and brought them to see that "exterminating the Communists" was a dead road; the mass of officers and men had no wish to die pointlessly in a civil war, and longed to fight their way back to their homeland in the Northeast. On the Long March the Red Army had defeated large Kuomintang forces, and once it reached northern Shaanxi and joined with the other Red Army units, not only did its military strength grow but the Party's political influence spread quickly across the country, until it became a force strong enough to decide China's fate. Zhang Xueliang began to grasp that the Communist Party and the Red Army were the ones truly resisting Japan, that resistance meant working with the Communists or it could not be done at all; and he understood, too, that in ordering him to "wipe out the bandits" Chiang Kai-shek was really using Communist strength to weaken and destroy the Northeastern Army, scheming to rid himself of a rival while he sat on the mountain to watch the tigers fight. His quarrel with Chiang grew sharper by the day.
In late October 1935, in the battle of Yulinqiao in northern Shaanxi, the Red Army wiped out the 619th Regiment, a crack unit of the Northeastern Army (part of Wang Yizhe's 107th Division), and captured its commander, Gao Fuyuan. The Center treated these prisoners well; it once took Gao and the others to visit the Central Red Army units near Wayaobao, and put to them plainly who was resisting Japan and who was not.
Gao Fuyuan saw how things really stood and was deeply moved; he decided the Red Army truly was resisting Japan, and asked to meet its leading comrades. He was received first by Li Kenong, head of the Central Liaison Bureau, and then Peng Dehuai came to talk with him.
Gao Fuyuan then carried a secret letter from our Party's leaders back to General Zhang Xueliang.
When Gao Fuyuan reached his commander Zhang Xueliang, he said the Red Army had sent him back and that he had a great deal to report. Then this tall, plain-spoken soldier poured out his heart, in tears as he spoke: if China went on with civil war, not only would the Northeastern Army meet its ruin and the lost lands of the Northeast never be recovered, but the whole country would stand in mortal danger. ... We should answer the Communist Party's call, join with the whole people and fight Japan shoulder to shoulder, and wash away the guilt of history we carry on our backs. The vice-commander must join the Communists against Japan! When he had finished, Gao Fuyuan sank to both knees and would not rise. He knew that in saying these things he had already put his life out of his own hands. He was waiting for Zhang Xueliang's verdict.
What Gao Fuyuan could not know was that his fear and dread were needless. His words moved both Zhang Xueliang and Wang Yizhe to tears. Zhang earnestly said he broadly agreed with him, and told him to rest a day or two and then hurry back and ask the Red Army to send a formal representative for proper talks. He told Gao to carry on this work with an easy mind: should anything happen to him, Zhang Xueliang and Wang Yizhe would see to his family's living and his children's schooling.
On January 16, an elated Gao Fuyuan returned as ordered to the Soviet area in northern Shaanxi and reported everything to Li Kenong in detail. Li was delighted and took him to see Chairman Mao Zedong and Vice-Chairman Zhou Enlai. The Party leaders thought highly of what Gao had done, and Mao decided on the spot to appoint Li Kenong plenipotentiary representative of the CCP Central Committee to hold talks with Zhang Xueliang and Wang Yizhe.
Li Kenong reached Luochuan, but since Zhang Xueliang had business in Nanjing, it was not until March 4 that he negotiated with him. In the talks Li mainly answered the questions Zhang raised, setting out our Party's policy, exposing Chiang Kai-shek's scheme of appeasement and surrender abroad and destruction of rivals at home, and using facts to show that the Northeastern Army's only way out lay in joining our Party against Japan.
The main gains of Li Kenong's talks at Luochuan with Zhang Xueliang and Wang Yizhe were these: (1) it was established that Zhang was willing to resist Japan; (2) Zhang acknowledged the Red Army as an anti-Japanese force and would be its friend; (3) Zhang would give the Red Army supplies, and the two sides would reach a military accommodation and hold their fire for the time being; (4) telegraphic contact was set up. A verbal agreement was also reached with Wang Yizhe, as follows: (1) mutual nonaggression, each holding its existing lines; (2) the Red Army agreed to restore transport and trade for the 67th Army (Wang Yizhe commanding) along the roads through Fu County (now Fuxian), Ganquan, and Fushi (now Yan'an); (3) the 67th Army units then stationed in the towns of Fushi and Ganquan could buy grain, fodder, and other supplies from the local Soviet-area people; (4) trade between Red and White areas would be restored. The agreement took effect on March 5.
By the time Li Kenong got back, the Red Army had already crossed east over the Yellow River. On March 16 he caught up with it at the front at Shilou in Shanxi, saw Mao Zedong and the responsible comrades of the Center, and reported on the Luochuan talks. Mao attached great weight to winning over the Northeastern Army; he thought Li had done this single-handed, and done it very well. Mao said the eastward crossing had been right, that the Northeastern Army must be won over, or at the least brought to an accommodation and a ceasefire, to preserve the anti-Japanese forces. The Center decided that Zhou Enlai would go and meet Zhang Xueliang.
On April 9, 1936, Zhou Enlai and Zhang Xueliang held a secret meeting in Yan'an. They talked very frankly and got on well. Zhang acknowledged that the Red Army was resisting Japan in earnest, and that "exterminating the Communists" and resisting Japan could not stand together. He said he had no wish to fight the Red Army, but for now he could not break with Chiang, on whom he also depended for money, and so he was in a bind. Among the Kuomintang's leading men, he said, the only one he respected was Chiang, who still had some national feeling and some capacity to lead, and so he hoped Chiang would resist. At the same time he admitted that Chiang was surrounded by pro-Japanese men, that Chiang could not bring himself to resist, and was deeply torn. If Chiang really went over to Japan, he would resign and strike out on his own. From two years' watching, he judged that Chiang Kai-shek might yet resist Japan. His idea was to press Chiang from within while the Communists pressed from without, to push him off his mistaken course and onto the road of resistance. Zhou Enlai said he would take these views back and lay them before the CCP Central Committee for careful thought before replying. This was an important factor in the Center's decision, some four months later, to "force Chiang to resist Japan."
At the same time, the commanders and fighters on our Party's hidden front stepped up their united-front liaison and intelligence work toward the Northwestern Army. Wang Feng was sent to make contact, with Wang Shiying, Nan Hanchen, Chen Yangshan, and others working in close support and pressing the effort forward.
Our Party's ties with the loyal divisions under General Yang Hucheng were formed comparatively early and ran comparatively deep. Yang was then the head of Shaanxi's local power-holders, serving as commander-in-chief of the Seventeenth Route Army and director of the Xi'an Pacification Office. He wanted to resist Japan, and held some progressive ideas. Under the leadership of the Party Center and the CCP's Shaanxi provincial committee, underground Party organizations had been built early on within Yang's 38th Army (commanded by Zhao Shoushan) and the New 35th Division (commanded by Kong Congzhou); much work had been done, and with effort the progressive officers and men had been drawn together, until these units became a model within the anti-Japanese national united front.
On December 5, 1935, the Center sent Wang Feng to Yang Hucheng carrying a letter in Mao Zedong's own hand. Before he set out, Mao said: the political situation is very much in our favor, with many favorable conditions besides; we have put forward a "great northwestern alliance," and by getting on good terms with Generals Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng we can then have a great alliance across the whole country. He added: the chances of the talks succeeding are good, though there is some danger too; the talks must not be allowed to break down, they must reach agreement. Zhou Enlai told Wang Feng: when you get there, first sound out Mr. Yang's thinking from the side, and go and look up Mr. Du Binchen.
In Xi'an Wang Feng delivered Mao Zedong's letter, met Yang Hucheng three times, and had several earnest talks with Yang's adviser Du Binchen, completing the Center's first direct contact with Yang. Because Yang had not known Wang Feng before and could not quite bring himself to trust a stranger's credentials, he sent word to Tianjin inviting Wang Shiying or Nan Hanchen to come to Xi'an for a face-to-face talk. (Nan had been Yang's chief secretary in 1931; both Wang and Nan were then in charge of the North China Liaison Bureau.) The reason was this: in November 1935, when Yang Hucheng attended the Kuomintang's Fifth Congress in Nanjing, Wang Shiying and Nan Hanchen had sent Shen Bochun to see him there, to acquaint him with our Party's "August First Declaration" and its call to "stop the civil war and resist Japan as one," and to urge him to join the Communists against Japan. Yang gladly agreed, and asked Shen Bochun to go back to Tianjin and get from Nan Hanchen and Wang Shiying the concrete terms of an anti-Japanese alliance with the Communists. Nan wrote at once, setting out six measures, which Shen carried to Yang.
In late December 1935, Wang Shiying, about to go to northern Shaanxi to report to the Party Center, came to Xi'an at Yang Hucheng's invitation in his capacity as a responsible Party man. Wang and Yang were old acquaintances, and this meeting fell just when Yang's position was difficult: Chiang Kai-shek was leaning on him by every means, and his relations with Zhang Xueliang had gone sour too. At their first meeting Yang asked outright, "Is Wang Feng your man?" Wang Shiying said, "He is. Send him to northern Shaanxi together with me!" At the second meeting Yang went to great lengths, for one of his division commanders, Feng Qinzai, had already thrown in with Chiang, and he feared exposure. He had Wang Juren (once a Party member, and known to Wang Shiying) show Wang Shiying into a room in a newly built house and lock the door, letting no one else know; he himself pretended he wanted to rest, went to another room and undressed as if for sleep, and only once his guards and orderlies had gone off to amuse themselves did he dress again and go to speak with Wang Shiying alone. The gist of the talk was this: for now no one was to be sent openly to negotiate with him; the Red Army would not attack him, and he would never attack the Red Army. The two sides reached a preliminary agreement to cooperate. Yang also gave Wang Shiying 500 silver dollars. Wang Shiying and Wang Feng were escorted to the base area in northern Shaanxi by Yang's regimental commander, the Communist Yan Kuiyao, and with the 500 silver dollars they bought a large stock of fountain pens, ink, and other stationery to carry north. In mid-March 1936 Wang Shiying reached the Damaijiao district of Xi County in Shanxi (now part of Jiaokou County) and reported on Yang Hucheng to Mao Zedong and the responsible comrades of the Center. In April 1936, coming out of northern Shaanxi, Wang met Yang once more and told him our Party broadly agreed with his views: we would not send an open representative or set up a radio station, but would send people to keep secret contact and build a secret courier line.
Because the united-front work toward both Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng had gone so well and opened up the situation, cadres had to be gathered to carry united-front work into the Northeastern and Northwestern armies. At this point Chen Yangshan and Chen Kehan came from Chongqing to Shanghai to report to the liaison station the important military and political intelligence they had obtained. They had meant to return to Sichuan once they had reported, but afterward the Center's instructions came: neither was to go back to Sichuan; Chen Yangshan was transferred to northern Shaanxi to study and Chen Kehan to work there.
In July 1936, on reaching Xi'an, Chen Yangshan was assigned to the Party Center's Xi'an intelligence station to take part in liaison with the Northwestern Army. He was to work under cover as a journalist and help Wu Xiru run the Cultural Weekly, spreading our Party's case against civil war and for a united resistance among the Northwestern Army.
The Cultural Weekly was funded by Zhang Xueliang and founded by the Anti-Japanese Comrades' Association, the secret core body within the Northeastern Army; it was a journal to guide the movement of resistance and national salvation. Its editor-in-chief was the left-wing writer Wu Xiru, and among those who often wrote for it were the left-wing writers Xu Pingyu and Ding Ling. It printed 5,000 copies an issue, sold well in Xi'an, Lanzhou, and across the whole northwest, made a strong case for uniting to resist Japan, and did real good in pushing the salvation movement forward.
Chen Yangshan's way into liaison with the Northwestern Army ran through Song Qiyun, Yang Hucheng's secretary and a secret member of the CCP. Song was an intelligence officer in the Military Commission system under Wang Shiying, and knew Chen Yangshan. He was then director of the Northwest Culture Daily in Xi'an, and through him Chen obtained a reporter's post at the paper. Through Song, too, Chen came to know many middle-ranking officers of the Northwestern Army and progressive figures in the press. Most of these men felt deep sympathy for our Party, warmly backed its call to stop the civil war and resist Japan together, and were sincerely willing to do something for it. Because they moved in wide circles and held a certain standing, Chen Yangshan, through his contact with them, gathered a great deal of intelligence about conditions in society.
After the Xi'an Incident broke out, on December 19 Zhou Enlai led a CCP delegation into Xi'an, and to keep in touch with the Party Center it was decided to set up a "Xi'an branch of the Red China News Agency." Zhou put Chen Yangshan in charge of building it, with Chen Kehan and Chen Bo (Bulu) among the staff brought in. Once the branch was established, it was decided that Li Yimang of the Shaanxi provincial committee would run it, and Chen Yangshan returned to his work at the Xi'an intelligence station.
The Xi'an branch of the Red China News Agency was, in kind, a branch of the "Red China News Agency," the forerunner of Xinhua. Its task was to supply the newspapers and public bodies of Xi'an with the Red China dispatches taken down by the Red Army's Xi'an office, and with the proclamations and declarations our Party issued.
After the Xi'an Incident, soldiers and civilians across the country rose to demand that the Kuomintang resist Japan. Our Party's policy of an anti-Japanese national united front won wide support, and the salvation movement flourished. In these circumstances the Kuomintang was forced to turn from appeasing Japan to agreeing to resist, and from staunch anti-Communism to a second period of cooperation it could not avoid. This fine political turn was the best possible moment for united-front work, and the social ground on which the Xi'an station gathered its intelligence. The station seized on the broad popular wish to stop the civil war and resist Japan together, and opened up anti-Japanese united-front work among every class without reserve: old contacts in the Northwestern Army; comrades who had lost their Party ties after the failed Shaanxi uprisings; enlightened gentry who favored resistance; salvation groups in the world of education; progressive soldiers in the Northeastern and Northwestern armies; and social notables. All of them admired our Party's sound stand in the Xi'an Incident, and drew willingly toward it. In a very short time, then, the Xi'an station had built a good social base.
Intelligence work must serve the needs of the political struggle. Given the political situation of the time, the Xi'an station concentrated on gathering intelligence of the following kinds: (1) the words, movements, and dispositions of the Kuomintang's military and political chiefs in Xi'an; (2) the Kuomintang's various plans to undermine the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region; (3) how Kuomintang agents watched, restricted, and disrupted our Party; (4) how the Kuomintang deceived, kidnapped, and murdered young people bound for Yan'an; (5) the state of the KMT-CCP talks and the people's response; (6) the disposition and movements of Kuomintang troops in Shaanxi; (7) the Kuomintang's handling of the Northeastern and Northwestern armies; (8) the Kuomintang's relations with Japan and its activities in Xi'an; (9) the views and reactions of the people toward our Party and army.
This intelligence came chiefly from people in the following quarters:
1. Figures of the middle and upper strata in every field. In Xi'an there was a large body of old soldiers who had come down from the Northwestern Army; they resented the Kuomintang's treatment of that army but deeply admired our Party's stand on settling the Xi'an Incident. They made contact with us through all sorts of channels, and some even offered to work for our Party. Because they moved in wide circles and held a certain standing and political weight, through our contact with them we learned much about the political currents in society.
2. People who had lost touch with the Party. The Shaanxi Party organization had launched several uprisings, all of which failed, and under the white terror some members had lost their Party ties. Some of these had found posts in Kuomintang government offices or in education and no longer sought the Party; some had recanted or turned traitor. In our contacts with them we found most were sound and still willing to work for the revolution. They had links in every quarter and could supply us with something of what was going on in society.
3. Progressive elements within the Kuomintang forces. A number of middle-ranking officers in the Northeastern and Northwestern armies wanted to join our Party and army, or felt deep sympathy for our Party and were willing to work for it, and reached us through various channels.
Among the Whampoa officers in the Kuomintang forces, some wanted to understand our Party's political stand and some were willing to give us intelligence. In our contacts with these officers we gathered a certain amount of military intelligence.
4. Progressive elements among officials, social notables, enlightened gentry, and the educated. These people came to us for different reasons; apart from a few with private designs, most were sincerely willing to do something for our Party, and some had already made contact with our Party or with anti-Japanese groups.
5. People in charge of confidential work in Kuomintang military and civil offices. With these we varied our means of contact according to their standing, occupation, and freedom of movement. Through our dealings with them we found that the great majority worked for us in all sincerity, and some of the intelligence they gave us was come by at real risk.
The Party Center's Xi'an intelligence station was set up in the spring of 1936, when people across the country were calling to stop the civil war and resist Japan together and the national scene was turning from KMT-CCP civil war toward a united resistance. It began with only three or four people and later grew to seven or eight; those who worked there over time were Qi Yuande, Chen Yangshan, Tao Shiyong, Luo Qingchang, Sai Xianfo, Cheng Ziping, Yu Zhongyou, and Xiao Fuxian, with Wu Defeng in charge.
The Xi'an station stood at No. 21 Caojia Lane on West Street in Xi'an, a single compound of two courtyards front and back, facing the back gate of the primary school attached to the city's women's normal school. Many of that school's teachers were young people of patriotic mind who backed our Party's call for resistance and national salvation. To reach these patriotic young intellectuals and carry salvation teaching to them, in 1937 Chen Yangshan enrolled his eldest son, Jianyu, at the school, using this as a bridge to the progressive young. It worked very well: many teachers, through their contact with us, grew close to us and were willing to work for us. Once, for instance, when Qi Yuande was out on an errand and an agent was trailing her, she nimbly slipped into the school and said she had come to fetch Jianyu home; Teacher Zhang, seeing she was in trouble, let her out by the school's back gate, and so helped her shake off the agent and turn danger into safety.
After the Lugou Bridge Incident of July 1937, the flames of all-out resistance were lit, the national salvation movement rose higher and higher, and the Xi'an station grew busier still. Chen Yangshan was out from dawn to dusk each day, and having two children at his side made things awkward; besides, Japanese planes were often harassing Xi'an then, and he worried for the children's safety, so he asked the organization to send them to Yan'an. As it happened, at the end of 1938 a group of cadres was transferred to work in Yan'an, and the organization sent Chen Yangshan's eldest son, Jianyu (aged eight), north with them.
In the early days of the war, Xi'an was a place both Kuomintang and Communists watched closely, a city of tangled circumstances. Here were the troops of Chiang Kai-shek's loyal general Hu Zongnan, the secret-service organs of the Kuomintang and of Shaanxi province, Zhang Xueliang's Northeastern Army, Yang Hucheng's Northwestern Army, the remnant forces of the Shaanxi warlords, and a few of our Party's own traitors, all jumbled together. To do intelligence work in a city of such tangled politics carried real risk without a nimble art of struggle, yet the Xi'an station never once came to grief.
During his time at the Xi'an station Chen Yangshan went by the alias Chen Mingjun, working under cover as a reporter for the Northwest Culture Daily on intelligence and united-front work. In four years he made many friends outside the Party, did a good deal of work, and obtained much intelligence of real value. In this period Chen worked with Nan Hanchen, Liu Ding, Xuan Xiafu, Xu Binru, Song Qiyun, and others to press united-front work forward. He often met famous patriots such as Du Binchen, Li Furen, Gao Chongmin, Yang Mingxuan, Han Zhuoru, Che Xiangchen, and Xu Zhongquan at the Lianhu canteen in Xi'an's Lianhu Park, run by Jiang Ziming as a secret Party contact point, to plan the work of resistance and salvation. He put in a great deal of hard work to push the salvation movement forward and to help build the anti-Japanese national united front, giving his own share and making a notable mark. Because it had built a good social base, the Xi'an station gathered much valuable and important intelligence.
The Xi'an station did its work without any source of funds, yet did it to great effect, and had a firm grasp of the enemy's situation in the Xi'an area. For this the Party Center rated its work highly.
### 2. Back in Yan'an: Rectification Study as Preparation for Battle
After the War of Resistance settled into stalemate, the enemy, to wreck the resistance and the revolutionary base areas, sent large numbers of spies to slip into them.
By this time Chen Yangshan, after sixteen cruel years of clandestine struggle in the White areas, was summoned to work in the Central Social Affairs Department at Yan'an.
One day in June 1940, Wu Defeng, who ran the Xi'an station, told Chen Yangshan: "Word has come from the Center transferring you back to the Central Social Affairs Department at Yan'an. Hand over your work and get to the Xi'an office quickly to make contact."
When Chen Yangshan had handed over his work, he went straight home and said to his wife, Zhang Suzhen: "Mother is gravely ill and I must go to Shanghai at once to see her. Pack quickly, we leave right away; I'll go hire a cab."
In no time Chen Yangshan had hired two rickshaws; he put Zhang Suzhen and their three children in one and rode in the other with the luggage, straight for the Xi'an railway station.
At the station Chen Yangshan had Zhang Suzhen go to buy the tickets. She said she did not have enough money on her, and Chen made a show of feeling in his pockets: "Blast, I've lost my wallet. Here's what we'll do: you wait here and I'll go borrow from a friend."
Zhang Suzhen paid off the fares, and once the two rickshaw men had gone off into the distance, Chen Yangshan hurried to the Eighth Route Army's Xi'an office at Qixianzhuang to make contact, and asked the office to send someone at once to bring Zhang Suzhen and her three children, four in all, back from the station. Only then did he tell her the truth: they were not going to Shanghai to see his mother but to Yan'an; the lost wallet had been a deliberate act, a guard against the rickshaw men being agents on their trail, which was why he had not gone straight to the office.
A few days later Chen Yangshan rode by truck with several other comrades bound for the Central Social Affairs Department, back to the revolutionary holy city of Yan'an.
For years Chen Yangshan had done secret work in the enemy-held areas under the white terror, forever in touch with enemy agents, where a moment's carelessness could mean arrest or death; he had been sharply on guard at every moment, in everything, everywhere, his nerves always drawn tight. Now, back among his own people, he felt his nerves slacken into freedom, and his spirits were wonderfully at ease and glad.
The day Chen Yangshan returned to the Central Social Affairs Department, its head, Kang Sheng, received the whole family. Chen had known Kang for a long time; in the 1930s, working in the Central Special Branch in Shanghai, he had served alongside him. Old friends meeting again, the two were warm with each other. In the course of talking, when Kang Sheng learned that Zhang Suzhen had still not joined the Party, he reproved Chen Yangshan gravely: "Zhang Suzhen joined the Special Branch back in 1929 and has shielded your work for more than ten years without a single slip, with a fine record; she is a nameless hero, and that you have still not helped settle her Party membership is really too bad." Afterward Kang directed the organization department to settle Zhang Suzhen's membership as soon as possible. Since she had already stood the practical tests of revolutionary struggle and met the conditions for membership, she was admitted to the Party at the end of that year, sponsored by Comrades Luo Qingchang and Qiu Jin, with her probation exceptionally set at six months.
On the question of Chen Yangshan's own assignment, Kang Sheng asked his views. Chen said he was fairly at home in intelligence work. A few days later he was made section chief of the local-work section of the Central Social Affairs Department, in charge of guiding counter-espionage work in the base areas. Before long an intelligence-cadre training class was set up, and he was moved to head it.
As for Zhang Suzhen's assignment, since her schooling was slight and she could not take on professional work, she was made head of the department's nursery. Once in the post she was especially diligent, setting an example in everything, and she was particularly careful in raising the children, so that each grew up sound in body and mind; the parents and her colleagues thought highly of her. For this, at the 1941 celebration of March Eighth, International Women's Day, deputy department head Li Kenong praised her by name.
In 1941 the intelligence network under Pan Hannian had penetrated a Japanese intelligence organ in Hong Kong, and cadres were needed to reinforce it. Who would be the right one to send?
After turning it over and over, Kang Sheng judged Chen Yangshan the best choice. Chen was a capable old intelligence hand who knew Japanese and was on good terms with Pan Hannian, which made for easy cooperation. So it was decided to send Chen Yangshan and his wife together.
That November, Chen Yangshan set out from Yan'an with his wife, Zhang Suzhen, and their small son, Zhenyu, meaning to go first to Chongqing and then to Hong Kong. When they reached Baoji in Shaanxi, the Kuomintang made trouble and held them up. On instructions from the Central Social Affairs Department, Chen was ordered to the Eighth Route Army's Xi'an office to await further orders, while Zhang Suzhen took their small son to the Eighth Route Army's Chongqing office to be assigned work.
In those days the staff of the Chongqing office were run off their feet, out from dawn to dusk with no time to look after their children, and since the office had no nursery they had to make shift as best they could to solve the problem. When Zhang Suzhen arrived and it was learned that she had headed the nursery at the Central Social Affairs Department in Yan'an and had experience with children, Elder Sister Deng Yingchao sought her out and asked her views on setting up a nursery. Zhang Suzhen said she would do as the organization arranged. And so a nursery was set up, with Zhang Suzhen assigned to head it. Once it was going, she worked hard and cared for the children with such attention that several who had been sickly and small soon recovered their health; the children there were all neatly dressed, ate and slept by a regular routine, and slowly grew plump and rosy. The parents were well pleased, and praised the nursery's work without end. For this Zhang Suzhen won the praise of Zhou Enlai and Elder Sister Deng Yingchao as well.
In February 1942 Chen Yangshan was recalled to the Central Social Affairs Department at Yan'an. Because he had long done secret work in the enemy-held areas, he had never had a chance to study; just then Yan'an was launching the Rectification Movement, and he was assigned to study at the Central Party School.
Studying in the same branch with him were Song Shilun, Wang Heshou, Xiang Zhonghua, Lei Renmin, Fu Yutian, Jia Zheng, Gao Langshan, Bai Xiangyin, and others.
This was the first chance to study Chen Yangshan had had since joining the revolution, and he treasured it, working with special diligence. In his long years in the enemy-held areas he had rarely seen the Center's documents, and so his grasp of the Party's line, policy, and strategy had been shallow, and the rights and wrongs and the achievements of his own work had been blurred to him. By studying theory and the documents and setting his own practice against them, he opened his eyes wide and came to see clearly the rights and wrongs and the achievements of his past work. Politically, ideologically, and in theory he was much raised, and he learned to bring the standpoint, viewpoint, and method of Marxism-Leninism to the study and handling of problems.
The Rectification Movement at the Central Party School began with the study of rectification documents, the exchange of views, and discussion of what each had learned; people opened their minds and spoke freely, and for a time the mood was mild as a gentle rain, and spirits were easy. From June on, however, the struggle grew overheated: from criticism of Li Guohua's cast of thought it moved to criticism of the "political problems" of Wu Xiru and Wang Shiwei, with mass meetings held one after another and the pitch climbing ever higher, until many comrades were caught up and branded traitors, agents, or Trotskyites.
In view of this, from October on the work shifted from remolding thought to organizational review: the branches were reshaped and the vetting of cadres began. At this point Chen Yangshan was recommended as secretary of the Seventh Branch. The Seventh Branch then often met together with the Fifth Branch, under Fan Shiren and Lin Yixin, and the Sixth Branch, under Mo Wenhua, Zeng Zhi, and Kang Keqing, to carry on the vetting of cadres. It was in these three branches that Jiang Qing and Ye Qun were vetted.
By Chen Yangshan's and Song Shilun's recollection, Jiang Qing's historical problems were among the matters reported to the school's Party committee.
After Jiang Qing was arrested in Shanghai in 1934, she was released a month or two later; asked how she had come to be freed, she was not only evasive and vague but feigned madness and wept, resorting to a hooligan's tricks. Since it proved very hard to carry the review further, no conclusion was reached.
Ye Qun's social connections were tangled and her own history unclear; she had taken part in the "Three People's Principles Youth Corps" and had also worked at the Kuomintang's central radio station. But for lack of evidence no conclusion was reached here either.
In the spring of 1943, at Kang Sheng's instigation, the so-called "Movement to Rescue the Fallen" was launched, which turned the Rectification away from its first aim of remolding study and thought, manufactured many false and unjust cases, wronged not a few comrades, and caused enormous confusion and loss. In this foul tide Chen Yangshan did not drift with the current, which was no small thing.
In October 1943 the Rectification entered the stage of cadre vetting and screening. The Party's Seventh Congress was also about to open, and delegates from the base areas were gathering at the Central Party School to study; for this the school reorganized its leadership, with Mao Zedong himself as principal and Peng Zhen as deputy, and Chen Yangshan, at thirty-seven, was made chief of the school's cadre section, in charge of vetting the Seventh Congress delegates, and put into the senior-cadre group studying and discussing the "Question of the Two Lines."
On April 21, 1943, the Seventh National Congress of the Communist Party of China held its preparatory meeting. The delegates, full and alternate alike, all attended save those excused for illness or other business. Taking part in the Seventh Congress was held to be a most exalted thing. The delegates numbered 755 in all: 547 full delegates and 208 alternates (without vote). They fell into eight delegations: the central directly-subordinate (including the army's directly-subordinate system), Shaan-Gan-Ning, Jin-Sui, Jin-Cha-Ji, Jin-Ji-Lu-Yu, Shandong, Central China, and the Great Rear Area.
Chen Yangshan took part in the Seventh Congress's preparatory meeting.
At the preparatory meeting Ren Bishi first reported on how the congress had been prepared. He said: the Seventh Congress should have been held long ago, but because of the war and the severing of communications it had been put off until today. This was of course a shortcoming, yet it had its positive side. For during the delay we had, first of all, built up our strength to the full. There are now 1.2 million Party members, close to a million troops under the Party's leadership, and a population of nearly a hundred million in the liberated areas. Tempered by all this, the Party has become the deciding factor in the nation's politics. A Seventh Congress convened on such a foundation is of very great meaning and effect. Second, the Party is more united in thought than before. Rectification, the study of line, and the vetting of cadres have brought unity of thought within the Party and greater purity in its organization. The "Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of the Party," adopted by the Seventh Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, is the very reflection of an unprecedented unity. This is the ideological preparation for the Seventh Congress, and its meaning too is very great. Ren Bishi went on to explain, point by point, how the delegates had been chosen, the congress agenda, and the state of the draft reports.
Mao Zedong gave a report on the guiding policy for the Seventh Congress. He said: the policy of this congress is to "unite as one and win victory"; put simply, one word is unity and one is victory. Victory means our goal; unity means our front, our ranks. We must have a united body of troops to overthrow our enemy and win victory; and the chief thing in those ranks, the thing that leads, is our Party. Without our Party the Chinese people cannot possibly win. He went on: in the twenty-four years of our Party's course it has drunk hardship to the dregs, fought heroically on a grand scale, and never feared sacrifice. There were, of course, differing views within the Party for a time, chiefly on three questions: first, who is the enemy and who the friend; second, how to organize the ranks; third, how to fight. Yesterday the Seventh Plenum in the main passed the resolution on Party history, to be handed to the new Central Committee after the Seventh Congress to adopt and amend. Our congress must look forward, not back. We have not yet won; there are difficulties ahead; we must be modest and prudent, guard against arrogance and rashness, and the whole Party must be as united as brothers and sisters, striving for victory across the country and never resting until we win.
From April 23 to June 11, 1943, the Seventh National Congress of the Communist Party of China was held in solemn state at Yan'an. Mao Zedong delivered the opening address; Zhu De, Liu Shaoqi, Zhou Enlai, Lin Boqu, and Okano Susumu, the Japanese Communist Party's representative then in Yan'an, spoke in turn at the opening ceremony, affirming the great achievements of the Chinese Communist Party and the correct leadership of Chairman Mao Zedong and wishing the Seventh Congress success.
Mao Zedong delivered the political report at the congress. He wrote out a political report, "On Coalition Government," and had a copy given to every delegate. This written report analyzed the situation at home and abroad, summed up the struggle between the two lines during the war, set out the Chinese Communist Party's general and specific programs, and pointed to the future in which the Chinese people should strive to defeat the aggressors and build a new China. Its contents are already well known. Liu Shaoqi delivered the "Report on the Revision of the Party Constitution," Zhu De the military report "On the Battlefields of the Liberated Areas," and Zhou Enlai the important speech "On the United Front." Ren Bishi, Chen Yun, and others also spoke.
To teach the whole Party to read more and raise its level of Marxism-Leninism, Mao Zedong at the Seventh Congress again proposed five Marxist-Leninist works. These were: the Communist Manifesto; Socialism: Utopian and Scientific; The Two Tactics of Social-Democracy in the Democratic Revolution; "Left-Wing" Communism, an Infantile Disorder; and the History of the CPSU(B).
The Seventh Congress marked Chen Yangshan's whole life deeply, and above all the five Marxist-Leninist works Mao Zedong named and Mao's "On Coalition Government" remained his lifelong reading.
In July 2004, when Chen Yangshan's son Chen Jianyu and grandson Chen Guobao came to Shanghai to hand over Chen Yangshan's treasured effects to the National Security Education Hall, among them were a yellowed copy of "Left-Wing" Communism, an Infantile Disorder and a copy of "On Coalition Government."
Guan Fushan, a fellow delegate to the Party's Seventh Congress, recalling this unforgettable time with Chen Yangshan, said:
{v} It was not early that I came to know Comrade Yangshan. But reckoning from when I first knew him only by sight, it was no short time. Back in 1941, studying at Zaoyuan in Yan'an, I already knew him. Whenever the Zaoyuan offices called a cadres' meeting, everyone would sit in a ring under those great pear trees, and there you would always see the face of a plain, elderly man; that was Comrade Yangshan. In truth he was only eight and a half years older than I, yet from a life full of hardship and trial he already had the kindly bearing of an elder.
{v} In early spring 1942 I entered the first section of the Central Party School in Yan'an as a delegate to the Party's Seventh Congress. Soon the study movement to rectify the three work-styles began. About then this familiar face began to appear again and again.
{v} Everyone says the Yan'an Rectification was a good thing. Under Comrade Mao Zedong's own initiative and leadership, from the spring of 1942, when the Party Center issued the "April Third Decision," we studied the rectification documents, exchanged views, and talked over what we had learned, longing to remake ourselves. For a time minds did indeed open and tongues run free, mild as a gentle rain, and spirits were easy. But the good days did not last, only two or three months. From the summer of that year overheated struggle broke out at the Central Party School. Beginning with criticism of Li Guohua's cast of thought, it grew into criticism of the "political problems" of Wu Xiru and Wang Shiwei, with mass meeting after mass meeting, the pitch climbing higher and higher, and no end of sarcasm and slander. Among them was a set of veteran "Left" zealots who did all they could to fan the flames. By the onset of winter the study and remolding of thought had simply been thrown aside for organizational review, which went further still, into the so-called "Movement to Rescue the Fallen" of the spring and summer of 1943. This took a crooked road and brought the opposite of what was meant, betraying the first aim of the Rectification and causing enormous confusion and loss. As I see it, this foul tide came of some "Left" zealots resisting the wise policy of rectifying the three work-styles. Not a few young intellectual Party cadres found it hard at first to grasp or accept that there could be any talk of factionalism within the Communist leadership. But as things unfolded, the very stubbornness with which the negative example displayed itself untied their perplexity. In all this overheated struggle, one might have supposed that Comrade Yangshan, with his deep seniority, would run with the tide too, the more so as he was then one of the branch secretaries of the school's first section and chief of its cadre section besides, ranking among the school's leading core. Yet in all that overheated struggle no one ever saw Comrade Yangshan make any childish show of the extreme "Left." This was a rare and admirable thing.
At the end of 1944 the Investigation Bureau of the Jin-Sui Military Region urgently needed leading cadres, and on He Long's recommendation the Central Intelligence Department sent Chen Yangshan to be director of the bureau. Chen Yangshan had returned to work on the hidden front.
### 3. Three Questions for Kang Sheng: A Comrade's Blood, a Comrade's Tears
The Soviet Union of 1937 stood in the third year since Kirov, a member of the Political Bureau of the CPSU Central Committee, had been assassinated.
The Kirov affair of 1934 had shaken the Chinese Communists too, and it bore all the more on the CCP Party branch then in the Soviet Union. The head of the CCP delegation to the Communist International was Wang Ming, with Kang Sheng as his deputy, and the two of them led the Party branch in the Soviet Union.
No sooner had the campaign to "suppress counter-revolutionaries and root out Trotskyites" begun than Kang Sheng, who had long had his plan ready, sprang forward. He and Wang Ming at once set up an office for the purge. Wang Ming took nominal command and Kang Sheng was his second; but in fact the real power lay in Kang Sheng's hands.
The moment the campaign opened, Kang Sheng struck a first blow to cow everyone. Working through the Soviet security organs, he held in his hand a stock of labels: "traitor," "agent," "spy," "Trotskyite," "collaborator." Anyone who had ever offered him a criticism had one pinned on him; anyone he took a dislike to, he could pull a label out of his "bag" and clap on his head.
"There is nothing wrong in my past. If you say I made mistakes in my work, I don't deny it, but as for traitor, agent, Trotskyite, none of it touches me." So answered, with one voice, several of the chief men Kang Sheng named: Xiao Shouhuang, Ouyang Xin, He Changzhi.
And yet, by 1937 the comrades-in-arms who had once fought beside Chen Yangshan in the Central Special Branch, Wu Hujing, Xiao Shouhuang, Ouyang Xin, He Changzhi, and others, had their blood spilled on Russian soil in the Soviet purge of the Party, and were secretly put to death.
Soon after Chen Yangshan returned to the Central Social Affairs Department at Yan'an in 1940, he heard it said that Wu Hujing, Ouyang Xin, Xiao Shouhuang, He Changzhi, and other comrades, cadres of the Central Special Branch under the former Central Special Committee who had been sent to study in the Soviet Union in the 1930s, had all been put to death by the Soviet security organs in the purge of the Party, on suspicion of being "Trotskyites," "agents," "traitors."
The news shook Chen Yangshan: these comrades, in their work in the Central Special Branch in Shanghai, had been heroes who fought the enemy at the risk of their lives, fearing neither hardship nor death; how could they have turned into "Trotskyites," "agents," "traitors" the moment they reached the Soviet Union? He did not believe it was true. So he went to ask Kang Sheng.
Kang Sheng told him these men had problems in their past.
Chen Yangshan asked whether all of them had problems in their history, and Kang Sheng answered that he was not clear on it. He spoke very vaguely, as if unwilling to say much. Chen Yangshan got no satisfactory answer.
Since these comrades, in their time working with Chen Yangshan in the Central Special Branch, had never been heard to have any problem in their history, and since when the Central Special Branch was wound up in 1935 and they were sent to the Soviet Union they had been vetted by the provisional Central Committee, Chen Yangshan simply did not believe they could have problems in their past. So he went to ask Kang Sheng once more.
Kang Sheng answered that these men had problems in their work while in the Special Branch.
Chen Yangshan asked what problems, and Kang Sheng reeled off some things that had nothing to do with their work. They were plainly an evasion, and Chen Yangshan was quite unsatisfied with the answer, and did not believe it.
During the Rectification Movement of 1942, Chen Yangshan went to ask Kang Sheng yet again. Kang said, with a touch of impatience, that he had been in France at the time and did not know the particulars of the killings.
Given this, there was no way to press the questions further. But Chen Yangshan felt that of all people Kang Sheng must know most about how these comrades had died, for at the time he had been deputy head of our Party's delegation to the Communist International, and could not but have known of so grave a matter. Yet he had answered very vaguely, with three different explanations, and there had to be some guilty secret in it. What was more, among the Special Branch cadres who had gone to the Soviet Union to study along with these comrades was Kang Sheng's own sister-in-law, Su Mei: how was it that Su Mei had not been killed? Plainly Kang Sheng bore toward these comrades some unspeakable guilt. But at the time there was no firm evidence, and it had to be let go. Still, the doubt never left him, and he resolved to find some way to get to the bottom of it. Later it was established that these cadres had indeed been murdered by Kang Sheng's design, using the chance of the Soviet purge. The facts were as follows:
When these comrades worked in the Central Special Branch, Kang Sheng was head of its Action Section. He had had no few comrades killed on false charges of being "collaborators," "traitors," "agents." These comrades knew this about him, and so he saw them as thorns in his eye. On the charge that Xiao Shouhuang, He Changzhi, and Ouyang Xin were "traitors," "agents," "Trotskyites," he had the three arrested through the Soviet security organs and thrown into prison. One night Kang Sheng interrogated Xiao Shouhuang, He Changzhi, and Ouyang Xin in person, pressing them to confess they were "traitors," "agents," "Trotskyites." The three answered with one voice: "If you say we made mistakes in our work, we do not deny it. In our history we have nothing wrong. You say we are 'traitors,' 'agents,' 'Trotskyites'; then produce your proof." Kang Sheng said savagely: "I say you have problems in your history, so you have problems in your history." Before long these three comrades, with no evidence at all, were killed by the Soviet security organs on the charge of being "traitors," "agents," "Trotskyites."
Having killed these three comrades, Kang Sheng had rid himself of a grave threat close at hand. But in his mind Wu Hujing, too, was one he must find a way to be rid of.
Wu Hujing was a Shandong man who had worked in the Central Organization Department in 1929; he was Kang Sheng's fellow townsman and colleague, knew Kang's true colors best of all, and was besides a senior cadre, having served as secretary of the CCP's Northern Manchuria Special Committee, a cadre of the Central Military Commission, a head of the Central Special Branch, and in other posts, before being sent to study in the Soviet Union in 1935. One day Kang Sheng came upon Wu Hujing at the guesthouse of our Party's delegation to the Communist International. Seeing Wu in poor health, he urged him to go into hospital, and before long Wu Hujing was indeed sent there for treatment. Kang Sheng went to visit him and said: "For the sake of secrecy, there is no need to let out anything about the work of the Central Special Branch in Shanghai; things here are very complicated." Then he asked Wu Hujing: "Since you came to Moscow, have you heard any talk about me?" Wu Hujing said: "Wang Ming handles people by striking with one hand and pulling with the other, boastful, high-handed, and overbearing; at home he was already resented, and in the Soviet Union he is the same as ever and has quite lost people's hearts. They say you are his helper and follow him close; that will come to no good end, and I hope you will not go turning about after him."
But Wu Hujing had not reckoned that these few words of honest counsel would give offense to Kang Sheng and Wang Ming. And so Wu Hujing was put on Kang Sheng's blacklist. Before long, by Kang Sheng's contriving, Wu Hujing was handed to the Soviet security organs on a charge of being both traitor and agent, and put to death.
This is the ironclad proof of how Kang Sheng, in the Soviet Union, murdered our Party's cadres under cover of the Soviet purge. In 1976, when the anti-Party crimes of Lin Biao and the "Gang of Four" were being reckoned up, Chen Yangshan exposed this crime of Kang Sheng's to the Party Center.
Working alongside Kang Sheng in the Central Special Branch in Shanghai in the 1930s, Chen Yangshan had already heard something of how Kang conducted himself, and this affair brought him to know Kang's character better still. From then on, in his dealings with Kang Sheng, he was always careful and wary, keeping a respectful distance.
Kang Sheng was a man of warped mind, sinister and crafty in his dealings, a schemer and careerist forever on the make in politics. In 1930 he followed Li Lisan and pushed the "Li Lisan line" hard; then, after Wang Ming seized the leadership, he clung close to Wang Ming, playing the vanguard in trumpeting Wang Ming's "Left" dogmatist line and setting about to strike down the comrades charged with the errors of the "Li Lisan line." And when the Wang Ming line came under criticism, he changed his coat again, dressed himself up as a "hero" who had criticized the Wang Ming line, and cheated Mao Zedong into trusting him.
During the Yan'an Rectification of 1942, Kang Sheng ran his "Rescue Movement" and framed not a few comrades. Mao Zedong was not pleased with the way Kang went about it. Though he too had overestimated how impure the cadre ranks were, he kept fresh in memory the error of over-expanding the purge in the days of the Central Soviet Area, and so he stressed again and again that the past error must not be repeated, that there must be no "coercion, confession, and credence." On July 1, 1943, Mao Zedong wrote to Kang Sheng, asking him to publish these few sentences in the sixth issue of Experience in Guarding Against Traitors:
{v} The two lines in the work of guarding against traitors. The correct line is: "The leader takes responsibility and sets to work himself; the leading core is combined with the broad masses; general appeals are combined with individual guidance; investigate and study; distinguish right from wrong and grave from slight; win back the fallen; train cadres; educate the masses." The wrong line is: "coercion, confession, credence." We should carry out the correct line and oppose the wrong.
The points Mao Zedong drew together for the correct line were what came to be called the "Nine-Point Policy" for cadre vetting.
That same month, at Zaoyuan, Mao Zedong spoke with Yuan Renyuan, the commissioner for Suide, and asked how the "Rescue Movement" was going there. Mao said: don't go in for coercion, confession, and credence. You press a man, and having no way out he talks wildly, and once he talks you believe it. Then you go and press the people he has named, and they talk in turn, and it grows and grows. In the purges of the past we had bitter lessons. This time, whatever else, we must not go in for coercion, confession, and credence; we must investigate and study, we must weigh the evidence, and where there is no material proof there must at least be witnesses. Do not take a man's word and believe it true; analyze it concretely, do not lightly trust a confession. Of those under suspicion, kill not one, arrest most not at all. Killing must be done with the greatest care: once you have killed a man, then, if evidence later proves it a mistake, you may set the record right, but the man is dead; the dead cannot come back to life, and only his good name can be restored. Nor should you arrest men at will: what would you arrest him for, and where could he run to? Mao Zedong's anxiety showed through in the way he said these things over and over.
On July 30, in a telegram to Peng Dehuai, Mao Zedong again restated the correct line for cadre vetting, the "Nine-Point Policy," pointing out: "This realistic, seek-truth-from-facts policy must be set wholly apart from the subjectivist policy that harmed the Party in the civil-war years, that subjectivist policy being the three words: coercion, confession, credence."
In a Rectification Movement whose main content was opposition to subjectivism, there arose the "Rescue Movement," in which the "subjectivist policy" ran rampant; it could not but shake Mao Zedong. In a notice issued on August 5, the Central General Study Committee accordingly called for the "Rescue Movement" against the fallen in Yan'an to be brought to a close.
During the "Great Cultural Revolution," Kang Sheng again threw in with Lin Biao and the "Gang of Four" and persecuted a great many senior Party cadres, many of them comrades he knew had rendered the Party service. Kang Sheng met the punishment history owed him: in 1980 the Party Center decided to expel him from the Party, and his ashes were cast out of the cemetery of revolutionary martyrs. Such is the end that must come to every schemer and careerist.
On February 15, 1979, Chen Yangshan wrote to the Center in Beijing, exposing Kang Sheng's crime of murdering our Party's cadres in the Soviet Union in the 1930s:
{v} After the Central Special Branch was wound up in 1935, some cadres were assigned to work in other cities and departments, and some were assigned to study in the Soviet Union. Of those assigned to study in the Soviet Union I know four: Xiao Shouhuang (from Dapu, Guangdong); Wu Hujing (husband of Comrade Hou Zhi); Ouyang Xin (from Hubei, who had studied artillery in the Soviet Union before 1929, looked like a foreigner, and whom we all called Lao Da or Lao Wang); and He Changzhi (from Hubei, whom we called Lao Hu, whose wife, it was said, had worked in Inner Mongolia). These comrades went to the Soviet Union one after another between the spring and summer of 1935. After they reached the Soviet Union nothing more was ever heard of them.
{v} After I returned from Xi'an to Yan'an in 1940, I made inquiries about them. I heard that in the Soviet purge of the Party they had all been shot on suspicion of being Trotskyites and agents. I was shaken to hear it! I did not believe it was true. For in their work in the Central Special Branch in Shanghai they had fought the enemy resolutely at the risk of their lives, fearing neither death nor hardship, and had never shown the least despondency; how could they have become "Trotskyites" and "agents" the moment they reached the Soviet Union? I could not make sense of it, and so I questioned Kang Sheng three times. The first time Kang Sheng said they had problems in their past. I asked, did all of them have problems? He said he was not clear. He was very vague, as if unwilling to say much.
{v} The second time I questioned Kang Sheng again. He said they had problems in their work while in the Central Special Branch. I asked what problems? He said some things of no consequence.
{v} The third time I asked Kang Sheng once more, and he said he had been in France then and was not clear about how they had died. And so there was no asking further.
{v} Of all people Kang Sheng knew most about these comrades, yet he gave three very vague explanations, each with a different cause. This shows plainly that Kang Sheng was guilty toward these comrades of a crime he could not own to.
