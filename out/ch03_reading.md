## Chapter Three

In the afternoon the whole sky was heavy with black cloud, and the heat was close and stifling beyond all bearing. It was already two o'clock, and the International Funeral Parlour had still not delivered the kind of coffin the Second Young Lady of the Wu house had specified — the sort whose lid was set with a thick pane of glass through which the old master's face could be seen. The coffin sent round earlier had in the end been vetoed by the combined forces of the Second Young Lady and the Fourth Young Lady. The hour of the encoffining had to be put back a full hour. Telephone calls and special messengers went off to the International Funeral Parlour without cease, chasing after it thick as shooting stars. High and low, the whole staff of the Wu mansion had everything in readiness, and waited only for that coffin to come so that the great business of the day might be brought to its close.

Many of the condolence-callers, too, had already gone. There remained only a few close kin and intimate friends, or people who had no pressing business of their own, waiting patiently to see the coffining; and these were now scattered about the cooler spots of the garden, chatting idly in little knots and clusters.

The great dining-room, front and back, that had been the liveliest place of all, was quiet now. Four or five attendants were clearing away the beer bottles and soda-water bottles and sweeping up the fruit-peel and shells that littered the floor. Now and then they exchanged a few grumbling words among themselves:

"Third Master really is an impatient one. A great affair like the old master's — how is it ever to be got through in a single day!"

"That's just his temper! — By what Gaosheng says, earlier in the day Third Master was in a towering rage in the study; Mr. Mo, the accountant from the mill, was very near frightened to death! — And say what you like, you have to grant the old master his luck was none so bad! If he had come out two days later, hey! — They say a telegram came this morning: the country folk over yonder have risen in revolt! — Third Master's rage, ten to one, is over that!"

The man who said this was called Li Gui. He had originally been a servant in the household of the Wu young mistress's own family, and after the year in which the young mistress's father and mother died one upon the other of sudden illness, this Li Gui had thrown in his lot with the Wu house. If one were to say that the thirty-odd menservants and maidservants of the Wu mansion had their factions too, then this Li Gui belonged to the young mistress's party.

"The cab-fares and meal-money alone have run to five hundred and sixty-odd dollars today. Soda-water and beer — thirty dozen of them drunk up."

Another of the attendants turned the talk in a new direction.

"Then the tip Third Master gives us by and by must come to a thousand dollars at the very least!"

It was Li Gui's voice again. Hearing those three words, "a thousand dollars," the attendants' faces all lit up ruddy; but the glow lasted only an instant and then faded again. From their own peculiar experience they knew that this so-called "one thousand dollars" would be doled out in tips graded by rank, and that even were it divided out in equal shares, then — counting in the men who carried the "usher" placards, those who waited before the bier, the odd-job men of every kind, and the great muster of "kitchen troops" sent over from the Juelin Vegetarian Restaurant — their servants' "company," a hundred strong all told, would find each man's portion a paltry pittance. Thinking of this, the four or five of them lost all vigour in their movements, and grew lazier even than before the tip had been mentioned. Their pent-up sense of grievance was still seeking some outlet when all at once a man came in.

It was Fan Bowen, and the listless, dispirited look on his face was in no way inferior to that of these "disappointed" attendants. Turning a slow circle in the middle of the room, Fan Bowen muttered to himself:

"What! Not a soul here either! — Here, Li Gui, have you seen the second Miss — Peishan?"

But without so much as waiting for Li Gui's answer, Fan Bowen suddenly took to his heels, cut through the back half of the great dining-room, ran out by the rear door onto the covered verandah, cast a glance about him on every side, and then burst in through the door that led to the "mourning hall," his searching eyes wide open. Within the "mourning hall" all was hushed and without a sound; not one of the ladies or young misses was there, only four or five maidservants "keeping the bier company," seated on stools against the wall like a row of black clay idols. Old Mr. Wu's body lay in the middle of the room, ringed about with heaped-up little hills of fresh flowers; and here and there amid this "mountain" of blossom, glinting with a cold and glittering light, stood five or six tall rectangular blocks of machine-made ice.

Fan Bowen could not help a shiver; he ducked quickly through the white cloth mourning-curtain, ran out onto the stone steps before the "mourning hall" and let out a breath, lifting his face to the sky. A cold, forlorn savour of loneliness and of a dreary, purposeless tedium came flooding into his "poet's heart."

At the foot of the steps, beside the plain white ceremonial arch, a band of "drummers and pipers" sat dozing over their instruments. They had toiled half the day, and now snatched the chance to close their eyes a moment and hoard up their strength against the last great exertion, when the coffining should come.

To Fan Bowen everything seemed an offence to the eye, everything commonplace and vulgar. He was, in plain truth, growing a little cross. Just then Wu Zhisheng came running up the asphalt path to the right of the steps, his whole face alight with the air of a man who has made some discovery; and seeing Fan Bowen standing there alone, he seized hold of him and made off with him at a run. Fan Bowen followed instinctively, putting once more that same question:

"Have you seen Peishan?"

"I'll tell you presently. But first come with me this instant and see something — no! a piece of live theatre!"

So saying, Wu Zhisheng hurried Fan Bowen through a dense row of lilac trees to a quiet, secluded corner at the far eastern end of the garden. Here stood a glass-roofed "hothouse," its roof now shaded over with an awning of reed matting. To the left of the hothouse was a small, three-bayed, Western-style bungalow; its windows stood open, hung on the outside with fine printed bamboo blinds of the Japanese kind, and gust upon gust of laughter came drifting out through them.

"This is the billiard-room. I've no taste for this sort of thing!"

said Fan Bowen, shaking his head. But Wu Zhisheng at once clapped a hand over Fan Bowen's mouth and hissed low in his ear:

"Don't shout! Look — look what sort of billiards they're playing!"

The two of them stole quietly to one of the windows and peered inside. What a merry crowd it was! The socialite Miss Xu Manli, barefoot, stood swaying with a willowy grace upon a billiard-table, dancing! She had spread her two arms wide and raised one leg — raised it so high; balanced on the point of one toe she spun swiftly round on the smooth, soft, level green baize of the billiard-table, and the hem of her dress flew out flat and open like an umbrella, laying bare her white and tender thighs and the pale-pink Indian-silk shift drawn tight over her hips. Zhu Yinqiu, Sun Jiren, Wang Hefu and Chen Junyi, the four of them, sat perched high on the tall-legged bench where onlookers watched the play, clapping and roaring with laughter. The short fat Zhou Zhongwei held a billiard-cue in his hand and swung it to and fro like the leader of a band. Suddenly, like a swallow, Xu Manli sprang from the table she stood on to another billiard-table. A thunderclap of applause! But at that very instant Xu Manli seemed to slip; her waist twisted, her hips jerked out, and it looked very much as if she would fall — happily Lei Ming darted forward and caught her fast against his breast!

"No, no! That's not the way to cop your bit of grease, surely?"

Tang Yunshan came forward in his turn to interfere, and on the bald crown of his head he balanced Xu Manli's black satin high-heeled shoe.

Then there was an uproar. Men and women were tangled together in a heap, the laughter growing more abandoned, the shouting wilder. The four who had sat looking on joined in too.

Fan Bowen drew Wu Zhisheng a step aside and said coldly, with a frown:

"And what is so remarkable in that! Dragging me here for all you were worth to look at it! There's worse than this to be seen!"

"But — the ones who on ordinary days hold forth so loftily about 'the great barrier between the sexes' — are they not just these very 'pillars of society'?"

"Hmph! What a bookworm's notion that is! 'The great barrier between the sexes' must indeed be kept up — and yet the 'dance of death' must be danced as well! Don't you understand? This is their 'dance of death'! The more the villages go bankrupt, the more monstrous and headlong the deformed growth of the cities; the higher the price of gold climbs, the dearer the rice; the fiercer the gunfire of the civil war, the more widespread the risings of the peasants — why, then, the wilder and more frenzied grows the 'dance of death' of these rich men! What is there remarkable in it? Why look at it at all? — We'd do far better to go and find Lin Peishan and the others!"

So saying, Fan Bowen turned to go, but Wu Zhisheng caught hold of him again.

By now the billiard-room had changed its game. Someone was singing low in a forced, piercing falsetto. Wu Zhisheng drew Fan Bowen nearer to look, and there was Xu Manli still dancing as before on the billiard-table, but dancing slowly now. Her pair of high-heeled shoes were perched at present on the head of the short fat Zhou Zhongwei; the match-factory owner, legs bent, squatted and rose, squatted and rose, in mimicry of a hopping toad. His mouth went "tsk — tsk —," but he was not singing anything. The one singing, in a forced, piercing falsetto and with the utmost gravity, was Staff Officer Lei. He held his chest straight and his head a little back; to all appearance, even when he sang a military march he could not have worn a more earnest or more solemn air than now.

Wu Zhisheng glanced back at Fan Bowen, then with a sudden bound sprang to the door of the billiard-room, flung aside the soft printed bamboo blind with one hand, dashed in through the doorway, and shouted out, taking them all by surprise:

"Bravo! A fresh new thrill — the dance of death!"

On the instant the singing, the dancing pose, and the toad-hopping all stopped, and this dissolute, revelling crowd froze where they were. But in that very instant the mingled sound of suona, flute and great horn came flying in from outside like a sudden burst of spring thunder! It was the funeral dirge! The hour of old Mr. Wu's coffining had come at last. Zhu Yinqiu was the first to leap up, and as he went he shouted:

"The time has come! Let's go!"

At this reminder they all picked up their feet and ran. Zhou Zhongwei, forgetting that the pair of high-heeled shoes was still perched on his head, ran out too. Xu Manli, barefoot on the billiard-table, hopped and cried out in her agitation. Staff Officer Lei seized the chance to catch Xu Manli up in his arms and came chasing out too, and only when he reached the side of the hothouse did he stoop to pick up that pair of dainty, exquisite black satin high-heeled shoes from the ground.

By the time this company reached the outside of the "mourning hall," the five stone steps were already packed with people. The many white paper lanterns hung among the tree-shadows all over the garden had all been lit by now. The sky was overcast and murky as at the hour of dusk, and the white paper lanterns glimmered with a wan yellow light in the depths of the heavy green. The great horn kept up without pause its uncanny "woo — doo, doo, doo," a sound to make one's flesh creep to hear it. One of the attendants, holding a great bundle of glowing joss-sticks in his hand, saw Zhu Yinqiu and his party of gentlemen come crowding up, and handed each of them a stick.

Fan Bowen took the incense and at once flung it down again on the ground; seeing a gap in the press of people, he squeezed his way in. Wu Zhisheng followed after him, but used the incense in his hand to open a path for himself.

Tang Yunshan craned his neck and looked a while, then turned and gave Sun Jiren a wink:

"What are we standing here for?"

"Shall we go back to the old place?"

"Better go to the dining-room; we've only to take the asphalt path round the back."

said Zhou Zhongwei, squeezed in beside Sun Jiren. At the same time his eyes sought the agreement of Wang Hefu and Chen Junyi.

"Have you noticed? We're short of some people: Staff Officer Lei and the socialite!"

said Zhu Yinqiu, fixing his eyes on them. But all at once a louder surge of funeral music swallowed up his words, and Chen Junyi had already taken his arm and drawn him along behind Zhou Zhongwei's party, cutting round by the corridor in front of the great dining-room. Just as they passed the trellis of banksia rose, they saw Lei Ming and Xu Manli come out from among the tree-shadows and hurry off toward the front of the "mourning hall."

Sure enough there was not a soul in the dining-room. But the door that led to the "mourning hall," in the front half of the dining-room, was shut. Zhou Zhongwei ran over and pulled it open, and full in his face burst the mingled sound of great horn, trumpet, suona and flute, and with it the weeping and the shouting. And there, right in the doorway, stood the coffin and the other things needed for the encoffining.

Zhou Zhongwei hastily pushed the door to, turned back and said, shaking his head:

"Better sit here after all. With a wall between us it comes to the same thing!"

As he spoke he gathered up the joss-sticks from everyone's hands and stuck the whole lot of them into a Fujian bodiless-lacquer vermilion vase that stood on the table as a sample, then buried his fat body in a sofa. For a good while no one said anything.

Zhu Yinqiu sat opposite Zhou Zhongwei, his eyes shut, drawing furiously on a Craven "A" and wearing the look of a man deep in calculation; all at once he opened his eyes and said, looking at Chen Junyi beside him:

"Not being able to get your accounts in around the festival is the effect of the war — it's the same for everyone; but surely the native bank you deal with could stretch a point for you?"

"I've talked it over with them a good many times, but they always put me off — money's tight, they say, and this and that — I'm downright annoyed by it. — By and by I mean to have a word with Zhuzhai; perhaps he'll be willing to help."

Chen Junyi sighed as he answered; it was as though the half-dead, grotesque face of that native-bank manager who would not stretch a point were close before his eyes, and at the same time, beside it, the affable, goat-like face of Du Zhuzhai swam into view; and to Chen Junyi this seemed a gleam of hope. But to his surprise Zhu Yinqiu only shook his head coldly and said this one vague but disheartening thing:

"Zhuzhai? — Ai!"

"What! You don't think it will come off? My figure isn't a large one — a hundred and twenty or thirty thousand would see me through."

Chen Junyi asked hurriedly, his eyes fastened on Zhu Yinqiu's face. Before he had got any answer from Zhu Yinqiu, Zhou Zhongwei suddenly cut in from across the way:

"A hundred and twenty or thirty thousand, and you still call the figure small! I ask only for fifty or sixty thousand, and even that's not to be had. The moment the financial world sees the likes of us mill-owners come to the door, their brows knit tight. And no wonder, either. They put their capital to work on the bond market at the exchange, and to clear a hundred thousand or eighty thousand in a single day is the merest commonplace to them —"

"Right, right! What Zhou Zhongwei says is fair enough, when all is said. That is why I am forever saying this comes of politics not being on the right track. Suppose politics were on the right track, and bonds were floated all to promote industry — then the financial world and the industrial world would be closely bound together, and there would be none of this present state in which the two have nothing to do with each other and reckon only on the interest. But to get politics onto the right track is not a thing the soldiers can bring about. The men who run industry — the industrial capitalists — ought to put out their strength and force politics onto the right track."

Tang Yunshan seized the chance at once to speak up for the political faction he served. He had always been most attentive to the mill-owners great and small of the industrial world, and most assiduous in cultivating them; and even though his grand disquisitions had long since grown familiar to every ear, the moment a chance offered he must still hold forth. He would often add this conclusion as well: our Mr. Wang is precisely the man who strives with all his might for the realization of democratic government and who genuinely wishes to develop China's industry; it is not that China has no money to run industry, but that, more's the pity, all the money is spent on military and administrative outlays. It was on this very point, too, that Tang Yunshan and Wu Sunfu had lately become the closest of friends.

But their talk was forced to break off for a time. From the "mourning hall" next door came a still more deafening noise of funeral music and wailing, and mingled with it the sound of some heavy wooden thing knocking and bumping.

The din went on without stopping, but once they had grown gradually used to it, the men in the dining-room picked up again the broken thread of their talk.

Zhu Yinqiu, whose whole mind was taken up with the anxious question of how to get through the Dragon-Boat Festival, did not exactly disbelieve that there was much reason in Tang Yunshan's arguments, yet he could not help feeling that they stood a little too far off from his own immediate interests. His problem was a simple one: how to put off the mortgage-loans that were falling due, and how to contrive at once not to have to sell his silk "at a loss" at a cheap price and yet to keep his filature running. In short, his problem was how to lay hold of a lump of ready cash. He was really not in debt at all; though a mortgage-loan of a hundred-odd thousand lay heavy on his back, had he not two hundred bales of coarse and fine filature silk in stock at that moment, and a great quantity of dried cocoons besides? The financial world ought to have felt easy about his loan. Yet in fact the financial world dunned him as hard as if he had been a penniless pauper.

Thinking thus, Zhu Yinqiu could not but feel resentful; it seemed to him that the financial world was deliberately setting itself against him, and that Tang Yunshan's arguments stood further than ever from his own immediate interests. He gave a soft snort down his nose and said coldly:

"Yunshan, say what you will, I hold that the men who deal in standard gold and government bonds have hearts of another sort! It's by no means certain that, if the government floated bonds to promote industry, they'd rush to subscribe. The chief part of a bank's business is lending; and to subscribe to bonds is one kind of lending too. But when it comes to lending to us — is there no security, no interest in it? Of course there is! Yet they will none of them lend; is that not deliberate —"

"Ha, ha, ha, ha —"

Zhu Yinqiu's grumbling was broken in upon by a burst of laughter from Zhou Zhongwei. The short fat man sprang up and flung his two arms wide, and standing between Tang Yunshan and Zhu Yinqiu as though to part two brawlers, said in a loud voice:

"Don't wrangle, the pair of you. Men of business all want to make money, and to make it with a will! Yinqiu has his hardships, and the bankers have their difficulties too —"

"Just so! The greater part of their reserves has been turned into bonds, so that when a falling wind gets up in the bonds, their very foundations are shaken, and naturally they must scrape together ready cash with all their might — and the loan advanced to Yinqiu, say, is one they're then in a hurry to call in. That's why I say it all comes of politics not being on the right track."

Tang Yunshan hastened to break in and defend his thesis once more. Just then Zhou Zhongwei went on to say:

"Just now Mr. Sun Jiren had an idea — a very sound one, a very sound one! It's no idle joke!"

This last sentence Zhou Zhongwei almost bawled out with a reddening face, and it actually drew everyone's attention. The eyes of Tang Yunshan and Zhu Yinqiu both turned toward Sun Jiren. Chen Junyi, more anxious still, asked:

"Do tell us, Jiren! What's the plan?"

But Sun Jiren only smiled, drawing slowly on his cigar, and would not speak at once. Wang Hefu beside him, however, could contain himself no longer; he glanced at Sun Jiren as though seeking his consent, then coughed once and, in an offhand, casual way, brought out Sun Jiren's "good idea":

"Jiren and I have talked this over a good many times. It's a very ordinary notion, really — the idea is to join with our fellow men of the industrial world and set up a bank of our own, a financial clearing-house for our own people. Just now all the ready silver of the interior has come flocking to Shanghai; raising the shares would be easy enough, and taking in deposits is even less of a problem. With a million in capital, and then a million or two of deposits taken in on top of that, we could most likely make something of a going concern of it. And if we could further get leave to issue banknotes, it would be easier still. — But it's only such a notion as this; we happened to speak of it, that is all, and have not set our hands to it. Now that Zhou Zhongwei has blurted it straight out, let us all talk it over."

Wang Hefu's voice was by nature extremely loud, but this time, of all times, he pitched it low, and the clamour from the "mourning hall" next door was really too fierce, so that everyone had to prick up their ears to hear, and only then did they take it in. Truly it was "an ordinary notion"! For men of industry to join with their trade-fellows and start a bank — of that there had long been no lack of precedents; only Sun Jiren's proposal was to unite the various trades and not a single one. The several industrialists here present were not of one trade, and the interests of each of them were not in every respect the same. As they listened quietly to Wang Hefu unfolding his case at leisure, the nimble thoughts of each industrialist turned at once to this point; and the private reckonings each made on his own behalf came crowding up, many upon many. When Wang Hefu had finished, they were all struck dumb, and there was a dead silence for a good long while.

In the end it was Tang Yunshan, who was no industrialist, that spoke first:

"The plan can't go wrong. What's needed is to draw in men of influence from every quarter and organize it on a large scale. I have a proposal: let us presently invite Wu Sunfu to come and discuss it. In an affair like this, to be without him won't do. Well, gentlemen, don't you all think I'm right?"

"Right, right! Jiren and I had that very thought from the first."

put in Wang Hefu, his voice loud again now as it usually was.

So they all fell to airing their views, and came little by little to the concrete arrangements. Chen Junyi and Zhou Zhongwei, whose own resources were none too ample, reckoned that Sun Jiren — a shipping merchant — and Wang Hefu — a mine-owner — must be well able to "turn money over"; while Sun and Wang, for their part, took it that Zhou Zhongwei and Chen Junyi, who had made their start as compradors for foreign firms, must command a very considerable scope in Shanghai. But in every mind the attention still fixed on Wu Sunfu. This Third Master Wu's financial power, his adroitness, his boldness, they had all long admired. Only Zhu Yinqiu, though he too talked away with a will, had at the same time some doubt whether Wu Sunfu would consent to take part. He knew that Wu Sunfu had suffered no squeeze from the financial world, and that at this moment, when the men of the silk trade were all crying out their misery to high heaven, Wu Sunfu's own case was the best of any: four or five months before, when filature silk had not yet plunged, had not Wu Sunfu unloaded a thousand bales of the foreign-trade lot? So that now, when the silk men were all thinking of shutting down for a time, Wu Sunfu was pressing on with the work to make his deliveries. Wu Sunfu had one difficulty, nevertheless, and that was a want of dried cocoons. As for new cocoons, the silkworm season just now was a poor one, and the cocoon price had opened high. He could of course fall back on Japanese dried cocoons; but ever since the eastward exchange had soared, Japanese dried cocoons, duty-free though their import was, worked out on reckoning to be none too cheap either. — These several calculations passed one after another through Zhu Yinqiu's mind, and drew him gradually into a deep abstraction, till at last he sat to one side and spoke no more.

All at once a new idea came bubbling up in his thoughts. He turned to look at Tang Yunshan, and it happened that Tang Yunshan was looking at him just then too.

"Yunshan, starting a bank is our own way of saving ourselves; but industry bears on the livelihood of the nation and the people — is the government, then, to stand by with folded arms? Just now you said that the bonds the government floats ought all to be used to promote industry — that, to be sure, is out of the question at present; and yet, to relieve some one particular industry, to float a special bond issue — that, one would think, ought surely to be done?"

Such were the words Zhu Yinqiu addressed to Tang Yunshan. They went roundabout; to Zhu Yinqiu himself, who had been turning the matter over for a good while, they of course seemed clear enough. But Tang Yunshan was struck dumb for the moment. Before he could answer, the door leading to the "mourning hall" over there suddenly opened, and the first to come in was Dr. Ding. Rubbing his hands as was his habit, Dr. Ding let out a soft breath and said:

"It's done. The International Funeral Parlour's handiwork isn't half bad! With the colour laid on, old Mr. Wu lies in the coffin just as though he were asleep, his face all rosy and ruddy! — What? Half past three already!"

At this two attendants brought in trays of refreshments. Soda-water, ice-cream, iced almond-cream, eight-treasure sweet soup, cream mille-feuille, and Western pastries of every kind covered a whole table. For a while this food stopped up the people's talk.

Dr. Ding looked the refreshments over carefully, shook his head, and ate not a morsel. His fastidiousness about hygiene was well known. Tang Yunshan was just about to make sport of him when suddenly a maidservant put her head in at the rear door of the dining-room and said, "Will Dr. Ding please come." It turned out that the Wu young mistress was a little unwell. When Dr. Ding had hurried off, in at the front door came Wu Sunfu, who had come expressly to thank them all. Tang Yunshan set down the refreshment in his hand at once, stood up and cried:

"You come at the very nick of time! We've a great scheme to talk over with you! It's a proposal of Mr. Sun Jiren's here, and Mr. Wang Hefu's."

Sun and Wang, the two of them, smiled modestly, and gave a rough outline of the matter they had just been discussing, the wish to start a bank; Wang Hefu stuck out the thumb of his right hand, pointed it aslant at Tang Yunshan, laughed aloud, and added a few words more:

"We were only blowing hot air, and never thought Tang Yunshan would drag Third Master into it straight off. You've had a wearing day of it; let's talk again another day."

"This very day! Once he sets to work, Sunfu knows nothing of weariness!"

Tang Yunshan objected. With an air of greater eagerness than anyone, he ushered them all into the back half of the dining-room while he gave rein to his thesis that "the industrialists must unite, and so bring politics onto the right track"; he held that to join in founding a bank was the first step toward a grand union of the industrialists.

Wu Sunfu gave no opinion at first, but let Tang Yunshan run on with his grandiloquence. The resources and abilities of these several industrialists here present Sunfu saw at a glance; on these few men alone no great thing could be brought off. But of himself Sunfu had never been willing to "think meanly"; with himself thrown in, the case would of course be altered — he had the knack of working middling stuff up into a team of the very best. Only he did not know whether these men before him would with one accord raise him up as their leader. Turning this over in his mind, Wu Sunfu set his keen eyes to work observing the look on each man's face. Zhu Yinqiu alone appeared cooler than the rest and said little. So, in a slight lull in the general talk, Wu Sunfu said to Zhu Yinqiu:

"Yinqiu, what do you think? Judging by the state of our silk trade at present, pressed as we are from several sides, I should very much like to see such a financial body set up to ease the strain on the world of enterprise."

"Ah, Sunfu, what a thing to say! We are all old acquaintances here and know each other's circumstances through and through; at present it's only you whose resources are ample, and we must all rely wholly on your great strength to help us."

There was, indeed, real feeling and plain sense in what Zhu Yinqiu said. And so Chen Junyi and Zhou Zhongwei were the first to clap in approval. But Wu Sunfu could not keep from knitting his brows a little. He now saw clearly that these three, Zhu Yinqiu and the others, had no zeal for founding a bank themselves, but only hoped that others might found one and then lend to them on indulgent terms, breaking the rule for their sake. He was just about to answer when Sun Jiren over there brought out several splendid remarks:

"Let none of you be too polite. My own idea from the first was to organize a bank for the express purpose of running several kinds of enterprise. When other men start a bank, it is nothing but to take in deposits and go in for speculation — land, gold, government bonds — and at the most to make loans to the business world. If this bank of ours is set going, we must certainly put the greater part of the capital into running several enterprises of the highest promise — long-distance motor-buses in Jiangbei, say, or the mines in Henan province. As for easing the strain on enterprises now run aground, that would be no more than one part of the business. — Only, for me alone, the will is there but the strength falls short!"

None had looked for Sun Jiren to be keeping such a grand disquisition in reserve, brought out only now; Chen Junyi and Zhou Zhongwei looked at each other in blank surprise, feeling that at bottom there was no great advantage in the thing for them, and half their eagerness cooled. But Zhu Yinqiu sat there smiling; hearing Sun Jiren bring up his long-distance motor-buses and his mines, he flatly concluded that Sun Jiren's bank was a matter of "bath-house-ism," of dipping in for a quick soak at others' expense; he was the very man to measure another's heart by his own.

Only in Wu Sunfu's eyes there flashed a light of excitement. His acquaintance with Sun Jiren was still new, and one would never have guessed that this little head on its long thin neck harboured so far-sighted and large a spirit. Wu Sunfu felt he had met a "comrade." Sunfu's ambition was great. He was rich, besides, in the spirit of adventure and the nerve to drive a thing hard; he liked to work with men of his own stamp, and when he saw some sound enterprise left in the hands of mediocrities without vision, without method, without daring, and reduced to a half-dead state, he hated it beyond all telling. Such half-dead so-called men of enterprise Sunfu often meant to strike down without the least pity, and take their enterprises into his own iron grip.

For the moment Wu Sunfu's keen eyes were fixed on Sun Jiren's face, and he nodded quietly; but he wanted still to know how large Wang Hefu's spirit might be, and turning his face to Wang Hefu on his left, he asked, of set purpose:

"And Hefu's own opinion?"

"Much the same, on the whole. But however our aim may lie, at the outset our methods must be a little more supple. To get people to invest in a bank that runs new enterprises and nothing else — the present state of things won't do for that, I'm afraid; at the start we shall most likely have to go by the ways of an ordinary bank."

said Wang Hefu, still smiling broadly. His perpetual broad smile, with its half-jesting air, was the very opposite of Sun Jiren's taciturnity. He had the humorous temper of a northerner, and a northerner's temper too for setting his heart doggedly on a thing and doing it.

Wu Sunfu broke into a laugh; he struck two fingers smartly on the arm of his chair and said with decision:

"Very well! With you two for a vanguard, I'll come in behind you and set to!"

"Third Master will have his joke again. Jiren and I are wholly at your command."

"Ha! You three are of one mind and one purpose, matched in talent and in strength! This three-cornered love-affair is bound to come off!"

put in Tang Yunshan, slapping his thigh and laughing aloud. But he checked his laughter at once and offered a suggestion:

"As I see it, why should you three not first get up a banking syndicate —"

So saying, he turned again to Zhu Yinqiu and the others — as though afraid of leaving the three of them out in the cold:

"Ah — Yinqiu, Junyi, Zhongwei, am I right in this? Everyone present today shall be a founding promoter."

The three who had been listening quietly had all supposed that a scheme so grandiose and impractical as Sun Jiren's could hardly win Wu Sunfu's approval; now that they heard the very opposite come of it, and Tang Yunshan pressing them so eagerly with his question besides, they were for the moment left with nothing to say. Not to speak of their really having no strength to spare just now, even if they could have turned their money over freely, they would still have hung back and watched from the fence at so over-ambitious a venture.

Things grew a trifle stiff. Just then the attendant Gaosheng came in to summon Wu Sunfu:

"Master Du begs to see you. He's in the small reception-room across the way."

Wu Sunfu seemed to guess what the matter was; he rose, said "Excuse me a while," and went off at once. But no sooner had he hurried out of the dining-room door than Zhu Yinqiu came after him from behind, and his very first words were:

"The mortgage-loan of Du Zhuzhai's that's falling due — I must ask you to mediate between us, Sunfu."

Wu Sunfu's eyes shifted; before he could answer, Zhu Yinqiu had already added:

"A three-month extension would do — even that would serve!"

"Didn't I speak to Zhuzhai only the day before yesterday? We're all the closest of friends, and when a thing can be stretched, stretched it should be. Only, by what he tells me, it seems there's difficulty there too. Money's tight, and he's afraid of the risk; every mortgage-loan that falls due he means to call in — and not yours alone, Yinqiu —"

"Then there's only one road left to me: to declare bankruptcy!"

Zhu Yinqiu said this with an air uncommonly grave, so that he all but made Wu Sunfu believe him; but after one sharp glance at Zhu Yinqiu, Wu Sunfu still judged it to be a diplomatic manoeuvre of Zhu Yinqiu's, though he did not expose him for it, and only said blandly:

"It won't come to that! Your assets exceed your debts — how can there be any talk of bankruptcy!"

"Then there's a second road: I'll shut the mill down for three months!"

This remark, however, all but made Wu Sunfu change countenance. He knew that the state of the silk filatures just now was like one great powder-magazine: let a single spark burst out at any one point and it would spread at once and grow into a general sympathetic strike; and he himself, at this very time, was hurrying on the reeling of the futures he had already sold short, and was most unwilling that any such thing as a strike should come about. All this, of course, Zhu Yinqiu knew perfectly well, and so this "shutting down for three months" of his was a kind of threat. Wu Sunfu pondered a moment, then shifted his tone:

"I'll do my utmost to speak for you. Whether Zhuzhai will grant the extension in the end — let's talk of it again by and by."

Not letting Zhu Yinqiu pester him any further, Wu Sunfu hurried off, a thin, savage smile breaking out on his face.

Du Zhuzhai was waiting in the small reception-room and growing impatient. He had taken a great pinch of snuff and sneezed twice, and half unconsciously went to the door and opened it to look, and it chanced that he saw Wu Sunfu come away from Zhu Yinqiu as though making his escape. That look of mingled resentment and vexation on Wu Sunfu's face gave Zhuzhai quite a start; he thought something must have gone wrong at Sunfu's mill, or else that another telegram had come from home. He came forward and asked hurriedly:

"What's the matter? — One wave hardly stilled, and another rising?"

Wu Sunfu still gave his savage smile and did not answer. He shut the door and dropped, as though utterly weary, into a sofa, and only then said:

"It's downright a soldier's life! Under your very feet the ground is all land-mines, ready to go off at any moment and blow you to bits!"

Du Zhuzhai's face changed at once. He supposed his own foreboding had unhappily hit the mark. But Wu Sunfu suddenly shifted his manner, gave a faint cold laugh, and added, as though nothing in the world troubled him:

"That fellow Zhu Yinqiu — he means to try his tricks too! Heh!"

"So it's Zhu Yinqiu, is it!"

Du Zhuzhai felt a weight lift from his heart, and straightway gave a great sneeze.

"Just so! You saw it just now. He asks that the mortgage-loan on your side be extended another three months — and at the very least three months, it seems! Leave that aside; but he actually means to play his tricks — this 'declaring bankruptcy,' this 'shutting down' — it's simply to browbeat me. He takes everyone else for a fool that he can shove about as he pleases!"

"Oh — and how did you answer him?"

"I said we'd talk again by and by. — But, Zhuzhai, will you grant him the extension?"

"If he's set against clearing it off, there's no help for it. Besides, it's a matter of only eighty thousand dollars, and he has security too — a hundred and fifty bales of middling dried silk."

Before Du Zhuzhai had finished speaking, Wu Sunfu had already sprung up; he paced a few steps like a lion about to seize its prey, then went back to the sofa, buried himself deeper in it, and said coldly, shaking his head:

"What need is there? Zhuzhai, you're no philanthropist; and besides, it's not worth doing Zhu Yinqiu a good turn. — Do you really believe he's genuinely unable to shift his funds about? Nothing of the sort! He's simply too hard-hearted, and too stupid besides; I hate above all things this kind of man who's both stupid and hard-hearted! Earlier, when the price of grade-B silk still stood at nine hundred taels — and by the reckoning he could already have got his capital back — he wouldn't sell out; that's being too hard-hearted! Then when it fell to eight hundred and fifty or sixty taels, he fondly imagined it might yet rally, and instead went and took in fifty bales of Sichuan silk; and that's his being too stupid — and the stupidity comes of the hard-heartedness too! What is a man like that fit to run any enterprise for! He can't manage a mill, either. The output of his mill is of the worst; in his silk-waste there's more women's hair than anything; he's ruined the good name of the whole silk trade! A fine set of new Italian machinery in his hands — it's a downright shame! —"

"By your account, then, what's to be done?"

Du Zhuzhai, a complete outsider to the managing of a silk filature, listened with growing impatience and cut short Wu Sunfu's disquisition.

"What's to be done? You lend him another seventy thousand, to make it a hundred and fifty thousand in all!"

"What! Lend him seventy thousand more?"

Du Zhuzhai's astonishment at this was no small one; his body gave a jump, and the great heap of snuff on the middle finger of his right hand scattered all down the front of his gown; but Wu Sunfu answered with a smile:

"Just so, I said seventy thousand! But it's not that the eighty thousand is extended and seventy thousand added on top. The eighty thousand that falls due is to be settled all the same; and a fresh mortgage-loan of a hundred and fifty thousand is to be made besides, with the principal and interest of that eighty thousand deducted from it —"

"I simply don't see why you must go so roundabout about it. Zhu Yinqiu only hopes to have the eighty thousand extended!"

"Listen! There's reason in it. — The fresh mortgage-loan of a hundred and fifty thousand runs only one month. As for security, filature silk, dry-reeled silk, ash silk — none of them will do; dried cocoons alone are to be taken as the pledge; and it's to be laid down as well that if it's not settled when due, the creditor may dispose of the pledge as he pleases. — And another thing: you're to figure as the go-between, and the fresh loan of a hundred and fifty thousand comes from another house — some banking syndicate, say — that you've introduced Zhu Yinqiu to."

When he had finished, Wu Sunfu fixed his keen gaze without a blink on Du Zhuzhai's goat-like face. He knew that this elder brother-in-law of his was greedy for gain and full of suspicion by temper, and could never bring himself to agree to anything out of hand. He could only wait quietly for Zhuzhai to finish his reckoning before saying more. At the same time he could not keep from fancying how, a month hence, Zhu Yinqiu's dried cocoons would pass into his own hands; and — though this was perhaps to look a little too far ahead — how, three or four months hence, even that set of new Italian machinery might, who could say, be transferred into his own experienced and rigorous management.

But just then a door at the back of the small reception-room opened, and in came the Wu young mistress, the colour of her face none too good. She went quietly to a chair opposite Wu Sunfu and sat down, as though she had something to say. Wu Sunfu remembered too that a while ago the young mistress had had a pain at the heart and had been sick, and had sent for Dr. Ding. He was just about to inquire when Du Zhuzhai stood up, gave a sneeze, and then said:

"Let it be done as you say. — And yet, Sunfu, to take dried cocoons alone as security is none too safe; suppose Zhu Yinqiu's dried cocoons aren't worth a hundred and fifty thousand as a pledge?"

Wu Sunfu could not help bursting into loud laughter:

"Zhuzhai, you're afraid they won't be worth a hundred and fifty thousand, and I'm afraid Zhu Yinqiu will grudge to hand them over as a pledge at all! It runs only a month, and unless by then he can turn iron into gold, the dried cocoons will bear the Zhu name no longer — and there again is Zhu Yinqiu's great folly! A middling mill like his — what does he mean by hoarding up close on two hundred thousand silver dollars' worth of dried cocoons? Last year, with that buying-up of his, he ran the price of cocoons right up and we suffered no end at his hands. So now there's nothing for it but to squeeze his cocoons out of him!"

"What a venomous creature you are!"

the Wu young mistress suddenly put in, a gorgeous smile like the flush of dawn spreading over her gloomy, sickly face.

Du Zhuzhai and Sunfu looked at each other and laughed aloud with one voice.

"That business is settled, then — when I sent for you just now there was another matter besides … oh! It's that Zhao Botao telephoned: the first step on that side is done, and as for the second, they say there's been a change in the market and we must talk over some safer way of going about it. He's in number two at the Cathay, waiting for us to come —"

"Then let's go at once! And there's the matter of the banking syndicate too — we'll talk of that in the car."

Wu Sunfu said crisply, and hurried out of the small reception-room with Du Zhuzhai; a minute later the sound of the motor-car's engine came from beyond the window.

Here, the Wu young mistress sat alone, letting the drifting fancies that rose and fell by turns close in about her for a while. First it was that sound of the motor-car that drew her far, far away — to the old days seven or eight years before, when she was still at a mission girls' school, the days of "Miss Lin Peiyao," and the first time she and her schoolfellows went out for a drive in a motor-car. In those days that band of them, sixteen and seventeen years old, tasted the "freedom" newly won after the "May Fourth," and never once thought that the brimming cup of youth's sweet wine before them must one day be drained dry too; in those days that band of them, who had read Shakespeare's The Tempest and Scott's Ivanhoe, had their heads full of images of gallant, valiant knights and princes, and of sea-isles, ancient castles, great forests, a lone tower under a slanting moon — such "poetic" scenes; and their school buildings, close by a great park off in the western outskirts of Shanghai, seemed themselves very like such scenes. What lovely visions of the future they cherished — she above all, the "Miss Lin Peiyao" of those days, who, having inherited her father's cast of a man of letters, had built up who knows how many castles in the air, and on how many nights of pale moon and clear breeze had opened her lovely eyes at midnight to savour the sweet dreams of her own imagining.

But such a "midsummer night's dream" is, in the nature of things, a brief one. The deaths of her father and mother, one hard upon the other of sudden illness, pressed the true taste of "reality" into the maiden heart of "Miss Lin Peiyao." Yet just at that time another kind of "tempest," valiant, ardent and tragic — the "May Thirtieth Movement" that shook the whole world — drew the attention of "Miss Lin Peiyao," who had newly lost her world. A young man who seemed to her something near to a knight of the medieval sort appeared all at once on the path of her life. How half-startled and half-glad she was! And when this "comet" of a young man suddenly vanished again, how it set her longing without end!

And after that came —

Reaching this point in her thoughts, the Wu young mistress gave a sudden start through her whole body and lifted her head, glancing to left and right as though frightened. Everything in the small reception-room was sumptuous, made to please the fancy of any fashionable woman: the great oil-painting on the wall, the curios on the shelves, the fresh flowers in their vases, the costly furniture, and — the parrot in its cage. Yet the Wu young mistress felt always as though something were wanting. Ever since she had become the mistress of this house, that feeling of "something wanting," come and go though it might, had been forever there in her heart.

The yearnings that her student days had drawn from English classical literature and brewed within her had not, in all these years, been washed from the membranes of her brain. In all these years, though she had come to know no little of the "true taste of reality," it had not yet been enough to make her see that her burly, resolute, purple-faced and pimpled husband was the very knight-hero and "prince" of the twentieth-century age of machine industry! Such men do not, like the knights and princes of the Middle Ages, fence and ride; they work the abacus and ride in motor-cars. But the Wu young mistress could not bring herself to see this, and there were even times when she forgot that she herself was a far different thing from a fair lady of the medieval age!

"A guest!"

Suddenly the parrot in its cage screeched out its garbled, tuneless words and startled the Wu young mistress from her reverie. In the doorway on the front right of the small reception-room stood a young man in military uniform, his waist held bolt upright, a smile half-playing on his handsome face with its touch of martial bearing, his eyes bright and keen: it was Staff Officer Lei!

The Wu young mistress gave a violent start. For an instant "reality" and "the dream-world" flowed together into one within her consciousness, and she could scarcely believe her own eyes. But now Staff Officer Lei, having made his bow, came nearer, and the sound of his trained footsteps struck upon the young mistress's ear and she came fully to herself. At the same time the habit of "duty" and courtesy pressed her the more tightly, and instinctively summoning up a smile, she rose to greet him:

"Staff Officer Lei! Please sit down. — You've come for Sunfu, I suppose; he went out just now."

"I saw him go out, Madam Wu. He asked me to stay and take supper at your house before I leave."

Staff Officer Lei answered in a soft and respectful voice, but did not take a seat; he stood before the Wu young mistress, some two feet away, his bright keen eyes fixed fast on her face, which still wore a trace of bewilderment.

The Wu young mistress instinctively smiled, and instinctively drew back a step, sinking into the sofa where she had sat before.

For a while neither of them spoke. A rather stiff silence.

Staff Officer Lei drew his eyes back from the young mistress's face, fixed them on the floor, and gave a slight shudder through his body. Suddenly he thrust his right hand into his pocket, stepped forward, and, his head still bent a little, said these few words quickly:

"Madam Wu! Tomorrow by the early train I leave Shanghai for the front; and this time, to all appearance, the odds are that I shall die in battle! This is the last time I shall see you, the last time I shall speak with you; Madam Wu! Here I have something to give you!"

As he spoke this last sentence, Staff Officer Lei lifted his head and drew his right hand from his pocket; in it was a book, and swiftly opening the book, he held it up in both hands and offered it before the Wu young mistress.

It was a worn old copy of The Sorrows of Young Werther! And on the opened page of the book lay a withered white rose!

The old memories of the student-union days in the early period of the tempestuous "May Thirtieth Movement" came flying all at once like a flash of lightning, from this book, from this white rose, and struck the Wu young mistress and set her whole body trembling. With one hand she snatched the book, and looking in alarm at Staff Officer Lei, could not utter half a word.

Staff Officer Lei gave a bitter smile, seemed to sigh, and then went on:

"Madam Wu! You may take this as a gift, if you will, or as something I ask you to keep for me, if you will. I have no parents above me, no brothers or sisters below. I have, besides, scarcely a single intimate friend. The one thing I have loved in all my life is this withered white rose and this book! Before I go up to the front, I greatly wish to entrust this most precious of things to the most trustworthy and fitting person —"

All at once the Wu young mistress gave a short, sharp cry, and a flush rose over her face.

Staff Officer Lei paused too, but at once went on, more hurried and more firm than before:

"Madam Wu! I have chosen you! I think you consent as well! The history of this flower and this book is never for one moment absent from my heart! Five years ago, on just such an unusual dusk as this, just such a close and sultry dusk, I received this flower from the hand of the most stately, most noble, most beautiful of women — it was the reward of my worship of her; and this book, The Sorrows of Young Werther, was witness to her and to me — Madam Wu, perhaps you would not object to calling that love! But I, a poor student, dared not be so bold; Madam Wu, you can doubtless guess that any further step was, in fact, not to be permitted then. In those days, in those days — Madam Wu, now you must surely understand why in those days I vanished all at once from before the goddess I worshipped: I went to Guangdong, and entered Whampoa! I fought my way from Guangdong to Hunan; I rose from company commander to regimental commander; I took Changsha, I took Wuhan, I took Zhengzhou, and I took Beiping; I have crawled through heaps of the dead by the thousand and the myriad! Through danger to my life again and again, I cast everything away — only this flower, this book, I never let leave me! And yet what did I see when I escaped alive out of death? Madam Wu, I searched in Shanghai more than half a year before I learned how ill my luck had been! Now my hope is gone, and my courage too, and going up to the front this time I shall almost certainly die! — And yet this book, this withered flower, Madam Wu, I cannot let them rot away on the battlefield too! I think I have now found the fittest person, to ask her to keep this shabby book, this ruined flower —"

By now Staff Officer Lei's voice too had begun to tremble a little, and a few beads of sweat came out at his temples. He drew a breath and sank down limply into the nearest chair. But the Wu young mistress's face had already turned an ashen grey; she gazed dully at Staff Officer Lei, without a sound and without a movement.

Staff Officer Lei smiled bitterly, then suddenly, as though wrestling with something inside him, threw out his chest, sprang up on the instant, came again before the Wu young mistress, and said slowly in a half-choked voice:

"Madam Wu! Now that I have had the chance to tell you this story, I can die with my eyes closed in peace!"

With that, Staff Officer Lei raised his hand in a military salute, turned, and made to go.

"Lei Ming! Lei Ming!"

The Wu young mistress sprang suddenly to her feet and called in a trembling voice.

Staff Officer Lei halted and turned round. But the Wu young mistress had nothing more to say. Her face had flushed red again now, her eyes were distraught, her breast rose and fell violently. Suddenly she flung her two arms wide. Staff Officer Lei sprang forward a step, and the Wu young mistress, as if drunk and dazed, threw herself against his breast, her face resting just at his shoulder. Staff Officer Lei bent down his head, and their two pairs of lips met as one.

"Brother! Oh, brother!"

The parrot in its cage suddenly gave an uncanny screech.

The two who stood clasped together both gave a jump. As though waking from a dream, the Wu young mistress thrust Staff Officer Lei violently away, and clutching that copy of The Sorrows of Young Werther, ran with flying steps out of the small reception-room, and with flying steps up the stairs to her own room, where she threw herself on the bed, and in an instant a rush of hot tears soaked through the spotless embroidered pillow-slip.
