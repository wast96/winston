## A Few Notes on the Shanghai Workers' Movement and the Gangs

{d} Zhu Xuefan

### I. The Gangs Were a Great Problem in Labor Work in Old Shanghai

When Li Lisan looked back on the labor work he took up in Shanghai in 1924, he once said that the biggest problem in working among Shanghai's workers was the problem of the gangs. The first time he organized, he paid the Green Gang no heed, and the capitalists used the Green Gang to break the workers' struggle at the Rihua Cotton Mill in Pudong. The second time, Li Qihan joined the Green Gang, but he trusted it too far, was sold out by the gang bosses, and failed again. Li Lisan then studied the history of the Shanghai labor movement and brought to bear against the Green Gang the methods he had used against the Hong Gang at Anyuan; and so, in the May Thirtieth Movement, the men who shielded him were Green Gang disciples. He came to Shanghai in the very year I passed into the Shanghai Post Office as a junior clerk. In those days the Green Gang had not yet gained a foothold inside the post office, and I knew nothing of it. Only afterward did I join, open a branch of my own, and use the gang to carry on work among the workers; and I came to understand fairly well the relation between the gangs and the labor movement, and to feel the truth of what Li Lisan had said. In my dealings, too, with the trade unions of other countries, I felt keenly that the gang problem besetting the Chinese labor movement, with all its intricacy, was something rarely met with in the labor movements of the world.

Across the whole country before liberation, Shanghai had the most workers in employment, the most out of work, and the most who had joined the gangs. After 1932, under the world economic crisis, many Shanghai factories closed; unemployment ran deep, and the great mass of working people struggled desperately to keep alive. The Shanghai Post Office once held an examination to hire mail carriers: the quota was only sixty-eight, yet more than 2,400 applied. In the winter of 1936, when it examined applicants for junior postal-service posts, three thousand sat the examination and only some fifty-odd were taken. People in those days called a customs post a "golden rice bowl," a bank post a "silver rice bowl," and a post-office post an "iron rice bowl," and the last drew no end of school-leavers, from primary to middle school and above, who had lost their schooling, their work, or their livelihood. Yet even inside the post office, keeping one's rice bowl whole was no easy thing, and the carriers in particular could be dismissed at any moment: a foreign firm had only to find fault with a carrier's delivery and write a letter to the foreign postmaster, and an "iron rice bowl" was smashed. That so many post-office staff joined the gangs, and that the gang factions were so many, had one great cause besides the factions' own jockeying for power and turf: the staff joined the gangs to find a backer who would safeguard their vital interests. The outdoor carriers, who delivered letters and hauled the mail sacks to the wharves, had their personal safety to think of as well, and needed all the more someone to stand behind them. Among the carriers, moreover, the ease or hardship of the work varied greatly, and some joined to have a voice put in for them, whether to be assigned lighter duties or, in money matters, to have their pay grade come up a little faster (the post office raised pay by fixed periods, and between a postal assistant, a postal cadet, and a postal clerk the pace of promotion could differ by half a year). And a few joined the gangs simply to skim a little extra on the side.

The staff of the British Tramways Company joined the gangs in some numbers too, and for the same chief reason: to hold on to a rice bowl and stave off unemployment. A tram post was not the equal of one in the customs or the post office, but when a worker landed a job with the British tram company his friends would congratulate him gladly, as on a rare piece of luck. The tram company, like the customs and the post office, had a retirement fund, a kind of old-age pension. The post office ruled that any employee of twenty-five years' service could, on retiring, draw a pension of twenty-five months' pay reckoned on his last month's wages, with half a month added for each year beyond twenty-five. The tram company's pension was smaller: a man of fifteen years' service who had reached fifty-five drew, on retiring, a twenty-fourth part, that is, half a month for each full year; at twenty years' service it was a full month for each year. But tram workers, like customs and post-office staff, found it no easy matter to lay hands on that pension, for once a man was dismissed he got nothing, and the tram company would sack a worker over the smallest thing. In the short five months of 1937, from the Battle of August Thirteenth to the year's end, more than a hundred were dismissed; and that winter the company sacked union delegates and union stalwarts without cause and, using the power of the patrol house, drove them out of the city. The company handed out punishments of every kind at will. A conductor was hauled into the office several times a month, sometimes a dozen, to be dressed down by a foreign clerk or fined, the fine running from a few dimes to a dollar or two. This kind of punishment went by the slang names "eating a big dinner" and "seeing the King of Hell." A conductor who wanted such punishment eased had, after the fact, to get someone to plead with a senior clerk or an inspector, and to send gifts. There was another punishment called "docking a man's shift": ordering him to stop work and stopping his pay. Let a conductor be thought to have pocketed fares, passed a fare, or rung up a wrong fare, or let a foreign passenger write in to complain, and, right or wrong, he was docked several shifts, given no pay, and made to report to the company every day and wait, three or four days or a dozen, until his shift was restored. Too many dockings in a year and he was dismissed. Drivers, mechanics, and inspectors alike stood in danger of every sort of punishment, even dismissal.

This lack of any security in one's calling, this being oppressed and squeezed at the whim of capitalists Chinese and foreign, was found across every trade and line of work; in some, a man's very safety was not assured, and he might be beaten and trampled by others. And so people had to seek out some force of solidarity and mutual aid to protect themselves, and joining a gang was one form it took.

Gangs have existed in our country from far back. Under the feudal order they were a primitive kind of secret popular association, bound together under patriarchal headship into organizations of many different names, some of them armed. People used such bodies to gain mutual help in the business of living, and at certain times to launch struggles against the officials and landlords who oppressed them. By modern times, the members of these bodies had spread from mainly ruined peasants, unemployed handicraftsmen, and vagrant proletarians to take in modern industrial workers, intellectuals, and even small and middling national capitalists; and their social standing, too, shifted step by step from a secret existence toward the open and the lawful. The change in their makeup and their standing poured new vigor into them and made them a peculiar force in society.

The number of Shanghai's working people who had joined the gangs was, by one estimate, about twenty percent of all the staff in the post office, and the proportion for the city's workers as a whole may have been larger still. Add to those in the gangs the members of the brotherhoods, sisterhoods, and Guandi societies that workers in every trade and workplace formed of their own accord, and the members of the native-place factions such as the Cantonese, Ningbo, Shandong, Hubei, and Subei gangs, and the proportion of the city's total workforce grows larger yet. In these organizations great and small, of ever so many names, not a few people had ties to the gangs and looked to them as a backer. These brotherhoods, sisterhoods, and Guandi societies, and the native-place factions, were all bodies of mutual aid for the mass of working people in their social life and their economic struggles. The rise of this phenomenon was no accident, but was made by the many economic and political factors of our country's semi-feudal, semi-colonial history: the mass of working people, to survive and to protect their vital interests, needed to combine and to help one another. Of course, they could find no way out through brotherhoods, sisterhoods, Guandi societies, or gangs. When they were unwilling to attach themselves to the Kuomintang but had not yet reached the awareness that would bring them close to the Communist Party, they chose these lawful, traditional social bodies as a tool with which to protect themselves for a time. And because the gangs were by their nature backward and narrow, and stood in a certain contradiction even with the vital interests of the working people who joined them, in the course of the working people's united struggle these bodies had a plainly transitional character, and might become one of the objects of the united front.

On the other side, there were within the gangs factors set against the vital interests of the working people—what Li Lisan called the class struggle reflected inside the gangs. This is a matter the study of the unions ought to attend to.

### II. How I Used My Ties to Du Yuesheng to Work Among the Workers

#### (i) How I Came into Du's Circle

After the Postal Workers' Union had become one of Shanghai's seven great yellow unions (the other six being the unions of the Commercial Press printing plant, the Commercial Press distribution office, the newspaper trade, the Nanyang Tobacco factory, the British-American Tobacco factory, and the China General Electric Company), Lu Jingshi, to tighten his grip on the Postal Workers' Union and to make a name among the seven, picked out eleven men (twelve counting himself) from the Kuomintang government's Ministry of Communications, the Directorate General of Posts, and the Shanghai Post Office; and in 1931, through the introduction of Chen Junyi—an executive committeeman of the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal party office, chairman of its Committee on Staff and Workers' Affairs, and a disciple of Du Yuesheng—they took Du as their master. The first batch was Lu and myself, both postal cadets. The second batch was the postal cadets Zhao Shusheng, Zhang Kechang, and Xiao Qingsong; the postal clerk Fu Dewei; Yang Ketian of the Ministry of Communications; Miao Hongjun, an inspector of the Directorate General of Posts; Liu Xinquan and Yu Songqiao, postal assistants of the Shanghai Post Office; the carrier Xu Ziyi; and the office boy Yang Deling. Du thought it a matter of prestige that post-office staff should come under his door, for the post office was run by the Chinese government, its director was a foreigner, and it stood high enough in society.

Not long after we came into Du's circle, the ancestral hall Du had built at Gaoqiao was completed, and every notable of the Kuomintang and the government, from Chiang Kai-shek down, sent gifts and congratulations. Lu Jingshi and I could not afford lavish presents, and we hit on a way to spend little and yet do Du honor: we cut a commemorative postmark for the completion of the Du ancestral hall, to be used only at the Gaoqiao postal sub-office. This was something quite out of the common at the time; people found it a novelty, and Du Yuesheng, taking it that the post office too was giving him face, was well pleased. Cutting a commemorative postmark privately was against post-office regulations; but with the dignitaries of party and government all pressing to congratulate Du, and the affair the talk of Shanghai, the postal authorities could only, seeing which way the wind blew, look the other way.

Not long after I had entered Du's door, I took part in founding the Heng Society. The name was proposed by Zhang Shizhao, drawn from the phrase "constant as the moon in its fullness," in praise of Du Yuesheng. Lu Jingshi laid it down that every member of the Heng Society must hold a certain social standing, and that not all who had come under Du's door as disciples would be made members. The Heng Society had a round emblem: in the center a bronze bell, and on the outer face of the bell, dead center, a new moon. The bell stands for the source of sound; the word for "sound" is a homophone of "sheng," and with the moon added it makes "Yuesheng." The society had a council to handle its affairs, on which Lu Jingshi and I were both standing members.

#### (ii) Founding the Dagong News Agency

From the 1920s into the early 1930s, Shanghai had not a single news agency devoted to reporting the labor movement. In 1932 I became chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union. It was just after the fighting of January Twenty-Eighth; Shanghai's industry and commerce, hit by the war, had sunk deeper into depression, and capitalists Chinese and foreign sought to shift all their losses onto the workers—lengthening hours, cutting wages, closing plants and suspending business, dismissing and laying off, until the unemployed numbered in the hundreds of thousands. To survive, the workers rose in struggle one after another; strikes broke out in trade after trade and plant after plant, but with little effect, and most ended in defeat. Beyond the owners' stubborn resistance and the suppression by Kuomintang troops and police, an important cause was that the strikes were for the most part spontaneous and scattered, lacking mutual support, and so were weak.

Coming out of the post office, I knew well the importance of getting word out, and so I founded the Dagong News Agency, devoted to gathering strike news and issuing, day by day, dispatches on the important activities of the labor movement. The agency was set up at Huayifang on Jiujiang Road. I was its head; Shao Xubai was its editor; Shi Xiangya handled general affairs. At the start there were two reporters, Lu Muqin and Yu Yaoqiu, and later Shao Jicu. The General Labor Union had its offices in the Puye Guild hall opposite the Little World in Nanshi; every evening I went to the news agency, either straight from the union or from somewhere else, but go I did, without fail, to talk over with Shao Xubai the dispatches we would issue. Through Heng Society brothers who worked at the chief newspapers—Yu Kongwo of the *Shen Bao*, Tang Shichang of the *Xinwen Bao*, Jiang Xiangqing of the *Shishi Xinbao*, the brothers Ge Zhongyi and Jiang Zhongdao of the *Shi Bao*—our dispatches were taken up, and the strike news that reached print grew steadily, the *Shen Bao* carrying the most. When a small plant struck, its workers would come to the Dagong agency to report it, or telephone. The agency drew its funds from two sources: the fees the newspapers paid for our copy, and the income of a cooperative printing shop I ran with Zhou Xuexiang, Ye Xiangju, Li Mengnan (of the Five Continents Guben Soap Works union), Shao Xubai, and others. The printing shop was at Jinjiafang by Fangbang Bridge in Nanshi; its manager was Ye Xiangju, and its printing was overseen by Peng Wenmei (that is, Xi Xiangmei). It printed the papers of the unions and workers of every plant and firm, and the strike leaflets that other shops dared not touch were brought there to be printed; and so business was good.

That the Dagong agency came to matter had much to do with the "Movement to Strengthen the Postal Foundation," launched jointly on May 1, 1932, by the National Federation of Postal Workers and the Postal Staff Union. The postal workers demanded, first, the abolition and merger of the Savings and Remittance Bureau; second, an end to all subsidies to the airlines; third, the upholding of every rule of postal employment; fourth, that postal revenue support the post alone. On May 12 the Shanghai postal workers struck, and by the twenty-third the postal workers of Beiping, Tianjin, Kaifeng, Taiyuan, Jinan, Nanjing, Anqing, Fuzhou, Zhengzhou, Hangzhou, Luoyang, Qingdao, Yantai, Suzhou, Changzhou, Wuxi, Zhenjiang, Xuzhou, Ningbo, Songjiang, and other cities had all struck—a great show of force that shook the whole country. Wang Jingwei stripped the director of the Directorate General of Posts, Qian Chunqi, and the head of the postal department in the Ministry of Communications, Long Dafu, of their posts, and ordered the mayor of Shanghai, Wu Tiecheng, to urge an immediate return to work. Seven leading figures of Shanghai society—Yu Qiaqing, Wang Xiaolai, Shi Liangcai, Lin Kanghou, Zhang Gongquan, Du Yuesheng, and Pan Gongzhan—came forward to mediate, and eight men accepted mediation on the workers' side: Lu Jingshi, Zhang Kechang, Qi Jiamou, Fu Dewei, Shi Yitang, Lao Jieming, Lin Zhuowu, and myself. These fifteen formed a mediation committee, put forward terms of settlement, and on May 26 all returned to work. The so-called Movement to Strengthen the Postal Foundation was in truth a factional struggle within the Kuomintang, merely made a pretext for a show. From its brewing to the strike, the postal workers won little for themselves: only a two-dollar cost-of-living allowance a month per man, with some improvement in how year-end bonuses were reckoned and in holidays. But because the postal strike touched the whole country and drew the public's concern, and because the news went out from the Dagong agency alone and was taken up by every paper, the agency's standing rose greatly, and the papers' old indifference to labor reporting began to change.

#### (iii) Organizing the Yi Society

I came into Du's circle to firm and strengthen my hold on the Postal Workers' Union; I organized the Yi Society to firm my place in the Shanghai General Labor Union and to widen my power among the unions of every trade in the concessions.

From 1932 I served as chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union of that day. On October 2, 1933, the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office instructed the General Labor Union to file provisionally for the record; the city government's Social Affairs Bureau, torn by its own quarrels, refused to recognize it, and the concession authorities paid it no mind at all. Since most of Shanghai's chief plants and firms lay in the concessions and the extra-settlement roads district, the General Labor Union's influence among the workers was slight. Not until September 1934, when the General Labor Union received an order from the Ministry of Industry of the Kuomintang government—citing Section 2, Item 8 of the Revised Plan for Organizing People's Bodies and Item 2 of the Revised Plan for Guiding Mass Movements, both providing that within a county or municipal district the unions might combine to form a general body, and stating that the Ministry had accordingly drawn up rules for the organization of county and municipal general labor unions, to serve as a standard—did the Shanghai General Labor Union begin to gain lawful standing.

The leading core of the General Labor Union, besides its chairman, was five standing committeemen, all gang men. I myself was of Du's door; the five standing committeemen—Zhou Xuexiang, Shao Xubai, Ye Xiangju, Li Mengnan, and Long Peiyun—had come under Jin Tingsu's door. Early in 1934 I began taking in "students" within the General Labor Union, the first batch being Fang Rusheng, Wan Banghe, Shen Biao, and others; then I began taking in "students" within the Shanghai Post Office, the first batch being Wang Dongbai, Xiao Qingsong, Li Kesu, and several more, and after that I spread out widely. Among the chief firms of the concessions—the British Tramways Company, the Bus Company, the Power Company, the Waterworks, the Yunfei and Xiangsheng taxi companies, the cotton mills, rubber plants, machine shops, and the tug trade—I took in "students" everywhere. Outside the concessions, my chief targets for "students" were the China General Electric Company and the Zhabei Water and Power Plant.

In the autumn of 1934, some of my "students" felt there ought to be a body to bring everyone together, and thought of forming one to be called an "Academic Study Society"; they drew up a charter, with the aims of fostering feeling and exchanging friendship, and set out particular subjects for study. The thing was talked over for some months and never formally founded. Early in 1935, at a dinner, it came up again; and there was talk of how the gang bodies of the day were commonly styled some society or other—Du Yuesheng's Heng Society, Jin Tingsu's Ming Society, and the rest. So, borrowing the sense of the phrase "perseverance is the root of a man's standing," and following the Heng Society, we named it the Yi Society. And, citing the precedent of the Heng Society, which had Du Yuesheng as its patron, the Yi Society had no chairman but asked me to be its patron, with a board of directors and supervisors to handle its affairs. Its seat was in the Puye Occupational Union at No. 117 Fuyou Road in Nanshi; having no place to meet, it was in effect a correspondence office. In 1936, when the Pudong Building on Avenue Edward VII (now East Yan'an Road) was completed—a building run by the Pudong Native-Place Association that Du Yuesheng presided over—I rented two rooms in it through my ties to Du, and only then had a place to meet and could formally hang out the Yi Society's sign. We also designed an emblem: a round red field, in its center an open book, and around the book a ring of chain. Its meaning was that the red stood for the word "Zhu," the book alluded to "Xue," and the ring of chain to "Fan," while together they signified solidarity and mutual aid—and so the emblem spelled out my own name. Made into badges, it was given to some of the members to wear. The Yi Society took as its watchword "I for everyone, everyone for me," and urged mutual cooperation and help. By 1936 the working people who had joined the Yi Society numbered, in all Shanghai, over 1,000, scattered through the plants and firms of many trades.

The swift, wide growth of the Yi Society drew the notice of the gang factions and brought various frictions, which could be smoothed over only because Du Yuesheng backed me. Du's disciples were leading figures in every trade, and on the union side it was chiefly through me that his sway was enlarged. Since Lu Jingshi had by then become head of the military-law section of the Shanghai Garrison Command and an executive committeeman of the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office, and had no time to spare for union affairs, Du handed all matters of the labor world to me and trusted me fully. And so the Yi Society gradually gained the upper hand among the many factions in the plants and firms of many trades, and its core people in those workplaces entered the leadership of the unions. They in turn, by sworn brotherhood and sisterhood, bound crowds of workers to them and widened their reach. Through my 1,000-odd "students" and the great body of workers they gathered, I built ties with the unions of every trade and their grassroots bodies, broadened the social base of the General Labor Union, and firmed and strengthened my place in it. But what made the General Labor Union count in society, and let it open a way in the concession districts, was chiefly Du Yuesheng's coming forward to mediate labor disputes.

#### (iv) Du Yuesheng Mediating Labor Disputes

After 1932 our national crisis deepened by the day, and under the world economic crisis and the dumping of Japanese goods, the state of Shanghai's industry and commerce grew worse year by year and the workers' plight harder. To win their survival, Shanghai's workers took up struggle in every form. By the Kuomintang's own figures, in 1932 the city saw eighty-two strikes, with more than 71,000 strikers, and two hundred fifty-three labor disputes touching more than fourteen hundred plants and firms. In 1933 the Chinese-owned cotton mills cut operations by twenty-three percent from April, the tobacco plants ran three or four days a week, the rubber plants shut down generally through the summer, and in October two-thirds of the city's silk filatures closed as the silk price crashed; the city saw eighty-eight strikes, more than 74,000 strikers, and 301 disputes touching more than seven hundred plants and firms. In 1934, with American cotton, American wheat, and flour pouring in, and Japanese goods smuggled and dumped besides, most flour mills stopped work and the cotton mills cut back generally; workers were thrown out in great numbers, and the city saw seventy-three strikes, more than 31,000 strikers, and two hundred thirty-three disputes touching more than nine hundred plants and firms. In 1935 the city saw ninety-four strikes, more than 78,000 strikers, and two hundred twenty-one disputes touching four hundred-odd plants and firms. In 1936, the December Ninth student movement in Beiping raised a high tide of resistance to Japan and national salvation across the country, and Shanghai's labor movement saw 110 strikes; above all, the victory in November of the anti-Japanese general strike by more than forty thousand workers at Shanghai's Japanese cotton mills carried the city's resistance and salvation movement to a new height.

In these years the Kuomintang, while pressing its "encirclement" of the Soviet areas, was also suppressing the labor movement, again and again ordering strikes sternly forbidden and readily using armed force and military law to threaten striking workers. And so, of the strikes of these years, only a few ended in the workers' complete victory: eighteen percent of all strikes in 1932, twenty-odd percent in 1933, twenty-eight percent in 1934, eighteen percent in 1935. That the workers, struggling in every form under the harshest conditions, could win even a part of their aims was owed chiefly to the brave fighting spirit of the great mass of workers; and another factor was that, once a struggle reached a certain point, they accepted mediation and stopped while they could. In the proper settlement of labor disputes and strikes at firms of wide social weight and plants of larger scale, Du Yuesheng's coming forward to mediate played its part. Here I set down a few instances I saw for myself.

On January 7, 1932, the whole body of some one thousand crewmen of the China Merchants Steam Navigation Company sent delegates to petition the company for the same treatment as its office staff and for a double year-end payment, and were refused. From the eighth the crews struck, and the China Merchants ships reaching Shanghai after struck in turn, seventeen in all. The company threatened the crews with the line "no ship may be laid up at will in a time of national crisis," and stood stubborn. In the end, through Du Yuesheng's mediation, three points were agreed: first, the crews would receive half the double year-end payment (half paid in the first month, the remaining half in March); second, no crewman would be dismissed without cause; third, if the company recognized its staff union, it would likewise recognize the crews' federation. And so the ships resumed sailing from January 12.

On April 2, 1932, the management of the *Shishi Xinbao*, seizing on the surge of unemployment after the January Twenty-Eighth fighting, forced its workers to print the *Da Wanbao* as well; when there were not enough hands, it used that as a pretext to dismiss more than eighty and hire the unemployed to replace them. Because the management had powerful men in the Kuomintang government behind it, the dispute dragged on more than a month unresolved. On June 1 more than a thousand workers of the *Shen Bao*, the *Xinwen Bao*, and the *Shi Bao* struck in sympathy (all the great Shanghai papers had Du Yuesheng's "students"), demanding that the *Shishi Xinbao* management restore the ousted workers to their jobs and pay their wages for the time they had been out. The management, harsher still, set the Kuomintang police to arrest more than twenty *Shishi Xinbao* workers. Under the great force of the mass struggle, Du Yuesheng came forward to mediate; all the arrested workers were released, and it was verbally agreed that the dismissed *Shishi Xinbao* workers would be paid severance.

At the end of August 1933, the management of the British-owned Wheelock Company, pleading slack business, dismissed more than a hundred workers. Mediation by the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office and the Social Affairs Bureau came to nothing. On September 19 the whole body of more than five hundred workers struck, all 102 cargo lighters stopped, and the merchants pressed the company with questions and demands for their losses. Through Du Yuesheng's mediation an agreement was reached: first, the twenty dismissed workmen would receive retirement pay; second, the other eighty-odd would all be reinstated; third, wages would be paid in full for the days of the strike.

On January 19, 1934, the whole body of outworking silversmiths of the jewelers' trade laid down their tools, protesting that the owners paid their wages at eighty percent and demanding the restoration of the "thirty-six split" (the worker to get thirty-six percent of the price of each piece) and the settling of arrears. The owners, pleading slack business, refused; mediation by the party and government offices of the Kuomintang came to nothing. On March 3, seventeen of the still-striking workers went together to the jewelers' guild hall on Huayuan Street outside the Great East Gate and began a hunger strike. The General Labor Union sent people to comfort them, urged them hard to drink milk, and asked Du Yuesheng to mediate. In the end it was settled that wages would go by a "twenty-seven split" (the worker to get twenty-seven percent of the price of each piece), and that the wages owed from the past year's Mid-Autumn Festival to the year's end would be paid off at twenty-three percent. On March 13 the whole body of workers returned to work.

More than fourteen hundred workers of the old and new plants of the American-owned Shanghai Power Company, protesting the owners' wholesale layoffs and dismissals, struck from September 29 to November 21, 1934. The union put forward seven terms, the chief of them: first, no worker to be dismissed without cause, and temporary workers of half a year's service to count as permanent; second, the order dismissing the workers' delegates to be withdrawn; third, the union to be recognized and given a monthly subsidy of three hundred dollars for expenses; fourth, wages to be paid during the strike. Because the American bosses stood stubborn, refused the union's terms, and colluded with the patrol house to force the old workers back and hire three hundred sixty White Russians as temporary hands to threaten the strikers, and because at the strike's start the office staff would not join in a common action, some Yi Society members among the strikers seized a leading figure among the staff, drove him by car to Gongwu Road in the Chinese district, pushed him out, and fired two shots into the air; the man, in a flurry of "yes, yes," went to rouse the staff to join the strike, and so the staff struck too. The strike hung in the balance fifty-four days, and in the end Du Yuesheng came forward to mediate: during the strike the owners would advance a month's wages, to be deducted later. This strike was in truth a defeat; Du Yuesheng's mediation only won the strikers back a little face, and gave the thing some sort of ending.

On July 4, 1935, the Council of the French Concession issued a notice ordering the more than forty thousand rickshaw men to register before the month's end, at five dimes a head, and the rickshaw men rose against it. On the morning of August 1 the Council set the patrol to register them by force in the streets, and several hundred rickshaws were impounded. That afternoon the workers met at the South Wharf and sent delegates to petition the Kuomintang party office, the Social Affairs Bureau, and the city government; mediation by the party and government offices bore no fruit. On the sixth the whole body of workers struck, chose more than 1,500 delegates, and set out in procession from the Great South Gate to petition the party office, the French Concession's Ratepayers' Association of Chinese, and the Council. When the column reached the neighborhood of Xieqiao it was blocked by the French Concession patrol, a clash broke out, and on the spot twelve workers were beaten and hurt, three arrested, one lost. Afterward Du Yuesheng came forward to mediate: the impounded rickshaws were returned, registration was made free for all, and the registration period was extended two months.

In November 1936 more than forty thousand workers of the city's Japanese cotton mills held an anti-Japanese general strike, putting forward five terms: first, a ten-percent wage rise; second, the machines to be stopped an hour at meals; third, that no worker be dismissed; fourth, that no worker be struck; fifth, opposition to extra Sunday hours. The strike was organized and set going by the underground Communists Zhang Weizhen, Han Nianlong, Zhou Lin, Chen Zhiyi, Guo Guangzhou, and others; it won support in every form from the Shanghai National Salvation Federation and from the various societies and schools, and became, under the banner of the anti-Japanese national united front the Communist Party had raised, a historic anti-Japanese action of unity by the working, student, and commercial people of Shanghai, the anti-Japanese salvation bodies, and people of every social stratum. To tighten their suppression the Japanese capitalists moved great numbers of marines, and spread spies and patrolmen through every plant to arrest workers. But because the workers held to their struggle and the whole city's people backed them, Funatsu Tatsuichirō, chairman of the Japanese cotton-textile association, had no choice but to ask Du Yuesheng to mediate. On November 25 Du called me and more than twenty striker delegates, together with delegates of the Kuomintang's Shanghai party office, Social Affairs Bureau, and police bureau, to the Zhonghui Building, and there settled seven terms: first, a five-percent wage rise; second, that the monthly bonus system be changed to a reward system, the deserving to be raised a grade and given more pay as merited; third, that no worker be dismissed without cause; fourth, that workers not be struck or cursed; fifth, twelve hours' work a day, fourteen on Sundays, the extra two hours' Sunday work to be paid separately; sixth, the machines to be stopped thirty minutes at meals; seventh, that all the plants' workers return to work on the morning of the twenty-sixth. Before this I had already arranged to have the arrested workers bailed out first from the Longhua Garrison Command. After the strike I backed the Toyoda mill workers in forming a workers' welfare society.

Before 1932 Du Yuesheng had come forward to mediate labor disputes, but seldom, and mostly in the French Concession. After I became chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union of that day, the times Du came forward to mediate grew steadily, and their reach spread over all Shanghai. This was because, after the January Twenty-Eighth fighting, the national crisis and the economic slump deepened by the day, labor disputes and strikes grew ever more frequent, and the General Labor Union met many difficulties in handling them. First, though the Shanghai General Labor Union of that day was under the Kuomintang party office and the Social Affairs Bureau, the two sides often disagreed on how to handle a dispute. The party office and the bureau always started from so-called safeguarding public order and preventing Communist activity, took the owners' side, met strikes with a policy of suppression, were eager to quell them, and would even set the military and police to arrest strikers. The General Labor Union held that one should take a conciliatory course, resolve some of the workers' real difficulties, and not use high-handed means, still less agree to the arbitrary arrest of workers. And so the party office and the bureau often brought pressure on the General Labor Union to make me yield to them. When this happened, I turned to Du Yuesheng. In the city government, the party office, and other organs there were Du's "students"—Wu Xingya, who handled local political matters under Wu Tiecheng; Lu Jingshi, who managed labor affairs for the party office and served as head of the military-law section at the Garrison Command; the executive committeeman Chen Junyi, and others—and once Du came forward to mediate, of course all had to give him face. Because he ordinarily kept out of the affairs of the labor world, and because the circumstances of a dispute were very tangled, in this matter he trusted me fully and spoke for me. There was another reason: Du Yuesheng's becoming a "notable" of Shanghai had much to do with his coming forward again and again to mediate disputes. Through the Dagong News Agency I always played up Du's part in a settlement, and after each dispute ended the units concerned would run notices in the great papers thanking Du for his mediation, so that Du felt himself much honored. And so he mediated disputes not for profit, and when I went to ask him to take the field, he never failed me. The second difficulty I met in handling disputes was the contradiction with capitalists Chinese and foreign. I stood for cooperation between labor and capital, but I also held to the aim of bettering the workers' lives; and among the workers of many trades my "students" often stood against the owners in a dispute. The owners not only joined with the party and government organs against the workers, and even arrested workers, but at times used the frictions among the gang factions, pulling one faction in to press another (many of the foremen and inspectors in the trades belonged to one faction or another), to threaten and beat the strikers. When I met such a situation, I went to Du Yuesheng for help against the owners and the other factions. That I could bail workers out of the patrol house, the Garrison Command, and the police bureau rested on my ties to Du: he put in a word, and I went and did the paperwork.

Du Yuesheng's mediation of disputes was not quite of a piece with the stance of the Kuomintang's party and government organs. He set store by face, took himself for an upholder of fairness, and wanted everyone to buy his account and heed his word. So I often had the workers make some concession first, to give Du a little face, and then pushed him to press the owners and force from them a larger concession still. At times he stepped over the workers' heads and answered for the owners on the workers' demands. He had a pet phrase: if such-and-such a boss will not put up the money, I will put it up, and my word is good. In the ordinary run of things this line held true. But Du Yuesheng too had his moments of falling short: once, mediating a dispute at the French Tramways Company, the workers came round to his terms but the owners would not, and he could only put up a few thousand dollars to fob the workers off.

#### (v) Organizing Gang Armed Units

When the July Seventh war of resistance began in 1937, I was on the return leg of a voyage, having set out by mail steamer from Marseille. Aboard ship I heard of the talk Chiang Kai-shek gave at Lushan. Reaching Shanghai on August 7, I went straight to Du Yuesheng and put to him my view that the workers should take up arms against Japan. He said that on July 22 Shanghai's various circles had already formed the Shanghai General Association for the Support of Armed Resistance; that he, with Wang Xiaolai, Qian Xinzhi, Pan Gongzhan, Zhang Shoushan, Tong Xingbai, Huang Yanpei, Lu Jingshi, Ke Ganchen, and others, made up its presidium; and that he also served as head of its fund-raising committee, in charge of raising all the funds to support the anti-Japanese forces, with the Heng Society's Luo Qinghua as one of the deputy heads. As for arming Shanghai's workers against Japan, the fund-raising committee could put up the money and he would put up the guns.

When the war of August Thirteenth broke out, Du Yuesheng informed me that he was making me commander of the Third Detachment of the Special Operations Corps, with an establishment of 1,500 men. I picked out more than 400 from the Yongjin Corps, roused a further batch from the workers of various trades, gathered them at Longhua Temple, and held a founding assembly, at which I spoke. The Third Detachment's headquarters was set up in the Star Film Company at Fenglin Bridge; my adjutant Wang Zhenbai and my clerk Wang Baochang were both my "students." The detachment had three battalions: one battalion commander was Shui Xiangyun, another was Han Jingxian, a drill instructor from the old Yongjin Corps, and the name of the third I can no longer recall. Yi Lirong introduced Professor He Hanwei to come and give political lessons to the detachment. Using a post-office car, I brought a case of pistols from Du Yuesheng and handed them out to the officers; the rifles brought over were all old, and not even enough to give one to each soldier.

The corps' commander-in-chief was Liu Zhilu, formerly an officer of the Northeastern Army, who often came and went at Du Yuesheng's mansion. Under the corps were five detachments. The First Detachment's commander was He Xingjian, a "student" of Du Yuesheng's; its ranks held both Green Gang and Hongmen men, and the Hongmen's Xiang Songpo brought in a batch to join. The Second Detachment's commander was Lu Jingshi, the Third was myself, the Fourth was Zhang Ye, and the Fifth was Tao Yiguan, who managed military training at the Garrison Command and put the shop clerks, apprentices, and young workers under training into his detachment. Liu Zhilu once called a meeting of the detachment commanders at some place on Hongqiao Road and issued a flag; after that he called no more meetings. He issued one official document, appointing me commander of the Third Detachment of the Special Operations Corps of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang Action Committee, with the rank of colonel. When I asked Du Yuesheng whose charge this Jiangsu-Zhejiang Action Committee came under, he said it had to do with Dai Li—and that was how the guns had been got.

The Shanghai resistance of August Thirteenth held on into mid-November, and the Third Detachment stood by the whole time, never going to the front. Three days before the Kuomintang armies withdrew to the west, Liu Zhilu ordered the Third Detachment to hand all its rifles to the Second Detachment in Pudong. On the night the Kuomintang armies pulled out of Shanghai I was quartered at the detachment headquarters; at first light my sentry reported that the police had withdrawn and two truckloads of Japanese troops were coming toward Fenglin Bridge. That both the troops and the police should have withdrawn without warning me struck me as very strange and left me quite unprepared. Having only pistols in hand and no power to resist, we withdrew by the back gate; but the iron gate of the French Concession was already shut, and so we crossed Yongjiabang into the concession. The next day I went to Du Yuesheng for disbandment money and disbanded the whole Third Detachment, officers and men. From the fund-raising committee of the Shanghai General Association for the Support of Armed Resistance, Du put up several thousand dollars, six for each officer and three for each soldier. I returned the pistols to Du, keeping back twenty-five, which I hid in the home of Wu Shuhe, dean of studies at Minli Middle School (I had served as secretary-general of Minli Middle School, and so knew Wu Shuhe). These pistols were later used to do away with the traitors Lu Liankui (former chief inspector of the International Settlement patrol house) and Lu Bohong (chairman of a maintenance association).

After the Kuomintang armies withdrew to the west, Du Yuesheng's movements were the concern of all quarters, the enemy and the puppets included. Yang Guanbei, Lu Jingshi, and I pressed Du hard to leave Shanghai, while Xu Caicheng and others of the Heng Society urged him to stay. Du heeded the few of us and agreed to go at once to Hong Kong. I saw him to the customs wharf and watched him board a motor launch and go up onto the ship before I turned back. A few days later I too reached Hong Kong. A month after that, Lu Jingshi came to Hong Kong as well, and met with Du Yuesheng and me.

### III. Changes in the Gangs After the CCP Proposed the Anti-Japanese National United Front

Around 1936 the Shanghai gangs, whether Green Gang or Hongmen (the Hong Gang), were fairly active, opening their gates wide and taking in disciples in crowds. The Yi Society I had organized also flourished in this stretch of time, drawing great numbers of working people into it. This had everything to do with the situation of the day. For as the resistance and salvation movement rose, the mass of working people grew more concerned with public affairs, and felt that to fend off the outsider's insult and resist the oppression of capitalists Chinese and foreign, the strength of the brotherhoods and sisterhoods they already had was too small; they pressed urgently to widen their combination and to join the salvation movement and other progressive activities. The tide of the resistance and salvation movement battered the old framework of the gangs, and some of the gangs' upper figures, pushed by the lower ranks and moved by the nationwide resistance and salvation movement, took up a stance of sympathy and support for it and joined in activities of every kind.

Du Yuesheng, from the January Twenty-Eighth resistance of 1932 to the August Thirteenth resistance of 1937, took part in the city-wide resistance and salvation activities. The Hongmen's Xiang Songpo (Xiang Haiqian) too, under the watchword of uniting to resist Japan, carried on activity among the working people. Before the July Seventh war, his subordinate leaders gathered workers of Shenxin Mills Five, Six, and Seven, Jardine's, the Heping firm, and other plants to a dinner at the Da Zhonghua Restaurant, held forth on uniting to resist, and called on the brothers to hold not only to the loyalty of the Hongmen but to the loyalty of Chinese.

After Shanghai became a solitary island, the gangs changed greatly within: some of their leaders took part in the resistance, and some turned their coats and went over to the enemy and the puppets. Du Yuesheng left Shanghai for Hong Kong and later Chongqing; one of his agents in Shanghai, Yao Huiquan, kept in touch with the underground Communists of Shanghai, helped raise funds for education and culture, and promised that if anything came up on the patrol-house side he could find a way to see it through. After the forces of the enemy and the puppets pushed into the concessions, the working people, to fend off the Japanese bandits' insults, chose the still-lawful gangs as their safeguard, and so the gangs' strength somewhat grew.

By this time the gangs within the Shanghai Post Office had changed greatly too. The Shanghai Post Office had had many gang societies: Du Yuesheng, Jin Tingsu, Wang Xiaolai, Yang Hu, Ma Xiangsheng, and Peng Baiwei all took in disciples among the postal staff, and I, with Fu Dewei, Shi Yitang, Zhang Kechang, Liu Xinquan, Lu Keming, and others inside the post office, took in disciples among the postal staff as well, each setting up his own door. More than three hundred of the postal staff had joined the Yi Society and more than two hundred Zhang Kechang's Chang Society, the two larger bodies. I upheld the anti-Japanese national united front the Chinese Communist Party had put forward, and after leaving Shanghai I carried on, from Guangzhou to Wuhan, the work of uniting the country's workers in resistance. Zhang Kechang went over to the puppets, and in December 1938 organized a traitors' union in Shanghai; most of the Chang Society, understanding well the greater cause, would not follow the enemy, though a small part were carried into it. Just at this time, the comrades of the Workers' Movement Committee of the CCP's Jiangsu Provincial Committee, who led the underground work in the Shanghai Post Office, moved some of the Chang Society's members to issue, under the name "the hot-blooded of the Chang Society," a *Letter to the Brothers of the Chang Society*. The text is not long, and I set it down here just as it was:

{v} Brothers of the Chang Society, chivalrous and righteous, faithful and unwavering: From the first day we joined this society, we swore to be filial to our families and loyal to our country, to draw the knife at the sight of injustice; the brothers' loyalty reaches to heaven, and blessings and burdens we share alike. Every disciple of this society should be a filial son at home and a good and law-abiding subject in the land, should seek out worthy masters and righteous friends, mend bridges and repair roads, care for the old and help the poor. Because our aims are upright and open and our loyalty runs high and long, the brothers respect and love one another, and the society's affairs have grown with it. Yet now there are unworthy men who, using this society's name, swindle and deceive; who, under cover of a dinner at the Huibin Lou, brought in the Korean Lin Zijiong, secretary of the puppet Chinese Workers' Welfare Association, to preach surrender, seeking to blind the brothers, to injure the sovereignty of the post, to harm the nation and drag the brothers into dishonor. Mindful that our society has always been clean, and will not now sink to be traitors and leave a stench for ten thousand years, we should quickly rouse ourselves and make plain our stand of "loyalty, filial duty, integrity, and righteousness—no becoming traitors." As for one Shen and one Dai, who conspire with the enemy and take the bandit for a father, we cry out and attack them, and will never let our society, upright and open, harbor such shameless disciples. Let every son of the Yellow Emperor, every child of the Chinese nation, seize them and put them to death. This we declare.

This open letter shook the Chang Society, and through the underground Communists who had worked their way into it the members were brought around: most of the Chang Society were won to the stand of "loyalty to the country, no becoming traitors," the tiny handful of turncoats were isolated, and the traitors' scheme was struck a blow.

In March 1940, when the puppet Wang Jingwei government was set up in Nanjing, Zhang Kechang became a deputy minister of social affairs in the puppet regime. By then the Chang Society's numbers in the post office had somewhat grown, and underground Communists were active among them, working hard to lead the Chang Society's lower ranks both to use the lawful shell to guard their own interests and to break out of the gangs' narrow circle and stand under the banner of the anti-Japanese national united front, doing some useful work for resistance and salvation.
