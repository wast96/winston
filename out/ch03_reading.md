## Chapter 3. From "Underground" to "Aboveground": Combining Open and Secret Methods of Operation

Ten years of civil war had painted the Communists as something sinister and grotesque: men who "held property and wives in common," "red-browed, green-eyed" demons.

Then came the second Nationalist-Communist alliance of 1937, and the Communists who had been hidden underground came up into the open one after another. They turned out to have the same black hair and yellow skin as everyone else, and to wear the same Zhongshan suits as government officials.

This change in how they lived and how they worked was, in its way, a "reform and opening" of its own.

Could men so used to working in the shadows carry off a part in the open?

And could men learning to act in the open still run their secret intelligence?

### 1. The "Eighth Route Army Offices" Blossom Everywhere

The whole nation was at war with Japan now; a war between nations had displaced the war between classes, and the Red Army under Communist command was folded into the National Revolutionary Army. Practical need led the Nationalists to allow the Communists to open Eighth Route Army offices in the territory they controlled. Because the Eighth Route Army was also designated the 18th Group Army, these were formally the offices of the 18th Group Army; everyone called them by the short name *ba-ban*, the "Eighth Route offices." Scattered through the great cities of the country, they were like little boats riding on the water: above the waterline they carried the open work of the united front, and below it they hid the secret work of intelligence.

The Eighth Route offices had a forerunner: the Red Army Liaison Office in Xi'an. Right after Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng seized Chiang Kai-shek in the Xi'an Incident, they invited the Communists to send someone to Xi'an to talk over the situation. In January 1937 the CCP Central Committee moved into Yan'an, and in February the Red Army Liaison Office put up its sign in Xi'an.

Dealing with the Nationalists meant understanding their cadres, so the job of running the liaison office went to Li Kenong, the one survivor of the "Three Heroes of Longtan." His first task was to set up a secret radio station in direct contact with Yan'an. Zhang Xueliang, for his part, urgently needed to act together with the Communists against a possible punitive expedition by the Nationalist army, and he backed Liu Ding in running a station out of the liaison office.

That station had double duty: it kept up the secret contact between Zhang Xueliang, Yang Hucheng, and the CCP Central Committee, and it relayed the New China News Agency's open dispatches from Yan'an to the rest of the country. It was busy work. At the time the Nationalist center and Zhang and Yang in Xi'an were at odds, and Xi'an's secret-service organs did not answer to Zhang Xueliang; so the Red Army's secret station also had to guard against a raid by the agents. Tong Xiaopeng and the other cipher staff dug out a cellar and fitted a movable wooden panel into a hole in its wall, so the radio could be hidden away at a moment's notice.

The Communists had come by this chance to surface only with great difficulty, and it was far from safe.

Small as it was, this Xi'an liaison office was the Communists' only open establishment in Nationalist territory. Inside it kept a secretariat, a cipher section, and a general-affairs section, along with dedicated purchasing and security staff. Many patriotic and democratic figures came here to find the Communists and talk over resistance; many young progressives passed through on their way to Yan'an and the revolution. The office also worked hard at buying scarce supplies for Yan'an: medicines, newsprint for the presses, cotton cloth, communications gear, all shipped on from here. When Zhou Enlai came to Xi'an to negotiate, he stayed here too.

From liaison to united-front work to foreign trade to diplomacy, the office carried out the many tasks the Central Committee laid on it. For the CCP leadership, tucked away in remote Yan'an, it became a window onto the world outside.

The great alliance of the Northwest came first; from there the Nationalist-Communist cooperation was pushed, step by step, out to the rest of the country.

In Xi'an, Li Kenong received the Nationalist center's negotiator, Zhang Chong, and then flew with him to Shanghai to continue the talks. Once there, Li took the alias Li Zhenzhong and, under the cover of quartermaster chief of the 17th Route Army, set up an office in the city. He had worked the Special Branch in Shanghai in his younger days, and back in the great city after long absence he was in his element. He chose a spot in the foreign concessions, on Fuxi Road, at No. 21 Duofuli: a two-story red-brick Western house, a telephone downstairs to receive Shanghai callers, the radio hidden in the attic for contact with Yan'an, and gates front and back opening onto different streets, easy to slip away from.

In Shanghai, Li Kenong also tracked down Mao Zedong's younger brother Mao Zemin. As bank president of the Chinese Soviet Republic, Mao Zemin was sitting anxiously on a large sum of American dollars that the Comintern had sent to support the CCP. The two men laid careful plans: first they changed the dollars into fabi, the Nationalist currency, wiring part of it through a Shanghai bank to a bank in Xi'an and converting the rest into gold, which Mao Zemin carried to Xi'an himself by train. Each time he reached Xi'an, Ye Jianying met the train with a car and got him past the Nationalist agents' checks.

At this stage these contact points had no formal blessing from the Nationalist center, and had to work half in the open, half in secret.

In August 1937 the Nationalists and Communists reached an agreement: the Red Army would be reorganized as the Eighth Route Army of the National Revolutionary Army, with Eighth Route offices in a number of cities. On the 19th, chief of staff Ye Jianying and Tong Xiaopeng set about establishing the capital office in Nanjing, and on the 27th its head, Li Kenong, took up his post. The building was a mansion that Zhou Enlai rented from his old Nankai teacher Zhang Boling. In early September the Communist representative Bogu came to Nanjing to negotiate with the Nationalists, and this Eighth Route office in the capital served, in fact, as the CCP's own mission to the capital. The Xi'an liaison office had never been recognized by the Nationalist center; the Nanjing office now became the first open, legal Communist establishment in Nationalist territory since the Second Revolutionary Civil War.

Legal standing meant legal rights to fight for, and the Nanjing office's first task was to rescue comrades from prison. Now that the two parties were cooperating, the Nationalists had no reason to go on holding Communists; so they set Li Kenong a hard test: produce the list of names. The Nationalist secret services assumed the Communists had no idea who was inside; in fact Li Kenong had long since obtained an exact roster through underground channels. The office submitted the names in batches, and in two months forced the Nationalists to release over a thousand imprisoned Party members.

Using the freedom the Nanjing office gave them, Bogu, Dong Biwu, and Li Kenong also set about restoring the underground Party organizations of the lower and middle Yangtze. The Communist underground in those regions had been badly broken up by the Nationalists and had hardly functioned since 1935. The office sent released members, Liu Ningyi, Tao Zhu, Qian Ying, Fang Yi, and Li Hua up from Beiping, to rebuild the Jiangsu Provincial Committee and the Nanjing Municipal Committee. This work stayed secret; the Nationalist secret services were not to know of it.

The Nationalist secret services, at that moment, were reeling.

Xu Enzeng, head of the Zhongtong and a professional anti-Communist, put it this way: "Before the war, we knew every last one of the Communists' highest secrets, clear as day." And: "As the Nationalists and Communists moved toward a settlement, my work gradually came to a halt; and when the Japanese invasion broke out in July of the twenty-sixth year of the Republic (1937) and the two parties joined hands again, I gave up the task of fighting the Communists entirely and turned to coordinating operations against Japan."

The top had turned; the bottom was dazed. Those Communists wore the same Zhongshan suits as government officials, the same uniforms as Nationalist officers. What was a junior agent to do, arrest his own superiors?

Still, the Nationalist secret services soon came to their senses. Xu Enzeng again: "But the change did not last long. Before long, as friction between the two parties mounted, we shouldered the burden once more. Only, the situation had changed by then; and the anti-Communist struggle that followed, in form, in substance, and in result, never matched the earlier phase." Alliance or no alliance, the agents still meant to wage their "struggle against the Communists"; they had merely switched their "method of struggle."

Around the Eighth Route offices the streets came alive: cobblers, cigarette sellers, peep-show men, passersby, all of them the agents' watchers. The Nationalists could not openly ban an open Communist establishment like the Eighth Route office, but they could watch it in secret.

None of these tricks got past an old Special Branch hand like Li Kenong. Once, when Zhou Enlai came to Nanjing to negotiate and needed to see a foreign friend, Li went with him. The moment Zhou's car pulled out, an agents' car was on its tail. Li had the driver speed up to open a gap, and just as they drew level with the foreigner's car, Zhou slipped out and climbed into it. By the time the agents caught up, Zhou was nowhere to be seen and the sedan was parked at the roadside, with Li Kenong lounging against it. "If you're not busy," he called to the agents, "come give us a hand with the car."

Under the agents' close watch, Li Kenong went on with his secret work all the same. The eminent professor Wang Kunlun was a longtime Nationalist Party member, at the time a member of the Legislative Yuan. Li met with him in secret; in fact Wang Kunlun had turned against Chiang as early as 1927 and had secretly joined the Communist Party in 1933. Through him, Li Kenong obtained important intelligence on the Nationalist upper reaches.

Beyond the secret work, Li Kenong threw himself into open activity too, preparing to launch a newspaper and put out propaganda. The Nationalists had agreed to cooperate, but they would not publish the *Manifesto of Nationalist-Communist Cooperation* to the public, and even the news of the Eighth Route Army marching to the front went unreported. When the Communists proposed founding the *New China Daily*, the Nationalists did all they could to block it. So Li Kenong arranged a backdoor takeover, placing the underground Party member Chen Nongfei, through his connections, as editor-in-chief of the *Jinling Daily*. As it happened, Japanese bombing had driven the old editors to resign one after another, and Li fitted out editor Chen with a whole staff of Communist editors. The *Jinling Daily* pushed the Communists' resistance news hard, printed the *CCP Central Committee's Manifesto of Nationalist-Communist Cooperation*, reported the great victory at Pingxingguan, and soon became a best-seller in Nationalist territory. Li Kenong also secured the consent of the Nationalist propaganda chief Shao Lizi and got the Nationalist elder statesman Yu Youren to write the masthead calligraphy for the *New China Daily*. Just as Li had bought the newsprint and rented the printing plant and was ready to bring the paper out, the Japanese army reached the city walls, and the Nanjing office had to withdraw to Wuhan. After that the Eighth Route Army had no office in the capital, Nanjing, but one by one it acquired offices in the other great cities.

To resist Japan and save the nation, every Nationalist army unit was opening rear-area offices, and the Eighth Route Army had the same right. Xi'an, Shanxi, and Lanzhou, three cities close to the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, held Eighth Route offices that were Yan'an's three great windows onto the country, airing it from the south, the east, and the west. The southern passage served mainly to keep in touch with the central government; the eastern one, to connect with the base areas of North and Central China; and the western passage had an international use: contact with "the far country."

"The far country," inside the Party, was code for the Comintern. The Comintern's headquarters were in Moscow, the Soviet capital, and the nearest route between it and Yan'an, whether by air or by land, ran through Lanzhou in Gansu. Wu Xiuquan, fluent in Russian, worked the Lanzhou office's liaison with the Soviet army; Soviet personnel were stationed here too, and he handled the links between Moscow and both Chongqing and Yan'an.

The Japanese high command, meaning to sever the China-Soviet link, fixed on Ejin Banner as a place to set up. In Inner Mongolia the Japanese had founded a "Mengjiang United Autonomous Government," and in that government's name they sent a dozen men out onto the grasslands of Ejin Banner to court the Mongol prince.

The Lanzhou office, learning that the Japanese had come into Gansu, moved at once to counter them. First it notified the Nationalist government; then, coordinating with the Gansu provincial authorities, it had the Communist Zhou Renshan appointed secretary of the banner government. Zhou Renshan rode out alone to Ejin Banner and drew the local Mongol and Han soldiers and people together to plan resistance. The Japanese were active too, cultivating Mongol and Han traitors and watching for a chance to assassinate Zhou Renshan. When Japan launched the Pacific War in 1941, its strategic ambitions swelled further, and it took to flying supplies in to Ejin Banner. The banner's quiet was broken, and Zhou Renshan, after consulting a Nationalist brigade commander stationed at Jiuquan, resolved to go over to the attack.

The Lunar New Year at Ejin Banner had a flavor all its own, with the Mongols' whole roast sheep and the Han's boiled dumplings alike. The Mongol prince laid on a banquet and invited every guest resident in the banner, and the Japanese came along cheerfully enough.

In the muddle of drink, gunfire rang out over the feast; the Japanese, caught unready, were killed to a man.

The head of the Shanxi office was a Special Branch veteran, Wang Shiying.

Before the war, the Communists' northern organization had been hit hard again and again, with Peng Zhen, Bo Yibo, and many other members arrested. Wang Shiying had brought Special Branch men up from Shanghai, organized Nan Hanchen, Wang Chaobei, Xuan Xiafu, Ji Hongchang, and others, and vigorously worked the Northwestern Army, laying a united-front foundation for the CCP's move to northern Shaanxi and its resistance in the North. After the alliance, the Central Committee named Wang Shiying to head the Shanxi office and work the Second War Zone. Shanxi, lying between Hebei and northern Shaanxi, was strategic ground both China and Japan had to have. Yan Xishan, who held Shanxi as his own domain, was bent on preserving himself through the Sino-Japanese war and was in secret touch with Japanese agents. Wang Shiying worked both sides: befriending the Shanxi generals for the united front, and posting intelligence men to watch Yan Xishan for signs of collusion with the enemy. The Shanxi governor Zhao Daiwen was Yan Xishan's schoolmate, but Zhao's son Zhao Zongfu was an underground Communist. Through Zhao Zongfu, Wang Shiying learned of Yan Xishan's secret parley with Japanese commanders and his plan to return to Taiyuan and surrender to Japan. Wang moved to talk him out of it and even called a mass rally to expose the Japanese plot. And so Yan Xishan, for all his secret dealings with Japan, never dared surrender to it openly.

The Wuhan office brought the most organizations together under one roof: the CCP Central Committee's delegation, the Central Committee's Yangtze Bureau, and the Eighth Route Army office. The Nationalist government was in Wuhan at this point too, so the Wuhan office was really the capital office again.

The secret Yangtze Bureau directed the entire underground Party of southern China. Li Kenong was secretary-general of the Eighth Route Army to the outside world and secretary-general of the Yangtze Bureau within, and he set about restoring and rebuilding the underground organizations of the southern provinces. In about a year, all thirteen southern provinces had provincial committees or working committees. In August 1938, as the Japanese took Guangzhou and menaced Wuhan, the Nationalist government moved its capital to Chongqing. Li Kenong led the Wuhan office's people on foot to the Changsha office, through enemy bombing.

After every hardship, the Wuhan people reached the Changsha office. They had barely slept a peaceful night when the Nationalist garrison at Changsha, panic-stricken, staged its "scorched-earth resistance" and set fire to the city itself. As the whole city burned, Zhou Enlai, Ye Jianying, and Li Kenong directed the Changsha office's people in hauling out the important supplies and fighting their way clear of the flames.

Then they split in two: Zhou Enlai took a party to Chongqing, Li Kenong to Guilin. And so there came to be a Chongqing office and a Guilin office as well.

A new period called for a new line, and the Party's great task now was to build a national united front against Japan. This "united-front work" needed a special kind of talent, people who could deal with the Nationalist upper crust; battle-hardened Red Army generals alone were not enough.

The CCP's "Four Elders" came out of Yan'an.

Xu Teli, Mao Zedong's old teacher, went to Changsha: a Hunan educator returning home, well placed to help the office's work.

Xie Juezai went to Lanzhou, where the acting governor of Gansu, He Yaozu, was an old acquaintance. The two saw much of each other, and argued from time to time. He Yaozu's wife, Ni Feijun, was of a progressive turn of mind and always took Xie Juezai's side. In this way Xie and He Yaozu came gradually to a working relationship of a kind.

The chairman of the Border Region government himself took the lesser post of head of the Xi'an office. This was because the head of the Xi'an field headquarters, Cheng Qian, was an old comrade from the Northern Expedition: back then Cheng Qian had been an army commander and Lin Boqu his Party representative.

Dong Biwu, a founding elder of the Party, went to the Chongqing office. Chongqing was the wartime provisional capital, so this was, in effect, the mission to the capital.

The Chinese set store by protocol, and rank must be matched, in public and in private alike. All four elders were veterans of the 1911 Revolution, senior even in the eyes of the Nationalist top brass.

The Communist leader Zhou Enlai personally headed the capital office, and the man he set out to deal with was the Nationalists' own supreme leader, Chiang Kai-shek. Cast the mind back to the Whampoa Academy: Chiang Kai-shek its commandant, Zhou Enlai head of its political department, the two of them once side by side on the Eastern Expedition. Chiang leaned on Whampoa men to run his army, and those Whampoa generals had to snap to attention and salute when they saw Zhou Enlai.

Li Jishen, head of the Guilin field headquarters, was firmly for resistance and on good terms with the Communists, so the Guilin office had even more room to move than the one in Chongqing.

In Guilin, Li Kenong made wide contact with the cultural world and mounted cultural activities for resistance and national salvation. A few especially secret Communists he handled on a single line himself, keeping them out of any lateral contact with the local Party organization. Hu Yuzhi and Fan Changjiang of the International News Agency; Zhang Yunqiao, dean of studies at the Guangxi local-construction cadre school; Hou Dian of the political department of the Guangxi Pacification Office; Zhou Kechuan of the Guangxi branch of the Youth League; Zuo Hongtao of the Fourth War Zone headquarters; and cultural figures such as Chen Hansheng, Jiang Junchen, and Meng Chao, all worked long under non-Communist cover, some without an open identity right up to the founding of the People's Republic.

The station Li Kenong built at Guilin became the Communists' central station in the south, again with an open set and a secret set. It kept contact with the CCP Central Committee in Yan'an; the Southern Bureau in Chongqing; the Eighth Route Army's front command; the New Fourth Army headquarters; the Eighth Route liaison offices at Shaoguan in Guangdong and at Guiyang; the Qiongya Column on Hainan Island; the CCP's Southern Working Committee; the Guangxi, Hunan, and Jiangxi provincial committees; and the underground organizations in Hong Kong, Southeast Asia, and the South Seas. Li Kenong also picked out reliable couriers to carry the Party's important documents in secret, and set up courier routes: Guilin-Wuzhou-Taishan-Macao-Hong Kong; Hong Kong-Shantou-Chao'an-Fengshun-Xingning-Shaoguan-Hengyang-Guilin; Guilin-Zhennanguan-Hanoi-Haiphong-Hong Kong. Every line ran through Guilin.

Guilin became a leg thrust out from Chongqing: openly in touch with patriotic Nationalists, secretly at work rebuilding the southern underground, and at the same time reaching all the way to distant Southeast Asia and the South Seas.

The Communist Party, having been through both alliance and rupture with the Nationalists, was no longer naive; legal standing or not, it kept its organizations in Nationalist territory underground. Xi'an, Nanjing, Wuhan, Changsha, Guilin, Chongqing, Lanzhou, every great war zone in the country now had an open Eighth Route establishment. By international custom, a diplomatic mission gathering and collating intelligence was doing open and legal work of its own proper kind. The Eighth Route offices were not merely rear-service depots forwarding grain and pay; they were the legs of the Central Social Affairs Department, reaching out across the country.

You change, and so will I. The Communists' intelligence work carried off the shift the new situation demanded, using the open to shield the secret and breaking through the agents' watch.

Chiang Kai-shek would not let the Communists grow strong, but he could produce no clever answer to them, and had to fall back on the resources of power and heavy-handed means.

On June 11, 1939, the Hebei Peace Preservation commander Zhang Yinwu attacked the Eighth Route Army's rear organs and killed over four hundred. On June 12, the special-service battalion of Yang Sen's army searched the New Fourth Army's Pingjiang communications office and murdered Major Tu Zhengkun and five others. On September 1, Cheng Ruhuai's unit besieged the rear organs of the New Fourth Army's Fourth Detachment in eastern Hubei and slaughtered over five hundred. On November 11, the magistrate of Queshan County, Xu Gongchao, led troops against the New Fourth Army's rear post at Zhugou and killed more than two hundred. Such vile means of wrecking the alliance drew condemnation from opinion at home and abroad.

In 1941 Chiang Kai-shek launched his second anti-Communist high tide, engineered the New Fourth Army Incident, declared the New Fourth Army's designation abolished, and at the same time ordered the Guilin office closed by force. The massacre in southern Anhui might be repeated at Guilin at any moment, and Li Jishen, head of the Guilin field headquarters, quietly warned Li Kenong to leave, even providing a travel pass. The Central Committee sent word that, since the Eighth Route offices across the country had lost both their point and their possibility of existence, they should be wound up quickly to preserve important cadres.

Li Kenong held to his work as the Central Committee directed while preparing to move. He sent Shen Guang to shift the Guilin central station to Hong Kong, keeping up the wireless link between the CCP Central Committee and the southern organizations. He carefully placed progressive cultural figures and warned those who might be persecuted to move ahead of trouble. Then Li Kenong led his secretary Long Qian and a dozen-odd rear-guard staff of the Guilin office toward Chongqing in one large truck and one small car. The Nationalist center had ordered the office's vehicles and people stopped and detained, and the checkpoints along the way bristled with danger. The quick-witted Li Kenong now produced Li Jishen's pass, now put on a major general's uniform to bluff his way through, and at the last gave a Juntong colonel a lift, until he reached the Chongqing office safely.

In March 1941 Li Kenong was ordered to bring part of the Guilin office back to Yan'an from Chongqing. Mao Zedong laid on a banquet for the whole Li family, with Zhu De to keep them company. "We thought you weren't coming back," Mao said warmly.

That same month Mao Zedong named Li Kenong deputy head of the Central Social Affairs Department. In September the Central Intelligence Department was founded, and Li was made its deputy head as well. Intelligence had always been his own line of work, and what he brought back to Yan'an this time was not only the Guilin office's people and supplies, but the underground organizations of thirteen southern provinces, and top-secret intelligence connections besides.

The united-front work of the Eighth Route offices created the experience for the Communists' open work abroad. After the victory over Japan, the Nationalists, the Communists, and the Americans set up a three-sided Executive Headquarters for military mediation, and Li Kenong was again a leading hand in it. After New China was founded, Li Kenong served concurrently as standing deputy foreign minister.

### 2. The Top-Secret "Chongqing Contact Chart"

Having driven the Eighth Route offices out with the New Fourth Army Incident, the Nationalists still did not dare break off relations entirely. Mindful of the alliance of the international anti-fascist front, they left the Communists one Eighth Route office in the provisional capital, Chongqing. And that gave Zhou Enlai room to move.

Xu Enzeng, head of the Nationalists' Zhongtong, drew up a "contact chart" in one of his own articles, a diagram of the Communists' upper-level united-front work in Chongqing. It showed three concentric rings, each radiating out from the center, each holding several small circles, with lines running between them.

"The one central figure in the Communists' upper-level united-front work in Chongqing was Zhou Enlai, and Zhou Enlai alone. He remained in Chongqing as the CCP's chief representative, a 'guest of honor' of the National Government." Xu Enzeng, it seems, understood Zhou Enlai's leading role in the Communists' united-front and intelligence work quite well.

Xu Enzeng described the several central figures around Zhou Enlai this way: "There were also several assistants not often in Chongqing, such as Qin Bangxian, Dong Biwu, Wang Ruofei, Deng Yingchao, Wu Yuzhang, and Ye Jianying; all of these were regarded as representative figures of the CCP and could keep contact with every quarter, openly and in their own right." These people not only held representative status the Nationalists recognized, but had long ties to the Nationalists, some of them longtime Nationalist Party members, so they naturally moved with ease. As Xu Enzeng said: "If we liken the Communists' united-front work to a spiritual atomic explosion, then this was the core of the blast."

Xu Enzeng went on to describe the radiating pattern of that Communist explosion: "The core organization centered on Zhou Enlai touched no wide circle, but its rays reached in all directions, striking every target that could then sway the political situation in Chongqing." "Through Feng Yuxiang and Shao Lizi to influence the Nationalist upper level"; "using Zhang Bojun and Luo Longji to influence the other parties"; "through Guo Moruo and Tian Han to draw in the cultural and educational world"; "using Liu Qingyang and Cao Mengjun, who had penetrated the 'Wartime Women's Work Guidance Committee' under Madame Chiang, to do women's work." "There were also Song Qingling and her two secretaries. One was Liao Mengxing, elder sister of Liao Chengzhi, an important cadre of the Communists' Chongqing office, and herself the daughter of the Nationalist martyr Liao Zhongkai; the other was Wang Anna, wife of Wang Bingnan, chief of protocol at the 18th Group Army office. The latter was a German Jew, fluent in six languages, English, French, Russian, German, Spanish, and Polish. In Chongqing, Song Qingling chaired the China branch of an international Comintern front organization, the 'World Anti-Aggression Movement Committee,' keeping contact on one hand with foreign correspondents and friendly foreigners in Chongqing, and on the other with the Nationalist upper level; her two women secretaries worked solely on liaison with foreign correspondents and friendly foreigners in the city." "One could see in particular that the Communists ran an unusual number of lines to the American organs based in Chongqing."

Xu Enzeng marveled at how thorough the Communists' deployment was: "Arranged like this, Zhou Enlai could lie snug on the great sofa at No. 50 Zengjiayan, and without stepping out the gate could raise wind and waves and rob you of sleep and appetite."

The contact chart Xu Enzeng drew was not complete, but it caught the essentials. Only, Zhou Enlai did not lie sleeping on the sofa just because the deployment was thorough; though the agents watched the Chongqing office at Zengjiayan closely, he still went out often.

The China Democratic League was the third great party of Chinese politics, after the Nationalists and Communists. The middle-of-the-road figures who founded it had consulted Zhou Enlai many times before it took shape, and when the League held its first congress, Zhou even came forward to help smooth over its internal frictions. Zhou Enlai kept up frequent contact with Nationalist democrats like Song Qingling, He Xiangning, and Feng Yuxiang, and passed word to Li Jishen, hoping Li and He Xiangning would form political groups and press for democracy. Later Li Jishen, He Xiangning, and others founded the Revolutionary Committee of the Nationalist Party, with Li Jishen as chairman.

Zhou Enlai also cultivated the local power-holders of Sichuan. Without an escort, slipping out of Zengjiayan in the dead of night, he met Liu Wenhui at a secret spot. Zhu De, for his part, gave the Yunnan governor Long Yun a cipher book and set up a wireless link.

Xu Enzeng's memoir has a good deal of self-knowledge: "These are the radiating lines we could see; there were others we could not see, that in fact must certainly have existed."

That, at least, was true. In the Chongqing office Zhou Enlai not only ran open united-front work but directed the secret underground as well. His identity in negotiating with the Nationalists was the CCP's chief representative; within the Party he had another, secret one: secretary of the CCP Southern Bureau.

The Communists' organization in the base areas was called the Soviet-area Party; its organization in Nationalist territory, the White-area Party. Ten years of white terror, plus the Central Committee's "Left" errors, had cost the White-area Party ninety-five percent of its strength, leaving intact only the provincial organizations of Hunan and Wang Shiying's intelligence system in the North.

When the CCP Central Committee and the Red Army reached northern Shaanxi and the Eighth Route Army built base areas in the North, the Party organizations there sprang up like bamboo after rain. But the south was still Nationalist territory, and the Communists were still under the boot there.

The secret mission of the CCP Southern Bureau was this: to restore and rebuild the Communist organizations in Nationalist territory.

Rich in the experience of secret work, Zhou Enlai laid down a set of concrete rules for it under the new conditions: open work and secret work strictly separate, upper-level work and lower-level work strictly separate. Cadres of the Eighth Route office, who had open identities, were as a rule to have no contact with underground members. Members engaged in secret work were as a rule not to come to the office at all. Underground members were further required to practice the "three diligences" and the "three normalizations": "diligent at a trade, diligent at study, diligent at making friends," and "socialized, professionalized, legalized."

This was a major change in the method of secret work. In the past the Communists' secret members had mostly been "professional revolutionaries" who often lacked any cover identity. The Nationalist agents had a rule of thumb for hunting the underground: a garret room, a single bed, a washstand on a stand, seize the man first and ask questions later.

Once the Communists went "socialized," the Nationalist agents got a headache; the Communists sank into the ocean of society and could not be told apart. Underground members who had lost contact could now read of Communist activity in the papers and came, one after another, to the Party's open base at Hongyan; in the southwest region alone, over five thousand restored their organizational ties. The Party secretary for eastern Sichuan, Liao Zhigao, landed a post in the Chongqing tax office and covered his underground work for five years, without the Nationalist agents ever getting his name straight.

### 3. "Idle Chessmen" and "Strategic Spies"

The Chinese have a point of traditional honor: you do not dig away at a friend's foundations. In the second Nationalist-Communist alliance, both sides were more mature, and in the talks they reached a tacit understanding: neither would develop organization inside the other.

Brothers who survive share a smile that wipes out old wrongs. At the start of the cooperation, both sides showed the greatest good faith. The Eighth Route Army gladly accepted the liaison staff officers the Nationalists sent, one per division, even though all three carried ciphers and had intelligence tasks.

Zhou Enlai had been appointed lieutenant general and deputy head of the political department of the National Revolutionary Army, and as deputy head he hired the secret Party member Guo Moruo to run the Third Bureau, sending out many performance troupes led by Communists to entertain and propagandize among the Nationalist forces.

Many Nationalist units welcomed Communists to do political work as well; the war against Japan needed the soldiers and the people roused, and rousing them was the Communists' specialty.

Zhou Enlai saw sharply: "This is a chance in a thousand years."

Party organizations everywhere hurried to move members in; any who could enter the Nationalist ranks, entered. The Shanxi army under Fu Zuoyi and the Yunnan army under Zhang Chong both took batches of Communists into their political-training offices. Unlike the Central Army, the provincial units had little regard for Chiang Kai-shek's taboos and eagerly brought in Communist political work, so that most of the field-service teams in these units were Communists and young progressives.

Zhou Enlai had a deeper design in mind as well.

Though the Nationalists were now cooperating, Chiang was a cruel and ruthless man who might turn on the Communists again at any time. It is wrong to mean harm to others, but folly to be unguarded against it. Zhou Enlai planned to bury a few secret members deep in the Nationalist camp, who would support the Nationalist war effort in ordinary times and do no Communist organizing, but the moment a Nationalist commander went over to Japan or turned on the Communists, would spring into action as intelligence.

Knowing the Nationalist army well, Zhou Enlai had his eye on the three great troop concentrations, under Hu Zongnan, Fu Zuoyi, and Bai Chongxi; the run-of-the-mill, second-rate generals he had no use for at all. To these three concentrations he sent "three great secretaries," the trusted private secretaries of the three Nationalist commanders, all of them secret Communists.

Hu Zongnan, a graduate of Whampoa's first class, was the ablest general under Chiang Kai-shek, commanding the crack "First Army under Heaven." Zhou Enlai, who had once run the political department at Whampoa, judged the man a patriot at heart, inclined to resistance, and worth winning over. Hearing that Hu Zongnan meant to gather young intellectuals through his front-line youth service teams, Zhou designed his move with care and chose one man for it.

Xiong Xianghui had secretly joined the Communist Party at Tsinghua University in December 1936, and headed the Tsinghua detachment of the open resistance body the "Chinese National Liberation Vanguard"; his father was a presiding judge of the Wuhan High Court, exactly fitting the profile Zhou Enlai had set. Xiong Xianghui had meant to go to Yan'an to study, but had to obey the organization's arrangement and join the Hunan Youth Field Service Team instead.

A young student, Xiong Xianghui, turned overnight into a man carrying out a "special task"? Dong Biwu talked with him himself: do not be in a hurry to find the Party; be content to serve as an idle chessman; hide your identity, develop no members, push Hu Zongnan toward resistance when you can, and if Hu turns anti-Communist, keep up appearances of agreement with him, white skin over a red heart. Adapt to your surroundings, join the current without being fouled by it; you may hold yourself a little above others, but never in your dealings. Zhou Enlai and Dong Biwu together gave him a maxim to keep by him: "You cannot get the tiger's cubs without entering the tiger's lair."

Dong Biwu's few words were Xiong Xianghui's whole intelligence course. Xiong worked hard to read Hu Zongnan's mind and to make friends with care. Among the fossils of the Nationalist camp, Hu Zongnan counted as a general of some insight, out to net young talent who understood both military and political affairs to help him realize his political ambitions. Xiong Xianghui knew Sun Yat-sen's revolutionary thought and understood Communist theory as well, exactly to Hu Zongnan's use, and the two even studied dialectical materialism together in secret. Hu Zongnan first sent Xiong Xianghui to the military academy to study and join the Whampoa direct line, then brought him to his own side as aide-de-camp and confidential secretary. And so the Communist Xiong Xianghui became a trusted intimate of the Nationalist commander Hu Zongnan, charged with the confidential files and entrusted with drafting operational plans.

It is rare to plant a man so deep. The Central Intelligence Department built a three-man intelligence cell around Xiong Xianghui, led by Chen Zhongjing and joined by Shen Jian. Through the war against Japan and the Third Revolutionary Civil War the cell made outstanding intelligence contributions and effectively guarded the safety of the Central Committee; the Communist intelligence world honored it as the "Latter Three Heroes," a match in glory for the "Three Heroes of Longtan" of the Central Special Branch.

This was a supremely successful case of high-level intelligence work. Zhou Enlai's choice of man and design, Dong Biwu's strategy for lying low, and the methods of Xiong Xianghui and the others could all serve as a model course in intelligence.

The Nationalist Guangxi-clique general Bai Chongxi was a fine hand at war, admired in the army as "Little Zhuge." Chiang Kai-shek had always squeezed the Guangxi clique out, but when the war called for able men he had to invite Bai Chongxi to serve as deputy chief of the general staff. Zhou Enlai planted two men at Bai Chongxi's side: the lieutenant colonel and secretary Xie Hegeng, and the senior adviser Liu Zhongrong.

Xie Hegeng had joined the Party in Beiping in the early thirties and been sent by the CCP northern bureau back to his native Guangxi to work; in the war he served as Bai Chongxi's confidential secretary. In May 1938, Xie Hegeng was ordered to draft a speech for Bai Chongxi to give at a training corps for officers of the rank of division commander and above. Xie buried himself in the writing for three days and three nights, produced a draft of more than fourteen thousand characters, and sent it to Li Kenong for review. The manuscript that came back to Xie Hegeng had over two thousand characters struck out and a great deal added. It turned out that Zhou Enlai had revised Xie Hegeng's draft himself, taking out the passages that sounded like Communist political-work language and adding guerrilla tactics. Bai Chongxi thought highly of the speech and trusted Xie Hegeng all the more from then on.

Liu Zhongrong had studied in his youth at Moscow's Sun Yat-sen University and was on good terms with students of both parties. His father, Liu Chenglie, had once been the Guangxi clique's representative in Tianjin, and the sociable Liu Zhongrong was valued by the clique too. Li Zongren sent Liu Zhongrong as his representative to Xi'an and Yan'an to make contact with the Communists, the Northeastern Army, and the Northwestern Army. Following Zhou Enlai's arrangement, Liu Zhongrong moved from Li Zongren's side to Bai Chongxi's, serving as an adviser at the Guilin field headquarters. Later Liu Zhongrong won the Guangxi clique's high trust throughout, and at the critical juncture of the Third Revolutionary Civil War was sent by the acting president of the National Government, Li Zongren, as secret representative to the liberated areas to negotiate with the Communists.

Xie Hegeng was sent in, Liu Zhongrong was drawn out; the pairing of this intelligence cell was a shrewd one.

Beneath these idle chessmen there were also the Communists' local intelligence sections.

When the war broke out, a young Shanxi progressive, Yan Youwen, joined the Nationalist Shanxi army under Fu Zuoyi. A fellow townsman of Fu Zuoyi from Wanquan in Shanxi, he quickly won trust. At this time Fu Zuoyi was inviting open Communists to do political work in his forces, and the CCP Shanxi provincial committee's special agent Pan Jiwen secretly enrolled Yan Youwen in the Party in 1938. In 1940, when Fu Zuoyi's forces expelled the Communists and Pan Jiwen was forced to leave, he arranged before going for the unexposed underground members Yan Youwen and Yang Ziming to stay lying low. Yan Youwen, deeply trusted at Fu Zuoyi's side, rose to be a major general, chief of the news office, and head of the *Struggle Daily*. On his superiors' instructions, Yan Youwen had penetrated Fu Zuoyi's side to stay long out of contact with the organization. The whole war against Japan passed and this high-level inside man was never activated. Only on the eve of the Third Revolutionary Civil War did the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office re-establish contact with Yan Youwen, and later pass this important connection to the Jin-Sui military region, and then to the Central Intelligence Department. And so it happened that Fu Zuoyi's scathing denunciations of Mao Zedong first passed through Zhou Enlai's hands for review and revision, and that Fu Zuoyi's negotiator with the Communists was himself a secret Communist.

This case, too, was a success of the Communists' secret intelligence work. That a spy could rise to high position in the enemy camp on his own ability was full proof of the high quality of the Communists' intelligence men.

A spy planted deep often goes through a long dormancy and does not necessarily act at once. Some therefore called such an inside man a "cold-storage spy." The impatient sort could not bear the cold storage and ended up exposing himself. But the master was another matter: a master of *weiqi* will sometimes lay down an idle stone, a lone piece that looks useless at first, until the right moment comes and it can turn the whole board at a stroke.

Zhou Enlai, too, was a master at laying down idle chessmen, and the good stones he set in the Nationalist camp were more than these three.

Shen Anna, a native of Taixing in Jiangsu, came to Shanghai to study in 1932, met a secret Communist among her fellow students, Hua Mingzhi, and eagerly sought to join in revolutionary work. Wang Xuewen of the Central Special Branch directed Shen Anna to slip, when the chance came, into a Nationalist office. On the strength of her fine brush hand, Shen Anna passed the examination for a stenographer's post in the Zhejiang provincial government in early 1935, gradually won the trust of the governor Zhu Jiahua, and took the opportunity to steal intelligence. Wang Xuewen trained her one on one, passing on the professional knowledge of intelligence, and directed Shen Anna and Hua Mingzhi to form a husband-and-wife team and lie low for the long term.

When the war broke out, Shen Anna made her way to Wuhan and came to the Eighth Route office for tasks. Zhou Enlai and Dong Biwu deployed her themselves, telling Shen Anna to seize the present favorable moment and drive in deep. Just then Zhu Jiahua rose to secretary-general of the Nationalist Central Party headquarters and, wanting to grow a following of his own, gladly took Shen Anna on and placed her as a confidential stenographer in the Central headquarters' cipher office. When the National Government moved its capital to Chongqing, Hua Mingzhi and Shen Anna followed.

In 1939 the Nationalists convened their Fifth Plenum, which resolved on "dissolving, restricting, and preventing the Communists." From memory, Shen Anna reported the resolution's contents to Zhou Enlai, and Deng Yingchao embraced her happily and told her to keep up the good work.

Meeting the highest leaders of both parties at once, Shen Anna felt the contrast too sharply and asked to leave Chongqing for Yan'an. Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao talked with her privately: "This work of yours is extremely important, no one can take your place, this is your post." Shen Anna said she accepted the task, and Zhou Enlai gave her further specific guidance: keep calm and unafraid in danger, find ways to protect herself and deal with the enemy. Deng Yingchao encouraged Shen Anna to be a willing nameless hero; she said she was no hero, but was willing to be a nameless soldier. That twenty-minute talk Shen Anna remembered all her life.

On the special approval of the Southern Bureau member Bogu, without an application and without a branch discussion, Shen Anna was secretly admitted to the Party. When her first daughter was born, a courier brought baby clothes, charged to the Southern Bureau's accounts.

Shen Anna lay low for the long term, taking down the records of the Nationalists' confidential meetings in shorthand and, once home, copying and hiding them in secret cipher, to be passed to Hongyan through the secret courier line.

In 1942, the secret courier Xu Zhonghang was arrested, and Shen Anna's letters were seized by the agents. Through the night the couple sorted their rooms and burned the hidden intelligence. Deep in the night they clasped hands without a word, close enough to hear each other's heartbeat. They made a pact: if either were arrested, they would say nothing though beaten to death; whichever got out would care for the children.

With the readiness to die for the Party, Shen Anna grew calm instead, and met her superiors' scrutiny with skill. The arrested Xu Zhonghang had not given her up. Zhou Enlai arranged personally, through Li Jishen, to have Xu Zhonghang released on bail.

Hua Mingzhi and Shen Anna's home stood with the Nationalist elder statesman Wu Zhihui on one side and the military police on the other. The couple lived day after day in a climate of terror, weeping as they gazed toward Hongyan. By the rules of secret work, in a case like this they could only wait quietly and not go seeking the organization.

Losing contact but holding her post still, Shen Anna went on writing intelligence in secret cipher and hiding it at home. To keep her post, Shen Anna contracted a lung ailment and coughed blood in the lavatory, so that the grandees, afraid of infection, would dismiss the little stenographer. To wait for the organization to come to her, Shen Anna dared not move house; by day she heard the screams from the military police interrogations next door, and at night lay long awake, listening for the secret knock at the door.

A tiny seed, growing in silence, seeking a crevice among tangled roots and driving down deep. When the war was won and civil war reopened, this little clerk at last played an important part.

An idle stone is not idle, a cold piece not cold; an idle chessman can sometimes turn a whole game around. This practice of long dormancy and activation at the crucial hour marked the Communists' intelligence work as already having a long strategic vision. Such a spy, planted at the enemy's high core to lasting effect, is what the international spy world calls a "strategic spy."

In intelligence work, watching from outside is not as good as penetration from within, and the deeper the penetration the better; you cannot get the tiger's cubs without entering the tiger's lair.

To go deep into the tiger's lair, one must first put on a tiger's skin. An inside agent must have a fitting open identity for cover.

The secret and the open could not only join seamlessly but grow together for the long term, in the service of far-reaching strategic tasks. This strategic vision, these strategic spies, showed that the Communists' intelligence work was ripening toward maturity.

### 4. The "Companies" That Made Money for the Party

Even the most brilliant swordsman must eat and spend. Zhou Enlai, with his crowd of friends, ran into the problem of money too.

When the Japanese seized the Northeast, its people, their homes lost, became the largest body of refugees in China, with no living to fall back on. The Northeastern Army elder Gao Chongmin launched the Northeast Salvation Association to relieve his fellow provincials and unite them for resistance. Gao Chongmin had resolved as early as 1935 to accept Communist leadership; yet in building his organization he did not go begging to the Communists, but went to the Nationalists alone for money. Chiang, who had detained the Northeast's Young Marshal Zhang Xueliang, owed the Northeast a debt.

Juntong's director Dai Li put up fifty thousand silver dollars, never knowing that the Salvation Association's vice-chairman Yan Baohang and its secretary-general Yu Yifu were both secret Communists.

Public funds went to public use; Gao Chongmin's own life was hard-pressed. When Zhou Enlai sent someone with a subsidy, Gao Chongmin would not take it. He knew the Communists' money came hard, that even Zhou Enlai himself lived frugally.

But Zhou Enlai insisted Gao Chongmin accept it. Democrats were not like Communists; a Communist could wage war off war, but a democrat had a family to keep and had to move in society's upper circles, and without money it simply would not do.

Whether to help a friend or to keep the Party's work running, money could not be avoided. And where was it to come from?

When the Nationalists attacked the Communists, they always jeered that the CCP "lived off rubles."

In truth, in its early days the CCP did take large subsidies from the Comintern. Its first general secretary, Chen Duxiu, was even ransomed out of prison with the Comintern's money. The "professional revolutionaries" of that time had no social occupation and could live only on the Party's stipends. International aid was limited and Party funds terribly tight; when the stipends from the Shun-Zhi provincial committee were cut off, Peng Zhen sold his wife's dowry ring to carry on, while his superior Wang Zaowen grumbled and dragged his feet.

Making revolution still called for self-reliance, and the CCP raised what funds it could of its own to lighten the Comintern's burden. The Military Commission's Special Work Section, set up in 1927, had as one of its main tasks the raising of money. Li Qiang used a small skiff to ferry across the river and speculate in currency, earning the Party a sum. When Mao Zedong founded the red regime in Jiangxi and took Zhangzhou, he seized 1,050,000 silver dollars and used them to open a central bank. The Red Army turned the goods it captured in the Jiangxi fighting into gold and sent it to Shanghai to support the Central Committee. In Shanghai, Chen Yun, who was building "auxiliary organizations," used a few thousand yuan to open twenty-odd little shops, and not only fulfilled his task of secret contact points but earned money for the Party besides.

Revolution needed an economy too; a Party that preached common ownership still had to make money. After the founding of the Central Soviet, Mao Zedong worked by every means to develop foreign trade, smuggling the Soviet area's tung oil and tungsten ore into Nationalist territory for cash. Foreign trade not only earned money but built united-front ties: the Guangdong militarist Chen Jitang was the Soviet area's trading partner and let the Red Army slip quietly through during the Long March breakout. On the Long March, how was a great column of tens of thousands to be supplied? The Red Army set up a supply department that confiscated bureaucratic wealth and struck at the local tyrants along the way. Mao Zemin, the central bank president, had prepared in advance: the state bank's vaults went along on the Long March, gold and silver dollars parceled out to the army corps to carry, and Mao Zemin ran a "carrying-pole bank" besides, with over a hundred shoulder-loads bearing an enormous sum of gold, silver, and paper money.

When the Red Army took the city of Zunyi, Mao Zemin did something that beggared belief: he issued currency.

Every cadre and soldier of the Red Army drew a stipend in the form of this money, and could go to market with cash in hand to buy the medicines and clothing they needed, and even treat themselves at a restaurant.

What were the merchants and common folk to do with this Soviet money? Mao Zemin set up several dozen exchange points and redeemed the currency with salt and silver dollars the Red Army had captured.

Money needs to circulate, and this circulation enlivened the Red Army's marching life and the economy of the places it stayed, and left the Communists and the Red Army an excellent name. After New China was founded, people in various places still brought Red Army money to the new government to redeem; these hoarders had believed all along that the Communists would one day rule the land.

Running an economy comes down to opening sources and cutting outflows, and the Communists, kept from power, had few chances to open sources. So the whole Party, high and low, formed the habit of cutting outflows and lived with extreme thrift.

Red Army officers and men were equals; from commander-in-chief Zhu De down to the ordinary soldier, the monthly stipend was five yuan flat. There was also a law of severe punishment for graft: embezzle five yuan and be shot. On the Long March, in the Tibetan region, the troops foraged for grain everywhere, and a young Red Army soldier entered a lamasery and took some food. Breach of discipline, shot. The men who carried out the sentence gave no thought to the fact that the condemned was the younger brother of the chairman's wife.

Life in the White areas had even fewer sources, and many Communists began their fundraising with themselves, ruining their own families for the cause. When Peng Pai launched the peasant uprising in Haifeng and Lufeng, Guangdong, his first act was to confiscate the whole estate of his own landlord family. During the war, when Lu Dingyi's father died and the family divided the property, Lu Dingyi handed the silver dollars that fell to him over as Party dues.

The Communists' money came hard, and those who handled it were unfailingly careful. The Southern Bureau's special accountant Xiong Jinding had a peculiar purse, a long cloth bag wound round his left wrist, its mouth always gripped in his hand. When Xiong Jinding's daughter fell suddenly ill with a high fever and needed an injection, a dose of penicillin cost ten yuan, and Xiong Jinding had no spare money of his own. He held a great sum of public funds in his hands, but he would not take out ten yuan of it. The delay cost the girl her life. Later, when another of Xiong Jinding's daughters fell ill, Mao Zedong wired from Yan'an to Chongqing and the organization released money for her treatment.

Frugal as the Communists were, what had to be spent was still spent. The Red Army gave technical cadres special preferential treatment: an artist who joined the army in the Jin-Sui region from Shanghai drew a stipend of seven and a half yuan, two and a half more than commander He Long. Zhou Enlai, in Chongqing, did all he could to help non-Party friends in hardship. On July 25, 1946, Zhou Enlai, then presiding over the Nationalist-Communist peace talks in Shanghai, hurried with his wife Deng Yingchao to the hospital, but his old friend Tao Xingzhi had just died; "his hand was still warm." The eminent educator Tao Xingzhi had spent his life founding schools to help poor children, but the cause of his own death was hardship and want of nourishment.

Zhou Enlai wired the Central Committee at once: "For the safety and health of our progressive friends we must take responsibility. I have told Pan Hannian and Wu Yunfu in Shanghai to give more economic and material help in relief, and to look after them politically at all times."

But where was the "economy and materials" to help these progressive friends to come from?

During the war the Communists' finances were desperately tight; the Nationalist center not only stopped their army pay but blockaded cotton, cloth, medicine, and other supplies. Mao Zedong had no choice but to call on the Border Region's soldiers and people to launch the Great Production Movement and provide for themselves.

Production takes a cycle, and supply could not stop for a single day, so Mao Zedong brought in the Special Branch veteran Nan Hanchen as finance chief. Nan Hanchen's first move was to intercept the Nationalist 22nd Army's pack train smuggling opium; he also had Zhang Daowu open up the salt ponds at Dingbian, and soon amassed a first pot of gold.

The base areas, knowing the Central Committee's difficulties, helped all they could. The Shandong base area had a gold mine at Zhaoyuan, and sent the gold the long distance to Yan'an.

The most and quickest money, though, came from running a company, and Zhou Enlai had a company right at hand that made money for the Party.

In great Shanghai, the richest were the Ningbo men. A Ningbo youth, Lu Xuzhang, was an apprentice at a shipping company and studied at the chamber of commerce's night school in his off-hours. In 1932 five classmates pooled five hundred silver dollars and went into the mail-order Western-medicine business, a company that competed with the foreign trading houses, called Guangda Huaxing. Guangda Huaxing was good at business and grew fast; in three years it had reached scale.

In 1936, when Shanghai formed a National Salvation Association of all circles to propagandize resistance, Guangda Huaxing took an active part, and even organized the Chinese employees inside the foreign firms into a "China Association." In October 1937 Lu Xuzhang secretly joined the Communist Party. In 1939, on the organization's instructions, he shifted Guangda Huaxing's business to the great rear in the southwest. Zhou Enlai received Lu Xuzhang at Hongyan and set the company a third-line secret task: keep no lateral contact with the local Party organization, do good business, and make friends widely.

Lu Xuzhang was very good at making friends. He worked capital jointly with his fellow Ningbo townsman Bao Yugang, obtained a major general's rank from Chiang Kai-shek's personal staff office, brought in the Zhongtong chief Chen Guofu as a shareholder, and became friends with the Juntong major general Liang Ruobing. Lu Xuzhang's reach ran to heaven and earth; he could get commercial secrets no one else knew, buying in heavily before sugar prices rose and making a killing at once. No merchant could do business with America and the Soviet Union at the same time as Guangda Huaxing did; its trade spread across Yunnan, Guizhou, and Sichuan, and reached out to Shanghai, Tianjin, Hong Kong, Taiwan, New York, and Tokyo. Its business grew from selling medicine to making it, and it invested in banks and founded an insurance company jointly with Lu Zuofu. By the end of 1944 it had become a great business group with assets of three hundred thousand American dollars.

The Southern Bureau's intelligence chief Liu Shaowen was full of ideas, and arranged for Guangda Huaxing to make money for the Party while working for it. First, use the commercial channels for secret courier work; second, make friends widely to gather intelligence on the Nationalist upper level; third, supply the Party with funds. Guangda Huaxing became the Party's cash dispenser; the Southern Bureau's accountant Yuan Chaojun and others came to Lu Xuzhang from time to time to draw money, and the organization got as much as it asked for, no receipts, no books.

Liu Shaowen proposed doing intelligence work in a commercial way. Making money for the Party, gathering intelligence for the Party, Guangda Huaxing joined the two tasks seamlessly, and by the time of the civil war the company had grown to its height. Among the Communists' "Party enterprises" were also the United Company, founded in 1938, with Qian Zhiguang as chairman and Yang Lin as general manager, and the Xinzhong Trading Company, founded by Wu Xuezhi and Mai Wenlan.

In June 1949 the three companies merged, and the largest Party enterprise of all, China Resources, was born, its main capital the one-million-odd American dollars from Guangda Huaxing. This China Resources later became New China's largest foreign-trade company, and in the era of reform and opening a listed conglomerate.

Men like Lu Xuzhang could be called China's great "capitalists." Yet these Communists hidden in the business world never forgot Zhou Enlai's charge: be the lotus of the eighth month, rising from the mud unstained, joining the current but not fouled by it. When Guangda Huaxing wound up its business, the shares and dividends of the non-Party staff were all returned to them, while the Communists Lu Xuzhang, Yang Yanxiu, Zhang Ping, Shu Ziqing, and others asked to hand over all their own staff dividends and shares as Party dues.

Money is not a man's highest pursuit.

Chiang Kai-shek summoned the Northwestern Army general Han Liancheng and rewarded him with fifty thousand silver dollars. Open-handed as the Generalissimo was, holding a great fund of special money, he graded his subordinates in his courtship of them, and a corps-level officer like Han Liancheng rated a top figure of fifty thousand. Han Liancheng took the old man's money, but he did not truly serve him; he came quietly to Hongyan village and asked Zhou Enlai to let him join the Communist Party. In China, any high official turned militarist had ways of getting money; what Han Liancheng had set his heart on was not money but a future, his own and the nation's.

Chiang Kai-shek's flowers added to brocade could not match Zhou Enlai's charcoal sent in the snow.

### 5. Distant "Overseas Work"

China's war against Japan was known internationally as the World Anti-Fascist War. Moving from the domestic to the international, the Communists began to step onto the great stage of the world.

To remote Yan'an, too, came distant guests: the South Seas overseas-Chinese leader Tan Kah Kee insisted on visiting Yan'an to see for himself what the Communist leaders were like. Receiving visitors from abroad called for an organ, so Yan'an set up the Overseas Chinese Salvation Federation, and the CCP Central Committee established a new "Overseas Work Committee" as well. Foreign affairs needed high rank, so Eighth Route commander-in-chief Zhu De was asked to serve as its head and chief of staff Ye Jianying as deputy. In fact these two led the overseas work not on their exalted posts alone, but on unusual experiences of their own: both were graduates of the Yunnan Military Academy.

That academy had over five hundred overseas-Chinese students, and Ye Jianying, a son of the overseas-Chinese country of Meixian in Guangdong, was himself a Malayan overseas Chinese. Some cadets, too, kept their identities secret, being foreigners: Korea's Choe Yong-gon and Yi Beom-seok, Vietnam's Vo Hai Thu, all later national leaders.

"Overseas work" was no Communist invention; the Nationalist center had always had an "Overseas Department," with semi-open Nationalist overseas branches in the countries of the South Seas, working among the local Chinese.

During the war, China's overseas Chinese numbered some seven or eight million. Many of China's coastal regions had a long tradition of emigrating overseas for a living, and the Qing suppression of the Taiping Rebellion had driven still more Chinese to take refuge abroad. Overseas Chinese often met with discrimination and anti-Chinese abuse, but they never forgot their old roots in the homeland and would send home what little money they earned.

The overseas Chinese are the mother of the revolution: Sun Yat-sen, who launched the 1911 Revolution, was an overseas Chinese, and the backers of his armed risings were overseas Chinese. The overseas Chinese are the father of patriotism: overseas Chinese the world over were resolute against the Japanese invasion. The overseas Chinese of each country vied to give: from Europe, the Americas, and the South Seas, the three great regions formed many bodies, donated 1.3 billion in funds, bought 1.1 billion in war bonds, and gave several hundred aircraft in planes alone. Over forty thousand Cantonese overseas Chinese came home to fight; the South Seas overseas Chinese formed a mechanics' corps, 3,200 men who drove trucks across the perilous Yunnan-Burma Road; and three-quarters of China's fighter pilots were overseas Chinese.

Out of the need to resist Japan, the Communists too began overseas work.

The overseas-Chinese cadres of the Eighth Route Army's Hong Kong office, Liao Chengzhi and Lian Guan, were in charge of liaison with overseas Chinese abroad. The Central Committee sent the Special Branch's Hu Yuzhi and others to Singapore to run resistance propaganda, and Xu Li, who had joined the Youth League in the Philippines in 1926, launched a resistance body there.

Across the South Seas, many seeds of Communism lay scattered. When the Nationalists split with the Communists in 1927, many Communists fled the slaughter to the South Seas, and though they lost their organizational ties, they spread progressive ideas of their own accord, some even joining the Communist organizations of their host countries. A Communist from Qionghai in Guangdong, having failed in the Bru uprising, took refuge in Singapore, joined the strike activities of the Malayan Communist organization, and was arrested by the colonial authorities and deported home.

The countries of the South Seas were mostly colonies of the Western powers, and the Japanese invasion meant a change of masters, so that even the British, American, French, and Dutch colonial authorities had to support resistance. And so the two currents of progressivism and resistance flowed together into a broad united front.

Many overseas Chinese came home to fight; the East River Column, the Qiongya Column, the New Fourth Army, and other southern resistance forces drew many overseas-Chinese youths into the ranks. The Filipino overseas Chinese Ye Fei was already a Red Army general; the Australian overseas Chinese Zeng Sheng and the Singapore overseas Chinese Zhuang Tian became famous fighting commanders; and the overseas-Chinese woman Li Lin rode into battle on horseback and died bravely at the Shanxi front.

Overseas Chinese were as a rule of higher education, and many became the cultural backbone of resistance propaganda. The two chief creators of the *Yellow River Cantata* were both overseas Chinese: the lyricist Guang Weiran came from Burma, and the composer Xian Xinghai was a Malayan overseas Chinese.

The woman journalist Huang Wei came home to report on the resistance, lived through the breakout at Xuzhou, and traveled from Yan'an behind Japanese lines in the North, several thousand li in three months, interviewing Eighth Route commanders like He Long and Nie Rongzhen, and even watched the Canadian doctor Bethune operate on the wounded. On the organization's instructions she then went to Chongqing to promote the resistance behind enemy lines, winning the praise of the Nationalist elders Lin Sen and Feng Yuxiang. Under persecution by the Nationalist agents, Huang Wei returned to the Philippines and, during the Japanese occupation, founded an underground resistance paper, the *Overseas Chinese Herald*.

The Filipino overseas Chinese Zhuang Yan joined the revolution abroad in 1933, and in 1938, introduced by Liao Chengzhi in Hong Kong, walked all the way from Wuhan to Yan'an. When Tan Kah Kee visited Yan'an, Zhuang Yan served as interpreter for Chairman Mao and commander-in-chief Zhu, and when the Central Overseas Work Committee was founded he was made its secretary.

Resistance at home was Nationalist-Communist cooperation; resistance in the South Seas was Nationalist-Communist cooperation too.

The Filipino overseas Chinese Wang Yuting gave up home and livelihood and brought his elder son Wang Weizhen home to fight, leaving his elder daughter Wang Shuangyou and Wang Ming'ai in the Philippines to do underground work. Wang Weizhen joined the Communist Party in Yan'an and became a writer for the New China News Agency, while his sister and brother joined the Nationalist Juntong's underground resistance body in the Philippines.

The Juntong southern-Fujian station chief Zhang Shengcai, ordered by Dai Li to the Philippines, passed through Hong Kong on the way, found the Eighth Route Army's Lian Guan, and offered on his own to help the Communists with intelligence. The Nationalists' overseas organization in the Philippines had operated openly for years and lacked underground experience, and the moment the Japanese entered the South Seas they killed nine of its consuls. So the Communists, skilled at underground work, sent young progressives to help Zhang Shengcai, and the Wang siblings became the radio operators and couriers of a resistance station.

There was international cooperation as well.

In Malaya, the Communist-led, mainly overseas-Chinese People's Anti-Japanese Army; the Nationalist-organized Overseas Chinese Anti-Japanese Army; and Force 136, formed jointly by the Nationalist center's Overseas Department and the British economic-warfare arm, these three overseas-Chinese forces joined together, and with the People's Anti-Japanese Army organized by the native Filipinos, all fought side by side and killed over two thousand of the enemy.

A long, long overseas line strung together the overseas Chinese of every country in common resistance. Wherever the Japanese army marched, there overseas-Chinese guerrillas harried it, and the Japanese Empire's Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere became an impossible dream.

The great Japanese army had encircled China and encircled Yan'an, but Yan'an's secret strength reached out overseas and formed a counter-encirclement of Japan.

The Communist leaders in Yan'an, bold and far-sighted, even convened in Yan'an a Congress of the Eastern Peoples Against Fascism, with Communists from Korea, Japan, and the countries of Southeast Asia in attendance.

Burma's situation was complicated: patriotic soldiers like Aung San resisted British colonial rule and came to China for support. But Japan exploited the local anti-British feeling, sent troops into Burma, and cut the Yunnan-Burma Road, the great rear's only channel to the outside.

The Burmese overseas-Chinese Party members Zheng Xiangpeng and Wang Chuhui, husband and wife, made their way home by every means and, skirting the Nationalist agents' blockade, reached Yan'an. They had meant to live happily "at home," but were sent back to enemy-occupied Burma: the CCP was to support the overseas-Chinese Party, and the Comintern was to build a local party there.

The specter of Communism roamed everywhere, and the figures of the overseas Chinese roamed everywhere; in any remote corner of the world, it seemed, some Chinese Communist was to be found.

The American Communist Party had a Chinese group, and Communists like Xu Yongying and Ji Chaoding transferred into the CCP system on returning home. The British Communist Party had a Chinese group too, and a few of its Communists, being natives of the Northeast, were especially suited to intelligence work against Japan. Zhang Weixian slipped back into the Northeast to find the anti-Japanese united forces, He Songting worked liaison in Beiping and Tianjin, and Yu Bingran joined the Northeast Salvation Association in Chongqing and even became a Nationalist major general.

To take part in the international anti-fascist united front, the Communists who had come out of the mountain gullies had to do some foreign-affairs work of their own.

The visitors from abroad each had a mission.

The Comintern's Far Eastern Bureau lay hidden in Shanghai, directing the secret Communist organizations of the countries of East Asia, and the Communists of those countries came and went and stayed in China. The Japanese Communist leader Okano Susumu, alias Nosaka Sanzo, was in Yan'an; the Vietnamese Communist leader Nguyen Ai Quoc, Ho Chi Minh, was in Guilin; and Korean Communists in China were more numerous still: Kim Il-sung, Choe Yong-gon, Kim Chaek, and others, all commanders of the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army.

The powers of the world all wanted to bring China into view; Shanghai, called the paradise of international adventurers, was a heaven for international spies too. In Shanghai, the greatest international city of the Far East, new Hollywood films had their first run and intelligence could be picked up in the cafés and dance halls. The American reporter Edgar Snow, not content to interview the government's leaders, actually went up to northern Shaanxi to interview the Communist chiefs; his one book, *Red Star Over China*, brought Mao Zedong onto the world stage.

The Communists' representative to the outside world, Zhou Enlai, coming to China's wartime capital Chongqing, naturally faced foreigners of every kind, the diplomats of every country posted to China, Soviet ones, American and British ones, German, Italian, and Japanese ones, and among private citizens, Jewish merchants, American sinologists, and the exile government of the Republic of Korea. Foreign countries had their friendly people too, and friendly foreigners were glad to help China resist. And so the Communists came to have international intelligence.

In the Second World War, China had not only cooperation among its own political parties but cooperation between China and foreign states. The Communist-supplied intelligence cadre Yan Baohang worked for the Soviet Union and detected the strategic intelligence of Germany's attack on the USSR.

After the Japanese took Hong Kong, the Communist-led East River guerrillas rescued the international friends held prisoner by the Japanese. The British Army Aid Group traded Japanese intelligence with the East River Column and successfully rescued eighty-nine British and American prisoners of war.

China's eastern seaboard was always strategic ground. In the early war the Japanese held the North and East of China, and China's outside aid could come only through the ports of the southeast coast; in the stalemate phase, American bombers flying from carriers to bomb Japan could land on their return only on the Zhejiang coast; and by the counter-offensive phase, when the American army planned to land troops in Japan, the takeoff point could only be the Communist-held coast of Shandong.

The Eighth Route commander-in-chief Zhu De and the American general Stilwell became comrades in arms, and the China-theater chief of staff Stilwell resolved to allot weapons and equipment to the Eighth Route Army, which fought Japan hard. The Generalissimo was displeased and began to squeeze out this famous American general.

What Stilwell wanted was victory in the anti-fascist war, and he cared nothing for the Generalissimo's private lust for power. To obtain geographic intelligence of China's coast, the Americans began to seek cooperation with China, not only with the ruling Nationalists but with the out-of-power Communists. Stilwell, the American commander in the China theater, sent two military observer groups: Colonel Barrett posted to Yan'an, Major Ou Daiyi to the East River Column. Yuan Geng, who led the East River Column's intelligence men, supplied the Americans with Japanese military dispositions along the Guangdong coast, protected the American ground men who guided American planes to bomb Japanese targets at Hong Kong, and even helped the Americans find the mysterious Japanese "Borei" unit, providing intelligence for the American landings behind enemy lines. Yuan Geng, with his experience of international cooperation, went on in the 1980s to build the Shekou development zone in Shenzhen and became a spearhead of reform and opening.

When an American plane bombing Japan was downed on its return by a Japanese fighter, the Communist-led East Zhejiang guerrillas rescued the American pilot from the Japanese pursuit, and the American side's thanks came as an intelligence radio air-dropped to the East Zhejiang guerrillas.

In a world war, the international allies' demand for intelligence on Japan grew and grew, and the Communists too were exploring cooperation on foreign intelligence.

The Communists' secret work moved from underground up onto the surface, and grew from the domestic to the international. In a sense, this too was a "reform and opening."

Zhou Enlai summed up the new experience of this reform and opening: the united front drives intelligence, and foreign affairs shield intelligence.

### 6. The Answer to the Great Contest in Xi'an

To join open activity with secret struggle was no simple thing. The Generalissimo, after all, held power in his hand: joint conferences of party, government, and army; combined operations of the military, the police, the gendarmerie, and the agents; a close watch on every Communist active in Nationalist territory.

When the Xi'an Incident broke out, Zhou Enlai had hurried at once to Xi'an to help Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng handle the situation. After the incident was settled and Zhou was to return to Yan'an, in that one hurried passage he left behind in Xi'an a precise arrangement: three organs, open, semi-open, and hidden.

The open organ: the Eighth Route Army's Xi'an office, headed by the Special Branch-era wireless specialist Wu Yunfu.

The semi-open organ: the CCP Shaanxi provincial committee, headed by the veteran underground member Ouyang Qin.

The hidden organ: the Xi'an intelligence station, run by the former head of the Central Communications Bureau, Wu Defeng.

Even amid cooperation there was struggle; the Communists were no longer naive and youthful, and always guarded against Chiang.

Zhou Enlai ran a three-line arrangement in every great city of Nationalist territory: open united-front work, the secret underground Party, and deeper still a secret intelligence system.

When Zhou Enlai flew from Yan'an to Xi'an, one of the men on the plane vanished on reaching the city.

Wu Defeng was a seasoned hand in the Communist intelligence system, brought into the Party in 1924 by Dong Biwu and Chen Tanqiu; during the first Nationalist-Communist alliance in 1926 he was police chief of Wuhan, and after the split he built a secret courier system in Shanghai, later serving as security chief of the Xiang-Gan province in the Central Soviet and of the Second Front Army.

Wu Defeng rented a courtyard house in Xi'an. The old Special Branch hand Chen Yangshan handled contact with the CCP Shaanxi provincial committee and the Nationalist government organs; the Red Army veteran Luo Qingchang handled contact with the Nationalist army; the Northeasterner Tao Siyong handled liaison with the Northeastern Army; and Yu Zhongyou, back from the Soviet Union, deciphered telegrams. With such a mixed household living together, they needed a family identity for cover: the woman member Jian Xianfo passed as Wu Defeng's younger sister, jokingly called "Third Aunt," and Wu Defeng's wife Qi Yuande was jokingly called "Sixth Granny" by Jian Xianfo. Luo Qingchang passed as a cousin, the young Xiao Foxian as a houseboy. This large household even had children: Chen Yangshan's son and Wu Defeng's daughter Wu Chisheng became live props.

The Xi'an station developed many important connections. Dai Zhongrong was chief of the confidential section at Hu Zongnan's headquarters; Xiao De was head of the agents' detective squad; Song Qiyun was secretary to the Northwestern Army commander Yang Hucheng; Li Xijiu was a counselor to the Xi'an field headquarters chief Cheng Qian; Zhang Churen was attendant secretary to the governor Jiang Dingwen; Shi Zhongwei was secretary to the corps commander Dong Zhao; and there were public figures like Yang Mingxuan and Du Bincheng. The deepest-planted of all was the three-man cell of Chen Zhongjing, Xiong Xianghui, and Shen Jian.

The Communists used the Eighth Route Army's Xi'an office, an open organ, to press hard at united-front work. The chairman of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region government, Lin Boqu, took the lesser post of head of the Xi'an office, the field headquarters chief Cheng Qian being Lin Boqu's old comrade from the Northern Expedition. A platoon of the Eighth Route Army was stationed at the office, under platoon leader Zhang Yaoci and squad leader Gu Yuanxing. Li Kenong's son Li Li was chief of the radio station. Luo Qingchang served concurrently as Lin Boqu's confidential secretary, in charge of intelligence and security and also managing the An-Wu youth training class. The wife of the office head Zhou Zijian, Wang Ping, a graduate of the Zaoyuan third class, managed security at the office as well.

The Xi'an office's quarters, No. 1 Qixianzhuang, had once been a dental clinic. The German Communist Herbert, persecuted by the fascists, had come to China and founded this clinic, and forwarded from here the medical supplies international friends donated to Yan'an. When the Red Army set up its office in Xi'an, it chose this reliable place for its quarters.

No. 1 Qixianzhuang was the Communists' window in Nationalist territory, and it became a thorn in the side of the Nationalist agents too. The Shaanxi provincial investigation commissioner, the Zhongtong chief Guo Zijun, sent his secretary Ma Zhuojiang with four action men to keep watch by turns at the office gate, hoping to spot members of the secret organization among those coming and going. The field headquarters' investigation-section chief Zhang Yifu also sent Juntong men to watch in secret. The Nationalist agents of the two systems watched the office so keenly that they came to blows over the jurisdiction. In 1939 the field headquarters' political-department head Gu Zhengding took on the post of secretary of the special-liaison reporting office as well, and seized the chance to gather the power to watch the office into his own hands alone. The head of the watch group, Chang Shengzhao, was a "Red Gang lodge master" who came to Xi'an bearing letters of introduction from the Zhongtong, but was in fact a veteran Juntong man. On taking over, Chang Shengzhao at once set to work at a tight watch: he set up an open sentry box outside the Qixianzhuang gate to watch those going in and out; he piled up a small mountain in the primary school beside Qixianzhuang, with a lookout tower rising above the wall to see into the courtyard; and behind the wall of the Zuoxiu girls' school across from Qixianzhuang he set up a hidden watching point, digging a small hole to peep through. The watch group had nine men in all, in teams of three, each team with two bicycles, one team on a fixed post and two out shadowing. Each point had a telephone to keep in constant touch with the special-liaison reporting office. Every evening the watch group filed a written report to the office.

A struggle of watching and counter-watching opened up outside the gate of Qixianzhuang.

One morning early, a bicycle suddenly shot out of the Qixianzhuang gate, something loaded on the back seat. Two of the watch group's agents at once set off after it. The Eighth Route youth rode fast, into the new city, out to East Main Street, out the East Gate, around again to the railway station, past the Bell Tower and out the West Gate, then up North Main Street and South Main Street, riding the whole city of Xi'an over, without a hint of any secret contact. He had been taking the agents for a spin. Shadowing was a wretched job: too close, and you were spotted; too far, and you were shaken off. The Qixianzhuang men grew ever more familiar with the Xi'an streets, and the agents' shadowing grew ever harder. One day of heavy rain, a stretch of the primary school's earthen wall collapsed, and the agent watching behind it was exposed. The office men across the way laughed and called out: "Hard work, isn't it? Come over and sit a while." As the agent stood there awkwardly, a cigarette came flying from across the way, and it so enraged Chang Shengzhao that he pulled the point.

In a fury of shame, the special-liaison reporting office simply arranged for the Xi'an garrison command to force its way into Qixianzhuang on the pretext of a registration check. By the agreement of the two parties, the Eighth Route office's personnel, small arms, and ammunition all had to be reported by number, and Gu Zhengding hoped to find some discrepancy to seize on. But Luo Qingchang's arrangements were so fine that every check found men and guns tallying, down to the last round, not one over, not one short. On one check they found five pistol rounds missing, and the agents were about to make a scene, when they found a magazine fallen on a bed, and the rounds in it came to exactly five.

The special-liaison reporting office found from its wireless monitoring that there was a high-power radio inside Qixianzhuang. The office's young men often played basketball outside the compound, and the Nationalist agents watched from the sidelines, in fact counting heads to work out which men were the radio staff. Li Li had long since spotted these agents, and simply hailed them: "What are you looking at? Come down and play a game." So the agents had to give up watching and go down to play, our side and the enemy competing on the same court.

The agents, getting nowhere at the truth of things, finally forced their way in on the pretext of a household-registration check, but they could never find the radio. One deep night the Xi'an police bureau made a surprise search, and still found no radio. This cleverly hidden secret station has now become one of the stops on the tour of the Qixianzhuang revolutionary site.

The office also purchased supplies for Yan'an, which was a legal act, but the watch group still shadowed in secret and even persecuted the merchants who dealt with the office. The chemist Wu Zishi of the Northwest Pharmaceutical Plant was secretly arrested and held three months before release. The plant's director Xue Daowu kept receiving threatening letters that menaced the kidnapping of his young son. The Fourth War Cadre Regiment claimed the plant harbored Communists plotting an uprising and openly hauled away its motor.

The complex conditions of struggle tested the young Communists. Wu Defeng, who had been Wuhan's police chief in the days of the great revolution, could hardly avoid running into someone who knew him on the street. When the office receiving clerk Peng Zongfan, suspected of embezzling donations, was seized in a surprise arrest by the Nationalist agents, he gave up the name of a Shaanxi provincial committee secretary called Wu Tiezheng, and this Wu Tiezheng was none other than an alias of Wu Defeng.

In these complex surroundings Wu Defeng was careful and prudent, and dodged the agents' shadowing and checks many times over. He also taught by his own conduct, painstakingly cultivating young intelligence cadres. Luo Qingchang, who served concurrently as Party branch secretary of the Xi'an station, was meticulous in his work, clear in his thinking, and possessed of a remarkable memory, fine material for intelligence work. Wu Defeng often took Luo Qingchang out on assignments and even concerned himself with Luo Qingchang's "personal problem," the Party's phrase for matters of love and marriage. Luo Qingchang had once had a sweetheart, herself a cipher clerk, who had died in enemy territory. Coming now to work in Xi'an, Luo Qingchang needed a woman at his side for cover. For this woman who might well become Luo Qingchang's wife, Wu Defeng chose with great care, and picked the clever, comely woman cipher clerk Du Xijian.

Luo Qingchang and Du Xijian went about together through the streets and lanes of Xi'an, working and courting at once, and got on happily enough. Returning to the Qixianzhuang office one deep night, they suddenly ran into a Nationalist search party and hid together in a dark corner, and the false couple at last became a true one. Luo Qingchang, having lived through the secret work of Xi'an, was transferred back to the Yan'an Social Affairs Department in 1941, first as secretary to the head and then as director of the guidance section, entering from then on the core of the Communist intelligence apparatus, in touch with all the Party's intelligence systems and with a full grip on the intelligence struggle against the enemy.

There was joy amid the hard and dangerous life, but the joy of secret intelligence men is always shorter than the danger. On July 31, 1938, the Xi'an office's major general representative Xuan Xiafu vanished mysteriously. Soon after, the adjutant Wang Ke, the escort clerk Guo Buhai, and four men who guarded the railway station vanished one after another.

To crack the case and safeguard the office fell as a heavy burden on the shoulders of the intelligence men.

The Communist side judged that Xuan Xiafu's disappearance very likely had to do with the Nationalist agents. The Xi'an office asked Jiang Dingwen and Hu Zongnan for the man again and again, and the CCP Central Committee in Yan'an even wired the Nationalist center directly to ask Chiang Kai-shek for him, but the Nationalists put it all off with a plea of ignorance, and Hu Zongnan went so far as to swear he knew nothing.

The Xi'an intelligence station set inside men to investigate. The Zhongtong's detective-squad chief Shi Yinsan had been a Communist in the days of the great revolution, later left the Party, and now made contact with it again. Luo Qingchang had him post men around the office, watching on the surface, guarding in fact. Shi Yinsan set several watching points, one a northern-Shaanxi stall-keeper, He Jiantai, who was one of their own. Through these connections, they finally pieced together the truth of the Xuan Xiafu affair.

After the Xi'an Incident, Chiang Kai-shek had used the chance to squeeze out the Northeastern Army and the Northwestern Army, and Xi'an's military world became the domain of Commandant Chiang's Whampoa clique. The field headquarters chief Jiang Dingwen had been a company leader of Whampoa's first class, and battle commanders like Hu Zongnan and Dong Zhao had been students of that first class. To open up work within the Whampoa clique, the Communists had deliberately transferred the former Whampoa first-class man Xuan Xiafu to Xi'an.

Jiang Dingwen, a fellow townsman of Xuan Xiafu from Zhuji, knew the man's ability well and deliberately had the Juntong Northwest district keep close watch on him. The Juntong district chief Zhang Yanfo directed a precinct of the Xi'an police bureau to set up a special Xizaimen police substation at the office gate, requiring every policeman in it to know Xuan Xiafu's build and features and to record his movements at all times, but never to shadow him. The task of shadowing fell to another group under the direct command of the precinct chief Li Hanting, to keep its identity secret so that Xuan Xiafu would not spot it. Zhang Yanfo also set the Zhuji townsmen around Jiang Dingwen to watch Xuan Xiafu's activities, and had men found specially in the Northwestern Army to note Xuan Xiafu's dealings with Du Bincheng, Sun Weiru, Zhao Shoushan, and others.

Chiang Kai-shek had always kept a tight grip on the gun. When the Red Army was reorganized into the Eighth Route Army and the New Fourth Army, Chiang refused to accept the lists of division cadres the Communists put forward, insisting on making the appointments himself, meaning to draw the Eighth Route commanders in by dispensing rank. When Ye Ting was appointed commander of the New Fourth Army his Party membership had not yet been restored, yet Ye Ting reported at once to the CCP Central Committee for instructions. The Eighth Route Army and the New Fourth Army stayed firmly in Communist hands throughout. Over his own forces Chiang kept an even tighter watch; even over Hu Zongnan, the truest of his true line, Chiang was not the least careless, but set a secret watch on him too.

Hu Zongnan, who had fought in the defense of Shanghai, though moved by Chiang Kai-shek to the rear to blockade the Eighth Route Army, still had resistance in his heart. Hearing that his old schoolmate Xuan Xiafu had taken part in Feng Yuxiang's defense at Gubeikou, he deliberately asked Xuan Xiafu to share his experience of fighting Japan. The Communist side was just then working to win Hu Zongnan over, and seized the chance to transfer Xuan Xiafu to Xi'an to work specially on the Nationalist military. Hu Zongnan asked Xuan Xiafu to compile resistance-guerrilla teaching materials for him and to draw up resistance operational plans, and the two grew closer and closer in private.

This roused Chiang Kai-shek's deepest alarm. Chiang had always regarded Whampoa men as his own true line; how could he suffer a trusted general like Hu Zongnan to be lost? Rather than risk wrecking the Nationalist-Communist alliance, Chiang would never let another lay a finger on his gun. Chiang wired Jiang Dingwen in secret: do away with Xuan Xiafu quietly.

On receiving Chiang Kai-shek's secret wire, Jiang Dingwen at once gave the Juntong Northwest district a handwritten order. Xuan Xiafu was a man of great influence, and even on receiving an assassination task the Juntong was cautious in the extreme. The fourth-section chief Xu Yijue, the action-section head Ding Minzhi, and the police-bureau chief Li Hanting conferred over and over and decided to act quietly by night and hide the body in a dry well.

Getting Xuan Xiafu out of Qixianzhuang had again to be Jiang Dingwen's doing. On the evening of July 31, 1938, Jiang Dingwen telephoned to invite Xuan Xiafu to his residence for a talk, and kept him talking until one in the morning before letting him leave. Xuan Xiafu rode back toward Qixianzhuang, and on the road the agents had long lain in wait. Two of them followed on bicycles behind Xuan Xiafu, while Xu Yijue and Li Hanting, with Li Liangjun and others in a small car, cut him off midway; when Xuan Xiafu rode up, the agents front and back struck at once, Xu Yijue and Li Hanting seizing Xuan Xiafu by the throat. Xuan Xiafu cried out in fury: "You mean to kidnap me?" The agents at once stuffed his mouth with cotton and looped a cord around his neck, Xu Yijue and Li Hanting hauling from either side, and Xuan Xiafu died on the spot. The agents carried the body to a dry well below Maling, at the southeast corner of the Xi'an city wall, that they had chosen in advance, and buried it under earth. The next day Xu Yijue reported to Jiang Dingwen, who handed down a reward of two thousand yuan; Xu Yijue kept half himself and split the rest among the dozen-odd agents who had taken part.

With Xuan Xiafu vanished in Nationalist territory, the Communists of course pressed the Nationalists for the man. Jiang Dingwen, fearing that Xuan Xiafu's body buried inside the city would be found, then had Zhang Yanfo secretly move it. Deep in the night the Juntong agents dug Xuan Xiafu's body up, carried it out through an air-raid tunnel bored in the city wall, and buried it in waste ground outside the city.

Inside the Juntong the reward was split unevenly, and the grumbling and loose talk were overheard by Shi Yinsan. The Xi'an station reported it to the Central Committee at once. The CCP Central Committee lodged a formal protest with the Nationalists and demanded that they answer for it.

Chiang Kai-shek had to say: "Xuan Xiafu was my student. He betrayed me, and I ordered him killed."

The subtle point of the case lay in Hu Zongnan. The Communists pressed Hu Zongnan for the man, because Hu Zongnan had had a good deal to do with Xuan Xiafu. Hu Zongnan truly did not know, because both Chiang Kai-shek and Jiang Dingwen had kept the principal in the dark. Chiang Kai-shek's quiet killing of Xuan Xiafu was precisely out of fear that he would turn Hu Zongnan.

The Nationalists kept the inside story of Xuan Xiafu's assassination tightly guarded. Only in 1988 did a memoir by the former Juntong Northwest district chief reveal the truth. Yet Zhang Yanfo and the other Juntong men knew only that Chiang Kai-shek had ordered Xuan Xiafu quietly killed; they did not know the deeper reason for Chiang's ruthlessness. The Communist side, too, long kept this inside story unpublished, and has made much of Hu Zongnan.

Under the Nationalist-Communist alliance, the Nationalists went so far as to assassinate an openly active Eighth Route major general? The Generalissimo's methods really could not bear the light of day.

And the Communists? It was not that Wu Defeng had no chance to retaliate; the Communists had several lines lying low in the Xi'an field headquarters, and the crack shot Yu Zhongyou could have taken out Hu Zongnan easily enough.

But the Communists did not stoop to Juntong's dirty means. Wu Defeng organized everyone to survey the political and ideological state of every class and every organ in Xi'an, and drew the conclusion that left, center, and right were "small at both ends, large in the middle." From this Mao Zedong held that the Nationalists too were no monolith, and that one should unite the left, win over the center, and isolate the small stubborn minority. From the data of the investigation he further proposed a "three-thirds system" in building the base-area governments: Communists, democrats, and mass organizations each to hold a third.

Cooperation breeds struggle, and the united front contains secret war; the Communists' struggle with the Nationalists was on just grounds, forceful, and measured, and held the moral high ground throughout.

### 7. Zhou Enlai's Way of Gathering Intelligence: Making Friends Widely

In this world there are only "enemies, ourselves, and friends," and the object of united-front work is precisely that "friend."

Making friends is nothing new; most social organizations have a public-relations arm. But for the Communists it was a great change: today's ally was the mortal enemy of ten years back.

Can the relation of enemy and friend be turned around?

The CCP Central Committee's leadership had long been "Left." When they launched armed uprisings and turned the White army, they "wanted the soldiers, not the officers." But soldiers could not seize military power, and the uprisings could not grow large. When the Central Soviet was under encirclement, outside help suddenly came: Li Jishen's Fujian government proposed cooperating against Chiang. Yet the Central Committee's man in charge said, "The middle-of-the-roaders are the most dangerous enemy," and so two forces that might have been comrades were broken one by one by Chiang, and the Red Army had to flee on the Long March.

In the war against Japan the CCP made a sharp strategic turn and began to build the whole nation's anti-Japanese united front. The moment the Red Army reached northern Shaanxi, the Central Committee laid down a policy of winning over the "Elder Brothers Society" and the "churches." Northern Shaanxi was a bitter, cold land where the secret societies and the religious powers ran strong, and to gain a foothold in the Northwest the Red Army had to unite every force that could be united.

The gun was the guarantee of survival. Cooperation had been achieved, but the Communists still meant to keep their military power; so how were the two armies to have dealings?

Mao Zedong's method was "making friends," and the Eighth Route Army and the New Fourth Army were to make "two million friends" in the Nationalist ranks.

Raise the open banner of friendship, and the friends come flocking.

Yang Gongsu had once been a Communist agent in 1927 but was not then a Party member, just a young fellow having his fun. When the war broke out, Yang Gongsu joined the Nationalist 93rd Army and even took to running guerrilla war behind enemy lines in the Communist style. His army chief of staff, Bai Tian, was of progressive mind and sent Yang Gongsu to lead a party to Yan'an to study, since the real art of rear-guard guerrilla war lay with the Communists. Reaching Yan'an, Yang Gongsu found Chairman Mao coming to call on him in person, and ran into progressive schoolmates everywhere. Then Yang Gongsu went to Chongqing, where he saw nothing but singing and dancing revelry, not a trace of resistance. After much comparing, Yang Gongsu chose the Communists, and the Eighth Route Army's deputy commander-in-chief Peng Dehuai served as his sponsor into the Party himself.

During the war many young intellectuals threw down the pen for the sword and joined the field-service teams of the Nationalist forces, coming to know the Communists through their work. In that very 93rd Army that Yang Gongsu left, Bai Tian, Yang Yi, and others went off to the Eighth Route Army's Taiyue base area too.

Making friends called for dedicated organs and dedicated talent, and the Eighth Route Army's front headquarters and its three divisions each set up a special senior advisers' office. These senior advisers were all men of long standing and considerable social position, and though some were not Party members, all were willing to help the Communists make friends. The senior advisers of the 129th Division, Wang Dingguo, Shen Bochun, Li Xinnong, and others, made many friends among the Nationalist ranks, so that when Juntong tried to sow discord between the two armies, many Nationalist commanders were unmoved.

The Nationalist First War Zone commander Wei Lihuang had an old grudge against the Communists, having led the first attack on the Fourth Front Army's E-Yu-Wan base area and taken its central county town, which was then named Lihuang County after him. But the war-zone deputy commander Zhu De let the old grudge go, and the Eighth Route Army coordinated actively in the Shanxi fighting: the former Fourth Front Army commander Chen Xilian raided the Japanese airfield at Yangmingbao and effectively secured the air safety of Wei Lihuang's forces.

Wei Lihuang, ignoring the agents' attempts to dissuade him, visited Yan'an to give his thanks, and Mao Zedong and Zhu De received him warmly.

On his return to his post Wei Lihuang ordered three hundred thousand rounds of ammunition released to the Eighth Route Army.

Do not imagine that Mao Zedong never went in for hosting a meal; frugality within the Party, courtesy without, and in united-front work Mao Zedong understood the ways of the world best of all. Chiang Kai-shek encircled the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, Hu Zongnan to the south at Xi'an, Deng Baoshan to the north at Yulin. When Deng Baoshan passed through Yan'an on his way from Yulin to a meeting in Xi'an, he did not go to the Border Region government's reception office but put up at an ordinary carters' inn. The city public-security bureau's watchers reported it at once, and Mao Zedong at once ordered Deng Baoshan brought into the reception office. Sightseeing together, theatergoing together, hosting meals, Mao Zedong entertained Deng Baoshan seven days running in Yan'an. Deng Baoshan went back from Yan'an to Yulin and yielded two Yellow River crossings to the Communists, and the blockade between northern Shaanxi and Shanxi was opened from then on.

In 1940 the CCP Central Committee issued a document that beggared belief, the *Directive on Expanding the Work of Making Friends*.

Making friends counts as work too, and calls for a document? The CCP may well be the political body in all the world that sets the most store by public relations.

A pity that the Red Army generals, used to fighting for their lives, were not skilled at social exchange. So Mao Zedong took the matter in hand himself.

The Northwestern Army's main force, the 38th Army, had had a secret Communist organization as early as 1931, but the work had risen and fallen, mainly because the policy on upper-level united-front work was unclear. Mao Zedong decided that the CCP's 38th Army working committee would come directly under the Central Committee, with Mao Zedong himself directing it on a single line. Mao Zedong gave concrete guidance to secret members like Fan Ming: no impatience, no exposure. When the 38th Army commander Zhao Shoushan made a trip to Yan'an, Mao Zedong received him in person, approved him as a special Party member, and even directed Fan Ming to disclose the list of secret members to Zhao Shoushan.

Under Mao Zedong's own guidance, the 38th Army working committee became a "model Party organization" and created a "model of upper-level united-front work." Throughout the war Zhao Shoushan coordinated with the Eighth Route Army to defend the Border Region, and when the civil war broke out this force was the first to revolt.

The interesting thing is that the 38th Army general Kong Congzhou later even became a relation of Mao Zedong's by marriage: making friends turned into becoming family.

In truth the whole intelligence world sets store by public relations and friendship; only the ends of the friendship differ. The ordinary spy merely wheedles intelligence out of his friends; the Communists meant to turn friends into their own.

History should record that the second Nationalist-Communist alliance of modern Chinese history began with the exchange of information between the two parties' supreme leaders in early 1936. History should not overlook, either, that this exchange used an intelligence network.

The Northwestern Army commander Yang Hucheng was on close terms with the Communists; his wife Xie Baozhen, his secretary-general Nan Hanchen, and his guard-regiment commander Zhang Hanmin were all secret Communists. The Northeastern Army's Young Marshal Zhang Xueliang had long wanted to make contact with the Communists but had no way to begin; the one Communist at his side had been shot at Chiang's urging.

Turning enemy into friend needs an adhesive, a figure all sides can accept. Zhou Enlai's connections ran wide; wherever he went, he could find friends. The eminent Northeastern patriot Gao Chongmin had joined the Revolutionary Alliance in his youth and been in touch with the Communists of the Northeast, and after the Mukden Incident had cast about in vain for a way to resist Japan in the Northeast. A long talk with his 1911 comrade, the Communist Nan Hanchen, made Gao Chongmin hope for contact with the Communists. In 1935, with Zhou Enlai's support, Gao Chongmin and Yan Baohang and others founded the Northeast Salvation Association. Through the good offices of Gao Chongmin and Nan Hanchen, the ties between Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng were connected up too.

Making contact and negotiating needs a channel, needs personal trust built between the two sides. Zhou Enlai was a man of feeling and honor and always won his opponents' respect. Internationally Zhou Enlai is acknowledged a master negotiator, able not only to bring off talks that would not otherwise come off, but especially skilled at turning enemy into friend through negotiation.

In the war against Japan the CCP came to maturity and put a united-front policy into practice. In Chongqing Zhou Enlai made friends widely, extending his working object to the upper reaches of society, the enemy's core, and foreign allies.

Making friends takes capital too. Chiang Kai-shek could give office and money in making friends; what did Zhou Enlai, short of the economic resources of power, have to win people with? What the Communists relied on was human resources, putting others first and themselves last. Yielding office: in the contest for seats on the National Political Council, the Communists gave up two to the Democratic League. Yielding money: when Tao Xingzhi died poor and ill, Zhou Enlai ran companies to subsidize the democrats. Charcoal sent in the snow beats flowers added to brocade. The men of insight in the upper reaches looked not to money but to the future of the nation and of themselves.

Rich in personal charm, Zhou Enlai could join Chinese traditional morality perfectly with advanced Communist ideas and win friends over. Making friends is a traditional way of social intercourse, but Zhou Enlai brought something new to it. He made not only many friends but deep ones, able to turn a friend into a comrade.

Making friends is competition too, and international competition at that.

When the American military observer group set out for Chongqing, it urgently needed to set up a radio. But a week went by and the bureaucratic procedures were not yet done.

The American observer group then went to Yan'an, and had a radio in contact the same day.

Coming from corrupt, inefficient Chongqing to democratic, clean Yan'an, the young American intelligence officers reported to Washington: in China, the Communists better embodied American values.

The American friends Zhou Enlai made in the 1940s were still working, into the 1970s, for the establishment of Sino-American relations. The American friends Zhou Enlai made in the 1970s were still working, forty years on, for Sino-American relations.

Zhou Enlai gave his personal charm full play. He carried the common public-relations method of friendship to its height, raising it into a basic method of the united front, and applied it deftly to intelligence gathering, winning not only victory in the secret war but the high respect of people at home and abroad. Zhou Enlai, people said, was the very spokesman for the Communists' image.

The more friends the better, the fewer enemies the better.

To join open dealings with secret intelligence, this too was an art.

The Nationalist Zhongtong specialized in anti-Communist secret work, and its director Xu Enzeng saw sharply that the Communists had "carried out a hundred-and-eighty-degree turn." He at once sent men to slip into Yan'an, and got from the resistance university a copy of Zhang Hao's teaching text, *The Party's Strategic Line*. From it Xu Enzeng analyzed the Communists' new strategy: "The Communists understood that after the Nationalists and Communists 'cooperated' once more, they had to organize their own army in good time, had to work in good time among the 'friendly forces,' had to keep as many chances for open activity as they could, had to advance steadily and avoid provocation, and above all, before their feathers were full-grown, had to hide their strength and yield, and avoid a rupture with the Nationalists." Xu Enzeng went on to judge the Communists' new tactics: "In sum, it is that every underground worker must have a proper occupation, and be in life and word exactly like an ordinary person, so that no one can tell in the least that he is a Communist; this is the mark of the Communists' new underground tactics: long concealment."

Xu Enzeng styled himself an anti-Communist expert and claimed that "before the war, we knew every last one of the Communists' highest secrets, clear as day." In those days the Zhongtong's case-cracking often left the other side helpless. But now Xu Enzeng fell into perplexity: "This new set of Communist tactics brought the National Government endless trouble, and I bore the brunt of it worst of all."

Xu Enzeng was shrewd all the same.

In the war against Japan the Communists' intelligence work was just then shaking off the Soviet model and forming a character of its own. This intelligence-gathering with Chinese characteristics left the Nationalists at a loss.
