## Chapter Three. Who Is Judas

### 1. Blood on Their Hands

The phrase "second landlord" comes out of Shanghai dialect, and in the Republican years it was on everyone's tongue. Shanghai then was a city of sharpers, and one stock sharper of that world was the middleman of the rental trade. His whole grubbing little racket came to this: he leased a flat entire from the real landlord, carved it into pieces to sublet, and lived off the spread.

Xia Yukui was one of these.

Xia Yukui, a Shandong man, lived on the ground floor of 178 Rue Bourgeat in the Shanghai French Concession, the street they call Changle Road today. By day he worked for the Tongshunli Lace Company, hawking lace curtains to foreigners; on the side he played second landlord, pasting to-let notices on the door that gave onto the street.

Xia Yukui had luck. The notice was barely up before someone came to the door looking to rent.

The caller gave his name as He: a man just past thirty, in a Western suit, well-turned-out and easy in his manner. He left Xia's wife with a good impression, and still she would not nod him in on the spot. She wavered, holding something back. The times were unsettled, and she had no wish to deal with a stranger from elsewhere whose story she did not know. She hemmed herself in with her own misgivings, and what she balked at most was his thick Sichuan accent.

He saw her hesitation and pressed his case again and again: he was a comprador for a motorcar company, a respectable man of business with an honest trade; he had a wife, a daughter, and a wet-nurse; he was no gutter tough, no hoodlum, no rogue, no confidence-man, and she could rest a hundred percent easy.

His earnest vows won her over. And he paid handsomely: six months' rent, the whole sum at once. At the sight of the money her eyes lit up, and she left off her hemming and hawing.

Early the next morning He moved in with his wife at 178 Rue Bourgeat. Madame He was a natural beauty, her hair in the newest style, her clothes in the latest fashion, and Xia's wife could only envy her. Madame He, for her part, clucked over her husband's good eye. What she liked best was the big open room on the second floor, the front and back chambers thrown together, the air moving north to south, roomy and comfortable, with a long bank of windows down the east side; across the way there was just room for a double bed, a pair of bedside cabinets, and a dressing table. As for the little girl carried in arms and her wet-nurse, an Anhui woman named Wu née Liu, they took the tingzijian. The tingzijian too had its table and chairs, its bed and its cupboard, everything in place, small and snug.

So the two households, He and Xia, became neighbors now, living under the one roof.

Each had their place, and they lived side by side without trouble.

Xia Yukui and his wife never dreamed that in grasping at a little they had lost a great deal, had drawn fire onto themselves, and were bound for ruin.

The old saying held: those who mean well do not come, and those who come do not mean well. He and his beautiful wife were no ordinary pair.

They were the shameless traitors who would sell out Luo Yinong: member of the Standing Committee of the Party's Central Politburo, and head of its Organization Bureau.

### 2. A Gathering Never to Be Repeated

Luo Yinong, also called Shanyang, styled Shenzhai, sobriquet Zhengang, alias Jue, was born in 1902 in Xiangtan, Hunan. In August 1920 he joined the Socialist Youth League; in May 1921 he went to the Soviet Union to study at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow, and that winter he became a member of the Chinese Communist Party.

On May 17, 2002, speaking at a forum marking the hundredth anniversary of Luo Yinong's birth, Hu Jintao called him "one of the early principal leaders of the Chinese Communist Party, an outstanding proletarian revolutionary and a famous leader of the workers' movement," "a revolutionary pioneer who made outstanding contributions to the cause of national independence and the liberation of the people."

It was after the failure of the Great Revolution that Luo Yinong rose, step by step, into the core leadership of the Party.

On August 7, 1927, the Party Center convened an emergency conference at 41 Sanjiao Street in Hankou (139 Poyang Street today, though Zheng Chaolin says number 123), in the front room of the second floor (Zheng Chaolin says the back room), to examine and correct the grave errors of the Party's late Great Revolution period and to decide a new line and new policies. Qu Qiubai and Li Weihan chaired it. On August 9, the first meeting of the Party's provisional Central Politburo elected Qu Qiubai, Li Weihan, and Su Zhaozheng to its Standing Committee. On November 9 and 10, the provisional Central Politburo held an enlarged meeting in Shanghai, and Luo Yinong was added to the Standing Committee.

On November 14, the provisional Central Politburo held a Standing Committee meeting and resolved to set up an Organization Bureau. On the Soviet model, it would bring the Central Organization Department, the Central Propaganda Department, the Secretariat, and the other organs directly under the Center into one administration. It was made up of Luo Yinong, Zhou Enlai, and Li Weihan, with Luo Yinong as its head.

After that, Luo Yinong and Li Zheshi came together and made a match of it, a pair of revolutionary companions.

Luo Yinong and Li Zheshi were joined on New Year's Day, 1928.

Li Zheshi had been, in Zheng Chaolin's words, "a woman intellectual who had sworn off marriage, relatively old, who through all those months of feverish romancing in Wuhan had stayed a spectator." (Zheng Chaolin, 1996) So when she found that she had, after all, fallen for the leader at her side, a man "expansive and bold, quick and clever, high in theory, decisive in action"; when that leader wanted to "buy a violin," or would "bring back a few pieces of Xiang embroidery from Changsha" for her, who "loved music" and had studied painting, and would take care to inscribe on one of them "Yinong and Zheshi," then her heart was "truly in a tangle," full of the feeling that "you cut it and it will not part, you smooth it and it snarls again." There was "a taste all its own upon her heart." Li Zheshi "respected" Luo Yinong; she "felt he was like the older comrades in the leadership, mature and steady, and yet sometimes very simple and lively, like a young student." He was not the "cultivated young man of poetry and painting" of her ideal. He was not like Qu Qiubai, who could take up her brush and dash off a few lines of handsome regular script or paint a gnarled and vigorous pine, who could take up her xiao or her flute and, on the spot, play so that "the large pearls and the small came pattering onto the jade plate." Under his chin he "wore three inches of thin, straggling whisker," which made her think of him more as "an old fellow," an "elder," a "leader." (Li Wenyi, 2004)

Li Zheshi thought to herself: "Does he really love me? A woman of my limits, not much of a talker, plain to look at, no great abilities, only honest and steady, what is there in me worth his loving?" And so she told Luo Yinong: "You had far better find yourself a pretty comrade." Luo Yinong said: "There is only you in my heart. From the first time I saw you, I liked you."

On November 4, 1927, Li Zheshi boarded the Shanghai steamer with Luo Yinong.

On New Year's Day, 1928, Li Zheshi married Luo Yinong at 28 Xinzha Village on Xinzha Road, today's Lane 876, Xinzha Road.

Luo Yinong's idea was to see out the old and ring in the new, and to stand everyone a meal. Li Zheshi objected to the signature on his invitations: he had gone and joined their two names together. She wanted him to strike hers off. Luo Yinong said: "Objection overruled. It stays." She said people would take it the wrong way. Luo Yinong said: "So much the better. Let it be our wedding feast, then."

Number 28 Xinzha Village was a house of two upper floors and two lower. Zheng Chaolin, who said he "often went round to visit," called it "the first house in the lane, well built, the furniture quite lavish, altogether like the mansion of a rich man." As to the "lavishness" of this "mansion," Peng Shuzhi said the house was "quite spacious," and "what struck one most was that in the parlor below, and in the small sitting room and bedroom upstairs, many pieces of Xiang embroidery hung on the walls, the most costly of ornaments; an ordinary middle-class household could never own such luxuries." (Peng Shuzhi, 2016) Li Zheshi said that the mansion-like house was not only their new home but "also an organ of the Party Center's Organization Bureau." So Luo Yinong had the Secretariat send two people to keep it. These were He Jiaxing and He Zhihua. With them came a little girl carried in arms and a wet-nurse. The four of them all lived downstairs. Of the two proper rooms above, one was Luo Yinong's, the other was for meetings and work. Of the two tingzijian besides (the small north-facing rooms set beside the stair landing in a shikumen house), one was Li Zheshi's bedroom and one held odds and ends.

Li Zheshi was from Hubei by origin, had no Shanghai dialect, and knew neither the place nor a soul in it; she could not find her way in the streets, and Luo Yinong teased her as an "amulin" (Shanghainese for a dolt, a fool). With nothing to fill her days, the "amulin" stayed home practicing her characters and painting, and reading. Luo Yinong borrowed a great stack of theoretical works and translated novels from the Propaganda Department. Whenever he set to writing in earnest, Li Zheshi would sit tucked close beside him at the edge of the desk, reading quietly: Zhang Ximan's translation of the Programme of the Russian Communist Party, or Lu Xun's Wandering, put out by the Beixin Book Company.

On November 29, 1927, the Party Center resolved that Yang Zhihua, Cai Chang, Deng Yingchao, Zhu Yingru, Wang Genying, He Zhihua, Zhuang Dongxiao, and Li Zheshi, eight in all, should form a new Central Women's Commission, with Yang Zhihua, Deng Yingchao, Zhuang Dongxiao, and Li Zheshi taking the chair by turns, a month each. Yang Zhihua was Qu Qiubai's wife, a member of the Party's Fifth Central Committee and head of the Central Women's Department. She said the White Terror was severe, and that no one should carelessly let her real name be known, so the commission's members took ages and called one another by sisterly rank. Li Zheshi was older than He Zhihua: Li Zheshi was Third Sister, He Zhihua Fourth.

Yang Zhihua assigned Li Zheshi to make contact with the women workers in Zhabei. But she could not so much as ride a tram, and was too shy to say so, so every time Luo Yinong flagged a rickshaw for her and kept it for the round trip. Among the women workers there was the barrier of language: she could not follow their Shanghainese, nor they her Hubei speech. It was awkward on both sides, and came to nothing, and Yang Zhihua stopped sending her.

On New Year's Day, Yang Zhihua was first to arrive. She wanted to doll Li Zheshi up. She wanted to do Li Zheshi's hair. She wanted to help Li Zheshi into her new clothes. Luo Yinong, meanwhile, hired a cab, went first to 34 Hengchangli on Yuyuan Road to fetch Zheng Chaolin, then to the corner of Avenue Road (today Beijing West Road) and Route Tunkadoo (today Shimen No. 1 Road), to the offices of the Party's Jiangsu Provincial Committee, to fetch Liu Jingzhen, and brought them all back together to Xinzha Village.

By now Qu Qiubai, Su Zhaozheng, Zhou Enlai, Deng Yingchao, Chen Qiaonian, Li Fuchun, Cai Chang, Wang Ruofei, and Xia Zhixu had arrived one after another. Later still came Deng Xiaoping and other members of the Central Secretariat, and even Chen Duxiu, the Party's former General Secretary, who had stepped down before the August 7 Conference. Everyone congratulated the couple, and the room was lively.

That evening there were four tables in all. Everyone ate their fill and drank their fill. After Chen Duxiu left, above all, they loosened further, sang together, played hide-and-seek like children, ducking under tables and squeezing into corners. To keep from being caught and made to perform a turn, Li Zheshi was forever scheming to hide far off and shrink into the smallest possible ball. How she envied Yang Zhihua, quick and nimble, caught to all appearance and then slipping the trap like a cicada shedding its shell. And how she loved to laugh, laughing at these Central Committee men who set the line for the whole Party. A little while before they had gone upstairs to rest, sipping their tea, sitting bolt upright and grave; either holding forth, dissecting point by point how the colonialist powers, Britain, America, Germany, Italy, Japan, France, jostled and schemed against one another in a tangle of contradictions, or filled with righteous fury, cursing the likes of Chiang Kai-shek, Wang Jingwei, Wu Zhihui, and Dai Jitao for their cruelty and shamelessness; so how was it that, the moment they came downstairs, every one of them romped worse than the naughtiest child? Once caught, they would go limp and cling, dragging down with them whoever had seized their collar and would not let go, until both rolled on the floor and lay there half the day.

This was not like Luo Yinong and Li Zheshi's wedding day.

This was nothing less than a carnival of the whole Party Center.

Outside, the deep of night, and utter stillness. Inside, the same racket, a room in an uproar.

Su Zhaozheng was drunk. Drunk, and moved by the scene from joy to grief, he gripped Zheng Chaolin's hand and said that Chen Yannian (Chen Duxiu's eldest son, member of the Party's Fifth Central Committee, alternate member of the Politburo, secretary of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee, arrested on June 6, 1927, and killed on July 4) had died too cruel a death, and that all they could do was swallow their tears.

Wang Ruofei was drunk. Drunk, Wang Ruofei waved his arms and stamped his feet and set about egging everyone on to rag Zheng Chaolin and his fiancée Liu Jingzhen. Zheng Chaolin, a mere scholar, could not hold them off, and fled out the back door into the street. Wang Ruofei would not let it rest but chased hard after him and hauled him bodily back off the road. Someone said enough, leave it, let it break up. Li Zheshi said, forgiving: "Let him play a little longer. A gathering like this will not come again." Wang Ruofei took heart, caught up the thread, and cried out gladly: "Sister-in-law has spoken. A gathering like this will not come again. No standing on ceremony!"

Decades on, looking back, Li Zheshi said with feeling past measure: "The facts have proved it: a gathering like that could never be had again."

Decades on, looking back, Zheng Chaolin still remembered that the "liveliest" one that night was He Zhihua, still remembered that graceful woman moving so easily among them all, radiant. Zheng Chaolin said: "He Zhihua was the liveliest, seeing to this guest and that. She and her sweetheart He Jiaxing lived downstairs in that very house."

### 3. Not Half a Minute to Spare

No one can still say for certain what the sky was like on April 15, 1928. As Luo Yinong strode out of Hengchangli that morning, as always, he might not lift his face to the leaden gray or the deep blue overhead, but he would never fail to use the moment of glancing down at his watch, or bending to flick the dust from his shoes, to take closer note of everything around him. The habit had formed of itself after Xiao Chunü, Chen Yannian, Zhao Shiyan, Xia Minghan, and others were killed one after another; it was forced on him by the White Terror of a reactionary regime that held it "better to kill three thousand by mistake than let one slip through." Only such vigilance could keep himself and others, the individual and the organization, safe.

Back from an inspection tour of the two Hu provinces, Hunan and Hubei, Luo Yinong had been lodging all this while at 34 Hengchangli on Yuyuan Road, which was the editorial office of Bolshevik, which was to say Zheng Chaolin's home.

Zheng Chaolin, born 1901, was from Zhangping in Fujian. In 1919 he went to France on the work-study program; in 1924 he joined the Chinese Communist Party; in 1927 he attended the Party's Fifth Congress and the August 7 Conference, and afterward followed the Party Center back to Shanghai, where, "as editorial-board editor-in-chief, or with the standing of a Propaganda Department secretary," he had charge of editing the Party Center's organ, Bolshevik.

It was Qu Qiubai who first had Zheng Chaolin take the lease on 34 Hengchangli. By Zheng Chaolin's own account in his memoirs, it was meant to be a "permanent liaison office to the various ports," because "the Center was preparing to move from Shanghai to Guangzhou once Ye Ting's army had taken Guangdong," and he was to be left "in Shanghai as director of the Center's Shanghai office." But when the Nanchang Uprising forces met grave defeat at Chaoshan, the plan to move to Guangzhou faded, and the house became instead the editorial office of Bolshevik and, with it, the Party's Central Propaganda Department. Zheng Chaolin took the south-facing front room on the second floor, and the office's secretary, Huang Jieran, the north-facing back room. (Zheng Chaolin, 1996)

Later Huang Jieran left and Luo Yinong came. Back from his tour of the two Hu, Luo Yinong took the north-facing back room that Huang Jieran had vacated; that is, he spent his nights at 34 Hengchangli, and by day he worked at Wangdeli, at the corner of Avenue Road (today Beijing West Road) and Gordon Road (today Jiangning Road).

On the evening of April 14, 1928, Li Weihan came to 34 Hengchangli on Yuyuan Road.

Seeing that Li Weihan was in a hurry, Luo Yinong dispensed with courtesies and asked at once what had brought him.

Li Weihan, standing where he was in the room, came straight to the point: "Tomorrow morning, people from two places are coming in to report to the Center. One is at Wangdeli on Avenue Road, the other on South Chengdu Road, east of Hardoon Garden. Which is easier for you?"

Luo Yinong answered without a second thought: "I'll take Wangdeli. I go there often to read documents, and I know He Jiaxing and He Zhihua, who keep the place."

Li Weihan said: "All right. You take Wangdeli, and I'll take South Chengdu Road."

By Zheng Chaolin's recollection in his later years, the organ Luo Yinong "often" went to for documents "seems to have been the third house in from the lane gate. It faced east, one floor over another, an ordinary Shanghai lane house." (Zheng Chaolin, 1995)

Yuyuan Road lies west of Jing'an Temple, Avenue Road east of it. Even on foot, half an hour at most would bring Luo Yinong from Hengchangli to Wangdeli. In other words, he would have reached Wangdeli that morning by ten o'clock at the latest.

Mu Xin (a former editor-in-chief of Guangming Daily and author of General Chen Geng) says that when Luo Yinong went, on the morning of April 15, 1928, from Hengchangli on Yuyuan Road to Wangdeli at the corner of Avenue Road and Gordon Road, it was to meet a delegate surnamed Wu who had come from Shandong. Zheng Chaolin, on the other hand, says this Wu was Wu Fang, "seemingly the secretary of the Nanjing Party committee," not a Shandong delegate at all, still less the secretary of the Shandong Provincial Committee. Zheng Chaolin said: "That day the foreign inspector charged in with his patrolmen. Luo Yinong was in the middle of talking with Wu Fang (Wu Fang seemingly the Nanjing Party secretary), and was arrested. Wu Fang gave himself out as a fruit seller, and the foreign inspector let him go." (Zheng Chaolin, 1995)

Shanghai's Jing'an has it, on the other hand, that when the patrolmen arrived the comrade from Shandong had not yet come, and that Luo Yinong, "understanding that the enemy was after him, and that the sooner he left the less the danger to the comrade from Shandong, drew the enemy away from his own lodging, calm and unruffled, to shield the comrade." (Li Guande, 2003)

There is also Deng Xiaoping's daughter Maomao (Deng Rong), who says her father, as an eyewitness, spoke of this in his lifetime. He told her: "That day I went to meet Luo Yinong. The business done, I had just gone out the back door when the patrolmen came in the front, and Luo Yinong was arrested. It was less than a minute between. Later Luo Yinong was shot. It was very dangerous then! Not half a minute to spare!" (Maomao, 1997)

To my mind, of the several possibilities the one nearest the truth of the matter is that Deng Xiaoping went to meet Luo Yinong, who was then talking with Wu Fang (a fellow townsman of Luo Yinong's, and a classmate besides at the Chuanshan School in Changsha, the Shanghai Foreign Language School, and the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow); that Deng had just left when a pistol with the safety off was pressed to Luo Yinong's chest.

Though Luo Yinong's whereabouts were unknown that night and he did not come home, Zheng Chaolin was only a little puzzled, not yet alarmed. The next day the courier Zhang Baoquan came to tell them that Luo Yinong had been arrested the day before at Wangdeli, and only then did everyone grow tense. Zheng Chaolin and his wife at once took a few clothes and moved to a hotel near the Great World, saying they were off to Hangzhou for a few days and would be back, and left the maid to keep house.

After Zheng Chaolin had moved, he went to Qu Qiubai's to learn what he could, and Yang Zhihua told him that word from the Gordon Road police station was that Luo Yinong had been sold by a married couple; but Qu Qiubai forbade her to say more.

### 4. A Rescue Dressed as a Funeral

Luo Yinong's sudden arrest was the first serious blow the Party Center had suffered since its return from Wuhan to Shanghai, and the Center took it with the utmost gravity. Zhou Enlai, then a member of the Standing Committee of the Central Politburo and head of the Organization Bureau, together with Gu Shunzhang, then an alternate member of the Central Politburo and a chief of the Central Special Branch, at once set the contingency plan in motion: on the one hand to attempt a rescue, on the other to pull out and relocate everyone concerned.

Zhang Guotao, in his Memoirs (1991), says: "One afternoon at two o'clock, Xiang Ying came running to tell me that two hours before, Luo Jue (Luo Yinong), secretary of the Shanghai District Committee, had been arrested at his secret office. This sudden turn might mean there was an informer among us, and might spread to the whole central apparatus. Only one courier knew my address, but he was a new comrade, and if he were taken there was no guaranteeing he would not give it away. Xiang Ying had just then found a new address that not a soul knew of, and he wanted my wife and me to hide there a while and take stock once the situation was clear. After we reached Xiang Ying's new address, he went out to make inquiries."

At the same time, Zhou Enlai had already sent people to sound out the concession authorities, hoping to buy off those grasping, money-hungry men and purchase Luo Yinong's freedom with cash.

While Zhou Enlai was doing this, Gu Shunzhang took Li Zheshi about with him, going every day to see men of the Green and Red Gangs, hoping through them to work on the reactionary regime's leading figures.

The men Gu Shunzhang took Li Zheshi to see talked wild and named an outrageous price: nothing less than forty thousand yuan. Reckoning that men so honey-tongued and hollow-hearted, so greedy and so faithless, would likely take the money and still not free him, and leave the Party out both the man and the money, the Center decided instead to put up twenty thousand yuan and, when the enemy transferred Luo Yinong from the concession to Longhua, to waylay the party and seize him back.

It was a plan at once ingenious and very sure of success. Its core was this: men of the Special Branch's Red Squad would dress as a funeral party and walk behind Li Zheshi in her mourning weeds, with guns hidden in the coffin, and when the prison van passed, they would open fire, rush forward, and fight to the death to save Luo Yinong.

So, on Gu Shunzhang's instructions, Li Zheshi went to the He Jiaxing couple's to feel out the news.

Li Zheshi waited several hours before He Zhihua came. He Zhihua wore a sky-blue long-sleeved velvet dress and a pink gauze shawl, stylish and lovely both.

The moment He Zhihua saw Li Zheshi she gave a foreigner's shrug, hands spread, the very picture of helplessness.

He Zhihua said she too had been terrified, and that had she not had her languages, German and English both, to leave the foreign inspector at a loss, she and He Jiaxing would not have come safely through.

Seeing the tears seep from the corners of Li Zheshi's eyes, He Zhihua at once made a show of tenderness and warmth, took her hand, drew her close, sat beside her on the edge of the bed, and said again and again how deeply she felt for her; then, low in her ear: "Whatever you do, don't cry out loud. We moved here in secret. The neighbors mustn't learn of the business over on Gordon Road."

Li Zheshi wanted He Zhihua to go again to the foreign inspector and learn when Luo Yinong would be transferred. He Zhihua said that to shield Wu Fang, who had come to make contact, and the Party's documents, she had at the time slipped the foreign inspector a diamond ring, but that he was not likely to grant any further favors for it.

Knowing she would get no answer soon, Li Zheshi could only leave. About a day later she went again to He Zhihua. It was gone nine in the morning when she got there, and He Zhihua was still lying in bed; only when she saw her come did she rise, languid, and say: "No telling. They won't tell us."

### 5. She Wanted Two Passports and a Fortune in Dollars

Early on the morning of April 22, 1928, Gu Shunzhang came again to where Li Zheshi lodged, woke her from her exhaustion, and said: "Get to Longhua, quick. On one of the roads there are four big characters, 'Wenzhi University.' Go and see what's posted on the telephone poles."

Li Zheshi at once hailed a cab and went straight to Longhua. The moment she saw the four square iron plates, "Wen-zhi-uni-versity," one character to a plate, hung on a wire strung across the mouth of a road, she got down and looked for a telephone pole, and read the notice pasted there: "By order of Chiang ××... The wanted Communist criminal Luo Yinong is to be shot at once. Qian Dajun, Commander of the Songhu Garrison. April 22."

Li Zheshi's legs would not move.

The kind cab driver helped Li Zheshi up and walked her on. She never knew how long she walked, or how far, before she made out, on a patch of grass set back on the right of the road, a pool of fresh blood, and beside it on the ground a paper marker toppled at an angle, reading "Wanted Red bandit Luo Yinong," and one clean white handkerchief, neatly folded.

The handkerchief was one Li Zheshi had given Luo Yinong.

Li Zheshi crumpled without knowing it, her right sleeve falling into the pool of blood.

Li Zheshi's weeping drew a crowd of passers-by.

One of them, a man in black with a blue cloth bundle on his back, gave a long sigh and asked Li Zheshi to come and rest a while at his house, not far off. He told her the execution had been carried out at two the afternoon before. At the last, before the shots, he had heard someone cry out, loud: "Long live the Communist Party!"

Li Zheshi went with him, still along that road. A little way on, on a plot of earth to the right, stood a new grave, a mound of yellow clay with a tuft of green grass planted on top, and in the ceaseless drizzle it bore no mark at all.

Li Zheshi made her offerings to Luo Yinong at Longhua, then went to Hengchangli to gather his things. The instant she entered that second-floor north-facing back room she threw herself on the bed where he had slept and wept aloud.

The next day Zhou Enlai arranged for Li Zheshi to stay with Wang Yizhi, the widow of Zhang Tailei. Later he came specially to see her, and finding her face streaming, choked past speech, yet afraid of rousing the neighbors, he had to bury his face in a basin of water and wash and weep at once.

And yet Li Zheshi was far from the only one who grieved for Luo Yinong.

On May 30, 1928, the foreword to the twentieth issue of Bolshevik mourned, in bitter grief, Luo Yinong's heroic death. His murder, the foreword held, had "robbed the Chinese proletariat of one of its most ardent leaders, and robbed the Chinese Communist Party of one of its bravest fighters." It called on the Chinese proletariat to keep their leader firmly in memory, to learn from Comrade Luo Yinong's ardent revolutionary spirit, and to "avenge" him.

The Central Special Branch, resolved to avenge Luo Yinong, followed Zhou Enlai's instructions: first list every suspicious sign in the affair, then trace the vine to the melon and get to the bottom of the mystery of his death.

Xiang Ying told Zhang Guotao that Luo Yinong's arrest had indeed come of an informer, and that the ramifications ran very wide. By word from the police station, first a beautiful woman who could speak German and English went of her own accord to the head of the station's political section to inform: she said she had in hand a list, with addresses, of more than 350 Communist Party members, most of them figures from the Nanchang Uprising. Let the station only promise her two foreign passports and fifty thousand American dollars, send her to a country of her choosing, and keep her secret, and she would hand it over. She said, too, that she was willing to give up Luo Yinong's address first and have the station arrest him at once, to prove her word true; after which the two sides would meet the terms and close the deal. The station acted on her word, and duly took Luo Yinong.

What Zheng Chaolin heard was that a woman first handed a letter to the Chinese patrolman on duty at the corner of Avenue Road and Gordon Road, to be passed to the station. The letter said that above where she lived was a Communist organ, that an important Communist, Luo Yinong, came there daily to work, and that she wanted the station to make the arrest, the exchange being passage abroad for her and her husband.

That being so, all eyes fell naturally on He Zhihua. She and her husband He Jiaxing lived at Wangdeli. Only she and her husband knew for certain that the man who came to work at Wangdeli each day was Luo Yinong, was an "important Communist."

Li Zheshi said that He Zhihua "was the concubine Comrade Zhu De took when he was an old-style officer in Sichuan, clever and beautiful, and he had taken her to Germany. She had picked up German too, and learned to ride and to dance. After Comrade Zhu De joined the Party and went to study at the Red Army Academy in Moscow, He Zhihua was at the University of the Toilers of the East, but she would not settle to her studies and clamored to see Zhu De. Discipline at the Red Army Academy was strict; no one could be received except on holidays. So she took up with He Jiaxing, a fellow student at the University who had studied in France, and after they came home she put a notice in the paper demanding to sever relations with Comrade Zhu De. Comrade Zhu De answered in the paper too, that he 'respected Madame He's wishes...'" (Li Wenyi, 2004) He Zhihua had a daughter by Zhu De, namely Zhu Min. Zhu Min said her own fair complexion was inherited from her mother: "My parents studied together in Germany and took part in revolutionary work together, but my mother's thinking changed greatly, and though she and Papa lived together, their hearts grew ever further apart, until at last she left my Papa altogether." (Zhu Min, 2001)

By Zhang Guotao's account, before He Jiaxing and He Zhihua went to work at Luo Yinong's side, they had worked at "a central organ that secretly received comrades coming up from Chaoshan." Zhou Enlai had sent Zhang Guotao to that organ "to direct the placing of the ordinary comrades who had fled from Chaoshan to Shanghai." Zhang Guotao knew that He Zhihua "had gone to Germany with Zhu De," that "when she came back from Germany through Moscow to Wuhan" there were again "some untrustworthy stories" about her, that she was suspected of being one of Zhang Zuolin's spies, and so he "worked there only two mornings, and after that would not go anymore." (Zhang Guotao, 1991)

By Li Weihan's account, the He Jiaxing and He Zhihua couple had at first kept an organ at Qu Qiubai's, living below him and posing as second landlords. But the two of them were forever bickering, quarreling past all bearing, and they had to be moved to keep the organ at Xinzha Village on Xinzha Road.

By the account of Zhang Guotao's wife Yang Zilie, He Zhihua, in her years abroad, "wherever she went carried on with young foreigners, old or young, turning none away." In Soviet Russia "she was as loose as ever." "The secretary of the Moscow branch of the University of the Toilers of the East was then Luo Yinong. Luo, shrewd and capable, had long heard what sort of woman He Zhihua was and thought very little of her, and at once separated her from Zhu De; Zhu De went off somewhere for military training, while He Zhihua was kept at the University, and from then on she hated Luo Yinong bitterly." But Yang Zilie also said: "He Zhihua was indeed a bewitching creature, willow brows and almond eyes, a smile in her anger and joy folded into her sorrow; before Zhou Enlai, Luo Yinong, and the other senior men she burned like a ball of fire, and who would ever have suspected the heart of a viper in her?" (Yang Zilie, 1970)

Could it truly be that so beautiful a woman, "burning like a ball of fire," with a "viper's heart" that roused not the faintest "suspicion" in anyone, sold out her own leader, the leader of the Party? Our Party never harms the innocent or wrongs a good man without cause; neither does it wink at treachery or let a bad one go.

To get to the bottom of He Zhihua and her husband He Jiaxing, Li Weihan sent people to them and first had them move out of Wangdeli and stay a few days at a small inn on Bubbling Well Road (today Nanjing West Road). Bubbling Well Road then had many large shops, some with a small mezzanine between two ground-floor storefronts, reached by a stair, all connected above and with several back doors. It was such a place that Li Weihan found for them: up a little stair to an inn above.

Once they were settled, Li Weihan went himself to soothe the still-shaken He Jiaxing and He Zhihua, and said: "You've had a fright. You can't stay at the old place. Better find another house and lie low a while." As he spoke he dug out some money and sent them to find new lodgings. Before he left, Li Weihan pressed on them again and again not to forget to tell the contact their new address in good time, so that touch would not be lost.

Watching Li Weihan's back recede, the money he had given clutched in their hands, He Jiaxing and He Zhihua must have felt a heavy stone drop from their hearts. So no sooner had they found the new lodging on Rue Bourgeat than they got in touch with the contact Li Weihan had named and gave the exact address, all of it, plainly.

They never dreamed that when Li Weihan came to see them at the inn that day, the Central Special Branch had laid its plans down to the last detail, with men above and below, close watch and covering fire on the front and back ways. Less still did they dream that the contact Li Weihan had named was, from the first, a Red Squad man whose charge was "beating the dogs."

He Jiaxing, with Li Weihan's money, moved contentedly into the big open second-floor room at 178 Rue Bourgeat, and that night lay down a little before He Zhihua. He got into bed but did not lie down at once; half-propped against the headboard, he put her a question: "Tell me, do you really believe it ends here, that there's no more trouble?"

He Zhihua, sitting at the dressing table taking off her makeup, wiping the powder gently from her face, answered without turning her head: "We'll pass our last days before going abroad here, quietly."

He Jiaxing asked again: "You're sure that within half a year we'll be free and far away?"

He Zhihua answered again: "I think I can smell the Aegean already."

He Jiaxing laughed, and He Zhihua laughed. One laughed brazenly, the other with reserve.

Had they known that Chen Geng and his comrades were already outside, gathering quietly, slipping in slowly, they would surely not have laughed again, nor had any brazenness or reserve left in them.

A great net was cast, drawn softly closed, and cinched without a sound.

### 6. The Truth of the Killing, Amid the Firecrackers

At around seven on the morning of April 25, 1928, the action against the traitors was formally launched.

Before it, the Central Special Branch had already gathered ample and certain proof that He Zhihua had turned traitor and gone over to the enemy.

Li Weihan recalled in his later years: "It came out afterward that He Zhihua had been in touch with the police station early and had made her arrangements in advance. The word at the time was that her price for selling Luo Yinong was three thousand American dollars, and passage abroad for the pair of them." (Li Weihan, 2002)

On the morning of April 15, the moment Luo Yinong reached Wangdeli, He Zhihua, on the pretext of fetching hot water, had He Jiaxing tip off the patrolman at the corner of Avenue Road and Gordon Road. The patrolman telephoned the station from his sentry box, and the Settlement inspector Locke came at once with two men to take Luo Yinong. He Zhihua greeted and spoke with them in German, and slipped Locke a diamond ring besides, so that Locke let He Jiaxing go and left the documents in the drawer.

Every day a traitor went free at ease was another day of hidden danger to the Party. Li Weihan, after conferring with Deng Xiaoping, decided the traitors must be eliminated. Li Weihan then met Chen Geng upstairs at the Central Secretariat and ordered the Special Branch's Red Squad to take decisive measures against the He Jiaxing and He Zhihua couple.

Mu Xin, in Zhou Enlai, Commander of the Hidden Front, says: "In the small hours of April 25, Chen Geng, on the Center's instructions, led the Red Squad in person, broke into the hotel where the He Jiaxing couple were staying, set one man to guard the back door, and took three men up. At the same time, on the pretext of a wedding, they set off deafening firecrackers outside the hotel." (Mu Xin, 2013)

And so some say the killing of the He Jiaxing and He Zhihua couple took place at the "Asia Hotel," where at the time "a bridal sedan came slowly up a small lane off Nanjing Road, gongs and drums and suona pipes making merry," and "some of the wedding party set off firecrackers, so that in a moment the crackers and the music blared as one." (Wang Jun, 2009) Others say the He Jiaxing and He Zhihua couple lived on the "topmost" floor of "a four-story French building," with "a French patrolman" on watch at the door besides; that a five-man "Special Branch action team" under Kang Sheng, using their "consummate skill at scaling walls, rolling up the bead curtain, and piercing a willow leaf at a hundred paces," "broke through the door" and shot He Jiaxing, "a pistol in his hand," until "his chest took round after round, thick as a honeycomb." (Xu Wenlong, 1996) This second version rather resembles the matching scene in the television drama The Road Through Vicissitudes, where "Liqing" too "brings an action team," goes down a rope let over the roof, "in through the window, tumbling onto the floor. A carp's flip, and up he springs from the broken glass, a pistol in each hand," and cuts down "the traitors He Zhihua and He Jiaxing, who sold out Comrade Luo Yinong." (Jiang Qitao, 2009)

In fact the true course of the whole affair was this. At around seven that morning the wet-nurse, Wu née Liu, was first up, and seeing He Jiaxing and He Zhihua sitting side by side on the bed reading the paper, she took the kettle and went out the back door to the nearby laohuzao (an old-Shanghai shop selling boiled water, so called because its stove was shaped like a crouching tiger) to buy some. When she came back with the water, there were already four strangers in the scullery: three in Chinese dress filing upstairs, one in a Western suit who pressed a revolver to her forehead, signing to her to keep still and hold her tongue. She stood at once, meek, like a figure of carved wood or molded clay. Outside, firecrackers thundered; up and down the lane the crackers went off together, deafening, without a break. The man in the Western suit put his gun away, joined the three who had come down from upstairs, and, covering one another, they withdrew one by one and left at their ease. It was only when the little girl upstairs screamed and screamed herself hoarse that the second landlord and his wife, in their robes and bare feet, hair wild and faces filthy, came rushing from their room, bawling at Wu née Liu.

No wonder the second landlord and his wife were in a fury. First thing in the morning, firecrackers and crackers, then wailing and uproar, a fine dream shattered, a good sleep spoiled: put anyone in their place and he too would look fit to eat you, the whole of his anger written on his face.

It was as well that the second landlord and his wife gave her a good tongue-lashing, which roused the stunned Wu née Liu, so that she could at last stammer out, trembling, the uncanny scene she had just lived through.

Xia Yukui went ahead, the two women following behind, taking courage from each other, up the stairs to see.

Upstairs was a scene of wreckage. Their tenants, who had been reading and joking on the bed only just before, were in the turn of an eye one dead and one hurt, flesh and blood a ruin, the bullet holes livid. Wu née Liu lost the three souls from her head and the five from her feet, went down like a heap of muddy slime, and fainted before the half-open peachwood door.

Xia Yukui at once raised the alarm and telephoned the Lokawei police station. The patrolmen turned out at the call, swarmed to the spot, sealed it off, and gathered evidence.

On examination, He Jiaxing had at least two mortal wounds, one beside the right nipple, one at the temple. He Zhihua by good luck still lived, shot in the head and blinded in one eye, and was rushed to Guangci Hospital (today Ruijin Hospital) on Route Père Robert (today Ruijin No. 2 Road).

After a while the men from the concession's Mixed Court arrived and, by procedure, summoned the eyewitness and pressed Wu née Liu: "Did the gunmen ransack your tenants' rooms? Is any gold or silver or valuables missing?" Wu née Liu answered: "No. Certainly not." The patrolmen too said nothing in the room had been disturbed. So the Mixed Court men had them take Xia Yukui and Wu née Liu (who took along the little girl from the tingzijian) back to Lokawei to give statements, leaving the patrolman Jiang Yanchun to guard the scene.

That afternoon Zhou Enlai went first to the apartment-hotel near the Great World amusement park to see Zheng Chaolin and Liu Jingzhen, and told them that Luo Yinong had been sold by the He Jiaxing and He Zhihua couple. Not long after Zhou Enlai left, Deng Xiaoping came. It was dusk, the sunset clouds flying, the western sky all the color of blood. Deng Xiaoping asked Zheng Chaolin for the evening paper, his manner a little out of the ordinary. Zheng Chaolin went straight out and bought one, and reading it there in the street came upon a murder that had happened early that day in the French Concession: a young couple newly moved into 178 Rue Bourgeat had been attacked amid firecrackers, the man dead in his bed, the woman rolled to the floor and gravely hurt.

On the afternoon of March 7, 2007, Chen Geng's son Chen Zhijian gave an interview in Beijing to the crew of Spy War Uncovered, on the Archives program of the Shanghai Television documentary channel. Chen Zhijian said that what his father "always regretted was not finishing He Jiaxing's wife off. He put one round into her, into her head, and she still didn't die; they saved her afterward. Once she was saved, she said not a word but bolted. They looked for her again and couldn't find her, and in the end learned she was in America, and by then she was beyond reach."

Chen Zhijian's account chimed with Li Weihan's own recollection. In Comrade Li Weihan on the Circumstances of Comrade Luo Yinong's Death (2002), Li Weihan said: "Early in the 'Cultural Revolution' the 'rebels' suspected that Luo Yinong's death was a trap I had set, a plot of mine, and made me account for it. I told them the whole course of it, and afterward they questioned me no further. But they made inquiries, and later told me He Zhihua was still alive."

Whereas Love Beyond Blood says that He Zhihua, hunted, "lost her reason and soon died." (Gu Baozi, 1998)

And whereas A Critical Biography of Yang Zhihua says that He Zhihua "was wounded but did not die, and later went to Sichuan, remarried, and died of illness before Liberation." (Chen Fukang and Ding Yanmo, 2005)

And Zhu Min, for her part, says it was in her fourteenth year that she first learned her mother had left this world. That morning "Grandmother, still in her quilt," called her by her pet name and, weeping, said: "Feifei, Grandmother can't keep you any longer. Your surname is Zhu; you are of the Zhu family, a child of the Communist Party. If those secret agents were ever to learn who you are, Grandmother... I couldn't bear the blame... How would I answer to your father? How would I answer to Mr. Zhou Enlai and Madame Deng? And worse still, I would fail your dead... mother..." (Zhu Min, 2001)

On investigation, He Zhihua, once healed of her wound, returned to Kaijiang in Sichuan and married again and bore two daughters. In 1938 the reactionaries came to Kaijiang, dragged He Zhihua from an inner room, and shot her in the courtyard, with the utmost savagery, forcing even her two daughters to look on with open eyes.

### 7. What Did He Zhihua Really Want?

For a long time most people have named not He Zhihua but her husband He Jiaxing as the chief defendant. He Zhihua's guilt ran deeper and heavier than He Jiaxing's, yet she lived the longer.

In The Count of Monte Cristo, the Abbé Faria says: "Whoever stands to gain the most from a plot is the one who directs it." So what plot did He Zhihua direct, what part did she play, what gain did she seek?

Was it truly no more than two foreign passports and three thousand or fifty thousand American dollars?

That was Chen Yangshan's belief, and his view was set down by his son Chen Jianyu in The Legend of Chen Yangshan, Lucky General of the Hidden Front (2006). That book traces the very source of the bad blood, and finally the falling-out and rupture, between He Zhihua and He Jiaxing on the one side and Luo Yinong on the other to the gulf between the poverty of the great majority of our Party's underground workers and the luxury of He Zhihua and He Jiaxing, and to Luo Yinong's bitter distress at that gulf. In the last hours of his life, this was the very thing Chen Jianyu pressed on me again and again.

On the morning of March 8, 2007, Chen Jianyu, ill, gave an interview in Beijing to the crew of Spy War Uncovered, on the Archives program of the Shanghai Television documentary channel. Chen Jianyu said: "He Zhihua and He Jiaxing were always out, either soaking in a dance hall till midnight or drinking themselves blind in a bar, always coming back to the organ very, very late, breaking the rules of secrecy, so Luo Yinong criticized them a few times. They bore Luo Yinong a grudge; they wanted revenge on him. Our people then lived rather hard, twenty yuan a month to live on. He Zhihua and He Jiaxing had grown used to soft living abroad, and twenty yuan was nowhere near enough, so they wanted to make something on the side. What to do? They made a connection with the police station in the British Concession. They traded our Party's leaders for foreign currency, to live abroad at their ease. Luo Yinong was the first one they sold."

But we must point out that all this is plausible and false, and very hard to verify. The same claims are nowhere to be found in the memoirs of Li Weihan, Zheng Chaolin, Li Zheshi, Zhang Guotao, and the rest; and set beside them, Chen Yangshan cannot be counted a witness to the affair. For Chen Yangshan joined the Central Special Branch, with Zhou Enlai's assent, only because he had recruited Yang Dengying (also called Bao Junfu) as the Party's first counter-intelligence relationship, by which time more than a month had passed since Luo Yinong's death.

On March 7, 1925, in a letter to Li Ji and Chen Qixiu, Zhu De wrote: "Last winter I wished to go to Moscow with Comrade Xinnong, but Moscow refused for want of places, and over the matter of the German cell's application, Zhihua was pressed out of the Party... I am now resolved to set out for Moscow two months hence. If the University of the Toilers of the East admits me, I will enroll and attend lectures; if it will not admit me, I will join the Moscow cell for some training, and even to lodge outside for a few months I would not refuse. Should Zhihua come with me, only the matter of housing will not be easy. If I am not allowed into the school, I must ask you to help me find lodging, for I am determined to come. And if in future I cannot stay, I will return to China and serve the Party alone." (Zhu De, 1997)

The "Zhihua" of Zhu De's letter is He Zhihua, and Zhu De's resolve then, one believes, was also He Zhihua's resolve. At that time He Zhihua must still have been of one mind with Zhu De, showing herself in the main as pure and single-hearted, "serving the Party alone"; but that she later gave her heart elsewhere does not mean her spirit was any less high.

On the question of her mother's desertion of her father, Zhu Min takes Zhu De's side wholly, saying "Mother treated Papa badly and grieved him." He Jiaxing, in his time at "the University" and on through the "split between the Kuomintang and the Communists," young and hot-blooded, must have had his points too, or how would He Zhihua have grown so "quiet" thereafter? And how would comrades in the Party have come to "all think He Jiaxing had a way with her, could keep He Zhihua in hand"? (Yang Zilie, 1970)

But neither could He Jiaxing satisfy He Zhihua as long or as well as Zhu De had. Li Weihan said: "The He Zhihua couple had at first kept an organ at Qu Qiubai's; Qu Qiubai lived upstairs and they below, posing as second landlords, and at night the two of them were always quarreling fiercely." (Li Weihan, 2002) This should be the very sign that He Zhihua was no longer content with He Jiaxing.

One believes that by then He Zhihua's eyes had turned to Luo Yinong.

The He Zhihua of this time meant to gather the moon nearest the water: to use her work as cover, slip into the breach, walk out of He Jiaxing's dull mediocrity and up to Luo Yinong, who had both Zhu De's standing and power and He Jiaxing's youth and ardor.

After all, Luo Yinong then was alone, without wife or family.

After all, He Zhihua was amorous by nature, and needed only to "meet Zhou Enlai, Luo Yinong, or another of the senior men" to "burn like a ball of fire."

After all, "He Zhihua was indeed a beautiful woman, thirty or thereabouts, nimble and well-spoken." (Peng Shuzhi, 2016)

And yet, when she made sure that the one Luo Yinong loved was Li Zheshi and not her, things changed in kind.

{v} He Zhihua saw Bilan and me come to visit Luo Yinong, and taking the chance while Bilan chatted, holding the little girl, with Luo Yinong's wife (Luo's wife was an old schoolmate of Bilan's from their Hubei Normal days and a comrade in the student movements, and had once been secretary of the Wuhan Women's Department), drew me into her room to sit. She rambled on to me about a good many things, chiefly that Luo Yinong now... was not like a leader of the Communist Party. One had only to look at all the Xiang embroidery hung on the walls to see their luxury. Why did she say all this to me? Because she reckoned, of course, that I was still an old leader of the Party and could give Luo Yinong a stern dressing-down before her, though I only listened and let it pass. For I knew how she had once lived in luxury with Zhu De in Sichuan, and in Germany too had gone in for all that ballroom dancing..., whereas now she had become a woman of no weight in the Party, and her husband held no position either. And Luo Yinong would not think much of her, which made her very jealous, and this was the source of the great tragedy that came after. (Peng Shuzhi, 2016)

Just because "Luo Yinong would not think much of her," the abandon of her spirits at Luo Yinong and Li Zheshi's wedding left a deep impression on everyone there, Yang Zhihua and Zheng Chaolin among them. She could not face with any calm the sight of Li Zheshi, all happiness, nestled warm in Luo Yinong's arms. She turned her liveliness into a venting of the heart.

He Zhihua and He Jiaxing, in the end, destroyed themselves in the very act of selling out Luo Yinong.

This, of course, is only one more possibility among many.

Perhaps it is for just this reason that Zhu Min, far from grieving in silence that "from my earliest memory I had neither father nor mother beside me," said to herself instead, stubbornly: "My mother forsook me, and even betrayed my Papa, to pursue a way of life wholly unlike Papa's faith, and utterly changed her whole life. On that count, as her daughter, I cannot forgive her." (Zhu Min, 2001)
