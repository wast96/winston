## Chapter 3

It was not long after that night before Stephen and I were together almost every other day. He was a medical officer aboard an American warship, thirty-two years old that year, lively and full of play. So long as there was fun to be had, his spirits never flagged. In those days I was like everyone else on the Solitary Island: in a life of alarm and unease I could not always settle to my work — and my work called for a very quiet mind, being a study in ethics and aesthetics, an attempt to trace beauty and goodness to a single philosophical source and, on that footing, to write a book. So I was driven to buy a passing stimulation and oblivion with money, and in this became Stephen's close companion in pleasure. By his own account, it was only after he began to go about with me that he first set foot on Chinese soil, came into contact with Chinese society, began to taste Chinese food of every kind, entered Chinese dance halls, and mixed with Chinese women.

In the past he had always frequented a few bars along Avenue Joffre and, on Bubbling Well Road and Yuyuan Road, a few dance halls got up for foreign soldiers; the rare times he ate Chinese food, it was always at Cantonese places that catered to Westerners alone. But now he often went with me to the little eating-houses of Fourth Avenue, and liked to seek out Chinese dance-hostesses who spoke no pidgin, and had even learned to give his friendship to the ones he favoured.

In the past he never went out but in uniform; now he liked to come out in plain clothes. From curiosity about the Chinese way of life he passed, by degrees, to being at home in it, and in the end to loving it.

In the past he liked to speak English with me; now he spoke Chinese, with a fine humour, and took it in good part when the dance-hostesses beside us laughed at his laboured Chinese.

He was a curious, healthy, straightforward, restless boy, quick to take an interest in anything new, and forever making sport of the things he disliked. His talk was bold and open, yet never vulgar; he was careless with money, spending it the moment he had it, never a thought for the future. That a man of such a temper should be my friend naturally had a great effect on my own state of mind. I too had once been fond of loose living and play, but what I loved more was to lose myself in the deeper arts and in nature. For the monotonous so-called pleasures of the senses that the city offers, I had only the feeling of a passing game — now and then a little noise and gaiety with a few friends, but never any strong taste for it. Now, first, because within the ring of the Solitary Island one could no longer travel out to the hills and streams, and second, because the misery of my spirits made work impossible while chances for the enjoyment of art were few and mostly tied to fixed hours — for these reasons I was very willing to be with him. But whenever I had played to excess and, growing weary, began to want to quiet down and read or write in peace, then let a few days pass without my seeing Stephen, and he would be sure to come looking for me — often in the dead of night, humming a tune, tapping at my lit glass window. Only when my lamp was out would he forbear to ring the bell; and when I came out myself to open the door, the moment he was in he would clap me on the shoulder and say, brisk and merry:

"Reading, in times of chaos like these?"

Then, by one means and another, he would work on me until my thinking dissolved altogether and my mind wandered clean off its subject, and in the end I would give in to him. Sometimes, when I had a letter to finish, he would wait beside me, switch on the wireless, hum to himself, and wait until I had written it and risen to change my clothes — picking out a necktie for me the while — and then he would take up the telephone and call a car, and we would go off and play till daybreak.

Naturally there were times when I sought him out too, but always by telephone; he had no fixed lodging, and the numbers I knew were two — one the C. R. Club, one Dr. Philip's clinic — the places he most often went. Whenever I reached him he was always in fine spirits, and there was never a day he failed to keep an engagement.

So the friendship went on all along — warm, romantic, full of interest; each of us seemed not to know whether the other had some cold private pain or a real, workaday life, or else we understood that side of each other too well, and so, of all things, never brought it up, never asked after the other's calling or work, and never once thought of using or helping one another. I knew nothing of his finances; I myself was forever in straits, yet never asked him for a loan — only, in our places of amusement, I let him pay all the bills, and he never reckoned such things, and when it fell to me to pay, he stood on no ceremony either.

Now and then he stayed the night at my place, but never ate there; his only object was that, on waking, he might go out with me again to carry on that life of wine and gold and glitter. When my appetite for play still ran high, one such stay would often last four or five days.

A boy like this, said to have a wife — who on earth would believe it? So although it plainly read "C. L. Stephen," I still suspected it was someone else.

Might it not be his elder brother?

Though I did not know his brother.

But he could have his brother do the inviting for him.

Then would his brother, too, be a C. L. Stephen?

Perhaps, being an officer, he ordinarily used his brother's name for his everyday dealings with the world.

I telephoned to find him at once, but could not; and this kept me uneasy and perplexed until, toward evening, a letter came telling me half the secret. It ran thus:

"Dear friend:

"Surprised you, haven't I? That I should actually have a wife — beautiful and virtuous, lovable and worthy of respect. I feared that, in your strangeness and doubt, you might not come, and so I write you this letter; and I hope that you too have a wife I have never known of, to astonish me at the dinner-dance. Failing that, I hope you will bring Bai Ping.

"C. L. Stephen"

The half-secret I speak of is this: that the card was indeed sent by Stephen, but very likely as a joke of his — that he had simply found some dance-hostess with a birthday, one perhaps of my acquaintance, borrowed a place for a night's amusement, and sent out this absurd invitation.

I would go, of course; and of course I had no wife to bring. As for dance-hostesses, I had plenty I might bring. I rather wished to bring someone he did not know, to surprise him, but feared he would take her for my wife; and since it was his wife's birthday, it was only fitting to bring someone who spoke English and had the better hand at company. That must be why he had named Bai Ping — and so I settled on her.
