## Longhua

The sixteenth of the last lunar month. From early morning the sky hung overcast, wrapped in heavy fog.

The gate of the Songhu Garrison Command, close by the Longhua Temple, stood only half open. Its four wooden door-leaves were sheathed in bulletproof iron plate; above the gatehouse the Blue Sky and White Sun flag flew high, and through the loopholes in the battlements the barrels of machine guns could dimly be made out. To the left of the gate, beneath the placard of the Songhu Garrison Command, stood two sentries shouldering bayoneted rifles; to the right, beneath the placard of the 32nd Army of the National Revolutionary Army, stood three more, likewise with rifles at the shoulder.

The two-story Western building directly facing the Garrison Command gate was quiet as ever, and Mu Chuan studied it covertly as he went in. The courtyard was overgrown with weeds, the brick path slick underfoot, the weeds forcing their way up without cease from between the bricks.

He walked into the office of the Judge Advocate, and as he turned to draw the door shut he glanced at the Bao'en Pagoda beyond the compound wall of the Songhu Garrison Command, murmured an Amitābha to himself out of habit, took off his overcoat, and called the orderly to carry it outside and beat the dust from it.

He drank a few mouthfuls of hot tea and, as was his custom, went to take a turn about the compound, as though inspecting his own domain. The lockup, the courtroom, the guards, the motor pool, the cells, the walls, the barbed-wire entanglements—half-consciously he hoped to make out something in the cold, dead winter light. In Nanjing, in Suzhou, he had liked to do the same. His round complete, he returned to the office and drank a few more mouthfuls of the black tea the orderly had brewed.

“Ask Captain You to come in,” he said, as if lost in thought.

The words were scarcely out when a hoarse voice came from beyond the door: “Director Mu, Tianxiao is here.”

Though You Tianxiao was Mu Chuan's subordinate, he held another, secret post: he was the man the Kuomintang's Central Party Affairs Investigation Section had stationed in Shanghai. The Investigation Section was a shadowy body. Its public address was on Dingjiaqiao in Nanjing, and for a time it had been housed within the Party's Central Organization Department, though that office held only a handful of clerks; its true stronghold lay elsewhere, near the Central Hotel, and later, as its numbers swelled, the Investigation Section moved again, into the Zhanyuan garden—those in the know all called it the Special Operations Headquarters.

This body dealt in political cases and nothing else, investigating not only the Communists but dissidents within the Party's own ranks. Toward the organization Mu Chuan did not look askance as so many of his colleagues did; but in honesty, even with You Tianxiao he was ordinarily no more than studiously civil.

You Tianxiao was not tall; his complexion had a greenish cast, and the corners of his eyes were often threaded with red. He stood before Mu Chuan's desk gripping a paper parcel. Being in uniform, he gave a perfunctory salute, and set down on the desk the book he carried, The Theory and Practice of Secret-Service Work.

“Director Mu, I've brought the book you wanted.”

Mu Chuan glanced at You Tianxiao and told the orderly to step out. He picked up the book, looked at the cover, idly turned two pages, and said with a smile as he laid it away in a drawer: “I've long admired this one from afar. It must have cost you no little trouble?”

“Few copies were printed, and there's someone set to guard them; the application formalities took a little time.”

“Very thorough!” Mu Chuan waved him to a seat. “There's no need to be so on edge about every little thing, though. Those machine guns up on the gatehouse could come down altogether, if you ask me.”

You Tianxiao did not take his meaning, and for a moment the two of them were silent.

“How long since you were last in Nanjing? You ought to make time to go and look in.”

“Nanjing is often in my thoughts—” You Tianxiao fixed his eyes on Mu Chuan's teacup. “They tell me you go back to Nanjing often, Director?”

“Hardly—” Mu Chuan, just reaching for his teacup, paused, tapping the rim lightly with a finger. “Who told you that?”

“They say you go up to Nanjing every week for meetings, Director.”

Mu Chuan smiled and leaned back in his chair. “Well—as they all say, Nanjing is where the work is done, but Shanghai is where one lives.”

“The work that falls to me is all in Shanghai.”

Mu Chuan burst out laughing, which puzzled You Tianxiao a little; he had plainly said something in all seriousness, only to have it taken for a joke.

“You had a hard day of it yesterday, Captain—but—” Mu Chuan lit a cigarette and passed one to You Tianxiao as well. “—it came off splendidly, all the same.”

“Six Communists taken; the rest got away. The library is run by foreigners in the Settlement, and their meeting-place was a room behind the stacks that no one ever goes into. There may be Communists among the librarians; the detective squad will go on looking into it.”

“What about the one who jumped—I hear the Municipal Police botched it?”

“The Municipal Police let word leak.” You Tianxiao lit his cigarette, and all at once slowed his speech. “The detective squad can't just make arrests in the Settlement as it pleases; we had no choice but to notify the station's Political Branch in advance and have them assist. The afternoon before, Detective Yao of the Central Station laid on the men; to keep it secret, they weren't allowed home that night, and the detective squad even stood them drinks—after which they went to the station to rest and await orders. Someone tried every trick to telephone out, said he wanted to see to things at home; it looked to be an uneasy night. Detective Yao lost his temper and said that once it was all over he would look into it properly. To make sure the arrests went off smoothly, I talked it over with Detective Yao and had them all herded into the station's little assembly hall—yet by the small hours the man had still slipped away. Detective Yao said he'd take charge of getting him back, went to his house, and found it empty. No one dreamed he would dare come charging into the market.”

“Was he a Communist?”

“He opened fire and wounded two men. A truly ruthless hand—he shot even his own colleagues from the station. In the end he was cornered in a storeroom, and jumped.”

“He meant to escape by jumping?”

“Right below the window he jumped from was their meeting-place. He jumped, it seems, to warn the ones downstairs.”

“Ah—then he died for nothing.”

The room was quiet a while.

“As long as they're taken, that's what matters.” Director Mu let out a thick mouthful of smoke. “I've reported it to the Garrison Command and put you up for a commendation, Captain.”

“I have you to thank for it, Director.”

“No need to thank me. That crowd at the Garrison Command—a talent like yours, Captain, will find its own quarter to be rewarded in.” Mu Chuan laughed heartily, then dropped his voice. “You've worked your ground inside the Communist Party with real skill; the intelligence is of high quality. You'll go on rendering the Party and the state good service for years to come—never mind that lot at the Garrison Command.”

You Tianxiao weighed his words, at a loss how to answer.

“The code name is Xi Shi, I gather—” Mu Chuan waved a hand, as if brushing off a fly, and changed the subject. “Get them extradited back from the Settlement as fast as you can, and interrogated as fast as you can.”

“Yes, Director. The formalities are done; today the Municipal Police will send men to convey all the prisoners to Baiyunguan. The stretch from Nanshi back to Longhua is rather a complicated one, and the detective squad is short-handed—might you get word to the Garrison Command's military police, Director?”

“The military police? Once the army trucks start rolling, isn't that making rather too much of a stir? Not one of these people has even been questioned yet. Over the years Longhua has taken in no end of false Communists—arrest them, release them, release them and arrest them again. The street toughs, the dope-peddlers, the badger-game swindlers, the cutthroats and robbers, the ones who teach school or do translation—nothing ever comes of the questioning, and in the end they're all simply let go.” Mu Chuan spoke as though it were nothing at all.

“A man threw his life away to charge into the middle of an arrest and warn them—on that count alone I feel fairly sure of it.”

“So—there's intelligence that the Communists mean to seize the prisoners on the road?” Mu Chuan paused, as though something had suddenly come clear to him. “Are these six all Communists? Might you not have swept up one of your own along with them, Captain?”

“No. None of my people among them,” You Tianxiao said with great gravity.
