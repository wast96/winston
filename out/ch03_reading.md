## Chapter Three. The Hour of the Horse, Second Half (noon)

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Horse, second half.

{d} Chang’an; Chang’an County; Guangde Ward.

He Zhizhang stood in the very center of the Jing’an Bureau’s great hall, a square bronze-gilt seal of office cradled in his palm, his manner serene. Li Bi stood facing him, his gaze sharp as loosed arrows, yet it could not sway the old man in the slightest.

Everyone else in the Bureau bent their heads and made a show of busying themselves with the work at hand; no one dared make a sound.

Just then a runner hurried in from outside the hall. He glanced first at Li Bi, then at the great seal in He Zhizhang’s hand, hesitated a moment, and only then cupped his hands toward He Zhizhang and reported in a gruff voice: “The watchtower in Huaiyuan Ward reports that Commander Zhang has been taken into custody and is being brought back at once.”

Though he had meant to keep his voice low, everyone around still heard him plainly.

That was precisely the effect He Zhizhang wanted. He nodded in satisfaction, then addressed Li Bi with weighty feeling: “Changyuan, do not resent an old man for using this Bureau seal to overrule you. The truth is that you have acted far too rashly—appointing a condemned criminal as Commander of the Jing’an Bureau? And a man guilty of the unpardonable crime of murdering his own superior? If this gets out, tomorrow the censors’ impeachments will bury you alive!”

Li Bi, his fly-whisk cradled in his arms, gave a cold snort. “Tomorrow? I wonder whether there will be any tomorrow left to speak of for this city of Chang’an.”

“Tsk. Changyuan... You are bold to shoulder responsibility—that much I understand. But will the men of the Orchid Terrace understand it? Will the chief ministers? And even if they understand, will they care?” Here He Zhizhang deliberately weighted his words. “Why do you think I came rushing back? Minister Li has already caught wind that the operation failed, and is trying to seize command of the Jing’an Bureau! For now I can still hold him off. But if he learns that you have staked the very survival of Chang’an on a single condemned man, then when the clamor rises even I will not be able to bear the pressure!”

Seeing Li Bi hold his silence, he shifted to a kindlier tone. “At court there are ambushes on every side; one careless step and it is ruin and overthrow. I am eighty-six this year—it no longer matters for me. But you are still young. Guard yourself!”

He Zhizhang had said all this in one breath, and it could fairly be called speaking from the heart; but Li Bi was unmoved. “For every lesson in the art of officialdom you deliver here, those Türks draw one step closer to success.” He glanced at the corner of the hall, where the water in the bronze clepsydra dripped on, pitiless as ever.

“I never said we should not catch the Türks!” said He Zhizhang. “It is only that I have heard the man’s resentment of the court fairly spills out of him. And you trust him, just like that?”

“I do not trust him. But he is the best—no, the only—choice we have now.”

“The western capital gathers the finest talents under heaven; the whole city is full of able men. Is there really not one among them the equal of that condemned convict?” He Zhizhang’s tone turned severe. “You have already erred once, and put the Jing’an Bureau under crushing strain. As things stand now, there can be no room for a second mistake!”

Li Bi took a step forward, his eyes sharp as thorns. “You only want to save the Jing’an Bureau. I mean to save Chang’an!”

At this the herald entered the hall a second time and bellowed hoarsely: “Report—Commander Zhang Xiaojing of the Jing’an Bureau and his party have reached the gate.” He Zhizhang flicked his sleeve. “No need to bring him in. Confiscate his waist-badge and escort him straight back to Chang’an County.”

Just then Li Bi suddenly gave a great shout: “Wait!”

“Changyuan.” He Zhizhang’s tone now carried a trace of displeasure. But Li Bi ignored the rebuke and pressed on: “A moment ago yellow smoke rose over the West Market, and then over Huaiyuan Ward—there must be some important development. Better to summon him in first and let him account for it clearly; there will be time enough afterward to decide his case.” He Zhizhang knew perfectly well that Li Bi was stalling, but he also understood the urgency of the moment, so he let out a light sigh and waved his hand.

Even so, he stationed four soldiers of the Lüben Guards to one side, so that the instant Zhang Xiaojing had finished his report they would step forward and seize him.

He Zhizhang would not lightly interfere in the Bureau’s affairs; but if Li Bi overstepped the rules, he would turn himself into halter and rein and haul the young man back. The Türk Wolf Guards must of course be caught—but on no account could he let his political rivals find a pretext to lay hands on the Jing’an Bureau.

All of this was for the safety of that one person.

Footsteps sounded, and Zhang Xiaojing strode brazenly into the hall, without the least alarm at his sudden dismissal. He winked first at Tanqi, then turned a curious gaze on the white-haired old man.

This man was famous indeed in the present dynasty, a master of both poetry and calligraphy, his name resounding through the Kaiyuan and Tianbao reigns for more than twenty years. Just ten days before, He Zhizhang had announced his retirement to his home village, and the Son of Heaven had set out a farewell canopy for him at the Green Gate east of the city, with the hundred officials seeing him off—an event of no small cultural sensation in Chang’an. Yet Zhang Xiaojing would never have guessed that this celebrated man had slipped back into the capital, transformed himself, and become a Director of the Jing’an Bureau with no connection to letters at all.

He was already past eighty this year. At his retirement he had held the third rank as Grand Master of Silver and Blue Imperial Favor and concurrently Director of the Palace Library by full appointment—which was why others honored him with the title “Director He”—so for him to serve as Director of a mere functionary’s office like the Jing’an Bureau was a lavish over-appointment indeed. Plainly, whoever had arranged it did not expect He Zhizhang to accomplish anything; they only wished, on the strength of his seniority and prestige, to have him hold the chief seal and preside, so that his deputy Li Bi could get the real work done beneath him.

Zhang Xiaojing suddenly smiled. He Zhizhang’s appearance had answered a question that had been nagging at him all along.

Responsibility for the defense of Chang’an was scattered among the Jinwu Guard, the Jingzhao Prefecture, the Censorate, the Gate Guard, and other offices, layered one atop another and riddled with conflict. For this Jing’an Bureau to have appeared out of nowhere and to stand above all those offices—without some powerful figure supporting it from behind, the thing could never have been managed.

Besides being Grand Master of Silver and Blue Imperial Favor and Director of the Palace Library by full appointment, He Zhizhang also held the title of Guest of the Heir Apparent; and Li Bi, for his part, served the Eastern Palace as a Hanlin academician-in-waiting. Who stood behind this Jing’an Bureau was, one might say, plain at a glance.

Although the heir apparent did not at present reside in the Eastern Palace, the arrangement of these staff titles still let one glimpse a little of the subtle design at work.

He Zhizhang noticed Zhang Xiaojing’s disrespectful stare, but he did not open his mouth to reproach him; he only lowered his brows and closed his eyes to rest.

Li Bi came forward and told him to make his report. Zhang Xiaojing rubbed his chin and laid the whole matter out from beginning to end. Li Bi’s expression changed. “You mean to say the Türks have already got hold of the ward map?”

It was the one and only lead they had; if it were cut, the Jing’an Bureau would have no choice left but to comb the entire city.

“It is not yet certain,” said Zhang Xiaojing. “I have already had Yao Runeng seal off the area around the Zoroastrian temple, and we are checking the nearby households one by one—” Before he could finish, He Zhizhang’s eyes snapped open and his tone turned harsh: “What monstrous nerve! Do you have any idea how great an uproar it will cause, to seal off a Zoroastrian temple on your own authority?”

“I don’t know, and I don’t care. My task is only to catch the Türk Wolf Guards.” Zhang Xiaojing answered without servility and without insolence.

“And have you caught them?”

“If you keep calling me back to ask pointless questions, then no, I won’t.”

Li Bi felt a faint flicker of satisfaction. This fellow Zhang Xiaojing always spoke with a certain mocking edge—and now it was old He’s turn to suffer the headache.

He Zhizhang knit his brows; this condemned man was insufferably rude. He raised the great seal, meaning to have Zhang Xiaojing seized and given twenty strokes of the rod first and questioned after—when the herald came running into the hall a third time.

“Report—the Great Sabao of the Zoroastrian faith requests an audience.”

Everyone in the hall with any acquaintance of Chang’an officialdom felt a jolt. Most of the city’s foreigners followed the Zoroastrian faith, and once a dispute arose, the mere agitation of the believers could whip up a great storm; so the authorities had always dealt warily with the Zoroastrians. The Great Sabao oversaw the many Zoroastrian temples of the metropolitan region and wielded enormous influence. For him to appear here so abruptly, he had surely come to demand redress.

He Zhizhang gave a cold laugh. This ignorant convict had not only wrecked their one and only lead but had stirred up a storm like this. He glanced at Li Bi. “Changyuan, that is the second mistake you have made today.”

He Zhizhang delivered the light rebuke, then turned his face away. “Bind him! Take him away!”

Li Bi stood where he was, discomfited, his eyes flickering. If this really had provoked a Zoroastrian disturbance, he would have no grounds to shield the man. Several guards, fierce as wolves and tigers, took the order, pinned Zhang Xiaojing down, trussed him hand and foot, and were about to shove him out of the hall—when suddenly a shrill scrape of a wooden chair-leg against the floor rang through the hall. Everyone turned toward the sound and saw Xu Bin rising to his feet with a look of some alarm; the clerks around him were all kneeling at their low desks, which made him stand out all the more.

He Zhizhang narrowed his eyes and fixed them on him without a flicker of expression.

Under the Director’s oppressive stare, Xu Bin trembled. He wanted to say a few words in his friend’s defense, but in his agitation his stammer only grew worse; his forehead ran with sweat, and he could not force out a single word. After struggling a long while, he finally gave up the attempt to speak, stepped out from the crowd, and walked quickly to Zhang Xiaojing’s side—Xu Bin’s mind held nothing so complicated: since it was he who had brought his friend into the Jing’an Bureau, it must be he who saw him out.

Director He is a great man; surely he won’t hold a trifle like this against me... So Xu Bin thought, as he reached out his right hand to steady Zhang Xiaojing’s arm and murmured, low: “I’m sorry.” Zhang Xiaojing, his hands bound behind his back, kept his usual composure. For a condemned man this was hardly the worst of outcomes: at most he would go back to his cell to await death, no different from before.

Only, to hold out to him a sliver of hope of living and then, in an instant, shatter it utterly—that was crueler than simply killing him outright.

He Zhizhang had already lost interest in this cornered swindler; what he was turning over in his mind was how to deal with the Great Sabao in a moment. The thing was decidedly strange, when he thought about it—since when had the Zoroastrians’ intelligence become so keen? The trouble had only just happened here, and there they were at the door already. Could there be someone behind them, watching for a slip by the Jing’an Bureau?

The moment his thoughts turned to court intrigue, the old man’s mind quickened.

Unexpectedly, as if reading his very thoughts, Zhang Xiaojing gave a chuckle. “Don’t rack your brains, Director He. I’m the one who had Yao Runeng send for him.”

Wen Ran’s fingers were long and deft—they could tease out the finest thread of aromatic wood, or embroider the most delicate gold-couched peony. Now, her back pressed against the wall of the cart, she jammed two fingers of her right hand into a crack between the boards, gripping the loosened head of an iron nail and working it, a little at a time. All the while she kept silent count, in her mind, of the direction and the number of the cart’s turns.

The cart rolled smoothly on, the compartment still dark. The four guards escorting it, two to a side, chatted idly among themselves. A fragrant scent pervaded the cart, given off by an incense-rack propped at an angle beside them. The Wen Incense Shop’s blended incense had always been famous for its rich, long-lasting fragrance.

Perhaps under the influence of the scent, the guards drifted, without noticing, onto the subject of the pleasure houses, every face lit with excitement. One of them turned his head and leered at the swell of Wen Ran’s breast. Shamed into fury, Wen Ran suddenly screamed aloud. The guard had to slap her across the face before she quieted. Once the guards had all settled back into their seats, Wen Ran slowly drew her right hand back: a moment ago, under cover of the scream, she had wrenched the nail bodily out of the crack.

In the darkness she clenched her fist, letting the sharp head of the nail jut out from between her fingers.

After a while the driver up front suddenly called out a long “Whoa—,” and the cart slowed once more. Today was the Lantern Festival; the streets were too crowded, and the cart had to keep starting and stopping.

Wen Ran’s eyes flew open. She sprang up and drove a fist at the guard who had just affronted her. The blow smashed square into his eye socket; the guard let out a hideous shriek, and when Wen Ran pulled her fist back, the nail-head between her fingers was slick with blood.

The other three guards were stunned for an instant, and Wen Ran seized the moment to shove over the incense-rack with her other hand, scattering blended incense across the floor. In the cramped space of the compartment it made an effective obstacle. She took her chance to rush to the front of the cart, tore aside the curtain, and dealt a savage blow to the back of the driver’s head.

The nail gouged into the driver’s skull; in his agony he yanked hard on the reins—and the cart was in the middle of a turn. The shaft-horse, startled by the jerk, reared and struggled, and the frame lost its balance at once, sending the people in the compartment behind sprawling every which way. Wen Ran set her teeth, twisted her body aside, and rolled out from under the cart. The moment she hit the ground she tumbled over a few times and, not daring to pause an instant, sprinted away eastward.

She had been reckoning the cart’s position all along, and guessed that they were somewhere near the cross-street between Zhiye Ward and Fengle Ward. Both wards lay on the west side of the Vermilion Bird Avenue. She had only to run east along the cross-street and she would soon come in sight of the avenue.

Two of the guards, alarmed and furious, leapt down from the cart and gave chase. They were strong and long-limbed, and their strides quickly closed the gap. The one in the lead ran fastest; after a hundred paces he was a single step behind her. The idle young rake grinned savagely and reached out to seize her by the hair—when Wen Ran spun around and hurled a packet of powder from her hand, which burst open across the bridge of his nose.

It was a scent-sachet she had snatched up before jumping from the cart: inside was the spirit-summoning rue-incense made specially for the young lady of the Wang house. The stuff was harmless to the body, but the Wen Incense Shop’s craft was fine, and its aromatics were all ground exceedingly small. The powder blinded the young rake in an instant, and he had to stop and rub at his eyes.

Seizing the chance, Wen Ran bounded up onto the Vermilion Bird Avenue.

She lifted her head, and far across the avenue she saw the golden spire of the Jianfu Temple’s pagoda; hope rose in her heart. That was Anren Ward!

***

At the very moment Wen Ran stepped onto the Vermilion Bird Avenue, the Great Sabao happened to be stepping through the gate of the Jing’an Bureau.

The Great Sabao was past sixty this year. He had changed into a long robe of white-figured satin with a standing collar, and across his neck hung two silk sashes worked with flame patterns—vestments worn only on the most formal occasions, a mark of how gravely the Sabao Office took this affair.

For a Zoroastrian prelate to be killed before the temple, in full view of the crowd—what an outrage it was.

On reaching the Jing’an Bureau he was led straight into a private room in a side hall. There were no serving-maids; only a burly, thickset soldier came in and set down a cup of tea. The tea was Jiange Beast’s-Eye, not bad in itself, but the tea-powder had been sieved too coarse, and one look at the froth scattered across the surface told him the brewer had been careless.

Before long an old man pushed open the door and came in.

The Great Sabao had lived many years in Chang’an, and one glance at the fish-pouch and the color of the robe told him this man’s rank was very high. The two exchanged salutes and gave their names, and only then did the Great Sabao learn that this was the illustrious He Zhizhang, at which his manner grew a good deal graver. He Zhizhang cupped his hands and spoke slowly: “It is with alarm that I hear ruffians have violated your honored temple and done injury to a man of your priesthood. Since the Jing’an Bureau bears the grave charge of guarding the capital against malefactors, it will by no means treat the matter lightly. Able officers have already been dispatched to investigate thoroughly, with all their combined efforts—there will be no leniency whatsoever!”

Wait a moment! The Great Sabao sensed something was off. By the drift of He Zhizhang’s words, the man meant from the very outset to clear the Jing’an Bureau of all responsibility. His brows shot up in anger, and in stiff, halting Tang speech he said: “It was plainly your honorable Bureau’s pursuit of the bandits that drew them into my temple—”

He Zhizhang cut in at once: “Fortunately your faithful, seeing what was right, acted bravely and beat the villain to death. I shall report it to the Sage and see them commended for it.”

These two sentences of He Zhizhang’s were half tug and half strike: they cleared the Bureau of blame, dangled a sweetener, and, incidentally, hinted that he had the ear of the Son of Heaven. But the Great Sabao would have none of it. He rapped his staff on the floor. “Your Jing’an Bureau, in seizing its bandits, got a prelate innocently entangled and killed. There must be an accounting for that. Otherwise the believers will rise in an uproar, and I will not be able to hold them down.”

Zoroastrianism was a minor religion in Chang’an, current only among the foreign merchant guilds, and the court held it on a loose rein through the Sabao Office. Its believers, however, were given to gathering in crowds, and any dispute could readily boil over into disorder. So in all matters touching Zoroastrian affairs the officials of Great Tang trod as though on thin ice, keeping conciliation foremost. This tactic had never once failed the Great Sabao.

Unexpectedly He Zhizhang’s expression changed all at once. “Does the Sabao know who that villain was?” The Great Sabao was taken aback, and He Zhizhang went on: “The man was a Wolf Guard of the Türk khagan, slipped into Chang’an, meaning on the night of the Lantern Festival to bring harm to His Majesty.”

At these words the tea-bowl in the Great Sabao’s hand clattered to the floor.

“The Türks? To harm His Majesty? O Mazda in heaven...” The report he had received said only that a prelate had been killed; of the Wolf Guard he had known nothing. If the affair touched the Türks, its whole nature was transformed. The Great Sabao knew this was the one red line the court could least afford to see crossed.

He Zhizhang caught the shift in the Great Sabao’s face at once, and pressed his advantage: “Though the man was beaten to death before the Zoroastrian temple, an important object was taken from his body and has vanished, no one knows where—and until that is cleared up, it is a catastrophe of the first magnitude.”

The implication was plain: if the object were not recovered, the Zoroastrians would not be able to disentangle themselves from the Wolf Guards. Should the Great Sabao insist on having his way and stir the believers to make trouble, that would be the crime of rebellion in secret collusion with the Türks.

The Great Sabao hastened to protest, loudly: “Our faith’s prelate was killed by the bandits. There is no possibility whatever that we colluded with the Türks.”

He had come to demand redress; but the instant those words were out, the balance of the exchange shifted. He Zhizhang, however, did not press his advantage. Instead he smiled faintly. “I have always known Zoroastrianism to be enlightened in its rites and sincere in its devotion. How could it collude with wicked men? It was merely taken advantage of by the bandits.”

The Great Sabao let out a breath of relief, and He Zhizhang went on: “The good god Mazda has said: good thoughts, good words, good deeds—all are meritorious. You who have renounced the three evils and hold fast to the three goods—how could you ever play the tiger’s lackey?”

At these words a keen light blazed in the Great Sabao’s eyes. Mazda was the name of the true god of the Zoroastrian faith; the “three goods” and “three evils” and all the rest were phrases from within the religion—how did He Zhizhang come to know them?

One must understand that the doctrines of Zoroastrianism were intricate, and the faith had never truly flourished in Chang’an. The court’s officials mostly referred to it dismissively as “the foreign heaven” or “the foreign god,” with no interest in understanding it deeply. The Great Sabao had come from Persia to Chang’an more than twenty years before, and had long grieved at how hard it was to find a kindred spirit. This speech of He Zhizhang’s was the first time an official of the highest rank in Great Tang had earnestly cited the scriptures of his religion.

Judging that the moment was about ripe, He Zhizhang composed his features, bowed, and spoke with deep feeling: “Today Chang’an is in peril, and I need Your Reverence to grant the Jing’an Bureau this accommodation—that together we may kindle the pure fire with our own hands and sweep away these skulking villains.”

At the four words “kindle the pure fire,” the rims of the Great Sabao’s eyes nearly grew moist. Zoroastrianism revered fire above all, and those words struck straight to his heart. The old man rose tremblingly to his feet, let go of his staff, cupped his two hands into the shape of a flame before his chest, and made He Zhizhang a deep bow.

“The Zoroastrian faithful will gladly go before Director He as his vanguard!”

The Vermilion Bird Avenue was a broad and majestic thoroughfare running north and south, the very axis of the whole city of Chang’an. Its surface arched up slightly at the center, with deep gutters along either side, and it measured some hundred and fifty paces from east to west. A thick layer of sand from the Chan River covered the roadbed, so that it resembled a great blue-white river, dividing the outer city of Chang’an into its two counties, Chang’an and Wannian. Tall, straight pagoda trees and elms lined both sides, and every one hundred paces stood a pair of stone carvings facing each other east and west—grand and solemn in their sweep.

This was the Son of Heaven’s imperial way; commoners could cross it only at nine designated crossings, and might neither step over the line nor move at a run. Once Wen Ran had set foot on it, she could do nothing but stand in the queue and inch forward. Luckily the two idle young rakes who pursued her did not dare misbehave on the imperial way either, and could only follow at a distance among the crowd.

Wen Ran reached the crossing on the far side without real mishap and let out a long breath. Anren Ward was home to a great many noble families, whose mansions were permitted to open their gates directly onto the street rather than through the ward gate. So a sweep of the eye along the ward wall took in a whole row of more than a dozen great vermilion doors with carved lintels. The gate of the Wang young lady’s mansion was the third from the right; beneath it stood four elms, two stone beasts of loyalty and duty, and twelve great halberds—easy indeed to recognize.

The young lady of the Wang house had a father who was a great officer of the court; once she reached her, she ought to be safe.

With that thought, Wen Ran quickened her step. But just as she was drawing near the vermilion gate of the Wang mansion, the great doors suddenly rattled open to either side, and out from within rolled a most unusual carriage.

The carriage was drawn neither by horses nor by oxen but by two white camels; its car had low rails of patterned wood to left and right and no canopy overhead, so that at a glance it looked as if it were hauling along a Luohan couch. A tall young woman stood leaning on the front rail, gazing ahead. Her hair was bound up in a high chignon with a silver cord, and she wore a lapelled long robe of jade green and red cloud-boots; she looked dashing and martial.

Standing beside the stone beast, Wen Ran called out: “Sister Wang!” The woman leaned down and laughed. “Why, if it isn’t Wen Ran! How sweet you smell—one could catch the scent ten li off. Did you bring the spirit-summoning rue-incense I ordered?”

Wen Ran was about to explain when the young lady of the Wang house waved a hand. “Come, get in and we’ll talk.”

Wen Ran caught up the hem of her skirt and sprang aboard. Inside the railed car lay a thick felt rug; a row of glossy lacquered food-boxes held sweetmeats of every kind, and in one corner sat a dainty six-sided incense-censer, while a maidservant tended these things with care—for all the world the trappings of an outing to the spring greenery.

The young lady of the Wang house was named Wang Yunxiu. She flicked a jade-white finger and boasted: “You’ve come at just the right time—I’ve only just got this Xi cart, and I was about to go out for a ramble. It’s the newest thing from the steppe, the only one in all of Chang’an; no other house has its like—

“here, throw on this foreign robe, or the ride won’t feel the part at all.”

Wen Ran had meant to speak of her own trouble, but Wang Yunxiu was plainly not interested in it and rattled on and on about the wonders of the cart. Wen Ran knew this well-bred girl to be proud and willful, and much taken with foreign fashion; not daring to spoil her pleasure, she could only accept the foreign robe, put it on, and wait with what patience she could for her to finish.

As they talked, the Xi cart passed out of the Wang mansion, turned south, and made its way down past the wards of Anren, Guangfu, and Jingshan. The two young rogues, seeing her climb aboard the Wang family’s Xi cart, dared not approach, yet could not simply leave; they had no choice but to trail along far behind. Luckily the camels went at no great pace, and on foot the two managed to keep up.

Once the Xi cart had passed Jingshan Ward, the passers-by grew far fewer. The southern part of Chang’an was not as thriving as the north; its dwellings and temples were sparse, giving the quarter a rather desolate air. When the cart reached a certain crossing, the driver suddenly halted the camels. Wang Yunxiu asked crossly what the matter was, and the driver said men from the Directorate for the Palace Buildings were repairing the road and wanted them to go around.

Ahead, sure enough, a willow-wood board had been set up, reading “Outside Works,” and some distance off a few laborers with bare arms and white cloths masking their faces were raking at the sand. Wang Yunxiu sneered. “Do the miserable bond-servants of the Directorate dare bar this young lady’s cart? Drive straight through!”

Wen Ran was still weighing when to speak up when a low rumble suddenly filled her ears. She turned her head, and in an instant her pupils contracted sharply. The ground here lay low, and down a high slope on the right side of the crossing a great firewood-cart, laden with stone and drawn by no horse, was hurtling straight down—bearing, across the distance, upon the Xi cart at the bottom of the slope.

The firewood-cart was immensely heavy, and as it charged down the slope it was like some crazed giant beast broken loose from all control, its wheels thundering, its momentum irresistible. Wen Ran screamed; the driver hurriedly urged the camels on, but in such haste there was no time. Carrying all the fierce, crushing force of wind and thunder, the firewood-cart smashed savagely into the side of the Xi cart.

There came a series of great cracks of splintering timber; the Xi cart was smashed apart and flipped clean over, the whole car overturned upside down on the ground and buried under stone in a moment.

The crash roused the constables in the nearby street-posts, who came hurrying over to look. All at once the several laborers of the Directorate straightened their backs, drew short knives from the heaps of sand, and rushed the constables. These men had long laid their plans and struck without mercy; the constables were cut down, almost to the last, in an instant. A fruit-selling woman who happened to be passing turned to flee, and one of the laborers flung a knife that caught her square in the back, and she too toppled into a pool of blood.

Having finished off the constables, the laborers gathered around the shattered wreck of the Xi cart. It lay with its wheels to the sky, trapping all its passengers beneath. By good fortune the car had low rails and a deep bed, and it had come down over them like a box rather than crushing them flat. The driver had not been so lucky: pinned beneath the two camels, his bones broken, he was plainly not going to live.

The laborers kicked in the side boards of the cart and dragged out the three passengers within, finding that the one dressed as a maidservant was already dead, while the other two had merely been knocked senseless by the shock. One laborer pulled the white cloth from his face, revealing the stern countenance of Cao Poyan.

“Which one is Wang Zhongsi’s daughter?” he asked. The others all shook their heads; they could not tell them apart. Both of the senseless women were dressed in foreign robes. Cao Poyan raised his head, glanced at the passers-by slowly gathering in the distance, and waved a hand:

“No time left. Cut off their arms and their heads—bring them all back and we’ll sort them out at our leisure.”

Cao Poyan raised his blade and was about to bring it chopping down when a Wolf Guard beside him named Ma Ge’er caught his arm. Ma Ge’er was a big, rough-hewn fellow, even taller than Cao Poyan. “His Excellency the Right Shad gave the order: they’re to be taken alive. Wang Zhongsi killed the Right Shad’s son, and he must watch with his own eyes as his enemy’s kin die.”

Cao Poyan barked: “At a time like this, still fretting over private grudges! Two live captives—what a burden that is! And where would we even put them?”

Ma Ge’er answered: “His Excellency the Right Shad said there is a spare house that could—”

“That still costs us men and time we can’t spare! The Wolf Guards are sworn to the Great Khagan, not to the Right Shad’s private grudges!” Cao Poyan put strength into his wrist and hacked down with all his might—only for Ma Ge’er, unexpectedly, to draw his own blade and, with a clang, block it.

Cao Poyan was enraged. This Ma Ge’er was a man he himself had picked for the Wolf Guards, and now he dared defy an order! He was about to deliver a rebuke when he caught something strange in the eyes of the ring of Wolf Guards around him. All at once he understood: the hair had been shorn from the crown of his head, and strictly speaking his standing now was lower even than a herd-slave on the steppe.

These Wolf Guards followed him now only because His Excellency the Right Shad had so ordered. If his commands ever came into conflict with the Right Shad’s, the Wolf Guards would pay no heed to the bond of brothers-in-arms, for the Right Shad stood for the Great Khagan himself.

Cao Poyan wished with his whole heart to render the Great Khagan loyal service; and the irony was that what thwarted him now was precisely the other Wolf Guards’ own unquestioning loyalty to that same Khagan.

The standoff did not last long. Cao Poyan let out a long breath and lowered his blade. Ma Ge’er felt a weight lift; he knew this old commander too well—if the man truly loosed his fury, no one present could have held him back.

“The goods from Yanzhou will arrive soon; that is the most important thing, and I must go to receive them myself. Take the hostages yourselves.” Cao Poyan turned and left without a backward glance.

Ma Ge’er dared not trouble him further, and quickly ordered the others to haul Wen Ran and Wang Yunxiu onto a large cart hung on all four sides with curtains, prepared in advance, and to leave the crossing at speed.

Some way off, the two idle young rakes stood rooted and dumbstruck, at a loss before a half-street awash in blood.

***

He Zhizhang walked back into the great hall. There was a subtle embarrassment on his face, and around his neck now hung a flame-shaped sash. The faintly comic get-up made everyone want to laugh, though no one dared laugh aloud.

He Zhizhang glanced at Zhang Xiaojing, said little, and went straight up to Li Bi to hand him a somewhat worn register. Li Bi merely leafed through it and passed it at once to Xu Bin. The Jing’an Bureau’s clerks set to pulling files and documents of every kind, and the art of the Great Archive was turning once more.

Zhang Xiaojing stood at the hall’s entrance, arms folded, staring at Tanqi with a certain insolence. She felt both disgust and helplessness, and would dearly have loved to fling her crescent mallet hard in his direction—but could not, for this leering lecher had just worked a miracle.

The meeting between He Zhizhang and the Great Sabao had been entirely Zhang Xiaojing’s idea.

By his reasoning, the Türks must have a man on the inside at the Zoroastrian temple in Huaiyuan Ward, passing himself off as a believer. The Wolf Guard’s flight to the temple had been deliberate, a thing planned in advance, so that his confederate could take the ward map in the confusion.

Zoroastrianism was a relatively closed community, its believers all known to one another. So this insider was unlikely to have been slipped in at the last moment; in all probability he had lain hidden there for some time.

Every Zoroastrian had to come to the temple at regular intervals to make offerings to the fire—incense, oils, and money—and all of it was recorded. To learn the man’s identity, the best course was to obtain the Zoroastrians’ register of offerings. With that register in hand, set against Chang’an’s household registers, the Jing’an Bureau’s formidable powers of calculation would readily bring the anomaly to light.

That was why Zhang Xiaojing had taken it upon himself to summon the Great Sabao. Without his cooperation, that register would not have been easy to get hold of.

The next question was how to persuade the Great Sabao to cooperate, and the deeply respected He Zhizhang was plainly better suited to the negotiation than Li Bi.

For all his lack of any liking for Zhang Xiaojing, He Zhizhang, for the sake of Chang’an’s larger good, could only bring himself, reluctantly, to do a condemned man’s bidding this once. That speech which had so moved the old Zoroastrian was precisely what Zhang Xiaojing had coached He Zhizhang to say.

The Zoroastrians did not care overmuch for money or power; the one thing that made them feel they had found a kindred spirit was someone who could speak with them about their doctrine. Persuasion along that line not only dispelled the threat of the believers’ unrest—the Great Sabao even offered his cooperation freely, and at once sent men to fetch the register of offerings for Huaiyuan Ward.

Tanqi looked at Zhang Xiaojing, her gaze complicated. This man seemed to have reckoned everything out well in advance, so that even one such as He Zhizhang had no choice but to act according to his design—and now came the most interesting part, Tanqi thought with lively curiosity: how would Director He deal with him? Would he take back his order, or hold to the expulsion?

But it was not He Zhizhang who moved first; it was Zhang Xiaojing. He lowered his arms, brushed the grit from the hollow of his eye, and started for the door. Li Bi frowned and asked where he was going. Zhang Xiaojing gave a smile that was not quite a smile. “That is hardly a question you should be asking me, is it?” For a moment the hall fell silent; even the clerks bent over their files slowed their hands a little.

He Zhizhang gave a cough. “The Jing’an Bureau has its own laws, and cannot suffer a mere condemned man to remain within it; but I bear you no prejudice. What you have achieved today will not go for nothing. If you have any request to make of your imprisonment, you may as well name it.”

“Send me some spirit-money, then.”

“Oh?” The request took He Zhizhang by surprise.

“I’d like to make an early offering to Chang’an and its people, who are about to die.”

At this answer He Zhizhang’s breath caught in his throat; the words made him tremble with anger. Zhang Xiaojing gave a chuckle and strode with his head high toward the door. Li Bi suddenly stretched out a hand to stop him and said sharply to He Zhizhang: “Director He! This man is of great use to us today—can we not bend the rules for once?”

Slowly and firmly He Zhizhang shook his head. This was a matter of principle.

Li Bi’s fine brows shot up, and he drew his own seal of office from inside his robe, about to set it down on the table. Tanqi was aghast—the young master meant to fall out with him and threaten to resign his post; for the sake of one condemned man, was it worth going so far?

Before the seal could be set down, a petty clerk in the corner of the hall suddenly called out: “Deputy Director Li, look at this!” and held out a sheaf of documents. One glance, and Li Bi hurriedly carried it to He Zhizhang. He Zhizhang’s eyes swept lightly over it—and all at once his shoulders began to shake violently, his expression like a man struck by lightning.

It was an intelligence report, from the street-post patrol of Yanshou Ward.

Every ward had its street-posts. When the common people had a dispute among themselves, or saw anything out of the ordinary, they generally reported it first to the street-post of their ward; this was called an intelligence report. So that the Jing’an Bureau might keep abreast of every stirring in the whole of Chang’an, Li Bi required that a copy of every such report, however trifling, be sent in, with men set aside to sift and sort them.

This report stated: a commoner had found the corpse of a man beneath the bridge beside Yanshou Ward. Preliminary examination showed that the dead man’s neck had been wrenched apart by tremendous force and his clothes stripped away. Drinkers at a nearby wine-shop had already identified him—Jiao Sui.

In Chang’an the love of wine was a very fashion, and among its devotees eight men were the most renowned, called the “Eight Immortals of the Wine Cup.” At their head was He Zhizhang himself, and with him seven others—Li Bai, Li Shizhi, Li Jin, Cui Zongzhi, Su Jin, Zhang Xu, and Jiao Sui—of whom Jiao Sui alone held no office. He Zhizhang had been his drinking companion since the early years of Kaiyuan, and the friendship between them was of the deepest.

He Zhizhang had never imagined that at just such a moment he would receive word of his old friend’s death.

Li Bi said gravely: “Near Yanshou Ward runs the North Yong’an Canal—the very place where we suspect Cao Poyan came ashore. The manner of Jiao Sui’s death is the same as Cui Liulang’s; I fear this too was the Türks’ doing.” These words struck harder still, and a wave of dizziness rose before He Zhizhang’s eyes.

“Quickly, hold Director He up,” said Li Bi, without a flicker of expression.

Tanqi hastily stepped forward and took He Zhizhang’s arm. She could feel the old man’s arm trembling faintly, his body swaying. He had long suffered from dizzy spells of the head, and the sudden dreadful news showed every sign of bringing one on.

Luckily the Jing’an Bureau kept yinyu medicinal wine on hand, and they hurriedly poured him a cup. The medicinal wine followed a formula of the Medicine King’s, and after He Zhizhang had drunk it his condition improved a little at last—but he looked for all the world like a man whose very soul had been drawn out of him. He was past eighty, after all, weak in body and failing in spirit, and nothing wounded the heart and mind like the death of an old friend.

He Zhizhang struggled to rise, but the dizziness only worsened. He heaved a long sigh, knowing that once this illness took hold he could attend to no business. He called Li Bi before him. “For now... I can only rely on you, Changyuan.” He paused, then lowered his voice: “This man Zhang Xiaojing—use him, but do not keep him. The moment the Wolf Guards are netted, he must be dealt with at once, or the trouble he leaves behind will be endless. The Jing’an Bureau’s enemies, you know, are by no means only the Türks...”

These few sentences had exhausted the old man’s every last reserve of spirit. Tanqi hurriedly sent someone to ready an ox-cart and summoned a physician to accompany him, and had him carried back to his own residence to recover. Li Bi stood grave and still where he was, his fly-whisk gathered before his chest.

After He Zhizhang had gone, Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his eyes and, out of nowhere, remarked: “Deputy Director Li has a fine sense of timing.” His tone was half admiration and half mockery.

“Desperate times call for expedients.” Li Bi’s face was expressionless.

The two of them spoke as if in riddles, and Tanqi, listening at their side, was thoroughly mystified. She set about tidying the documents on the desk. Jiao Sui’s report lay on top, and she gave it an extra glance in passing—when suddenly she noticed something strange. The upper right corner of a report ordinarily bore the time Li Bi had signed it in; this one had been signed at the second quarter of the hour of the Horse, just before He Zhizhang returned to the Jing’an Bureau.

Her moth-brows knit. The young master had seen this news long before—so why had he waited until just now to speak of it to Director He? Could it be that—

It was too outrageous. Tanqi shook her head and drove the absurd thought from her mind.

By now Xu Bin had come running up with a scroll of documents in his arms. By means of the art of the Great Archive, matched against the Zoroastrians’ household records, he had swiftly picked out one suspicious man.

The man was called Long Bo, from Kucha. In the twentieth year of Kaiyuan he had come to the capital and been entered on the market register, and in that same year had been received into the Zoroastrian faith; he lived within Huaiyuan Ward and had always been single. The records of offerings showed that over the past half year his gifts to the temple had risen sharply, for which he had even received special commendation. At the end of the second year of Tianbao the market registers had been remade in a general revision, yet Long Bo’s household entry was still that of the twentieth year of Kaiyuan. An old clerk of the Ministry of Revenue had keenly noticed this small discrepancy. A household register was required to set down a man’s appearance clearly; if the old entry had not been redrawn, there was the possibility of an impostor taking another’s name.

Yao Runeng was still near the Zoroastrian temple at this time, and Li Bi had the watchtower send him word to go at once to Long Bo’s dwelling and search it.

Within the Jing’an Bureau there fell a sudden lull. Just then Li Bi remembered something. “Hm? Where’s that wretched boy, the one called Cen Shen?” The fellow had spoiled the Bureau’s work at a crucial moment; whether or not he was in the pay of the Türks, it would not do to leave him unquestioned.

Cui Qi, beside him, answered at once: “His identity has been established. He’s a provincial-tribute candidate from Xianzhou, registered in Nanyang, come to the capital to sit the presented-scholar examination this coming spring.” He added: “The Cen family’s forebears served as chief ministers for three generations. In Ruizong’s reign the clan was implicated in a case and banished. His father, Cen Zhi, was once prefect of the two prefectures of Xian and Jin. He should have nothing to do with the Türks—he’s simply... rather a blockhead, I’d say?”

A scion of a fallen official house—no wonder he had stuffed so many poems and essays into his saddlebag; he meant to present them for patronage and make a name before the examinations opened.

Li Bi’s mind was wholly on the Wolf Guards now, and hearing of Cen Shen’s background, he flicked his sleeve. “Hmph. He spoiled a matter this grave; let him not think to escape responsibility. Shut him up for a while and we’ll see.” Those around understood well enough: if the Türks truly brought off some great outrage, here was a ready-made scapegoat. This poor scholar who had come to the capital for the examinations would this time not only fail to pass; he might not even keep his life.

Zhang Xiaojing muttered, “The boy’s not bad with his hands, though,” and said no more. Time pressed harder now, and all these irrelevant matters were set aside for the moment. Together the two men stepped toward the sand-table, looking at the model marked “Huaiyuan Ward.”

At that moment, in the actual Huaiyuan Ward, Yao Runeng kicked in a wooden door with one savage blow, burst inside, and swept the room with his crossbow raised—only to find it empty of anyone.

Long Bo’s dwelling was a courtyardless row-house; through the door were only a main room and a side chamber. The buliang men crowded in and packed the place full in an instant. The man lived alone and had little furniture. Without much trouble the Jing’an Bureau turned up from under the bed a batch of small articles heavily in the Türk style—gold and silver ware, sheets of parchment, and a few boxes of horse-fat salve.

It seemed beyond doubt, then, that Long Bo was in league with the Türks. The pity was that the man himself was not in the house, and no one knew where he had gone. Yao Runeng sent men to ask the neighbors nearby, and the neighbors all said that Long Bo rarely had dealings with anyone; they did not know how he made his living or where he was in the habit of going.

Unwilling to give up, Yao Runeng went back inside and circled the rooms a few more times, when suddenly he spotted something suspicious. In the main room stood a stove, and above the stove was pasted an image of the Stove God. Zoroastrians revered fire as a god, and the faithful had to make offering to the stove-fire at home three times a day—how could such a man have pasted up a Han Stove God over it? He went closer and saw that the paper was clean and smooth, with scarcely a trace of soot; reaching out to touch it, he found the wall behind the paper somewhat hollowed. Something stirred in Yao Runeng’s mind, and he tore the image away, uncovering a brick niche within, in which sat a square wooden tablet.

The wooden tablet was about the size of a palm, its four corners carved with peony and plantain-leaf patterns, all incised in relief and traced in white. On its face were carved three characters in regular script, “Pingkang Quarter,” and on its back the words “First Lane.”

Yao Runeng was startled. The Pingkang Quarter lay on the eastern side of Chang’an, a first-rate haunt of flowers and spent gold, known to every soul in the capital. This tablet was called a “gratitude token”; only regular patrons were issued one, and with it a man could pass straight behind the curtains. For all his frugal life, this Long Bo had clearly laid out no small sum in that place.

Long Bo, lying hidden under the guise of a believer, was careful and circumspect day in and day out, and it was only natural that a stifled emptiness should build within him. His one release was to while away his hours in the Pingkang Quarter. There, where patrons came and went and every affection was feigned, he could relax for a little—which suited the state of mind of a planted agent very well.

But the girls of the Pingkang Quarter were too many, and each was in the keeping of a “foster-mother.” Which foster-mother had issued this tablet still needed looking into.

Yao Runeng quickly relayed the news back to the Jing’an Bureau, and Li Bi said to Zhang Xiaojing: “The Pingkang Quarter lies within Wannian County, your old jurisdiction. Revisiting familiar ground, you should find the business an easy road well traveled.”

“An easy road well traveled, indeed...” Zhang Xiaojing gave a chuckle, and the officers around him all wore knowing smiles. Tanqi shot him a look of disgust, thinking that men the world over were all cut from the same cloth: set them before the women of the Pingkang Quarter and they could not lift their feet to leave. By contrast the young master kept himself clean and upright, and was worth far more than the whole lot of them.

Zhang Xiaojing called Yao Runeng along and turned to go. Then Li Bi suddenly called him back. “Hm... about what happened earlier, I hope you’ll bear no grudge. Now that Director He has relinquished his authority, my promise stands unchanged.” For him, this counted as a roundabout apology.

“I don’t have time just now to accept apologies.”

With this curt reply, Zhang Xiaojing hurried off.

Li Bi watched Zhang Xiaojing’s retreating back and was greatly moved. The man was bold in action yet meticulous in thought: when he had taken over the investigation, every last lead had been severed, and yet out of nothing he had hacked a path clean through. More formidable still, the Zoroastrians’ protest, which had begun as a great disaster, he had turned over with an idle flip of the hand into three birds felled by one stone—quelling the Sabao’s wrath, gaining a fresh lead, and stopping He Zhizhang’s mouth besides.

Ten years a soldier in the Western Regions, nine years a chief in Chang’an—his reputation was no idle boast.

A subtle unease suddenly welled up within Li Bi. Would a man like this really submit, willing and content, to be used? Could so grand a cause as the lives of a whole city truly hold him in check?

Li Bi reckoned that, were he in Zhang Xiaojing’s place, he would certainly harbor resentment over what had just passed. To toil and rush about in loyal service, and be repaid with suspicion and humiliation—who then would put his whole heart into the work? At the thought of that faint mockery forever hovering at the corner of the man’s mouth, Li Bi felt a touch of a headache; this sense of things slipping out of his control was not a good one at all.

It seemed there was something, after all, in what Director He had said: with this man one ought to keep a measure of wariness ready in advance. Yao Runeng, when all was said, was too green, and Cui Qi too coarse; the two of them might well not be able to handle him.

But before that, there was another, thornier matter that urgently needed settling.

At this thought a certain weariness rose, unbidden, in Li Bi’s breast. He draped the fly-whisk over his arm and called out loudly:

“Tanqi, come with me!”

With that call Li Bi led her into a retiring-room behind the hall and deliberately shut the door. Once he had made sure there was no one about, Li Bi said: “I have to go out for a while.”

“Eh? Where are you going? For how long?”

Tanqi was a little bewildered—the situation was of the utmost urgency; he would leave at a time like this? Li Bi raised a hand and pinched the bridge of his nose. “With Director He gone from his post, many things must be arranged anew, and I must go and give an account to that person in the palace. I’ll be back in about half a double-hour. Tell anyone who asks that I’m resting in the retiring-room, and let no one in.”

Tanqi thought of that suspicious report and could not help blurting out: “Director He... so it was you, young master, all along—” The words were no sooner out than she regretted them; the young master always had his reasons for what he did—why lay them bare?

But Li Bi did not take offense; instead he heaved a long sigh. “I do not regret it. Only, Director He is high in rank and heavy in renown, with too much bound up in him, and I must confess the whole matter, cause and consequence, to that one person, so that he is not caught off guard.”

“But... if you don’t tell him, young master, who would ever know?”

Li Bi shook his head, his voice deepening. “I, Li Bi, will never lie to him.”

***

Zhang Xiaojing gave his horse free rein and galloped all the way straight for Pingkang Ward, and partway there Yao Runeng came hurrying up to join him.

Only now did Yao Runeng get the chance to tell Zhang Xiaojing what had happened. When he had reached the Yuanlai Trading Post, before he could even go in, he had heard a burst of neighing from the stable beside it, and then more than a dozen stout horses had come stampeding out. Unable to dodge in time, he was knocked to the ground by the one in the lead and gashed his forehead. When he picked himself up and showed his credentials, the hands at the trading post said he was an impostor, and one thing led to another until they came to blows, so that he had had to set off a smoke signal for help.

Zhang Xiaojing asked: “Where is the stable in relation to the trading post?”

Yao Runeng said: “This trading post does no retail trade, so it has no shopfront. The stable is on the right side of the premises, connected to the interior by a sloping horse-ramp.”

“Was the stable door open or shut at the time?”

Yao Runeng thought back. “It was pushed to, I believe—I remember a bronze lock on it, but it was only hanging from the bolt.”

“I remember seeing two columns of smoke, one black and one yellow. Where did the black smoke come from? When did it start?”

Yao Runeng said: “The black smoke rose only after the panicked horses came charging out. I didn’t see the source of the flames, but it must have started behind the stable—perhaps the horses kicked over a brazier?”

Zhang Xiaojing gave a chuckle at this. The stable was heaped with fodder—who would set a brazier down anywhere near it? The Yuanlai Trading Post dealt in livestock as a matter of course and could never have been so careless. He seemed on the point of saying something, then held back, and in the end only shook his head and muttered: “Never mind. That’s the sort of thing better left for Deputy Director Li to worry about.” Yao Runeng was curious, but thought it best not to press.

Pingkang Ward lay within Wannian County. Setting out from Guangde Ward, they had to run east without pause across five crossings, and it took them the better part of two quarter-hours all told to reach that most flourishing haunt of ravished souls in all the capital.

Before they had even entered the ward, the two of them could hear the faint strains of strings and pipes drifting out. Voluptuous melodies rose and fell one after another, instruments of every kind sounding together, threaded through with the winding of song. Without yet seeing it, one already pictured a scene of splendor and abundance. It was only high noon now, and already so lively; come nightfall, it would surely be ten times as much.

Pingkang was called a ward, but its inner layout was utterly unlike that of an ordinary ward. Zhang Xiaojing’s party entered by the north gate and turned left; ahead lay three winding lanes—north, middle, and south—with three round-moon arched gateways ranged side by side, hung along their edges with silk gauze, their eaves powdered and their walls white, painted respectively with peonies, peach blossoms, and willow branches.

Called winding lanes, they were in fact quite broad of surface, wide enough for two twin-shafted covered carts to pass abreast. Just now carts and horses came and went in great number, most of the carts bearing beautifully dressed women, their flowered coronets and veils of every color dazzling to the eye—even the dust ground up beneath the wheels carried a faint fragrance of rouge and powder. The Lantern Festival brought many a wine-feast, and everyone wished to choose a presentable companion for the lantern-viewing, so all had come early to make their arrangements.

The wooden tablet Yao Runeng had turned up bore the words “First Lane.” Of the three lanes of the Pingkang Quarter, the South Lane and the Middle Lane held the accomplished courtesans, frequented mostly by officials and literati, princes and nobles; the North Lane, close by the ward wall—also called the First Lane—drew mostly common folk, petty-rich merchants, and the poor examination candidates and office-seekers come up to the capital, and its surroundings ranked lower by degrees. The layout showed it plainly: the South Lane was thick with tall pavilions; the Middle Lane was mostly separate courtyards and private residences, with a winding stream threading among them; only here in the North Lane was it divided into some dozens of gaudy towers, high and low, ranged in disorder. The three lanes were as distinct as clear water from muddy—plain at a single glance.

Zhang Xiaojing stood at the entrance and gazed up for a while, then said to Yao Runeng: “Once we’re inside here, don’t go acting rashly.” Yao Runeng was rather taken aback—the man had been overbearing and reckless in the West Market; how was it he had suddenly reined himself in here? Zhang Xiaojing pointed to a great mansion far off across the street. “Do you know whose house that is, over there?” Yao Runeng shook his head; he was a man of Chang’an County and did not know the eastern side well.

Zhang Xiaojing gave a low laugh. “That used to be the mansion of Duke Li of Wei; but the one who lives there now is the Right Minister.”

“Li Linfu?” A chill ran through the young man, and when he looked again at the ridge-beasts atop the mansion, they seemed all at once to have taken on a sinister cast. That a great minister of the reigning court should live so close to the Pingkang Quarter, delighting day and night in the reds of orioles and the greens of willows, was a marvel of a sort in itself.

They stepped into the First Lane, and Zhang Xiaojing, looking neither left nor right, went straight ahead like a man on well-traveled ground. From the towers on either side came a few scattered calls of solicitation, and then nothing more. The girls all had an eye for such things: these two walked with steady tread and wore grave faces—plainly not men come for pleasure.

Winding this way and that, the two of them came to the middle stretch of the First Lane. Zhang Xiaojing veered aside and turned into a small alley off to one side. To either hand were only crude wooden shanties, joined in one dark mass, the gaps between them heaped with refuse and rubbish.

Along both sides of the streets of the Pingkang Quarter ran gutters, roofed over with gray tiles for drainage and for sluicing the roadway—apart from here, only six of Chang’an’s main streets enjoyed such a thing—and all these gutters were led into this low-lying alley to drain out into the watercourse beyond the ward. So the little alley ran with filthy water crosswise, and its stench was no small matter.

Yao Runeng wondered at it, thinking: why, instead of tracing where the tablet came from, had they come to a squalid place like this? But seeing that Zhang Xiaojing’s steps did not hesitate in the least—this was clearly no whim of the moment; the man plainly had a plan already—he could only follow in silence.

Zhang Xiaojing went up to one of the shanties and knocked three times. A man opened the door, poking his head out warily, and at the sight of Zhang Xiaojing he flinched as if stung by a scorpion and moved instinctively to shut it again. Zhang Xiaojing threw out an arm and slapped it against the doorframe. “Don’t worry, Xiao Yi. I’m not here today about your case.” The man called Xiao Yi shrank back a step, not daring to bar the way.

Beyond the shanty lay a hidden world: a gambling den, no less. Great pains had been taken here—from outside it looked like nothing but a few tumbledown sheds, but within, the partitions had been knocked through into one broad common room, with tables and mats, though the light was dim.

At the moment several dozen gamblers were bent over three high tables, watching in high spirits as three bankers cast the dice, copper cash strewn everywhere about them. The instant Zhang Xiaojing entered, every eye turned to him. For a heartbeat the den fell silent, and then the crowd exploded: half of them started scrambling out the windows, the other half ducking under the tables, while a few hands did not forget to sweep up the money—a scene at once chaotic and comical.

A beggar-chief came rushing over in a great fury to see who was making the disturbance. When he saw Zhang Xiaojing standing there, he gaped as though he had seen a fiend, and for a moment forgot even to calm the gamblers down.

“Zhang... Chief Zhang?”

Zhang Xiaojing said, without any change of expression: “So this is where you ran off to?” The beggar-chief’s face showed his shame, and he dared not speak. Zhang Xiaojing said: “Take me to see your house-master.” The beggar-chief hesitated, but in the end did not dare refuse aloud. He turned back inside, asked for instructions, and then led them toward the rear.

“Beggar-chief,” “house-master,” and the like were all slang from the world that shunned the light. Yao Runeng, observing the way the man walked, found it much like Zhang Xiaojing’s, and guessed that he too had once been a man of the yamen, fallen somehow to this pass.

The shanties ran together into one sprawl, partitioned within by countless rooms and earthen walls, dark and sunless, like threading through a maze of tunnels. As they walked, one could faintly hear weeping and sounds of grief, as though someone were being held prisoner here.

A shiver of awe passed through Yao Runeng; he knew that he had now brushed against another Chang’an. This Chang’an shunned the light, steeped in blood and greed, with neither law nor morality, chaotic and cruel as the Buddhists’ hell of the asuras; those who could survive here were all men of the deepest wickedness. Even the authorities did not lightly venture deep into this layer of the world.

His throat went dry and his heartbeat quickened; he could not help looking ahead, and found Zhang Xiaojing walking on with steady tread, showing no discomfort at all. The outline of the man’s back was blurred and indistinct, as though it had melted into the murky background.

This former buliang chief must have ventured many a time into the tiger’s den and done many a battle with the forces of evil. So long as he followed the man, he could surely come to no harm. Besides, wicked men and thief-catchers were natural adversaries; if he trembled at so much as a glance at this place, how would he ever contend with them hereafter? At the thought, Yao Runeng plucked up his courage afresh, clenched his fists, and set his gaze ablaze.

He felt, all at once, a touch of regret: had Zhang Xiaojing not been a condemned man, he might well have been his superior now. The man was a bit too much of the underworld, perhaps, but there was truly a great deal to be learned from him.

They walked a good while, and then the way brightened before them, opening onto yet another hidden world: a small courtyard of brick and stone. The yard was not large but quite tidy, and on a stove in its center sat a lacquer-black medicine pot giving off a smell of herbs. A figure wrapped in a great scarlet fur sat cross-legged by the stove, a small yellow cat cradled in its arms.

Zhang Xiaojing said: “Old Ge. I trust you’ve kept well.”

The great fur stirred, and an aged voice came from within it: “Brother Zhang? I never thought to see you again.” The tone was flat—not a question, but the statement of a fact.

“Nor did I.” Zhang Xiaojing had no wish to explain.

“One return of yours, and my gambling den’s in an uproar, dogs and chickens flying—truly, the tiger may be dead, but the bones still stand and the terror of it lingers. What have you come to see me about?” the old man asked.

The great fur slid down, and only now did Yao Runeng see that the man wrapped within it was a small, withered old fellow, his skin black as ink-charcoal, his hair a mass of curls, his lips broad and flat—no man of the Central Plains, but, unmistakably, an old Kunlun slave! This Kunlun slave’s eyes were bright and ferocious, and he spoke a fluent official Mandarin without a trace of an accent. From their exchange, the two had plainly been acquainted for a long time, though clearly on no very good terms.

The strange thing was that Zhang Xiaojing, who had been so brutally rough in the West Market and at the Zoroastrian temple, was here, face to face with a truly wicked man, all courtesy instead. Yao Runeng had already braced himself for a fight to the death, yet neither of the two men before him showed any intention of coming to blows.

Zhang Xiaojing said: “Old Ge, you still owe me a favor.” Old Ge gave a click of the tongue and patted the cat in his arms. “Debts of money paid in money, debts of blood in blood—that is this old slave’s way of dealing with the world. Say what you want.”

Zhang Xiaojing drew out the wooden tablet and tossed it down before him. “This belonged to a man of Kucha named Long Bo. I want to know which house issued it to him, which girls he was intimate with, and where those girls are now. And I want to know at once.”

Old Ge picked up the tablet in a withered hand, studied it a moment, then reached over, lifted the lid of the medicine pot, and tapped it against the pot’s rim. A wiry, capable servant came into the courtyard; Old Ge gave him a few instructions, and the servant hurried off.

Old Ge gazed at Zhang Xiaojing. “This isn’t a Wannian County case, is it?” Zhang Xiaojing produced his waist-badge, inscribed “Jing’an: Curb Revolt, Bring Peace,” gave it a flourish, and put it away again. Old Ge rose slowly and said, “It won’t do for me to serve tea to men of the official side here; make yourselves at home,” and with that turned and went into the house.

Meeting Yao Runeng’s puzzled look, Zhang Xiaojing gave a brief account. This Old Ge had originally been an overseas Zanj slave, sold into Chang’an around the Shenlong years; he had first been a slave in the household of a vice-minister surnamed Ge, and was later sold into a pleasure house as a manservant. The ordinary Kunlun slave was honest and docile by nature and none too quick of wit; only Old Ge was an exception. Glib and resourceful, playing every side to advantage, he had thriven mightily, and before long had even talked his master into manumitting him, striking him from the slave register.

In the years since, he had made a trade of kidnapping people for the pleasure houses of the three lanes, and if a girl proved unruly or ran off, he took charge of breaking her and hunting her down. As time went on, Old Ge, by sheer ruthless cruelty, became the greatest slaver in the Pingkang Quarter and, in effect, a tyrant of the ward. The shanty district was his domain, and every girl knew: better to provoke a young lord than to anger Old Ge.

During his time in Wannian County, Zhang Xiaojing had worked several cases of abducting and selling free persons. But Old Ge was too cunning and had never once slipped up, and to this day sat secure in his shanties. Coming to the Pingkang Quarter on business now, Zhang Xiaojing knew that if he dealt with the madams they would fob him off with every excuse and waste his hours; far better to enlist Old Ge.

“But isn’t this colluding with a wicked man?” Yao Runeng could not understand it.

Because several elders of his family had died at the hands of bandits, Yao Runeng could least of all abide such villains running rampant. To his mind, the instant one met them one ought to strike them down, without a moment’s hesitation. Never had he imagined that Zhang Xiaojing, a man of the authorities, would sit down and bargain terms with them.

Zhang Xiaojing said: “Rats have their runs and snakes have their holes; wicked men have a wicked man’s ways, and there are some things the authorities simply cannot do.”

“But this shanty district is plainly right inside the Pingkang Quarter—a few dozen thief-catchers could sweep it clean. How can the authorities tolerate a kidnapper-and-slaver living free and easy here? This flatly violates the statutes of Great Tang!”

“Work it out for yourself. The answer to that question is your second lesson,” Zhang Xiaojing answered.

Yao Runeng bit his lip, unconvinced, thinking the answer dodged the substance for empty words. It suddenly struck him that Zhang Xiaojing, a man who had served nine years as a buliang chief in Chang’an, must carry a mountain of hidden secrets about him. Old Ge said he owed the man a favor—could it be the two of them had colluded before?

That being so, Zhang Xiaojing’s own hands could hardly be clean; perhaps it was for just such dealings that he had ended up in the death cells. At the thought, Yao Runeng, without a flicker, took a step farther off, remembering the other duty that had been laid upon him.

Before long Old Ge sent back word. The tablet had been issued by the house of Zhao Tuan’er in the First Lane; Long Bo had begun frequenting the place half a year ago, coming once every ten days, and each time he asked for a girl named Tong’er. He was not lavish with his money, but he never fell behind on the fees.

“Walking the horse, or staying to bathe?” Zhang Xiaojing asked. These were the cant of the Pingkang Quarter: “walking the horse” meant taking a courtesan out on an excursion, “staying to bathe” meant lodging the night.

“Now and then he stayed the night, but mostly he took her out.”

Something flickered in Zhang Xiaojing’s eyes. Huaiyuan Ward was a long way from here, and its neighbors were mostly devout Zoroastrian believers; Long Bo could never have taken Tong’er back there—which meant that he had some other place to put up.

“Where is Tong’er now?”

“The little minx got her heart all aflutter and ran off a day ago with an examination candidate.”

Zhang Xiaojing smiled faintly. “Since when did any bird fly free of Old Ge’s hand?” At these words the creases of Old Ge’s black face relaxed, and his thick lips parted to show white teeth, like a row of human bones laid out in the night.

He crooked a finger and said, Follow me.

Old Ge drew the great fur tight about him and led them into the maze of shanties. Their roofs were thatched with straw of uneven thickness, so that as they walked the sunlight filtering down flickered bright and dim by turns, giving every face a blurred, dreamlike cast. Along both sides of the passage were small stalls, one after another, some with their wooden doors locked fast, some flung wide open, but all without exception giving off the reek of rotting straw. Within them shadowy figures stirred, soundless, like walking corpses.

As Yao Runeng walked, a skeletal hand suddenly reached out of the darkness, startling a cry out of him. Looking more closely, he saw that it was a woman, thin as a dry stick, sprawled before a door. Old Ge let out a low snarl, and the woman hastily drew her hand back.

Old Ge did not slacken his pace, and his voice sounded cold amid all this ghostliness: “Outsiders all call the Pingkang Quarter a paradise of ravished souls, every girl an immortal maiden and a divine beauty—little do they know how much filth lies behind it. Girls with the running sores of the pox, once-celebrated beauties whose faces have been ruined, children born crippled and deformed... with nowhere to go and no one to take them in, they all flow together like sewage and gather here, sitting and waiting to be reborn. This old slave has done every evil there is, and has never feared going down to any Avīci hell—heh, I am already in the midst of that reckoning, and long since ceased to find it novel.”

Yao Runeng was appalled to hear it; he had never imagined that the dark corners of the Pingkang Quarter could be so foul and sordid. He turned his head aside and saw that Zhang Xiaojing’s face had not changed in the least—plainly he had known all along.

At last they came to a dim firewood shed. When the door was opened, two people hung within, a man and a woman, both with faces caked in blood and their spirits crushed. The woman’s goose-yellow gown was torn to tatters, baring skin as white as ivory. The man was soft-skinned and delicate, of a frail, scholarly cast, his head drooping as if he had already fainted. A misshapen dwarf with a crooked face stood to one side, a leather whip in his hand.

Zhang Xiaojing was about to step forward when Old Ge put out a hand to stop him and drew them into the room next door. “Brother Zhang, your favor goes only this far.” His meaning was plain: I’ve told you where this woman is, and the debt is repaid. Whatever you mean to do with her next is another reckoning.

Zhang Xiaojing said: “Then I’ll owe you a favor.” Old Ge sneered. “The favor of a man about to die is base coin. Offer something else.” Yao Runeng hastily broke in: “The Jing’an Bureau can pay you a handsome reward.” Old Ge shot him a glance, unmoved, as though looking at some comical stage-clown.

Yao Runeng was in a fever of impatience—how could they be held up here by an old Kunlun slave? He drew the saber at his side and said loudly: “Obstruct the Jing’an Bureau in its case, and I’ll have this shanty of yours razed within a single double-hour, believe it or not!”

Old Ge shrugged; the threats he had heard in his life likely outnumbered all the words this youngster had ever spoken. Zhang Xiaojing patted Yao Runeng on the shoulder to make him step back, then looked at Old Ge. “What do you want?” Old Ge narrowed his eyes and sized him up, as if pondering what might be squeezed out of this condemned man. All at once his face broke into a smile, the swarthy creases quivering, and he held up two fingers. “Two.”

Zhang Xiaojing’s two short brows suddenly knotted together; after much hesitation, he answered with a single finger. Old Ge pondered a moment, then laughed. “Let’s do it that way, then.” Zhang Xiaojing’s face was far from pleasant, but he nodded all the same.

Yao Runeng was rather at a loss; the two of them were speaking in riddles again—what on earth did it mean?

Old Ge cupped his hands, said, Allow me to withdraw a moment, and vanished into the gloom. Zhang Xiaojing stayed where he was, leaning at a slant against a pillar, a finger brushing the grit from the hollow of his eye. The faint light coming down through the roof sketched him in a dim profile.

“Commander Zhang, what were the terms you struck with him?”

“Just now I promised to give him the name of one of the authorities’ planted agents,” Zhang Xiaojing answered indifferently.

Yao Runeng’s shoulders gave a violent start, his eyes went round, and he could not help crying out: “You... how could you do such a thing?”

Zhang Xiaojing had been buliang chief of Wannian County, and he knew to the last detail the forces the authorities had planted in the underworld—might even, once, have run them himself. Never in the world had Yao Runeng imagined that the fellow, for the sake of his own convenience, would sell a comrade to the bandits! It was beyond all comprehension!

Zhang Xiaojing said: “It is the only way to win Old Ge’s cooperation.”

Yao Runeng quietly slid his right hand to the hilt of his saber, Li Bi’s parting charge rising up in his mind.

Before they set out, Li Bi had seen him alone: the moment he detected any sign that Zhang Xiaojing meant to flee or turn traitor, he was to raise the alarm at once, and if he were somewhere the alarm could not be raised, to deal with it himself. Yao Runeng felt that Zhang Xiaojing had now shown his hand. He simply did not believe that dealing with a single villain required such abject compromise. Something was wrong, and it had to be stopped before the man sold off yet more of the authorities’ interests.

But the instant Zhang Xiaojing saw him make to act, he lashed out first with a flying kick that sent Yao Runeng crashing hard to the ground, a killing intent surging up in his single eye. “Sit still!” Yao Runeng struggled, but could not get up—so heavy had that kick been. He curled himself into an agonized ball, yet his eyes blazed with fury.

To trade away one of the authorities’ own planted agents for intelligence—it was the very depth of shamelessness! Yao Runeng struggled up from the ground and demanded loudly: “Why sell out your own people?”

Zhang Xiaojing swept him a glance and said coldly: “Deputy Director Li’s order is to stop the Türks at any cost—do you understand? At any cost.”

“And to gain your ends, are you to throw away even the bottom line of decency, and all morality with it?” To Yao Runeng the argument was the height of absurdity.

“I care only whether these several hundred thousand lives in Chang’an can be saved.”

Stung by the retort, Yao Runeng flushed and argued back: “That’s sophistry. A gentleman does some things and refuses others. If these bandits demanded that you do something truly wicked—er, plot treason against the Son of Heaven, say—would you agree to that too?”

Zhang Xiaojing gave a slight nod. “The life of one man, naturally, weighs less than the lives of the multitude.”

At words so treasonous and outrageous, Yao Runeng was simply dumbfounded. “You dare—” He had not finished before a force suddenly seized him by the throat and slammed his back against the wall with a thud. Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye was almost pressed to the tip of his nose, and his hoarse voice rasped viciously in his ear:

“Listen. There are only three double-hours left before Chang’an is destroyed, and we haven’t so much as touched the edge of the Türks. If you won’t help, then get out of my sight!”

Yao Runeng stiffened his neck, giving no ground: “Drop the act—you don’t care in the least for Chang’an’s safety. You’re a condemned man; you must have done some wrong; you hate the court!” In the light and shadow, Zhang Xiaojing’s expression underwent a subtle change—a bitter smile beyond words, with mockery and sorrow buried deep within it.

“That’s right. I hate this court—but I am the only one who can save it.”

Just then came a dense patter of footsteps, and more than twenty men filed in one after another, every one of them male, of varying height and varying age, all in short jackets and white shirts. Yao Runeng recognized several of the faces—men he had seen in the gambling den. Old Ge had them stand in a row, then made a gesture toward Zhang Xiaojing.

Yao Runeng went rigid all over; even without knowing the cant, he understood what Old Ge meant. He had not thought the Kunlun slave so ruthless—not only was Zhang Xiaojing to speak the planted agent’s name, he was to point him out to his face. What came next needed no thinking through: Zhang Xiaojing would have to kill the agent with his own hand before the bargain was complete—this was what they called an “oath-token of blood.”

Yao Runeng looked tensely at Zhang Xiaojing and was about to speak up in protest when a sudden chop to the side of his neck from the latter knocked him out cold on the spot.

Old Ge gave a chuckle. “You’re quite tender toward this little official hawk—he’s a good deal like you were, back in the day.” Zhang Xiaojing did not take up the remark, but walked over and swept his gaze around the twenty-odd men.

The muscles of Zhang Xiaojing’s cheek twitched faintly. Even for a condemned man, to help a former enemy identify a comrade demanded the overcoming of a great inner barrier. His arm rose slowly—when Old Ge suddenly spoke again: “Chief Zhang, in fact there is another road you might choose.”

“Oh?”

“These old eyes of mine can see it—this job, the authorities are holding it over you with a pardon from your death sentence, aren’t they?”

Zhang Xiaojing kept silent, but he did not deny it.

“Heh, heh. They do love to do things that way.” Old Ge laid his fingers elegantly together. “How about we make a different bargain? I won’t force you to point anyone out; only tell this old slave what’s afoot in Chang’an, and I’ll see you safely out of the city, and from then on—the sea is wide, let the fish leap; the sky is high, let the bird fly. Wouldn’t that be a fine thing?”

It had to be said, Old Ge’s offer was greatly tempting. Once out of Chang’an, Zhang Xiaojing would be a wholly free man; the Jing’an Bureau and Li Bi would have no leisure to pursue him—they did not even know whether they would live through the night—and the price Zhang Xiaojing would have to pay was next to nothing at all.

This road was far easier than killing a former comrade for intelligence and then, under a burden of suspicion, going on to hunt down the Türk killers.

The room went very quiet, with only the faint weeping of a woman coming from next door. Zhang Xiaojing stood in the shadows, closed his eyes briefly, and opened them again in less than the snap of a finger, raising a hand to brush the grit from the hollow of his eye. “Sorry, Old Ge. This time, I still can’t leave.”

“You’re that fond of playing the court’s running dog?”

“No. This time it has nothing to do with the court.” Zhang Xiaojing tilted his head up, and a faint light spilled down through the gaps in the thatch.

“Pedantic fool,” Old Ge pronounced acidly, then stretched himself lazily. “All right. This old slave has done all that kindness and duty require. Point out the planted agent, then—best of all the one you brought in yourself, in the old days; that’s just the kind of show I love to watch.”

Zhang Xiaojing swept his gaze over the men once more, and his eyes turned resolute. Suddenly he dropped to one knee and, composing his face, cupped his hands. “What I do today is truly forced upon me by desperate straits, and cannot be helped. When we meet below, in the Nine Springs, I will beg your pardon then.”

One man in the row changed color and hastily sprang backward. Zhang Xiaojing rose and struck without warning; a flash of the blade, and it slid across the man’s throat. Before the others could so much as react, the man crumpled limply to the ground, dead—none other than Xiao Yi, who had opened the door for them a while before.

The beggar-chief from the gambling den stood in the row, his legs trembling violently.

“Tsk, tsk. I’m a little sorry now that I let you do it yourself.” Old Ge licked his lips with faint reluctance. “Had he fallen into our hands, he’d not have finished dying in three whole days.”

His face iron-gray, Zhang Xiaojing raised the blade again. The beggar-chief from the gambling den dropped to his knees with a thud, wailing over and over: “I really only came to throw myself on Old Ge because I couldn’t make a go of it in the yamen anymore—I did it for the money, I’m no planted agent!” He was still crying out when suddenly a blood-soaked finger dropped to the ground before him. Bewildered, the beggar-chief looked up and saw that the little finger of Zhang Xiaojing’s left hand had been severed clean at the root, blood pouring from it without stop.

The whole place fell silent as a graveyard, and only Zhang Xiaojing’s voice rose up: “Xiao Yi was brought in by my own hand, and betrayed by my own hand. For the greater good, I have no regret. This killing is a sin I will repay sooner or later—but not now. So I sever a finger to mark it, and I ask you all to bear me witness.”

Old Ge shook his head and sneered. “Pedantic fool. It’s only one life; sold is sold—is there any need to reproach yourself like this?” Zhang Xiaojing paid him no heed, but drew a square of silk cloth from inside his robe and set about binding the wound one-handed. The beggar-chief from the gambling den glanced timidly at Old Ge, and seeing no reaction from him, hastily rose and bound Zhang Xiaojing’s wound for him, all eagerness.

He was well practiced at the task; in his yamen days he had dressed Chief Zhang’s wounds often enough. Once the wound was seen to, Zhang Xiaojing lifted the hem of his robe, wiped the blood clean from his blade, and said to Old Ge, word by word, his face pained and savage:

“Old Ge. Your turn.”

The fierce killing intent pouring off him now silenced even the old black slave. The latter moved his lips, but in the end offered no more mockery.

...Yao Runeng came slowly to and found himself lying in an interrogation room, a man and a woman bound tightly before his eyes. He was just in time to see Old Ge snap his fingers, and the dwarf hand the leather whip to Zhang Xiaojing.

Had Zhang Xiaojing already finished pointing them out? Killed all the planted agents? He was about to open his mouth to ask when a hand pressed him down to the floor. Old Ge turned his head and hushed him with a “shh.”

Ahead, Zhang Xiaojing gripped the whip-handle, his gaze traveling back and forth between the two, then settling on the woman. He said to Tong’er: “I am going to ask you one question about Long Bo, and I hope you will answer it truthfully.”

Tong’er jerked her head up and cried shrilly: “Unless you let me and Young Han go, don’t dream of making me speak!” She and her lover had been held captive a day and a night and were near despair, and now, having at last caught hold of a life-saving straw, she clung to it for dear life. Zhang Xiaojing studied her a moment: the woman’s body was covered with whip-scars—plainly she had been beaten who knew how many times, and torture would be no use on her.

Zhang Xiaojing said: “Tell me, and I can ask a favor of Old Ge and let you go.”

Tong’er sneered. “Don’t think you can drive us apart! We have sworn an oath—to live and die together, and never to go on alone!”

Zhang Xiaojing shook his head and went instead to stand before Young Han. The man raised his head, and seeing that these were men of the authorities, was about to cry out for rescue when the whip-handle was thrust into his mouth. Beside him, Tong’er cried out again: “It’s no use! Kill Young Han, and I’ll die for love along with him.”

Zhang Xiaojing ignored her and said to the man: “I can save only one of you and let that one go. You may choose which—but remember, you may choose only one.”

With that, Zhang Xiaojing stepped back a few paces and watched with a cold eye. The man was first startled and doubtful, then wild with hope, murmuring to himself again and again; but each time he looked at Tong’er, hesitation rose in him, and he would not clearly speak a name. Then Zhang Xiaojing suddenly leaned in, put his ear close to the man, and nodded.

“Good.” Zhang Xiaojing set down the whip, and with one stroke of the blade cut through the hemp rope from which the man hung.

Young Han tumbled to the ground and was dazed for a moment—he had said nothing at all, after all. But the words rose to his lips and then, all at once, he hesitated. He edged a few tentative steps, saw that the fierce-looking men made no move, and then a wild joy welled up from the depths of his eyes—as though someone had made the decision for him, so that he need feel no guilt over it. He glanced left and right, and, no one barring his way, covered his face with his sleeve and fled in a panic toward the exit.

Once he had gone far off, Zhang Xiaojing went back to stand before Tong’er. She stared blankly at the rope lying broken in two on the ground, her lovely head bowed, as if she could not believe it was real.

“You tricked me—he never said anything at all!” Tong’er suddenly raised her head and cried out in fury.

“With a man, you must not listen to what he says; you must watch what he does. If he had truly had no wish to leave, how could I have moved his two legs for him?” Zhang Xiaojing’s tone was flat, as though he were stating a simple fact.

Tong’er broke into loud sobs. Yao Runeng, unable to bear the sight, turned his head aside. With one small test of human nature, Zhang Xiaojing had pulled the firewood from beneath the cauldron and destroyed the girl’s hope. But then, come to think of it—the man had not so much as blinked at betraying a comrade; what was a thing like this to him?

Zhang Xiaojing lifted Tong’er’s chin with the tip of the whip. “Now, will you answer my question?” She refused no longer; she had no reason left to hold out.

By her account, Long Bo had chosen her the very first time he came to the Pingkang Quarter and had never changed to another girl since. The man spoke little, never revealed anything of who he was, and made scarcely a sound even in bed. Several times he had taken her out “walking the horse,” going to a great mansion southwest of the crossroads in Xiuzheng Ward. The mansion was very large, and she had asked Long Bo where it came from. Long Bo only said he was minding it for someone, without saying who.

Zhang Xiaojing turned to Old Ge and said he had taken it upon himself to let one of them go, and begged his pardon for it. Old Ge laughed. “We’re no sadists. All this display is only to teach the girls to give up such fancies. With a single word, Brother Zhang, you’ve let Tong’er learn the full harm men can do, and saved us the trouble besides—now she can go straight back to her foster-mother.”

The misshapen little man untied Tong’er and dragged her from the room.

Yao Runeng could bear it no longer and at last spoke up: “Commander Zhang, to bully a weak woman like this—is it not a departure from the way of benevolence and righteousness? ...Of course! You’d even kill your own comrade; what would a thing like this be to you?” It stuck in his throat like a fishbone, and not to say it was truly unbearable. Zhang Xiaojing raised his head, his eyes full of mockery. “Oh? You mean her end would have been better than this, had she followed a man like that home?”

Yao Runeng let out a strangled “er” and could not answer. He had come across cases like it, and indeed scarcely one had ended well. Zhang Xiaojing said coldly: “Everyone must answer for his own choices. She chose this road, and she ought long since to have made her peace with it. If you find her so pitiable, then marry her and take her home.”

Yao Runeng flushed red to the ears and shut his mouth, struck dumb. But he had already made up his mind: the moment he left the Pingkang Quarter, he would report at once to the Jing’an Bureau that Zhang Xiaojing’s conduct had gone entirely past the bounds.

***

Cao Poyan’s elbow had been aching dully all along. It was thoroughly uncomfortable, but at least it kept him alert at all times. In this city bristling with hidden dangers, nothing mattered more than a keen edge of the senses.

He now stood at the entrance to a secluded great courtyard, watching a train of carts roll slowly in. The train numbered a full ten carts, all twin-shafted covered wagons, hung on all four sides with thick blue curtains, their roofs arched high. From the depth of the wheel-ruts one could tell that the goods loaded inside were quite heavy. Every cart was caked with dust and mud, and both the shaft-horses and the drivers showed their exhaustion plainly.

From the green-edged triangular pennant planted at the front of each cart, one could tell that they belonged to Su’s Cart-and-Horse Company. This company specialized in the haulage of civilian goods north of Chang’an and enjoyed a fine reputation.

The head carter leading the train jumped down from the first wagon, slapped the dust from his clothes, and let out a great sigh of relief.

This run from Yanzhou prefecture to Chang’an had been a good one: the client paid readily, and what they carried was nothing valuable, so there had been no need to travel in dread along the way. The one demand the client had been strict about was the timing—the goods had to arrive, no matter what, on the day before the Lantern Festival. Now that the train had made it into the depot smoothly within the hour of the Horse, he had nothing left to worry about.

By the rules, in fact, such bulk goods could only be hauled into the two markets, East and West, and distributed from there. The other ward gates were all fitted with dragon-barriers, and a wide-tracked cart simply could not get through. But this depot was out of the way and little frequented, its entrance opening directly onto the street, and the dragon-barrier had long since been taken off.

This sort of little dodge to save a bit of tax the head carter had seen plenty of, and thought nothing strange of it at all.

All that remained was to check off the goods with the party receiving them and collect a tally-slip, and the job would be done. The head carter had already settled on his plans for the afternoon: find a bathhouse and have a good long soak to ease his body, then go to the West Market and buy his woman some foreign wares, and in the evening get a jar of fine sanle cordial, find some high spot, and drink while watching the lantern show—a perfect day!

The head carter looked around and picked out Cao Poyan at a glance as the man in charge here. He sidled over, his face wreathed in smiles: “Good sir, I’ve not failed the commission—every last piece of the goods accounted for, and the timing just right.” Then he held out a rolled-up bundle of mint leaves, used to keep a driver alert on the road, and grown only in the Jiang-Huai region.

But Cao Poyan did not take it at all, and said expressionlessly: “When you entered the city, was there any hindrance?”

When bulk goods of this kind entered Chang’an, the gate-wardens had to examine and register them before letting them pass. But the goods were many and the clerks few, and an inspection often dragged on for several days. Su’s Cart-and-Horse Company ran goods year in and year out and was on very good terms with the gate-wardens, so it could shorten the customs time—this was the strength it relied on to dare run the Chang’an route.

Hearing him ask, the head carter clapped his own chest, full of self-satisfaction: “We had a friend to smooth the way—no trouble at all. We cleared customs at the hour of the Dragon, and were let through in less than two double-hours. The papers are all here, every one of them.”

With that he handed a stack of documents to Cao Poyan, who leafed through them briefly and asked again:

“Did they inspect the goods?”

The head carter said with an ingratiating smile: “Unless you hold a title of nobility, there’s no getting out of that. But I watched the whole time—they only spot-checked two of the pieces, gave them a poke with a spear, and sealed them back up. Then again, this stuff you’re hauling—it’s neither contraband nor over the sumptuary limits; what could possibly go wrong with it? You worry far too much...”

Cao Poyan had no wish to hear him prattle on, and made a gesture with one hand. “Unload.”

Rebuffed—his warm face pressed to a cold backside—the head carter dropped his eager chatter. He turned away and gave the orders, and the drivers, shouting at their horses, wheeled the carts about and backed them slowly toward the mansion entrance, tail-ends aligned.

The place had already been converted into a makeshift depot, with a raised unloading platform. The carts were parked beautifully, their tail-gates fitted snug against the platform’s edge with scarcely any gap. The hands inside gathered round and opened the tail-gates; in each cart stood ten great cypress-wood casks, bedded on straw three fingers thick. They laid down a few long planks and rolled the casks down one by one. The head carter noticed that the hands all had foreign faces—not a single man of Tang among them.

What he did not notice, however, was that several of the hands went to the depot entrance and barred the great doors.

The great cypress casks were unloaded onto the platform one after another. Cao Poyan went up to one of them, pried out the stopper at its top, thrust a dagger inside and stirred, then lifted it out to look at the oily film on the blade. After checking a few casks, Cao Poyan nodded in satisfaction: this shipment was without any fault—top quality, tightly packed, not a drop spilled or leaked along the way.

These pitiable carters believed they were transporting ordinary goods, never knowing that it was the very soul of the “great” Que-le Huo-duo.

Setting down the dagger, Cao Poyan asked the head carter: “After you entered the city, did you come straight here?”

“Of course. We’d never waste a client’s time.”

“Then—does anyone else in Chang’an know that you have arrived?”

“No. One keeps a client’s confidence, after all. Only once we’ve finished unloading with you and collected the final payment will we go to the brokers’ guild to report the job done.”

The next instant, Cao Poyan drove the oil-dripping dagger straight into the head carter’s chest and gave the handle a twist. The head carter staggered back a few steps, craning his neck in an attempt to crawl away. The last sight he had of this world was the bloody spectacle of the other carters being slaughtered.

It was a swift and silent massacre, over in the space of a breath. These travel-worn carters, who had not even had time to rest, died wretchedly beside their carts, and of the whole train not one was spared.

The clamor soon ended, and the depot returned to quiet. This small commotion had disturbed no one. Cao Poyan ordered his hands to take Su’s carts and shaft-horses apart, paint over the brands on the horses’ rumps, take down the pennants, and wipe away every trace that belonged to Su’s Company.

Just then a soft knock came from outside the depot. Cao Poyan frowned, went over, and looked out through a hole in the door-planks. Standing before the door was a man in a shabby cloak of mottled colors, the futou on his head worn to rags, the headcloth beneath showing through. The common folk of the Three Adjuncts were dressed more or less like this.

“Whither does the steppe’s blue-grey courser run?” Cao Poyan asked through the door-planks, in Turkic.

“Where the arrow-head points, there the horse’s head turns,” the newcomer answered, in a voice thin and high as a woman’s.

The passwords matched, and Cao Poyan drew back the bar and let him in. The newcomer threw open his cloak, revealing a gaunt, withered face and a sharp, jutting hooked nose.

“I am Long Bo.” He split his mouth in a wide, radiant grin.

Cao Poyan frowned. He had never seen Long Bo before; he knew only that the man came from Kucha, lay hidden in Chang’an, and had arranged everything single-handed, this out-of-the-way depot and the safe-house included. In truth, Long Bo had been recruited by His Excellency the Right Shad, and Cao Poyan knew nothing whatever about him.

But he had not expected the man to be a Tang Chinese.

“I need a token to prove your identity.” Cao Poyan gripped the dagger tightly, full of wariness.

Long Bo suddenly crouched down, and Cao Poyan jerked back a step, a fierce light flaring in his eyes. Long Bo gave a little laugh. “Oh, come now, why so jumpy? Am I going to eat you?” As he spoke he pulled off the soft-soled thick boot from his left foot, snapped the sole open with a crack, and drew out a packet of thick, yellowish paper.

To keep out the damp, the paper had been steeped in oil, and it felt slick and greasy in the hand. Cao Poyan carefully unfolded it and looked: it was indeed a ward map of Chang’an, marked in the finest detail—the street corners of every ward, the constables’ posts, the ceremonial arches, the barracks, the government offices, the watchtowers, the bridges, and even the courses of each ward’s hidden culverts and the mansions of its great households, all set down. The whole panorama of Chang’an, plain at a single glance.

This ward map had originally been made in secret by the Xifu gold-and-silver shop, then carried by a Wolf Guard to the Zoroastrian temple in Huaiyuan Ward, where Long Bo had taken it in the confusion. Since he could produce the ward map, he was beyond doubt Long Bo himself.

Cao Poyan held one corner of the ward map, his heart a tangle of feelings. For this thing he had lost fully fifteen of his elite men. Now that the ward map had arrived, the last bronze clasp had at last been fitted onto His Excellency the Right Shad’s nine-linked-rings puzzle.

“For this wretched scrap I can never show my face in Chang’an again; His Excellency the Right Shad had better add a good deal more to my pay,” Long Bo complained.

At these words Cao Poyan frowned. “The Jing’an Bureau has found you?”

“By now I’d say half of Chang’an is looking for me—even a newly crowned top-scholar doesn’t get such treatment.” Long Bo was even a little pleased with himself over it.

The clouds on Cao Poyan’s face darkened. “Then those houses you handled, and this depot—might they trace them?”

Long Bo cocked his head. “These places I all rented under false names through different brokers, and I left no proof at my lodgings either. Unless they’re immortals, there’s no way they’ll find them—eh? What are you standing there gaping for? Let me in, quick.” Long Bo urged him on. Only then did Cao Poyan throw off his tangled thoughts, step aside to let him in, and shut the door again behind him.

Long Bo came into the courtyard, saw the ground strewn with corpses, and caught the thick reek of blood in his nostrils. Far from being shocked, he looked this way and that. “So then—the goods from Yanzhou prefecture have already been delivered?”

“Safely into the storehouse. And those who needed dealing with have all been dealt with clean.”

“Tsk, tsk. These carters, so pitiable—a thousand li to deliver their own deaths.” Long Bo prattled on as he walked up to the depot platform and patted the huge cypress casks. “So this is what you people call the soul of the Que-le Huo-duo—and where, then, is the body of the Que-le Huo-duo?”

Cao Poyan was much displeased with his flippancy, and answered grudgingly: “Things are ready at the bamboo-ware shop. Once the carts have been refitted, I’ll bring the body here. And then it will fall to you to complete the final step, the assembly.”

It was an irony that the Que-le Huo-duo stood for the wrath of the Türk khagan, and yet only Long Bo, this master craftsman of Kucha, understood how to assemble it.

Long Bo paced in a few circles, and as if chanting a ballad, said: “When the two, soul and body, are made one, the great Que-le Huo-duo will come back to life. And this ward map will guide it to destroy all of Chang’an.” Then he could not help letting out a snort of laughter, and muttered under his breath: “That code-name your Türk khagan came up with—it really is a scream!”

The corner of Cao Poyan’s mouth twitched; he felt the Khagan had been insulted. He gripped the dagger, bent his right leg slightly into a stance from which he might strike at any moment, and resolved to teach this fellow a lesson. Long Bo took a few steps forward and suddenly bent down, as though to dodge the killing thrust. Cao Poyan’s body swayed, his muscles taut, and he half-believed his intent had been seen through.

Luckily Long Bo only meant to pick something up off the ground: a dainty little gold-traced silk reckoning-pouch, dropped, most likely, by the head carter as he struggled. Inside the pouch lay a dozen or so bundles of mint leaves rolled into little columns. A light came into Long Bo’s triangular eyes; he took up one bundle, tossed it into his mouth, chewed a few times, and let out a contented snort through his nostrils.

Cao Poyan quietly lowered the dagger, warning himself not to make new complications for the moment.

Long Bo chewed away ceaselessly at the mint leaves, a gleam flashing in his jet-black pupils. “When will the body be brought over?”

“The carts will set out within a quarter-hour and return in half a double-hour. I hope you can finish the final assembly within two double-hours.”

Long Bo looked around. “There aren’t many men working in the depot—where are Ma Ge’er and the others?”

“I only act on orders. Where they are, you can go and ask His Excellency the Right Shad,” Cao Poyan said with a cold sneer.

Long Bo made a helpless gesture. “No time to lose—get out all the tools and materials; I’m going to start the assembly.” He shook out his wrists, his mouth chewing without a moment’s pause.

***

Taiping Ward lay at the northernmost end of the second street west of the Vermilion Bird Avenue, directly facing the Hanguang Gate of the palace city, very close to the offices within the palace city. In the Shiji Temple in the southwestern corner of Taiping Ward stood a Pure Land Cloister reputed to be “the finest in the capital.” Within its grounds stupas and banner-pillars rose thick, and among a bamboo grove stood a hundred and eight clay Buddhas of good works—the very air steeped in meditative calm.

At this moment, in a small upturned-eaved pavilion deep within the bamboo grove, two men stood side by side. One wore a plain green gown and a white kerchief—Li Bi, just come from the Jing’an Bureau; the other was robed in vermilion and girded in purple, nobility fairly radiating from him. Had a third person been at hand, he would at once have recognized this thin-faced nobleman as none other than the reigning heir apparent, Li Heng. The two of them gazed out over the railing into the distance, seemingly appreciating together the meditative mood of the temple woods beyond—yet the words that passed between them had not the least thing to do with Buddhist doctrine.

“So it really was you who forced Director He out?” Li Heng was close to Li Bi in age, and his face was full of worry.

Li Bi bowed slightly, but his manner was firm. “Just so. As I said a moment ago, while Director He stays, the Türks cannot be rooted out. In this I have done no wrong.” Li Heng pointed up above their heads and sighed. “Director He is like this very pavilion: with him to shelter us, we can play our game of chess at our ease. Tear it down, and there’s room enough to move about, true—but should a storm blow up, what then? Changyuan, you’ve handled this rashly.”

“With a fierce tiger at hand, ready to devour us, where is there leisure to mind a storm?” Li Bi threw back at once. His manner left Li Heng a little discomfited; several times he wanted to lower his face and deliver a rebuke, but each time the words reached his lips he glanced at Li Bi and forced them back down.

Between him and Li Bi there was long since more than the mere concord of prince and minister. Li Bi had entered the Eastern Palace as a study-companion when very young, and after all these years together their friendship was deep, with nothing they could not say to each other. The pity was that, for all Li Bi’s great talents, his heart was set on the Dao, and he had little interest in an official career. To set up the Jing’an Bureau this time, Li Heng had had to plead with him a good long while before he could talk Li Bi into coming down from the mountains to help him.

Li Bi never softened his words for Li Heng. Li Heng, knowing his temper, could only wave a hand and say, in a tone of entreaty: “Ah, what am I to do with you—go and invite Director He back, won’t you?”

“No. There’s no time for that.” Li Bi’s face was grave. “There are not three double-hours left now before the lantern festival, and the business of the Türks is still without a clue. Were it not for my concern that Your Highness might read too much into it, I ought not to have come even to the Pure Land Cloister at all.”

Li Heng gave a click of the tongue and patted his back. “I won’t read too much into it. It’s only... er, how shall I put it. Director He is the anchor-star: with him or without him, the standing of the Jing’an Bureau at court, and in my imperial father’s eyes, is a very different thing.”

As far back as the third year of Tianbao, He Zhizhang had been chosen as tutor to the heir apparent, to instruct him in his reading. The bond of master and pupil between the two went back more than twenty years, and Li Heng’s closeness to He Zhizhang was in no way inferior to his relationship with Li Bi.

He Zhizhang stood very high in the Son of Heaven’s regard, and when Li Heng had first asked him to serve as Director of the Jing’an Bureau, it was in the hope that he could overawe the petty men and let Li Bi work with an easy mind. He had never expected the two to fall out—still less that the ever-modest, ever-serene Li Bi would drive He Zhizhang away... And with him gone, the situation was hard to speak for.

The Jing’an Bureau was the most important card in Li Heng’s hand, and should his political enemies seize on some handle against it, the matter would turn grave indeed.

He had, first, no shelter from the inner palace; second, no answering support from the frontier commands; third, he dared not cultivate the emperor’s close attendants. Even in this newly founded Jing’an Bureau, the only one who could truly be called a confidant was Li Bi, and no other.

“You know, being heir apparent of Great Tang has never been an easy thing...” Li Heng complained bitterly.

“Your Highness fears the talk at court—do you not, then, fear His Majesty?” Li Bi said quietly.

Li Heng’s face changed in a flash—this, what kind of talk was this?

Li Bi took a step forward and lowered his voice: “As suspicious as His Majesty is, he has nonetheless handed the defense of Chang’an over to Your Highness to manage. What is the reason for that?” Li Heng fell silent at once.

The Son of Heaven’s suspicion of his imperial sons was known to all the world. Before, an heir apparent had been deposed; after, there had come the calamity of the three commoner-princes. Since becoming heir apparent, Li Heng had not so much as entered the Eastern Palace. That the Son of Heaven should this time, in an unprecedented step, tacitly permit the heir apparent to establish the Jing’an Bureau, its authority overriding all the other offices, entrusting the whole of Chang’an to him—plainly he did so with a mind to test him.

This was at once a test of the heir apparent’s intentions and a test of his ability.

This maneuver Li Bi saw through utterly, and He Zhizhang saw through it too. But the two men’s ways of thinking were altogether different. He Zhizhang would rather the thing were not done well, so long as his heart was set right; Li Bi was just the opposite—do the thing as well as possible, and never mind giving offense.

“It may be three days before our political rivals make their move. But it is only three double-hours before the Türks make theirs!—so do not mistake where the weight lies, Your Highness. If Chang’an comes through unharmed, His Majesty’s dragon countenance will be delighted, and Your Highness’s position will be as steady as Mount Tai. But if Chang’an cannot be saved...” He slowed his tone and drew in his expression. “Mm—then there will be no ‘afterward’ at all.”

Li Heng was frightened by this tone, but still somewhat unreconciled. “Director He wants to catch the bandits, and you want to catch the bandits—can the two of you really not pull together in harmony?”

“No, we cannot—there’s no time for it! The Jing’an Bureau’s orders must issue from a single source!” Li Bi flicked his fly-whisk, and a note of reproach entered his cold, clear voice. “I have descended into the mundane world and broken my heart set on the Dao, laboring at all these vexing petty affairs—does Your Highness suppose that I am scrambling for power and profit?”

“Nonsense! I never thought any such thing,” Li Heng hastily protested.

Li Bi made no sound. He tilted his head up, his gaze passing over the pavilion’s eaves to the sky, and suddenly heaved a sigh.

Li Heng gave a rueful smile and went over to take him by the arm. “I know you do it for me; I’m not doubting you. It’s only that this upheaval is a bit of a muddle, and I can’t help being cautious about how I proceed... Ah, never mind, never mind. Since Director He has already retired on grounds of illness, let the matter rest there for now.” He was about to add a few more words of caution when Li Bi cupped his hands. “The hour has come; I must return to the Jing’an Bureau.”

Li Heng said, put out: “Then what more do you need me to do?”

“For these three double-hours, Your Highness must stand firmly on my side and support every decision I make. There is no time for questioning or discussion; everything must go entirely by my rules.”

“Changyuan’s rules? And what are those?” Li Heng was suddenly curious.

“That there are no rules at all.”
