## How Gu Zhuxuan Rose in Zhabei and Opened the Tianchan Stage

{d} By Wang Delin

Before Liberation, when Gu Zhuxuan was deputy commander of the Zhabei Defense Corps, I was commander of its Ninth Company, and for a time we served together; so I came to know a little of Gu's life. Here, so far as memory serves, is a brief sketch.

Gu Zhuxuan was a native of Yancheng in northern Jiangsu. His given name was Songmao, Zhuxuan being a style-name. He was one of four brothers, the fourth in order; and because his nose was especially large, the Shanghainese hung on him the nickname "Gu the Fourth Big-Nose."

Before the founding of the Republic, Gu came to Shanghai to make a living and lodged at the south end of Tianbao Lane in Zhabei. It happened that the police of the old British Concession were recruiting Chinese constables; the main requirement was that a man be young, strong, and tall, no account being taken of his schooling. Gu was of powerful build and fit the bill, and after sitting the examination he was taken on. After a time — over what, no one knew — he was dismissed. Someone then introduced him to a wealthy household, where he pulled a private rickshaw. Before long, again over some unknown affair, the private-rickshaw work fell through, and he took to pulling an ordinary rickshaw for his bread. So it went until about the seventh or eighth year of the Republic, when he had saved a little money and bought several rickshaws, which he rented out for others to pull while he sat and drew the rents — what the Shanghainese call "putting out rickshaws" — living off what he squeezed from them.

Old Shanghai was a world of hooligans and bullies, in which decent folk lived in unbroken terror. For a man like Gu Zhuxuan, "putting out rickshaws" and hoping to meet with no mishap, there was nothing for it but to take shelter under some evil power or under the authorities; and so, on the strength of a shared native place, Gu took Cao Youshan of the French Concession as his "old man." In the Green Gang of that day Cao belonged to what was called the "Da" generation, of whom by then only a handful were left in Shanghai. From this time on Gu took in disciples wholesale in Zhabei and spread his power.

Rich and powerful though Gu Zhuxuan was, he remained at bottom a gang hooligan; and to do his violence and villainy in the open he had to be in league with the authorities. So whenever a new chief took over the police station of the Fourth District of Zhabei, Gu would make him a present of a new rickshaw; his everyday feasting and gift-giving, his eating, drinking, whoring, and gambling, need hardly be mentioned. The other sergeants and constables all had their share of the gravy as well. Gu Zhuxuan and they abetted one another in wickedness — it was like giving wings to a tiger — and his arrogance swelled the more.

After the Qi–Lu War, Wang Yanbin, a man of wealth and standing in Zhabei, set up the Zhabei Defense Corps under the pretext of keeping local order, though what he was really about was building a private armed force. He made himself corps commander, invited Gu to be his deputy, and placed corps headquarters in the charity hall on Datong Road in Zhabei. The corps was organized thus: a commander, a deputy commander, and a chief instructor, one of each; below the corps came the companies, each with a company commander and three platoons under three platoon leaders; each platoon held three squads under nine squad leaders; each squad held one section, and each section ten corpsmen. Besides these, every company had a clerk and an instructor. That is to say, apart from those holding rank, each company numbered ninety corpsmen. In those days Datong Road, Gonghe Road, the Sun Temple, Tanziwan, Zhujiawan, and other quarters of the Zhabei district were formed into nine companies in all. I was commander of the Ninth Company, charged with guarding the barge traffic on Suzhou Creek. The corps' funds were raised locally, and its arms and ammunition it likewise provided itself; but its personnel had to be reported by headquarters to the Songhu Garrison Command for registration and commission, and the corps as a whole came under that command — a local armed force, one might say, official in oversight but privately run. The corpsmen were taken on by recruitment, most of them stragglers, disbanded soldiers, and street riffraff. Such men came with no real thought of keeping order, but to put on the "tiger's skin," take up arms, and so lord it over the decent and do as they pleased. Some were the wastrel sons of good families, who enlisted for the chance to show off, gather a crowd, and stir up trouble. All of them had some tie to Gu Zhuxuan; and because Wang Yanbin wished to control such men and turn them to his own use, he invited Gu Zhuxuan to be deputy commander. From then on Gu Zhuxuan became a leading figure, half official and half ruffian, riding roughshod over Zhabei; the name Gu Zhuxuan was on every tongue thereafter, and no one spoke of Gu Songmao any more. This defense corps held together until the fighting of August Thirteenth broke out, when it fell apart.

It was around this time, too, that Gu Zhuxuan, trading on his double identity, took up with a wealthy widow, meaning to use the money he got from her to open a theater in the British Concession. There was at that time in the Shanghai British Concession a Dangui Theater (later renamed the Dangui Stage), which did a fine business and had made a good deal of money. Gu Zhuxuan looked on with envy, and so, through Ji Yunqing, the hooligan chief of the British Concession, and through the police, he opened a playhouse directly across from the Dangui and named it the Tianchan Stage.

Once it opened, it put on the serial play The Creation of the World for several months on end; business was splendid and the takings large.

The Dangui, hard hit in its trade by the sudden rise of the Tianchan and by such pieces as The Creation of the World, then paid a heavy sum to lure away Chang Chunheng, who had played the leading part in The Creation of the World, and staged the same play itself, until many playgoers deserted the Tianchan for the Dangui once more. This cost Gu Zhuxuan both face and money; and when he fell back on threats, demanding that Chang return, and Chang took no notice, Gu sent his henchmen one evening, while Chang was performing at the Dangui, to shoot him dead with a Mauser at the Dangui's door.
