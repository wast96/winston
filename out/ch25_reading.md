## The Chōshū Army Storms In

The Shinsengumi had long since had a Kyoto dealer put a set of armor together for each of them. In case of war, it was to be worn by the jokin and above. Every suit was near enough to an antique.

Kondō had two.

Toshizō too had bought his, and properly. Still, this was the one and only time, before or after, that the whole officer corps put armor on.

A jokin named Takeda Kanryūsai, a rōnin of Izumo (later put to death within the corps), being well versed in warrior usages, drilled the company, going about to teach each man the fastening of his armor and the tying of a warrior's straw sandals.

Kondō's dressing was helped along by Takeda.

Presently, seeing Kondō with the cords of his helmet drawn tight,

{j} "You might be the war-god Marishiten come again. Ah, a heartening sight,"

{j} he said, glib as ever.

Toshizō could not abide this Takeda Kanryūsai. To hear the man's teeth-setting flattery of Kondō made his whole body prickle.

"Hijikata-sensei, let me help you as well,"

{j} said Kanryūsai, sidling up, but Toshizō, with a sour face,

"No need."

{j} — that, and no more. To be sure, Kanryūsai for his part was forever in fear of Toshizō and took care not to speak to him.

"As you please, then,"

{j} and, plainly displeased, he went back to Kondō's side. Kondō, for all his airs of a commander, was weak against flattery. In fine humor, he sat listening to Kanryūsai's glib tongue.

(A man not to be trusted.)

Toshizō was displeased too. By the by, in the autumn two years on, it came out that Kanryūsai had been in treasonous dealing with the Satsuma residence, leaking the corps's secrets fast and freely, and by the joint decision of Kondō and Toshizō he was cut down by the hand of Saitō Hajime, the finest blade in the corps.

Toshizō was a deft man. It was the first armor he had ever put on, yet he had it on him in a trice. He wore his battle-surcoat, and flung his helmet up behind.

Okita Sōji came along and,

{j} "Ah — a Boys'-Festival doll, all finished,"

{j} he crowed.

Toshizō did not even answer. By Kanryūsai's reckoning Kondō was Marishiten and he himself a festival doll: a poor bargain.

"Sōji, are you ready?"

"Just so, as you see."

Okita and the other jokin had, over their armor, thrown on the corps's uniform coats.

"You understand the thing. What of the rest?"

"They're out in the yard already."

Toshizō went out to look.

Sure enough, they were assembled. The rank-and-file wore chain shirts, and over them the leather-faced fencing-cuirass, and over that the corps coat; some had a brow-plate, some only a headband — no two alike.

That evening a runner came from the Protector's residence and delivered their posting:

"Halt the main body of the Chōshū army, which is coming up north from Fushimi by the Takeda highroad, in the neighborhood of the Kanjin Bridge at Kujō-gawara."

{j} such was the order.

"The Chōshū main body?"

Kondō was pleased. He thought, no doubt, that this Kanjin Bridge on the Takeda highroad would be the fiercest field of the fighting.

"Toshi — it's the holding of the main body, they say."

"Is it."

He gave a small nod. Toshizō had his doubts. But he did not speak them before the runner. It would have put the Aizu domain to shame.

"The order of battle is this,"

{j} the runner delivered in full. Their allies in that position were: the Aizu troops, two hundred of them, under the domain's karō Jinbō Kuranosuke Toshitaka; and Makita Sagami-no-kami Hirotaka, lord of Bitchū-Asao at ten thousand koku and the officer in charge of the Kyoto Mimawarigumi, leading three hundred of the Mimawarigumi under the shogunal retainer Sasaki Tadasaburō. And the Shinsengumi.

The men sent into the field were a hundred and some, no more. Toshizō picked out the ablest hands with special care and held to a policy of picked men; the rest he used to keep the barracks and to gather intelligence.

They took up position on the west bank of the Kamo River, astride the Kanjin Bridge on the Takeda highroad, past sunset on the eighteenth of the seventh month of the first year of Genji.

They raised the corps-banner, the single character "Makoto" dyed on a red ground, at the west end of the bridge, and heaped up watch-fires all around it. The banner, lit by the fires, told friend and foe alike, even from far off, that the Shinsengumi was posted there.

Toshizō sent scouts running to every quarter within the city and without, and pried unceasingly at the movements of friend and enemy. It was the very way this man had gone about a brawl back home in Tama.

"There is something wrong,"

{j} and his doubt grew heavier still. It was the shogunate side's disposition of forces.

The shogunate — the Kyoto Protector — was making its main strength of the two great domains of Aizu and Satsuma, and besides them was moving the troops of some thirty-odd domains: Ōgaki, Hikone, Kuwana, Bitchū-Asao, Echizen-Fukui and Echizen-Maruoka and Echizen-Sabae, Tango-Miyazu, Yamato-Kōriyama, Tsu, Kumamoto, Kurume, Zeze, Odawara, Iyo-Matsuyama, Tanba-Ayabe and Tanba-Kaibara and Tanba-Sasayama and Tanba-Sonobe and Tanba-Fukuchiyama and Tanba-Kameyama, Tosa, Ōmi-Nishōji, Tajima-Izushi, Tottori, Okayama, and the rest. The strength was forty thousand.

The Chōshū side had gathered chiefly at three places — Saga (with the Tenryū-ji at its center), Fushimi, and Yamazaki (with Tennōzan at its center) — watching for a chance to enter Kyoto; but its strength was only some few hundred at each, a thousand-odd all told, so on that head there was no problem. The question was where its main force lay.

The shogunate judged it "Fushimi." And so it stationed there the fudai daimyō — Aizu, Ōgaki, Kuwana, Hikone — and set the Shinsengumi among them.

The reason was that the Chōshū troops gathered at Fushimi were led by the karō Fukuhara Echigo.

"But the strong one is Saga, surely,"

{j} Toshizō said to Kondō.

"Chōshū has set its commander-in-chief at Fushimi, true enough, but that is for show; once it is a matter of storming into Kyoto, it's Saga that will strike where least looked for and do the work."

"How can you tell?"

"At Saga there are rōnin who have quit domains all over, and their commander is Kijima Matabee, famed even in Chōshū for hard strength. And by the intelligence their spirit is running enormously high. But Fushimi, where the commander-in-chief sits, is another matter. Its hatamoto guard is a picked-vanguard corps got up out of the domain's own hereditary retainers. Fellows like that, sated for generations on fat stipends, can neither fight nor anything else. There's no need to spread this great pompous array against such weak troops."

The holding of Fushimi did not mean the Kanjin Bridge position with the Shinsengumi in it alone. Forward of it, on Inari-yama, was the Ōgaki domain; at Momoyama the Hikone domain; against the Chōshū residence in the town of Fushimi itself, the Kuwana domain; and further, as a mobile reserve, the two domains of Echizen-Maruoka and Kokura were posted — a formidable show.

"They'll be outflanked, this lot,"

{j} Toshizō bit his nail. Kondō could not well follow him.

"Come, it's the order of battle fixed by those above. What's the harm?"

"But Kondō-san. At this Kanjin Bridge no eye-opening feat of arms is going to come rolling our way."

"And yet, Toshi, we can hardly throw over our posting and push out to Saga."

"Well — the thing is to watch our moment and act."

Toshizō broke off the talk there, and that was the end of it.

***

Sure enough, it fell out just as Toshizō had said.

It was at midnight of that day that the Chōshū karō Fukuhara Echigo, encamped at Fushimi, began to move.

At first he made to enter Kyoto by the Daibutsu highroad, but his advance was not an offensive one. The mild Fukuhara Echigo would not give up his posture of going, not to give battle, but to lay a petition before the imperial court. Only Matsudaira Katamori, the hated Aizu Middle Counselor, was to be struck (this bill of denunciation had already been scattered abroad by the Chōshū samurai Tsubaki Yajūrō).

Fukuhara's force of five hundred, coming up north, ran at Fujinomori into a barrier held by the shogunate's van, the Ōgaki domain, under Toda Uneme-no-shō Ujiakira.

Fukuhara Echigo, armored in a mounted man's light harness,

"Fukuhara Echigo of the Chōshū domain, passing through on a matter of petition to the court"

{j} — with only that call he passed without trouble.

The Ōgaki troops watched him by in silence. The lord of this domain was Toda Uneme-no-shō, but, being ill, he had Ohara Jinbee as his deputy commander. Ohara took the pen-name Tesshin, was already a famed strategist of the day, and was especially versed in Western gunnery.

They let him through in silence, and just as the Chōshū army was about to finish crossing the Sujikai Bridge — four hundred meters north of the barrier — they suddenly spread their gunners out and poured a quick volley into it from behind.

In an instant a gun-fight was on.

It was the dead of night of the nineteenth, before four in the morning.

"Toshi — it seems to have started,"

{j} and Kondō jerked his chin at the gunfire off in the dark.

"By that quarter, that would be Fujinomori,"

{j} said the keen-eared Okita Sōji.

"Fujinomori, then it's the Ōgaki domain. A domain they called 'Ōgaki of the guns' — they'll do a fair bit."

Kondō, gripping the Naganuma-school baton of command he had had Takeda Kanryūsai make him, kept his composure.

(Tcha — there's a limit to playing the commander.)

Toshizō was fretting. Kondō had of late grown sluggish. At once Toshizō gave the order and sent the scout Yamazaki Susumu running. At the same moment a messenger ran too from the camp of the Aizu troops under Jinbō Kuranosuke.

Yamazaki Susumu rode flat along his horse's back.

The Daibutsu highroad, where Fujinomori lies, runs parallel to the Takeda highroad, and the only way linking them is a track through the paddies.

Yamazaki, too, was a bold man. Hanging out no saddle-lantern, he rode blind through the dark, steering by the cluster of torches and the gun-flashes at Fujinomori.

Reaching the field of battle on the Daibutsu highroad,

{j} "Where is the Ōgaki domain's headquarters? Yamazaki Susumu of the Shinsengumi,"

{j} he called, wheeling his horse amid the bullets. Just then two or three shots grazed past his ear, and spear-armed troops came swarming up.

—This fellow, he's Shinsengumi.

At that, Yamazaki hastily wheeled his horse's head south. He had blundered, it seemed, straight into the midst of the Chōshū troops.

From the saddle he cut one man down. Pressing his face into the mane, he rode. Chōshū and Ōgaki were so tangled together on the road that there was no telling the two camps apart.

"A messenger, a messenger,"

{j} Yamazaki cried, riding desperately. Before long, at the stone rail before the Fujinomori shrine, he came on the Ōgaki commander Ohara Jinbee.

"A runner of the Shinsengumi,"

{j} Yamazaki made to dismount, but Ohara pushed him back up into the saddle:

{j} "Bring up reinforcements at once. Chōshū is doing fairly well."

By what came out later, this weakest of Chōshū's units broke again and again under Ōgaki's fire and charges, but each time the domain's own man Ōta Ichinoshin swung his camp-sword and drove them on with a shout,

{j} —Fall not back! Fall back and I cut you down!

{j} — and led them, it is said, with fearful spirit. Ōta Ichinoshin was one of the commanders of the Saga quarter, but at Fukuhara Echigo's asking he had hurried up as a temporary captain a little before the fighting opened.

When Yamazaki came back to camp and reported, Toshizō looked at Kondō.

Kondō nodded.

At once he was on his horse.

"The Sujikai Bridge."

{j} That was the whole of Kondō's order. The squad leaders were in such accord that even that was enough. They would attack from the north end of the Sujikai Bridge and catch the Chōshū troops between two fires.

The Aizu troops and the Mimawarigumi began to move as well.

But when they reached the field, the Chōshū troops had thrown down their own dead and fallen back several chō to the south in disorder. The commander Fukuhara Echigo himself, shot through the cheek from the side and his face all blood, got back as far as the Chōshū residence in Fushimi; but there too he could not withstand the Ōgaki pursuit, and ran further south to fling himself into the camp of Yamazaki under the karō Masuda Etchū.

Morning had already come.

When Kondō and Toshizō and the Shinsengumi entered Fushimi in pursuit of the beaten enemy, the Chōshū residence in Fushimi was ablaze with a fire the Hikone troops had set.

(A day late for the fair.)

Toshizō was out of temper. For nothing he had let the Ōgaki and Hikone domains make their names.

Meanwhile, in the western outskirts of Kyoto, the Chōshū army of eight hundred at the Tenryū-ji in Saga, led by the karō Kunishi Shinano, was pushing in toward the city.

Just as Toshizō had foretold, this force was, unlike the one at Fushimi, valiant as if of another breed of men. Its vanguard commander was Kijima Matabee, its inspector-of-forces Kusaka Genzui, and mixed into its ranks were a great many revere-the-Emperor rōnin from every domain, ready to throw their lives away that day and no other.

The supreme commander Kunishi Shinano, a youth of but twenty-five, came on in the very dress of a great domain's councillor: a folded-eboshi cap, a court robe of Yamato brocade, armor laced in pale green, and over it a surcoat of white silk gauze with a cloud-dragon drawn on the back in ink. And before his horse,

{j} Revere the Emperor, Expel the Barbarian,

{j} Smite the Aizu Traitors, the Satsuma Rebels

{j} — such was the great standard he flew as he pressed on. The shogunate had made almost no provision for the Saga quarter, so with none to bar the way he entered the city, advanced on the palace, and reached the front of what is now the Gōō Shrine at about four in the dead of night.

There Kunishi took up battle order. He gave Kijima Matabee two hundred men and sent him against the Hamaguri Gate, and gave Kodama Minbu two hundred likewise and sent him charging against the Shimodachiuri Gate.

Kunishi's own main body made for the Nakadachiuri Gate.

The battle men call the Battle of the Hamaguri Gate begins from this moment. The Chōshū plan — the feint at Fushimi to draw off the shogunate's troops — had worked.

When Kunishi had come as far as Nakadachiuri Avenue, he met the Hitotsubashi troops. It was the Hitotsubashi troops who opened fire.

That was the very thing Chōshū had been waiting for. To be the first to fire near the imperial palace was to earn the name of rebel past escaping.

Kunishi Shinano ordered fire and charge. The Hitotsubashi troops broke and fled in no time.

Next he met the Chikuzen — Kuroda — troops. Both sides fired, but the Chikuzen men, being sympathetic to Chōshū, made a special point of retiring.

Presently the Chōshū army forced the Nakadachiuri Gate open and burst at a rush into the palace. Facing the gate was the Kugyō Gate, and that was the Aizu domain's post.

Kunishi, seeing the crest,

{j} "That is Aizu. Kill them to the last man,"

{j} he ordered. From the very start — from the coup at the palace gates down to the Ikedaya affair — it was Aizu that had, first and last, stood against Chōshū.

The Chōshū charge was terrible. The Aizu troops were cut down one after another.

By and by the guns spoke at the Hamaguri Gate, and Kijima Matabee's two hundred stormed in. Almost at the same moment Kodama Minbu's two hundred burst in through the Shimodachiuri Gate. Their aim was not victory in the fight. It was to strike down the Aizu and Satsuma domains.

At that hour the Shinsengumi was at Fushimi.

The scouts Toshizō had scattered through the city galloped to Fushimi and brought urgent word of the battle at the palace. There was no need of the word: flames were going up endlessly into the Kyoto sky.

(There, you see. The shogunate has bungled it.)

Toshizō pressed hard on Kondō.

"Let us turn back to Kyoto."

"Toshi. The men are all worn out. Whatever we do, three ri at a run to Kyoto now will change nothing."

"We run it."

Toshizō stood planted in the road, poised as if to break into a run that instant. The sun was climbing higher and higher, but the men lay sprawled asleep under the eaves of the houses. They had done nothing but chase the enemy, never once closing with him, yet since the night before they had not had a wink of sleep.

"Can they work, in this state?"

{j} said Kondō.

"No — we make them work. That the Shinsengumi was not on the field that mattered — that rumor I cannot bear."

Was this not the very moment when they were remaking themselves into a fighting body?

"Toshi, there's no use fretting. The luck of war was not with us. Take it so."

{j} Kondō spoke like a commander. And yet, Toshizō thought. In this one battle, a snatching-away of the Son of Heaven, to not be at the palace — what was that? It was not a thing to be resigned to.

"Hijikata-san—"

{j} Okita Sōji came out of the house across the way, smiling all over his face. In his hands he cradled a black-lacquered pail.

"How about it? Won't you have some?"

"What is it?"

{j} he said sourly. Okita thrust the pail up under Toshizō's nose. The dreadful reek peculiar to this sushi came drifting off it.

"Funa-zushi. Your favorite, I believe."

"I'm busy just now. You eat it."

"I won't eat the stuff. A thing this rank — who but you could eat it?"

"Share it out among the men."

"Not a soul will touch it — no one but the vice-commander of the Shinsengumi."

"Sōji. What are you getting at?"

Toshizō, there being no help for it, gave a wry smile. Okita, it seemed, meant by the sushi to say something else.

Before long word came of the Chōshū defeat. Kijima Matabee, after fighting like a fury, had set his own spear butt-upward on horseback and run it through his throat and died; Kusaka Genzui and Terashima Chūzaburō had killed themselves at the Takatsukasa mansion; the greater part of the Chōshū army had fallen within the palace and without; and Kunishi Shinano, guarded by a bare handful, had escaped, they said.

The shogunate troops, hunting out the beaten enemy, went about setting fire to the houses of the townsfolk, and by this the city of Kyoto became a sea of flame, the smoke covering the sky until even the heavens over Fushimi went dark.

The Chōshū remnant fell back to Yamazaki, and there held its last council of war.

There was talk of shutting themselves up on Tennōzan and fighting once more, but it could not easily be settled, and in the end they came round to the plan of retreating to their home province. They came down the hill at once and ran west.

But there were those who stayed in the camp at Yamazaki: seventeen of the rōnin corps led by Maki Izumi-no-kami. They climbed Tennōzan, behind the main camp at Yamazaki, and on the twenty-first flew from the summit that same banner — "Revere the Emperor, Expel the Barbarian," "Smite the Aizu Traitors, the Satsuma Rebels."

When the Shinsengumi led the way up the hill at a run, the seventeen had already cut their bellies and died.

"—The luck of war was against us,"

{j} said Kondō.

The Chōshū army, which had failed to seize the Emperor, must have felt it so; but for the Shinsengumi, which had not been able to cross swords with those Chōshū troops even once, the luck of war had been against them too.

On the twenty-fifth the corps returned to barracks at Mibu.

They took up the ordinary patrols of the city. The greater part of Kyoto had been burned away in the battle-fires of those days.
