### The "Wu Hao Notice": Its Fabrication and Its Collapse

### The Forged "Wu Hao Notice" Appears

Two months after Zhou Enlai reached the Central Soviet, another affair erupted in Shanghai: Kuomintang secret agents forged a so-called notice, "Wu Hao and Others Announce Their Withdrawal from the Communist Party."

The forgery was in truth a continuation of the Gu Shunzhang defection, an aftershock of that affair. Through the notice the Kuomintang reactionaries meant to slander Zhou Enlai, to ruin the good name of the Chinese Communist Party, and to break up the Party's revolutionary strength in the White areas.

The political situation in China at the time was this. The three "encirclement and suppression" campaigns the Kuomintang reactionaries had launched against our revolutionary base areas had all ended in crushing defeat at the hands of the Red Army's counter-campaigns. After the September Eighteenth Incident of 1931, with the nation in mortal danger, the Kuomintang reactionaries not only refused to resist Japan but raised the slogan "internal pacification before resistance to foreign aggression," bent on concentrating their forces to wipe out the Chinese Communist Party and the Workers' and Peasants' Red Army. They were busy preparing a fourth "encirclement and suppression" campaign, and plotting at the same time to destroy in one stroke the Party Center then based in Shanghai. One chief target of their manhunt was Zhou Enlai. He was then a member of the Central Political Bureau, secretary of the Central Military Commission, and head of the Central Organization Department, and he had overall charge of the Party's intelligence and security work; his activity was a grave threat to the Kuomintang reactionaries.

Such was the political background against which the Kuomintang reactionaries forged the so-called notice of "Wu Hao and Others' Withdrawal from the Communist Party."

"Wu Hao" was Zhou Enlai's pen name, and one he had used for a long time. In 1919, when the May Fourth Movement erupted, Zhou Enlai was studying in Japan. Moved by a letter from a younger schoolmate of his Nankai Middle School days in Tianjin, which asked, "If the nation is no nation, what is the use of study?," he resolved to give up his studies and return home, and on September 16 he joined the Awakening Society, the famous revolutionary group at Nankai University. In January 1920, when the first issue of Awakening appeared, the pen name under which Zhou Enlai published his writings was "Wu Hao." At the time all the Awakening contributors wrote under assumed names: they first drew lots for a number, then took a name from the sound of that number. Deng Yingchao drew number one, so her pen name was "Yihao."

As it turned out, Zhou Enlai drew number five, and his name? "Wu Hao," of course.

This pen name of Zhou Enlai's was familiar to many, inside the Party and out. The enemy knew very well that he was one of the chief leaders of the Party Center, a man of the highest prestige within the Party, and by so shameless a device they hoped to sow confusion in our ranks. But the forged notice had an obvious hole in it; one glance showed it for the enemy's mummery. Set aside the absurdity of its content and the clumsiness of its wording: its signature alone gave it away. It was signed by Wu Hao and others, 243 persons in all. If it were genuine, why write out only the one name, "Wu Hao," and none of the rest?

After Gu Shunzhang was arrested and turned traitor, the enemy's scheme to "net the whole Party Center in one haul" had come to nothing, and in bitter fury they fixed the main weight of their manhunt on Zhou Enlai. On September 1 that year the Kuomintang had already issued an order offering a reward for his capture, and at the end of November, in Gu Shunzhang's name, they ran an "urgent notice" in the newspapers offering a bounty for Zhou Enlai's arrest. But Zhou Enlai had slipped into hiding in time and kept up the struggle with quick wits, and in early December he made his way to the Central Soviet, taking up the post of secretary of the Central Bureau of the Party at Ruijin, the Soviet's capital.

The enemy never did find any trace of Zhou Enlai, and their "bounty for his arrest" became an empty joke. At their wits' end, they fell back on their old trick of rumor and slander. From February 16 to 21, 1932, they ran, one after another, in Shanghai's Shibao, Xinwenbao, Shen Bao, and Shishi Xinbao, a forged advertisement, the so-called notice of "Wu Hao and Others' Withdrawal from the Communist Party":

We are deeply convinced that the course the Chinese Communist Party now pursues, the so-called expansion of the Red Army to tie down the present government, is nothing less than the destruction of China's power to resist Japan, and must in the end make us a puppet of Japan and sink the Chinese nation into an abyss beyond recovery, betraying the original purpose for which we took up revolution. Moreover, the international line that Party follows is but the self-serving policy of the Soviet Union. The Soviet Union loudly proclaims its opposition to imperialism, yet itself compromises with imperialism. Look at the present: Japan invades China, and the Soviet Union not only fails to keep strict neutrality but lends the Chinese Eastern Railway to move Japanese troops, and has signed a non-aggression pact with Japan, feeding the flames of its aggression. What it usually calls aid to weak and small nations is nothing but a slogan to deceive the people. We, in the awakening of our conscience, hereby withdraw from the internationally directed Chinese Communist Party.

Wu Hao and others, 243 persons in all.

The notice was concocted jointly by two men: Zhang Chong, chief secretary of the intelligence subsection of the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters, and Huang Kai, that Section's special agent posted to Shanghai. When Gu Shunzhang was escorted under guard from Wuhan to Nanjing, the agents of the Kuomintang Investigation Section had turned out in full force; led by Zhang Chong, they descended on Shanghai to hunt down Zhou Enlai and the other responsible comrades of the Party Center, and came up empty at every turn. Baffled and helpless, the enemy hit on this rotten idea to vent their spite and take revenge. Zhang Chong drafted the text, and Huang Kai had men carry it to the Shanghai papers to be printed. On the morning of February 15, 1932, it was delivered to the front office of the Shen Bao building.

The lawyer in the Shen Bao advertising office saw that the notice claimed 243 people had left the Party but bore only one name, "Wu Hao," an obvious flaw, and "decided not to print it on the 16th for the time being." But on February 16 and 17 the Shibao Morning Extra was the first to carry the notice. On the 18th, Xinwenbao ran it too. On the 19th, the Kuomintang's Press Censorship Office in Shanghai sent men to the Shen Bao building to demand why it had not printed the notice, and Shen Bao then ran it on the 20th and 21st. Shishi Xinbao carried it on those same two days.

After liberation, Chen Yangshan, who in those years had worked in the intelligence section of the Central Special Section, became director of the Shanghai Public Security Bureau, and in September 1950 was transferred to head the Nanjing Public Security Bureau. In the campaign to suppress counter-revolutionaries, launched with great fanfare from 1950, Kuomintang agents were caught one after another. On March 7, 1951, Huang Kai, the Zhongtong agent and Shanghai special agent who had taken part in concocting the Wu Hao Notice, was arrested. Between 1930 and 1933 this man had taken part in wrecking Chinese Communist Party organizations in Hankou, Shanghai, Nanjing, and elsewhere in a whole string of cases; he had murdered the Central Committee member Yang Pao'an, the couple Luo Qiyuan and his wife, and the whole family of Chen Hua, head of the Hubei Provincial Committee, along with more than twenty others of the provincial committee; and he had taken part in the arrest and killing of Deng Yanda, leader of the Third Party, and earlier in the arrest of the arch-traitor Gu Shunzhang and in coercing his defection. His crimes were beyond counting, his hands soaked in the blood of Communists. But through interrogation after interrogation Huang Kai stonewalled and held out, refusing to confess. Qin Jie, chief of the preliminary-examination section in the political security division of the Nanjing Public Security Bureau, who was in charge of the questioning, was young, only twenty-five at the time, and knew little of the struggle against the enemy in the early 1930s. There was no way to get a grip on this savage tiger, and the interrogation stalled. Then out of the blue came a telephone call from Chen Yangshan: "Do you have a prisoner there named Huang Kai? I'll question Huang Kai; I'll come tomorrow." Qin Jie, at his wits' end, was overjoyed to hear it, and said quickly, "Good, you question him, I'll sit in." The next day Chen Yangshan came in person, and with complete assurance laid out the man's various crimes. Huang Kai stammered and hedged at first, denying everything, still refusing to confess. Then Chen Yangshan asked abruptly, "What was the business in February 1932, when you forged the 'Wu Hao Notice of Withdrawal from the Party' and spread it through the Shanghai papers?" Huang Kai heard that this was an old adversary who knew the whole inside story, and, knowing his crimes could no longer be hidden, at once confessed: "Zhang Chong came to Shanghai to handle a funeral, and got to talking with me about the CP's financial difficulties, saying many Party members might waver; so we used Zhou Enlai's alias to fake a notice of withdrawal from the Party. Zhang drafted it, listed over 200 people, and I had it sent to Shen Bao, Xinwenbao, and the rest. Who knew it would draw no response at all; for a long time not one person came to any office to give himself up. …"

Afterward Qin Jie set down how Chen Yangshan had conducted this interrogation of Huang Kai himself: Comrade Yangshan questioned Huang Kai in person, with Comrade Lian Chengyi keeping the record and me sitting in. Comrade Yangshan questioned him for a full two or three hours, and Huang Kai gave a systematic account of his activities during his time in the Zhongtong secret-agent organization: of his crimes in Wuhan, Shanghai, and Nanjing in persecuting Communists and progressives, of how he had tried and failed to seize Zhou Enlai in Shanghai, and of how afterward, together with Zhang Chong, another Zhongtong agent, he had impersonated Zhou Enlai under his alias, Wu Hao, and run the "Wu Hao Notice" in Shen Bao and Xinwenbao, fabricating a declaration in which Wu Hao, in the name of some 200 Party members, announced his withdrawal from the Party. After the notice appeared, not one Communist gave himself up. Before long, Barrister Ba He, acting for Wu Hao, published a statement in Shen Bao refuting the rumor. …

In the course of questioning Huang Kai, Comrade Yangshan also asked about the criminal activities of related Zhongtong agents in the 1930s. Huang Kai had already gathered that Comrade Yangshan was his old adversary from those years; knowing his crimes could not be hidden, he confessed in fairly concrete detail. From then on he wrote out his confession systematically. After this interrogation by Comrade Yangshan the case took shape, and was soon closed.

During the "Cultural Revolution" the "Gang of Four" used the "Wu Hao Notice" to frame Comrade Zhou Enlai. The confession Comrade Yangshan obtained from questioning Huang Kai proved, and proved powerfully, that Comrade Zhou Enlai was innocent.

On June 7, 1953, in prison, Huang Kai wrote again in a confession: "In the long years before the War of Resistance, he" (meaning Zhang Chong) "was Chiang the Baldy's chief accomplice, and I was his chief accomplice. He drafted the Wu Hao Notice in his own hand. … It ran in all the great papers, Chinese and foreign, for a week, and produced not the slightest of the hoped-for effects." After Huang Kai had served his sentence and been released, he wrote another article recounting how he and Zhang Chong had forged the notice: "Zhang Chong came to Shanghai to manage a funeral. Afterward he got to talking with me about the CP's financial difficulties, saying many Party members might waver, and that we might try using Zhou Enlai's alias Wu Hao to run a fake notice of withdrawal from the Party. Zhang drafted the notice 'Wu Hao and Others Withdraw from the Communist Party,' and I had men take it to the Shanghai papers. Who knew it would draw no response at all; for a long time no one came secretly to any office to give himself up. On the contrary, in Shen Bao a prominent lawyer published a statement on Wu Hao's behalf. Ji Yuanpu warned Shi Liangcai of Shen Bao that he ought not to have printed it, but Shi flatly refused, saying, 'An advertisement is a commercial matter; and besides, from a legal standpoint, a man surnamed Wu whose name has been usurped is entitled to make a statement.'"

### The Provisional Central Committee Refutes the Rumor for Zhou Enlai

Crude as the forged notice was, this stroke of the enemy's was a vicious one. They reckoned that with the White Terror at its most severe, this cudgel-blow left us no way to hit back: for us to refute the rumor openly in the press would be to walk straight into the net. But the Communist Party does not scare so easily. The Provisional Central Committee in Shanghai moved swiftly against the enemy's scheme, taking a range of measures to strike back at the Kuomintang's slander.

On February 18 the Party organization sent someone to the advertising office of the Shen Bao building with a "Wu Hao Notice" drafted on the Party Center's behalf, meant to give the lie to the Kuomintang reactionaries' forgery. But the lawyer in the advertising office, having read it, said that Shen Bao had not carried the notice "Wu Hao and Others Withdraw from the Communist Party," and so it would be awkward to run this "Wu Hao Notice."

On February 20 the Party organization distributed leaflets in Shanghai striking back at the Kuomintang reactionaries. Signed by the Propaganda Department of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee of the Chinese Communist Party, the leaflet was titled "Against the Kuomintang's Shameless Rumor-mongering." It declared: "The advertisement recently carried in the pile of reactionary papers, Shibao, Xinwenbao, and the rest, that Wu Hao and 243 others had left the Communist Party, is but one instance of the shameless rumor-mongering of the Kuomintang, running dogs of imperialism." And: "However these curs slander and defame, they cannot shake the Communist Party's standing among the toiling masses!"

Shen Bao too carried the forged notice on February 20. The Party organization again sent someone to the Shen Bao building to ask: "You have run 'Wu Hao and Others Withdraw from the Communist Party'; why will you not run the 'Wu Hao Notice'?" The lawyer in the advertising office answered that the shop-guarantee formalities were not in order, and the "Wu Hao Notice" could not be printed. After repeated negotiation, the paper, with the backing of its general manager Shi Liangcai and his adviser Tao Xingzhi, at last agreed to run an advertisement that in effect refuted the rumor for Wu Hao: "To Mr. Wu Hao: the advertisement you sent in on the 18th of this month could not be published, because the Fuchang Bedding Company denied its guarantee and the formalities were not in order."

This advertisement said nothing directly about the matter, yet it told readers very clearly that the "advertisement" Wu Hao had wished to run but that "could not be published" was aimed at denying the notice "Wu Hao and Others Withdraw from the Communist Party." The timing, too, was subtly arranged: the notice Wu Hao wished to run but did not was sent to the Shen Bao building on February 18, while the Kuomintang had begun running its slanderous notice in Shibao on February 16, and on the 18th Xinwenbao too had carried the slander. Chen Yun said later, on June 13, 1972: "At the time the Provisional Central Committee managed to place a small advertisement, in the form of the newspaper replying to Mr. Wu Hao, indirectly making it known that Wu Hao had a statement denying and exposing the Kuomintang's rumor, but that because of the guarantor question it could not be published. By this small advertisement we let the comrades in the White areas and throughout the Party know of the Kuomintang's plot, so that they would not be taken in." Zhou Enlai, too, praised the placing of this clever notice, holding it "the way, in the circumstances of the time, of publicly denying the forged notice."

On February 27 our Party ran, in issue 4 of Struggle (the organ the Provisional Central Committee published in Shanghai, issued openly under the name of Shibao), the "Wu Hao Notice" together with an article titled "Another Specimen of Kuomintang Rumor and Slander," rebutting the Kuomintang's calumny and setting out the true facts within the Party.

The full text of the "Wu Hao Notice" the Party published ran as follows:

Recently the papers have carried a notice, "Wu Hao and Others Withdraw from the Communist Party," which says many of the things the running dogs of the Kuomintang are forever saying, plainly another new trick of Kuomintang rumor and slander! That the Kuomintang has capitulated to imperialism and sold out the interests of the Chinese nation is a fact plain to all the toiling people of China and indeed of the whole world. It was the Kuomintang government that sold the Three Eastern Provinces to Japanese imperialism, that handed the jointly Sino-Russian-run Chinese Eastern Railway to Japanese imperialism with both hands, that let Japanese imperialism and all imperialism use the Three Eastern Provinces and the Chinese Eastern Railway to attack the Soviet Union; and in the Shanghai Incident it was the Kuomintang government that struck deals with imperialism behind the backs of the brave soldiers of the Nineteenth Route Army! And so it is impossible, without overthrowing Kuomintang rule in China, without creating the workers' and peasants' own Soviet government and their own armed force, the Red Army, of the hundreds of millions of Chinese toilers, to overthrow Japanese imperialism and all imperialism and wage a revolutionary national war. We are now struggling, under the leadership of the Comintern and the Chinese Communist Party, to overthrow imperialism and the Kuomintang and to win the independence and liberation of the Chinese nation! Not one of the Kuomintang's rumors and slanders against the Comintern, the Chinese Communist Party, and me personally can save the Kuomintang from its doom!

February 20

When the Kuomintang reactionaries' forged notice of "Wu Hao and Others' Withdrawal from the Communist Party" reached the Central Soviet, Mao Zedong, in February 1932, in his capacity as chairman of the Provisional Central Government of the Chinese Soviet Republic, signed and issued a Central Government Proclamation to expose the enemy's shameless plot. The proclamation stated: "Shanghai's Shishi Xinbao, Shibao, Shen Bao, and other papers, around February 20, 1932, carried for several days running the impostor's notice of 'Wu Hao and 243 others,' declaring their withdrawal from the Communist Party; but in fact Comrade Wu Hao is serving in the Military Commission of the Soviet Central Government, has absolutely not left the Communist Party, and still less would issue the absurd and reactionary statements in that notice. This is plainly the rumor and slander of Kuomintang party men who butcher workers, peasants, and soldiers and sell China to imperialism."

In Shanghai, to refute the rumor even more clearly and forcefully in public, the Party Center took a further step, and after much maneuvering published a direct rebuttal in Shen Bao. Pan Hannian had Huang Dinghui (Huang Mulan) find a way to approach Chen Zhigao, a lawyer at the French Concession police, to come forward and refute the slander on Wu Hao's behalf. As has been said, Chen Zhigao was a well-known lawyer. He did not then know who Wu Hao was, but he had read Wu Hao's articles in the papers the Party Center ran. When Huang Dinghui explained the matter, he said gladly that he would be greatly honored to defend Mr. Wu Hao. But after thinking it over he said to her: if I come forward myself, the Kuomintang authorities are sure to dig to the bottom of it, which would do more harm than good; whereas a foreigner, protected by "extraterritoriality," would not suffer their persecution. And so it was arranged that Chen Zhigao would step back and, for a heavy fee, engage Ba He, a French lawyer then in practice in Shanghai and Shen Bao's standing legal adviser, to come forward, and on March 4, 1932, in a prominent place in Shen Bao, there appeared "Barrister Ba He's Urgent Notice on Behalf of Zhou Shaoshan" (Zhou Shaoshan being an alias of Zhou Enlai's well known within the Party):

On the statement made to us by Mr. Zhou Shaoshan: that in his written contributions he had used the alias "Wu Hao"; that a notice recently carried in the papers, "Wu Hao and 243 others withdraw from the Communist Party," has troubled relatives and friends at home and abroad to write and wire their concern; that, however, apart from his written work he has never used the name Wu Hao in any external dealings, so that this Mr. Wu Hao must be another person; that the matter of 243 persons withdrawing together from the Communist Party has in truth nothing to do with him; that, the matter touching his personal reputation and being apt to breed misunderstanding, and there being reason to fear that unscrupulous persons might turn right and wrong on their heads and make trouble on this pretext, he has expressly commissioned this barrister to make a statement on his behalf, and to thank his relatives and friends for their letters and wires of concern; and so forth. Accordingly, this barrister publishes the statement on his behalf as above.

Offices: 41 Rue du Consulat, sixth floor, No. 5.

Li Yimang, who took part in the affair at the time, spoke in his memoirs of how it was handled:

After we had all talked it over, we decided to have Pan Hannian find Chen Zhigao, then a lawyer at the French Concession police, to run a notice of denial on Wu Hao's behalf. But Chen said that although he worked at the concession police, he was after all a Chinese, and it was not convenient for him to come forward; if the Kuomintang traced it to him, he would have a hard time explaining. He suggested we find a French lawyer, Ba He, to run an urgent notice on behalf of Zhou Shaoshan.

And so this notice appeared in Shen Bao on March 4, 1932.

The notice used not Wu Hao's name but Zhou Shaoshan's, and said further that Wu Hao was Zhou Shaoshan's own pen name. This little maneuver was very clever, for after the notice appeared the Kuomintang sent men to find Ba He and ask where Wu Hao was. Ba He said: my client is Zhou Shaoshan, whose alias is merely Wu Hao; the Wu Hao you are looking for is of course not this one, and besides he has run a notice of his own, so you may go straight to him. The content of this notice made it plain that the other "Wu Hao Notice" was a forgery, and so we did not go into the tedious business of rebutting his anti-Soviet, anti-Communist rhetoric. Within the Party, at the same time, we ran rebuttal articles in some mimeographed publications.

For the time, spending a hundred taels of silver on this notice was a bit dear, but it served our purpose. Since Zhou Enlai had already left Shanghai, he would not know we had done these things. Nor did anyone ever mention the matter to him afterward.

This notice was worded with great subtlety: it accorded with the law of the Kuomintang-ruled areas and at the same time cleared up the true facts, dealing the enemy a heavy blow. Once the advertisement appeared, the truth of the "Wu Hao Notice" was laid bare for all the world to see. The Kuomintang's scheme of rumor and slander failed utterly, and the Party won a decisive victory in this contest.

### Cutting Down the Poisoned Arrow Jiang Qing Loosed

By rights, Gu Shunzhang, together with his masters the Kuomintang to whom he had gone over, and the "Wu Hao Notice" they had manufactured, had long since all gone to the devil. Yet nearly forty years later, during the "Great Cultural Revolution," the Lin Biao and Jiang Qing counter-revolutionary cliques, who saw Zhou Enlai as a huge obstacle to their seizure of supreme power in the Party and the state, once more made a great stir with this forged "Wu Hao Notice," bent on using it to do Zhou Enlai harm. With the most venomous of wolfish ambitions they deliberately used the affair to concoct a so-called "Wu Hao Affair," whipping up a wave of hostility to Zhou Enlai in society and sowing confusion, that they might fish in troubled waters and, amid the chaos, usurp the state and seize power.

In May 1967, Red Guards in Tianjin sent Jiang Qing a photocopy of the notice "Wu Hao and Others Withdraw from the Communist Party," which they had turned up in the old Shanghai papers. Getting hold of this utterly worthless forged "notice," Jiang Qing meant to make a great deal of it and mount a sudden assault on Zhou Enlai. On May 17 she wrote to Lin Biao, Zhou Enlai, and Kang Sheng, enclosing the "notice." She said: "We have turned up an anti-Communist notice, headed by Wu Hao (Zhou ××), and I ask to meet with you face to face."

Zhou Enlai saw through Jiang Qing's scheme at a glance, and on May 19 wrote a furious comment on this letter of hers: "The notice 'Wu Hao and Others Withdraw from the Communist Party' is a pure enemy forgery. It names only 243 persons and no other single name; that alone shows it beyond doubt a forgery. I was by then already in the Central Soviet; in Shanghai, Comrades Kang Sheng, Chen Yun, and others all knew it to be the enemy's doing, and so took measures." The same day, Zhou Enlai set everything else aside, called up all the main Shanghai papers of that period, went through them carefully, and wrote a letter to Mao Zedong in his own hand, together with a Chronicle of Events he had compiled of the relevant matters from 1931 to 1932, and sent them off. In his letter to Mao Zedong he wrote: "It is now clear that the so-called 'Wu Hao and Others' Notice' was the forged notice of February 18, 1932," and "the forging of the notice and the arrangements made through the Shen Bao building all took place after I had gone to Jiangxi."

After reading the letter and materials Zhou Enlai sent, Mao Zedong wrote: "For Comrade Lin Biao to read, then pass to the comrades of the Cultural Revolution Group to read, and file." At the end of 1967 a student in Beijing wrote to Mao Zedong raising the matter again, and on January 16, 1968, Mao Zedong wrote: "This was cleared up long ago; it is Kuomintang rumor and slander." On May 8, 1968, Mao Zedong said again, in the course of a conversation: "A man of sixty-odd like Xu Shiyou did not know this affair was an enemy forgery. One sees how hard it is to grasp the historical circumstances of that time." Besides these two occasions on which he cleared up the forged notice himself, Mao Zedong also charged Zhou Enlai to speak of it to the comrades at a suitable meeting, and to have it recorded and placed in the archives, so that later generations might know of the matter.

Jiang Qing and her crew, heedless of the facts, went on fanning the flames over the question, working through the Red Guards. One morning around ten o'clock that October, when Wu Faxian, a chief criminal of the Lin Biao and Jiang Qing counter-revolutionary cliques, met Jiang Qing at Building 10 of Diaoyutai, she held out a large plastic bag for him to see and said, pointing at it: "I have material here on all sorts of people, sent to me from everywhere; this whole bag is material on Premier Zhou." On October 10, the materials group of the Shanghai Revolutionary Committee, whose director and deputy director were Zhang Chunqiao and Yao Wenyuan, chief criminals of the Jiang Qing counter-revolutionary clique, worked this enemy-forged "Wu Hao Notice" into issue 55 of their Bulletin on "Ferreting Out Traitors."

Zhou Enlai knew well how vicious and cruel Jiang Qing was, and that she would not let the matter rest. In October and November 1967 he had his staff photograph and file the newspapers carrying the forged notice, his own letter to Mao Zedong, Mao Zedong's comment, and other materials. On December 22, a history student named Fan ×× of P.O. Box 6406 at Peking University, Beijing, wrote to the Central Committee, raising the "Wu Hao Notice" matter again.

On January 10, 1968, Zhou Enlai wrote to Jiang Qing again: "In 1931 and 1932, everyone familiar with Shanghai politics and the Communist movement knew this to be a forgery. I gave a full account of it at the central symposium held during the Yan'an Rectification, in the latter half of 1942; this year, when I have time, I shall speak of it again at a small working meeting, have it recorded, and enter it in the Central archives."

After Mao Zedong's comment of January 16, 1968, that "the Wu Hao Notice is Kuomintang rumor and slander," Jiang Qing, who had been raising demons over it all along, had no choice but to shut her mouth for a time. Kang Sheng and Xie Fuzhi, too, made statements on the matter and spoke out.

As for the truth of this so-called "Wu Hao Notice," Kang Sheng had known it perfectly well all along. For he had been in Shanghai at the time, and was one of the responsible men of the Central Special Section who took part in handling the affair. He had also taken part in the operation to execute Gu Shunzhang's family, and had helped Chen Yun contrive a way to deal with the "Wu Hao Notice" that Gu Shunzhang, in collusion with Kuomintang agents, had forged. In the Urgent Notice of Gu Shunzhang, Offering a Reward for the Capture of the Murderer Zhou Enlai and Others, carried in the Shanghai papers day after day from December 29, 1931, the phrase "the Communist chiefs Zhou Enlai, Zhao Rong, and others" occurs in two places, and "Zhao Rong" was Kang Sheng's alias at the time. Kang Sheng had spoken of the affair more than once before the "Great Cultural Revolution." On October 31, 1962, he had written in his own hand on a document touching the notice "Wu Hao and Others Withdraw from the Communist Party": "This is pure rumor and slander, a version that stands entirely with the enemy. The true facts are these: after Gu Shunzhang turned traitor, his whole family was done away with by us; the enemy, to take revenge, used their secret-service apparatus to manufacture that rumor. In fact Comrade Zhou Enlai had long since gone to the Soviet area at the time, and no such thing existed at all." On December 27, 1963, Kang Sheng wrote again on another related document: "The comrades who were in Shanghai at the time all knew of this." Yet only three years later, after the "Great Cultural Revolution" had begun, when the two counter-revolutionary cliques of Lin Biao and Jiang Qing used this "notice," forged in those years by Kuomintang agents, to whip up a wave of persecution against Zhou Enlai, Kang Sheng played deaf and dumb and held his peace. Only after Mao Zedong had made his clear comment did Kang Sheng, on February 18, 1972, dictate a document: "The so-called 'Wu Hao Notice' is entirely a forgery by Kuomintang agents, used to attack and slander our Party and Premier Zhou. The Premier was in the Soviet area at the time, and the facts utterly shattered the enemy's forgery plot."

Xie Fuzhi was the vice-premier of the State Council who held the great powers of politics and law and served concurrently as minister of public security. During his grave illness, on September 20, 1971 (he died of illness on March 26, 1972), he said to his family: "The so-called 'Wu Hao Notice' was forged by the Kuomintang. Chairman Mao has said this notice is false; Chairman Mao and Comrade Kang Sheng knew the truth of the matter long ago."

After making the comment mentioned above in January 1968, Mao Zedong spoke again on May 8 of that year, during a break in receiving a group of delegates who had come to Beijing for a meeting, in conversation with members of the Central Cultural Revolution working group and several vice-premiers and marshals who took part in the reception: "The enemy's and the puppets' papers cannot be wholly believed either. A man of sixty-odd like Xu Shiyou did not know the 'Wu Hao Notice' was an enemy forgery; one sees how hard it is to grasp the historical circumstances of that time. This 'notice' was signed 'Wu Hao and 243 others'; if it were genuine, why write out only one man's name and none of the rest? Some cadres, unclear about the history, took fright at the sight of it." And so, besides clearing up the truth of the forged notice twice himself, Mao Zedong also charged Zhou Enlai to give the comrades, at a suitable meeting, an account of the whole course of the affair. On the spot Zhou Enlai spoke briefly of the origins of the "Wu Hao Notice." He said: I reached the Central Soviet in mid-December 1931. When I got to Yongding in Fujian, I ran straight into the Ningdu Uprising. Kang Sheng and the others were in Shanghai at the time; when they saw this fake notice the enemy had forged, they very soon had the Shen Bao advertising department run an exposing notice in the advertisement column, reading, "To Mr. Wu Hao: …," and that proved the earlier "Wu Hao Notice" a forgery. Zhou Enlai went on: In 1931, when Gu Shunzhang turned traitor, it was fortunate that Comrade Qian Zhuangfei, in Xu Enzeng's office at the Zhongtong, got word of it fastest, so that the central organs were spared destruction. Qian Zhuangfei, Li Kenong, and Hu Di were together, and so were sent to the Central Soviet. Later Qian Zhuangfei died dodging a plane on the Long March; Hu Di was killed when he joined up with Zhang Guotao, for Zhang Guotao was giving a speech in the Fourth Front Army and Hu Di, in the audience, said Zhang Guotao was like a fascist, which was overheard by Zhang Guotao's guard and reported to Zhang, and afterward he was killed. There was still the International Settlement in Shanghai then, and Bao Junfu, who was in charge of extraditing Communist cases, tipped us off and was a great help. … At last Zhou Enlai said: I have had the newspapers of that affair and my report photographed, and I mean to write up a document as well. Nie Rongzhen, who was present, said too: it would be good to talk it over while there is the chance, lest in future there be no one left who knows.

In June 1972 the Central Committee convened the Criticize-Lin-Biao Rectification Briefing Conference in Beijing. Mao Zedong asked Zhou Enlai to give the conference an account of the ins and outs of the "Wu Hao Notice" question. By this time Chen Yun took part in the conference; since 1969 he had been "sent down" to Jiangxi by the Lin Biao and Jiang Qing crew, and had only returned to Beijing in late April that year. On June 13 Chen Yun said at the meeting: "I was in the Provisional Central Committee in Shanghai at the time. The ones who knew of this affair were Comrade Kang Sheng and myself. On a historical question of such importance a Communist must take responsibility; one must take a responsible attitude toward the whole Party and toward the world Communist movement, and make it clear. I remember perfectly well that this affair was a Kuomintang plot. The declaration that Wu Hao and some 240-odd others were leaving the Party came after Comrade Zhou Enlai had already reached the Central Soviet." That same day Chen Yun also wrote a written attestation: "I now state in writing that I remember this affair perfectly well: it was a Kuomintang plot."

On June 23, at the last plenary session of the Central Criticize-Lin-Biao Rectification Briefing Conference, Zhou Enlai delivered his Report on the Question of the Kuomintang's Rumor-mongering and Slanderous Publication of the So-called "Wu Hao Notice," giving a detailed account of the Kuomintang agents' forging of the "Wu Hao Notice" in 1932. In his speech he said: "This question was raised by someone in the early days of the 'Great Cultural Revolution.' At the time it was reported to the Chairman, and the Chairman gave a comment. Afterward, for want of an occasion, I did not speak of the question to more of the comrades. This time, when the meeting opened, the Chairman charged me at the outset to give an account of it, to give it to more than 300 comrades, and have it recorded, so that in future it may become an archive of the Party and be preserved. Because of the very nature of this slander, it used my pen name 'Wu Hao' directly, it must be made clear. …" He also recounted the report he had written to Mao Zedong on this matter on May 19, 1967, and made public the comment Mao Zedong had written for it on January 16, 1968. When the recording of the speech was being transcribed, Deng Yingchao herself set the title: Report on the Question of the Kuomintang's Rumor-mongering and Slanderous Publication of the So-called "Wu Hao Notice" — Comrade Zhou Enlai's Report on the Evening of June 23, 1972, at the Criticize-Lin-Biao Rectification Briefing Conference; place, the East Hall of the Great Hall of the People, Beijing. At the meeting, on the decision of the Central Political Bureau and by Mao Zedong's wish, it was announced that the recording of Zhou Enlai's speech, the transcript made from it, and the relevant documentary materials would all be preserved as archives in the Central Archives Office; and that a copy would be kept by the Party committee of each province, municipality, and autonomous region, so that the truth of the question might be known throughout the Party and no one in future could use it to stir up trouble. But the "Gang of Four," resorting to delaying tactics, refused to carry out the decision of the Central Political Bureau and Mao Zedong's wish, and shelved the matter.

Zhou Enlai was boundlessly loyal to the Party, selfless, never weighing his personal honor or disgrace, gain or loss. All his life he bore burdens and swallowed insults for the sake of the whole, disciplined himself strictly, and endured much malicious attack that an ordinary man could not have borne; but the trampling of his political life he could not endure. On September 20, 1975, gravely ill, Zhou Enlai needed to undergo surgery. Before entering the operating room he deliberately called for the transcript of the recording of his report on the truth of the so-called "Wu Hao Notice," delivered at the Criticize-Lin-Biao Rectification Briefing Conference, read it through carefully over a long while, and then, with his hand trembling from his grave illness, signed his name and the date of the report, noting as well the circumstances and time of his signature: "On entering the operating room (before), September 20, 1975." Thus he discharged one of his greatest wishes. As he entered the operating room, Zhou Enlai cried out in a loud voice: "I am loyal to the Party, loyal to the people! I am no capitulationist!" Deng Yingchao, who was present, asked Wang Dongxing to report this to Mao Zedong.

History proved Zhou Enlai's foresight once again. On January 8, 1976, Zhou Enlai, worn down by the torments and persecution of the "Gang of Four," left the world in indignation. Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, and their crew, their thieves' hearts undying, went on plying their ghostly tricks, bent on continuing to use this notice forged by Kuomintang agents to "stir up trouble." Zhou Enlai's remains were not yet cold, and while hundreds of millions of people were still steeped in boundless grief, the "Gang of Four" and their confederates could not wait to leap out and, using the instruments of public opinion they controlled, published one reactionary article after another, wantonly wounding and attacking him. They schemed with all their might to blot Zhou Enlai's shining image from the hearts of the Party, the army, and the people, seeking to destroy this monument standing firm in the hearts of the whole nation. But every schemer of evil heart must in the end "lift a rock only to drop it on his own foot." By manufacturing a monstrous lie and slandering Zhou Enlai without scruple, they gravely wounded the feelings of hundreds of millions, roused the indignation of the broad masses, and kindled the fury of the whole nation, and so touched off the Tiananmen Incident that would bury the "Gang of Four." Only a few months later, this pack of state-usurping thieves was swept, every one, into the rubbish heap of history by the iron broom of the people. Far from doing Zhou Enlai the slightest harm, this vile crew only made his name shine the more brightly at home and abroad, immortal for all time.
