## Chapter 25

I thought that not laying bare a person's fault made it easier for that person to mend. So Bai Ping's manner ought to mean she had come to her senses?

But no; from the next day on she never brought the matter up again, and her life went on as before, her social round as before. The difference was that I joined in the social round: on many occasions I became her protector; on many occasions I became her secretary; and on many others I became her dance partner.

At first there was still my own private intention, of stopping her from sinking further and urging her to come inland with me. Now there was only the duty Mei Yingzi had assigned.

In a cleverly arranged setting, Mei Yingzi had me make the acquaintance of a great Japanese merchant, Honsa Jiro, told me to pretend to go into business with them in partnership, and told me to join these two great merchants in playing up to Bai Ping. Later, under the pretext of commercial convenience, these two merchants gave banquets for many Japanese officers; after several rounds of such comings and goings, my world had merged into one with Bai Ping's. But Mei Yingzi stayed forever behind the scenes; she considered my socializing and activity a great success, yet assigned me no special work.

In society I now appeared in the guise of a merchant who had made a little money, seemingly a brainless profiteer myself; not only did the Japanese have no suspicion of me, but I myself often doubted whether my life was any kind of work at all.

When this kind of life was beginning, Bai Ping would sometimes remind me:

"What's this? You've changed completely!"

"Why may you step into this society and not I?" I would always say.

"You, comparing yourself with me." She was angered, with a cold laugh.

"When you give up this life of yours, I'll give it up too."

"Fine; you wait, then."

After such exchanges we would always leave off, perhaps for fear of hurting each other, perhaps for fear of rousing each other's suspicion. We went on living our lives.

But now Bai Ping and I no longer spoke of these things. In many places I secretly protected her, and in many places she too secretly shielded me; yet our whole spirits grew further and further apart, even as our lives were often thrown together in noisy company.

True, in our lives we were often thrown together in the crowd, but chances to be alone together grew fewer and fewer; or perhaps the chances were not few, but we had no need to be alone together, and when such a chance did come, there was no longer the old mood of mutual care and expectation.

So the days passed. In our circle, my position at Bai Ping's side had reached a point where no one was jealous of it. This was entirely owing to Bai Ping's ascendancy in society; among the many Japanese officers she always seized the leading place. From the look of things, at first some may have harbored special designs on her, but now she had become merely the idol of their gatherings, and I, naturally, was no more than one of her crowd. If I was quietly closer to her than the others, it was wholly because I had known her longer; at her side I had something of the character of an attendant, half a servant, for at large gatherings Bai Ping would often direct me to do odd tasks. So it was quite natural that when the night wore late and the company dispersed, if some Japanese officer wished to see her home, which in the past, they said, she had always refused, she now often accepted, and at the same time would say to me in a commanding tone:

"Come along."

"I won't go," I would say on purpose.

"Come," she would say. "Tomorrow I'm giving a party, and I want you to plan it for me." And so I would obey and go along; after several times, seeing her home became my fixed duty.

Such a duty was one no one envied or admired, nor did I count it an honor; often in the car not a word passed between us, and after seeing her home I would say "goodbye" and let her get out of the car, seldom going up again to talk quietly in her home.

One day a Japanese officer invited us to eat Japanese hot pot on Avenue Joffre. Everyone drank a little wine, and when the meal was over many were for going dancing, but Bai Ping insisted on going to a gambling house, and gambling houses were places absolutely forbidden to Japanese soldiers. So an officer called Colonel Arita proposed that we gamble at his home, a thing that had never happened before; but Bai Ping accepted. In my relation to Bai Ping I had to go along, and in my secret duty I had to follow as well. Of the party, Colonel Arita and Major General Takeshima had cars, so we divided ourselves between these two cars. I had no idea where Colonel Arita lived, and later learned that Bai Ping seemed not to know either; the car drove all the way to Hongkou, turning from North Sichuan Road into Scott Road. It drove in before a very large lane, where Colonel Arita, acting as our guide in low-class Shanghainese, told us that the officers billeted in front were all junior ones, each taking two rooms on one floor, while the senior officers at the back had a whole house each; and so we stopped before one of the houses inside. Colonel Arita led us in with an air of pride; the reception room had, of all things, Chinese paintings hanging in it, the furniture all Western style, the carpet an old one, all of it, no doubt, plunder. Colonel Arita entertained us very courteously, and directed the servants to prepare the gambling things upstairs. Then we hurried upstairs, and as the seats were being assigned I stood for some reason a moment before the window. This window looked straight onto the back window of the house in front, which had a white gauze curtain drawn across it, but the lamplight cast two figures onto the window, and naturally I gave them a glance; it seemed a man was pressing a woman hard and the woman was giving ground in fear, and it seemed the man was a Japanese soldier and the woman a Westerner, and it seemed, I was struck with horror.

"What are you looking at?" said Bai Ping, coming over.

I pressed down all my alarm and said nothing, and when Bai Ping had come to my side I said, low and firm:

"Look."

Bai Ping was taken aback.

"Do you know her?"

Bai Ping almost cried out. I reminded her coolly:

"Steady!"

But the shadows in front made it impossible for me to be steady, for the woman was almost in the man's grip. Just as I was about to urge Bai Ping to save her quickly, Bai Ping had already cried out:

"Helen!" The cry was urgent and loud. I was startled.

"Bai Ping." Helen answered sharply and urgently, a note of terror mixed in.

I saw a coarse hand clap over her mouth. My heart pounded, but I mastered it with all my strength, trying by cool reason to find a proper way; then Bai Ping, of all things, changed to a lively, cheerful tone and said:

"What luck, Helen!" she said. "Bai Ping is here at Colonel Arita's!"

Colonel Arita, wondering who it was, came to the window too, but Bai Ping turned to head him off, and said:

"It's a friend of mine, what luck! Go and ask her to come and join us." So saying, she pulled Colonel Arita off downstairs.

My heart settled at last; struck by Bai Ping's quick wit just now, I believed Helen's danger could surely be lifted. But how had Helen come to be there? That was a thing I could not understand. It had been a very long time since I had gone to her home; since my last visit, I had twice sent someone with money, but the second time her mother had returned it to me, with a very earnest letter enclosed, telling me that Helen had found employment, that their circumstances had taken a turn for the better, and later she even repaid, bit by bit, the money borrowed before. I wrote her mother two letters in return, saying there was no need to take such a small sum so seriously, and hoping she would not stand on ceremony but come to me again when she was in want. After that I felt quite at ease about them, and, partly because my mind was troubled and my life busy, had not thought to go and see them; but how had she come to be here now?

In front was the card table, ringed with lively gamblers who were toying with the cards, chatting and laughing, waiting for Arita and Bai Ping to come back. I sat on the sofa smoking, turning this question over in my mind.

First came the sound of a Japanese soldier's boots, then Bai Ping's laughter, and then I saw Bai Ping come in, together with a very modishly dressed woman.

A white Cossack hat, a white long-napped light woolen coat, a matching white woolen muff on her arm; she walked wearily beside Bai Ping, her face made up in the most brilliant colors, but tears seeming still to glint at the corners of her eyes, stately, it seemed, yet holding within it alarm and shame.

And this was Helen, Helen of all people; had I glided past her in a car, I should certainly not have known her. She had grown plumper, more beautiful, more vivid, more mature. I went over and greeted her.

She smiled and shook my hand; Bai Ping, at my side, gave me a look, and said:

"What luck, two more friends, so we can make a merry night of it." Then I introduced Helen to everyone. Behind her came Arita, and behind Arita a Japanese officer of about thirty who, in build and features, was not too ill-looking; I was sure he was the man who had forced himself on Helen just now. I studied his face and eyes; he had plainly been drinking, and now seemed to wear a somewhat abashed, shamefaced air.

"This is Major Yamao." Bai Ping introduced him to us with easy grace. Everyone greeted him very politely, and I did my utmost to seem wholly ignorant of what had just happened, accepting the introduction in all sincerity. I noticed that his red-veined eyes still dared not look anyone in the face; no one else present knew of what had happened, only Bai Ping and I, and we managed to keep up the pretense well, so that in the end Major Yamao recovered something of his ordinary manner, though he still dared not glance once at Helen. Helen was beside me, and I helped her off with her coat; by now she seemed a little calmer, and I saw that she too even supposed I did not know of what had just happened.

In my heart was the glory of victory, and I began to admire Bai Ping's cleverness, her alertness, and her knack for defusing an awkward moment. In such a matter I knew that only by not exposing it could one win; otherwise every move was a defeat. Yamao wore the clothes of a man and wished to be taken for a man; strip off his robe and cap, and he would give up all thought of covering himself, and that was dangerous. Moreover, to strip off Yamao's robe and cap was to strip off Arita's robe and cap too; for a while Arita might keep up the pretense, but that, stung with shame, he would turn to fury and seek revenge was beyond question, which would endanger not only Helen but, I feared, Bai Ping still more. As things were, Yamao would go on with the pretense and try to pass for a fine fellow before Arita, so that all seemed well. I glanced at Bai Ping, but Bai Ping was talking to Yamao without the least concern.

I made a show of being wildly keen on the gambling, and drew Helen over to the card table.

After several rounds of heavy play, Yamao's manner had returned to normal, his excitement and tension wholly absorbed in the game. He was a coarse man without cultivation, one who in Bai Ping's hands would be very easily controlled. But Helen... how had Helen come to this, and fallen into Yamao's hands? I gambled, and turned these questions over in my mind.

Helen was silent throughout, dejected in the aftermath of her fright; gambling was no longer any excitement to her, and for all my urging, by hint and open word alike, she showed neither excitement nor gaiety. Had Yamao had a little more sense, I do not think he could have sat so easy at heart; I feared others would see that Helen's coldness sprang from her fright at Yamao's, though this of course was an overwrought anxiety. But Helen's weariness only deepened, her thoughts seeming never to have left the scene of a moment ago, until at last, when she and I had both thrown in our cards, she said to me softly:

"I want to go back."

"No, no," I said. "Bear it, bear it; cheer up!"

"I'm not well."

"You mustn't say that." As I spoke I secretly nudged her leg with my knee, then took a cigarette from beside her and said: "Do everything as I say, I beg you."

Then she gave a faint sigh and fell silent. I found her eyes filled with the most tangled emotions: deep grief; a faint terror; the unsteadiness of a soul not yet recovered from its fright; a smoldering, secret anger; loathing for this atmosphere, hatred for Yamao; and boundless gratitude to Bai Ping. Somehow I thought of Mei Yingzi: could it be she who had drawn Helen into these surroundings? A child like this, was Mei Yingzi making use of her? And so I thought of Helen's employment: from her dress and her manner, what sort of employment had she taken up? Plainly it must be Mei Yingzi's stipend that drove her; then she too, like me, was one of Mei Yingzi's subordinates. But she was a child, a pure child, a child with little knowledge of the world, without the power to take on such a thing.

Bai Ping was gambling heavily, smoking, laughing and clamoring in a shrill voice, seeming to pay no heed to Helen and me. Amid the noisy revels of so many Japanese she always stood at the summit of the merriment, egging the others on, stirring up the whole atmosphere; whoever fell silent, she urged on, and she kept up a matchless, matchless zest, not stopping until every last one of the others was worn out and proposed to rest. On many recent occasions I had felt that just this manner of hers was the fullest expression of vulgar taste, and the feeling had set a greater distance between us. But when the party broke up and each went home, sometimes, alone with me in the car, she would heave a deep, utterly weary sigh and sit beside me without a word, her eyes gazing blankly at the road ahead; at such times I felt a special tenderness for her, yet all my words of comfort she now ignored, sometimes taking no notice, sometimes listlessly answering with a word or two, sometimes cutting off my meaning with a sarcastic tone. She would lean serenely against the seat-back, her eyes staring dully out of the car, then suddenly close for a moment, then lift again lifelessly, drift lightly round in a circle, and return to the emptiness outside the car. Much as I understood her weariness, the comparison of the two, her excitement with others and her coldness when with me, made me feel that in any case her friendship for me was far less than before. But today, in this one instant, from Helen's plight and from Yamao's unwitting zest at the gambling, I came to understand why Bai Ping was what she was, why she bloomed an unfading flower among so many bestial men, why all who came near her only circled about her, like insects round a light bulb, like a swarm of bees round a flower under a cover.

She was like a tiger-tamer, letting the tiger's strength be spent on all manner of stimulations, so that it had no strength left to eat people, until at last it took the tamer for a superman beyond the reach of those it might devour. Before me, now, she was taming the tiger: tender and strong, light, lively, beautiful. Two or three times she played against Yamao, dashing and lovely, with light pouts and faint smiles. Yamao's face was flushed crimson, extraordinarily anxious, naturally because he was losing, and not from the effect of a moment ago; but I could imagine that the black shadow I had seen from the window must have worn the same beast's aspect, the same ugliness, perhaps with anger and shamelessness besides. So I glanced at Helen, and Helen seemed to have the same association: her eyes full of hatred and anger, glinting with a fearful light of tears, she stared at Yamao. That she could so lose her self-command! It raised an anxiety in me; but luckily everyone was watching the card-battle between Bai Ping and Yamao, and I at once tapped Helen's leg with my knee and hit on an unexpected remark:

"Helen, and your mother, how has she been lately?"

"Ah?... eh?"

"How has your mother been lately? It's a long time since I called on her."

"Ah." She closed her eyes a moment and smiled: "Very well, very well, thank you."

"Do you still sing often?"

"I haven't sung for a long, long time!"

"Your play!" Bai Ping smiled evenly, pointing at the money on the table before Yamao.

Helen and I were both drawn to look; I saw that Yamao did not dare lay down his cards, and Bai Ping, with her long slender fingers, slowly spread her cards out before Yamao, five nails finely lacquered a bright red pressed upon the five cards, a pair of jacks. She looked at Yamao with a sweet smile.

Yamao looked at Bai Ping's cards, sweat running on his brow, and dejectedly tossed his cards onto the other discards.

"What? You had nothing at all?" asked Takeshima.

"I knew he was bluffing!"

Takeshima raked the money over before Bai Ping.

Bai Ping's victory always made me glad, and Helen too flashed a look of vengeful triumph; but Bai Ping paid us no heed at all, did not even look at us, nor did she straighten the banknotes pushed before her, which included both Japanese notes and national currency, but only talked with Takeshima about the course of the hand.

By what Bai Ping showed now, in my impression she had indeed grown great, and I became aware that she was my political enemy. But why was she my enemy? From my revulsion at the thought of Mei Yingzi's using Helen, I felt that Bai Ping's generosity, courage, and quick wit were the more an unattainable thing. Yet she was my enemy! The object of my work! Then could it be that Bai Ping was the one using Helen, that Bai Ping had brought Helen into her present plight?

Helen was a riddle to me: a few days out of my sight, and she had changed. What sort of life was leading her on? What sort of employment had she taken? If it was employment that had brought her into such a life, then who had recommended her for it? And had the one who recommended it some purpose fixed beforehand? Was that person Bai Ping? No, then was it Mei Yingzi?

But all conjecture was empty; I would soon get it out of Helen, but for now,

The cards on the table were dealt round, and I gave mine an indifferent glance and threw them in, my heart possessed by scattered feelings and thoughts. I got up from my seat, took a little fruit from the tea table to eat, and then, smoking, walked over to the window.
