## Jiaoli

For years Qingpu County had wanted to build a road running straight into the Shanghai city center; a few years back the work had at last begun, but to this day it had come to no more than a dirt track and a few wooden bridges. Cui Wentai did not know why he had driven the car out to this place. He was at the end of his rope.

On the morning of New Year’s Day, outside the bank, the moment Ling Wen set the suitcase on the back seat of the sedan, a rush of heat had gone to his head. For one instant he seemed to catch the smell of the gold bars. He could not have said what the smell really was; he only knew his heart was pounding, his temples fit to burst, and the people and cars on the street around him had all turned to drifting shadows — looking back, it was like a dream. Yet the string of things he did he remembered perfectly clearly: press the clutch, push the gearshift, let out the clutch, off the brake, on the gas, turn the wheel — the trouble was that none of it had passed through his own head at all; even what came after, flooring the accelerator and shooting the car right past the detective-squad agents, seemed not to have been his own doing in the least.

All along the way he cursed — the people crossing the road, the rickshaws, the cars coming the other way, the cars in front of him; he did not even spare the people standing at the roadside. He did remember to get petrol; but as luck would have it, no sooner had he filled up than he saw the boss of his own car-hire firm come running across the street. He remembered then that since taking the car out the day before he had never once returned it to the firm, had not even remembered to telephone — and with a start he realized that in his daze he had driven right up to the filling station near the Jinji firm. He scrambled into the car and was off the instant he touched the gas, with never a thought for the bald fat man behind the car, standing in the middle of the road with his arms flung wide, shouting and stamping his feet.

After driving a good while his head slowly cleared a little. He knew well enough that this time he had gambled big. What was he, Cui Wentai, to dare offend the Nationalists and the Communists both at once? The only thing that could save him now, he thought, was those five big gold bars in the suitcase on the back seat. Even so he dared not stop the car to open the case and look. It was not that he feared being robbed on the road; the feeling was rather like the timidity of a man nearing home — for he had dreamed all his life of making a great pile of money, and the gold bars in that suitcase were the largest sum of money he had ever set eyes on in his life. Yet though he dared not open the case at once to look at the gold, he did stop to buy a newspaper from a paper-boy, and finding the day’s quoted price of bar gold, could not help rejoicing.

He meant to go and find Xiaowuzi under cover of dark; with this money in hand, he felt he had something solid under him. If Ye Qinian really meant to hound him without let, he would leave Shanghai. He was a first-rate driver and could find a bowl of rice to eat anywhere — for which, in truth, he had Old Fang to thank. Sometimes, thinking of Old Fang, he was not altogether free of guilt; but he had truly never meant to get the man killed. He had only told them that Old Fang had very likely gone to the barbershop. That day he had driven Old Fang to North Sichuan Road, and watching him get down and slip into the lane, had guessed at once where he meant to go. He had not meant to get him killed; the end of it was not all his doing — though the memory always left him a little ill at ease.

But now he had five gold bars, worth several thousand silver dollars to exchange; perhaps he too could be a boss, open a car-hire firm somewhere. He could go to the newly founded “Manchukuo” — once there, surely Ye Qinian’s arm could not reach him?

He did not dare enter Xiaowuzi’s lane. He stopped the car across the street and watched for a few minutes, and in the end decided to leave. He had seen two men — in cold like this, what were they doing standing at the mouth of the lane? Did they know Xiaowuzi? Did they know where her home was? None of this could he be sure of. But of one thing he was quite sure: if he went in at her door now, the sort of looks Xiaowuzi’s several elder sisters would give him. He thought it over, and drove away.

But he had nowhere to go. He drove west all the way to the banks of Suzhou Creek, across a great stretch of farmland; snow began to fall from the sky, and after the car had jolted a long while along the field paths he turned onto Brenan Road. He could not turn back, and he could not stop. By this time, he supposed, every police station in Shanghai had his photograph: he was not only a wanted Communist criminal but would be hunted as a gold-bar robber besides. Not only would the Communist underground not shelter him — they too would be after him, the traitor who had sold out his comrades.

Save for the short stretch of road the headlamps lit, all around was pitch dark. Cui Wentai crossed Warren Road; the street was near its end, and ahead lay nothing but waste fields and open country. He had no choice but to turn onto Rubicon Road and drive south along a little river, coming up onto Hongqiao Road. Some dim lights showed ahead, and only when he had driven up to them did he see it was the airfield: snowflakes danced in the lamplight, and several soldier-police stood outside the sentry box staring at the car in astonishment. In his panic he wrenched the wheel hard over; the car swung onto a dirt track to the left of the airfield gate, and heedless of all consequence he floored the accelerator, the wheels bounding over ruts and stones as he plunged headlong into the black night.

The dirt track seemed to stretch on without end, with many plank bridges along it. Only as day was breaking did he discover that it was a road under construction, its earthen bed just laid.

He did not know it was the Zhu-Hu county road, nor that he had driven the car into the Dianshan Lake country. He had never been to this place before. In the small hours he drove past a village, with two characters — “Songze” — carved on a stone by the road, a place-name that meant no more to him than the rest. But the car grew harder and harder to drive; there were many little watercourses, and great stretches of barren shoal grown over with withered reeds. Ahead the road broke off, and he had to stop. He sat a while in the driver’s seat, then fished out a cigarette and drew on it a few times, and then, opening the suitcase on the back seat and looking inside, felt everything go black before his eyes…

Some hours passed before he came to himself. He started the car again, drove it slowly deep into the reeds, and stopped at the lake’s edge. Luckily the weather was cold; though snow had just fallen, the shoal-mud was frozen hard, and the wheels did not sink into the mire. He walked out of the wasteland on foot; on every side there was not a soul.

His wits scattered, Cui Wentai drifted about the Dianshan Lake country for ten days and more. First he found a small inn in the town of Zhujiajiao, but two days there and he began to take fright: the place was close to the Qingpu county seat, and a wanted notice might be pasted up in the streets at any moment. So he took a little boat to the west of the lake, to a small town called Shangta. In the old days merchants trading between the two prefectures of Suzhou and Songjiang all went by water, a journey of two days, and would put up here for the night; Shangta means simply the place where traders lodge. There were a good many inns in the town, and for two dimes a day one could have, of an evening, a bowl of straw-tied pork and a couple of mouthfuls of old Shaoxing wine. Ringed on every side by the lake, the place made Cui Wentai feel very safe. But he had not lodged there a week before he found his money gone. That night he changed what little he had left into straw-tied pork and wine, and drank himself into a stupor across the inn’s square eight-immortals table.

When day broke he took a boat away from Shangta. He had decided to slip quietly back to Shanghai. He still had a car — a Dodge. He could sell it. He had turned the notion over long before; when he first came to the Jinji firm as a driver, he had asked about everywhere, wanting to know whether it might be possible to sell a car off on the sly and make a great sum. An old car like this was worth two or three thousand silver dollars; even stripped and sold for parts and engine, he could get eight hundred or a thousand out of it. With that money he could just as well run off to some place far from the reach of officialdom and live well.

He meant to find a spot outside Xiaowuzi’s home to wait for her, and have her find him somewhere to hide for a few days. Then he could sell the car at his leisure and make good his escape. If she was willing, she could come away with him; if not, that was all right too — he could get himself settled and come back for her, or find another woman. His life had had its ups and downs, he had seen the world in all its breadth, and he believed that one day he would surely, once again, flip like a salted fish come back to life. Today was the twelfth of the first month; he must remember the day, for it was the first day of Cui Wentai’s rallying of his forces.

In the afternoon he got off the ferry. The fifteenth had not yet come, and the townsfolk were still keeping the New Year; there were few people in the streets, and only a handful of shops had their doors open. He did not mean to linger in the town, but made straight for that stretch of reeds by the lake. He found the car and poured a spare can of petrol into the tank, silently praising his own foresight — when he had filled up that day he had bought two extra cans and put them in the car. With this petrol, he reckoned, he could not only drive the car back to Shanghai but on to the buyer’s hands.

He dared not turn the car in the reeds, but put it in reverse and slowly backed it off the lakeshore up onto the dirt track. Sunlight lay on the reed-beds; the thawing surface of the mud showed a mass of little holes, and far off a flock of birds wheeled above the lake. Cui Wentai felt in good spirits. These last days he had read the papers and learned that the road under construction was called the Zhu-Hu county road, running from the Qingpu county seat at one end to the Hongqiao Road airfield at the other. This time he decided he would have a proper look at the airplanes. Just now he sat in the car like a stray cur in his terror; but who was to say that one day he, Cui Wentai, might not board an airplane himself and look down on the world from the sky?

But he had no more time for dreaming. On the dirt track ahead appeared two black motorcars, and at a glance he knew them for cars of the Songhu Garrison Command. He threw the car into reverse and backed with all his might toward the reeds. But behind him lay Dianshan Lake; there was no retreat.

You Tianxiao came picking his way over the uneven ground, meaning at first to crack a joke or two with this “Xi Shi”; but by the time he stood before him, he was already somewhat angry.

He said with a cold laugh: “A fine runner you are — fled all the way to Jiaoli. Cold as it is, have you come to tickle for fish and shrimp?”

Jiaoli is what the natives of Zhujiajiao call the place; whatever You Tianxiao spoke of, he liked to make himself out an old hand at it.

Cui Wentai was dragged from the car and pressed down into the mud of the reed-marsh; it was a good while before he caught his breath and grew a little calmer, and then he said in a voice near to weeping: “Fish and shrimp — what fish and shrimp? Hidden here ten days and more and I’ve not seen so much as a shrimp’s whisker. Every day so frightened I couldn’t sleep, in dread of being seized by the Communists and shot. It’s a load off my mind to see you, Captain You. Captain You — the underground want to take me; I can still be of service to you.”

“It’s not just the Communists who want you now. We Nationalists, brother, are under orders to come and take you too. Both parties, Nationalist and Communist, after you at once — you’ve played no small hand this time. And no wonder: who told you to be the Special Operations Headquarters’ famous ‘Xi Shi’?”

The men You Tianxiao had brought this time were all his own trusted people, who knew that Captain You was not only chief of the Garrison Command’s detective squad but also the Nanjing Special Operations Headquarters’ station chief in Shanghai.

Hearing You Tianxiao bring up “Xi Shi,” Cui Wentai took a little heart again: “Captain You, please take me to see Director Ye. I have things to say to him.”

Someone took the suitcase from the back seat of Cui Wentai’s car and set it down on the mud.

“There’s nothing in the case but old newspaper and a few scale-weights,” Cui Wentai hastened to explain.

That day, opening the suitcase on the back seat, he had found it stuffed full of old newspaper, wrapped around a few iron scale-weights; the weights were smeared with coal dust, most likely lifted from the scales of some coal yard. He had not dared throw the case away — feeling, perhaps, that sooner or later he would be caught, and that having got no gold, if he threw the case away as well the thing would be harder still to explain.

He guessed he had been tricked, taken in by Chen Qianli. On the road he had thought it very likely that Chen Qianli had simply wanted him to use the suitcase to draw the agents off — the man was too cunning by half, to deceive his own comrade so. For one moment he even indulged the wild fancy that, if he had drawn the agents away, that still counted as a service rendered; that Chen Qianli might not have found out they had been betrayed by him, and that he could yet go back and find them. But afterward even he came to feel that this was to take other people for fools like himself.

Sure enough, there was nothing in the case but newspaper and scale-weights. The old newspaper had been rolled into balls and packed the suitcase full to bursting.

“Where have you put the gold bars?” said You Tianxiao sharply.

“Captain You, there’s no gold in the case. I never dared lay a finger on it.”

“You hauled this case all the way from Tianjin Road to here, and still dare say you never laid a finger on it?” In truth You Tianxiao was not much angered. Ye Qinian did not care about the gold, and neither did he. They had meant in earnest to take the Communists, and this fellow had wrecked a fine operation. And to call himself “Xi Shi,” the Special Operations Headquarters’ most precious planted agent! He had long borne the man a grudge — Teacher Ye’s favorite “Xi Shi,” with every task the headquarters set revolving around him; these past days the whole Shanghai station had been at the service of this one man.

Very well: Teacher Ye had said to do away with him quietly. You Tianxiao understood how Teacher Ye felt — his best-loved subordinate turned into such a prize piece of goods, making off with a few gold bars; how shaming, were it ever told. There were those at headquarters who had always borne Teacher Ye ill will. In killing this fellow, You Tianxiao felt he would get at the least a double pleasure, and perhaps more than that.

“Captain You, let me see Director Ye — I have things to say to him.” In some panic Cui Wentai cried out to You Tianxiao.

Ye Qinian had no wish to hear him talk; You Tianxiao, though, had a question he wanted to put: “Tell me the truth. What did you go running off with a few scale-weights for that day? What made you take it into your head, all at once, to bolt?”

“If only I’d known they were scale-weights— That day I was truly bewitched; when they put the case in the car, I seemed all at once to catch the smell of gold.”

He had told the truth, but You Tianxiao only laughed, and said, weighing out each word:

“The smell of gold is the smell a man breathes when he’s sunk to the bottom of Dianshan Lake.”

Cui Wentai’s legs went weak with fright; he knelt on the ground, stretched out his hands to You Tianxiao, and cried till his voice cracked: “I have things I must say to Director Ye.”

You Tianxiao said with a smile: “Director Ye doesn’t want to hear you talk. But I can hear you out — three sentences. Go on, then: three sentences.”

Cui Wentai was checked a moment, then hurried on: “The gold’s most likely still at the bank…”

You Tianxiao waved his hand, and two burly men of the detective squad came forward, trussed Cui Wentai hand and foot with rope, and made to stuff him into the back seat of the car to sink him in the lake.

You Tianxiao stopped his men: “To bury him with a car thrown in would be letting him off cheap. The detective squad confiscates it.”

From the town in the distance came a burst of firecrackers, mingled with Cui Wentai’s ceaseless cries:

“Captain You, you said you’d hear my three sentences… Captain You, you can’t go back on your word…”

Around eight that evening You Tianxiao came back into the city. He went straight to the Zhengyuan Hotel near North Station, and there saw Ye Qinian. On the face of it the place was a hotel, but in truth it was a property the Special Operations Headquarters had paid to set up; the headquarters’ Shanghai station was housed on the top floor, and the guests put up in the rooms below were mostly headquarters men come to Shanghai. Should some casual guest who knew nothing of all this come to the desk asking for a room, he would most likely be told the house was full; and a suspicious-looking few might even be hauled to the back and have their identities looked into.

Ye Qinian was not greatly concerned with what became of Cui Wentai. But hearing You Tianxiao speak of the scale-weights, he did remark: “This Chen Qianli — making off with the scale-weights from a coal yard; how’s a man to do business?”

“Teacher, I’ve been wondering all along — could the gold still be at the bank?”

“I had the bank looked into. On the morning of New Year’s Day, just a quarter of an hour before you took your men to Tianjin Road, someone got into the bank in the guise of a rich merchant and opened a new safe-deposit box. This man was inside the vault, storing his things, for a full hour. Which is to say, when that Lin Shi went in, there were two men in the vault. Chen Qianli worked a switch; with Cui Wentai kicking up his fuss, our attention was thrown into confusion, and it never occurred to us the thing might still be at the bank.

“That same afternoon the man came to the bank a second time, claiming he had deposited only part of his things in the morning, and asking to have the vault opened for him once more. Though the box is still under lease, I think the things have already been taken away by him. This man — guess whether it was Chen Qianli? Of course; it could be no one else. Cui Wentai’s little turn, I reckon, was wholly unlooked-for; Chen Qianli has not the skill to talk Cui Wentai into helping him queer the game. Had it not been for Cui Wentai’s performance, the moment the suitcase was brought in that day and opened, I’d have guessed someone had made a switch in the vault. Chen Qianli hit on a wretched plan, and then ran into a stroke of luck. But the next time he may not be so lucky.”

“Only now there’s no more ‘Xi Shi’…”

“That is no concern of yours,” said Ye Qinian. “Tomorrow Chen Qianli comes back to Shanghai on the Guisheng; around twelve o’clock the boat will berth at the Gonghexiang Wharf. On the underground’s side, Yi Junnian will go to meet him. You take your men there, keep out of sight in the car, and show nothing. I’ve posted a marksman besides, up on the roof of the wharf company’s building. If he does not fire, you are to make no move; if he fires, you go out at once, let Yi Junnian go, and — dead or alive — bring Chen Qianli to me.”

“And that Yi Junnian — needn’t we take him too, along with the rest?”

Ye Qinian thought a while: “Let him live a few days more.”
