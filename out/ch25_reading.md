## 11

Zhongliang did not leave Shanghai after all. He moved into an apartment building near Hongkou Park. This was a quarter where Japanese residents lived close together; Su Lina had rented the place after they agreed to Kruger's request. Across the street below was a Japanese tavern called the Qingyuan, and every night, deep in the night, a drunkard would sing Japanese folk songs there like a man gone mad. The first time Su Lina brought Zhongliang here, she leaned against the windowsill and said that the most dangerous place was also the safest. As she spoke, she put a key into Zhongliang's hand, looked back at the street below, and added, “Let's hope neither of us ever needs it.”

There were tinned foods in the kitchen, and men's and women's clothes hanging in the wall closet; only there were no photographs on the walls. The place was more like the warm nest of a pair of wild mandarin ducks.

After a whole night of Japanese folk songs, Zhongliang could bear it no longer. From the wardrobe he picked out a tweed suit and an old overcoat, changed into them, and looked like a junior clerk at a foreign firm hurrying to work. But the moment he reached the foot of the Suzhou Creek bridge, he changed his mind. The place was full of civilians lined up for inspection, and the usual police had been replaced by armed Japanese military police. Zhongliang bought a Japanese-language newspaper at the roadside and went back to the flat as if nothing were the matter.

It was in the newspaper that Zhongliang saw Xiufen. Two men and a woman, three photographs, their faces bleached white by the flashbulbs. Xiufen lay on her back on the ground, her eyes open, her gaze at once calm and lost.

At dusk the next day, when Su Lina came in through the door with a jewelry box in her arms, Zhongliang was still clutching that newspaper. He looked at Su Lina with blood-red eyes, and it was a long time before he asked her what was wrong. What had happened?

Su Lina had kept watch two days and two nights in a ward of the army hospital. Qin Zhaokuan had been shot in the chest; after the operation there was a saline drip on his arm and an oxygen tube in his nostrils, but his spirits were remarkably good. When all the visitors had gone, he had Su Lina take from his hand the ring he had worn for years, and told her to take the ring to a pawnshop on Fourth Avenue run by a Japanese, and there to find its proprietor, Mr. Harada. Qin Zhaokuan went on, “When he sees the ring he'll give you a box. You must do exactly as I say.” Qin Zhaokuan said it all in one breath, then weakly closed his eyes. Su Lina gripped one of his hands. “I'm not going anywhere. I'll stay with you.”

Qin Zhaokuan shook his head. “I can't let you die together with me.”

Su Lina said, “You're going to get better.”

Qin Zhaokuan shook his head, opened his eyes, looked at the woman before him, and suddenly broke into a smile. “You people shouldn't have killed Nakamura.”

Su Lina's eyes went wide all at once; she stared at him but could not get out a single word.

Qin Zhaokuan's gaze was calm and tender. He drew out his hand, reached it to Su Lina's face, and let it rest there. “Silly girl. I never knew what you were — how else would I have leaked you so much intelligence? Not once did the two of us share a bed and dream different dreams.” As Qin Zhaokuan spoke, his hand slid down onto the bed, and the smile on his face vanished with it. He looked at Su Lina in earnest and said the Japanese must be investigating every Chinese who had been present that night; they would surely take the two bullets he had caught for a self-inflicted ruse.

Su Lina fixed her eyes on his. “What in the world are you?”

The smile rose again on Qin Zhaokuan's face. “Your man,” he said. Then he added, “A pity. I won't live to the day I marry you.”

These were the last words Qin Zhaokuan left in the world. After Su Lina had gone, he gazed vacantly at the ceiling until the light in his eyes burned out like a candle. When the doctors and nurses crowded into the ward and lifted the quilt, they saw that fresh red blood had long since soaked through the bandage on his chest. Though Qin Zhaokuan lay in a pool of blood, he looked more as though he lay amid a bank of fresh flowers, so serene and content was he.

Su Lina found the Japanese pawnshop called Harada Shichiya on Fourth Avenue, and when she handed the ring to its proprietor, Mr. Harada, the aged Japanese was silent a moment, bowed to her deeply, then turned and went to the inner room to bring out, in both hands, a lacquer-sealed jewelry box, which he presented to Su Lina.

In the jewelry box, besides some gold bars and American dollars, there was a letter in Qin Zhaokuan's hand, which read: To be delivered to Chen Taining, Longxin Warehouse, Shiliupu Wharf.

Su Lina looked at Mr. Harada, thinking he would say something more, but he only shook his head, bent again at the waist, made a gesture of invitation, and respectfully saw Su Lina all the way to the shop door. Then he hailed a rickshaw and watched Su Lina until she vanished into the flow of people. When she had almost reached her own door, Su Lina suddenly changed her mind and said to the puller, “Don't stop. Keep going.”

The puller turned his head and looked at her strangely. “Miss, keep going and you'll be at the Huangpu.” Su Lina made no sound; she turned her face away and, out of the corner of her eye, watched the plainclothes men just then entering the gate of her courtyard.

When Su Lina had gone over the day's events once more in her mind, she stubbed out her cigarette, took out the letter, and handed it to Zhongliang. “I want to know what's inside.”

Zhongliang nodded, rose, went into the kitchen to light the kerosene stove, boiled half a kettle of water, and, working the steam with a practiced hand, opened the envelope — inside was a card gone yellow with age, and an enameled Blue Sky, White Sun badge. The card was printed: Qin Zhaokuan, Party Affairs Investigation Section, Bureau of Investigation and Statistics, Central Executive Committee of the Chinese Nationalist Party.

That night the two of them leaned against the tatami, wrapped in the quilt, and did not sleep. They smoked every cigarette in the place and drank all the water in the place. Early the next morning Su Lina washed her face and went to the Longxin Warehouse at Shiliupu Wharf.

Chen Taining was a bald man, at once meek and mean-looking. He sat alone behind a ledger-desk in the warehouse; but the moment he took the letter from Su Lina's hand his eyes changed, and after he had torn open the envelope and seen the card, he clenched the badge tight in his hand, stood up, and cried out, “Miss Su.” Su Lina was taken aback. “You've seen me before?”

Chen Taining shook his head, opened his palm, and said, “I've seen this.”

Two years before, when Qin Zhaokuan gave his orders, he had set this badge and the yellowed card down before him and said that if he ever saw these two things again, he must get his woman out of Shanghai. Chen Taining had nodded and said yes. Qin Zhaokuan had fixed his eyes on him. “Even if you die, you must make sure she is safe.”

Chen Taining had laughed. “Sir, you worry too much.”

Qin Zhaokuan laughed at once as well, said nothing more, and the two of them looked together at the Huangpu, its whistles sounding one after another. Chen Taining remembered that on the river that day the dying sun was red as blood.

When Su Lina learned from Chen Taining that Qin Zhaokuan was dead, she shook her head hard. “It's not possible. He watched me leave.”

Chen Taining did not argue. He sat down and said coldly, “I'll arrange for you to leave as soon as possible.”

“I'm not going anywhere.” With that Su Lina turned to go.

“Miss Su.” Chen Taining caught her by the arm, but at once, carefully, let go again; propping himself on the ledger-desk, he looked at her straight, his gaze grim, and said, “Don't make Mr. Qin worry about you any longer.”

All the way out of the warehouse the light of tears glittered in Su Lina's eyes; a host of old memories came at her like a cold wind, until she could barely keep her feet. And yet, when she came before Chen Taining again with Zhongliang, not a trace of expression could be read on her face. She set the box of gold bars and American dollars down before Chen Taining and opened it. “Count it as his buying a boat ticket from you.”

Chen Taining shook his head. “My task is to send you out alone.”

Su Lina said, “To stay here is to leave him waiting for death.”

“That's not my concern.” Chen Taining said, “People die in Shanghai every day.”

“Very well.” Su Lina snapped the rosewood box shut. “Then you may as well send the two of us to the military police.”
