## Chapter 5. A Full Reckoning: Remarkable People, Remarkable Deeds

### A Review of the "Juntong Bureau's" Work in the Early Years of the Eight-Year War of Resistance

By the early days of the war of resistance I mean the span of time from the Marco Polo Bridge Incident of July, the twenty-sixth year of the Republic, down to about the twenty-ninth year. History has drawn no such division; it is no more than a way of setting things down that the writer has adopted for himself.

To my own eyes, in these few years the whole of the "Juntong Bureau's" work was already very well done. How do I know it? The heaviness of our sacrifices was itself the measure of what we achieved. Yet our leader, Mr. Dai Yunong, was not satisfied; and this, it may be, was because the places we severally occupied were different, so that in our sense of things there were naturally variations one from another.

Secret-service work does not lend itself to the holding of "work-review meetings"; men cannot be gathered into one hall to speak out and discuss in the open, and such review of the work as there was came wholly to light in the directions given by the higher ranks to the lower. In the twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth years Mr. Dai issued a number of work-directions; these I here divide under the several heads of intelligence, sabotage, and action, and, without departing from their original sense, excerpt their chief points as follows, that there may be a fuller understanding of our side's work through the years of the war.

As to intelligence.—"That which is of the first importance in this Bureau's intelligence lies in the reporting, throughout the several fallen zones, of the movements of the enemy's forces. Our Second Section must review this at all times and press it forward by every means, that both the quantity and the quality of our military intelligence upon the enemy may be improved. It is found that the poverty of our military intelligence upon the enemy greatly affects the advance of this Bureau's work, and we must contrive to better it; wherefore the several 'Districts, Stations, and Groups' in the fallen zones are to be enjoined to appoint more men charged expressly with the investigation of the enemy forces' movements. Beginning from the —— month, those who show results in reporting the enemy's movements, whether units or individuals, shall each be severally rewarded, even as a reward is given for the success of an action case. The assessment branch of our Second Section must strengthen its work, and let a general order go out at once that the several 'Districts, Stations, and Groups' all take note."

This one work-instruction is the original text of a hand-order from Mr. Dai to the Second Section, which had charge of intelligence business; its main purport was the strengthening of the collection of military intelligence in the fallen zones. Looked at in its substance, this military intelligence belonged to the general military activity of a tactical kind, which a man of some little military knowledge might undertake; only that in the passing of it there was much difficulty, and it was very hard to keep it timely, and once it lost its timeliness its value in use fell away with it. This was the very reason it could never be done to the full. Let it be marked that all this was the affair of forty years and more ago; were it in our own day, then, by reason of the advance in the art of communication, it would be another matter again.

There is another work-instruction, whose text runs thus: "The center of gravity of this Bureau's present intelligence work, as regards the fallen zones, is:

"1. To ascertain by scouting the movements of the enemy forces and the storage of their munitions;

"2. To inquire in detail into the enemy's political and economic establishments, and into the state of the puppet organizations and puppet forces.

As regards the war zones:

"1. To investigate the discipline of our regular forces in the field, the daily life of officers and men, and the manner of their fighting;

"2. The investigation of the several guerrilla bands' activities, their strength, their pay and provisioning, and the thought and life of those who command them;

"3. The scouting of the Communist Party's activities and the source of its arms, and of the Communist Party's posture toward the enemy and puppets and toward the friendly forces;

"4. To attend to the state of smuggling by the troops.

As regards the rear:

"1. To attend to the scouting of the administration of conscription;

"2. The investigation of the distribution of military and civilian grain and of transport of every kind;

"3. The organization and activity of the Communist Party, and above all attention must be given to the words and deeds of those who hold leading positions in that Party;

"4. The exposure of corruption among public servants, and above all attention must be given to the manner of life of those staff who have to do with this Bureau;

"5. The life of the officers and men at the several replacement-training depots, and the manner in which they train the new recruits;

"6. The carrying-out, by organs of every rank, of the Leader's commands: close attention must be given to the degree of their compliance.

"The matters set forth above are those which this Bureau's work must press forward and accomplish. It is my hope that the several sections of our Second Section, each within the compass of its charge, will press their assessment the harder and strengthen their guidance; and that, as to the capacities of the intelligence men now in hand, the lines of their work, and the manner of their life, all these should be examined and set on register, so that they may be adjusted, and the work of each intelligence man may day by day grow the more specialized. This is a great work and one full of meaning; and it is my hope that the comrades of the several sections of our Second Section will work with easy minds and strive after progress—then shall I be imperishable."

This piece, again, is the original text of a hand-instruction from Mr. Dai to the Second Section of the Bureau's head office; its main purport lay in setting forth the demands of the work, with, besides, the sense of review and betterment. In the hand-instruction, according to the circumstances of the time, the whole country was divided into three sorts of region; and thus, in the demands upon intelligence, each had its differences.

The "fallen zones" spoken of in the hand-instruction were the zones under enemy occupation; the "war zones" were the zones where our forces and the Japanese were engaged; the "rear" was where the Central Government had its seat. The fallen zones of that time took in the Northeast, North China, and the provinces of Rehe, Chahar, and Suiyuan; besides these, portions of many another province, the city of Shanghai among them. The zones of engagement were the whole or part of the provinces of Jiangsu, Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Hunan, and Hubei. As for the rear, or the great rear, there were only Sichuan, Yunnan, and Guizhou. To be sure, Xikang, Tibet, and Xinjiang should be reckoned as rear, and Shaanxi, Gansu, and Ningxia might be taken as war zones. In truth there was no strict division at all.

In the intelligence demands above it is not hard to find three great features. First, the regular forces in the war zones—their discipline, down to the very manner of their fighting—were all taken into the compass of investigation; and though this did indeed set up the operating authority of the "Juntong Bureau," it drew upon us at the same time reproach and resentment. Second, whatever posture the Communist Party might take, the "Juntong Bureau" held it always to be a chief object of the work; not that we made a show of any rare discernment, but that in the course of our struggle with the Communist Party we did in truth come to a deeper understanding of it. Third, the exposure of corruption and smuggling, not to be lightly regarded, showed that the "Juntong Bureau" had won a high trust—and this went on without a break to the end of the war.

As to sabotage.—

"At this present day the work of sabotage behind the enemy's lines is of the greatest moment. This Bureau bears such a charge and may not cast it off; moreover this Bureau, by command of the Chief of the General Staff, conducts a training class for demolition men, which is itself the token of its bearing this charge. Wherefore, upon the strength of what has already been accomplished, of the present need, and of this Bureau's past results in the sabotage of railways and highways, a further memorial should be made to Chief of the General Staff He, praying that funds be issued as before and that we be permitted to carry our action on; and let Director He be asked by letter to set forth to the Chief of the General Staff the purport of the Generalissimo's earlier endorsement."

Judging by the tone of the text above, the piece seems to have been a "ruling" or "instruction" of Mr. Dai's to the unit in charge. Now "the work of sabotage," carried on behind the enemy's lines—that is, in the fallen zones—had for its chief aim the cutting of the enemy's lines of transport, and the dealing of a heavy hurt to them, so as to lower their power of attack.

Of the organizing and the carrying-out of "the work of sabotage," the rough shape may be seen in yet another document. The full particulars of the original run as follows:

1. "First, a Demolition Brigade for the middle section of the Tianjin–Pukou line shall be set up, charged with the work of sabotage on the railway between Xuzhou and Jinan, with Comrade Qin Qirong (a man of Shandong, of the sixth class of Whampoa) to hold the post of brigade commander besides, and with Comrade Qin to recommend some able and experienced schoolfellow (the writer's note: he means a fellow-alumnus of the Whampoa Military Academy) for brigade adjutant. The brigade shall be divided into three companies, together with one directly-attached special-service section, the officers, men, and porters of the whole brigade to number some five hundred.

2. "The brigade's funds are for the present fixed at twenty thousand yuan a day; should results be shown, a bounty shall be issued at discretion.

3. "The brigade shall have one political-training instructor and one assistant; each company shall have one political-training instructor, charged with the guidance and the assessment of the officers' and men's manner of life.

4. "The cadres of the brigade, at every rank, must all be men who have had military training.

5. "Two wireless sets are granted, and at least three operators, to go forth to the work.

6. "There are granted twenty Mauser pistols with ten thousand rounds, and twenty revolvers with four thousand rounds, for the brigade's use, together with a part of the demolition matériel.

7. "There is issued to the brigade a temporary sum of twenty thousand yuan for opening costs and the like, and two months' funds besides, the pay to run from the day the founding is reported.

8. "Four students of the demolition training class are granted, to go forth and join the work.

9. "This Bureau shall choose out one who understands soldiering and has a political head, to go with Comrade Qin Qirong to Shandong and there treat with and aid him in his work.

10. "As for the organizing of the Qingdao action group, let Comrade Qin Qirong and Comrade He —— arrange it in concert. Three cells are granted leave to be formed, each cell to have one leader and from five to six action-men, the monthly funds fixed for the present at 2,400 yuan, with three thousand yuan of opening costs besides. In this disposing of the action, no lateral link is to be suffered between the cells; every action-man must have the cover of an occupation, that he may move about. The objects of this action group's work shall be chiefly the sanction of important traitors, the sabotage of the enemy's camp-works and factories, the burning of the enemy's stores, and the repair-shops of the Jiao-Ji railway. There are granted twelve Mauser pistols and ten revolvers, two hundred rounds to each piece, with two months' funds issued in advance for Comrade Qin to carry with him.

11. "The cipher-books to be issued as before."

The writer will add a word or two more, according to his own understanding of the matter, as follows:

The text above was very likely a hand-order Mr. Dai issued to Comrade Qin Qirong; for in that age the keeping of a "duplicate" was not yet nicely observed, and so Comrade Qin had, on the strength of the "original" of this order, to apply to the several responsible units for funds, arms and ammunition, wireless sets, cipher-books, demolition matériel, and the like—and to treat, too, of personnel matters with the department in charge of personnel.

As for the scale of living: this "Demolition Brigade for the middle section of the Tianjin–Pukou line," about to be formed, had by regulation over five hundred officers and men in all, yet its funds came monthly to but twenty thousand yuan of fabi; take away the sundry public charges and the needful outlays of activity, and the average came to less than forty yuan a month for each man, and might fall as low as under thirty. Beyond this there was no other supply—that is to say, they had to find their own board. To speak of it, it was more liberal than the common soldier's; but measured against the hardness of the work's nature, it was in truth a bitter lot indeed.

The "Demolition Brigade" having above five hundred officers and men, why were but forty pistols issued to it? Because a "demolition unit" is not a "guerrilla band"; it has no need to give battle. The use of the pistol lay only in covering advance and retreat; and at times the pistol might serve, too, as the "fuse" and the "detonator" in a work of sabotage.

In the ninth point—"to choose out one who understands soldiering and has a political head, to go with Comrade Qin to Shandong and aid him in his work"—I take it this man must surely have borne besides the charge of "secret inspector." In the common run of things, "the work of sabotage" is but a part of "the work of action"; that is to say, whether "action group" or "action unit," besides carrying out the sanction of traitors it went on at the same time with sabotage against the enemy forces, and so it was ever with our "Shanghai District." The setting-up of the "Demolition Brigade" was, in the main, still to accord with and support our regular forces in their fight against the Japanese; its aim leaned toward the cutting of the lines of transport and the wrecking of the means of carriage. As for the task of the "Qingdao action group," it took for its chief aim the enemy's military stores and their apparatus of production.

The important directive below is more particular in its substance, and carries a clear self-examination; its original text runs thus:

"It is found that the work of sabotage in the several fallen zones was, by command of Chief of the General Staff He last year, made over to this Bureau to conduct, the Ordnance Department supplying us with the matériel of sabotage, and the training likewise laid upon us. Now, by reason of the slowness of the training, the weakness of our forces of sabotage upon the several railways and highways, the difficulty of carrying demolition materials into the fallen zones, and the want of thoroughness in this Bureau's design, guidance, and inspectorate, there has been no result shown at all; and I, taken to task by conscience and by duty, am filled with fear and shame beyond measure! Now, besides charging the Loyal and Patriotic Army to strengthen the sabotage of the Nanjing–Shanghai, Shanghai–Hangzhou, and Suzhou–Jiaxing railways, and of the Nanjing–Shanghai, Nanjing–Hangzhou, and Shanghai–Hangzhou highways, we should for the Hunan–Hubei line make use of Fang Buzhou, for the Tianjin–Pukou line make use of Xie Bing, and for the southern section of the Beiping–Hankou line wire Yue Zhuyuan to choose out with speed men of southern Henan and, in the country south of Xinyang and north of Guangshui, take up under our colors a part (about a battalion) of an armed force, charged expressly with the sabotage of the railway; while for the Canton–Kowloon line we should wire Xie Zhennan to treat with Zou Shi, and near Shilong to organize a railway demolition unit (of officers and men about a company), and set it stirring in earnest, its funds to be drawn from the one hundred and fifty thousand of funds issued before Chief of the General Staff He for the stirring-up of work in the Northeast; and as for the several other railways and highways, along whose lines there are organized demolition units or action groups, they too should straightway be set to a planned stirring-up—this is of the first moment. And the personnel of the demolition training class should be strengthened at once, for Chief Instructor Liu cannot by himself set it moving."

In the directive above it is plainly pointed out wherein lay the cause of there being no result shown: on the side of men, in the want of thoroughness in design, guidance, and inspectorate; in the matter of fact, the difficulty of transporting matériel and the weakness of the organized forces. And so it was resolved to strengthen the disposition, whose compass took in many a line of rail and road, spreading over the breadth of eight provinces.

As to the work of sabotage, Mr. Dai had a disposition even for the far-off Beiping–Suiyuan railway; his instruction to Brother Gao Rong, chief of the "Suiyuan Station," ran as follows:

"For the work of the 'Beiping–Suiyuan railway demolition unit,' Gao Rong should be wired to choose out with speed, from the 'plain-clothes mixed unit' of the Eighth War Zone, one hundred and twenty picked officers and men, and between Zhangjiakou and Guisui to carry out sabotage; should the arms fall short, this Bureau will supply them at discretion, and their funds too shall be issued by this Bureau. And the men already belonging to the 'Beiping–Suiyuan railway demolition unit' shall all be brought in under Gao Rong's organizing and command."

Brother Gao Rong and the writer were twice schoolfellows; I have made mention of it in the fourth section of "Rooting Out Traitors in the North"—that was in the twenty-fourth year. By the twenty-ninth year he was still chief of the "Suiyuan Station," with another post besides in the Eighth War Zone. In the thirty-ninth year we met in Hong Kong and idled away a stretch of days together. By then he had come into the estate of his father-in-law, and kept a women's clothing shop of no little fame, caring no longer to speak much of bygone days.

The "Juntong Bureau's" work of sabotage against the enemy in the early days of the war, though Mr. Dai called it "no result shown at all," was so only of certain zones, and was besides a form of words to spur his subordinates on. In fact the single unit of the "Shanghai District" alone had done some scores of such things, great and small, and the hurt dealt to the enemy forces was not to be called small. By what the writer knows, the "Suzhou Station" and the "Hangzhou Station" too had a very good showing of results—and many comrades, likewise, gave their lives for it.

Having reviewed the work of sabotage, there is one thing that must be appended here, and it is this: in the fallen zones, whenever a work of sabotage had been carried out, straightway there followed, more or fewer, common Chinese folk who came to grief—either hurt under the enemy's cruel racking to make them confess, or, by the enemy's burning in reprisal, brought to the ruin of their households and the loss of their lives. In a case like this, weighing gain against loss, it is a hard thing indeed to measure.

As to action.—

"The cadres for the work of action in the several places are wanting, nor is its organization everywhere spread; for the sanction of important traitors there is a want of inside lines, nor is there any sufficient standard."

This was Mr. Dai's terse review of the work of action. In the years from the twenty-eighth to the twenty-ninth, the field units the "Juntong Bureau" had disposed in the fallen zones were no fewer than a hundred-odd; of these, those having an "action group" or "action unit" were less than a third—chiefly for the constraint of circumstances, and next for the hardness of finding basic cadres. For work behind the enemy's lines is, after all, a different thing from the charge and shock of regular troops.

"The traitor Wang has mounted the stage; we must, in the two places of Nanjing and Shanghai and along the Nanjing–Shanghai line, enlarge our action, that we may set forth our authority. Let word go at once by wire to the comrades Gongshu, Xinmin, Gongshao, and Daosan, to stir up those under them to a vigorous action, whereby to chill the gall of the traitor-villains, as a scheme to draw the firewood from under the cauldron."

This telegram was a hand-order Mr. Dai sent down, distributed by Mr. Mao Renfeng of the Bureau's head office (acting secretary-general) severally to four men: the writer himself, of the "Shanghai District"; Qian Xinmin, chief of the "Nanjing District"; Liao Gongshao, chief of the "Hangzhou Station"; and Zhou Daosan (Weilong), commander-in-chief of the "Loyal and Patriotic Army."

Speaking of drawing the firewood from under the cauldron, I could not but think again that only by doing away with Wang would there be a plan that struck at the root.

"The plan your office has drawn up for the expansion of the Shanghai action organization I have already approved. I pray you seek out loyal and brave men and set it up with speed; for the arms needed, I pray you contrive to buy them up on the spot."

This was a small passage from a telegram Mr. Dai sent the "Shanghai District." "To expand the action organization" meant to fill out the seven action brigades the "Shanghai District" already had, and to form yet more action brigades besides—whence came the setting-up of the "Eighth Action Brigade." Only, because men were hard to recruit, and arms and ammunition hard to seek out, though in the matter of organization we were under no limit of number, it was never after this expanded further.

The above is a rough account of the "Juntong Bureau's" review, in the early days of the war, of the three works of intelligence, sabotage, and action in the fallen zones. The tasks the "Juntong Bureau" bore were many in their kinds, and there is here no room to set them all down; their chief heads may be seen in the "summary of the review" below.

As to the summary of the review.—The present shortcomings of this Bureau's work:

1. "Though there is a plan of the whole, it cannot be carried out step by step.

2. "Though the work can grasp its center, it cannot be pressed forward by a plan.

3. "Though personnel are on record, the capacities of this or that particular man, his past history, and his real present manner of life cannot be made clear, and so he is misplaced.

4. "Trained men are wanting and the materials commonplace; above all the choice of the deputy heads and political-training group leaders of the several classes has been ill-made, and training and work cannot be brought into one.

5. "The inspectors are few in number, and those charged with inspection want a keen eye, a fine and careful head, a warm ardor, a grave and upright bearing; they are besides unclear as to the present state of things, the state of the place, and the capacities and lives of the work-people—wherefore the oversight is not strict and the inspection not thorough, so that the work in the several places has come to the loose and laggard show it presents today.

6. "The secretaries of the secretariat being few in number, they cannot be spread to the work; and the choice of them being, moreover, not strong, the review, the guidance, and the planning of the work are all deficient.

7. "The middle and lower work-people of the several units being low in political level and poor in ability, the heads of the several units are busied with the daily work and have no leisure for review, design, or guidance.

8. "The pay of all the work-people being too meager and their life hard, the spirit of the work is affected.

9. "The wireless men being unpractised in their art, and too few besides to be spread to the work, and the strength of the central station being still weak, the calls and answers do not go smoothly, and delays are very many; and that the wireless men want political training is one cause of it too.

10. "The Second Section, in the guidance and assessment of intelligence, and in the compiling, examining, and gathering of statistics, cannot make a constant, general, real, and fine execution of it, so that upon this or that question there is no concrete and correct understanding, no good contribution is made to the Leader, and no materials for our own training can be got from it.

11. "For the work of action in the several places the cadres are wanting and the organization not everywhere spread; for the sanction of important traitors there is a want of deep inside lines; for the sabotage of transport in the several places the strength is not made ready, so that in the whole of our action there is much that has not been able to fit the needs of the military operations, and in the sabotage of the enemy's economic resources and of their aircraft-stores there is no marked showing of results either—for want of a sound organization and of full preparation.

12. "For the movement of turning the puppet forces to the right side there is a want of a good go-between, and the Central Government too has no better treatment for puppet forces that come over; and our work-people in the several places have not been able to make their way into the puppet forces to a strong stirring-up—these are all great causes of there being no marked showing of results in the work of turning the puppet forces over.

13. "With the present work of 11,000 men, there is monthly but 510,000-odd yuan of recurring and extraordinary funds, so that the monthly funds are very far short, borrowed here and shifted there, and forever in straits and want; add to this the difficulty of remittance to the fallen zones, and the work in the several places can hardly but be affected by it.

14. "In all the open organs we hold in our grasp, the several chief officers bear this Bureau, for the most part, a suspicious and jealous mind; and so toward the men this Bureau sends they on the one hand court and humor them to the utmost, and on the other strive with all their might to hinder any advance—so that the men this Bureau sends forget their own mission and leave this Bureau's ground, and thus a rift is formed and a show of contradiction breeds.

15. "As for what pertains to myself (Mr. Dai Yunong speaking of himself): for want of aides, whether in the handling of documents, the receiving of guests, or the meeting of daily affairs, I have had in all to take it upon myself in person, so that time has fallen short and many things been delayed; in the carrying-out of the daily work, the ordering of time has been ill-done; in the use of cadres and the grasp of organization, I have not been able to do all well; my touch with the several quarters has been too little, and the keeping-up of it not enough; my chances of receiving the middle and lower work-people one by one have been too few, so that our feeling cannot be closely joined; I am strong of character and heavy in my own opinions, and so among the comrades there are but few views and counsels upon the whole of the work; beset day by day with meeting affairs, I have not been able to do all well in review, planning, and oversight."

In the whole of the work-review the following points were stressed: namely, "meager pay," "a low political level," "a want of instructors," "straitened funds," "a low level of science and technology," "a lack alike of matériel and of teaching," "being pushed aside where one stood," "every work falling short of the ideal," and Mr. Dai's own "being strong of character and heavy in opinion, so that the views of high and low could not be wholly brought into accord, and planning and oversight could not be done to the full."

The several sections below are the "Juntong Bureau's" inner general review at the twenty-ninth year of the Republic—wholly the true state of things, with nothing false in it. Only, because each comrade's place in the work was different, some slight want of full accord in point of view is to be allowed. After this the "Juntong Bureau's" organization spread wider day by day, its works of every kind grew ever more, and Mr. Dai was in truth beset with a myriad affairs and hard put to it to cope; and materials of this sort, of work-review, were no more to be seen.

### Accepting Without Leave a Consignment of Donated Arms; the Handling of It

A friend of the "Shanghai District," and a volunteer worker of the "Juntong Bureau" too, Brother Hu Yongquan, came back from Hong Kong to Shanghai before the lunar New Year. He went out of his way to call on me at my house on Seymour Road; and he was the only guest I ever received at home, for that place was one he had rented for me.

To pay a call and visit was, one may say, a courtesy of the lips; more to the purpose was that he brought me a favor both great and heavy. This favor—to refuse it I could not bring myself, yet to take it I feared there might be trouble—had me fairly at a stand, and for a while I durst not lightly come to a decision.

The favor Brother Yongquan brought back was no gold or silver or precious thing; in the Shanghai of that day it was worth far more than such things, and of more real use besides. It was a batch of Mauser pistols, a hundred-odd of them—the exact number should be one hundred and forty-four, though, for the drawing-out of samples, it might fall short of this—each fitted besides with two spare magazines, and ten thousand-odd rounds thrown in.

The guns were new, and had lain all along in Shanghai, to be drawn out at any time upon a warrant; their keeper was a German. This friend, to show the sincerity of his heart, had even sent the warrant for taking delivery of the goods along with Brother Yongquan.

Who was he? I am not free to disclose it, for he had dwelt abroad for years and but rarely came home once, so that there was no getting his consent beforehand; and though the affair had long since passed away like a cloud, it was after all no common one. In his own mind, it may be, he reckoned it but a small matter, and by now, perhaps, has long forgotten it; yet in my remembrance I have always cherished this precious friendship, and hold it for a great matter still.

By rights the bond between me and this friend was not deep; we had only been together for the time of a month's training, close by day and night, and had talked to a mutual liking, that was all. In a tie like this, the common way is that, once the training is over, each goes back to his post, and whether one ever meets again hangs upon chance. Yet at the time this friend and I had made our appointment to meet a second time outside; and, past all looking-for, I could not come by so much as a chance to take my leave of him over the telephone, but in the haste of the moment was ordered forth—a great matter for regret indeed.

Since the beginning of August of the twenty-eighth year, when I broke the appointment and could not come to the meeting, half a year had passed to now, and this friend and I had had no more touch; not only had no letter passed, I did not so much as know where he was. As to how he could have come to know that I bore in Shanghai the charge of a part of the work behind the enemy's lines, and how he could be sure that Brother Hu Yongquan would surely hand that batch of things over to me—these were matters rather hard to make out. And as for Brother Yongquan, he was the famous "man who knew every road"; that I knew well enough—only, how he had struck up the tie with this friend in Hong Kong, and had come to win this friend's trust, I could but take upon Brother Yongquan's one-sided word; where was I to go to prove it? In truth there was no need to prove it either: a hundred-odd ready-made things could be had in hand at any time, and that was the very truth of it.

The question now lay not in the origin of this batch of things; for without a bond deep enough, or some settled tie of interest, they would never be sent to one's door for nothing. What needed serious weighing was still whether they could be taken. And if they were taken, what consequence would come of it?

I was yet turning it this way and that, wavering and unresolved, when Brother Yongquan of a sudden thought of one thing more he had forgotten to tell me. Brother Yongquan said: "When your friend gave me the warrant for the goods, he charged me particularly with one word, bidding me be sure to carry it to you. Your friend says: this is a gift to you—it is not a gift to your Boss!" Hearing it I was struck of a heap; and with this, I had the more to ponder.

I remembered how, the last time Hu Yongquan came back from Hong Kong, he had, out of a wholly good heart, wished to solve for me the hard matter of funds, and had in Hong Kong got from some warm-hearted person a generous aid, five hundred thousand yuan set aside for use. At the time I had indeed a mind to take it, meaning to use it to open out the work; and so I made a report of it to Mr. Dai, that no handle might be left for others afterward. I even thought I had done it with due measure—when in truth it was still the rawness of youth, wanting in seasoning, and I had actually broken a great taboo.

This time, when Brother Hu Yongquan came back from Hong Kong, he brought me a "goodwill" again, which of course I was willing to take in, and which, in fact, I had just such a need of. Only, since that friend had these two words to say—that it was a gift to me and not a gift to our Boss—there was plainly some spleen in it, and I could not but handle the matter with all care and heed. So I asked Brother Yongquan to keep the warrant for the goods a while, and to tell him when I had come to a decision. But Brother Yongquan thought otherwise; he held it was no matter—one simply need not tell him at all: what could be wrong in one's thinking out a way of one's own and getting the thing done with the tools in hand?

Brother Yongquan was a straight-hearted man; how should he see through the deep subtlety in this!

That same evening I went to Brother Qi Qingbin's house to work the matter over with him, and laid out for him too the reasons of my being in two minds whether to take it or leave it, hoping he would settle upon a course, that a decision might be made. Brother Qingbin was ever slow and deliberate; he thought a good while before he answered: "Take them; there is no need to report to Mr. Dai. First find a safe place and store them away—or divide them, and put them in two or three places, it is all one—only be sure to wait until they can be used before using them." Then he explained further: "By the time they can be used I mean this: in the ordinary carrying-out of work they are not to be used; nor, in setting up a new organization, may this batch be issued out; but should there come some sudden change of circumstance, and a chance to meet a large-scale action, then they may be brought into play."

I signed to him with my eyes, looking for him to explain his reasons further; and he was just making ready to go on. He said: "A great achievement can always cover over small faults; and, turned about, it is the same—should one meet with a great failure, all the small faults are gathered in with it, and the account is reckoned together on the small side." So saying, Qingbin went back into the inner room and fetched out a little slip of paper; pointing to it he said to me: "This is a telegram that came from Mr. Dai; you have seen it already. It happens to bear upon this matter, and we may as well look it over once more, and then we shall understand the reason it may be taken."

The content of the telegram—a few phrases of it were quoted above, under the head "action" in the Bureau's work-review—runs in full thus: "The plan your office has drawn up for the expansion of the Shanghai action organization I have already approved; I pray you seek out loyal and brave men and set it up with speed; for the arms needed, I pray you contrive to buy them up. And as for our fighting officers and men now detained in the Sihang Warehouse and in the French Concession, we should by every means secretly take up contact with them, so that at need we may treat with the authorities of the two Concessions for their release, whereby we may act in concert. And as for the police-guards who keep the arms-stores in the police stations of the two Concessions, our side should straightway find out a line to them, and buy them up with heavy money; at need we may ask them to open the store-doors and let our comrades take the arms for use; failing that, then at the time the arms should be burned, lest they be turned to the enemy's and the puppets' use."

Aye—should the notion or supposal in the telegram, which was the very "sudden change of circumstance" that Brother Qingbin spoke of, come indeed to pass, then, though there were at that time a hundred-odd guns too many, it could by any account be reasoned out. Looked at so, Brother Qingbin had in truth more discernment than I. And what was more, these were from the first a public thing, and could not be looked on as private property; used to slay the enemy and root out traitors, what could be amiss in it?

When I was making ready to leave, Brother Qingbin, fearing lest Hu Yongquan be careless and rough, counseled me that I ought to meet the German in person; and if he consented, it were best I should look the goods over myself, that I might be easy. In the end we settled it so.

On the way home I was still somewhat puzzled: why had that friend, for no cause at all, made me a gift of a batch of brand-new arms? And having given them to me, why must he add the one word, "it is not a gift to him"? Were he and I not one and the same? Perhaps between the two of them there was some want of harmony; only, because the level I stood at was too low, I had no standing at all to inquire into it, and it were best not so much as to think upon it.

There was yet another point on which I was still uneasy. By the rule of our work, it was on no account allowed to take of oneself any goods coming from without—arms of course among them; one may say, too, that arms were graver than common goods. Had I taken them, I had already trespassed against the statute—was this not to know the law and yet to break it? Only, thinking of what I and Brother Qingbin had just agreed upon, I let it be.

This was no urgent affair; some days passed before I made an appointment with Brother Yongquan over the telephone, to talk a little more at his house. The Hu family no longer lived in the Carlton Apartments; he had moved to the middle stretch of Bubbling Well Road, into a house roomier than the old one and more tastefully furnished. It was still the two of them, husband and wife, with only a maidservant added. Under the warm welcome of the pair of them I always felt some shame; for since we had made friends in Tianjin, it had been all their trouble on my account and their putting-out for my sake, and I had never done them one thing that was of help. So it is that some say: let a man make friends with one who does secret-service work, and he has met with ill-luck.

When we were seated, the third Mistress Hu withdrew, and Brother Yongquan and I fell again to working over how the batch of guns should be dealt with.

I told Brother Yongquan: "Let it be as you say—take that friend's goodwill and receive this batch of things, but for the present send no report up; when the chance comes, and it may be reported, then report it. As for how to thank that friend, let that wait; it is not convenient to write just now."

Brother Yongquan heard me and nodded that it was so. The question now was only how to take delivery. So I asked Brother Yongquan: "Do you know that German, have you met him?" He shook his head to say he did not know him, nor had he met him. That being so, I begged Brother Yongquan further to make first contact with the German; if he consented, then I meant to meet him face to face, and hoped at the same time to look over that batch of things. I knew Brother Yongquan could speak a few words of "pidgin" English—simple phrases, enough to make his meaning out; he could no doubt manage. And as for the German, surely he would not fail to understand? Only, should he be willing to meet me, it were best to be accompanied by one who understood German, which would suit the better.

Whether we had in our "Shanghai District" a comrade who knew German I could not, for the moment, call to mind; and even had there been, I was not minded to trouble them, for the fewer who knew, the better, and this was a matter kept hidden alike from above and below. I asked Brother Yongquan: "Have you a trustworthy friend who can speak German?" He thought a while and said: "That must be asked about." To be asked about would not do; and while I wavered, of a sudden a man came to my mind.

Of late I had often been troubled with a one-sided headache; it did not ache without ceasing, but when it ached it was very ill to bear. I had seen a doctor, and taken medicine and injections, but got no better. In talk someone said it might be that the number of my spectacles was wrong, and it were no harm to find an eye-doctor and be looked at. Passing by along the street in my daily going, I had, with half a mind, seen a brass plate graven with the words "Doctor of Ophthalmology, Nie Chonghou"; and it was he I went to. Doctor Nie tested my sight with care and said there was nothing amiss with my eyes; he only wrote out a slip, directing me to the Maochang Spectacle Company to make a fresh pair of glasses. I wore them some days, and the headache was as before—whence it was plain it did not rise from the sight.

Doctor Nie's trade was rather thin; whenever I went to be seen, I seldom met another patient, and so we fell to chatting. For him it may have been to dispel his tedium; and for me, besides easing a little my strained mood, I thought too whether there might be found in him aught that could serve the work. Mr. Nie Chonghou, a man of Jiangxi, had studied in Germany and taken the degree of Doctor of Ophthalmic Medicine. He was ever of an honest, kindly cast, neither like a pedant nor with any huckster's air, plain and unassuming, with much the look of one who sought no name—a true gentleman indeed. He told me he had first practised in Guangxi, but that, the living being poor and the times unsettled and full of alarms, his wife could not bear it, and so some years back they had moved to Shanghai (afterward, by Nie's telling, he was one posted to Shanghai by the Guangxi clique). And Shanghai, he said, was a place where one must have some drawing-card to get on: neither asking a man of note to make him known, nor putting out advertisements, how should he thrive there? Only, being able to come by a bowl of rice, he let it pass.

In our closer talk I learned that his wife was a German; they had been married many years and had had no children. Partly to spare the outlay, and partly that husband and wife might keep close together and be free of loneliness, the surgery was the dwelling too, one house serving a double turn; and this manner of life, he held, was after all a snug one. Once Doctor Nie asked his wife to come out and meet me; and she, save for a nod of courtesy, had not one word of Chinese.

This was the man I had just thought of, who might do our interpreting for us. It was, to be sure, somewhat deep speaking upon a shallow acquaintance; but by my own instinct I always held him to be a man one might trust, and what I could not be sure of was only the fear that he might scorn to do it. I set this meaning forth to Brother Yongquan, and he said it was well; then let it be settled that we should each arrange our part—Brother Yongquan to treat with the German and fix a time to meet; I to seek Doctor Nie's consent. How it turned out was made good over the telephone.

In truth the affair was, past looking-for, a simple one; both sides consented, and only Doctor Nie asked me what the German's name was—and I told him I truly did not know.

That we might not be exposed before the crowd, and thanks besides to Brother Hu Yongquan's open-handedness, the meeting was fixed at the Hu house; Brother Yongquan was to fetch the German, and I to come along with Doctor Nie Chonghou. When we were all met, it turned out that Doctor Nie and the German friend were old acquaintances, and had known each other long since. With this, all went smoothly; in a few words everything was settled.

The German friend's name, in its sound, had "Shi" for its first syllable; so let us call him Mr. Shi. Mr. Shi was a captain of the reserve, one who had first been engaged as adviser by a certain department of our government; his standing was a very particular one, and staying on in Shanghai he of course bore a task besides. To trace it to the bottom would draw in too much, and I shall not say more of it here. From the time I first came to know this Mr. Shi, and by Doctor Nie's connection, we kept up our tie throughout, and often too exchanged views in private; and as time went on we became friends at last, down to the spring of the thirty-fifth year.

As for that batch of pistols, it was Brother Zhang Zuoxing I asked to take them back. When they were checked over, all of them were new, and, having been well kept, without a whit of rust or damage. The count was one hundred and forty Mauser pistols, with two hundred-odd magazines besides, and 13,000-odd rounds. After I and Brother Qingbin and Brother Zuoxing had talked it over, we all held it better to store them divided than to lay them in one place, and so they were kept severally in two places, Zuoxing charged with their keeping. This matter was known to us three alone—and Brother Zuoxing did not yet know the whole of it.

To receive arms privately is no common thing, and so I would say a few words more beside the point, to make plain my heart and keep the true likeness of it.

That friend in Hong Kong, in giving me the guns, did it purely out of goodwill, without the least other design; and though he had by word of mouth two phrases spoken in spleen, a young man will not be without such, and what is there to wonder at in it? The reason I did not see fit to report it up was my care for Mr. Dai's temper and character, and it was besides not allowed by the discipline of the body. That, in a case of knowing and yet doing, I durst still act by expedience, rested wholly on the interest of the work, with no other thing in my heart.

Though Mr. Dai's telegram plainly said, "I pray you contrive to buy them up," I would by no means feign the case and report falsely that they were bought with money. In those days, in the Shanghai country, the buying and selling of guns had sellers but no market; there was no settled price of trade, and how much money it would take to buy a No. 2 Mauser with a hundred rounds—it was said, about several hundred yuan of fabi to a thousand-odd for each, while for a whole batch it was harder still to reckon. Say what one will, the worth of this batch of guns and ammunition was no small thing; taken roughly, it would come, in all, to about 100,000 yuan, would it not? Had it been used as my own "living-and-activity expenses" of that time, it would have served for as long as twenty years. This is no set showing-off of my not taking so much as a mustard-seed; rather it should be said I had not yet learned to go bad.

### A Remarkable Man of Political Background Who Yet Served No Double-Agent Ends

After Yu Yefeng was put to death, tracing the matter back to its root, our superiors sent order upon order by wire that the progress of the sanction of Zhang Xiaolin be reported up in full and by the truth, to cut off after-trouble.

Zhang Xiaolin was a man of note in Shanghai, commonly called one of the three tycoons, a chief of the underworld; to call him the very representative of evil in that misshapen society would be no overstatement. A man of this stamp, could he turn to good, might become a hero of the greenwood; turning to evil, he sank into a great traitor and a mighty vermin.

Zhang Xiaolin was not to be compared with Yu Yefeng; he was ever on his guard. Since the Yu affair he had kept close within doors and gone seldom abroad; his dwelling was like a fortress, and when he stirred abroad he went with a train before and behind, so that to come near him at all was very hard, let alone to make any move against him.

The "Shanghai District," though it sent forth its two assistant secretaries to treat directly, each apart, with the heads of more than ten units and groups upon the chance of laying an inside line, could yet come by no concrete line; and so, for the time, there was in truth no way to make report. A thing of this sort brooks neither haste nor vexation; nor can one lightly speak a word and put it off with an empty answer.

In the midst of this blankness I thought of what Brother Hu Yongquan had told me: that Master Pan the Seventh, Mr. Pan Zixin, of great repute in Tianjin in years gone by, was now living hidden in Shanghai and no longer meddled in outside affairs. Mr. Pan in Tianjin was much as the three tycoons in Shanghai; only, this man had much of the scholar in him and set great store by the moral tradition of China, and by this one thing alone he was already far unlike them. We had no deep bond, yet each bore the other a goodwill. Hearing that I had come from Tianjin to Shanghai, he had had a mind to ask me to a plain meal and find some quiet place to talk; but he had never been at leisure, and so I had not yet met with him.

It was said that Mr. Pan Zixin had of old had dealings with Zhang Xiaolin; whether there was still any touch between them now was not to be known. Be that as it might, that Mr. Pan must have some understanding of Zhang was a thing one might be sure of. So I begged Brother Hu Yongquan to make a journey, first to bear my regards, and to fix a time that I too might go to wait upon him. As for the place, anywhere would do; let Mr. Pan appoint it.

Brother Yongquan brought back word that Master Pan the Seventh asked me to a plain meal at his house, the time chosen for half past five on the Saturday afternoon; for it was winter, and the sun set early, and by that hour the sky was already darkening. Master Pan the Seventh had charged him, moreover, with a special word: that he had asked two friends from the North besides to sit with us after the meal, and that they were all "of our own," and I might set my mind at ease. I was much puzzled—who again were these two friends?

On the Saturday it was Brother Yongquan who went with me; he had told me beforehand that he had another engagement he could not put off, and that when the time came he must go on ahead—he would not stay to the meal, nor come to fetch me back. As we went along the way, not far from my old dwelling on Route Doumer, the car came to the mouth of a lane on Route de Grouchy, and Brother Yongquan bade the driver stop, and signed to walk in with me. This lane on Route de Grouchy—I had of old come to it often, and often too at just such a dusk hour to take a short stroll, only to shun others' eyes and to avoid meeting an acquaintance. The mysterious person mentioned in the first chapter above—Fan Xing—it was in this lane that he lived.

Brother Yongquan led me past the door of Fan Xing's house, and I could not but turn my head to look a time or two more. Going further in, to the last house but one at the lane's end, Brother Yongquan pointed and said: "The Seventh Master lives on the third floor." Brother Yongquan led me up ahead, and the Pan household's servant had long since opened the door and stood waiting there.

When Mr. Pan Zixin and I had exchanged our courtesies, Brother Yongquan took his leave and went on first. In the room were only a master and a servant, and no other guest—this too, it may be, had been arranged beforehand. I took the chance, while none other sat by, to tell in short the course of my leaving Tianjin and going back to Chongqing to take a post and be trained, touching only, in the whole of it, upon the Hanoi Wang affair I was not free to raise. That I should thus give an account of myself was, of course, in the hope that Mr. Zixin too would disclose something of his own case.

Mr. Zixin spoke with much reserve. He first made plain his own stand—that he would on no account herd with the vile and do a thing to wrong his country; and after that he let fall too a thread of grief, that, lying hid in Shanghai, he had no more the strength to put anything forth. Truly, a tiger gone down from the mountain—this is what is meant by it.

In the talk I asked, with a purpose, one word: "Do you not go out and about?" Mr. Zixin answered: "For the present there are not many places worth going to; now and then I go to the Zhang house to play a round of ——-chess." This word I had not clearly caught, when the servant, standing by, broke the thread of talk with a "please come to table."

After the meal I much wished to ask again what the "——-chess" was that Mr. Pan spoke of; for the word above the "chess" I had not clearly caught—it seemed like "copper-chess"? But what then was "copper-chess"? It happened just then that the guests bespoken beforehand arrived again; and so Mr. Pan Zixin made us known to one another, and, hearing the names, I found they were none other than the two "Shanghai District" work-comrades Liu Shaokui and Liu Junqing.

The two Brothers Liu both held posts in the International Settlement police, and had been in Shanghai many years, and were, one might say, thoroughly versed in the ground of the International Settlement.

In the twenty-seventh year of the Republic they were drawn in by Brother Liu Fangxiong, the former secretary of the "Shanghai District," and took up the work; and in their help to the "Shanghai District," above all in the matter of safety and protection, they had in truth spared no strength, even to setting aside their own gain and hurt—so that I had long looked up to the two of them and held them in deep regard, only that I had never met them face to face.

As for why Mr. Pan Zixin, knowing full well that they and we were "of our own," should yet ask them to a meeting—at the time it was of course not seemly to ask; but when we had all fallen into talk, it came out that this good intent of Mr. Pan's was to "draw us the closer together" for me, in the hope that each might be a stay to the other.

Of this matter my remembrance is already much blurred; but for the former part, I saw in Brother Liu Shaokui's "memoir" a passage of this drift: "I had a telephone call from the elder Mr. Pan Zixin, bidding me and Junqing, after supper the next evening, come to his place for a talk. It was the first time I came to know Mr. Chen ——." And Brother Shaokui too much commended Mr. Pan, saying: "Master Pan the Seventh, a native of Jiangsu, kept the National Grand Hotel in Tianjin for many years; open-handed and free by nature, honest and true to his word, his one promise weighing as nine tripods, he was called the Du Yuesheng of Tianjin."

From this time, besides our work-tie, I and the two Brothers Shaokui and Junqing did indeed build up a friendship. In my term as District Chief, though the "First Group," which Bi Gaokui held besides his own charge, kept up the ordinary link with them, I asked Brother Zhang Zuoxing too to stand for me and keep a direct touch with the two Brothers Liu at all times—there was in this no private feeling, but only that in the handling of an urgent affair it made for dispatch.

My first wish had been to get at Mr. Pan Zixin's some little news of Zhang Xiaolin; and in the end I heard but half a phrase of no import. Happily there would be a next time, so let it wait a day or two. When I took my leave, Mr. Pan would by all means see me down the stairs; and after much yielding back and forth, it was still the two Brothers Liu who walked out of the Pan house along with me.

In the lane, passing again by Fan Xing's dwelling, I looked once more; there was no light within, and he had surely not yet come back. I had meant to go in and see him, but since he was not there, I gave over the thought. Reaching the lane-mouth, I parted from the two Lius and went straight home. And thinking of Fan Xing, my cares were stirred up again.

Fan Xing was now a nameless "communications man" of the "Shanghai District," charged expressly with the gathering of intelligence, and his showing was worth remark. His one fault was that, as of old, we could never make out his "source of intelligence." Our superiors had wired to inquire of it again and again, but no sure answer was ever to be found. It was for this very thing that I was troubled.

In the matter of Fan Xing I bore a share of the responsibility, for I had kept back part of the true state of things, and the stakes were great: should a misunderstanding be stirred up, or a slip occur, it would be a thing past all clearing by a hundred mouths, a guilt one could not bear. Fan Xing, also named Fan Jiman, had in the twenty-second year been a communications man of the "Beiping Station"; because he had a high line of intelligence, he was much prized by Mr. Zheng Jiemin, this Bureau's special commissioner for North China. Though it was suspected that his political background was tangled, still, by the way of "letting out a long line," he was borne with and put to weighty use. At that time the several principles Mr. Zheng Jiemin laid down were: "If it be money he is after, then what should be spent, spend without grudging; suppose he has besides some political background or international tie, do not fear it—that is the very line of work we could not ask better of; sundry points of doubt, be not in haste to seek an answer to them at once, but observe coolly and search with care; by all means give him no shock, that he may be kept steady, and best of all a private feeling be built up with him. Only, as for the intelligence materials he brings, they must be handled with all care." It was by following this direction that I dealt with Fan Xing. Afterward, through my dereliction and his going, somehow it came about that Fan Xing actually took over my post and was made chief of the "Beiping Station"—a thing quite past looking-for. And by the time I had undergone my punishment and been sent back once more to North China as chief of the "Tianjin Station," Fan Xing had already vanished none knew whither.

This time our meeting in Shanghai was wholly by chance; and, save that the course of it has been set down in full in the second chapter above, as to how he had left Beiping and come to Shanghai—was it after a transfer that he went, or was it his own doing?—these several months had still not been able to clear it up. I much wished he would tell me of it of his own accord; but he would hum and haw, and there must be some hidden thing hard to speak of. Wherein, then, lay the hardness? I believe this was his greatest secret. Upon this matter I had a supposal—grounded, of course, in reason—whose chief points were as follows:

First, since Fan Xing owned that he had not been sent by this Bureau, then he had left his post without leave. For of those who had been "station chief" in this Bureau, never was there one who threw it up in some dim and doubtful way—no, nor a common work-comrade either; which is to say that, whether one stayed or went, there must be a reckoning of it, and it was by no means possible to go wherever one had a mind to go.

Second, I held that his leaving without leave was a last resort forced on him under an irresistible pressure, wholly passive, with no free will in it at all. How so? Were it Fan Xing's own notion, he might well have made a pretext and nursed a chance, and had no need to take the way of flight and leave a trace behind. He himself must surely have understood that go he might, but that it could not be let end unaccounted for. Who, then, was it that put the pressure on him? And why? I say it was not any one man, but an organization—and this organization was the so-called political background.

Third, to search it further: what sort of organization was it that gave Fan Xing his orders and could make him obey to the letter? By the many signs seen of old in Beiping and now in Shanghai, and by the judgment drawn from what I saw with my own eyes "within the Dong'an Market of Beiping," it was very likely the Communist Party. If so, then was not Fan Xing a member of the Communist Party? This could not be wholly affirmed; one can but say "the component is very high," which is the more agreeable to logic. For one moved and placed by the Communist Party is not of necessity a party member. This is set by for the present, and cannot at this moment be answered: his coming to Shanghai—what, after all, was he doing there? Was it truly to open a secondhand bookshop and make his living by it? Or was there besides some secret activity we had not yet found out?

Fourth: at present, though he takes the terms and is willing to furnish intelligence, there is yet a train of questions hanging in doubt, as for example:

That he is willing to furnish intelligence—is it simply for the two hundred a month?

Where, after all, does he get his intelligence materials? Are they come by directly, or filched out of the midst of something?

If his intelligence should have a counter-effect, why is the greater part of it of reference value, now and then even of use value, and never a doubtful point found in it?

And, most important of all: his tie with us—is it his own doing, or is there someone behind directing him, or backing him?

For these questions, so hard to find an answer to, I and Brother Qingbin turned them over with the greatest care, and worked them again and again as one draws the thread from the cocoon; some, to be sure, could be explained through, but some in truth could not be fathomed. To speak of the source of his intelligence alone, it was by no means to be gathered by the ability of Fan Xing on his own; and if one must say he was under another's direction, then what was the aim of it? Was it a scheme, after winning a fair trust, to begin only then to pour in their own effect?

The conclusion I and Brother Qingbin came to in our working was to set out first from this one point—"whether or not Fan Xing's background is the Communist Party"—and set about finding a clue, and then to draw up some course of advantage to the work. And so we gave our heed to the doings of the Chinese Communists in the Shanghai region.

The Chinese Communists, as we then understood them, were thus:

About the time the war broke out, the leading body of the Chinese Communists in Shanghai, that is, the organization highest in place, was at first named the "Jiangsu-Zhejiang Committee," and a little later changed to the "Jiangsu Provincial Committee," in short the "Jiangsu Provincial Committee." Its secretary was Liu Xiao, its deputy secretary Liu Changsheng; and on the provincial committee were Zhang Aiping (military affairs), Liu Ningyi (labor movement), Wang Yaoshan (organization), Sha Wenhan (propaganda), Zhang Zhiyi (united front), and others. This form of organization lasted but a very short while; afterward the personnel changed often and fast, and by the time Shanghai fell there was, for a spell, a like disorder and each going his own way. Men often used to say the Communist Party was tightly organized; in truth it was nothing so very much.

The Chinese Communists in Shanghai did intelligence work too, several factions of them carrying it on there at once, so that even they themselves could not make out who was who. By what was understood, the more shaped among them, besides the one knot of the Chinese Communist Central's Shanghai office, were two small streams headed by Liu Shaowen and Pan Hannian. What their relation of subordination was—was it not still a matter of their own telling? One may say too: when something is made of it, all snatch at it; when a slip is made, they push it off on one another.

In the time of the war, the one active on the Shanghai scene was Pan Hannian's stream; and it was this very man who dealt with the Wang puppets' Special Operations Headquarters (No. 76) and had touch from time to time with Li Shiqun, Ye Jiqing, and the rest. And in the intelligence materials, the one whose name came up oftenest was still Pan Hannian.

At that time the Chinese Communists' intelligence work in Shanghai did not lay its weight on the gathering of materials for the increase of understanding of the other side, for they themselves had not yet the means of putting intelligence to use. In other words, even were an important secret told them, beyond a blank "so it is" and an "understood," it would work no effect. And their so-called intelligence work was no more than, by the strength of a single mouth, talking wild and loose, to lure into the net some few who were of present profit to them, and make of it a stock to swell their own strength. Failing that, it was to stir things a little here and rake them a little there—only let the water be muddied, and it was good fishing; while as for the whole nation's war of resistance, they seemed to have not the least concern with it. This is to speak of the Chinese Communists' intelligence work in Shanghai in the period about the outbreak of the war. By the twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth years the tricks grew more yet.

To say all this—what has it to do with the affair of Fan Xing? To prove a thing, one must of course begin from the root; was it not spoken of before? The intelligence furnished the "Shanghai District" through Fan's hand was not only of reference value, now and then even of use value—it was not a thing the Chinese Communists' Shanghai intelligence units of that day could bring forth; one may say too that all the intelligence materials the Chinese Communists had in Shanghai then did not come up to Fan Xing's level. This being so, it proved by contrast that Fan Xing did not get his from the Communist side; and it proved besides that neither was it the Chinese Communists using Fan Xing's hand for a double-agent's ends. Reasoning on, whether the one who directed Fan Xing from behind was the Communist Party grew not so certain after all; and thus my first supposal began to be shaken.

At the outset Fan Xing and I had an agreement to make a review after three months, and now was just the time.

This meeting of Fan Xing and me was set at a cigarette-and-paper shop (a small general store) in the middle stretch of Bubbling Well Road. To buy cigarettes here, or change small money, one could always stay three to five minutes; and if one were punctual and met no block of traffic on the way, one could surely keep the tryst.

We arrived, the two of us, within a minute of each other. Without a word, I took his hand and led him out of the little shop's door, walking toward the Jing'an Market. On the way I let go his hand and said to him: "Let us walk a few steps and find some out-of-the-way place, to say a few words." He was silent and made no answer, with a look of helplessness.

It was told in the second chapter of this book that Fan Xing went every day to a secondhand bookshop in the Jing'an Market, and that Fan Xing's lady-friend, Peng Yaluo, had shown herself there too. Wherefore we judged this secondhand bookshop very likely to be one he kept. Plainly it was not opened to do trade, but must have some other use. The reason I walked this way was of set purpose to lead him to the secondhand bookshop in the Jing'an Market, and, breaking through the outer shell first, to see what stir he would make, and what show.

This stretch of road was no great way; in two or three minutes we came to it. Only, one had to cross the street to reach the Jing'an Market on the other side. When I crossed first, it may have been my own fancy, but I always felt the color of his face somewhat unnatural. This market was not large in compass, and had ever been a place of little trade, not thriving; but for doing some hidden thing it was a good place indeed. The secondhand bookshop Fan Xing came to every day stood in the middle of the market, like a round pavilion, with doors on two sides and windows all round; pity was, it had no manner of adornment—perhaps he had no wish at all to draw custom.

Entering the market, and turning past two shop-fronts, one saw this secondhand bookshop. By a rare chance, at one glance I saw Peng Yaluo sitting within, reading a book, lost in thought. I came to myself; Fan Xing followed behind, and he in his heart understood well what it was about. Just then he took two steps ahead, hastened to the front, and hailed her: "Peng, look who has come!" Peng Yaluo laid down her book, bade me sit, and said: "We have nothing at all here, and have never entertained a guest; you and Jiman have your talk—I shall go buy a few things and come back." So saying she rose and went out; it was on a pretext to keep away, with no other meaning in it. Well and good—it was just convenient for our talk.

At this moment there were left in the shop but the two of us. He drew his body near, close beside me, and in a voice lowered as low as it could go said to me: "I do indeed have great need of money; two hundred yuan a month, though no great sum, is in truth of the utmost weight to me." Coming here he paused a moment, looked once more toward the door, and, still in the low voice, went on: "The things I give you were not only hard to come by, but are all of true material and real worth—set your mind at ease, they will never put you in a strait, still less bring you into trouble." And again he paused, seeming to cast about how to frame it aright, and then said: "I know well of course that what is reported up, the ones above must ask the source of; and this—how am I to put it fitly? Were I to tell you that I myself do not know either, could you believe it?" What—the things, whence they came, he himself did not know? This word was much worth turning over. I must ponder it with care.

Oh—could it be that he was a "keeper"? Or, failing that, perhaps a "handler."

Fan Xing and I had not yet finished our talk when Peng Yaluo came back, holding a packet of something in her hand, and, coming up before me, opened it and felt it too with her hand as if it were very hot: it was newly-out-of-the-oven "xieke huang" (a kind of small baked cake of Shanghai), a smell of scallion oil striking the nose. They would not have me hold off, and I must eat two; and eating, I asked the two of them: "Are you married?" The two looked at each other and smiled without answer. I pressed the question on close: "Since you keep two separate places of outlay, why do you not live together?" They still would not answer, but drew in their smiling looks. I thought in my heart: these two, whatever they were about, must surely not be happy.

We were at a stand a while, none of us opening his mouth; but the affair of me and Fan Xing must have some conclusion after all. I knew not what Fan Xing's thought was. Just then a thing of a sudden came to my mind, that I had all but forgotten clean away: was it not said there was a foreigner of no known nationality who often came here and had touch with Fan Xing? Then it were best to leave the sooner, lest we run upon him, to Fan Xing's discomfort and to my no gain either. I was just about to go out, when Fan Xing, as if of one mind with me, said too: "Let us go to a place nearby and have a cup of coffee." Good—we were of a mind without contriving it; then let us go.

We found a place and, seated, before I could open my mouth, he spoke first: "There are many things you need not ask; and if you would ask, what I can answer would not content you either. Let me say this: to you, Mr. Chen, I bear no ill will at all—I believe I never shall. It were best we still keep up our present tie; what I hope is that you will, so far as you can, add a little to the money and solve my hard case, and I will surely contrive to get you the things that are of use to you."

Strange to tell, I did believe all this speech of his was true. Though he kept back the whole true likeness, here and now, so long as the intelligence he furnished was of value and held no counter-effect, there was no need to press in haste for an understanding of it. Only, our superiors were forever pressing after the source of his intelligence: he might say "I do not know either," but I could not so answer our superiors—how was this to be solved? I asked Fan Xing again upon it, and he said: "As to the true source of each piece of intelligence, I do indeed not know it; and since you must needs ask me, then I have only to put on a false one. I think it better not to make a false one; by comparison, it is the more convenient for judgment."

Since Fan Xing spoke thus, there was nothing for it but to leave it to my own handling. We ended this talk; on the spot I promised him an increase of one hundred yuan a month, three hundred in all with the former sum, keeping still the old agreement to meet once or twice a week, and, for any question, to work it over when we met a few days later.

At the parting handclasp he gripped me tight and said: "I am a man of feeling; you may believe that word." Coming back, I and Brother Qi Qingbin again exchanged views upon the matter, and in our reckoning we had made this amendment:

That Fan Xing must surely have a political background, but not of necessity the Chinese Communists;

That the signs showed a possible tie with some international intelligence organization, only that active proof was still wanting;

And that this might be affirmed: the intelligence materials he furnished were by no means gathered directly, and so he was judged to be, if not a "keeper," then a "handler."

Down to the present, no attempt at any "double-agentry" had been found.

Wherefore the decision we came to was this:

To handle with all care the intelligence Fan Xing reported, and to add our notes of judgment the more closely, to make good the want of a clear source.

To devise some effective course within our power, and search out what his background truly was.

I have said long since that "the story of Fan Xing" has yet far to run; and, be he what manner of man he may, he was in truth "a man of feeling."

The sons and daughters of China, in the successive wars of the nation against outward wrong, have shown a spirit of sacrifice, and wrought deeds of valor, that are for the most part told over in the world and looked up to by those who come after. In the eight-year war against Japan there are, to be sure, many stirring facts that pass down to this day; only, on our "Juntong Bureau's" side, bound as it was by the nature of its own work, under the tradition of military secrecy and of doing without telling, it comes to this—that in the whole course of the war of resistance, just how many comrades gave their lives? and how did they give them? and who were they? and who again? For these questions, though we too have chest upon chest of files and roll upon roll of documents to search, no one can say they are without omission; and it is believed that those never so much as entered on register, of whom no record can be traced, are not a few. Set other units aside; in the "Beiping Station," the "Tianjin Station," and the "Shanghai District" alone, for which the writer had charge, there are instances past all counting—nay, some of them even I myself can no longer clearly remember.

The persons and affairs set down in this chapter are for the most part of this sort. Though the writer has had no chance to seek proof of them widely, nor to check them over once more against the parties concerned, yet this may already be affirmed: that of some part of them there is surely no detailed record; and even where there is a registered case, the circumstances set down, I believe, do not so surely tally without any divergence with the true course of events.

In the labor-movement activities of Shanghai too there were sacrifices; but ask a word—who were they?—and not so much as a name can be called out.

Or take the Xiao family: five joined the work, three of them saw the inside of a gaol, and two died for the country; yet there is no complete record.

A "used man" of the "New Group One" was seized and died a martyr in a work of action; and were it not that comrades who took part in it in those years have raised the matter, it is to be feared none would ever have known.

There were work-comrades too, missing many years, without a trace; and it turned out the man had, through a leak in the plan, actually been buried alive.

Or again, the puppet "Special Operations Headquarters," in a few months of the twenty-eighth year, murdered eight of our men; and of these there is a source for but three. And of the three, only one has his deeds in any fullness; the other two are but a drop and a trace.

The men of old said: "Death may be heavier than Mount Tai, or lighter than a feather"—and this is said by measure of a man's worth and his achievement. But the comrades of ours who gave their lives were not so; they reckoned neither name nor gain, but sought only to profit the nation and the race; and whatever the price of their death, all alike were ground to powder, body and bones, their livers and brains strewn upon the ground!
