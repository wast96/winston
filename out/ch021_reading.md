### Sitting in the Room

When you go to a third-grade house — a pheasant house, that is — or to a salt-meat house to pay a tea-visit, you must by custom give the girl a dollar before you may sit a while and trade a little banter. She sets out two dishes besides, one wet and one dry, and presses you to taste; the girls call this "sitting in the room," or else "laying out wet-and-dry." At the salt-meat houses, though, there is no wet-and-dry to eat: they give you nothing but a cup of plain tea.

If you pay your tea-visit at a second-grade house — a yao-er house, that is — it is called "moving the tea," and on leaving you must likewise give her a dollar. But go for your amusement to a first-grade house — a changsan house — and, if you are well enough acquainted, not only need you give nothing, they will even furnish you with cigarettes, sweets, and refreshments. The same tea-visit, and yet what a range of differences in it!
