## Chapter 53

We had eaten our meal past one o'clock, and afterward Mei Yingzi leaned back against the bunk. Seeing how weary she was, I urged her to sleep a while, and she lay down aslant and was soon asleep. I took out my very last cigarette; Cishan, watching me twice or three times move to smoke and then not, said:

"I'll go and buy you some presently."

I felt how absurd my own behavior was. I lit the cigarette, and began to feel a deep desolation and forlornness.

Just then a man with a filthy head and grimy face came craning and peering at the stern of the boat. I could not help a start of alarm; then Cishan's mother saw him and said to him:

"Here again — what do you want?"

The man made no sound at all, but drew back; the boat rocked and tilted a little, and he made his way along the gunwale to the bow, and sure enough showed himself at the bow. With a base, furtive look he glanced at the sleeping Mei Yingzi and then at me, and at last, stealing a look at Cishan's mother, said in a voice of utter piteousness:

"Second Auntie, spare me eight jiao more."

"I've none, none," said Cishan's mother.

"Just this once, Second Auntie — I'll never come to trouble you again."

"Why don't you go and ask your Third Uncle for it?"

"I can't find him." The newcomer's voice seemed almost to issue from a man being strangled; he said: "Even four jiao would do — take pity on me just this once."

"I've none, none," Cishan's mother said again.

Feeling on the one hand that the man was pitiable and on the other that he was odious, and wanting to be rid of him quickly, I took three or four dollars in small notes from my wallet, folded them into a little wad, and tossed it into the gap at the bow, saying:

"Take it, and make no more fuss."

"You needn't give it to him," said Cishan's mother.

As she said this, I saw that the man had already stretched a leg in to pick it up. He wore a blue cotton gown steeped in grease, its wadding hanging out below; he had no socks, and his blackened feet dragged a pair of shoes holed at toe and heel; he was worn thin as a skeleton, and the clothes on him were like withered leaves on a tree. As he picked up the money I saw the yellow-black nails on his shrunken hands; and at last, in the instant he took it up, I saw his face — the crust of tears and the crust of snot and the grease floating over it left me unable to make out his eyes and nose.

I thought he must surely be a taker of white powder; and just as I meant to look at him a moment longer, he had already snatched up the money, and without so much as raising his head, with a sidelong squint, stepped over the gunwale and was gone along the boat's side.

"There was no need to give it him," said Cishan's mother. "Give it him, and it only goes on white powder."

"Who is that?" I asked.

"He's a son of the eldest uncle — Bingfu, he's called," said Cishan's mother, sitting down. "He was a strong young fellow once, in his prime, and the family had a few mu of land too. When his father died he took to gambling and drink and brawling, and his mother would have no more of him. Later his Third Uncle found him a porter's job here, but he still would not mend his ways, and now he's turned into a wastrel, taken to white powder, and there's nothing to be done for him at all."

"And his mother?"

"In the country, well enough; the land doesn't quite keep her, and my household and his Third Uncle help her out now and then. It's a pitiful thing, a son gone to the bad; but she's set on having nothing more to do with him."

Then I asked her a little about the countryside, and about what she took from her fields and her boat, and I found the simplicity and goodness of her heart wholly of a piece with her kindly face; and at last she rose to busy herself with her chores.

Only now did I notice that Cishan had gone off while we were talking; where she had gone I did not know. Mei Yingzi lay on her side on the bunk, seemingly fast asleep; I could not see her face, only the hair I had cut and her curled-up form. In an instant I felt a myriad loneliness. I wanted to smoke, but the cigarettes were gone; I felt cold, and drowsiness came over me, and I yawned. At last Mei Yingzi turned over and slept peacefully again, and now I could see her face — very beautiful, very beautiful, yes, yes; she slept sweetly, like an innocent child. How different this was from her in the car, at Bai Ping's, at the Liti Café, at the Benner Inn, at Umetake's residence, and in all the other places. Was it this fringe over her brow, these silver rings at her ears, these countrywoman's black clothes and blue trousers, that had wrought so many changes in her? Or was there some other cause? Suddenly I thought of Bai Ping — how beautiful Bai Ping had been, asleep on the train back from Hangzhou; I had once made a few sketches of her, and one was very like, and I remembered tucking it in my wallet; later, while I was staying at her house, I seemed to have taken it out, and whether it got slipped into some book or what, in any case I never saw it again after that — and Bai Ping now? A grief like a welling spring poured up in me, beyond my power to master; I thought of how, the night before, Mei Yingzi had held her back, and why I had not held out more firmly. Perhaps, had I truly held out, Bai Ping might have heeded me; I was consumed with a remorse I could not face, and I hated myself. I did not know what to do. As I watched Mei Yingzi sleeping, I wanted at once to find the sketch I had drawn of Bai Ping, and though I knew well it had long since gone from my wallet, still I took the wallet out and went through it. Nothing, of course nothing; and ever since I had found it gone, I marveled that I had never drawn Bai Ping another, nor ever asked her for a photograph — but a photograph: it struck me suddenly that in all my time at Bai Ping's side, in her rooms, I had never once seen a single one. There was one — in the paper the day after she was shot, and that likeness was perhaps an old one, and not much like her; and now her voice and face seemed wholly wiped from the world, and what lived on in my heart was so abstract, and I had not one photograph of her. And — suddenly I looked at Mei Yingzi again; I had never seen any photograph of her either, and if she were gone, what should I have to hold to? I had an impulse to make a sketch of her, as I had drawn Bai Ping's likeness — but what had my state of mind been then? And what was it now? Never mind that the circumstances were wholly different; even were they wholly the same, I could not find that frame of mind. In these few months I had aged a great deal: before, whatever was past would often, in recollection, seem to be right before me, whereas now, when I looked back on things of a few months ago, it was utterly as though a lifetime lay between.

Why was this? Why?

In the midst of my wild imaginings, Cishan came back. She had in her hand two small packs of Great Britain cigarettes; but just as I was about to thank her for her kindness to me, I saw that her face was flushed red, her eyes darting in alarm, her mouth and nose panting, as if she wanted to speak and yet could not get the words out.

I said:

"What is it, Cishan?"

"What's the matter — don't be frightened, tell it properly," said her mother, pushing her and looking at her.

Then Cishan, faltering and rubbing her eyes with the back of her hand, said brokenly:

"I went out to buy cigarettes, and coming back, passing — passing over there, I saw Bingfu right there; he was telling people we had a guest here in a Western suit who'd given him four dollars. And then I heard them talking about us, so I stood behind a matting awning and listened a while, and just then I heard someone ask:

"'A man in a Western suit?' 'That'll be the one wanted in connection with today's blockade,' said a man with a hoarse throat.

"'Bingfu,' another called out, 'your chance to get rich has come — tip off the Japanese and there's a reward in it for you.'

"'Don't talk rot,' cried another. 'Tip them off wrong and it's you that gets the beating.'

"'I have it,' said the hoarse-throated man again. 'When the white-powder peddler comes tomorrow, have him take you along to lay the information. If it's right, you'll be rich — maybe you'll even get an official post.'

"'…' When I heard all this, I ran straight back."

Cishan finished and fell to sobbing again; for a moment I was at a loss, and Cishan's mother too seemed a little alarmed.

I went over and woke Mei Yingzi.

"I must have slept myself stupid," said Mei Yingzi, straightening her legs and rubbing her eyes.

I then passed on to her what Cishan had said, adding an account of this man Bingfu. Mei Yingzi only kept silent, her resolute gaze fixed on the roof of the awning, without a sound. I too was left standing there dazed, my mind in great confusion, with no calm consideration in it. But an impulse I could not suppress had hold of me, and I said:

"In any case, we had best get ready to go, and soon."

"That won't do." Hearing me say this, Cishan's mother came over; she seemed to have grown calmer, and said: "I reckon they'll not dare go and inform just yet, and the white-powder peddler doesn't come till tomorrow — you've time enough if you leave tonight."

"How do you know the white-powder peddler won't come again this afternoon?" I asked.

"When that fellow came begging money just now, it was precisely because he was in a hurry to go and buy white powder off the peddler," said Cishan's mother. "Once they've had their fill of white powder, they turn to wicked schemes. Don't you fret; I'll send Cishan now to go and talk it over with her Third Uncle."

"Her Third Uncle?" I began to feel uneasy.

"Set your mind at rest — he's a good man, and he'll surely help you." Then she called: "Cishan!" Cishan came, and she went on: "Go and look for your Third Uncle — most likely he's over at the shingle wharf; bring him here if you can, and if there's no one else on his boat, then tell him the whole of it, and have him think of a plan quickly."

"…" Still uneasy, I asked Mei Yingzi: "What do you think?"

"I think Cishan's mother surely understands her Third Uncle," said Mei Yingzi, turning a questioning look on Cishan's mother.

"Rest easy, rest easy," said Cishan's mother, and charged Cishan again: "If there are others over there, mind his boat for him, and have him come across first, and quickly."

So Cishan boldly and swiftly went up. I watched her back until it vanished in the gap of the boat's awning.

Then came a heavy, heavy loneliness again. On the table were the cigarettes Cishan had bought for me; I took them and opened them, giving to Mei Yingzi and to myself, and we smoked, no one with anything to say, awaiting in stillness the disposal of fate.

"Set your minds at rest, at rest," Cishan's mother went on comforting us.

Half a cigarette later, Mei Yingzi suddenly caught sight of the clothes beside her, and said:

"Why haven't you changed yet?"

The words recalled me to myself, and I set about changing. I tucked my suit trousers into my socks and pulled the blue cloth trousers on over them; then I took off my overcoat and jacket, undid my tie, put on the padded jacket, and last took the keys, handkerchief, and watch from my suit pockets and hid them in the pocket of the suit trousers, and put my wallet in my shirt pocket; then I belted the blue cloth trousers. I did not put on the padded trousers, for they had no pockets and seemed most inconvenient. When I had got myself up, I found I had no way to dispose of the pistol. When Cishan's mother was not watching, I asked Mei Yingzi in secret. She said:

"Watch for a chance and let it go to be a fish at the bottom of the river."

I laid the clothes I had taken off in a corner of the bunk; the pistol was still in the suit pocket. Last I took out Cishan's mirror and parted my hair down the middle, letting it hang slantwise in front. Two days' unshaved stubble lent me a fine finishing touch of itself.

I put on the cloth shoes, and felt the socks still would not do: black though they were, they were too new and trim, so I asked Cishan's mother for a little stove-ash and smeared it carelessly on the socks and scattered it on the shoes, and last I rubbed my face with my hand.

In an instant I no longer knew myself, and felt this was all very fitting. Mei Yingzi, watching, could not help laughing; she sprang up and worked the rest of the stove-ash onto her own clothes and hair, and smeared it on her own hands and face too; then she sat down by the table and began to cut her nails and scrape off her nail-lacquer, saying:

"Just so — tonight we must slip through the barrier, whatever comes."

When we had everything ready, our mood sank into stillness again. Time dragged on, but still Cishan did not come. I asked Cishan's mother:

"Could she have failed to find her Third Uncle?"

"She won't have," she answered me with assurance.

"Is her Third Uncle unloading cargo over there?" I asked purposelessly.

"He loaded up and came along with us, and after he'd unloaded, someone asked him to help out with a bit of unlicensed ferrying, running a few things across. He told us to go back first. I don't think he'll have gone far."

Whether or not her words were to be trusted, we had in any case to wait for Cishan to return; and even had we meant to act on our own, it was still too early now — so I lapsed into silence again.

I looked at my watch: it was already five o'clock. Mei Yingzi sat in great unease, and I told her to lean against the bunk again, and covered her feet with the cotton quilt; I felt the cold myself, and drew the overcoat over my knees once more. And so we waited in stillness as the time stole by.

In that instant it flashed on me — my dinner engagement with Miyama Yoshiko. Had Bai Ping heeded Mei Yingzi's words, she would not have died, and this was the very hour I should have been going to meet Miyama Yoshiko, and the world would have been utterly otherwise. Now, needless to say, I could not go to keep the engagement — and even had it been possible, I could not have gone; for from the moment Bai Ping was seized or killed, I was of course the very criminal they wanted. —

As I was thinking thus, Cishan came back in high excitement, and the moment she was aboard she ran up before Mei Yingzi. Perhaps because we had just come through a spell of peril, and perhaps too because Mei Yingzi's present get-up seemed to her more approachable, she was now quite without constraint, and said:

"Third Uncle will come presently; he says he can find a way for you."

By now Cishan's mother had come over too, and she asked:

"Did you find him?"

"Of course," said Cishan, smiling. "Two more crossings and he'll be done, and when he's done he'll come."

"Did you tell him everything?" her mother asked again.

"I told him roughly," said Cishan. "He says he can manage something."

"When you told him, was there no one else about?" I asked.

"No, of course not — rest easy." Cishan smiled and said to Mei Yingzi: "Now you may rest easy; Third Uncle will surely have a way to get you out."

"What kept you so long?" Mei Yingzi smiled.

"We waited for you in such anxiety."

"When I went, I couldn't find Third Uncle; Xiao Heizi said he was over on the far side, so I waited a while for him."

Now we began to wait in another frame of mind, a waiting that seemed brighter and more full of hope, but seemed too more keyed-up and anxious. Of the two packs of cigarettes Cishan had bought, one was nearly smoked up by us. The sky had darkened; layer on layer of gloomy grey cloud drifted by, and then a fine drizzle came sifting down, and the weather seemed bleaker and colder than before.

The sky darkened, darkened; across the water the lamps suddenly came on, and the oily black surface threw back their points of light; Cishan and her mother were busy with the meal. Mei Yingzi kept gazing out of the boat, while I watched the lamplight leaping at the bottom of the water, which seemed gradually, gradually to multiply, and glancing up now and then I saw that many boats too had lit their lamps. As I smoked, I lit as well the stub of candle set by the boat's side and brought it to the table; and just then a boat, a lamp hung at its bow, came steering up toward ours and slowly drew alongside. I was somewhat alarmed, but in the instant it closed with us the man aboard suddenly called out: "Cishan, Cishan."

Cishan ran over in excitement, and said:

"Third Uncle's come." So saying, she went to the bow to meet the boat, and before long sprang across, and after saying who knew what few words within, she came back and called us over to her Third Uncle's boat. Cishan's mother went over too then, and Cishan said to her mother:

"Third Uncle says he has a way — he can take them across right now."

"I've cooked the meal already," said Cishan's mother. "Better eat before you go."

"No," I said. "The sooner we can go, the better we go."

Mei Yingzi had risen by then and was about to go across; she said:

"Goodbye, granny; the kindness you've shown us I shall repay you one day, without fail." Then to Cishan: "You've been so good — I hope I may see you again."

Cishan just then was holding the mooring rope of her Third Uncle's boat; the one hailing us from across it was a boy of fifteen or sixteen, and her Third Uncle was still at the tiller over there. Mei Yingzi, carrying her handbag, sprang across; I, carrying the clothes and the overcoat, said:

"Goodbye, granny; Cishan — one day I'll come and see you."

"Goodbye," said Cishan, bashful and smiling. "You must come and see us with that young lady — you can ask Third Uncle the name of the place."

So I too sprang across; but just then Cishan's mother suddenly came hurrying after us, saying:

"Wait, wait — there are still the young lady's clothes." And so saying she took Mei Yingzi's clothes and handed them to Cishan.

Mei Yingzi saw the clothes. She said:

"I'll take none of these — Cishan, keep them as a keepsake."

The Third Uncle, at the stern over there, had all this while said nothing to us. But now he suddenly said, gravely:

"Cishan, better bring all these across."

His seasoned, grave voice left us not knowing what he was really about, unable to answer; Cishan had already handed the clothes to the boy of fifteen or sixteen, and her mother passed over Mei Yingzi's leather shoes too, and she took them and handed them to the boy across the way.

And so we hastily thanked and took our leave of Cishan and her mother, and Cishan cast off the line.

As we stepped into the boat's cabin, her Third Uncle had already come inside; the boat rocked a little and drifted slowly to midstream, a lamp flickering on its wall, and very dim. By that dim light I looked at Cishan's Third Uncle's face: he was perhaps forty-odd too, of a very burly, solid build, with thick brows, big eyes, and lips pressed tight, without a trace of a smile. He said:

"Now you may call yourselves entirely safe. I can take you across there; once past four bridges you can go ashore, and across the road is the back door of a tailor's shop, where I have a friend. But go out the front door and there's the Japanese cordon of ropes. I can go with you as far as the tailor's, and have them give up the second-floor room to you; after that I'll go, and you can watch from the window, and when there are no Japanese soldiers passing to and fro, open the door and cut across."

"Splendid — thank you for saving us," I said.

"But," said the other, still cool and firm, "I think I may speak plainly: how much are you willing to pay?"

"Money?" said Mei Yingzi, looking at me — and her meaning was quite clear to me: that morning she had given several hundred dollars to Cishan's mother, and there was little money left now in her handbag.

"If it's a matter of talking price," I said, "then name it, friend."

"Twenty thousand."

"Not dear," I said. "But I have only a little over six hundred dollars on me — unless you'll take my clothes and things." And with that I drew the suit lying on my right over toward me.

"That's no way to do business," he said.

"Then do you mean to hand us over to the Japanese?" As I asked it I began to think I ought to defend myself with the pistol.

"You think too little of me there," said the other, still cool and firm. "I'm a Chinese — why should I sell you to the enemy? Here, to tell the truth, your lives are all in my hands; there's no need of the enemy to do you harm. If it were only for money, were I to hand you to the enemy it would be a good deal more than twenty thousand, would it not?" And suddenly he let out a mocking little laugh: "What we're discussing now is only business."

"But to demand of a man in his hour of peril a payment beyond his means — that is extortion," I said.

"Then, as you please," he said. "Go ashore by yourselves."

"That's as good as sending us into the tiger's jaws," said Mei Yingzi, suddenly rousing herself and saying gravely: "I think you had better simply tie us up and deliver us to the enemy — for us it's the same death, and you may make yourself a fortune; from the nation's standpoint it might be more worth the doing. And I think that with the money in hand you'd not, like your nephew, spend it on their poison."

Mei Yingzi's voice was not loud, but very certain; and as she spoke, my hand was already in the suit pocket at my side, gripping my pistol. But when her words were done, the other seemed blunted by the edge of her speech, and for a long while did not speak. Mei Yingzi kept watching him with her shining eyes, but now, quick as lightning, she shot her gaze at me; her brows went up, she sprang to her feet, and in a commanding, almost fierce tone said to me:

"Don't draw the gun — we'll let him bind us and take us. Since this too is the will of the Chinese people, let him go and make his fortune."

I was a little flustered, but grew calm at once; still, slowly, I drew out the pistol, and as I passed it over I said, low and cool:

"Friend, she is not wrong: on the Japanese side our lives are worth at least two hundred thousand. But you are Cishan's uncle, and she saved our lives, and we have not yet repaid her; so if you make your fortune, do not forget that these lives were saved by Cishan, and you must give her at least the half." I laid the pistol at last before him, and said: "Here is the proof. It was I — I am the chief culprit in the small-hours affair on Youheng Road; it was I — I am Bai Ping's confederate; it was I — I killed one of their men…"

"You? You?… You?" The other's thick brows knitted slightly, his big eyes went round, and a smile came to the corners of his mouth; slowly he stood, put out his two great coarse hands, and laid them heavily on my shoulders, saying: "It was you! Then we are our own people." And suddenly he turned his head smartly and called:

"Xiao Heizi, cast off, quick!"

It turned out Xiao Heizi had all this while been holding the tiller steady, and now, at a word of assent, the boat began slowly to sway and rock.

Mei Yingzi and I were both dumbstruck for a moment; Cishan's Third Uncle sat down again and said:

"Sit, please sit."

For an instant I did not know whether it was fright or joy; shaken and bewildered by this turn of events, I sat down, and only after a good while recovered a little of my reason, and said:

"But I still don't know — was Bai Ping wounded and taken? Or is she already dead?"

"Dead! She's most surely dead!" said Cishan's Third Uncle mournfully. "We've had people who saw her body!"

"Do you know how things stand at her house?"

"I don't," he said. "There's been no word yet, and the papers say nothing of it either." So saying, he drew a newspaper from inside his coat, and Mei Yingzi and I snatched it to look: it was the such-and-such Evening News, and in the local-news column was a headline in seven-point type:

"Bai Ping is Dead!" — and then, in the largest type, a subhead:

"American Spy, a Noted Dancer, Shot Resisting Arrest on Youheng Road" — and below it this account:

"The noted Paramount dancer Bai Ping, lately discovered by the Japanese side to be a spy in the employ of the American navy, had long been under surveillance. About five this morning, as Bai Ping went to Youheng Road on her work, she was detected by Japanese secret agents who were about to arrest her, when unexpectedly, from a distance, a confederate of Bai Ping's opened fire, and one agent fell dead on the spot. The other agents then also fired, striking Bai Ping in a vital place, and she too fell dead. Police whistles shrilled; Bai Ping's confederate fled by car and got away midway, the car exploding of itself, the number said to be forged too, and after the blast so blurred that its origin could not be traced. It is heard that the Japanese side is pursuing inquiries, that the scene is now wholly sealed off, and that the residents can none of them come or go."

This report was not wholly accurate, and made no mention at all of the state of Bai Ping's lodgings — a thing the enemy would by no means let pass. Neither Mei Yingzi nor I spoke then, but the same swell of feeling moved in our hearts; Bai Ping's death was borne in on me once more, an unspeakable grief surged in my breast, and, leaning back, I sighed deeply and could not help murmuring low:

"Bai Ping is truly dead!"

Cishan's Third Uncle looked at me sorrowfully, and said:

"It's rather absurd, their taking Bai Ping for an American spy."

"They must have confused her with someone," I said. But Mei Yingzi was making eyes at me, and so I said no more.

Cishan's Third Uncle rose, as if he too meant to go and steer the boat. I stopped him and, taking out my wallet, said:

"Take these six hundred dollars from me first, and I'll send you more by and by."

"Nonsense," he said. "Between our own people, do we talk of this? It's my duty."

"But it's only to show our private thanks."

Still he would not take it, and at last I said:

"Then, please keep my pistol and my overcoat and clothes. Let them be my keepsake to you."

"I can't," he said. "I can by no means accept them."

"But the fact is I can't carry them either — carrying them is only an encumbrance."

"Then I'll keep the pistol," he said, taking it up in his hand; "as for the clothes, tell me the place, and I'll have them sent to you in a day or two."

"Do you suppose I have any fixed place now?" I said. "It's impossible."

He put the pistol away and thought a moment, then suddenly said:

"Then leave them at my place. My home is at — ah, I'll write out an address for you, and you can come and find me by and by."

"I've been wanting your address," said Mei Yingzi. "One day I must certainly go and see Cishan."

"But on no account tell her my work," said Cishan's Third Uncle. "None of them knows of it." So saying, he took out a pencil and searched along the boat's side for paper to write the address.

"When I say 'one day,' I fear it must be after the enemy is driven off; naturally I'll say nothing of it to her. But my clothes and shoes — I'd meant them for Cishan; why must you have me carry them?"

"I'll carry them for you, that's all," said Cishan's Third Uncle. "It's very dangerous to leave them on their boat. I think that if someone informs tomorrow, the enemy will surely go and search."

These words much surprised me; I felt sure he must be a man fine and quick in his work, and Mei Yingzi too was surprised just then, for she was praising him:

"You really are too good. I hope we may be friends forever after this." So saying, she took the little money there was in her handbag and, tossing the bag in with the clothes, said again: "Please take this too to Cishan." Then she took the other's address; I vied to see it — his hand, though not very practiced, was quite clear. Having given the address to Mei Yingzi, he rose and went to the stern to steer the boat.
