## Chapter Eleven. The Wild Swan

### 1. The Young Master

"Xiaokai" — the young master — was Shanghai's loose word for a rich man's son, slang that ran through the city in the 1930s. It came out of a whole gallery of street labels. The maidservant of a great house was a niangyi; a man in freakish clothes and loose manners an afei, a young tough; a jobless drifter who lived by begging or petty theft a biesan; a garishly turned-out streetwalker with her sash worn slack — a lasan, the local word for a shameless "fringe girl" who wants it all — a "garbage cart"; an idle neighborhood bully who battened on the little people a baixiangren, a street-corner tough. And the "housewife" (jiazhupo, Wu dialect for a man's wife, jia sounded like ga) of an "Uncle Binggen" — the street-corner tough of the beloved Shanghai farce The Seventy-Two Tenants — or a shrewish "tigress" of a second landlord who sublets her rooms, was a baixiangren saosao, a tough's old woman.

The xiaokai gave off airs — chige (Shanghainese for swagger), laoqie (throwing his weight around, qie sounded jia), biaojin (looking down on everyone) by the bucketful — the very picture of a smart-set dandy playing the daluolao, the man of consequence, all pomade and Western suit and rakish charm and lofty carriage. He knew everyone and dabbled in everything, and knew none of it well: not a full bottle but a half-full one, sloshing loudly. He was, as the old jibe has it, "the reed on the wall, top-heavy and shallow-rooted; the bamboo shoot on the hill, sharp of tip and thick of skin and hollow within."

The old-Shanghai xiaokai had four unmistakable marks: he was xiangege (Shanghainese for self-inflating, playing to the crowd), shendoudou (all pose and overstatement), shoudada (a fool who could not read the room and loved to make a spectacle of himself), and yehuohuo (unreliable, worse than useless, wrecking whatever he touched).

Most xiaokai were wastrel sons, pretty boys, "hollow grandees" (kongxin dalaoguan, Shanghainese for all show and no substance). They had a little family money and no capacity whatever to manage it or to make their own way in the world. Their one great talent was sponging off their parents, drinking and dreaming their lives away, sitting idle until the mountain was eaten bare.

Pan Hannian's own fiction is full of the type. Fei Yun, at least, the hero of his early novel The Bitter Cup, carries a whiff of the xiaokai about him, a touch of gold on the outside and rotten wadding within. A xiaokai like that could only inhabit a "world of darkness," drinking his fill of the "bitter sacrament" of "despair" and "sorrow."

And yet "Xiaokai" became, in the end, Pan Hannian's own code name — his name in the Shanghai literary world, his name in Party work, his name in the Central Special Branch, his name on the hidden front — just as "Mister" stood for Chen Yun and "the Boss" for Kang Sheng.

After Pan came from Yixing to Shanghai, he gradually took to wearing Western dress. He had no taste at all for the long gown and mandarin jacket, and would not put on the Zhongshan tunic that others were apt to denounce as "going red." The Western suit was a mark of his, and it served him well in the years of struggle against the enemy that lay ahead; on its account, people who did not know him took Pan Hannian for a capitalist's son. And so it was, in that tingzijian, in those rather threadbare days, that the name "Xiaokai" was pinned on him. Pan did not mind. He let it stand, and even signed his articles, quite openly, "Xiaokai," "Kai," "Little K," "K"; in time it went so far that on the reports he sent up to Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai, and to the Comintern and the CCP Center, he would put the code name "Little K." (Zhang Yun, 2017)

Pan Hannian was born in 1906, a native of Yixing in Jiangsu. The Pan clan of Guijing was a great family there, but by the time Pan was born it had already come down in the world; beyond the lingering air of a scholarly house, it owned nothing solid. Ever after, whenever he filled out a personal history, Pan Hannian never left off the two words "fallen gentry."

In 1925 Pan Hannian left his home country for Shanghai, and there, sponsored by Ruan Zhongyi and Wang Bi, he joined the Chinese Communist Party.

In 1927 Pan Hannian put down the pen for the sword, serving in Nanchang as editor-in-chief of the Revolutionary Army Daily and in Wuhan as compilation-section chief in the propaganda department of the National Revolutionary Army's General Political Department.

In 1928 Pan Hannian returned to Shanghai and founded the weekly Battle Line.

In 1929 the CCP Central Propaganda Department set up a Cultural Work Committee — the Central Cultural Committee for short — and Pan Hannian became its secretary.

In 1930 the China League for Freedom was founded, and Pan Hannian was elected to its executive and made secretary of its Party group. When the League of Left-Wing Writers was founded, Pan was secretary of its Party group. When the China League of Left-Wing Cultural Circles — the Cultural League for short — was founded, Pan was secretary of its Party group.

In 1931 Pan Hannian was transferred to head the propaganda department of the CCP's Jiangsu Provincial Committee. That May he was moved again, to be chief of the intelligence section of the Central Special Branch and a member of the Central Special Commission.

From then on Pan Hannian seemed to vanish. His friends could hardly catch sight of him. If they ran into him somewhere by chance, he would only nod, give a small smile by way of greeting, exchange a few words at most — all so unlike his old self, who was always the life of the talk, a fountain that would not stop. If they met him in the street, he looked through them like a stranger and hurried on. Lou Shiyi, who had once worked in the same branch cell as Pan Hannian, said as much: "Hannian was by then a full underground worker. There was no longer anywhere to go and chat with him. Nobody knew what he was up to. In a word, it was all very secret." (Yin Qi, 1996)

The sense that it was "all very secret" was not Lou Shiyi's alone; Xia Yan felt it too. Xia Yan had "not seen Pan Hannian for five or six months" when Pan asked to meet him at a hotel. Xia Yan asked where he had been; Pan was evasive, and gave no "straight answer."

It was around the summer of 1931. Pan Hannian, whom I had not seen for five or six months, reached me through a bookshop and asked me to meet him in a room at the Juelu Hotel, where we talked from four in the afternoon until dusk. To begin with I told him something of how the "Left League" and the "Dramatists' League" were faring, all of which he seemed already to know. So, naively, I asked him where he had got to in this stretch of time. He said he had gone nowhere, only changed his post. I asked again what work he was doing, and this he would not say. (Xia Yan, 2006)

It was not that he "would not say," but that he could not — like Elisa in Andersen's tale. For little Elisa, in The Wild Swans, is forbidden to speak. Let her utter a single word and the eleven brothers whom the queen's magic has turned into swans will die. By now Pan Hannian was "a full underground worker," and Chen Yun wanted the work of the Central Special Branch driven deep into society and sealed off entirely from the Party's other organizations.

In the past the Special Branch's organization and its personnel had not been strictly walled off within the Party; when Chen Yun took charge of the Branch, this was the first thing he changed. On the principle of few but choice, he reorganized the inner structure. Leading cadres already compromised to some degree, who should no longer do secret work — Li Qiang, Li Kenong, Chen Geng and others — he moved out of Shanghai; at the same time he abolished the Fourth Section, handing the wireless-communications work to the Central Secretariat, and pared the remaining three sections down. Chen Yun took the First Section himself, Pan Hannian the Second, Kang Sheng the Third. He changed the Branch's way of operating too, requiring every worker to have a real, settled occupation in society, so that he could move among people, build relationships through that ordinary life, and use those relationships to cover the Branch's work. And he tightened precautions, insisting on single-line contact and strict limits on any dealings between one worker and another. (Party Literature Research Office of the CCP Central Committee, Chen Yun Study Group, 2018)

With the Special Branch's structure and methods thus overhauled, Chen Yun, Pan Hannian and the rest turned their energies to the question of how to revive and expand intelligence work.

The work of planting agents inside the enemy to gather intelligence had stalled after Gu Shunzhang's defection. To keep the CCP Center safe, it had to be rebuilt, and fast. Pan Hannian played the openings as they came. He sent two early Party members — Shen Shouya, code-named "Old Gun" and once secretary of the CCP's Jiading County Committee, and Mei Gongbin — to work their separate ways toward Wu Kaixian, a hard man of the Kuomintang's CC Clique and head of the organization department of the Shanghai party headquarters, hoping to prise an opening out of him.

### 2. From the Shanghai War to the Fujian Revolt

Mei Gongbin (also Mei Dianlong), whom Pan Hannian ran directly, had organized students in the days of the Great Revolution and was on good terms with Wu Kaixian. After Wu Kaixian went over to the Kuomintang and became organization chief at the Shanghai party headquarters, he and Mei Gongbin kept up their friendship. In 1931 the Center decided that Mei Gongbin should be formally linked to the Central Special Branch, run at first directly by Pan Hannian, and told to draw close to Wu Kaixian and learn what he and the Shanghai party headquarters were planning by way of "anti-Communist" work. Later, through a magazine he ran, Mei came to know the generals of the Nineteenth Route Army and became a political officer in it. Wu Kaixian, for his part, wanted Mei to report the movements of the Nineteenth Route Army's commanders. During the "January Twenty-Eighth" Shanghai War, it was through Mei Gongbin that our Party held a round of talks with the Nineteenth Route Army about joint resistance to Japan. Pan Hannian spoke for the CCP, Xu Minghong for the Nineteenth Route Army, and the two sides reached an accord to resist Japan together. But with Chiang Kai-shek withholding his support, our own Party hampered by its "Left" line, and the upper ranks of the Nineteenth Route Army wavering, the army withdrew from Shanghai in the end. Afterward, with the organization's consent, Mei Gongbin kept up his ties with its generals.

Xu Minghong — courtesy name Yuyi, sobriquet Aoxiang — was born in 1897, a native of Fengshun in Guangdong. He graduated in 1919 from the Chinese department of the Beijing Higher Normal College. In the spring of 1926 he too put down the pen for the sword, returning to Guangdong to join the National Revolutionary Army as director of the political department of the Tenth Division of Li Jishen's Fourth Army. He marched north with the army into Hunan, took Wuhan, then went north again into Henan, and there secretly joined the Chinese Communist Party.

I had a student, the closest of all my followers, named Huang Genyong. Huang Genyong was a Guangdong man; Xu Minghong was a Guangdong man too, and it was Huang who introduced Xu Minghong to me. Xu Minghong, Huang Genyong, Wang Pingshu — the three of them all went to Guangdong to join the National Revolution and followed the Northern Expedition army to Wuhan. At Wuhan the three parted ways. Xu Minghong joined the Communist Party, and later died for the revolution, gave his life for the revolution. (Liang Shuming, 2010)

On June 10, 1931, Chen Mingshu flew into Ganzhou and wired his acceptance of the post of commander-in-chief of the right-wing army group of the "bandit-suppression" forces in Jiangxi. The next day, massing his troops at Ji'an, he set up the Nineteenth Route Army under Jiang Guangnai as commander-in-chief, Cai Tingkai as army commander, Dai Ji as chief of staff, and Xu Minghong as secretary-general; in late November it took up quarters in Shanghai.

On January 28, 1932, the Shanghai War began. Cai Tingkai, in high righteous temper, proclaimed to the world through the China Weekly Review: "Every inch of this ground is stained with Chinese blood, and we will not lightly give it up, hand it over to the Japanese for nothing. So long as one man and one bullet are left to us, we will hold this place. We fight for independence. Even if Japan's design of seizing Shanghai should for a moment succeed, and we fall back to the banks of the Yellow River, that will still not be the end of the war. The final victory must be ours!"

But Cai Tingkai's mind was not Chiang Kai-shek's mind. The Nineteenth Route Army swore to live or die with Shanghai; Chiang, meanwhile, "brooded day and night," thinking only that "to fight, there were no conditions for fighting; to make peace, the nation would be against it." And so whenever anyone opposed his doctrine that "to resist the outside one must first pacify within," he would rain abuse on them as "a people without knowledge, without cultivation," who "could not tell right from wrong or gain from harm" and "had nothing to do but wait for ruin."

It was this that gave Chen Mingshu and his fellows the idea of turning their coats on the eve of battle, of forsaking the dark for the light. They "told Mei Gongbin of their intention to launch a military revolt against Chiang," and invited him "to go to Fujian and train cadres for the Nineteenth Route Army." (Mei Gongbin, 1994)

They also had Mei Gongbin arrange for Xu Minghong to negotiate with Pan Hannian, to lay joint plans for resisting Japan hand in hand.

Not long after Chiang Kai-shek stepped down he came storming back, with Wang Jingwei's help, and signed the humiliating Shanghai Ceasefire Agreement with the Japanese imperialist invaders. By then Sun Ke's government had fallen, Chen Mingshu had been forced to announce that he was going abroad, and Chiang, at the invaders' demand, moved the Nineteenth Route Army out of Shanghai. What Chiang did enraged the officers and men of the Nineteenth Route Army. Cai Tingkai, filled with righteous fury, pounded the table and cried, "North to fight Japan!" But Chiang would not let the Nineteenth Route Army stay in Shanghai, nor let it go north to fight the Japanese; he ordered it south to Fujian to fight the Red Army instead. The sharpening antagonism drove Chen Mingshu and the others to plot a military revolt against Chiang. Seeing all this, I grew all the more confident of winning the Nineteenth Route Army over. Before it left Shanghai, I arranged for Cai Tingkai's representative, Xu Minghong, to meet Pan Hannian. That crucial meeting laid the ground for Cai Tingkai's later truce talks with the Red Army in Fujian. (Mei Gongbin, 1994)

Late May 1932 was the watershed at which the Nineteenth Route Army passed from "for Chiang" to "against Chiang." Bao Jinyuan, son of Bao Wenwei, relates that Shen Jian — grandson of Dai Ji (then Shanghai garrison commander), and a professor and doctoral supervisor in the history department of East China Normal University — told him that before the Nineteenth Route Army pulled out of Shanghai it raised a stone tablet "to declare its heart" in the great hall of headquarters. The inscription, in Dai Ji's own hand, denounced "in anger" the Shanghai Ceasefire Agreement: it banned all resistance to Japan, made Shanghai a demilitarized zone, pulled the Nineteenth Route Army back, forbade China to station troops anywhere from Shanghai to Suzhou and Kunshan — and yet let Japan garrison Shanghai.

After the ceasefire, with his own crack divisions massed along the Nanjing–Shanghai and Shanghai–Hangzhou lines, Chiang Kai-shek resolved to bring the "insubordinate" Nineteenth Route Army to heel and first move it away from the Nanjing–Shanghai region. Bent on breaking it up, he ordered on May 6 that one division go to Jiangxi under the Nanchang Field Headquarters, one to Wuhan under the Wuhan Field Headquarters, and another to Anhui, while the army's general and headquarters staffs were to stay for the time being in Nanjing. But Jiang Guangnai and Cai Tingkai, backed on all sides and buoyed by their men's unwillingness to fight a civil war, argued their case on its merits though the reactionaries pressed them harder, and would not obey the order to carve the army up. Chiang had no choice in the end but to send the whole force to Fujian. (Li Yikuang, 1963)

Once in Fujian, the Nineteenth Route Army again "negotiated a truce with the Red Army." The purpose of the "talks" was still to "resist Japan." The plenipotentiaries on the two sides were, as before, Xu Minghong and Pan Hannian.

In October 1933 Xu Minghong and Chen Gongpei (Wu Ming) traveled together to Ruijin in Jiangxi. Chen Gongpei was an early Party member too: in August 1920, together with Chen Duxiu, Li Hanjun, Yu Xiusong, Shi Cuntong (Shi Fuliang), Chen Wangdao, Shen Xuanlu (Shen Dingyi), Li Da and others, he had founded one of the earliest Communist cells at the editorial offices of New Youth in Shanghai. That same year he went to France on the work-study program. In 1924 he entered the second class at Whampoa, served as deputy director of the political department of the National Revolutionary Army's Fourth Army, took part in the Nanchang Uprising, and afterward lost contact with the Party.

No sooner had Xu Minghong and Chen Gongpei reached Ruijin than they were warmly received by Mao Zedong, Zhu De, Zhou Enlai, Lin Boqu and the other chief leaders of the Provisional Central Government of the Chinese Soviet Republic, the Central Revolutionary Military Commission, and the First Front Army of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army. Mao Zedong, Zhu De, Zhou Enlai and Lin Boqu all said the CCP wholly supported the "Fujian Provincial Government" and the Nineteenth Route Army in resisting Japan and opposing Chiang.

On October 26 the "Preliminary Agreement on Resisting Japan and Opposing Chiang, between the Provisional Central Government of the Chinese Soviet Republic and the Workers' and Peasants' Red Army on the one side and the Fujian Government and the Nineteenth Route Army on the other" took effect on the initialed signatures of Pan Hannian and Xu Minghong. It laid down that "both sides shall at once cease military operations"; that "the Fujian Provincial Government and the Nineteenth Route Army shall, on the principles of this agreement, issue a declaration against Chiang and at once prepare military action against Japan and against Chiang"; and that "the two sides shall, within the shortest possible time, conclude a further, detailed operational agreement against Japan and against Chiang."

On November 20, 1933, the Nineteenth Route Army launched its anti-Chiang Fujian revolt. The "People's Revolutionary Government of the Republic of China" acclaimed Li Jishen as chairman; Mei Gongbin, on behalf of the presidium of the provisional delegates' congress, handed Li Jishen his seal of office and gave an address, and the whole hall took up the cry: "Down with Chiang Kai-shek!" "Down with the Nanjing government that sells the country and ruins the people!" "Down with Japanese imperialism — recover the lost land of the Northeast!"

On January 5, 1934, 150,000 of Chiang's troops marched into Fujian to give battle. Outnumbered, the Nineteenth Route Army fell back step by step. Yanping, Shuikou, Gutian and Fuzhou were lost one after another, and the Fujian-revolt government dissolved in gloom. The "Republic of China" passed into history as a name, having lasted just fifty-three days.

### 3. Our Own Man

After the "January Twenty-Eighth" Shanghai War of 1932, Chen Yun left the Central Special Branch to become Party-group secretary of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, and Kang Sheng took over the Branch. From the second half of the year Pan Hannian led it, and together with Xia Caixi, Wang Zichun and Xie Dezhao he set up a Special Branch Committee.

During his time "leading the Branch," Pan Hannian was in close touch with Yang Du, and strengthened that intelligence tie.

Yang Du — born Chengzan, courtesy name Xizi, later renamed Du, with the sobriquets Hugong and Huchan and the further names Huchanshi, Hutuotuo and Shihu — was born in 1874, a native of Xiangtan in Hunan.

Yang Du first made his name by lending his voice to the "Gongche petition," following Kang Youwei, Liang Qichao and their circle in pressing for a constitutional monarchy. Just because he held that only "reform" could save China, he could bring Sun and Huang together — introducing Huang Xing to Sun Yat-sen — and yet never endorse Sun Yat-sen's republican revolution. As he put it: "I stand for constitutional monarchy; if my cause succeeds, I hope you will help me; you call for a national revolution, and if yours succeeds, I will throw over my own program to help you. The work of the nation lies in this present day — let us not stand in each other's way." It was because the conviction that "if China does not abolish the republic and set up a monarch, there is no hope of a strong country, no hope of a rich one, no hope of a constitution, and nothing at the last but ruin" had bitten so deep into him that, when Yuan Shikai moved to restore the throne, Yang Du joined Sun Yujun, Yan Fu, Liu Shipei, Hu Ying, Li Xiehe and others in founding the Chou'an Society, making it his business to "urge Yuan on," and fervently preached "away with the sham republic and on to a true constitutional monarchy" — for which Yuan Shikai hailed him as "a rare talent of the age."

But that arch-schemer of his age, crying at the last "Yang Du has ruined me," let go of the world; and Zhang Taiyan, weighing what had doomed the Hongxian monarchy, offered his own verdict of "three men against three men," holding that "Liang Qichao's opposing Yang Du" was the crux of the crux. So it was that on the wanted list of monarchy's chief culprits issued by the incoming president, Li Yuanhong, Yang Du's name stood first. Yang Du, hounded and homeless as a stray dog, could do nothing but retreat into the cloister, to Buddhist study and Chan meditation.

After 1927 Yang Du lived in retirement in the French Concession of Shanghai, moving among the city's power brokers. A certain great notable had once presented Yang Du with a disciple's card and called him teacher, and Yang was glad enough to draw a stipend from him day by day. In truth the old man's ambition was not dead; in secret he was still taking part in political affairs. Yang Du had a younger fellow-townsman, one Wang, who was kin to Chen Geng — a native of Xiangxiang County in Hunan. Wang, in his dealings with Chen Geng, had come to some understanding of the Chinese Communist Party, and in Yang Du's presence he had let slip, now by design and now by accident, his connection with Chen Geng. Far from being alarmed, Yang Du told Wang to arrange a meeting between himself and Chen Geng. Chen Geng, knowing Yang Du had once been a "feudal remnant," hesitated, and asked Zhou Enlai for instructions. At that time Zhou Enlai had charge of the Party's political-security work in Shanghai, and Chen Geng, under Zhou Enlai, was chief of the Second Section (the intelligence section) of the Central Special Branch. Zhou Enlai judged that Yang Du, with his wide social reach and his knowledge of Chinese politics, was worth making contact with.

And so Chen Geng became a welcome guest at Yang Du's. Yang Du was a man of many contradictions in thought and manner. In his last years he gave himself to the study of Buddhism and wrote a treatise on Buddhist doctrine. Through his dealings with Chen Geng, Yang Du came to see that communism shared the "no-self" and "saving of the suffering" of Shakyamuni, and grew all the more sympathetic to the revolution. When word of this reached Li Lisan, then head of the CCP's Central Propaganda Department, Li was astonished. Li Lisan, himself a Hunanese, knew Yang Du's life well, and thought it past belief that a man who had spent his last strength on restoring the throne should now embrace the revolution. He therefore sent Pan Hannian — the man in charge of the Cultural Work Committee under the Propaganda Department — to meet Yang Du. Pan had been a propaganda officer under Deng Yanda and Guo Moruo at the General Political Department in the Wuhan days. After the Nationalist–Communist split he had returned to Shanghai to run open cultural work and organize the China League for Freedom, and was known for his ready tongue and his gift for cultivating men of standing. When Pan, with Luo Qiyuan and Li Qiushi (Li Weisen), was preparing to bring out Red Flag Weekly, he thought of Yang Du's fine calligraphy and asked him to write the paper's masthead. Yang did it without a second thought, at a single stroke; Pan carried the inscription back to report to Li Lisan, and Li Lisan, convinced at last, handed Yang's calligraphy straight to Luo Qiyuan to be cut into a plate and printed. (Zuo Xiangjun, 1946)

The "younger fellow-townsman, one Wang" of Zuo Xiangjun's account was Wang Shaoxian, a Hunanese painter then living in Shanghai.

Wang Shaoxian, whose family came from Xiangtan in Hunan, was a descendant of Wang Xin, a noted Xiang Army general of the Xianfeng years, and was on close terms with both his fellow-townsman Qi Huang — Qi Baishi — and Yang Du. Qi Baishi not only set down Wang Shaoxian's name in his own reminiscences but also joined Yang Du in inscribing a colophon on the Joined Scroll of Plum Blossoms that Wang Shaoxian owned. That scroll had come down to Wang from his ancestors; its painter, Yang Wujiu (Buzhi), was a master of the Song and especially skilled at ink plum, so much so that the Qing writer Xu Qin said in his Register of Ming Painting: "The line runs from Yin Bai and Huaguang, and passing down to Yang Buzhi of the Southern Song, it reached its height." And so, when Wang Shaoxian asked Qi Baishi and Yang Du for colophons, the two set brush to paper gladly — the one writing that "this scroll of Buzhi's in Mr. Shaoxian's keeping is of especial power, a thing to treasure," the other that "Shaoxian … asked me and Baishi for an inscription. I have set down here, in brief, what I have seen and heard over the years, with a word on Yin's painting, to make clear the provenance of Yang's scroll and the source of its plum manner."

Xu Canleng said that Wang Shaoxian was "underground Party," who used his standing as a painter for cover, came often to Yang Du's house, and had Yang "shut the door and read" certain progressive books and journals.

Xu Canleng was Yang Du's concubine. She remembered an evening in 1929 when Wang Shaoxian brought a man to call; Yang Du asked them upstairs, closed the door, and talked for a long while.

Xu Canleng didn't know what Yang Du and his visitor discussed, but she did know that the visitor was Chen Geng.

Chen Geng was then chief of the intelligence section of the Central Special Branch, with Liu Ding as deputy chief. The routine "contact" with Yang Du was in Liu Ding's "particular charge." In his dealings with Yang Du, Liu Ding "gathered a great deal of political intelligence and established links with the Kuomintang Reorganizationists and with Du Yuesheng, the famous chief of Shanghai's Green and Red gangs, and through these channels kept abreast of the movements of the Nanjing Kuomintang ruling clique and the doings of its leading men." (Li Tao and Yi Hui, 2002)

In May 1931, after the Gu Shunzhang affair, the Central Special Branch was reorganized. When Chen Geng was transferred away, he handed his connection with Yang Du to his successor, Pan Hannian, and Pan and Yang kept up a single line of contact. In the course of that contact and their dealings, the twenty-five-year-old Pan Hannian and Yang Du, more than twice his age, built a warm and easy friendship. (Yin Qi, 1996)

Before long Pan Hannian passed the Yang Du connection on to Xia Yan. Xia Yan wrote, in "Remembering Comrade Pan Hannian" (1982): "Not long after the Party's Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee in 1931, Pan Hannian delivered the plenum's resolutions to the Party members of the various leagues under the 'Cultural Committee,' at the offices of the 'Cultural League' then (above the Wangxingji furniture shop on Nanjing Road). After that, Feng Xuefeng took over his post as 'Cultural Committee' secretary. For more than half a year I did not see him. Then, one evening in the late autumn of that year, he reached me through the Liangyou Book Company. When we met, he called a taxi and drove to a little Western-style house on Route Stanislas Chevalier (today Jianguo Middle Road) in the French Concession, where he introduced me to a gentleman just past fifty. The two seemed quite at ease with each other, with none of the usual courtesies between them. Comrade Hannian began at once: 'In a few days I have to go far away, and there is no telling when I will be back, so …' He pointed at me: 'From now on he will be your single-line contact. His surname is Shen; he is steady and reliable.' The old gentleman shook my hand. Pan added a word: 'He is six or seven years older than I am; we are old friends.' They talked idly for a while, and of the content, and especially the names of the people mentioned, I understood nothing. As we parted, the old gentleman handed him a box of cigars; Pan took them without so much as a thank-you, and I guessed it was no parting gift. Only outside did Pan tell me: 'This is a well-known man, a secret Party member; I have always been his single-line contact. He will tell us many useful things, and you must never treat him lightly.' After a pause he added: 'This house is Du Yuesheng's; the Annamese patrolmen dare not touch it, so in a moment of danger you can come here for refuge.'"

Xia Yan said that only "after about half a year of contact" did he learn that the "gentleman just past fifty" was Yang Du, Yuan Shikai's chief running dog, and that it gave him "a great shock."

On September 17, 1931, Yang Du died of illness in Shanghai. He had written his own funeral couplet: "The true way of emperors — all of it now a bygone thing; to heal the people and save the nation — there will be others to take it up."

To the last Yang Du was still "charging his sons Yang Gongshu and Yang Gongzhao to carry on his work. Yang Gongshu was eager to take up his father's cause. Because Yang Du had had ties with us while he lived — people said he was of the royalist party, bound up with Yuan Shikai — we called him a 'special Party member,' and later even dropped the word 'special.' Yang Gongshu was secretary-general of Tsinghua University, and he brought the university's president, Weng Wenhao, to me; since it was a political matter, we ate at his home, and his household did not join us — only Weng Wenhao, Yang Gongshu and I, the three of us."

Yang Du's spirit was worthy of honor. Zhou Enlai and Pan Hannian, too, "went in secret, in person, to mourn him," bidding a last farewell to a comrade who had risked "the loss of his head and the extinction of his clan," who had "joined the Party at the very height of the White Terror." (Liu Renshou and He Luo, 1995)

On December 11, 1946, Zuo Xiangjun's article "That Freak Yang Du" appeared in the United Daily Evening Edition (later renamed the United Evening News), telling for the first time in public how Zhou Enlai had agreed to Chen Geng's "cultivating" Yang Du, and how Pan Hannian had "asked Yang to write the masthead of Red Flag Weekly."

In 1947 the literary figure Song Yunbin asked Xia Yan to confirm it: "Did Yang Du join the Communist Party in his last years?" Both Pan Hannian and Xia Yan, present at the time, answered that he had. Song Yunbin wrote in "The Late Redemption of Yang Xizi" (1985): "A man's stubbornness is not to be feared; what is to be feared is stubbornness that will not yield. Let a man be capable of change — let him once find a place to rest his life and settle his mind — and not only is his own conscience at ease, but his past errors are covered over as well. As the ancients said: 'He began in evil and ended in beauty, redeeming himself at the last.'"

On February 22, 1949, Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai received Yan Huiqing, Shao Lizi, Zhang Shizhao, Jiang Yong and others at Xibaipo. Zhang Shizhao, who was on personal terms with Mao Zedong and a fellow Hunanese, fell to talking of what other men of note modern Hunan had produced. Zhang Shizhao said, "About the only one worth counting would be Yang Du." Mao Zedong said, "Yang Du is one of our own!" Zhang Shizhao, dumbfounded, could not credit it. Mao went on: "Yang Du was a Party member; he joined secretly in Shanghai."

A few days later, arranged by the CCP Central Committee's Hong Kong Bureau, Liu Yazi, Ye Shengtao, Cao Yu, Song Yunbin and others took leave of Hong Kong, boarded ship northward, and made their way to Beijing by way of the Shandong Liberated Area. On the passage Song Yunbin spoke of Yang Du's ties with the CCP in his last years, and the whole company was "astonished"; Ye Shengtao in particular set down in his diary that day the words "never heard before."

To his death Yang Du never disclosed to anyone that he was a CCP member. His family said: "Even for many years after he died, all we knew was that he had ties and dealings with the Communist Party; it was only after 1978 that his Party membership was truly confirmed."

On July 30, 1978, Wang Yeqiu, then director of the State Bureau of Cultural Relics, published in the People's Daily an article in memory of Zhou Enlai, "An Unforgettable Memory," in which he said: "A few months before the beloved Premier Zhou passed away, he one day sent his secretary to tell me: back when Yuan Shikai proclaimed himself emperor, the first of the 'Six Gentlemen of the Chou'an Society,' Yang Du, in the end joined the Communist Party, introduced and directly led by Premier Zhou himself. The Premier said: please tell the editors of the Cihai in Shanghai that if there is an entry for 'Yang Du' in the Cihai, the fact of his joining the Communist Party at the last must be written in."

Zhou Enlai's charge was a solemn one; but actually to write into the Cihai the fact that Yang Du had "joined the Communist Party at the last" still called for corroboration from many quarters.

It was then that Yang Yunhui came forward.

Yang Du had two wives and eight children. Yang Yunhui was the eldest daughter of Yang Du and his concubine Xu Canleng.

When Xu Canleng came under attack in the ten years of turmoil, Yang Yunhui turned to Zhang Shizhao for help. Zhang Shizhao told her plainly: "Your father did indeed join the Communist Party, and he did so under Premier Zhou. Chairman Mao told me this with his own mouth, though it was never made public outside the Party."

On September 6, 1978, the third page of the People's Daily carried Li Yimang's article "On the Question of Yang Du's Party Membership," saying, after Wang Yeqiu, that "Yang Du was indeed a Party member, indeed a comrade." Li Yimang was then a standing deputy head of the International Liaison Department. He said he had heard as early as 1930 that Yang Du was a Party member, and that Yang had written the masthead for Red Flag Weekly, brought out by the CCP Center in 1930. In his memoir The Blurred Screen (1992), Li Yimang confirmed it again: "The four characters of the masthead, 'Red Flag Weekly,' do appear to be Yang Du's writing."

The same page carried Xia Yan's article "A Few Things About Comrade Yang Du." Xia Yan wrote: "Nowadays many know that Yang Du was one of the 'Six Gentlemen of the Chou'an Society,' and few that he was a Communist. Those of us who worked with him therefore have a duty to state the facts honestly and to honor the integrity of his last years."

By Xia Yan's account, Yang Du joined the Party "not in 1926 or 1927, but in the autumn of 1929. After Comrade Li Dazhao was martyred, a great change came over his thinking. He and Mr. Zhang Shizhao went about trying to rescue arrested Communists and to relieve the families of the stricken. After reaching Shanghai he joined the 'China Relief Society' and gave a considerable sum to it. After a period of examination, and on someone's introduction, he applied for membership, and with Zhou Enlai's approval he became a secret member of the Chinese Communist Party."

Xia Yan said further that after Zhou Enlai left Shanghai, the organization decided that he, Xia Yan, would be Yang Du's single-line contact. The two met once a month; he would bring Yang the Party's internal journals and "banned books" not to be had on the open market, and they would discuss affairs at home and abroad. Yang Du, more than once, would put what he had written in his own hand about the inner workings of the Kuomintang into a large envelope sealed with wax and hand it to Xia Yan to pass on to the organization. At first Xia Yan knew only that this was a secret Party member surnamed Yang; only as they grew familiar did he learn it was the renowned Yang Du.

Xia Yan said too that Yang Du had once told him: "I made two great mistakes in my life. The first was before the 1911 Revolution, when I refused to work with Mr. Sun Yat-sen — I said Huang Xing could join hands with you (meaning Sun Yat-sen), but I could not; for that I later owned my error to Mr. Sun Yat-sen. The second was that I was always anti-Manchu, yet I did not believe China could work a republic, and held that China must have an emperor to rule it; and of this I did not own my error until Zhang Xun's restoration."

As for his own motive in joining the Party, what Yang Du told Xia Yan was this: "I joined at the very height of the White Terror. If you say I was speculating, then what I speculated on was the chance of losing my head and having my clan wiped out."

With Zhang Shizhao's testimony as relayed by Yang Yunhui, and the articles of Xia Yan, Li Yimang and other principals besides, the Cihai editorial office at last carried out Zhou Enlai's charge.

In September 1979 the "Yang Du" entry in the three-volume Cihai was revised by the hand of Chen Xulu (then a professor of history at East China Normal University) and settled by the Shanghai Lexicographical Publishing House. The final entry, of nearly three hundred characters, read:

Yang Du (1874–1931), of Xiangtan in Hunan in the modern era. Courtesy name Xizi. A disciple of Wang Kaiyun; studied in Japan. In 1902 (the twenty-eighth year of Guangxu) he founded, with Yang Dusheng and others, the journal Youxue Yibian; later he drafted the report for the five ministers sent abroad by the Qing court to study constitutional government, and served as superintendent of the Bureau for the Compilation of Constitutional Government. In 1907 he edited the monthly China New Journal, advocating constitutional monarchy and demanding that the Qing court convene a parliament. After the 1911 Revolution broke out, at Yuan Shikai's instigation, he organized with Wang Jingwei the Society for the Common Weal of the Nation. When Yuan Shikai dissolved parliament in 1914 he became a councillor of the Political Participation Council; the next year he joined with Sun Yujun, Yan Fu, Liu Shipei, Hu Ying, Li Xiehe and others to form the Chou'an Society and plot the restoration of the monarchy. After Yuan Shikai's death he was placed on the wanted list. Later he inclined toward the revolution; around the time of Li Dazhao's arrest by the warlord Zhang Zuolin in 1927, he did much to try to save him. In his last years he moved to Shanghai and joined the China Relief Society and other progressive bodies. In the autumn of 1929 he joined the Chinese Communist Party, and held to the Party's work under the White Terror.

In 1986 Yang Du's new tomb was completed, at the Song Garden in Hongqiao, next to the burial ground of Song Qingling.

The gravestone was the old one, on which Xia Shoutian had written "Tomb of Mr. Yang Xizi of Xiangtan." Xia Shoutian was Yang Du's lifelong friend, and the two had all too much in common: Xia too was a Hunanese, too held a degree under the Qing, too beat the drum for Yuan Shikai's imperial bid, too spent his last years in Shanghai — the difference being that Yang Du came to the aid of our Party.

The stone Xia Shoutian inscribed is a large upright one; below it stands another, smaller and set crosswise. That one, inscribed by Zhao Puchu, reads "Tomb of Yang Du" — plainly for fear that people today wouldn't know who "Yang Xizi" was.

Before the small crosswise stone that Zhao Puchu inscribed lies a slab of white marble as well. On the white marble is carved Yang Du's life:

In 1922, entrusted by Mr. Sun Yat-sen, he traveled north and south, working to save the people and the nation and to seek the truth of the revolution. In 1927 he did all he could to rescue the Communist Li Dazhao. In the autumn of 1929, introduced by Comrade Pan Hannian and approved by Comrade Zhou Enlai, he joined the Chinese Communist Party and made his contribution to the revolution.

Zhang Yufa, academician of Taiwan's "Academia Sinica" and a historian, has said: "That Yang Du did intelligence work for the CCP in his last years is not hard to explain. He had experience of such work earlier in his political life — in the early years of the Republic, for instance, he did intelligence work for Yuan Shikai at Qingdao and Wuhan; and in his maneuvering among the Kuomintang men, he may well have been working for Yuan Shikai too. In the late Qing, maneuvering between the constitutionalists and the revolutionaries, whether he was doing intelligence work for those in power is likewise worth studying." (Zhang Yufa, 2019)
