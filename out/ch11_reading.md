## Chapter 11

At half past three in the afternoon, Bai Ping and I came to the Liti Café. Stephen was there before us, and came over gladly to meet us. He asked me:

"Was it you who went to fetch her?"

"Yes," I said. "Have you been here long?"

"A while," he said.

"You didn't go to fetch Mei Yingzi?"

"No," he said. "I thought she might come first, herself."

"But she has not come yet."

"You've only just arrived yourself!" Stephen laughed.

"Somehow I had a feeling she would come early," I said.

"Yesterday you were well and truly defeated," Stephen said with a laugh.

"Defeated in what?"

"I say Mei Yingzi has taken command of your feelings."

"You think so?"

"Even my wife feels the same," he said. "Go on like this, and in four days' time you will surely be leaning on her to hold your life together."

"Wait and see," I laughed.

Bai Ping said not a word, but sat there smiling, seeming today unusually calm and serene.

Stephen and I fell to talking of other things, and the time slipped quietly by.

By four o'clock Mei Yingzi had still not come, and I began to feel a certain expectancy. I said:

"Why is she still not here?"

"You mean Mei Yingzi?" said Stephen, with a mischievous smile. "She will come only when you have passed from anxiety into despair."

Bai Ping still smiled serenely at our side.

But half past four came, and still no sign of Mei Yingzi; I grew truly uneasy—could it be that Mei Yingzi would break her word? I said:

"I'm afraid she will not come now."

"Perhaps," said Stephen. "But what is that to us? We shall live as we always do."

But Bai Ping had been watching for her all this while; she looked out of the window and smiled at us; and just then I saw a car stop outside.

"Is that Mei Yingzi?" I asked Stephen.

Stephen looked, and stood up:

"It is she!"

Mei Yingzi pushed hurriedly in at the door, in a short pale-grey cheongsam, a pure-white short woollen jacket, and a great loose overcoat of yellow camel-hair thrown over her shoulders, carrying a small, exquisite leather case and walking briskly, on her feet a pair of dark-grey rubber-soled travelling shoes. Stephen went to meet her and took the case for her; she greeted us, and came over all smiles to say:

"Forgive me—your host has come late."

"Is that little case to go back home?" Stephen asked.

"After dinner let us take the train to Hangzhou."

"To Hangzhou?" I asked.

"I've bought five train tickets today," she said. "Today I am the host."

As she spoke she fished a handful of things from her overcoat pocket—odd banknotes, sundries, letters and the like. From one envelope she drew five train tickets and five special passes from the Japanese command headquarters, and said to Stephen with a brisk laugh:

"Well? And your wife?"

"She isn't coming."

"Then go and ask her."

"Surely you know her by now?" said Stephen. "She has no taste for junketing of this kind."

"Do you suppose we mean to go and invite her?" Mei Yingzi then asked Bai Ping and me.

"That is no affair of mine," said Bai Ping, with a faint smile. "My affair is only to obey and go along to Hangzhou."

"It is just as well if only the four of us go," I said.

"Just as well," said Bai Ping. "Then I must go back once to fetch a few things."

"What I've brought is enough for the two of us already," said Mei Yingzi.

"There's time yet," said Bai Ping. "I must go back and leave word, too. Wherever you go to dine later, I'll come and find you there."

"The Golden Gate, then—how would that do?" said Mei Yingzi.

"The Golden Gate—very well," said Bai Ping. "I shall certainly be there by half past seven."

"Shall I go with you?" I asked.

"No," said Bai Ping. "I shall be quicker going alone."

So I telephoned to call a car for her.

After Bai Ping had gone, Mei Yingzi said:

"Why is Bai Ping so downcast today?"

"I noticed it too," said Stephen.

"Can it be because she is wearing black today?"

"Perhaps—yes…"

"Or is it out of jealousy?" said Stephen.

"Perhaps," I said. "Yesterday Mei Yingzi ought not to have gone off with the others and left her."

"Why don't you say it's because you yourself were too taken up with Mei Yingzi?" Stephen laughed.

Mei Yingzi laughed too, a laugh with triumph and mockery in it.

"Later last night she saw you at the bar, still together with those two Japanese."

"…?" Stephen too seemed a little surprised.

"Yes," said Mei Yingzi, her smile changing to a quiet one.

"When her guest is snatched away by others," I said, "how should one of her competitive temper not be jealous?"

"Then today she is angry with Mei Yingzi."

"Might she go off and not come back at all?" Mei Yingzi asked.

Mei Yingzi's words gave me pause; it struck me that Bai Ping's not wanting me to go with her just now might have been a plan not to come back at all. So I said:

"Let us go early to the Golden Gate and wait for her; and if she is still not there by half past eight, let us split up and go looking for her."

This proposal met with the agreement of them both, and at half past six we left the Liti Café and walked to the Golden Gate.

We reached the Golden Gate before seven, and sat in the smoking-room to wait for Bai Ping. At about a quarter past seven it suddenly occurred to me to telephone her. I went to the telephone room, but both booths were occupied, and I waited outside. Glancing by chance at the glass of the left-hand booth, I saw all at once that the woman telephoning within was got up exactly like Mei Yingzi—the same pure-white woollen jacket over a grey worsted cheongsam. While I was still staring in astonishment, the door of the booth opened and the woman bent down; I saw her gather up her overcoat, and it too was of yellow camel-hair. Watching her take up a small leather case, I looked then at her shoes—were they not, likewise, dark-grey rubber-soled travelling shoes?

Not a hair's difference. And so, as she turned to come out, I stepped up to her and said:

"Forgive me, miss—may I carry this case for you?"

"…" She was startled at first, but then laughed: "Thank you."

She walked briskly at my side, seeming much fresher than a moment before. I said:

"They all thought you might not come."

"Why should they?" Bai Ping laughed. "I might do such a thing—but never when Mei Yingzi is host."

Bai Ping's costume astonished both Stephen and Mei Yingzi. I said:

"Let everyone take them for sisters."

"How should I come by such an honour?" And Bai Ping went on, to Mei Yingzi:

"Then from today you shall be my little sister." She walked arm in arm with Bai Ping to the dining-room, and we followed behind. Stephen said to me:

"The two of them are actually the same height."

But this remark reminded me of the points in which Bai Ping's bearing fell short of Mei Yingzi's, and set me thinking, too, of why I had always felt Mei Yingzi the taller of the two. I said:

"But Mei Yingzi has the better proportions."

"Isn't it that Bai Ping gives a younger impression?"

"But length of leg is the emblem of dignity; the difference between a crane and a hen lies just in the length of the leg."

Stephen laughed.

At table I studied the faces of Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping—how different in type they were. Mei Yingzi's face was of the oval kind; such a face most of all dreads stiffness, yet hers held within it every hint of liveliness, and had besides a peculiar noble dignity. Bai Ping's face was of the round kind, with large eyes and long lashes, and seemed livelier and quicker than Mei Yingzi's, but wanted the higher nose, so that it lacked a certain dignity and nobility. She was smiling, like a lily just opening, with a child's sweetness, faint dimples flickering over her cheeks—which is what forever leaves in one an impression of youth, and easily draws one to a tenderness for her. It came home to me all at once that Mrs. Stephen's soothing of her in the car the night before, and Mei Yingzi's fondness for her today, were none of them mere hollow courtesy.

It was the wine—the wine had brought colour to both Bai Ping's cheeks, and her lively talk made her face like the bright moon of autumn; tonight she gave out all the beauty stored within her, without a trace of affectation or reserve, her character and her looks in beautiful accord. Yet always I felt that Mei Yingzi, beside her, eclipsed all her radiance. Mei Yingzi's face was nothing less than the sunset clouds of summer, with ten thousand changes, ten thousand beauties—who knew how much radiance set it off from behind, or how many colours attended it on every side? The wine deepened the charm of her brows and of the shadows about her eyes; she used every breath she drew and every line of her flesh with a supple art. Say, if you like, that she was forever employing affectation and reserve; but in her, affectation and reserve were only a beautiful glittering.

Stephen seemed to notice that I was paying too much attention to Mei Yingzi's face, and said to me with a smile:

"And only the second day?"

I did not answer, but raised my glass and said aloud:

"A last glass—let me drink to Mrs. Stephen."

Everyone raised a glass, and set the empty glasses down.

It was the most exhilarating feast of all.
