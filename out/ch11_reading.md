## The Tenant

A muddy Winter Solstice, a clear New Year. Since the week before, the sun had shone warm day after day, and today the sky was a washed and cloudless blue. Though the northwest wind blew without letup, it counted all the same as one of the rare fine days of the last lunar month. Wei Dafu had been on his feet all afternoon, and in his padded gown he actually felt a little too warm.

Every winter he turned over in his mind the wish to buy himself an overcoat, so that he might work the streets in that foreign suit of his. That would look the part. The houses he led clients to see were all good ones, cement-built Western houses, and in a great padded gown he felt his very confidence in talking a client round was cut by half. Every winter the number of houses he closed was far fewer than in spring or autumn. With an overcoat, he could slip it off on entering a room, and stand there in suit and leather shoes talking to a client with a full chest.

Around four in the afternoon the sun leaned to the west, the wind dropped, and the treetops did not stir. The worshippers before Jing'an Temple had thinned out, everyone waiting to burn the first incense on the first day of the year, but the little stalls and peddlers around it had grown a good deal more numerous. In years past, once the year's end came, no one went out to rent a house, and at this season Wei Dafu would generally be idling about the streets. This year was a little different: the people coming to the office to arrange a viewing with him had not let up at all.

Reaching Jing'an Temple, Wei Dafu halted and pressed his palms together toward the gate in a bow. And what to pray for? That the comrades all be safe, then. If there were an agent tailing him behind, he thought, perhaps it might set them off their guard? And yet, if it were one of his own comrades who happened to pass by, he would no doubt be scolded another day for feudal superstition. He laughed to himself and set off for the Tiangu Estate not far away.

At the crossing of Yuyuan Road and Difeng Road, the clients were waiting for him.

“Mr. Wang, Mrs. Wang?” Wei Dafu came a few steps nearer and cupped his hands to the pair in greeting.

Mrs. Wang chided him: “We've waited half the day, and there you come dawdling up from way off.”

Wei Dafu put on a rueful smile: “Mrs. Wang, don't be cross. I've been running about all afternoon; this street-runner's bowl of rice isn't an easy one to eat.”

“You chose to eat it yourself; no one invited you to,” Mrs. Wang said, half smiling, half not.

Mr. Wang shook the Shen Bao in his hand: “We're putting you to trouble, Mr. Wei. Do get on and show us the house.”

Mrs. Wang added: “Any longer and it'll be dark.”

Wei Dafu led the two clients toward the inside of the lane.

“The advertisement spoke of a new-style street-front house with a glass shopfront, and here you lead us off into a lane,” Mrs. Wang ran on without pause. “It said, too, a bowshot from Jing'an Temple. Has the archer really got an arm that strong?”

The three of them turned into a cross-lane on the left, went in, and up to the second floor. Wei Dafu opened the door, stepped in one stride to the window, pushed open the tall casement, and turned to the clients: “A lane house in a terrace like this isn't as roomy as one with east and west wings, but this front room is itself more than a zhang across, it faces south for good sun, and with the neighbors' gable walls on either side it's warm in winter and cool in summer. It's quite comfortable to live in.”

“Facing the lane, and so close, that whatever happens in the room the people opposite see the whole of it.” The wife found fault; the husband said nothing.

“The better the district, the dearer every inch of land. Country houses are set well apart, true enough.” Wei Dafu was growing a little impatient. “Shanghai households all hang curtains; draw them and do as you please.”

He glanced out of the window. The lane was in fact quite open; the place he lived in himself was what you would call cramped. He was about to say a few more words when footsteps sounded behind him, and the two clients had already gone up to the third floor on their own.

He drew back the words that had reached his lips, turned, and started. A stranger stood suddenly in the room.

The door had shut.

“Whom are you looking for?” Wei Dafu was somewhat uneasy.

“I've come to see the house too.”

“And who might you be? Have we an appointment?”

The newcomer took a step forward, and Wei Dafu took two steps back toward the window. “My name is Chen. Chen Qianli.” The newcomer moved into the bright light by the window.

“Which of the agency's people gave Mr. Chen this address, I wonder?”

“It's you I've come to see, Mr. Wei.”

“Where has Mr. Chen come from?”

“That place—” Chen Qianli spread his hands, as if to set Wei Dafu at ease, “—is very far away.”

He sat down by the round table at the window and ran a hand over the dust on it: “Good furniture.”

“If you really want to rent, I can find you another. This room is already spoken for.”

“Very well, tell me about it.”

Wei Dafu drew out a round stool, sat down opposite the newcomer, lit a cigarette, and, reaching over, pushed open one half of the window that stood ajar to tap his ash outside.

“Might I ask, Mr. Chen—are you in Shanghai on business? Have you relatives here? Renting a house requires a surety bond.”

“That's easily managed.” Chen Qianli had been observing him the whole afternoon. The day before, Chen Qianli had gone out to Zhaojiabang and met Li Han at the coal yard by the creek. When they fled the secret meeting place upstairs at the market, Li Han had followed close behind Wei Dafu. But by the time Li Han reached the end of that staircase, the agents had already blocked the stairs. Both Yi Junnian and Li Han had mentioned Wei Dafu: at the time everyone had been stunned, yet he had seemed to know all along that the enemy was just outside the door.

Wei Dafu tapped more ash out of the window: “That's good. If Mr. Chen is comfortably off, a new-style lane compound like the Tiangu Estate isn't at all bad. The entrance hall is laid with mosaic, the rooms have waxed floors and steel-framed windows, the sinks and flush toilets are the latest English goods, and Jing'an Temple is close at hand, handy for a businessman to burn incense and make his kowtows.”

Chen Qianli looked keenly interested: “Are there any apartment blocks?”

“There are, indeed. If Mr. Chen hasn't brought his family, an apartment block is quieter, and easier to come and go from. A lane is full of people and prying eyes.”

As he spoke, the young couple came down from upstairs, pushed the door open, and glanced inside.

Wei Dafu rose and asked: “How did the two of you find it?”

“Won't do. See—you!” the woman drew the words out and tossed them off, and, taking the man's arm, went swaying down the stairs.

Wei Dafu chased as far as the door, then stopped, and came back to sit by the round table.

“If Mr. Chen wants an apartment block, our Donglu agency has several in hand.” He waved a hand out of the window. “Turn from here onto Avenue Haig, then over by Shanzhong Road to Zhaozhujiao Road, and there are several buildings, four-story cement houses. The rent, though, is a bit steep.”

“Your firm can find any sort of house, it seems.”

“The houses are all there; it's the clients that are hard to find. Sometimes a client makes an appointment, we talk for half the day—” Wei Dafu flicked the cigarette end from between his fingers out of the window “—and only at the last do you find he never really wanted a house at all.”

Chen Qianli rose and shut the window: “It was Mr. Yi who told me to look for you.”

Seeing Chen Qianli close the window, Wei Dafu too went to the door and shut it softly: “Which Mr. Yi?”

“Mr. Yi Junnian. He said you had a place ready for me.”

“A place where?” Wei Dafu hesitated.

“Route Massenet has a T-junction at either end.”

Wei Dafu thought a moment, then said: “Route Massenet.. I wonder which end you mean?”

Wei Dafu rarely used this recognition signal; by the rules, it was used only when an upline comrade came to find him. But for these two years his upline had been only Old Fang and Yi Junnian, and he had all but forgotten the countersign.

The room was growing gradually dark; someone was taking in the washing hung out to dry, and from beyond the window came the sound of a rattan beater thudding against cotton quilts.

Wei Dafu came out with: “Shall I put the light on?”

Chen Qianli broke into a smile: “Of course. There's nothing we need say in the dark.”

Wei Dafu got up and pulled the light-cord above the round table; the bulb beneath its glass shade came on, the light a hazy yellow, and the room was a little cold.

“I've seen Old Yi, and he never said anyone was coming to make contact.” He raised his head and looked at Chen Qianli. “But you'll be the comrade sent down from above, no doubt? He did say he'd gotten in touch with a comrade from above.”

“What do you make of Old Yi?” Chen Qianli asked him suddenly.

“What—make of him?” Wei Dafu was rather at a loss. “Old Yi joined the revolution very early; he's an Old Bolshevik, rich in the experience of struggle—”

“Do you find him any different, before his arrest and after?”

“After I went underground, Old Yi was always my upline; he's the leader, and it was always he who came to me. Barring special circumstances, I mustn't go to him. He's especially good at this work, cool-headed in a crisis—”

“Cooler than you?” Chen Qianli asked with a smile.

“There's no comparison.” Wei Dafu fished out his cigarette case. “I didn't see anything different about him; he just seemed a bit anxious. Before, he'd always send a letter to Jin Delin, the street-runner at the agency—young Jin left the job last year—and Old Fu in the mailroom would stick all the runners' letters on the wall by his desk, where a row of cloth bags was nailed up. Old Yi would draw three linked circles on both top corners of the envelope, and stuck up there it was very easy to spot. The letter said only irrelevant things, but there would be a time and an address in it, and at the appointed time I'd go there and wait for him.”

Wei Dafu went on: “But this time he came straight to the agency, and gave me a fright, just as you did coming in just now. I hurried him out into the street, and he said he'd made contact with the higher-ups, that we must lose no time in checking for leaks within, and so must hold a meeting. A meeting—I asked him whether calling people together for a meeting at a time like this might not draw the agents' notice. And he took me to task, saying my will to struggle was weak.

“Then he blamed me too for what I'd said at the meeting that day. But I'd said nothing. Things suddenly turned chaotic outside, and I said, let's hurry and meet and hurry and disperse. Yet Old Yi said I had wavered. How had I wavered! Of course I don't blame him; he'd only just come out of the lockup and was surely in low spirits, and that's why I say he seemed a bit anxious. I put it to him gently that the most urgent thing now is not to resume work but to stay hidden, and wait a while, and once the enemy stops paying us any mind, then slowly begin to resume. In the old days he'd have said the very same thing to me himself.”

“That day at the Fourth Avenue market, when the Municipal Police surrounded it, how did you get out?”

“I must have been the first to rush out of the room, with a big fellow behind me—I didn't know him, he wasn't on our line. I got into the stairwell, and by the third floor I knew things were bad; there were a great many footsteps on the stairs from the second floor to the third. I quickly pushed open the door at the side of the third floor and hid in the lavatory off the corridor. Thinking that I couldn't stay hidden there long, I hurried out again and ran the other way, and, as it happened, there was a lift at the back of the building too, a goods lift. Coming out of the lift downstairs, I ran straight into Mrs. Li, who often brought people to the agency to rent, and taking my chance I helped her carry her vegetable basket and mixed into the crowd with her, and so got out.”

“And once you were home, nothing out of the ordinary?”

“I didn't dare go straight home. I went first to the agency and worked as usual. In fact I didn't dare sit long at the agency either; I took a listing, said I was off to work the streets, and came out, and wandered the roads the whole day long. It was bitterly cold that day; the wind made your very teeth ache. At night, quaking, I went home, and stood a long while at the mouth of the lane, and only at midnight did I dare go in. Those few days it was just work and back again; and on the third day I went alone to the market, but didn't dare go in. The vegetable peddlers said a good many people had been arrested that day, all of them Communists.”

“Did you go looking for Old Fang afterward?”

“I did. By the rules, if something serious happens, one may send him a signal asking to meet. But he gave me no answer; I waited a whole week without a word, and thought he too might have been arrested. I didn't know what I could do—perhaps I should have pulled out—but the only superiors I could contact for instructions were Old Fang and Old Yi, and the two of them had both vanished.

“I cleaned out my home from end to end, in case the agents should come bursting in. There was a Soviet-area newspaper from the year before last that had reached me somehow or other, and I couldn't bear to throw it away. Red China, with the news of victory in the Third Counter-Encirclement Campaign in it. There was nothing else.

“Some time ago Old Fang told me to make ready a few more surety-bond forms, and told me specially not to tell Old Yi first. I supposed that might be work on another line, that the organization might need to rent a batch of houses on short notice. For a short-term let, one can use these forged forms.

“I handle these forms all the time. Some shopkeepers don't much mind: say you've lost one, and they'll stamp it again. Some men make a business of just this—renting a shopfront, hanging out an agency sign, and standing surety for people as a trade. The agency where I work can stand surety too; for the stamp you have to go to the manager, but there's always a chance to get a few blank forms stamped besides.

“The moment Old Fang asked, I did it; but keeping these forms at home would only invite trouble. If the agents searched them out, I'd have no way to explain, and it might bring loss to the organization. I turned it over and over, and then I burned all the forms. I work here, and if nothing's amiss it's no trouble to get a few more at once. To tell the truth, it was a pity to burn them—forms like that fetch money. A surety document from a solid, well-off shop can sell for a dozen-odd silver dollars.”

“Old Fang told you to tell no one, and at our very first meeting you've told me everything.” Chen Qianli pushed the matchbox on the corner of the table over to Wei Dafu, so that he could light the cigarette he had held so long between his fingers. “You really never told Old Yi afterward?”

“Old Yi said you were sent down from above; you're Old Yi's superior, which to me makes you the superior's superior, so there's surely no harm in my telling you. But of course it can't be told to Old Yi—I know the rules. Don't judge by my chattering; my mouth is tight enough.”

“Have you seen Old Yi these last few days?”

“Today, I have. Old Yi has just told me to come to a clinic for a meeting tomorrow night. Everyone who got away from the meeting, and everyone arrested and released, is to go. Old Yi said that as things look now, we're all to meet together—we have to organize ourselves, or the ranks will fall into disorder.”

“How so?”

“Old Yi said some comrades are suspecting one another. He swore, and said it had gotten quite out of hand; he didn't go into it, and it wasn't for me to pry—he's the superior.”

Chen Qianli thought a moment: “Find me a house. It must be large, with a hidden way in and out, and best of all on the border between the Settlement and the Chinese city.”

“There is one, as it happens. The landlord is a Ningbo man who took a new wife and went off to Guangdong to do business; he's not likely to come back. And even if he did, they'd surely not want to live there again. I'd meant one day, given the chance, to take it over for myself, and so I've never brought anyone to see it.”

“Find time to go and have a look.” Chen Qianli suddenly broke into a smile. “And this one you're not to tell Old Yi—even though it was he who sent me to you to rent a house.”
