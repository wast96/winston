## Chapter 11. The Great Turning: The Highest Art of Secret Struggle

In the three years from 1946 to 1949 the face of China changed utterly: first Chiang Kai-shek drove Mao Zedong out of Yan'an, and then Mao Zedong drove Chiang Kai-shek off the mainland. Looking back on the vastest war in Chinese history, people marvel that Mao commanded troops like a god.

But Mao was a man, not a god, and to command like a god is only a figure of speech; a figure of speech cannot get at the inner secret of why Mao was so good at war. Sun Tzu's *Art of War* says: "Know the enemy and know yourself, and in a hundred battles you will never be in peril." Mao changed it to: "Know the enemy and know yourself, and in a hundred battles you will win a hundred." The "knowing yourself" that Mao prized included security work; the "knowing the enemy" depended on intelligence work.

Zhou Enlai ran spies like a god too.

### 1. Electronic Warfare on the Loess Plateau

Mao pulled out of Yan'an but refused to leave northern Shaanxi. He meant, he said, to go fishing on the Loess Plateau, using himself as the stinking bait to draw out Chiang Kai-shek's strategic reserves. This suited old Chiang exactly. The surprise assault on Yan'an was meant to cut out the heart and take off the head, to swallow the Communists' supreme command and finish the whole business in a single stroke.

The two sides thought as one, and Hu Zongnan's army of 230,000 men spent its days combing the gullies of northern Shaanxi for Mao Zedong.

Mao knew he was playing a dangerous move, and it had to be hedged. After the withdrawal from Yan'an, the Party Center met at Zaolingou and decided to split in three. Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai, and Ren Bishi formed the Central Front Committee, which would fight its way across northern Shaanxi and direct the war nationwide. Liu Shaoqi, Zhu De, and Dong Biwu formed the Central Working Committee, which moved to Pingshan in Hebei, in the Jin-Cha-Ji Liberated Area, to lead land reform and base-area construction. Ye Jianying, Yang Shangkun, Li Weihan, and Li Kenong formed the Central Rear Committee, which went to Linxian in Shanxi, in the Jin-Sui Liberated Area, to handle logistics. It was a defense in depth: lose the first line, and there was still a second. Mao even arranged for two Soviet doctors to be moved to the rear, lest anyone say foreigners were taking part in China's civil war.

The Front Committee was the first line, buried deep behind enemy lines and at the greatest risk, so of course it needed a guard unit. Called a regiment, it was really one pistol company, two infantry companies, and one cavalry company. Could four small companies protect the supreme command? With this thin force Mao still wanted to try his hand. As they marched along he suddenly stopped, saying he wanted to have a look at the enemy for himself.

This threw the security men into a panic. Luo Qingchang, chief of the First Office of the Social Affairs Department, and Wang Dongxing, chief of the Second Office, were traveling with the Front Committee, in charge of intelligence and security respectively, and now they were needed. Luo Qingchang rode off to fetch the troops back and keep Chairman Mao safe while he watched the enemy. Ren Bishi, the column commander, came up with a scheme: send Wang Dongxing to watch the enemy in the Chairman's place.

Wang Dongxing led a reinforced platoon and held one position after another along the Hejiagetai–Wangjiawan line, and under enemy air attack he stubbornly blocked Hu Zongnan's main body for twelve hours. Only then was Mao pleased: he had felt out the enemy's fighting strength. From that day Wang Dongxing stayed at Mao's side, and stayed a lifetime; until Mao's death he was always in charge of security for the Party Center and for Mao himself. That willingness to fight was perhaps one reason Mao valued him.

Mao did not fear having too few troops; he feared having too many. The Front Committee fighting across northern Shaanxi was not a beggar matching treasures with the Dragon King, but a mouse outwitting an elephant. The whole point was to shrink the target, to let Hu Zongnan catch the scent but never the man.

The Central Front Committee, staff and troops together fewer than a thousand men, traveled light. The command organ, the Military Commission's Second and Third Bureaus, and the New China News Agency were formed into four battalions under the cover name Ninth Detachment. The leaders all took aliases too: the Military Commission chairman Mao Zedong became Li Desheng ("Li the Victor"), the chief of the general staff Zhou Enlai became Hu Bicheng, the Ninth Detachment commander Ren Bishi became Shi Lin, and the political commissar Lu Dingyi became Zheng Wei. This lean band could hide itself in any small hill village it happened upon.

When the Ninth Detachment fell back to Wangjiawan, Mu Fengyun of the Social Affairs Department at once sat down with the peasants to learn the little village's class makeup, its society, its production, its life, and the people's views of the war. Mao read the report and immediately decided to set up a new investigation section at the detachment headquarters, with Mu Fengyun as its chief. To add a whole section to so tiny a detachment: Mao did love his investigation and research.

With a small force, lodged in a small village, Mao had the smallest command post in the world. He lived two months in that poor little village, and in its cave dwelling he wrote "On the Operational Policy for the Northwest Battlefield." To take on the great with the small looked to outsiders like a reckless gamble at long odds. But Mao was perfectly composed: he knew the enemy and he knew himself.

This game of cat and mouse was always one side in the light and the other in the dark: wherever the Ninth Detachment went, Hu Zongnan did not know; wherever Hu Zongnan's troops went, Mao did know. At the great commander's side was a secret radio, taking down Hu Zongnan's movement orders day by day.

On the very eve of the central organs' withdrawal from Yan'an, in the midst of a thousand urgent tasks, the Party Center made a startling decision: the Central Urban Work Department, responsible for work in the Nationalist areas, and the Central Social Affairs Department, responsible for intelligence, would set up a joint secretariat under Zhou Enlai. Tong Xiaopeng, secretary-general of the Urban Work Department, became its director; Luo Qingchang, secretary-general of the Social Affairs Department, became its deputy director. A cipher section was assigned from the Central Confidential Office and two radios from the Military Commission's Third Bureau, all placed under the secretariat. Zhou Enlai was a secretary of the Central Secretariat, head of the Central Urban Work Department, and deputy chairman of the Central Military Commission concurrently serving as chief of the general staff, surely the busiest man alive. And this busiest of men wanted to run military operations with one hand and radios with the other? No: better to say he ran the military war with one hand and the intelligence war with the other.

Tong Xiaopeng brought the call signs of the Party organizations in the Nationalist areas, overseas, and in the South Seas; Luo Qingchang brought the call signs of four behind-the-lines intelligence stations at Xi'an, Beiping, Lanzhou, and Shenyang. The two secret stations took in confidential intelligence from inside the Nationalist camp every day, and Zhou Enlai forwarded it at once to the several field armies.

Radio contact was a double-edged sword: it could win the enemy's intelligence, and it could leak one's own secrets. Xiong Xianghui reported secretly from Xi'an that Hu Zongnan's troops had been fitted with the newest American radio direction-finding equipment, built to catch Communist radio signals and so to fix the location of the Communist command.

Zhou Enlai planned the communications scheme for the military high command himself. On pulling out of Yan'an he ordered the headquarters station to stop transmitting for three days. He wired the field armies to send nothing by radio before or during an operation, or else to move a headquarters' own large station aside and switch to a small one, relaying through the large station to throw the enemy off.

This too was electronic warfare. Hu Zongnan could never settle where the Communist supreme command had gone—now they said north, now they said across the Yellow River—and his army could not catch up, and his planes could not find it to bomb.

Once Hu Zongnan had taken Yan'an he was eager for a decisive battle with the Liberation Army's main force, but his intelligence was blind; flailing his fists he could find no opponent, and had to guess, throwing his main body toward Ansai to look for a fight. The Communists' Northwest Field Army stayed hidden in the shadows; its commander Peng Dehuai and political commissar Xi Zhongxun, working from reliable intelligence, kept a constant hold on Hu Zongnan's movements and watched for a gap.

The 31st Brigade covering the right flank was relatively isolated, and Peng Dehuai seized on it: at Qinghuabian his men took brigade commander Li Jiyun alive. Hu Zongnan, only six days into his occupation of Yan'an, was galled by the defeat and at once sent troops in pursuit. Mao designed a "mushroom tactic": let Peng Dehuai avoid battle and lead the Nationalist army in circles through the gullies, grinding down its edge.

Here was another fruit of intelligence. The listening stations of the Military Commission's Second Bureau intercepted the Nationalists' movements, and at Wayaobao his men wiped out the Nationalist 135th Brigade in a single fight.

Zhou Enlai then put out false intelligence, and Chiang Kai-shek concluded that the Communist headquarters was at Suide and ordered Hu Zongnan to strike northeast. In fact Mao was hidden at Wangjiawan, more than four hundred kilometers away, directing the distant war from that quiet little hill village.

Hu Zongnan's main body ran to Suide for nothing, a kind of "armed parade," while the Northwest Field Army's main force cut across his rear and rolled up his supply base at Panlong.

Forty days out of Yan'an, the Liberation Army had won three great victories. Zhou Enlai suddenly appeared at Zhenwudong, at the gate of Yan'an, and spoke openly at a victory rally.

Hu Zongnan rose to the bait again and gave chase toward Zhenwudong. His army came within five li of Wangjiawan, where Mao lay hidden; that night, in heavy rain, the Ninth Detachment and the enemy troops actually converged. The Ninth Detachment marched along a ridge, not daring to make a sound; the enemy marched through the gully below, men shouting and horses neighing. Only at daybreak did the Ninth Detachment slip clear of danger.

For days on end the Ninth Detachment stayed within range of the enemy's guns. The men did not take off their clothes, the horses did not have their saddles removed, and planes hung overhead. Yet the enemy simply could not find Mao. On the Long March Mao had often ridden in a litter; now, ten years older, he was stronger in body and mind, and never once used a litter. With the situation urgent, Mao even sent his deputy chief of staff Wang Dongxing with a company deep into the enemy's rear to fight a guerrilla campaign around Yan'an and Ansai; Wang Dongxing led them for thirty-four days before returning to report.

Mao moved safely on to Xiaohe village. The front commanders Peng Dehuai, Xi Zhongxun, He Long, Wang Zhen, and Chen Geng gathered under a single awning and held the crucial conference that laid out the strategic counter-offensive.

Chiang Kai-shek, directing a nationwide war, relaxed the intelligence war not at all. Juntong imported large stocks of the newest direction-finding gear from America and broke a Communist secret station in Beiping. Following the thread, it cracked the secret station in Xi'an, and the Wang Shijian intelligence system, stretching across the Northwest, North China, and the Northeast, was broken up: more than a hundred underground Party members were arrested.

Getting word of it, Zhou Enlai at once convened a confidential-communications conference at Xiaohekou. With the head of the Military Commission's Second Bureau Dai Jingyuan, the head of the Third Bureau Wang Zheng, the head of the Central Confidential Office Li Zhizhong, the secretariat director Tong Xiaopeng, and the deputy director Luo Qingchang, he spent more than twenty days studying how to counter the Nationalists' aerial monitoring, underground sabotage, and code-breaking.

This struggle on the confidential-communications front, Zhou Enlai said, was a struggle that joined politics with technology. In technology we lag behind the Nationalists, but we can learn and improve, and one day we will catch them; in politics, though, we are the advanced side. Our people have a high political consciousness and a strict system, and that they can never catch. They have the technology, but politically they are rotten: bureaucratism, officials shielding one another, high and low deceiving each other, rules unenforced, and many holes we can exploit. So long as we strengthen political and ideological work, enforce the rules strictly, and mind our technical progress too, we are bound to defeat them.

This important conference overhauled the Communists' radio-communications system, secured the safety of the behind-the-lines stations and the concealment of the frontline stations, and fixed the working policy of joining politics to technology.

By Zhou Enlai's rules, confidential staff were always to stay at the leader's side; the radio was to reach each new billet ahead of time and open contact; the confidential section and the radio were to keep a twenty-four-hour watch; telegrams were to be received, decoded, and delivered on the spot, never delayed. On the march and in battle, a decoded telegram went straight to Zhou Enlai, and any wire graded "ten-thousand-fold urgent" or higher was cause to wake a sleeping man. Mao often went without sleep the whole night. Before bed he always went first to the confidential section to see whether a telegram had come. On waking, his first words were to send Ye Zilong to the section to fetch the telegrams.

For the supreme leadership to deploy electronic warfare in person is a great rarity in the world's intelligence history.

The commander's banner flew on. On the plateau of northern Shaanxi the great commander pinned down old Chiang's strategic reserve army without a scratch, freeing his own main forces so that Liu-Deng, Chen-Su, and Chen-Xie could thrust into the Central Plains along three routes.

In guarding that banner, the secret front played no small part.

### 2. Who Received Hu Zongnan's Battle Orders First?

Yan'an's security system had for years devoted itself to guarding the Center and guarding the Border Region, work that was essentially internal defense. When the War of Liberation began, that internal-defense work rose into a war of self-defense—a sign that security work had entered its most intense phase.

An intelligence and security organ is a sword. In peacetime the sword hums in its scabbard; in war the sharp blade leaves it. Yan'an's intelligence and security men were all excited, longing to cut down the strong enemy with their own hands. Yet even in wartime the chief function of the intelligence and security front was not to charge and kill in the field. Blade against blade, gun against gun—that is the specialty of regular troops. The intelligence and security men's real trade was still to carry out intelligence reconnaissance.

Intelligence work in the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region shifted its focus to the military struggle as well. The Yihe conference the Northwest Bureau held late in 1947 caused intelligence work to contract and stall for a time, but in September 1948 the Northwest Bureau issued another directive on strengthening intelligence work, and the Border Security at once threw itself into restoring and expanding it.

The Suide sub-district restored its work in Yulin, developed toward Dongsheng, the Zhaqi banner, and Wangfu, and kept up its work toward the Suiyuan–Baotou area. The Sanbian sub-district developed toward the enemy's strategic second-line cities in Ningxia. The Longdong sub-district drove with all its strength toward Gansu's second-line strategic cities, Lanzhou, Tianshui, Pingliang, and Xifeng. The Guanzhong sub-district made the enemy's central city of Xi'an its strategic focus and at the same time opened work along the Hanzhong line and the Sichuan-Shaanxi road. The Huanglong sub-district began work first in the nearby districts of Pucheng, Fuping, Chaoyi, and Dali, watching for the chance to reach out to Tongguan, Xi'an, and southern Shaanxi. The Fuxi sub-district opened work in the Fenzhou, Changwu, and Baoji areas, and developed toward Hanzhong, Tianshui, and the Sichuan-Shaanxi road.

These were two kinds of concentric circles. When the Communists entered Yan'an in 1937 and set up layer upon layer of defense, the circles drew inward, ring by ring. When they gave up Yan'an in 1948 and turned to the offensive, the intelligence deployment radiated outward, layer by layer. Communist intelligence work had fixed on an offensive strategy and was building a grand intelligence structure.

The Northwest Field Army had only thirty thousand men, and to slug it out with Hu Zongnan's 230,000 it still lacked the capital. There were two ways to add to that capital: first, the northern Shaanxi people's support for the Communist Party, and second, intelligence work to grasp the enemy's movements. The fighting around Yulin was fierce. In the attack on Zhenchuan the plan had been for an inside man to bring a company over in revolt and open the gate from within while the assault came from without. But once the attack began, the gate stayed shut fast, and many soldiers fell beneath the strong walls. The needless sacrifice made Commander Peng lose his temper. The Border Security men grew more cautious still: military turning work was no easy thing.

Every department of the Border Security set about building inside lines within the enemy camp, doing solid work. An inside man in Hu Zongnan's logistics system, Liu Bugu, bought a radio; another, Hao Dengge, became a county magistrate and had a radio too; but the Border Security had no operator to send them. In Longdong they recruited Liu Piqing, chief of a hidden station of the Second Section of the Northwest Field Headquarters at Zhenyuan—but the Border Security's own radio was too weak to raise him. Just as Zhou Xing was fretting over the radio problem, a radio man came knocking from inside Hu Zongnan's own camp.

Hu Zongnan had entrusted his confidential-office chief Wang Wei with training a batch of reliable radio-communications men for his troops. One of them, Lü Chu, only fifteen, was assigned on graduation to the radio of the 34th Army Group, where, far from going to the front to fight Japan, he spent his days copying down the Communists' New China News Agency broadcasts to gather intelligence. Through that job Lü Chu instead came to understand the Communists' program and grew more and more disgusted with the Nationalists. In August 1945 Lü Chu joined deputy commander Gao Shuxun's revolt in the Eleventh War Zone. Sheng Beiguang, deputy chief of the enemy-work section of the Jin-Ji-Lu-Yu Military District, set Lü Chu the task of penetrating the Nationalist forces in Xi'an.

Lü Chu returned to Xi'an in June 1947 and, on the strength of his classmates from the Xi'an communications officers' training class, entered the communications battalion of Hu Zongnan's headquarters. There he recruited six more men and formed a secret intelligence group of seven: Xue Haoran, Xu Xuezhang, Li Fuyong, Gao Jian, Wang Guanzhou, and Zhao Jixun. But Lü Chu's original Jin-Ji-Lu-Yu unit had already thrust into the Dabie Mountains, and he could not reach Sheng Beiguang. With the situation urgent and the Border Security also eager to build intelligence ties inside the enemy, Gao Bulin, the Hancheng security-section chief, sent Gao Mianzhai to Xi'an to find his fellow-townsman Gao Mengji and learn the state of Lü Chu's group. In October 1948 Lü Chu and Xue Haoran slipped off to the Border Region on the pretext of a business trip, made contact with the Jinnan Public Security General Bureau's intelligence station in Shaanxi, and passed under Border Security leadership.

Lü Chu's group was the Border Security's treasure trove of intelligence. A single delivery brought: a pair of advanced small British agent transceivers; two sets of the Second Section's codebooks; the Second Section's organizational layout and the résumés of its officers at every level; the names and addresses of the men running the Second Section's ten intelligence groups and stations ringing the Border Region; the personnel and working rules of Hu Zongnan's headquarters confidential office; the establishment, call signs, wavelengths, and contact times of the 4th Communications Regiment and of each army's and division's radios; the codes and ciphers used by the Nationalist forces in the Northwest; the designations of each of Hu Zongnan's divisions; the names, addresses, and internal telephone numbers of the Party, government, military, and secret-service chiefs in Xi'an; the names and code names of the agents dispatched by the training class of Hu Zongnan's headquarters confidential office; street maps of the Xi'an urban area and plans of the city's defense works; the situation of Hu Zongnan's newly formed strategic mobile corps, Pei Changhui's 5th Corps; the garrisons, equipment, and morale of Hu Zongnan's six armies; the internal organization of the Ministry of National Defense's Xi'an Telecommunications Monitoring Head Station and its reconnaissance of the Northwest Liberation Army's radios; the state of sinking morale within the Nationalist forces; and the infighting between Hu Zongnan and the Shaanxi provincial governor Zhu Shaozhou.

For the enemy facing the Northwest Field Army headquarters and the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office, there were no secrets left.

The Border Security set up an intelligence station of its own, with Zhou Shichao, a Long March radio operator, as its chief; it used two common texts, "The Premier's Testament" and a *Provincial Atlas*, as the codebooks for its enciphering tables. In February 1949 the station of Hu Zongnan's headquarters, worked by Li Fuyong, came through first; then the agent station of Xue Haoran came through; and on March 19 the 5th Corps station of Lü Chu, Xu Xuezhang, and Wang Guanzhou came through. The three confidential radios of the Nationalist forces were linked directly to Yan'an. The Nationalists had more than sixty monitoring stations in Xi'an, and never once suspected the waves their own radios were sending out.

The three stations sent two or three telegrams a night, and whenever an important operational telegram came, they would deliberately push back the Nationalist units' contact time by six hours while sending it to the Border Security two to four hours early. Zhang Jizu of the Border Security intelligence section kept watch by the radio every night; once a telegram was received and decoded, it went straight to Commander Peng Dehuai and Secretary Xi Zhongxun, one copy each.

Peng Dehuai and Xi Zhongxun got Hu Zongnan's battle orders earlier than his own army and division commanders.

In the Fumei campaign, where the enemy was many and our side few, Lü Chu reported Hu Zongnan's dispositions to the Border Security two weeks in advance, and his station deliberately delayed the traffic between Hu Zongnan's and Ma Hongkui's forces. The Liberation Army seized the chance to pass through the seam between the Hu and Ma armies, crossing cliffs and deep gullies and marching 150 li by night to encircle the rear, and though it fought at a disadvantage it annihilated more than 43,000 of the enemy. The Ma clique's army fell back into Gansu, and the Liberation Army pressed its advantage, taking Baoji and Lanzhou one after another.

In the Qinling campaign, Hu Zongnan, seeing the Northwest Field Army's main force advance into Gansu, tried to counterattack toward Baoji. This plan too was reported to the Border Security by the secret station, and two Liberation Army divisions hid in advance in the deep mountains and dense forests of the Qinling; Pei Changhui's corps marched straight into the ambush, and 13,000 men were wiped out in a single day.

Over some nine months, the three secret stations of Lü Chu and the others sent out more than six hundred intelligence reports. On July 20, 1949, Zhang Jizu and Yuan Xinhu, whom they had never met, served as their sponsors, and Li Qiming and Xi Zhongxun signed the approval; the news of their admission to the Party traveled by radio wave into the enemy camp, the two stations clasped hands across the air in celebration, and the new members swore their Party oath through the air.

Besides Lü Chu's group, the Border Security had several other important intelligence organizations planted inside the enemy army. Intelligence from many channels cross-checked one another and guaranteed the high quality of the military intelligence. The Communists were at a disadvantage in weapons and equipment, but in intelligence work they far outstripped their opponent.

### 3. The Most Successful Period of Intelligence Work

Before the outcome of the war was even clear, the Nationalists were already losing move after move—and this, in fact, was the result of both parties reorganizing their intelligence work at the same time.

Just as the democratic parties were denouncing the Nationalists' rule by secret agents, the Juntong chief Dai Li was killed in a plane crash on March 16, 1946. Juntong, built around one man's command, was badly shaken and was reorganized under a new name. The Ministry of National Defense's Bureau of Confidential Investigation, headed by Mao Renfeng, saw its authority and effectiveness fall sharply.

The Party Center reorganized: each major unit set up a Social Affairs Department, a Nationalist-Army Work Department, an Urban Work Department, and an intelligence section. The Center moved Kang Sheng, head of both the Central Social Affairs Department and the Central Intelligence Department, off to work on land reform, and put the work in the hands of deputy head Li Kenong, with Liu Shaowen, Tan Zhengwen, and Chen Gang as deputies.

Li Kenong was the Communists' finest intelligence expert—able to go into action himself and take intelligence directly, and possessed as well of strategic vision and a gift for organizing and planning. As soon as he took over, he deployed the Communists' intelligence strength into every strategic zone in the country.

The central cities of North China, Beiping and Tianjin, held great strategic weight. During his time with the Beiping Military Mediation office Li Kenong personally approved the building of a batch of intelligence assets inside the headquarters of the Nationalist Eleventh War Zone: Xie Shiyan, a major general and chief of operations; Ding Xing, a major general and deputy chief of the military-justice section; Shi Chun, a major and staff officer; Zhu Jianguo, acting chief of the operations subsection; and Zhao Liangzhang, a staff officer at the headquarters of the Second Air Force District, among others, who could obtain important military intelligence on the enemy in North China.

The Northeast was strategic high ground both sides had to have. The Central Social Affairs Department sent Wang Jinxiang and Zou Dapeng to be first and second directors of the Northeast Bureau's Social Affairs Department, and built an inside line inside the headquarters of the Northeast Pacification Command. Zhao Wei, who ran its confidential office, supplied the Northeast Bureau's Social Affairs Department with important secrets of the Nationalist forces in the Northeast. After Zhao Wei was exposed, Wang Shaowen carried it on.

Nanjing and Shanghai were the heartland of the Nationalists. The work of the former Southern Bureau there was taken over by the Shanghai Central Bureau. Liu Xiao and Liu Changsheng were in charge; Qian Ying brought the intelligence connections from across the south; and Zhang Zhiyi organized a turning-work committee.

Wu Kejian went to ground in Shanghai with a radio. Wu Kejian, who had been secretary of the Special Branch in 1930 and had led the Southern Bureau's intelligence work in Chongqing, returned to Shanghai in 1946 to start afresh, and quickly opened a way into the depths of the enemy camp. The Nationalists' highest and most sensitive posts were penetrated through and through: Shen Anna in the Central Party Headquarters, Wang Lifu in the Combined Logistics Headquarters, Guo Ruguai in the Ministry of National Defense's operations department, Duan Boyu in Chiang Kai-shek's personal staff office, and Communists in the Board of Military Orders, the Ministry of Military Administration, and every war-zone headquarters.

As the decisive battles came on, Mao's command post for directing the three great campaigns was set up in the little hill village of Xibaipo. Not far off, at Donghuangni, stood modern Soviet- and American-model radio stations. From here the intelligence net cast out by Li Kenong, head of the Central Intelligence Department, covered the far and wide battlefields.

The intelligence groups in the Nanjing–Shanghai belt steadily took the Nationalist Center's highest military-strategic intelligence.

The Northeast's Liaoshen campaign opened first. Communist agents were planted in the Nationalists' Northeast Pacification Command and in the operations section of Liao Yaoxiang's corps headquarters, and had the whole set of intelligence before the fighting began. During the campaign they even schemed to drive the Nationalist vanguard into the tiger's mouth. Lin Biao's command post even held Northeast Pacification commander Wei Lihuang's personally endorsed originals.

The Huaihai campaign was of the greatest difficulty; even the two great armies of Liu-Deng and Chen-Su had fewer men than the Nationalist forces facing them. Pan Hannian's system sent up "The Situation of the Xuzhou Pacification Command," and Wu Kejian's system sent up "The Ministry of National Defense's Estimate of the Huaihai Campaign" and "The Nationalist Army's Dispositions for the Xu(zhou)–Beng(bu) Battle."

Intelligence served the decisive battle. From strategic intelligence to tactical intelligence, from the whole picture to the local detail, whatever intelligence Mao wanted, Li Kenong could provide. The Communists' wartime intelligence work had reached the level of an art.

Mao said: the intelligence work of the War of Liberation was the best.

The enemy saw this too. After his defeat at Qingfengdian, Chiang Kai-shek wrote on the Ministry of National Defense's review report: "From this one can imagine the bandits' intelligence work. Before every operation they have investigated our army's situation with perfect thoroughness. Then they decide their battle plan in light of our army's circumstances and strike at us. I have studied it carefully: how they can have investigated our situation so thoroughly is, to be sure, partly because we ourselves leak secrets, but chiefly because the bandits' intelligence work is done to the last detail. The bandits have a slogan—'if the enemy's situation is unclear, do not fight'…"

### 4. The "Five Martyrs of North China"

Even in the best period of intelligence work there were losing moves.

Chiang Kai-shek launched the civil war trusting to America at his back. America not only supplied arms but gave intelligence support. The U.S. Navy and Juntong jointly ran the Sino-American Cooperative Organization, which trained senior agents for Juntong. America supplied great numbers of the newest radio direction-finding vehicles to help the Nationalists listen for the Communists' secret stations.

The signals-inspection section of the Beiping Field Headquarters detected a suspicious station signal but could never fix its exact position. Juntong assigned the cat-burglar Duan Yunpeng, who climbed walls and roofs and searched house to house by night, and found the secret station at No. 24 Jingzhao East Street.

Li Zhengxuan, the station's radio operator, broke discipline by keeping drafts of the telegrams, and betrayed still more secret stations, planted agents, and lateral connections.

The consequences of Li Zhengxuan's defection were grave in the extreme: following the thread, Juntong then arrested Wang Shijian.

The secret stations at Beiping, Xi'an, Lanzhou, and Shenyang were broken; more than a hundred agents were arrested, and only a few, Zhao Wei among them, escaped. As it happened, Chen Zhongjing, Xiong Xianghui, and Shen Jian, studying in America, were not caught by Juntong, and with the organization's help they returned to the Liberated Areas.

More than a hundred people were taken under escort to Nanjing for trial, of whom five military men were dealt with under military law. Chiang Kai-shek issued the order himself: Ding Xing, Xie Shiyan, Shi Chun, Zhu Jianguo, and Zhao Liangzhang, five men, were shot.

An intelligence system that had once been highly effective was gravely wrecked. Li Kenong drew the bitter lessons and made a self-criticism to the Center. Zhou Enlai at once adjusted the dispositions and directed that reconnaissance continue.

Having smashed the Communists' intelligence organization in North China, Chiang Kai-shek decided to stake everything on one throw.

Chiang had long tried to carry out a decapitation against Mao, but in northern Shaanxi Hu Zongnan simply could not find Mao's position. Only when Mao crossed the Yellow River from northern Shaanxi and passed over the Taihang Mountains into Hebei did Juntong pick up the trail.

Mao moved into Chengnanzhuang, and an agent planted in the headquarters of the Jin-Cha-Ji Military District quietly poisoned his food; luckily the guards would not let Mao eat the dead fish, and he escaped by a hair.

The Nationalists then sent planes to bomb; Mao had only just been carried out of the small courtyard when the bomb fell from the sky.

That was an agent scattering broken glass on the ground, to guide the target from the air.

Both decapitation attempts having failed, Mao moved to Xibaipo. Chiang Kai-shek came up with another stratagem: he had Fu Zuoyi send a fast column out of Beiping, ostensibly to relieve Taiyuan but in fact to make a lightning strike on Shijiazhuang.

The plan was quite clever. At that moment Xibaipo had no main force to guard it; even the Central Guard Regiment had been sent off to fight at Taiyuan. Fu Zuoyi gave careful instructions: among the prisoners, look for the man with tobacco-yellowed fingers—Mao was fond of smoking.

A thousand-li raid to cut out the heart and seize the commander: the civil war would be won at a single stroke.

But Xibaipo reacted at once. A New China News Agency dispatch announced: we have learned that Fu Zuoyi will attack Shijiazhuang, and we call on the army and people of the Liberated Areas to annihilate the enemy. This made Fu Zuoyi hesitate: should the surprise attack become a frontal assault?

A second dispatch laid out the enemy's attack plan in detail and called on the army and people of the Liberated Areas to lure the enemy in deep and destroy him where he gathered. What Fu Zuoyi feared was exactly this—a lone force pushing in deep and falling into ambush—and his troops slowed.

The News Agency then put out a third dispatch: Chiang Kai-shek's whole northern front, Fu Zuoyi's whole system, would probably be finished in a few months, yet here they were still dreaming their dream of Shijiazhuang. Fu Zuoyi now also learned that militia were blocking the way and that the main force of the North China Liberation Army, the 3rd Column, had come up by forced march, and he had no choice but to cancel the raid.

Military historians marvel that this feat of Mao's command was the Empty Fort Stratagem come again. In fact Mao held better cards than Zhuge Liang: he had intelligence.

The Communists' intelligence work in Beiping used multiple layers.

When Xie Shiyan and four others were arrested—five agents in all—the Eleventh War Zone headquarters held a mass meeting to hunt down the Communist spies. The lieutenant and clerk Liu Guangguo, standing there to hear the harangue, was so frightened his legs shook—for he himself was a Communist spy.

Luckily the enemy were only bluffing, and Liu Guangguo was able to stay under cover. He and his group moved from Sun Lianzhong's Eleventh War Zone into Fu Zuoyi's Twelfth War Zone, obtained in good time Fu Zuoyi's plan for the surprise attacks on Shijiazhuang and Xibaipo, and swiftly reported to their superior Ganling; this urgent intelligence sped to Xu Jianguo of the North China Social Affairs Department and to Commander Nie Rongzhen of the North China Military District, and on to Xibaipo. At the same time Liu Shiping, an underground Party member in the North China Urban Work Department, also got the intelligence through a fellow-townsman in the army.

The Central Military Commission at Xibaipo saw through Fu Zuoyi's surprise attack in good time; Zhou Enlai marshaled the troops and Mao wrote articles to expose it—how could they fail to win?

After the Wang Shijian system was wrecked, some parallel intelligence organizations still held on in the north. Yang Yindong's military intelligence group obtained Hu Zongnan's plan for the Yichuan operation and reported it up through Wang Chaobei's radio. Wang Chaobei detected a great deal of military intelligence during the War of Liberation, and the Center rewarded him with 40 taels of gold.

There is no general who always wins in open war, and in secret war too the two sides trade victories.

In the spring of 1948 the Communists' underground organization in Sichuan was gravely wrecked again. The story of loyalty and betrayal shook the soul, and later took shape as the long novel *Red Crag*.

To struggle is to have sacrifice, whether in open war or secret war.

The old intelligence hands say: the War of Liberation was the best period for intelligence work, and also the period with the most martyrs.

### 5. Subduing the Enemy Without a Fight

Mao always valued the design at the top. Whenever the strategy shifted, he set the intelligence departments policy-level requirements in advance. As the decisive battles came on, Mao raised a new demand: "Not only intelligence, but strength."

That is to say: not only use intelligence to help the battle decisions, but make the enemy defect on the field of battle.

The Communists' army had always valued political work, and its political departments had a special organ few foreign armies had: liaison. This trade changed its name more than once: Destruction Department, Enemy Work Department, Nationalist-Army Work Department, Liaison Department. The several renamings show how the working policy evolved.

The Red Army had a "Destruction Department" whose special task was to wreck the enemy army and set off mutinies. It waged class struggle, held that "we want the soldiers, not the officers," and that "the middle-of-the-roaders are the most dangerous enemy."

When the Red Army reached northern Shaanxi at the end of the Long March, it faced two strong enemies: the Northwest Army and the Northeast Army. A three-way fight would leave all three hurt, which was bad for resisting Japan and good for old Chiang, who wanted both to wipe out the Communists and to be rid of the miscellaneous armies not his own. Mao's strategy was to unite the three armies and form a separatist hold on the Northwest. So a Central Liaison Bureau was set up, charged specially with work on the Northeast Army. Mao changed the "Destruction Department" into the "Liaison Department": wanting both the soldiers and the officers, and winning over the middle-of-the-roaders. The liaison bureau chief Li Kenong slipped into enemy territory to talk in secret with the Young Marshal of the Northeast, Zhang Xueliang, about joining forces.

Liaison work was not to wreck the other side, but to unite with it, form a united front, and face the enemy together. Do not make light of that one change of name: from killing the enemy to turning the enemy, turning enemies into friends—this was a great turning point.

Make friends inside the Nationalist army? Enemies to the death in yesterday's bloody battles, now toasting one another over wine? The Red Army commanders could not adjust to it at once, so Mao took a hand himself and ran a pilot case.

The 38th Army, mainstay of the Northwest Army, had had a secret Communist Party organization since 1931, but the work had risen and fallen, chiefly because the policy for united-front work at the upper levels was unclear. Mao decided that the Communist 38th Army work committee would come under the Center's direct leadership, with Mao himself directing it on a single line. Mao personally instructed secret members like Fan Ming not to be rash and not to expose themselves. When the 38th Army commander Zhao Shoushan came to Yan'an, Mao talked with him himself, approved him as a special Party member, and even directed Fan Ming to disclose the roster of secret members to Zhao Shoushan.

Under Mao's personal guidance, the 38th Army work committee became a "model Party organization" and created "a model of upper-level united-front work." Through the War of Resistance Zhao Shoushan always cooperated with the Eighth Route Army to guard the Border Region, and when the civil war broke out this force was the first to revolt.

By turning destructive spy warfare into liaison-style united-front work, Mao created a new method of secret warfare. A saying went round the Nationalist army: "Fear nothing in heaven or earth, only fear the Communists' heart-digging talk." During the War of Resistance this one department had split in two: the Liaison Department did work on friendly Nationalist armies, and the Enemy Work Department did the work of breaking down the Japanese and puppet forces. In the civil war the two kinds of work merged into one, called the Nationalist-Army Work Department, and later the Liaison Department again.

When the British journalist James Bertram asked Mao to explain the Red Army's political work, Mao said the people's army had three great principles in its political work: "unity of army and people, unity of officers and men, and breaking down the enemy army." Toward the enemy the aim was not only to destroy but to win over politically—not as a tactical device, but raised to a basic principle. Such a principle foreigners had never heard of; it was, beyond doubt, a new method of war.

This task of breaking down the enemy army fell to the liaison departments, and was given also to the local united-front departments and others; the intelligence system took on one more heavy charge, that of "turning."

In eight years of war against Japan, Chiang Kai-shek had used the chance to prune away the regional warlords and bring every army in the land into the Nationalist order of battle. Having at last become commander of the whole country's whole army, old Chiang lost his head and put out big talk: "Wipe out the Communist Party in three months."

But Mao knew that old Chiang's host was no single slab of iron.

Strike with one hand, pull with the other. Breaking down the enemy army and instigating revolts was always an important part of the War of Liberation. The Party Center set up a Nationalist-Army Work Department under Wang Shiying (later renamed the Enemy Work Department) to take special charge. Taking part in this work were the army's political departments, the Party's Urban Work and Social Affairs Departments, and the security departments of the local governments.

On October 30, 1945, the Jin-Ji-Lu-Yu Military District under Liu Bocheng and Deng Xiaoping instigated deputy commander Gao Shuxun of the Nationalist Eleventh War Zone to lead more than ten thousand men in revolt at the front, raising the curtain on Nationalist-army revolts in the war of self-defense.

On December 15 the Party Center fixed "The Working Policy for the Liberated Areas in 1946": "On the one hand, our army will carry out open, broad political propaganda and a political offensive against the Nationalist troops, to break down the fighting will of the Nationalist civil-war army; on the other hand, we must, from within the Nationalist army, prepare and organize revolts, develop a Gao Shuxun Movement, and, at critical moments of the war, bring large numbers of Nationalist troops over to the side of the people after Gao Shuxun's example."

The "Gao Shuxun Movement" worked wonders.

Jinan, a city held to be impregnable, held out only eight days before the Liberation Army took it. American newspapers cried out: from now on the Liberation Army can take whatever city it wants to take. In fact an important reason the campaign ended early was that Wu Huawen, who held the fortress, revolted on the field.

In February 1947, at the deadlocked stage of the Laiwu campaign, the Nationalist commander of the Reorganized 46th Division, Han Liancheng, vanished from the field and threw the whole line into chaos. Far from being punished, this lieutenant general, because he had once rescued Chiang Kai-shek, was given important posts: aide-de-camp at the Presidential Office, then Lanzhou security commander. Only when the Liberation Army took Lanzhou did he turn up as deputy chief of staff of the First Field Army—an underground Communist all along.

The Huaihai campaign was the hardest of all; the two sides were close in strength and neither could easily swallow the other. At the deadlocked stage, the Nationalist deputy commanders of the Third Pacification Zone, Zhang Kexia and He Jifeng, led two armies in revolt at the front and tore open the Nationalist line. Zhang Kexia had joined the Communist Party in 1929, and He Jifeng had joined secretly during a visit to Yan'an in 1938; two old members in one and the same unit, neither knowing of the other, until at the crucial moment of the War of Liberation the Center decided to bring them into play.

The balance of forces in the Huaihai campaign shifted: 500,000 swallowed 500,000, a result that startled even Stalin.

In fact the numbers cannot be reckoned so simply. A defection on the field cannot be counted by subtraction alone; there is addition too—the figure subtracted from the enemy is added onto our side. Sometimes it even calls for multiplication: the effect of a revolt on the enemy's morale is beyond reckoning.

The power of turning was fiercer than heavy guns. Through every stage of the war in the Northeast, field revolts played a key part.

In the period when the enemy was strong and we were weak, Pan Shuoduan's Haicheng revolt of October 1946 shook the morale of the Northeast army.

In the period of stalemate, Wang Jiashan's Yingkou revolt of February 1948 changed the balance of forces.

At the decisive hour, in November 1948, Zeng Zesheng led the 60th Army in revolt at Changchun and toppled the last line of defense.

In the closing stage, the Shenyang campaign: though the defending troops did not revolt, they laid down their arms and ceased resistance.

In truth, a revolt is not easy. For a senior general who held the Nationalists to be the legitimate line to turn toward the men he had spent years hunting as bandits meant a painful passage of the heart.

Zeng Zesheng's force was besieged at Changchun, its food supply coming only by airdrop. At that point Chiang Kai-shek dropped Zeng Zesheng a letter in his own hand, calling him brother. Zeng Zesheng was touched and had the leader's handwriting mounted and framed—what an honor. Not long after, Zeng Zesheng had his adjutant burn the scroll: he would not be buried alongside a dictator.

The Communists' work on the Yunnan army had run a long road. In 1922 the noted Yunnan-army general Zhu De left his old command, went to Germany, and joined the Communist Party. In the era of the Great Revolution a group of Communists set up a political-work system inside the Yunnan army. In 1938 the Communists established a Party branch in the 60th Army, and it was never broken thereafter. When the Yunnan army reached the Northeast in 1946, the Party Center specially directed the founding of a Yunnan-Army Work Committee: "not to break down their army, but to oppose Chiang's dictatorship and to win their own survival." The Center transferred the underground couple Liu Hao and Lu Shiying from Yunnan to Yan'an, where Zhu De, Mao Zedong, and Liu Shaoqi personally briefed them on their turning task; and it sent a batch of Yunnan-born members from Yan'an to the Northeast to work on the Yunnan army through fellow-townsman ties.

Old Chiang drove men by cunning; Zhu De saved men by feeling and loyalty. At the crucial hour of the Liaoshen campaign, Zeng Zesheng decided to revolt.

The revolt still had a hard problem: the Liberation Army asked the 60th Army to take a stand against the New 7th Army, but Zeng Zesheng could not bear to raise arms against his old comrades-in-arms.

Again it was Mao who understood men's feelings; the Center wired: "Do not press them beyond what they can do."

Better to break down the enemy army than to destroy it, and to break it down you must use the great method of turning the enemy.

The Pingjin campaign created three ways of winning outright.

The liberation of Beiping, with Fu Zuoyi accepting a peaceful reorganization, was the combined fruit of the whole Party's work on the enemy army.

Fu Zuoyi was famous in the Nationalist army as a fine fighter. He was on good terms with the Eighth Route Army during the War of Resistance, yet in 1946 he was Yan'an's enemy face to face. He made a surprise attack on Jining and forced the Liberation Army to lift its siege of Datong; feinting east and striking west, he took Zhangjiakou, the largest city in the Liberated Areas. The National Assembly delegates honored Fu Zuoyi as a "restorer" of the Nationalists.

Flushed with that success, Fu Zuoyi was insufferably pleased with himself and issued an "Open Telegram to Mao Zedong." He jeered at the Liberation Army: "The day before you routed, the Yan'an broadcast had already announced that the Nationalist army in this war zone was completely encircled, completely routed, completely annihilated by you; but the next day's facts gave the cruel proof that those encircled, routed, and destroyed were not the Nationalist army, but the very forces of He Long and Nie Rongzhen you boast took part in the so-called 'Twenty-five-thousand-li Long March'…" And he rebuked Mao: "If they, under your mistaken leadership, made war and stirred up chaos to ruin the country and harm the people, then it is you who killed them; in the still of the night you should feel the blame, and should suffer the punishment of the whole nation."

General Fu had denounced Mao. The brilliant piece called to mind the manifesto Chen Lin drafted against Cao Cao in the Three Kingdoms, and the one Luo Binwang drafted against Wu Zetian in the Tang. The *Central Daily News* printed it in full under a large headline: "Fu Zuoyi Wires Mao Zedong to End the Fighting and Join the Government." General Fu, master of both pen and sword, was renowned throughout the land.

What Fu Zuoyi did not know was that the great scholar who drafted this manifesto for him, Yan Youwen, was a Communist planted at his very side. Having been given the task of drafting the telegram, Yan Youwen asked the organization for instructions, and Zhou Enlai directed: make the open telegram scold hard, so as to rouse the indignation of the army and people of the Liberated Areas, and to lead Fu Zuoyi into arrogance.

The able and battle-hardened Fu Zuoyi meant to make a surprise strike on Shijiazhuang and win fresh glory, but fell victim to Mao's Empty Fort Stratagem. Back in Beiping, Fu Zuoyi soon found himself trapped: a great army ringing the city outside, and secret forces closing in within. His sworn brother Zeng Yanyi, his fellow-townsman and friend Du Renzhi, and his old teacher Liu Houtong were all persuaders of the North China Urban Work Department; even his eldest daughter Fu Dongju was an underground Communist. And unknown to him, the negotiators Fu Zuoyi himself sent out, Li Bingquan and Yan Youwen, were in fact underground Communists too.

Fu Zuoyi's smallest thoughts, even a single flash of anger or a single hesitation, were reported in good time to the Liberation Army's Pingjin front headquarters and to the Communist headquarters at Xibaipo. The secret work was fine to the last detail, and in the end it won over this vanguard of the civil war. His heart truly convinced, Fu Zuoyi went in person to Suiyuan to persuade and direct the peaceful revolt of Dong Qiwu's forces.

Mao summed up three ways of settling the enemy army: the "Tianjin way," settled by fighting; the "Beiping way," peaceful reorganization; and the "Suiyuan way," keeping the existing establishment.

By the Suiyuan way, Dong Qiwu's forces were reorganized as the Liberation Army's 20th Corps, with Dong Qiwu as corps commander. When ranks were conferred across the whole army after the founding of the state, Dong Qiwu, a full general in Nationalist days, remained a full general.

The Nationalist lieutenant general Chen Mingren rose in the Liberation Army to full general.

Chen Mingren had blood feud with the Communists: this was the very man who beat back Lin Biao's forces at the battle of Siping. And Chen Mingren had merit with the Communists: this was the very man who led his troops in revolt in Hunan and welcomed Lin Biao into the city. Worth noting is that Chen Mingren's force was Central Army—and the Central Army was old Chiang's own reserve of strength.

Most who had revolted before this were regional troops. The Hengshan, Gao Shuxun, and Zhang Kexia revolts were all remnants of the Northwest Army, which had long resented old Chiang. Zeng Zesheng was Yunnan army, Fu Zuoyi was Shanxi army, Liu Wenhui and Deng Xihou were Sichuan army; to old Chiang these regional troops were stepchildren. Through the Communists' work, every regional faction in China took the road of turning against Chiang, and only the Central Army was hard to move.

Now, with a model like Chen Mingren, Mao made a point of being photographed with him in Beiping, and had the picture printed in the newspapers so the not-yet-revolted Central Army commanders could see: the Communists would take even a man like Chen Mingren.

He who wins men's hearts wins the realm. In the later stage of the War of Liberation the Communists, working along many social threads at once, brought over great numbers of Nationalist military and civil officials, and drew whole batches of enemy troops into the Liberation Army's order of battle.

The Border Security assets planted in Hu Zongnan's forces fell back with Pei Changhui's corps. The corps operations chief Li Fukui and chief of staff Li Zhusheng decisively took control of the corps headquarters and stopped Pei Changhui on the road to talk him round. After Pei Changhui revolted he actively urged Hu Zongnan's own 38th Army to revolt, further hastening the great collapse of Hu Zongnan's forces.

Even the Nationalists' modernized units turned one after another to the "country Eighth-Routers" with their millet and rifles.

The air force had been the Nationalists' "darling of heaven," holding absolute superiority in the air. Yet early in 1947 the air force pilot Liu Shanben flew his plane down at Yan'an and set off a nationwide sensation. Yan'an instructed Shanghai to have Pan Hannian look well after the pilot's wife Zhou Shuhuang, who had stayed in Nanjing. Hua Kezhi, posing as a journalist, ran the agents' blockade to convey the Communists' concern to Zhou Shuhuang. In Liu Shanben's wake, Nationalist warplane after warplane took the risk and flew north.

The navy too was one of the Nationalists' steel fortresses, holding fast the Yangtze defense line. The Yangtze is China's natural barrier; the South-North divisions that recurred through Chinese history all stemmed from ruling by this dividing line. Chiang Kai-shek mustered land, sea, and air forces to build the Yangtze line, yet could not stop the Liberation Army, with only wooden boats, from crossing. The Jiangyin fortress held the narrowest reach of the Yangtze, its shore batteries powerful; yet the shells fired from here, far from hitting the Liberation Army's wooden boats, struck the Nationalist defense works on the bank instead. The Second Fleet patrolling the Yangtze also revolted on the field under Lin Zun, and became the main force in building the East China Navy.

The Ministry of National Defense's Reserve Officers Bureau was headed personally by the crown prince, Chiang Ching-kuo, and the young officers of its training corps were to form a Youth Army of three hundred thousand for him. Yet Chiang Ching-kuo's most trusted deputy chief, Jia Yibin, led this officers' corps in revolt at the critical moment. Even Chiang Kai-shek's household guard, the Capital Guard Division, revolted under the Communist Nanjing underground's instigation.

Cheng Qian and Chen Mingren revolted in Hunan; Long Yun and Lu Han in Yunnan; Tao Zhiyue in Xinjiang; the cruiser *Chongqing* in the navy; the two Hong Kong airlines in civil aviation; the National Government's Resources Commission…

The Liberation Army annihilated 8.617 million of the enemy in all; of these, the Nationalist troops that revolted, surrendered, accepted peaceful reorganization, or laid down their arms of their own accord came to 1.89 million in 320 instances, and that 21 percent was precisely the fruit of turning work.

"To subdue the enemy without fighting is the finest skill of all." The highest realm of the Chinese strategist is not slaughter, but winning without fighting—"stopping the fighting is the true martial virtue."

Mao himself claimed three great treasures: the leadership of the Party, armed struggle, and the united front.

And how formidable the united front was: using every relationship that could be used, making friends with everyone who could be befriended, hidden and formless in secret, turning enemies into friends in the open, so that a thousand armies and ten thousand horses served our ends.

### 6. Death of a Lieutenant General

Do not suppose that the enemy army always collapses like a landslide. Do not suppose that turning is merely a matter of standing high and giving a shout.

Hao Pengju revolted early and was treated very well, honored as "Commander-in-Chief of the China Democratic Allied Army"; his force kept its old command system, the Liberation Army did not reorganize it, and only sent in a few political cadres. But the Liberation Army's tolerance did not buy Hao Pengju's sincerity. Once the Nationalists counterattacked, that turncoat general turned again, and even seized Zhu Kejing to hand over to old Chiang for a reward. Zhu Kejing was an old Party member from the era of the Great Revolution who had devoted his whole life to breaking down the enemy army, and now he died at his liaison post.

Chen Yi directed a counterattack, and Hao Pengju, only a month into his betrayal, was captured again. This time there could be no more pardon: public trial and the firing squad.

Turning always needs someone to go into the enemy camp and talk him round—to go alone, sword in hand, into the lion's den.

The Juntong major general Zhou Gao would go alone into the lion's den for the Communists too. During the War of Resistance Zhou Gao had gone to ground in Nanjing, always working with the secret Communist Xu Chuguang; by the War of Liberation he was resolved to cross into the Communist camp. Eager for merit, Zhou Gao meant to turn Sun Liangcheng. The organization warned Zhou Gao to mind his safety: if the other man turned on him, the persuader would lose his head. Zhou Gao went bravely all the same, and Sun Liangcheng did indeed turn on him.

A Juntong major general too gave his life for the Liberation Army's turning work, and Zhou Gao was commemorated as a martyr.

Martyr—for the Chinese, this is the highest tribute to one who has given his life.

Mao said: "The intelligence work of the War of Liberation was the most successful."

That verdict was hard-won. Communist intelligence work was founded in the period of the Second Revolutionary Civil War, a period of resolute struggle and cruel sacrifice. It matured in the War of Resistance, a period of great progress that yet could not be pushed to the full. In the War of Liberation it changed suddenly and violently: every past failure turned into experience, and all the strength stored up over the years came alive—truly the right time, the right ground, and the harmony of men.

The wholly victorious War of Liberation had its dark passages too. This most successful period of intelligence work was also the period with the most fallen intelligence workers. In East China there was the martyr Li Bai; in Chongqing, Zhang Luping and the other seven martyrs who penetrated Juntong; in North China, the five martyrs who penetrated the Eleventh War Zone at Beiping: Ding Xing, Xie Shiyan, Zhao Liangzhang, Zhu Jianguo, and Shi Chun; in northern Shaanxi, the martyr Mao Peichun, who penetrated Juntong…

The place with the most martyrs was, most likely, Taiwan.

There is a forest park in the Western Hills of Beijing, and in 2013 a Nameless Heroes Plaza appeared there.

The names of more than eight hundred martyrs are carved on a stone wall; all were Communist intelligence workers who fell in Taiwan. Nameless heroes hidden for years, they at last won lasting fame.

Most striking are four statues: Wu Shi, Zhu Feng, Chen Baocang, and Nie Xi. The woman Communist Zhu Feng was a secret courier; the other three passed openly as Nationalist officers: Wu Shi and Chen Baocang were lieutenant generals, and Nie Xi a lieutenant colonel.

Of the martyrs in the intelligence system, Wu Shi held the highest Nationalist rank. A lieutenant general and deputy chief of the general staff at the Ministry of National Defense, in a post that gave command of all of Taiwan's military dispositions, his intelligence value was immense.

In China's military world Wu Shi was a model professional soldier. Born to a poor scholar's family in Minhou, Fujian, he joined the Northern Expedition student army as a young man and entered the Baoding Military Academy in 1915, graduating at the very top. That Baoding academy was China's first military school, and it produced the Nationalist commander-in-chief Chiang Kai-shek. Among Wu Shi's classmates of the third class were noted Nationalist generals Zhang Zhizhong, Bai Chongxi, He Jian, and Zhang Zhen. Wu Shi then studied in Japan at the artillery school and the Army War College. The War College trained officers, whereas Chiang Kai-shek attended only the Japanese army's cadet school. From that top institution, which had trained all of the Japanese army's senior generals, Wu Shi graduated first in 1934.

Back home, Wu Shi became a chief instructor at the Army War College, China's highest military school. A master of both Chinese and foreign military science, he wrote a dozen or so military works. After the Mukden Incident, foreseeing that China and Japan must go to war, he devoted himself to studying Japanese intelligence and wrote "The Second Staff Office Primer." The "Second Staff Office" was the army's intelligence department, and this "primer" was a textbook of military intelligence. In the 1939 Battle of Nomonhan the Soviets urgently needed intelligence on the Japanese army, and the Japanese generals facing the Soviet forces were all Wu Shi's classmates from the Japanese War College. At that time China and the Soviet Union had an intelligence cooperation, and the intelligence on the Japanese army that Wu Shi supplied helped the Soviet forces to victory.

Indifferent to fame and gain, Wu Shi set his whole personal will on military affairs and took no interest in party strife. Such a soldier was always kept by Chiang Kai-shek as a senior staff officer in charge of operations and intelligence, but never given the chance to command a great army. It was as well that Wu Shi did not covet power and could still concentrate on soldiering and resist Japan with energy. But once the War of Resistance was won, even a man as little concerned with politics as Wu Shi saw Chiang Kai-shek's corruption and decline and could bear it no longer. This from the heart he could speak only to He Sui.

He Sui, a fellow Minhou man six years Wu Shi's senior, had an even longer record of service. He took part in the revolution in 1904, went to Europe and America in 1917 to observe the First World War, and in 1924 was a lieutenant general and air force commander, chief of staff of the Third Army, and acting head of the Whampoa Academy. This Fujian-born general loved poetry, calligraphy, and painting, kept a wide circle, and in the War of Resistance became a good friend of the Communists, on close terms with Zhou Enlai and Ye Jianying. He Sui, who helped the Communists' work, never joined the Party himself, yet his three sons and one daughter were all underground members—a veritable "family of intelligence." The He and Wu families were old friends; the two households not only visited back and forth but pointed each other the way.

Early in 1947 Wu Shi talked long with He Sui and resolved to turn against Chiang. He Sui passed Wu Shi's attitude on to Zhang Zhiyi, head of turning work at the Communists' Shanghai Bureau. Among Nationalist generals Wu Shi had not come to it early, and he was a man Chiang Kai-shek and Bai Chongxi trusted; could such a man be used? Zhang Zhiyi, who had both done underground work and fought in the field, had the bearing of a great commander, and decided at once to take Wu Shi in. In April the Shanghai Bureau chiefs Liu Xiao, Liu Changsheng, and Zhang Zhiyi gave Wu Shi a banquet at the Cathay Hotel, and from then on Wu Shi secretly accepted Communist leadership.

He Sui's son He Kang served as liaison between Wu Shi and Zhang Zhiyi, and Wu Shi reported enemy intelligence to Zhang Zhiyi mostly at He Sui's home. Wu Shi, as head of the Ministry of National Defense's Historical Bureau, had charge of more than five hundred crates of secret archives that were to be shipped to Taiwan; Wu Shi cleverly asked that they be sent first to Fujian, and in the end left a great store of secrets for the Liberation Army. In March 1949 the "Yangtze River Defense Deployment Map" that Wu Shi handed to He Kang gave the unit numbers down to the regiment. Zhang Zhen, chief of staff of the Third Field Army, said this intelligence was a great help to the crossing of the Yangtze.

The Party organization greatly valued Wu Shi's intelligence contribution and asked him to assist in the liberation of Fujian and then return to his own camp. But Wu Shi asked, of his own accord, to go to Taiwan and give his strength for the complete liberation of China.

At a Shanghai dance hall, three Communists saw Wu Shi off: Zhang Zhiyi, He Kang, and He Kang's wife Miao Xixia. Wu Shi sang a stirring, sorrowful song and recited: "The wind blows chill over the Yi River; once the brave man leaves, he returns no more…"

Set on death, he went bravely to die. This was the heroic spirit of the hidden fighter.

On June 10, 1950, Wu Shi was executed at Machangding in Taipei; with him went Zhu Feng, Chen Baocang, Nie Xi, and others.

To prepare for the liberation of Taiwan, every Communist intelligence system sent agents there. The situation was urgent, and to bring back intelligence in good time they had to disregard sacrifice; more than a thousand were arrested one after another.

The woman Party member Xiao Minghua, in her last testament, arranged for her own bones thus: "Just let her stay in Taiwan."

The courier Liu Guangdian had the luck to escape the great arrests, hid in the deep mountains, and held out alone for four years. When the agents seized Liu Guangdian they were astonished, and tried again and again to win him over. But Liu Guangdian held firm and would not turn, and in the end he was killed in 1959.

Who can say how many more martyrs' honored names the Nameless Heroes Plaza in the Western Hills should still add?

### 7. The Great Enterprise of Founding the Nation

With the three great campaigns won, the whole military situation of the country was settled, and the focus of the Communists' intelligence and security work in the later War of Liberation carried out two shifts: from the countryside to the cities, and from the military to the political.

China's cities had always been strongholds of Nationalist rule, and the Communists had had to build rural revolutionary bases first and then surround the cities from the countryside. That revolutionary road was quite successful; by the later War of Liberation the Communists already controlled the country's vast countryside and many small and medium cities, and Nationalist rule had been squeezed into a few large and medium cities. The Party Center reversed the old "left" ways of white-area struggle and adjusted the deployment of urban work in good time. After all-out civil war broke out, Zhou Enlai, a secretary of the Central Secretariat, concurrently headed the Central Urban Work Department, and organizations everywhere facing the cities set up urban-work departments. The underground Party organizations lurking in the Nationalist areas adjusted their organization and cadres, and the newly fixed working policy was "keep hidden and lean, lie in wait for the long term, store up strength, and bide the moment." The working method joined open work with secret work, and legal work with illegal work. In organizational relations they deployed in depth along a first, second, and third line, separating city from country, upper from lower, and open work from secret work. In this way the Communists' urban work too took on a full set of methods.

Just as the military opened people's war, the cities too set off mass struggle on a large scale. The Nationalists, relying on America, signed an unequal Sino-American Commercial Treaty; American troops entered China's cities, and outrages against Chinese citizens came one after another—even the rape of the Peking University student Shen Chong. Students rose up in protest across the country, and the mass movement swept dozens of large and medium cities.

In February 1947 Zhou Enlai raised the mass movement in the Nationalist areas to the level of a "Second Front," proposing that in the Nationalist areas the call be to struggle for survival, tied, as the situation developed, to fitting political slogans. On May 20, the opening day of the fourth session, third meeting of the Nationalist People's Political Council, students in Nanjing, Shanghai, Beiping, and elsewhere held a great "Anti-Hunger, Anti-Civil-War, Anti-Persecution" march; the Nationalists sent out military police to suppress it, and in the capital, Nanjing, more than five hundred students were injured. The "May 20 Bloodshed" roused the anger of the whole nation, and students in more than sixty cities of the Nationalist areas took to the streets in protest.

The mass movement in the Nationalist areas went by three steps: first anti-America, then anti-Chiang, then support for the Communists. Nationalist rule in the cities tottered, and they were forced to pull a tenth of their strength back from the front to keep order in the cities. Mao pointed out: within China there are now two fronts. The war between Chiang Kai-shek's invading army and the People's Liberation Army is the first front, and now a second front has appeared, which is the sharp struggle between the great and just student movement and the Chiang Kai-shek government.

In the very heartland of Nationalist rule, hearts too turned to the Communists. This changed China's political landscape most profoundly of all.

With victory in the War of Resistance, a batch of centrist parties such as the Democratic League emerged in China, urging the Nationalists and Communists to make peace and form a coalition government. The Nationalists tore up the peace agreement and launched all-out civil war, forcing all of China's centrist forces to choose their own political direction.

On October 7, 1947, the Nationalists shot Du Bincheng, the Democratic League's Shaanxi chairman; on November 6 the League was forced to declare its own dissolution. On March 29, 1948, the Nationalists convened a National Assembly in Nanjing, and with no Communists or democratic parties like the League taking part, Chiang Kai-shek was elected president. On April 30 the Nanjing National Assembly closed.

On that same day the Party Center's Secretariat met at Chengnanzhuang in Pingshan, Hebei, and, while discussing the Central Plains war situation, put forward the "May Day Slogans." One of these slogans was quite fresh: "Fifth, let the various democratic parties, people's organizations, and public-spirited figures swiftly convene a Political Consultative Conference, to discuss and realize the convening of a People's Congress and the founding of a democratic coalition government." That is to say, the Communists meant not only to wipe out the Nationalist army but to seize Nationalist power, to build their own government in due form and lead China openly and squarely.

The premise for founding a government was to convene a Political Consultative Conference and complete the legal procedure. To convene such a conference could not mean one-party dictatorship as under the Nationalists, but required inviting the democratic parties and democratic figures to gather and take counsel together on the affairs of state. But if the Communists were to hold the conference in the Liberated Areas, the democratic parties' delegates were far away at the ends of the earth. The Democratic League chairman Zhang Lan was under Nationalist house arrest in Shanghai; the Revolutionary Committee chairman Li Jishen had taken refuge in Hong Kong. The Communists had to find a way to bring these democratic-party leaders to the Liberated Areas in the north for the conference—and that meant passing through the Nationalists' many blockades.

Sending the army south, sending the politics north: Zhou Enlai deployed the work on both fronts at once.

To bring the democratic figures out of Beiping, Tianjin, and Shanghai in the Nationalist-ruled areas, and out of British-ruled Hong Kong, was a task out of the ordinary and full of hardship. These people were the very top figures of the Chinese political world, and had now become political counters in the Nationalist-Communist contest, watched by public opinion and watched by the agents, with many eyes fixed on them at every moment.

Zhou Enlai decided to bring the secret forces into play. The deputy head of the Social Affairs Department Pan Hannian and the head of the Eighth Route Army's Hong Kong office Qian Zhiguang slipped into Hong Kong and, together with the Communist Hong Kong sub-bureau secretary Fang Fang, the sub-bureau's united-front chief Lian Guan, and Rao Zhangfeng, Zhang Hanfu, Xia Yan, and others, took joint charge of moving the democratic figures Li Jishen, Shen Junru, He Xiangning, Tan Pingshan, Liu Yazi, and Zhang Bojun out of Hong Kong.

Wu Kejian's intelligence system in Shanghai put all its strength into protecting and escorting Song Qingling, Zhang Lan, Luo Longji, Shi Liang, and others.

Traveling in disguise, screened by inside lines, slipping cleverly through checkpoints, braving the sea… one by one these democratic figures, none of them skilled at secret action, crossed a thousand li of danger under the secret system's guard and gathered safely in the Communist-led Liberated Areas.

The new Political Consultative Conference opened on schedule, and the new state was born without a hitch. The hidden front, too, could serve the great enterprise of founding the nation.

During the War of Liberation the intelligence and security system had three great tasks: obtaining military intelligence, turning the enemy army, and preparing to take over the cities. Military intelligence came, sheet by sheet, to the great commander's hand, and the Nationalist troops came over, batch by batch, arms in hand. But how would the work inside the cities go?

The Nationalist agents would not concede defeat. Facing the coming loss of the great cities, the agent organs made "destruction" and "lurking" dispositions. Blow up the factories, blow up the bridges, and leave a crippled city. Plant agents, plant radios, and bury unseen time bombs.

Taking over the cities and taking over political power would become a grave contest between the Communist intelligence and security organs and their adversaries.

The Yan'an cadres were said to be mostly "country bumpkins" from the gullies.

Could "country bumpkins" enter the cities and govern?

### Principal Sources

Wang Dongxing: former head of the Central Guard Bureau, director of the General Office of the Central Committee, and vice-chairman of the Central Committee; interviewed April 24, 1995. As the man responsible for security in the Central Front Committee column on behalf of the Social Affairs Department, Wang Dongxing concurrently served as the column's deputy chief of staff. During the war he kept a diary by flashlight, of which the "fighting across northern Shaanxi" passages were published first. During the "Cultural Revolution" Jiang Qing tried to get hold of these diaries, but Mao Zedong would not agree.

Mu Fengyun: former head of the General Bureau of Frontier Defense; interviewed July 10, 2001. During the fighting across northern Shaanxi, Mu Fengyun did security work in the Central Front Committee column throughout, first as investigation-section chief and then, taking over from Liu Jianfu, as guard-section chief. When Fu Zuoyi attacked Shijiazhuang, Mu Fengyun burned his own diary of following Chairman Mao across northern Shaanxi and the photographs Ye Zilong had taken of the Chairman.

Luo Qingchang: former head of the Central Investigation Department; interviewed November 11, 1999. The author had taken on the task of scripting the film *Sharing Weal and Woe* and urgently needed to understand how Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai had, during the War of Liberation, prepared for the convening of the new Political Consultative Conference. The telegrams then exchanged between the Center and the underground Party organizations in the enemy-occupied areas all used code words that no one today could read. But Luo Qingchang, past eighty, relying on memory, quickly straightened out the historical truth from dozens of telegrams. After the founding of the state Luo Qingchang served as head of the Central Investigation Department, deputy secretary-general of the State Council, and deputy director of the Premier's Office.

Tong Xiaopeng: *Forty Years of Wind and Rain*, Central Party Literature Press. This book gives a full and accurate account of how Zhou Enlai organized electronic warfare during the fighting across northern Shaanxi. After the founding of the state Tong Xiaopeng served as deputy head of the Central United Front Work Department and director of the Premier's Office.

Dai Jingyuan: former head of the Third Department of the General Staff; interviewed in Beijing on March 1, 1993. Dai Jingyuan had done intelligence work since the Jiangxi Soviet days of 1933, and headed the Military Commission's Second Bureau during the Third Revolutionary Civil War. In the early years after the founding of the state he was struggled against and transferred away, and wrote an influential book, *Recollections of the Long March*. Later Ye Jianying brought this intelligence expert back from local work to the army. Ye Jianying once said: in the War of Liberation the Second Bureau's work was the best.

Lü Chu: former deputy head of the Xinjiang Department of Security; interviewed May 18, 2006. Because for a long time his superior Sheng Beiguang could not be found to confirm it, the date of Lü Chu's joining the revolution was once counted late, but was later confirmed.

Yu Sang: former deputy minister of public security; interviewed May 4, 1994. As the cadre in charge of leading Lü Chu's intelligence group, Yu Sang once wrote a letter of commendation at the time.

Kai Cheng: *Li Kenong: The Outstanding Leader of the Communists' Hidden Front*, China Friendship Publishing. At the start of the War of Liberation Li Kenong took over the Social Affairs Department and the Intelligence Department from Kang Sheng.

Zhao Wei: former deputy bureau chief of the Central Investigation Department; interviewed March 7, 2008. Zhao Wei was recruited by Zhu Jianguo, joined the Wang Shijian intelligence system, went to ground as a staff officer in the operations section of the Nationalists' Northeast Pacification Command, and detected important operational intelligence. When the Wang Shijian system was wrecked and Zhu Jianguo and the others were arrested, Zhao Wei escaped alone and went over to the Liberation Army.

Guo Ruguai: *The Memoirs of Guo Ruguai*, CCP Party History Press. Guo Ruguai came out of Whampoa, supported the Communists during the "April 12" coup of 1927, and afterward went to Japan to study. In the War of Resistance he fought bravely and won the trust of Chen Cheng and Chiang Kai-shek, who gave him the important post of operations department chief. In the civil war he provided intelligence to the Communists.

Yao Wenbin: former cadre of the Central Investigation Department; interviewed May 11, 2010. Yao Wenbin was an important member of the Wang Shijian system and chief of the Shenyang secret station. Arrested by the Nationalist agent organs in 1947, he returned to the organization after liberation and underwent review. The old man of ninety said earnestly: "I have not the least complaint about the organization's review." "Now that I have told you, I can go!"

Ding Lingwu: son of Ding Xing; wrote a letter on May 7, 2006. Three months after Ding Xing went to prison his son was born, and the martyr, before his death, named the boy "Lingwu." Lingwu and his sister were sent to the Military Commission's nursery, and Lingwu, grown up, took up work in science and technology.

Xie Peng: son of Xie Shiyan; interviewed May 13, 2008. After Xie Shiyan's death his wife fell ill and died, leaving a daughter and a son, who were taken in by the Central Investigation Department's deputy head Zou Dapeng.

Zhang Dingzhong: *The Secret War of the Founding*. Liu Jinchang, chief of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Baoding station, used fellow-townsman ties to recruit Liu Jinchang, a deserter from the Jin-Cha-Ji 4th Column; Liu Jinchang's Fuping group then recruited a fellow-townsman, Liu Congwen, who worked in the Jin-Cha-Ji headquarters. This Liu Congwen was the mess officer of the headquarters' special kitchen, arranging the meals of Nie Rongzhen and Mao Zedong directly. Zhang Dingzhong handled the interrogation and closing of the case, and this book records it in full detail.

Liu Guangguo: former deputy head of the South China Investigation Department; interviewed August 17, 2009. Liu Guangguo and the others successfully penetrated Sun Lianzhong's Eleventh War Zone from its founding, later moved into Fu Zuoyi's Twelfth War Zone, and kept a constant hold on the operational intelligence of the North China Nationalist army.

Yang Zhe: *A Documentary Record of the Underground Struggle of the Communists' East Sichuan and Sichuan-Xikang Organizations on the Eve of Liberation*. The wrecking of the East Sichuan Provisional Committee and the Sichuan-Xikang Special Committee by Nationalist agents was a great loss to the southern underground Party in the War of Liberation. The famous novel *Red Crag* is a vivid expression of this stretch of history.

*The Memoirs of Fan Ming*: During the War of Resistance Fan Ming long did liaison work with friendly armies in northern Shaanxi, and under Mao Zedong's direct guidance led the 38th Army's Party branch. In the War of Liberation he served as liaison-department head of the Northwest Field Army, and when Mao met Fan Ming he set out the turning policy: "Turn enemies into friends, and friends into our own."

Han Jing, Li Yingxuan, and Bing Zhe: *An Illustrated Life of Han Liancheng*, Central Party Literature Press. Han Liancheng had early on applied to Liu Zhidan to join the Party but lost contact; in 1942 he established secret ties with Zhou Enlai in Chongqing, and after the war, taking over Hainan, secretly protected the Communists' Qiongya guerrillas; he later became a Liberation Army lieutenant general.

Gai Jun: *A History of the Chinese Communist Party's Struggle in the White Areas*, People's Publishing House. This book gives a full account of the Communists' secret work in the Nationalist-ruled areas, including military intelligence work in the War of Liberation, the turning of Nationalist troops, and the lying-in-wait for the right moment.

Wang Zhengming: former cadre of the Shanghai Municipal Public Security Bureau; interviewed September 14, 2011. Work on the secret front was cross-cutting and combined: security, intelligence, and liaison were often in one department. As intelligence-section chief of the New Fourth Army, Wang Zhengming did turning work during the War of Liberation, made contact for Wu Huawen's revolt in the Jinan campaign, and directed the Jiangyin fortress revolt on the spot in the crossing-the-Yangtze campaign.

Xia Jicheng: *The Secret War of the Huaihai Campaign*. This book vividly records the whole picture of the East China Field Army's turning work.

Wu Xueya (Zhou Gao's wife) and Wu Yalong (Zhou Gao's daughter): interviewed May 4, 2013. From Juntong major general to Liberation Army martyr, Zhou Gao's road was out of the ordinary. After Zhou Gao's death the organization posthumously recognized him as a martyr, and his wife worked in the Shanghai public-security system.

*The Collected Works of Zhang Zhiyi*: From the turn of spring and summer in 1946 to the end of 1948, Zhang Zhiyi, on behalf of the Shanghai Bureau, entered Taiwan four times to inspect and set up work, probably the highest-ranking Communist cadre to enter Taiwan.

Zhang Jisheng, Zhang Haisheng, and Zhang Mosheng: children of Zhang Zhiyi; interviewed June 10, 2013. Zhang Zhiyi's article recalling the Shanghai Bureau's work first disclosed Wu Shi's true identity.

*He Sui's Posthumous Papers: From the Xinhai Revolution into New China*: This book records He Sui's many contributions to the Chinese revolution, including his recruitment of Wu Shi. He Sui came of a revolutionary family; his sons He Shiyong, He Shiping, and He Kang and his daughter He Jia all joined the Communist Party before the founding of the state.

He Kang: former minister of agriculture; interviewed June 13, 2013. He Kang joined the Party in Chongqing in 1938, was later transferred to Shanghai for intelligence work, and served as Wu Shi's liaison. At the end of 1949 He Kang took an open post at the Shanghai Public Security Bureau, which forced his father He Sui, then lying hidden in Taiwan to cover Wu Shi, to withdraw.

He Jia: interviewed June 14, 2013. Wu Shi, lying hidden in Taiwan, had to relay his intelligence to the mainland through Hong Kong, and He Jia handled the contact and reception in Hong Kong.

Hao Zaijin: *Consultative Democracy*, Jincheng Press. This book gives the first full account of how the democratic-party leaders and democratic figures went north to take part in the new Political Consultative Conference and to build the state in consultation with the Communists. On Fu Zuoyi's surprise strike at Shijiazhuang, and on how Li Jishen, Zhang Lan, and others were protected by the Communist intelligence system and passed secretly through the Nationalist blockade, it gives vivid accounts.
