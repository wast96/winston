## Chapter 10. The Maze Tower

The soft grass was soaked through with dew; the night had deepened further.

Huo Tianqing crossed the garden slowly, the lamplight from the little tower far off falling on his pale, worn face. He looked very weary; lonely, and weary.

The green water of the lotus pond was a mirror, holding the reflected starlight and moonlight of the whole sky; and with his hands clasped behind him he stood at the head of the nine-turn bridge, and as a breath of wind passed, a single leaf fell.

He bent, picked up the fallen leaf, and said of a sudden, "You've come."

"I've come."

When Huo Tianqing raised his head, he saw Lu Xiaofeng.

Lu Xiaofeng had drifted in over the wall like a fallen leaf, and come to rest on the far side of the lotus pond, watching Huo Tianqing in his turn.

Ten zhang of lotus pond lay between them; yet the distance from the one to the other seemed to them very near.

Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "You seem to have been waiting for me?"

"I was waiting for you," said Huo Tianqing.

"You knew I'd come?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Huo Tianqing nodded. "I knew you could not but come."

"Why?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"After you left," said Huo Tianqing, "a great many things happened here."

"A great many things?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"You don't know?" said Huo Tianqing.

"I know of only one," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"You know Dugu Yihe died here?" said Huo Tianqing.

Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "But I don't know whether he truly deserved to die."

Huo Tianqing was silent, then of a sudden sighed too. "You'll not know, of course, that his death had to do with me as well."

"Oh?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"But for me," said Huo Tianqing, "he might never have died under Ximen Chuixue's sword at all!"

"Oh?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"I've never had any love for a man puffed up with his own importance," said Huo Tianqing; "and Dugu, as it happened, was just such a man. So, before Ximen Chuixue came, he had already crossed hands with me."

"I know it," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Huo Tianqing was much surprised. "You know it? How could you know?"

Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "When Dugu crossed hands with Ximen, he had at most half his true force left; and there aren't many hereabouts who could have drained half his force from him."

Huo Tianqing nodded slowly. "Just so; that much you might well have worked out."

"Is there another thing I haven't worked out?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Huo Tianqing nodded.

Lu Xiaofeng smiled again. "No matter if I can't work it out. What I want to know now is only this: where is Shangguan Danfeng?"

"That is the very thing you haven't worked out," said Huo Tianqing.

"What thing?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"She never came here," said Huo Tianqing, "and I fear she won't be coming at all!"

Lu Xiaofeng stood stunned; he had indeed never thought that Shangguan Danfeng would not be here.

"You may wonder," said Huo Tianqing, "how I come to know she won't be coming."

"I do wonder," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"When you've read this letter," said Huo Tianqing, "you may wonder no longer."

And he did indeed draw a letter from his sleeve, and toss it out; and the letter came drifting toward Lu Xiaofeng like a wisp of cloud.

Danfeng is hard to win; little Phoenix, turn back. Turn not back, and your life is hard to keep.

There were but these sixteen words on the letter, the writing very fine, the paper very choice.

On the envelope was written, of all things: "To be delivered to Lu Xiaofeng."

"This letter was meant for you," said Huo Tianqing; "and now I've given it to you."

"But I don't see what it means," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"It means," said Huo Tianqing lightly, "that you'll have a hard time finding Shangguan Danfeng now; so you'd best turn back in good time and leave this business alone, or someone will have your life."

And he knew well enough, of course, that Lu Xiaofeng understood the meaning too.

"Who was it asked you to pass this letter to me?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"I don't know!" said Huo Tianqing.

"You don't know either?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"If you'd written such a letter and had me pass it to another," said Huo Tianqing, "would you have handed it to me to my face?"

"No," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"So the man who wrote this letter did not hand it to me to my face either," said Huo Tianqing. "I merely found it beneath Boss Yan's spirit-tablet; of the rest I know nothing."

Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "You wouldn't know, of course."

"But you ought to," said Huo Tianqing.

"Ought to know what?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"To know who wrote it," said Huo Tianqing.

Lu Xiaofeng smiled wryly. "I only hope it wasn't Boss Yan writing from inside his coffin."

Huo Tianqing's eyes flickered. "You ought to know, too, that besides Boss Yan there's one other who'd not have you meddle in this."

Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "The pity is that I happen not to know."

"You know one, at the least," said Huo Tianqing.

"Who?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Me," said Huo Tianqing.

Lu Xiaofeng smiled.

But Huo Tianqing did not smile; his face grave, he said, "Since Shangguan Danfeng won't be coming, then if you too let this business alone, wouldn't all the boundless wealth of the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour be mine already?"

Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "But I know the master of the Heaven's Bird sect would never do such a thing."

Huo Tianqing gazed at him, and at last a smile came to the corner of his mouth too; and he said of a sudden, "Would you care to go and drink a cup of wine?"

"I would," said Lu Xiaofeng.

The wine was in a jar of blue-and-white porcelain; poured out, it was colourless and without smell, near enough to plain water; but mixed with a little new wine, its fragrant, mellow savour filled the small, elegant room at once.

Lu Xiaofeng sipped slowly, and let out a long sigh. "Now this is true Daughter's Red."

"You know your wine," said Huo Tianqing.

Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "So next time you've wine as good as this, you ought to ask me to drink it; I, at least, won't waste your good wine on an unworthy palate."

Huo Tianqing smiled. "I don't come by wine like this so very often myself."

"Oh?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"This wine was a gift," said Huo Tianqing, "from a neighbour, when I last went to call on him."

"I envy you," said Lu Xiaofeng. "A neighbour as good as that is harder to come by these days than good wine."

"But he's a very strange man too," said Huo Tianqing; "you must surely have heard of him."

"I know a fair number of strange men," said Lu Xiaofeng; "I wonder which one you mean."

"He's called Huo Xiu," said Huo Tianqing.

"Huo Xiu?" cried Lu Xiaofeng, his voice failing him. "How comes he to be your neighbour?"

"He doesn't live here often," said Huo Tianqing; "but he's built a little tower on the mountain behind, and comes to stay a month or two every year."

Lu Xiaofeng's eyes lit of a sudden. "Do you know what he comes here to do?"

"Beyond drinking wine," said Huo Tianqing, "he seems to do nothing at all."

Lu Xiaofeng asked no further, but seemed lost in thought. As a rule he was little inclined to tax his brains over his wine; but this time was an exception.

Huo Tianqing did not mark his look, and went on: "So any good wine you could name, he has it, near enough all of it. I'm no great lover of wine myself; yet even I, once I'd reached that little tower, was half loath to come out again."

Lu Xiaofeng said of a sudden, "Do you know what wine tastes the best of all?"

"No," said Huo Tianqing.

"Stolen wine," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Huo Tianqing smiled again. "You'd have me go along and steal his wine with you?"

Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "Just so!"

"There's but one sort of man in this world who can't drink so much as a drop of wine," said Huo Tianqing. "Do you know what sort?"

"No," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"A man with no head on his shoulders," said Huo Tianqing. "So if you'd keep your head to drink with, you'd best give the notion up in good time."

Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "Stealing wine is like stealing books: it's genteel thievery. Even caught at it, a man's never charged with a head-chopping crime."

"That depends on who catches you at it!" said Huo Tianqing.

Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "You and Huo Xiu were of one family five hundred years back; what have you to fear?"

"But he told me with his own mouth," said Huo Tianqing, "that on that little tower of his there are a hundred and eight kinds of hidden contrivance and ambush; and unless a man is a guest he has invited, whoever breaks in will find it hard to come out alive."

He sighed, and went on, "And those contrivances know no faces; be your surname Huo or what you please, it makes not the least difference."

At last Lu Xiaofeng sighed too. "I have four eyebrows, and to lose two of them would matter little; but of heads I have only one, and can't spare so much as half of it."

He smiled wryly, and added, "When a man must guard even a few jars of wine with a hundred and eight contrivances, it's small wonder he's grown rich."

"Perhaps it's not to guard against wine-thieves at all," said Huo Tianqing.

Lu Xiaofeng's eyes flickered. "You mean there's some other secret on that little tower of his?"

Huo Tianqing smiled, and said lightly, "Every man has his secrets, more or less..."

"Only there's but one sort of man who can truly keep a secret," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"What sort?" said Huo Tianqing.

"A dead man," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Huo Tianqing's eyes too were flickering. "Huo Xiu is no dead man."

"No, he is not," said Lu Xiaofeng.

***

The most fearful thing of all, too, is the dead. However gentle and lovely a person may have been in life, let them but die, and they turn to something fearful.

And so a white cloth had been laid over Shi Xiuxue's body.

On the table stood a lone lamp, and Hua Manlou sat silent beside it, without a stir. He had gone already, and then come back.

Whether Shi Xiuxue were dead or living, he could never leave her here alone.

The keeper of the little shop had long since slipped away, leaving only this one lamp behind, as though he had forgotten that a blind man has no use for a lamp at all.

On every side was silence, not a sound to be heard; and when Lu Xiaofeng came in, he too made no sound.

But Hua Manlou had already turned his head to face him, and said of a sudden, "You've been drinking?"

Lu Xiaofeng could only own it. "A little."

Hua Manlou said coldly, "That after all that's happened you should still be in the humour to go drinking is a rare thing indeed." He kept his face stern; he was very seldom stern.

Lu Xiaofeng blinked. "Aren't you full of admiration for me?"

He had a trick for dealing with an angry man: since you're angry already, best make you angrier still, and see just how angry you can get, and whether it kills you or no.

Hua Manlou fell silent. He knew Lu Xiaofeng well, and had no wish, just yet, to be goaded to death by Lu Xiaofeng.

It was Lu Xiaofeng, on the contrary, who was left at a loss; and he said sheepishly, "You really ought to take a cup of wine yourself. The great virtue of wine is that it can make you forget a great many things there's no use thinking about."

Hua Manlou took no notice of him; and after a long while he said of a sudden, "I saw someone just now."

"You saw a great many someones just now," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"But this was someone I'd have thought I could never see here!" said Hua Manlou.

"Who?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Shangguan Feiyan," said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng started. "She isn't dead?"

Hua Manlou said, low and grieving, "She's not dead yet; but she lives a life not far from death."

"Why?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"She seems to have fallen into another's hands," said Hua Manlou, "her every movement wholly at this man's command."

Lu Xiaofeng was moved. "Do you know who this man is?"

"She didn't say, and I don't know," said Hua Manlou; "only, by my guess, this man must surely be..."

"Must surely be who?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Huo Xiu!" said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng had just sat down; of a sudden he stood up again, his voice failing him. "Huo Xiu?"

"Shangguan Feiyan was driven to come to me this time as well," said Hua Manlou, "to bid me leave this business alone; and the only one left now who'd not have us meddle in it is Huo Xiu."

Lu Xiaofeng sat down again; and after a long while he said of a sudden, "There was someone I didn't see just now."

It was a curious thing to say, near enough past understanding.

"There are a great many people you didn't see!" said Hua Manlou.

"But this was one I'd thought I surely would see," said Lu Xiaofeng. "I went to the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour to seek her in the first place."

"Shangguan Danfeng?" said Hua Manlou.

"Just so," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"She wasn't there?" said Hua Manlou.

"She never went at all," said Lu Xiaofeng; "but someone left a letter with Huo Tianqing, to pass to me!"

"What did the letter say?" said Hua Manlou.

"There were but four lines on it," said Lu Xiaofeng, "half sense and half nonsense, near enough to so much wind."

"What lines?" said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng said: "Danfeng is hard to win; little Phoenix, turn back; turn not back, and your life is hard to keep!"

Hua Manlou mused. "The meaning of these four lines seems, too, to be bidding you leave this business alone."

"The only one left now who'd not have us meddle in it," said Lu Xiaofeng, "is a single man."

"So you hold that the man who wrote this letter must also be Huo Xiu?" said Hua Manlou.

"I know only that once this man has set his hand to a thing," said Lu Xiaofeng, "he'll never leave it half-done."

Men who succeed never do leave a thing half-done.

"Sikong Zhaixing failed to steal Shangguan Danfeng away," said Hua Manlou, "and perhaps that was no surprise to him; so he had set other men waiting on the road beforehand, and carried off Shangguan Danfeng in the end after all."

"I've just drunk half a jar of his wine," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Hua Manlou was surprised again. "You've seen him already?"

"I have not," said Lu Xiaofeng. "The wine was his gift to Huo Tianqing; he has a little tower on the mountain behind the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour."

Hua Manlou was moved. "A little tower?"

"Just so," said Lu Xiaofeng, one word at a time. "A little tower!"

Hua Manlou rose to his feet as well, then sat down again; and after a long while he said slowly, "Do you remember what Sun Xiuqing said just now?"

Lu Xiaofeng remembered, of course: "Dugu Yihe came to Guanzhong this time because he'd had word, had learned that the First Blue-Robe Tower is at..."

Hua Manlou's face too lit up. "You think Huo Xiu's little tower is the First Blue-Robe Tower?"

Lu Xiaofeng did not answer; the thing needed no answer.

"But by the Great King of the Golden Roc's account," said Hua Manlou, "the head of the Blue-Robe Tower was Dugu Yihe!"

"The word he had was not necessarily right in every part," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Hua Manlou owned it. "Any man may be wronged; and any man, likewise, may sometimes wrong another."

Lu Xiaofeng sighed of a sudden. "The pity is that Zhu Ting isn't here now."

"Why?" said Hua Manlou.

"They say there are a hundred and eight contrivances and ambushes on that little tower," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"You mean to go and take a look at that tower?" said Hua Manlou.

"I do, very much," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Have those contrivances and ambushes frightened you off, then?" said Hua Manlou.

"No," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Once Lu Xiaofeng had set his hand to a thing, he too would never leave it half-done. Nothing on earth could ever make him leave a thing half-done!

***

The mountain was not high, but its lines rose steep and fair. A few li up, one could see a point of lamplight, uncommonly bright in the dark.

But before Hua Manlou's eyes was only darkness.

"I can see the little tower now," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Where?" said Hua Manlou.

"Through the wood ahead, and we're there," said Lu Xiaofeng; "there's a light up in the tower still."

"Do you think Huo Xiu might have come here too?" said Hua Manlou.

"I don't know," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"As I said just now," said Hua Manlou, "any man may sometimes wrong another."

"I heard you," said Lu Xiaofeng coldly; "I'm not deaf."

"I only mean to remind you," said Hua Manlou, "that Huo Xiu is your friend, and has always been good to you."

Lu Xiaofeng said coldly, "You think I'd wrong him? Wronged I often am, but I've never yet wronged another."

He grew of a sudden very restless, for there was a division in his own heart.

To end this business quickly, to lay the secret bare quickly, was of course best of all; yet he truly had no wish to find that the sinister, venomous master of the Blue-Robe Tower was in very truth his friend.

The wood was fragrant with the fresh scent of early-spring leaves; and though the cold in the wind was heavier now, all between heaven and earth was at peace, and still.

No people, no sound; the din and vexation of the red dust seemed shut off wholly beyond the green hills.

Only, some of the most dangerous and dreadful things in this world lie hidden, more often than not, in just such a stillness.

Lu Xiaofeng said of a sudden, "I don't like this."

"Don't like what?" said Hua Manlou.

"It's too quiet here," said Lu Xiaofeng. "Both when it's too loud and when it's too quiet, I feel on edge."

"Why?" said Hua Manlou.

"Because every strange thing I've ever met with," said Lu Xiaofeng, "has happened in just such a case!"

"If you're truly on edge," said Hua Manlou, "you'd best talk the more; talking will often make a man forget his unease."

"And what would you have me talk of?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Talk of Huo Xiu," said Hua Manlou.

"But don't you know a great deal about the man already?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"I know only that he's a solitary, cranky old millionaire," said Hua Manlou, "who loathes company above all things, so that even his most trusted underlings can often never find him."

"He loathes not only company but women too," said Lu Xiaofeng, "and so he's an old bachelor to this day."

"Yet a man must have some fondness or other," said Hua Manlou.

"His one weakness is wine," said Lu Xiaofeng; "he loves not only to drink it, but to hoard famous wines of every land and every kind under heaven."

"They say his martial skill is good too," said Hua Manlou.

"I've never truly seen him use it," said Lu Xiaofeng; "but I'll warrant his lightness-skill, his inner force and his point-sealing stand below no man's alive."

"Oh?" said Hua Manlou.

"And what he practises is the Virgin-Body discipline," said Lu Xiaofeng; "and of men who've truly the constancy to practise the Virgin-Body discipline, there are not, to my knowledge, above ten in the world."

Hua Manlou smiled. "The sacrifice for that art is great indeed; a man not born loathing women could scarcely keep such constancy."

Lu Xiaofeng laughed too. "Of others I can't say; I know only that I'd never practise such a wretched art myself. Cut off my head, and still I'd not practise it."

Hua Manlou smiled. "If it were another thing they cut off, you'd have no choice but to practise it."

Lu Xiaofeng roared with laughter. "So you're no true gentleman either."

"Keep company with a man like you often enough," said Hua Manlou, "and even a true gentleman would go to the bad."

They laughed aloud, seeming to have no fear of being found out; for since found out they must be, sooner or later, would not skulking about be, if anything, the greater loss of dignity?

"It's handed down from of old," said Lu Xiaofeng, "that any man with the constancy to practise the Virgin-Body discipline is sure to bring his skill to the very summit."

"That's no legend, but the plain truth," said Hua Manlou. "Only practise the Virgin-Body discipline, and every other art comes at half the labour for twice the gain."

"And yet in all the ages, past and present," said Lu Xiaofeng, "not one of the masters who truly reached the summit of the art was a practiser of the Virgin-Body discipline. Do you know why that is?"

"No," said Hua Manlou.

"Because a man who practises the Virgin-Body discipline must be an old bachelor," said Lu Xiaofeng; "and an old bachelor has always some little thing amiss in his heart; and a man with something amiss in his heart can never bring his skill to the summit."

Hua Manlou smiled. "And so you don't practise the Virgin-Body discipline."

"Never," said Lu Xiaofeng. "Whatever they cut off me, I'll not practise it."

"The pity is," said Hua Manlou, "that whether you practise it or not, you'll scarcely bring your own skill to the summit."

"Why?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Because everything that stands in the way of practising the martial arts," said Hua Manlou, "you love to death; as, for instance..."

"As, for instance, gambling, drinking, and meddling in others' affairs," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"And the most important point of all," said Hua Manlou: "that you're by far too little given to loathing women."

Lu Xiaofeng roared with laughter; and then found that they had already passed into the wood, and come to the foot of the little tower.

This path, to another man, would have meant trembling steps and a quaking heart; but they had walked it lightly and at their ease.

The road is the same road; all that matters is how you walk it. And the road of a man's life is just such a road as well.

***

The vermilion door was shut; but on the door was one great character: <i>Push</i>. So Lu Xiaofeng pushed, and at a push the door opened.

Any door at all can be pushed open; it is only a question of whether you're willing to push it, whether you dare to push it.

Within the door ran a broad, winding passage; and a stretch along it, at a turning, was another great character: <i>Turn</i>.

So Lu Xiaofeng turned; and after a few turns he came up onto a stone platform, and full in his face was yet another great character: <i>Stop</i>.

Lu Xiaofeng stopped; and Hua Manlou, of course, stopped with him, but could not help asking, "Why have you stopped all of a sudden?"

"Because there's a 'Stop' here," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"It bids you stop, and you stop?" said Hua Manlou.

"And what if I don't stop?" said Lu Xiaofeng. "There are a hundred and eight contrivances and ambushes here; do you know where they are?"

"No," said Hua Manlou, "not a single one."

Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "Since you don't know, why not be handsome about it?"

"And since there may be an ambush ahead as well," said Hua Manlou, "why not simply stop?"

"Just so," said Lu Xiaofeng. "So when they bid me stop, I stop; and when they bid me go, I go."

Hua Manlou sighed. "A man as biddable as you is a rare thing to meet with indeed."

"Since I'm as biddable as this," said Lu Xiaofeng, "how could anyone have the face to move against me again?"

Hua Manlou could not help laughing. "Whatever you do, you seem to have some queer, out-of-the-way method of your own; only I've never once known whether your methods are right or wrong."

Lu Xiaofeng had not yet opened his mouth when he found of a sudden that the stone platform on which they stood was slowly sinking.

Then he found they had come into a six-sided stone chamber, and on a stone table stood another great character: <i>Drink</i>. In the middle of the table, side by side, stood two bowls of wine.

Lu Xiaofeng smiled. "It seems the biddable always come off well."

"Come off well how?" said Hua Manlou. "Wine for the asking?"

"Just so," said Lu Xiaofeng. "This time they've asked us to drink; next time, who knows, they may ask us to eat meat as well."

"This is true Luzhou Daqu," said Hua Manlou. "It seems whatever Boss Huo brings out is good wine indeed."

"But good wine's not drunk with the nose," said Lu Xiaofeng. "Come: a bowl for you, a bowl for me."

"This wine's too fierce," said Hua Manlou; "one bowl and I fear I'll be drunk."

"Well then, you don't drink; I will," said Lu Xiaofeng.

He took up a bowl of wine and tipped it into his mouth, drinking the better half at a draught; then he found of a sudden that Hua Manlou's face had changed, and could not help stopping to ask, "You're unwell?"

Hua Manlou's very lips had gone white. "There seems to be some strange fragrance in this room," he said. "Do you smell it?"

"I smell only the wine," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Hua Manlou seemed scarce able to stand; of a sudden he put out his hand, felt for that bowl of wine, and drank it too at a draught. The face that had gone grey took life again on the instant.

Lu Xiaofeng's eyes turned. "So the wine can cure sickness too," he said with a smile.

He finished off his own half-bowl of wine as well, and only then found that on the bottom of the wine-bowl there was a character too: <i>Smash</i>.

So he flung the bowl away, and with a "clang" it struck the stone wall and shattered to bits.

Then he found the stone wall of a sudden begin to move, laying open a hidden door. Behind the door were some dozens of stone steps, leading down into the earth.

Below was the belly of the mountain; and before Lu Xiaofeng had even gone down, he saw a sheen of pearls and a glow of treasure.

The belly of the mountain was hollow, some tens of zhang across, heaped with bundles of red-tasselled spears, sheaves of headsman's sabres, and chests upon chests of gold and jewels.

Never in all his life had Lu Xiaofeng seen so many arms and so many treasures.

But what astonished him most was neither the treasures nor the arms, but four men. Four old men.

Their faces were all pale, plainly having gone many years without sight of the sun; and each wore a robe of brocade worked with gold and coiling dragons, and a jade belt about his waist, in the very dress of an emperor.

Below stood four chairs carved with golden dragons. On one sat an old man, lost in a foolish daze; another squatted on the ground working an abacus, muttering to himself, as if reckoning up the wealth of the place; another faced a bronze mirror, counting the white hairs on his head.

And yet another paced back and forth with his hands behind him; and seeing Lu Xiaofeng he came forward at once, his face stern, and said sharply, "Who are you? How dare you break into Our bedchamber unannounced? Do you not know that this is a crime punished by the slow slicing?"

His bearing was solemn, and had, for a wonder, a real touch of imperial majesty about it; he did not seem to be joking.

But Lu Xiaofeng was taken aback, and could not help asking, "You say this is a palace? And who, then, are you?"

"We," said the old man, "are the Great King of the Golden Roc, the thirteenth of that line."

Lu Xiaofeng was struck dumb again; never had he dreamed there would be yet another Great King of the Golden Roc here.

And who could have known there was more than one Great King of the Golden Roc here.

The old man had scarcely finished speaking when the other three old men came rushing over, each cutting in ahead of the rest: "Pay no heed, whatever you do, to that madman's ravings; We are the true Great King of the Golden Roc, and he a counterfeit."

"It's he who is the counterfeit... all three of them are counterfeits."

The four old men spoke, for a wonder, with one voice, every one of them saying the very same words; and each, red in the face and neck, argued so hot that the kingly majesty of a moment since had wholly vanished.

Lu Xiaofeng felt of a sudden that all four of these men were mad, mad at the least to some degree.

The best way to deal with such people is to make off at once, quick as you can; and though all the treasures on earth were here, and all of them given to him, he had no wish to stay a moment longer.

The pity was that, when he made to draw back, he found the door at the head of the steps already shut, and the four old men had hemmed him round, all cutting in together: "Tell us which of us is the true Great King of the Golden Roc... speak your honest mind."

On their pale, aged faces there had risen of a sudden a mad and savage look; and Lu Xiaofeng knew that whichever he named for the true one, the other three would fall on him for their lives that instant.

Never in all his life had he met with a thing at once so absurd and so dreadful. It had never so much as crossed his mind.

Just then he heard of a sudden three clear, sweet strokes of a bell; and in the mountain wall behind, a doorway opened once more.

Four comely youths in yellow robes, got up as palace eunuchs, came filing out, each bearing a vermilion food-box in his hands.

The four old men hurried back at once and sat down on their coiling-dragon thrones, setting solemn, dignified looks on their faces again; and the four youths knelt each before one of them, and, raising the food-boxes in both hands, said, "May it please Your Majesty to dine."

Lu Xiaofeng felt of a sudden a great ache in his head, for he truly could not make out what was going on.

Could all four of these old men be the true Great King of the Golden Roc? Or how else should there be eunuchs to wait on them at their meat?

But this was plainly Huo Xiu's country retreat; how should there come to be four such men here?

The door in the mountain wall behind still stood open; he gave a quiet tug at Hua Manlou's sleeve, and the two of them sprang across together.

Behind the door was another passage, and at the passage's end another door; and there they saw Huo Xiu.

Huo Xiu wore a suit of blue cloth washed white with age, and a pair of broken straw sandals on his bare feet; and he sat on the ground warming wine on a small red-clay stove, in a battered pewter pot.

Fine-scented wine.
