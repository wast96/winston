## The Hills of Iga

Counting from the Tenshō rising in Iga, this year fell in the thirteenth. Back from the capital to Otogi Pass, Tsuzura Jūzō let idle days and months flow over him once again.

As autumn deepened, Jūzō pushed up into the hills to cut brushwood for his winter fires; and when snow began to fall over the Iga basin, he took his gun and followed game far off, into the deep mountains that run on into Ōmi and Yamashiro. On days when his spirits were low he went down to the valley to fish for char, and now and then, as if remembering, he would sit before the household Buddha his grandfather had enshrined in the hermitage and chant a sunset gatha or two. When he heard the listless gong he himself was striking, his mind would drift off into a kind of stupor, and, as in the old days, he would begin once more to love his own indolence beyond all measure.

In the capital a rumor was going about that Yodo-gimi had conceived a child by Hideyoshi. The birth was expected by the eighth month at the latest, and the fifty-eight-year-old father was said to be hurrying from Nagoya Castle, in the Matsura district of Hizen, back toward Ōsaka. Jūzō heard as much at a ninja's house in Nabari, where he had stopped on his way home from a hunt.

For Jūzō, the pregnancy was not welcome news. Hideyoshi's regime, its sun already past noon and beginning to slant, would be shored up even a little by the birth of an heir. And for just so long, Jūzō's own work would be put off.

Yodo-gimi bore the child who would later be Hideyori a few days after that, on the third of the eighth month, Bunroku 2. Late the following night Kuroami came up from the capital to the hermitage at Otogi Pass, and no sooner had he stripped off his travel clothes, soaked with the night dew, than he said:

"A babe's been born."

"A girl?"

"A boy. The house of Toyotomi doesn't know when to make an end, does it. A sick man doomed to die who rallies for a spell—that's all it is. In time, because this child lives, the realm will see no end of useless suffering."

"Shrewd words, for Kuroami. Where did you hear that?"

"Coming back from delivering a finished blade to the Sanjōnishi house's villa at Iwakura, I fell in with a wandering monk near Ichijōji, and it was he that said so."

"Well, come over here and lie down."

"That I cannot. Tomorrow I've business that wants me back in the capital while the light holds, ye see."

"Business thriving—good. Though our real trade, the shadow work, will soon enough come to a time when we can't fritter the days away so leisurely. Every day that newborn grows, the foundations of the Toyotomi house harden. Do away with the father before the child can reason, and the lords of the realm will race one another to kneel before the Kantō. Next summer at the latest would be the time."

"The seventh month, or the eighth?"

"The seventh will do. Hard on Yodo-gimi's child—O-Hiroi, they're calling him—but that day would fall on the death-anniversary of his father the Taikō, would it not."

"Hard on him? I can scarcely wait for it."

Kuroami gave a little shudder of pleasure through his small frame. If Hideyoshi's killing of tens of thousands across half a lifetime was work, then the killing of Hideyoshi—by Jūzō and Kuroami, one man against one—was work as well.

Once the day of Hideyoshi's death was fixed, a change came over Jūzō's life. He gave up hunting. He no longer went down to the valley for char, no longer walked the ridges after the tracks of deer. And not hunting only: he even stopped slapping the mountain mosquitoes from his skin. On a summer evening, as he ate alone at the hearth, gnats would settle like scattered sesame over his bare torso, each swelling with blood until it dripped, and Jūzō, unmoved, would not alter the set of his body over his food by so much as a hair.

Among the monastic precepts is one against the taking of life. The deeds of the body it divides into three—no killing, no stealing, no lust—and for the deeds of the mouth it sets down four: the double tongue, the harsh word, the lie, the idle flourish. Precept or discipline, to the ninja's cast of mind not one of them has any bearing at all. Yet in the Iga school, when a man sets himself to an assassination, he lays upon himself, until the day the thing is done, the precept against killing—to goad his own long patience by it. And not the keeping of the precept only: through that whole span, to hold his discipline in order, he chants the forty scrolls of the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya.

In the third year of the Empress Jitō, certain waters—Takashi-no-umi in Kawachi, Muko-no-umi in Settsu—and certain moors—Nagi-no in Kii, Mi-no in Iga—were proclaimed grounds where the taking of life was forbidden. That the Iga ninja, of all the precepts, took only the one against killing into their own creed may owe something to that old tradition. But the strange thing about them is this: they keep the precept against killing most fiercely at the very time they are plotting the gravest killing of all, an assassination. To play the bodhisattva's part in order to become a demon of carnage—there, perhaps, is the very heart of a patience that must drug even its own soul.

At this moment a dozen and more gnats were eating through the skin of his whole body. He set down his bowl, finished the meal, and moved into the Buddha-room; still the mosquitoes, strewn over him like a rash of black moles, would not leave his skin. Even to fly off was beyond them: the human blood they were steeped in to the brim had left their wings powerless. In a low voice Jūzō began to chant the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, said to have been rendered into Chinese by Buddhabhadra of the Eastern Jin. And as he chanted on through this scripture that preaches Thou shalt not kill any living thing, the image of Hideyoshi rose ever more vividly in his mind, and his fixed resolve to cut that man down writhed like a serpent, working at his heart. This was no rite of subjugation, needless to say; the sutra carried no damp of sorcery either. And just for that, Jūzō's heart, even as it mounted toward the passion to kill, sank at the same time into a pity for the killing—an odd music of the mind. Perhaps, in pitying Hideyoshi's life as he chanted, Jūzō was already, through the chanting itself, beginning to hold a requiem for Hideyoshi's spirit before ever the body was slain.

That night, as he chanted, Jūzō felt of a sudden a faint breath of wind pass across his right cheek. The shadow of the short lampstand wavered, and—unable, it seemed, to bear even so slight a wind—several mosquitoes dropped from his back to the floor.

Jūzō left off his chanting. Beyond the paper screen the shrilling of insects had at last grown clamorous. He shut his eyes, as if to send his mind out into the sounds all around him, and presently:

"Who's there?"

"No need to hold back. As you see, this is a ramshackle hermitage. Come in from wherever you like."

"I am already here."

When he turned, a figure was standing at the end of the dark corridor—come in, at some unmarked moment, from who knew where.

"It's Kohagi. Come to kill me, have you?"

"Nothing of the sort."

"That's a relief. Still, you found me here well enough."

"Oh, that. Ever since you vanished from the capital, Master Jūzō, I've had a faint sense of it. But this is the first time I made up my mind to call."

"If you knew, why not come sooner? Not that you'd be welcome."

"You went to such trouble to hide; it would be unkind of me to go hunting you out."

"So you see clean through it all, do you."

"Ho…"

Jūzō watched steadily as she covered her mouth with her sleeve and laughed to herself, then said:

"State your business."

"For one thing, the money."

Kohagi pushed toward him a bundle wrapped in fine silk crepe that she had set beside her—funds delivered at regular intervals from the Ōkurakyō-Hōin of Sakai. Jūzō reached out and, without a word, set it by his knee.

"And the other?"

"There is something I've kept in hand, taking it for a thing left in my charge by you, Master Jūzō."

"A thing left in your charge—"

"It is not an object."

"You've lost me."

"It is a young woman. A girl who says she is the daughter of one Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon."

"Ho. Kisaru, is it."

"I didn't trouble to learn her name. But she is of a rare beauty—one might fairly call it that."

"But I've no memory of ever leaving her in your charge."

"I'm relieved to hear it. If she's nothing to Master Jūzō, then how I deal with her is my own affair. You'll not object, then?"

"Wait."

Jūzō held up a hand.

"Tell me how it stands."

"Hohoho. Now you're flustered."

"Of course I am. This Kisaru is my own master's daughter. I can't take it lightly."

"I dare say. And moved, perhaps, by that regard of yours, Master Jūzō, she's fiercely set on you."

"You're tiresome. What is it, exactly, that's happened?"

"Three nights ago, a thief stole into my lodging."

The masterless men Sōkyū keeps to guard Komatsudani seized the intruder, and found—so the tale went—that this thief, in black crow's garb with a ninja's blade slung at the back, was a woman. What was more, the instant she was dragged out into the garden of the study, she—

"So you're Kohagi."

—flung out the words, without a flicker of fear. The hooded lantern threw a ring of light on the ground, and from the wet veranda Kohagi looked down, half-dumbfounded, at the small intruder sitting cross-legged within the ring. Presently:

"It's all right. I'll do the questioning myself."

She took the lantern from the men's hands, sent them off, and then:

"You appear to be a woman. Who are you?"

—she asked, gently, and:

"Daughter of Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, of Iga."

—came the answer, without a trace of shame.

"Your name?"

"Ren, they call me. Though no one ever calls me anything but Kisaru."

"An Iga woman, then. Even so—what an innocent little rappa you make."

"Why?—"

Kisaru put on a suddenly guileless face. At the sight of it Kohagi could not help a small laugh, and said:

"I never heard of a rappa who, once caught, confessed her birth so glibly. From the look of you your ninja's craft is well enough, but your mettle as a ninja is plainly not yet made.—Here, now."

Kohagi stepped down from the veranda and laid a gentle finger against Kisaru's tapered chin.

"Why did you come to this house?"

"I came to see you."

"That was a deal of trouble. Who put you up to it?"

"No one put me up to it."

Bound as she was, she threw out her chest like a boy at the height of his mischief. When Kohagi saw there was no malice in the way she carried herself, she put out the lantern and loosened the cords.

"Since you came to see me, you are a guest. Come up inside."

Kohagi brought a light into the room, seated Kisaru in the lower place, and then took from a letter-box a small pistol and set it down with a click on the armrest.

"This tube can fire without a fuse-cord. No mischief, mind. Now speak plainly—this errand you came on."

"Plainly, is it?"

"You needn't be shy of it."

"If you want me to speak plain—I came to get a good look at your face. I can't help hating you."

"…?"

"Master Jūzō is sweet on you. And I won't have him taken from me by you."

"You've lost me."

"You told me to speak plain, so I said it. But that isn't the open errand I came on."

"I see. So there's a front and a back to it."

"There is. My open errand: I came to ask where one Mari Dōgen of Kōga lodges. I reckoned you'd know."

"Dōgen? Why?"

"To avenge my father. Strike him down, and I can beat you."

"Beat me?"

Coming to this, Kohagi began to think the little girl's head might be a shade wrong.

"Talk it out more calmly."

"I can take Master Jūzō from you."

"How troublesome. I've done nothing at all to take in this Jūzō you speak of."

"Vixen."

But Kohagi let it pass, and said:

"You're the one—baring your teeth like that, you put me in mind of something."

"—A monkey?"

Kisaru's mouth fell suddenly open, blank and guileless. Her moods changed with violence. Kohagi burst out laughing in spite of herself.

"Do you think so yourself?"

"My father used to say so, now and then."

"Though for a monkey, you're far too beautiful."

"Really—am I beautiful?"

"Yes. Your eyes."

"And my face?"

"Your face too—beautiful."

Kohagi, at something of a loss with her, went on:

"Let me hear about this Dōgen."

"Dōgen killed my father Jirōzaemon."

"And so, revenge? You?"

"Mm."

"So you'd have me tell you where Dōgen lodges. I'll tell you. The tenement of Maeda Gen'i, the Kyoto magistrate.—But give up the one thought of striking that man down. Even your father was killed by him; he's no opponent you could take with the skill in your arms."

"I'll strike him down, you'll see."

"No matter what?"

"Strike him down, and Master Jūzō will take me to wife."

"What a wicked one."

"I am?"

"No—that person you call Master Jūzō."

"Don't play the innocent. Chasing after this 'Master Jūzō' of yours on horseback, weren't you."

"Ah."

Kohagi, feigning to notice only now, said:

"So it was you—the one who knocked me from my saddle."

"Obviously."

Kisaru gave a snort of scorn. Kohagi passed it over and went on:

"Master Jūzō is cruel too. To send a fine girl like you, eyes open, into the jaws of the wolves. Far from making you his bride—for all one knows, he may be scheming to be rid of you."

"Lies. Master Jūzō is not that kind of man."

"He's a ninja. What's in his heart, none can know."

"I tell you he's not that kind of man."

"Kisaru."

"What is it?"

"If you're set on striking him down no matter what, then it can't be helped. I'll shut you up here in this house."

"Kisaru has legs of her own."

"Then I'll put you in a cell."

In that instant Kisaru knocked the screen down and burst out into the corridor.

But in the shadows of the corridor the Komatsudani men lay thick in wait. Piling on, they pinned Kisaru down and thrust her into a sealed, plaster-walled cell fitted out on the upper floor of the storehouse.

It was several days after that when Kohagi set out for Iga. As she left the villa she chanced to look up at the storehouse's upper floor: against the early-autumn sky its whiteness was almost painful to the eye. Kisaru, who at first had beaten the walls and screamed, had gone quiet, her presence sunk out of hearing.

Kohagi's true reason for confining Kisaru was certainly not to keep her from striking down Dōgen. For one thing, it may have been jealousy over Jūzō. For another, she judged that by sniffing out the drift of what this Iga-born girl, newly turned up before her, let slip, she might bring something further to light.

"Master Jūzō."

This woman Kohagi, seated face to face with him in the hermitage at Otogi Pass, was to Jūzō a riddle still. And yet this woman, half her presence wrapped in mist, would now and again, when she spoke his name, let a raw, almost breath-catching note into her voice.

"Mm. What is it?"

"Do open your eyes."

"I'm awake. Sleep carelessly, and there's no telling when you might put a blade in me."

"You will have your jokes."

"It's no joke. Kohagi. Isn't it about time you had done? Tonight's as good a chance as any. Why not show me here what you truly are? Though I've a fair guess of it already."

"Kohagi is the daughter of Sōkyū, the Ōkurakyō-Hōin of Sakai—and no more than that."

"A line I've grown weary of hearing."

"Hohoho. Perhaps I've grown weary of saying it, too."

"Can't you give me something a little fresher to the ear?"

"You are free to guess as you please. And from my own lips, that is all I can tell you."

"One more thing I'll ask—"

"Ask me nothing. Rather—can you not look on Kohagi simply as a woman? Kohagi, for her part, would now look on Master Jūzō simply as a man. If there is in you, too, any true tenderness, I would have even the smallest part of it for myself."

"And what is it you'd have me do?"

"The matter of Kisaru, for instance. …You told her, I hear, you would take her to wife."

"I did. Once she'd avenged her father."

"That was a ruse, surely. It must have been."

"Now, I wonder. A ruse or not—even I don't know. From boyhood I trained as a ninja, and so within me any number of men live, each apart, each thinking his own thoughts, each speaking with his own tongue. Meet with danger, and the man best fitted to that danger flashes up like lightning and carries me past it; meet another person, and one or another of the men inside reads off what the other is thinking. A convenient thing, truly—but for its sake I've lost the one self that was mine. What you see is all there is: the body of Jūzō. And that body, so a ninja is, is no more than a lodging-house that takes in the various men within. Which of the men who dwell in this body is Tsuzura Jūzō himself—that even I don't know. Perhaps no such man is there at all. To bid a man tell you the true heart of one who isn't there—that's rather a tall order, wouldn't you say?"

"That is how it is with the common run of ninja. But you above all, Master Jūzō—surely you have no such shape-shifter's heart."

"You mean I'm too much like a man. And by just so much as I'm like a man, I'm the poorer as a ninja. The wider a man's inborn heart spreads, the darker grows his eye for the things of the world as a ninja must see them. Somewhere in my twenties I turned my back on the world, and in those years, I suppose, my inborn heart put down roots, and I came back—a little—to being human. Set beside the other Iga men, there are times I'm ashamed of it. But you, Kohagi—you are otherwise."

"How so?"

"You are a shape-shifter of the first order."

"Whatever can you mean? I, who am not even a ninja?"

"Playing the innocent—that, precisely, is what makes a shape-shifter. I only worked it out from overhearing Dōgen: to have been raised in the Mochizuki house of Kōga is a ninja pedigree of no small order."

"That is old history. Now I am only a merchant's daughter."

"That plausible face of yours. Not even a fox or a badger could hold so straight and maddening a face as that."

"As you please.—Whatever you may make of me."

Kohagi quietly shifted the two hands folded one over the other on her lap, and said:

"But in this one thing—that my heart loves Master Jūzō—there is no falsehood. Peel away the skins of Kohagi's heart one by one, and it is the true heart left at the last that speaks. Will you not grant a hearing to what this heart says?"

"Grant what?"

"What I've asked all along: your true heart, Master Jūzō.—Will you love Kohagi?"

"You needn't ask; one of your gifts should know without being told."

"A woman's heart isn't satisfied so. Not unless I have it firmly, in words."

"Even one like Kohagi—is a woman, after all, so foolish a thing as that?"

"A woman's life burns on by one thing only—by longing for a man. And for that, without the words, it cannot be."

"—Please."

Jūzō held his breath a while, then:

"And were I to say I'd keep you at my side all my life, and cherish you…"

Kohagi caught her breath. The lampstand's flame drew up thin as an awl, and then—the wick burned out, perhaps—the light gave a fretful, sputtering cry, and all at once the room went dark. Jūzō drew a long breath, let it slip quietly out, and at last, flatly:

"What would you do?"

"Yes—?"

Kohagi gazed steadily at Jūzō's face and said it all in one breath:

"Run away—with me. Leaving everything behind."

As she finished, a smile rose for the first time about Kohagi's eyes, still as the center of a lake.

Jūzō shut his eyes and gave no answer. Arms folded, motionless as a clay image, he only waited on her words. Drawn in by his silence, Kohagi grew voluble.

"As long as you go on with this present work of yours, Master Jūzō, your life will end, in time, by violence."

"—"

"To overturn the Kampaku's world is a monstrous, presumptuous thing. There are gentler ways to live in this world than that. Kohagi has known love now, and since then she has come to see it. Otherwise, if things go on as they are…"

"—you will kill Tsuzura Jūzō."

With a gasp Kohagi pressed her sleeve to her mouth.

"There'll be no mistake of it. Ishida, the Jibu-no-shō, gulled Sōkyū and slipped you in as his adopted daughter to lay the whole plot bare."

"…"

This time it was Kohagi's turn to fall silent.

"If Jūzō's surroundings are left untouched for now, to Mitsunari's thinking it must be so that Jūzō may be worked to the full and the evidence of Sōkyū's crime got in hand. More than that: to swell Sōkyū's guilt and fatten his own credit, he's slipped in the Kōga woman Kohagi and set her, in open daylight, even to abetting the plot. A monstrous piece of scheming."

"That's a lie."

Kohagi, eyes cast down, managed only that much, and in a small voice.

"It is no lie. Go on as things are, and you and Jūzō must in time face each other as sworn enemies. A day will come when one of you has to kill the other. A hard business, that—all the more when it's a man and a woman who've come to love each other."

Jūzō said it with a wry grin, as if teasing some stranger, but Kohagi's face gave nothing back.

"That is why I say we should flee, now, like this.—Let us make a home in some land where no one knows us. Come, make up your mind, and quickly."

"Born a man, Jūzō counts it more than his due to be thought of so by you."

"What are you saying. Do not mock me."

"No, it's my true heart."

"Then you'll say it—that you're fond of Kohagi—won't you."

"Mm."

"—Then, to flee as well?"

"That I refuse."

"What? What did you say?"

"I refuse. Jūzō is a man. And a man, being a man, tires in time even of the woman he loved—but of his work he never tires. That is what a man is. It sounds a heartless thing to say, but Jūzō cannot drown in tenderness and betray his work."

"So even trampling a woman's heart, the work weighs more with you—that is what you're telling me."

"Just so.—As a samurai gives his loyalty to his lord, a ninja holds his own work dear."

"I've heard your heart clearly. Then Kohagi, too, goes back to her work. And Kohagi's work—it may, perhaps, be the killing of you, Master Jūzō."

"And why not. For that cruelty of yours toward your own heart, I—as one rappa to another—am truly, wholly smitten with you."

"Smitten. Is that the truth?"

"No lie in it."

"That makes me glad. I'll press you no further; only let me, this one night at least, attend you beneath the coverlet."

When she had said it, Kohagi flushed red. Jūzō took her hand and, with a tightening of his arm, drew her upper body down across his knees. Then, close by her ear, he whispered low:

"In return—from tomorrow, mind, I'll not answer for however I may deal with you."

"Then be on your guard. For Kohagi is no different."

Kohagi said it with her face buried in Jūzō's chest. Outside, at some point, rain had begun to fall. The sound of mountain rain sweeping the eaves seemed to thicken as the night wore on.

Next morning, when Jūzō rose from the bed, Kohagi no longer lay there. She had slipped out before dawn, put herself in order, gone down to the valley to draw water, and was now in the kitchen, briskly setting the morning meal to rights.

Jūzō, still in the bed, had been aware of it early. That within Kohagi's cast of mind as a ninja—cold, one might even say to the point of cruelty—such gentle devotion should lie hidden, was more than he had thought to find.

(A strange creature of a woman,)

he murmured inwardly, feeling a surprise vivid to the very eyes. The clink of vessels came to him, and presently the smell of steaming brown rice drifted in. Within it hung the soft scent of a woman's skin as she made the morning meal. It was a thing he had never known. In the heart of Jūzō, who had never had a wife, a small ripple stirred. The air Kohagi gave off held a strange, drugging power—power enough to loosen, for a moment, the tension that had held his heart taut ever since he came to manhood. That human pleasure Kohagi had spoken of—could it have meant this, the fragrance of such a morning meal?

"Are you awake?"

"Mm."

Jūzō kept himself from looking Kohagi's way. To look, he felt, would be to see the whole taut frame of the solitary life he guarded crumble before his eyes.

"Your breakfast is laid, over there."

"I'll take it."

To wash his face, Jūzō left the hermitage and went down into the valley. And so, in the end, he never returned to the hermitage. He had not the confidence to go back. Following the ridge road toward Yamashiro, Jūzō thought that never in his life had he known a peril to match that morning's.
