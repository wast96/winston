## A Brief Life of My Late Father Xu Langxi

{d} Xu Xiaogeng

My late father Xu Langxi was born in 1885 (the eleventh year of the Qing Guangxu reign) at Donglibu in Sanyuan, Shaanxi. The family was well off, owning several salt wells and tea plantations in Sichuan. In his early years, while studying in Japan, he followed Sun Yat-sen in the cause of democratic revolution and was one of the first members of the Tokyo Revolutionary Alliance.
Mr. Sun had always set store by the part the gangs could play in the revolution, and had himself joined the Hongmen. When he organized the Revive China Society in Tokyo, the Three Harmonies Society chief Zheng Bichen came over to him with his followers; the Hongmen brethren took part in one anti-Qing action after another before 1911; and later, in his Three People's Principles, Sun said that "the Qing, the Hong, the Li, and the Bai are all revolutionary bodies." When the Revolutionary Alliance was first formed, my father was charged by Mr. Sun with keeping in touch with the gangs at home. At that time the Japanese Toyama Mitsuru and Miyazaki Torazo had connections with the gangs all over China, and it was through them that my father was able to join. Because the Hongmen has the rule "tell not your father and mother above, pass it not to wife and children below," my father never told the particulars; all I know is that in the Hongmen he was mountain-lord of "Yuyun Mountain," and in the Green Gang too a "Da"-generation figure, holding a certificate conferred by the "Li" generation. He was by origin a mere scholar, and moved among the gangs out of the needs of the revolution, keeping wide company with such men as Yuan Hanyun, Bu Linwu, Cao Youshan, Ruan Mubai, and Duan Xiechen.
After the victory of the 1911 Revolution, Mr. Sun, holding that the Alliance's old program was now out of date while the Hongmen's rules still followed the feudal usages handed down from the Ming, once called together the nine Great Dragon Heads of the Hongmen for a "National Nine-Dragons Conference" and proposed doing away with the old system, adopting a military organization instead, to keep pace with the modern current. The "Nine Dragons" agreed with one accord, but because the old ways were too deeply set to change easily, and because the times were in turmoil, it was never carried through in earnest; only the "Yuyun Mountain" that my father headed left off holding the traditional rites and conferring posts of a feudal character. As for changing the organization, that too was never put into effect as Mr. Sun had proposed.
Before long Mr. Sun resigned the provisional presidency and took instead the post of national railway director under the Nanjing government, with a plan to build 100,000 miles of railway, and made my father his secretary to draw up the plans for him; but this was abandoned when the Second Revolution broke out.
In 1918 (the seventh year of the Republic), Duan Qirui refused to restore the Provisional Constitution, dissolved parliament, forced President Li Yuanhong from office, threw in his lot with Japanese imperialism, and crushed all who opposed him. At that time the Nationalist parties of seven provinces — Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia, Qinghai, Sichuan, Yunnan, and Guizhou — formed an alliance and organized a joint army to campaign against Duan Qirui, electing Tang Jiyao commander-in-chief. After deliberation, and with Mr. Sun's assent, all agreed that the best strategy of attack was to take Shaanxi first. The provinces of the lower and middle Yangtze — Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Jiangxi, Hunan, Hubei — were all within the Beiyang warlords' sphere; though split into the Zhili and Anhui factions, they had not yet openly broken, their forces were strong, and they would not be easily taken. In Shaanxi, on the other hand, the military governor Chen Shufan, though a creature of Duan's, had weaker forces, and at Sanyuan there was the Jingguo Army under Yu Youren to answer the call. So Mr. Sun appointed my father vanguard commander-in-chief of the seven-province Jingguo allied army for the relief of Shaanxi.
When my father received the appointment in Shanghai, Mr. Sun presented him with a scroll bearing the four great characters "The world belongs to all." My father did not grasp its meaning and asked: "I am leading an army to relieve Shaanxi — what use are these four characters?" Mr. Sun said: "This campaign depends on your military gifts, and you are a Shaanxi man besides; fighting your way home this time, you will first win the people's hearts and are certain of victory. But should things change midway, you may pass these characters on to those who come after, as a keepsake of your part in the revolution." So my father received it with reverence, and made it the motto of his whole life.
My father went by way of Guangdong and Guangxi to Yunnan, called on Tang Jiyao, and asked that troops be sent into Sichuan; Tang consented, and appointed Yao Yijia commander-in-chief of the Relieve-Shaanxi Army, Xu Yongchang chief of staff, and Yan Zhiyuan adjutant-general. My father led from the front, personally commanding two brigades, and after half a year of bloody fighting his forces reached Ziyang in southern Shaanxi, with every prospect of settling Guanzhong at a single stroke. Then a telegram arrived from Tang Jiyao, to the effect that Duan's side meant to settle the affairs of state peacefully, and asking that the troops stand by so as not to disturb the peace talks. My father, on reading it, was deeply indignant, holding that Tang had thrown over the officers and men fighting and bleeding at the front to compromise with Duan — an act of downright bad faith that ignored the larger cause — and so he laid down his arms and returned to Shanghai, and with that the seven-province army's campaign to relieve Shaanxi and chastise Duan came to an end.
During Chiang Kai-shek's April 12 coup of 1927, my father, braving every danger, worked hard to rescue revolutionary comrades. In the early days of the War of Resistance, the Communist Party member Zhang Guowei wrote to my father: "You, sir, who hold the public good at heart and stand with the toiling masses, have again and again helped our work in the past, for which we are ten thousand times grateful; we welcome you to stand with us on a single front against Japan and save the nation from destruction." To this end, after Shanghai fell, my father, acting for the common good against the enemy, besides keeping in touch with the Communist underground, introduced three Kuomintang Juntong underground agents — Cheng Kexiang, Peng Shengmu, and Peng Shou — to Zhou Fohai, so that they could penetrate the Wang Jingwei puppet regime. This was just when Zhou Fohai was anxious to draw closer to Chongqing, and he used Cheng Kexiang and the others to make contact with Dai Li and pass along some intelligence. Most of this intelligence my father reported in good time to the Communist Party's Shanghai underground — but I need not dwell on it.
Through the War of Resistance and the War of Liberation, my father kept in close touch with the Communist Party's Shanghai underground through comrades such as Hu Zhenjia (that is, Hu Ying), and used his own social connections to do some work for the Party. To rescue revolutionaries who had been arrested, I too, at my father's bidding, made the rounds of those secret-police offices; some were bailed out, while others, against whom the agents held firm evidence, were hard to bail out and could only be looked after in their daily needs.
All his life my father despised the opium traffickers, holding men of the stamp of Sheng You'an, Li Xiaobao, and Wang Yongkang in contempt, and he loathed Huang Jinrong's and Du Yuesheng's drug-dealing. When my mother died, my father meant to found a library at the Chinese Academy of Fine Arts in her memory; the academy's dean of studies, Wang Yachen, hearing of it, dropped a word to Du Yuesheng, and Du had Wei Tingrong send over 500 silver yuan to help buy books, which my father politely declined. Later Du said to him: "My money may not come by honest means, but if I give it for you to do something good for society, why refuse to take it?" He answered: "Thank you for your kindness. I know well enough that buying books with this money for everyone to read would do society good; but should the readers, opening the books, catch the smell of opium off the pages, how could I ever face them again?" And so in the end he did not accept the gift.
From this one may see that, close as his ties with gang men were, he would never make common cause with them in their filthy doings.
On the eve of Shanghai's liberation, my father, on instructions from comrade Hu Zhenjia, made contact with organizations such as the Shanghai wharf and pedicab guilds to prepare to welcome liberation. By this time the Kuomintang agents had sensed that my father was in touch with the Communist underground, but having no concrete proof did not dare move against him. In the spring of 1949, Nanjing sent men to Shanghai to urge my father to move his whole family to Taiwan, and Yu Youren and Dai Chuanxian even came in person to "press him to go." My father and old Yu, being fellow townsmen and having stirred up revolution together from their youth, could speak without ceremony. When old Yu urged him to move the family to Taiwan and offered to see to their living, my father said: "Beard! What would I go to Taiwan for? I'd sooner be a 'loyal subject' in Shanghai than run off!" Old Yu had come to press him on Chiang Kai-shek's orders; my father, Chen Yingshi, and Chiang had all been sworn brothers in their early days, but my father, later dissatisfied with Chiang, had gradually drawn away from him and secretly taken part in anti-Chiang activity, and so he retorted, twitting old Yu: "You were against Chiang once yourself, but the moment Chiang made you a Yuan president, it was 'Long live the Generalissimo'!" With that he burst into hearty laughter, and would not be moved.
At the same time the Communist underground was urging my father, along with a group of democratic figures, to go first to Hong Kong and then on to Beijing. Though my father's heart was set on Beijing, he was strongly attached to home and hearth and could not easily decide his course. As it happened, I was one day dining with several senior officers of the Kuomintang garrison command, and when the wine was flowing and spirits were high, someone asked the chief of staff at the table, "If Xu Langxi refuses to go to Taiwan and keeps up his contacts with the Communists, what shall we do about him?" The chief of staff said flatly: "If it comes to that, we'll have no choice but to stop being polite with him too!" His meaning was to hint that I should pass it on to my father — otherwise he would never have let the secret out in front of me. I came home and told my father, who talked it over with comrade Hu Ying; on their inquiry they concluded that, when it came to it, the garrison command and the other secret-police offices really would take deadly action, and this decided my father to make up his mind and fly secretly to Hong Kong and on to Beijing.
Fifteen days before Shanghai's liberation, I drove my father, in disguise, to Longhua airfield myself, where he took the last flight to Hong Kong, and from there went by ship to Tianjin and on to Beijing, to attend the first Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference as a specially invited delegate. After the conference he returned to Shanghai, and served in turn as a Shanghai municipal people's representative and a member of the CPPCC, dying of illness in October 1961.
(Set down by Yang Shi.)
