## Chapter 11. The "Wu Hao" Notices

In December 1931, Kang Sheng and Pan Hannian came for me once more to see Comrade Enlai. The car drove to the front of a cotton-yarn-and-cloth exchange on the Bund; we got out and went up to the third floor, where I saw two offices, with a gold-on-black signboard hung outside, the name of which I do not remember. In the outer room stood several desks at which ten-odd people sat, some copying out forms, some on the telephone, some going about the various kinds of business of an exchange, for all the world a busy trading-house. We went straight into the inner room, where Comrade Enlai sat at a writing-desk, intently studying the board on the wall that showed the rise and fall of cotton-yarn-and-cloth prices, altogether with the air of a great magnate. When we had exchanged greetings, Kang Sheng and Pan Hannian left, and Comrade Enlai asked me kindly how my health had been of late. I said I was much better. He bade me look after my health, praised me again for the successful rescue of Comrade Guan Xiangying, and then told me that ever since Gu Shunzhang's defection the enemy had been spreading rumours everywhere that he (Zhou Enlai) had already turned traitor and surrendered, and he asked what clever and effective method I might use to refute the rumour for him and expose this vile and shameless trick of the enemy's. Sitting by the desk, I thought hard for a while. It occurred to me that Comrade Enlai's code name in the Awakening Society at Tianjin during the May Fourth Movement had been "Wu Hao," which he had later used as a pen name when publishing articles in the Party Centre's organs, the magazine Red Flag and the Red Flag Daily, and which was well known to many both inside and outside the Party. So I took up a pen at once and drafted a piece: "Notice from Lawyer Chen Zhigao on behalf of Wu Hao: I am in good health, and beg all my relatives and friends not to be anxious on my account." Reading it, Comrade Enlai was well pleased, and praised me there and then: "Mulan, you really are our lady Zhuge; one knit of the brow and a plan springs to mind! Please go first and talk this matter over with Lawyer Chen Zhigao, and see how it might be done both effectively and quite safely!" And he added, "You have done your work very well, and Comrade Pan Hannian is quick-witted and able too; the two of you work well together. The Party organization will send someone to keep in regular single-line contact with you; be sure to be careful and to take good care of yourself."

At the time he did not tell me that the Centre had already decided to transfer Pan Hannian to work in the Soviet area. By then the Red Army under Mao Zedong and the others had won three campaigns against "encirclement and suppression"; the two revolutionary base areas of southern Jiangxi and western Fujian had been joined into one, forming a Central Revolutionary Base Area that held twenty-one county towns, an area of 50,000 square kilometres, and a population of 2,500,000, and the Provisional Central Government of the Chinese Soviet Republic had been established. The situation there was very good, and cadres were badly needed to carry the work forward. In the White areas, by contrast, the situation was grim. After the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, the Party Centre's leadership had come to harm under the erroneous line of Wang Ming and others, a line even more "Left" than the "Li Lisan line"; and, with Gu Shunzhang and Xiang Zhongfa, who held the Centre's core secrets, arrested and turned traitor one after another, the Party's secret working apparatus in the White areas had been gravely damaged, many comrades arrested or even killed. The Centre judged that Shanghai's conditions were too vile, and that to concentrate in Shanghai so many leading cadres of great ability and rich experience in struggle was extremely dangerous and could not let them play the part they should; it therefore decided to withdraw many cadres of the central organs in an organized way, batch by batch, and move them to the Central Soviet area in Jiangxi. In this way they could give full play to their powers and do more, and it was forced upon them, too, by the objective conditions of the struggle. Before long the Party Centre itself was forced to leave Shanghai for Ruijin in Jiangxi. In the end only a part of the Central Special Branch remained in Shanghai, along with a few comrades who bore special missions and had good social standing or work cover; I was one of them. Of these Party members who stayed on to carry on the struggle in the Shanghai White areas, not a few could from then on no longer take part in organizational life, and could keep only single-line contact with the organization: this was a contingency measure the Centre adopted as the situation changed!

After taking my leave of Comrade Enlai, I went with the draft I had written to consult Chen Zhigao. Chen Zhigao said, "To defend a contributor to Red Flag would be a great honour, and I would by no means refuse" (he much admired the articles Wu Hao wrote, though at the time he did not yet know that this was Zhou Enlai); "but if we do it this way, then once the notice appears in the papers the Nanjing National Government will surely pursue the matter and question me: Who introduced you to this Wu Hao? Or, who commissioned you, on Wu Hao's behalf, to place this notice for him? How am I to answer? Don't be hasty; let me think it over carefully and come up with a way that will both refute the rumour publicly and be perfectly safe, without the least risk."

In February 1932, beginning on the 16th, the enemy openly ran, day after day, in Shanghai's major newspapers, the Eastern Times, the News, Shen Bao, the China Times, and others, the so-called "Wu Hao Notice," which spread the lie that "Wu Hao" and others, 243 persons in all, had jointly renounced the Communist Party. Shen Bao held off until 20 February and then ran it on two successive days; but immediately afterwards, on 22 February, it published in its advertising pages, in the name of the Shen Bao Office advertising department: "To Mr. Wu Hao: the advertisement notice delivered to us on the 18th of this month was not published, because the Fuchang Bed Company declined to stand surety and the formalities were not in order." By this self-contradictory statement they meant to let a careful reader see that the so-called "Wu Hao Notice" was a fabrication.

Li Yimang, in the book Pan Hannian in Shanghai, compiled by the Party History Research Office of the CCP Shanghai Municipal Committee, writes:

At that time Zhou Enlai had already left Shanghai for the Jiangxi Soviet area. The renunciation notice placed in the name of Wu Hao was plainly forged by Kuomintang special agents to be anti-Soviet and anti-Communist, and a way had to be found to clear the matter up. After we had all talked it over, it was decided that Pan Hannian should approach Chen Zhigao, then a lawyer at the French Concession's Municipal Police, to place a notice of denial on Wu Hao's behalf. But Chen said that although he worked at the police station he was, after all, a Chinese, and it was not convenient for him to come forward; if the Kuomintang's people came looking for him, he would find it hard to explain. He proposed to find for us, in his stead, a French lawyer named Baho, to place an urgent notice on behalf of Zhou Shaoshan.

On 4 March of the same year, Shen Bao published the "Urgent Notice from Lawyer Baho on behalf of Zhou Shaoshan." The original text reads as follows:

Mr. Zhou Shaoshan has come to this office and declared: In writing and submitting articles he has used the two-character byline "Wu Hao"; of late the newspapers have carried a notice that Wu Hao and others, 243 persons, have left the Communist Party, whereby he has been troubled by inquiries, by letter and telegram, from relatives and friends at home and abroad. But his name "Wu Hao" has never been used for anything beyond his written compositions, and never for any outward activity; the Wu Hao in question must therefore be another person. The alleged matter of 243 persons simultaneously leaving the Communist Party has in truth nothing to do with him. As his personal reputation is at stake, and as misunderstanding may easily arise, and as he further fears that unworthy persons may turn right and wrong upside down and make trouble on this pretext, he has specially entrusted your lawyer to make this declaration on his behalf, and to return thanks for the letters and telegrams of inquiry from his relatives and friends, and so forth. Accordingly, this notice is placed in the newspaper on his behalf, as set out at left.

The office: No. 5, sixth floor, 41 Rue du Consulat.

This notice did not use the name of Wu Hao but the name of Zhou Shaoshan, and it further said that Wu Hao was Zhou Shaoshan's own pen name. It was a neat little move; for after the notice appeared, the Kuomintang sent men to find Baho and ask where Wu Hao was. Baho said, "My client is Zhou Shaoshan, whose byline merely happens to be Wu Hao. The Wu Hao you are looking for is of course not this one; and he too has placed a notice, so you may go and find him directly." The content of this notice made it plain that the Wu Hao notice of February was a forgery, and so we did not trouble to rebut, point by trivial point, the anti-Soviet and anti-Communist statements in that notice. At the same time, articles of rebuttal were carried in some of the Party's mimeographed publications.

This notice could at once refute the enemy's shameless slander that Wu Hao had turned traitor and gone over, shatter the reactionaries' scheming plot to defame the leaders of our Party Centre, and yet cleverly slip past the enemy's investigation. Baho was a French national and a lawyer, protected in those days within the Concession by "extraterritoriality," so that the Nanjing government could not interfere with him directly. Though the enemy later tried by every means to trace the threads of the affair, and on that account grew somewhat suspicious of Chen Zhigao, they had no hard proof, and so it came to nothing. Cao Bingsheng had warned Chen Zhigao to be careful, saying that the police station was investigating just who had commissioned Lawyer Baho to come forward and place that notice, and that someone was beginning to suspect him. Chen Zhigao charged Cao Bingsheng to tell the people at the station that he had gone abroad to study in France and was no longer in Shanghai. In fact he had not left Shanghai; he had merely shut himself up at home for a time, letting the trouble blow over.

Chen Zhigao was never a "lawyer of the French Concession's Municipal Police." Because of his connection with old Master Jieqing, he found it especially easy to come and go at the French Concession police station when handling cases, and it was for this reason, perhaps, that people mistook him for a "lawyer of the French Concession police." Lawyer Baho, too, was an old friend of old Master Jieqing's; when Chen Zhigao asked him to come forward, he indulged the man's tastes, bringing out a precious old painting from home to give him, and did not make the underground Party pay one cent. The rumour that "one hundred taels of silver were used" is likewise pure fiction.

More than half a century later, my youngest son, Chen Wenzhong, came across an article in the American newspaper Overseas Chinese Daily by Wen Chongshi, entitled "How Did the 'Four Evils' Persecute Lord Zhou?", "Lord Zhou" being Zhou Enlai. The article revealed how, during the "Cultural Revolution," the "Gang of Four" had used ignorant Red Guards to make the "Wu Hao Notice," dug out of the old newspapers, into a heavy shell for slandering and attacking Zhou Enlai as having "problems in his history," stirring up a political countercurrent to oppose and strike at Zhou Enlai. Wen Chongshi's article could not, of course, tell the inside story: that it was I who had asked Chen Zhigao to commission Lawyer Baho to come forward and see to the "Urgent Notice of Zhou Shaoshan," refuting the enemy's slander. When I received the newspaper my son had sent, I passed it at once to Zhang Chengzong, head of the United Front Work Department of the CCP Shanghai Municipal Committee, and Zhang Chengzong had Wen Chongshi's article reprinted in full in the Shanghai Propaganda Bulletin. Only then did more people come to know that in the early 1930s there had occurred this "Wu Hao Notice," a matter of the greatest concern to both sides, enemy and ourselves; and that in the "Cultural Revolution" of the 1960s it had been played up again, to make waves, by dissident elements within the Party.

On 25 January 1932 (the eighteenth day of the twelfth lunar month of the year xinwei), my father died of illness, to my great grief; the telegram my family sent me was forwarded through Chen Zhigao's law office. In deep sorrow, I asked the organization at once for leave to hurry home for the funeral, but because the rescue work required it, the organization did not grant my request. By old custom, children away from home would all return for a parent's funeral, and if one did not, people would grow suspicious: could it be that she had cut herself off from her family? Two or three such questions, and one's true identity might easily be exposed. After some deliberation, the organization decided that Qi Yuande should take me to the Shenjiang Hospital to hide for the time being, while Chen Zhigao was told that I had gone home for the funeral, and was asked to inform his parents of it. For by the custom of the time one could not, before the mourning had "reached the sevens" (seven times seven, 49 days), go into another person's house, or one would bring them ill luck. Had I not reported a death, yet stayed away so long from my adoptive parents, I feared the old people might grow suspicious or displeased. When Chen Zhigao's parents heard that I had gone home for the funeral, they even sent twenty dollars and a length of blue woollen cloth (in fact a piece of material for clothes), a very handsome condolence gift in those days. I promised Chen Zhigao that I would surely return to Shanghai once the "sevens" were done. There were still several cases I had entrusted to him, not yet concluded, and I had no choice but to leave everything to him.

The Shenjiang Hospital was run by the underground Party. It stood on Avenue Joffre (today's Central Huaihai Road) and was set up expressly to treat the Party's leaders, serving at times as a transit point for receiving important central leaders passing through. The doctors, nurses, and orderlies there were every one of them our own comrades, and it held a proper business licence for the outside world. The hospital's first floor was a clinic and wards openly receiving ordinary patients; the second and third floors were closed to idlers, kept especially as places for leading comrades to be treated or to take temporary refuge. I had long had a stomach ailment, and now, grieving over my dear father's death, I could not eat, and the ailment flared up worse than ever; so it happened that I stayed upstairs to rest and be treated, and never once went downstairs. To the outside world, Chen Zhigao included, I had merely gone home to Hankou for a funeral.

It was just then that the Chinese army, chiefly the Nineteenth Route Army, was heroically resisting the Japanese invasion of Shanghai (the Chinese-administered districts outside the Concessions), in the famous "January 28th" Shanghai War. The people of Shanghai, of every walk of life, were of one mind in supporting the Nineteenth Route Army's resistance, and many students from the medical colleges and members of the Red Cross rushed to the front to take part in battlefield relief. Chen Zhigao's two younger brothers, Chen Yingu and Chen Shijun, then studying at the Tongde Medical College, also joined the relief work at the front. Hidden away in the hospital, I could not of course go out to take part in this stirring movement of resistance and national salvation, and could learn something of what was happening outside only by reading the news in the papers.

It was not until after the "sevens" were done, on the seventh day of the second lunar month of the year renshen (13 March 1932), that I dared step out of the hospital gate. While buying things at Baxianqiao, I unexpectedly met Chen Zhigao's sister, Chen Baozhang. The moment she saw me she cried, "Sister Shuyi! Why haven't you written to us since you left? The whole family has missed you so! Father is ill, and gravely so; Sixth Brother" (Chen Zhigao) "has come down with diphtheria and is under treatment. Did you know?" I could only say, "Oh dear! I only got back to Shanghai today and came out to buy a few daily things; I had no idea that my adoptive father and Zhigao were both so seriously ill. I'll go and see them tomorrow." The next day I went first to a shop at Baxianqiao that sold Hunan local products, bought some native specialities, and took them to the Chen house. Old Master Chen was indeed gravely ill; the famous Western physician Professor Pang Jingzhou and the renowned Chinese doctor Yu Tongfang had both been called in, but both experts said it was beyond remedy. By then the old lady, from watching over the old master day and night without rest, had fallen ill from exhaustion; the eldest daughter-in-law of the Chen family, who had given birth to her second son, Chen Shengnian (now named Peng Qing), was not yet a month past her confinement and could not come downstairs to care for the two elders; the eldest sister, Baozhang, was studying at the Sino-French College of Pharmacy, her studies pressing, and could not take leave; and the several other daughters still at their books were all too young to nurse and wait upon a sick man. And so I, as an adopted daughter, took up as a matter of course the heavy charge of attending upon the two elders, until I had seen the old master to his end and managed the funeral, fulfilling my duty as an adopted daughter. I called at the Chen house, I think, on the eighth or ninth of the second lunar month, and the old master died on the twelfth day of the second month (18 March 1932), at the age of 66.

After the old master's death, Huang Jinrong, mindful of the debt of a life saved in years past and of the regard due a man who had for many years been his superior in the French Concession, made a special word to the police station and, by way of exception, allowed the old master's coffin to lie in the house for 100 days, as a particular favour. By the custom of old China, during this period monks were to be invited to the house to chant sutras and perform penances, in the seven-day rites called "doing the sevens"; and, together with the receiving of the mourners who came without cease to pay their respects, the whole household was busy day and night. Chen Zhigao told me not to go back to my own lodgings, but to help his family keep the vigil and see to the funeral. I could only slip quietly back to the Shenjiang Hospital, explain the situation to the director, and ask him to report it to the organization on my behalf, and to go back when there was a chance to look in.

Here I feel I must clear up, on Zhigao's behalf, one matter that touches his reputation through a misunderstanding. Dong Zhujun's memoir My One Century, published a few years ago, is rich and vivid in content and of high value as a historical source, but in one place it is at odds with the facts. It relates that in 1932, when Dong was wronged and caught up in a lawsuit in Shanghai, isolated and without help, her daughter went to seek the aid of the great lawyer Chen Zhigao, whose fame she had heard, only to be met with a "demand for an enormous fee" and left to sink or swim. This is a colossal misunderstanding! By the time Dong gives in her book, Zhigao was in the midst of a hundred days of mourning at home for his father, old Master Jieqing, and was also lying low over the "Zhou Shaoshan Notice"; he had shut his door and would take no cases at all, and could not possibly have accepted Dong's suit, let alone taken advantage of another's distress to extort a great sum. It was very likely, I think, that someone extorted money under Zhigao's name, and that Dong, in her anger, laid the account wrongly to Zhigao, who knew nothing of it, and brooded over it ever after. But what puzzles me is this: during the War of Liberation, in Shanghai, we were charged with liaison work in literary and artistic circles, and often held garden parties and dinners at home, the feasts supplied either by Elder Sister Wu Mei's Meilongzhen Restaurant or by Elder Sister Dong's Jinjiang Restaurant; we had a most cordial and quite frequent relationship of friendly cooperation, so why did I never once hear Dong betray the least dissatisfaction over this matter? To be sure, in 1932 Elder Sister Dong had not yet joined the revolution; her social identity was still that of a wife newly divorced from Commander Xia, and of a proprietor running an enterprise in partnership with others, and it is not impossible that some individual lawyer, ignorant of Elder Sister Dong's real straits, might have demanded an enormous fee on the strength of her position and standing alone; but it was certainly not Zhigao, whose circumstances at the time do not fit, and still less does it fit the principles by which Zhigao conducted himself all his life. Now that both Dong Zhujun and Chen Zhigao have passed away, I have no wish to stir up a new dispute here; I only set out the truth as it was, that the error may not go on being passed from mouth to mouth.
