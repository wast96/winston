### Gunshots off Avenue Joffre (Part 1)

### The Attempt on Zhou Enlai

In August 1929, on information laid by the traitor Bai Xin, five comrades were seized in Shanghai: Peng Pai, member of the CCP Politburo, secretary of the Central Peasant Committee and concurrently secretary of the Military Commission of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee; Yang Yin, alternate member of the Politburo and head of the Central Military Department; Yan Changyi, member of the Central Military Commission and of the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission; Xing Shizhen, a cadre of that provincial commission; and the Communist Zhang Jichun. Four of the five were put to death. It was a bitter loss.

Peng Pai (1896–1929), whose original name was Hanyu, was a native of Haifeng county in Guangdong. In his early years he studied in Japan, took part in the anti-imperialist patriotic activity of the Chinese students there, and was placed by the Japanese police on their "black list" of socialists. He graduated from Waseda University in 1921, and on his return joined the Chinese Communist Youth League in Guangzhou, crossing over to the Chinese Communist Party in 1924. From 1922 he ran the peasant movement in his home district and founded a peasant association; from then until 1927 he gave himself wholly to that work, one of the earliest leaders of the peasant movement in the whole Party. In those years he served as head of the Haifeng County General Peasant Association, head of the Huizhou Peasant Union, chairman of the executive committee of the Guangdong Provincial Peasant Association, director of the first and fifth sessions of the Guangzhou Peasant Movement Training Institute, executive member of the Kuomintang's Guangdong provincial branch and head of its peasant department, member of the CCP Central Peasant Movement Committee, and member and secretary-general of the provisional executive committee of the All-China Peasant Association. He threw all his energy into the peasant movement, amassed a rich store of experience, and carried it through to a body of theory. He was hailed as "the King of the Peasant Movement" and "the leader of the Chinese peasant movement." In the same years he served as secretary of the CCP Hailufeng Special Branch, member of the CCP Guangdong District Committee and head of its peasant committee, took part in both Eastern Expeditions, and served as standing committee member of the CCP Shantou District Committee. At the Fifth National Congress of the CCP, in May 1927, he was elected to the Central Committee. After the failure of the Great Revolution he joined the Nanchang Uprising and served on the CCP Front Committee; at the August 7th Conference he was elected to the provisional Politburo. That November he led the Hailufeng armed peasant uprising and set up the Hailufeng soviet, founding China's earliest soviet power and the Hailufeng base area, and so helped open the correct road of the Chinese revolution. At the time of the Guangzhou Uprising he was named people's land commissar of the Guangzhou Soviet Government. After the uprising failed, his troops were reorganized as the Red Fourth Division, and Peng Pai and the peasants gave the insurgent army an ardent welcome. The next year Haifeng and Lufeng fell to the enemy's encirclement, and Peng Pai led the Red Army and the armed peasants back into the base area, withdrawing to the Danan Mountains on the border of Puning and Huilai, where he kept up guerrilla warfare under harsh conditions. At the Sixth National Congress of the CCP, in 1928, he was again elected to the Central Committee, and at the first plenum of the Sixth Central Committee to alternate membership of the Politburo, later filling a vacancy as a full member. Early in the winter of that year Peng Pai came to Shanghai, where he lived on Daxi Road in Bailu Li and took up the posts of secretary of the CCP Central Peasant Committee and secretary of the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission.

In his essay "In Memory of Comrade Peng Pai," Qu Qiubai set a high value on all that Peng Pai had given to the revolutionary struggle: "After the Wuhan government turned traitor, Peng Pai led one of the first peasant guerrilla units to appear and began to fight along the Guangdong–Hunan border, then pushed on into Haifeng, where, on the instructions of Comrade Zhang Tailei, then secretary of the Party's Southern Bureau, he set energetically about preparing the Hailufeng rising. Out of the peasant self-defense corps and the revolutionary soldiers who had fallen back on Hailufeng after the failed drive against Guangdong — the men of Ye Ting's regiment — he organized the Hailufeng worker-peasant army, and that army played the decisive part in bringing off the Hailufeng rising. Out of that rising China's first soviet government was born, and it lasted from November 1927 all the way to April 1928. The Hailufeng Soviet Republic gave an enormous push to the growth of the Red guerrilla movement in Hunan, Jiangxi, and the northeast of Guangdong."

Yang Yin (1893–1929), styled Beitan and also known as Menghuo, Yingli, and Dianle, was a native of Faheng village in Xiangshan (today Zhongshan) county, Guangdong, from the same village as Sun Yat-sen. His granduncle Yang Heling was a schoolmate and close friend of Sun Yat-sen and, with Chen Shaobai and You Lie, one of the so-called "Four Bandits" of the anti-Qing cause, forever publishing revolutionary broadsides against the foreign aggressors and the Qing court. In his boyhood Yang Yin was steeped in Yang Heling's democratic-revolutionary ideas and held in deep reverence Sun Yat-sen's unbending revolutionary spirit. He joined the Tongmenghui in his early years and followed Sun Yat-sen into the Constitutional Protection Movement. After the May Fourth Movement broke out he began to come into contact with Marxism, and in the winter of 1922 he joined the Chinese Communist Party, afterward going to the Soviet Union to study. When he returned in 1923, the Party organization, considering the wide and close ties he had once had within the Tongmenghui and among the workers and the high standing he enjoyed, decided he should build the workers' movement in Guangzhou. In 1924 he was sent to lead the workers' movement on the Guangzhou–Hankou, Guangzhou–Kowloon, and Guangzhou–Sanshui railways; he organized a Guangzhou support committee and helped fund the great strike of the Nanyang Tobacco workers in Shanghai. In 1925 he took part in putting down the revolt of the Yunnan and Guangxi warlords Yang Ximin and Liu Zhenhuan. After the May Thirtieth Massacre he went to Hong Kong and, with Su Zhaozheng and others, formed the CCP Hong Kong Party group, organized the All-Hong-Kong Federation of Labor Unions, and launched and led the Canton–Hong Kong Strike. The needs of struggle at the time kept Yang Yin from holding any open leading post in that strike, but he made a vital contribution to it and stood very high among the Hong Kong workers. For this the Party Center later called him "the leader of the most famous strike in Chinese history, the great Canton–Hong Kong Strike of Guangdong." In 1926 he became a member of the CCP Guang-Guang District Committee and secretary of its supervisory commission. In April 1927 he became a member of the CCP Guangdong Provincial Committee and head of its provincial revolutionary military commission and of its committee for the elimination of counter-revolution. That December he helped lead the Guangzhou Uprising and served as head of the counter-revolution-suppression commission of the Guangzhou Soviet Government. After Zhang Tailei fell, Yang Yin was made acting chairman of the Guangzhou Soviet Government, leading the revolutionary forces in a counterblow against the Kuomintang reactionaries' frenzied assault. When the uprising failed he led the Red Guards in covering the retreat, at last breaking out with ten-odd others and moving to Hong Kong, where he took charge of the aftermath. He heartened his comrades, saying: the uprising has failed, but the blood will not have been shed for nothing; it is already the dead of the twelfth-month winter — is the spring not almost here? Early in 1928 he went to Hailufeng and stood with Peng Pai in the Hailufeng revolutionary struggle. In June 1928 Yang Yin attended the Sixth National Congress of the CCP in Moscow, where he was elected to the Central Committee, to alternate membership of the Politburo, and to alternate membership of its standing committee. On his return he became head of the Central Military Department, assisting Zhou Enlai, secretary of the Central Military Commission.

Yan Changyi (1900–1929), styled Jiefu, was a native of Anxiang county, Hunan. He graduated from Mingde Middle School in Changsha in 1918, just as the work-study movement in France was rising, and like many patriotic young men he followed the new tide in search of the truth that would save the nation and its people. Early in 1920 he went to France on the work-study program, first taking French at the Montargis preparatory school. That August, with Hunanese work-study students at its core, the World Work-Study Society was founded, and Yan Changyi was among its first members. In September 1921, in protest against the Franco-Chinese University of Lyon refusing to admit the work-study students already in France, he joined in occupying the school; on October 13 the Chinese legation in France and the Lyon university authorities, in collusion with the French government, forced Yan Changyi, Chen Yi, Cai Hesen, Li Lisan, and altogether 104 Chinese students to leave France, reaching Hong Kong on November 23 and returning to Shanghai by year's end. Late that year he joined the Chinese Communist Party and went to study at the Franco-Chinese University at Xiangshan in Beiping; after graduating in the summer of 1924 the Party sent him to the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in the Soviet Union. Back home from August 1925 he worked on the Central Military Commission and the Hunan Provincial Military Commission, and in September 1926 was transferred to the CCP Jiangsu-Zhejiang District Committee, taking part in the first and second armed uprisings of the Shanghai workers. After the April 12th coup he was sent back to the Central Military Commission. At the Nanchang Uprising, when the Front Committee headed by Zhou Enlai was formed, Nie Rongzhen, Yan Changyi, and He Chang were named to make up the front military committee, and Yan Changyi served concurrently as Party representative of the insurgent army's Twenty-fourth Division. When the Nanchang insurgent army marched south, Yan Changyi returned directly to Shanghai on the Front Committee's instructions, since the force campaigning south into the Chaoshan region of Guangdong had fallen into a heavy enemy encirclement and suffered grave reverses. In mid-October 1927, carrying the Center's instructions for the Southern Bureau and the Guangdong Provincial Committee, Yan Changyi went to Hong Kong to confer on the aftermath. He then went on to Hailufeng and found the more than 1,200 men led by Dong Lang, whose ranks had been broken in the fighting at Liusha; reorganizing them into the Fourth Regiment of the Second Division of the Chinese Worker-Peasant Revolutionary Army, soon raised to the Red Second Division, Yan Changyi was named the division's Party representative and a member of the East River Special Committee, with Dong Lang as its commander. Joining forces with the Hailufeng worker-peasant militia, the Red Second Division launched Guangdong's third armed uprising on October 30, took Haifeng and Lufeng, and set up the Hailufeng soviet government. Afterward, in the counter-blows against the encircling Kuomintang troops, he was gravely wounded, and after great hardship made his way by a roundabout road through Hong Kong to Shanghai for treatment; once healed, he became a member of the Central Military Commission and secretary of the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission.

Xing Shizhen (1903–1929), whose original name was Xing Lüxiang, was a native of Fenxi county, Shanxi. In his early years, at the county higher primary school in his home district, he came under the influence of the May Fourth Movement; after graduating he went south to Hankou and enlisted. In 1927 he joined the Chinese Communist Party and served as a cadre of the CCP Central Military Commission and of the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission, charged with directing work among the soldiery. He was often active around Wusong in Shanghai, leading and rousing the masses in struggle against the imperialists and the Kuomintang reactionaries.

Zhang Jichun (?–1933), a native of Liling county, Hunan, and a member of the Chinese Communist Party, was a graduate of the second squad of the first class of the Whampoa Military Academy. In 1929 he served as deputy chief marshal of the picket corps of the Shanghai General Labor Union, and that year helped lead the two great anti-reactionary demonstrations in Shanghai on August 30 and August 1. (He was released from prison in the winter of 1931 and died of illness in 1933.)

On the afternoon of August 24, 1929, Peng Pai, Yang Yin (then a standing committee member of the Sixth Politburo and head of the Central Military Department), Yan Changyi (a member of the Central Military Commission and of the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission), Xing Shizhen (a cadre of the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission), and Zhang Jichun were meeting at Bai Xin's home at No. 12 Jingyuan Li on Xinwen Road in the Huxi district of Shanghai — a regular meeting place of the Central Military and Peasant commissions — to discuss the work of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee and its military commission. Because the traitor Bai Xin had informed on them to the enemy beforehand, Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the other five were arrested by the Kuomintang reactionaries in collusion with the Shanghai Municipal Council of the Concession.

Learning that Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others had been taken, Zhou Enlai convened an emergency meeting that same night, working out a rescue with Gu Shunzhang, Chen Geng, and the other heads of the Central Special Section, and himself directing the effort to save the comrades. To keep the CCP Center secure, on September 10 he put to the Politburo a proposal to improve the way it worked: that it should meet once a week, vary the times and places, and take up political questions alone; that not every Politburo member need attend; and that of the five standing committee members one should always be held back and kept outside. On the 14th, his heart full of grief and anger, he wrote the essay "How the Four Comrades Peng, Yang, Yan, and Xing Were Seized and Killed by the Enemy," and published it under the pen name "Guansheng" in the CCP central organ, the Red Flag Daily. In that essay he set down in some detail how Peng Pai and the others had been taken, and called on the revolutionary people to "press forward along the bloodstained path of the fallen martyrs, ever onward in effort, ever onward in struggle."

Zhou Enlai wrote: "The arrest of the four comrades Peng Pai, Yang Yin, Yan Changyi, and Xing Shizhen took place at about four in the afternoon on August 24, 1929. Armed imperialist patrolmen and Chinese detectives of the Public Security Bureau, riding in several red-hulled armored cars, descended as though upon a great foe on the Huxi home of the traitor Bai Xin and his wife. Once they had posted men through the lane and around it, they went up and made the arrests; as though they knew each face beforehand, they seized by name five men in all (besides Peng, Yang, Yan, and Xing there was Comrade Zhang Jichun), while leaving Bai Xin and his wife untouched. When all had been taken, from under Bai Xin's bed they turned up a few revolutionary journals — the Bolshevik, the Red Flag, and Party central circulars. The five arrested comrades were at once bundled by the detectives into motorcars and driven straight to the Xinwen police station. After it happened, every paper was forbidden to print a word, so that the broad masses never knew their leaders had been taken."

The traitor Bai Xin was a fourth-class graduate of the Whampoa Military Academy. In 1927 he had been Party representative of the training battalion of Ye Ting's Twenty-fourth Division; in 1928 he worked in the Hailufeng revolutionary base area of Guangdong, and in 1929 he was transferred to the Central Military Commission in Shanghai. Before his defection he was secretary of the Central Military Commission. In those days the commission often called meetings of the departments concerned, chaired by its secretary Zhou Enlai, which as commission secretary Bai Xin naturally attended; the time and place were sometimes fixed by Bai Xin himself (the place this time was his own home). It was later established that Bai Xin's younger brother, Bai Yunshen, was director of the reserve bureau in the Ministry of War of the Nanjing National Government; Bai Xin had secretly sent his wife to Nanjing to make the link with Bai Yunshen, and had turned himself in to the Kuomintang municipal party branch a full month before, meaning to catch the responsible people of the Central and Jiangsu military commissions all in one net at the meeting — his purchase price for selling himself to the Kuomintang, and a large reward as well.

Ke Lin, who knew Bai Xin's history well, has said: "The matter of Bai Xin's defection was, to my mind, no accident. Bai Xin had been an instructor in the political training corps of Ye Ting's unit. At the time I was chief medical officer at the headquarters of the Fourth Army of the National Revolutionary Army; he was often ill and would come to me for treatment, and so felt kindly toward me. After the Guangzhou Uprising he followed the army to Hailufeng, and later to Shanghai, and there too he came to me for his ailments. At first he told me he had a relative who had been a fairly senior political worker in the Kuomintang's Wuhan forces, then joined the Nanchang Uprising and went south with it to Hailufeng; when the insurgent army was beaten at Chaoshan the man tried to flee, was found out by Peng Pai, and shot. As Bai Xin told me this, he showed a gnashing hatred of Peng Pai. This private grudge of Bai Xin's against Peng Pai I had once mentioned to Yang Yin, but because Yang Yin failed to report it to the Party Center in time, the Center sent Bai Xin to the Jiangsu military commission and made him Peng Pai's secretary. Afterward the Party organization established that once in Shanghai Bai Xin had entered into close ties with Fan Zhengbo, a responsible man of the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal branch, and was secretly selling out the organization and his comrades. After becoming Peng Pai's secretary, Bai Xin was forever seeking a chance to wreck the Jiangsu Provincial Committee and help the reactionaries arrest Peng Pai. On August 24, when Bai Xin notified the responsible people of the Jiangsu military commission of the meeting at the Xinwen building, he had already reported the place and the people to Fan Zhengbo in advance. When the meeting opened, Bai Xin failed to appear; the Kuomintang reactionaries' police came instead, armed to the teeth, descending as upon a great foe, and carried off Peng Pai and the other five responsible people."

Zhou Enlai had been due to attend that meeting. The target of this enemy blow was plainly, first of all, Zhou Enlai. It was his good fortune that, having some sudden business, he did not go that day, and so escaped the enemy's snare. A great pack of detectives burst in and every comrade present was carried off. To throw dust in people's eyes, the enemy afterward marched Bai Xin away with the rest.

But no fox, however cunning, can fool the hunter for good. As told above, for a long time Chen Geng, working through the counter-espionage tie Yang Dengying had built inside the Kuomintang's professional anti-Communist organs, had planted men in the enemy's vital departments, controlled the enemy's detective apparatus in Shanghai, gathered a great deal of most important intelligence, and grasped the enemy's patterns of action. In that period the enemy did learn the addresses of not a few of our Party organs, but through Yang Dengying we got word in advance and moved, and so escaped the blows. This blow, however, was planned by the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal branch, and carried out by Fan Zhengbo, its standing committee member. Fan was a C.C. Clique man, tied to Chen Lifu, and nominally an inspector of the Shanghai Public Security Bureau. The mark of this blow was that the one who dealt with the political section of the British police station was not Yang Dengying but Fan Zhengbo; that the name used was not the Songhu Garrison Command but the Shanghai Public Security Bureau; and that the arrested comrades were handed over not to the Songhu Garrison Command but to the Shanghai Public Security Bureau. And so of this blow we had no warning. But once it had happened, Zhou Enlai at once told Chen Geng to have Yang Dengying find out, and it was quickly known that Bai Xin had laid the information. On reading Chen Geng's report, Zhou Enlai that same afternoon put out the alarm that Bai Xin had turned, to spare the organization further wrecking.

### The Armed Rescue Fails

On the very night Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others were seized, at the emergency meeting Zhou Enlai chaired, he gave the Special Section two urgent tasks: first, to rescue Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others; second, to keep watch on Bai Xin's movements and resolutely eliminate him. At this time Chen Geng and Gu Shunzhang moved specially into the New World Hotel at the corner of Xizang Road and Nanjing Road to direct this heart-stopping struggle.

Peng Pai was first held in the Municipal Police station of the Concession, and on the 26th was handed over to the Shanghai Public Security Bureau, confined in the lockup of the detective squad at the Shuixian Temple by the Xiaobeimen (the Shuixian Temple stood at the meeting of the Concession and the Chinese city, with the French Concession pressing close to the north). Before the handover, when the enemy interrogated him, the Kuomintang reactionary Fang Naibin, mayor of Shantou, came to court to identify him; Peng Pai, magnificent in his righteous defiance, poured scorn on the traitor's shamelessness and laid bare the crimes of the Kuomintang reactionary government and its chieftain Chiang Kai-shek in ruining the country and the people, and was then handed over. In transit Peng Pai went on making propaganda to the soldiers escorting him. Some held him in deep respect, some openly showed sympathy, some bowed their heads and sighed; so the reactionaries gave orders forbidding the soldiers to come near him. After he was thrown into a cell of the enemy's Longhua garrison command, his fellow prisoners were full of concern for him and found ways from every cell to send him their comfort. Peng Pai kept up his revolutionary propaganda without pause, a great encouragement to them. In prison he suffered every kind of savage torture, several times reviving from the edge of death, his leg bones broken, and yet he never bent, resolved with an iron will to fight the enemy to the end. He even swept every obstacle aside to make contact with the Party organization, wrote a report to the Party Center on how they were struggling with the enemy in prison, and set out a plan for rescuing the comrades.

In the report he and Yang Yin secretly sent to the Center over their joint names, they wrote: "(1) Do everything possible to spare all five the death penalty. (2) If that cannot be done, then there is no help for it but to sacrifice An (Peng Pai's alias was Meng An) and Nao (Yang Yin's alias was Meng Nao), the two against whom there is no defense, and find a way to save the three against whom there is no confession…"

The cruel persecution Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others suffered after their arrest, and the brave struggle they waged in the enemy's prison and before its court, are set down in detail in Zhou Enlai's essay in the Red Flag Daily: "The 25th was a Sunday, and the provisional court did not sit. On the 26th the court opened; the imperialist running-dog judge and the Public Security Bureau's representative went deliberately through their motions and then ruled that the men be handed to the bureau. At the hearing only the five arrested comrades were produced; their photographs had been made ready the very day of the arrest, and they were questioned by name — plainly the traitor Bai Xin had borne witness against them in secret. At the handover the bureau too moved the men into the city in armored cars under guard. The hearing and the handover were reported in that evening's papers, but their true names were withheld. This the ruling class did on purpose, to avoid the broad masses' fierce and direct resistance."

"When the five comrades Peng and Yang reached the Public Security Bureau, the hearing opened that same evening, and the interrogators got nothing from them. Peng and Yang and the five had begun their propaganda in the lockup, and many of the security guards were moved by it. On the afternoon of the 27th the hearing reopened and ran four or five hours. When they came to Comrade Peng Pai, someone stepped forward to identify him, and Comrade Peng Pai openly acknowledged who he was. Asked to recount his life, Comrade Peng spoke with fervor for more than an hour, from his peasant work in Hailufeng before he ever joined the Party down to the founding of the soviet there. When he came to the punishment of counter-revolutionaries in Hailufeng, he cried out at the interrogator: you gang of counter-revolutionaries — how many of you we killed in Hailufeng, past counting; you need ask no more, just shoot me and be done. Even the interrogator was moved. When they came to Comrade Yang Yin, he too openly acknowledged his own course. The hearing over, back in the lockup, the five comrades sang the Internationale together in shared defiance, and it rang through the whole hall."

The Center threw the Special Section's every resource into rescuing Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others, the plan being to storm the prison van by force on the road when the enemy moved them to Longhua. Word came out, the moment they were pushed into the lockup, that the Public Security Bureau meant to "interrogate" them. The Central Special Section went into action at once. First, Li Qiang was posted near the detective squad at the Shuixian Temple to keep watch (he lodged in a small inn on the French Concession side, north of Minguo Road). Second, Chen Geng had Yang Dengying arrange, through his part in "sitting on the bench," to send in a comrade Peng Pai knew, to stand behind Yang Dengying and signal to Peng Pai that the organization was working to save him, so that he might be prepared. Third, the Action Section had the Red Squad stand ready to act. This "interrogation" was held on the night of August 26. That day Yang Dengying played the part of a savage inquisitor, cursing furiously, but with great cunning passed the Party's meaning to Comrade Peng Pai.

At dawn on August 28 the enemy moved Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others from the lockup to confinement in the Longhua garrison command. Getting this word, Zhou Enlai ordered out every man in the Special Section who could shoot, to lie in wait along the prison van's route and try to stop it and free them. That day the Red Squad members and many others disguised themselves as a film crew shooting on location. The weapons were packed in a single leather case, and a man was assigned to bring it to the spot by motorbike. This time everything was carefully arranged, every man turned out, some disguised as small peddlers, some as passersby, all coming to the scene and forming up as though to "storm the execution ground." But the man bringing the weapons — Fan Mengju, proprietor of the "Sanmin Photo Studio," who was himself to turn traitor in 1931 — arrived late; and clearing the lubricating grease from inside the guns took some time. They could not be used at once, and washing off the grease with kerosene and oiling the guns took better than an hour; on top of that the enemy's guard was tight, so that to go ahead with the original plan would surely have brought heavy casualties and small chance of success. Zhou Enlai at once ordered the action called off, and our people withdrew swiftly from the scene.

Li Qiang, who was on the scene that day and helped direct the operation, has said:

The Central Special Section's armed operations were usually carried out by the "Red Squad" under the Third Section. This rescue of Peng Pai was on a larger scale; the "Red Squad" had not hands enough, so Zhou Enlai ordered out every man in the Central Special Section who could shoot. About twenty took part in the rescue. Once they had their task, they worked through the night on the preparations, going out to buy some urgently needed gear, such as tools for breaking iron locks. Early on the 28th the Central Special Section comrades gathered at the Section's organ on Tongfu Road, and when Fan Mengju brought the guns by motorbike, they opened the little case and found the Mausers all coated with a layer of grease and unusable. They at once sent someone out for kerosene, washed off the grease, and oiled them afresh, which took an hour or two. When all was ready they set out by truck. To cover the operation, the comrades had set a tripod on the truck bed with a movie camera on it, as though shooting a film on location. The truck drove to a fork in the road at a place called Fenglin Bridge that the prison van had to pass, and there they got down and spread out to act. But they waited a long time and saw no enemy van come. As it turned out, the Central Special Section comrades had missed their chance; the enemy had already taken Peng Pai and the others off to Longhua. The armed rescue of the prison van did not succeed.

Ke Lin has recalled: "On the day of the rescue, Chen Geng and I waited for word in the French Park; a long time passed with no shots, and only then did we know the rescue plan had not been carried through."

The rescue failed, but the operation shook Shanghai, and the enemy was badly shaken.

### Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and Four Martyrs Die Heroically

This rescue of Peng Pai and the others did not bring the hoped-for result, and they were escorted by the enemy to the Longhua garrison command. Zhou Enlai's essay records: "After the second hearing the Public Security Bureau, in deep dread of some mishap, transferred them to the garrison command at dawn on the 28th. It happened that very night that Chiang Kai-shek was fired upon, and every suspect guard was marched to the command; from Xiong Shihui at the top down to the judicial section, all were thrown into a scrambling panic. The guards under arrest, put to savage torture, their flesh flying and their bones and limbs broken, not one would give up the source of the shots at Chiang. And so, in the three days the five comrades were held (the 28th to the 30th), no hearing was held at all; only, hands cuffed and feet chained, the irons clanking, they were kept under close guard in the command's lockup. In those three days the five let slip not a moment, ceaselessly making propaganda to the prisoners and to the soldiers within the command. When Comrades Peng and Yang spoke to the soldiers of the bitterest things, there were soldiers who beat their breasts and wept, cursing that the Kuomintang warlords must be killed to the last man. When they spoke of the stirring things, they sang the Internationale and the Young Vanguard song together, and the soldiers and the prisoners raised the slogans in answer, so that the grief-choked prison turned all at once into a stirring battlefield. Some of the robbers, driven by poverty to plunder, were moved to see the light, saying: only by following the Communist Party is there a proper road out for us poor folk. Some of the masses jailed on suspicion of revolution grew all the firmer, saying: from now on there is only one road for us, the road of revolution. Some of the arrested comrades said: after all they are our leaders at the Center, fit to be our model. And some who had long heard Peng Pai's great name, hearing that Peng Pai was here, vied with one another to come and look, while a few who knew Peng Pai counted it an honor to have known him."

"Once inside the garrison command they knew they must die, and so most of the letters they got out were words of farewell. They charged the comrades in the Party not to grieve over their arrest but to press on and win the revolution's advance. They charged the Party's important responsible comrades to spare themselves for the Party's sake. They hoped that within the Party the struggle against the opposition would rely more on education, to educate the whole membership. When they gathered to talk, they touched on many political questions too, though what they said was never got out. They talked and laughed as ever; Comrade Yang Yin once said with a laugh, 'Hearing the Way in the morning, one may die content at evening,' and of the last wishes for their own dear ones, they charged that these too be given for the Party's sake."

With the threat of death pressing close before them, in life's last hour, Peng Pai and Yang Yin wrote jointly to Zhou Enlai ("Guansheng") and set down their last secret report to the Party Center:

Guansheng, and all the household, young and old:

Our being taken this time through Bai Xin's doing is now past saving. Zhang, Meng, and Nao have all openly acknowledged who they are and done their utmost to spread the word; the common soldiers under the enemy (that is, the "gray tunics," the Kuomintang men set to guard us) and our fellow prisoners show us great sympathy. The soldiers in particular, hearing us talk, sigh aloud and beat their breasts. Our spirits here are very good. Brothers, do not grieve that we die; only take good care of yourselves.

The rest still hold out and admit nothing.

— Nao and An

Before he met his death Peng Pai also wrote a letter in blood, taking leave of his wife Xu Bing (Xu Yuqing) and charging her to "press forward" and carry on the revolutionary struggle: "Bing, my sister: from here we part forever. May you press forward. I thank you for your love! Take the greatest care of yourself! There is no end to what I would say. Your Pai."

On August 30 the four comrades Peng Pai, Yang Yin, Yan Changyi, and Xing Shizhen were at last killed by the enemy. That day Peng Pai walked head high to the execution ground, calm and self-possessed, magnificent in his righteous defiance; on the way there they shouted slogans the whole road: "Down with imperialism!" "Down with the traitor Chiang Kai-shek!" "Long live the Chinese Red Army!" "Long live the Chinese Communist Party!" — showing to the full their unshakable resolve to give their lives for the communist cause and their courage in facing death as a homecoming. The day they fell was but a week from the day they were seized.

Of how Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others met their end, Zhou Enlai's essay in the Red Flag Daily records: "On that 30th, the provisional court suddenly and deliberately summoned the five again for a retrial, and the command accordingly, that afternoon, escorted the five under close guard, for form's sake, to the court. There came with them a platoon of infantry, a number of the bureau's detectives, several adjutants of the command, and station patrolmen carrying two submachine guns as a guard. The court sat in a special chamber, no onlookers allowed; the questions and answers were as before. The judge and the assessors — the imperialist consuls and the station's lawyers — mumbled a few things and never made public just what the crimes of Peng, Yang, and the others were. The defense counsel too said a few things, to which the judge and the rest paid no heed, and in the end no one could hear what the judge read out in the verdict. Not until Peng, Yang, and the four comrades had already been shot did the English-language papers of Shanghai make a great show of printing that the court's verdict sentenced Peng, Yang, and the others to eight years' imprisonment. This retrial was simply a farcical piece of murder played out between the imperialist bandits and the Kuomintang warlords in concert. They meant to deceive the masses with the forms of law, yet feared above all that the masses might know. In truth the broad revolutionary masses long ago put no faith in this cursed law of yours! From the 'April 12th' of 1927 to now, the imperialists and the Kuomintang have assassinated and murdered on false charges more of the masses than can be counted. The blood shed in slaughter to make a river has long dyed red the hearts of the toiling worker-peasant masses, who know only to answer your White Terror with the fighting strength of the masses — who still cares for this cursed law of yours!"

"After the hearing at the provisional court, they were escorted at once under heavy armed guard back to the garrison command. On the way from the court to the prison van, Comrade Yan Changyi raised his hand and shouted 'Long live the Communist Party!,' and the five looked at one another and smiled. At the command they were taken first to the lockup, and a table of food and wine was sent in; the five comrades all knew their hour of death had come — who would touch this table of infernal 'reward feast'! In about an hour Peng Pai, Yang Yin, Yan Changyi, and Xing Shizhen were brought out for execution. The four spoke their last words with fervor to the soldiers and the prisoners, and went out the prison gate singing the Internationale and shouting slogans, so that the common soldiers and the jail's inmates alike wept aloud, and even the lockup keepers covered their faces. The killing was done in secret, the place of shooting not even outside the command — the first such case in Xiong Shihui's tenure — and one may well imagine the gravity and the fear of it. The shots that killed the four comrades were heard faintly by the prisoners. One inmate went so far as to kill a fowl and make offering to the sky, so true was the feeling they stirred! At their death the four left behind three undershirts as keepsakes, their last belongings. After they died the command sent men to bury them in secret, to wipe out the traces. But though the broad revolutionary masses cannot see their leaders' brave remains, their leaders' brave battle-record will be remembered in every heart, forever bright, never to fade!"

"When the five comrades Peng and Yang were first taken, the Kuomintang Center in Nanjing at once had the boastful merit-report from the Kuomintang municipal branch in Shanghai. Their answering wire was an order to Xiong Shihui to shoot the men without delay; and with the affair of the shots at Chiang keeping these running-dogs too busy to eat or sleep in peace, the shooting was carried out all the faster."

His heart full of the deepest grief and a boundless rage, Zhou Enlai, having set down how the five comrades Peng, Yang, Yan, and Xing were seized and killed by the enemy, went on to declare: "In today's China, where the class struggle rages fierce, how many of the revolutionary masses, how many revolutionary leaders, die under the enemy's open guns and hidden arrows past all counting! This is a thing scarcely to be avoided before the revolution succeeds — and it is the blood-foundation of that success. Without revolutionary fighters pressing on over the fallen, the great road to the revolution's victory cannot be built! Every revolutionary fighter who falls, whether at the front or in the rear, has his great worth. Above all the fall of a revolutionary leader has its indelible battle-record, shining in the hearts of a thousand thousand of the masses, fusing into the driving force of the great revolution, kindling the revolutionary ardor of every oppressed one, all rushing together toward the revolution's fire! And so before the fallen martyrs we have no need of weeping grief, but need to carry on all the more keenly, all the more firmly, the will of the fallen martyrs, to press forward along their bloodstained path, ever onward in effort, ever onward in struggle!"

Zhang Ji'en, who worked at the CCP Central organs at the time, has said: "After Peng Pai and the others fell, the Center put out a circular in memory of the four martyrs. Zhou Enlai drafted it. It quoted from Peng and Yang's farewell the line 'Hearing the Way in the morning, one may die content at evening.' Zhou Enlai was in deep grief; he wrote weeping, and we copied at his side, and he said it must go out quickly. He said too: we must wipe the enemy out, we must finish Bai Xin."
