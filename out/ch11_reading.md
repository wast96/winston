## 11

On the day Lin Nansheng brought Miss Lan and her son into the Edinburgh Building, he had gone to the trouble of engaging the chef from the Red House to cook a French dinner at home. Then, gazing at her with tenderness, he said, "I remember you like a fried steak."

Miss Lan said nothing. She looked down at the mushroom soup on the table, and only after a long time took up the spoon and drank, mouthful by mouthful, very slowly, very carefully. Ever since the Eighth Route Army office in Chongqing had secretly sent her back to Shanghai, she had become, overnight, another person. Every day, besides seeing her son to and from school on time, she bought vegetables, cooked, tended flowers, tidied the rooms; and at night, bundled under the quilt, she would knit a sweater and teach her son Shanghainese and English at once. The boy had lived three years in the nanny's home and had picked up a mouthful of Subei dialect, like a wild little thing just fled from the north bank in a famine.

That day, as he finished the last of the dessert, the boy said to Lin Nansheng, "I want more."

Miss Lan said, "Zilu, remember: before you speak, you greet the person first."

So her son grinned at Lin Nansheng and called out, "Uncle."

Lin Nansheng smiled. "You should call me Papa."

Miss Lan's eyes came up all at once, to find Lin Nansheng turned toward her, watching.

But just then Zilu said, in Subei-accented Shanghainese, "My papa died long ago."

Late, when the house had gone still, Miss Lan tucked the quilt around her son, switched off the bedside lamp, slipped soundlessly out of bed, and felt her way in the dark to Lin Nansheng's room. The moment she was under the covers she drew one of his arms beneath her head for a pillow, let out a long breath, and said, "In Shanghai, do you know what they call a woman like me?" Without waiting for Lin Nansheng to answer, she went on, "A worn shoe, a wild chicken, an oil-bottle drag, a broom-star..."

"I've booked the church," Lin Nansheng cut in. "For this coming Sunday."

"Better call it off. Can a woman like me even set foot in a church?"

"I've asked Mr. Du to be our witness."

"I won't marry you." Miss Lan reached out in the dark and felt for Lin Nansheng's face. "Don't forget, from the moment I left Chongqing I became a wanted woman to the Bureau."

"All that is past," Lin Nansheng said. "I have the power to protect you now."

Not long after the Juntong was reorganized into the Baomiju, Lin Nansheng was appointed intelligence commissioner for the Shanghai district and director of the municipal government's investigation office, and concurrently head of the Eastern News Agency, with full charge of the gathering and analysis of party, government, and military intelligence in the Shanghai area, answering directly to headquarters in Nanjing.

After a moment's silence Miss Lan let out another long breath, buried her face in Lin Nansheng's chest, and said, "You should find yourself a good woman and have a child of your own."

Lin Nansheng thought all at once of Zhu Yizhen. He put his arms around Miss Lan and suddenly, at her ear, gave a soundless laugh. "It may be that I'm the one dragging you down. I'm a man who could lie down to sleep any day and never wake."

"Then every year I'll sweep your grave, and every day I'll burn incense for you," Miss Lan said in earnest, "until I die."

But the one to die before either of them was, of all people, Zilu. One night some months later, at the door of the Metropol Theater on Avenue Foch, there came a gunfight between military police and civil police that shocked the whole nation. After three military policemen beat a patrolman at the theater door, the affair swiftly grew into a brawl. Police came in force from the station to reinforce, the military-police regiment turned out two truckloads of its own, and fully armed gendarmes sealed off every road around the scene.

That day was a Sunday, and the theater was showing Dragon and Phoenix Wedding Candles. When the gunfire broke out in one solid wall of sound, the nanny took Zilu and pressed with the terrified audience toward the exit. Just as they ran down the steps a stray bullet, come from far off, struck the nanny, and struck Zilu with her. Many townspeople fell amid the screaming.

For three days running Miss Lan locked herself in her room, would not eat or drink, made no sound, and, as on the day years before when she learned her husband had fallen, showed not a trace of tears on her face. On the fourth day she opened the door and came out. She did not spare Lin Nansheng a glance, but sat down at the table, drained the bowl of thin gruel in one draught, and ate nearly every last morsel on the table. Then she went back to her room, lay down, and fell into a heavy sleep.

The fifth day was the day of the public memorial, at the Central Funeral Parlor, where the commissioners of the Ministry of the Interior and the Ministry of National Defense would all attend. Miss Lan rose at first light and carefully trimmed her nails, bathed, washed and dried her hair, pinned it up, and made up her face. At the last she changed into a plain cheongsam, found a veiled hat and put it on, then went straight to the study, pulled open Lin Nansheng's drawer, took out his Browning pistol, checked it over with a practiced hand, and racked a round into the chamber with a single motion.

In five days it was the first time Miss Lan had looked Lin Nansheng full in the face. "I told you," she said. "Sooner or later I would drag you down."

"I'm not afraid." Lin Nansheng looked back at her. "But I can't let you go to your death."

"When he was killed at Xiaguan, I told myself I would avenge him, and I would live on for my son." Miss Lan said it calmly. "Now, all I want is to die."

With that she started for the door, but Lin Nansheng caught her in his arms. Miss Lan did not struggle; she only turned her head to a potted landscape on the windowsill.

Lin Nansheng lifted her veil, turned her face to his, and made her look into his eyes. "Can you not live for me?"

Something shifted, faintly, in Miss Lan's eyes — and in the same instant she pressed the pistol up under Lin Nansheng's jaw. "Don't try to stop me. I'll shoot."

Lin Nansheng held her still, though his voice came drier and drier. "If you live, at least you can sweep their graves each year, and burn incense for them each day."

Zhu Yizhen's core work was to transmit the intelligence Meng Annan gathered back to the base area in northern Jiangsu. Sometimes, too, she received the base area's directives, setting their cipher down on paper or else stitching it straight into a piece of embroidery — which of the two depending entirely on the grade of the directive — and then delivering them to the Wuyue Embroidery Shop on Ximaqiao Lane, from where they were parceled out along the several lines.

After the Chinese Communist delegation withdrew from Shanghai, she received directives ever more often, going to the embroidery shop once or twice almost every week. One day, the moment she left the shop she felt herself followed, and after a long, roundabout course found she had only been imagining it. But no sooner had she got home and changed into house clothes than there came a knock at the door.

Zhu Yizhen opened the door and saw, at a glance, Lin Nansheng. He wore a gray gabardine gown and a felt hat, a newspaper in one hand, standing in the doorway as though coming home. He took off the hat and handed it to Zhu Yizhen along with the paper. "I found you after all."

Zhu Yizhen stood there dumbstruck, and even as Lin Nansheng came inside she was still biting hard on her lip.

Lin Nansheng looked around the four walls, and let his eyes come to rest at last on the wedding photograph high on the wall. "Is this your new superior?"

Zhu Yizhen faltered. "He is my husband. I'm married."

Lin Nansheng looked again at the man in the photograph. "He's at least ten years older than you."

Only now did Zhu Yizhen notice she still held his hat and newspaper. She set them on the table, sat down on a chair to one side, and, turning her face to the spotless floor, said, "Where are the men you've brought? Let them all come up."

"So you knew all along I was in Shanghai." Lin Nansheng sat down in silence across the table, stared at her a long while, then lowered his eyes. "You should have let me know you were still alive."

"Could I have let you know?" Zhu Yizhen said flatly. "If you haven't come to arrest me, then please go."

"But I have a great deal to say to you." Lin Nansheng did not move from his seat. He picked up the hat from the table and turned it over in his hands a while. "Why don't you ask me how I found you?"

Zhu Yizhen sat without stirring a hair, though her heart leapt in that instant into her throat.

In truth Lin Nansheng had found Zhu Yizhen by a piece of embroidery. Two months earlier the Baomiju's action squad had broken up a Communist courier post at Xinzhuang, and among the great mass of goods seized Lin Nansheng had seen an embroidery worked in the Butterflies in Love with Flowers pattern, and thought at once of the days he had lived with Zhu Yizhen in the attic. But he gave nothing away. Alone, over nearly half a month, he combed almost every embroidery workshop in Shanghai, and only at the Wuyue Embroidery Shop did he meet again those stitches, those colors, that composition he knew so well. For weeks after that, whenever he had a free hour, he would sit in the teahouse across from the shop over a pot of Anji white tea, playing go with the other patrons and watching, through the window, every woman who came and went at the shop.

But he told Zhu Yizhen none of this, nor did he mention Ji Zhongyuan. After a long silence he only sighed. "As long as you're alive, nothing else matters." With that Lin Nansheng put on his hat, rose, and walked to the door — then stopped short. "Don't worry. I won't come again."

Zhu Yizhen still sat without moving, watching him calmly, her gaze so black there was scarcely a gleam of light in the eyes. Only when the sound of Lin Nansheng's steps had died away on the stairs did she slump into the chair like a punctured ball. But Zhu Yizhen was quickly on her feet again. In a few strides she reached the window, watched Lin Nansheng's back until it vanished at the street corner, then went to the bedroom, changed out of her house clothes, gathered a basin of washing things, and hurried out and down the stairs, across the road, into a bathhouse opposite.

She bought a ticket and went in at the front, and when she came out at the back a moment later, the basin she had carried was gone from her hands.

Where Zhu Yizhen went was the French Photo Agency. The moment they met, in a room piled with odds and ends, Meng Annan said sternly, "I told you, you can't come here."

"But it's urgent." Zhu Yizhen told him quickly all that had just happened, then added, "I'm certain he tailed me from the moment I left the embroidery shop."

"What is he to you?"

"This is no time to go into that. You have to give the order — clear out the embroidery shop at once."

"If something were going to go wrong, it would already be too late." Meng Annan said it without a moment's thought. "I've read the file from your investigation. Why did you never once mention this man Lin Nansheng?"

"Could I have mentioned him? If I had, I'd be a Nationalist agent, and long gone from this world." Zhu Yizhen said, "The order I was given at the time was to use the chance of an intelligence exchange to draw him over and turn him."

Meng Annan considered. "If I judge it rightly, the reason he came to your door was to pass you one message: that the embroidery shop may already be blown."

Zhu Yizhen's eyes went wide, and it was a while before she said, "That's not possible. He's an agent."

"In our circle, who isn't an agent?" Meng Annan thought a moment. "Now go home, move the wireless set, then wait for me at the fallback place."

"Can I even go back?"

"If you could get out, you can certainly get back in." Meng Annan suddenly smiled. "If he wanted to land the big fish," he said, smiling, "he'd first haul you in and wring a confession out of you, then set a watch on the house and guard the stump to wait for the hare. He wouldn't choose to tip you off for no reason at all."

"You seem to know their arrest procedures very well."

"Of course." Meng Annan said, "If we didn't know them, how would we ever defeat them?"

"Perhaps he means to strike the mountain to startle the tiger, and then watch us."

Meng Annan laughed again. "With the fighting at the front come to this pass, would they still have that kind of patience?"

Late one night some days later, in their fallback room, Zhu Yizhen lay on her back on the bed and said, "I propose we wire the old home and ask them to verify Lin Nansheng's identity."

Meng Annan turned over on the floor. "As an intelligence officer, you shouldn't harbor that kind of curiosity."

"It isn't curiosity," Zhu Yizhen said. "It bears on our work from here on, and on our safety."

"But can you be sure the verification itself would be safe? That at none of those steps there'd be an enemy mole planted?" Meng Annan sighed, then went on slowly, "You have to understand, our intelligence people in Shanghai aren't only the East China Bureau's — there are Yan'an's as well, and the Jiangsu Special Committee's, and the Comintern's. To verify a man who isn't on your own line means risking a crossing of lines, and that brings the danger of exposure to both sides."

Zhu Yizhen had nothing more to say. She closed her eyes in the dark, and yet the past came rushing at her once again.

After a long silence, Meng Annan suddenly spoke again. "Does this man's identity really matter so much to you?"
