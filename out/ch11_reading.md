## Chapter Eleven. The Hour of the Dog, Second Half (8 p.m.)

{v} But Li Bi saw at a glance that each of the pavilion’s four columns was a full five arm-spans around; the cost of freighting in the raw timber alone would have been enough to ruin ten-odd modest households.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Dog, second half.

{d} Chang’an; Wannian County; Pingkang Ward.

Set against the lantern-viewing crowds of the other wards and markets, Pingkang Ward, the most bustling of them all on ordinary days, was by contrast far quieter now. For the girls of the Pingkang Quarter had every one been carried off on outings by their noble patrons, and the pleasure-houses stood empty. Only around the second watch, deep in the night, would the girls and their patrons drift back in one by one to set out the wine and make merry.

The moment she came into the ward Tanqi wrinkled her nose in distaste. The street just now was thick with the smell of storax, given off by the perfumed carriages out driving since the lamps were lit. The scent had been blended far too rich and coquettish, yet it clung; once it caught on a sleeve there was no shaking it off. She had no wish to be mistaken for one of the escort-girls.

“Rest easy,” said Zhang Xiaojing. “No one will mistake you. Tonight every powder-tablet with the least standing is out there beyond the walls.” At first Tanqi was reassured; then, thinking it over, she saw it was plainly a jab at her. She was on the point of flaring up when Zhang Xiaojing raised his whip and said, “That there is the Right Minister’s mansion.”

Tanqi looked. So Li Linfu’s residence stood directly across from the Pingkang Quarter, its walls high and its tiles gray, and inside, she feared, it ran a good ten courtyards deep. Twelve long halberds were ranged before the gate, and to either side stood a pair of merit-columns, capped with tile-tube crowns of black, giving off an air out of the common run. Strangely enough, though a row of red paper lanterns hung under the eaves, their light reached only a few zhang before the gate; everywhere else was pitch dark. Seen from afar, it was like some black beast opening a blood-red maw.

The man who thwarted the young master at every turn lived here . . . For no reason she could name, Tanqi shivered, and hurriedly urged her horse on a few quick steps, as if lingering too long might get her eaten.

“Oh yes—where has the deacon Yisi got to?” Tanqi suddenly remembered there was this one more following along. Zhang Xiaojing turned his head and swept a glance about; there was no sign of him on the avenue. He had not been seen since crossing the Vermilion Bird Avenue—gone astray, most likely.

“Never mind him. Let him do as he likes.”

Zhang Xiaojing knew this quarter like the back of his hand. The two of them passed two crossroads and came upon a cluster of gray-tiled residences at the northeast corner.

These residences looked to be the work of army artisans: nearly identical in style, ranged in strict order, each of three courtyards and seven rooms. The one thing that told them apart was the bird-and-beast banner streaming high over each central court—here a bear, there a tiger, here a falcon, there a river-dragon, no two the same. These were precisely the resident-agent courtyards that the ten military commissioners maintained in Chang’an, each court’s banner answering to its commissioner’s army-ensign, so that a single look told you whose courtyard it was.

Across the street from the resident-agent courtyards ran a jumble of shops, all dealers in luxuries—pearls and gems, aromatics, gold and silver ware, silks, lacquerwork. Every year the resident-agent courtyards bought up great quantities of gifts in the capital, and the merchants were hardly going to let such a fine opportunity slip.

Just now, though, the shops had all shut their doors, the owners and their hands run off to see the lanterns, and the whole street was all but deserted. Zhang Xiaojing and Tanqi took their bearings and, turning this way and that, came to Liu’s Bookshop at the very end of the lane. Its shopfront was smaller than the others, scarcely the width of two door-panels, hemmed on either side by a cart-and-horse company and a silversmith’s. At this hour the bookshop had long since closed, its shutter-boards all put up.

By the assassin’s own confession, this Liu’s Bookshop was a firepoint of the Shouzhuolang. “Firepoint” was a cant word of their own, meaning the contact-point through which their commissions were handed out. The man in charge of a firepoint was called the firemaster, and he too was a key figure Zhang Xiaojing had come to find.

By rights he should first have had the assassin call the door open, explain the situation, and only then go in to parley with the firemaster. But before turning into the lane Zhang Xiaojing had confirmed with the watchtower that the cart escorting the assassin was still on the road, and would be a while yet coming up.

Zhang Xiaojing could wait no longer. Ever since learning that the Jing’an Bureau had been attacked, he had in truth been even more on edge than Tanqi. That ill foreboding within him grew stronger by the moment. He had to seize every last snap of the fingers.

He did not go to knock on the shutter-boards, but walked instead to the wall on the left of them. It was a wall of yellow rammed earth, coarsely tamped, pocked all over with hollows large and small. Zhang Xiaojing counted to the tenth little hollow from the right in the third row, worked a finger in, and at the bottom felt the end of a short cord.

The end of the cord was tied in a loop, its other end threaded through a small hole in the wall. Zhang Xiaojing hooked his finger in and tugged gently—five tugs, a brief pause, then three more, and at the last two quick flicks.

This was the contact-method the assassin had disclosed. Without pulling this cord, or pulling it wrong, the bookshop would never show you its true face.

Not long after he had finished, a shutter-board came down from within with a clatter, and a wary eye flashed in the gap: “Spring River?”

“A wisp of white cloud drifts slowly off.”

This was the seventeenth line of “A Moonlit Night on the Spring River,” and also a token by which to prove one’s identity. There was a moment’s silence within, and then: “You are not Liu Shiqi, and you are not Mojialuo.” Zhang Xiaojing flashed his bronze medallion: “I am Zhang Xiaojing, Commander of the Jing’an Bureau. Liu Shiqi sent me. I have urgent business to discuss.”

“Then where are Liu Shiqi and the rest?”

“On the road, over by Yongle Ward; they will be here shortly.” Zhang Xiaojing glanced back at the watchtower.

The watchtower had just then flashed over a burst of signal: the cart had already passed Yongle Ward, and was only two or three crossings away.

“Then let us talk when he arrives.” With that the other made to put the board back up. Zhang Xiaojing slapped a palm down hard on it, his manner hard as iron: “Government business does not wait. Will you have me come in now, or wait for the county commandant to bring a party in person?”

The threat seemed to work. There was silence in the room for a moment, then another shutter-board was quickly taken down, opening a gap of half a door. Zhang Xiaojing and Tanqi turned sideways and slipped in; a hand within lit the tortoise-shaped candlestick on the table and held it up.

The firemaster was a white-haired old man, his skin cracked and dark as a jujube, giving no hint of what people he sprang from. Behind him stood row upon row of bamboo bookshelves, laden with all manner of costly silk scrolls, each on an ivory-white roller with crystal clasps, their kinds marked with tags of five-colored cloth. A faint scent of camphor pervaded the place, clearing the head and rousing the wits, and keeping the bookworms off as well.

These books were not for reading, but were gifts kept expressly for presentation to grandees and nobles. A firepoint had all sorts of contact-documents to handle every day, and a bookshop made the perfect cover for it.

Zhang Xiaojing did not trouble with pleasantries; the moment he was through the door he asked point-blank: “I want to know who put out the commission that sent Liu Shiqi and Mojialuo to assassinate the elder Puzhe at the Persian Temple.”

The old man held up the candlestick, its light falling into the deep folds of his face, layer upon layer of light and shadow, so that no one could quite grasp his true expression.

“The Commander must know that we Shouzhuolang keep our clients’ secrets. That request I must beg leave to refuse.”

Zhang Xiaojing gave a cold snort: “This assassination commission is now bound up with a great case that threatens the whole of Chang’an. The court must have its answer; anyone who deliberately conceals it will be dealt with as an accomplice!” The old man smiled in disdain: “The Shouzhuolang are founded upon good faith—how else should we win the trust of all under heaven? Never mind the Commander; were the Prefect of Jingzhao himself to come, still we could not tell.”

Rage blazed up in Zhang Xiaojing, and he drove a fist hard into the wall, so that the shelves in the room all shuddered. But the old man held the candlestick steady in his hand: “There is only this one old fellow here. The Commander may bind and torture me as he pleases; I shall not resist—but neither should you look for me to say anything.” With a hiss Zhang Xiaojing whipped out his crossbow and pressed it to the man’s forehead, and said in a chilling voice: “Liu Shiqi said that too, at the start.”

He left the rest unsaid, but his action made it plain enough. To enter here with Liu Shiqi’s passwords was proof that he had had the whole thing laid out for him. The old man’s right brow gave the faintest twitch: “Shiqi broke the rule, and brought disaster on his kin; him I cannot save. The Shouzhuolang, the Shouzhuolang: every kindness repaid, every debt settled.”

This was the maxim of the Shouzhuolang. When a Shouzhuolang went out on business, his household was kept behind within the garrison-town. Liu Shiqi had betrayed the secret of the firepoint; even if he escaped with his own life, his family were as good as dead.

Zhang Xiaojing said: “He is the least of it. If anything befalls Chang’an, the whole of the Shouzhuolang will die!”

Seeing how fierce Zhang Xiaojing was in look and voice, the old man sighed: “The client’s name and standing this old fellow can by no means disclose; but if the Commander wishes to ask something else, within the bounds of what I may say, this old fellow will hold nothing back.”

A man who could serve as a firemaster in Chang’an was, sure enough, no ordinary sort. He knew Zhang Xiaojing had the government at his back and was not to be offended too far, and so proposed a way around it. The Shouzhuolang had an intelligence network all their own in the capital, and might well hold facts the Jing’an Bureau did not.

So Zhang Xiaojing told him the whole matter of the Turkic Wolf Guards and the Que-le Huo-duo, and asked whether he had heard anything of it. When the old man had heard him out, he was aghast: “This old fellow has not been out of doors today, and knew nothing of what was afoot . . . that so great a thing should have happened. Allow me to go and make inquiry.”

Holding the candlestick, he turned and walked off into the depths of the shelves.

Zhang Xiaojing set his crossbow down on the table and waited, a little vexed. He too was deeply anxious over the attack on the Jing’an Bureau; that fist a moment ago had been less a threat to the firemaster than a way of venting the dread within him.

Just then Tanqi quietly tugged at Zhang Xiaojing’s sleeve: “This old man—he has the smell of storax on him, but no scent of camphor.” Zhang Xiaojing gave an “Mm” and did not react at all. Tanqi grew a little impatient—how could a man be so dull about such things? “He says he has spent the whole day in the bookshop; then how is it there is not the least trace of camphor on him, but nothing except the storax from outside?”

Zhang Xiaojing’s pupils contracted sharply. With a great sweep he shoved aside the table before him and leapt fiercely into the shelves. The candlestick hung on a bronze hook beside the bamboo frame, and beside it was no one at all.

No—to be exact, there was one more. Here lay a short-bearded fat man of fifty-odd, wrapped in a fox-fur robe, sprawled among the shelves, his throat opened by a very fine, precise cut, his eyes still round and staring.

In an instant Zhang Xiaojing understood: this was the true firemaster. That old man was, he feared, someone the mysterious organization had sent to silence him. They had put out the assassination commission with the Shouzhuolang, and the go-between had been this firemaster; kill him, and the trail would be cut off for good.

Who could have known that no sooner had the deed been done than Zhang Xiaojing came knocking. An ordinary killer, having struck, would simply leave, paying no mind to a knocking at the door. But this fellow’s quickness to adapt, and the boldness of his conduct, made one gasp. In the shortest span he had thought instead to impersonate the firemaster, and so drawn out how far the Jing’an Bureau’s inquiry had come.

This time even an old hand like Zhang Xiaojing had been fooled. Had Tanqi not sniffed out the flaw in the scent, they might still have been kept in the dark.

Zhang Xiaojing had only just grasped all this, and had not yet turned to raise the alarm, when a loud man’s scream of agony rang out from within the shop, and then the whole row of shelves beside him toppled one into another like a run of dominoes, burying him and the firemaster’s corpse beneath. Zhang Xiaojing first shouted to Tanqi to get out of the shop, lest the man come back at them, then heaved with both arms and shoved the shelves upright again.

It was lucky these were bamboo frames, laden only with scrolls, and not too heavy. But the crushing weight set the wound in the firemaster’s throat spurting blood again, which spattered onto Zhang Xiaojing’s short tunic.

Zhang Xiaojing got to his feet and rushed to the back of the shop, to find the rear window open. He thrust his head out and saw, on a rooftop far off, a black figure leaping and racing away, its lithe agility nothing at all like an old man’s.

He was on the point of giving chase when a shriek sounded again in his ears—this time from outside the shop’s front door. It was Tanqi!

Zhang Xiaojing had no choice but to give up that quarry for now and turn and dash for the door. The moment he was out, seven or eight lanterns had already flared up outside, and ten-odd men, got up like smiths and carters, were closing in on Tanqi with ugly looks on their faces. Seeing Zhang Xiaojing come running out, they whipped out their anvil-hammers and iron bars.

“Where is the firemaster?” the one at their head roared.

These men too were Shouzhuolang, the guards charged with the firepoint, who lay hidden as a rule in the cart-and-horse company and the smithy to either side of the shop and did not readily show themselves. It was hearing that scream just now that had at last brought them out.

The color drained from Zhang Xiaojing’s face. So that scream had not been a real scream at all, but one the old man had deliberately let out in imitation of the firemaster’s voice, precisely so that these guards would hear it. This old wretch—the depth of his calculation was frankly terrifying. In one brief exchange, how many snares he had laid.

Ringed now by these guards, Zhang Xiaojing had no way at all to give chase. Several of the guards shoved Zhang Xiaojing aside and rushed into the room; soon they backed out again, murder in their eyes.

They had all heard that heavy blow against the wall a moment before—plainly the visitor had fallen out with the firemaster. Then had come the firemaster’s scream, and hard upon it this man had run out covered in blood. Now the firemaster’s corpse had been found in the room, and beside the overturned table they had also picked up a hand-crossbow belonging to this man.

The facts could not have been plainer.

“The Shouzhuolang, the Shouzhuolang: every kindness repaid, every debt settled.” A man who looked to be a squad leader chanted the watchword and swung up an iron smith’s hammer. There were ten-odd men here, and they had already sealed off the narrow lane; even had Zhang Xiaojing three heads and six arms, he was no match for them.

Tanqi spoke up angrily: “It was not we who killed the firemaster.” The guards sneered, giving no credit at all to this feeble plea. Zhang Xiaojing raised his bronze waist-medallion and shouted: “I am Zhang Xiaojing, Commander of the Jing’an Bureau. Liu Shiqi brought me here to question the firemaster. I never laid a hand on him; the killer is someone else.”

The squad leader knitted his brows. If the man was here on court business, he was truly awkward to deal with. He signed to his men to hold off for the moment: “You say Liu Shiqi? Where is he, then?”

“He should be here at any moment.”

The squad leader said: “Good; we will wait for him to come, and then settle whether you live or die.” He tossed the iron hammer in his hand up and down, up and down, the veins standing out on his muscles, the killing intent in his eyes undimmed.

Far off, a black figure cleared the bounds of Pingkang Ward in a few bounds.

***

Hearing Ji Wen’s announcement, Yao Runeng stood rooted to the spot, turned to a statue of stone.

Abduct Wang Yunxiu? Collude with a foreign enemy to attack the Jing’an Bureau?

To pin these two charges on Zhang Xiaojing struck Yao Runeng as utterly absurd. Yet in the eyes of the Jing’an Bureau’s new chief, it was the most natural inference in the world.

In the eyes of the world a criminal was the least trustworthy of fiends. Just as Ji Wen had said a moment ago—a condemned man who had killed his own superior, why should he not offend a second time? Never mind Ji Wen; when Li Bi first raised Zhang Xiaojing up, Yao Runeng himself had harbored the same prejudice, certain the man must have some ulterior aim.

This time was not like the last. Last time it had been Cui Qi acting on his own head, forcibly detaining Zhang Xiaojing with no charge whatever, so that in the Right Xiao Guard’s papers they had not even dared set down his name. But this time the public accusation against Zhang Xiaojing was of a wholly different nature; there would be no place left for him in the capital.

No—I must go and set it plainly before Deputy Director Ji!

Yao Runeng shoved aside the colleague beside him and rushed to the front of the Cibei Temple. Ji Wen was talking with several surviving recorders, parceling out the work. Heedless of courtesy, Yao Runeng broke in by force: “Vice-Duan Ji, you have made a mistake!”

“Hm?”

“Ji . . . Deputy Director Ji . . .” With the greatest reluctance Yao Runeng changed his form of address.

“Speak.” Only then did Ji Wen let him go on.

“I am Yao Runeng, a thief-taker of the Jing’an Bureau, and I have followed Commander Zhang throughout the investigation. His searching out the Wang family’s young lady, his stopping of the Turkic Wolf Guards—these were feats done in full view of everyone. How could he possibly be in league with them? There must be some misunderstanding in this!”

Ji Wen stroked his beard and said with a mild smile: “Young master Yao, I had that same doubt myself just now. But Deputy Director Li once said that the Turkic Wolf Guards were only a chess-piece, with another hand behind them. Zhang Xiaojing’s cutting down of the Turkic Wolf Guards was, I fear, no more than a blind they were using.”

By bringing Li Bi forward, he left Yao Runeng, for the moment, with no way to answer. Ji Wen suddenly clapped his hands, as if it had just come to him: “I have only just heard that a girl named Wen Ran was found in Changming Ward—and that it was you who found her, was it not?”

“Yes.”

“Yet I hear that Zhang Xiaojing deliberately deceived the Jing’an Bureau, falsely claiming he had found a lead to Wang Yunxiu so that Deputy Director Li would divert great resources to the rescue. And the one rescued turned out to be his own mistress.”

The words were venomous, harboring the most sinister of insinuations, yet the greater part of what they said was fact. Li Bi had indeed been quite displeased over it, as Yao Runeng knew. But . . . but this had no connection at all with Zhang Xiaojing’s being a traitor within.

At this the official beside them who had read out the proclamation put in his word too: “When Zhang Xiaojing was in Wannian County, his byname was the Five-Faced Yama—vicious, venomous, ruthless, stubborn, pitiless. A bravo of that stamp is not a man just anyone can hold in hand.”

The remark had nothing to do with the matter at issue, but in the ears of most present it became the finest footnote to Zhang Xiaojing’s character—and dragged Li Bi in along with it.

Yao Runeng clenched his fists, meaning to speak out against it, when suddenly something occurred to him.

Ji Wen had received his appointment from the Secretariat Director; he was the Right Minister’s man. He would surely be most zealous to prove that Li Bi was wrong, that the heir apparent was wrong. So however one argued, Zhang Xiaojing had to be branded a spy. Yao Runeng looked at Ji Wen again, and at last, out of that mild and gentlemanly face, made out a few strokes of the sinister.

His heart was full of rage and despair. Chang’an had already been set over the oil-cauldron, and here these men, inside the cauldron, were scheming to do away with the one person who was actually fighting the fire! What in hell kind of business was this!

In the old days, Yao Runeng’s blood would have rushed to his head, and he would long since have spoken out in protest heedless of everything, or else simply hung up his cap and walked away. But in these few double-hours he had already seen too much filth beneath the fine trappings, and knew that in Chang’an, mere reason and the courage of hot blood would get one nowhere.

He had to keep his useful self intact, if he was to be any help to Commander Zhang.

Seeing that Yao Runeng had nothing more to say, Ji Wen turned back to the other recorders and went on: “Deputy Director Li’s whereabouts are now unknown, and the only lead rests upon Zhang Xiaojing. I have already sent out forty-odd foreign servants to deliver the warrant of arrest to all the wards of the city. You must repair the great watchtower as soon as you can and restore the watch over the whole city—that is the first task.”

The recorders all looked troubled, and one of them said: “The watchtower system was built up by Deputy Director Li’s own hand, and it is extremely intricate. We are all men of clerkship and judicature; as to this . . . we could only ever enjoy its fruits ready-made.”

Ji Wen was somewhat displeased: “Do you mean to tell me that of the men who understand the watchtowers, not a single one is left alive?” The recorders hemmed and dared not speak. Beside them Yao Runeng suddenly raised a hand: “I understand a little of it.”

“Oh?”

“The post I held before was precisely the work of translating the watchtowers’ flag-code and lantern-code.” Yao Runeng was telling no lie, and the recorders all bore him out. Ji Wen nodded: “In that case, this matter falls to you. Within one double-hour, the watchtowers must be working again.”

Yao Runeng rejoiced in secret: so long as he had the great watchtower in hand, there was a chance to help Commander Zhang. For that, he had to hold his nose and dance attendance on this hypocritical new chief—the very sort of conduct he had most despised before.

Now at last he understood what Zhang Xiaojing had meant by “the wrong thing that ought to be done.”

Just then a hand patted Yao Runeng on the shoulder. He turned to look, and found it was the official who had read out the proclamation.

“My name is Yuan Zai, courtesy name Gongfu, Evaluator of the Court of Judicial Review. I now have the honor to serve as Censor Ji’s deputy.” Yuan Zai spoke with a narrow-eyed smile, giving the register in his hand a little shake. “You said your name is Yao Runeng, yes? There is a thing I would just ask you about.”

“Ask on, Evaluator Yuan.”

“I looked over the records just now: there is a woman named Wen Ran, taken out of the prison by you, and lodged somewhere nearby—is that right?”

“Ah? Yes . . .” No sooner were the words out than Yao Runeng regretted them. Yuan Zai’s gaze wandered and would not settle, so that it was hard to be on one’s guard against him in any pointed way, and in a moment’s carelessness one’s defenses were breached.

Yuan Zai’s eyes lit up: “This woman is no distant connection of Zhang Xiaojing’s; to catch Zhang Xiaojing one must rely on her—where is she lodged?”

“I will go and bring her at once.” Yao Runeng dodged Yuan Zai’s question and made to leave. But Yuan Zai’s eyes rolled, and he blocked him: “You are to go and repair the great watchtower; there is no need to be held up by so small a thing. Just tell me the address.”

He pressed hard, allowing Yao Runeng no room to think. Yao Runeng could hit on no good way to put him off . . . yet on no account could he hand her over to this fellow, for that would be the end of Commander Zhang.

The smile was still on Yuan Zai’s face, but his tone now carried a note of impatience: “Out with it. Surely you are not deliberately trying to shield her?”

Yao Runeng knew that if he let Yuan Zai grow suspicious, Ji Wen would never let him go and repair the great watchtower, and then he could be of no help to Zhang Xiaojing.

Now he had to make a choice between Zhang Xiaojing and Wen Ran.

Yao Runeng clenched his teeth, wishing he had no choice to make.

A cart lay toppled across the street, already half wrecked.

It had run headlong into a huge lantern-frame and then keeled over on its side. There by the lantern-wheel a crowd of singing-girls and maidens had been strolling and singing to greet the spring, when the cart suddenly ran out of control and came smashing into them, cutting a broad swath through the poor girls; dainty cries and moans rose on every side, and flower-crowns and rosy shawls lay scattered over the ground. The scene was all in chaos.

The lantern-viewing folk round about gathered close in sympathy, supposing the driver had drunk too much during the festival and so brought about such an accident.

A soldier crawled wretchedly out of the cart, and then dragged out the assassin Liu Shiqi after him. But the latter had already breathed his last, a fresh red line across his throat.

A short while before, as the ox-cart passed the Xuanyang-Changxing crossroads, a black figure had suddenly leapt across the roof of the cart at tremendous speed, first killing the driver and overturning the cart, then plunging into the carriage-box amid the confusion. This fellow’s blade-work was uncannily precise: the instant he sprang into the box, his short knife drew cleanly across Liu Shiqi’s throat. The guard had not even the chance to draw his blade before the black figure had withdrawn, sprung nimbly down from the cart, and made off over the ward wall by way of the lantern-frame.

“No—what I saw was two black figures, one ahead and one behind.” This was the soldier’s last thought before he passed out.

***

Yuan Zai made his way toward the apothecary of raw and prepared medicines beside the Cibei Temple. He was very happy just now; even his step had turned light.

There was no reason not to be happy: everything was developing in the direction most to his satisfaction. No—more to his satisfaction even than his most satisfied hopes.

In the beginning he had merely been asked to draw up a requisition order; then, on discovering that Feng Dalun had abducted Wang Yunxiu by mistake, Yuan Zai had of his own accord proposed a second plan, killing two birds with one stone. Then he had gone straight to the Censorate, where by chance the officer on duty was Ji Wen, an acquaintance of his. Yuan Zai had only just finished the pleasantries, and had not yet begun to speak, when Ji Wen abruptly received a secret letter from the Right Minister, bidding him go at once and seize the post of Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau.

Ji Wen was rather unsure of the matter and consulted Yuan Zai. At the hearing of it, that restless great head of Yuan Zai’s began to turn again, and he soon spied a superb opportunity in it, and revised his plan a third time.

Next, under the name of “assisting,” he accompanied Ji Wen to the front of the Cibei Temple and proclaimed Zhang Xiaojing the mastermind behind both the attack on the Jing’an Bureau and the abduction of Wang Yunxiu.

What a simple decision it was, and what an exquisite arrangement. Prince Yong would be grateful to him, for Zhang Xiaojing would be hunted to death throughout the city; Feng Dalun would be grateful to him, for someone had taken the blame for abducting Wang Yunxiu; Wang Zhongsi and Wang Yunxiu would be grateful to him, for it was Yuan Zai who had single-handedly “rescued” her; and Ji Wen, with Li Linfu behind him, would look on him with new regard, for he had helped Ji Wen take over the Jing’an Bureau in short order and dealt a heavy slap to the heir apparent’s face.

What had at first been only a small trade in official documents had now been worked up, by Yuan Zai, into a grand and consummate game of chess played to every side, doing so many people a good turn. Were the inner secrets of it not unfit for outside ears, Yuan Zai would fairly have liked to write an essay commemorating this uncommon stroke of his.

Yuan Zai had just found Wen Ran’s whereabouts in the report, and it came to him with a jolt that Feng Dalun had let slip that Prince Yong seemed to have taken an interest in Wen Ran. To hand her to Prince Yong would be yet another great favor done!

So, after weighing it over and over, Yuan Zai resolved to come and seize Wen Ran in person, to mark this historic moment. Yet he did not take the enemy lightly; before nearing the shop, he directed the buliang men at his side to seal off the surroundings first. In all his dealings Yuan Zai held to leaving not a drop to leak through; the smallest slip had to be guarded against.

Even on Yao Runeng’s side, Yuan Zai had quietly planted a lookout. The moment Yao Runeng was seen whispering to anyone or passing a note, word was to be brought over at once. Truly foolproof!

With everything arranged, Yuan Zai walked slowly to the door of the apothecary. He gazed with pity at the poor turtles and terrapins in the vats, raised his right hand, and made ready to bring it down hard in a single stroke—completing, with this most symbolic of gestures, the last step of his masterpiece.

But his arm, halfway through the stroke in midair, halted all at once.

With a great crash, a horse came bursting out of the shop, kicking the door to pieces. Its charge was so violent that several of the buliang men nearby were sent flying at a stroke. The rest dared not go near, and could only ring it round and shout. The horse wheeled about several times before the shop but did not at once bolt away. Only then did the buliang men see clearly that a man and a woman lay low on its back.

Unshaken by the turn of events, Yuan Zai stood his ground and shouted aloud: “Hold your positions!”

He had seen it: the horse had made its terrific show only in that first burst; the rider himself did not know where to go. So long as the cordon held, the two of them had no chance to escape. The buliang men came to themselves as well, and each drew out his iron rod, closing on the horse from three sides. That way, however fierce the mount, there would always be one party of attackers trained on its most vulnerable flank.

The rider too became aware of this peril. He looked all about him, gave the reins a shake, and drove the horse charging toward the one direction where there was no enemy.

Yuan Zai sneered, watching the cornered beast’s last struggle.

The direction the rider ran was the one gap in the cordon-ring, and the spot where it lay happened to be the main gate of the Jing’an Bureau. Just then the great hall was still blazing furiously, with not the least sign of dying down.

It was for precisely this reason that Yuan Zai had not sealed off this side. Anyone fleeing this way would be blocked by the fire in any case—a dead end.

But the smile suddenly froze on Yuan Zai’s face.

The main gate of the Jing’an Bureau was very narrow and would not admit a horse. But to keep the blaze from spreading, the fire-fighters had already torn down the wall nearby and cleared a firebreak. The rider drove his mount, and with the greatest ease cleared the broken wall and its rubble; horse and two riders soon vanished into the roaring flames.

What were they doing? At the end of their rope and bent on suicide?

No!

Yuan Zai’s mind whirled at speed, and then he cried to the buliang men: “Quick—get to the Jingzhao Prefecture, and to the ward wall outside the rear garden!”

Yuan Zai had studied the layout of the Jing’an Bureau; the buildings within stood well apart. If a man’s resolve were hard enough and his speed great enough, he could just barely pass between the burning great hall and the side halls to either side, and reach the rear garden or the side gate of the Jingzhao Prefecture.

Even now Yuan Zai was not much worried. To bore into the Jing’an Bureau was a fine move—and then what?

The walls of both places, the rear garden and the Jingzhao Prefecture, were intact; the rider could only abandon the horse and climb over. A man and a woman going forward on foot—how far could they get under a manhunt?

Under their superior’s stern command the buliang men split into several parties. One party entered the Jingzhao Prefecture to block the side gate; one went round to the outside of the rear garden’s ward wall, and even the water-channel was brought under control; and one more put on fire-proof cloth and, steeling themselves, plunged into the burning ground.

Soon two of the parties reported back: no sign of anything. After a further while, the third party, which had gone into the fire, came running back in disarray; all they had seen was the horse abandoned in the courtyard, with the people nowhere to be found.

Yuan Zai flew into a rage—where could they have run to? Could they have flown up into the sky?! He pressed down his palm and had the buliang men search again, carefully! Wen Ran must be found; no flaw could be left upon this wonderful night. The buliang man said, in his difficulty, that to force their way in again might mean casualties. Yuan Zai looked at him: “If you do not go in, there will be casualties right now.”

Ashen-faced, the buliang man had no choice but to go and muster men again for another rush into the fire. Just then, unexpectedly, Yuan Zai said one word: “Wait.”

He lifted his head, saw that behind the great hall there stood yet another building towering high, and a possibility suddenly came to him.

The great watchtower!

The great watchtower stood there in the rear garden. If they had abandoned the horse to flee, they could only have climbed the ladder to the top and hidden up there, to come down and get away once the danger had passed. That was it—was that wretch Yao Runeng not repairing the great watchtower at this very moment?

At this thought Yuan Zai’s face turned cold. A mere petty clerk of the Jing’an Bureau dared to play tricks before him? He gave the order to muster the buliang men and, leading the party himself, set off to catch the terrapin in the jar.

You may be able to get up—but coming down will be another matter!

To repair the great watchtower, the rescue crews had opened up a relatively safe way in. The repairers did not have to force a path through the three burning great halls; instead they went through a hole knocked in the wall on the Jingzhao Prefecture side into the adjoining prison of the Jing’an Bureau, and then over from the little garden before the prison into the rear garden.

Leading his men, Yuan Zai came into the rear garden by this route. Foremost of all, he clambered up the wooden ladder hand and foot, and in one breath scrambled to the top.

The top of the great watchtower was very broad—a wide, square platform about twelve zhang on a side, spread with a thick layer of felt carpet, railed on all four sides, its central pivot-post holding up a winged pavilion-roof to keep off wind and rain.

On the platform just now the corpses of eight constables lay sprawled every which way. Lizard-skin drums, five-colored flags, purple lanterns, and other signaling gear were flung all over the ground, and there too lay scattered such daily necessaries as a rice-cauldron, water-skins, a hand-warmer, and cloaks. Yao Runeng and two other menials were crouched there examining them one by one. Apart from these, there was no one.

Seeing Yuan Zai come clambering up so suddenly and fiercely, Yao Runeng was much taken aback. Yuan Zai swept his gaze around, found there was truly nowhere here to hide a person, and barked at Yao Runeng: “Where have you hidden Wen Ran? And who was that man?”

Yao Runeng answered with an air of innocence: “The moment I received the order I hurried straight here to repair the great watchtower—was that not your own requirement? Where would I have had the time to hide anyone?”

Yuan Zai leaned forward, his great brow all but pressed against Yao Runeng’s face: “If you had not tipped them off, how would they suddenly have fled the apothecary?” He turned his head to the other menial: “You—speak! Did you see anything?”

This menial was the very lookout he had planted. Seeing his superior’s anger, the man answered tremblingly: “I report to the Evaluator: I kept close by Yao Runeng’s side throughout, and he . . . he truly passed no message to anyone.”

“Impossible! That is only because you did not see it. Tell me every word he spoke to anyone, everything he did, exactly as it happened!” Yuan Zai rubbed his fingers together in vexation, hardly able to believe that under his very eyes Wen Ran had been let slip away.

The menial had an excellent memory. Yao Runeng had first spoken with several recorders, the matter being nothing more than the preparing of materials and manpower for the repairs; on the spot he had requisitioned a batch of large lanterns from before the Cibei Temple. Then he had asked the fire-fighting soldiers to open a safe passage, and carried this batch of materials up the great watchtower to assess the damage.

The menial remembered every detail of Yao Runeng’s dealings with others, all perfectly clear, without a single point in doubt. Yuan Zai would not give up, and demanded where that batch of lanterns had gone. The menial pointed: they were hanging just then along the outer edge of the great watchtower’s pavilion-roof. This was to warn the surrounding watchtowers that there was a fault here, under repair.

Yuan Zai lay out over the edge of the railing, craning to feel over the lanterns one by one, and several times nearly pitched himself out. But to his disappointment, apart from the wan-character motif, he saw no writing at all upon them. Yuan Zai drew himself back in and looked down upon the Jing’an Bureau below, all pitch black.

This time he truly could not think where Wen Ran and that mysterious man could possibly still be hidden.

“Repair it as fast as you can, or be heavily punished!”

With a flick of his sleeve Yuan Zai clambered down from the great watchtower in a sulk. He had far too much to do, and could not waste time here.

Watching him climb down and go off into the distance, Yao Runeng wiped away his sweat and cried inwardly again and again what a lucky escape it had been. He bade the two menials go on turning over the corpses, then turned his back and gently rotated one of the lanterns.

The paper shade of this lantern was divided into two halves, one of thin paper, one of thick. If the lantern were made to turn, then from one fixed angle the candlelight would seem to flare and dim by turns. There was a regularity to Yao Runeng’s handling of it, and soon, in a grim stand of trees near the great watchtower, a very small cluster of light glimmered. The cluster of light flickered a few times, as if answering the great watchtower, and then went out.

Yao Runeng was now entirely at ease.

After Yuan Zai had pressed the apothecary’s address out of him, he had at once put it to Ji Wen: with the whole city out viewing the lanterns, it was hard to bring repair materials from anywhere else, so better to take them close at hand—for instance, those large lanterns hung before the Cibei Temple.

The reason was entirely sound, and was approved on the spot. Then, on the pretext of an inspection, Yao Runeng had climbed up before one of the lanterns.

He knew that, in the apothecary far off, Cen Shen was watching this lantern, playing at the game of rhyme-word substitution. Yao Runeng rotated the lantern to send the signal out, and prayed silently that Cen Shen would notice the change and read it out in time.

Time was pressing, and Yao Runeng could only tell Cen Shen to get Wen Ran away as fast as possible, to plunge into the burning ground and come to the wall near the right side hall of the Jing’an Bureau.

Earlier Li Bi had set up a temporary council-chamber in the thatched hut of the neighboring Cibei Temple, and had put two wooden ladders against the wall for going back and forth. That the hut existed was known to only five people: Li Bi, Zhang Xiaojing, Yao Runeng, Tanqi, and Xu Bin.

Cen Shen was a poet indeed, and sure enough he caught the message exactly. He at once seized a horse, carried Wen Ran into the fire, then swiftly climbed the wall, drew the ladder away, and hid in the thatched hut. However boundless Yuan Zai’s powers, he could never have guessed that the Jing’an Bureau had a foothold in the neighboring Cibei Temple as well.

Now that Wen Ran was safe for the time being, Yao Runeng could at last turn his attention back to the great watchtower itself.

The great watchtower was manned in all by eight constables, seeing to the sending and receiving on all four sides. But now these eight were all dead up here, and every one killed by a single stab to the heart. The Pifu had plainly struck the great watchtower first, to blind the eyes of the Jing’an Bureau, and only then carried out their next move.

There were no marks of a struggle on the scene. Yao Runeng did not believe there was anyone in this world who could, in so cramped a space, do away with these eight men without a sound. He searched carefully for a good while, and found the rice-cauldron overturned on the ground, the mutton broth in it all spilled across the floor. He rubbed at it with a finger and held it to his nose to sniff, but could make nothing of it. Then he opened the water-skins: the clear water in them had long since leaked away.

Yao Runeng wondered whether poison might have been put beforehand into the mutton broth or the water, so that these ten-odd men, having taken the poison, were then attacked and so had no power at all to resist. What had truly happened, he feared, could only be learned when the coroner came to open the bodies and examine them.

If this surmise held, then the poisoner must be a traitor the Pifu had planted within the Jing’an Bureau—and that traitor was very likely still alive. At the thought, Yao Runeng’s heart sank despite himself.

It could well be imagined: the Pifu were the organization behind the Turkic Wolf Guards, and in attacking the Jing’an Bureau they must surely have some deeper design.

Yao Runeng told the menials to call more men to carry the corpses down. The menial answered with his mouth while his hand hauled up a corpse by the ankle and flung it off the platform; a moment later a “smack” came up from the ground below. Yao Runeng flew into a fury and gave the menial a slap across the face: “Show some respect! These are martyrs who gave their lives for the country!”

The menial merely took it for revenge over the surveillance, and covered his face, bowing and scraping. Yao Runeng paid him no more mind and went on assessing the losses to the great watchtower.

The signaling gear—flags, drums, horns, lanterns and the rest—was still there, none of it much damaged; but to find another eight constables who understood the flag-code would be very hard. Training such men cost a very great deal, so the great watchtower had only two rotations of shift, and the other eight men were now scattered across the whole city, impossible to gather at such short notice.

What was more, with the whole city ablaze with lanterns just now, it could be called the worst day in the year for watchtower communication. Even if service were restored, no very complex message could be sent.

More troublesome still, the ring of watchtowers around the great watchtower had all put out their lamps; very likely their guards too had met with disaster. In other words, the great watchtower could only skip over this ring of watchtowers and pass its signals to the more distant ones, and that would carry a large margin of error.

To repair the great watchtower within one double-hour was all but impossible.

Yao Runeng drove a fist into the railing and all at once felt heartsick and cold. The Jing’an Bureau was utterly destroyed, Deputy Director Li’s whereabouts unknown, and Zhang Xiaojing, the one able lieutenant, was now branded a traitor. All that he himself had done was in vain; however hard he tried, he could not stop the plot of the Que-le Huo-duo.

Yao Runeng slowly let his body lean half against the pavilion-post and gazed listlessly out at the pitch-black night sky, his heart brimming with the despair of defeat. Chang’an had shown its monstrous nature at last, devouring bit by bit those who refused to be assimilated.

Deputy Director Li and Commander Zhang were both powerless to stop it—how much less a raw recruit like me? The one thing I can do is to stand here and witness this city’s destruction.

But after a few snaps of the fingers had passed, his eyes suddenly went wide, as if he had seen some strange movement. Gathering all his concentration, he watched the distant cluster of watchtowers carefully for a while. He noticed that among those watchtowers a regular exchange was going on, purple lamps flickering in and out of sight, seeming to carry all the way off to some very far place.

Eh? The watchtowers should have the great watchtower for their hub—how had they begun passing messages among themselves? Yao Runeng looked more closely: they were not passing them to one another, but in one particular direction. Though where that direction led he could not tell, Yao Runeng judged at once that a new hub must have formed there.

“It is Commander Zhang!”

Yao Runeng grew abruptly excited. He remembered: the only man with the standing to command the whole watchtower system, apart from the great watchtower, was Zhang Xiaojing, who held the watchtowers’ commission.

For the working of the watchtower system, be it understood, was wholly independent of every other office. Even though Zhang Xiaojing was wanted throughout the city, so long as the great watchtower had not revoked his commission, the other watchtowers would still obey his orders.

Commander Zhang—he had not given up! He was still hard at work.

Chang’an had not yet lost its last shred of hope.

A passion surged in Yao Runeng’s breast that he could scarcely master. Gripping the railing, he suddenly realized that his own position was, to Commander Zhang—no, to the whole of Chang’an—of the greatest importance.

So long as he himself held the great watchtower, Zhang Xiaojing could go on using the watchtower system to pursue the trail, and then there was yet a thread of hope of stopping the Que-le Huo-duo. The fate of Chang’an would rest on how long he could hold out up on the great watchtower.

The tide of things is already so hard; if I give up now, then there will be no hope left to speak of!

Yao Runeng’s gaze turned all at once resolute. He lifted a purple lantern and sent off a clear message in that direction, repeating it three times. Then he set the lantern down and clenched his fists.

From here on, he would hold this place to the death—just as Commander Zhang had once held the beacon-fort at Balhuan in the Western Regions to the death—though it meant setting himself against the whole Jing’an Bureau, he would not count the cost.

Zhang Xiaojing and Tanqi stood in the lane before the bookshop, gazing anxiously outward. At the mouth of the lane ten-odd Shouzhuolang had sealed off the way out, every one of them glaring like a tiger eyeing its prey.

It had stayed very quiet outside the lane; travelers kept passing along the avenue, and from far off came the faint sound of strings and pipes. But the convoy Zhang Xiaojing had promised would soon arrive still gave no sign of coming.

“How much longer will you have us wait? Where is the convoy? Where is Liu Shiqi?” The Shouzhuolang’s squad leader took a step forward, the iron hammer in his hand raised high, his look ill-boding. The Shouzhuolang under him had already lost patience, and, hefting their weapons, stood closer and closer.

“On a lantern-viewing day, a delay on the road is nothing strange—” Zhang Xiaojing held out his bronze medallion and said sternly, “Do not act rashly. This would be an assault upon the court.”

The squad leader sneered: “Even the court’s own grandees, once they have killed a man, cannot simply walk away.” Taking the impostor for one bluffing, he swung his arm and gave the order to seize him.

The men rushed forward all at once, each vying to be first.

With the firemaster killed, these guards would surely be heavily punished; only by catching the killer could they lighten their own guilt. Seeing that the situation was almost past holding down, Zhang Xiaojing whipped out the blade at his side and pointed its tip ahead: “He who comes near dies!”

“Every kindness repaid, every debt settled!”

The Shouzhuolang chanted the rallying-cry in low voices and closed in slowly. Zhang Xiaojing tried to call out to them again, but the men opposite kept up a low unison growl and would not answer at all. Weapons of every sort came stabbing at Zhang Xiaojing and Tanqi.

Zhang Xiaojing could not dodge, for Tanqi was right behind him. He could only meet them head-on. From the first clash he found these weapons deeply unfamiliar, and was actually driven onto the back foot.

The Shouzhuolang’s weapons were chiefly artisans’ tools—iron hammers, sickles, horse-whips, chisels, hay-forks, and the like, of every shape. In a garrison-town there was no special armory to forge weapons; the inhabitants each had a tool in hand, used for work in ordinary times and as a weapon in war, and so over long years they had formed a whole style of combat all their own.

Luckily the lane was narrow, and the Shouzhuolang could not throw themselves all into the fight at once. Zhang Xiaojing set his teeth and, making the most of that last little advantage of ground, held on for dear life.

The two or three men in front were beaten down, but the enemies behind came on without end. Zhang Xiaojing felt that things could not go on like this, and drew three smoke pellets from his waist and flung them out.

The smoke billowed up, and the whole lane fell at once into a blur. The lanterns turned to hazy clusters of light in the fog, and the shifting figures could not be told one from another. Zhang Xiaojing seized Tanqi’s hand and ran for the outside with all his might. Tanqi knew their lives hung in the balance now, and without a word let Zhang Xiaojing drag her along.

As the two of them were nearly out of the lane’s mouth, the Shouzhuolang had recovered their sight as well and came in hot pursuit. Zhang Xiaojing gave Tanqi a hard shove and pointed ahead: “The post constables at the ward corner—go, call the authorities!”

“And you?”

“I will hold them off!” Zhang Xiaojing whirled about and set the blade level across his chest.

The Shouzhuolang were, after all, an underground body; however the authorities might wink at them, they would not tolerate their making trouble in Chang’an. Once the post constables were roused, the Shouzhuolang would know the game was up and draw back.

“Remember! Give my name!” Zhang Xiaojing shouted.

Tanqi turned and ran, the clang and clash of weapons ringing out behind her. Without a backward glance she ran full tilt for more than two hundred paces, until her lungs felt fit to burst, and ahead she could already see the brightly blazing night-alarm lamp at the door of the constables’ post at the ward corner.

Compared with the guards of the other wards, the post constables of Pingkang Ward had it fairly easy. Most of the residents had gone off outside, and within the ward there was in fact little to do. Several constables sat clustered around an iron pot, all delight in their faces. In the pot stewed some camel hooves, the thick brown broth bubbling and rolling, filling the whole room with steam.

The heat being about right, a plump constable carefully drew out a fine little silk pouch. He took a pinch of ground pepper from it and, rubbing his fingers with care, sprinkled it in bit by bit, afraid of putting in too much.

Just then the door was shoved open with a bang; the constable’s hand jerked, and a whole fistful of pepper went into the pot. A rich aroma wafted up from it, and the constable’s face went white with the pain of the waste.

“Who dares to break into the constables’ post?” he roared in a fury; then, looking again, he saw that the intruder was a richly dressed young woman. The moment she was through the door the woman cried out urgently: “We are of the Jing’an Bureau! We have been set upon by bandits, and my companion urgently needs aid.”

The constables looked at one another, but not one of them stirred his backside. The camel hooves would be ready to eat at any moment—who would want to go?

Seeing they did not move, Tanqi was much angered, and urged them loudly: “Go, quickly! Lives are at stake!” The plump constable spoke up lazily: “Where are the ruffians, what are their names, where did they do their violence? You must write out a formal complaint, and then it will be easy for us to act.” A few of the others tittered and picked up their chopsticks to take the meat from the pot.

“You had best think this over. The man ringed out there is called Zhang Xiaojing!” There was an edge of sharpness in Tanqi’s voice.

At the speaking of that name, the movements of the several constables in the room all froze. The plump constable asked tremblingly: “Which Zhang Xiaojing?” Tanqi said with a cold laugh: “The Five-Faced Yama—who else?”

The name seemed to carry a magic power. The constables hastily set down their bowls and chopsticks—those with forks taking forks, those with blades taking blades—and followed Tanqi out of the post one after another.

Leading this pack of slack constables, Tanqi rushed toward the lane of the bookshop, and coming the other way saw Zhang Xiaojing running toward them. He seemed to have gained a good many more streaks of blood, and the Shouzhuolang behind him were fewer by several, but they still pursued relentlessly.

The two groups charged all the way to the middle of the little crossroads before they just barely halted, forming a standoff. On this side was a band of somewhat frightened post constables; on that, the fierce and menacing Shouzhuolang; and in the middle the panting Zhang Xiaojing, badly wounded and unsteady on his feet, held up in an instant by Tanqi.

Time seemed to stand still for a moment; the two sides eyed each other, and no one dared act rashly. The plump constable ventured to speak: “Chief Zhang . . . come over here, quickly.”

Tanqi glanced at the Shouzhuolang and, supporting Zhang Xiaojing, walked toward this side. There was a stir among the Shouzhuolang, but the men opposite were, after all, the authorities’ soldiers, and they dared not be too reckless. The constables raised their forks and blades high, tension on their faces. They knew how fierce the Shouzhuolang were; if they really rose up and attacked, these few men could not hold them at all.

The silence of the standoff was suddenly broken by a string of footsteps coming from afar. Soon a young runner came panting up. Seeing this standoff, he was startled. The plump constable told the others to keep watch, then stepped back half a pace and asked what he had come for.

The young runner complained: “How is it none of you are at the post? You made me hunt all over! The Jing’an Bureau has sent out a three-feather order!”

One feather was a routine order, two feathers an express order; and three feathers meant an emergency order, to be carried out at once. But that this order should have come from the Jing’an Bureau meant nothing to the constables—though the shoulders of Zhang Xiaojing, held in Tanqi’s arms, gave a jolt.

The young runner unrolled the document in his hand and said to the plump constable: “Listen now, quickly—I will read it out; once I have, I must be off elsewhere.” The great majority of constables could not read, so such documents were not sent down to every constable-post; instead a runner notified each post in turn and read the thing out on the spot.

The young runner cleared his throat and read aloud: “Be it known that the grave criminal Zhang Xiaojing—long of face, with a short beard, blind in the left eye, roughly six chi and two fen in height by the greater measure—is to be killed on sight by any who come upon him . . .”

Before the young runner had finished reading, Zhang Xiaojing suddenly shoved Tanqi aside and darted through between the Shouzhuolang and the constables. Neither side, nor Tanqi, had time to react before he had already run far off.

“After him!” The squad leader at the head came to himself at last, and the whole pack thundered off in pursuit. The constables stood where they were, looking at one another, and all turned their eyes to the plump constable. The plump constable had a mind to withdraw his men back to the post, but he found the young runner still standing beside him, taking it all in, and so he set his teeth: “After them!”

One constable said timidly: “But that is Chief Zhang . . .” Whether the words sprang from old feeling or from dread of the Yama Zhang’s ferocity, there was no telling. The plump constable glared: “After him all the same!”

Whether you catch him or not is a question of ability; whether you give chase or not is a question of attitude.

So the constables too hurried off in that direction, though they did not run very hard at it. Whether by intent or not, no one paid Tanqi any mind, nor left a single man behind to question her; they simply abandoned her there alone.

Tanqi stood dazed at the crossroads, empty in an instant, at a loss what to do. She knew that Zhang Xiaojing, fearing to drag her down with him, had run off first alone—for after all the warrant named but one name.

But what was the meaning of this warrant? How had Zhang Xiaojing become a dangerous criminal wanted throughout the city? What had it to do with the attack on the Jing’an Bureau? Had the young master been there, he would never have allowed such a thing to happen . . . At this thought Tanqi’s heart went suddenly cold—did it not mean, then, that the young master was now no more?

Tanqi looked toward Guangde Ward, off in the black curtain of night, and then toward the street where Zhang Xiaojing’s figure had vanished. She trusted only these two men, and both had left her; neither could be leaned upon any longer. Despair and a vast flood of questions surged into Tanqi’s mind, leaving her dizzy and sick, scarcely able to keep her feet. Slowly Tanqi crouched down, feeling a loneliness and a fear she had never known before.

The young master gone, the Jing’an Bureau burned, and now Zhang Xiaojing fallen to a criminal wanted throughout the city—there was no longer anyone who cared what became of Chang’an.

This feeling was like being carried back to her childhood, when she was cast off by her father and left adrift in the streets. That fear, long since sunk out of memory, rose again to the surface and set Tanqi shuddering without end.

She stood blankly for a while, wanting to weep aloud; but in the very instant the tears burst from her eyes, a saying of Zhang Xiaojing’s rushed into her mind: “Your young master consented to let you follow me because he believes you can do things of more worth than waiting on people.”

Tanqi lifted the back of her hand and wiped the tears from the corners of her eyes, stood up again, and drew in a fierce breath. Yes—my abilities are not only for waiting on the young master; I can do things of more worth! I will not be looked down on by that lecher, and still less will I fail the young master.

The tide of things is already so hard; if I give up now, then there will be no hope left to speak of!

A resolute light came into Tanqi’s eyes. Just then she saw a watchtower far off sending a purple-lamp signal her way, like a guiding star risen in the night sky.

The signal was very simple, only two words. Unversed as Tanqi was in message-work, even she could read its meaning:

Do not fall back.

After a very long time in darkness, the world before Li Bi’s eyes suddenly grew bright.

Not the dawn—rather, the hood had been taken off his head. What showed before Li Bi’s eyes was a splendid courtyard ablaze with lamplight. The courtyard was vast, artificial hills and climbing vines set here and there in pleasing disorder, interspersed with such rare foreign trees as sal and golden peach. There were aloeswood posts painted vermilion and balustrades of sandalwood; even the well-railing was sheathed in glittering gilt inlay, and the surrounding gallery was hung all round with a trellis of purple wisteria—the very height of extravagance.

In the middle of the courtyard stood a pavilion with upswept eaves; the pavilion itself was nothing remarkable. But Li Bi saw at a glance that each of the pavilion’s four columns was a full five arm-spans around; the cost of freighting in the raw timber alone would have been enough to ruin ten-odd modest households.

“A fine eye, Deputy Director Li—this Self-Raining Pavilion is no common thing.” Long Bo stood grinning at his side and lifted an arm, like an attentive host showing off to a guest. “See there: along the pavilion’s edge runs a movable water-catching rim. When it rains, the water is stored and not let go; and in the height of summer, one has only to raise the rim a small crack, and clear water comes pouring down from the eaves on all four sides, like a curtain of water—now that is cool. The rich certainly know how to amuse themselves, tsk, tsk.”

Li Bi observed all this carefully, a flicker in his eyes.

Behind the Turkic Wolf Guards there must be this very organization called the Pifu—and the identity of the master behind them must stand no low place in Chang’an, or he could never possess so broad and lavish a courtyard; his fortune too must be astonishing, or he could never have gathered so well-equipped and formidably skilled an army.

The great merchants of Chang’an who could carry off a stroke on this scale were not many in number—who on earth could it be?

Noticing that Li Bi was observing things, Long Bo tapped his own hooked nose and gave a chuckle: “Deputy Director Li really is born to worry. You are already at the end of your road—why think so much? Better simply to enjoy the fine scenery.”

Li Bi threw out his chest, showing not a trace of fear, as fierce as he had been in the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau: “You did not kill me at the Jing’an Bureau, but took all this trouble to carry me off here instead—surely not just to admire this pavilion?”

“Ah, the Deputy Director’s eye is keen as fire—the boy prodigy who talked chess, after all.” Long Bo scratched his head in some embarrassment, drew another roll of mint leaves from his waist, and held it out to Li Bi: “A mouthful?”

Li Bi did not stir: “Who is the master behind you?”

Long Bo raised a fingernail, picked a shred of mint from between his teeth, and flicked it to the ground: “How does the Deputy Director come to think we must have a paymaster behind us?”

“A scale like this, a stroke like this—how could an ordinary man achieve it?”

Long Bo smiled a smile that was not quite a smile: “The Deputy Director is a great personage set high above, of the finest birth and rank; even in defeat, he can only be defeated by a foe of equal standing—while obscure, low-born little people like us are not worthy to beat you, is that it?”

Li Bi did not answer; he thought the question too stupid to need one.

But Long Bo went on: “That is no fault of the Deputy Director’s, either. A traveler on the road naturally guards against bears and tigers and leopards—who would stoop to trouble himself over tiny insects and ants?” His boot came down all at once with a stamp, and when it moved away, on the patterned-stone floor lay the flattened corpses of a few ants. “Their life and death lie in one tread of a great man—what is there worth minding in that?”

Li Bi kept his face unmoved, trying to guess his motive from these few resentful words.

Long Bo flung out a hand: “And yet not all insects and ants are fated only to be crushed under a boot—among them there is one kind called the pifu. Born pure white, no bigger than a grain of rice, pitiably small. But their mouths are of the hardest, and they gnaw wood for their food, loving above all to bore into rafters and burrow into pillars, to eat away walls and worm through beams. Even a mansion a hundred zhang broad, a dyke a thousand li long, can be eaten hollow by these tiny winged things and come crashing down.”

As if to bear out his words, a few white pifu that had grown wings flew out from a crack in the hall behind him, chasing and dancing in midair. Spring had come, and it was just the mating season of the pifu.

Li Bi said coldly: “You have the nerve to raid the Jing’an Bureau in the very heart of Chang’an, but not the nerve to speak the truth to a single prisoner?”

“This is the truth. We take the pifu for our name, so naturally we are all little people—only not quite so willing to accept it.” As Long Bo spoke those two words, his look carried a faint pride and self-mockery. “The world knows only the wrath of the mighty dragon, that leaves a million corpses in the dust; it does not know that the wrath of the pifu can topple cities and shake trees as well.”

A scene rose in Li Bi’s mind: pifu darkening the sky as they beat their wings, gnawing at every last structure of this city of Chang’an.

Long Bo bade his men untie the ropes that bound Li Bi, then made a respectful gesture: “Please, come with me. I will take you to see just how we tiny pifu are shaking this great city.”

Sentries stood all around; Li Bi knew there was no possibility of escape. He rubbed his shoulders, sore from the binding, gave a cold snort, and strode forward with his head high. Long Bo walked at his side, and together they went off into the depths of the courtyard.

They passed through the pavilion and skirted the artificial hills. Along the way could be seen many sturdy, well-built men patrolling back and forth with pocket crossbows in hand, Han and foreigner alike, on the sternest guard. These, no doubt, were the men who had raided the Jing’an Bureau with Long Bo; there was about them a quality unlike that of common bandits.

Common brigands might be fierce, but they were mostly a loose sheet of scattered sand; whereas these soldiers advanced and fell back in good order, their bearing exact, and with so many of them standing guard in the courtyard there was, astonishingly, not a sound—not bandits, but even the capital’s own palace guards could scarcely have managed as much.

This was not something to be raised up by money alone. Recalling again Long Bo’s parable of the pifu, Li Bi’s heart sank.

Long Bo whistled as he walked, paying no heed at all to Li Bi’s scrutiny.

They came to the edge of a dark-brown grove of sal trees at a corner of the courtyard. These trees had all been brought and transplanted from Tianzhu, each one of no small value, their trunks wrapped in hemp cloth to fend off the north’s bitter cold. At the grove’s edge Long Bo halted: “Deputy Director Li, we are here. Look closely.” Li Bi glanced around: “What is it you want me to see?”

Long Bo said with a grin: “Why, the very thing you have been chasing these several double-hours, of course.”

“The Que-le Huo-duo?”

Li Bi said in a low voice. The Turkic Wolf Guards had smuggled in Yanzhou rock-oil and refined it in Changming Ward into fierce-fire thunder. Of it, fifteen barrels had already gone off, while the other two hundred-odd barrels had been nowhere to be found all this time—and here, of all places, they were hidden in this courtyard!

Long Bo gave a slightly awkward click of his tongue: “‘Que-le Huo-duo’ is a nickname the Türks came up with; to be honest, it is far too crude. Those Türks had no idea of the true use of the thing—they knew only how to drive carts about setting off blasts at random, as vulgar as the name itself.”

Li Bi scanned every corner, but saw nothing suspicious. By rights, with the fierce-fire thunder running to two hundred-odd barrels, it could not be hidden so well out of sight.

Long Bo pointed a finger up at the sky and cried aloud: “Let there be light!”

Soon points of candlelight sprang up here and there not far off, at first one or two, then a patch, then a ring, and quickly they sketched out a perfect disk.

Only now did Li Bi see that nearby there stood, of all things, a great bamboo-framed lantern-wheel more than five zhang high. It was only that a moment ago there had been no light, and in the dark it could not be made out at all. Now several dozen torch-candles flickered all at once, lighting the grove as bright as day, so that the details could at last be seen clearly.

This lantern-wheel had a skeleton pieced together from thick bamboo, pasted over with oiled paper, made into a turning wheel like a waterwheel. Candles were set in cell after cell in its hollow, and the paper face outside was divided into twelve zones, each painted in color with the image of one of the twelve zodiac animals, with gold and silver tassels and lucky-insect satin hung at the corners. Below ran a water-channel, the current driving the lantern-wheel to turn slowly, so that the twelve zodiac animals revolved round and round, a figure for the passing of the hours. At the center of the lantern-wheel was a fine painting of the Three Stars—Fortune, Longevity, and Rank—gathered together.

This lantern-wheel was not on the scale of the lantern-towers in the East and West Markets and the Xingqing Palace, which ran to ten-odd zhang at every turn; but its designer’s mind was subtle, able to think of borrowing the working principle of a waterwheel and turning it into a figure for the wheeling of the hours—quite distinctive.

Like the Self-Raining Pavilion in the courtyard, it was full of ingenuity, a thing none but a man of both leisure and wealth could contrive.

Li Bi gazed up at it for a while: “And what has this to do with the Que-le Huo-duo?” Long Bo patted him on the shoulder, signing to him to be a little patient.

The lantern-wheel turned in silence for a time, and then, all at once, in the Dragon-hour zone, a ball of fire flared up. No—not flared, but burst up. Li Bi saw clearly that it had burst out from within the bamboo. The lantern-wheel was still turning, and this tongue of flame spread with it to the neighboring Rabbit-hour zone and Snake-hour zone; the bamboo on both these sides too burst crackling into flame, and in almost an instant a quarter of the lantern-wheel was blazing fiercely.

Li Bi’s eyes went round; in the candlelight he saw it very clearly. The reason the fire spread so fast was that, once the bamboo burst open, a black liquid flowed out from within. That liquid took fire the moment it touched flame, and burned most fiercely.

The black liquid, bearing the flames, ran over the whole body of the lantern-wheel and turned it into a blazing torch. Soon the fire reached the central bamboo tube of the lantern-wheel, and in a few snaps of the fingers Li Bi saw a ball of flame burst violently out of the tube; the bodies of the Three Stars—Fortune, Longevity, and Rank—split apart into countless fragments. Hard upon it, the twelve hours too were torn to pieces by the sudden storm of fire. So exquisite a lantern-frame collapsed thus with a roar.

That sound of explosion Li Bi knew well; it was exactly the same as the blast in the West Market, only smaller in scale.

“Trial D, complete.” The voice of an observer came from the grove. Hearing it, Long Bo clapped his hands in delight and turned to Li Bi: “Well? Do you see now? What a splendid sight this is.”

Li Bi reached out a hand and steadied himself against a sal tree. He saw it all now.

No wonder the Jing’an Bureau could not find where those two hundred-odd barrels of fierce-fire thunder had gone: the Pifu, in Changming Ward, had poured the refined rock-oil into bamboo tubes and then carried the tubes openly away. The watchtowers and the constables everywhere, straining to find carts hauling wooden barrels, had of course been galloping off in the wrong direction, and turned up nothing.

If these rock-oil bamboo tubes were fitted onto lantern-frames—the small tubes to feed the fire, the large ones to set off the blast—then once they went up, given the density of Chang’an’s lantern-viewing crowds, the casualties would, he feared, be appalling.

Long Bo was still gazing up and sighing with feeling: “Such a wonderful spectacle—a pity those Türks will not get to see it, a great pity. Do you suppose they might kneel down on the ground and worship?”

“I do not understand . . .” Li Bi murmured. “The lantern-frames were begun days ago; why did you not fit them out during the building, but wait deliberately until after the lamps were lit at the Lantern Festival to go and fit them?”

Long Bo scratched vexedly at the tip of his hooked nose: “There was no help for it. This rock-oil stuff cannot be set off unless it is heated beforehand. Once heated, if it is not detonated within half a double-hour it goes cold, and has to be heated again.”

Li Bi understood: this property of the fierce-fire thunder decided that it could only be fitted and fired on the spot, and could not be planted in advance. He knew Long Bo was not lying; when the Turkic Wolf Guards had charged with their carts at the start, the rock-oil in those wooden barrels had been at a boil too.

But the sheer amount of labor . . . was it not rather too great?

Li Bi ran the burning scene through his mind once more, and suddenly saw that, in the lantern-wheel just now, only a few of its parts had really caught fire. In other words, one lantern-frame needed only three or four of its bamboo tubes replaced to be turned into a huge fierce-fire thunder.

The bamboo lantern-frames in common use in Chang’an were bound together out of joint after joint of bamboo and hempen cord, loose in structure, so that whether one dismantled or replaced them, it was extremely easy. These men had only to replace a few tubes with these rock-oil ones under the name of maintenance—not much labor, and half a double-hour was more than enough.

This stratagem was cleverer than the Türks charging in with their fierce-fire thunder, and better hidden, and would cause far greater casualties. This was the true Que-le Huo-duo! Unless known of beforehand, it simply could not be guarded against.

There were now at the least tens of thousands of lantern-frames in all of Chang’an; to check them one by one . . . wait, no—there were only two hundred-odd barrels of rock-oil, which could not possibly cover the whole of Chang’an. Unless—unless what the Pifu sought was not the whole surface, but a single point!

A layer of cold sweat suddenly broke out down Li Bi’s spine.

The fierce-fire thunder’s half-double-hour detonation property, the reach of two hundred barrels of rock-oil—reasoning back from these two points, it meant that what the Pifu sought was not slaughter over a wide area, but an attack against a specific place at a specific time.

Could it be . . . A ghastly, terrible surmise tore open Li Bi’s mind and burst forth from him, roaring out at the real world. His two legs began to tremble beyond his control.

Though Li Bi did not know why they had abducted him, it must surely be bound up with this earth-shaking plot. His gaze hardened, and he suddenly flung himself with all his strength at that solid courtyard wall—for he had realized that the one way to foil this earth-shaking plot was to die.

Just as the crown of his head was about to strike the wall, a hand seized Li Bi by the lapels and dragged him back.

“Deputy Director Li really is decisive in the killing stroke—a pity the body was a step slower than the resolve.” Long Bo said mockingly.

Several men came forward and pinned Li Bi down, to keep him from any further attempt at suicide. Li Bi closed his eyes in disappointment, a sense of helplessness binding his whole body like a rope.

Long Bo leaned in close to his face: “What I most love to savor is exactly this look of despair on a clever man like you, who has seen through everything and yet can do nothing.”

Li Bi opened his eyes and said, word by word: “Even if I am gone, there will still be someone to stop you.” Long Bo laughed aloud: “The Jing’an Bureau is indeed worth dreading. But it has already been burned to bare ground—with what will it stop us?”

But Long Bo soon found that Li Bi, of all things, was smiling too. Having witnessed the might of the Que-le Huo-duo, this young high official could still, astonishingly, manage a smile. Long Bo found that he was, of all things, just a little afraid—and that all at once made him intensely uneasy.

Smack!

Long Bo swung his arm and dealt Li Bi a heavy slap: “You have nothing left to lean on—why can you still smile?”

There was a little blood at the corner of Li Bi’s mouth, but the smile on it had not changed: “Because the one thing you have left out is that most dangerous fellow of all.”

“Zhang Xiaojing?” Long Bo knew the name, of all things.

Li Bi noticed that the other’s flippant look had vanished, and in its place was a gravity greater than any he had shown before.
