## Chapter 1. Bloodshed Against the Enemy, Valiant Resistance

### A Most Urgent Order Flies Across Ten Thousand Li of Passes and Mountains

In January 1939, with the Sino-Japanese war being waged at its fiercest, the center of our military and political command and leadership had already moved to Chongqing in Sichuan.

One day—which day exactly I truly cannot recall—there came, all at once, a telegram from Mr. Dai Yunong. In point of urgency it was ranked "most urgent, ten-thousand-fold," sent from Hong Kong and relayed by way of Chongqing to Tianjin. In the security marking at its head were the two words "for personal deciphering." When I had deciphered it myself into plain text, there were but these few words: "Come to Hong Kong without delay; telephone XXXXX to make contact." The telegram came, indeed, out of the blue, with not the least sign beforehand, and I could by no means fathom what the matter was.

I took the telegram to show my secretary, Comrade Zeng Che, meaning to sound out his view; he pondered it a while, and turned the little slip over and over in his mind for a good long time, yet could make nothing of it either. Do not take it for a mere scrap of paper: this was an "urgent order" from above, leaving no room at all for deliberation, and admitting not the least delay; and since I was ordered to Hong Kong without delay, I had at once to set my affairs in order and take the road.

The running of the Tianjin Station ought properly to be left in Comrade Zeng Che's charge as acting head, for the station secretary is the equal of a chief secretary or deputy head in an ordinary office; as for the affairs of the "Luan-Yu Guerrilla Command," those need give me no concern, since Mr. Wang Tianmu, its commander, presided over them himself. By our custom, when there was a transfer of personnel there was no need even to take one's leave: for one thing, word would be sent down separately from above; for another, there were cases where it was best that others not know.

Beyond that, out of personal feeling I much wished to see three men—Wang Wen, the action-group leader; Qi Qingbin, chief of staff of the Luan-Yu Command; and Zhang Zuoxing, director of its political department—but they were all off in the country at Xin'an town in Baodi, and there was no longer time for it.

As I was about to leave Tianjin for Hong Kong, I supposed I should be coming back, and so I did not even settle my household, but hurried off just as if I were going to Beiping as usual. Who could have known that this one going would keep me away eight full years before I set foot in Beiping and Tianjin again? As for my wife and children, it was only when the three of them—mother and two daughters—heard that I had reached Chongqing that they decided of their own accord to take ship to Shanghai.

In the year after I left, grave misfortunes befell the work at Beiping and Tianjin, which must be touched on here; and one may see too in a man's lot how truly it is that "life and death are decreed by fate, and fortune and disaster keep no constancy."

Comrade Zeng Che, secretary of the Tianjin Station, after taking acting charge of it, was—amid frequent transfers of personnel and work grown ever heavier—further promoted to be district chief of the upgraded Tianjin District, and to lead directly the ever more formidable "Anti-Japanese Traitor-Killing Corps." Most unhappily, before long, on September 27, 1939, he came to grief and was seized on Dajing Road in the Hebei quarter of Tianjin.

After his capture Zeng was cruelly abused under the illegal tortures of the Japanese military police for more than three hundred days, and at last, on September 9, 1940, met his death with a high heart at Beiping, dying nobly for the cause—only twenty-seven years of age. Nor was he ever able to marry that Miss Bai.

Writing this, I cannot but call to mind how Zeng Che used to address me. He did not call me "station chief," nor did he call me by my name; in public and in private alike he would call me "Second Brother." Now, closing my eyes and thinking hard, I find the scenes of those old days truly present before me, one and all. Rest in peace, brother Zeng! Your spirit-tablet is already enshrined in our "ancestral hall," that we who die after you may forever cherish the memory of heroes like you—"loyal and brave, a model for all."

Next there was Comrade Wang Wen, leader of the Tianjin District action group and, at the same time, commander of the battalion directly under the Luan-Yu Command. He had struck the enemy and their puppets hard at Beiping and Tianjin and in East Hebei, and had made a name of terror; the enemy's puppet gendarmes and police dreaded him like a very demon-god, yet hated him to the marrow, and had long wished to take him and be done, going so far as to post an open reward for his "arrest and delivery to justice." In September 1939, Comrade Wang Wen was ordered once again to venture secretly into Beiping to work; his identity exposed, he came to grief and was seized, and suffered every kind of savage torture. Steadfast and unbending, he met his death at last with composure at Beiping—his whole body a mass of wounds, his bones broken and his flesh crushed, no longer in the shape of a man—being just turned thirty.

Wang Wen and I, working together, shared one aspiration; we hoped, of course, that one day we might both make our mark in the work, and so he would now and again admonish me and lance my faults—he was also the first man willing to rebuke me to my face. I understood it, and forgave it: it was that he "hated the iron for not turning to steel." The fault was only mine, that I proved no vessel, and failed his good will in the end.

Comrade Zeng Che! Comrade Wang Wen! We were partners in the fighting, fellows in the work, friends in affection; and only because we were born into a great age of upheaval, and followed this calling of ours as well, did the one dying and the other living become a common thing. If our lots be weighed, there was fortune in them and misfortune, to be sure; but seen over the long run, the good fortune is yours, and the ill must be mine! That you died in the very flower of your promising years is, of course, a great grief; yet your laying down your lives to keep your virtue whole, your names handed down in the annals, have won you the honor paid to martyrs and the reverence paid to heroes—and this is the price of a life! And I? Though I have dragged out another forty years and more, down to this day—confident once, and always sure I could recover what had been lost—yet in the several decades gone by I have done nothing to speak of, let alone achieved anything; and set against you, this too is my misfortune. To carry it a step further: the laws of society never err. The penitence that comes after, the emptiness within, are graven upon the heart and the bone; and, take it as lightly as one may, they amount at most to a little self-consolation. Some time hence, when once the coffin-lid is closed over me, what the world will make of me is hard indeed to foretell. If Heaven favor me, I shall rot in silence with the grass and the trees; and if not—there is no telling yet what they will make me out to be.

When the two comrades Zeng Che and Wang Wen came to grief, one after the other, I was already in Shanghai, and heard nothing of it at the time; it was, as I recall, only after Comrade Qi Qingbin came to Shanghai that I learned of it.

It is said that in August, after I had been transferred away, Tianjin too was flooded, as was the region of East Hebei at the same time, and the waters did not recede for thirty or forty days. Just as people were resuming their ordinary business, trouble broke out in Tianjin; and from then on our organizations in the two cities of Beiping and Tianjin suffered the enemy's and the puppets' destruction one after another. Among the responsible officers implicated and lost were Comrade Chen Ziyi, who had been transferred from the Beiping First Station to be head of the Tianjin Station, and Comrade Zhou Shiguang, who had risen from deputy to be head of the Beiping Station, together with cadres of the Anti-Japanese Traitor-Killing Corps—several dozen men in all. Comrades Chen Ziyi and Zhou Shiguang, too, gave their lives for the country, one after another. This time, in truth, the sacrifice was grievous and the dying heroic; to say that it "startled heaven and earth and made the very spirits weep" would be no exaggeration.

From Tianjin to Hong Kong there was no air route, and so no flying; by rights there should have been a ship going direct by sea, but there was none either, and no other way was open but to take a steamer first to Shanghai and there change vessels. On the Tianjin-to-Shanghai run most of the shipping companies had suspended sailings, and only two firms still kept the passage between Tianjin and Shanghai: the British Butterfield & Swire and the agency of Jardine, Matheson. In the warm season one could board from the bank of the British Concession at Tianjin and go out to sea by the Hai River to the Tanggu bar; but at that time it was deep winter, the ground frozen and the sky bitter, the Hai River long since locked in ice, and everything depended on a small icebreaker to grind open a channel, by which, with much ado, one took a lighter to Tanggu before one could board the great sea-going steamer.

All this to-do brought its worries for one's safety, for the moment one left the British Concession, every place one passed through was fallen territory under Japanese occupation; and though the Japanese authorities were not yet wholly without scruple toward Britain, the Japanese soldiery, overbearing by nature, would at times do just as they pleased and pay no heed to any of that.

The lighter ran along the channel for two hours and more, and all that while it was as though I sat in an ice-cellar—cold, close, and hard to bear. The lighter's fittings were mean: though there was an awning overhead, it let the wind through on every side, and the cold air, keen as a knife, came whistling in through the cracks, till I shivered from head to foot. A glance out through the blurred glass window showed only sparse, withered reeds swaying yellow on either bank, and setting off a great sweep of gray.

Anxious and afraid the whole way, I got to Tanggu at last, only to find that all the passengers must still go ashore. It seemed one had yet to walk a stretch before boarding the great ship. My companion, Brother Hu Yongquan, gave me a look and jerked his chin, as much as to say: go on and don't fret, there'll be no trouble. The passengers, some dozens in all, formed a single file and went forward in a line; and a little way off was a sentry-post, where armed Japanese soldiers and an interpreter—a mean, contemptible, ill-favored creature—stood waiting already. Bodies searched and baggage examined, a few of us questioned as well, we came through safe in the end: a fright, but no harm.

As was mentioned in the fifth section of the first book, Rooting Out Traitors in the North, the "Tianjin Station" had a volunteer comrade, Hu Yongquan, who had been brought in by Mr. Dai himself. He was said to bear an important charge, though what its inner nature was I never did learn. His "escorting" me to Shanghai this time was at my own request; and he too, at the same time, had business that took him to Shanghai.

For the Tianjin-to-Shanghai leg we took a passenger-cargo ship of the British Butterfield & Swire—the Shengjing, or else the Shuntian; I can no longer be sure which. She was of no great tonnage; the first-class "saloon" had but four to six berths, long since booked up; what remained was second-class cabins, four to a room in upper and lower bunks, and we were given one side of one, Brother Hu Yongquan letting me have the lower berth and taking the upper himself. Three meals a day, and good ones; if one did not get seasick, the days passed easily enough. Strangest of all: the ship's stewards were forever getting up a game, and anyone so inclined might sit in for a few rounds of mahjong—but Brother Yongquan said there was something "fishy" in it, and that I had best not go looking for trouble.

Brother Hu Yongquan was kindness itself, and I owed much to his care the whole way. At Shanghai we stayed but a single night; from taking a hotel and buying the boat-ticket to seeing me aboard, he attended to it all, and only when he had watched the ship weigh anchor and put out did he wave and bid me a fond farewell. Let me note here in passing: greater Shanghai had by this time fallen too, and only the concessions of Shanghai kept their old aspect, with not the least air of wartime to be seen. This time I boarded from the bank of the Huangpu within the concession, and going out past the Wusong bar saw no Japanese soldiers at all.

For the Shanghai-to-Hong Kong leg I took the Tjisadane of the Dutch Java-China-Japan Line. Called a mail-steamer though she was, she was not, in point of quality, a distinguished one. My ticket was first-class, two to a cabin, but the other berth stayed empty the whole way. Board and lodging aboard were wholly Westernized, to which I was truly little used. The steward was a Cantonese, and from talking with him I learned that in the great steerage below there was every kind of thing—eating, drinking, and amusement, all to be had. This too was a novelty such as I had never heard of. In another respect, though, the foreigners ran a tight ship: a great iron door shut off the lower deck from the upper, and there was no passing between them, however one might wish it.

This was my second time in Hong Kong. My first, as I recall, was in mid-December 1927; I did not go to Hong Kong to throw myself on anyone, but was merely passing through. Nothing of it is graven deeper in my memory than the wretched plight I was in that time. We were three then, traveling together; pooling every last coin we had upon us, we bought three steerage tickets to Shanghai, and had next to nothing left. There lay the ship at the bank, and one might not go aboard until the cargo was loaded and unloaded; and asking about, we learned it would be the next morning at soonest, and might well drag on to the afternoon. This was a kind of contempt shown to steerage passengers, and it was a plain fact besides, for steerage is nothing but the hold, and the passengers can board only once the loading and unloading are done—so wait we must. To take a small hotel for the night would surely cost money, and how much more that scant ten-odd dollars must yet be made to stretch, there was no knowing. Nothing to do but bear the hunger, the cold, and the weariness, and there along Connaught Road near the wharf we wandered this way and that, sat under the eaves of an arcade to rest, and only after long weary hours worked our way at last into that reeking steerage hold.

Coming as I did this time—dining well the whole way, sleeping under wool blankets, and the moment I landed springing into a taxi to the hotel—the difference in my circumstances was as that between heaven and earth; and set the two side by side, how could I not remember it! Yet in one thing they were much alike: the first time my road ahead was a blank, and I knew not where I was bound; and this time, all bewilderment, again—where now?

Brother Hu Yongquan had once told me that in Happy Valley there was a middling-sized place, the Sanguang Hotel, quiet and cheap, and far better than the hurly-burly of the Wan Chai quarter. I put up there to try it, and found it good indeed. This time the telegram had given me only a single telephone number for contact, and I could not help worrying that, if the call would not go through, I should be put to no end of trouble. In the event the worry was needless: that very evening I was asked to meet Mr. Dai on Pok Fu Lam Road in the Mid-Levels.

The "Mid-Levels," there on the hillside, had long been Hong Kong's quarter of fine residences. The place I was told to go to was but a single detached house, neither new nor old, in no marked way different from the few neighboring houses about it—ordinary, and not in the least mysterious. Yet within it, little as one would have guessed, lay marvels without end.

Mr. Dai and I had not met in several years; and though we shook hands as usual, it was coolly done, wanting the warmth of a reunion after long parting. This did not mean he was cold toward me; rather, his mind was too heavily burdened to spare much for courtesies—perhaps a great many problems were awaiting his solving?

We faced each other in silence, neither of us speaking; he was, I thought, turning over some other matter in his mind. So another minute or two passed, and, anxious as I was, I had no choice but to curb my patience and wait for him to speak first. While I was still lost in my own thoughts, he said to me all at once: "We are going together. The Hong Kong District is seeing to the formalities; you have only to keep in touch with Wang Luqiao." These two sentences left me all at sea; and what I cared for most was—what in the world had I come all these thousand li to do?

Go with him—where? I was just about to ask, when he said there was yet another matter of great urgency that must be dealt with at once, and with that turned on his heel and went off into another room. I was much put out. What was I to make of this!

Comrade Wang Luqiao and I had met once in Nanjing—a handshake and a word of greeting, no more than that. Wang Luqiao was a Shandong man, a graduate of the police academy, who had served as one of Mr. Dai's bodyguards, and who had just now, having finished an important piece of work in Guangxi, been kept in Hong Kong to await orders. He was courteous and respectful toward me, and I much admired that trim, dashing air of his; though in fact there were but five or six years between us in age, so that in saying so I seem to give myself airs. I asked him plainly: "Just now the Boss, Mr. Dai, said I was to go with him—do you know where we're going?" Luqiao told me frankly: "To Hanoi. They're getting the visas and booking the tickets these few days; we can probably set off tomorrow or the day after. I'm coming along with you too."

Luqiao saw me back to the Sanguang Hotel in Happy Valley, and we chatted a while, mostly idle talk. I much wished to know more of what concerned me; but at that time our acquaintance had not yet ripened to the point of confiding, and there were things it would not do to press. As for him, whether he knew or not, he dared not say much either; for had I asked, and had he known but found it awkward to tell me, would it not have put him in a hard place?

On leaving, Luqiao charged me to rest much and go out little, lest word should come and I not be found. Luqiao gone, and I left alone, I had leisure to think—and think, in truth, I must. First and foremost, what I could least set my mind at ease over was my household, left wholly unsettled when I went off at a word's notice; and no less on my mind was the Tianjin working station, and whether I might return to my post there—two questions bound up, of course, with each other. As for what I was to do at Hanoi—that, I thought, must surely have close bearing on Wang Jingwei's desertion of Chongqing. Had not Wang Luqiao said he was going along too? Had he not just been transferred to Hong Kong after dealing, in Guangxi, with a "professional strong-arm man"? Had not I myself, in North China, directed or shared in many an operation? Reasoning from this, I had more than half the answer.

Yet thinking again, I judged it not wholly so; for Wang's rank and standing were out of the common, and he was not quite as an ordinary man who conspired with an enemy state—surely, then, they would not go so far as to do anything to him?

Think as I might, I could not think it clear. But since come I had, there was of course nothing to do but obey orders!

The next day—it was the middle or last third of January 1939—Mr. Dai Yunong, together with me and Wang Luqiao, three of us, flew direct from Hong Kong to the chief city of Annam, Hanoi. On the same plane was a certain Mr. Chen, who more than ten years afterward would serve as ambassador to a certain country; since he had seen to his own separate formalities and declared he was on other public business, it is very likely he had nothing to do with our case. I mention it here only for the record. Mr. Chen stayed but a single night at Hanoi and then hurried away; and in that brief while there were signs that he presented to Mr. Dai a certain man—a man of great help to the "Hanoi operation." This, however, is no more than a surmise; for as there is yet no proof of it, one cannot take it wholly for truth.

To speak again of the several of us on the plane: Mr. Dai and I sat in one row, he by the window and I near the aisle, while Luqiao was in the row behind, seated beside a foreigner. There was time enough on the journey, I thought—surely he might tell me a hint of it now? But he never opened his lips. Several times I broached a topic, and still he gave two grunts and would say no more. This was perhaps because the roar of the plane's engines was too loud, so that a low word could never be made out; while to speak up, in such a place, was to risk being overheard, and the secret of the business thereby let slip—which would not do at all.

The one who reached Hanoi before us was Mr. Fang Bingxi. Brother Bingxi is one of the three men who took part in the "Hanoi operation" and are yet alive today. By the report of a friend who had once traveled in Europe, Brother Bingxi was in trade in Brussels, the capital of Belgium, and had even been seen there, and was doing well enough. Reckoning his age, he is two years my senior, and already seventy-four. Since we parted at Hanoi in March 1939, more than forty years have gone by and we have never met again. The meetings and partings of a life, how hard they are to speak of! Let me take this occasion to wish him, from afar, health and happiness and long years of good fortune.

No—that is wrong! The paragraph above was rewritten some months ago; but at the beginning of December this year, chatting one evening with a younger relative, I heard him speak of Mr. Fang Bingxi, and so learned that Brother Bingxi had died two or three years since! I even asked him, "Are you sure?"—and he answered, "No mistake." And with that, of those who took part in the "Hanoi operation," only two are now left alive!

What must be set down here is that Brother Bingxi, though come to the great age of seventy, was still serving his country without thought of name or place—and truly he saw it through from first to last. Brother Bingxi, I shall faithfully record your deeds, as my lasting remembrance of you.

Brother Bingxi had reached Hanoi a dozen days and more before us, and had already, following Mr. Dai's instructions, made considerable preparations. In a somewhat quiet spot within the city of Hanoi he had rented a house, and all the formalities Brother Bingxi had likewise seen to. The house was of no great plan: two stories, one up and one down. Above were two bedrooms with a lavatory set between them—not like what is nowadays called a suite; below was a parlor giving onto a dining room, and in the yard a separate kitchen and servants' quarters. This was our lodging, and the command post, too, of the "Hanoi operation."

It is not that I have forgotten: from that time until I left Hanoi I never did know what this street was called; and as to the sign that marked the street's name—whether it stood at the head of the street or at the end of the lane—of that too I have not the least recollection.

For the needs of the work—or, if you like, for ease of coming and going—Brother Bingxi took it upon himself to buy a secondhand Ford motorcar. It had but two doors, so that with many aboard the getting in and out was none too free; but as a means of getting about it saved a deal of trouble. Secondhand though the car was, its parts were sound, and in two months and more it never once needed repair. Its great merit was that it started nimbly: turn the engine over, and away it went at once.

When we reached the Hanoi airfield, it was in this Ford motorcar that Brother Bingxi came. He spoke fluent French, which counted for more even than the Annamese tongue, and so all the customs formalities went very smoothly; he took the three of us to this new rented dwelling, while that Mr. Chen who had been on our plane went off elsewhere.

Mr. Dai stayed at Hanoi but two days in all, less than forty-eight hours by the reckoning. The first evening he went out and did not return, and we knew not where he had gone, and had of course no chance to touch on the matter of the work; the second day, close upon noon, he came back at last, and we did at least sit down together to a midday meal. I had supposed we might surely speak of the work after the meal—but who would have thought it, he went out again on business with Brother Fang Bingxi. He, I thought, had his whole plan ready, while we were left uneasy in mind.

In the evening Mr. Dai called the four of us—Fang Bingxi, Wang Luqiao, and me—to go out together to a small Cantonese eating-house. The dishes were ordinary, no match, of course, for Hong Kong's; cheapest of all was the French wine, for red and white alike, and fine brandy too, cost here several times less than at home, so that if one had the capacity one might drink one's fill and spend but little. That night we all sipped a little and left off—partly for having no heart to drink, but chiefly hemmed in by the cheerless air of the thing. I called to mind the year 1932 in Nanjing, when I and my two elder brothers Jianqiu and Binghua had likewise gone with Mr. Dai to a small eating-house: how unlike that easy, lighthearted scene this was.

After supper Mr. Dai went off elsewhere with Brother Fang Bingxi by pedicab, bidding Luqiao and me go back and wait for him. I sent the driver on ahead, and then Luqiao and I hailed two pedicabs of our own, meaning to take the air and see something of the streets. The pedicabs of Hanoi in those days were most amusing to watch others ride, but a little fearsome to ride oneself; they were the front-seat, rear-push sort—the "Kongming cart"—each for a single rider, which is why we took one apiece. The passenger sits in front, while the pedicab-man behind pushes with his hands and treads with his feet; being light, it runs along at a great pace, and the passenger, perched up there, is like a shrimp curled in a dustpan, with nothing at all before him for a guard—so that in any collision the first to come to grief would beyond doubt be the passenger. Only think of it: perched up there, along with the fine view you may well take fright.

Luqiao and I had gone two rounds when we lost our way; and, having no common tongue with the pedicab-men, we pointed here and gestured there and grew only the more muddled, the more so as the light had failed and we could no longer tell east from west or north from south. Were reconnaissance work to be done in such a fashion, it would be a great joke indeed. With much ado we found the Chinese Consulate-General at last, and having asked the way we should go, so came back to our lodging.

When Mr. Dai came back it was already the dead of night; and in the few hours before he was to leave, he gathered the three of us—Fang Bingxi and myself among them—in the ground-floor parlor, and began to entrust to us the mission of this journey.

### Limited Only to Surveillance of Movements and Reconnaissance of Activities

To explain the mission of this journey, Mr. Dai first gave a brief outline of how Wang Zhaoming (Jingwei) had deserted Chongqing, and specially fetched out a newspaper clipping of that "Yan Telegram" which Wang, on December 29, 1938, had had Lin Baisheng and the others publish at Hong Kong, and showed it to the several of us. Only of how Wang, before quitting Chongqing and fleeing, had already been in collusion with the Japanese side, did he say not a single word.

It is, to tell the truth, hard to credit; but before this—whatever the two of them, Fang and Wang, may have understood of this business of Wang Jingwei—for my own part I had, at the very most, caught a scrap of it in passing at my ear, and had paid it no particular heed at all. The reason is simple enough: for one thing, I had already more work of my own than I could ever finish, and truly no leisure to look aside; for another, we were in the occupied zone, too far off, and some tidings never reached us; and again, for a worker of my rank to be said to concern himself with affairs of state to so lofty a pitch, before the matter had come clear, would be idle talk. Now that the thing was upon us, and much hung on it, how could I take it lightly again? And having these many days longed to have this stopped gourd opened, I now, of course, gave my whole attention to hearing Mr. Dai go on.

Only then did Mr. Dai come to the point, saying: "Our coming to Hanoi this time is for just this affair! At present Wang is still keeping up an unbroken contact with the Japanese side. These last two days, though I have met with several of the connections here and have learned somewhat of the real state of things, I feel it is still not enough. I hope you will all put forth a common effort, and see that we make the most of our working power, so as not to fail the expectations of those above."

Having said this, Mr. Dai fell silent again, and, fixing his eyes on me a moment with purpose, spoke out: "This is a most rare chance; we must not only take good hold of it, but must show something for it, or else we shall have no ground to be buried in!"

I understood: this was meant for my ears. And meant, of course, for everyone's. In the words there was a savor of reproach and admonition, pointing at the poor showing of my work at Beiping and Tianjin—or at the least, that he was none too satisfied with what I had done. Besides which, before laying the mission upon us, he meant—the better to further the work—to deepen imperceptibly our sense of responsibility, and at the same time to hint that the comrades present should be of one heart and mind and obey the command.

Mr. Dai went on: "I hope you can all recognize how special and how grave this affair is. It is no common piece of work; should we mishandle it, we shall stir up trouble, and may even bring about the contrary effect—you must be especially careful and circumspect." His words, spoken now and halting now, seemed to leave much unsaid.

Bingxi, Luqiao, and I exchanged glances, none of us clear as to where the heart of the matter really lay. In truth, one had only to listen with patience: he would surely give a plain instruction, for how could a thing so weighty be left to mere conjecture? That he spoke so roundabout was, I suppose, a manner of leadership; or else it was that the case was not yet clear, and it would not do to speak his whole mind.

Mr. Dai rose and opened a bottle of wine, and Luqiao drew open a drawer and found four glasses, pouring one before each of us. The thought, I suppose, was to talk as we drank, and so thin a little the air that had frozen up between us; and besides, the night being deep, it was as well to rouse our spirits. The wine, under the lamplight, shone and glittered a golden yellow, a fine sight to see; raised to the nose, it gave off a queer smell that stung the nostrils; touched first with the tip of the tongue, it was neither sweet nor sharp, and what its taste was one truly could not put into words. Such wine I had never drunk before, and I guessed it was likely some very common fruit wine. The taste being poor, and the drinking of it in such a manner, the whole thing was most unnatural.

We forced down a glass apiece, and then Mr. Dai turned the edge of his talk and made, at last, the following definite decisions. He said: "My plane at half past seven this morning is to hurry me back to Chongqing. The affairs here Brother XX will take charge of and see to; whether I can come back within a short while is, for the moment, still uncertain, and I hope Brother Bingxi and Luqiao will help Brother XX with all their hearts and strength.

"As to the mission, there are two points I can now decide upon: first, to keep strict watch on Wang's movements; and second, to attend by every means to the doings of the Wang faction's men. Beyond that, once I am back in Chongqing, telegrams will come from time to time. The wireless set and the operators can be here within days, and will report at once. We shall keep in constant touch."

Mr. Dai said further, to my face: "Brother XX, I hope you can bend your whole mind to directing this work; the ordinary business Brother Bingxi can see to, and you need not divide your attention over it; Luqiao's work, as the case may require hereafter, you shall assign. I well understand the difficulties on the ground; once I am back, I shall at once draw off able comrades and send them post-haste to help—set your mind at rest on it all."

With this he had, in the main, given us all our instructions; and now, the sky drawing near to dawn, we all had need of a little rest. Luqiao went back to his room for a nap, and Brother Bingxi made ready to sit a while in a chair. Mr. Dai beckoned me into his room—to speak with me alone, no doubt, on some matter?

In truth I had a bellyful of things to say, pent up these several days, and had long wished for a chance to put my questions to him. Besides, he was about to leave, and it was needful to settle a few more matters with him, so that, in public affairs and private both, I might have some rule to go by within. I asked Mr. Dai: "As to the work on Wang—apart from those two points you have just laid down—is there need to make any further, or any contingency, preparation?" Mr. Dai looked at me with both eyes but gave no answer. So I pressed a further question: "Should we not bring up Wang Wen and his men from Tianjin?" Mr. Dai considered a moment, and answered: "Sending telegrams, waiting on sailings, seeing to formalities—that is too much delay, and there is no controlling the time; I think it need not be. Once I am back, I shall make suitable arrangements of personnel."

I knew well enough there was no need to ask further, yet, unable to curb my impatience, I put still one more question: "When the work here has come to a stage, am I to go back to Tianjin?" For my post was at Tianjin and my family too was at Tianjin, so that the question was only proper; but, little as I looked for it, it drew forth a few sharp words. He set his face hard and glared at me, saying: "Why, when you married, did you not report it and get the organization's leave, as the rules require?" Where was this to come from? In fact it was already a thing of five years past, and two children born of it—how could he bring up such matters now? I was much angered.

It is not that I failed to see his meaning: he took my wish to return to Tianjin for a hankering after wife and child. For men in our line of work, and in a time of war besides, how much better were it to be without the burden of a household, and spared so many cares! But this was an understanding of the reasoning, working sort; and where private feeling and human nature are concerned, it would gall any man to hear it laid upon him; the more so as I am a man of no great reserve, so that, since he had answered beside my question, I simply said nothing more at all.

He too could see that my manner was strained, and so he unfolded a faint smile and urged me to work in earnest, promising that when things had come to a stage he would surely consider my future course, and would ask my own view before deciding anything. And with that we brought the talk back again to the actual work at hand.

Mr. Dai gave his instructions, saying: "These few days I have got in touch with a man of great help to us; his position is a most peculiar one. I have already arranged with him that you personally are to be his contact, and that no third party may be sent to seek him out. Most of all, he can furnish high-grade intelligence, and can also give us leads for our operations." At this he drew from his pocket a name-card; and looking at the name upon it, I did not know the man at all, nor had I ever heard of him, so that I thought it might well be that mysterious personage. Mr. Dai handed me the card, and I asked, "Is this the man?" He shook his head: "You have only to take this card and go to him; he will surely receive you, and you may settle everything with him. How you keep in touch thereafter, you may fix between yourselves. And take note, if you please: he is not a comrade of our work; toward him you must be courteous, and keep a proper measure too."

Afterward we exchanged a few more private words, and he charged me on no account to act rashly. The rest I shall not set down at length here.

Before Mr. Dai left, I asked him to leave me that clipping to look over, and he gave it to me. To avoid exposing our identities, Fang Bingxi alone saw him to the airfield; neither Luqiao nor I went. We had supposed Mr. Dai would come again, but he truly could not spare himself, and though he several times spoke of coming, he was never able to make the trip. After Mr. Dai had gone, Luqiao and I slept each in a separate room, and the first thing I set myself, once my mind was quiet, was to read once more, and with care, that "Yan Telegram" Wang had published. Not to have read it were one thing; but having read it, it truly made us callow young fellows feel a little sorry for him—that such quibbling, forced, unreasoning arguments he could bring himself to write! By this one sheet of a telegram alone, he was unfit to be a political leader; and if by it he thought to rally the empire, then he had set the multitude wilfully at naught. This is by no means my own bias, nor is it to slander him on purpose; but now, before we pass any judgment at all, let us look first at the original text of this "Yan Telegram":

To the Central Party Headquarters at Chongqing; to Director-General Chiang; and to all the comrades of the Central Executive and Supervisory Committees—greeting:

In April of this year, the Manifesto of the Extraordinary National Congress, in setting forth the causes of the present war of resistance, declared: "Ever since the Tanggu Agreement, the reason we have borne humiliation and shouldered hardship in our dealings with Japan has been none other than the wish to halt military action and adopt peaceful methods—first to seek the preservation of the several northern provinces, and thereafter to seek a reasonable settlement of the question of the Four Northeastern Provinces, holding, in the political sphere, the maintenance of sovereignty and administrative integrity as the irreducible minimum, and, in the economic sphere, mutual benefit and equality as the principle of cooperation." Since the sudden outbreak of the Marco Polo Bridge Incident last July, China, judging that these hopes could not be realized, was driven at last to resistance. I have now read the Japanese Government's exposition of the twenty-second of this month concerning the fundamental policy for adjusting Sino-Japanese relations. The first point is good-neighborliness and amity. It solemnly declares that Japan seeks no territory of China and demands no indemnity for war expenses; that Japan not only respects China's sovereignty but will, following the precedent of the years before the Meiji Restoration, on condition of allowing freedom of business in the interior, restore the concessions and abolish extraterritoriality, so that China may complete her independence. The Japanese Government having made this solemn declaration, we, proceeding by peaceful methods, may not only preserve the northern provinces but recover as well the various places fallen since the war began, and may yet maintain the independence and integrity of our sovereignty and administration; and thus, in accordance with the Manifesto, we may seek a reasonable settlement of the question of the Four Northeastern Provinces, taking this for our due resolve and course. The second point is joint defense against communism. In former years the Japanese Government several times proposed it, and we, out of the fear that it might thereby interfere in our country's military and internal affairs, hesitated. Now that the Japanese Government has made its exposition, a Sino-Japanese anti-Comintern agreement should be concluded in the spirit of the Japan-Germany-Italy anti-Comintern agreement, and this fear may be dispelled. The aim of defense against communism is to prevent the disturbance and intrigue of the Communist International, and it bears no effect upon relations with the Soviet Union. The Chinese Communists having declared their willingness to strive for the realization of the Three Principles of the People, they should forthwith cast off utterly their organization and propaganda, and abolish their border-region government and the special organization of their army, and observe in full the laws and institutions of the Republic of China. The Three Principles of the People being the highest principle of the Republic of China, all organization and propaganda contrary to this highest principle we must, of our own accord and actively, sanction, so as to discharge our duty of upholding the Republic of China. The third point is economic cooperation. This too the Japanese Government has for several years past repeatedly proposed, and we, the political disputes being yet unresolved, held that economic cooperation could not be spoken of. Now that the Japanese Government has solemnly made clear that it respects China's sovereignty and the independence and integrity of her administration, and has made clear besides that it seeks no economic monopoly in China, nor to demand that China restrict the interests of third countries, but wishes only, in accordance with the principle of Sino-Japanese equality, to seek the realization of economic cooperation—then this proposal should in principle be assented to, and upon this principle various concrete plans should be negotiated. As to these three points, Zhaoming, after mature deliberation, holds that the National Government should forthwith take them as its ground, and exchange sincerity with the Japanese Government, in the hope of restoring peace. The Japanese Government's statement of the third of November has already altered the attitude of its statement of the sixteenth of January; and if the National Government, upon these three points, enter into negotiations for peace, then the path of parley is opened. The aim of China's war of resistance is to seek the survival and independence of the nation; and after a year and more of resistance, our wounds are grievous and our pain deep. If it be yet possible to end the war by a peace consonant with justice, then the survival and independence of the nation can be preserved, and the aim of resistance is thereby attained. These three points are the principles of peace; as to their particulars, they must be weighed with all care that they be made fitting. Of these the most essential is that the Japanese armies be wholly withdrawn from China, and this universally and speedily; the so-called permission to station troops at specified places during the term of the anti-Comintern agreement to be limited, at the most, to points in the neighborhood of Inner Mongolia. This bears upon the independence and integrity of China's sovereignty and administration, and must be so, that China may bend her strength to recovery after the war and to the building of a modern state. The lands of China and Japan adjoin, and good-neighborliness and amity have their natural place and necessity; that for years past they have run counter to one another—the cause must be deeply sought out, and each must understand its own responsibility. Henceforth China should indeed make good-neighborliness and amity her educational aim, and Japan yet more should have her people cast off their traditional thought of aggression against and contempt for China, and establish in education a policy of friendship with China, so as to lay the foundation of a lasting peace between the two nations: this is the effort we ought to make toward the happiness of East Asia. At the same time, toward the peace and order of the Pacific and the safeguarding of the peace of the world, we must likewise strive in concert with all the countries concerned, to maintain and increase their friendship and common interest. This I respectfully propose, and earnestly beg that it be adopted. Wang Zhaoming. Yan. (The twenty-ninth of December in the twenty-seventh year.)

Wang's "Yan Telegram" was headed: "To the Director-General Chiang of the Central Party Headquarters of the Chinese Nationalist Party, and to all the comrades of the Central Executive and Supervisory Committees—greeting." Yet the fact was that at this time Wang Jingwei's own standing was that of Vice-Director-General of the Nationalist Party. Having so intimate an organizational tie, he might well have declared his position at a party meeting, or gathered other comrades to put forward their views and submit them to open counsel. Why, then, set himself thus apart, and needs must slip away from Chongqing and go abroad to issue a public telegram? Herein one may see that he had an ulterior design.

Where, then, lay his design? This was common knowledge: he was answering the third statement issued by the Japanese premier Konoe Fumimaro on December 22, 1938—the so-called "Three Principles of Peace." In the "Yan Telegram," after his opening words, Wang set down as his marker: "I have now read the Japanese Government's exposition of the twenty-second of this month concerning the fundamental policy for adjusting Sino-Japanese relations." That first sentence of his is already not quite right. Look at his use of the words "have now read," as though he had but just seen it in a news report: in truth he had long known the main substance of Konoe's statement. Forty days before Wang issued the Yan Telegram—that is, on November 19 and 20, 1938—Gao Zongwu and Mei Siping and the rest, representing Wang Jingwei in his collusion with Japan, and Kagesa Sadaaki and Imai Takeo, representing the Japanese military in their secret parleys with Wang, together with Itō Yoshio, a shokutaku of the South Manchuria Railway Company who, having a subtle connection with the Japanese Foreign Ministry, plied the needle and thread between them, met secretly in an empty house near the New Park at Hongkou in Shanghai, and as the fruit of their talks drafted three documents: the "Record of the Sino-Japanese Agreement," the "Items of Understanding in the Record of the Sino-Japanese Agreement," and the "Record of the Secret Sino-Japanese Agreement." On November 22, Gao and Mei left Shanghai and flew by way of Hong Kong back to Chongqing to report to Wang Jingwei on the course of the talks, and laid the "Record of the Agreement" before him for his confirmation; and, none dissenting, the Japanese side then issued its statement, and Wang answered it at once. All this is fact with proof to show for it; yet he put on the guise of one still in the dark—was this not to lie and to feign, and to think to cover the eyes and ears of all the world with one hand?

Next there is that face-saving phrase in the opening of the "Yan Telegram": "adjusting Sino-Japanese relations." At this moment the Sino-Japanese war had gone on a year and a half; the Japanese army had occupied the greater part of China's soil and slaughtered Chinese people by the million; and though no formal declaration of war had been made, we had long been in a state of war. What "relations" were there left to exist? And there being no "relations," from what was there to begin "adjusting"? This is nothing but talking to oneself, deceiving oneself and others.

Then there is that word "expound" in "exposition of the fundamental policy." Here the two words "set forth" would have been enough; to use "expound" leaves always a faint savor of fawning on the foreigner, of "lending the thief a hand." This is by no means to pick at words on purpose, nor to harbor prejudice and cavil by design; but from a single sentence of his we can point out three blemishes, and thereby know that this one sheet of a telegram was, in truth, blind to the larger interest, and wanting in good sense.

In the middle part of the "Yan Telegram," he actually prettifies the "Konoe statement," picking out first the pleasant things to tell, while of its main substance—the ruin it works upon China—he says nothing at all. In a matter so grave as the survival or extinction of the state, how could one, with the pettifogger's brush of a Shaoxing clerk, twist it at will and gloss the thing over?

In the "Yan Telegram," Wang lays especial stress on this: "Now that the Japanese Government has solemnly made clear that it respects China's sovereignty and the independence and integrity of her administration…" But how is this to be understood? Had Japan truly respected China's sovereignty, then she ought not to have launched, so brazenly, this war of aggression; ought not to have fashioned a "Manchukuo" upon our Northeastern territory, and forced us besides to acknowledge it; ought not to have demanded the "permission to station troops" upon Chinese soil. This "independence and integrity of administration," then—from what is it to be spoken of?

In the "Yan Telegram" Wang declares his stand: "Zhaoming, after mature deliberation, holds that the National Government should forthwith take these for its ground, and exchange sincerity with the Japanese Government, in the hope of restoring peace." Deliberate carefully he did indeed—but the fact was that he was even then in secret dalliance with Japan, engaged in the traffic of bartering away sovereignty and disgracing the country. Look: his very tone is changed; the matters he had privately agreed in secret with the Japanese, he would now have his own government take those traitorous terms for its ground. How could he, against his conscience, bring himself to say it!

It is understood, on good grounds, that before Wang Jingwei issued the "Yan Telegram" he had supposed he might well win some substantial response, and a response of no light weight—including this: that within the party and government organs he would gain the assent and support of a part of the chiefs; that among the local governments at least one province would offer him ground to set himself up as a king; and that among the front-line combat forces there would surely be, of one war zone or more, men of real military strength who would break off the fighting and make peace for his sake. But all these wishful longings of Wang's came, in the end, to nothing whatever.

After Wang Jingwei issued the "Yan Telegram" on December 29, 1938, the first lesson he got was expulsion from the party. On New Year's Day, 1939—the third day, that is, after the Yan Telegram—the Chinese Nationalist Party convened an extraordinary session of its Central Standing Committee to consider Wang Jingwei's "Yan Telegram." Director-General Chiang, though he wished for lenient handling and to grant a chance of repentance, found the standing committee members Lin Sen, Zhang Ji, Wu Jingheng, and the rest in extreme indignation; and the discussion at last resolved: "Wang Zhaoming to be expelled from the party forever," and passed that the resolution be drafted by Wu Jingheng (Zhihui).

The resolution ran as follows: "Wang Zhaoming, having received the grave trust of this Party, did, at a moment of crisis in the war of resistance, desert his post without leave, hide himself in a foreign place, and spread abroad views contrary to the national policy. In his telegram of the twenty-ninth, he actually urged that the enemy premier Konoe's rabid statement, aimed at the utter destruction of our nation, be taken for a basis on which to sue the enemy for peace; and, spreading it broadcast in the newspapers on the one hand, under the name of a proposal to the Central Government, he plied his art of shaking and misleading the hearts of men. And within his telegram, above all, he everywhere glosses over the enemy's demands with twisted words, not scrupling to turn right and wrong upside down and to lend the enemy a hand; and, going yet further from bad to worse, helps peddle the enemy's deceit. In respect of his conduct, this is in truth to collude with the enemy and sue for surrender. …"

The resolution points out that Wang "everywhere glosses over the enemy's demands with twisted words—this is in truth to collude with the enemy and sue for surrender." By this one sentence alone, Wang's crime is sufficiently adjudged.

Only now did I fully understand the one reason I had been made to fly across the passes from so far a region. And at the same time I felt deeply the weight laid upon my shoulders—how should I dare not be careful and circumspect, and toil at it with all my might?

A dozen days and more after Mr. Dai had hastened back from Hanoi to Chongqing—that is, in February 1939—the working comrades dispatched from various places to Hanoi were still but five or six; and though our task was limited to the two points, "to keep strict watch on Wang's movements, and by every means to reconnoiter the doings of the Wang faction," we still could not get room to work.

On the side of the Bureau headquarters at Chongqing, in order to support the smooth carrying-out of the Hanoi work, able comrades were still being drawn continually from various places and were, one after another, setting out on the road.

From what the newly arrived comrades relayed, we came to learn the substance of the so-called "Three Principles of Peace" put forward by the Japanese premier Konoe; and it differed greatly from those glib evasions of Wang's "Yan Telegram," which slid over the heavy matters and made light of them—anyone could see that this was, of course, a playing of tricks.

At the same time we read in full the address the Director-General Chiang made in answer to Konoe's statement, under the head "Exposing the Enemy State's Plot and Setting Forth the National Policy of Resistance." Its tone rang clear and sonorous, lucid to the very bottom; it not only enlightened the soldiers and people of all China, but laid bare, too, the whole ugly face and aggressive ambition of the Japanese warlords before the eyes of the world, with nowhere left to hide. It must be among the documents of greatest historical value in the Sino-Japanese war.

Appended after it, Mr. Chen Bulei gives a further account of how this address came to be composed—to show the Director-General's eye keen as a torch, and the firm, unshaken resolve of the resistance. When we had read Konoe's statement of the twenty-second, the Director-General Chiang's address of the twenty-sixth, and Wang's Yan Telegram of the twenty-ninth, the war party and the peace party stood as plainly apart as the Jing and the Wei, and there was no room for any misunderstanding, nor the least mystery in it. Yet men of a common political sensitiveness, and quibblers with the brush, must make much of it, so that all manner of far-fetched and fanciful tales arose; when in fact there was nothing at all of the unfathomable in it. On this head the writer will, so far as he knows, give a most careful and clear account within these pages.
