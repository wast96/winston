### Poem Riddles, at Your Service

Playing at poem-riddles is also called "drawing the word-slips." The thing is a gambling game of the literati, and of no recent date. In the late Qing, when we went up to Songjiang to sit the district boys' examination, the local gamblers would set out a stall of an evening along the streets and lanes and lure the young candidates into play; but the stakes in those days were small, one or two thousand copper cash changing hands at the very most.

Six or seven years ago, at the height of the poem-riddle craze in Shanghai, the stalls stood thick as fish-scales within every amusement hall, everywhere the eye fell, with one in every wall-corner and at every turning. What passed at them was, they said, only cigarettes and play-tickets; but under the counter one might stake hard money — silver dollars — as well. At the same time not a few people rented premises and set up so-called "×× Poetry Societies"; and others paid for advertisements and ran a notice in the papers reading "Poem-Riddles, at Your Service," which was gambling in the open and no mistake. Later the authorities suppressed the stalls in the amusement halls, and they were all wound up together; the rented societies too dwindled away one by one (only the Guanyun Poetry Society on Avenue Édouard VII is left today).

Go to such a place for amusement, and you have only to stand a moment by the stall for the attendants to fawn on you and press you to sit. Once you are seated, there is tea, there are cigarettes, there are sweets, all yours to eat and drink freely without paying a cent — and then, of course, you feel it too shabby to leave, and can only put down a stake. Those who play this game do best if they have studied the old poetry: they win the more readily by a lucky hit, whereas the man who cannot make a verse and yet plays the connoisseur, staking wildly, comes off in the end with small winnings and heavy losses. Yet even one who can make a verse cannot hit on every slip; for the stall-keepers all claim their poem-riddles have a pedigree and are "matched to the old text," and poetry is in truth as boundless as a sea of mist — however wide your reading, how are you to have read every line and have it all pat in your breast? Now and then you meet an unfamiliar line, and can only take a blind stab at it and try your luck.

Those who keep the poem-riddle stalls or societies are for the most part unable to make verses themselves; their word-slips must all be got up beforehand by hired men of letters who know poetry, at a brush-fee of a few dollars a hundred slips. At the height of the craze there were a good several dozen literati who lived by making poem-riddles. But what sort of thing is this poem-riddle, after all? Here are two, by way of example:

英才盡至▢, with the slips 曹, 齊, 陳, 秦, 韓 ranged beside it and the mark "two" hidden below; and ▢看白髮新, with the slips 重, 驚, 同, 儌, 羞 beside it and the mark "three" hidden below.

The two poem-riddles above are five-character lines (there are seven-character ones too, and alliterative ones). Take the first, 英才盡至▢: the last word is left blank, and any of the characters set beside it — 曹, 齊, 陳, 秦, 韓 — may be slotted in and read equally smoothly; but you must stake on 齊, the second, to have hit, for the little "two" printed under the slip is the hidden mark of the winning answer. Before the slip is drawn only the line above shows, the "two" beneath being covered over with a paper sleeve. The second riddle is on the same plan as the first, save that here you must stake on 同, the third, to win.

On a winning stake they pay one to three — that is, put down one dollar and, if you hit, you take three. Is this not a game, then, that only a man versed in the old poetry should attempt? The joke of it is that your common philistine who knows no poetry, and your illiterate country bumpkin, must needs lay claim to elegance too, and play the connoisseur at staking on the poem-riddles and drawing the slips — until their purses are turned inside out and their silver all lost, and the stall-keepers laugh at them besides for a first-class "big silver sucker."
