### Author's Preface

The condition of Shanghai society is, as the saying goes, a thing of five colours and eight patterns, of a thousand goblins and a hundred freaks: there is no marvel it does not hold. This book, for all that it lays bare no more than a single scale and half a claw, still shows the leopard by one of its spots, and that is enough to catch the notice and leave a reader the wiser. For the most part, when a young man first sets foot in the world, he is governed by his surroundings, and neither mind nor body is quite his own to command. Wanting the discernment to read men, taking his colour from whatever he is set beside, with no sure means of knowing what to choose, his very sense of right and wrong confounded amid the clamour of the crowd, he leaves the imps and the goblins their opening to work their arts; and those who are drawn down into the pit, never to haul themselves out again, are everywhere to be seen. Mr. Yu Muxia once compiled his *Collected Maxims*, whose one aim was to set men's hearts to rights. Back in the age of the warlords a copy was in every hand, the commanders would deal it out to their troops, and for a season it had a great vogue, so that its sequels ran at last to twenty volumes. Today it has been laid on the high shelf; but its purpose is unchanged, and so he has put together this book as well, that the talk over the dregs of the tea and the last of the wine might serve for a mirror down the ages. For the matters it takes up, trifling though they be, always carry their lesson of warning and correction; and in the lives of men who have made good, it dwells above all on the hardship of the building-up, the better to hearten the young and rid them of despondency and gloom, so that it is truly a medicine prescribed to the very ailment of the age and of the human heart. Set beside the storyteller's art, which raises castles in the air merely to divert, it is not to be spoken of in the same breath. Zhuangzi says: "Grand music does not enter the ears of the villager; but strike up 'Breaking the Willow' or 'The Bright Flowers,' and he will laugh aloud." It is just this principle that Mr. Yu has taken for his own, what the ancients called keeping one's aim low and forgoing lofty talk; and one may say for a certainty that it will take effect faster even than the *Collected Maxims* did.
