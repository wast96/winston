### The Evening Papers

In the countries of Europe and America, where the newspaper offices are highly developed, papers come out every day — a morning paper in the morning, a noon paper at noon, an evening paper at night — and whenever some urgent matter breaks they will print an extra on the spot besides, the number of extras being fixed by nothing at all. Our own country's press is still in its infancy, and of evening papers there are fewer still. Formerly there was only the one, Shen Zhuowu's China Evening News, which began early enough — some dozen years of history, by the reckoning. Since Shen Zhuowu drowned the year before last, this old-established evening paper has shut down and died a natural death.

After the "September Eighteenth" calamity befell the nation, the Shibao office was the first to bring out an evening extra. Once the "January Twenty-eighth" fighting broke out at Shanghai, the Shenbao, the Xinwenbao and the Shishi Xinbao all issued extras in turn. Then, when the Nineteenth Route Army fell back and the situation shifted, the extras of both the Shenbao and the Xinwenbao suddenly stopped, having run no more than a week, and later the Shishi Xinbao stopped its extras too. After Rehe was seized and Yuguan lay in peril, the Shenbao and the Xinwenbao brought out evening editions again; before long the Shenbao's evening paper was the first to stop once more, while the Xinwenbao's Xinwen Yebao goes on appearing as usual. The Shibao, after the Chenbao office brought out the Xin Yebao, gave up its extras and formally renamed its own the Yebao. All the evening papers named above are sidelines of the great dailies; as for evening papers issued on their own account, since the China Evening News closed there has been only the one, the Da Wanbao. That paper was got up by Zhang Zhuping during the fierce days of the Shanghai fighting; at first it brought out only a single quarto sheet a day, carried no advertisements and printed nothing but news, and only after half a year's hard struggle, having made a fair name, did it enlarge its columns and take advertising. Its circulation has now shot ahead, and in everyone's mind the Da Wanbao stands as the most famous evening paper in Shanghai.

Whenever the situation takes a violent turn, there is sure to be a pack of speculators who throw something together any old way, spread wild rumours, and print a sort of bean-curd-cake of a "flash" or "bulletin," which they hand off wholesale to the newsboys to cry through the streets. These speculators are one and all the proprietors of little print shops, out to scrape up a bit of extra cash. What is a scrap of print this small, set against a real evening paper or extra? It can only be called a silly handbill. When the situation quiets down, their flashes and bulletins vanish without a trace.

Of the various evening papers current now, besides the independent Da Wanbao, there are the Xinwenbao office's Xinwen Yebao and the Chenbao's Xin Yebao, two in all; the Yebao put out by the Shibao has now ceased. Lately there is also the Shehui Wanbao, run by Cai Diaotu, whose reporting lays stress on society news; and there is one more, the Da Mei Wanbao, put out by Americans.
