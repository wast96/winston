CHAPTER UNDER REVIEW

Chapter 8. Yan'an's First Great Counter-espionage Case: The Winning Policy of "Turning the Enemy to Serve Us"

For all the Communists' vigilance and all the energy they poured into counter-espionage, they still could not seal the Border Region against every Nationalist agent who wanted in.

In Yan'an itself, the seat of the Party Center, a case broke that shook the heavens. A whole crop of agents, graduates of Juntong's Hanzhong training class, had slipped into the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region, and other base areas, and burrowed into sensitive posts: the Military Commission's Second Bureau, the Joint Defense Headquarters, the Shaanxi Provincial Committee, the Security Office.

1. Juntong's Special Training Class for "Expendable Agents"

The case began at Qingyang, in Longdong.

Qingyang was a hub where four provinces met: north to Yinchuan in Ningxia, east to Yan'an in Shaanxi, west to Lanzhou in Gansu, south to Hanzhong in Sichuan. Longdong was a garrison and recruiting district for the Eighth Route Army, home to the 385th Brigade under Wang Weizhou, with Geng Biao as his deputy; Cai Chang's public title was chair of the Qingyang Committee to Guide the Anti-Japanese Mass Movement, but in truth she was the Communist county head. And yet the county also still held a Nationalist government and a Nationalist party branch inside its walls.

The two parties fought over power and over the people's loyalty, and the people of Qingyang, caught in the tug of war, had to learn to live in the cracks. Wu Nanshan was principal of the short-term free primary school at Fanjiamiao; the local peasant-association chairman and the *baojia* headman were both Communists, and they often had him draft their documents. When the Nationalist government stopped paying teachers' wages, it was Wu Nanshan who led the complaint. Late in 1939, as the friction between Nationalists and Communists in Longdong turned fierce, the Qingyang education director tagged him as a "Red" and refused him any posting. Left to fend for himself, Wu Nanshan set off for Chongqing to join a wartime cadre training corps that promised free schooling.

He kept the appointment and came to Hanzhong, where his contact Du Changcheng met him. He never reached Chongqing. Instead he was led to Xinglong Temple, in the countryside outside Hanzhong, and only once inside the gate did he grasp what the place was: not a school at all, but a Juntong training class for secret agents.

The schedule was punishing—classes morning, afternoon, and night, not an hour of the day one's own. The curriculum chilled the blood. There were political and social subjects: the Premier's Testament, the Director-General's words and deeds, international politics, "the Communist question," conditions in the Northwest, mass psychology. And there were the trade subjects: political detection, transport, marksmanship, demolition, communications, weapons, pharmacology, seizing-and-grappling, disguise, and the lore of the *haidi*.

Discipline was iron. "Enter alive, leave dead": once you were in the class you were Juntong, and you surrendered your personal freedom to the discipline of the body. "We are the leader's eyes and ears, we are the soul of the revolution." "Comrades are as hands and feet, the body is the family." "No marrying in wartime." The instructors openly declared their masters to be Germany's Hitler and Italy's Mussolini. "Slack off, and there is a man behind you with a whip; desert, and there is a man behind you to shoot you dead." Students were beaten on any pretext. Once, Wu Nanshan jiggled his leg and knocked the desk, and the instructor came straight over with a slap and two blows to the chest.

The class gave great weight to its lessons on the Communist Party; Juntong's "house style" was anti-Communism. The textbooks were written by the Communist turncoats Zhang Guotao and Ye Qing themselves, expounding such topics as "the difference between communism and the Three People's Principles," "whether communism suits China's conditions," and "the Chinese Communist Party today." The last lecture was a digest of Chiang Kai-shek's sayings: "Where there are Communists there is no me, where there is me there are no Communists." "The Communist Party is the Soviet Union's armed spy ring." "One woman enters the Border Region and becomes everybody's wife." Wu Nanshan had met Communists. They were nothing like this.

The class director, Cheng Yi, announced with pride: "We are Juntong's training class for expendable agents." Among themselves the students murmured: "Juntong and the Communists are mortal enemies. A Juntong man taken by the Japanese may not die for it, but a Juntong man taken by the Communists can forget about living."

This was a hell on earth. Wu Nanshan resolved to get out early: you deceive me, and I will deceive you.

He feigned a willing heart and studied hard, and even reported to his instructor that he had come precisely to work against the Communists, so if he went back now he could still say he had failed his entrance exams and returned—but any longer and the story would not hold. The director, Cheng Yi, saw the sense of it and let Wu Nanshan graduate early and return to Qingyang. Before he left he compiled his *haidi*: his own name, his other names, his cover names, his permanent and mailing addresses; the cover names of his intelligence targets, the cipher for their correspondence, and last of all the oath. He memorized it, and the original stayed at the Hanzhong class. All future secret contact would run on this *haidi*.

Wu Nanshan was the first of the fourth session to leave. He made contact at Juntong's Xi'an station to set up his communications, then went to Xifeng, in Longdong, to make contact with Zhang Mingzhe and place himself under his direction.

He had gone looking for a way to make a living, and stumbled instead into a dangerous nest of agents. Now, though he had escaped the tiger's jaws of the Nationalist secret service, would the Communists of Longdong take him in?

In the days Wu Nanshan had been away, Qingyang had turned upside down. The Nationalist county government and party branch had both been driven out. The Communists were in power, and the first thing they took in hand was education. No sooner had he returned than Wu Nanshan joined in founding the Longdong Middle School and doubled as deputy head of the student life brigade.

Under Nationalist rule he could not even keep a primary teacher's rice bowl; here under the Communists he had become a middle-school teacher. The Communists were holding democratic elections in the Border Region, and Wu Nanshan's fellow teacher Lu Weigong was elected Qingyang's first democratically chosen county head, with Wu Nanshan himself an invited delegate to the county people's council.

Seeing the Communists trust him so, Wu Nanshan resolved to make a clean breast of things, and went first to Lu Weigong, whom he knew well. The matter was grave; Lu Weigong told no one, and the next morning reported it straight to Ma Wenrui, the prefectural party secretary. Party rules required that intelligence and security work be held personally in the hands of the region's highest party official. By the time Ma Wenrui heard of it, the Border Security had just put forward the policy of "actively winning over every force that can possibly be used, to weaken the enemy's power to do us harm." Longdong had not yet taken up such intricate work, but Ma Wenrui knew where things stood: the Center was adjusting its policy, encouraging the building of intelligence assets inside the enemy.

That evening Ma Wenrui, Li Fushan, and Lu Weigong sat down together with Wu Nanshan. Ma Wenrui warmly encouraged him: "Turning from the dark to the light—very good." He offered Wu Nanshan work as well, and laid out three points plainly. "First: the enemy deceives you and lays traps for you; we Communists redeem you and protect you. Second: the enemy means to use this method to damage our Border Region, so we will use the enemy's own method, and keep up contact with the enemy in order to fight the enemy. Third: this must stay secret. You deal only with Li Fushan, take your orders directly from him, and have no dealings with anyone else." The highest Communist leader in Longdong had handed him the policy in person, and Wu Nanshan's heart settled.

Li Fushan then took Wu Nanshan to the security-section quarters, where he spent two days writing up his account. From then on, whenever Wu Nanshan came to make contact, he went in and out by the section's back gate. On Li Fushan's arrangement, Wu Nanshan carried on his secret-ink correspondence with Xi'an, and Zhang Linghan, disguised as a cloth peddler, carried it.

2. Casting the Line and Weaving the Net

Wu Nanshan became a secret field operative of the Longdong security section, his open identity still that of a middle-school teacher. In the spring of 1941, on the strength of his ability and to ease his field work, they promoted him to chief of Qingyang's Third Section—the education section.

Around April 1941, Zhao Cangbi, head of the Border Security's plainclothes squad, was ordered to Longdong "to lend a hand." That spring the Border Region had expelled the Nationalist regime; county governments multiplied, and to tighten control the Communists set up new sub-districts and planned to establish security sub-offices at the sub-district level. Zhao Cangbi had come to Longdong to relieve Li Fushan and take over as head of the Longdong security sub-office. But the Hanzhong-class case was so tangled that it kept Li Fushan tied down.

Zhao Cangbi, a man who studied detective work in earnest, did not take Wu Nanshan on trust at once. He set a trap to test this defector. He had someone copy out a piece of intelligence and hand it to Wu Nanshan, and at the same time posted the public-order chief, Tu Zhankui, to intercept the courier Zhang Linghan on the road and see whether he was carrying anything else. The check proved that Zhang Linghan carried only the item Zhao Cangbi had supplied; Wu Nanshan had slipped in nothing of his own. That settled it: Wu Nanshan was reliable.

The two of them, Li Fushan and Zhao Cangbi, ran the investigation together, and set Wu Nanshan three main lines of work. "Angling": using his ordinary contact with Xi'an to draw the enemy onto the hook. "Luring": using his open position to tempt agents to come to him. "Searching": using the fact that he had trained at Hanzhong to spot other agents.

Angling bore fruit fast. Zhao Cangbi had Wu Nanshan write to Xi'an in chemical ink, saying he had intelligence to report. Juntong sent a man named He Zhu, once a clerk at Qingyang's Jingshengtang pharmacy, to come and go on the pretext of selling medicine, bringing Wu Nanshan operating funds from time to time—all of which went to supplement Longdong's security budget. The intelligence Wu Nanshan handed over was harmless Border Region gossip, padded out with a little invention, all drafted in advance by the investigation chief Hao Su and signed off by Li Fushan. He Zhu's movements led them to another agent, Liu Zhicheng, who had by now become head of the Heshui county drama troupe.

Luring worked too. Juntong tried to build a cell in the Border Region and sent three Hanzhong graduates, Gao Wei among them, to Huan county. Gao Wei, unable to find a cover for himself, came to Qingyang looking for Wu Nanshan.

Searching often brought windfalls. Once, on business at the prefectural office, Wu Nanshan came face to face with a Hanzhong classmate, Zheng Chongyi, who was going by the cover name Chen Ming and working as a clerk in the secretariat.

Through Wu Nanshan, the Longdong security section came to know Juntong's whole underground network in Longdong, and headed off its mischief effectively. But lacking any experience of fighting Juntong's apparatus head-on, they were at a loss for how to press this "Hanzhong class" case any deeper.

Over in Xi'an, Cheng Muyi, chief of Juntong's Northwest Special Investigation Station, was himself scheming to push the Hanzhong class deeper.

The Hanzhong class was itself the fruit of Juntong's long planning. China had no academy for secret agents; both the Communists and the Nationalists trained theirs through special classes. Juntong's classes were founded in 1938 and called, for the outside world, "special police classes." Each took the name of its locale: the Ling class at Liling in Hunan, the Qian class at Qianyang in Guizhou, the Xi class at Xifeng in Guizhou, the Yu class at Chongqing in Sichuan, the Lan class at Lanzhou in Gansu, the Southeast class at Jian'ou in Fujian—and among them they graduated close to twenty thousand students. Every Juntong "special class" had Dai Li himself as its titular director, so every agent it turned out was Dai Li's disciple. Some were posted to the occupied zones to do anti-Japanese intelligence; some to the Nationalist areas, working under police or other open covers; some kept at Juntong's own headquarters. The most dangerous were sent to burrow into the Communist base areas, and these the trade called "expendable agents."

The Nationalist agent's chief task was anti-Communism, and so the classes took particular care to train a batch of men who could penetrate the Communist Party. Before the united front, Ma Zhichao had set up a "special police training class" inside the Xi'an police bureau and trained fifty or sixty men; a few of them, Xue Zhixiang among them, slipped into the Soviet areas, but they could not take root and soon withdrew. It was the Communist turncoat who found the way: Zhang Guotao entered Juntong and put a proposal to Dai Li. The Communists were recruiting the educated young in great numbers; if the Nationalists meant to get in, they should start by training young students. From then on Juntong made a point of taking young students into its classes.

How to get into Yan'an was the central problem these classes studied. Dai Li directed that every agent who had been to Yan'an write up a paper, "How to Get Into the Border Region," setting out exactly how he had gone, what to watch for, and how to penetrate. Among Juntong's core agents, those who had been to Yan'an included a certain Qin, Lai Guomin, and Shen Zhiyue. Shen Zhiyue had gone twice, had even joined the Communist Party at Kangda, and was riding high within Juntong.

The agents Juntong's Northwest Region sent to Yan'an were mostly graduates of the Lanzhou class. The Lan class had departments of intelligence, police administration, communications administration, foreign affairs, frontier work, and military affairs; it trained five sessions in all, over two thousand graduates, and its whole purpose was to penetrate the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region. Most of the students it chose had homes or relatives in the Border Region. Getting into the Border Region was not so hard for them, but they could not bring radios in, and building organizations or doing sabotage was harder still. The Yulin station's students, once inside Yan'an, were all caught and openly sent back to the Nationalist areas, to Dai Li's great embarrassment. What Dai Li did not know was the other reason the Lan class kept failing: the Border Security's own man, Mao Peichun, had long since penetrated the Lan class as an inside source, and those classmates had been exposed from the start.

Only with the Hanzhong class did Juntong's agents truly break into the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region.

In April 1938, just as the Juntong bureau was founded, Dai Li directed Cheng Muyi, the Shanghai action-group chief, to set up a "special reconnaissance group" aimed solely at the Communist base areas. Cheng Muyi had always worked against the Communists; he had once penetrated a Jiangsu district committee in Shanghai and wrecked it. Taking on this major assignment, he first ran a "special class" at his home town of Wenzhou, then in September 1939 moved it to Hanzhong, nearer the Border Region, running it in the suburb of Chenjiaying under the name "Tianshui Field Headquarters Guerrilla Cadre Training Class." Within the trade it was called the "Hanzhong class."

The Hanzhong class was an "expendable agent" class, and its staff was strong. By Juntong custom the director was Dai Li himself; the actual work fell to the deputy director, Cheng Muyi, under the cover name Cheng Yi. When Cheng Muyi was away, it fell to Shen Zhiyue (cover name Li Guodong), head of the political guidance office. The political instructor Zhu Zengfu (Zhu Guocai) had penetrated Yan'an in June 1938 and taught "The Communist Question." The trade-skills instructor Du Changcheng, a Suiyuan man, had trained in both the first Lan-class session and the first Hanzhong session, and later became one of Juntong's demolition experts; in 1948 he was made major-general commander of the special-skills corps, in charge of the sabotage during the retreat from Nanjing, Shanghai, Chongqing, and other great cities, and he once directed the blowing-up of Guangzhou's Pearl River bridge. The military instructor Wang Shaowen, a star pupil of the Lan class, ran marksmanship and other military drill. The Hanzhong class even grew its own talent: Li De of the second session, Qi Xixian of the fourth, and Li Changsheng of the sixth all rose to instructor after graduating.

Within Juntong the Hanzhong class was not famous, not like the Linli or Lanzhou classes, but at penetrating the Border Region it was the most successful of all. Cheng Muyi took care to enroll not only educated youths but local Border Region people. Most of the Hanzhong students came from a few middle schools around Pingliang and Yulin; after training it was easy for them to sit the entrance exams at Yan'an's schools as progressive young people, and from there be assigned by the organization into Communist bodies.

Dai Li set great store by the Hanzhong class. In the autumn of 1940 he made a special trip from Chongqing to Hanzhong to address it. He urged them on: "The students of the Hanzhong class are all special talents; you must all make special contributions. In time you will produce the nation's finance minister, communications minister, interior minister, and foreign minister." He gave them their task: "Pull the masses back from the Communists' hands, and pull the traitors back from the Japanese." He set his demands: "You must all be nameless heroes, the soul of the party, the eyes and ears of the leader. Believe in the leader to the point of superstition, obey the leader to the point of blindness. You must all be the Generalissimo's loyal guards—first, want no father or mother; second, want no wife or children; and every day remember one man alone, and that is Generalissimo Chiang. Live for Generalissimo Chiang and die for Generalissimo Chiang, live so the heavens shake and die a hero's death." Beyond the moral rousing, Dai Li had cash rewards too: 200 yuan for Cheng Muyi, and 20 to 50 yuan for each of the rest.

This so-called class for "expendable agents" was, one might say, Juntong's "favored sons of heaven."

By 1941 Cheng Muyi's agents had already seeped into the Communist-led Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia, Jin-Cha-Ji, Jin-Ji-Lu-Yu, Yu-Wan-Su, E-Yu-Wan, Ji-Lu, and Lu-Su Border Regions, and into the Jiangxi and Zhejiang base areas as well. Dai Li directed that this fruitful "special reconnaissance group" be expanded into the "Northwest Special Reconnaissance Station," with eighteen special-investigation groups under it, at Yan'an, Huan county, Fugu, Hancheng, Changzhi-Yichuan, Wutai, Xinxiang, Huangchuan, Mengcheng, Sixian, Jimo, Dingtao, Pingyang, Lishui, Xiaofeng, Yancheng, Lishui, and northern Jiangxi—plus a Southeast sub-station set up in Zhejiang. Its main task was to penetrate the sensitive posts and lie buried for the long term.

Getting into the "bandit areas" had always been Juntong's hard problem, and now that the Hanzhong class had succeeded, Dai Li took it very seriously. At a Juntong meeting, just as Cheng Muyi was about to claim the credit, Dai Li cut him off: not at the meeting. Afterward Dai Li briefed Cheng Muyi in person—the Yan'an group must produce something spectacular within a year or two.

Dai Li's favor spurred Cheng Muyi on, and he set his mind on making something of himself against the Party Center in Yan'an. But the Hanzhong students, once inside the Border Region, scattered to different places, lost touch with the Northwest Station, and could not act as a body. Cheng Muyi convened a special meeting in Xi'an to work out how to link up with his agents lying low in Yan'an.

Cheng Muyi decided to send Zhao Xiu as chief liaison for Yan'an, with Qi Xixian, Li Changsheng, and Wang Jiwu as liaisons for the first, second, and third groups, connecting the agents in Yan'an's various offices. He sent Zhu Zengfu to Yulin, to use his ties with the local Nationalist garrison and, under cover of the 22nd Army's wagon train, travel between Yulin and Xi'an and pass through Yan'an on the way. He sent Zhang Linqing to open the line between Luochuan and Yan'an. He sent Feng Xiaoquan to Yan'an and Han Zizhong to Qingjian to set up secret contact points under the cover of running shops.

After the meeting Cheng Muyi brought out all the *haidi* he had so carefully kept, so each liaison could learn the situation of the agents lying in wait. The four liaisons were instructors, yet even they had not known how many Hanzhong men had gone into Yan'an, and the *haidi* startled them.

3. A Case to Shake the Heavens!

In October 1941, as the Border Region government convened a meeting of the county education chiefs, Wu Nanshan ran into an acquaintance on the road—his Hanzhong classmate Qi Sanyi.

The Longdong security section judged that Qi Sanyi might be on an important mission, and that for now it would not do to startle the snake in the grass. They decided that Wu Nanshan would keep Qi Sanyi under inside surveillance and Tu Zhankui under outside surveillance, to learn what his errand was. But twenty-odd days passed with no new move from Qi Sanyi, only his repeated pressing of Wu Nanshan for when he could arrange the papers to go to Yan'an, insisting that any more delay would ruin things.

Qi Sanyi's eagerness to get to Yan'an and make contact—did it mean a whole nest of agents lay hidden there? The Longdong security section reported it at once to the Border Region Security Office. Bu Lu, the Border Security's security-work chief, hurried to Longdong and joined the sub-office in cracking the case.

Under swift interrogation Qi Sanyi confessed: the Northwest Special Reconnaissance Station wanted the agents scattered through Yan'an's various units linked up for action, and because he had been a demolition instructor and knew the students of every session, he had been sent as their liaison. And Zhao Xiu, he said, had also been sent to Yan'an as chief liaison.

Bu Lu saw at once what it meant: at the Hanzhong class this fellow had been known as the "king of demolition"—going to Yan'an, he might well be there to carry out sabotage.

The Longdong case sent a shock through Yan'an. Until now the Nationalist secret service in Yan'an had mostly worked from open government cover, which made it easy to keep under watch and had raised no great problem. This Juntong case was of another kind entirely. These agents had penetrated in secret, so the Border Security could hardly track their movements; they were mostly local men, easy to hide behind their covers; and they lay in wait in batches, quite capable of forming a large organization. And now a "king of demolition" had come to link them up—one could not help but suspect the Nationalists meant to work some great sabotage in Yan'an.

Zhou Xing at once had Yi Li, of the Border Security secretariat, pull together the related threads. Earlier the Border Security had turned up a batch of suspects at the Suide Young Cadres School: Tao Hua, Guo Jiwu, Li Fengbi, Yang Zhichang, Yang Chengzhang, Li Feng, six in all, whose real identities could not then be pinned down, and who had simply been scattered to different posts. After Yang Zhichang and Yang Chengzhang reached Mizhi, a single trip to the Nationalist 86th Division would bring them back with funds, and they had ties to the Renaissance Society men in the county government. Li Fengbi and Li Feng were assigned to Wayaobao; when nothing came up they moved on to Yan'an, and Li Fengbi even got into the Security Office. Tao Hua and Guo Jiwu turned up clean at Gulin and Fu county, and Tao Hua was even brought in as a member of the security work-net. In 1940 the Border Security found a Nationalist secret-communications *haidi* sewn into the quilt of Chen Ming (Zheng Chongwen); Chen Ming said he was a man of the Nationalist party branch, and the matter was let slide, so that Chen Ming later worked his way into the Longdong prefectural office. In 1941 the Guanzhong sub-district turned up a lead as well: Zhu Langzhou reported that Feng Pingbo and others were agents, and Feng Pingbo in turn confessed that Zhu Langzhou, Jin Guang, and others were Hanzhong graduates—but at the time it was not taken seriously enough, and none of it was pressed home.

The short of it was that, before the case broke, the Border Security had badly underrated the secret work of the Nationalist agents.

Wu Nanshan's voluntary confession and Qi Sanyi's arrest laid bare the scale and the intent of the Hanzhong class lying in wait, and this could not but command the closest attention. Zhou Xing, head of the Border Security, put the security-work chief Bu Lu, the deputy chief Wang Fan, Li Qiming, and others on cracking the case with all their strength, and himself went to Zaoyuan to report to the Central Social Affairs Department.

The Social Affairs Department leadership took the case very seriously. The minister, Kang Sheng, judged it a rather important one. The Nationalists, he said, had made very good use of Communist turncoats. Ren Zhuoxuan, or Ye Qing, had behaved well the first time he was arrested; the firing squad failed to kill him, he escaped, and went back to the revolution. But arrested a second time, he was talked into turning, and rose all the way to Nationalist minister of propaganda. Now we too must win men over, and make the Nationalists' agents serve us.

In this period the Social Affairs Department was mounting a great campaign against the Nationalist agents. The Longdong case did not stand alone. In January 1941 the Nationalists had openly encircled and annihilated the Communist-led New Fourth Army in the south, touching off the second anti-Communist upsurge, and Nationalist agent activity had escalated with it; other base areas were reporting the same intensification. The Social Affairs Department had obtained intelligence that Zhongtong was running a "high-grade special-intelligence blitz competition," which it judged "worthy of the whole Party's grave attention and high vigilance." It also laid down clear policy: as for handling internal traitors, the incorrigible were to be firmly suppressed; the wavering and deceived were to be met with one blow and one pull, encouraged to turn back to the good; those who bitterly repented and wished to serve us in earnest were to be won over by swift, secret persuasion; those exploiting the moment to penetrate deeper were to be schooled harder and put on their guard; those already talked over by the enemy were to be given a last effort at persuasion to repent; and those released after a secret enemy arrest were to be quietly investigated, watched, and handled as the case warranted.

The case the Border Security had reported turned up agents in batches not only in Longdong but with still more leads in Yan'an. The Social Affairs Department's Second Office handled security work; its chief Wang Jinxiang and public-order chief Chen Long guided the case actively, and drew in Ye Yungao and Wang Jun of Northwest College's "Old Third Class" to help the Border Security.

This "Old Third Class" was the class the Social Affairs Department had long run at Northwest College to train intelligence and security cadres; many cadres of both the Social Affairs Department and the Border Security had passed through it. Ye Yungao was a Jiangxi Red Army man, and before enrolling had been chief of the Border Security's Third Section, the interrogation section. Wang Jun had joined a plainclothes squad in central Hebei in 1937 and edited the central Hebei *Masses News*; sent up to Yan'an to study, he had worked on Kangda's security committee, and was then drawn by the Social Affairs Department into the Northwest College course.

By now Bu Lu, with Qi Sanyi's help, had spotted and arrested six or seven agents. Ye Yungao and Wang Jun interrogated those already found a second time, combed through the whole thing again, and concluded there was a great deal more to the case. The Hanzhong class had trained several sessions; there had to be more than these few.

Zhou Xing and Bu Lu, with Ye Yungao and Wang Jun, went again to report to the Social Affairs Department.

Kang Sheng and Li Kenong gave their directions on the investigation. Li Kenong judged the case one of real reach, a great case. The Hanzhong curriculum had not only intelligence but a sabotage side (demolition, assassination, poisoning, guerrilla work, photography, tailing, secret writing, and more), and if it was not cracked in time, the harm to the base areas would be great.

Li Kenong set out three principles. First: weigh evidence, not confession, and use no torture. Fail to hold the line on this, he said, and false words would bring trouble on yourself and wrong the innocent. Second: investigation and interrogation must both fight for the mind, resting on ideological work. Some Nationalist agents were themselves victims—hot-blooded young men who had thrown themselves into the resistance, blundered into the enemy's camp, and were not true reactionaries; schooled, most could be turned around. Third: investigation and interrogation must go together, and inside sources, outside sources, and counter-use must go together too, each supporting and spurring the other, none minding only its own patch. Li Kenong stressed it: every thread now found must be gripped tight and not let slack; the thing must be pursued to the bottom, the whole net drawn in, and no trouble left to fester.

With their superiors' directions, those working the case felt sure of their footing. Yan'an's security cadres had had few chances to match wits with Juntong agents directly, and how to "turn the enemy to serve us" was a delicate matter of policy; now that the leadership had spoken, in principle and in detail both, everyone went to work boldly.

4. Tracking the Agents' Couriers

The "Hanzhong class" case was ranked among Yan'an's priority cases. Because these were agents of Dai Li's Juntong system, it was called for short the "Dai case."

Cracking the "Dai case" had one great advantage: Qi Sanyi, the inside source, through whom agents could be identified with real ease. Bu Lu handled Qi Sanyi personally, going over the policy with him again and again. Seeing the Communists' high security organs place such trust in him, Qi Sanyi worked hard at it.

Bu Lu placed Qi Sanyi at the primary school near the New Market, one of the liveliest spots in Yan'an, where every sort of person was bound to show his face. Roaming the market day after day, Qi Sanyi came face to face with Liu Yiqing (Liu Zhiping) of the Hanzhong class. Liu Yiqing told him that Ma Ming (Ma Ruying), Zhao Binglian (Zhao Hanmin), and others had already graduated from Kangda and been posted to the 385th Brigade. As the New Year approached, Qi Sanyi found, at the Tianshengheng dumpling shop in the New Market, a bookkeeper who turned out to be another liaison, Yang Chao. The two liaisons put their heads together, and the agents in Yang Chao's hands were duly reported through to Qi Sanyi—turning up Fan Jinzhong and Tao Qihua, who had penetrated the Administrative College; Yang Zhichang, who had penetrated the Trade Bureau; and Liu Jialing, who had penetrated the bank's vault.

Before long another liaison, Li Chunmao, surfaced. Li Chunmao had gone first to Kangda in Yan'an, and among his fellow students were the Hanzhong men Ma Ming, Zhang Bingjun, Zhao Xihu, and Liu Yiping. When they fell to talking idly, every one of them had a tangled heart. Ma Ming brooded in silence: he had lain hidden in Yan'an for months, and not one of those blue-faced, fanged Communists the Hanzhong class had described was anywhere to be seen. Liu Yiping could not hold back his tears—he had been doing fine as a policeman in Baoji, and now, playing the agent, he had lost his fifty yuan a month; who would keep his old mother? Zhao Xihu said in anger: the Hanzhong class calls the Communists traitors, when it is the Communists and the Eighth Route Army who are the real, genuine article in resisting Japan. One of them even composed a poem: "A hero's spirit soaring past the clouds, / with a bold and ardent heart I came to Yan'an; / only now do I see my resolve was cheated, / I set out to paint a tiger and made a cur. / The higher I climbed, the louder I fell— / and now, repenting, I ask my heart: for what?" Li Chunmao resolved to break with the agents' organization, yet dared not go over to the Communists either, and so decided to have no more to do with politics, and to spend the rest of his life a doctor under a hidden name.

Through his dealings with Li Chunmao, Qi Sanyi further turned up Wang Xingwen in the fine-arts department of the Lu Xun Academy of Arts, and Zhang Zhigang at the Ansai arsenal. Chen Ming, the man swiftly interrogated in Longdong, had come to Yan'an and made contact with Li Chunmao too. And Li Fengbi, whom Wu Nanshan had recognized in Longdong, once back in Yan'an was persuaded by the Border Security to give himself up, and gave up Yang Chengzhang in Suide.

By now the three liaisons the Northwest Station had sent out, Qi Sanyi, Yang Chao, and Li Chunmao, were all within the Border Security's field of watch. Through these three, another batch of hidden agents was brought under control.

The next step was gathering evidence. Qi Sanyi, using his post as deputy leader of the Yan'an group, asked each hidden agent for a written report. Li Chunmao turned in two, Liu Yiqing two, Zhang Zhigang one; only Yang Chao would not write, seeming to have his doubts about Qi Sanyi. Before long Zhu Guocai passed through Yan'an and handed Yang Chao a written order to circulate to Li Chunmao and Qi Sanyi. And so all these papers became hard evidence in the Border Security's hands.

The Border Security meant to meet and size up these men directly. Li Qiming had Qi Sanyi arrange, on the pretext of a mahjong game, to sit down with Li Chunmao and Zhang Bingjun. After watching a while, the Border Security judged Li Chunmao a man who could also be won over.

Li Chunmao was called to the General Political Department's Anti-Traitor Department, and before the investigation expert Qian Yimin he had no choice but to confess he was a Nationalist Juntong agent. To his surprise he was treated well; the department head, Ouyang Yi, even had him to dinner at his home. The Anti-Traitor Department placed Li Chunmao in the secretariat of the Military Commission's Health Department, to go on aiding the Border Security's work on the case.

Of the three liaisons Cheng Muyi had sent to Yan'an, the two, Qi Sanyi and Li Chunmao, had now come over; that left Yang Chao. His thinking was reactionary, and the Security Office decided not to move on him for the moment, but to use his connections to keep turning up other hidden agents. But Yang Chao was cunning too; he announced he was going back to Xi'an for instructions, and never returned.

Of the three liaisons, two won over and one fled, only the chief liaison Zhao Xiu had yet to show himself. Bu Lu and Li Qiming were beside themselves: without landing this big fish, they could not draw in the net.

At the May Day celebration in 1942, all quarters of Yan'an gathered on the Nanguan parade ground. Li Chunmao, on duty at the rally, suddenly caught sight of Zhao Xiu. There he was in a blue woolen uniform, a Western walking stick in hand, filing into the ground with a party of men from the Nationalist offices posted in Yan'an. Zhao Xiu, it turned out, had gone from Xi'an first to Yulin, and through his ties to Deng Baoshan had landed the post of chief of an "air-defense watch post" in Yan'an.

By now every liaison the Northwest Special Reconnaissance Station had sent to Yan'an had fallen into the Border Security's net, and three of them, Qi Sanyi, Li Chunmao, and Wang Xingwen, had been successfully turned. The Border Security now set its mind on winning the chief liaison too.

How to storm this last stronghold without letting the thing leak out? Bu Lu designed it with care, marking out a roundabout path. He secretly arrested Qi Sanyi, Li Chunmao, and Wang Xingwen, then set Wang Xingwen to persuade Li Chunmao to give himself up, and then the two of them, Wang Xingwen and Li Chunmao, to persuade Qi Sanyi. This time the persuasion failed—Qi Sanyi was still a staunch Juntong man. So for the third step Wang Xingwen and Li Chunmao were sent to persuade Zhao Xiu to surrender; Zhao Xiu refused, and was taken to the Security Office, where Bu Lu and Li Qiming worked him over. Under the assault of policy on his mind, Zhao Xiu owned up to his chief-liaison identity and declared himself willing to turn over a new leaf and work for the Communists. After that Wang Xingwen and Li Chunmao went again to persuade Qi Sanyi, and only now did Qi Sanyi consent.

The order was exactly reversed. Qi Sanyi should have surrendered first, then in turn Wang Xingwen, Li Chunmao, and Zhao Xiu; doing it backward was to shield the most reliable man, Qi Sanyi. Should anyone waver, Qi Sanyi's standing as deputy group leader could still be preserved, to go on drawing the Northwest Station onto the hook.

By now every core man of the Northwest Special Reconnaissance Station's Yan'an group was in the Border Security's hands—while in Xi'an, Cheng Muyi, none the wiser, went on sending along operating funds.

5. Digging Out the Independent Cell

Making use of the liaisons could not identify every hidden agent. In the course of interrogation, Ye Yungao and Wang Jun, sent by the Social Affairs Department, found that Li Chunmao had once come across a man surnamed Hu, who was said to be running a cell hidden somewhere. Bu Lu had Qi Sanyi sound Zhao Xiu out; but Zhao Xiu only said: "That is other work. It's no concern of yours."

Beyond Qi Sanyi's threads, there might be another line held directly by Cheng Yi. This cell of the man surnamed Hu stood outside the liaisons' reach, and very likely lay hidden deeper still.

Since the man surnamed Hu was of student stock, he had most likely worked his way into Yan'an by sitting entrance exams. So the investigation began with the schools. They combed Kangda, Shaanbei College, the Women's University, the youth-training class, and the Administrative College, and nowhere was there this man surnamed Hu; then Wang Jun shifted his focus back to Kangda, and on the roster of the drill section was a "Hu Yaonan"—and the name stirred Wang Jun's memory at once: his own classmate at Kangda.

Wang Jun had been transferred from the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region to Yan'an and had studied in the ninth company of Kangda's second battalion, and among his classmates the three, Hu Yaonan, Yang Xiaowei, and Xia Bingshu, had come together. Wang Jun was then only a secret party member of the study group, serving as security-team leader, with a few net-members under him. At the time Hu Yaonan and the other two were honest and uncomplaining, never talked out of turn, and were the first to pitch in at any task, leaving Wang Jun a good impression. Looking back now, that eagerness of theirs seemed a sham.

Wang Jun checked their graduation postings, but could not find the three; he went to the General Political Department's organization section, and still could not find them. Racking his brains, he thought of a Kangda net-member, Yang Yu, and finally ran Yang Yu down at the military justice office; through a fellow Gansu townsman he learned at last that Hu Yaonan was working in the Military Commission's Second Bureau, had already become a branch secretary, and was right there at the radio station in Ansai.

The Military Commission's Second Bureau was the intelligence organ of the CCP Central Military Commission—and a Juntong agent had penetrated it.

The Social Affairs Department ranked the case a major special case. Bu Lu set up surveillance at once, and soon found that all three, Hu Yaonan, Yang Xiaowei, and Xia Bingshu, were lying low in the Second Bureau. Before long they intercepted a report Hu Yaonan had written in rice-water invisible ink.

To this cell that had penetrated the Communists' core intelligence organ, Cheng Muyi attached great weight, keeping in touch with it directly on a single line, not handing it to Zhao Xiu and Qi Sanyi of the Yan'an group. The cell went under the code name "Nanweishu," and had already sent ten batches of intelligence to Xi'an by secret writing, covering social conditions in Yan'an, the organization and teaching at Kangda, and the structure and tasks of the Second Bureau.

This was the most deeply hidden and most dangerous cell among the Hanzhong agents, and the Social Affairs Department ordered the arrests at once. After schooling, the three—Hu Shiyuan (Hu Siyuan, Hu Yaonan, Hu Youlian), Yang Xiaowei (Yang Zicai), and Xia Bingshu (Xia Zhenqing)—confessed fairly honestly, and the Social Affairs Department made a point of sending them to Northwest College for training, meaning to use them in intelligence work.

Investigation everywhere showed that the Hanzhong agents had already penetrated the Border Region in great numbers. Though they were not yet all in touch with one another, these scattered agents had already sent back a good deal of intelligence to Juntong by secret writing; and among them many had trained in assassination, poisoning, demolition, and other sabotage, and might act at any moment. This Nationalist agent network had come to pose a vast threat to the Border Region's security.

6. Turning Enemy Agents to Our Use

By May 1942 the Border Security had in the main mapped where the "Dai case" agents lay hidden in the Border Region: Hu Shiyuan's cell in the Military Commission's Second Bureau; Wang Zhihe in the Joint Defense Headquarters; Feng Shanshu and Zhu Langzhou in the Shaanxi Provincial Committee; Guo Liqun and Li Fengbi in the Border Security; Shi Jinzhong and Gong Zhaofeng in the Suide prefectural office; Chen Ming in the Longdong prefectural office.

Juntong agents had penetrated a great many of Yan'an's sensitive departments.

The Social Affairs Department and the Security Office decided to make the arrests.

In May they took Fan Jinzhong, Yang Zhichang, Tao Hua, Guo Jiwu, Liu Jialing, Liu Yiqing (Liu Zhiping), Zhang Bingjun (Zhang Zhigang), Li Chunmao (Li Changsheng, Li Jun), Wang Xingwen (Wang Jiwu), and others. Interrogation turned up Wang Zijie in Suide. In June, Suide arrested Wang Zijie, and after he was persuaded he gave up Wu Yamin, Wang Huanzhang, Han Ziqi, and others. In July, Guanzhong arrested Zhu Langzhou and Jin Guang, whom Feng Pingzhou had earlier given up. In September, the anti-traitor section of the 385th Brigade stationed in Longdong arrested Zhao Binglian and Ma Ming (Ma Ruying), who were serving as culture instructors. In October the Social Affairs Department arrested the three at the Ansai radio station, Hu Shiyuan, Yang Zicai, and Xia Zhenqing; Hu Shiyuan gave up Lai Peng (Lai Dongyuan), who had penetrated Yan'an University, whom the Border Security promptly arrested. At the Ansai factory they found Gou Zhensheng; at the Yichuan primary school, Wang Jintang; and there were Wang Shu, Yang Yintang, and others besides.

By the end of 1942 the Hanzhong training-class case was cracked entire, with 32 hidden Juntong agents uncovered in all: 1 who came forward on his own, 20 found by investigation, and 11 given up by those arrested, along with 7 pieces of physical evidence.

To net so many agents in a single case was a vast victory for Yan'an's counter-espionage work.

Mao Zedong praised the cracking of the case in person. Hearing of Bu Lu's ability, Mao Zedong said: "We need men like Bu Lu. Ten or so of them would do us fine."

The cracking of the Juntong Hanzhong-class case removed a great hidden danger for Yan'an. Yet for all this brilliant success, the Border Security kept the news as quiet as it could—for it meant to press the victory further still.

Wu Nanshan, Qi Sanyi, Li Chunmao, Wang Xingwen, Zhao Xiu, Zhang Zhigang, and others were all taken on as field cadres of the Border Security, to keep up their contact with Juntong.

But a secret is hard to keep.

Li Fengbi under arrest had given up Yang Chengzhang, and Wang Zijie under arrest had given up Wang Huanzhang; but all three, Yang Chengzhang, Wang Huanzhang, and Zhang Zhijun, got word and fled before they could be taken. Before long Li Fengbi, sent by the Border Security to work in Guanzhong, seized his chance and fled too. And earlier there was Yang Chao, who had left on the pretext of reporting to Xi'an and never come back. Five men were now out of control, one after another; Cheng Muyi might well already know his Yan'an group had been broken.

The Border Security did not give up for that.

The arrests had all been made in secret, and the turning and persuading had gone by a great roundabout path, so the five who fled did not know the truth of how the others had come over. And so Cheng Muyi, for all he sensed something, might well not know how deep the damage went.

So the Border Security scattered these men and went on using them.

Zhao Xiu stayed on in Yan'an as chief of the air-defense watch post, keeping secret touch with Luochuan and Xi'an. Wang Xingwen and Zhang Zhigang went to Suide, to make contact by letter with Yulin. Li Chunmao went to Fu county, to find a way to link with Luochuan. Qi Sanyi went on teaching at the New Market primary school, keeping that secret contact point alive. Wu Nanshan stayed in Longdong, still luring the enemy onto the hook.

This lot of Juntong agents had become important chess pieces in the hands of both parties. Who could put them to living use?

The high stroke in the way the Communist security organs cracked this case lay in "counter-use."

"Counter-use," also called "turning the use," is close in meaning to Sun Tzu's "turned spy": to take the spy the enemy sends and, in reverse, have him reconnoiter the enemy's own intelligence for us. The Yan'an phrase for it was "turning the enemy to serve us."

The case was uncovered through the voluntary confession of Wu Nanshan, a Nationalist Juntong agent; it was developed chiefly through Qi Sanyi, Li Chunmao, and others won over after capture; and its result was the turning of a great many of the arrested into intelligence assets for the Communists. From which one may say that the brilliance in the way the Communist intelligence and security organs cracked this case lay precisely in this "counter-use."

Whether you call it "counter-use," "turning the use," or the "turned spy," this business of "turning the enemy to serve us" was a thing dangerous in the extreme and hard in the extreme.

Five great problems loomed before them.

How to dare take the political risk and give men a free hand? Cast the mind back to the purges in the Soviet areas: the Nationalists had sent two men posing as Reorganizationists to the E-Yu-Wan base to turn Xu Jishen, and Xu Jishen had handed them to the security organs for disposal—yet he himself remained under suspicion, and was even put to death for it. Now, to counter-use Juntong agents, more dangerous still, could a security cadre help fearing it would fix a political label on him?

How to win a trained spy's surrender and test his loyalty? Look at the agents who had penetrated Yan'an before: the Hanzhong instructors Shen Zhiyue, Zhu Guocai, and the like were all staunch anti-Communists. The students they had trained, now surrendering—might this not be a performance of the self-injury ruse?

How to draw out the counter-used man's zeal and protect his safety? The Nationalists in power held far more power and property; the ease and pleasure were not on our side, the high office and fat salary were on the enemy's. What advantage in resources did the Communists have?

How to widen the intelligence victory through a deft counter-use? The Nationalist secret service had considerable experience in counter-surveillance; how to slip past its eyes and ears and lure it onto the hook?

How to arrange the counter-used man's political standing? The Communists ran a strict system of cadre vetting; a man who had once been a Nationalist agent—could he join the revolution, could he be a cadre, could he join the Party?

On June 10, 1942, at the Youth Canteen in Wenhuagou, outside Yan'an's north gate: this eatery, which had made something of a name in Yan'an, was that day booked out entire, no outside guests admitted.

The men walking into the banquet hall proved to be Bu Lu, Wang Fan, and Li Qiming of the Border Security, with an impressive party of guests beside them: Qian Yimin, Zhang Ming, and Peng You of the General Political Department's Anti-Traitor Department. The hosts were all Yan'an's security chiefs—and the guests of honor?

The guests filing into the hall were each of them ill at ease: Qi Sanyi, Li Chunmao, Wang Xingwen, Zhao Xiu, Zhang Bingjun—every one a former Nationalist Juntong agent.

Bu Lu raised his cup to toast the five men joining the revolution. Because these five had played an important part in cracking the case, the Border Security had decided to take them on for its field work.

The next day, Zhou Xing, deputy commander of the Yan'an garrison and head of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office, received the five in person. The honor of it exceeded any ceremony the Security Office had ever given new recruits.

Before long a man came from Xi'an raising doubts about Qi Sanyi. It made the Border Security think it might have been Li Fengbi who had passed on word of Qi Sanyi's arrest. Clearly, if it came to it, Qi Sanyi would have to be exposed to preserve the others. The Border Security had Zhao Xiu write to Cheng Muyi, claiming that he too had his doubts about Qi Sanyi. But Xi'an was slow to write back.

In November 1942, Zhang Zhigang fled again. This man had been at the Border Security's banquet welcoming the five over—and now the secret was hard to keep.

Bu Lu had another idea. He had Zhao Xiu write in a report that an agent had surrendered in Suide, meaning to muddle Cheng Muyi's mind and make him suspect that Zhang Zhigang's flight was something the Border Security had deliberately staged.

And sure enough, Wang Zhiding, the acting chief of the Northwest Station, hearing the two reports and unable to tell true from false, held Zhang Zhigang under investigation.

Yan'an went on fishing. Before long Xi'an wrote back, recalling the two of them, Zhao Xiu and Qi Sanyi, to Xi'an.

Qi Sanyi would not go. He had set his heart on staying in Yan'an; why go back to Xi'an? Zhao Xiu wanted to go, but the Border Security would not let him. This man was not so reliable as Qi Sanyi; they could not loose a tiger back to the mountain.

Recalling men to Xi'an was itself Wang Zhiding's device. Seeing his two group leaders, Zhao Xiu and Qi Sanyi, slow to return, Wang Zhiding cut off contact with Yan'an. From then on Zhao Xiu lived in dread. His family was at Hui county in the Nationalist area—would they suffer for it? By day he ate, drank, whored, and gambled; by night he wept in secret. Uneasy, Bu Lu put him under house arrest for a week, and Zhao Xiu only turned the more listless, muttering something about "getting through one day at a time." In the spring of 1943, after a stretch of testing, the Border Security sent Zhao Xiu to work in Nationalist Yulin—and he seized the chance to run back to Xi'an.

To cover himself, Zhao Xiu told the station chief, Wang Zhiding, that the moment he reached Yan'an he had been ringed by Communist agents, and only his open cover had saved him, for the Communists dared not wreck the united front and could not lay hands on him. Zhao Xiu heaped the blame for the Yan'an group's ruin all on others: "Qi Sanyi and Li Chunmao are Communists who penetrated the Hanzhong class. Once those two reached Yan'an, every man Juntong sent there was finished." Zhao Xiu confirmed the case against Zhang Zhigang, and Wang Zhiding reported it to Chongqing at once.

Dai Li interrogated Zhao Xiu himself, and learned that the Hanzhong agents who had slipped into Yan'an had been netted whole by the Communists. The Hanzhong-class textbooks called the Communists a "traitor party" at every turn, and the Communist base areas "bandit areas" at every turn—which laid the Nationalists' anti-Communist inner workings bare for all to see. How were the Nationalists to explain their "cooperation" now?

Dai Li had no choice but to take drastic measures. He dissolved Juntong's Xi'an Special Reconnaissance Station and stripped station chief Cheng Muyi and deputy chief Lin Jizhi of their posts; he dissolved the Northwest Station's Yan'an group inside the Border Region, and at the same time the various groups in the counties around it; and he dissolved besides the Northwest Station's subordinate groups that held Hanzhong-class men—the Ningxia, Luoyang, Zhengzhou, Jiaodong, Anhui, and Xinjiang groups. Henceforth no Hanzhong graduate was ever to be trusted with an important post again.

The Northwest Station's successful penetration of Yan'an had been one of Juntong's shining feats, and now that it had collapsed overnight, Dai Li had to answer for it too. At Baigongguan, the site of Juntong's Chongqing training class, a "Memorial for the Martyrs Fallen at Yan'an" was held, where the heroic deeds of forty-odd martyrs moved heaven and earth. Chiang Kai-shek came to view it in person and directed Dai Li: "You may display their photographs as well." Dai Li said quickly: "Report to the Generalissimo—they are nameless heroes. The photographs may be shown to the Generalissimo alone, and to no one else."

Chiang Kai-shek left satisfied. Dai Li turned around and issued a nationwide wanted order: a bounty on the Juntong turncoats Qi Sanyi and Li Chunmao.

These two Juntong turncoats had been exposed—but there was an earlier turncoat still, buried deep.

Wu Nanshan, still in Longdong, had gone on working in secret for the Border Security. Even after Qi Sanyi's capture, the Longdong security sub-office kept Wu Nanshan's true identity hidden from Juntong. Zhao Cangbi had Wu Nanshan write to Cheng Muyi, claiming he held the lawful post of county education chief, and proposing that men sent into the Border Region in future go through him first, to be sheltered and placed.

Wu Nanshan kept the trust of the Nationalist secret service. Yu Jichuan of the fifth Hanzhong session came forward to report that the Northwest Station had sent the instructor Zhu Guocai to slip into the Border Region and take charge of its hidden agents. The Longdong brothers An Yongshan and An Yonglu, back from the Hanzhong class, came first to Wu Nanshan to make contact, and even told him their specific tasks. Luo Honglie, a Zhongtong agent at the party-affairs communications office in Xifeng and a schoolmate of Wu Nanshan's, revealed his identity to Wu Nanshan when he came to work in the Border Region. The Zhongtong agents Deng Yingxian and Deng Yingde, slipping into the Border Region, came to Wu Nanshan for help as well. All these agents fell, of course, under the Border Security's secret watch.

Wu Nanshan also helped the leadership win other agents over. Zhang Yichang, a Hanzhong graduate, slipped into Qingyang to make contact with Wu Nanshan, and was swiftly counter-used by the Longdong security sub-office. Wang Wenhan, a teacher at Qingyang's No. 1 primary school, told Wu Nanshan that the dean of Pingliang Middle School had sent word of a free school one could attend. Wu Nanshan thought at once of another Juntong training class, and reported it to the Longdong security sub-office. The field-team leader, Chen Shiqi, briefed Wang Wenhan face to face and set him to penetrate the enemy's secret service.

In Qingyang, Wu Nanshan had held such open posts as education chief, drama-troupe head, and primary-school principal, but his secret post was always field-team leader for Qingyang under the Longdong security sub-office. The first impression Wu Nanshan had formed at the Hanzhong class was that the Nationalists did not trust the young, and that he had lost his freedom there; the first impression the Communist leadership gave him was the exact opposite, for the first time he confessed his past, Ma Wenrui affirmed to his face that he had been deceived.

Just before that talk with Wu Nanshan, Ma Wenrui had himself been wrongly arrested during the purge in northern Shaanxi. He had come within a hair of being shot for a false charge—so how did Ma Wenrui find the courage to take in Wu Nanshan, this Juntong defector?

One cannot help thinking that the great shift in policy was what worked at the root. In the early years of the War of Resistance the Party Center had corrected its old "Left" errors, and only so were the intelligence and security workers relieved of their burden and made bold to use every force that could be used inside the enemy.

In the Hanzhong-class case, 32 Juntong agents were captured in Yan'an, and, save for Wang Huanzhang alone, the Border Security counter-used every one of them, each to some degree of effect. Among them were 6 who fled of their own accord—Yang Chengzhang, Li Fengbi, Zhang Zhigang, Zhang Zhijun, Wang Huanzhang, and Zhao Xiu—but these 6 never won Juntong's full trust either.

The life of a "turncoat" is a wretched one.

The new master naturally values him, while the old, betrayed, hates him to the bone; then the old side keeps working its counter-espionage stratagems, and the new side cannot help growing suspicious; and in the end he is left the man neither side will own.

The Nationalists, for all their skill at using Communist turncoats, kept a wariness of turncoats in their hearts throughout. Gu Shunzhang's betrayal of the Communists had come within an inch of netting the whole Party Center, and cost him the lives of his own entire family. Even so, Zhongtong did not trust Gu Shunzhang, and gave him only idle posts; later, suspecting him of gathering a following of his own, they shot him. When Zhang Guotao deserted to Juntong, Dai Li made much of him and put him in charge of a "special political cadre training class," but when it produced nothing much he too was left to languish in the cold. Chiang Kai-shek's logic was: if you can betray the Communists, you can betray me again.

The Communists were now to take in the Nationalists' "turncoats" as well, and to "counter-use" them. As all knew, the Communists had suffered worse from turncoats, and their political morality toward turncoats was the harshest of all; the Communists laid it down plainly: a turncoat may be used, but may not rejoin the Party.

So much for the "turncoat"—and the "agent"?

In the Hanzhong-class case, everyone involved was a bona fide Juntong agent. To suspect a false surrender was only natural—had six not already fled?

And the ones who had not fled? They too carried a weight in their hearts: did the Communists truly trust me? The few taken on to work in the Border Security were still not quite of one mind with the other cadres. On the surface they seemed honest, doing whatever they were told, but they kept their grievances shut in their bellies. Forever saying they hoped the organization would trust them, they were forever touchy about their leaders' manner; yet the moment the organization showed them a kindness, they resented being treated as guests. Walking down the street, catching sight of someone behind them, they would suspect the organization had put a tail on them. One man wrote in his report: "Last night I dreamed of a black dog—and but for the master's face, I'd have thrashed it with a stick."

For all its want of experience in dealing with Juntong, the Border Security was skilled at the Party's security policy, and had a sure grip on the shifts in these men's minds. As the work went deeper, their frame of mind steadily improved.

When the War of Liberation broke out, these Hanzhong classmates faced off and killed one another across the two camps. Chen Ming, He Zhicheng, and others went back to their old masters, and lured and murdered classmates like Ma Ming; Wu Nanshan, Qi Sanyi, and others, heedless of the danger, led armed work teams in a fight to the death with the enemy.

After the founding of the state, Wu Nanshan, Qi Sanyi, Wang Xingwen, and others held important posts in various fields, the public-security organs among them; and though they too were struggled against in the "Cultural Revolution," they were later all rehabilitated, and in old age enjoyed retirement on the same terms as other cadres with no historical stain, meeting no discrimination.

If "war is the continuation of politics," then one may say that intelligence and security are the deepening of politics. And "counter-use" is by no means a mere technical matter, but a political one of the utmost intricacy.

Counter-espionage is a long struggle, one that cannot be finished at a stroke.

Who would have thought that a Juntong case cracked in 1942 would reach all the way into the 1960s.

In the early years of the state, public-security organs across the country gradually pieced together more of the Juntong Hanzhong class: the two places, Zhejiang and Hanzhong, had trained 320 men in all, with 40-odd more whose whereabouts were unknown.

In 1955, when the People's Liberation Army took Yijiangshan Island from the Nationalists, they captured a written report by Cheng Muyi stating that the two training classes, Zhejiang and Hanzhong, had trained 670 men, most of them worked into the Communist Party, some already in important posts, and recommending that the Bureau of Confidential Investigation get in touch with them.

Were there still Hanzhong agents lying in wait?

The Ministry of Public Security placed the matter under "Special Case 603" and set the whole country, Gansu's public-security organs above all, to clearing it up entire.

The Gansu Provincial Public Security Department, under deputy director Su Zhenrong, after more than two months of detailed investigation, determined that the Hanzhong class had enrolled nine sessions in all and trained 631 agents, 37 of them instructors. This figure was close to Cheng Muyi's report, but far beyond the 320 the public-security organs had known of before, and far beyond still the 55 captured in the Yan'an years; the newly found Hanzhong agents alone came to as many as 160.

Apart from the three who had fled to Taiwan—Cheng Muyi, Du Changcheng, and Wang Xitian—the agents left scattered across the mainland mostly ceased their activity after the founding of the state; but many of them never disclosed their ties to the Nationalist secret service, four had even worked into the organs, and a few went on with anti-Communist work. A certain Zhou and his wife, a certain Zhang (a woman), slipped past the cadre vetting and lay hidden in the army for years, confessing only in 1946 when the Northeast public-security office vetted them. A certain Li and a certain Lü confessed during the 1943 Rectification and went on working in the army system after the state was founded. A certain Chen made a tentative confession during the Rectification and then retracted it, rose after the founding to be training-office chief at the navy's Qingdao base, reaching division rank, and only in 1955 was his case pinned down and he expelled from the Party.

The investigation showed that some men had truly broken with the agents' organization, while others might merely have gone into hibernation—and when the moment came, would some take up the old trade again?

Counter-espionage practice proved that one must not lightly speak of drawing in the whole net.

Counter-espionage is a long and intricate struggle.

The cracking of the Juntong Hanzhong training-class case was the Communists' greatest counter-espionage fruit of the War of Resistance, and a brilliant page in their history of counter-espionage. If to call it "China's first great counter-espionage case" is not yet sure, to call it "Yan'an's first great counter-espionage case" is beyond dispute.

Yet the case bred grave consequences too. As the Yan'an Rectification passed into its cadre-vetting phase and grew into the "Rescue Campaign," both were bound up with the uncovering of these agents.

The great influx of the Nationalists' "expendable agents" into the Border Region put the security departments abruptly on the highest alert. In the cadre-vetting campaign, ever more weight fell on the task of hunting agents and rooting out traitors. And the surrender of these Juntong agents, some willing and some not, led the organization to lean harder still on the policy of confession.

By the time the "Rescue Campaign" broke out in 1943, the Juntong Hanzhong class's "confessors" had all become "model" figures.

*A Draft History of Chinese People's Public Security* (Police Officer Education Press). This Juntong Hanzhong-class case was the Communists' greatest counter-espionage fruit of the War of Resistance, yet for a long time after the founding of the state it was never made public. The author has kept asking why. That the Communists do not mention it—perhaps because the case fed into the cadre-vetting campaign? That the Nationalists do not mention it—perhaps to shield the agents still lying in wait? This book is the first published work to disclose the case, though it remains too brief.

Hao Su, "Recollections of Public-Security Work in Longdong." Written at the invitation of the Longdong public-security office, this is the only written record left by anyone who worked the Hanzhong-class case.

Wu Nanshan, "An Account of Cracking Enemy-Agent Cases in Qingyang." This material, written by Wu Nanshan in 1984, is the important written narrative of a man caught up in the Hanzhong-class case.

Shen Zui, *The Inside Story of Juntong* (China Literature and History Press). The memoirs of former Juntong men published before the "Cultural Revolution," Shen Zui's writings among them, never once mentioned this Hanzhong class. Only in this book is there an essay that mentions Cheng Muyi's Hanzhong class, and mentions too that in the spring of 1942 Dai Li personally picked three master assassins and sent them to Yan'an to kill the Communist leaders—one of them, a man named Jiang Gengsheng, being a student of Shen Zui's—but they did not succeed.

Zhang Weihan, "Dai Li and the Vast Juntong Organization," in *Zhongtong and Juntong in Detail* (Biographical Literature Press). Published in Taiwan in 1992, this book at last writes of Cheng Muyi's training class, though under the name "Xi'an Special Investigation Class." And it does not mention that the graduates of this class had the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region as their destination.

Li Wenji and Ma Ruyao, "A War of Annihilation on the Hidden Front: Notes on the Early-1940s Anti-Agent Struggle in the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region," in the Lanzhou Municipal Public Security Bureau's *Selected Materials on Public-Security History*. This piece records in detail how the Border Region Security Office cracked the Juntong Hanzhong class, with precious appendices—the record of interviews with Wu Nanshan, the roster of Juntong agents the Border Security took, the agents' confessions, and more—the fullest written material on the case before the present book. Wu Dingjun, son of Wu Nanshan; interviewed August 27, 2002. Wu Dingjun once helped his father put in order the written material on the Hanzhong class, knows the matter well, and understands his father's inner course.

Li Wenji, former cadre of the Lanzhou Municipal Public Security Bureau; interviewed August 28, 2002. Li Wenji pursued this case for years, interviewed Wu Nanshan several times, and produced a fairly complete written narrative.

Ma Wenrui, former vice-chairman of the National Committee of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference; interviewed May 30, 2001. As the highest leader of the local party organization, Ma Wenrui directed the security work and held the policy, and wrote of Wu Nanshan's contribution as well.

Li Fushan, former deputy chief procurator of the Shanxi Provincial People's Procuratorate; interviewed March 1998. Tu Zhankui, former deputy director of the Qinghai Provincial Machinery Department; interviewed 1999. Tu Zhankui gave a vivid account of his own reconnaissance and Hao Su's interrogation of Qi Sanyi. Hao Su later transferred to the Border Security interrogation section and made a name in interrogation work, perhaps set on that road by this very success.

Zhu Zengfu, "How the Juntong Special Reconnaissance Station's Yan'an Group Set Up Its Underground Operations," in the Lanzhou Municipal Public Security Bureau's *Selected Materials on Public-Security History*. Zhu Zengfu (Zhu Guocai) was arrested in 1949 after the founding of the state, and in prison wrote out how Cheng Muyi had convened the Xi'an meeting to arrange the linking of contacts.

Lü Huang, former cadre of the All-China Women's Federation; interviewed July 14, 1994. In those days Bu Lu led Lü Huang and others to Longdong to guide the work, and brought Qi Sanyi back to Yan'an to crack the case.

Li Qiming, former standing secretary of the Yunnan Provincial Party Committee; interviewed October 18, 1995. Li Qiming took part in Yan'an in cracking the Hanzhong training-class case.

Wang Jun, former deputy minister of State Security; interviewed November 24, 1999. Wang Jun stayed on in the Social Affairs Department after this, later serving as Northwest College class director and Social Affairs Department cadre-section chief, and after the founding of the state in 1949 as cadre-bureau director of the Central Investigation Department, president of the Institute of International Relations, and deputy minister of State Security.

Li Jun (Li Chunmao), *The Downfall of the Nationalist Juntong's Hanzhong Training Class*. This book, written by the eyewitness Li Jun, records the whole course of the Hanzhong class in detail; it is not yet published.

Ouyang Yi, former deputy political commissar of the Artillery; interviewed October 2002. The General Political Department's Anti-Traitor Department, under Ouyang Yi, handled the cracking of the "Dai case" agents in the army system. In 1994 the author went to Guangzhou to interview Qian Yimin—but this figure, one of Yan'an's three great investigation experts, was by age and illness no longer able to tell much.

Wang Yantang, former deputy minister of State Security; interviewed January 28, 2003. Wang Yantang points out that the cracking of a case is often the sum of many quarters' work, owing something to the security departments and something to the intelligence men alike. Wang Yantang, Wang Chu, and other former Social Affairs Department cadres relate that the reach of this case went far beyond Longdong, even beyond the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, with some suspects slipping into other base areas—there was even one at the side of Yang Xiufeng, chair of the South Hebei Administrative Office of the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region. Before the case broke, the Social Affairs Department had already found some leads through other channels, and once the Border Security made its discovery, the Social Affairs Department took unified charge of cracking it. Besides the forty-odd taken in Yan'an, other base areas took some as well; the number caught up at the time reached more than two hundred, and more than six hundred were traced after the founding of the state.

NOTES (these are prose too; judge them):

- [training class for expendable agents] “Expendable agents” renders 死间 (sijian), literally “dead agents”—one of the five kinds of spy in Sun Tzu’s Art of War, the doomed agent fed false information and sent knowing he is likely to be caught and killed. Juntong used the term for the men it planted to burrow into the Communist base areas, as Sun Tzu’s spy-types are glossed earlier in the book.
- [the lore of the] The haidi (海底, literally “sea bottom”) was Juntong jargon for the secret personal dossier each agent compiled and memorized: his real and cover names, his permanent and mailing addresses, the cover names of his contacts, the cipher for their correspondence, and his oath. The original stayed on file at the training class, and all later clandestine contact was keyed to it.
- [the Renaissance Society men] The Renaissance Society (复兴社) was an outer organ of the Nationalist “Blue Shirts,” the network of Chiang Kai-shek loyalists built in the early 1930s around the inner Vigorous Action Society (力行社), noted earlier in the book. Its members served as Nationalist party and security cadres.
- [Ren Zhuoxuan] Ren Zhuoxuan (任卓宣, 1896–1990), pen name Ye Qing, was an early Communist—once head of the Party’s Paris branch—who, after his arrest, defected and became the Nationalists’ foremost anti-Communist theorist and a propaganda official. Kang Sheng cites his career as the model of a turncoat put to use. (Corroborated.)
- [trained 631 agents] The chapter gives several figures because they count different things from different sources. The 32 agents of the core Yan’an “Dai case” (1 who came forward, 20 found by investigation, 11 named by others) are the single case cracked in 1942; a later recollection puts the Yan’an arrests at forty-odd and the whole Yan’an-period haul at 55; the 631 trained over nine sessions is the Gansu investigators’ 1955 count for the entire class, close to Cheng Muyi’s own captured report of 670, against 320 known earlier and 160 newly found. The book does not reconcile the tallies.
- [took Yijiangshan Island] The People’s Liberation Army’s assault on Yijiangshan (一江山), a small island off the Zhejiang coast held by the Nationalists, in January 1955 was China’s first combined army-navy-air amphibious landing; the captured documents included Cheng Muyi’s report. (Corroborated.)
- [the Bureau of Confidential Investigation] The Bureau of Confidential Investigation (保密局, Baomiju) was the postwar successor to Juntong, formed in 1946 under the Defense Ministry; that renaming is why the wartime Juntong is meant here.
- [At Baigongguan] Baigongguan (白公馆, the “White Mansion”), a villa at Geleshan outside Chongqing, served Juntong as a prison and as part of its Chongqing training apparatus; it belonged to the SACO complex noted in Chapter 7, where many captured Communists were held and killed.
