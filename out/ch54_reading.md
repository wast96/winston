## Chapter 54

After we had passed through four bridge-gates, the boat drew slowly in among the other craft. Cishan's Third Uncle told Xiao Heizi to mind the boat well, then sprang out himself, made his way along the gunwale outside the awning to the stern, put his head in, and said to us:

"Come ashore with me."

So I beckoned Mei Yingzi to follow him across, and Cishan's Third Uncle took up the mooring rope and went ahead, treading over the boats alongside; we followed behind, and after crossing three boats we reached the wharf, and up the stone steps was the road.

The road was wide, but very dark. Passers-by too were few, and Cishan's Third Uncle crossed it and went on along the houses on the far side; I put Mei Yingzi in front and followed behind her myself. I realized that, dressed as we were and walking so, no one would ever suspect there was anything odd in the tie between us.

This row of houses was old, but still the dwellings of middle-class families. Along them we passed one dustbin after another, one back door and kitchen window after another, some shut, some open. It was just the dinner hour: the electric lamps were lit within, oil and greens sizzled, hot rice sent up its fragrance, and now and then came sounds of laughter and talk that all seemed familiar; and this stirred in me, unbidden, a yearning for home and a hunger I could not hold down, and in an instant I felt a boundless desolation and chill. I quickened my steps to Mei Yingzi's side, but ahead of us Cishan's Third Uncle had already stopped at a back door and was knocking.

"Who's there?" asked someone within, in a Shanghai accent.

"It's me," said Cishan's Third Uncle, still in his Yangzhou-inflected Mandarin.

The door opened then, and he turned and called us in. Inside was a small courtyard, with the kitchen beside it; but we did not go in there, going straight on through the courtyard, and just within was a staircase. Ahead the lamp was burning bright: it was a tailor's workshop. With a knowing look he signed to us to wait a moment by the stairs and went off to the front himself. Presently he came out with another man, whom I later heard the others call the proprietor. He was very short, his skin rather pale, a little plump, and he seemed to wear a smile always. The moment he came out he told us to go up; the stairs were very dark, and he went ahead, Mei Yingzi and I following, Cishan's Third Uncle behind me. The proprietor went up and opened the door of the little room over the stair-landing and told us to go in. After we were in he shut the door, and outside it he seemed to be talking with Cishan's Third Uncle.

The little room was very small, a bed taking up half of it; besides that a table and two chairs, and on the table an old-fashioned clock, which just then pointed to seven thirty-two. Mei Yingzi and I sat down to one side, all of us silent, listening to the clock's ticking. About ten minutes on, the door suddenly opened, and the proprietor beckoned us to the front upstairs room.

The front room was more open, but the things in it were heaped in great disorder; by the window stood a square table with chairs on three sides. We sat down on the chairs, but the proprietor did not follow us in, and Cishan's uncle too stood at the door. Just then a scabby-headed apprentice brought up two cups of tea, and the proprietor said:

"Whatever you want, just have him go and buy it."

Then the proprietor went downstairs; he had not talked with us at all. Cishan's Third Uncle came in and said:

"Have something to eat? Send him to buy it."

The apprentice waited to one side, and I began to ask Mei Yingzi. Mei Yingzi said:

"Anything will do." I thought the simplest was noodles, so I took out five dollars and gave them to the apprentice, telling him to buy two bowls of noodles.

After the apprentice had gone, Cishan's Third Uncle shut the door and said:

"The hands downstairs will scatter after the meal, and then the proprietor will watch for a good chance and come to call you. Once through the rope barrier at the front here, you're out of the sealed-off zone." He drew a breath, and, thinking a moment and finding nothing more to say, said: "I'll go now — goodbye."

"Thank you," I said, going over to take his hand; "goodbye."

For that great coarse hand — I can feel even now the unspeakable emotion it roused in me then; the tears all but sprang from my eyes, for before me was a man so tall and strong and stout, and yet through his thick brows and big eyes shone the tenderest feeling; like a loving mother he clung to us, loath to part, as if he had something to say and yet had not. Mei Yingzi came over then too, and I saw that she also had been moved; she stood before him with bowed head, took the little finger of his drooping left hand, and said low:

"Goodbye — tell Cishan I shall certainly come to see her one day."

He nodded, but did not speak.

"We shall surely meet again. Goodbye!" I said. "I shall always remember the help, the kindness, and the friendship you gave us."

And so his heavy hand slid out of my palm; quietly he turned, and went slowly to the door, and slowly pulled it open, and then, turning his head back, looked from Mei Yingzi to me and said warmly:

"Goodbye." But he gazed at me a long while and said: "Take care on the road." And so he shut the door heavily, and then I heard the sound of his heavy tread going down the stairs. —

This was the first time I ever knew this strange emotion, an emotion that cannot be imagined save by those who have lived it, nor be explained; if I had to compare it with some other experience, I think it is only like the feeling of parting when, far from home, on a strange and toilsome journey, one meets a fellow-countryman who warmly gives you aid and then must at once go his separate way. Who knows whether, at the ends of the earth, there is ever a time of meeting again? Who knows what manner of fate throws people together? A dim, vague feeling in me made me sorrowful!

Now we had again to wait patiently for the time to pass. In the silence I began to feel uneasy; I went to the window, meaning to open it and look out, but Mei Yingzi stopped me, and so I peered out through the dirty yellow glass. There were very few passers-by on the road; opposite, only a few little shops had their doors open, and just off to the right, aslant across the way, a road turned off — I could not make out which road it was, nor call its name to mind, but in my heart I reckoned we should have to turn out along that road once we went. Suddenly I thought of the purpose of our going out, and I drew back and sat down, and said:

"When we go out, shall we go first to Bai Ping's house?"

Mei Yingzi looked at me in wonder, and laughed, and said:

"Isn't that walking straight into the net?"

I felt too how childish and absurd I was, but I said:

"Then?…"

Mei Yingzi did not answer me; she was thinking.

"Mrs. Stephen's place, then."

She shook her head.

"I thought perhaps Helen's place would do."

"Better find some out-of-the-way little hotel first," she said suddenly. "I'll go and make inquiries tomorrow, and then settle what to do."

"That's reasonable too," I said.

"Which hotel do you think would suit?" she said. "It must be a place where we'll never run into anyone we know."

I thought a while, and said:

"Over toward Rue du Consulat, perhaps — there are many small hotels over there."

"Good — that's settled."

This decided, we fell silent facing each other again. The sounds of laughter and talk came up clearly from below, and I heard too the sound of tables and chairs, of bowls and chopsticks. Just then the apprentice from before brought up our noodles. Mei Yingzi and I sat facing each other and ate, and this bowl of noodles filled not only our bellies but our spirits and our lives. When I had finished I seemed still not quite full, and much regretted not having had him buy a little more; Mei Yingzi too seemed to find it too little, and finished quickly, but did not care to order more, so I did not have anyone buy more either.

I offered Mei Yingzi a cigarette, and she said with a laugh:

"You even brought along the cigarettes someone else bought for you."

"Because I thought I'd have need of them," I said. "When I left, I still left five hundred dollars on Cishan's uncle's boat, thinking he would have need of it too. Beyond what we have need of, there's no meaning in keeping anything."

"Is this some philosophy you've lately discovered?"

"It's only a feeling!" I said.

Mei Yingzi fell silent again. Below there began the sound of stools being moved, of Peking opera being hummed, of water being poured, of laughing and cursing, and then a clatter of footsteps, and the sound of the back door closing time after time; at last the sounds faded, and I heard the far, far barking of dogs and the sound of carts.

"What a bleak and murderous night it is!" I said.

"But well worth a night of our lives to know," said Mei Yingzi.

I did not answer, for my gaze was glued to two things before me. Ever since I had come into this room my mind had been wholly on what lay outside the front window, my attention always on the front of the room; but now my eyes chanced to sweep over the back, where there was a bed, on it two or three bundles of bedding in disorder. To the right of the bed was stacked a pile of trunks, and on top of the trunks two more bundles of bedding. The first of these trunks was a small white leather case, and the two beneath were big cases of red and black; and between the two was the very thing that had caught my eye — two yellow-grey grips, packed so full they were like the bellies of children who have eaten too much. At first they gave me a sense of having seen them before, and then it flashed on me that these were the very cases Bai Ping had kept in her inner room. When I was staying at Bai Ping's they were kept in the inner room; when I went to steal the documents they were kept in the inner room too — and now, how came they to be here? I could not help going over to look closely, and — yes, quite right — bound to the grips was a strip of white cloth gone grey, and on the white cloth were the very words "held for the Tao residence." Seeing me thus, Mei Yingzi could not help asking:

"What is it?"

"Aren't these the cases from Bai Ping's place?"

"Truly?" said Mei Yingzi, coming over.

"Quite right — there's no mistaking it," I said. "How can they be here?" Just as Mei Yingzi was bending to examine them, there was a sound on the stairs, and then a knock.

"Come in," I said.

In came the proprietor, wearing still his placid, fair-skinned smile, and saying easily and naturally:

"Ready to go now?"

I badly wanted to ask about the cases, but got no chance, for he suddenly handed me a wad of banknotes and said:

"Xiao Heizi sent these — he says you forgot them over at his place."

"Ah," I said, "but I meant them for him. Now, then, please keep them by you for a time, and when there's a chance, please pass them back to him — I think he'll have need of them."

"Very well," said the proprietor, and to Mei Yingzi: "Let's go."

Mei Yingzi went down first, I after her; the light was very dim, and the proprietor behind only kept saying:

"Mind your step, mind your step."

Down the stairs, Mei Yingzi stood a moment, and the proprietor turned to the front; we followed him to the front tailoring floor, where four apprentices were laying out work-boards, seeming about to sleep, and they only looked at us and said nothing. The front shutters seemed still all up; the proprietor went up to them, with not the least air of haste or dread, and softly drew a door open, glanced out through the crack, then opened it a little wider and glanced again, and said easily, with a smile:

"It's all right." And then he opened it a little wider still, and stood to one side to let us pass.

"Thank you," said Mei Yingzi, and went out; I followed her out, saying:

"Goodbye, thank you."

Stepping outside, there was the footway, very dark, and along the footway the ropes. We looked to both sides — no one — and ducked through under the ropes, and I drew Mei Yingzi quickly across the road, then slowed our steps. All through this my heart had been pounding, and only at the turning did I ease a little.

That road was busier, but there were no cars; we walked in silence, and crossed another road before there were rickshaws to hire, and I called a rickshaw and went straight to Rue du Consulat.

We passed ourselves off as a brother and sister up from the country to buy things, though we may already have been taken for a chauffeur and a maidservant at their tryst; and we took a room at a place called the Lu'an Hotel.

For convenience in talking we took but one room, though it had two beds; but the bedding was very dirty, and we could only lie down in our clothes. We were indeed very tired by now.

This was a very ordinary little hotel on Rue du Consulat, very noisy and disorderly — the calls at the gambling tables, the ribaldry of the streetwalkers, singing and shouting, everything. I saw that Mei Yingzi seemed to fall asleep quickly. But I lay tossing and could not sleep; I thought of Bai Ping, thought of Stephen, thought back to when I first came to know them — how it had gone from the gambling den to the church, how I joined Mrs. Stephen's birthday dance, how we went to Hangzhou, how I came to stay at Bai Ping's, how Bai Ping was shot, how I moved out, how I joined Mei Yingzi's work, how I went to steal the documents, how I was shot and wounded by Bai Ping, how I lay in the hospital, how I was in Arita's house, how at Umetake's dance I met Miyama Yoshiko, how I — the disordered fragments of the past struck my brain, my heart, every nerve-end of me, like bullets, one after another, and my whole body burned, past all mastering. I put out the lamp, but the lights in the corridor, outside the window, next door still lit our room bright as day. And so I thought of the day Bai Ping came back from Hangzhou and fell ill, and I had put out her lamp and come from the silver room, and how I had felt the silver sorrow of the silver girl in the silver bedding — and now, where did she lie? And I thought of Stephen lying rigid in the Gaolang Hospital, his purple lips, his lightless gaze, his gaunt bones — and where did he live now? And I — I now lay in a bleak and dreary room, on a strange bed, unable to feel one soul's touch, one pulse's tie, with them; then what was it that had bound us in those days when never a day passed but we were together?

There was the sound of sobbing, and I called softly:

"Mei Yingzi!"

"Ah!" she sighed.

"Don't grieve — sleep, and soon," I said, and the tears had already run from the corners of my eyes to my earlobes.

This is life; all this is life!
