### Eating from a Hundred Households

In households of the old style, when a red or white affair is held—that is, a wedding or a funeral—a troop of menservants and maidservants must be taken on for the occasion to lend a hand: the men as "second masters," the women as wedding-matrons and the like. These people call themselves eaters from a hundred households, because in all four seasons of the year they go dashing here and there and everywhere to earn their keep. But the eaters from a hundred households are not the second masters and wedding-matrons alone; the monks, the Taoist priests, the amateur players, the brass bands and the rest of them eat from a hundred households too, and then there are the monks and priests who beg alms along the road and the beggars who go bowl in hand from door to door—these, one might say, eat from a thousand households, from ten thousand.

Nowadays the newly promoted group weddings, and the funerals where the coffining is done at the funeral parlor, have no need of menservants and maids to lend a hand; and in a few more years the eaters from a hundred households too will be sighing that they have come to the end of their road, with never a meal to be had.
