### Afterword

This is a book the author has long wanted to write. Of the struggle Comrade Zhou Enlai waged against the enemy in Shanghai in those years, leading the Central Special Section, I had heard something as far back as the war years. After I went to work in Chen Geng's unit in the early days of the War of Liberation, I often heard Comrade Chen Geng, in the lulls in the fighting, talk at length of the Special Section's exploits (later, in Beijing, I called on him about it more than once). Those stories, touched with the color of legend, fascinated me, and gave rise to the wish to write such a book. When I was writing a biography of Chen Geng in the late 1970s, I wrote up his experience in the Central Special Section alone as Comrade Chen Geng in Shanghai, which the Beijing Literary and Historical Materials Press published in July 1980 in a printing of 73,500 copies; it sold out within no long time, a sign that readers, too, were fairly interested. Although that book touched on the Central Special Section's activities of those years in their various aspects, it was written as part of the chapters for General Chen Geng, and dwelt on the fighting experience of its subject. And so some old friends urged me to go on gathering materials and to write, as soon as I could, a full picture of the Central Special Section's activities in those years, giving a full and faithful account of Zhou Enlai's achievement in directly leading the Special Section to foil the wrecking of enemies at home and abroad and to safeguard the Party Center.

I have no intention of writing the whole history of the Central Special Section; that is beyond any one person. This book chiefly records the outstanding contribution of Zhou Enlai, the founder and leader of our Party's hidden front, in creating and leading the work of the Central Special Section. The Central Special Section was the earliest intelligence and security organ set up expressly to safeguard the Party's organizational security, a fighting fortress of the Party under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership. Under the extraordinarily harsh conditions of that time, the Special Section played a considerable part in safeguarding the Party's central organs, gathering intelligence, punishing traitors, establishing radio communications, consolidating the Party's organization, and advancing the development of armed struggle. Short as its life was, the achievements the Central Special Section built in the history of the Chinese Communist Party's struggle can never be effaced.

Though this book is not a systematic history of the Central Special Section, in its narrative I have striven for accuracy and detail, with warrant for every word. That, of course, is not easy to achieve. The Special Section's work in those years was wholly clandestine, bound by the strictest organizational discipline and rules of secrecy, run on single-line contact; all activity was kept absolutely secret, no written record was permitted, and no one was to inquire into matters beyond his own. Those who took part had a very limited knowledge of the Special Section's affairs; some, after years of work, did not so much as know the name of the unit. As comrades who once worked in the Central Special Section have said: because it was a clandestine working environment, with no written record, one could rely only on memory; and since everyone then used false names and formed the habit of not remembering names, and the events are now so many years past, what can be recalled is only a rough outline. To gather the relevant materials of those years half a century later has therefore been no easy thing. On the details of certain events especially, since the parties did not know one another's business and memory has faded with the years, accounts often differ and even contradict one another. Where this is so, some points can only be left in doubt; some can only be passed over for the time being, or explained in a note. Should there be gaps in what I have consulted, or improprieties in what I have recorded, I earnestly hope the learned will not grudge their correction.

Because the Central Special Section's activities were extraordinarily hidden, and its struggle uncommonly sharp and complex, people often have a sense of mystery about it, and may even fail to understand it. In truth the matter is very simple. A political party is an instrument of class struggle, and the struggle the Chinese Communist Party waged against the Kuomintang reactionaries and all class enemies at home and abroad was a life-and-death fight. When the enemy comes at you with a knife, you must take up a knife and cut back. Only so can you preserve yourself and destroy the enemy. It is the same as a fight on the battlefield, differing only in the form of the struggle. The Central Special Section was our Party's professional organ for intelligence and security work. Whether in political struggle or in military struggle, intelligence is a necessary weapon, and reliable intelligence an important factor deciding victory or defeat; traitors and secret agents gravely threaten the safety of the Party's organization, and if traitors are not severely punished the Party suffers very grave harm. The damage Gu Shunzhang did the revolution after his arrest and betrayal is the most striking case in point.

In the Shanghai of those years, shrouded in White Terror, Zhou Enlai led the Central Special Section in a life-and-death struggle with the enemy. They faced not only powerful and savage reactionaries at home and abroad, but had also to guard against the cold arrows shot from behind by traitors and enemy agents; the struggle was uncommonly bitter, and many comrades gave their precious lives for the revolution. They advanced wave upon wave, facing death unflinching, and showed the staunch and unyielding heroism of Communists, writing a splendid chapter in the history of the Chinese Communist Party.

In the Afterword to Comrade Chen Geng in Shanghai, I once wrote: "In the 1950s the author called on Comrade Chen Geng about this matter more than once; and in writing the present work I also called several times on Li Qiang, Chen Yangshan, Liu Ding, Ke Lin, and other comrades who had long worked alongside Comrade Chen Geng in the Central Special Section, and who furnished many important facts; Comrade Yang Xianzhen, too, from his sickbed, recalled the circumstances of his struggle together with Comrade Chen Geng in Tianjin; and the relevant departments and comrades furnished much precious material besides." Since then, in order to write this book, I have gone on gathering the relevant materials without a break. As early as February 20, 1962, I called on Chen Yangshan about it, and I kept in regular touch with Chen Yangshan and Li Qiang right up to the end of their lives. They warmly supported my study of the Central Special Section's activities, and furnished, one after another, much valuable material. In the course of writing this book I have had the support and help of still more comrades: some furnishing newly discovered materials, some sending relevant newspapers and journals, some helping to collect and reproduce photographs; and I have drawn on some relevant articles in books and periodicals. To all of them I here express my heartfelt thanks.

July 11, 2001, Beijing.
