## Chapter 27

It was a brand-new, sumptuous piano, and on the piano were vivid flowers; the golden sunlight, passing through the spotless gauze of the window, fell on the vase, and the shadow of the flowers was cast onto the water-green carpet. The furniture was a set of gleaming chromium and glass, and its transparent flashing raised my spirits. The walls had been done up anew, and there was a photograph of a gorgeous, exquisite young lady, smiling in a chromium frame.

This ought to have been a place I had never been to, but there was no mistake: this was the Manfields' home, and the one smiling in the framed glass was none other than Helen. Helen Manfield.

Mrs. Manfield, in a dark-blue velvet gown, gave me her plump hand and shook mine warmly, and at once thanked me for the matter of that night, of escorting Helen to Bai Ping's home.

"And Helen?"

"She's gone out." Mrs. Manfield bade me sit down. "She'll be back soon."

"She's very busy these days."

"Busy, busy," she said. "Engagements, always engagements!"

"And how are you? You've grown thin!" She heaped up a kindly smile and said with concern: "You must take care of your health! Helen's health is better now; she's very busy, but I look after her comfort. Nourishment is the most important thing. She often comes home very late, and I always make her drink a glass of milk before bed; for people who keep late hours like you, the glass of milk before sleep is the most important thing. Now that our circumstances are rather better, I can use all sorts of methods to keep up Helen's health. I won't let her read before bed; I pick out the most soothing and lovely records to lull her to sleep, and in the morning I make the quietest surroundings and the most suitable temperature, so that she may sleep sweetly. Calm and sufficient sleep is the root of health..."

"Of course, of course." I broke in on her words, stood up, went over to the table to light a cigarette, and gazed at Helen's photograph on the wall. I said admiringly:

"This photograph is really beautiful."

"Very pretty, isn't it?" she said. "Everyone praises her."

"..." I did not answer, still gazing at her photograph.

"There are several more inside; go and have a look."

She took me into Helen's bedroom, and from this bedroom alone one could tell how dazzling a star the mistress of the house had become. There were two photographs of Helen: one of her sitting beside the piano, ringed round with flowers, and one that seemed to be at the broadcasting stand, with a great circle of flower-baskets at her feet, hung on the wall; and on the table there was a small one besides, of Mrs. Manfield seated with Helen standing beside her, Helen's look innocent, Mrs. Manfield wearing a complacent smile. With so beautiful a daughter at her side, who could hold back such a smile? While I was looking, Mrs. Manfield took from the five-drawer chest an envelope of photographs, all of them of the one Helen, but each in a different costume, a different adornment, a different pose.

When I had finished looking, I put them back in the envelope; but Mrs. Manfield, as she was returning it to the five-drawer chest, took one out and said:

"Shall we change this one for the one out there?"

"Certainly." As I spoke I took it out for her; it was a full-length photograph in fashionable dress, seemingly posed in the manner of a Hollywood star.

Out in the front room, I took down the half-length photograph again, and Mrs. Manfield in high spirits took it out of the frame, put the full-length one in, and I hung it up again.

When it was hung, I looked at it a moment and said:

"Like this it's a little like Mei Yingzi."

"Like Miss Mei Yingzi?" Then Mrs. Manfield sat down and talked to me about Mei Yingzi. She praised Mei Yingzi's beauty, her looks, her cleverness, her capability, and praised her good heart besides; she said:

"Ever since you helped us, Mei Yingzi came before long to see us, and said she could recommend Helen for employment; but of the several posts she mentioned, Helen would go to none, and later she recommended her to the radio station to broadcast, and our life came onto the right track from then on; only Helen's social round is too busy. Sometimes I feel too lonely."

Though I did not want to say anything about Mei Yingzi in Mrs. Manfield's home, I really did want to say how bad this employment was for Helen's future. Mrs. Manfield was so satisfied with Helen's present situation, however, that I naturally had no way to say anything more; I only said:

"Does Mei Yingzi come often?"

"She hasn't come for a long time now," she said. "She must be very busy. Many friends come often to visit when we are prospering, and when we were in difficulties never came at all; Mei Yingzi is just the opposite — in those days, for the sake of Helen's employment, she came several times, and now she doesn't come, and truly she is a good person." Then a firm light flashed in her eyes: "You must run into her often?"

"Now and then," I said.

The doorbell rang, and Mrs. Manfield stood up and said:

"Helen's here."

A maid in white came out from within, and as she passed by the door to answer it, Mrs. Manfield too went to meet her at the door.

Helen was truly a star now — that fragrance, that dress, that carriage, that voice in talking.

Mrs. Manfield said loudly:

"We have a guest!"

Helen came over and shook my hand. When Mrs. Manfield went out carrying Helen's coat, Helen said to me in a low voice:

"You didn't tell my mother about that night, did you?"

"No," I said. "You didn't tell her?"

"I only said a Japanese was pestering me, and that you and Bai Ping got me free of him and escorted me to Bai Ping's home," she said. "I was afraid it would worry her to hear it."

As Helen spoke these words, I discovered from her look that she was uneasy about her present life. I said:

"Do you feel your present life is a happy one?"

"It's nothing much," she said. "But since that night, I've felt I must find a way to get out of that world."

"Do you really feel so?"

"I felt very early that this life did not suit my character. I am weary of socializing with big merchants, politicians, and soldiers, not necessarily because they are Japanese, but because of the atmosphere — this atmosphere that makes me feel, when I come home, that I am no more than an ornament to other people's pleasures," she said. "But for the sake of a living..."

"Is it entirely for a living?"

"Naturally, in the wild gaiety and bustle of that life, I taste my own glory too, and I forget my own reality. And besides, my mother — my mother seems to like me this way."

"..." I mused a while, meaning to say something. But Helen heaved a deep sigh:

"Now I really want to find a way to resign."

"And what do you mean to do?"

"I can't think of anything at all." She fell silent.

I too was silent, and this instant's silence set me thinking of the past: her smiling, nestling closeness, her particular tenderness, her infatuation with philosophy, her indifference to the affairs of the world, her weariness of singing; then her loneliness and solitude, her faint melancholy, and her deep, dim stillness; then the vanishing of vanity, the lively, delicate rhythm, the bright and nimble talk; and at the last, the bleakness of her circumstances, the despair of her prospects, the broken pessimism. And so I saw the haggardness of this now bright and dazzling room, the piano thick with dust; I saw her grave, dulled expression, and I heard her sing that song, once, that would remain forever in my heart — so deep, so far-reaching, calling up the cry of wild geese down the long sky, calling up the nightingale of the moonlit night; leaping in short, urgent music, then winging far off from the deep, long strains, like a boat in a great storm, in one instant flying up into the void, straight into the clouds, in the next drifting down, none knew whither; and at the last it rose and fell in trembling waves of sound, like a fierce wild bird in combat, wounded and struggling, from a rousing upward effort to utter exhaustion, gasping a pitiful breath, moaning over and over, until at the last, one long cry, and then a sudden hush. Then I saw her come away from the piano, not a trace of expression on her face, teardrops brimming at the rims of her eyes—

Not a trace of expression on her face. And Helen, teardrops brimming at the rims of her eyes, was of all things standing here before me — was she then, in this instant's silence, recalling all of this, just as I was?

"Helen!" I called to her softly.

"..." She was quivering; she sat down on the sofa, buried her face in her hands, and of all things sobbed aloud. I had no words to comfort her; I only said softly:

"Helen!"

Just then Mrs. Manfield came in; the moment she saw how things were, she looked at me and said:

"What is it, dear?" She sat down and caressed her: "Dear, are you not well?"

"No, it's nothing." Helen wiped her tears dry and lifted her head, and in that instant I saw that the glow on her face was the ease that comes after grief has been let out; the pure tears had washed away her affectation and laid bare so venerable, innocent, and pure a face. She still pressed her eye-corners lightly with a snow-white handkerchief. Mrs. Manfield asked this and that without cease, and Helen only shook her head; at last Mrs. Manfield said:

"Go and sleep a while."

"It doesn't matter, Mama." Helen said with a smile: "Do go on; let me talk with Xu a while." Mrs. Manfield came over again to charge me not to hurt her heart, and only then went quietly out, and the room was left again to Helen and me.

"It's very quiet and pleasant here." I went to the window and hunted at random for something to say.

"Yes." Helen came and stood beside me, gazing out of the window too, and said: "Now that I am often out, my old friends come less, and the new friends I have made I have never brought here."

"Doesn't Mei Yingzi come often either?" I turned my head and asked her.

"She hasn't come for a long time. She must be very busy."

"Were many of your new friends introduced to you by her?"

"Around the time she found me the post, she introduced me to a good many people, and later I came to know others through them."

"Japanese officers?"

"A few people on the Japanese navy's side."

"Japanese naval officers, I think, are all better bred than those on the army's side."

"Yes, they have all been to Europe and America."

"Then this kind of socializing, for you, is..."

"How can you say such a thing?" Helen's tone turned suddenly sharp and urgent; she sat down, mused a while, and said: "My father and my brother are both serving in the American forces; do you suppose I think it an honor to keep company with Japanese soldiers?"

"Then have you never thought that the work you do is a help to the Japanese army?"

"Do you suppose this kind of reporting is any help to their propaganda?"

"That's hard to say."

"Do you often believe the wireless reports of these days?" She laughed.

I did not answer; I was thinking. I thought of her life, thought of Mei Yingzi, and felt that for Mei Yingzi to use Helen in this way was in any case not right; but standing where I stood, I had no way to explain Mei Yingzi's purpose, nor any way to declare my discontent with Mei Yingzi. All I could do was to make Helen aware that this life was at odds with the life of her soul; her standard of living now was not a thing that ordinary help could settle, and so I meant to take my leave. But Helen insisted on keeping me, and said:

"Stay for tea. I still have things to say to you."

"Ever since we became friends, I have always felt I could be of good to your life and your soul; but now I find that I have all along been the one who threw your life and soul off their balance, and that only when I am far from you do you live a balanced and happy life."

Helen knitted her brows a little, as if savoring my words; then she let out a bright, clear smile and said:

"The contradiction is my own, and each time it is you who prove it to me. I ought to thank you." Then her eyes suddenly flashed an unusual light:

"Bai Ping really is good; that night..."

Mrs. Manfield came in and broke off the talk; seeing that Helen no longer had her sorrow of a moment ago, she seemed much relieved, and said cheerfully:

"The tea is ready; we can go to the dining room to talk." The dining room, naturally, was not the dining room of former days either — bright, dazzling, and young. The tea things were already set out, most delicate and lovely.

"Japanese goods," I thought to myself.

Mrs. Manfield poured tea for us, and said:

"This was a prize from a Japanese primary school's fête."

I said nothing; it was a very silent while. Then Helen said slowly:

"At Christmas the Japanese navy is holding a dance; would you be willing to take me?"

"I?"

"That is to say, I have no way of not going," she said, glancing at her mother, "but it is such a troublesome gathering; I should like to ask you to accompany me, so that I might come home a little earlier."

"But what standing have I to take you there?"

"I'll find a way to have them invite you; they have invited many Chinese besides — it's said to be a gathering to fraternize with the Chinese."

"I think there will surely be someone coming to escort you."

"If you and Bai Ping are with me from the afternoon, then even if there are others, we can go together and come back together."

Helen's meaning was very plain: since the fright of that day, she had grown very anxious about her own path.

"Very well," I said. "We'll telephone each other when the time comes."

After tea, back in the drawing room, Mrs. Manfield said to Helen with a smile:

"Haven't you noticed some change in this room?"

"What change?" Helen looked all round.

"Look." Mrs. Manfield pointed at the photograph.

"Ah, you've changed the photograph." Helen went up before the photograph as she spoke, and said to me:

"Do you say this one is better than the one before?"

"I like the one of you on the piano."

"The one hanging in my room?" She laughed. "You went and looked?"

"Yes."

"Then I'll give it to you, because this chapter of my life is about to close." So saying, she went quickly into the bedroom.

When she brought it out, I meant to open the frame, but she said:

"What more? Don't you like this frame?"

"Thank you."

Helen handed me a newspaper, and I wrapped it up.

I was in the middle of a cigarette, so I sat on a few minutes more, and just then the electric bell rang, and the maid came back from the door with a name-card and said:

"Colonel Nomura's car has come for you."

Just as Helen took the card, I took my leave.
