## Impressions of Xu Xu

Xu Xu

The Knight of Sorrowful Countenance

by Joseph S. M. Lau

In the mid-seventies of the last century, through a friend's introduction, I managed to meet Mr. Xu Xu once. We had arranged to meet at a coffee house. Not long after we had sat down, Mr. Xu asked me why Mr. C. T. Hsia had not discussed his work in his A History of Modern Chinese Fiction. I was taken aback. What I said to him then I no longer remember, but I am certain the moment was an awkward one—a very awkward one.

The History was originally an English work, published by Yale University in 1961. The later task of translating it into Chinese was carried through by me and a few friends together. When Mr. Xu asked me why his name was absent from the roll, he was, I suppose, only asking idly, for he must have known that a translator has no standing to question an author's choice of subjects. Mr. Hsia, too, never once put it to me—why this author of "popular fiction," who in the mid-forties had won renown the length of the country on the strength of The Whistling Wind alone, had been "left off the list."

Xiao Si has an essay saying that Xu Xu once, in 1963, taught a course on modern fiction at New Asia College. She went to sit in on it, and the sight of his utterly expressionless manner and his low, sunken voice frightened the students off. She herself, after a few classes, went no more. Teacher Xu took no offence; on the contrary, he would often seek her out for coffee. The coffee house he liked was the Gaohua Hotel in Wan Chai. "The first time I saw him come in," Xiao Si recalls, "he had actually put on white gloves." Stranger still, once seated, face to face with Xiao Si, he had little to say: "now and then he would sigh and say that Hong Kong held no friends worth having, that the literary world had dried up."

It would seem that in my coffee-house meeting with Mr. Xu I was luckier than Xiao Si, for in seeking me out "for a chat" his main object was to learn why Mr. Hsia had been "so blind"; so that when we sat down together to talk, there was always a topic to be had. By comparison, to sit stiffly before one's teacher, watching his coffee spoon stir the black liquid, listening to him complain that Hong Kong had no friends worth having, that the literary world made no progress, and so on and so forth, with no way to slip off and no chance to escape—that, it would seem, was a tedium truly unbearable in life.

That day when Xu Xu met me at the coffee house, whether or not he had white gloves on his hands as he first came in I do not recall—my attention, I suppose, had been drawn to his gaunt and faintly careworn face. Before this I had never met Mr. Xu, nor even seen a recent photograph of him; why, then, on shaking his hand, did I have a sense of having "met him somewhere before"? After I had taken my leave of him, on the road home, it came to me in a flash of lightning: yes—how like Mr. Xu was to the figure on the cover of a Don Quixote I had once chanced upon in an American bookshop! On that cover stood Don Quixote, lance couched and horse rearing, his whole face full of sorrow. The country folk fondly called him The Knight of Sorrowful Countenance.

Beyond a certain likeness in the sorrow-struck countenance, Mr. Xu had no more to do with Quixote—that Spanish "knight" who fought his three hundred rounds against the windmill faction—than a horse has to do with an ox. A man of sorrowful aspect would, one supposes, be in no humour to champion a kind of humorous writing that is "not the least bit serious." Yet, would you believe it, one of the magazines Mr. Xu founded in Hong Kong was actually called Humour. Humour was a fortnightly; it came into the world in 1952. Knowing the magazine to be hard to come by, Xiao Si copied out for our reference five of its "Ten Rules of This Magazine," which served in place of a foreword.

One: This magazine does not confine itself to humorous writing, nor does it hold that humour awakens the age or that humour saves the nation.

Two: This magazine does not seek renown with the powerful, but seeks only to give no offence to the common people.

Four: This magazine serves no god and no ism, but does not object to others worshipping gods, or ghosts, or things.

Eight: Between the two schools of opinion in modern medicine, this magazine holds sleep to be of more account than exercise.

Ten: This magazine does not believe in ghosts, but fears them; should it see a ghost, it will cease publication.

The foreword of a magazine flying the banner of "humour" ought, besides "stating its aims," to seize the occasion for some specimen of the humorous style, no? Yet look at the five rules above: except for the notion that "sleep matters more than exercise," which is a little unexpected, there is nothing here one could call fresh. The prose, too, is truly flat and savourless.

It would seem that Mr. Xu himself was not overly keen on this business of humour. Critics are in the habit of taking Xu Xu for the very type of the "foreign-quarter talent," his work romantic to a fault. I myself, at the age when one "knows not the taste of sorrow," loved to read Xu Xu. In those days no one had yet stepped forward to play language police; rhetoric and grammar were as you pleased, and you might write however you liked. Xu Xu's fiction drew, in its day, no few "fans" who preferred coffee to tea, for his work was steeped in a good deal of "foreign flavour." What threw those fans into still greater raptures was the "Europeanized" scenes and sentences he wrought with such care. Behold: within a boutique coffee shop a young woman sits, chin in hand, idle before the window, gazing sorrowfully at a garden full of autumn, the silver spoon in her hand stirring ceaselessly at the liquid in her cup, as though weighed down with cares. Of a sudden an uninvited guest steps up and accosts her: "Miss, may I sit down and help you drive away your loneliness?"

To personify "loneliness," making it an object that can be driven off, is a very cute conceit, but it is not the language we common folk use at home—people just don't talk like that. Mr. Xu's "doing" humour did not come off, because, one supposes, his temperament was wrong for it. When Xiao Si first read the "Ten Rules" of Humour, she was very young and did not understand what the tenth rule's "should it see a ghost, it will cease publication" referred to. Had Mr. Xu understood humour even a little, he might have offered the youngster a few witty words. But guess how Mr. Xu answered? "He only said, wooden-faced: 'You are young; you have never seen a ghost. Read what I wrote—"The Tail of Mankind: A Myth of the Devil."'" Xiao Si found it and read it, still did not understand, and dared not ask him again. Later, when she had put on some years, she came at last to understand what the ghost was that Xu Xu had spoken of.

Xu Xu had dealings with Lin Yutang from time to time during the Republican years, but in temperament he seems to have taken nothing from the "Master of Humour." Lin Yutang ran The Analects Fortnightly—a solemn, upright name over the most preposterous of contents. Why does a man have an Adam's apple and a woman none? According to Master Lin, when Adam first tasted the forbidden fruit his heart misgave him, so that he ate it half in and half out, and part of it lodged in his throat—and this is the origin of the Adam's apple. Eve ate more freely, the apple went whole into her belly, and "became the womb. It is said that the pains of a woman's childbirth and the foulness of her monthly courses are all a punishment God deliberately laid upon her for eating this apple."

This is one instance of nonsense talked under the borrowed name of humour. Master Lin's uniquely dashing prose had the good fortune to appear in an age when the common mind was still unopened. Such "jokes," making sport of the physiological differences between man and woman, would, were they to appear today, most certainly be denounced and disgraced by the champions of women's rights. In truth, making sport of others is not without its risks—it depends on the standing of the one at whom you pull your faces. Mr. Lin's one unforgivable error was to make sport before the gun-wielding overlords. Among the commandments set out in The Analects was one that ran: "We do not comment on those we despise, but those we cherish we criticize to the utmost."

This was no small matter. Besides humour, it came peddling a mawkish "to beat is to love" philosophy: the blow lands on the child's backside, but the ache is in the mother's heart. Lin Yutang's fond calculation was simply this—that the men in power who took the scolding, on reading this "commandment," ought not to turn hostile, for it is because a man thinks well of you that he scolds you. Among the pieces published in The Analects was one titled "Chiang Kai-shek Is Also of the Analects Party." What Generalissimo Chiang felt on reading it, one cannot say. Might he have thought that, being able to hail the Analects men as brothers, his own worth would thenceforth climb a "level"? What we do know is that the wielders of the power of life and death, who were not of the Analects party, found this fellow Lin Yutang not the least bit amusing. And so, in the fifteenth year of the Republic (1926), the Duan Qirui government issued warrants for the arrest of forty-eight figures from cultural and educational circles. Our Master Lin, promoter of a modern Analects, was on the roll.

In an age when rites and music were not yet in ruins, the Chinese, schooled from childhood in the family precepts, were accustomed to guarded speech and cautious conduct; however bold, they would not, in a public place, let their tongues run loose and say a thing like "a speech should be like a woman's skirt—the shorter the better." "Humour" was, to begin with, no native product. And whether by coincidence or not, the well-known writers of the past hundred years whose writing so often leaves one helpless with laughter have all been given to a fawning worship of the foreign, having drunk, more or less, of some foreign water. I have always been captivated by the Liang Shiqiu of the Refined Hut days in wartime Chongqing. His prose has little waste in it. Hear him tell it in his essay "Nonsense": "Often a caller comes; before I have opened the door, his first words are: 'You haven't gone out?' Of course I have not gone out—if I had gone out, how could I be opening the door for you now?"

The Taiwan poet Guan Guan is truly cool; in his essay "Obsession" he tells us, frankly and openly: "Even dogs have their obsessions—how can a man have none? Some have an obsession with killing; I do not. My obsession is with letters." What is an "obsession"? A "partiality," I suppose; and so he is "obsessed with Laozi's uselessness, obsessed with Zhuangzi's free wandering." It would seem Guan Guan shares our tastes, for he says: "I am obsessed with Liang Shiqiu's Refined Hut. I am obsessed with Lin Yutang's essays. I am obsessed with Shen Congwen and Eileen Chang. I am obsessed with Wang Zengqi's fiction. I am obsessed with the prose of Wu Luqin and Zhang Xiaofeng."

Wu Luqin (1918–1983) was my teacher when I studied at National Taiwan University, giving the courses on English and American prose and on Western literary criticism; open and free by nature, he had no habit of setting a high price on himself. A man who thinks too well of himself will never poke fun at himself. Not a few of Mr. Wu's fine essays make an example of himself, taking himself for the butt of the joke. In his essay "At Sixty-One, Stating My Wish," the teacher said that the years of his life after sixty ought to be called his "surplus years," for the men of his generation, having lived through war upon war and yet survived, ought to count that survival as a "dividend over and above their due."

A man toils all his life, and the time that is his own is precious little. We go to school and study books we hate to study, meaning at heart to give it up, yet fearing to grieve our parents. We go out to work, and at every setback we think of "retiring," yet, remembering that the old and young at home would then go without their evening meal, we can only swallow it in silence. Happily, all this is now safely behind us. The "remaining life" ought, at last, to be one's own.

And so Mr. Luqin set down this famous line: "I am past sixty now, and can no longer go on being so proper."

Mr. Wu wrote with a free and easy hand, because he was open of heart. At one gathering a reporter spoke with him of life and death, and what he said was truly startling: that after his own death he asked "only to rot quickly." Heard for the first time, the remark is not the least bit amusing; but you must understand that Mr. Wu spent the greater part of his life in Taiwan. In those days, at "birthday" occasions, the words one heard most often, in fond wishes and blessings, were "may your fortune be as vast as the Eastern Sea, your years as long as the Southern Mountain." Professor Wu's words spoiled his own scenery and no one else's; hurting none but himself, they may be called harmless enough. Or one might take them for "humour of another kind."

When all is said and done, I feel that Xu Xu—fond of his white gloves, and for all the romance he carried off in his tales of love—because he lacked in his make-up the gene for the art of irrelevance, was truly not fit for the humorous trade of looking one way while talking another.

The World Was Always Cruel

by Liu Bo

Mr. Liu Shaoming wrote an essay, "The Knight of Sorrowful Countenance," recalling Mr. Xu Xu. When the two first met in the seventies of the last century, Mr. Xu suddenly asked why C. T. Hsia's A History of Modern Chinese Fiction had not discussed his work. Mr. Liu, one of the book's Chinese translators, pressed in this way, felt very awkward: "When Mr. Xu asked me why his name was absent from the roll, he was, I suppose, only asking idly, for he must have known that a translator has no standing to question an author's choice of subjects."

Xu Xu's putting the question to the translator was not necessarily idle; there was, in truth, a grievance he could not easily speak. It goes back ten years earlier, to 16 January 1961, when Lin Yutang delivered at the United States Library of Congress a lecture titled "Chinese Literature Since the May Fourth Movement." The text of the lecture was in English; the United States Information Service's World Today magazine in Hong Kong engaged one Shi Dong to translate and publish it, and the translation abridged and even altered the original. Xu Xu pointed out, without ceremony: "In this lecture Mr. Lin mentions me in two places. One is where he speaks of poetry; he says: One exception is Hsu Yu, who now lives in Hong Kong. His lines, instinct with rhythm, come naturally. And the translation turned this into: 'Xu Xu's poetry is still readable; his lines ring out in cadence, their rhythm natural.' The other is where he speaks of the short story: Of the writers of short stories, the works of Lu Hsun, Shen Ts'ung-wen, Feng Wen-ping (less well-known) and Hsu Yu are the best. And the translation turned this into: 'Among short-story writers, Lu Xun, Shen Congwen, and Feng Wenbing (Fei Ming) are the best' (Xu Xu deleted). I naturally will not concern myself with what Mr. Shi Dong the translator thinks of me; but to tamper with Mr. Lin's opinion of me is truly a feeble-minded trick." Lin Yutang's estimate of Xu Xu's poetry was, in its original sense, this: "The worst of it is that these poets, instead of using a varied rhythm, insist on rhyming. Let the last words of two lines be forced into a rhyme, and they take it for poetry. Xu Xu, who now lives in Hong Kong, is an exception; his poetry is full of rhythm and attains to the natural." The translation changed this to "Xu Xu's poetry is still readable"—a great shift in meaning. As for striking Xu Xu's name from the list of China's best short-story writers, that was downright malicious. Why it came to this there is no way to trace; but Xu Xu's "faith" in translators was, in the end, discounted, and hence the awkwardness of Mr. Liu Shaoming already described.

That C. T. Hsia does not mention Xu Xu is not necessarily a denial of his literary achievement. Xu Xu's sudden rise in the literary world began in March 1943, when the Chongqing Sao dang bao serialized his novel The Whistling Wind; and the third section of A History of Modern Chinese Fiction, "The War Years and After (1937–1957)," points out that the state of the literary world in the Nationalist interior during the war of resistance is extremely hard to assess, because many wartime publications and periodicals were never preserved—not in Taiwan, not in Hong Kong, not in America. For want of sufficient materials, the writers who had taken refuge in the great rear area were, in Hsia's field of view, a blur, far less vivid than the writers who had stayed on in occupied Shanghai; so it is no wonder that he says the most gifted new writers of the war years arose not in Chongqing or Yan'an but in Shanghai, and singles out three in particular—Eileen Chang, Qian Zhongshu, and Shi Tuo. The trouble is that Xu Xu looked down on Eileen Chang; he said her "fiction presents a very small range of characters, its material confined to a narrow field of vision, its themes much of a muchness, its touch a heaping-up at whim; here and there in the loose and rambling stretches a flash of talent shows, but there is far too much low and callow showing-off of the pen." To Xu Xu, that C. T. Hsia, in writing a history of modern Chinese fiction, should exalt Eileen Chang and yet not devote a single word to him, who in his day was the more famous by far, could not but leave his mind out of balance.

Surveying Xu Xu's fate—the fading into obscurity after a great blaze of glory, the bustle of half a life and the loneliness of the other half—there are political reasons, but it has also to do with his proud and aloof character. Mr. Dong Qiao put it to perfection: "Mr. Xu's loneliness was a plot, a design, that he deliberately arranged for his own life; and it came, in the end, to be taken for the truth, and is very affecting—like his fiction." That the literary world forgot Xu Xu is no tragedy of Xu Xu's; it is the tragedy of the literary world. But—"there is no help for it: writing the same, Jin Yong became a man of means, while Xiao Tong and Xu Xu died in poverty." So the Hong Kong writer Chip Tsao sighs: "The world was always a cruel place."

Lu Xun and Xu Xu: The Traffic Between Them

by Liu Bo

On 5 April 1934, This Human World, edited by Lin Yutang, began publication in Shanghai. Scarcely had this fortnightly of the familiar essay—self as its centre, ease and leisure as its manner—come into the world than it drew the spoken and written assaults of the left-wing writers. One day Lin Yutang invited Lu Xun to dinner; amid the clink of cups Lu Xun teased: "Do you know which pieces are the ones I wrote?" Lin Yutang answered with a laugh: "Always the few that are not childish." Lu Xun said: "Not necessarily; I am just now learning to write childishly." This lively detail was set down by Xu Xu, who "sat in the lowest place" at the same table.

The Xu Xu of that time was not yet the great writer who, with Ghost Love and The Whistling Wind, would grow famous to the point of purple; he was only a small editor, a beginner at writing who had just found a livelihood at This Human World. About two years before, when Lu Xun lectured at the Third College of Peking University, the two had met without exchanging a word. After Xu Xu came to Shanghai, he had no particular dealings with Lu Xun either. The inward and slightly proud Xu Xu appraised himself thus: "I have little talent for fawning on the great and famous, and so I have never, with any purpose, sought to draw near anyone's gate." He knew that Lu Xun had always mocked Lin Yutang as the Master of Humour and must, he supposed, be sparing of smiles; yet the impression Lu Xun gave him at the banquet was, in two words, exactly humorous. Xu Xu was a little astonished: "Their talk was full of wit; there was really no clear line of 'foe' and 'friend.'"

As an editor of a literary magazine, Xu Xu's first dealings with Lu Xun were chiefly for the purpose of soliciting a manuscript. In its early days This Human World used a great deal of copy; a celebrity like Lu Xun, and a friend of the editor besides—how could he be let slip? Though Lu Xun had written pieces scolding Lin Yutang, Xu Xu did not mind. He said: "I have always had a liberal prejudice: that, as an editor, one should hope for articles of differing opinion to appear together in This Human World; so before long I wrote a letter to Lu Xun asking him to write something for This Human World. I think I said that if he did not approve of the leisurely attitude of This Human World, all the more reason he should write some daggers-and-spears piece for it. He wrote back declining my request, and one line I still remember: '...merely to look on, in quiet, while the masters do their tai chi.'"

This letter that Xu Xu kept in memory must be Lu Xun's letter to Tao Kangde of 7 April 1934. Lu Xun, in replying to Tao Kangde at times, would add at the end a line, "the same to Mr. Xu Xu, no separate note," which shows that some letters were written to the editorial office and not to Tao Kangde privately. On the matter of the manuscript Lu Xun answered thus: "The kind wishes of Mr. Lin and of yourself, urging me not to hoard my poor skill, I feel keenly. But after ten years of fighting my strength is worn out, and I have come to a certain realization: I have resolved that from this year on, unless it be a periodical with which I have some long-standing tie, I will join none, that I may win some leisure to stand with folded arms against the wall and watch the masters do their tai chi—some soaring as if to clutch the void, some wheeling as if to grope the earth—and, looking on in quiet, be content; though the crisis of the familiar essay be right before my eyes, I will not stir."

If a manuscript could not be got, an interview surely could. This Human World ran a column, "Interviews with Writers," and Xu Xu, together with Tao Kangde, wrote once more, jointly signing a letter to Lu Xun asking him to receive them for an interview, to have a photograph taken with his study for a background, and to sit for a group photograph with Xu Guangping and Zhou Haiying. Lu Xun, on getting the letter, replied at once, on 25 May: "The name of 'writer' is a fine one; once, not knowing my own measure, I thought I might make bold to fill out its ranks; of late I have come a little to my senses, and am ashamed to speak of it. Moreover there is no thought in my head, and no study in my lodging; as for 'wife and son,' they have still less to do with the literary world; your three gracious requests I dare not, any of them, accept. Should you one day compile instead a 'Brief Lives of the Pseudo-Writers,' I will lay out my books, put on my airs, and sweep the path to welcome you." All courtesies, and, in a word: I am sorry, I will not do it.

As far back as the Analects period, Lin Yutang's leisurely humour ran clean counter to Lu Xun's fighting posture, and the founding of This Human World was as a whip to a fast horse, hastening the two men's rupture. Between 1934 and 1935 Lu Xun wrote several pieces—"The Vitality of the Familiar Essay," "Think Before You Act," "The Hermit," and others—criticizing the vulgarity of This Human World, and said in a letter to a friend: "As for the likes of This Human World, they are at bottom a narcotic, and their vogue too is a thing to be expected, of a piece with the Chinese fondness for smoking opium." Lin Yutang, of course, would not accept Lu Xun's harsh censure; even in his memorial essay after Lu Xun's death he said, with rancour: "When This Human World came out, the left would not forgive my literary views, and I would not sacrifice my views to fawn on a left that fancied itself enlightened at the crow's first cry; Lu Xun was displeased, and there was nothing I could do. Lu Xun was indeed the older and the more caustic; but I, inclining toward the Confucian clarity of nature and reason, and Lu Xun, whose partisanship ran ever deeper while I knew not what partisanship was—no wonder we jabbed at each other and could not agree."

Xu Xu was Lin Yutang's assistant, and owed to Lin the debt of a man recognized; yet he was not hemmed in by factional loyalties. Toward Lu Xun his attitude was, from first to last, neither blind rejection nor fanatical worship—inseparable from his faith in liberalism. "Lu Xun was not my idol, nor did I approve his thought; but he was a writer I revered," Xu Xu said. "I dare not presume upon Mr. Lu Xun; I would neither call him 'my friend' nor rank myself as his student, still less as his close comrade-in-arms. I am only one who believes Mr. Lu Xun to have been a man of literary genius and literary cultivation. I revere his genius, and for that very reason do not believe him a sage; genius always has the flaw of a warped streak in its character, and Lu Xun could hardly be exempt." To Lu Xun's criticism of This Human World he retorted thus: "What Lu Xun called the pointlessness of This Human World and The Analects means only the so-called 'not revolutionary,' 'not fighting to the death,' or the so-called 'out of step with the pace of revolution.' This attitude of viewing a question from a single aim is precisely the 'urgent' attitude of utilitarianism. In truth, great as China is, how could there be no room for a single This Human World or Analects? And in fact the reason The Analects and This Human World won such favour then was precisely that in those days there were too many writers of grand articles, and those pieces of either revolution or national salvation slowly sank into the tone of the eight-legged essay, while the style of The Analects and This Human World cut cleanly through that eight-legged fog." In the polemic between Chen Yuan and Lu Xun, Xu Xu took Lu Xun's side. In the short piece "The True Story of Ah Q and Yu Jun" he took the knife to Yang Zhensheng's novella Yu Jun, pointing straight at Chen Yuan's "Ten Works Since the New Literature Movement" as padding out its numbers with make-weights: "Contemporary Review may be called a clique of the Anglo-American school, and their mutual puffery went to every length. ...At that time Lu Xun's The True Story of Ah Q had long been a masterpiece known to the whole nation; Chen Yuan, on the one hand feigning objectivity, saying 'I cannot, because I do not respect Mr. Lu Xun's character, therefore refuse to say his fiction is good...' to make a show of fairness, and on the other hand setting the mediocre Yu Jun beside The True Story of Ah Q, to the effect of puffing up Yu Jun." In 1966, Su Xuelin published in Taipei's monthly Biographical Literature an "Essay on the Life of Lu Xun," reviling Lu Xun's character as petty, his temper vicious, his conduct base, and his financial help to poor young writers as small favours to win their hearts. Xu Xu, hearing of it, was much displeased and stepped forward to say: "Su Xuelin's essay on Lu Xun, mean and insidious, seems to go too far. Especially on the point of Lu Xun's stinginess with money, I feel it is the exact opposite of the fact."

Toward Xu Xu, Lu Xun showed throughout the affection of an elder for a junior—encouraging when he did well, criticizing when something displeased him—and even in a 1934 letter to Cao Juren, where he wrote of Xu Xu thus—"As for Tao and Xu, they are Lin's Yan Hui and Zeng Shen, falling far, far short of the Master, but there is no help for that either"—one can see no ill will in it whatever. That Lu Xun did not publish copy in This Human World did not mean he had cut off relations with its editors; on the contrary, when young authors entrusted him with manuscripts to find a place for, and he judged this periodical suitable, he would forward them on. The nineteen-section short essay "Gleanings from the Silt," by Xu Shiquan, was carried, on Lu Xun's recommendation, in issues three, four, five, six, eighteen, and nineteen of This Human World. Toward Xu Xu's own writing Lu Xun took an encouraging and affirming attitude. On 20 May 1934, issue four of This Human World published Xu Xu's discursive familiar essay "On '……' and '□□□□,'" which took to task the human failing and cultural malady of "wordless speech." Lu Xun, having read it, at once wrote a piece, "A Supplement to 'On "……" and "□□□□,"'" in response, pointing out that "……" is a foreign import, like the emperor's new clothes—in fact nothing at all—while "□□□□" is a native product, seen before only in the writings of the ancients, past all supplement, yet now seen in the writings of the moderns, which one would supplement and cannot. Making the topic a springboard, he laid the lash to the literary world's false grandeur. The piece named Xu Xu by name; his patronage of Xu goes without saying.

A check of Lu Xun's diary shows that in the two years from 17 April 1934 to 11 April 1936, Xu Xu wrote sixteen letters to Lu Xun, and Lu Xun replied with ten (two of them jointly to Tao Kangde)—frequent traffic enough. The content of Xu Xu's correspondence with Lu Xun was, apart from the communication of editor and author, the inquiries of a junior to his elder. In December 1935 Xu Xu asked about the Mulian opera of Shaoxing, and Lu Xun answered: "This kind of drama seems to exist only in Shaoxing; it takes Mulian's journeying as its thread to portray the ways of the world, and its language is extraordinarily sharp and striking, which loses its colour turned into common speech. There seems to be no script; short of going under the stage yourself in summer to take it down in shorthand, there is no other way. I think that if one only watches several performances running, one will have it noted down; it is not, after all, hard to do. Now I hear that two of its segments, 'The Little Nun Comes Down the Mountain' and 'Zhang Man Beats His Father,' have already been banned by the men of virtue of Shaoxing, and are sure, in time, to perish hand in hand with the fairy tale and the folk song. I have meant, these many years, to go back in summer and copy it out, but being under gracious warrant of arrest I have not dared act rashly, and there is no other suitable person to entrust it to; if only there were some other busybody, that would be well." This is the only Lu Xun letter to Xu Xu now to be seen, preserved because it was published on 20 August 1939 in issue two of the familiar-essay fortnightly This Human World, founded by Tao Kangde and Xu Xu. With this, Xu Xu had at last realized his long-cherished wish of publishing a Lu Xun "manuscript"; only, Lu Xun had long since passed away, could not see it, and had no say in it.

In the autumn of 1936 Xu Xu boarded the steamer to study in France; in October, Lu Xun died. The next year, Xu Guangping issued in the magazine Zhongliu "Xu Guangping's Notice on Soliciting Mr. Lu Xun's Letters," saying that "the replies Mr. Lu Xun wrote to people of every sort, known to him and unknown, are very great in number, and used up a part of his life... so I, Guangping, think there is a need to put them in order into a volume and give them to the public." At that moment Xu Xu was in France and may not have known. In 1949, when he learned that the Lu Xun Memorial Committee was soliciting Lu Xun's correspondence, he very much wished to hand over the letters Lu Xun had written him; but these letters, together with many precious scrolls and paintings, were stored in his old home on Xiaoshani Street in Ningbo, and there was no going to look for them. Soon he hurried off again to make a living in Hong Kong, and for the thirty years thereafter never once set foot back in his native place, so that the whereabouts of the Lu Xun letters became unknown. Of this Xu Xu was not without regret: "In an age of upheaval I ran east and west and preserved none of them; lately someone found in Mr. Lu Xun's diary that our correspondence ran to ten or so letters and hoped I might copy them out for him, but this too I was unable to do."

The most widely known episode in the traffic between Lu Xun and Xu Xu is that Lu Xun copied out with his own hand and presented to Xu Xu two poem-scrolls. In 1968 Xu Xu wrote an essay, "Mr. Lu Xun's Calligraphic Treasure and Kind Words," recalling it: "Lu Xun's letters were all written with a brush on white xuan paper, written most neatly, and I was much ashamed to see them, for I always wrote with foreign paper and a foreign pen. I thought his hand very lovely, and so I wrote a letter saying I should like to ask him to write me a scroll; he wrote back and actually agreed, but said, humorously, that he would not write maxims and the like; so I bought xuan paper and sent it to the Uchiyama Bookstore, and I think I wrote a letter too—I was then about to marry, and I said I hoped that in setting up house I might have his calligraphy to grace my cold studio—and before long he wrote to me, telling me to go to the Uchiyama Bookstore to fetch it." That was 22 March 1935, when Lu Xun "wrote a scroll each for Imamura Tekken, Masuda Wataru, and Feng Jiancheng, and two for Xu Xu, all transcribing the Jinqian yuxiao." What he wrote for Xu Xu were a hanging scroll and a horizontal strip. The hanging scroll read: "The books I read of old are all laid high on the shelf; only the classic of self-approval have I now clean forgotten. When the hour comes, sing a stanza—who knows who made it? Take care not to mishear it; besides, it is of no use. From Suonan Weng's Jinqian yuxiao, the first; transcribed at the elegant request of Mr. Bo Xu. Lu Xun." The horizontal strip read: "In the Jin family's fragrant lane a thousand wheels sound; in Yang Xiong's autumn chamber no vulgar noise. A line of Li Changji's; transcribed at the request of Mr. Bo Xu; the third month of the year hai. Lu Xun." From the form to the choice of text, one may feel Lu Xun's regard for Xu Xu.

In 1950 Xu Xu took leave of wife and daughter and went off alone to try his fortune in Hong Kong; the mounted Lu Xun manuscripts, awkward to carry, were left behind in Shanghai. In 1954 he divorced Ge Fucan, and the two scrolls stayed all along in the Ge family's keeping. In 1970, to spare the Lu Xun manuscripts the ravages of the Cultural Revolution, Ge Fucan donated them to the state. The Chronicle of the Shanghai Lu Xun Memorial Hall records: "On 12 January 1970, Ge Fucan (Xu Xu's wife, a retired teacher of Huaihai Road Primary School, Shanghai) donated to this hall two pieces of Lu Xun's calligraphy (transcriptions of Zheng Sixiao's Jinqian yuxiao and Li He's Lüzhang fengshi). In 1982 this hall gave in return The Complete Works of Lu Xun and other volumes, in token of thanks." These two pieces were first included in Lu Xun's Poetry Manuscripts, edited by the Lu Xun Memorial Hall and published by the Shanghai People's Fine Arts Publishing House in 1961; because Xu Xu's identity as a "reactionary man of letters" was sensitive, the editors concealed his name. This book was later reprinted by the China Printing House of Hong Kong, and Xu Xu, chancing to see it in the market, was extraordinarily grieved and indignant. He said: "The original dedication line had all been cut away. I do not know whether this was sold off after my family fell into ruin, or offered up in response to the solicitation of Lu Xun's calligraphy, or seized when the house was ransacked and so confiscated. It is well that Mr. Lu Xun is dead; otherwise, having given these two pieces to me, he too might have faced being purged and called to 'give an account,' might he not? ...These two pieces have been in my hands all along, and if they should now be handed over to Lu Xun's memorial hall, that too is only right; but to cut away the dedication line to me shows all too plainly the small-mindedness of the Chinese Communists." When the Shanghai Lu Xun Memorial Hall re-edited and republished Lu Xun's Poetry Manuscripts in 1976, it restored the dedication line, and the later editions of 1983, 1991, 1998, and others all kept the calligraphy in its original form unchanged. Xu Xu thought of it from time to time, and once wrote to the Shanghai Lu Xun Memorial Hall asking that a copy be reproduced for him to keep as a memento, at his own expense, but got no answer; not until 1980, before his daughter Ge Yuan went to Hong Kong to visit family, did Shi Boying and Zhou Guowei of the Shanghai Lu Xun Memorial Hall bring reproductions of the two pieces and ask her to convey them on their behalf.

On 20 September 1980, on his sickbed at the Ruttonjee Sanatorium for tuberculosis in Hong Kong, Xu Xu beheld these two treasures he had longed for day and night. By then he was in the late stage of lung cancer, only fifteen days from the end of his life. Ge Yuan's memoir, Waning Moon, Lone Star: My Father Xu Xu and I, sets down the scene: "I took out the reproductions of the two calligraphic pieces Mr. Lu Xun had once written for my father, and with Brother A's help unrolled them one by one before him, and told him their history, their whole beginning and end. My father sat up in the sickbed, lifted his head and looked closely, his lips faintly stirring, as though he would say something and yet made no sound, and nodded with a deep and lingering meaning."

In a flash, forty-odd years have gone by. In that moment, language was already superfluous.

Xu Xu on Avenue Joffre

by Shen Xicheng

To depict Mr. Xu Xu is not hard: six words will do it—"gaunt, melancholy, sparing of speech." He was the very type of the literary man of the May Fourth era's ten-li foreign quarter, singular in temperament, distinctive in bearing. My first meeting with Xu Xu was for an interview. In the mid-seventies I was an editor at Daren Weekly, and for each issue's cover figure I thought of Xu Xu. The editor-in-chief, old Mr. Sun Baoyi, said to me, "This Mr. Xu is not an easy man to handle; when you go to interview him, be very careful indeed!" Hearing it, I had a knot in my heart, and hurriedly prepared: I dug out Xu Xu's fiction and essays, picked several volumes and read them over several nights, and, reckoning I understood him a little, was not so afraid. It was an autumn dusk, I think; the homeward sails were all in, the weary birds back at their nests. Walking east from the Man Yee Building to the courtyard of City Hall, I found the withered leaves a bitter yellow, fallen blossoms carpeting the ground, all desolate and still. I went into the Garden coffee room; Xu Xu sat on a wooden chair in the corner, one hand holding a coffee cup, the other holding a book he was reading. A brown thin-flannel jacket, pale-yellow trousers, set off by a dark-green checked shirt, a goose-yellow scarf about his collar—out of the elegance there breathed a faint air of the scholar. When I had sat down, I handed over my card and stated my errand. Xu Xu first taught me how to order, gently enjoining: "Coffee, then! The coffee here has something of the flavour of the 'Xiaoxiao Café' on Bubbling Well Road in Shanghai—" "The coffee at 'DD's' on Avenue Joffre wasn't bad either!" I put in. Xu Xu seemed surprised at my acquaintance with the "DD's" café, and I hastily explained the reason in the Shanghai dialect: "I have read Dinggong's Old Tales of Shanghai; it is mentioned there." Before the coffee was brought, he had already closed his book, toying with the spoon in his right hand; after a while he set it in the cup and stirred, saying that only so would the coffee give up its flavour.

When the steaming coffee was brought, I too stirred it a few times in imitation of him. Xu Xu took a sip: "Mr. Sun tells me you want to interview me—good! Mr. Shen, which of my books have you read?" I hurriedly reported the ones I had spent several nights chewing through: Jianghu xing, The Absurd English Channel, The Lament of the Mentally Ill, Chuanxin ji... "And what did you make of them?" Xu Xu asked, word by word. I was dumbfounded; I had truly never expected him to ask such a thing. Usually, interviewing a "figure," it is the other party who does most of the talking while I take it down, and back at the magazine I write it up once through and hand it to Mr. Sun to correct and fair-copy; the person interviewed rarely asks one to give an opinion of his work, and I could only say what came to mind. Xu Xu said, "Mr. Shen, from what you say, you don't understand my work very well—how are you to interview me, then?" His tone was deadly earnest, no joke at all, and all at once I was mortified. The blood rushed up, both cheeks flushed red; I wished I could find a hole in the ground to crawl into. It was Xu Xu, again, who smoothed it over for me in Mandarin: "Let's do this—you go back first, and I'll have my student Yuanyuan write it." I felt as though I had received a pardon, and bolted off like a wisp of smoke! Once outside the coffee house, I remembered I had not settled the bill. Go back and pay? I dared not! A week later, Xu Xu telephoned the magazine and arranged for me to meet him once more at the Garden. It was dusk again, the withered leaves a bitter yellow again, fallen blossoms carpeting the ground, but the homeward sails were not yet all in, and the weary birds were leaving their nests. I began by apologizing for not settling the bill the last time; my expression, perhaps, was odd, for Xu Xu laughed—the only time I ever saw him laugh. Before him, as before, stood a cup of coffee; the difference was that a cup already stood waiting before my seat. He said Yuanyuan's interview was written and posted to old Sun. The business being done, why had he called me out? I wondered. Xu Xu took from his brown briefcase a book and set it before me—it was, to my astonishment, Where Time Goes. "This is a collection of poems I wrote in Hong Kong; you haven't read my poetry, I take it?" I understood then: to mention only the fiction and the essays was not enough; only with the poetry added was the tally complete. That day we talked for an hour, and I asked him why he always liked to live in Kowloon City. Xu Xu answered wonderfully: "The few streets over that way—Grampian Road, Junction Road, Nga Tsin Long Road—are quite like Avenue Joffre in the Shanghai French Concession!" Xu Xu, a writer who lived forever in the old Shanghai French Concession—he never once made a home of Hong Kong!

The Most Noble A

by Chip Tsao

The crisis of the European Union, with the tumour that is the euro metastasizing, is for the moment past all remedy.

The trouble began with Greece—everyone knows that. But Greece's gross output is only 2.5 percent of the European Union's; that Greece has rotted has but limited effect on Europe.

But the crux lies in the two words "confidence." In a large family, when the smallest little brother comes down with malaria, the whole household feels ill at ease—the more so when Spain, Portugal, and Ireland have caught it one by one.

As things stand, it is a fate self-invited. Set beside industrial powers like Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden, Greece has no exports. Greece has only two things by which to earn its money: shipping and tourism. Global shipping is in decline—it once produced the shipping king Onassis—and with oil prices rising, of the two great taps one is already running poorly. Tourism, meanwhile, suffers under the burden of the euro's high value: ask any Hong Kong housewife—if she had a wad of euros in hand, would she rather go to France to sweep up Louis Vuitton, go to Harrods department store in London, or go to the shores of the Aegean to stroll and meditate like Socrates?

That the euro's monetary union is unworkable—one needs no honours degree in economics from Cambridge to see it—is plain from the standpoint of common sense. Nor is that all: over the past ten years Greece caught the American-style bubble disease of Wall Street, fancying that, having joined the great family, it could live off whatever lay to hand—the hill by the hill, the water by the water—with subsidies past all spending. Wall Street's banks lent to Greece for all they were worth, and blew the euro into a bubble.

The only course now is either to drive Greece out of the Union, make it use its old currency again, let it devalue wholesale, and—after the pangs of surgery—learn once more to scrape a living from the bottom; or else for Germany, France, the Netherlands, and Northern Europe to withdraw from the eurozone and let the euro devalue of itself.

Both would bring great upheavals, but there is no help for it: the good times only last so long. Greece, Portugal, and the rest, infected with that Yankee future-tense spending of the revolving credit card, have laid their own bombs beneath themselves.

Then comes the cutting of spending, the paring of welfare, the levying of a heavier profits tax on the rich—and so there is salvation. But here is the trouble: such strong medicine can never get past the voters' gate. Western democracy has bred an inertia; grown used to holding out a great palm and taking for free, it is like Cinderella—when the clock's hand passes midnight and she is struck back to her true form, it cannot be borne.

This is the policy of "Austerity." The word, with a capital A, has become a proper noun in modern British history. Having lived through two world wars, in which British soldiers died in great numbers and the treasury was drained, Britain, needing to recover after the war, had no choice but to enforce the rationing of eggs and meat. Postwar Britain had to use ration coupons, the aristocracy not excepted; the whole people tightened their belts, right up to 1954.

From thrift to luxury is an easy passage. Hong Kong in the fifties was likewise all wooden huts across the hills, the poor everywhere; the Hong Kong people of that day knew little of the "mother country," not knowing that in those years the mother country was just as poor. The east end of London was all nineteenth-century slums; in the documentary films the government shot then, the dwellings of the London workers, like Hong Kong's tenement flats, were cramped and mean; the British Chancellor of the Exchequer had to cross the sea to borrow money from America, suffering every cold shoulder and humiliation from the Eisenhower government. The money borrowed had to be planned and used well; a national health service was rolled out; and the Jewish economist Keynes was, at that time, the framer of Britain's economic policy.

Britain never "exploited" Hong Kong; the suzerain had no time to spare for itself. The fifties are, for the whole world, a stretch of years remembered with mingled feelings: the Republican men of letters who came south, of whom Qian Mu is the representative, huddled in Kowloon City and Diamond Hill; film people waited on Lion Rock Road for work to begin; refugees were more than the jobs could hold; and the colony had only a few textile mills run by the Tian, Tang, and Wang families of Jiangsu and Zhejiang who had fled south—whom Grantham held fast, marking out land for them to build factories and so resolving the colony's crisis of unemployment.

In those days everyone was poor, the bosses included, and all had to reckon every penny. To catch the air of that time, look at the new poetry of Shu Xiangcheng, at Huang Guliu's The Story of Shrimp Ball: a poverty-smell of cross-streets and narrow lanes comes pressing upon you. Truly, in these works, between the very lines, there is a breath of the age, and lamplight too, and the smoke of human dwelling.

The colonial government's hands-off ways had, in those days, one great advantage: freedom. Those with a skill to their name—whether they could "cook words to stave off hunger," or do kung fu in the streets, or tell fortunes by a little face-reading learned in Shaoguan—racked their brains in the streets and struggled on the line between life and death; and how many of them, unable to hold out, died wronged and unremembered? There was no help for it—writing the same, Jin Yong became a man of means, while Xiao Tong and Xu Xu died in poverty. Directing the same, Li Hanxiang became a minor rich man, while King Hu among others, and many besides, fell into ruin. The world was always a cruel place.

The crisis of the European Union is not, in fact, hard to solve; one need only turn the clock back to the old days of common sense: born with no money, you must not look to the government to hand it out; having found a job, the wages meager, you grit your teeth and bear it, and do not lightly hand your boss his walking papers. The Chinese especially are a people that can share hardship but not share wealth; and having caught that Wall Street bubble of money-farming, Hong Kong was long ago deep in the drug's grip—kick it? Like Europe, that is hard now.

And yet today's second-generation rich have never known what poverty means: waited on from childhood, they then flaunt their wealth. The rich heirs know, above, nothing of how high heaven is or how deep the earth; in the middle, nothing of the ways of the world; and below, they neither respect nor do anything but look down on those poorer than themselves. Many of them are arrogant and rude, lacking the art of getting on with people; set beside their fathers' generation, with its warm attentiveness and good faith of "every man a sworn brother," the rich heirs go to study in the West with none of the Chinese wisdom of the world and yet keep none of the Western spirit of Fair Play either; young in years, they already own mansions and yachts, and, having no experience of the world, are early surrounded by shoe-shine flatterers. When will Hong Kong riot? Once Donald Tsang of the Special Administrative Region—this colonial generation—has withdrawn from the stage of history, and the rich heirs have taken over the world of commerce, there will surely be riots—when even Britain, Greece, the countries of North Africa, and Wall Street in New York have all had their occupations, what standing has Hong Kong, this self-styled "international city," to "keep itself alone unspotted"? By what right the exception?

Once there is rioting, the mainland side will surely send troops to suppress it. On the eves of "the First of July" and "the First of October," the Chinese garrison stationed in Hong Kong has held "exercises," one of them with live ammunition. To this unusual movement the Hong Kong people paid no heed at all—which shows how dull their political senses are.

My own generation was fortunate: touched from above by the lingering influence of the Republic, and steeped in the middle by the British culture of the postwar years. I detest brand names most of all; I have never driven a new car worth more than two hundred thousand; my food may be simple, but my dress must be plain. Studying in Britain, I had many good friends of the older generation, and they told me how poor the British had been, not how rich. Though I never left home to become a monk, in that stretch of days studying in Britain I came to know deeply this word Austerity—of all the words in the English vocabulary, I hold it the most noble, the most laden with meaning, and the truest emblem of the English-speaking peoples' spirit of taking their stand in life. The time and the place of my incarnation were both so good: had the roving of the sperm carried me ten kilometres further north, toward Shenzhen, or had I been born twenty years later, with no billionaire father, then, being a Chinese, I should never have been delivered from that round of suffering—and so I hold that in my former life I did many good works; I believe in cause and effect.
