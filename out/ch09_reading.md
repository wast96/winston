### The Action Section and the "Red Squad"

### "Master Magician Hua Guangqi" — Gu Shunzhang

The Third Section of the Central Special Section was also called the Action Section, and its chief was Gu Shunzhang, who held the post concurrently.

Gu Shunzhang (1895–1935), originally named Gu Fengming, also went by the aliases Liming and Zhang Hua and the stage name Hua Guangqi. He was a native of Baiyang, in Baoshan County, Jiangsu. He was born into a lumpen-proletarian family, and this marked his whole life. Idle and shiftless from boyhood, he took a Green Gang hoodlum boss for his master. After coming to Shanghai he first worked as a fitter in the machine shop of the British-owned Nanyang Brothers Tobacco Company's cigarette works in Yangshupu. Good at pushing himself forward, forever bullying the workers, he won his employers' favor and rose to be a "number one" (a foreman). Those who knew him well described him this way: short and stout, round-faced, rather dark, his nose first sunken and then jutting out, his eyes cruel, his whole face full of menace. He was sinister and treacherous by nature. At the factory he had joined the union, and he was at once an activist in union work and a leading figure in the secret societies. He had knocked about the underworld since boyhood, and he carried many of the lumpen-proletarian's weaknesses and had picked up a good many bad habits. He had little schooling. He loved to play with guns and clubs and to brawl, could shoot with both hands, and could do magic. (Lin Jinsheng, the agent the enemy assigned to guard him for close to two years after his defection, said: Gu Shunzhang's cultural level was not high, but he had a very ready tongue. He was no great shot, as I found when we tried it out face to face. Besides his conjuring, he had one gift I much admired: his art of disguise was extraordinary. Let him make a man up even a little, and you could hardly know him again. I remember he once fitted a set of false teeth over his own, and his whole look changed completely.) Under the stage name "Master Magician Hua Guangqi" he performed in public at Shanghai's Great World amusement hall and at many private parties. He also opened the "Qixing Magic Shop" at 22 Xieqiao Road, selling magician's props, its glass cases full of every kind of outlandish little curio. His grand illusions — "Sawing the Lady in Half" (the "lady" played by his wife, Zhang Xinghua), "The Talking Skeleton," and the like — drew warm applause. When he performed, Red Squad members of the Central Special Section — Tan Zhongyu, Zhang Alian, Zhang Wenhu, Zhang Wenlong, and others — all took the supporting parts. Also linked to Gu Shunzhang was a servants' hiring agency (a so-called "recommendation shop") near the Marris market (today's Chongqing Road market), its rooms always crowded with men and women waiting to be taken on. The proprietor, Zhao Jingfu, was not a Party member but worked on the Party's periphery. Through it the Party could reach into the great gated mansions and come by all sorts of "treasures" ordinarily beyond its grasp.

Gu Shunzhang threw in his lot with the revolution in 1924, worming his way into the Chinese Communist Party. After joining he served as secretary of the workers' branch at the factory and led several strikes at the Nanyang cigarette works. He then took up work in the Shanghai District Committee. During the May Thirtieth Movement of 1925 he led the tobacco-union workers out on strike, and showed himself fairly bold. To carry the strike through, the Party had him organize secret workers' pickets to put down turncoats who informed to the enemy and scabs the enemy kept in its pay. Afterward the Party sent him to organize a strike in the machine department of the British Tramway Company, where he crushed the scabs who broke the strike. In struggle he cared nothing for method or tactics and had no notion of policy; he would seize a few scabs, beat and curse them, and let them go without any education. Gu Shunzhang also served as chairman of the Shanghai dockers' union in Pudong and was put in charge of training armed workers. After the May Thirtieth Movement he was moved into the CCP Central organs. In the autumn of 1926 he was sent to the Soviet Union with Chen Geng and Lu Liu to study intelligence and security work. Returning home from the Soviet Union in February 1927, he became director of the military commission of the CCP's Jiangsu-Zhejiang District Committee. Later he came back to Shanghai, and on March 21, 1927, he took part in the Third Armed Uprising of the Shanghai workers, led by Zhou Enlai and others.

In that uprising Gu Shunzhang was a member of the Special Military Commission that directed it under Zhou Enlai (the Special Military Commission had five members — Zhou Enlai, Gu Shunzhang, Yan Changyi, Zhao Shiyan, and Zhong Rumei — with Luo Yinong and Wang Yifei added later, making seven). He was also commander-in-chief of the workers' armed pickets, and personally directed the fighting of the Pudong district pickets. Under the workers' fierce assault, within two hours they took the Pudong police station, smashed the local garrison, and beat off the Northern warlord troops stationed at the Qinci Niangniang Temple. The other districts of Shanghai were soon carried, and the struggle came to a head in Zhabei, where the enemy's forces were thickest. At the time the Shandong troops under the warlord Bi Shucheng, wanting to make their retreat easier, had gathered mainly at Zhabei, where the railway station stood. At four that afternoon Gu Shunzhang rushed to the Zhabei front, the fiercest fighting and the enemy's strongest point, and together with the workers fought the reactionary army through the streets. In the fighting he charged everywhere on sheer brute daring. Carrying a red flag, he led the way into the puppet Shanghai city government. After the victory, on March 23, the Party led the calling of a Shanghai citizens' assembly, and at the citizens' representative assembly Gu Shunzhang was elected a member and deputy mayor of the "Shanghai Special Municipality Provisional Government." Here his arrogance and self-importance stirred the people's displeasure. Later, when the risen workers suffered the Kuomintang army's slaughter, he again showed himself timid and wavering.

After the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup, Gu Shunzhang went with the CCP Central to Wuhan, where at the Fifth National Congress he was elected to the Central Committee and made chief of the special-service section of the Central Military Commission. He took part in the August 7 Conference, where, in the emphasis then placed on promoting men of worker origin, he was elected to the Provisional Politburo. In July 1928 he was elected to the Central Committee at the Sixth National Congress. On November 14 that year the Standing Committee of the Politburo resolved to set up a Special Committee to lead the work of the Central Special Section; Gu Shunzhang was a member of this Special Committee, took charge of the Special Section's day-to-day work, and served concurrently as chief of the Third Section. In 1930 he became a member of the presidium of the Central General Action Committee. In September 1930 and January 1931, at the Third and Fourth Plenums of the Sixth Central Committee, he was elected an alternate member of the Politburo.

Gu Shunzhang held high posts, yet his character was base and his political level low, quite out of keeping with the leading role he then held. Du Ning (Yang Zhihua), who at the time worked in the CCP Central organs in Shanghai and knew him well, once said: "The work Gu Shunzhang held was very important, yet he never read documents from one day to the next and voiced no political opinion of any kind. … Though he is an old Party member, and though in the past he carried out the Party's decisions very bravely, what was the substance of that bravery? He grasped neither doctrine nor policy, and all his work was empty of revolutionary consciousness. The common lumpen-proletarians of Shanghai are ready enough to be brave; but for that very lack of revolutionary consciousness they cannot protect themselves, cannot fight through to the end for the whole proletariat's interest — some even become traitors to their class. Gu Shunzhang is the very type of the Shanghai lumpen-proletariat."

There was another man — Wang Fanxi, then still in the Party, later expelled for joining the Trotskyists — who said: In 1929, when he and the eighteen who had studied at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow returned to Shanghai, Zhou Enlai, director of the CCP Central Organization Bureau, met them and afterward opened a training class for them in a three-story foreign house at the end of Xinzha Road, where the Central leaders all came to speak. He was much struck by the talks of Zhou Enlai, Yun Daiying, and others, and said they were warmly received. Gu Shunzhang came and spoke too, but the impression he left was this: "Gu Shunzhang lectured us on secret work — he was then running the Central's special service (section). The man reeked of the hoodlum; thought was beyond him; and though he came of worker stock and was uncommonly quick, one cannot in the end entrust the leadership of revolutionary special work to such a man. For his later treachery and villainy, I think those who esteemed and promoted him cannot be free of blame. No revolution can do without such men, and to refuse the adherence of the lumpen-proletariat is to make no mass uprising at all; but as to how to tell their positive from their negative role (to see clearly that negative role, which far outweighs the positive) — Marx and Lenin have taught us fully. The CCP's leaders did not take that lesson to heart, and so in long years of revolutionary work they suffered at the hoodlums' hands times without number. Gu Shunzhang is only the most famous instance." (Reminiscences of Shuangshan.)

The American writers John Byron and Robert Pack, writing of Gu Shunzhang in The Claws of the Dragon, say: "A child of the Shanghai slums, Gu Shunzhang craved political and social importance. As a member of Du Yuesheng's notorious Green Gang, Gu could easily reach the intelligence of every kind of Shanghai police force, for every police force was riddled with Green Gang members. He frequented nightclubs and smoked opium, and won a reputation as a magician, performing often under the name Hua Guangqi at the famous Great World entertainment center. The Great World was a tall building, shaped like a wedding cake, that stood on Avenue Edward VII; inside it offered every form of amusement, from dancing and prostitution to shooting galleries, fantan, and roulette. Zhang Guotao, who worked with Gu Shunzhang during the May Thirtieth Movement, noted that 'his looks and habits were somewhat those of a Shanghai dandy.' Sheng Yue, a former Communist who knew him, likewise spoke of his 'extravagant and profligate private life and conduct.'"

The Third Section of the Central Special Section worked mainly to lead the Red Squad in striking at the enemy's agents, in eliminating traitors, in protecting the Central's responsible comrades, in rescuing those who were arrested, and in guarding the Central organs and the various meetings the Central convened, so that they could be held in safety. After his defection, in the book he wrote in July 1933 to curry favor with the enemy, The Theory and Practice of Secret Service Work, Gu Shunzhang set down what he knew of the Third Section of the Central Special Section: "The Third Section does protection work only. If the C.P. is to hold some important meeting somewhere today, it notifies this section to go to the meeting place and see whether there is any unsafe ground. Their protection relies entirely on electrical devices and weapons; the moment danger arises, they split at once into two parts, one to hold off the enemy, one to get the important people out — that is protection inside the house. As for protection outside, cyclists have the duty of patrolling, and many small tradesmen also carry the charge of protection. If anyone comes to attack them, or it is learned that someone has caught wind of a meeting place, they at once ring the doorbell by a prearranged special signal, to warn all those inside charged with protection to be specially on guard. If, to protect the Party's safety, they must sometimes kill an enemy, there are many ways — on the road, or in the enemy's house, as the case dictates, with nothing fixed. As for those of our own who turn themselves in, they try to take them alive and bring them back for questioning, to see whether there is some further plot, some cell or other members, and only then do they kill. … Besides this, the Third Section has one more duty: if the C.P. runs short of funds, they must use extraordinary means, or raise money by other methods, to supply the Party's expenses. But we should note that what they do is not for their own selfish gain, but for their Party."

### The "Dog-Beating Squad" That Struck Fear in the Enemy

The chief members of the Third Section of the Central Special Section were the "Red Squad," which specialized in eliminating agents and traitors. The Red Squad was also called the "Red Terror Squad," but everyone called it the "Dog-Beating Squad." Its numbers were not large, but its capacity for action was enormous, its fighting power strong, its record rich. To guard the safety of the CCP Central and the Shanghai municipal Party organization, to strike at enemy agents, to eliminate traitors and inside spies, it fought bravely and rendered outstanding service, a deterrent force that made enemy agents and traitors quail at its name.

The Red Squad appeared well before the Central Special Section was born; it can be traced back to the workers' pickets of the May Thirtieth anti-imperialist patriotic movement of 1925 and to the armed workers' pickets of the Third Armed Uprising of the Shanghai workers, which grew step by step into the later "Red Squad." Chen Yangshan, a veteran of our Party's intelligence work, has said:

The "Red Squad" came into being through a process. It formed step by step with the development of the revolution and the needs of the struggle. After the great May Thirtieth strike of 1925 broke out, the various union systems, to keep order among the strikers and guard against scabs and hoodlums, each set up workers' pickets. In those days a factory of about a thousand hands would have a picket unit of a few dozen to a hundred or two, its members chosen from among the union activists and the Party and Youth League members. Besides keeping strike order in its own factory or enterprise, the picket unit was charged with mediating disputes among the workers, and it was an important department of the union.

In the latter part of June 1925, the workers had been out on strike more than twenty days, and imperialism and its various running dogs pressed harder to break the strike. On the one hand they took advantage of the hardship some workers fell into after striking, sowing discord in an effort to split the workers' ranks; on the other they used hoodlums and scabs to attack, in an organized and calculated way, the strike leaders, union heads, and activists. Some comrades were even secretly beaten to death or badly wounded.

To meet this, the Party organization and the union on the one hand actively sought to help the workers in difficulty with their concrete troubles; on the other, against the hoodlums and scabs who broke the strike, they issued open warnings or dealt firm blows, according to the gravity of each case. To deal with the handful of scabs who broke the strike openly and went on doing evil in defiance of warnings, we picked from the workers' pickets a few comrades who could shoot, and gave them the task of putting down hoodlums and scabs. When we killed these men of many crimes, the workers backed us and society sympathized; the upshot was that we beat back the enemy's schemes and strengthened the strike.

From the May Thirtieth period down to the eve of the Third Armed Uprising of the Shanghai workers, the pickets had no guns. In the Third Uprising we first seized part of the police's arms, and later some weapons from the warlord troops, and so the pickets were armed.

After the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup of 1927, the White Terror lay over Shanghai and the whole country. Now not only did the problem of hoodlums and scabs go on, but there was also the problem of dealing with Kuomintang agents and the Party's own traitors and inside spies. The Kuomintang agents and traitors knew the pattern of our activity, they threatened us more, and the struggle against them was harder. The Party organization was hounded without cease, and many Communists were killed. In the face of the enemy's frenzied slaughter, most Communists rose to fight back and held to the struggle; a few opportunists were frightened out of their wits, grew despondent and wavered, turned their coats and surrendered, and became shameless traitors.

The lesson of the mass arrest and killing of Communists and revolutionary masses made our Party feel keenly the need for some comrades set apart to deal with the sabotage of Kuomintang agents and traitors. And so, following the earlier experience of dealing with hoodlums and scabs during strikes, and building on the workers' pickets already organized, the CCP Jiangsu Provincial Committee first chose comrades who were politically firm, good shots, and well acquainted with the Shanghai scene, and formed a crack armed unit to take on the task of eliminating agents, traitors, and inside spies. Everyone called them the "Dog-Beating Squad."

That September the CCP Central organs moved back to Shanghai from Wuhan in stages. Among the comrades who returned from Wuhan, those who had belonged to the special-service section of the Military Commission also did this kind of work. So in Shanghai there were now two "Dog-Beating Squads," one under the CCP Central and one under the Jiangsu Provincial Committee. Because intelligence work had not yet developed, the "Dog-Beating Squads" ran into quite a few problems in practice. Not all traitors acted in the open; some were secretly bought and used by the Kuomintang's special-service organs to do the Party harm, or were planted inside to spy. Against such hidden enemies the Red Squad had no way to act. In the struggle against traitors and agents, the Red Squad played its part in punishing the open traitors and struck down some scoundrels; but for want of intelligence work it often did not understand a situation, and some who should not have been killed were killed.

When Zhou Enlai came back to Shanghai from Hong Kong, the CCP Central organs had already moved from Wuhan to Shanghai in stages. He looked into how the "Dog-Beating Squad" was faring, and against the enemy situation of the moment held that to protect oneself well one must attack the enemy. To strike accurately, one must first know the enemy's situation, and for that intelligence work must be built up. Otherwise we would be forever on the defensive with no defense enough — how could we win? In clandestine work, intelligence is decisive; it is the Party's eyes and ears. So the Central Special Section quickly set up an intelligence section to keep timely track of the enemy's movements. He pointed out, too, that the Central and the Jiangsu Provincial Committee now had two "Dog-Beating Squads," their command not unified, their steps not in time, rather in disorder, and that they ought to be brought under one command. On Zhou Enlai's instruction, in 1928 the two "Dog-Beating Squads" were merged, the ranks put in order, and bad elements cleared out. Tan Zhongyu was appointed to head this work, under the Central Special Section's leadership. Intelligence work was strengthened at the same time. The Red Squad's actions now rested on accurate intelligence, and it struck hard and true. Because we could learn the enemy's movements beforehand, our work turned from passive to active. Once put in order, the Red Squad threw itself into battle in a new posture and won victory after victory. After the "Dog-Beating Squad" put down several open traitors one after another, it broke the swagger of the traitors and agents for a time, and these degenerates no longer dared move openly.

The Red Squad was very famous in Shanghai. For a first period, the Kuomintang agents and police and the foreign detectives and constables all knew the Communist Party had four guns. From their analysis of the cases that had occurred in Shanghai, they reckoned that all the "Dog-Beating Squad's" weapons were four pistols. Yet these four guns could charge where they liked and range over all Shanghai. Because the might of the Shanghai working class from the Great Revolution still lingered, the Red Squad eliminated traitors and agents with ease, its record brilliant, striking shock and dread into the enemy. In those days the Party's activity in society was almost half-open; the constables and police dared not stir. Now and then they found leaflets, but they dared not interfere. Only when someone informed on the Party's activity would the constables dare to move against a case, and even when they came out to make arrests, they did it with a great clamor, hustling people off.

As noted, in the Red Squad's early days, because no counter-espionage intelligence system had yet been built, it could not stop the enemy's sabotage beforehand and could only trace the traitors' activity afterward and put it down. After the Central Special Section set up its intelligence section, the Red Squad's work changed greatly. Through intelligence work it could get timely word from inside the enemy of the activity of traitors and spies, and without much trouble could eliminate traitors accurately and in time. Sometimes it could uncover the enemy's plots beforehand, striking first and forestalling trouble. In this way the Red Squad gained eyes and ears, its work grew more active, and it carried out its tasks more smoothly. In this period the Red Squad completed many hard tasks without mishap, and this was inseparable from its close coordination with the intelligence section's counter-espionage work.

By the latter half of 1929 the Red Squad had grown to its peak: it had more than forty men, its chief weapons Brownings (a make of pistol), revolvers, and Mauser pistols, along with a batch of chemical grenades that made the enemy weep — everyone called them "tear bombs." After any action, if the enemy gave chase, they would throw one out to slow the enemy down (the throwers themselves put on special goggles and were unaffected). At that time the Third Section could shift weapons in from outside at any time, and could even bring in machine guns (in this period the Special Section had built up contacts among the gunners of the Kuomintang artillery battalion stationed in Pudong). It could also buy guns from the foreign firms at any time, delivered by men the firms sent. For an important action, the First Section would send men to a rice shop to buy sacks of rice in bulk, load the sacks onto a truck as cover for the Red Squad's fire, and make of it a moving fortress driven to the scene to shoot at the enemy. The Special Section's means of transport were small cars and motorcycles, but it relied mainly on bicycles. These bicycles were all unregistered, without plate or number, and at a pinch were simply thrown away.

The Red Squad came under the Third Section. In its work, apart from its close ties with the Second Section, which handled intelligence, it also worked closely with the First Section, which handled general affairs. Whenever the CCP Central held an important meeting, the First Section would first choose the site with safety in view, and then the Red Squad would send armed men to guard it — some patrolling nearby on bicycles, some disguised as small tradesmen keeping watch before and behind the house and at the mouth of the lane, some standing guard at the meeting place. Should danger arise, the men on the outer ring would give the alarm, and those at the meeting place would split at once: one part to hold off the enemy, one part to get the responsible comrades away.

In May 1930 the site of the preparatory meeting for the Congress of Delegates from the Chinese Soviet Areas was arranged by the First Section. This meeting drew many people, ran a long time, and made for a heavy load of work. Two sites were prepared, so that if trouble broke out at one they could move to the other. One was a row of foreign-style houses behind the Carlton Theatre (today's Changjiang Theatre) on Burkill Road (today's Fengyang Road); the other was a foreign house at the corner of Avenue Road and Carter Road (today's junction of West Beijing Road and Shimen No. 2 Road). In fact both sites were used, one after the other.

The First Section also rented many hotel rooms for the comrades attending, and laid in a good deal of fine clothing for them to change into before they entered the meeting place. It was ruled that once a delegate entered he could not come out, but ate and slept on the spot, for safety's sake. The meals were seen to by two comrades brought in specially by the First Section under Zou Zhishu. During the meeting, comrades of the First Section and the Red Squad all turned out to stand guard and do protection work. At the same time Chen Geng had Liu Ding come forward and rent another room close by, setting up a station there as a passage for the swift removal of those at the meeting: at need one could cross from the roof of the "red building" to the roof of Liu Ding's room and go out through his lodging's front door. The First Section had Li Yimang act as householder, with another man kept specially as accountant, seeing to the meter readings, the water and electricity bills, and the rent, so that no outsider entered the main building where the meeting was held. The Third Section also sent the Red Squad to stand guard around the building, ready, should the enemy get wind of it and constables come to search, for the Red Squad to open fire at once, while a man appointed to the task led the delegates out through Liu Ding's lodging.

The Red Squad's chief task was to eliminate traitors and agents and to guard the safety of the CCP Central organs. From 1928 to 1931 it fought a whole series of actions to eliminate traitors and agents. Some of the great cases — such as the elimination of the traitors He Jiaxing, Bai Xin, and others — shook Shanghai deeply.

In brave struggle to eliminate traitors and spies the Red Squad was tempered and grew, becoming an important force in guarding the Party. Quick-witted and good in a fight, it carried out its task of protecting the Party to the highest mark. The brilliant victories the Red Squad won were inseparable from the Central's careful nurturing of it. In its early days, for want of experience, it had made mistakes in its work, but the Central had taken timely and effective measures to overcome them, drawing the lessons, putting the organization in good order, and strengthening the political education and technical training of the Red Squad's members. In 1930 the Central Special Section also ran a short training course on Carter Road for the Red Squad. The training covered: a close knowledge of the names of Shanghai's streets and lanes, the routes, and the particulars of where people lived, and a knowledge of the enemy's police and agent organs and the hoodlum gangs; the art of disguise, so as to conceal oneself and reconnoiter and watch the enemy effectively; how to break the enemy's codes, write secret letters in invisible ink, open one's own shackles after arrest, and wreck the enemy's cars by putting sugar in the petrol; and marksmanship. Whenever they practiced shooting, they hired a boat out past the mouth of the Yangtze and learned to shoot at sea.

It was precisely the Party's boundless care and careful nurturing of the Red Squad, forever raising the red fighters' political awareness and professional skill, that made its fighting strength rise step by step and grow ever firmer. Against traitors and spies it waged a brave and stubborn struggle; in guarding the Party's safety it created an imperishable record. Moving like ghosts through a Shanghai held down by strong enemies, Chinese and foreign, tempered into steel through long, sharp, and tangled struggle, in countless stirring feats of daring, in countless heroic deeds that move one to song and to tears, it left a deep mark on the hearts of Shanghai's working people, and to this day the old Shanghai hands who know that history of struggle speak of it with relish.

The American writers John Byron and Robert Pack, in their book The Claws of the Dragon, set down the Red Squad's Shanghai activity as they saw it:

"For actions of some force, the Special Section hired a band of skilled gunmen. Known by the name of the 'Red Squad,' they provided bodyguards for the whole Party's senior cadres in Shanghai and had charge of the safety of the various meetings; they also kept close watch on the agents of the Investigation Section, famed for their special effectiveness against Communists, and marked them as targets. But the 'Red Squad's' most important duty was to punish and deal with those Communists who wavered in their proper duty or defected to the Kuomintang side — a task that won the 'Red Squad' the name of the 'Dog-Beating Squad.'"

"In their actions the 'Red Squad' used every kind of ruse and disguise. Sometimes the gunmen dressed as beggars or as small tradesmen crying their wares along the street. The women of the hunting parties would sometimes make themselves up as household maids; for the more elaborately planned actions the gunmen would dress as film cameramen and technicians. But the theatricality of the 'Red Squad's' methods could never hide the deadly seriousness of their task or the crispness of their action."

"From the middle of 1927 on, the Party's guards carried out a whole series of attacks and defenses. The archives of the Shanghai Municipal Police record that in the International Settlement alone, between 1927 and 1931, the 'Red Squad' eliminated at least forty opponents or traitors — a minimum figure, for many of the 'Red Squad's' actions were never uncovered. They were often quietly forgotten, so that their fate remains unknown. Beyond this, many actions were carried out in the French Concession and in the Chinese-administered Greater Shanghai, and so the Shanghai Municipal Police kept no record of them. … Of the forty 'Red Squad' casualties that the Shanghai Municipal Police did record in detail, at least thirteen were Communists who had gone over to the police to become informers or defected to other anti-Communist bodies. Traitors were usually shot dead, but the 'Red Squad' too suffered grievous losses: twenty-six 'Red Squad' members charged with assassination were arrested by the Shanghai Municipal Police. For a secret organization, the Communists' casualties were very high, and in a society where informing and private dealing were the means of survival, this was the inevitable result."

### The "Soviet Congress" as Recorded by Li Yimang

The earth-shaking struggles the Red Squad waged in those years stirred people within the Party and without, and left a deep impression on the Shanghai public as well. Li Yimang, then working in the CCP Central organs, saw these Red Squad struggles in Shanghai with his own eyes. When the preparatory meeting for the Congress of Delegates from the Chinese Soviet Areas was held in May 1930, Li Yimang was charged with setting up a "temporary family" and acting as the householder of the meeting site, and he took part in the meeting. In his memoir The Blurred Screen he set down in detail the whole course of this meeting, which the Central Special Section had organized with such care:

In May 1930 a national Soviet congress was held in Shanghai. This meeting was convened in the name of the CCP Central Committee and the All-China Federation of Trade Unions. So the preparatory work was all in the charge of Lin Yunan of the Federation. The preparatory office was at the corner of Avenue Road and Carter Road (today Nos. 690–696 at the junction of West Beijing Road and Shimen Road). The meeting itself was held in a row of upstairs rooms behind the Carlton Theatre (today's Changjiang Theatre), on Burkill Road (today Fengyang Road). Before the meeting, the Central decided I should be the householder of the house where it was held. Zhao Yimin and Li Yichao (a woman, also called Li Kuntai) had only lately come back from the Soviet Union — they had both returned in the winter of 1928 — so I made one of them my younger brother and the other my younger sister, and since I still had a wife and two little boys, put together we made a very convincing family.

Zhao Yimin worked in the Northeast before Liberation and after Liberation was a deputy head of the CCP Central Liaison Department. It is Li Yichao who is to be mourned: she was a Sichuan woman, a native of Yibin County, famed for its Wuliangye liquor — none other than the celebrated Zhao Yiman, who later joined the Anti-Japanese United Army in the Northeast and gave her life. Her time as a family member was short, but she was open and bright by nature, and being a Sichuanese too and a Li as well, our ties as comrades, fellow-provincials, and clansfolk were very good. After this "family" broke up we still met privately a few times, but afterward I did not know where she went to work, and I too left Shanghai. Not until the early days of Liberation, when the film Zhao Yiman came out, did I learn that Zhao Yiman was Li Yichao. She had gone to the Northeast as early as 1932 and taken part in local and military work there, serving as political commissar of a regiment, and was killed at Zhujiang in Heilongjiang in 1936. All this I learned only after seeing the film Zhao Yiman. In the accounts now given of Zhao Yiman's life, whether in Shangzhi County where she died or in Yibin County where she was born, there is nothing of her taking part in the Soviet congress, of this stretch as a member of my Li family. Probably, besides Zhao Yimin and me, there is now no one left who knows of this brief passage of her life.

This apparatus was organized with great care by Gu Shunzhang and his people; I merely presented myself to the world as a householder. The kitchen was theirs to run, the car was theirs, the furniture and fittings of the house were their doing, and the watch inside and out was set by them. We were nothing but grown-ups and children playing mahjong downstairs and running the gramophone, screening the meeting. Besides our temporary family, of course, there were the working staff attending the meeting, who did not live there but came and went. I remember He Chang, who sadly died later in Jiangxi; Ruan Xiaoxian, who later defected; Li Chunfan, that is, Ke Bainian, who did Marxist-Leninist theoretical work in Shanghai in the 1930s, served after Liberation as director of the American and Australian department of the Foreign Ministry, and later was ambassador to Denmark and to Romania; and of course Lin Yunan. Who else there was, I cannot clearly recall.

Those attending this meeting came to some forty-seven or forty-eight in all. The Red Army delegates were two to a corps, one officer and one soldier, and since they all came from the army I knew none of them. But I do remember two comrades: one from Jiangxi, who may have represented the Third Army Corps, Teng Daiyuan, and one from Hubei, He Changgong. Teng Daiyuan in particular became known for going to Fourth Avenue and buying a woman's raincoat. The CCP Central's delegates were probably Xiang Zhongfa and Li Lisan, and the All-China Federation of Trade Unions' delegate was probably Xu Xigen; since Lin Yunan handled the preparatory work for the Federation, whether he was a delegate is not clear. As for Shanghai's other mass organizations, the Youth League Central's delegate was probably Hu Junhe, and the League of Left-Wing Writers' delegate was surely Rou Shi. Besides, one Hu Yepin (a Fujianese) and one Feng Keng (a Guangdong woman, also called Feng Meiling) also took part; whether they represented the Anti-Imperialist Grand Alliance or the Mutual Aid General Society is now hard to say. Rou Shi later wrote a piece, "A Great Impression," on his experience at the meeting. Sadly these three comrades went on to the Oriental Hotel meeting of January 1931 and were all arrested and killed.

This meeting was held in very formal style: not only was a hammer-and-sickle flag hung, but the Internationale was sung too. The political report was given by Li Lisan; there is a written text, so I will not dwell on it. Since it was led by Li Lisan, and this was just the period when the Li Lisan line was taking shape, all the meeting's documents carried the color of the Li Lisan line — unavoidable, and there was no help for it. In giving his report, Li Lisan left his text to say one thing: "When we have taken Wuhan, we will tell the Third International." This grand pronouncement flew at once through the whole house, so that even we householders, men and women, came to know it, and it may truly be said to have startled the room. Looking back now, this congress, for all its heat, overrated our subjective strength and laid the theoretical ground for the Li Lisan line. He drew up ten documents: one, the national political situation and the tasks of the Soviet areas; two, the law on Soviet organization; three, the expansion of the Red Army and the arming of the peasants; four, a labor law; five, a provisional land law; six, an address to the workers of the whole country; seven, an address to the peasants; eight, an address to working women; nine, an address to the young; and ten, a manifesto of aid to the oppressed nations of the East in their revolutions. So many documents, and only words worked over — whether they fit the facts, whether they were too fussy, in any case the meeting was held. Because Li Lisan played the leading role, I think this congress had no great effect on the Soviet movement's actual development afterward.

Most of those attending this meeting did not use their real names, and one cannot make out who was who. For instance, the chairman of the first session (May 21) had no name or surname at all, only "C.P. Delegate" written down. The agenda of the eighth session (the afternoon of May 23) was the report, discussion, and conclusions on the Red Army question, with Liao Taicang as reporter, though which Red Army delegate that was I do not know. This time I helped keep the minutes, and afterward on the documents the three characters "Li Yimang" were noted after the word "minutes." Li Chunfan (Ke Bainian) also served as a minute-keeper at the congress.

Of course, once the meeting ended, this temporary family broke up.

This is the fullest account of the meeting I have found, and so I have quoted it in full, for the reader's pleasure.

### No Mercy in Eliminating Traitors

As noted above, the chief task of the Third Section was to strike at agents and eliminate traitors. "A fortress is most easily taken from within." In waging clandestine struggle under a savage White Terror, traitors and inside spies did the Party enormous harm. Through the informing and betrayal of traitors and inside spies, some Party organizations were destroyed and some of the Party's responsible comrades killed.

From the time the first counter-espionage tie, Yang Dengying, was drawn out of the enemy's own detective organs, working together with intelligence men planted inside the enemy's military and police organs, the Central Special Section could promptly learn the movements of enemy agents and the activity of traitors and inside spies, and put them down at any moment. This was all the more so once, by the end of 1929, Yang Dengying had won the growing trust and favor of the Kuomintang agent chiefs Chen Lifu and Xu Enzeng, and had built up close ties with the concession police of the British and French Concessions; add to this the intelligence section's men planted inside the Kuomintang agent organs of Nanjing and Shanghai, and it became possible to grasp the enemy's intelligence beforehand, smash the enemy's plots beforehand, and avoid great losses. When the Nanjing Kuomintang agent organs and the Shanghai concession police moved to wreck our Party's plans and orders, the Central Special Section could learn of it in time through Yang Dengying and its other ties spread through the enemy. In this period, though the enemy came to know a good deal of our Party's own hiding places, whenever someone informed and the enemy was ready to make an arrest, the ties planted inside the enemy could give us word beforehand, and only then would they send out the constables and detectives; the result was that most got away and turned danger to safety. To break the enemy's swagger, the Special Section, on the CCP Central's instruction, also used this line to eliminate in secret those traitors who turned themselves in and informed to the enemy.

In August 1928 Dai Bingshi turned himself in to the Kuomintang and defected, becoming an inside spy. Yang Jianhong, who then had charge of the Kuomintang's agent work in Shanghai, handed this traitor over to Yang Dengying and told him: this man will supply important intelligence when the time is ripe. A few days later Yang Dengying was ordered to carry a sealed official letter from the Songhu Garrison Command to the political department of the British concession police. The writer was none other than Dai Bingshi, the inside spy who had wormed into our Party; he had given the Kuomintang's Songhu Garrison Command intelligence on the secret organ of the workers' union in the Hudong district. Tan Shaoliang, deputy at the concession police, at once asked Yang Dengying to go with him to Pingliang Road to search our Party's organ, and seven people were arrested. Among them was Dai Bingshi's sister-in-law.

After the secret arrests, Yang Dengying at once reported to Chen Geng and had us deal with Dai Bingshi. Before long Dai Bingshi came directly to Yang Dengying to plead: he said the case had been his own doing as informer, and that Yang Jianhong had promised beforehand that, so as not to bring him under suspicion, they would arrest his sister-in-law along with the rest, and release her the moment all the arrested were handed over to the Songhu Garrison Command. Now he asked Yang Dengying to make good Yang Jianhong's promise, so that his wife would not nag him day and night. Yang Dengying reported all this to Chen Geng, and after the Central's careful investigation Dai Bingshi proved to be indeed the inside spy who had wrecked the Pingliang Road organ. It at once approved the Red Squad's putting this spy to death, so that he should do the Party no greater harm. Red Squad members eliminated him while he was waiting at the Andong Hotel on Hubei Road for his sister-in-law.

In the summer of 1929 the case of Chen Weinian, who had secretly turned himself in and defected to become an inside spy, was also uncovered. At that time Tan Shaoliang introduced to Yang Dengying a hoodlum named Wang Songsheng (who later became an agent), and had Yang keep in touch with him. Tan Shaoliang said: Wang Songsheng knows the Communist Party's affairs best and is well worth using. Not long after, Wang Songsheng got hold of a secret Party document, a Central Circular (under the cover name Huazi Bulletin), brought it to Yang Dengying, and asked whether he wanted it. Yang Dengying feigned haggling with him and took the chance to sound out his depths, and learned that he meant to sell documents to the concession police as well. Yang Dengying asked where the documents came from. Wang Songsheng said it was a man named Chen Weinian who, to raise money, had entrusted him to sell the Communist Party's documents and intelligence. Wang Songsheng said: if you want them, there are other secret publications and documents, and I can keep supplying them from now on; he offered, too, to introduce Chen Weinian, who furnished these documents, to Yang Dengying, so that Yang could deal with him directly thereafter.

Yang Dengying at once reported all this to Chen Geng. Chen Geng told Yang Dengying to meet the traitor Chen Weinian and buy up the documents in the traitor's hands. Yang Dengying sent for Wang Songsheng, said he wanted to buy these documents, and said he would meet Chen Weinian to talk price. A few days later Yang Dengying stood a meal, and had the hoodlum named Wang bring Chen Weinian along. The Red Squad sent men too, to learn Chen Weinian's face; after the meal they tailed him home and found out where he lived. Then they set a watch on Chen Weinian's lodging, setting up a stall of picture-books at the mouth of the lane where he lived, to watch those who came and went, learn his social ties, and mark the times he came and went and the pattern of his movements. After a short investigation it was found that Chen Weinian had not only secretly turned himself in and sold documents, but was a spy of the Songhu Garrison Command planted inside us, and did great harm. To get still more money, he had sold the documents again to a hoodlum of the British concession police, and so had fallen into our hands.

Because the Special Section made a careful investigation and a thorough plan, this case was cracked in under a month. In the end the Red Squad executed the traitor at the mouth of the lane.

In October 1930 Huang Dihong, a graduate of the first class of the Whampoa Military Academy, had lately come back to Shanghai from Moscow. Seeing the Kuomintang reactionaries everywhere boasting of their "victory in the bandit suppression," he grew despondent about the revolution's prospects and resolved to betray the Party and go over to the enemy. He wrote secretly to Chiang Kai-shek, saying he was "out of favor" in the Communist Party, asking to meet Chiang face to face but wanting no one else to know; he also told Chiang the place where Zhou Enlai was to meet him and his own contact address, and asked Chiang to send someone to make contact.

The first to see letters of this kind was, as usual, Chiang Kai-shek's agent chief Chen Lifu. Chiang passed it to Chen Lifu to handle, Chen Lifu passed it to Xu Enzeng, and Xu Enzeng gave it to Yang Dengying to go and make contact with Huang Dihong, telling him the method: the place was the Nanjing Road post office, with both sides holding a briefcase and a newspaper.

Huang Dihong, also called Huang Jinghun, was a native of Pingjiang County, Hunan. In 1924 he had passed into the first class of the Whampoa Military Academy, in the sixth student team. After graduating he stayed on at the academy, working in the compiling office of the political department, whose director at the time was Zhou Enlai. Later Huang Dihong went to study in the Soviet Union, and had lately come back from there.

On word from Xu Enzeng, Yang Dengying reported Huang Dihong's secret surrender to Liu Ding (Liu Ding kept in touch with Yang from time to time). Liu Ding, hearing it, felt the matter grave, and on the one hand had Yang Dengying hold the case off for a while, on the other reported it to Chen Geng to pass up to the Central. Zhou Enlai knew this man, having been in direct touch with him. Huang Dihong had indeed written asking Zhou Enlai to meet him at the post office beside the Sincere Company. On getting the report, Zhou Enlai instructed the Special Section first to isolate Huang Dihong and at the same time to make a thorough investigation and learn whether it was true. Cunning as this traitor Huang Dihong was, twice Yang Dengying arranged to meet him at an appointed place and sent men to wait, but he did not come either time. With a guilty conscience he moved house often, and he was not easy to find. But after careful investigation the Party proved it altogether true that Huang Dihong had written to Chiang Kai-shek to turn himself in, seeking to collude with the enemy in a great blow against the Party. The Central then resolved to put the traitor Huang Dihong secretly to death. The Central Special Section pressed its reconnaissance and at last found Huang Dihong's lodging and eliminated him in time. It rid the Party of a great menace and guarded the safety of Zhou Enlai and the CCP Central, so that the enemy's plot came to nothing.

Over these several years the Red Squad also killed a number of other shameful traitors, inside spies, and hateful agents and detectives. Two of the great cases that once shook Shanghai were the elimination of the inside spies He Jiaxing and He Zhihua, the couple who sold out Politburo Standing Committee member Luo Yinong, and the elimination of the traitor Bai Xin, who sold out five men including Politburo member Peng Pai and alternate Standing Committee member Yang Yin.
