## Chapter 8. The Four Beauties of Emei

The rainstorm had been like a prodigal breaking at dead of night into some rich woman's scented chamber: sudden in the coming, and as swift in the going. Yet once it had passed, all that it had touched lay nourished by it, and altered.

The leaves of the spring wood had been washed to the green of jade; and the blood on the corpses had been rinsed away as well, so that scarcely a death-wound could be found on them. Yet of those ten-odd men, not one was left alive.

By the time they came upon the bodies, Sikong Zhaixing was already gone.

"He leaves us his dead," said Shangguan Danfeng bitterly. "Are we to coffin them for him?"

"These men were never killed by him," said Lu Xiaofeng. "He has always been sparing of murder."

"If not him, then who?" said Shangguan Danfeng.

"The man who sent them to set the fire," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"You mean," said Shangguan Danfeng, "that he was afraid we'd trace him through them, and so silenced the lot?"

Lu Xiaofeng nodded, his face grave. Of the three things he hated most in this world, killing came first.

"But he could have let them go," said Shangguan Danfeng. "Why must he kill them to stop their mouths?"

"Because a dozen men with their right hands hacked off are an easy thing to find," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Shangguan Danfeng sighed. "And yet the killing served no purpose. We know where they come from all the same."

"You know?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Can't you see they're of the Blue-Robe Tower?" said Shangguan Danfeng.

Lu Xiaofeng was silent a long while; then, slowly: "I can see only one thing."

"And what is that?" said Shangguan Danfeng.

"I can see that you'll be hurrying off to the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour," said Lu Xiaofeng, "to send men back with coffins and gather up the dead."

Shangguan Danfeng shot him a glare, then dropped her head and bit her lip. "And what else do you see?"

"And that then, of course, you'll have them heat you water, and take a bath, and choose the most comfortable room in the place, and settle down to a good long sleep," said Lu Xiaofeng.

He smiled, and added, "Don't forget the place is all your own now."

***

Lu Xiaofeng lay back in a great tub of hot water with his eyes closed. To be soaked to the skin by the rain, and then to find somewhere for a hot bath, was a very pleasant thing indeed. His luck, he thought, had held well enough after all; the big brass kettle on the stove beside him was near the boil, and the room was full of the warm breath of the water, so that a man felt safe and snug.

Hua Manlou had bathed, and by now was surely asleep; Shangguan Danfeng had surely reached the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour. Loath as she was, ten thousand times over, she had gone off meekly enough, for all the world as though she truly did as Lu Xiaofeng bade her. That pleased him too. He liked an obedient girl.

Only he could not shake the feeling that the thing had not gone as it should, that somewhere in it there was something amiss, though for the life of him he could not say where.

Yan Tieshan, before he died, had confessed the old wrong; Huo Tianqing had promised to settle the account. The charge the Great King of the Golden Roc had laid on him was a third discharged now, and had gone smoothly enough.

What was there, then, to be discontented with? The rain had long since stopped; now and then a drop fell from the eaves, and the night wind came fresh and clean. Lu Xiaofeng sighed, put his wandering thoughts away, and did his best to be a man content with his lot.

Just then he heard, all at once, the sound of the door. He had not heard wrong: the door had indeed been pushed open.

But whether his eyes played him false he could not tell, for the people who came in from outside were four women.

Four young and beautiful women; beautiful in their persons, and beautiful in their bearing too, their close-cut clothes setting off the slender grace of their figures. Lu Xiaofeng liked best a woman with a fine waist and long legs; and their waists, as it happened, were all fine, and their legs all long.

Smiling, easy and unabashed, they pushed in at the door as though they had never seen that there was a naked man sitting in a bath in the room. And yet those four pairs of bright and lovely eyes were every one of them fixed on Lu Xiaofeng's face.

Lu Xiaofeng was no bashful man; but now he felt his face burning, and needed no mirror to know that he had gone red.

Someone laughed and said, "They say Lu Xiaofeng has four eyebrows. How is it I see only two?"

Another laughed. "You see two; I can't see even one."

The first to have spoken was the tallest, with a pair of long, fine phoenix eyes that seemed, even as she smiled, to carry a killing air fit to press the breath out of you. Anyone could see she was not the sort of woman who poured a man's bathwater.

But she walked over and lifted the kettle from the stove, and smiled. "The water seems to have gone cold. Let me heat it up for you a little."

Lu Xiaofeng eyed the steam off the kettle. He was startled enough; but to stand up naked before four women was more courage than he had, and a whole kettle of water at a rolling boil, tipped over him, would be a good deal less pleasant still.

While Lu Xiaofeng was still wondering whether it were better to rise or to sit still, he found of a sudden that, had he wished to move, he could not have moved at all.

The girl who had not once spoken, and looked the meekest of them, had drawn from her sleeve a short sword a foot and more in length, its blade flashing keen, and laid it against his neck. The cold breath off the steel raised gooseflesh from behind his ears to his shoulders.

The tall, phoenix-eyed girl tipped the boiling water slowly into his bath-tub, and said lightly, "You'd do best to keep still. My fourth sister looks gentle and mild, but she has never yet blinked at killing a man; and this water's just off the boil. Scald you, and if it doesn't kill you it'll take a layer of skin."

As she spoke, she poured. The water in the tub had been hot to begin with; now it was scalding past bearing.

The sweat had started on Lu Xiaofeng's brow, and still only a quarter of the boiling water had gone from the brass kettle. Were the whole kettle poured out, the man in the tub would lose a layer of skin at the least.

Then Lu Xiaofeng laughed. He actually laughed.

The girl who was pouring glared at him out of those charming, imperious phoenix eyes, and said coldly, "You seem merry enough."

Lu Xiaofeng did look very merry. "I only find it funny," he said, smiling.

"Funny? What's funny about it?" She poured the faster.

But Lu Xiaofeng went on smiling. "If I should ever tell people that the Four Beauties of Emei topped up my bathwater for me while I washed, why, it would be a wonder if a single soul believed it."

For he had guessed, it seemed, who they were.

The tall, phoenix-eyed girl gave a cold laugh. "I didn't think you'd have an eye in your head after all. Quite right; I am Ma Xiuzhen."

"And this one, who kills without a blink," said Lu Xiaofeng, "would that be Shi Xiuxue?"

Shi Xiuxue's smile grew gentler still. "But when I kill you," she said softly, "I'll be sure to blink."

"So we've no wish to kill you," said Ma Xiuzhen. "We've only a few questions to ask. Answer quickly, and this kettle won't be tipped again; but if we have to wait till it's poured dry..."

Shi Xiuxue sighed, and took it up. "...then I'm afraid you'll be about cooked through."

"A pig cooked through you can still sell as roast pork," Ma Xiuzhen sighed. "A man cooked through is fit for nothing but to be thrown to the dogs."

Lu Xiaofeng sighed too. "I seem near enough cooked already. Why don't you hurry and ask?"

"Very well; I'll ask you," said Ma Xiuzhen. "Did my martial-brother Su Shaoying die by Ximen Chuixue's hand?"

"Since you know it already," said Lu Xiaofeng with a wry smile, "why come and ask me?"

"And where is Ximen Chuixue?" said Ma Xiuzhen.

"I'm looking for him myself," said Lu Xiaofeng. "If you see him, you might let me know."

"You truly don't know?" said Ma Xiuzhen.

"It's only when I'm drunk that I lie to women," said Lu Xiaofeng, "and I'm quite sober yet."

Ma Xiuzhen set her teeth, and of a sudden tipped a good deal more of the boiling water down, and said coldly, "You'd best speak honestly before me."

"How could I be anything but honest now?" said Lu Xiaofeng wryly.

"The woman who's with you: is she truly the princess of the Golden Roc?" said Ma Xiuzhen.

"She is, and no lie," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"The Great King of the Golden Roc is still living?" said Ma Xiuzhen.

"Still living," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"And it was he who sent you to find Yan Tieshan?" said Ma Xiuzhen.

"It was," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Who else did he send you to find?" said Ma Xiuzhen.

"He'd have me find Shangguan Mu and Ping Duhe as well," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Ma Xiuzhen frowned. "And who are these two? How is it I've never so much as heard their names?"

Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "The names you've never heard would run, I'm afraid, into the millions."

Ma Xiuzhen glared at him.

Lu Xiaofeng sighed again. "I've no clothes on, and when you glare at me like that, I blush."

His own face had not reddened; Ma Xiuzhen's had. She turned abruptly, set the brass kettle back on the stove, straightened her dress, and made Lu Xiaofeng a formal bow.

Shi Xiuxue's sword came down as well.

Four neatly-dressed young beauties bowing all at once to a naked man in a bath: had you ever seen such a thing, you could not have dreamed, even in a dream, what it looked like.

Lu Xiaofeng seemed dumbstruck; he could not think how these four overbearing, high-handed girls had turned so meek and mannerly, all in a breath.

Ma Xiuzhen bowed low. "The Emei disciples Ma Xiuzhen, Ye Xiuzhu, Sun Xiuqing and Shi Xiuxue, by our master's command, are come to beg Master Lu to a plain midday meal tomorrow. Will Master Lu do us the honour?"

Lu Xiaofeng sat stunned a while, then said with a wry smile, "I'd like nothing better; only, even had I wings, I could never reach the Xuanzhen Temple on Mount Emei by noon tomorrow."

Ma Xiuzhen grinned. "Our master is not at Emei either. The old gentleman waits even now at the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour for the honour of your coming."

Lu Xiaofeng started again. "He's come too? When?"

"He arrived only today," said Ma Xiuzhen.

Shi Xiuxue said sweetly, "If we'd never been to the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour, how would we know of last night's doings?"

Lu Xiaofeng laughed again, wryly, of course.

"If Master Lu will honour us," said Ma Xiuzhen, "we'll trouble you no further, and take our leave."

"You've nothing else to ask me?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Ma Xiuzhen smiled and shook her head, gentle and mannerly now, as though she had clean forgotten that a moment since she had been for boiling a man alive.

Ye Xiuzhu, who was an honest soul, could not help laughing. "We've long known Master Lu's great name; that's why we dared come to you only while you were bathing."

"In truth," said Lu Xiaofeng wryly, "you might come whenever you pleased and ask me whatever you pleased, and I'd refuse you nothing."

Shi Xiuxue blinked. "Master Lu truly isn't angry?"

"How should I be angry?" said Lu Xiaofeng. "I'm merry as can be."

Shi Xiuxue was taken aback. "We treat you so, and still you're merry?"

Lu Xiaofeng smiled, and this time it was a true smile. "Not only merry," he said, "but grateful to you besides, for the fine chance you've given me."

Shi Xiuxue could not help asking, "What chance?"

"You could break in on me while I bathed," said Lu Xiaofeng easily; "so if I should break in on you while you bathe, why, you'd surely not mind either. A chance like that doesn't fall to every man. How should I not be glad of it?"

The faces of the Four Beauties of Emei went red as one; they spun round and rushed one another out of the door.

Only then did Lu Xiaofeng let out a sigh. "It seems," he murmured, "that next time I take a bath, I'd best keep my trousers on at least."

***

The place where Lu Xiaofeng had bathed was a kitchen, and outside it lay a small courtyard, and in the courtyard grew a white-fruit tree. The night was still and clear, the first-quarter moon hung in the treetop, and the heavy shade of the leaves shut off its light; and there in the shadow under the tree a man stood, utterly unmoving, tall and straight, his robe white as snow, and across his back, slung slantwise, an oddly-fashioned long sword in a black scabbard.

The moment the Four Beauties of Emei rushed out they saw this man; and seeing him, they felt against their will a chill run from their hearts to their very fingertips.

"Ximen Chuixue?" cried Ma Xiuzhen, her voice failing her.

Ximen Chuixue looked at them coldly, and slowly nodded.

"You killed Su Shaoying!" said Ma Xiuzhen in fury.

"You want revenge?" said Ximen Chuixue.

Ma Xiuzhen gave a cold laugh. "We were out searching for you, and little did we think you'd have the face to come here yourself!"

Ximen Chuixue's eyes lit of a sudden, lit terribly, and he said coldly, "I do not kill women. But women have no business learning the sword; and she who learns the sword is a woman no longer."

"Rubbish!" cried Shi Xiuxue in a rage.

Ximen Chuixue's face darkened. "Draw your swords. Come at me together."

"There's no need for all of us," said Shi Xiuxue sharply. "I'm enough on my own to kill you."

She looked the gentlest and mildest of them, and was in truth the most fiery, the worst-tempered of the four. She fought with a pair of short swords, in the sword-form handed down from Gongsun Daniang, the famed sword-dancer of the Tang. On her fierce cry the swords were in her hands, their light flickering like a dragon loosed in the sky, like lightning struck down out of it; and blade and body together she flung at Ximen Chuixue.

Then a light cry sounded: "Wait." The three words were barely out when a man appeared among them, all at once.

Shi Xiuxue's twin swords had only just darted out when she found that neither could move; the tips of both blades were caught, held, between two fingers of the man who had come from nowhere. She had not even seen how his hand had moved; she pulled with all her strength, but the sword-tips seemed to have grown roots in his grasp.

Yet the man's look was easy as ever, and there was even a smile on his face.

Shi Xiuxue's own face had gone red with anger. "I didn't think Ximen Chuixue kept a helper," she said with a cold laugh.

"You take him for my helper?" said Ximen Chuixue coldly.

"Is he not?" said Shi Xiuxue.

Ximen Chuixue smiled coldly, and struck of a sudden: a single flash of sword-light, like a rainbow of startled lightning, that as suddenly was gone. He had turned already, his sword back in its sheath, and said coldly, "Had he not moved, you would this moment be as that tree."

Shi Xiuxue was about to ask him what he meant about the tree; but before she could speak, she saw the tree topple, of its own accord, out of the empty air. That one flash of sword-light had cut clean through the great tree, a full arm-span round, and sheared it in two.

By the time the tree came down, Ximen Chuixue was gone.

Shi Xiuxue's face changed too. Was there such swordsmanship in the world? Such lightness-skill? She could scarcely believe her own eyes.

The tree was toppling now onto the man who stood opposite; and he turned of a sudden, put out both hands, and with the lightest lift and push laid the great tree slowly down upon the ground. His look was calm as ever, that gentle, peaceable smile still on his face, and he said slowly, "I'm no helper of his. I have never helped any man to kill."

Shi Xiuxue's pale face reddened again. She understood his meaning now, of course, and knew too that Ximen Chuixue had spoken no more than the truth. Bad-tempered she might be, but she was no fool to kindness; and at last she bowed her head, gathered her courage, and said, "Thank you. What is your honoured surname?"

"My surname is Hua," said the man. He was Hua Manlou, of course.

"I... my name is Shi Xiuxue," she said. "The tallest one is my senior sister, Ma Xiuzhen."

"The one who spoke a moment ago?" said Hua Manlou.

"Yes," said Shi Xiuxue.

Hua Manlou smiled. "Her voice is an easy one to tell apart. I'll surely know her again."

Shi Xiuxue thought this a little strange, and could not help asking, "You have to hear her voice before you can know her?"

Hua Manlou nodded.

"Why is that?" said Shi Xiuxue.

"Because I am blind," said Hua Manlou.

Shi Xiuxue stood stunned.

This man, who could pinch her sword-tips still between two fingers, was blind. She truly could not believe it.

The moonlight fell on Hua Manlou's face, and his smile looked as warm, as tranquil, as ever. Anyone could see he was a man filled with love of life, who had never railed at Heaven for his blindness, nor envied another man his better luck; for he was content with what was his, and had all this while been savouring the beauty of his life.

Shi Xiuxue gazed at him, and there rose in her heart of a sudden a feeling she could not have named, whether pity, or compassion, or love, or reverence, she did not herself know. She knew only that she had never felt such a thing before.

Hua Manlou smiled. "Your sisters are all waiting for you. Isn't it time you went?"

Shi Xiuxue kept her head bowed, then said suddenly, "When we meet again, will you still know me?"

"I'll know your voice, surely," said Hua Manlou.

"But... what if by then I'd become a mute?" said Shi Xiuxue.

Hua Manlou was stunned too.

No one had ever asked him such a thing; it had never once crossed his mind that anyone would.

He was still at a loss how to answer when he found that she had come to stand before him. She took his hand, and said softly, "Touch my face. Then even if one day I can't speak, you'll only have to touch my face, and you'll know me. Won't you?"

Hua Manlou nodded without a word, and felt his fingertips brush a cheek smooth as silk.

And in his own heart, of a sudden, there rose a feeling he could not name.

Ma Xiuzhen watched them from a distance, and seemed about to go and draw her sister away; but of a sudden she held back.

She turned her head. Sun Xiuqing and Ye Xiuzhu were watching too, a strange smiling light in their eyes, as though they had gazed themselves into a daze.

That Shi Xiuxue should do so did not surprise them; they had always known this little sister of theirs for a girl who dared to love and dared to hate. Did they too wish, in their hearts, that they had her courage? For to love, one must have courage as well.

***

Lu Xiaofeng leaned in the doorway, watching Hua Manlou, a smile at the corner of his own mouth as well.

Shi Xiuxue had gone; they had all gone. Four young and lovely girls together came like a gust of wind, and left like a gust of wind; no one could catch when they would come, still less when they would go.

Hua Manlou still stood there without stirring, as though he too were a little lost in a dream. The wind blew softly, the moonlight came down thin and pale, and he was smiling, and looked at peace, and happy.

"I'll lay you a wager," said Lu Xiaofeng of a sudden.

"Wager what?" said Hua Manlou.

"I'll wager you won't want to wash that hand for three days at least!" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Hua Manlou sighed. "I don't see why you must always take everyone else to be just like yourself."

"And how am I?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Hua Manlou put on a stern face. "You're no gentleman. No gentleman at all!"

Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "The lovable thing about me is just that I never trouble to pull a long face and play at being a gentleman."

Hua Manlou could not help laughing too.

"I think," said Lu Xiaofeng suddenly, "you'd do well to take a little care of yourself just now."

"Care?" said Hua Manlou. "Care for what?"

"You seem to have come into a run of peach-blossom luck of late," said Lu Xiaofeng. "And when a man comes into peach-blossom luck, trouble comes treading on its heels."

Hua Manlou sighed again. "There's another thing I don't understand."

"Oh?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Why is it," said Hua Manlou, "that you can always see other men's troubles, and never your own?"

Lu Xiaofeng could not help sighing too, and said with a wry smile, "Because I'm a scoundrel."

Hua Manlou smiled. "A man who can own himself a scoundrel has some hope in him yet."

Lu Xiaofeng was silent a while, then said of a sudden, "Who do you suppose it was that set Sikong Zhaixing on to steal Shangguan Danfeng away?"

Hua Manlou answered at once, without a moment's thought: "Huo Xiu."

"Just so," said Lu Xiaofeng. "It must be he."

"There aren't many men," said Hua Manlou, "who could lay out two hundred thousand taels of silver to hire Sikong Zhaixing."

"Which shows the Great King of the Golden Roc told no lie," said Lu Xiaofeng. "Huo Xiu must be Shangguan Mu."

Hua Manlou agreed.

"And Dugu Yihe, of course, is Ping Duhe," said Lu Xiaofeng. "That's why he came to the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour, and why he sent his disciples to find me."

"When he came," Hua Manlou put in, "he can hardly have known yet that trouble had already broken out here at Yan Tieshan's."

"Had he arranged beforehand to meet Yan Tieshan," said Lu Xiaofeng, "and talk some matter over?"

"Very likely," said Hua Manlou.

"And the matter they meant to talk over," said Lu Xiaofeng, "could it be how to deal with the Great King of the Golden Roc?"

"That too is very likely," said Hua Manlou.

"By sending the Four Beauties of Emei to me with those questions," said Lu Xiaofeng, "he's as good as owned that he has a hand in the affair of the Golden Roc."

"So you hold that he ought never to have done it," said Hua Manlou.

"We've no shred of proof that he is Ping Duhe," said Lu Xiaofeng. "He need never have owned to a thing, unless..."

"Unless he already has a way to make you let the whole business alone?" said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng nodded slowly. "Unless he has hit on a very good way."

"There is only one best way," said Hua Manlou.

"Just so; only one," said Lu Xiaofeng. "Once a man is dead, he can meddle in no one else's business ever again."

"You think he's laid a trap there, and waits for you to spring it?" said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng smiled wryly. "He need lay no trap. That 'Saber-and-Sword Double Kill, seven times seven, the forty-nine forms' of his may well be enough to put a stop to my meddling for good."

"They say," said Hua Manlou, "that of all the masters of the seven great sword-schools alive today, his skill is the most fearsome. For besides bringing the Emei sword-art to its last perfection, he has arts of his own, sinister, overbearing arts that no one has yet seen him use."

Lu Xiaofeng sprang suddenly to his feet. "Come, let's go now, this minute."

"Go where?" said Hua Manlou.

"To the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour, where else?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"The meeting is set for noon tomorrow," said Hua Manlou. "Why must we go now?"

"Better early than late," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"You're anxious for Shangguan Danfeng?" said Hua Manlou.

"A man of Dugu Yihe's standing will hardly stoop to harm a girl," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Then who is it you're anxious for?" said Hua Manlou.

"Ximen Chuixue," said Lu Xiaofeng.

Hua Manlou was moved. "Yes; since he knows Dugu Yihe is at the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour, he'll surely have gone there by now."

"My one fear is that he can't hold against Dugu Yihe's Saber-and-Sword Double Kill!" said Lu Xiaofeng. And he went on, "With a swordsman of his skill there should be no cause for anyone to fear; but he's too proud, and pride breeds carelessness, and carelessness a fatal mistake."

Hua Manlou sighed. "I've no love for the man; yet I can't but own that he has something worth being proud of."

"He saw Su Shaoying bring out no more than his twenty-one strokes," said Lu Xiaofeng, "and took it he could already break Dugu Yihe's 'Saber-and-Sword Double Kill', forgetting that Su Shaoying is not Dugu Yihe."

"What manner of man is Dugu Yihe, when all's said?" said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng pondered, then said slowly, "There's a kind of man I'd not care to have for a friend, but would care far less to have for an enemy."

"And Dugu Yihe is that kind of man?" said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng nodded, and sighed. "Any man who knows he has such an enemy can't sleep at night. So we had better go now."

Hua Manlou smiled of a sudden. "I fancy he's not sleeping just now either."

"Why?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Because any man who knows he has an enemy like you," said Hua Manlou, "can't sleep at night either."

***

Dugu Yihe was not asleep. The night was far gone; the spring wind of the fourth month seemed to carry a chill out of late autumn, and it stirred the white mourning-drapes of the funeral hall. The coffin was of purple nanmu, strong and costly. But a man once dead, what did it matter in what coffin he lay? The candle-flames swayed in the wind, and the hall was filled with a gloom and desolation past the telling.

Dugu Yihe stood quiet before Yan Tieshan's spirit-tablet, and had not stirred for a long, long while. He was a most solemn man; his back was straight as ever, his steel-needle hair and beard still jet-black. Only the lines on his face were many now, and deep; and it was only when you looked at his face that you felt him to be an old man. Now that stern, resolute face wore a look of desolation and grief. Was this too because he had grown old, and had come to know how sad and dreadful a thing death is?

Just then light footsteps sounded behind him. He did not turn his head; but his hand had already closed on the hilt of his sword.

His sword was thicker and heavier than most, the blade uncommonly long and broad, the brass guard polished bright; but the scabbard was old, and set into it was a small eight-trigram sign, the very mark of the sword borne by the master of the Emei school.

A man came slowly up from behind and stood at his side. Without turning to look, he knew it was Huo Tianqing. Huo Tianqing's face too was heavy with grief; over his close-fitting black he wore a robe of coarse yellow hemp, mourning-dress, that showed his bond with the dead man to be no common one.

Dugu Yihe had never before set eyes on this proud young man; the young man had never once come here.

Huo Tianqing, who had stood silent at his side a long while, said of a sudden, "The Reverend is not yet abed?"

Dugu Yihe did not answer, for it was a thing that needed none: standing there, he was plainly not asleep.

But Huo Tianqing asked again, "The Reverend has truly never come here before?"

"No," said Dugu Yihe.

"So that even I never knew Boss Yan and the Reverend were such close friends!" said Huo Tianqing.

Dugu Yihe's face was grave. "There is much you do not know," he said coldly.

"The Reverend is an elder of the martial world," said Huo Tianqing mildly. "No doubt you know a great deal more than I."

"Hmph," said Dugu Yihe.

Huo Tianqing turned his head, his gaze fixed on the old man's face keen as a blade, and said slowly, "Then the Reverend must know, too, how it is he came to die."

Dugu Yihe's face seemed to alter. He turned abruptly and strode out.

But Huo Tianqing had already barked: "Stand!"

Dugu Yihe's foot had no more than stamped down when the flagstone underfoot split in pieces on the instant; the veins stood out one by one on the back of his hand, and his Taoist robe stirred though there was no wind. A long while passed before he turned slowly back, his eyes blazing, and glared at Huo Tianqing, and said, one word at a time, "You bid me stand?"

Huo Tianqing had set his face as well. "Just so," he said coldly. "I bid you stand!"

"You are not fit to!" said Dugu Yihe sharply.

Huo Tianqing gave a cold laugh. "Not fit? In years, true, I am your junior; but in standing, Huo Tianqing ranks no lower than Dugu Yihe."

"And what standing have you?" said Dugu Yihe in anger.

"I know you don't know me," said Huo Tianqing; "but this stroke, at least, you should know." He had been standing face to face with Dugu Yihe; now of a sudden he twisted his waist to the right, his arms spread a little, the Phoenix Spreads its Wings, his two left fingers pinched into a phoenix-beak, and stabbed swift at the Tiantu point at the nape of Dugu Yihe's neck.

Dugu Yihe's right palm rose slantwise, slicing at the other man's wrist.

But Huo Tianqing's foot slid lightly, and of a sudden he had glided four chi and stood behind Dugu Yihe's right shoulder. The move was still that same Phoenix Spreads its Wings; but the line and the mark of it had all at once wholly changed, and now it was the right-hand phoenix-beak that stabbed at the great vein behind Dugu Yihe's neck.

Simple as the change looked, the cunning of it was beyond any words to describe.

Dugu Yihe cried out in spite of himself: "The Paired Phoenixes in Flight!" And on the cry he twisted suddenly to the left, looking back at the moon, and brought his left palm up to meet Huo Tianqing's phoenix-beak.

Huo Tianqing loosed his breath with a shout, and turned his palm outward with the force of the Little Sky-Star.

There came a soft "pff"; the two palms met, and both men on the instant went utterly still.

Huo Tianqing, who had shouted out his breath, now said slowly, "Just so; this stroke is the Paired Phoenixes in Flight. Years ago, when the Old Man of Heaven's Birds went up Emei alone and matched palms with your master, the Taoist Hu, on the Golden Summit, he brought out this very Paired Phoenixes in Flight; and you, I do not doubt, were standing by and watching."

"Just so," said Dugu Yihe. Two words only; and his face seemed to have gone a little livid.

When masters cross hands and it comes to a pitting of inner force, they cannot, as a rule, open their mouths to speak. But the Old Man of Heaven's Birds, a talent without peer in his age, had trained up an inner art that let him speak; and speaking, he lost no force by it, but rather seized the chance to expel the stale breath from his dantian.

Huo Tianqing's inner art was the true transmission of the Old Man of Heaven's Birds; and now he meant to use that art to bear Dugu Yihe down.

He went on, "Most masters, meeting this stroke, twist to the right and take it on the right palm; but Master Hu, who was a great master of his generation, did the very opposite, and took it on the left. Do you know where the difference in that lies?"

"The right palm is quicker," said Dugu Yihe, "but its own changes are then spent; the left, once brought out, still holds force in reserve, and can yet change at will..."

He had had no wish to speak; but neither could he show weakness. And there, at that point, he felt his breath come short of a sudden, and could go no further.

"Just so," said Huo Tianqing; "and it was for that reason the Old Man of Heaven's Birds could only use this stroke of sheer, brute inner force to pin fast the changes that were to come after..."

Dugu Yihe, as though he would not let him say more, cried out of a sudden, "How could you know of this?"

"Only because the Old Man of Heaven's Birds was my late father," said Huo Tianqing.

Dugu Yihe's face changed.

"Master Hu and my father were friends who ranked as equals," said Huo Tianqing mildly. "That too you must surely know."

Dugu Yihe's face went blue and white by turns; not only could he no longer speak, in truth there was nothing left for him to say.

So high did the Old Man of Heaven's Birds stand in generation that none of his day could reach him; that he had ranked as an equal with Master Hu had done Master Hu very great honour.

Proud and fierce as Dugu Yihe was, he could not throw into disorder the generations of the martial world.

"My standing you now surely know," said Huo Tianqing mildly; "but I have a few more things yet to ask you!"

Dugu Yihe nodded, his teeth clenched; beads of sweat had risen on his brow.

"Why did you have Su Shaoying change his name and pass himself off as a scholar?" said Huo Tianqing. "You and Boss Yan had no dealings; why come bursting in here the moment he was dead?"

"These are matters that do not concern you," said Dugu Yihe.

"Am I not to ask, then?" said Huo Tianqing.

"You are not," said Dugu Yihe.

"Do not forget," said Huo Tianqing coldly, "that I am still the steward of this house. If I may not ask of what passes here, then who may?"

The sweat ran streaming down Dugu Yihe's face; the flagstones underfoot cracked apart one by one; his right foot kicked up of a sudden, and his right hand was already on his sword-hilt. But in that very instant the force in Huo Tianqing's palm vanished away, and, borne on the strength of his own thrusting palm, Huo Tianqing floated lightly off and away. Dugu Yihe, his balance gone all at once, seemed about to fall; then came a flash of sword-light, and a ringing "ting," and a spray of sparks, and the long sword in his hand had driven itself into the ground.

And when he looked again, Huo Tianqing was already gone.

The wind stirred the white drapes; the candle on the spirit-table flickered, and of a sudden went out. Dugu Yihe, propped on his sword-hilt, faced a wall of darkness, and felt of a sudden very weary; he was an old man, when all was said. He drew the sword up, and slid it into its sheath, and walked slowly out; and in the dark there seemed to be a pair of bright eyes watching him, coldly. He lifted his head, and saw a man standing stock-still beneath the white poplar in the courtyard, robed all in snow.

Dugu Yihe's hand went again to his sword-hilt. "Who is there?" he said sharply.

The man did not answer, but asked instead, "Ping Duhe?"

Dugu Yihe's face tightened of a sudden. The man in white came slowly out of the dark and stood in the moonlight, his snow-white robe without a fleck of dust, his face wholly without expression, an oddly-fashioned long sword in a black scabbard slung slantwise across his back.

Dugu Yihe was moved. "Ximen Chuixue?"

"Yes," said Ximen Chuixue.

"You killed Su Shaoying?" said Dugu Yihe sharply.

"I killed him," said Ximen Chuixue. "But he ought not to have died. It is Ping Duhe who ought to die!"

Dugu Yihe's pupils had drawn in small. Ximen Chuixue said coldly, "So if you are Ping Duhe, I mean to kill you!"

Dugu Yihe laughed wildly of a sudden. "Ping Duhe is not to be killed. It is Dugu Yihe who may be killed."

"Oh?" said Ximen Chuixue.

"Kill Dugu Yihe," said Dugu Yihe, "and your name will ring through all the world!"

Ximen Chuixue gave a cold laugh. "Very good."

"Very good?" said Dugu Yihe.

"Duhe or Yihe, whichever you are," said Ximen Chuixue, "I mean to kill you."

Dugu Yihe gave a cold laugh in his turn. "Very good!"

"Very good?" said Ximen Chuixue.

"Duhe or Yihe, whichever it is you mean to kill," said Dugu Yihe, "you may as well draw your sword."

"Very good," said Ximen Chuixue. "Very good indeed."

Dugu Yihe stood with his hand on his sword-hilt, and felt his hand colder than the hilt itself; and not his hand only, his heart was cold as well. Illustrious fame, exalted rank: though he would give up all of it now, he could not win back the strength he had lost a moment since. As he looked at Ximen Chuixue, his mind was on Huo Tianqing; and of a sudden he felt a deep regret, the first true regret of his whole life, and perhaps the last as well.

He longed of a sudden to see Lu Xiaofeng; but he knew, too, that Lu Xiaofeng would never come now.

He could only draw! Now there was no room left him to choose.

And of a sudden, in the dark, sword-air surged again to the sky. The wind was colder now; and when Ximen Chuixue's own blood should flow, it too would be dried by that same wind...
