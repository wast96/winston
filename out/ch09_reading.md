## Chapter Nine. The Riddle of Xiang Zhongfa's Disappearance

### 1. The Delle Motor Garage

Around nine on the morning of June 22, 1931, near Jing'an Temple in the heart of Shanghai, the traffic was heavy and the street loud with life. People of every sort hurried past, and not many paid much attention to the strange, furtive look on the face of one middle-aged man.

That was exactly what he wanted.

He wanted his safety, he wanted his ease. He hated having eyes on his every move.

Once he was sure no one was tailing him, he stepped into the Delle Motor Garage. The Delle Garage in those days stood at the Yuyuan Road corner, kitty-corner from the back gate of the Jing'an Temple police station.

He meant to hire a car, to order himself a taxi.

But several plainclothesmen who had been lying in wait inside the garage suddenly closed in. They gripped him by the neck, pinned his arms behind him, shoved and hustled him out of the garage, and packed him straight into a small sedan parked at the curb.

Only now did the people around sense that something was off, out of the ordinary.

They stirred by instinct and pressed closer.

Someone shouted "Robbery!" Someone shouted "Kidnapping!"

Shanghai in those years ran wild with soldiers and bandits, and the scene inside that taxi garage struck people, on first reflex, as a robbery, a kidnapping, a gang of brazen abductors who in broad daylight had "invited off" a "God of Wealth" — a God of Wealth who, by his clothes at least, did not look like a God of Wealth at all, did not look like a man with much fat left to squeeze.

But it was no business of theirs, so they let it hang high and stayed out of it.

Shanghai people love a good commotion, love to crowd in for the excitement. Shanghai people are also careful of their own skins, and would rather not take a knife in the ribs for anyone or court trouble.

They calculate.

Their blood does not run too hot.

So the moment that sedan pulled away from the scene, the pack of vicious-faced plainclothesmen scattered like birds and beasts, and all those passersby who had just gathered drifted off again, sullen, each about his own business.

They did not care who the middle-aged man was, the one some plainclothesmen had lain in wait for and carried off by sedan; and even had they known he was Xiang Zhongfa, then chairman of the CCP Central Politburo and of its Standing Committee, they would not have been much surprised.

### 2. The Man Had Only Nine Fingers

About Xiang Zhongfa's arrest, Huang Mulan has told us a story.

The story is "recounted in her own words" by "the most beautiful red woman 'agent'" herself, in *The Autobiography of Huang Mulan*.

In January 2013, when the "2012 China Influential Books" list was fresh off the press, *The Autobiography of Huang Mulan* (2012) was warmly "recommended" by a panel of "more than twenty experts from the media, the universities, and research institutions" as the top title in biography — the "grounds" being that the author, "in writing her memoirs, strove for plainness," and "plainly" set down one "legendary woman," one "beautiful spy," and her "legendary career, unrepentant through nine deaths." That "career," it was said, rivaled "an encyclopedia of our Party," taking in both her "saving of Zhou Enlai and Guan Xiangying" and her "hurrying to find a way to report to the Party Center" that Xiang Zhongfa had proved "utterly useless — one turn in the electric chair and he couldn't take it, and confessed."

{v} That afternoon, around four o'clock, Chen Zhigao invited me for coffee at the Donghua Café, and suggested that after dinner we all go together to see a film at the Grand, saying he had already bought the tickets and meant to invite his sisters along as well. (Huang Mulan, 2012)

To an outsider this was the most ordinary courtship, a game of love. On one side, the shy girl half-hiding her face behind the lute; on the other, the eager suitor who cannot pluck the water-clover as he pleases. Each had a small heart and small stratagems. And for Huang Mulan there was one layer more: a part played to the occasion, a sham phoenix mated to a false one. *Faintly I revere the sages of old; Mulan's deeds I keep in my heart* — so Huang Mulan parsed her own name. She "took herself for a heroine in the mold of Hua Mulan," she said; from childhood she "read history, read the *Lives of Exemplary Women*," "ran about and played freely with the boys," "conceived the will to resist, resolving that when she grew up she would firmly oppose and root out the evil power of the warlords," and "often helped her grandfather in the good work of handing out medicine and dressing wounds."

{v} My great-grandfather was a physician who kept an apothecary's shop and gave freely all his life, spending the family's savings down to nothing, so that by my grandfather's time the house had fallen on hard times. When I was born, my family had not an acre of land nor a single room of its own; we were a poor scholar's household. (Huang Mulan, 2012)

Huang Mulan says her father was an only son who passed the licentiate examinations in his teens, a "fellow townsman and schoolmate and close friend" of Tan Sitong, who once "engaged him as a private secretary."

Huang Mulan was her father's eldest daughter. "Though I was born into a poor scholar's family," she said, "neither of my parents held sons above daughters. My father even freed my mother's already-bound feet, so of course I was spared the suffering of footbinding."

Huang Mulan also said: "What I liked best to read was *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*; as for *Dream of the Red Chamber*, I did not care for it. Perhaps, girl though I am, I have something of a boy's hard, forthright temper, and neither understand nor enjoy those clinging, sentimental love stories between men and women."

In the spring of 1926, this Huang Mulan who "neither understood nor enjoyed those clinging, sentimental love stories" — married off by her elders to a husband who "not only smoked opium but beat and cursed the maids at will" — "rose up to resist," and "walked out of the family to join the revolution."

Swept into the great current of the revolution, Huang Mulan "felt she had become another person entirely: not only could she take up a pen and write, she could speak at a mass meeting without notes; not only could she organize and rouse the masses, she could take the stage and act — as if all at once she had grown clever and capable, and developed in every direction."

In November 1926 Huang Mulan, nominated by the Communist Youth League, transferred to full Party membership, and at the same time took up posts as an executive member of the Wuhan special-municipality Party headquarters, head of its women's department, supplement editor of the *Republican Daily*, chair of the preparatory committee of the women's association, and chief commissioner of the committee for the foot-unbinding movement.

In March 1927 Huang Mulan married Wan Xiyan, the union "publicly announced by Dong Biwu at a meeting." Wan Xiyan had joined the Communist Party in 1923 and, as a Party member, had attended the Kuomintang's First Congress; he was then an executive member and propaganda chief of the Wuhan special-municipality Party headquarters.

Huang Mulan said: "Comrade Wan Xiyan already had a first wife back in his home village, and a daughter by her. Because it was an arranged marriage, Xiyan had long since separated from her. This first wife did not wish to remarry; she stayed on in the Wan household, and after Liberation was even granted a martyr-dependent's benefits, which I think was only right. From the May Fourth Movement through the Northern Expedition, young men and women opposing arranged marriages and choosing their own partners had become a commonplace of the age. Men like Lu Xun and Guo Moruo and many another pioneer of the New Culture Movement had wives at home to begin with and later chose new companions, but none formally divorced his first wife. It was not that these male comrades flouted monogamy and took three wives and four concubines as men did in the old society; this reflected the social tide of the time against the feudal system of arranged marriage. As for monogamy in law, it was only with the new China's promulgation of the Marriage Law that it was clearly set down."

In January 1928 the Center transferred Wan Xiyan — a standing member and propaganda chief of the Jiangxi provincial committee — to serve concurrently as secretary of the southwest Jiangxi special committee, leading the local peasants in armed struggle. That May, leading a peasant force against the town of Wan'an, Wan Xiyan was killed in action.

At the end of that year Huang Mulan came from Nanchang to Shanghai and was assigned to the Central Secretariat, working both as a secretary and as a confidential courier.

Huang Mulan's third marriage must date to 1929. Her third husband was He Chang, a member of the Sixth Central Committee and secretary of the Hubei provincial committee.

Huang Mulan said her union with He Chang had Zhou Enlai's blessing. Zhou Enlai said: "Very good. Comrade He Chang is worth your love; I approve your marriage."

Huang Mulan said that in January 1931, on He Chang's recommendation, the Center decided to place Pan Hannian and herself on a "single-line" footing in their "organizational relationship," with her "responsible for outside liaison" and "Pan Hannian responsible for inside contact."

{v} By the usual custom, it should have been the man for the outside and the woman for the inside. Why did the Center assign me the outside? Because my record of social activity during the Northern Expedition helped in opening upper-level ties beyond the Party. And my family gave excellent cover for my work. As for myself, some years of underground work had sharpened my ability to adapt, and fitted me to stand on my own and move a little more freely on every front. (Huang Mulan, 2012)

Huang Mulan went on: "The first important task the Party gave me was to rescue Comrade Guan Xiangying."

To rescue Guan Xiangying, Huang Mulan sought out Chen Zhigao.

Chen Zhigao, Huang Mulan says, "already held left-leaning views while a student at Aurora University in Shanghai, and had taken part in class boycotts"; after graduating from the Shanghai College of Law, he "hung out his shingle as a lawyer." Because his father "had served eighteen years as presiding judge of the criminal division of the Mixed Court in the French Concession," everyone "made much of" Chen. He "came and went on his cases in a sedan, every inch the great advocate," and carried real weight in the courts.

Huang Mulan, posing as an "upper-class lady," went to Chen Zhigao and told him she was "a woman who had broken with the Communist Party's organization, whose husband was the famous Communist Wan Xiyan, since fallen a martyr, leaving an orphan to be raised by the family, and who had come to Shanghai to look for work."

Huang Mulan told Chen Zhigao further: "The man to be rescued was a good friend of my dead husband Wan Xiyan, and also my cousin on my father's side. He came to Shanghai to do a little business and, for reasons unknown, was taken for a Communist and arrested. But he really is not a Communist, so the relatives and friends of both families have asked me to find a way, whatever it costs, to save him."

At the last Huang Mulan said to Chen Zhigao: "I have long heard that old Master Chen and Chen the great advocate are enlightened, progressive men, full of a sense of justice and compassion. Please, take every trouble to help, and secure the release of this wronged and innocent man."

Chen Zhigao found Huang Mulan "very bookish and refined, her speech and manner most elegant, truly like a young lady of a scholarly house," a "well-bred upper-class woman," and so he took the case, using his father's name and his own connections to work on the judges.

One thing led to another, and Chen Zhigao fell for Huang Mulan.

He took Huang Mulan for a weary bird under the lonely sky, a solitary boat on the boundless sea.

He wanted to cherish her, care for her, comfort her, with all he had.

Only then did the two of them walk together into the Donghua Café on Avenue Joffre (Mu Xin says it was "the Donghai Café beside the Paris Cinema").

Only then did they, at the Donghua Café, happen upon Cao Bingsheng, an interpreter at the French Concession police station.

Cao Bingsheng was Chen Zhigao's "classmate and roommate and close friend from his mission-school days."

Chen Zhigao and Cao Bingsheng, meeting by chance, exchanged greetings. Then the one flattered the other: Cao told Chen how well he had done, already a famous advocate in Shanghai; Chen played Cao up, saying that as an interpreter in the French Concession police station he had a magician's reach, eyes and ears everywhere, and asked whether there was any big news to tell.

Cao Bingsheng glanced around, saw no one, dropped his voice, and said quietly that Nanjing had just seized a Communist bigwig — a Hubei man, about fifty, a mouth full of gold teeth, a grog-blossom nose, and only nine fingers.

{v} The speaker meant nothing by it; the listener took it to heart. The two of them chattered away in high spirits at my side, while I, my face unchanged, stirred the coffee in my cup lightly with a spoon, feigning complete indifference to the matter, putting in not a word, only listening quietly. Then Cao Bingsheng said again: "Hah! This fellow was utterly useless — one turn in the electric chair and he couldn't take it, and confessed." (Huang Mulan, 2012)

The two men rambled on, chatting a while, until Cao Bingsheng said he had to go, that he was on night duty, and that a man who ate the government's rice was not his own master.

Huang Mulan, a little unwell at his side, took the opening to say her headache had come on again, and begged off both the dinner and the film.

Chen Zhigao quickly asked whether she wanted to go to the hospital.

Huang Mulan said no, she only wanted to go home and lie down.

Chen Zhigao could do no more than drive her home in the sedan, stopping on the way at a pharmacy to buy Huang Mulan some aspirin.

### 3. The Messenger's "Strongest Impression"

The moment I got home, I called Pan Hannian at once. So says Huang Mulan.

By now the setting sun was blood-red, and the evening glow burned across half the western sky.

Pan Hannian "was living above a tobacconist's shop in Xujiahui," and though he said he would "come right away," it still took a while.

{v} Pan Hannian came very soon. I repeated to him everything I had heard Cao Bingsheng say, and told him: "Because I had asked Chen Zhigao to rescue Guan Xiangying, he had just asked Cao Bingsheng whether there was any news from the police station — meaning to ask after the progress of the Guan Xiangying case — and Cao Bingsheng, without meaning to, let slip a case as big as this. I count them over and over, and the only one who fits the features of the arrested man Cao Bingsheng described is Xiang Zhongfa. When Xiang Zhongfa worked as a dockhand in Wuhan he loved his drink, and had a grog-blossom nose. He wore a mouthful of gold teeth, and one of his fingers had had a bit chopped off by someone, so that he looked to have only nine fingers." Pan Hannian said he too thought it must be Xiang Zhongfa. I said again: "By the sound of Cao Bingsheng's words, Xiang Zhongfa couldn't take the electric chair and has very likely turned; there's no telling what worse thing may happen this very night!" And I urged him to hurry off and report to the Party organization at once, and he left in haste.

{v} After Gu Shunzhang's defection, the work of the Central Special Branch was in Kang Sheng's charge. Pan Hannian left my lodgings, circled the streets a few times, and once he was sure there was no "tail" behind him, went straight to Kang Sheng. Kang Sheng, hearing it, at once reported to Zhou Enlai, who was in effective charge of the Party Center's day-to-day work. (Huang Mulan, 2012)

Told this far, the whole story is full enough, round enough, lively enough. But the author still found it not enough, and had to add one more stroke — and the result was to overreach and botch it, to paint legs on a snake.

{v} In early April 1980, Comrade Deng Yingchao sent her secretary Zhao Wei to Jie'er's home with word that a car would come the next day to take me to Zhongnanhai for a talk. The following day I reached Big Sister Deng's office in Zhongnanhai; as she was busy with official matters, she had her secretary Yang Yindong receive me in her stead. Yang was deferential in manner, and waited downstairs to greet me.

{v} Three days later Big Sister Deng had Secretary Yang come to Jie'er's home to convey her regards, and asked me to write out once more an account of the circumstances surrounding my discovery of Xiang Zhongfa's defection, since the matter had long gone unpublicized within the Party, and I was the one who had first discovered and reported it to the Party organization, and so ought to set down the circumstances of the time for the Center's files. I duly wrote the account as instructed and sent it up. (Huang Mulan, 2012)

Huang Mulan "duly wrote the account as instructed and sent it up," but Deng Yingchao did not act for a full eight years more. Huang Mulan says Deng Yingchao "asked" her "to write out once more an account of the circumstances surrounding the discovery of Xiang Zhongfa's defection" because "the matter had long gone unpublicized within the Party," while she "was the one who had first discovered and reported it." Yet when Deng Yingchao wrote to the CCP Central Secretariat on August 30, 1988, stating that "Xiang Zhongfa was a traitor proven by conclusive evidence," it was because all along "some had doubted whether Xiang Zhongfa ever defected at all."

Deng Yingchao held in her hand the account written by "the one who had first discovered and reported it to the Party organization," yet she made not the slightest mention of Huang Mulan or of her "long-unpublicized" "first discovery." She stressed instead that at "noon" on June 23, 1931, she and Zhou Enlai had "received precise word from inside" that Xiang Zhongfa "had been spotted by a traitor while waiting for a car at the Jing'an Temple end of Bubbling Well Road, and arrested on the spot"; that "in the afternoon" of the same day came "further word of his (Xiang Zhongfa's) defection"; and that they "then had inside word that he (Xiang Zhongfa) had already led traitors and armed police to the one Central organ he knew of (the place where documents were read), and arrested the three comrades working there." Deng Yingchao's two points differ plainly from Huang Mulan's "plain" recollection. Set beside them is what Sun Xiaocun says in *My Recollections* (1993): that "the largest case I met with in the rescue work was the arrest of the Communist Party's General Secretary Xiang Zhongfa," which left on him "the strongest impression." He clearly remembered that on the day it happened Huang Mulan had merely "carried a message" on behalf of Chen Zhigao's father Chen Jieqing, passing word to Sun Xiaocun that "the French Concession had seized the Communist chief Xiang Zhongfa," and to "go raise funds" to save him, which brought in "a suitcase full of money."

### 4. He Opened the Door with His Own Key

After Gu Shunzhang's defection, the Central Special Work Committee (the Central Special Committee, for short) underwent a thorough overhaul.

To reorganize the Central Special Committee from top to bottom, the CCP Center decided that "the Special Committee's own organization, from its supreme steering committee down, was to be reconstituted throughout, and its leaders had to be cadres — worker cadres above all — who were politically firm, long in the Party, and tested in struggle." Because Chen Yun had shown his capacity for work in helping Zhou Enlai handle Gu Shunzhang's defection, and met the conditions the Center had set, the CCP Center decided to reconstitute the Central Special Committee out of Zhou Enlai, Chen Yun, Kang Sheng, Pan Hannian, and Kuang Hui'an, to direct the work of the Central Special Branch.

The Special Work Department of the Comintern Executive Committee, in its "Written Report on the State of Secret Work and Special-Service Work of the Communist Parties in the Far Eastern and Near Eastern Countries" (June 3, 1932), said: "Gu's exposure pointed the Communist Party sharply to one problem: on the principle of tighter secrecy, the Party's technical work must be reorganized, and the several links kept as far apart as possible, and so on. The whole so-called Central Special Work Department was also to be reorganized at once and divided into three main sections: the Party's technical work; military work; the struggle against traitors. These sections were led by three comrades appointed by the Politburo. The whole was directed by Comrade Mo(skvin)." The "three comrades appointed by the Politburo" meant Chen Yun, Zhao Rong (Kang Sheng), and Pan Hannian. Under the direction of Zhou Enlai — the "Moskvin" of the Comintern department's report — they carried on the struggle on the hidden front. Chen Geng, Li Qiang, and others were transferred out of Shanghai, some going north, some to the Soviet Union.

After leaving Shanghai, Chen Geng went north to build the North China Political Security Bureau (the Beiping Special Branch) and, in Tianjin, held a long talk with Wu Chengfang — then a special commissioner of the CCP Shun-Zhi provincial military commission — conveying Zhou Enlai's instructions. Wu Chengfang first made contact with Xiao Ming, then, together with Xiao Ming, sought out a fellow townsman and Army Medical School classmate, Zhou Yi, forming the three-man core of the Beiping Special Branch, with their secret meeting-point fixed at the Chunqiu Bookstore. (Xiao Ming, originally Xiao Zhensheng and also known as Xiao Xiaowen, Xiao Ming, and Peng Ming, was born in 1896, a native of Xintian in Hunan; he joined the Communist Party in 1922 and served in turn as secretary of the CCP Weinan county committee, member of the eastern Shaanxi special committee, member of the Shun-Zhi provincial committee, and secretary of the Beiping municipal committee.)

The Beiping Special Branch soon gained others: Wang Dingnan, Zhao Meisheng, Zhao Ling, and then Liu Jizeng, Ji Piyang, Wu Huazhi, Zhu Qiwen, Yuan Jing, and more. (Wang Dingnan — who had also gone by Wang Zenan, Wang Zhenghua, and Wang Zuobin — was born in 1910, a native of Neixiang in Henan; he joined the Communist Party in 1930 and served as Party-group secretary of the CCP Beiping Anti-Imperialist League, Party-group secretary of the Beiping Mass Education Federation, and branch committeeman and secretary at Peking University. Zhao Meisheng — originally Zhao Zuolin, also known as Zhao Changyuan — was born in 1906, a native of Pingshun in Shanxi; he joined the Party in 1926 and served as committeeman, standing committeeman, propaganda chief, and organization chief of the CCP Beiping municipal committee. Zhao Ling — Zhao Shizhao, Xiao Ming's wife — was a teacher at the Beiping Qiuzhi School.)

{v} The Party learned of the work of Dingnan and the others in this period; the cadre who had come from the Center (Shanghai) to Beiping found Xiao Ming, and accepted Xiao Ming back into the Party. Dingnan and Zhao Zuolin, through Xiao Ming, likewise returned to the Party and took up work in the Party's special-committee system. (Tang Hongqiang, 1998)

Tang Hongqiang was Wang Dingnan's wife. The "cadre who came from the Center (Shanghai) to Beiping" of whom she speaks is Chen Geng. The "Party's special-committee system" she means is the "Beiping Special Branch." And when she speaks of Xiao Ming, Wang Dingnan, Zhao Zuolin, and the rest "returning to the Party," she means that while building the "Beiping Special Branch," Chen Geng also helped those comrades — wrongly hounded and cast out by Chen Shaoyu (Wang Ming) and his clique for having "opposed the Fourth Plenum" — to stand straight again. All of this, too, owed to Zhou Enlai's guidance and support, even as Zhou, forced by Xiang Zhongfa's arrest and defection, had to "hide himself away more strictly than ever."

{v} With Xiang Zhongfa arrested and turned, Zhou Enlai had to conceal himself more strictly still, cutting off contact with the other leaders. By this time it had grown very hard for him to go on staying in Shanghai. Before long the Center decided he should stop work and await transfer, though Zhou Enlai did not stop work entirely. (CCP Central Party Literature Research Office, 1998)

Huang Mulan says that after Xiang Zhongfa's arrest and defection, Zhou Enlai "at once notified Li Fuchun, Cai Chang, and other Central figures, arranging to move to the Metropole Hotel and lie low for a time." "Around eleven that night, these leaders, all in Western suits, playing the part of big business bosses, strolled openly into the French-run Metropole Hotel without rousing the least suspicion." (Huang Mulan, 2012)

In step with Huang Mulan is Mu Xin. Mu Xin says that after Zhou Enlai "destroyed some confidential documents kept at home," he "moved with Deng Yingchao into the foreign-run Metropole Hotel on Fourth Avenue (at the corner of today's Fuzhou Road and Jiangxi Road)." (Mu Xin, 2013)

But Zhou Huinian says that in those days Zhou Enlai stayed at her home.

Zhou Huinian was a veteran of the Central Special Branch; her husband Tan Zhongyu, succeeding Gu Shunzhang, became chief of the Special Branch's Third (Action) Section and captain of the "Red Squad."

Zhou Huinian said while she was alive: "After Xiang Zhongfa's arrest, Zhou Enlai stayed at my home for several days; he came at night." (Zhou Huinian, 1998)

Zhou Huinian's son Li Zhao says too: "My mother spoke of this while she was alive. She said that one night in June 1931, very late, when she and Tan Zhongyu had already gone to bed, someone knocked. Tan Zhongyu got up to open it, and there stood Zhou Enlai, alone, his face grave. Tan Zhongyu asked him in, and Zhou Enlai spoke with him in a low voice; after a while Tan Zhongyu took up his gun and went out. A little later he came back and reported to Zhou Enlai. Though they spoke very softly, my mother still heard Xiang Zhongfa's name. That night Zhou Enlai stayed at their home."

Zhou Huinian said: "Zhou Enlai and my husband Tan Zhongyu slept in the big bed at my home, and I slept in the small one. The task the organization had given me then was to keep the safe house. They did their work outside. What exactly they did, only the comrades directly involved knew."

Li Zhao says too: "Zhou Enlai and Tan Zhongyu slept in one big bed, and my mother made up a small bed beside them. She said that after she lay down she still heard Zhou Enlai and Tan Zhongyu on the big bed talking quietly about Xiang Zhongfa. My mother remembered those days as very tense; Zhou Enlai did not leave, and stayed at their home at least three days."

Zhou Huinian said further: "Before Zhou Enlai went to the Central Soviet he once stayed at my home for a spell. He went out to work at night and stayed in by day. Made up, he looked very much like a Japanese, so he often went out disguised as one. Once, very nervous, he put both legs into one trouser-leg; I laughed, and he laughed too. Because he'd put them on wrong."

By the account in the *Chronicle of Zhou Enlai (1898–1949)* (2007), on that very night when "Tan Zhongyu took up his gun and went out," Zhou Enlai, fearing a trap, had also "gone in person to watch for the signal near the lodging, and, once he had confirmed it, quickly gone into hiding."

Ren Bishi's wife Chen Congying, in *What I Know of Xiang Zhongfa's Arrest and Defection* (2018), says: "Comrade Zhou Enlai went in person to the high embankment behind Xiaoshadu, a spot from which one could see the back window of his lodging; seeing the curtain drawn open (this was the signal), he knew something had gone wrong."

Huang Mulan, for her part, says the ones sent to "watch near the places, known to Xiang Zhongfa, from which Enlai and Fuchun had moved out" were men Kang Sheng dispatched. She says: "Kang Sheng sent two Special Branch comrades disguised as peddlers, shouldering wonton stalls, to watch near the places, known to Xiang Zhongfa, from which Enlai and Fuchun had moved out. Sure enough, around one in the morning they saw a man in handcuffs and leg-irons leading men from the police station, who opened the door of Enlai's home with a key and barged straight in."

Xiang Zhongfa had been staying at Zhou Enlai's home before he was taken, so he could well have had a key to it.

If he really did "lead men from the police station and open the door of Enlai's home with a key and barge in," that could stand as iron proof he had confessed. The trouble is that confessing is not the same as defecting. After all, the Xiang Zhongfa "arrested at nine in the morning" only "around one in the morning" led "men from the police station" into "Enlai's home." He had been missing sixteen hours; however slow to react Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao might be, surely they would not still have been living in that house?

No. 44 Yong'anli on North Sichuan Road has now been designated by the Hongkou District People's Government of Shanghai as a site of Zhou Enlai's early revolutionary activity in the city and marked with a commemorative plaque; Zhou Erliu calls it "the most important and most secret refuge of Zhou Enlai's early revolutionary work." (Zhou Erliu, 2015)

### 5. Our Minds Ran as One

Pan Hannian said that after Xiang Zhongfa's arrest, Zhou Enlai very much wanted to gather more related intelligence through Bao Wenwei, Wu Xingya, Wu Hanqi, and the younger brother of a certain Xu.

*The Biography of Chen Yun* (2005) writes: "The Kuomintang authorities were then preparing to receive a prince from a certain European country, and were looking for someone who spoke French to interpret. Chen Yun and Pan Hannian seized the chance and sent Bao Wenwei in as a French secretary, through which he came to know people with ties to the Garrison Command, and then established a link with a clerk in the command's judge-advocate's office — a link that played an important part in learning that Xiang Zhongfa had indeed defected after his arrest and had been executed." But Bao Wenwei's children do not entirely accept this account.

Bao Wenwei's son Bao Jinyuan found me through Shanghai's *Labor Daily*. On his introduction I met, in Beijing, his sister Bao Xiaona and her husband Miao Changfa as well.

Bao Jinyuan once gave me a copy of "A Brief Account of Bao Wenwei's Part in the Underground Struggle Against the Enemy" (manuscript). He said he had put it together from his father's own spoken recollections before his death. Those who examined it hold that, though the account was "written down" by his children, it "has also been found in other materials, and confirmed to be old Bao's own account, true and reliable."

"A Brief Account of Bao Wenwei's Part in the Underground Struggle Against the Enemy" opens plainly: "In March 1932 I returned from France to my native Yixing in Jiangsu. Pan Hannian, then directing the underground Party's struggle in Shanghai, wrote to me (we were fellow townsmen and close friends) asking me to come to Shanghai to talk. After I reached Shanghai I met Pan at a secret spot; he asked what I meant to do now that I was home, and I said I ought to keep a promise to a fellow student from France, and meant to take a teaching and research post at the Sino-French University in Beijing where they were. Pan said, Stay in Shanghai and make revolution with us, and I agreed (I had known Pan since around 1920; our minds ran as one). He at once directed a stout man surnamed Li (given name unknown) to find me lodgings and await orders. Around May, Pan used his connections to place me as a French secretary in the Longhua Garrison Command in Shanghai, whose commander at the time was Dai Ji of the Nineteenth Route Army."

Why, if Pan Hannian said in his lifetime that he had "sent" Bao Wenwei to the Shanghai Garrison Command as a "French secretary" before June 1931 — so that Bao Wenwei's penetration of the Songhu Garrison Command ought to predate Xiang Zhongfa's arrest — did Bao Wenwei say he had "worked for the Party for two and a half years, from May 1932 to September 1934, under the cover of French secretary of the Shanghai Garrison Command"?

Why do the "principal revolutionary activities" in "A Brief Account of Bao Wenwei's Part in the Underground Struggle Against the Enemy" include "reading off the command's secret cipher-codes character by character into memory, then writing them out on slips" and taking them to "the upper floor of a certain shikumen house now west of Jing'an Temple"; and getting a map of Nanchang from "the Xujiahui Cathedral" and "sending it to Jiangxi, to the great convenience of the Red Army's operations"; and helping to have "a certain Kuomintang public-security bigwig executed in the elevator of a certain grand hotel"; and drawing out of "a relative of Hong'en, then the public-security chief" "the exact place where Liao Chengzhi was held," so that "his mother He Xiangning could go openly to make a great scene at the place where Liao was held, forcing Chiang Kai-shek to order Hong'en to release him" — and yet make not the slightest mention of how "the Central Special Branch, through Bao Wenwei, who had penetrated the Songhu Garrison Command as a French interpreter, and Ouyang Xin of the intelligence section," bought over the judge-advocate's clerk and another registrar at great cost, and at last "stole out Xiang Zhongfa's whole confession"? (Mu Xin, 2013)

Why did Pan Hannian say in his lifetime that when the Center got word that "Xiang Zhongfa had already been secretly executed inside the Longhua Garrison Command," it "was just preparing to mobilize the whole Party for a memorial meeting and lightning rallies and the like; at that point the clerk, to earn himself a fee, at last got through to the other registrar who kept Xiang Zhongfa's confession, and stole out the whole of it. As soon as I had it I sent it up to Zhou Enlai and Kang Sheng. On Zhou's careful study, many secrets known only to a few at the Center had each been exposed in the confession, which confirmed that Xiang Zhongfa had indeed defected, whereupon the Center urgently notified everyone to cancel the memorial" — while "Circular No. 10 of the Eyuwan Central Sub-Bureau — On the Movement to Mourn Xiang Zhongfa on the 'September 7' Memorial Day," as late as August 23, 1931, still called Xiang Zhongfa "the Party's Central Secretary, the sole leader of the nation's workers and peasants," called his death "an unprecedented and grave loss to the Party and to the workers' movement of the Chinese revolution," and further declared that "from September 1 through the 'September 7,' the red flags everywhere are to fly at half-mast (raised only halfway) in mourning for the Communist Party's leader Comrade Xiang Zhongfa," and were to "report to every mass meeting" "the heroic history of Comrade Zhongfa's leadership of the worker-peasant struggle" and "call on the masses to rise in a movement against imperialism and the Kuomintang's slaughter of worker-peasant leaders, in support of the Chinese Communist Party's preparations for the 'September 7' memorial rally"?

In fact, as late as December 10, 1931, the "Order of the Council of People's Commissars of the Provisional Central Soviet Government — On the Warrant for the Revolutionary Traitor Gu Shunzhang," jointly signed by Mao Zedong, Zhang Guotao, and Xiang Ying, still wrote in plain sight: "Gu Shunzhang then followed the leads he knew of, and went on sending his relatives and household to scout out in Shanghai the organs and leaders of the CCP and the various revolutionary bodies. Sadly it was in this arrangement of his that the CCP General Secretary Comrade Xiang Zhongfa was arrested and met his death — the single greatest contribution of Gu Shunzhang's betrayal of the revolution and surrender to counter-revolution."

The Special Work Department of the Comintern Executive Committee, in its "Written Report on the State of Secret Work and Special-Service Work of the Communist Parties in the Far Eastern and Near Eastern Countries" (June 3, 1932), speaking of Gu Shunzhang's defection and Xiang Zhongfa's death, likewise says: "The exposure of certain links of the apparatus, the arrest and killing of the Party's Central Secretary Comrade Xiang (Zhongfa), the killing of students of the wireless school, and so forth, were all the result of Gu's betrayal, and became the turning point in the Party's struggle against spies and traitors." If Zhou Enlai, having "confirmed that Xiang Zhongfa had indeed defected," at once had the Center "urgently notify everyone to cancel the memorial," how could the Comintern's declassified archive still speak of "Comrade" Xiang Zhongfa being "arrested and killed"? And how could the Eyuwan Central Sub-Bureau, on August 23, 1931, solemnly issue its Circular No. 10 calling for "the red flags everywhere, from September 1 through the 'September 7,' to fly at half-mast (raised only halfway) in mourning for the Communist Party's leader Comrade Xiang Zhongfa," and declare "Comrade Zhongfa's martyrdom truly the Party's greatest loss"?

In April 1991 Bao Wenwei died of illness, at the age of eighty-nine.

"A Brief Life of Professor Bao Wenwei" (unpublished manuscript) says that Bao Wenwei "took up the teaching of French and English and the study and translation of French and European literature from the mid-1930s on, for more than forty years. In that time he taught in turn at Peking University, the Sino-French University, Northeastern University, Beiyang University, Shandong University, the PLA Institute of Foreign Languages, and the Beijing Foreign Studies University, and served in turn as head of a teaching-and-research office, department chair, and dean." He was "rigorous in scholarship and tireless in teaching; besides his teaching posts, he achieved much in the study and translation of French classical and modern European literature, a master in his own right. With painstaking care and a graceful, flowing pen, he introduced to our country's readers the masterpieces of Balzac, Hugo, La Fontaine, Buffon, and others. He poured his whole heart into a fine translation of *Gargantua and Pantagruel* by the French humanist master François Rabelais."

Here I must also say a word in particular about Bao Wenwei's fifth younger brother, Bao Wenjie.

Bao Wenjie, born in 1911, came to Shanghai to study in 1933 and entered Nanyang Commercial High School, where he was "on good terms with Shu Yuexin, an old schoolmate from Wuxi Middle School; they exchanged progressive books, influenced each other in their thinking, and the two of them together inclined toward revolution."

In fact Shu Yuexin was by then already a Communist. He had long been reading works of social science and by progressive writers, exploring the advanced ideas and the road of revolution as if famished. Facing a society without a ray of light, watching the common people struggle in fire and flood, he not only led the way out of school and into the streets, joining the demonstrations against Japanese imperialism's mad invasion of China, but was also brought into the Party, in glory, by the Communist Lu Zicheng, and placed under Wang Xuewen.

In the autumn of 1933 Bao Wenjie "decided to join the Communist Party, and told his eldest brother, who heartily approved." Bao Wenwei then "introduced his fifth brother to another Communist with ties to Pan Hannian, 'Fatty Li'" (Bao Wenjie said he "never knew his real name, and never saw him again afterward"). "Fatty Li" in turn brought "a certain Wang" to meet Bao Wenjie. This was in the winter of that year. From then on Bao Wenjie "joined the Chinese Communist Party's Shanghai underground organization (the Central Special Branch)."

During his time in the Central Special Branch, Bao Wenjie "ran a small shop as cover for the Party, did courier and liaison work, and secret-communications work." He said the "people I contacted above were called only 'Old Wang' or 'Old Liu'; I never knew their real names or where they were from. Once they were changed, I never saw them again. It was the same with the people I contacted below." He remembered that he "once wrote out his name and native place for the organization, and once paid Party dues, but was never issued a Party card."

Bao Wenjie's memory calls back to my mind a scene from *Lurk*: the scene where Wang Cuiping brings Yu Zecheng into the Party. I do not care whether that ceremony makes sense, still less about the paying of "dues" or the issuing of a "Party card." As it is, "once you have faith, joining the Party is only a form, far from so important." As it is, the whole man, the whole soul even, has already been given away! Given to the Party, to the liberation of the Chinese people, to the great renewal of the Chinese nation! The real surname is gone, the true given name is gone, the true year and month of birth, the whole line of ancestors, all gone; what one has is all false, all made up to fool the enemy — then, apart from the ideal, the ideal buried deep in the heart, apart from the faith, the faith that nothing can break, what is there that cannot be given up?

You will forever be an outstanding member of this organization. So the organization judged Yu Zecheng. And so our Party pays its highest tribute to all the nameless heroes who, under the White Terror, in the struggle on the hidden front, lay buried in the enemy's heart.

### 6. The Wanted Notice and the Sellout

Having spoken of Bao Wenwei, of Bao Wenjie, of Wang Xuewen, I must speak again of Xiang Zhongfa.

Xiang Zhongfa, also called Xiang Zhongfa (written with a different middle character), with the aliases Yu Daqiang, Yang Tesheng, and Tesheng, was born in 1880, a native of Hanchuan in Hubei.

Xiang Zhongfa's own account: "I was the son of a ruined farming family. At fourteen I entered the Hanyang Arsenal as an apprentice, and stayed twenty-nine months in all. Falling out with the foreman, I was dismissed. Meeting a relative, one Liao, I was recommended to the mint, where I stayed four years; when the mint closed I went as a servant to the whole household of the notable Wang family in Jiangxi, for over three years. Later, on the same man's recommendation, I took a post with a steamship company he ran (running between Jiujiang and Nanchang). At the company, thanks to my employer's introduction, I served only four months before being promoted to second mate, and after two years as second mate rose to first mate. Later, when the company's steamer collided with and damaged the boat the Salt Commissioner rode in, I quarreled with the Salt Commissioner and, being made the subject of a warrant, fled to live in Hubei. I lived in Hubei more than a year; by then the mint had reopened, and I went in and worked there a year. Then, with the Wuchang Uprising, the mint shut down, and on someone's recommendation I took a post on the Hanyeping Company's ship No. 180, and only broke away in 1923."

It is generally said Xiang Zhongfa joined the Party in 1922, though he himself said it was in 1921, while he was deputy chairman of the Hanyeping Company's union, that Xu Baihao brought him in, and that seven days later he became branch secretary.

In 1923 Xiang Zhongfa took part in the great Jinghan Railway workers' strike, and as a delegate of the Wuhan-area Party organization attended the Fourth Party Congress; he served in turn as a committeeman of the CCP Wuhan district executive, a committeeman of the CCP Hubei district executive, chairman of the Hubei provincial federation of trade unions, and an executive member of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, was elected to the Central Committee at the Fifth Congress, and elected to the CCP Central Politburo at the August 7 Conference.

The late CCP Party-history scholar Feng Jianhui writes, in *From Chen Duxiu to Mao Zedong: A New Look at the CCP's Six Leaders* (1998): "The reason Xiang Zhongfa could rise so quickly from a grassroots union cadre into the Central leadership was first of all that 'he had fairly rich lower-level practical experience, was brave and active in revolutionary struggle, and showed considerable capacity for activity and some capacity for organization.'"

In May 1925, when the May Thirtieth Movement broke out and shook China and the world, Xiang Zhongfa threw himself into the May Thirtieth struggle in the Wuhan area.

In September 1926 the Northern Expedition's army took Hankou; in October, Wuchang. Xiang Zhongfa became chairman of the Wuhan General Labor Union and commander-in-chief of the Wuhan workers' picket corps.

In early 1927, when the Kuomintang right wing brazenly did to death Chen Zanxian — deputy chairman of the Jiangxi provincial federation of trade unions and a Communist — and broke up by force the Nanchang municipal Party headquarters that upheld Sun Yat-sen's "Three Great Policies," rousing the anger of progressives and the revolutionary masses, four hundred thousand people of every walk in Wuhan held a "memorial rally for the martyred worker-peasant leaders of Hubei and Jiangxi." Xiang Zhongfa spoke there on the spot, a speech brimming with passion, and those present all said: "Xiang Zhongfa, good man, a true son of our working class!" "Xiang Zhongfa, don't be afraid; do this for us workers, and the workers' children and grandchildren will remember you!"

In July 1927 Wang Jingwei betrayed the revolution. The streets and lanes of Wuhan were plastered with reactionary slogans, "Down with Chen Duxiu" and "Seize Xiang Zhongfa," and Xiang Zhongfa was forced to slip away in disguise to Shantou, to catch up with the Nanchang Uprising army.

In October 1927 Xiang Zhongfa and others made the long journey to the Soviet Union for the tenth anniversary of the October Revolution.

In February 1928 Xiang Zhongfa and the others not only attended the Ninth Plenum of the Comintern Executive Committee and met Stalin, but, in the name of the CCP delegation, joined the CPSU delegation under Stalin in drafting the Resolution on the Chinese Question.

From June 18 to July 11, 1928, the Sixth CCP Congress met at the "Silver Villa" in the village of Zvenigorod outside Moscow. Stalin criticized the CCP sharply for still having a few "great intellectuals" in the lead, saying it should draw more workers into the Center and must have men of worker stock as the CCP's leaders. So of the thirty-six Central Committee members the Sixth Congress elected, twenty-one were workers, and Xiang Zhongfa stood out above the rest — "regarded by the Comintern as the symbol of China's proletariat" — and was made chairman of the CCP Central Politburo and of its Standing Committee.

Luo Zhanglong describes the Sixth Congress's election of the Central Committee: "Stalin attended to give guidance. Stalin sat in the chief seat at the left end, Wenhu (Luo Zhanglong) to his right, Su (Zhaozheng) and Shi (Wenbin) to his left; in the lamplight there were crow's-feet at the corners of Stalin's eyes. He looked levelly ahead, his voice low. The gist of his talk: that the Chinese revolution had no high tide at present, that it stood between two high tides, and he sketched the shape of a saddle with his forefinger. At the last he urged the delegates to work well on returning home and to strive to bring the high tide sooner. His words done, he withdrew. Mif took his place and announced that the International had decided to appoint Xiang Zhongfa CCP Secretary. Mif pressed Xiang forward as Secretary, and the whole room was stunned. Only after Mif explained that this was Stalin's wish did everyone reluctantly assent. Seeing this, Mif and the others led the applause; those who applauded were pitifully few." (Luo Zhanglong, 2005)

Yet his rise, meteoric as it was, brought Xiang Zhongfa no further chances.

That his name is still known to us today is because, in his three years as a CCP leader, hostage to the Comintern's remote control and command, hostage to an opportunism each turn more "left" than the last, he was battered by wave upon wave of criticism and counter-criticism. It is because he let himself sink, and did not keep his last years clean.

On April 24, 1931, Gu Shunzhang — alternate member of the CCP Central Politburo and head of the Party's intelligence and security apparatus — betrayed the Party and went over to the enemy, and gave out two chief marks of Xiang Zhongfa: one, that he spoke in a Hubei accent; two, that his right forefinger was missing a joint.

Gu Shunzhang's betrayal forced the great shutdown and great transfer of the CCP's Central organs. Zhou Enlai arranged for Xiang Zhongfa to move into a house on Rue de Sieyès (today Changshu Road).

*The Iron Fist of the Red Terror: A Record of the CCP Central Special Branch* (1993) says the house on Rue de Sieyès was the Suguangcheng grain shop. Ye Yonglie says it was the Suguangcheng tailor shop.

Ye Yonglie's version is probably the right one. For Cao Juren, in *Shanghai Chronicle* (2016), tells this joke about the famous newspaperman Ye Chucang: "He was drinking at the famous Gaochangxing tavern. Warm with wine, he suddenly asked us: 'What kind of shop has the most signboards in Shanghai?' Some friends named a few. He shook his head at each. At last, with a wry humor, he came out with the three words 'Su-Guang-Cheng.' Everyone roared, for he had lopped off the first three characters of 'Suguangcheng tailor shop.' Indeed, Shanghai had some two thousand-odd tailor shops and more than forty thousand tailors, and some two hundred thousand people lived by the trade, nearly a tenth of Shanghai's population then. The tailors advertised 'Suzhou style' and 'Cantonese style,' and you might say those stood for the two most fashionable cuts."

Gu Shunzhang came up empty and failed to seize Xiang Zhongfa in the first instance. Enraged into shame, the reactionary authorities at once ordered a citywide warrant for Xiang Zhongfa.

At the same time, a bookkeeper at a taxi garage began to stir, and set about his contemptible business of selling a man. This bookkeeper was named Ye Rongsheng (also called Ye Hansheng and Ye Shunxing); he had once worked in the Mutual Aid Society, where Huang Mulan was head of the rescue department, had heard Xiang Zhongfa give a report, and had kept an impression of him, so he knew that the man who so often came to his garage for a car was Xiang Zhongfa. So without much hesitation he informed on him to Fan Mengju.

Fan Mengju was the proprietor of the Sanmin Photo Studio, and was carrying on with Ye Rongsheng's elder sister.

Fan Mengju, also called Yuren, a Shanghai man, had once bought weapons and carried secret documents for the Central Special Branch. The Sanmin Photo Studio he ran was a secret depot and arms cache of the Special Branch's general-affairs section.

Fan Mengju and his cousin Fan Zhong, hearing from Ye Rongsheng that Xiang Zhongfa often came to the Delle Garage for a car, joined the CCP traitor Huang Dalin — an inspector of the Shanghai municipal public-security bureau — and Qin Boyan, an agent of the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal Party headquarters, and others, and sought out Zou Lianhe, an attendant secretary of the "National Government military councillor" Yang Hu, to negotiate; and once the sum of the bounty was fixed, they set to work each on his own part.

And so a band of plainclothesmen, pistols in their coats, began to keep watch in and around the Delle Garage, waiting by the stump for the hare, their net spread.

Almost at the same time, Gu Shunzhang, following the vine to the melon, had also drawn silently near to the Xiang Zhongfa on Rue de Sieyès.

To the reactionary authorities, seizing Xiang Zhongfa was as easy as reaching down for it, as catching a turtle in a jar; all it wanted was time and patience.

### 7. A Trap Underfoot

Zhang Jinbao, a pioneer of the Chinese workers' movement and a member of the Sixth Central Committee, says in her memoirs: "Xiang Zhongfa was originally a boatman; during the Great Revolution, in Wuhan, he always wore coarse cloth." At the turn of spring and summer in 1930, when she came to Shanghai for the National Congress of Soviet Areas, she found Xiang Zhongfa "dressed in a silk gown, a pocket-watch chain even showing at his little pocket," which she "could not abide." (Zhang Jinbao, 1985)

The truth is that after Xiang Zhongfa came back from the Soviet Union, not only his clothes and his dress changed, his whole way of living changed. He plainly had long had a family, had long been married to Liu Xiuying, a village girl from Xiangtan in Hunan, and the Center had long since brought his wife and children up from Wuhan to Shanghai to reunite the household; but he would not see reason, gave himself to wine and women, and fell headlong under the skirts of his mistress Yang Xiuzhen. Even when Zhou Enlai had him move to Rue de Sieyès on his own, he secretly summoned Yang Xiuzhen there to share his bed.

Chen Congying and Huang Jieran say Yang Xiuzhen was a prostitute.

Xu Enzeng says Yang Xiuzhen was a dance-hall girl.

Gu Shunzhang says Yang Xiuzhen was a rich man's concubine.

Zhang Ji'en says: "Yang Xiuzhen was tall, and had a certain looks about her."

Xu Enzeng says: "She was around twenty-five, dressed in the height of fashion, and her face and figure met the standard of beauty."

After going over to the enemy, Gu Shunzhang could not find Xiang Zhongfa for a time, and so sought out the amah he himself had once found for him.

Zhang Jinbao says: "The old servant-woman at Xiang Zhongfa's home had been found by Gu Shunzhang, and she knew Gu Shunzhang well; so after Gu Shunzhang's defection, Xiang Zhongfa dismissed this old servant and moved house. Later Gu Shunzhang found this old servant and had her think of a way to find Xiang Zhongfa, and the old servant told Gu Shunzhang that Xiang Zhongfa had newly made clothes at a tailor's shop that he had not yet taken away, and that men could be sent to watch that shop."

Since Xiang Zhongfa was at that time living above the Suguangcheng tailor shop, the latter half of Zhang Jinbao's recollection — that "Xiang Zhongfa had newly made clothes at a tailor's shop he had not yet taken away" — may well be a little off. The more logical truth ought to be that on hearing the amah, Gu Shunzhang had her keep close watch on that tailor shop. The result was that the amah Gu Shunzhang set on it found Yang Xiuzhen by watching that shop. Had the maid's furtive movements not been spotted in time by Xiang Zhongfa, who was standing behind an upstairs window looking out, and had he not taken Yang Xiuzhen and fled fast, the consequences would truly have been past imagining.

Afterward Xiang Zhongfa moved into Zhou Enlai's home on Xiaoshadu Road, under the same roof as Zhou Enlai and his wife.

Zhou Enlai warned Xiang Zhongfa again and again that he must not go out as he pleased, and above all must not go to the Dehua Hotel near Jing'an Temple to see Yang Xiuzhen, who was rooming there with Chen Congying.

But Xiang Zhongfa could not master his longing, and after holding out only a few days grew restless again.

On the afternoon of June 21, 1931, the besotted Xiang Zhongfa took advantage of a gap while Zhou Enlai and his wife were out on business, left his hiding place without leave, and stole off to Yang Xiuzhen's lodging.

Chen Congying was a woman of strong Party discipline. Taking it that Xiang Zhongfa's coming must have Zhou Enlai's approval, she thought it improper to ask much, and did not interfere.

But when by midnight Xiang Zhongfa was still in Yang Xiuzhen's room, Chen Congying had no choice but to knock and remind him. Again and again she pressed him to leave quickly, but Xiang Zhongfa shamelessly refused to open the door, saying only: "Don't mind me — I'll go first thing in the morning!"

Xiang Zhongfa stayed in Yang Xiuzhen's room until a quarter to nine the next morning, and only then, still unsated, left in a sullen mood.

Xiang Zhongfa had no notion at all that a vast trap already lay underfoot, and walked as usual into the Delle Garage at the Yuyuan Road corner. As he stepped into that taxi garage he so often patronized, he was still savoring the raptures of the night before. And when the plainclothesmen sprang on him, gripped his neck, pinned his arms, shoved him out the garage door, and packed him headlong into that sedan at the curb, he still did not grasp whether all that had befallen him was living reality or an empty, drifting dream.

### 8. On His Knees to Escape Death

On June 23, 1931, Shanghai's *Shen Bao* ran, in its "local news" column, word of Xiang Zhongfa's arrest, saying: "Xiang Zhongfa, chief of the Communist Party, was arrested at a quarter to ten yesterday morning on Rue de Sieyès (an error for Yuyuan Road) and taken to the Songshan Road police station. He was then transferred to the political section of the Lujiawan station, and the Songhu Garrison Command, holding the prisoner to be a Communist chief, has already sent men to fetch him over."

That afternoon Chiang Kai-shek, then at Mount Lu, received the first urgent telegram from the Songhu garrison commander Xiong Shihui, saying the "Communist chief" Xiang Zhongfa was in the net but was stonewalling and would not tell the truth. Chiang Kai-shek slapped the table and ordered him "shot on the spot."

At Mount Lu, Chiang Kai-shek had just given the order to "shoot on the spot"; in Shanghai, Xiang Zhongfa had already given up resisting and laid down his arms.

The internal Kuomintang Zhongtong publication *The Turn* carries, in an appendix, the full text of "Report of the Former Bogus CCP General Secretary Xiang Zhongfa" — the Confession Xiang Zhongfa wrote after his "turn." The Confession not only laid out Xiang Zhongfa's own origins and career plain and clear, it gave in detail the names of the head of the Comintern's Far Eastern Bureau, of members of the CCP Central Politburo, of members of the special-work committee, of Soviet-area leaders, of upper-level leaders in the various places, and of the commanders of the Red Army's seven armies.

In the Confession Xiang Zhongfa also gave, one by one, the "actual state" of the Party's distribution across the country — "the Jiangsu provincial committee being the strongest, and Shanghai the most so, though even it is extremely weak"; the seven armed columns fixed by the Central Bureau's decision, "with a Red Army numbering more than a hundred and twenty thousand in all, and more than seventy thousand rifles"; and the Party's "sources of funds," chiefly aid from the International and relief from the Red areas.

And at the very end of such a Confession, Xiang Zhongfa also sold out, all of them, "Zhou Enlai's address and every important organ," together with "the Youth League General Secretary Qin Bangxian" and the Central-organ workers Zhang Ji'en, Zhang Yuexia, Chen Congying, and the rest.

So thorough was Xiang Zhongfa's confession that he did not even leave out Yang Xiuzhen, who twenty-odd hours before had shared his bed. And yet he knew better than anyone that Yang Xiuzhen was only his bedmate, and no comrade of his.

Could it be that even at the point of death he wanted someone to take the fall with him?

Or was he so incurably rakish, his roving heart unquenched, that even on the road to the Yellow Springs he had to lay down a little spring color and scatter a few roses?

But Zhang Ji'en says Xiang Zhongfa did not defect.

In his own hands was the internal Kuomintang Zhongtong publication *The Turn*.

He read Xiang Zhongfa's Confession closely, and says it was forged by the enemy.

His reasons: "After the Party's Central organ was raided, the organ workers — comrades Zhang Ji'en, Zhang Yuexia, Su Cai, Zhou Xiuqing, and others — at once made up false statements to fob off the enemy. Xiang Zhongfa knew perfectly well that all four of us were Party members, and knew our real names, the nature of the organ, and the true situation; yet the enemy never said our statements were false, and never even arrested Su Cai or Zhou Xiuqing. Before the enemy, in court, I gave the practiced set of statements we had prepared, and the enemy never once refuted or doubted them, nor ever pointed out the real names and true circumstances of Zhang Yuexia and myself; right up to the verdict, through countless interrogations, the enemy never pointed out that my statements were fabricated. We did not have our identities exposed. … Another arrested at the same time as Xiang Zhongfa was Chen Congying (Ren Bishi's wife), who was living at Xiang Zhongfa's home at the time. Xiang did not give her Party membership away to the enemy. She too was declared innocent and released on bail." (Zhang Ji'en, 1979)

But Xu Enzeng says that once Xiang Zhongfa "had been identified, the pitiable figure he cut in 'surrendering to the enemy' was ten times more remarkable than any non-proletarian fighter. He first told us he was only an ordinary worker, that he had no ability, that the post he held in the Communist Party was really that of a puppet; he even went down on his knees before us to beg, asking that his life be spared. He volunteered the locations of four important Communist command organs to show his loyalty. All of this was beyond anything we had expected."

Deng Yingchao also, for her part, had a talk with Huang Jieran.

Huang Jieran, in *Some Historical Circumstances Around the Party's Sixth Congress* (2018), says: "There are two accounts of how Xiang Zhongfa behaved after his arrest: one says he defected, one says he did not. The grounds of the latter are that neither the comrades tried in the same case as Xiang Zhongfa nor the already-arrested comrades known to him came to any harm. It seems this question still awaits further checking."

In September 1983 Deng Yingchao met Huang Jieran for a talk and told him the precise facts as she knew them.

On August 30, 1988, Deng Yingchao wrote another account, titled "On the Question of Xiang Zhongfa's Defection," calling "Xiang Zhongfa a traitor proven by conclusive evidence."

Before sending it to the Central Secretariat, Deng Yingchao had also shown her account to Chen Yun. Chen Yun noted on it: "Xiang Zhongfa did indeed defect; Comrade Deng Yingchao's view is correct."

### 9. The "Secret Cable" "Exists" Still

It is said that after Xiang Zhongfa's defection the Songhu garrison commander Xiong Shihui sent Chiang Kai-shek a second telegram, and that Chiang Kai-shek reversed his order and wired back to "stay execution."

But the major Shanghai papers published on June 25, 1931, all the same disclosed that Xiang Zhongfa had been executed in Shanghai the day before. One foreign correspondent said that at his execution "his ears were cut off first, then his nose, and only then was he put to death."

If Xiang Zhongfa really did turn traitor, then death would not have expiated his crimes, and however cruel his end, no one would have pitied him. But since he had already confessed, and Chiang Kai-shek had already given the order to "stay execution," why was he still put to death by the Songhu Garrison Command? Huang Mulan's explanation is that it was a crossed-wires mishap: he was "fated to die."

Huang Mulan says that after Xiang Zhongfa was put to death she once sought out Chen Zhigao to talk of it "in a curious tone," saying of herself that she was "rather interested, actually, in how such people end up."

Chen Zhigao's answer was: "This fellow was fated to die. It seems the men who caught him wired Chiang Kai-shek, then at Mount Lu, saying they had taken the Communist Party's General Secretary Xiang Zhongfa. Chiang Kai-shek wired straight back with the order to 'execute him on the spot.' The men interrogating him then sent a second telegram, saying Xiang Zhongfa had already surrendered and turned himself in, and had helped uncover the Communist Party's underground printing house and seize some Communists. Before Chiang Kai-shek's second wired reply even came, the man who had received Chiang Kai-shek's first telegram, to grab the credit and reward first, had him shot at once. A man like that really did deserve to die."

Huang Mulan's account is borne out by Xu Enzeng as well.

Xu Enzeng says: "Since Xiang Zhongfa had shown willingness to turn, his wish to live ought to have been granted; but this time a slip occurred. By the time I received in Nanjing the report that Xiang Zhongfa was willing to turn, the Shanghai Garrison Command had already ordered him shot. Such a disposal was really a loss to the conduct of my work."

Beyond this, Xu Enzeng has one more version, rather singular, and not without its oddity.

Xu Enzeng says Xiang Zhongfa was "sold out" — and the one who "sold" him was none other than Wang Ming. Wang Ming, "to seize personal power, did not scruple to sell his own comrades to the 'enemy.'"

Xu Enzeng says: "One day a capable-looking young man came to our office to report, saying he knew Xiang Zhongfa's address and was willing to lead us to him. We did not at first dare to put full faith in this gift dropped at our door. For this young man held no important post in the Communist Party, and by the rule of Communist underground work he could not have known Xiang's address. But since there was no harm in trying, we let him lead us to the upper floor of a jewelry shop on Avenue Joffre in the French Concession, where we arrested a coarse-looking old man past fifty, thick of speech and seeming not much to understand politics; by his looks he was very like a merchant, and living in a jewelry shop suited the part. At first he would not admit to being the CCP's number-one leader; distrusting our informant to begin with, and seeing this, we too believed there might be a mistake. Just as we were at a loss, a colleague of ours — a fellow townsman of Xiang Zhongfa's who had also been a boatman — said he knew Xiang Zhongfa and knew his history: that in his boatman days Xiang had gambled like a fiend, and once, coming back from the tables having lost all his money, had sworn to give up gambling and, to show his resolve, chopped off a small piece of his own left ring finger. On his identification we checked Xiang Zhongfa's left hand again, and sure enough the ring finger was short a piece. Xiang Zhongfa could stonewall no longer, and had to bow his head and confess."

As for that "young man who informed" on him, Xu Enzeng says: "Once we had confirmed the man arrested really was Xiang Zhongfa, we gave him a bounty and a temporary post; since he had come to serve us of his own accord, we paid him no particular attention. About a month after Xiang Zhongfa's death, this young man suddenly vanished. Only when he was gone did it dawn on us that he had come 'under orders' to work a 'kill with a borrowed knife' stratagem; Xiang Zhongfa once dead, his task was done, and why would he stay?"

If Xu Enzeng's gossip is not some ill-meant lie or joke, then the whole story of Xiang Zhongfa's betrayal would truly need rewriting. At the very least "the Delle Garage near Jing'an Temple" would have to become "the upper floor of a jewelry shop on Avenue Joffre in the French Concession," and "right forefinger missing a joint" would have to become "left ring finger short a piece."

And yet what concerns us more is the "Report of the Former Bogus CCP General Secretary Xiang Zhongfa" in the appendix to *The Turn*. If Xiang Zhongfa really "knew barely a few characters, and couldn't even read a document," as Chen Congying and Huang Jieran say, then it is truly hard to imagine that on the eve of his death he could still have written out in his own hand a Confession so orderly in its reasoning and so far from muddled in its phrasing. We must bear in mind that from arrest to execution was, all told, less than three days — and out of that we must still subtract the escorting, the extradition, the trials; subtract the eating and drinking and sleeping; subtract "leading, in handcuffs and leg-irons, the men from the police station" in a raid on "Enlai's home"; and subtract "swooping straight" on "the Party's underground printing house — the Red Flag Press" right by "Enlai's home," where they "carried off several comrades." (Huang Mulan, 2012)

Chen Yinan's doubt was raised after "a recent reading of *The Memoirs of Xiong Shihui*." (Chen Yinan, 2013) Chen Yinan holds that Xiong Shihui "was not in Shanghai at the time," and so "the several so-called Xiong–Chiang cables back and forth should not have existed at all."

*Haisang Ji: The Memoirs of Xiong Shihui* (2010) does indeed record that on June 18, 1931, Xiong Shihui, "just recovered from a bone injury, went to Jiangxi on a cane to take up the concurrent post of chief of staff of the headquarters, leading the headquarters staff to Jiangxi, together with Yang Yongtai, Cao Haosen, Zhou Fohai, Wei Suzhong, Chen Guoping, Peng Chunshi, Li Dezhao, and others." On June 19 Xiong Shihui "reached Nanchang and roughed out the dispositions, first organizing the two committees for Party affairs and for politics." On June 20 Xiong Shihui "quietly took the measure of the situation within and without, and felt keenly that the political strength for bandit-suppression had yet to be born anew." On June 22, "the commander-in-chief reached Nanchang, called a council of generals, and resolved on the Third Encirclement." On June 25, "headquarters ordered He Yingqin front commander of the bandit-suppression and concurrently commander of the left column, and Chen Mingshu commander of the right column."

But Xiong Shihui's being "not in Shanghai" does not mean he would not "cable Chiang Kai-shek about Xiang Zhongfa's arrest." Even though he was "with Chiang Kai-shek every single day," he still "had use for cabling Chiang Kai-shek."

We cannot prove that "the several so-called Xiong–Chiang cables back and forth" all "existed"; yet in Volume Eleven of the *Shilüe Gaoben* we did find one of "the several so-called Xiong–Chiang cables back and forth."

{v} (June 23) … (2) Cable to Xiong Shihui and Yang Hu, informing them that the bandit chief Xiang Zhong[fa] is to be forthwith shot according to law, as a matter of importance.

Is this at all strange? We think it perfectly ordinary.

It does not show that "Xiong Shihui may have had something hard to speak of about Xiang Zhongfa's arrest and subsequent execution"; it shows only that "though he was in Jiangxi at the time, the Songhu Garrison Command was, after all, still headed by him," and so the "Xiong–Chiang cables back and forth" do "exist" still.
