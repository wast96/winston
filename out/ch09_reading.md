## Zhang Renkui and the Ren Society
Zhang Renkui, styled Jinhu, was a native of Teng county in Shandong and a "Da"-generation man of the Green Gang. By the Republican years the gang's seniority had descended through the four characters Da, Tong, Wu, and Xue. Very few of the "Da" generation were left — you could hardly count a handful — and gang people looked on Zhang as one of a rare and precious few. In his later years he organized the Ren Society in Shanghai, which stood with Huang Jinrong's Rong Society and Du Yuesheng's Heng Society as one of a trio, the three most powerful bodies among the Shanghai gang organizations. The members of the Ren Society were mostly what the old society called "people of the upper crust," which set it apart from the more mixed membership of the Rong and Heng societies.
Zhang Renkui began as a salt smuggler, and for years he ran salt about the rivers and lakes in the following of the salt-smuggling chief Xu Baoshan; together with Zhao Hongci, the water-police district commander at Guazhou (a post equivalent to head of a waterborne police bureau), he was one of Xu Baoshan's ablest lieutenants.
During the 1911 Revolution, Xu Baoshan raised the banner of revolution, recruited troops and bought horses north of the Yangtze, and, eyeing the region like a hawk, showed every sign of meaning to carve out a domain and make himself its master — which earned him the enmity of Yuan Shikai. Learning that Xu had a passion for antiques, Yuan secretly sent men, on the pretext of presenting a gift, to plant a powerful bomb inside an exquisitely finished wooden case; when Xu opened the case to look, the bomb blew him to pieces. After Xu's death Zhang took over his old troops, wearing the "twenty-four dou" (a gang term denoting seniority), and his men rallied to him.
In the Second Revolution of 1913, Feng Guozhang led the old Beiyang forces against Nanjing, drove out Huang Xing, the city's garrison commander, and took over as military governor of Jiangsu and inspector-general of the Yangtze, incorporating Zhang Renkui into his forces. Through the patronage of Li Chun and Qi Xieyuan, Zhang was made commander of the 76th Composite Brigade of the Jiangsu Army and garrison commander of Tonghai, stationed at Nantong.
After Zhang came to Nantong he took Zhang Jian as his teacher, calling every day at Zhang Jian's "Haonan Villa" to pay his respects and inquire after his health, observing to the letter the courtesies of a disciple; and Zhang Jian in turn treated him as an honored guest.
I came to know Zhang Renkui in 1924. I was only twenty-three then, and manager of the Dasheng No. 8 Textile Company (the Yongfeng Company) in Nantong; we met on the social circuit. He was fond of fifty-cent poker, and among those who gambled with him were Han Fengchi, head of the five salt fields of the Tongtai district; Jiang Xiatang, manager of the Dada Inland Steamship Company; Zhao Hansheng, manager of the Nantong branch of the Shanghai Bank; Zhu Xiaozhu, comprador of the Dahe Steamship Company; and Xu Zechu, Zhang Jian's private secretary. The stakes were too small to interest any of us, but since it would not do to dampen Zhang Renkui's high spirits, we went along with it for form's sake. Once, Zhang suddenly began to grumble: "Mr. Zhang the Third [meaning Zhang Cha, styled Tui'an, Zhang Jian's elder brother] is at odds with me. I've knocked about the rivers and lakes all this while and take everything lightly now; the Third Master is a senior of my father's generation — why should he quarrel with me? Whatever he wants to do, would I not go along with it?" There was something rueful in the way he said it, half a laugh and half a sigh.
In the First Jiangsu-Zhejiang War, Qi Xieyuan wired orders for Zhang Renkui's troops to move up to the front as reinforcements. Zhang requisitioned a great many junks, ferried his troops ashore at Liuhe, and deployed them along the Jiading–Nanxiang line. By his own account, Gong Bangduo, who commanded that sector, won the opening engagements; the enemy facing him was He Fenglin's force under Lu Yongxiang, and it was on the point of giving way. Then Yang Huazhao's troops came up and counterattacked with such force that they could not be held. At the most desperate moment the troops Zhang led arrived: Battalion Commander Zhao (meaning Zhao Zichao, the father of Zhao Dan) fixed bayonets and charged, and hand-to-hand fighting broke out; his men fought with a will, each vying to be first, and beat down Yang Huazhao's assault by main force, and only then was the line steadied.
Zhang Renkui spent many years at Nantong and put by some means; back in his native place in Shandong he bought up a good deal of land, as provision for his old age.
In 1927, when the Northern Expedition reached Jiangsu and Zhejiang, Nantong changed its colors. A routed unit mutinied there, firing off their guns and looting the shops, and Zhang Renkui went out in person to put them down. A distant nephew of his, hoping to fish in troubled waters, had fallen in with the mutineers; when Zhang found out, he had him executed on the spot as a warning to all and his head hung over Nantong's west gate. After this Zhang pleaded age, resigned, and moved to Shanghai.
In 1929 Zhu Chengzhang, general manager of the China National Products Bank, was kidnapped and wounded and died of his injuries, throwing the Shanghai banking world into alarm. It had begun when Zhu contracted with the monk of the Jing'an Temple to lease a strip of land at the terminus of the Jing'an Temple tram line, running from the Dafa native-goods store to the Yuanyuan hat shop, meaning to put up shophouses to let; he had already registered it with the land office and completed every legal formality. At the same time Zhang Xiaolin also wanted to lease this same strip, and had someone approach the Jing'an Temple monk, but the monk refused, preferring to let it cheaply to Zhu Chengzhang rather than to Zhang Xiaolin. Zhang went to Zhu and offered to take it in sublease at a higher rent, and Zhu would not agree to that either. One morning Zhu Chengzhang drove out from his home on Dianyuan Road, with his nurse Miss Deng and his daughter in the car. The car had barely left the lane when kidnappers suddenly fired on Zhu and ordered it to stop. Deng was shot through the left palm and Zhu through the lower belly, and they carried Zhu off on the spot. When the China National Products Bank learned of it, it sent people everywhere to make contact and hunt for leads. Toward evening that same day the bank's steward, Zhou Zibin, led the party that ransomed him back and took Zhu to the Red Cross Hospital on Haige Road for treatment, but he died of his wounds. Afterward I asked Zhou Zibin about it, and all he knew was that the patrol house had told him to go, that at the far end of Yangshupu Road he had ransomed Zhu back, and that Zhu was lying in a paddy field, barely breathing. Whom they had dealt with, and how much had been paid out, he did not know. The ransom money had been packed in a gunny sack sealed with wax, and he had never counted how much it was. The banking world, unnerved by what had happened to Zhu Chengzhang, took counsel on how to protect itself. In the end Qian Xinzhi of the Bank of Communications called in Zhang Renkui and kept him in style at Xiuyuan on Haige Road, where he was known as "the Venerable Master Zhang."
Chen Guangfu, general manager of the Shanghai Commercial Bank and chairman of the Shanghai Bankers' Association, had taken up with a nurse and wanted to make her his concubine. Chen's wife, a famous "tigress," flew into a rage when she heard, and fought him tooth and nail, until Chen, helpless, had to break it off with the nurse. As it happened, the nurse was a disciple of the Shanghai big hoodlum Gao Xinbao; she went weeping to her "old man," and Gao agreed to take the matter up with Chen, sending a letter to Chen Guangfu that laid down terms: first, that he divorce his lawful wife and marry the nurse properly; second, that he pay the nurse a consolation sum of 100,000 yuan — failing which they would deal with Chen Guangfu as they had dealt with Zhu Chengzhang. Chen, thoroughly frightened, turned for help to Xu Jingren, general manager of the Puyi Cotton Mill. Xu, when he had run the salt administration in northern Anhui, had been Zhang Jian's chief of general affairs and enjoyed Zhang's full confidence; through this he knew Xu Baoshan well and had connections with Zhang Renkui too, and so he arranged for Chen Guangfu to take Zhang Renkui as his "old man" and asked Zhang to "talk terms" with Gao Xinbao.
Chen prepared the customary gifts and sent Zhang Renkui a pupil's card. Zhang said: we are close in age, and both of us are followers of Mr. Zhang the Fourth (that is, Zhang Jian), so it is enough that we call each other brothers; on no account can I accept a pupil's card. He returned the card there and then, exchanged it for a sworn-brotherhood register, and, styling himself the younger sworn brother, at once settled this affair of the heart for Chen. Once the story got about and the banking world saw that there was something to be gained from Zhang Renkui, still more people worked through intermediaries to become his disciples. Coming in at the same time were Li Tongcun, general manager of the Daye Company run jointly by the Shanghai Bank; Xu Erkang, a branch manager of the Shanghai Bank; a Mr. Hua, chief engineer of the Shanghai Telephone Company; and Chen Xiangtao, general manager of the China Travel Service.
Ge Guangting, chairman of the management committee of the Jiaoji Railway, had studied at the Japanese Military Academy and was of long standing among the Beiyang warlords, on good terms with Song Zheyuan and Han Fuju under Feng Yuxiang. After leaving office, Ge bought a house of his own at No. 19 Shangfang Garden, Lane 1285 Avenue Joffre (today's Central Huaihai Road), directly across the way from mine. Ge too took Zhang Renkui as his teacher and often had him to dinner, sometimes asking me to keep them company. I had not seen Zhang in more than twenty years, but when we met he still knew me, and he talked with me of the rise and fall of the Zhang family of Nantong and of the dreadful violent death of Xiaoruo (Zhang Xiaoruo was Zhang Jian's son, stabbed to death at home by his own bodyguard) — there was much old feeling in it.
Han Fuju too was introduced by Ge Guangting and took Zhang Renkui as his teacher. I asked Ge: Han has power and position, why should he do obeisance to a Zhang Renkui who has neither? Ge said: "You don't understand. Long ago, when Zuo Zongtang sent troops into Xinjiang and the march reached Pingliang, the whole army suddenly reined in and would go no further, asking Zuo for a day's halt. Zuo asked them the reason, and the answer was that they had to welcome the Great Dragon Head. Consider: with all Zuo's authority and the strictness of his orders, the officers and men were willing to risk military law and even to offend Zuo himself in order to go and welcome the Great Dragon Head — you can see how deep the power of an 'old man' runs, how deep the rule of the founding patriarchs reaches into people. Zuo later did obeisance to an 'old man' too, and besides military law used gang rules to keep his troops in hand, and so drew closer to his officers and men and won his victories in the field. Xiangfang (Han's style-name) is of the Northwest Army, and Feng Yuxiang plays this game as well; Zhang Zhijiang and the rest are all gang men. Once Xiangfang did obeisance to the Venerable Master Zhang, he could open his own incense hall and set up his own house."
There are two ways of doing obeisance to an "old man." One is to send a card, which makes you a "pupil"; the other is to open an incense hall, in which a sponsor first presents you, and then, with the "old man's" consent, your birth date and eight characters are set down, a date is fixed, and only after the great ceremonies — three kneelings and nine kowtows, drinking the blood wine, swearing the oath, and hosting a banquet — are you entered as a disciple. Zhu Xiaozhu, comprador of the Dahe Steamship Company, did obeisance to Zhang Renkui as his "old man" through the ceremony of opening an incense hall and "setting the tables" (hosting the banquet). Zhu Xiaozhu often came to my house to ask me out for a chat, and he told how "the Venerable Master Zhang" had taken in two more powerful disciples, one Jiang Dingwen, the other Zhu Shaoliang. He urged me to take Zhang as my "old man" as well, saying I would gain "the old man's good word and the help of my fellow disciples." He said: "For someone like me who makes his living on the water, among all sorts and conditions of men, without the Venerable Master Zhang's name behind you there's no getting on at all."
Many of the subordinates of H. H. Kung and T. V. Soong took Zhang as their teacher — men such as Wei Jingzhou, director of the Central Mint, and Sheng Shengyi, director of the Jiangsu Bureau of Stamp, Tobacco and Liquor Tax.
Zhang's students organized the Ren Society as a place to gather, a means of keeping in touch and cultivating good feeling. There was a Ren Society branch at Hangzhou as well, its premises near "Twin Peaks Piercing the Clouds," with the residence of Yuan Liang on the left and that of Zhou Xiangxian on the right; they bought the land and put up a marker board, but never got the meeting hall built.
In the gang, the first disciple to enter is called the "open-the-mountain-gate" disciple and the last the "close-the-mountain-gate" disciple; the first and last are usually an "old man's" favorites. Zhang's opening disciple was said to be Wang Fenglou, adjutant-general of the 76th Composite Brigade, and his closing disciple was Chen Chujun, chief of the Haimen police bureau in northern Jiangsu.
Chen Chujun also opened incense halls and took in disciples at Haimen. Many of Zhang's subordinates stationed there took Chen as their "old man." A good number of Chen's disciples were police officers and patrol sergeants, and once out of work some ran contraband salt and some ran opium, all paying their respects to Chen. As I recall, there was a tea shop in Haimen where the men who roasted the tea were mostly strapping young fellows fond of a brawl; some of these took Chen as their "old man" too, and often went off to join in gang fights, and when they were beaten they would clench a towel between their teeth and not let out a single cry. Anyone who so much as groaned had no "grit," and after that the gang would not own him as a stout fellow. I once asked Chen Chujun why the disciples taken in by Zhang Renkui, Huang Jinrong, and Du Yuesheng were all so refined, while the disciples he took in were forever going in for "three knives and six holes" and coming to blows. Chen said: "You know the one but not the other. The students they take in are strictly graded: the top sort has its own tier, the low sort its own, the literary have theirs, the martial theirs. What you see is mostly the top, literary tier; the low, martial ones you never see. If there were only civil accomplishment and no martial skill, what would be the point of the Green Gang, what would be the point of an 'old man'?"
The Green Gang has a rule: you may pose but you may not welsh; the Hong Gang's is the reverse — you may welsh but you may not pose. If you are not a gang man but understand the Green Gang's rules, you are called a "hollow one," and the gang will entertain you all the same, paying your food and drink and your hotel bill. With the Hong Gang it is different. When I was director of the Bureau of Stamp, Tobacco and Liquor Tax in Hubei, the flower tax and the stamp duty were farmed out to a Hong Gang brother, Wang Jingzai, manager of the Chaobei Hotel. Wang told me that once a country bumpkin came to Hankou and smoked opium in a stall in the French Concession; the way he set down his pipe, the way he stuck in the opium-pick, where he put the teapot, how he held the teacup, his posture as he smoked a cigarette, his manner of brewing tea — all of it had the air of a "big brother," so that when one of the "little ones" (the junior brothers) saw him, he reported to his big brother that a big brother from some lodge had arrived. We went to entertain him and to "argue the way" with him (to question him closely), but the man's answers had nothing to do with what was asked. Asked how he dared impersonate one of our big brothers, he said he had not; asked how he dared fly our big brothers' flag, he protested his innocence to the last. It was all sheer coincidence; he knew nothing whatever of the gang's rules, methods, postures, or placements. After everyone had looked into it, they judged that he had impersonated a brother deliberately and so had broken gang law. The countryman kowtowed and begged for mercy; in the end, though he was spared a death sentence, they cut off one of his ears.
The students Zhang Renkui took in at Shanghai were all vetted by him personally, with no low hoodlums slipping in among them, nor did he use gang rules to keep his disciples in line. They paid their respects to him chiefly of their own will — those with means gave more, those without gave less — and he never haggled over it, unlike Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng and their sort, who used birthday celebrations and the like as pretexts to send out cards and cadge for gifts.
Chen Shichang, Du Yuesheng's own "old man," also took Zhang as his teacher. One day Wu Qiding, chairman of the Ningbo Commercial Bank, gave a dinner for Du Yuesheng at which I was a guest. Du said to me: "I hear you know old man Zhang. Why should old man Zhang need to take in students at all? Whatever his monthly expenses come to, I'll simply give it to him." After the party broke up, Wu Qiding said to me: "Du Yuesheng is unhappy that Zhang Renkui takes in so many students — first, he fears it cuts into his own trade; second, he fears that the more students Zhang takes, the more of his own 'shishu' there will be, for Du is Chen Shichang's disciple, and Chen is of the 'Tong' generation, so anyone who does obeisance at old man Zhang's gate is also of the 'Tong' generation, and therefore Du Yuesheng's senior in the gang."
The men who worked hardest for the Ren Society were Zhang Zhuping, general manager of the China Times, and Ge Futian, manager of the Yudechi bathhouse. The raising of the endowment and the collection of the monthly dues were both in their hands. The society's finances were kept separate, and did not mix with Zhang Renkui's private accounts.
When Chiang Kai-shek betrayed the revolution, he made use of the evil power of Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng, and Zhang Xiaolin to wreck the Shanghai General Labor Union housed in the Commercial Press in Zhabei, and to lure and seize its chairman, Wang Shouhua — a counter-revolutionary atrocity that outraged heaven and man alike. Reckoning that the gangs had done him good service, Chiang courted and bought their leaders for all he was worth. He held Zhang Renkui too in much respect, and once used him to go to Shandong and make contact with Han Fuju. After the War of Resistance broke out, Chiang urged Zhang to move west with the rest, but Zhang did not go. The enemy and their puppets worked on Zhang as well, but he pleaded illness and stayed out of it. In the last years of the war Zhang died of illness at his Shanghai home, and Chiang Kai-shek, in the name of the Chongqing government, still issued an order commending him.
As I see it, the members of the Ren Society joined for one of five purposes. First, men like Chen Guangfu and Li Tongcun, who liked to dally with women, and who with Zhang Renkui behind them need not fear a hoodlum's shakedown. Second, men like Zhu Xiaozhu and Chen Xiangtao, who made their living on the rivers and lakes and who, with Zhang Renkui to lean on, could get on wherever they traveled. Third, men like Jiang Dingwen, Zhu Shaoliang, and Han Fuju, who took up Zhang Renkui's mantle and could use the gang as a rallying cry to strengthen their own power. Fourth, men like Qian Xinzhi and Wei Jingzhou, who flew Zhang Renkui's flag and thereby secured their safety. Fifth, men like Zhang Zhuping and Ge Futian, who through their connection with Zhang Renkui could rub shoulders with a few more rich and powerful friends and gain advantages in business.
Zhang Renkui was tall and powerfully built, with bright, piercing eyes and a face that gave little away — a man with much of the "strength of the North" about him. He had three sons: the eldest fell into low ways and died of illness at Nantong; the second and third both followed honest callings. The third, on Qian Xinzhi's recommendation, worked at the Zhongxing Company.
