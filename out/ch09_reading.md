## Chapter 9

The car stopped at Stephen's house on Route Lafayette, and the moment I came through the gate I heard the sound of a piano. Crossing the hall, Stephen made straight for the stairs; we followed him up, and he pushed open the door of a room above and said:

"I've brought a whole company of beautiful guests."

We went in after him, and I saw Mrs. Stephen, in a yellow dress, rise from the piano bench, two reddish-brown English dogs at her heels.

On every side were books; the ceiling overhead was cream-white; on the piano a great spray of Chinese roses seemed only now to be waking to the music. A small round table stood in the middle of the room, its pale-yellow cloth embroidered at the four corners with green leaves; and there were pale-yellow curtains, hung half-drawn at the window, quite naturally strewn with leaves of green cloth. The sofas all round were slipped in pale yellow, the cushions all of a light green, a quarter of them embroidered with yellow flowers; and then I noticed the pale-yellow carpet, so clean, so beautiful. I sat down on one of the sofas, beside me a flower-stand from which the thick, soft fronds of a dwarf bamboo hung right down to my hair. Now I saw that all the furniture round about was cream-coloured, close to the hostess's own complexion, and that these yellow furnishings seemed made to echo the hostess's dress. Sitting on the sofa, I felt a comfort past telling, and it came over me all at once that the whole room, in its whole arrangement, was like a living, breathing thing, a person, a companion clever and quiet and gracious and glad. I sat silent. I had a longing to find a book—but which book was fittest to read here? I thought of Schelling, thought of Fichte, thought of Bergson, thought of Zhuangzi, thought of Dongpo; I thought of many philosophers and poets, and of many lives besides. In such surroundings, I felt, any book at all would be fitting. So I took up a book from the writing-desk near me; it was the essays of Virginia Woolf. I saw Mrs. Stephen talking with Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping, the three of them together, as though she and the very air of the room had dissolved the other two into itself. Stephen had gone out by now, and I, as if forgetting what I had come here for, began to open the book in my hand. But Mrs. Stephen came over. She turned on the standing lamp behind me and said:

"Is this a little more comfortable?"

The light slid down over the thick, emerald leaves of the dwarf bamboo and fell straight on the pages of my book.

"Thank you," I said. Mrs. Stephen moved away again.

Mrs. Stephen turned on the room light as well, and its pale-green shade made me feel the whole room was bathed in clear, bright moonlight.

I do not know how much time had passed when Bai Ping suddenly stood up.

"Now," she said, "Mrs. Stephen, let us go to dinner."

"Then—forgive me," said Mrs. Stephen, "let me go and change my dress."

After Mrs. Stephen had gone out, Stephen came in. Bai Ping said:

"Has the call been put through?"

"Yes," he said. "I've booked very good seats." Then he came over to me and said: "Well, my dear fellow, so you've settled down there to read?"

"In a room like this," I said, "I've lost all wish to eat—or to go out at all."

"Then I hope you'll come often from now on."

"…" I did not answer. I was envying this air, this light, this colour.

"This is a home," said Mei Yingzi. "Does even a confirmed bachelor envy a home?"

"I only envy this beautiful light and colour."

"You don't envy him such a beautiful wife?" Bai Ping laughed.

Mrs. Stephen came out in a white evening gown, a short jacket of deep-purple velvet over her arm, wearing her grave smile. I began to ask myself: with a wife like this, would I be willing to give up my bachelorhood?

"No," I answered myself; "—perhaps, if it need not pass through falling in love."

Mei Yingzi went out, Bai Ping went out, and then Stephen went to telephone; only Mrs. Stephen and I were left in the room. I said:

"Today, for the first time, I know that your world exists somewhere off this earth."

"It is only my own little garden."

"You don't often go out?"

"I wish I could," she said, "but it is not often possible."

"Then to have sought you out today has much disturbed you."

"Once in a while it is rather amusing."

"Forgive me," I said. "Today was entirely of my instigating."

"Truly?" she said, with her kindly, grave smile. "Then hereafter you must forgive me too, as Stephen does."

"Of course—I understand it completely now," I said. "Even I, having once come here, have no wish to go out again."

"Then come and visit whenever you are free."

"It won't disturb you too much?"

"No," she said, "not if you only wish to sit on the sofa and read. Ah—Saturday; hasn't Stephen told you?"

"He has," I said. "I shall certainly come."

Mei Yingzi came back in, her face freshened. Here in the moonlight she was for all the world a sprite flown out of the mountain woods. Then Bai Ping came in too, glowing with a boundless radiance—a sprite as well? Yes; but one risen from a lake.

We went downstairs together. Stephen was in the drawing-room listening to the wireless. Mei Yingzi suddenly took up the telephone; what she spoke was fluent Japanese. I understood not a word. Afterwards Bai Ping told me she had been gracefully declining a dinner engagement.

And so, together, we set out on that night's revelry.

We dined at a Zhenjiang restaurant, and at nine o'clock we went to the concert.

The Shanghai Municipal Orchestra was not, in quality or in numbers, quite equal to a symphony of Beethoven's, but tonight it did its utmost. Among the chorus were several Chinese girls I knew; but there was one Western girl, standing in the last row, whose face too seemed familiar, though try as I might I could not think who she was.

Coming out of the theatre, Mrs. Stephen asked me:

"Were you satisfied?"

"They tried hard, at all events."

"Let us go to the Paramount," said Bai Ping.

"No," I said. "My ears are no longer fit for the din of jazz."

"To the Arcadia, then?"

"Does Mrs. Stephen agree?"

"Very well," she said.

On the way, Mrs. Stephen asked me:

"Did you not find your lamp today?"

"Ah…" I pondered a while, and suddenly it dawned on me that the Western girl in the chorus was none other than last night's Helen Manfield. I laughed, and said: "Helen Manfield! But I scarcely knew her—how different tonight is from last night!"

"Yes, she has changed her hair. I wonder you never asked me about her."

"She studies singing?"

"She is a pupil of Professor Paci—very gifted."

"…" I did not answer; a certain tenderness I had felt about me the night before, and had not been able to trace, rose up again in my heart.

"But is she the lamp you need?" said Mrs. Stephen.

"You mean…?"

"The lamp that dissolves a man's bachelorhood."

"I hadn't thought of it," I said, with a smile.

—

The Arcadia was very lively just then; cars were parked in a crowd outside, and going in we could find no good seats, so we sat in a corner.

When Stephen and his wife rose to dance, I did not know with whom I ought to dance; but whomever I danced with, one of the ladies would be left sitting alone. So I simply did not dance at all.

For the second piece I asked Mei Yingzi to dance, and Stephen and Bai Ping came down onto the floor too. On such occasions there is always the chance of one girl being left to sit alone. I do not know how many pieces later it was that, when Mrs. Stephen and I, and Stephen and Bai Ping, had finished dancing, we found two Japanese men in Western suits, and a woman, sitting in our places and talking with Mei Yingzi; seeing us come back, they all stood up. The woman turned out to be Sophie, a dancing-girl from the Xiangong, who greeted me very warmly. The two Japanese seemed to know Bai Ping well, and were talking with her in Japanese. Mei Yingzi began to make the introductions:

"This is Mr. Suzuki Jiro, and this is Mr. Yamao Motohara." But Bai Ping said, with a mischievous laugh:

"Why not say Major General Suzuki Jiro and Colonel Yamao Motohara?" But when Mei Yingzi introduced "Dr. Stephen," Bai Ping was talking of other things with Sophie. As they seemed to have nowhere to sit, Stephen invited them to sit with us. I did not at all approve of Stephen's doing this; but when the two Japanese had gone off to dance, I said:

"Let us go—somewhere else."

"Isn't it rather good fun to mix with them a little?" said Stephen.

"Perhaps," I said. "But look at all these Chinese here—what sort of man will they take me for?"

"You are a philosopher," he said. "The whole world ought to be matter for your thought."

I did not answer, feeling that to walk off so abruptly would look like cowardice; yet sitting there was dull too, and I had little heart for dancing, taking the floor only once or twice, so that for most of the time I still talked with Mrs. Stephen. The two Japanese seemed very pleased; they talked with me without pause, in excellent Mandarin, but with Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping they spoke always in Japanese. Mei Yingzi in particular was on easy terms with them; yet each time she had laughed her fill, she would glance across at me. I said very little to them. Bai Ping noticed my silence, and when a new piece struck up, she said:

"Come and dance this waltz with me."

As we danced she asked:

"Do you like Mei Yingzi?"

"Of course," I said, smiling. "Is there a girl like that whom no young man likes?"

"Then you really do love her?"

"No, no," I said.

"Then you really don't love her?"

"But first I must know what you mean by love."

"You don't wish to possess her?"

"I do not."

"You don't wish to sacrifice yourself to pursue her?"

"Sacrifice what?"

"Your youth and your time."

"I might rather trade mine for hers," I said, as if in jest.

"Your honour, then?"

"Why should I want honour?"

"I ask you only this," she said: "if you had to sacrifice your honour in order to possess her for a short while, would you be willing?"

Bai Ping's manner was very grave; I pondered a moment and said:

"Honour? And what is honour?"

"It is a second life," she said steadily.

"No—I hold it very cheap," I said. "It is a commodity: opportunity plus money."

"Thank you," she said with a cold laugh. "Then suppose you had to sacrifice your faith?"

"Why do you ask me this?" I was being pressed into discomfort.

"Forgive me," she said calmly. "I count myself your friend."

"What is it you are really driving at?"

"If you take me for your friend, answer me honestly."

"If you take me for your friend," I said, "such a question is an insult to me."

"No," she said. "In our friendship the word 'insult' has ceased to exist."

"Then…"

"It seems you have analysed it all very clearly?"

"Yes."

"I hope all that you are conscious of is correct."

"I think that if it were not," I said, "I should soon find out."

"And tell me when the time comes?"

"Of course," I said.

—

At three o'clock Mrs. Stephen wished to go home, and we all came out together.

Suzuki seemed to be asking Mei Yingzi to let him see her home. Bai Ping said to Mei Yingzi: "Won't you come to my place?"

"No." Mei Yingzi looked at me sidelong, smiling.

"But I am still the host, with full powers."

"It is no longer yesterday." Mei Yingzi's laugh was natural and lovely, her fresh almond-tinted front teeth flashing as she said: "My turn as host begins at half past three; I shall wait for you all at the Liti Café."

The two Japanese shook hands with us; Sophie got first into Suzuki's car, then Mei Yingzi, who gave me a coquettish smile as she stepped up, whereupon the two Japanese shook our hands in triumph. Stephen saw Bai Ping and Mrs. Stephen into the car, and I, carrying Mei Yingzi's smile away with me, got in after them. Stephen said:

"Shall we take Bai Ping home first?"

"Of course—Avenue Joffre." There was a vague roughness in my voice. I felt a loneliness beyond telling.

"Let us all come up and sit at my place a while," Bai Ping said, with forced gaiety.

"No, Bai Ping." Mrs. Stephen spoke as to a little sister: "You should get to bed early too."

"Then will you still come with us tomorrow?" Bai Ping leaned against Mrs. Stephen, coaxing like a child: "Come to my place tomorrow evening."

"Tomorrow I shall not go out," said Mrs. Stephen. "I no longer have the age or the humour for such amusements." She put her arm around Bai Ping.

Bai Ping said nothing, silent as though savouring that rare tenderness; and we were all silent. I began to feel weary—was I weary because of the silence, or silent because of the weariness?

The car drove on and on; I heard the sound of the wheels grinding against the earth, and in the shifting street-light there floated Mei Yingzi's smile.
