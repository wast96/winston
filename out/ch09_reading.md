## 9

When spring came the next year, but for the air-raid sirens that still tore the sky now and then and the searchlights that swept it, one might well have doubted the war was still on. The foreigners' guesthouse at the foot of Mount Gele had sunk utterly into a nightclub for American officers. Every weekend the quartermaster's men trucked in gaudy, powdered women from the city by the load. Most were dancing girls out of work, university students in flight from the fighting, concubines fallen on hard times, army wives who had lost their husbands. In a hall hung with crystal chandeliers they laughed shrilly, danced, drank, and made merry in the dark with the young American officers — sometimes in their beds, sometimes in an open jeep or against some wall. Then, bearing away the men's seed, their nylon stockings and their chocolate, they were driven back through the night into the silent city.

Countless times now, when Lin Nansheng had drunk himself past making out the face of the woman in his arms, the same thought would rise in him unbidden: to let himself die here, quietly, wearily, on the body of some woman whose name he did not know. But every time he woke the next morning, he thought of Zhu Yizhen — of the brief, cold dawns when they had woken together.

It was on just such a night that Lin Nansheng met Miss Lan again. She turned up in the banquet hall of the foreigners' guesthouse in a water-blue poplin dress and, like the roving orioles of the low song halls, refused no man's embrace. That night Lin Nansheng went very still. He leaned in a corner of the bar and watched her as though it were nothing to him, watched until, drunk, she went out of the hall on the arm of an American officer.

Late in the night a whistle blew. It was the signal for the women to leave. Lin Nansheng caught Miss Lan by the truck. "You didn't come here on assignment, I think."

The paint had long since worn off Miss Lan's face, leaving it pale and puffy. She threw Lin Nansheng a languid glance, as though she had never laid eyes on the man before, and said coldly, "What business is it of yours?"

With that she reached up to a companion on the truck and hauled herself aboard.

Not until Miss Lan was down off the truck and back in her little rented room did she stir. She sat a long while on the bed before she rose, filled a wooden basin with clean water, stripped, and began to wash herself.

The water, cold to the bone, brought her all at once to her senses.

When the morning light came through the cracks in the window lattice, Miss Lan was still huddled in the basin, like a wax figure about to melt, her head bowed, her hair fallen across her whole face. But after a full day's sleep, when night came down again, Miss Lan was radiant. She made up her face carefully before the mirror, went to the row of clothes hung in the corner and chose a cheongsam to put on, looked once more in the glass, blew out the oil lamp, took up her handbag, and went out.

Duyou Street was the liveliest place in Chongqing. Come nightfall it turned into the city's own Nanjing Road, ablaze with light from end to end, thick with carriages and crowds, packed with officials and merchants speaking the dialects of every province, with soldiers in every kind of uniform and correspondents from every nation. The place was a great hotchpotch of the whole city — and it was where Miss Lan worked each night. Like many a streetwalker she loitered on the pavement, arms folded, hawking herself to the passersby with her eyes. Sometimes, a cigarette between her fingers, she would go up to some neatly dressed man for a light and haggle with him.

A few nights later, when Lin Nansheng appeared before her out of nowhere, her eyes froze on the instant into two blades of ice. "Go away," she said.

"Don't be like that," Lin Nansheng said. "Come with me."

"I don't do business with you."

Lin Nansheng thought for a moment, then seized her by one arm and began dragging her toward a jeep parked at the curb.

Miss Lan wrenched hard but could not break loose. She tried a grappler's hold, and still could not tear free of that hand. Then all at once she bent her head and, like a she-beast, bit down on the back of Lin Nansheng's hand — bit until the blood ran down his wrist and stained the cuff of his shirt red.

Lin Nansheng felt nothing. With his other hand he took her by the shoulders and, before all those watching eyes, forced her down into the car.

"You needn't pity me." The moment Miss Lan was in the car she went calm. She took a handkerchief from her bag, wiped the blood from her mouth in the rearview mirror, then bound it around Lin Nansheng's wound and said not another word. Arms folded, she smoked one cigarette after another.

Lin Nansheng said nothing either, until he had stopped the car before the quarters of the Central Bank's senior staff, drawn her upstairs, and into a room. He switched on the light. "If you're going to do business, do it with me alone."

This was one of the boltholes Gu Shenyan had made ready for himself while he lived. Before he had even reached Chongqing he had had it rented at great cost, yet had never once used it. As though he had known all along that this day would come for Lin Nansheng, he had said, over the go board, that the key was left at the front desk of the Jialing Hotel.

Miss Lan's eyes traveled once around the four walls; then she walked slowly into the bedroom, switched on the bedside lamp, tossed her bag onto the coat stand, and became another woman. She undid the buttons of her dress, stripped herself bare in no time, then turned her head to Lin Nansheng in the doorway. "What are you waiting for?"

Lin Nansheng stood in the doorway. "The truth is," he said, hesitating, "it isn't this I came for."

Miss Lan pressed her lips into a smile, came forward, took him by both hands, and drew him to the bed. Their lovemaking, before the end, was more like a fight. Afterward Miss Lan reached out and switched off the bedside lamp, and lay rigid in the dark a long time. Then all at once she said, "If you really pity me, help me get back to Shanghai."

When the Juntong's Shanghai station pulled out along its whole line, Miss Lan was ordered to Wusongkou, and only once aboard the ship was she told that the place they were bound for was Chongqing.

Miss Lan's eyes flew wide. "Then what about my child? I can't just abandon him in Shanghai."

The man in charge of the evacuation was middle-aged, with the look of a shop manager. He shook his head. "At your rank you can't bring dependents."

"Then I'll stay. I'm not going anywhere."

The man in charge shook his head again. "My orders are to deliver you all to Chongqing — not one more, not one fewer."

Miss Lan looked back at the men and women seated in the ship's hold. They were her colleagues and comrades-in-arms; many were meeting for the first time, yet now every one of them had lifted their eyes to her.

Miss Lan turned and walked out of the hold, all the way to the wheelhouse, and said to the boatmaster, "Put in to shore."

The boatmaster did not look at her; he looked past her, at the man in charge.

"If you get off this boat, the only thing I carry back is your corpse." The man in charge leveled a pistol at Miss Lan. "Orders from above." Miss Lan said nothing. She stared into the black hole of the muzzle, stared until he lowered it, and then lowered his eyes as well.

The man in charge sighed. "Better to obey orders. Don't make it hard on yourself. And don't make it hard on me."

At least the postal road to Shanghai had never once been cut. As soon as Miss Lan reached Chongqing she was assigned to an outlying inspection office, her daily work the checking of letters and parcels going to and from the occupied zone. In the beginning she wrote several letters a month to the nanny's household, and when her wages fell short she sold the jewelry off her own body to remit them money, begging them to raise her son as they would their own child. But one day the men from the inspectorate came without warning to talk to her. They produced a thick sheaf of letters and money orders. "Your child is only six," they said. "Can your nanny even read?"

"This place is bombed every week," Miss Lan said. "I only want them to know I'm still alive."

Not many days later Miss Lan was moved off the inspection office, and forced out of the headquarters quarters as well. She went to the telephone exchange as a switchboard operator, on a monthly wage that could not keep pace with the soaring prices.

It was just then that the nanny had someone write to say her son had reached school age. That night Miss Lan left the exchange's dormitory. She walked the streets a long time, then pushed into a bar where foreigners gathered, and not until she woke the next morning in a strange bed did she remember that the man asleep beside her was a Canadian machinist.

It was late one night that Miss Lan resolved to slip back to Shanghai. In the sky the air-raid sirens shrilled, the drone of aircraft swelling from far to near; the blacked-out streets were pitch dark and had long since emptied of every soul, yet she stood like a post before a stone memorial arch. The explosions began, and the earth heaved and the sky burned. Miss Lan stood without stirring a hair.

When the inspectorate's plainclothesmen appeared before her again, they brought the steamer ticket she had paid a great sum to have booked — and a pair of handcuffs. "You know," Miss Lan said, "I still have a son to feed in Shanghai."

"And you know that going back into the occupied zone without leave puts you under suspicion of going over to the enemy."

Miss Lan was taken back to the headquarters lockup, and held there a full year and more before she was let go.

Of all this Miss Lan never spoke to Lin Nansheng, and Lin Nansheng never asked. Every weekend, the moment his work ended he left the drill ground and went back to the Central Bank quarters, like a considerate, dutiful husband; after the meal he would sometimes even help with the washing-up. It was the most peaceful stretch of days Lin Nansheng had known.

But one evening, after the meal, Miss Lan lit a candle and sat staring into the flame in silence a long time, then said that today was her son's eighth birthday. When she had said it, she bowed her head, and her eyes darkened with it, and she said she had always thought it was the child who could not do without its mother; only now did she understand that in truth, more often, it is the mother who cannot do without her child.

That night Lin Nansheng did not speak for a long while. After his bath he stood at the window, looking at the grocery across the street that had not yet closed. Then Miss Lan came up without a sound, put her arms around him from behind, rested her chin on his shoulder, and said, "They've taken on a new hand over there." When she had said it she moved her face slowly to his back and pressed it there. "You know," she said, "sooner or later I'll drag you down with me."

Lin Nansheng still said nothing, still watching, without a movement, the dimly lit grocery.

Some weeks later the Central Daily News ran a seven-character regulated poem, 'Ode to the Plum Blossom,' signed Huang Shanyun. Not many days after that, a jeep from the garrison command drove in through the gate of the Sino-American Cooperative Organization and carried Lin Nansheng off to a villa below Futuguan.

The moment Lin Nansheng entered the sitting room, a middle-aged man in a well-cut Western suit came forward smiling, saying he had been sent by the Eighth Route Army office to meet Lin Nansheng on behalf of Mr. Ji in Hong Kong. He put out his hand. "This poem — we've been waiting a long time for it."

Lin Nansheng nodded. He stayed on his feet, and said, hesitating, "I'd like to ask your help... in getting someone out of Chongqing."

"To where?"

"Shanghai."

The middle-aged man thought a moment. "With the reach you have now, you could manage it perfectly well yourself."

"If I could, I wouldn't have come to you." Lin Nansheng sat down in an armchair, then went on, "You can take it as a condition of mine."

The middle-aged man laughed and settled into another chair. "Communists don't make deals."

"Where there's intelligence there's always a deal," Lin Nansheng said. "Without deals, there'd be no Mr. Ji in Hong Kong standing between us."

The middle-aged man laughed again. "What we make revolution on is faith."

"Rome wasn't built in a day." Lin Nansheng turned and looked him in the eye. "You'll have to make allowances for a man who has only just made his choice."
