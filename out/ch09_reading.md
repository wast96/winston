## Section 4. Impatience Breeds a Grave Blunder

### 1. Winning Over the Enemy's Own Confidant as an Inside Man

That the Ji Hongchang case had not been carried through to its full success left one man alone unhappy—Wang Wen, who chafed and brooded over it. More than once he made known to me that he would find some way, come what might, to "recover what had been lost." I took this for no more than a passing heat, a thing said in a sigh; little did I know that he was in dead earnest.

Wang Wen asked me to send him to Tianjin, and to put into his hands the task of sanctioning Shi Yousan. This was a thing that ought to be done, and rare it was that he should have such zeal for it; without a second thought I gave my consent.

It was for this very business that Wang Wen made a special journey to Tianjin, to get in touch with the inside man Xian Hongxia; how matters were advancing there, he would report to me from time to time.

Let that rest for the moment. In the Ji Hongchang case set out in the last section, there are yet a few things not made wholly clear, and I may as well take them up here.

On the afternoon of the ninth of November, at the same time that I sent Miss Yang Yushan to the Guomin Hotel to spy out Ji Hongchang's movements, the reason I begged group chief Lü Yimin to hurry back and hold fast to the two men Zheng Enpu and Fu Danchi was that the turn of events had not squared with what they had reported, and so my trust in them had begun to waver. That is to say, I suspected that what they told me was not true, and might even hold something to my own hurt. Had the re-checking shown that Ji Hongchang was no longer at the Guomin Hotel, then not only would the misunderstanding have deepened, but the whole great matter might very likely have been ruined thereby. Happily it was proven afterward that there had indeed been good cause for it all, and thus a misunderstanding was dispelled; though it did make us seem, it must be said, over-suspicious by half.

Miss Yang Yushan was sharp and capable, brave and composed; by her outstanding showing in this case she became one of the fine cadres of our work. Because she was the liaison of the Tianjin "Intelligence Group," she was often in close touch with Zheng Enpu; and after the "Military Group" that Zheng Enpu headed was set up, the "Tianjin Station" still sent Yang Yushan to keep liaison with the "Military Group." For this reason, as the days went by feeling grew between Zheng and Yang, and they became man and wife. Thereafter the two of them shared trouble and hardship together, their love the deeper for it, a happy household. In 1949 elder brother Zheng Enpu came to Taiwan alone; his wife Yang Yushan, kept back at Beiping by circumstance, could not follow—and who could have foreseen that this one parting would prove a parting forever!

When the writer came home from overseas in 1958 and clasped hands once more with elder brother Zheng Enpu, it was at a temple on Yangmingshan; and amid our mutual sighing I learned that he had already taken to a plain vegetarian's fare and the chanting of the sutras, and shunned all talk of the past. Some years before, he had gone further and taken the tonsure, his dharma-name "Xingci," and become the abbot presiding over the Daci Temple; by now he was nearing his ninetieth year. I had one wish—that we might contrive to meet again and talk out a whole day together. But hindered always by the affairs of the world, I put it off day after day, and so it has drifted down to now. (Writing to this point, the time is the end of July 1980.)

My wish is not to be fulfilled. On the fourteenth of August, 1980, word came that the old monk Xingci of the Daci Temple had passed into nirvana. My honored elder brother Zheng Enpu had bidden the world farewell. Here again was a thing undone through mere putting-off, a matter for a remorse past all mending. Elder brother Zheng! Let me first beg your pardon before your spirit-tablet; while yet I have life in me, grant that I may find some way to redeem it.

To speak again of Mr. Fu Danchi: in the later work of sanctioning Yin Rugeng, and of turning the two commanders of the East Hebei puppet Peace-Preservation Corps, Zhang Qingyu and Zhang Yantian, he made a great showing; but the particulars I shall leave for the next section.

As for that gallant young man ready to lend a hand—none other than little Chen Guorui, Yang Yushan's own younger brother—he was sent up to Nanjing for training. A year later we should meet again in a most awkward scene, which was a coincidence too. That the brother and sister bore different surnames was owing to his having been given out in adoption to carry on another line.

I mentioned in passing in the last section that Wu Ping, after the Ji case was over, was at once recalled to Nanjing, and, it is understood, held a month in close confinement. What it was truly for, one can only guess blindly: if not to make expiation for the dead Wang Zixiang, the former "Tianjin Station" chief, then it was to appease the wrath of Wang Zixiang's younger sister Wang Yumei; for over Wang Zixiang's death, Wang Yumei always held that Wu Ping had delayed in getting him to a doctor for emergency aid—which was a wrong done him vast as heaven. After Wu Ping had served out his confinement, Mr. Dai recommended him to a clerkship in the Executive Yuan; by 1939 he had risen at Chongqing to be chief of the general-affairs section, and coming to Taiwan in 1949 he still held that same post. Upright and self-denying, he won much praise. It is a pity that heaven granted him no long years: he came to grief in 1951, when a pedicab overturned crossing a bridge, and, too badly hurt to be saved, he died, being but a little over forty.

In 1936, when the writer took up again the post of chief of the "Tianjin Station," the former chief of the "Tianjin Station Intelligence Group," Lü Yimin, was no longer in office. Whether he had been posted elsewhere, or had already been released from the working connection, in these several decades since I have never again heard any word of him.

As for how our superiors received the Ji Hongchang case, it was no longer with the warmth the Zhang Jingyao case had drawn. To judge the thing plainly, the Zhang Jingyao case was done clean and neat; the Ji Hongchang case was, throughout, something of a draggled and muddy affair. To go unpunished was already a mark of our superiors' forbearance.

Setting aside the standpoint of the task, and speaking only of the state of a working man's mind: the reason an executor dares to brave danger and hardship is, at the highest, grounded wholly in the idea of the nation and in political ideals; and next after that comes the will to excel and the sense of responsibility. As for fame, gain, and reward, these of course are never laid to heart. Beyond this there is yet another realm, and that is a certain satisfaction in a success that is one's own.

Once the Ji case had happened, it was in truth far greater in its effect than the Zhang Jingyao case; for Zhang Jingyao was but a small knot of the leavings of a warlord clique, without organization and past their prime, wanting alike in political consciousness and in any planned design, and having in their bearing no set of fine-sounding phrases to rally men with, so that they could hardly win the sympathy of the common run. With Ji Hongchang it was otherwise: he not only had a political background propping him up behind the scenes, but he was himself a member of that very political body; and the rest of them—the Communist elements Xuan Xiafu, Nan Hanchen, Zhang Mutao, and so on—were no simple sort either. Thus, being deep-laid in scheme and wide in wiles, they could hatch a great many devilish notions for the working of evil. Most of all they gave themselves to fabricating fine-sounding lies and false doctrines, and, plying their tricks of propaganda, to the befooling of the broad masses. But after that one blow at the Guomin Hotel, this pack of ox-devils and snake-spirits were struck dumb as cicadas in the cold, and drew in their heads and would not stir; even within the foreign concessions they no longer dared carry on openly. And so, in point of actual effect, it forced the Communist Party at the very least to give up the plot it had laid, and to change its manner of working.

In the several decades since, the Chinese Communist propaganda and cultural departments, for the sake of Ji Hongchang's death, have brought out one after another some five or six books and periodicals, and made besides a moving picture, "General Ji Hongchang," to hold up Ji Hongchang as what a great and wonderful "revolutionary" figure he was. All this is enough to show how important to them was Ji Hongchang's dying of his own blundering ruin.

The case of the shooting of Ji Hongchang at the Guomin Hotel in the French concession of Tianjin in 1934, one dead and several wounded—after the passage of more than forty years, this is the first time its inner story has been laid bare and made public; I beg it may be held of worth.

Next I shall set down the course of the sanctioning of Shi Yousan, that traitorous vermin under standing warrant of arrest.

The newly come action man of the "Beiping Station," Wang Wen, offering himself for the work, had already gone back and forth between Beiping and Tianjin many times to win over Shi Yousan's personal adjutant Xian Hongxia. On his latest trip, coming back to Beiping from Tianjin, Wang Wen made his oral report in all earnest: "I have met with Xian Hongxia. This business holds much hope, only it cannot go forward by the appointed procedure. The state of things at present is this: Xian Hongxia is my sworn brother in life and death; by the great principle of duty, he ought to forsake the dark for the light, and by our private friendship, he is bound too to keep faith with what is right. So he asks nothing beforehand—to speak plainly, he sets no conditions whatever. First, if he can but not disgrace the charge, and by good fortune accomplish the task, then everything hereafter he leaves wholly to our superiors' disposing. There is, however, one thing: he does not wish his name to be spoken until he has shown some result. I know this is a hard thing, but I have already struck my chest and given him my word on it; and on this point I must beg Mr. Chen to bear the responsibility."

I turned it over in my mind: Xian Hongxia's unwillingness to give out his name might well spring from misgivings of his own—likely not from fear for his own safety, but perhaps from the thought that, should the thing miscarry, why leave a handle against himself? In truth this is but common human feeling; and since Wang Wen asked me to bear it, then I might as well simply grant it.

Then, thinking again—no, it would not do. At the very least a case must be lodged with our superiors beforehand; only so would it be proper. I told Wang Wen my meaning, and Wang Wen held that once he had been in touch with Xian Hongxia one more time and settled on a concrete plan of action, it would still not be too late to report the case for the record.

Such, in the main, was how the work of sanctioning Shi Yousan began. The time was about December 1934, not long after the Ji Hongchang case.

The "Beiping Station" at this period had undergone no great change; the general run of the work had got onto a steadier footing than before, and its chief tasks lay still in the two great heads of "intelligence-gathering" and "action against traitors."

The "Beiping Station" was set up on Wofosi Street in the western city; the house was a very large one, with four courtyards in depth. The main gate gave onto the street, and turning left within it there was a second gate, and only past the second gate came the first courtyard. Brother Bai Shiwei, with his family of four, lodged in the three southern rooms; my wife and I, with a serving-woman besides, took up the four northern rooms—the Beiping folk call these the principal rooms. Of these, one served as bedroom, one did double duty as parlor and dining room, the largest was made into an office, and the smallest was where the servant lived. There were besides three eastern wing-rooms, given over to kitchen, storeroom, and the like. Through a covered passage was the second courtyard, which had its own court, but only four northern rooms; there Qi Nanpu and his wife lodged, together with his wife's people. The third courtyard also had four rooms, tenanted severally by the courier Chen Guodong, the driver Chen Guozhi, and the head cook Li Huaizhang. Coming to the fourth courtyard, there was yet another row of rooms, which, save for the coal and odds and ends heaped there, stood empty for want of use. Many as the rooms were, the place had no proper plan to it; the rent still ran to forty yuan the month, which in those days was reckoned no cheap figure.

Writing to this point, I must put in a few words of explanation, for it bears on the way the story unfolds. Above there was that phrase, "my wife and I." Just so: by this year I was already twenty-five, and had a household of my own. Mr. Dai thought very little of my setting up a family, yet was too delicate to say anything of it outright, and in the end could only feign not to know. By our rule—that is, our inner discipline—a matter of marriage, whether taking a wife or giving a daughter, might be carried out only after being petitioned for and granted. Because in those days the rule was not strictly enforced, and because my own marriage was somewhat out of the common, I had not put in an application beforehand. Yet this had its effect upon me, and upon my work, alike.

The situation of the "Beiping Station" was exceedingly quiet: passers-by were few, and seldom did anyone halt nearby. On the left lay the Wofo Temple, a cold, deserted shrine where no incense burned; on the right were dwelling houses, each sweeping the snow from its own door and never coming and going with the rest. Its greatest fault was that it had no back gate. Between the second and third courtyards stood a low wall, and to guard against thieves we had asked the landlord to put up a run of barbed wire, but the landlord would never do it. Beyond the low wall lay a lonely little lane, and only after two turnings did it come out onto the main street.

The office was like an ill-appointed study: many of the documents were not put out of sight, but were sometimes left in a drawer or laid out on the desktop. To call it a place of secrets was hardly fitting. Because at that time the larger scene had Minister He presiding over the military and civil affairs of North China, we did not come up to the strict requirements of secret work—which was truly a thing that ought not to have been.

The only one who regularly handled the paperwork in the office was the "Beiping Station" clerk Wang Yunsun; only the weightier matters did he bring for me to see. In truth, what were called documents were no more than a few small slips of paper, big and little and unmatched. At times, too, we met here with comrades of the inside and field staff; but as for outside liaison, save where a place was fixed by ad hoc arrangement, it was still mostly carried on at our place of origin—No. 18 North Chang Street.

Mr. Wang Pingyi, the inspector of the Beiping District, had his office elsewhere, and rarely came the once to the "Beiping Station" on Wofosi Street; we seldom so much as met. On the face of it he seemed to trouble himself little about the affairs of the "Beiping Station," but whether behind my back he made any report to our superiors, that I did not know. To tell the truth, because I in no way approved of this "inspectorate system," I had, in consequence, no great regard in my heart for the "inspector" stationed in Beiping either; and though I did not go so far as to be thought haughty and ill-mannered, I must always have given an impression of coldness. It came of my being young then, and ignorant of the ways of the world.

To say that a body of men can hardly escape the trouble of faction and strife seems a thing grown commonplace; yet in the Juntong of Mr. Dai's lifetime it never once arose—which may be counted, too, among Mr. Dai's gifts. Let me cite one instance in proof:

The Beiping District inspector Wang Pingyi had studied in Russia, and was formerly a subordinate of Mr. Deng Wenyi in the Investigation Section of the Nanchang Field Headquarters. After Mr. Dai Yunong took on the section concurrently, and the Second Bureau of the Juntong swallowed up the Investigation Section of the Nanchang Field Headquarters, a batch of new men were of a sudden added within the Second Bureau of the Juntong. Of these, the greater part were students returned from Russia and men who had broken with the Communists; and their average level was superior to that of Mr. Dai's old cadre, whose backbone was his fellow-students of the Military Academy—this is no mere civility, still less a flattery, but a plain fact. And so they won much of Mr. Dai's heavy trust.

By rights, in such a case, between old and new there could hardly fail to be a drawing of lines, and it were easy enough for jostling and friction to arise. Yet under Mr. Dai's masterly leading it was handled so that the two blended like water and milk, and all went off without a hitch. Of course single incidents were not wanting; but taken as a whole, it must be said the thing truly reached the point of being welded into one, of a seamless cooperation. As for my own want of cooperation with Wang Pingyi, that was a small matter thrown up by the "inspectorate system," and no strife of houses at all.

The above is the general state of the "Beiping Station" at the close of 1934; it was also the most settled moment the "Beiping Station" had known since its founding. From this point on, through the ceaseless befalling of mishaps, the opening-out of the work and the firmness of its foundation were greatly shaken. If blame is to be reckoned up, it must be laid to my own dereliction and misjudgment. And this must be told from the sanctioning of Shi Yousan onward:

Wang Wen came back again from Tianjin and laid before me a full report: "The sanctioning of Shi Yousan has advanced by a great stride; the man we sought to win over—Xian Hongxia—has now been brought into firm and settled contact."

Wang Wen went on: "Xian Hongxia is a careful, close-thinking man, who goes at no task rashly. In principle he has accepted our request, but in the manner of it—that is, in the choice of the means of carrying it out—he hopes it may best be left to him to weigh the circumstances and act as expediency shall require."

Hearing thus far, and eager to know whether concrete steps had yet been settled with Xian Hongxia, I asked Wang Wen: "Are there still difficulties and hindrances?" Wang Wen went on: "Up to now, nothing concrete has been decided—not that he wavers or hangs back, but the chief reason is that he feels keenly how, single-handed and alone, one hand cannot clap.

"Xian Hongxia is much afraid we shall not make allowance for him, and he laid it before me very earnestly: in the circumstances he stands in, even were he to stake his very life and fight it out to the death, he might still not fully accomplish the task. So he means to spend a little further effort in drawing in a partner from among his fellows; and if that be granted, then a road can be marked out. At present things rest just here; at the next meeting it may all take clearer shape."

What Wang Wen reported I of course believed without doubt; yet for prudence's sake, and to ease the work forward, there were some things it was still needful to understand more deeply. So I put to Wang Wen a number of questions in all seriousness. What I most cared to know was still how Wang Wen kept in touch with Xian Hongxia, and by what manner of liaison between them; and on this point Wang Wen gave a very clear account. He said:

"In the western quarter of Tianjin there is a grain-and-provisions store that deals besides in cotton and country produce, an old-established firm, the Sanyicheng, in business now these many years. It is run by folk of Baodi, our own native place. Sometimes our fellow-townsmen entrust this grain store with the carrying of a letter or the sending of money, and as the days went by it came quietly to do a small trade in remittances as well, which the townsfolk find convenient. The old proprietor of this concern is a full generation above us, and we all address him as Old Uncle Liu; the chief manager is the old proprietor's own nephew, Liu Zhaonan, of about our own age, who studied together with us when we were boys, so that whenever old times are brought up one feels a particular closeness. My contact with Xian Hongxia is all kept up through his go-between offices.

"Because there is this bond among the three of us, to leave word or carry a message we entrust it all to Liu Zhaonan to see to. I know there is good in this and bad alike: the good is that we run no risk of the link being broken off; the bad is just that our business he too must come to know in outline. Still, I trust he will not noise it abroad—for should word once leak out, it would do him no good either."

Hearing Wang Wen say this, I thought it a happy stroke of chance; a manner of liaison such as this was as though made in heaven, and could not be more to be relied upon.

As for the go-between's coming to know something of the matter, that too was a thing not to be helped, and made no grave misgiving.

Next I asked further about Xian Hongxia's standing and station in Shi Yousan's household, and the general state of the guard kept at the Shi place. Wang Wen said that, by what he knew from what Xian Hongxia had let fall in talk at one time and another, the case was in the main thus. He said:

"Shi Yousan's dwelling is on a quiet street in the Japanese concession—if I have it not wrong, it is Akita Street. The Shi house stands at the eastern end of this street. All the neighbors to either side of the Shi place are deep mansions within great courtyards, holding no traffic with one another. Outside the main gate it is cheerless from morning to night! Even the peddlers who cry their wares down the lanes rarely pass this way. A high wall fronts the street, its black-lacquered great gate always shut fast; on the gate, beyond the house-number, there is not so much as a sign, so that one who does not know the inside of it—who would ever guess this to be the Shi mansion? Within the main gate are gatehouses to left and right: in the left is stationed a guard in plain clothes, the right is the porter's lodge. Beyond a long narrow little court is yet a second gate, a gate that stands, though set there, forever open—what is called a pass-through gate. Within the second gate is a large oblong court: the three eastern rooms house two Japanese gendarmes, the three western rooms hold five attendants in all, Xian Hongxia among them. Facing south with its back to the north is a two-storied building, with eight or nine halls and rooms, large and small; here Shi Yousan himself and his womenfolk dwell. At present there are three wives at his side, and the best-favored is the Third, fullest of schemes, letting no matter pass unmeddled with. Behind the building is yet a back court, where the menservants and maidservants, the cook and the coachman, all lodge in this one row of rooms. Beside the back court there was once a small corner door, but for tightness' sake it has been locked and left unused, so that the servants too go in and out all by the main gate.

"Among the five attendants, Xian Hongxia and the rest, there is one headman, a lieutenant colonel by rank in the army; but Shi Yousan holding now no office, in the Shi mansion they all address him as Staff Officer He. These five all have some old tie with Shi Yousan; one may say every one of them is Shi Yousan's own trusted man. Of the guards at the gatehouse in front there are but two men, and of the porter in the lodge but one; and these are all under the command of that Staff Officer He—they can be reckoned no more than his feelers thrown outward.

"The Japanese gendarmes are toward Shi Yousan, it may be, at once protectors and watchers; their way is to keep close within themselves, and they have little traffic with Shi's subordinates. Or one might say they hold those few attendants somewhat in contempt. The Japanese gendarmes on duty at Shi Yousan's are changed by an irregular rotation, relieved every week or two; and the highest in rank among them is no more than a sergeant-major.

"Shi Yousan smokes the great smoke, and goes late to bed and late to rise. In the common way he seldom goes out, and the guests who call are not many either. Whenever he does go out, he never says beforehand to what place; only once the car is moving does he point this way and that, east and west, so that not even the driver knows where they are bound. Shi Yousan's going-out too is confined within the concessions; to Chinese ground of course he never dares go at all. He knows well enough that the government has a standing warrant out to take him and bring him to book."

I heard this passage through with careful patience, and felt it grow harder and harder. In such a case, to seek an opening to strike—whether at his house, or on the road as he went, or by choosing a fixed spot to lie in wait for him—all would need a close and finely wrought plan. To lean on Xian Hongxia's strength alone, even were he to show a moment's valor and fight it out to the death, would, I feared, hardly bring the thing off.

### 2. The Choice Between Shooting and Poisoning

Shi Yousan was by nature fickle and inconstant, suspicious and given to change; he made a practice of trimming and seizing chances, of watching the wind to set his helm, and for many years he never once let go that one sure trick of holding troops to guard himself. Yet, to judge his doings, he belonged to the breed of the "small stir-up-trouble" sort, and could never in the end mount the great hall of state.

Consider, then, some of Shi Yousan's base doings, for which there is firm evidence.

In 1929, the Second Group Army of Feng Yuxiang's forces rose against the government; on the fifteenth of May, headed by his general Liu Yufen, it issued a circular telegram opposing the Central Government, and put Feng Yuxiang forward as "commander-in-chief of the Northwest Army for the Defense of the Party and the Salvation of the Nation." But two other generals of Feng's forces, Han Fuju and Shi Yousan, sent a telegram of their own upholding the Central Government, and so cut the ground from under Feng Yuxiang's feet. Feng thereupon beat a retreat, giving out that he had gone "into the mountains to read books."

In 1930, Yan Xishan and Feng Yuxiang joined hands to betray the Central Government—what the histories call the "Yan-Feng Revolt." Shi Yousan once more went over to Yan and Feng, and was named "governor of Shandong province."

Before long Yan and Feng were beaten, and Shi Yousan again treated with the Central Government to submit; the Central Government named him once more commander-in-chief of the Thirteenth Route Army, garrisoning Shunde in Hebei, under the control of Zhang Xueliang.

In 1931 the Southwest raised a great work of secession and defied the Central Government. Shi Yousan, together with other military commanders of North China—Yu Xuezhong, Wang Shuchang, Liu Yifei, Li Peiji, Song Zheyuan, Sun Dianying, Pang Bingxun, and the rest—jointly issued a circular telegram denouncing the Southwest for its wrongdoing. This again was plainly a speculator's turn that cost him nothing.

On the twentieth of July, 1931, Shi Yousan led his troops in revolt at Shunde, and the Central Government thereupon, on the twenty-fourth of July, openly issued a warrant for his arrest. Shi Yousan made his way to Dalian, and, throwing in with the Japanese, sank to a traitor to the state.

On the eighth of November, 1931, the peace-keeping authorities of Tianjin municipality received intelligence to this effect: that certain warlord leavings—Shi Yousan, Zhang Bi, Li Jichun, and others—at the instigation of the Japanese soldier Doihara Kenji, were organizing a "plainclothes corps" within the Japanese concession, with intent to enter the city of Tianjin and stir up disorder.

At half past ten that same night, there did indeed gather, awaiting orders, some two thousand and more plainclothes ruffians at the Haiguang Temple within the Japanese concession (a place-name; the site of the barracks of the Japanese garrison army). About eleven o'clock, more than a hundred plainclothes ruffians first went into action, seeking to fall upon the police of the Chinese quarter; thereafter several hundred more rushed out from the Japanese concession, and, dividing, struck at the Hebei provincial government, the Tianjin municipal government, the Tianjin public security bureau, and other places, firing wildly along the way and flinging hand grenades at random. In one bout of confusion many were hurt, and, friend and foe alike, they were all Chinese!

It ran on into the morning of the ninth: from the Japanese concession more than thirty shells were fired, striking without aim into the streets and lanes of the Chinese quarter, and again many of our Chinese countrymen were killed and wounded. On examining the shell-fragments, there were on them the words "Made in Taishō 15." The rifles that the Tianjin peace-preservation corps and the police seized from the "plainclothes corps" were likewise Japanese-made Type 38s, and rifles turned out at the Shenyang Arsenal, taken by the Japanese after their seizure of the Northeast.

By the confession of the ruffians arrested, each man had drawn forty silver dollars in reward.

This affair of disorder was not put down until the eighteenth, having begun on the eighth.

By the twenty-sixth of November, at Doihara's instigation, Zhang Bi, Li Jichun, Shi Yousan, and the rest raised a disturbance once more. The Japanese army went so far as to move up marines in support, and the Japanese Kwantung Army stationed in the Northeast at once sent out troops to answer them, swiftly taking Xinmin and spying toward Jinzhou. This action was for the time broken off because of opposition from the high command of the Japanese army. But that Shi Yousan and the others should have been content to play the hawks and hounds of the Japanese—this was indeed a pitch of frenzied depravity.

In 1932, the Japanese made use of Shi Yousan to recruit stragglers and idle soldiery, form them into an army, and carry on harassing military activity in the border region of Rehe and Hebei, one after another.

At the beginning of 1933, in the "North China Joint-Rule Government" that the deputy chief of staff of the Japanese Kwantung Army, Itagaki Seishirō, was plotting at Tianjin, Shi Yousan too was set down upon the list.

In March 1933, Shi Yousan plied his old trick again: at Tianjin he gathered his scattered old troops once more, bought up local rowdies and rascals, formed a "plainclothes corps," and harassed the peace of the district, to fit in with Itagaki's scheme of action.

On the eighteenth of May, 1933, Shi Yousan mustered a small band of his old troops and declared independence at Luan county in Hebei; but, ill-founded as it was, it fell apart the moment it was raised.

In April 1934, the "chief of the Fengtian Special Service Agency" of the Japanese Kwantung Army at Shenyang, Doihara Kenji, under the borrowed name of the "North China People's Patriotic Association," put forward to the Japanese General Staff in Tokyo a memorial on the "policy for the salvation of North China," in which he urged that "in order to avert the outbreak of a great war in the Far East, and to secure the peace of East Asia, the most pressing need at present is to establish a new North China regime."

The "new North China regime" that Doihara spoke of was still in the brewing, its name not yet fixed; those taking part in the affair included Li Jichun, Shi Yousan, Bai Jianwu, and others of the sort. And the army of this "new regime" they proposed to call the "Dingwu Army."

The above is the general run of Shi Yousan's fickle inconstancy, his trimming and chance-seeking, his owning a bandit for a father—a frenzied depravity, a shamelessness, a small-souled meanness, a losing of face beyond all further loss. But it did not stop even here; in what came after there were many turns and changes yet. At this moment, Shi Yousan was lying hid in the Japanese concession of Tianjin, plotting rebellion afresh, and so kept a very strict guard. And it was just at this time, too, that we, with the action man Wang Wen in charge, had talked round Shi Yousan's personal adjutant Xian Hongxia, and were watching for the chance to sanction him.

So far as the ease or hardness of the work went, this was no good moment for it; yet just because his evildoing was so manifold, it were best he should be rooted out the sooner, that the sprouts of disorder might be checked. Therefore it was with a most vigorous zeal that we pushed the business on.

I called together, more than once, Brother Bai Shiwei, who had charge of the action work, and comrade Wang Wen, who was to carry out this task, to weigh it over; our aim was to settle upon a manner of carrying it out—that is, given that Xian Hongxia was to be used as our inside man, what method were the more fitting.

What I held for was still to take the hard course of armed sanction—to say it plainly, to make an end of him with firearms. For to do it thus lay not merely in wiping out a single traitor to the state, but at the same time in making those who were sunk in their delusion or gone astray take warning; it could work too the deterrent effect of "killing one to warn a hundred."

On this point Bai, Wang, and I had no difference of view; what they weighed was rather the question of how much chance of success there was, and of the odds for and against. By what Xian Hongxia had said to Wang Wen's face—that even were he to fight it out reckless of all, staking his life, he might still not gain his end—it was plain this thing must cost great pains, and so we must work out a set method truly and thoroughly.

Xian Hongxia hoped, to be sure, that he might weigh the circumstances and act as expediency required; yet we might as well frame a number of suppositions, to lay before him at need for his choosing, and at the least to help open up his lines of thought.

The common conclusion of the three of us, after weighing it, may be set out under two suppositions.

The first: supposing action be bided for within Shi Yousan's own house. To rely on Xian Hongxia alone as our inside man would, I feared, leave us too weak and lonely of force; even should a chance offer, amid barrier upon barrier there would be no laying hand to it. Plainly, Xian Hongxia could offer only his own willingness, and could not talk round or win over all the rest. Yet among Shi Yousan's five attendants, his two guards, and the several servants, to spend some effort in drawing one or two over to our side was not a thing past all hope.

Shi Yousan slept on the second floor, and smoked the great smoke on the second floor; he sat and rose and took his meals, and now and then received a guest, in the principal hall on the ground floor. In the ordinary way, unless he called for them, or a maid brought his word, the attendants waiting in the western wing-room—all save that Staff Officer He—might not walk into the principal hall of their own accord and at will. For this reason Xian Hongxia's chances of coming near Shi Yousan by himself alone were not many. And even were there a chance to come near Shi on the spur of the moment, he could not necessarily fire and turn on his heel and go. As one may well imagine, if all were not arranged beforehand, it would be very hard to get clear of the scene afterward—hard even to get out at the great gate of the Shi house; and even had he broken out of the Shi house, the time lost would leave no getting out of the Japanese concession.

In the action work planned and directed under my hand, I held fast throughout to one fundamental principle, and that was "the work foremost, safety weightiest." The meaning of this saying is: though every possible thing be done to accomplish the task, there must yet be planned, for the action men, the keeping of a way to live. An action plan with no "road of life" in it could, in my drafting and review, by no means stand.

To be sure, no action plan is carried out without some brush with danger; and where there is danger, sacrifice of course can hardly be escaped. But this I hold to be the fruit of a plan not close-wrought enough, or of some sudden change of circumstance. As for the so-called resolve to "die for certain," that is a spurring phrase standing for spiritual force; had I foreseen beforehand that a man went thither "to certain death with no return," I would sooner amend a plan already fixed, or even give up that plan and seek another way, than "order" my subordinate to go forth "to his death."

True, I too have used such means as "hard fighting" or "staking all" to slay the enemy and gain the end; but in such a case the ground I stood on was this—that only by fighting so that "you die" can "I live"; if it be "jade and stone burned up together," or worse, if unable to overmatch the foe one throws away one's own life instead, then that is a "battle lost," and no sending of a man "to his death" at all!

The reason I am thus long-winded is to make it plain: we hoped indeed, by using Xian Hongxia's connection, to rid ourselves of Shi Yousan smoothly and with success; but this is a thing wholly apart from the notions of "fighting poison with poison" or "using the barbarian to curb the barbarian." We held Xian Hongxia to be a patriotic man of honor, deep in the understanding of duty; and to bring such devices to bear upon him would be against the rule of morality, and unjust besides.

Before ever Xian Hongxia had joined the ranks of our work, he was, to be sure, Shi Yousan's personal adjutant; but once he had entered our line of battle, then, whether he showed a result or no, there could be no slighting him in the least. So we had absolutely no settled design of bringing Xian Hongxia to perish together with Shi Yousan. That is to say: to gain a task's end by playing tricks, laying snares, sinking a man into sacrifice without his knowing it—such a way of doing was never once weighed in the work planned under my hand. Not only would it fail of the frank and the upright, the open and the square, but even were it brought off it would leave one uneasy a whole life long.

In the "long foreword" at the head of this book I touched on the question of the moral bounds of secret-service work. The passage above may serve just as an instance of it. And here we would earnestly proclaim to the world: that the secret-service work of the Republic-of-China model keeps a "moral order," in full accord with China's own inborn cultural tradition. Those worthless things that flaunt their tyranny and play at "united front," let them be; but even the countries that make great parade of "human rights"—if their secret-service doings be measured by our moral standard, then, to tell the truth, they do not pass.

This, though it be talk beside the point, has all to do with the question of safety that ought to be weighed in a work plan. What we looked for from Xian Hongxia was to smite a stubborn foe with all our might; but on no account would we have Xian Hongxia perish for nothing. Our conclusion, then, was this: that if we counted on Xian Hongxia's single strength to take action within Shi Yousan's house and yet withdraw in safety, it was a thing hardly to be looked for at all.

The second: supposing action be taken while Shi Yousan is out—to see whether there be a chance in it?

Of late Shi Yousan went out very little, though now and again he did stir abroad. We knew that in no case did he dare set foot on Chinese ground—he dared not so much as cross a single bridge to the Italian concession east of the river. The places he went were, beyond the Japanese concession, confined too to the British and French concessions, those two alone. Because of the happening of the Zhang Jingyao, Ji Hongchang, and other like affairs, he was already on his guard in his mind: he knew well it might very likely come to be his turn. Whenever he went out, he never told beforehand what place he was bound for, nor whom he would meet, nor how long he would stay abroad, nor when he might come home, and the like; and in such a case there was, of course, no foretelling of it to make dispositions. Say, for instance—had one known beforehand when he would come out, one might have laid an ambush and waylaid him; had one known beforehand what place he was going to, one might have made arrangements and set upon him; had one known more still beforehand, one might have used the several methods of matching within to without, of a joint attack from inside and out, to deal with him.

The pity was that of what we ought to have known, we knew nothing whatever; and what Xian Hongxia could learn beforehand was very limited too. Xian Hongxia, though Shi Yousan's personal adjutant, did not necessarily go out with Shi Yousan every time; only that staff officer surnamed He was the fixed and indispensable attendant. That being so—supposing one could win over the man surnamed He, would not the thing then be easy? But this again was near to a wishful fond reckoning; after Xian Hongxia had turned it over and over, he held it wholly impossible, and dared not make the attempt.

And so the notion of finding a chance outside, of laying a plan to deal with Shi Yousan while he was abroad, was, under the conditions we then had, a thing I feared past our doing; it would take much further effort before it could be managed.

The conclusion of the three of us in council was but this. In a word: having got Xian Hongxia as our inside man, we could not yet act rashly; to press on heedlessly and in haste would but bring the thing to ruin.

To move the business forward, Wang Wen was to make another journey to Tianjin. Before Wang Wen set out, I spoke my hope of meeting Xian Hongxia once, and explained: "I think that to meet him, and, on our superiors' behalf, to give him encouragement, might strengthen his confidence the more. My meaning is only this, and there is no other point of unease in it." Wang Wen understood me well, and said: "To do so is of course good, only it must depend on how Xian Hongxia takes it; if he agrees, I will arrange the time and place to meet."

This time Wang Wen stayed but two days at Tianjin and came back. There was a look of triumph on Wang Wen's face, showing that real progress had been made. In his oral report he spoke quite in the concrete, and it was truly matter for rejoicing.

He said: "This trip was a great gain. Xian Hongxia has talked round the fellow who is with him—that is, one of the five attendants in Shi Yousan's mansion. By what Xian Hongxia tells me, the man is surnamed Shi; whether he be truly surnamed Shi there is no way to look into, nor is it well to press the matter, but I think it of no import—it is enough that our inside force be increased by one.

"Adjutant Shi and Xian Hongxia are on very good private terms; of the five they two are the closest, in the common way helping one another in a pinch, each opening his heart to the other. Though not quite bosom friends, they may be reckoned good friends. This man is full of grievances, and is most of all discontented with Shi Yousan's doings and with the harsh, ungenerous way he uses his men; in secret he has told Xian Hongxia more than once that he no longer wishes to go on—a great strapping fellow, and can he not feed himself by some work or other? Once Adjutant Shi asked Shi Yousan to his face for a long leave, and was roundly cursed for it, and forbidden by force to stir a step from him.

"To say that he therefore bore a grudge in his heart—that goes too far; but if, taking Adjutant Shi at a moment when his mood was low, one led him on, it was easy enough for him to be shaken. It was in just such a case that Xian Hongxia talked him round.

"At first Xian Hongxia had not been quite sure enough to speak out our final aim, for fear that, refusing, the man might sour the whole matter instead. Afterward—by no telling how much talk it cost—he did at last, under the great principle of ridding the people of a scourge and rooting out a traitor for the nation's sake, move him: he nodded and gave his consent.

"Xian and Shi, the two of them, taking as their theme what were the most favorable circumstances and the most fitting moment, had exchanged views many times; and they held that to do it in the Shi house was still too full of misgivings, and that, the thing once done, it would not be easy to withdraw in safety. It were best to wait for a time when the two of them together attended Shi Yousan out of doors; then they might turn the chance to convenience. Even were Staff Officer He guarding at his side, a man of that Staff Officer He's sort would never again lay down his life for Shi Yousan—he would watch the wind to set his helm, and fold his hands and not meddle; while as for the car's driver, he never carried a gun, and, by Shi's common usage of his men, he too would not put a hand in—the more so as he could do nothing anyway.

"This, among the many workable ways, is the most favorable choice: it can both accomplish the task and withdraw in safety. Its one defect is that there is no taking the initiative—that is, one can only wait for the chance. So in the matter of timing it is very hard to reckon in advance: it may be tomorrow, or it may be some weeks' waiting. And if one force a chance, one may very well show a flaw.

"It is just because one cannot take the initiative, and yet dreads the long night with its many dreams, that Xian Hongxia has put forward besides another proposal. He says he is at work drawing over the cook who does the cooking, old Chu; and if once that be talked through, then, by putting a little something into the food, it would be not only easier to lay hand to and safer, but able all the same to gain the end—only this would need us to provide him an absolutely sure drug. Xian Hongxia's notion, though it differs from what we asked at first, is not without its worth as a way that might yet be weighed again."

At this point in Wang Wen's telling, he offered further views of his own besides, holding that, whatever method were used, so long as the task were done—to poison him or to shoot him—there was no very great difference, and we need not insist it must be done just so and no other way.

Wang Wen said again: "There is one fact we cannot overlook, and that is the real difficulty of the man who carries it out." The drift of these words I understood very well; yet I could not argue with him to his face, for fear of stirring up needless misunderstanding. There was this further thing, too—I saw that he spoke wholly out of good will.

Still, here I would set out a little of my own mind: I hold always that to sanction a man is to carry out an execution on the nation's behalf; and if one strike the traitor down with a clean, ringing blow, it is far better than to do away with him by stealth and in the dark—for one thing the show of it is different, and its effect too is otherwise; which is what we often call the working of the deterrent effect of "killing one to warn a hundred." History is not wanting in earlier instances: cases of poisoning have mostly become riddle-like affairs, left hanging unsolved.

On this matter I did not hold stubbornly to my own view. In the main, our superiors had given us no instruction as to how it should be done; and since Wang Wen and his friend both had this notion, why should I for my part insist on being obstinate? And so I agreed to hand Wang Wen that bottle of drug which the late "Tianjin Station" chief, Dr. Wang Zixiang, had given me in his lifetime, to carry to Tianjin.

It came to my mind that this drug had already been kept four or five months, and would likely not yet have lost its power; but for prudence's sake I charged Wang Wen that he must first try it on a rabbit or a dog, and, if aught were amiss, think of some other way.

I asked Wang Wen how the matter stood of his sounding out Xian Hongxia, as I had bidden him last time, on being ready to meet me. My asking this called up the thing that Wang Wen had forgotten. Wang Wen said: "Xian Hongxia is very willing to meet you, and, so long as he can get out, at any time and place. But that Adjutant Shi has told Xian Hongxia that he too would like to meet you. For this reason I would ask you one thing: is it to be one you would see, or two? Let me know, so that I may arrange it when I go this time."

I said: "Better meet with Xian Hongxia first; for that Adjutant Shi, we shall decide after I have seen Xian Hongxia." Wang Wen told me: "Xian Hongxia's meaning is that, once I have met with Adjutant Shi, the two of them will take turns hereafter coming out to keep liaison with me, lest too many trips abroad breed suspicion." I thought their forethought truly quite thorough.

Wang Wen did not mean to stay long; once the power of that bottle of drug had been tried, and if all were well, he would go straight on to Tianjin. As we parted at his setting out, Wang Wen brought up again how warmly Liu Zhaonan, the chief manager of the Sanyicheng in Tianjin, had lent his help; when this thing was brought off, we ought to make him some acknowledgment too. That, of course, went without saying; only, I was uneasy that this man knew too much of our affairs, which was never quite well. But because Wang Wen had full confidence in him, I said no more.

From Accountant Lin, who kept the money, I borrowed for the time two thousand dollars, and gave it to Wang Wen as a reserve fund over and above his traveling costs; I thought Xian Hongxia and the others might have need of money somewhere. Should that cook be willing, there would of course be all the more such need.

What I could not set my mind at ease about was still this business of using the drug or not; and so I made yet a special explanation to Wang Wen. I said: "As to using the drug, I have not the least assurance in it. For one thing I know far too little of drugs; and next, I have no experience at all. Though it be tried on a small animal, that after all is no man; and what its true effect may be—it is only taken for granted, and not necessarily reliable to the last degree. Think—how then can we say this meaning plainly out to them? Were we to say it, we should be bound at once to shake their confidence, and that would be worse still." The reason I spoke so much to Wang Wen, sparing no pains, was in the hope that Wang Wen might have some mental preparation, and, being able to do without the drug, might do best to leave it unused. To speak the truth, I still leaned toward the one ringing report, and a swift, clean end of it!

Wang Wen promised to decide as the circumstances should require. By his reckoning, on this trip a great many questions would all be settled. In three or four days at most he could be back to report.

After Wang Wen had gone, I wavered a good while: was I to report the matter as it stood to our superiors now, or wait until Wang Wen came back this time with a decision on principle? Of a sudden I recalled myself—better wait until after I had met with Xian Hongxia.

### 3. The Grievous Consequences of Excessive Haste

Nothing was ever more beyond all expectation than this.

Wang Wen was to be back in three or four days; from the evening of the third day I began to look for him, and looked on until the morning of the fifth, and still there was no word of any kind. Though no set day for his sure return had been agreed, by the common way of it, when he said three or four days, then three or four days it was, and he would never let it drag to a fifth. This meant a hitch might well have arisen.

What made one restless was that, short of waiting for him to come back himself, there was no other way to get in touch with Tianjin. In truth the "Beiping Station" had men enough to send; to have dispatched a comrade to go along with him at the time would have been easy and only right, and why it was not done must be laid to my own carelessness. Then again, Wang Wen had no lateral tie with the working unit at Tianjin; even could he have found the men of the "Tianjin Station," there was no means of liaison for direct word with the "Beiping Station." Besides, though a long-distance telephone call was no fit thing for saying much over, still a word that all was well would have been good—but perhaps that had not come into his head.

By the sixth day, a little past noon, Wang Wen came back at last. His look was crestfallen, and for a good while he could get out no word; and by his face and bearing one felt already that things were out of the common—as though he had gone down in defeat before ever the true trial was joined. We sat in silence facing each other a spell, and it was I, quick of temper, who spoke first to ask: "How did the thing go?" Wang Wen said, stammering: "Xian Hongxia and that head cook old Chu are both come to grief; they have been delivered to the Japanese gendarmerie, and the proofs are all there. I fear these two lives are surely finished! To superiors, to friends—how am I to answer! And how shall the aftermath be seen to?" Saying this, the rims of his eyes went red.

I soothed Wang Wen, bidding him first grow calm and not give way, and best first tell me how it had gone, that, once I understood, we might do our duty by it. For the present, if by any means it could be done, the most pressing thing was how to go about rescuing the captured; and at the same time we must lay before our superiors a report of the whole case. And if it were the sorrow that the captured had already perished for our work, then what we ought to do was more still.

I watched Wang Wen, and saw his two eyes sunk deep, his weariness past bearing, so that even his speech seemed to come without strength. This was plainly from want of sleep, and from a spirit racked with torment. Set such a thing on any man, and few, I think, could bear up under it.

I hold that with all men it is the same: a matter of triumph one may tell over a few times and no harm; a matter of defeat one had best not tell at all. But this was no time to speak of moods and feelings—one must first get to the bottom of it! I had thought first to go out with Wang Wen for a bath, to ease his weariness and settle his spirits, and then talk it over as the better way; but Wang Wen would not have it. His meaning was that only when he had told the whole thing out plainly would his heart be at ease; and besides there were many things still not understood, needing urgent counsel, to decide what the next step should be.

I knew Wang Wen touched neither tobacco nor drink; I poured him a cup of strong tea and passed it to him, but he thought the tea too bitter, and of his own hand poured himself a cup of plain boiled water, and, wetting his lips only, drank no more. Truly he was come to the pitch of having no heart for tea or food.

At this Wang Wen began at last to set out the course of the thing and the whole story of the work's failure. He began very slowly, his voice very low, his tones filled with a boundless remorse; and his very first words were:

"It is because I was greedy for merit, and rushed the thing besides, that I botched it; and Xian Hongxia's warm heart's blood, I fear, has been spilled for nothing!

"On the second morning after I reached Tianjin, I got in touch with Xian Hongxia; he had come out on an hour's leave begged of Staff Officer He, in the gap while Shi Yousan lay abed late and not yet risen. We met, as before, at the old place, the Sanyicheng; and because Xian Hongxia's time was short, we talked at most ten-odd minutes, not a full twenty, and he was gone. Little did I think this might be our last meeting.

"The matter of Xian Hongxia's working upon the cook old Chu was already done. Old Chu had agreed to lay hand to the food, and Xian Hongxia had promised that, the thing accomplished, he would scrape together a little capital for him to open a small eating-house. I asked Xian Hongxia there and then about how much capital would be needed; Xian Hongxia could put forward no definite figure, and it was I in the end who asked, would four or five thousand dollars be enough? Xian Hongxia thought this point of no great import: to satisfy old Chu's wish of many years, it was enough only that we should not do him wrong.

"I handed that bottle of drug to Xian Hongxia, and charged him carefully: this drug is a deadly poison; injected into a cat or dog, it kills in about thirty seconds; taken by a man, two or three minutes can end his life. The one we would wipe out is Shi alone; on no account must the innocent be involved, and above all it must not be cooked in the pot to poison a whole company to death.

"Xian Hongxia said several times over that he knew, that he had it by heart. He said he would surely talk it over with old Chu, and find a way to mix it into what Shi most often ate, what Shi most loved to eat. I gave him a further hint—it were best put into the almond tea, so it would be the more without a seam, for this drug carries a slight almond savor.

"I charged Xian Hongxia again and again to make it clear to old Chu: the drug in the bottle can be used but once; used, the empty bottle must on no account be flung down at hand, but were best smashed and buried in the earth, or poured into a drain, that no trace be left.

"Xian Hongxia was in haste to go; he promised to come with Adjutant Shi at this same hour the next day to meet me once. Should the circumstances not allow it, they would change it to the morning after; and were that too not to be done, there would be a telephone call.

"As Xian Hongxia was leaving, I bethought me of that sum of money I had brought; I asked Hongxia, this time I have brought a little money—if you have need of it, you can take it back now. Hongxia shook his head, and said that even if it were needed he could not carry money back. When he said this, I understood too.

"We ended this talk, and he went off in haste; I did not even see him out. Little did I think that in that one night after he had gone, so great a change would come to pass."

Wang Wen's tones grew heavier, his pace quicker too, his feeling the more plainly stirred:

"That same night—that is, the second night after reaching Tianjin—I lodged as before in the Sanyicheng grain store; and because I carried two thousand dollars on me, I was a little uneasy, and thought it best to hand it into the counting-room for keeping, and take it when it was wanted. So I stepped over to the counting-room and gave it to the chief manager Liu Zhaonan; and because we were all old acquaintance, I asked for no receipt or note of any kind.

"That night I tossed and could not sleep, my whole mind still hanging upon Xian Hongxia and the rest, and thinking without cease of the morrow's meeting with the two men, Xian and Shi. If all went smoothly, and the dispositions were in the main laid, I would first go back to Beiping to report, and then return to wait for word. In the dead of night I still heard folk going out and coming in, and marveled much at heart: this was no inn, I thought—how at such an hour should there still be so many coming and going? Rising to make water, I stepped outside and looked, and in the western wing-room the lamplight flickered and shadows of men wavered, a crowd of them clustered there at gambling. Having made the thing out—that this was all it was—since it was no concern of mine, I went back to my room and slept.

"A whole night ill-slept; past eight in the morning, reckoning the time, if they could come they ought by now to come. With all my mind bent on it I awaited their coming. I watched with straining eyes clean to noon, and not so much as a shadow. Xian Hongxia had said that, should the circumstances not allow, it might be put off to the next day. So it was: for the two to come out together was, I feared, not so easy—better wait with patience. But when supper-time came, how was it there was not even a telephone call? This was strange indeed.

"A whole day waited in vain, and no word at all. Xian Hongxia was a man very true to his word; if he still did not come, he would surely find a way to send word—and thinking so, I grew somewhat quieter and less on edge.

"The next dawn was the fourth day of this coming to Tianjin. The moment I opened my eyes my mood was strained; yet, beyond hoping, there was no good thing to be done—strength I had, but no use to put it to. Again from morning I waited to noon, thinking they might come at any moment, and dared not even go out to eat a bite. Noon past, my heart's flags wavered, patience wholly lost—I truly could hold in no longer.

"In the past, Xian Hongxia had met me by appointment five or six times, and every time it was in the forenoon, and never off by more than fifteen minutes. That he had not come yesterday forenoon was still within reason; that he did not come this forenoon either, and not even a telephone call, that was not right. To think of it well, he had been held up by something, or perhaps had gone out attending Shi Yousan and could not get free. But this was not very likely, for it could not be that in some scores of hours he had not even the chance of a telephone call. To think of it ill, eight parts in ten there was some fault: Hongxia had always been exact and orderly, and, unless some special mishap had befallen, he would never leave me hanging by a thread, to wait on in vain.

"The more I thought, the more it was wrong. I guessed it very likely some mischance had already fallen out—and what was to be done? I had meant at the latest to go back to Beiping today; if I did not go back today, the Beiping side must certainly be anxious at my delay. And so Beiping waited on me, and I waited on them—it was as if a thing were jammed, so that, one joint stopped, joint upon joint was stopped. Yet, short of waiting until there was some outcome, I could think of no better way. At this point I decided first to write an express letter back to Beiping to report that all was well. Only, express letter though it be, sent today, it would at the soonest not be had until tomorrow.

"That night, shut alone in the room, I could neither sit nor stand, and strolled out into the courtyard to walk. Of a sudden it came to me it were as well to go to the counting-room and chat with the chief manager Liu Zhaonan, and by the way to ask him again whether he had had any telephone call from Hongxia. I knew well this was a needless asking, but there was no harm in trying my luck—I might chance to catch some hint of a thread; and, in such a smother of dejection, to find someone to say a word or two to might break the loneliness. Stepping into the counting-room, I saw Liu Zhaonan leaning against the back of his chair, dozing; hearing someone come in, he rose listlessly at last to give me a seat. He was not, as he was wont, all talk and laughter, but seemed as if some great care were pressing on him. I sought to draw him into talk, and he showed himself sorely out of patience; I asked him whether Xian Hongxia had telephoned, and he only shook his head, and then added a word: 'If there had been a call, would you not have been fetched to take it long ago!' I felt it was all very cheerless. Well, then—better wait on and see.

"My heart grew the more stifled, and I thought it were as well to go out and walk. So I charged Liu Zhaonan in all earnest that he must, come what might, keep watch for me; that while I was away, should any come to seek me, whoever it were, he was to say I would be back at once, and beg them by all means to wait a little; and if there were a telephone call, to ask them to call again in an hour's time. I took that express letter to the post office and sent it, drawing the receipt (in 1934, to post an express letter, one had to draw a receipt at the post office, unlike the registered-express of the present day). Then I hired a rickshaw bearing a Japanese-concession permit (a vehicle license) to Asahi Street in the Japanese concession. Getting down, I walked a stretch, then changed to another rickshaw to Akita Street. In truth I had another notion—only to pass by the door of the Shi house, to see whether there was any stir there. Sitting in the rickshaw I was pulled from this end to that; the puller asked me what number I sought, and I bade him go back the way we came, and, in the passing to and fro, I saw only that the black-lacquered great gate of the Shi house was shut fast and still without a sound, and nothing out of the way.

"A journey run for nothing, with nothing gained. On the way back, my whole heart hoped only that there was by now someone sitting to wait for me at the store."

Several times the writer thought to break in on Wang Wen's telling, meaning to put in a word begging him to make plain first the course of the failure, and where the three men—Xian Hongxia, Adjutant Shi, and old Chu—now stood, dead or alive. But I would speak and then held back, feeling always that this would not do too well, and that I ought rather to hear him tell it slowly on. When Wang Wen came to the sending of an express letter, as if in spite of myself there slipped out the words: "Your letter has not come." By this it may be seen that I had no self-command at all: at times, once stirred, I could not hold myself in. Not waiting for Wang Wen to answer, I added: "An express letter from Tianjin takes a day and more too; it will likely come today. Let us go on with what we were at—pray go on."

Wang Wen's spirits had by now come back near to their wonted state, but the sound of his speech still trembled. His eyes held the glisten of tears, yet he would never let them fall; and so, quickening his pace, he told out this passage of a martyr's grievous and valiant deeds.

He said: "I hurried back from Akita Street to the Sanyicheng—as if any were there to wait for me; it was but a fond hope playing me false. Still, having gone out and stirred about a little, I was no longer stifled as before to the point of drawing no breath.

"The sixth day—the sixth day after coming to Tianjin—by the calendar it was the twentieth of December, 1934. Rising in the morning, my face washed, just as I was eating breakfast, a hand of the store brought a stranger to seek me. I looked him over: this man was near my own age, tall of stature, big-eyed, very stoutly built, only that his dress did not well match him. By his look, his whole face was clouded with care, a picture of bewilderment; and I guessed he might be Xian Hongxia's fellow, that Adjutant Shi who wished with all his heart to give us his strength. In my haste, quick-tongued, I blurted out: 'Are you young brother Shi?' Sure enough, he answered at once, and opening his mouth cried out, 'Brother Wenhan!' Then he named himself, surname Shi and given name Dachuan—Shi Dachuan; and he said in earnest: 'I have come on purpose to bring word to Brother Wang.'

"I drew his hand and clasped it fast, and bade him sit and first drink a mouthful of hot tea to warm himself. And I owned to him in all sincerity: I was Wang Wen, Wang Wenhan, none other, and indeed Xian Hongxia's good friend, worthy too to be called his sworn brother in life and death. Whatever there was to say, let him say it freely, needing to shrink from nothing, nor to hide aught—let his mind be wholly at ease. I reminded him: this place where we spoke was territory under our Chinese government, whither the power of the Japanese did not reach; say what he would, there was nothing to fear.

"Shi Dachuan nodded; he seemed indeed to care nothing for such things. And so he told me, from first to last, a scene of tragic misery he had seen with his own eyes."

The great passage that follows is what Shi Dachuan said: "That day, about ten o'clock or so, after Hongxia had come back from your place, he even drew that bottle of white powder from his pocket and showed it to me; he was all smiles then and in the highest spirits, and said that with this we should be spared much trouble.

"Hongxia and I had said but this one sentence, when he slipped off alone to the back. I understood in my heart: the head cook old Chu always came back from buying the vegetables about this time, and Hongxia must surely have gone to find old Chu. In a little while Hongxia came back from the rear, just as there were folk in the room, and we two could not well talk. Only after the noon meal did he tell me: he had carefully handed it over to old Chu, and old Chu had agreed with a full heart, saying: 'Only let him' (meaning Shi Yousan) 'call for it' (meaning the food), 'and I'll put it in for him' (meaning that bottle of drug); 'if it comes handy today, I'll do it today.' Hongxia said too: 'One would never have told old Chu had such mettle; hearing those two words of his, how staunch he is—I see him full of confidence and firm of resolve, and I think this thing is now full of hope.'

"After that, Hongxia and I discussed how, having appointed to meet Brother Wenhan the next forenoon, we must find some pretext to come here together; but rack our brains as we might, we could think of no fit reason. And that Staff Officer He being a crafty, sharp-eyed fellow, we feared to bungle the pretense and fail to hide it from him, and so spoil the thing instead; and we decided at last to wait until evening to weigh it closely. That I came here to seek out Brother Wenhan is thus how it came about.

"Who could have foreseen that, ere evening came, the thing broke out; and that from then on Hongxia and I should never again have the chance to take counsel together."

Hearing this word, I gave a start. What? Hongxia come to grief! Shi Dachuan stared at me without a blink, no look at all on his face. I pressed him: "What in the end has become of Hongxia? Where is the man?" Shi then blinked his eyes and answered: "I do not yet know—truly do not know—but I fear it bodes more ill than good."

Shi Dachuan went on: "Whenever the Shi house took its supper, because the folk were many and did not sit at one table, it was like a running feast, one set finishing and the next set eating. Only when all, first and last, had eaten did old Chu wait on Shi Yousan and the concubine at their supper, and this was commonly about nine o'clock.

"In the ordinary way, once Shi had finished supper, the day's work of us few was at an end, and mostly he would not call us again. But by rule none might go out, still less pass a night abroad. Having nothing to do, we would mostly set up a table and play a few rounds of mahjong, and then go to sleep. This day, before ever the game was set—just at the point of 'you draw mine and I draw his,' of who was to come and who was not—of a sudden we heard Shi Yousan cry aloud: 'Staff Officer He, are they all here?' Staff Officer He answered in haste: 'All here.' Shi Yousan roared: 'Bring them all to me!'

"None knew what had happened. Hongxia and I exchanged a glance; at the same moment I clenched my fist and crooked my forefinger, meaning I would draw my gun, and Hongxia took my meaning. But time allowed us no further counsel, and besides it might not be for that matter at all. At this Staff Officer He urged us all to hurry out.

"The western wing-room we lodged in was but a dozen-odd paces from the great hall of the principal building; one had but to step out the door and turn left, and there it was. Staff Officer He led, and we four came behind; Staff Officer He went into the great hall, and we four stood in a row outside it. A glance within—it was dreadful! There was Shi Yousan, fierce of aspect, a gun in his hand, his finger on the trigger, as though to fire at any moment; and the head cook old Chu knelt on the ground shaking. To say he knelt—in truth he had already collapsed to the floor, frightened into a heap of soft mud.

"Shi Yousan cursed on without stop, and with the gun in his right hand beat old Chu's head again and again, kicking the back of the chair with his foot the while, and again raising the gun-barrel to point at us as he pressed old Chu hard, saying: 'Tell me the truth—they are all here; who in the end is it? Point him out to me at once, and if there be one lying word, I shoot you dead.'

"But old Chu lifted his head, and cast a glance at those standing outside the great hall, as though about to point one out; and in the instant before he had yet raised his hand, I saw Hongxia reaching his hand back, making ready to feel for the gun stuck at his waist. Only, he hesitated a moment, and his movement was not quick enough, and it was marked by Staff Officer He, who had his eye fixed on us; He flew up his leg and gave one kick, aimed at Hongxia's lower parts, crying aloud: 'What would you do?' Then, thrusting his chin at me: 'Why do you not hurry and take his gun from him.'

"Shi Yousan, seeing it, his veins standing out, was mad with rage. First he cast old Chu aside unheeded, sprang two steps forward, and, pointing the gun at Hongxia, cried straight out: 'You little bastard, you'd have my life—good! You watch how I deal with you.' Shouting the while, he turned toward Staff Officer He: 'Why do you not tie him up!'

"My face just then must surely have been amiss; luckily the light beneath the eaves was dim, and so it was covered over. The scene of a moment past was truly grievous to see, and it crossed my mind too: since I had a gun on me, I ought to draw it and finish this dog of a Shi. But then again I thought: at this pinch, save for taking him off his guard by a sudden stroke, if I meant to draw the gun, then, before all those watching eyes and their wariness, I might not necessarily bring off a clean hit in one. And so I should not only fail to kill Shi and fail to save Xian Hongxia, but throw away a life for nothing besides. I trusted Hongxia would never give me up, and so I thought it better to swallow the pain for the moment—there might yet be a chance."

"As I stood dazed, Shi Yousan kept crying to tie Hongxia up, and Staff Officer He had already torn a long strip of curtain-cloth from the window and taken it in hand; and mumbling to me he said: 'A thing like this is all of one's own seeking; none can look to another. Why do you not hurry and lay hand to it—what do you stand there stunned for?' My heart was struck with dread; pressed by the circumstances, I could not stand aside unmoving, yet in conscience how could I bring myself to it? At this the two adjutants Zhao and Cui, one to a side, wrenched Hongxia's two arms back, and Staff Officer He bade me lay hand to it together with him, and we bound Hongxia fast."

"Hongxia was worthy of the name of a true man: he cursed roundly, kicking out with both feet, and my own thigh took several kicks in a row."

"Shi Yousan turned about, kicked old Chu with his toe, and again, pointing at Hongxia with the pistol, said to old Chu: 'Lift your head and look—was it he who gave you the poison? Beside this fellow, speak out, who else is there?'

"Old Chu lifted his head with an effort, but dared not look Hongxia in the eye; and this made it worse still. The more he dared not look, the more it showed the thing was so, and was as good as pointing him out. Shi Yousan gave a cruel, cold laugh: 'Ah, I see now—you dare not look at him because you feel you have wronged him, is it not so? I ask you once more: beside him, who else?' Old Chu, half sprawled and half kneeling, kept shaking his head."

"I understood in my heart: though old Chu were beaten to death, he could point out no second man. For only Xian Hongxia alone had dealt with him, and he never knew at all that there was a second man."

"This while seemed to pass very long, very long; in truth it was but a few minutes. The thing was already very plain, and what remained were but side-matters. So Shi charged Staff Officer He to send Hongxia and old Chu at once to the western wing-room and hand them to the Japanese gendarmes, saying these two were of the Blue Shirt Society, anti-Japanese men sent from Nanjing, and asking the gendarmes to take them back to the Japanese gendarmerie and, dead or alive, to get the whole truth out of them."

"At this Staff Officer He whispered a few words in Shi Yousan's ear, and Shi shook his head and then nodded; what devilry they were at I could neither hear nor guess. At the same time Xian Hongxia too was speaking low to Adjutant Cui; I thought it very strange, but could not hear clearly what he said, nor dared I strain to listen close. Yet, for all that Hongxia spoke alone, Adjutant Cui's face was wooden, and he never once opened his mouth. I thought Hongxia might be making his last effort, in hope of the one chance in ten thousand."

"Staff Officer He directed the three of us to hold Hongxia up, while he himself dragged old Chu toward the eastern room; I pushed hard from behind. Going a few steps, I purposely thumped Hongxia several times on the back, and Hongxia too raised his leg and kicked back at me a few times. This was our 'leave-taking.'"

Wang Wen, relaying Shi Dachuan's words, coming to this point could not help choking. He recalled again: "Hearing Shi Dachuan tell it so, my heart was grieved past bearing; but Shi Dachuan was more grieved than I—he could hold in no longer, and wept aloud. I thought there was no more piteous thing under heaven than this one scene."

Wang Wen paused a little, and the writer poured him another cup of water, and then he went on relaying Shi Dachuan's words:

"Behind us, Staff Officer He dragged old Chu like hauling a dead dog, and hauled him by force to outside the door of the eastern wing-room, and there dumped him on the ground. This old Chu was truly something of a poor spineless thing: his whole body had gone limp, and it seemed one never saw him get to his feet again.

"The two Japanese gendarmes who kept their post ever in the eastern room, hearing the shouting in the principal building, were already standing at the door and peering out. There was no making out the few words Staff Officer He grunted to them, and they took both Hongxia and old Chu into the room. We stood outside the door and could not see clearly; we heard only two clicks, which must have been the clapping-on of handcuffs. From that time on—when the Japanese gendarmes took Hongxia and old Chu away, and how much torture they suffered, how much shame they underwent, or how they died in misery—thus, living, none saw the men, and dead, none saw the bodies; never again were they seen.

"We few had just made to go back to our own room in the western wing, when that dog again bade Staff Officer He bring us to him for questioning. Just then it crossed my mind that perhaps some flaw had shown somewhere and roused his suspicion; but thinking again, had he truly grown suspicious of me, how should he have waited till now? Staff Officer He led us few a second time to the great hall—the first time it had been four men, this time three were left; the first time we had stood outside the hall under the eaves, this time we stood within the great hall. Shi Yousan, his rage still unspent, cursed Xian Hongxia and old Chu for their utter want of conscience, and then questioned us one by one on what we had commonly heard Xian Hongxia say, and on our dealings with the outside, and the like. Once I heard it was after all without proof or ground—that he was but asking at random to rid himself of suspicion—I was set at ease.

"Just at the point of Shi's questioning, my eye fell on the chafing-pot on the dining table, still giving off steam. There was a set of bowl and chopsticks that seemed not to have been used, and another pair of chopsticks still stuck in the pot, not taken out; and over the table, a heap here and a lump there, lay the dishes that had been picked out. Against the table-leg leaned aslant a wooden tray, and on the ground was a stain of soup that had run from the tray, winding and snaking a foot or two long—one could see it had splashed from the great pot into the tray, and from the tray run out onto the ground. In my heart I understood then: it was just here that this disaster had failed!"

"After Shi Yousan had done his questioning, he spoke a few words more, half coaxing and half cozening, to win men's hearts and soothe their feelings. At the very end he charged Staff Officer He besides to find some vessel and gather up the soup and dishes left in the pot, to be handed to the Japanese gendarmes to take away for analysis—this being the 'proof' that Xian Hongxia had conspired with the cook old Chu to put poison in the pot."

By Wang Wen's relaying of Shi Dachuan's words, the crux of this case's failure was more than half understood. Reviewing the whole, it must be laid to a heart greedy for merit, and to over-haste. Had it not been so—an inside connection so rare and so deep as this—had one but a little patience, and set the dispositions at leisure, sooner or later there would have come a better chance, whereby not only might the task be fully accomplished, but all the connected persons too withdraw in safety; and in the working of "intelligence work," it might perhaps have reaped even more than "action work" could look for.

As to the course by which the case was seen through at the time, because none—not even those who told its inner story—had seen it with their own eyes, and those who took part in the actual work on the spot were seized then and there, there is no way to trace it out exactly. Reasoning it out: it must be that, after old Chu had put the poison into the chafing-pot, when he carried the pot and the other vessels up, his flurried heart threw his movements out of true and he betrayed himself—very likely his two hands shook so that he could not hold the tray steady, and even the soup in the chafing-pot was jolted and spilled. And Shi being a man so keen, at one sight of things amiss, he had but to give a shout, and old Chu would be frightened out of his wits; and were Shi then to take up a morsel with his chopsticks and order old Chu to eat it that instant, then the rest may be known without asking.

By the time Shi Dachuan saw old Chu kneeling on the ground and shaking, that was already the sequel of this same state of things.

### 5. Not Daring to Face Reality: A Flight into Frontier Exile

It is the clever man alone who does the muddle-headed thing; and I, who fancied myself clever, was in the end a muddle-headed man after all. That a senior cadre such as I, charged with the responsibility of a whole region in the ranks of the revolution, should nonetheless heedlessly do a thing against law and against discipline and not know it—this is truly a joke vast as heaven; and to write it out, how shame-faced a thing it is.

To speak the truth, at the time I gave my tacit leave to Wang Wen's doing this thing, I felt myself in the right and full of confidence, with something of the air of carrying out Heaven's justice; yet I never once weighed with care whether the thing squared with legal procedure, and what its aftermath would be. Had I thought a little more, I would not have stirred up so great a trouble.

This was a fall no light one: it not only knocked away five hundred years of hard-won cultivation, but came near to cutting off the whole prospect of my career. Reckoned up, this too was calamity of my own drawing, with none to blame but myself. What I regret most of all is that I dragged in many an innocent subordinate, and even my wife—who knew nothing whatever of it and had no part in it—suffered an unjust prosecution.

I have mentioned before that the office of the "Beiping Station" was my own home—or one may say, my home was used as the cover of the "Beiping Station." It was a very large mansion, four courtyards deep from the main gate to the back court. After Wang Wen had, easily enough, fetched that Liu Zhaonan from Tianjin up to Beiping, he lodged the man in an empty room in the back court, neither bound nor tied, no lock put on the door, still less any man set specially to keep watch on him. That is to say, the man was fetched, but the thing was never laid to heart at all; we thought only to have him frighten Shi Dachuan off, and to make him honestly speak out and render some account of the money he had swallowed—at most give him a lesson, urge him to be an honest man thereafter and never again take advantage of another's peril, and let it go at that. As for whether so doing was, in form and in manner, lawful, and how in the future he was to be "let" go back, none of this was thought of at all. I supposed Wang Wen must surely have made ready beforehand; and Wang Wen supposed I must surely have arranged it; and in the end the two ran clean apart, and the others knew nothing of the inner truth, and so a great blunder came of it.

For all that it was so large a house, being set in a remote spot it had no running water. What was drunk and what was used came wholly from the water-carriers, supplied at set hours each day. To speak of pushing a barrow to bring water—this too was a sight of the old capital Beiping: the water was well-water, borne by a man-pushed single-wheeled barrow, the barrow made of wood with a great wooden bucket to left and right. The well-water was drawn from the well by a windlass, poured first into a great wooden water-trough for storing, and then, with a long-handled wooden dipper, ladled dipper by dipper into the wooden buckets of the barrow. It cost strength and cost pains, but this clumsy method had held so these several decades, without the least change. The lads who pushed the water-barrow needed a good strong arm, and so were all young and stout of body.

The moment the water-barrow entered the lane, one could hear far off its "creak, creak…" This sound was all its own, and might be figured too as a strange sort of instrument; only the water-pushing single-wheeled barrow, going over the not-too-even earth-and-stone road, could strike up such a strain, both pleasing to the ear and plucking at the heart. When the water-carrier came, few needed to wait at the door; for the very moment that "creak, creak" was heard, every house had long since flung its door open.

As for the water-fee, by custom "water-tallies" were used. These "water-tallies" were made of little bamboo slips, some large and some small, branded with a mark on them; we could not tell what number it stood for, but the carrier of course kept his count. By their old rule, water was reckoned by the "load," and one load was two wooden pails, that is, two buckets. What was most not to be forgotten was that they had nailed a good many wooden strips inside the buckets, so that, the buckets looking very large without, the measure of water within was by so much the less. Our household had many mouths and its courtyards were deep, and so in every court a great jar was set for storing water, and each time the carrier came it was filled to the brim. For this reason he would go in and out load upon load a great many times, so wonted a thing that none took heed of it; but that Liu, shut up in the empty room, laid his schemes with all his heart upon the "water-carrier." In the end, taking advantage of the carrier's going in and out, he secretly passed out a letter saying he had been "kidnapped." To me this was a thing to make one a laughing-stock among the learned; and writing to this point I cannot help laughing too—it has the same fine turn to it as a police station getting robbed by a thief. I never did learn to whom this letter of his was sent, but it took effect.

It was the dead of winter, the twelfth moon; that day, a little past seven in the morning, I was already awake, but for the great cold still lay abed loath to rise. Just at this moment I heard of a sudden a clamor of voices in the courtyard, a thing that had never once happened. I rose in haste, and, not staying even to dress, threw a fur gown over my nightclothes, and, bare of foot, dragging on a pair of cloth shoes, went leaping and bounding out toward the courtyard. No sooner had I come out the parlor door than I met, face to face, a man in plain clothes with a little iron rod in his hand, whom I did not know. This man asked me point-blank: "Is your surname Zhou?" In my mind it was as though a flash of light told me at once what mischance had befallen; hearing the drift of it was wrong, I shook my head and, pointing to the room over yonder where the serving-woman slept, said, "The one surnamed Zhou lives in that room"—and, speaking and gesturing the while, I moved my feet slowly, going toward the back court. Taking the instant while this man went into the room to seek the person, I had already come to between the second and third courtyards; a turn to the right, and there was a small side-court, and in it a privy, the privy abutting on a low wall. Some while back I had asked the landlord to add a run of barbed wire atop that low wall—had he done so, I could not have got over.

This wall, to call it low, was not so very low; it stood a good half-head taller than I. Coming to this spot, careless of whether any saw me or no, I stepped back a pace or two, reached up and heaved with all my strength at the wall-top, and, by some strength come I know not whence, got up over it with surprising ease. Seeing it the same height within as without, I dropped lightly down, for all the world as though I had a whole body of martial skill—this, it may be, is what men call the hidden power of a man.

This was, it turned out, a narrow lane, through which in days past I had never once passed, nor did I know what this lane was called. A glance to left and right—no one there; and, quickening my step, I walked a stretch and came already to the mouth of the lane, where, just outside it, there happened to be a rickshaw. I got up on it and bade him pull to Anyuan Hutong. Looking back once more, there was not even a passer-by.

The clerk of the "Beiping Station," Wang Yunsun, lived in Anyuan Hutong, which from Wofosi Street where I lived was, by rickshaw, five minutes at the most. I rang and called at the gate, and the one who came to open it was Yunsun's wife, Yu Xuenong. We knew each other well; asking her on the one hand to pay my rickshaw fare, I walked straight in on the other. Wang Yunsun, dressed and trim, was just about to go to the "Station" to work, and at the sight of my sorry plight he was simply struck dumb. I could not spare the time to say much to him, but first snatched up the telephone and called back to the "Station" to ask how things stood. The one who took the call over there was the cook who did the cooking, Lin Huaizhang; the moment he heard my voice he said to me, all a-tremble: "Second Master, just now some dozens of men came, inside and out, and have already taken away Mr. Bai and that one surnamed something-or-other in the back court; and there are still not a few keeping watch here now, and will not let us go out or stir about—hurry and think of some way!" I asked him further: "And the mistress?" Huaizhang gave one "hello," and the telephone was cut off; calling again, there was only the buzzing sound. By this I had already guessed where the fault lay. Only now did I feel how grave things were—that I had brought on a great disaster.

It was awkward to speak plainly to Wang Yunsun; but sharp-witted as he was, from the one thing of my going without socks in the dead cold he could reckon eight or nine parts of it. I asked Wang Yunsun to go to a used-clothes shop and buy me a short cotton-padded jacket and thin padded trousers, and besides a pair of black foreign socks and black felt boots (winter shoes made of felt, very thick and heavy). Only with something to wear could I go out and see to the aftermath; but this was as good as telling Wang Yunsun that I could no longer go back to the "Beiping Station." In only a little while Wang Yunsun had it all bought and brought back. I asked him to wait at home for my word, and that he need not go to the "Station"; I reckoned that around noon, if I did not come back, I would surely telephone.

I left Anyuan Hutong in the western city, and went first to the Deyuancheng cotton store on the North Great Street of Dongsi Pailou to find Old Uncle Liu—a bosom friend of my late father's, and my elder brother's father-in-law besides. Before my aunt died, it was through my hands that a little of her savings, two thousand dollars, had been placed on deposit at his counter to draw interest; and now, in dire need of money, I meant to draw this sum out for the emergency. When I had made known my errand, Old Uncle Liu said little, but bade the manager give me the money; and he charged me to bring out the passbook when convenient, to reckon up the interest.

These two thousand dollars were all ten-dollar notes, and I divided them into several stacks and stowed them severally in a number of pockets. While at the Deyuancheng I telephoned Wang Yunsun; it rang a good while, and the one who took the call was his wife. I asked her: "Where is Yunsun?" She said: "Not five minutes after you left, Mr. Wang Pingyi came and fetched him away." Hearing this, plainly it had some bearing on what had befallen at my house that morning, and the thing had grown greater still. I asked Mrs. Wang further: "Did Mr. Wang Pingyi ask after me?"

She said: "He asked; we told him you had just left here, and would surely telephone again." I have said before that Wang Pingyi was the inspector for the Beiping region, charged on our superiors' behalf with the enforcement of the body's discipline. Between us there had always been little dealing, because I for my part did not approve of an inspectorate system for secret-service work, and so, whether in mind or in actual conduct, I little cooperated with him—which he too understood very well. Today's affair, since even he now knew of it, showed the thing had spread past all mending.

Since Wang Pingyi had asked after me, plainly the very object was myself, and they were of course bound to find me. Reckoning the time, Wang Pingyi and the rest ought by now to be back at his inspector's office. So I gave my number and put through a call to Wang Pingyi. The one who took it was not himself; the other asked a few questions, and, hearing it was I who called, Wang Pingyi took the receiver over to speak. He said, stammering: "It is you, Brother Gongshu—excellent, I have been looking for you. Someone has laid an information with the peace-keeping authorities that you have kidnapped a man and held him to ransom. Those who went just now to your Beiping Station were men of the Beiping Municipal Detective Squad and of the Third Regiment of the Gendarmerie, and they took the victim on the spot there in your place. The thing is a wretched mess; it is not convenient now to go to the 'Beiping Station,' so pray hurry over to me here that we may first talk it over—or else we can meet somewhere outside, that will do too."

I considered a little, and did not answer him straight, but asked: "Is Wang Yunsun with you?" He said: "It was I who fetched him here." I asked again: "And Bai Shiwei and Wang Wen?" He said: "Both at the Third Regiment of the Gendarmerie." I ventured a further question: "On what ground did they take my wife too?" Wang Pingyi said: "When we meet you will understand." Last I asked: "Have you reported to Mr. Dai?" He hemmed a good while and gave me no plain answer, so that he must already have sent word up. Over the telephone Wang Pingyi still waited for my reply; I resolved not to go for the time, needing to think it calmly and well over. So I told him: "That word you have heard, of kidnapping and ransom, I can tell you it is nothing of the kind; when I have made a little arrangement, I shall naturally clear it up." Because I felt very ill at ease at the tone of his speaking to me, at this point I hung up the telephone.

I bowed my thanks to Old Uncle Liu, took my leave, and, at a small eatery near Dongsi Pailou, ate a little something as it came, my mind too made up.

Against the wind and blown sand, I walked to the used-clothes shop on Longfu Street, and chose a fur greatcoat neither new nor old, and got besides some other things against the cold, and, most needful of all and not to be done without, that fur cap of the "three-tile" sort that could both keep off the wind and cover the face. I was resolved to flee! I must go, there was no not going!

On the face of it, thinking back now, this thing did indeed look like a "kidnapping"; but to say "held to ransom," that was to twist the fact and overstate it by far. Still, to go "give myself up" now, with circumstances against me, I might very likely be in the right and unable to argue it—leaping into the Yellow River, I could not wash myself clean. Besides, our Mr. Dai set great store by face, and a thing like this, once noised abroad, must in the end make a man unable to hold up his head. Now he must be at the very height of his wrath, and to run up against him would be but adding oil to the fire; there was nothing for it but to let it cool a while and shun the edge of his anger.

That night I went out alone by the Xizhimen gate, and at the station of the Ping-Sui Railway bought a first-class ticket with a berth, to go by night to Guisui and seek refuge with my elder brother. Before the train started, I still had some misgiving at heart; only when the train pulled out of the station—it was by then past midnight—did I suppose I was not to be "taken."

This day, whether it was in December 1934, or was already come to January 1935, I truly cannot call to mind; but I can be sure that the lunar New Year had not yet passed. Within the carriage and without, the temperature was far apart: the glass windows had frozen over with a thin skin of ice, and in the lamplight there was reflected in a sheet of white frost; within, there was steam-heat, and, having taken off greatcoat and fur gown, in cotton jacket and cotton trousers alone I still felt a little warm, but let me open the window a crack, and with a hiss a stream of cold air shot in like a flying arrow, and the steam-warmth of the carriage was of a sudden all gone, and it turned cold again at once, so that one could but shut it up again. A whole night got no sound sleep; before dawn we passed Datong, and not long after sunrise we had reached the city of Guisui, formerly called "Guihua City."

My elder brother served as a quartermaster in a cavalry unit; and because his unit was of a shifting kind, our correspondence was passed on by way of a horse-store. This time, coming to seek refuge with him, I had first to go to the Liyuanzeng horse-store to inquire out his lodging.

The Liyuanzeng horse-store was in the old town of Guisui, not too far from the busy commercial quarter; hiring a rickshaw, one could be pulled straight from the railway station to its door. A horse-store is a trade-house purveying to the merchants who come to buy horses—no inn, but it lodges too the merchants who come buying horses. That a horse-store should have dealings with a cavalry unit is, of course, a thing in the natural course of matters. The manager of the Liyuanzeng horse-store was surnamed Ai, a man of the new sort who had had a higher education, by no means of the huckster kind. At our first talk the impression on either side was good. He asked me to rest in the store hall, and forthwith sent a hand off to the headquarters of the First Cavalry Division to find my elder brother.

By about noon my elder brother came; he was greatly taken aback at my sudden coming from so far, yet could not well ask me anything before Manager Ai's face. He said: "Let us first go home and see your sister-in-law, and after we have eaten we can consider the question of lodging." So I left my baggage for the time at the horse-store, and, following my brother, walked five or six minutes on foot and came there. It was, it turned out, a great tenement court, of which my brother and his wife rented but two northern rooms and one western wing-room. They lived in the northern rooms; the other western room was for storing things.

I had not seen my sister-in-law a full ten years, since the winter of 1925 when, after a quarrel I had with her, I went off in a huff to Guangdong. Meeting now, it was again my sister-in-law who spoke first, calling me "second brother," and I too called out warmly "elder sister-in-law"; for the childish temper of those years I could but bear a contrite heart.

My elder brother being my senior, I could not keep it from him, but told him the whole course of the mishap. My brother thought the run of folk at the horse-store too mixed a lot: to lodge there a few days for the nonce would pass, but for the long run it would hardly do. If I moved back to his home, he would for the time first clear a clean space of the broken odds and ends on the k'ang of the western wing-room and spread out bedding to sleep. My brother proposed: "Should you not be leaving Suiyuan for a while, then in a day or two we can find a house and move."

I stayed one night at the Liyuanzeng horse-store, and the next day moved back to my elder brother's home. Though the k'ang was all a-litter with books, my heart was easy at last. One day I went out alone to the streets to bathe; and for fear of running into an acquaintance, I took care to bathe in a private tub-room. As I was taking a little nap there, I heard of a sudden two men talking in the next room; of these, the one I knew at a hearing for who he was; the other, his voice too low, I could not at first make out, but listening close I found it too was an old schoolmate and friend of mine. The first I made out was the "stammer" who spoke Shandong speech—that was the Beiping-region inspector Wang Pingyi; the other, in a Suiyuan accent, was Gao Rong, the chief of the "Suiyuan Station." Gao Rong and I had been schoolmates from our junior-middle-school days, he a class above me. In 1925 we went together to Guangdong to enter the Whampoa Military Academy, and enlisted in the same regiment besides; and now we were colleagues once more. When I had made certain it was these two, I could not but draw a cold breath—truly I had a great, great fright! Say what one would, they had surely come for my sake, past all doubt; and inwardly I cried: "How fast they have come!"

Now I must deal with this pass warily and with care, and on no account let a mishap arise. Grown a little steadier, what I first needed to make out was whether they had yet found me. Weighing it close, I judged this but a coincidence; for had they truly known I was in the very next room, then what were they waiting for—they would long since, without ceremony, have come in to greet me, or even bidden me to the "Suiyuan Station" for a "talk."

Reasoning on further: Wang Pingyi of course had come for my sake, and Gao Rong was doing the host's part. Now they had come to bathe, and would not stay too long; when they had bathed they must surely leave; and, to be wholly safe and not found by them, I must wait until they had gone before I went, and hold out a good half-hour and more after they had gone.

I made up my mind first to take a nap, and to shun as far as might be having the bath-man come in to talk with me. But sleep would not come; quietly I listened to what they said, and their voices being too low, I could not hear it clear. Thus I whiled away an hour and more, and they went at last. I bade the bath-man buy me something to eat, and, purposely tarrying another hour, left as the sky went dim.

That same night I told this thing to my elder brother, and he held that here it was not to be stayed in a single day, and firmly urged an instant leaving of Guisui. Otherwise, so long as there was a thread to follow, in not more than three to five days they might come knocking at the door; and by then it would be the more past mending. My brother was kind and generous, and no coward at all; that he was so came wholly of his love for me. But in such haste, whither was I to turn!

My brother pondered a moment, and counseled me to be a little patient and not fret, and let him go out and take counsel with a colleague, to see whether I might be conveyed under escort to some out-of-the-way place; and if there were not many difficulties, and it could be managed, then perhaps that very night I might set off.

My brother's colleague and good friend was the chief of staff of their divisional headquarters; only let his understanding and support be had, and a letter in his own hand be written to the garrison's Colonel Gan, and every difficulty could be solved. In the end it went very smoothly: Chief-of-Staff Han sent a major of staff, made two horses ready, and, bearing his letter written to Colonel Gan, in the faint gray before dawn, at whip and gallop, with one halt at noon, by the high sun of the after-part of the day we had reached Ulanhua, one hundred and fifty li on the road.

This was the very depth of the "nine-nines" cold, the twelfth moon, the north wind piercing to the bone, the temperature about twenty to thirty below; the warm breath one breathed out, settling on the collar of the fur coat, turned in a moment to a skin of white frost, and, frost upon frost with more warm breath, was soon strings of ice chopsticks. Three to five years grown used to city life, and short of exercise besides, this journey worked me sore. At the start, light and glad, I did not feel it much; after thirty li I began to feel the strain, but young as I was, it was not well to slow down, and I bore up as best I could till the halt—had I not got off the horse then, I should have been unable to grip it and have tumbled off. Resting a while, I mounted and rode on again, hardly to be borne. Perforce I begged the major of staff to make shift and rein the horses a little slower. And after a long, long stretch of road more I got there at last. It was well I had once a groundwork of enlistment in me, or else I might not have been able to crawl up again. Even so, my two arms could not be lifted for several days, and my two hip-bones would not pull apart either.

At my setting out, my brother had charged me that, once come to Ulanhua, Colonel Gan would treat me with the courtesy due a guest, and I might stay a month or two with no matter, for he and Chief-of-Staff Han were on the deepest of terms, and all might be set at rest. As to why I had come, for no reason, to so wild and remote a spot—that all understood without a word being said, and Chief-of-Staff Han's letter would surely make it clear. To pass away this stretch of dry, bitter days, I might well take a few more books to read; and once the New Year was over, at the Lantern Festival at the latest, as things looked I would send a man to fetch me back. The love of a brother welled over in his words, and moved one deeply.

Colonel Gan did indeed treat me very well, kind and true past hope, with no false show at all. Mindful that my life was too dull, and that in the freezing cold there was no good place to go, every other day or so he found a few men to keep me company at a few rounds of small mahjong. Beyond this—who could have guessed that even a place like this should have its "worn shoes" (native harlots), while to draw a couple of pipes (opium) was a thing more common still. One may say that eating, drinking, whoring, and gambling were all to be had complete.

I stayed a full month and more, and muddle-headedly passed a dizzy, addle-brained old New Year. Coming to my senses of a sudden, I bethought me of the money I carried, which, what with losing and what with spending, was near all gone. To go on so was no lasting plan either; so I wrote a letter, entrusting it to a man of their unit to carry to my elder brother at Guisui. In the letter I said he need not come to fetch me; let me but take counsel with Colonel Gan, and as soon as there were a man and horse going that way, I would return at once to Guisui.

By my reckoning I had been away from my post in flight more than seventy days, and the affair ought by now to have cooled. It was but right to seize the chance to seek a way to settle it; and so I meant, once back at Guisui, to slip secretly back to Beiping for a trip, see how the wind lay, and then fix my course thereafter.

For the days I lingered at Ulanhua I had much of Colonel Gan's care; and besides thanking him in all sincerity, I laid before him without the least reserve my identity, and how I had done a wrong thing and come hither to shun the edge of the storm, and told him all. Only so could my heart be at ease, and I not fail a friend's good will.

Back at Guisui, my elder brother, after going with me to thank Chief-of-Staff Han, kept for the time to our former lodging. My brother told me that since my going, no one had come from the Liyuanzeng horse-store's side to make inquiry, nor had any stranger ever come to the house. Hearing this I thought it strange indeed: for both in the "autobiography" of my personnel file, and in the "personnel investigation forms" I had filled out many a time, there was set down my brother "Chen Gongzhi" and the unit he served in—one inquiry, and it was known. Wang Pingyi had come to Guisui before this—why then was there no stir at all? If they meant to find me, was this not the surest thread? On this point no answer was to be had, do what I might. Yet it did prove that the thing was not so pressing and grave as had been imagined.

My brother urged me to stay some more days, and, when all sides had been fully weighed, to go to Beiping—it would not be too late. But my heart was pressing as a shooting star, and I longed to go that instant, the sooner to seek a day of coming out into the open.

My brother and his wife made ready for me some clothes against the change of season, and got together besides a hundred dollars, and amid a thousand chargings and myriad cautions of their care I set out straight for Beiping. Little did I think that on the train I should again, of all things, meet an acquaintance, one Mr. Lu, who did political-training work in the army and was a comrade of the "Renaissance Society" too. Whether he had already heard of my affair there was no guessing; watching his manner toward me, there seemed no look of surprise; but all the way I was greatly ill at ease.

Getting off the train, we greeted each other and each went his own way. I went first to lodge at the house of Mr. Wang Ruizheng in Gongxian Hutong of the eastern city—Mr. Wang was the brother-in-law of my schoolmate and old friend Zhang Zuoxing, a man who had watched us grow up, of a deep and warm friendship, with whom nothing could not be talked over. The first word he said at the sight of me was: "What great daring you have—we hunted everywhere for you and could not find you, and here you dare come back!" Mr. Wang Ruizheng told me all he knew: "It is said several of the men connected with you have been sent up to Nanjing. Of them I know Bai Shiwei, Wang Wen, and your wife; the ones I do not know I cannot name. For more of the particulars, we who stand outside can hardly understand it."

None of this took me by surprise; and what I wished to know, Mr. Wang of course could have no way of learning, so that I must contrive some other channel. I asked where Brother Zhang Zuoxing now was. Mr. Wang told me he was at present chief of the public security bureau of Jize county in Hebei, and would not be coming to Beiping in the near term. I meant to stay a few days at the Wang house for the time; but before I could open my mouth, Mr. Wang had already said: "Hereabouts there are many lodging-houses where students live, at no more than eight or nine dollars a month; you may first rent a room and settle in, and come here morning and evening for your meals, and give your washing to your elder sister to wash—so, I think, all will be convenient." I could not fail so kindly a heart, and did as he meant. In truth, that he was willing to look after me thus was friendship enough and to spare.

And so, near Gongxian Hutong, at Donghuangchenggen, I rented a room in a lodging-house, lamp and tea-water found, at nine dollars a month, paid half-monthly, and settled myself first before aught else.

From the Wang house I inquired out the lodging of my fellow-enlistee at the Military Academy, Brother Jiang Tian, and at Brother Jiang Tian's found his second sister Jiang Yiqing; and Jiang Yiqing told me further that her third sister Jiang Taiqing was at present convalescing of an illness at the Western Hills Sanatorium, and would for some time not be discharged. I made yet another trip to the Western Hills, and at Jiang Taiqing's at last got word of Fan Xing's lodging.

Fan Xing has been mentioned in both the two sections above. He was the "Beiping Station's" own directly attached communicator, who made a special business of gathering international intelligence—not only of vast resource, but leaving besides a great many riddles unsolved. Fan Xing had two women friends, one Peng Yaluo, one Jiang Taiqing, both of whom I knew; but I could never make out the subtle relation among them—perhaps there was some political factor mixed in it too. All this I had reported in person to Mr. Zheng Jiemin, and the gist of Mr. Zheng's instruction was: "Let it out long and long; draw it tight and tight; and watch what comes of it." At present, because of my flight, I did not know whether this line was broken or no.

Work is work; but Fan Xing and I were on very good private terms, and my daring to seek him out lay in my firm belief that he would not make things hard for me, and that what I wished to know, so long as he knew it, he would the less hide from me. Third sister Jiang Taiqing told me: Fan Xing no longer lived near the Art Institute, but had long since moved, a single man alone, to the YMCA on Mishi Street.

I chose to go find him at night. First, downstairs at the YMCA, in the Guanglu Cinema, I watched a picture, which broke up at about eight forty-five. I went up the stairs to the fourth floor, which was all the dormitory of the YMCA. In the dim lamplight I found the room he lodged in, and knocked softly; waiting a little, I knocked again, and no one answered. Without thinking I turned the door-knob, and, neither bolted nor locked, it opened at a push. By the lamplight shining in from the window, I could see there was no one in the room.

Aiya, surely a mistake! Already saddled with the charge of "kidnapping and ransom," were I now to add the suspicion of "thieving in the dark of night," that would be a finer "show" still. I hastily flung the door wide, and, turning the electric light on as I went, saw hung upon the wall a small oil painting, signed with the two words "Jiman." No mistake—this was Fan Xing's very sobriquet; but why go out and not lock the door? I might as well sit down and wait for him a while—perhaps he had stepped out for the moment and would soon be back.

Having waited near a quarter of an hour, I could hold out no longer, and wrote a note that ran: "Brother Jiman: I called and missed you, to my great chagrin. Tomorrow at eight in the morning I shall come again; pray by all means wait a little. If you have an engagement already, pray leave word appointing a time to meet." I did not write my name, for he knew my hand.

I pressed the note down on the table, turned off the light, drew the door to, and went down by the stairs; and just as I reached the turn at the foot of the stairs, I chanced to meet Fan Xing coming back from outside. Catching sight of me all of a sudden, he was much startled, and forthwith put out both hands and gripped mine fast, unwilling to let go. He asked: "You have come to find me?" I nodded, and he drew me upstairs and into his room again.

I could not spare the time for idle greetings, but with my very first word asked: "Do you still keep up the working connection?" He answered: "I am at present acting chief of the 'Beiping Station.' Our superiors have sent Mr. Zhang Yanyuan to be district head of the 'Beiping District,' and Mr. Mao Wanli district secretary. The 'Beiping Station' now is wholly under the command of the 'Beiping District,' and has no direct tie with the bureau headquarters. So far as I know, save me alone, there is only old Yin who keeps the gate on North Chang Street; the rest are all posted away."

I asked him further: "How do those who have left Beiping now fare?" Fan Xing said: "I do not wholly know. By what I hear, Bai Shiwei and Wang Yunsun are in training; Qi Nanpu has been posted to other work, not in North China; Yang Ying was moved to the Tianjin telegraph office; Wang Wen and that one surnamed Liu are still in confinement. And your good lady is being kept in a certain place under favorable treatment, only she may not move about at will."

Hearing this speech of Fan Xing's, I understood in the main their general state. Then, half in earnest and half in jest, I sounded him, asking: "My coming now to see you is wholly a private act; by your duty, what ought you to do?" Hearing this he was struck aghast, and said in great perplexity: "What ought I to do? Elder brother, do you still not trust me? Our tie is not as another's; come what may, I would never fail a friend—the more so as you have committed no monstrous, unpardonable crime."

Fan Xing was much concerned for my movements and purposes hereafter, and counseled me to clear the matter up as soon as might be. Of course, he too could put forward no good plan. I told him frankly: "My aim in coming here is to inquire out news and learn how things stand; and until I have got it thoroughly clear, even I myself do not know what were best to do."

Fan Xing put forward, in good will: "Elder brother, have you not thought of going straight to Nanjing to see Mr. Dai? I think that, after an explanation, he would understand." I said, hiding nothing: "This thing so damaging to face is indeed my own mishandling; and beyond being ashamed to face Mr. Dai, I am truly a little afraid of him—if you call it dreading punishment, I will not deny that either. So I would wait until things had cooled, and then decide. But sooner or later I must go back. And speaking of it here, I let fall to you one word: this thing came about because of a man sacrificing himself for the work, which stirred it up; there was at the outset no ill intent whatever."

Our talk went just so far, and no further. Fan Xing gave me one hundred and sixty dollars, and never so much as asked where I lodged, or what I meant to do hereafter. With this money I could hold out yet a while.

### 4. Mishandling: Missteps, and No Room to Advance or Retreat

At the time, when the writer had heard out Wang Wen's oral report, and knew that the sanctioning of Shi Yousan was already a failure, and past all mending too, this was the first taste of defeat the "Beiping Station" had had since it began its work. And what most weighed on me was still the questions of whether Xian Hongxia and old Chu were alive or dead, and how Shi Dachuan now stood. By Wang Wen's account: "Of Xian Hongxia's fate, alive or dead, there is not the least word yet; the hard of it is that we lack a line by which to verify."

As for Shi Dachuan himself, because after this affair had come to light he felt his footing in the Shi house grown very ill—Xian Hongxia and old Chu being now seized, what should come after none could foretell; and were Shi to press the inquiry further, or lay hold of some clue or trace, the peril ahead might be known without asking. At the same time Shi Dachuan had long since loathed this post in the Shi house, and had long wished to be quit of it. And so he firmly declared he would not go back to the Shi house again.

Therefore I bade Shi Dachuan lodge for the time at the Sanyicheng grain store, to await our disposing of him.

I asked Wang Wen first to rest, and to be a little patient and not fret, and let me weigh it calmly a few minutes to reach a decision. I thought the matters pressing for present handling ought to be these: to dispatch Wang Wen with all speed to Tianjin to fetch that man of valor and honor, Shi Dachuan, up to Beiping and lodge him well; and, by every possible means, to spy out how Xian Hongxia and the rest stood at the Japanese gendarmerie in Tianjin, that our understanding might grow, and fitting steps of rescue be the readier taken.

On the other hand, to report the particulars of this case at once to our superiors for the record, and at the same time to put forward three requests: first, as to the two men Xian Hongxia and old Chu, if living, to press on with their rescue, and if dead, to grant a generous solatium; second, to send Shi Dachuan up to the capital for training; third, that the various costs already paid out in carrying out this case be permitted to be reckoned and reimbursed as actually spent. When it was drafted, I weighed it over once more, and, holding it reasonable and just, resolved at last to do it so.

So I made known to Wang Wen all that I had decided. This time, for the sake of close liaison, and to avoid another parting of the ways, I sent the driver Chen Guozhi to go with Wang Wen to Tianjin.

As to the matter of costs: besides the two thousand dollars borrowed last time from the accountant, which had been given to Wang Wen to carry to Tianjin for use, and was still for the present kept at the Sanyicheng—I charged Wang Wen first to give this two thousand dollars to Shi Dachuan, as a token of comfort. And besides, I made up at the Station a further five hundred dollars, and gave it to Wang Wen for general working expenses.

Who could know that the way the thing went would be greatly beyond all expectation? Wang Wen and Chen Guozhi came back from Tianjin to Beiping the next day about noon, but Shi Dachuan had not come back.

Toward the evening of the next day, Nanjing's reply telegram also arrived, the gist of the wording being: "The several points stated are, on search, without prior case on record to go by; it is hoped that concrete proofs will be produced and the matter reviewed again; a travel allowance may as fitting be issued to Shi Dachuan to come up to the capital and report." This was as good as to say: since no case had been lodged beforehand, then on the strength of this after-the-fact report alone the thing was not only hard to deal with, but could win no man's belief; and so the "Beiping Station" was to bring forward proof and the matter be discussed anew. This was not Mr. Dai's own endorsing reply, for the telegram bore not his signature. It was of course dealt with by the office in charge after the common run of cases. And so the "Beiping Station" was in a hard place indeed; and the writer, who had charge of the affair, was not only left between tears and laughter, but knew not how the aftermath should be seen to!

Worse yet was this: when Wang Wen hurried back to the Sanyicheng at Tianjin, Shi Dachuan, who had been appointed to wait there, was nowhere to be seen! and had left not even a scrap of writing. Wang Wen went to the counting-room to ask the chief manager Liu Zhaonan what in the end this meant. Liu said that Shi Dachuan had drawn out the two thousand dollars deposited at the counter, and had gone off never to return. Wang Wen pressed Liu Zhaonan further: at what time had Mr. Shi drawn the money? and at what time gone out? and had he left any word going out? and had he said aught of when he would come back? But Wang Wen, seeing the frightened look on Zhaonan's face, and the words he answered but mumbled and evasive, and his very manner shunning to lift his head and meet an eye, had already perceived there must be some hidden thing in it, and that the fault lay perhaps with Liu Zhaonan himself.

Wang Wen laid out the sense and reason of it thus: "Shi Dachuan had no cause to depart of his own will against the appointment—unless he had some other design, and even that whole tale of the mishap he told were a fabrication; else this unlooked-for outcome could never have come to pass. But then, where has Xian Hongxia gone? I believe Xian Hongxia would never deceive me, nor set Shi Dachuan on to come and play some trick—the less so as there is no need of it. Again, supposing Shi Dachuan had sold Xian Hongxia, and been sent by the other side to spy out our inner state—that too is against reason; for were it truly so, they ought to have sent another man, since he who sold Xian Hongxia being he, and he who came to verify the fact being the same, is as good as no verifying at all. Yet I hold there can be no such thing—even to have such a thought seems a wronging of the man."

I asked Wang Wen: "This Shi Dachuan, who says he came to bring you word—is he in the end truly the Adjutant Shi that Xian Hongxia won over to take part in our work, and how can it be made certain? What ground is there enough to prove this point?"

Wang Wen answered: "What made the deepest mark on me was Shi Dachuan's air as he spoke. Above all when he told of how he had then struck Xian Hongxia with his fists, and Xian Hongxia had kicked him a few times—the grief in the hearts of the two men being past all bearing, and yet not to be openly shown, they had made this a tacit sign between them; it was by no means put on. And so I am sure this is Shi Dachuan, the very Adjutant Shi that Xian Hongxia won over, not the least of it false."

Wang Wen added further grounds: "They—Xian, Shi, and Chu, the three of them—had from our side received no material reward whatever, still less got from us any intelligence to their own advantage. Were there fraud within, where lay the motive? and for what? And besides, it was I who took the lead, going again and again to seek out Xian Hongxia; it was not Xian Hongxia who came to seek me. For this reason I dare the more to be certain."

Wang Wen went back over the earlier course: "When I had heard Shi Dachuan tell how, the head cook old Chu betraying himself in his flurry, Xian Hongxia was thereby drawn into the mishap, and heard him firmly declare he would go back to the Shi house no more, I asked him to lodge for the time in the store. I had gone to the counting-room and charged the chief manager Liu Zhaonan, saying I was going straight back to Beiping on business, and that the Mr. Shi who had just come to seek me would lodge a night in the room I had; that I would not come back tonight, but would surely be back early next morning. Should that Mr. Shi need money, or to buy anything, let him draw it freely from the two thousand dollars deposited at the counter, and I would settle it on my return; and Liu Zhaonan nodded again and again, saying yes. As I left, I charged Shi Dachuan besides that, having no pressing business, he had best go out little; and if he needed money, or to add a little to his clothes and wants, he might go to the chief manager Liu Zhaonan at the counter and draw it, for I had already given the word. I still clearly recall that Shi Dachuan patted his pocket, signifying he had no need."

That this question must be cleared up was the pressing thing of the moment. Wang Wen, to save time and not wait upon a train, asked to use the Station's car, driven by Chen Guozhi, to speed straight to Tianjin, and I agreed.

Wang Wen reached Tianjin and went straight to the Sanyicheng grain store. And there, by a lucky chance, of all things it was in the midst of a telephone's ringing that the riddle of Shi Dachuan's flight was laid bare. The course of it was thus:

Wang Wen had from the first grown suspicious of Liu Zhaonan; only, Liu Zhaonan being the own nephew of the Sanyicheng's old proprietor, was after all one of their own, and had besides at the outset many times helped Wang Wen bring off the liaison with Xian Hongxia, and so he had no doubt of him. But this time, whenever he met with Liu Zhaonan, his bearing was always shy and shrinking, quite unlike the all-talk-and-laughter of before; and his eyes forever shunned Wang Wen's gaze upon him.

That very night, to draw out Liu Zhaonan's drift, Wang Wen purposely sat on in the Sanyicheng counting-room and would not go, and, with nothing to say, found things to say and chatted idly with Liu Zhaonan, bringing up without cease the names of Xian Hongxia and Shi Dachuan. Wang Wen watched his reactions close: Liu Zhaonan showed himself very ill at ease, and the words he answered were shifty and wide of the mark. Just as Wang Wen and Liu Zhaonan were talking on—the one with a fire kindling in his heart, the other feigning composure—the telephone rang; Liu Zhaonan picked up the receiver as a matter of course, listened, said a "Not in," and hung up. At this Wang Wen took no heed. A minute or two later the telephone rang again; Liu Zhaonan, not waiting for the other to say aught, as though out of patience, said the one phrase "You have the wrong number," and set the telephone up. Wang Wen, marking it, thought it very strange, and said to himself: could this be Shi Dachuan calling? Thinking so, Wang Wen only now raised his watchfulness. The Sanyicheng's telephone was set on the wall by the counter, and the two of them sat severally on either side of the counter, Liu Zhaonan the nearer to it; where Wang Wen sat, he must stretch his arm to reach the earpiece. Therefore Wang Wen moved his stool a little forward, reckoning in his heart that, if the telephone rang again, whoever it was that called, he would first snatch it up and hear who it might be before saying more.

Sure enough, it fell out as Wang Wen had reckoned!

Two or three minutes on, the telephone bell rang again—this was the third time within ten minutes. Wang Wen was quicker than Liu Zhaonan, and at one snatch had the earpiece in hand; and he heard the telephone say: "Hello! I have not the wrong number—pray by all means do not hang up again. I know who you are; since you were so kind as to warn me to shun this disaster, why then will you not hear me say a few words more?" Wang Wen, mimicking Liu Zhaonan's tone, answered the other: "Then say it." The telephone said: "Has that fellow surnamed Wang been back? Did he come with men? What did he show when he saw me not there?" Hearing this far, Wang Wen already understood the most of it, and, not waiting for the other to go on, hastened to explain: "Young brother Shi, listen close again to my voice—I am your Brother Wenhan. Think—how should we do a thing to wrong you? There must be some deep misunderstanding in this; hurry back, and we will talk it out face to face; and if you would then go, none shall stop you. Am I not right? What say you?"

This Shi Dachuan, hearing it was Wang Wen, was greatly stirred in feeling, and said over the telephone: "So you are Wang Wenhan? Good! Hongxia was blind to make such a friend of you, unkind and unjust—he threw away his life for you, and I, at the risk of mine, came to bring you word; and you not only would not hear it, but held me for a spy sent by the Japanese, and even meant to send me to the army headquarters to be dealt with. Is this how you treat men?"

Wang Wen, hearing it, was wronged past all telling—whence came these words?—and, eager to explain it clear, said without weighing his words: "Where are you? If you will not come back to the store, my coming to see you does as well." Shi Dachuan said: "Do not cozen me back—I will not be taken in. I alone can live by whatever I do; you had best feel your own conscience!" Saying just so much, he said no more, and with a click hung up the telephone.

Wang Wen held the receiver dazed a good while, wronged indeed and amazed past measure; and, thinking back over the days since he entered intelligence work, he had never once used "any means whatever," and had held even the harboring of such a thought a kind of sin. How came he then to say these things?

At this moment Wang Wen, facing Liu Zhaonan, saw him ashen as death, unable so much as to lift his head. And so Wang Wen, in anger, upbraided Liu Zhaonan to his face: "Hear me! This is all your doing—you have botched the thing; what in the end was it for? We are friends, we have never had a falling-out, still less done you any wrong; and besides, my dealings with Xian Hongxia owed much to your help between; and of what we did he knew not a little. Can it be you did not approve the thing? If not that, then what was it for?"

Liu Zhaonan would not lift his head, and, for all that Wang Wen pressed him hard to explain, would not answer a single word. Wang Wen's rage flared, and, quick with anger, he urged him: "Shi Dachuan's coming here was an act of honor and duty—do you understand? We welcomed him with our whole heart, and feared only we could not do enough; why must you fright him off with fearful threats? Speak the truth—did Shi Dachuan indeed take two thousand dollars from your hand?"

Liu Zhaonan still bowed his head and answered not. Wang Wen was so worked with anger he could get no word out; for he had always a stammer, and, the angrier he grew, the more he stuttered and could not speak plain. Wang Wen thought to himself: "This Liu Zhaonan is at bottom no bad man—how has he turned of a sudden so dreadful? There must be a cause in it." Wang Wen mused a while, and called to mind the night he had seen the crowd gaming for money in the store; and thence he thought further that Liu Zhaonan had very likely lost no little at play, and not only gambled away the two thousand dollars deposited at the counter, but come short in the counter's own money besides.

If his guess were not wrong, Liu Zhaonan had frightened Shi Dachuan off just to swallow that sum of money, and so leave the two beyond cross-checking.

Wang Wen spoke this meaning straight out to Liu Zhaonan; but Liu Zhaonan still would not answer, only shaking his head vehemently and refusing to own it. Wang Wen said: "If it be not so, you must yet explain it plain; to keep your mouth shut is no way—how is the thing ever to be ended?"

Liu Zhaonan, in the shame that turns to rage, stood up of a sudden and, gesturing with his hand, said: "What we keep is a grain-and-provisions store, not a command-post for killing men. Since you speak the word 'friend,' then let us have naught to do with one another—pray suit yourself, and come to the store no more hereafter."

Wang Wen, hearing these two phrases of Liu's, was near to bursting with rage, and for the moment truly longed to give him a beating. But Liu Zhaonan was much the stronger of the two; force would not do, nor would it solve the thing. Wang Wen, holding his peace in silence, went off to the counting-room to find the driver Chen Guozhi, meaning to take counsel on some good way of dealing with this wretch.

Chen Guozhi was young and strong, brawny and headstrong; hearing Wang Wen out, he put out his hand and snatched up a bent iron bar used for cranking the engine, and would go to the counting-room to beat that fellow surnamed Liu. Wang Wen, seeing it was ill, said that one blow of the bar might really beat a man to death, which would be worse still. So he hastily held Chen Guozhi back, and counseled him on no account to be rash, but that they should settle on a plan before laying hand to it. The two of them took counsel a while, and the upshot was to find a way to get Liu Zhaonan to Beiping!

There, to get to the very bottom of it.

This old crock of a car brought over from Beiping, standing in the courtyard, was of no bad performance still; if driven by night, from Tianjin back to Beiping along the highroad, it would take some three hours and more. They resolved to cozen Liu Zhaonan up onto the car, Wang Wen taking the front seat to drive instead, and Chen Guozhi, being strong, watching Liu Zhaonan in the back seat—that would serve. The pity was they carried no weapon; Chen Guozhi found in the car a "wrench" for mending it, wrapped it in a towel, and, held reversed in the hand, it looked much like a pistol. Used in earnest, brought down heavily on the head, it could knock a man senseless too. Chen Guozhi checked the fuel tank and the water tank, added a little engine oil, and, thinking a moment, locked one of the two doors of the back seat, leaving only the other to open and shut. And so the preparation was reckoned complete.

Wang Wen went back to the room, gathered up his odds and ends, and, a canvas travel-bag in hand, with Chen Guozhi following behind, went to the counting-room of the Sanyicheng grain store to take leave of the chief manager Liu Zhaonan. Wang Wen came to the counting-room, Chen Guozhi standing outside the door, and said to Liu Zhaonan: "Since you do not welcome my staying in your store, there is no need to wait for daylight—I will drive back to Beiping this very night. Friends as we have been, let us speak no more of anything; so, till we meet again!" Having said it, Wang Wen went to shake Liu Zhaonan's hand, and, drawing him slowly outward, Liu, too ill at ease to refuse, took Wang Wen's hand and saw him out. Out of the counting-room, not far from the car standing in the courtyard, they walked and talked, each putting the other off, and came up to the car, where Liu Zhaonan courteously opened the car door for Wang Wen. In that very instant, Chen Guozhi, taking him unawares, from behind crooked his right elbow of a sudden about his neck, and, with the force of it, hauled him back; his left hand heaving up hard from between the man's thighs, at one lift he raised Liu Zhaonan and flung him into the back seat of the car. There was no help for some knocking and jolting, but Liu Zhaonan took no hurt.

At this the car had already started. Liu Zhaonan kicked and cried out in the car, and Chen Guozhi, pressing the iron bar against his arm, said: "Raise a stir again, and it is your own death you seek. Look out there—the dead of night, the open waste with no man in it; lay you down and push you out, and none would ever know. And another thing—whatever we meet ahead, you are to sit here honestly and not stir; if you play any crooked trick, then blame me not." Wang Wen, in the front seat, took it up: "Zhaonan! Set your mind at rest! We mean nothing else; we hope only that you will speak the truth and tell plainly how it went, and we can turn the car about and send you back to Tianjin at once. But if you set yourself against speaking, then that is not so certain."

In truth this affair was very simple; but Liu Zhaonan, a man cross-grained past everything, would just not speak, and so they hauled him to Beiping and lodged him in an empty room in the last courtyard of the "Beiping Station."

To go on, then: after Wang Wen had brought Liu Zhaonan from Tianjin back to Beiping, what we had first to weigh was that the line of rescue for Xian Hongxia and the rest, broken off by Shi Dachuan's flight, must somehow be opened by another path, that the rescue might be carried on. Next was how to answer our superiors' inquiry, and furnish true and solid proof fit to be believed, so as to close this case. In truth Shi Dachuan was the best living witness; but to what place in Tianjin was one now to go to seek him? The questions were many, and at this hour, this moment, a way to solve them was hard to come by, every one.

As for how this Liu Zhaonan was to be dealt with—for one thing there was no fear of his not speaking the truth, and for another he might be kept as a corroborating witness. For this reason he was lodged in the back court, well fed and well drunk and well used, and the thing not laid to heart at all.

There—it was all for so trifling a matter that a great disaster was stirred up!

### 6. A Man Like Shi Yousan Naturally Comes to No Good End

I stayed some days in Beiping, as one with his soul struck out of him. Whether to screw up my courage and go to Nanjing to take my punishment—I could by no means come to a resolve on it. And so a second time I went out to Guisui, and again threw myself on my elder brother. Half a year over, my daily life lost its center; day upon day I idled and loafed, my mood unsteady besides, and one may say I stood upon the very brink of ruin.

In this stretch of time the one thing worth the mention was that, egged on by a set of newly made friends, I passed—from having been formerly "one foot within the door and one foot without"—through the mysterious rite of "setting up the incense hall and opening the mountain gate," and became of the Green Gang a man of the "Tong" generation, "the twenty-second." At that time the Green Gang was still fairly active in the Northwest, and its so-called "generation-standing" was higher too than in the Southeast. I had meant to use this connection to break into some stratum of society, and perhaps work something toward the rallying of the masses; little did I know how green and how naive such a notion was. In fact, unless there were a "reliable backing" to support one, or, one may say, unless "uncommon methods" were used as one's means, it would take no effect at all. And as for me, I had no such qualifications whatever, and so of course it came to nothing but empty dreaming. So after this I never again showed this standing of mine, and in all the working days that followed I never once turned such a connection to use. I recall that in class during training there was once a course on "the secret societies of the community," and this was the very matter it treated; but theory in the end is theory, and to bring theory into actual use there must be joined to it a good many more of the conditions of reality.

In mid-October 1935, the ninth moon by the lunar reckoning, the season was already deep autumn; and in Suiyuan it was not only cool weather but held a fair chill besides. In the midst of my utter listlessness, meeting this season too, I could not but feel some desolation of mood. What would come and make me uneasy was that, through the worthlessness of me alone, so many men were dragged in and made to suffer. What was this worth? Even could I drag out a bare life, I could not face the light of day; and rather than bear this guilt forever, better to come forward the sooner and seek to make amends. Only let the charge of "kidnapping and ransom" not be made to stand, and there would be no capital crime; as for the breach of discipline, at the very most it would be a few years in prison.

I took counsel with my elder brother, and he too urged me to it; and only then did I pluck up courage, and, hesitating no more, packed my baggage and set out once again in haste for Beiping.

Come to Beiping, I had meant to find Fan Xing, and through his liaison to meet first with the district head of the "Beiping District"—who was also my bosom friend of old, Mr. Zhang Binghua (Yanyuan)—to ask his counsel on how I should do, and then fix the steps thereafter. But going to inquire at the YMCA where he had lodged, it was said Fan Xing had moved away some months before, and so I drew a blank.

I must by all means find a way to Fan Xing, or else there would be no getting in touch with the "Beiping District." Last time it was from Jiang Taiqing that I had inquired out Fan Xing's lodging; this time I would go seek out Jiang Taiqing again and try. But little did I look for it—it took four or five days to find Jiang Taiqing; and, on inquiry, she said she had not seen Fan Xing these several months, and had heard he was gone to Shanghai—whether true or false, there was no guessing.

Since Fan Xing was not to be found, I thought of other ways to reach the "Beiping District." I asked round very near all the places I had known when I was chief of the "Beiping Station," and still could not find it.

This, I suppose, was what is called "at the end of one's road." I had once too the thought of buying a train ticket and going straight back to Nanjing, but always I hung back; and, turning it over and over, I came at last to a resolve: heedless of the suspicion of "leaking secrets," I actually wrote a short letter, dropped it into the post-box, addressed straight to Mr. Dai Yunong at No. 53 Ji'e Lane, Nanjing. The wording of the letter I can no longer clearly recall; the gist was: "I am willing to come back and receive my punishment; my one request is that Brother Lian Mou (Liangshun) be sent to Beiping to fetch me. If it be granted, then, once Brother Liangshun has reached Beiping, a small notice in the Shijie Ribao will suffice." My writing this letter to Mr. Dai was truly a great daring; all know that he would never allow a subordinate who had erred to make any condition before receiving punishment—and above all to name a newspaper for a public advertisement, and to write a letter leaving no return address, and the like, would anger him the more. But in the end I made the trial.

As to why I asked that Lian Mou alone be sent to fetch me back—this too was a little scheme of mine; for I and Brother Lian Liangshun were on the closest of private terms, and his joining the Juntong was through my introducing him to Mr. Dai, who thought highly of him too; so that if he should put in a few good words besides, it must surely tell in my favor. Deeper yet: should things go too ill for me—say it came to a sentence of death—I believed he would let me slip the halter and bid me not come; and he alone had the nerve for it, and dared so to do. I know men will call this favoritism; but in my own heart I hold it his sense of honor.

After the letter was sent, my mind grew ill at ease, torn between hope and fear, dreading it might draw down a graver end. Men commonly use the two words "torment and pressing" to picture such a state—and truly so.

About eight or nine days on, in the Shijie Ribao there did indeed appear a small advertisement, which ran: "Brother Renfeng: I have reached Beiping, and now lodge at the Huayuan Hotel; hoping you will soon come to meet me. Liangshun." Just so—"Zhou Renfeng" was a cover-name I had used.

Old Lian had indeed come; I was overjoyed—it could not be a false thing? At once I telephoned the Huayuan Hotel, and, taking the call and listening, it was indeed Liangshun's voice—then what was there to misgive? I said: "I come at once."

The eastern city was a long way from Bicai Hutong in the western city; by rickshaw it took near forty minutes and more to arrive. These forty minutes and more were truly too long; they gave me a fine chance to reflect—had I but at the outset been a little more careful, how should I have come to so sorry a pass!

Not seen these several years, Brother Liangshun was still as bright and open as ever; taking my hand he told me: "I am now on the staff of the Wuhan Garrison Command; getting the telegram, I hurried to Nanjing, and Mr. Dai showed me the letter you had written—so it was you who bade me make the trip. In public and in private alike, I could not in honor refuse, and so I came."

What I was eager to know was, how stood Mr. Dai's attitude? Had he made any concrete sign? Brother Liangshun said: "There is no problem at all; set your mind at rest. Were there a problem, I would not have come." Hearing it I felt a good spell of ease; yet, one stone not fallen to the ground, my heart still hung.

That same evening, Brother Liangshun bade the district head of the "Beiping District," Mr. Zhang Yanyuan, and the district secretary, Mr. Mao Wanli, together to give me a supper; this was a most rare gathering of old friends, but because my heart was heavy with care, I spoke little, and could not make the scene a merry one. The three of them, as if by tacit accord, never once touched on the main matter either; and their best device was but to drink a few cups more. I drink whatever wine there is, and, minded to drink my fill—the better if I fell down drunk—I fell to hard drinking.

While my wits were yet clear, I purposely led the talk round to the general state of the "Beiping Station," and above all to how the personnel had been shifted about. But before I could say much, they turned the talk off to some other quarter. It may be they shunned it of set purpose. Afterward, being by then muddled with drink, what they all said I could not recall to save myself.

The next day, Brother Lian Liangshun went with me by the Blue Steel express of the Tianjin-Pukou line straight to Nanjing. We rode in a first-class berth. Their "courtesy" to me was of the utmost; and yet, in truth, one might as well call it a "sending-up under guard," and that too would fit.

Come to Nanjing, by chance Mr. Dai was gone to Hangzhou on business; Brother Liangshun again went with me and took a room at the Anleyuan Hotel, and we waited two days before Mr. Dai came back. Then Brother Lian Liangshun went with me once more to Ji'e Lane to see Mr. Dai.

This was a most awkward moment, a moment that decided my fate, a moment too there was no escaping. Mr. Dai Yunong, as ever of old, first shook hands, let us be seated, and then waited to speak. After a long stretch of silence—I reckoned he would fly into a great rage, but he did not—he said to me very mildly: "The state has its law, the household its rules; a body of men must needs have the discipline that holds it in being. Were I not to punish you, not only would others talk, but there would be no answering to the general run of comrades…" Saying this, he paused a moment, and made ready to say more, when Brother Liangshun put in: "Brother Gongshu, it is as well you take this chance to cultivate yourself a while; I think Mr. Dai is generous, and it will not be more than a year at most." Mr. Dai, hearing what Lian Liangshun said, showed himself much displeased, and, turning to Brother Liangshun, said: "Is it you who fix the one year?" Brother Liangshun laughed; but I, though I would laugh, could not.

To show a little spirit, I broke my silence and said: "The thing ought by right to rest on me; my coming back is just my honest readiness to receive punishment—let all be as Mr. Dai shall decide." One word I would have said, but did not utter—that was to plead for the friends dragged in on my account. The reason I did not say it was that I felt it would be but too much of a show; and besides, I had no room to put in a word.

Mr. Dai said no more; of what was past he uttered not a syllable; he rose and directed: "Go you and get in touch with Liu Yiguang yourself."

I walked out of the parlor first, alone, and heard only Brother Liangshun still speaking with Mr. Dai Yunong—what, I could not hear clearly. After a little, Brother Liangshun caught up and clapped me on the back from behind, and comforted me, saying: "Take great care of yourself; it will surely not be more than a year, and when the time comes I will surely come to fetch you out. Today I must go back to Hankou."

We walked to the main gate, and just then met my schoolmate Liu Yiguang—perhaps he was even then waiting for me. We were not only schoolmates of the Military Academy, but had trained together at Honggongci; since our graduation this was the first time we met, and yet under such circumstances, a thing past all looking for. When I had passed on to him Mr. Dai's meaning, he agreed to let me go back and put my things in order, take a bath, and cut my hair short, and the next forenoon he would go with me to report.

This place was called Yangpi Lane, an old-style low, flat-roofed house; to look at it without, it was no different from any dwelling. Going in at a black-gray wooden plank door, within stood a plain-clothes guard, and a turn to the right gave onto two narrow little offices; the man in charge of the place was already standing at the door to receive us.

When I raised my head and looked close, the man standing at the door to receive us was none other than Hou Zichuan, the earliest communicator the "Beiping Station" had taken on; because he had furnished a line for an action, he could no longer keep his footing in Beiping, and so was moved back to Nanjing and posted to other work—little did I think that he who "managed" me today should be he.

Hou Zichuan, at the sight of me, flushed red in the face; in truth it was I who should have blushed, not he. He gave me a seat, poured me a cup of plain boiled water, and would say something, but before Liu Yiguang's face felt it awkward, and so hemmed and hawed and got it not out.

Liu Yiguang was Hou Zichuan's superior, and he charged Hou Zichuan especially to give me all the "favorable treatment" he could. And so a single room was set apart for me in the great common hall—that is, I dwelt alone in a cell; and truly this had rather something of "ill-treatment" in it, for I was too lonely, too forlorn.

Hou Zichuan told me that by the rule one might not give out one's true name; every man had a number to stand for his name, and my number was "162." For this reason, on the washbasin and the rinsing-cup too was marked a "162." In truth this was but a form; and afterward, in private, my fellow-inmates all made known to one another their true identities.

That same night, the guard on duty, coming in on his round as he checked over the count at the changing of the watch, pushed the door open; he smiled at me, set a bundle on the table, and went off without a word. I saw this man was tall and big and swarthy-coarse, and very familiar, but could not for the moment call to mind where I had seen him. When I opened the bundle, within was a small packet of tea, a box of cakes, and, tucked in besides, a little slip of paper, on which were written the seven words "Respectfully, your former subordinate Chen Guorui." Oh—he was the temporary hand who had lent his strength in the case of sanctioning Ji Hongchang at Tianjin, and had after it been sent up to Nanjing for training; recalling the dust of the past, it was truly a thing not to be borne to look back on.

No one summoned me to a "talk," nor did any open a court to "examine" me, still less did any bid me write a "confession" or the like—this was one great feature of this stretch of imprisonment.

And there was this too, hardest of all to bear: no term of sentence was declared. Not I alone was so, but every man in hold was so; and thus every man in hold guessed at his sentence in a hundred ways, which was too a daily lesson set for him. Some said it turned on one's conduct and showing during the term of custody; but tried out, it was nothing of the sort at all.

Hou Zichuan, going his daily round, always gave me a courteous smile, and one could see he purposely shunned to speak with me; and I well understood his hard case too. One day, a Sunday, when the managing staff took their turns of leave, Hou Zichuan, seizing the gap, "brought" me to his office to sit a while, and only then had we the chance to talk a good deal more freely; and from what he let fall I came to know not a little.

This place's unofficial name was "Site B," and those held there were all one's own working comrades—that is, it was the place for those who had broken the body's discipline; and, broadly, the sentences were none of them heavy, but no man knew which of them would be let out, or when.

Beyond "Site B" there was yet a "Site A," a place of especially favorable treatment, wholly on a household model, where, save that one might not go out, all the other restraints were of the lightest; it was said Miss Yang was held there. The Miss Yang of Hou Zichuan's mouth was my own wife, Yang So-and-so.

Beyond Site A and Site B, there was besides, marked off within the Laohuqiao Army Prison, a separate portion set apart for the holding of grave-sentence criminals, called "Site C"; and it was said that Wang Wen and that root of the trouble, Liu Zhaonan, were both held within it.

I asked Hou Zichuan whether there were any way to pass word to my household; Hou Zichuan pointed me the way—wait till Chen Guorui was on duty, and take counsel with him; he would surely have a way, for, being on the rota of duty, he was sometimes sent to "Site A" too.

Hou Zichuan said he was truly powerless in my affair, and dared not give me any special care; but that, choosing a fit day, he would arrange in his office to order a few dishes and stand me a meal.

In this talk of ours, the several main points I wished to understand were all answered; and at the same time he gave me a hope besides—a hope of eating a meal by stealth. And do not slight this meal: over and above a very heavy weight of human kindness, to a man in prison, when he has lost his freedom and can get nothing he thinks of, to have "a bite" to eat can content him a moment—the more so a good "square meal"!

I looked forward several days, and looked at last to Chen Guorui's coming on night duty; I broached the matter of carrying word to my household, and he agreed with a full heart. So I hastily wrote a little slip of paper, and gave it him to carry to my wife, held at "Site A." Its content was no more than to tell her I had come back to Nanjing, and would be out before too long. I would have written a few words more of comfort, but feared it would be awkward should another read it.

Speaking of imprisonment, I had already been imprisoned many a time; but this was the most peaceful of all, for, though I had still some misgiving whether the term were one year, my heart yet felt it would surely not go beyond a year. At times I came to see that the meaning of a prison sentence lies just in wiping out, from a man's whole life, this stretch of blank time; as for such talk as "reflection," it is but pleasant to the ear.

In the early summer of 1936, by the record of "Site B," I had been held five months and seven days in all. There was not the least sign of it beforehand; that day, a little past noon, Hou Zichuan of a sudden called to me across the wooden bars, and said to me low: "Mr. Chen, pray gather your things at once and come with me—Mr. Zhang Yanyuan is waiting in the office."

My heart beat hard, and one may call it a joy beyond hope; clasping a few things, I followed Hou Zichuan to his office, and Brother Zhang Binghua, all smiles, shook my hand, and said in jest: "Last year at Beiping I saw you off onto the train; today I must see you onto the train again." He went on: "Mr. Dai has a written order in his own hand, sending you to take charge at Tianjin; he, having business, has gone to Hangzhou, and before setting out charged the secretary-general Brother Zhang Yifu to stand for him and give you a supper, a send-off. All the particulars, this evening Brother Yifu will tell you himself.

"Now, let me first go with you and take a hotel room; whatever there is to say, we can say at the hotel." No formalities of any kind were gone through; and so, following Brother Zhang Binghua, I left "Site B" of Yangpi Lane, and was reckoned to have got back my freedom.

On the road, Brother Binghua told me he had been posted back to the bureau headquarters at Nanjing; that his coming today to fetch me was no assignment, but that, seeing Mr. Dai's written order, he had of set purpose begged to come and do a friend's part. We came to the Central Hotel, and Brother Binghua went out to telephone; I seized the chance to bathe first, and in a little while there came a knock at the door, and, opening it, I was wild with joy—little had I looked for my wife to come so fast; we had been parted near a year and a half, and she had for my sake borne a prosecution past all sense, which, to speak of it, truly shamed me past bearing.

Binghua took his leave and went, charging us not to go out for the time, but that in a while a car would be sent to fetch us to the feast.

This meal was eaten none too easily, the chief reason being that after it we must set off; again and again I begged to stay one day more at Nanjing, my aim being to see my old friends, and to render some account of this past matter of my regret, but by no means would they consent. The acting secretary-general Zhang Yifu handed me a packet, in which were the means of liaison for after I reached Tianjin, the cipher-book for wiring with Mr. Dai, and two first-class berth tickets besides, and a special allowance for fitting myself out. As for the tasks hereafter and the like, it was said instruction would be given after I arrived.

This was as good as a restoring to my former post, only that the place was changed. The one most singular point: this whole affair, from first to last, was never openly discussed, nor criticized to my face, by any man.

The writer, cut off from the work a year and a half, had no way to know of a great many things; the more so, hid away in the out-of-the-way region of the city of Guisui. Only after my restoration as chief of the "Tianjin Station" did I, little by little, in the odd scraps of documentary and intelligence reference material, come upon some records touching Shi Yousan; the time was about July 1936.

At that time the state of North China was of a special cast: the North China Political Affairs Council, with General Song Zheyuan single-handed bearing up the whole, dealt with the Japanese military under conditions most uncommon and full of pressure. In truth this could no longer be reckoned "diplomatic parley," but was mere putting-off, humoring, and playing for time.

Next I shall speak again of that fickle, inconstant Shi Yousan:

In October 1935, when the Japanese were plotting the "North China Autonomy Movement," Shi Yousan, still driven at the Japanese bidding, went back and forth between Tianjin and Dalian, running about to organize a "North China Army Corps Headquarters." But because he overstepped the bounds of activity the Japanese side had set, he was driven off by them to Tianjin, and forbidden to go to Dalian again.

Shi Yousan was not thereby a whit checked, and so turned to work upon the Japanese garrison army at Tianjin, seeking to rise again. In February 1936, the commander of the Japanese garrison army at Tianjin, Tada Hayao, asked Song Zheyuan to give him some title; but because Shi Yousan's warrant of arrest had still not been withdrawn, Song had no way to employ him.

In July 1936, there was on the Japanese side again talk of "aiding Shi Yousan to set up a North China Autonomous Army," but it came to nothing in fact.

In October 1936, Song Zheyuan came from Beiping to Tianjin, and met with Tashiro Kan'ichirō, the first commander of the Japanese garrison army at Tianjin after its reorganization and upgrading. Tashiro had again asked Song Zheyuan to his face to employ Shi Yousan and give him a military post. And so Song Zheyuan, on the nineteenth of October, openly proclaimed Shi Yousan "Peace-Preservation Commander of the Northern Hebei Border Region," his command headquarters set at the Yellow Temple outside the Andingmen gate of Beiping. And he marked off the border tracts of Changhua, Shunyi, and Tongzhou (within the town of Tong county was the seat of Yin Rugeng's puppet East Hebei government) as his defense district. Song purposed further to move a regiment of peace-preservation troops from Chahar to Beiping to be under Shi's command, and appointed that at the Yellow Temple the forming and drilling should begin, to help him make up his army. Why Song Zheyuan should do this had, of course, a good many reasons behind the scenes besides. As for how he petitioned the Central Government to withdraw the warrant against Shi, we made no further inquiry into it.

After Shi Yousan had "returned to the right," Song Zheyuan once secretly ordered Shi's forces to recover East Hebei. Shi asked that Song lend him besides a part of the armed force of the Twenty-ninth Army, and that under his own name he would attack East Hebei; succeeding, he begged Song to petition the Central Government to place him as "administrative commissioner of the East Hebei region"; and failing, he might cast off the secretly-worked tie with the Twenty-ninth Army and take it wholly on himself, that the Japanese army might have no pretext to work harm to Song. While Song was yet wavering, this word too was got wind of by the Japanese army, who strengthened their guard the more, and put questions of challenge; and so this plan to recover East Hebei died in the womb.

At that time the writer much wished, through some personal connection, to go and ask Shi Yousan just what in the end he had done with the two men Xian Hongxia and old Chu. But even this little thing, which by rights might have been done, was, being barred by the larger circumstances, not done. We—the whole working organization included—truly failed the loyal and heroic sacrificed; and the writer himself must of course bear the more of the blame.

After the July 7 War of Resistance, Shi Yousan's forces withdrew southward together with the Twenty-ninth Army; and before long Shi bred an evil design once more, and was in the end openly put to the law. To speak of it fairly, this too was but the just deserts of a fickle, inconstant, chance-seeking man.
