## The Hachiōji Raid

—Toshi's demon-feet.

Speak of them, and anyone around the Hino station who had known Toshizō as a boy would know at once. This man's legs were swift as a demon's.

Okita Sōji, for one,

{j} —You're a monster, Hijikata, a very Idaten,

{j} had once teased him. At the time Toshizō (what's he on about) had only sulked and held his tongue; but he was a man to nurse even such a trifle, and a good many days later, out of nowhere,

{j} "Sōji, didn't you know? A man quick on his feet is quick in the wits too—that's the whole of it."

Such were his legs. Once he set off he fell into a sullen silence, his eyes alone glittering restlessly, and with that peculiar unfriendly scowl he went biting off the road with his feet, stride upon stride.

That evening, at the very hour Shichiri Kennosuke was still in talk with Kondō Isami, Toshizō slipped out of the hall at Yanagichō in Koishikawa like a shadow.

When he had run the thirteen ri of the Kōshū Highway without a break and crossed the Asa River bridge at Hachiōji, day had not yet broken. Demon-legged, sure enough.

(Shichiri won't have got back yet.)

As he came into the post-town, the tea-stalls for early-starting travelers were already sliding open their shutters.

Behind the wayside shrine just over the Asa River bridge, Toshizō changed his clothes and, as usual, made himself over into a peddler of "Ishida Powder."

He wrapped his face in a dark-blue cloth—though the road was still dark—and, his sword rolled in a straw mat, went into the Edoya, an inn in the Yokoyama sub-station.

"It's me."

Well, fancy that, cried the serving-girls, making a fuss over him. It was an old familiar lodging. Here, of course, they took Toshizō for nothing but a medicine-peddler.

Here he slept sound for a couple of hours, then had a tray brought, doused his rice with soup, and ate his fill.

(That will do.)

He went out to the road. The morning mist was cold.

Travelers moved in the mist. From here to the Hiruma hall in Senninmachi was some two kilometers. But Toshizō did not go straight there. Once he set a thing going he would run mad, throwing off sparks; until then, though, he laid his dispositions with an almost needless care.

First he looked in at the Senjubō, that same temple. It was to learn how things stood at the hall. As he made for the temple-servant's hut beside the drum-tower in the precincts, the old abbot, sunning himself on the veranda of the abbot's quarters, spotted him sharp-eyed and,

{j} "The medicine-man, is it?"

{j} beckoned him over. His luck was in. Toshizō put on a smile for the abbot.

"And how have you been keeping lately?"

{j} The abbot sat Toshizō on the veranda and with his own hands made him green tea.

"Much as ever, thank you."

"Good, good."

The abbot laid a pinch of pickled greens on Toshizō's palm.

"By the by—the young lady you gave in marriage to the Hiruma hall, is she well?"

"Sen? Thank you. She keeps her health."

{j} said the abbot—a man like a living Buddha. Little did he dream that this peddler was a scoundrel past all mending, and the girl his victim.

"But, you know—"

{j} the abbot, talkative as ever,

{j} "things over at the Hiruma hall seem to be in a fair muddle these days."

"Oh?"

{j} Toshizō cocked his head prettily.

"How so?"

"Ah, it's much like gamblers squabbling over turf. In the old days the Asa River yonder was the boundary—Tennen Rishin-ryū to the east, Kōgen Ittō-ryū to the west, so it was fixed; but the world's grown rough with all this expel-the-barbarian to-do, and now each side means to seize the other's ground by main force. A throwback to the warring days of Genki and Tenshō, you'd think."

The old monk, narrowing eyes single-lidded like his daughter Osen's,

{j} "By my son-in-law's account there's some fellow—born out Ishida way by Hino station, head student of the Tennen Rishin-ryū, what's-his-name—a scoundrel there's no handling; and the Hiruma side had a plan to lure him out to Bubaigawara and cut him down, but instead he left several of their men wounded and bolted for Edo with his tail afire."

"A droll fellow, that."

"Droll, is it. A brute you'd hate the very look of, I'll be bound."

"Aye."

{j} Toshizō sipped his tea slowly.

"Well? Another cup?"

"Aye, thank you kindly.—But by the talk along the Kōshū Highway, this Shichiri Kennosuke, the head student at the Hiruma hall, is a fair scoundrel himself, with an ill name."

"So it seems,"

{j} the old monk nodded,

{j} "By all accounts this Shichiri was never a Hachiōji swordsman nor a Kōgen Ittō-ryū man to begin with, but a hired head student drifted down from Jōshū. My son-in-law Hiruma Hanzō is inwardly at his wits' end with him, they say; but since the man came, the farmer and gambler pupils have swelled greatly in number, so my son-in-law shuts his eyes to it, I suppose. Still, they say he's terribly good with a blade."

"Well, well."

"He goes about boasting that the Kōgen Ittō-ryū of Hachiōji will yet crush the mongrel schools of the Three Tama and lord it over all the western Musashi country. Well—another cup?"

"Eh?"

{j} Toshizō was thinking of other things.

"Tea, I said."

"I've had a plenty, thank you."

He bowed, rose, and passing out at the temple gate returned to the highway.

The mist had cleared. Keeping to the eaves of the post-town, Toshizō walked west.

The Hachiōji station was the greatest on the whole Kōshū Highway, stretching long toward the west, and dividing, if you counted the sub-stations, into fifteen. Walking those sub-stations—Yokoyama, Yōkaichi, Hachiman, Yagi—by the time he reached the corner of Senninmachi, where the samurai residences stood in their rows, the sun was already high.

(Now, then.)

Toshizō did not hesitate. He strolled at his ease past the front gate of the Hiruma hall and carried straight on round to the back wicket. Nor was that all: bold as brass, he simply stepped in among the grounds. It was much like a housebreaking in broad daylight.

Luckily, not a soul in sight.

(Careless of them.)

Hunching his shoulders, Toshizō went slowly along the narrow passage between the hall and the house. He knew the lay of it. Carry straight through and there would be another wicket, and beyond it, he knew, a whole spread of mulberry field.

Just as he made to pass through, behind him a door slid rattling open.

(………)

At last he stopped his feet. Yet he would not turn to look behind. Were he challenged,

—Aye, only the medicine-man, sir.

{j} —he had the words ready. At this hall such a disguise would no longer pass; but Toshizō had ready, too, the brazen gall to carry it off without a blush.

"………"

Still Toshizō did not turn. But—strange to say—the one behind him too stayed dumb, and would not call out. Only a hard, quick breathing reached him. A woman.

(Osen.)

Convenient. That one should meet, all at once, the very person one had come to meet—is there, then, between a man and a woman who have known each other's bodies, some unseen thread that runs between them? But Toshizō,

(It's me.)

{j} did not even say it, and walked on.

Behind that peddler's figure Osen stood trembling, the color gone from her lips. This was no longer the feeling of a love-affair. Terror, one might call it. For what purpose did this man come, and come again, to the house she had married into?

Osen knew, of course, that Toshizō was no peddler but the head student of the Tennen Rishin-ryū, and she knew the whole tale, from the cutting-down of Rokusha to the brawl at Bubaigawara.

For that very reason she was afraid.

Just short of the wicket the peddler turned easily to the right. There stood a shed. He knew the place well. The shed was hemmed in by the miso storehouse and the utensil storehouse, and that neither household nor pupils ever came there—this too the man knew, like a well-practiced thief.

One thing more the peddler knew well: Osen's heart.

(She will follow, surely.—)

And follow she did: hushing her footfalls, drawn as by a thread, Osen came after Toshizō's back. Between the shed and the storehouse, Toshizō was waiting.

Toshizō reached out, drew Osen to him, and of a sudden crushed her in his arms.

"Do I trouble you?"

{j} he whispered at her ear. Trouble her he did, of course. Even as he whispered, with his left hand he parted the skirt of Osen's robe and set a merciless work going. Osen stood where she was, held. Her bare feet trod a clump of dokudami, and the green juice of the leaves wetted her toes. A gentle woman.

She did not twist away and resist; yet, in what was for this woman a very utmost effort,

{j} "Pray—do not come to me any more,"

{j} she said in a small voice. Just then the sun clouded over all at once. A wind rose against the west range of the storehouse, and the chestnut tree began to stir.

"An ill man you have bound yourself to."

{j} Toshizō's voice was dry.

"Oh, no—"

"Be still a while—do not move."

{j} Toshizō's fingers tightened. Osen was near to weeping. But struggle as she might, Toshizō's one arm held her body fast and would not let her move.

"But—like this—in broad day.—"

"By night, then, you would say it is well?"

"I am too afraid, now. Pray do not come here again."

Even as she spoke, the feet by which Osen barely stood were trampling the dokudami blindly.

"That—I cannot grant.—"

"Then—tomorrow night at ten o'clock, leave open for me the back wicket that faces the mulberry field. I will come in secret. Let me have my last desire of you, and I will never come again. You will consent to this—yes?"

"Yes."

{j} She nodded faintly.

(That is well.)

Toshizō's business with Osen was done. What remained was only to lead Okita Sōji, Harada Sanosuke, Nagakura Shinpachi and the rest in through that opened wicket, and the thing was as good as finished.

(You villain.)

{j} Toshizō did not so mock himself. For the present, only winning the fight was of any importance to him.

***

The next evening, as Toshizō waited at the Edoya, Okita and the others arrived as arranged.

By the plan laid beforehand, so as not to rouse the innfolk's suspicion, they lodged downstairs as guests entirely separate from Toshizō.

After the meal, Okita Sōji came up alone to Toshizō's room.

"………"

Toshizō, head bent, was busy at some handiwork on his knees. Looking closer—he was putting the powder into a five-gō flask of hot sake.

"What's that you've got?"

"A sovereign remedy for bruises and broken bones. Put it in sake and drink it beforehand, and it works the quicker."

"So that's the famous Hijikata family remedy, the Ishida Powder?"

"It's my stock-in-trade."

{j} An unfriendly face, as ever.

The raw material, as was written earlier, is gathered from the bed of the Asa River, that tributary of the Tama which runs close by the Hijikata house. It is a thorned water-weed that still today grows thick all over the riverbed; they take it, dry it, and in the farming off-season char it black, grind it down in the medicine-mortar, and make the powder. In the Hijikata house, for the gathering of this weed—each year on the Day of the Ox in the dog-days—and for the season of compounding, they would muster the whole village to the work; and Toshizō, by the time he was twelve or thirteen, was seeing to the whole of it: the rounding-up of the hands, their posting, the command. That Toshizō was so good at moving men had part of its root here.

"This stuff works, I tell you."

{j} Toshizō looked pleased.

"Does it, though? But Hijikata, the man's got a broken bone. Can a thing you drink do any good for that?"

"Medicine is drunk with the spirit. Set your heart firm, believe it will surely work, and work it will."

"A wondrous medicine, that."

"Take this downstairs and give them all five or six cups apiece."

"They'll weep with gratitude, every man of them."

{j} Okita put out his tongue with a flick.

"The weapon is the wooden sword. Even if the other side comes with live blades, we beat them down with wooden ones. At Bubaigawara it was open country, so it did well enough; this time it won't do. It's the Hachiōji station, after all."

"We fall on them in their sleep, then."

"No,"

{j} said Toshizō.

"We hold a proper match. The only difference from an ordinary one is that we knock the other fellow awake and force a wooden sword into his hand—that is all."

"I see."

A wild conceit. Whatever the reality, in form it kept the shape of a match to the last. Win, and your name is made. In the way of the sword, between the side that has gained a name and the side that has lost one there is, in everything, a difference of heaven and earth.

"Once Shichiri Kennosuke came to our hall and flung down his word, we may take it the match had already begun. Whoever lets his guard down is the one in the wrong."

"The break-in—from where?"

"I've got it all worked out. On the spot, just follow my orders."

There was still time before ten o'clock. Toshizō drove Sōji off and lay down.

As he dozed, a serving-woman past her prime, an old acquaintance of Toshizō's, came up, and

{j} "Well? How about it?"

{j} she said. Would he not sleep with her, she meant.

"No, leave it."

"Not good enough for you, am I?"

"I can't abide the reek of face-powder, that's all."

"Queer one, you are. Well then, I'll wash the powder off and come back up, so wrap yourself up snug in the bedding and wait, there's a good boy. And mind, I'm not saying it for trade. A fine-looking man sleeping alone is a mean, shabby sight, so I say it out of pure charity."

"Much obliged. But tonight I've got to make a night start and be off for Kōshū, you see."

"Oh—you're for a night start too? Give it up. The samurai gentlemen downstairs said they were making a night start as well."

"Samurai are samurai, and I'm myself."

"Even so—"

{j} the serving-woman peered in,

{j} "there's something queer about that lot. They're not going to make a fight of it with the Hiruma hall, are they?"

(What—!)

But Toshizō wiped the surprise from his face and rose slowly. The plan had leaked.

"Where did you hear that?"

"A hunch, is all."

{j} The woman tittered and teased him. Toshizō turned his face away. The powder sunk into the wrinkles of the woman's whitened nape was more than he could stomach. A serving-woman she might be, but she must be all of fifty.

"My own hunch, my dear,"

{j} the woman said, with an air of triumph.

"………"

For all that he was a womanizer, there were times when women, as a kind, turned his stomach with a shudder. Or rather—at heart, perhaps, he hated and disliked women. That Toshizō had never once lost his heart to a woman may have been because women frightened him, and he was forever poised to run.

"Come—don't you want to hear?"

{j} With a bony finger the woman prodded Toshizō's knee.

{j} "It'll be a rare show—a play only I know of is about to start, right out here on the road."

"What do you mean by that?"

"It's like this."

A while before, when the woman was easing herself at the downstairs privy, there had been a samurai out on the road. Odd, she thought, and stepping out under the eaves she saw there were several of them—strolling the road with no errand, or standing in the shadow of the rain-barrel by the inn across the way—and the look of it was not ordinary.

(An arrest, perhaps?)

{j} she thought; but they were no arresting-party. Every face was one of the young fellows from the Hiruma hall.

"The Hiruma hall?"

{j} Toshizō caught his breath.

It was out.

The other side had already forestalled him and was watching the Edoya, it seemed. Likely enough they had men enough posted in the darkness at the edge of the station.

(Who let it out?)

The blood drained from Toshizō's face. It must be Osen who had told. There was no doubt of it. Unable to keep it shut in her small breast, she must, in one way or another, have let it out to her husband Hanzō, or to Shichiri Kennosuke.

"My dear."

{j} The woman laughed blithely and poked Toshizō with her finger.

{j} "You've deceived your share of women in your time, haven't you."

"What?"

{j} Toshizō started. It fit far too closely with the reckoning in his own breast. But the woman's jest carried no hidden barb; she wound an arm about his neck and,

{j} "Because you're a fine-looking man, that's why,"

{j} she said.

"Get off."

Toshizō was already on his feet. Downstairs were Okita Sōji, Harada Sanosuke, Nagakura Shinpachi, Tōdō Heisuke. Whether so many could get clear of Hachiōji unharmed—of that even Toshizō had no sure reckoning.
