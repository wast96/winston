## Chapter Nine. The Hour of the Rooster, Second Half (6 p.m.)

{v} The carter reined the ox-cart to a halt and coughed once. Inside the carriage, the infirmary apprentice drew a dagger from his waist and stabbed down at the patient on the stretcher. From beneath the stretcher’s blanket a great hand shot out, quick as lightning, and clamped fast around the apprentice’s wrist.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Rooster, second half.

{d} Chang’an; Chang’an County; Yining Ward.

The confession room was cramped and dark; one person left too long in it would feel the breath choke off—all the more so now, with two crammed inside.

Tanqi and Zhang Xiaojing were trapped in the dark, all but face to face, with no room to shift, each able to feel the other’s breath. Holding this awkward posture, Zhang Xiaojing called out a few more times; outside there was not the least stir. Had that deacon Yisi truly just walked off and left them?

Never mind Tanqi—even Zhang Xiaojing had not expected this elegantly-spoken Nestorian monk to turn on them so abruptly. He had read countless men in his time, yet he had utterly failed to see through this monk called Yisi. That face, that bearing, were deceptive in the extreme.

Zhang Xiaojing pounded the little door hard with his fist; it did not budge a hair. The wooden hut looked flimsy, but it was built of cypress—dense, tight-grained timber that no human strength could shake.

“Mistress Tanqi—forgive me.”

Zhang Xiaojing lifted his upper body and pressed close before Tanqi’s face—he meant only to free up room at his waist so he could draw the barrier-knife. Tanqi understood his intent, yet her heart still hammered wildly. Never had she been so close to a man; feeling that heavy, coarse breathing bore straight into her nostrils, she was too frightened to move a muscle.

Slowly Zhang Xiaojing worked the blade free, carefully set its point to the crack of the door, and slid it downward. The thin edge could just reach the chain outside. But the little room was too narrow to bring any force to bear, let alone to hack the chain through. The only way was to grind at the door’s wooden pivot with the point—and that would take far too long.

The whole business struck Tanqi as absurd. The Que-le Huo-duo was somewhere unknown, Chang’an teetered like a stack of eggs—and here they were, shut up by some senseless Nestorian deacon, for some senseless reason, in this senseless hole of a place.

She looked at Zhang Xiaojing—surely this fellow would think of a way out soon enough! Just as at the Right Xiao Guard, he always had some scheme. His single eye strained wide in the dim light, his lips pressed tight, like a fierce beast caged in a box. This time, though, even he seemed at his wits’ end.

Tanqi caught herself with a start: since when had she come to lean on him as her prop? That lecher had said that in borrowing her along this time, it was to draw upon her own wits. If she did nothing, only waited for him to decide, would that not shame her young master! At the thought she too strained to turn her neck, to see whether any sliver of a chance offered.

The two moved at once, and before either was aware of it their faces knocked together. That rough face scraped Tanqi’s cheek to a stinging soreness. She flushed in a rush from cheek to neck—and could not, for all that, draw away.

Just then footsteps sounded outside, and both of them froze.

Yisi’s voice rang out smugly from beyond the door: “The two of you must be cursing me in your hearts—calling me honey-mouthed and dagger-hearted, no doubt… Oh, forgive me, forgive me; I forgot that ‘honey-mouthed and dagger-hearted’ is a forbidden phrase. Let us say ‘a fair tongue and a fine face’ instead—for the ‘fine face,’ at least, I can fairly claim, heh heh.”

The fellow had come back at some point—or perhaps had never left at all. Tanqi had known no small number of men, but one who dwelt with such relish on his own good looks was a first.

“You passed yourselves off as man and wife and forced your way into our humble temple—what exactly did you mean to do?” Yisi asked. His tone was less angry than excited.

Tanqi was on the point of some sharp retort, but Zhang Xiaojing checked her, unhooked the waist-tally from his person, and rapped it against the door-plank, his voice urgent: “I am Zhang Xiaojing, Commander of the Jing’an Bureau, pursuing a great case that bears on the safety of Chang’an itself. You must release us at once. This is the Jing’an Bureau’s waist-tally—you may verify it with the authorities.”

“The Jing’an Bureau? Never heard of it. This wouldn’t be idle talk, would it?” Yisi glanced at the tally through the little window. “Permit me to call at the Bureau of Sacrifices tomorrow; the matter will surely be made plain then.”

“By then it will be too late! Let us out now!” Zhang Xiaojing heaved his body against the wall so hard that the whole wooden hut shuddered.

Yisi extended his slender, tapering fingers and wagged them with a tut-tut. “I have the unworthy honor of serving as the Nestorian deacon, charged with the temple’s defense. Since villains have entered our temple under false colors, were I not to look into it thoroughly, should I not become one of those who hold office idly and eat their bread for nothing?”

He spoke in a bookish, mincing way that, just now, grated on Tanqi’s and Zhang Xiaojing’s ears more than words could say.

Zhang Xiaojing said in a low, hard voice: “Listen to me. Hidden in this Persian Temple right now is an extremely dangerous man, one who holds hundreds of thousands of lives in the balance. If you delay the court’s great business, you will answer for every consequence!”

Hundreds of thousands of lives? Extremely dangerous? The two phrases made Yisi’s eyes light up. “First: we are called the Daqin Temple, not the Persian Temple. Second: if there truly is such a dangerous man, it is for this temple’s deacon to go and deal with him—this eminent monk you are looking for, is he the one?”

“Yes. He is the Türk noble the Right Shad, come to Chang’an within these past three months. The Jing’an Bureau believes he has been passing as a Nestorian monk, hidden here in this Persian Temple.” Zhang Xiaojing spoke very fast; he could not let this pedantic, phrase-flaunting Persian set the rhythm of the talk.

“I did say it is the Daqin Temple… mm.” Yisi seemed moved by these words; his eyes rolled, and a flicker of an excited smile crossed his handsome face. “You two shall repent in here a while; permit me to go and look into it, to see whether what you say is false or true.”

Now Zhang Xiaojing was truly alarmed, and shouted at the top of his voice: “The powers behind this Türk are very strong; you must not go probing at him rashly. Open the door at once and leave him to a hand skilled at catching thieves.”

“Oh? You mean the two skilled hands I have shut up in the confession room?” Yisi laughed aloud and tapped his own eyes with two forefingers. “These eyes of mine, Yisi’s eyes, have been washed by the autumn waters; whatever you can see through, I can see through the deeper.” And then, heedless of Zhang Xiaojing’s shouting behind him, he turned and left.

Yisi strode down the corridor, his expression as calm as ever; but one corner of his white robe flew up high, betraying the eagerness within its wearer.

The Nestorian temple prized austerity and humble self-effacement; from one year’s end to the next there were scarcely a handful of quarrels. Yisi prided himself on having read the classics of the Middle Kingdom deeply and on possessing rare arts, yet he had never had a chance to show them off, which he counted a lasting regret. Having at last, with such difficulty, seized an opportunity, he would by no means let it slip lightly.

If what that man said was no lie, this would be a superb opportunity. Yisi had just come to the main hall; seeing the cross raised on high, he pressed his palms together in devout prayer: “My Lord above. If merit is to be won this time, the court will surely look on us with favor, and we may set right the true name of our Nestorian faith.”

His prayer finished, he made straight for a cluster of dwellings beside the main hall. There was a patch of vegetable beds there, planted with melons, fruit, and greens. The Nestorian monks, high rank and low alike, all held that a man should till with his own hands, and so their quarters too were built beside the vegetable plots—a whole row of flat-roofed little two-story stone houses.

As deacon, Yisi knew the comings and goings of the temple’s people in fine detail. A month before, a monk had indeed arrived here, by name Puzhe, a Sogdian of mixed foreign blood; the ordination certificate he carried came from the Nestorian temple of Kang, and his standing was that of an elder. After Elder Puzhe reached the Nestorian temple of Yining Ward, he had gone about very quietly, keeping mostly to himself and mixing little with others, save that he went out rather more often than most. The temple took the elder simply for a man zealous in spreading the faith, and left him alone.

By Zhang Xiaojing’s description, this Elder Puzhe was the only man who fit.

He was past sixty, and the temple had set aside for him a private room tucked in a corner of the second floor. Yisi called over a Nestorian monk who kept the dwellings, and together they climbed the steps. Reaching the door, he knocked and called out, “Elder Puzhe,” but no one answered. He gave a push; the door had been left on the latch, and with a creak it swung open.

The furnishings of the little front room were no different from any other cleric’s. Beneath the window stood a gilt cross; on each side sat an arch-lidded square kerchief-chest; and on the floor was spread a thick camel-hair felt rug.

At a glance Yisi noticed a makara ewer overturned in the very middle of the rug, blood-red grape wine running from its mouth and soaking a great patch of the felt. At once he grew wary; first he lifted the hem of his robe and tucked it into his belt, then slowed his step and made toward the sleeping-chamber.

The moment Yisi stepped in, the first thing to meet his eyes was Elder Puzhe’s two eyes staring wide, his face fixed in a nameless horror. His head lay on the threshold, his body sprawled face-up on the floor, a keen blade still buried in his chest amid a mess of blood and flesh. The elder’s arm still trembled faintly—whether a last breath yet clung to him, or some rancor lingered unspent after death, there was no telling.

Yisi started violently. This… was this not an extremely dangerous villain? How came he instead to be the one killed?

The Nestorian monk behind him came up, and at the sight of this bloody scene let out a cry of “Mother!” and slumped down onto the floor. Yisi’s eyes darted about; he did not hurry to bend and examine the body, nor to press into the room, but swept his gaze swiftly once around the chamber.

After a few finger-snaps of this silence, he suddenly snatched up a bronze candlestick at his hand and hurled it hard at the corner of the room.

In that corner stood a small two-leaf bamboo screen, kept there to hide the chamber-pot. Being light and flimsy, at the blow of the heavy bronze candlestick it clattered down at once—and out from behind it sprang a masked man.

“Did you think so paltry a trick could escape the eyes of Yisi?” he barked, half in excitement and half to steel his own nerve.

The window here faced due north, and, being on the second floor, looked straight out on the bright lantern-glow of the imperial way. Yisi had noticed it a moment before: as the lamplight fell into the corner, there should have been a slit of light between the shadows of the two screen-leaves, yet for one instant the two shadows had merged into one—which meant someone was hiding behind the screen.

No doubt this killer, having done the murder, had not yet had time to get away when he heard the knock at the door, and could only hide for the moment behind the screen—never expecting Yisi to call him straight out.

Exposed now, the masked man wasted no words but caught up his blade and lunged at Yisi. Yisi backed away in some alarm, but it was already too late. One thought flashed through his mind: he should have feigned that nothing was amiss just now, withdrawn, and reported it to the authorities.

But regret came too late; the masked man’s blade bore down fast and fierce. Casting dignity aside, Yisi flung himself flat on the floor and barely dodged the stroke. Before the man could draw the blade back for another thrust, he snatched up a foot-warming brazier from beside the couch and flung its contents full in the man’s face.

This foot-warmer was shaped like an iron scoop, filled with burning charcoal for warmth in the night. As Yisi thrust it outward, some fine powdery ash—it seemed something had once been burned in it—was flung out, and the tiny room filled at once with a haze of smoke. Seizing the chance, Yisi crawled a few paces clear of the masked man’s reach, rose, and gripped the brazier in his hand.

Suddenly he heard a scream—it came from the Nestorian monk who kept the dwellings and had followed him in. There was no need to ask: the masked man, having missed his blow at Yisi, had simply killed the monk behind him instead.

Yisi was enraged. These men had broken into the Nestorian temple and killed two of its monks one after another—it was the gravest insult to a deacon that could be. He flung the last of the ash from the brazier out with all his might, then leapt up onto the couch.

A monk of an elder’s rank would always have a staff hanging by his couch, its wood cut from the fig tree of Sham—the land where the Lord of the faith arose—carried so that one should not forget one’s roots. The masked man’s weapon was keen, but Yisi knew the room’s furnishings better.

Yisi took the staff down from the wall, and his heart steadied a little. He did not need to win; he had only to hold out a while longer, and the temple’s guardian monks would come. Relying on the staff’s advantage of length, he pinned the masked man into a corner of the room.

The masked man soon saw that his opponent was playing for time, and so, without tangling with him further, he spun about and leapt clean out of the window.

Yisi darted to the sill and looked down at the ground, but saw no trace of the man. Lifting his head, he found that the masked man had used the jut of a balcony to swing himself up onto the roof.

Did he really take us Nestorian monks all for soft, bookish weaklings?

Yisi gave a cold laugh, gripped the staff in his teeth, hauled himself up backhanded over the upper edge of the window-frame, swung his body, and flipped swiftly up onto the roof as well.

The Nestorian temple’s roofs were flat and broad, well suited to running. The two of them raced on, pursuer and pursued, bounding from one roof to the next without a moment’s pause underfoot. Nimble as the masked man was, Yisi gave not an inch, and in agility he had even the slight advantage.

Yisi had grown up from childhood in the deserts of the Western Regions, and his favorite pastime had been to drift and swing among the cave-grottoes and sand-hollows; over the years he had trained up a whole art of light-bodied climbing and vaulting, so that any high or perilous place he crossed as though walking on level ground—he called it cave-running.

The assassin, fleeing this way, had scratched him right where he itched.

Seeing Yisi close on him, the masked man once more sprang across the gap between two roofs, then whipped around and stabbed his blade into empty air. Yisi, behind him, had already leapt high and was hurtling straight onto the edge of the blade. Unable to swerve in mid-air, in his extremity he flicked the front skirt of his white robe forward; the instant the blade pierced the cloth, he gave a violent tug that dragged the point aside by a hair, so that it grazed just past his shoulder, opening a streak of blood.

Using that momentum, Yisi drove head-first into the masked man’s chest and knocked him flat. The two rolled over and over on the roof, grappling into a single knot. Yisi let the staff drop from his teeth into his grip and, hammering at the man’s head, snarled: “I am, when all is said, of the blood of a Persian prince—how dare you show off your tricks here!”

As he hammered away, a crossbow bolt suddenly came flying through the air and pinned itself into the head of Yisi’s wooden staff. Had it swerved but a hair more, the bolt would surely have gone into his throat. In the instant he was stunned, the masked man shoved him off and leapt down the two stories to the ground.

Yisi had not reckoned that this assassin had a confederate. He ran a few steps to the roof’s edge and saw, far off, a man leveling a crossbow straight at him. He ducked his head at once, and another bolt skimmed past over his scalp.

In that moment the masked man had picked himself up from the ground and, limping, ran to join the crossbowman. The crossbowman flung his weapon aside, and the two vaulted the eight-sided stone sutra-pillar and made straight for the Nestorian temple’s gate.

To give chase now was already too late. Yisi could only shout at the top of his voice, hoping the monks at the gate would hear. But those Nestorian monks were busy handing out gifts to the sightseers, and with all the din about them, how should they dream that two assassins were running up at their backs?

Yet the monks were not the only ones at the gate.

That party of Lüben Guardsmen, obeying Zhang Xiaojing’s order, had long been posted at the gate; seeing the murderous air of these two, they drew their blades and spread into a fan to close on them.

The two killers reacted with lightning speed: each pulled a fistful of copper coins from his breast and flung them up into the air with a whoosh, so that they came raining down like the heavenly maiden scattering blossoms. The sightseers all around began shouting: “Flower-scattering coins! Flower-scattering coins!”

Flower-scattering coins were a custom of Chang’an: at the lantern-viewing one flung copper cash for anyone to snatch up, and the more one scattered, the thicker the blessings earned. But this vulgar practice bred trouble again and again, and the authorities had banned it. The sightseers, hearing that someone was actually scattering coins in the open, were all delighted; one told ten and ten told a hundred, and in a moment a countless press of people came surging toward the spot, men and women, old and young, scrambling and grabbing in a mob, so that the whole scene fell at once into chaos.

By the time the coins were nearly all gathered up, the two killers had long since vanished without a trace, leaving ten-odd Lüben soldiers standing where they were, looking this way and that. By now Yisi had flipped down from the roof and hurried to the gate. Taking in the scene, he asked at once: “Have you a commander named Zhang Xiaojing? A weathered, cracked face and a blind eye?”

The soldiers looked at him blankly and said nothing.

“Er—I mean, a face all covered in wrinkles, and blind in one eye.”

“Oh—that’s him, all right, Commander Zhang.” Only now did the soldiers understand.

Yisi rubbed his head, an awkward look crossing his handsome face. Glib as he was, he did not know how to explain to this officer that Commander Zhang had just been locked up by him.

Guangde Ward; the Jing’an Bureau.

The first to be attacked was a petty clerk carrying documents. He was walking toward the great watchtower with a dispatch in his hands when he suddenly saw ten-odd black shapes rushing at him. His eyes had scarcely widened before a short-spined knife ran him through the throat.

Next to be struck were two guards. They were charged with watching the passage joining the rear garden to the great hall in front, and were chatting idly, off and on, when all at once both of them stiffened and fell to the ground, a crossbow bolt lodged in each one’s neck.

The black shape at the head came to this spot and halted for a moment. He was the man who had climbed the great watchtower earlier, and the leader of this band. Bending down, he drew the bolts from the two guards’ bodies, loaded them back into his crossbow, and then made a sign that all was safe.

Five black shapes at once moved forward, seizing several points on the heights and the flanks and training their crossbows on the road leading to the rear garden. Then several others turned back to the gap in the water-channel and dragged over a number of heavy sackcloth bags. Opening them, each man took out a crude squirt-pump and a few small clay jars.

This kind of squirt-pump was a round bamboo cylinder, with a hole at the front and a water-rod at the back; one end of the rod was wrapped in packed cotton wadding and pushed inside the tube. Thus with a single pull one could draw water in through the hole, and with a single push spray it out again. The thing had first been made for fighting fires, but it broke very easily and the water it sent out was hardly better than nothing, so it had never come into wide use.

For a single use, though, it was handy enough.

Methodically they charged the squirt-pumps from the clay jars. The leader stood where he was, watching the distant eaves-corners of the Jing’an Bureau’s great hall, filled with the excitement that comes before a killing. Abruptly he raised a hand, pulled off his mask, tossed a roll of mint leaves into his mouth, and began to chew, his face empty of expression.

Long Bo’s great hooked nose looked all the more sinister under the night sky.

During this time, two or three more petty clerks of the Jing’an Bureau came wandering up on their way to the privy, and every one of them, without exception, was killed in an instant, the bodies all flung into the ditch alongside.

When all of them had charged their squirt-pumps and slung them on, Long Bo gave his orders in Sogdian: “Split into three parties. One for the main hall, one each for the left and right side-halls; and let the party for the left side-hall see to the rear hall as well. Once the assault begins: crossbows for the guards, blades for the clerks, squirt-pumps for the fittings—make sure of controlling the ground in the first instant.”

He stressed again: “All of this must be done within a single quarter-hour.”

The men all nodded together. Long Bo spat the chewed mint onto the ground and settled his hood back into place: “Come—let us go and bring the lords of the Jing’an Bureau their lanterns.”

The little door of the confession room was flung open with a clang, and light long missed came back into their eyes. Tanqi and Zhang Xiaojing both squinted for a moment, unused to it.

Yisi, for his part, made no attempt to hide it, but came forward of his own accord to apologize, reeling off a great string of crabbed, tongue-twisting phrases—“forever mindful of my fault,” “bitterly reproaching myself”—until he had all but recited through the penitential edicts of former dynasties.

Tanqi cut him off without ceremony and asked what exactly had happened. Knowing himself in the wrong, Yisi recounted the whole affair; Zhang Xiaojing’s face frosted over as he listened, but he had no time to reckon with the deacon and said only, “Take me to see it, now.”

The gravely wounded Elder Puzhe had already been carried into a quiet prayer-room, where the temple’s physician was fighting to save him. The knife had gone into his chest; the wound was deep, and he had long since fallen unconscious.

Zhang Xiaojing drew near and studied him closely: a long, narrow horse-face all cracked and chapped, with a broad nose and slit eyes, in no way a Middle-Kingdom cast of features—though whether it was a Türk face one could not be sure either.

This was an awkward business. Whether Elder Puzhe was in truth the Right Shad could not, for the present, be proved—and the Jing’an Bureau had to be certain to the last degree before it could set about its next step.

His sleeping-quarters had already been searched once over; apart from that one ordination certificate, there was nothing bearing on his identity. And even the certificate was worth little, for the Türks could easily forge one—could even seize a real Elder Puzhe, kill the man, and simply keep his papers.

Zhang Xiaojing thought a moment, then bent to pull at Elder Puzhe’s robe. Yisi said hastily: “To handle a holy body so roughly—is that quite proper?” Tanqi said coldly: “If he is the Türk Right Shad, what is there to say of holy body or no?” The spell shut up had left her with a bellyful of resentment, and she loathed this clever-clever fool of a deacon to the marrow.

Zhang Xiaojing waved the physician aside and tore the robe open, baring an aged body. On the lower right of the belly ran a long, ghastly scar, coiled along the flank like a snake, the skin curled up on either side. Zhang Xiaojing reached out and felt it once, then looked up and said that this was the scar of a modao.

The modao had a haft four chi long and a blade three chi long, the elite Tang army’s weapon reserved for mounted combat. By the length and place of the scar, the man must have taken half a stroke of a modao across the body while on horseback, and yet not died—a rare piece of luck to be alive.

Zhang Xiaojing pulled the lower garment further open: on the inner thighs were thick calluses of wear, surely the marks of long years in the saddle; and on the flanks, outside each hip, rose two curved arcs of callus. When a man goes about always in armor, the swinging lower edge of the skirt-plates rubs the skin and wears just such marks—and armor of a very high rank at that.

Long years on horseback, long years in armor, and wounded besides by a Tang army’s modao—the true identity of this unworldly, quarrel-shunning Elder Puzhe lay plain to see.

“Now I know why the Türk Wolf Guards wanted to seize Wang Zhongsi’s daughter—it was the Right Shad noble’s private motive after all.” Zhang Xiaojing rose and clapped the dust from his hands.

The steppe had an old tradition of blood-vengeance: a wound dealt by an enemy could be soothed only by the living blood of that enemy’s offspring. The Right Shad noble had likely clashed with Wang Zhongsi in years past and taken a grievous wound whose hidden ache had never left him. Coming to Chang’an this time, besides presiding over the Que-le Huo-duo, he had meant also to seize Wang Zhongsi’s daughter along the way, to cure himself.

Then again, had he not harbored this private motive, the Jing’an Bureau might truly never have traced the Wolf Guards at all.

Tanqi asked, puzzled: “But then—who would come to kill the Right Shad?”

Zhang Xiaojing said: “The ones using the Türk Wolf Guards, of course. With the rock-oil in hand, the Right Shad had no further use to them. To keep us from following the vine to the melon, they had to cut every last thread of connection—this man schemed and plotted to sell out his own tribe, thinking to buy the wealth and honor of his latter years; heh, little did he guess it would be the star of death that came knocking.”

As he said this his worry deepened. This mysterious organization went about its work with a ruthless, decisive hand; besides the Right Shad, its other potential leads were likely being cut off one by one, so that the Bureau’s search would grow ever harder. And that they had suddenly begun to sweep away their traces meant some great event was at hand—of which the Jing’an Bureau still knew nothing at all.

The Right Shad lay unconscious, and nothing could be got out of him; nor was there any worthwhile clue in his room. Zhang Xiaojing’s mind turned furiously, yet he could think of no way to break the deadlock quickly. A groundless weariness welled up in him, and he felt all at once something close to despair.

By rights he was not a man to give in so easily. Perhaps he really was too tired; perhaps it was the pressure long piled up. Zhang Xiaojing leaned his back against the wall of the quiet prayer-room, closed his single eye, and could not even be bothered to brush off the dust.

At that very moment the Right Shad on the couch suddenly coughed loudly, as though about to wake, spittle flecked with blood, his whole body convulsing violently. The physician threw himself over him to pin his limbs, sweat streaming down his face: “He must be taken to an infirmary, or it will be too late!”

Dang—dang—dang—

The great bell above the main hall of the Persian Temple suddenly began to toll. The Nestorian monks stopped in their tracks, not knowing what had happened. Two men, one before and one behind, carried a makeshift wooden stretcher out from the dwellings, a camel-hair blanket laid over it, and made their way toward the temple gate.

The monks all around pointed and murmured; word was that an eminent monk had been stabbed and was being taken to an infirmary. And so they prayed devoutly, each of them, for this brother.

It was as well that today was the Lantern Festival’s lantern-market, when the infirmaries of every ward stood ready and did not close all night. Outside the gate an oil-canopied ox-cart had just come up. Such a cart, drawn by an ox, was slow and steady, with a rolled-mat awning above and curtains hanging down at either side—just the thing for carrying a gravely wounded patient.

The two men carefully lifted the elder into the carriage from the rear. An infirmary apprentice was already waiting inside; he helped lay the patient flat and fed him a ginseng life-prolonging pellet. The carriage being narrow, the two men could not stay aboard, so the apprentice told them to go on ahead and wait at the infirmary, then hung a blue-and-white mourning bell outside the cart and called to the carter to set off.

As the ox-cart moved off, the mourning bell swung and jangled. It was filled with lead, and its sound was quite unlike an ordinary bell’s. Hearing it, the sightseers around knew that someone was being rushed to a physician, and drew aside to open a path, that they might not be touched by the ill luck.

The ox-cart pulled slowly away and, amid the ringing of the bell, passed through the bustling streets and crowds toward the infirmary. When it had gone about half a li and was clear of the Persian Temple’s sight, it suddenly turned off the thronged thoroughfare into a narrow alley. No lanterns had been hung here, and so it was pitch dark.

The carter reined the ox-cart to a halt and coughed once. Inside the carriage, the infirmary apprentice drew a dagger from his waist and stabbed down at the patient on the stretcher. From beneath the stretcher’s blanket a great hand shot out, quick as lightning, and clamped fast around the apprentice’s wrist.

The blanket was thrown back, and a fierce-faced, one-eyed man sat up from the stretcher, grinning wide: “A healer is meant to have a parent’s heart—why so cruel a hand?”

The infirmary apprentice, knowing himself trapped, went pale and stabbed backhand in haste. The dagger struck the other’s body—but rang with a clang. Zhang Xiaojing, who had long since put on chain mail, produced a small blackened iron hammer and struck at the man’s leg-bone. In the cramped carriage that hammer was a deadly weapon indeed: there was no dodging it and no blocking it, and a single blow shattered his knee.

The apprentice let out a shriek and toppled backward, his jaw twitching all at once. Seeing it, Zhang Xiaojing at once brought the hammer down again on his temple and knocked him senseless. Then, pinching the apprentice’s lower jaw with his right hand, he tipped a jet-black poison pellet out of his mouth.

The carter, hearing the commotion in the carriage, sensed something was wrong and turned to look—when from the end of the alley two arrows came whistling and pinned him through one hand and one foot, so that he pitched straight down off the cart.

The sniping archer standing at the alley-mouth lowered his great bow, and the Lüben Guardsmen beside him rushed forward and surrounded the ox-cart; but alas, once the carter hit the ground, knowing there was no escape, he had swallowed his own poison pellet and died with his face gone black.

Beside the archer, Tanqi let out a long breath.

She had questioned Yisi closely just before and learned that when the assassins left, Elder Puzhe had not yet breathed his last. She had judged that they would surely come back to make sure he was dead—and so Zhang Xiaojing had turned their own plan against them and laid this trap.

Only one had been taken alive, but it was better than sitting helpless with folded hands.

Zhang Xiaojing helped the senseless infirmary apprentice down from the cart and handed him to the soldiers beside him. He took off the chain mail and felt at his lower ribs; the stab just now, though it had not reached the bone, had still raised a livid bruise. He rubbed it with a wry smile—this must be the lightest wound he had taken all day.

The Lüben Guards raised several large lanterns at the alley-mouth, lighting up half the view. Leaning against the ox-cart, one hand pressed to his wound, Zhang Xiaojing looked toward the lights. Under the candle-glow the figures stood scattered, and of them all the graceful shape standing at the alley-mouth was the most striking.

It was thanks to Tanqi’s judgment this time that they had taken a captive alive—she was well worthy of one trained up by Li Bi.

This girl was something. For the first time, the murky pupil of Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye held Tanqi’s reflection a little more deeply.

Tanqi had no idea what Zhang Xiaojing, there in the shadows, was thinking; she was busy dealing with an irritating fellow.

Yisi had hurried over from the temple, and seeing the trap sprung to good effect he could not help letting out a breath of relief. Had the two assassins truly gotten away, the Persian Temple—no, the Daqin Temple—would not only have lost face but might well have been charged with ‘colluding with bandits.’ The Nestorian faith had spread through the Middle Kingdom only with difficulty, and could ill bear fresh setbacks.

Tanqi glared at Yisi: “Did you not boast of your sharp eyes? Come and tell us—are these the two assassins who fought you?” Yisi was just opening his mouth when Tanqi snapped: “You may say only yes or no.”

Yisi had to swallow a great heap of words; he went over and examined them, and soon made out that the carter was the assassin who had killed the Right Shad, and the ‘apprentice’ the one who had backed him up outside. He raised his head: “Er—yes…”

“You are certain?” Tanqi did not much trust this fellow.

“These two eyes of mine see the tip of an autumn hair; to me it is as plain as watching a fire.” Yisi proudly held up two fingers and gestured before his pair of green eyes. The two phrases came, one from the Mencius and one from the Book of Documents—elegant diction, and aptly alluded.

A pity that Tanqi only answered “Oh,” so that all his pains went for nothing.

Now the assassins’ identity was confirmed, and one captive had been kept alive as well. Tanqi said to the soldiers beside her: “Report back to the Jing’an Bureau—have them make ready to interrogate him.”

The signaler raised the special purple lantern and sent a message to the Yining Ward watchtower. The lantern rose and fell several times; then the signaler’s brow knit slightly—something was not right. Far off, the purple light on the Yining Ward watchtower flickered, as though relaying some very long message.

At last the purple light went out. Only then did the signaler turn back and say to Tanqi, in a tone of astonishment:

“The watchtower reports: signals from the great watchtower have been cut off. There is no reaching the Jing’an Bureau.”

***

Just now the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau, like the world outside, was ablaze with light and thronged with coming and going. But here the candles were plain candles and the people were busy people, a far cry from the leisured, idle, richly self-indulgent air of the lantern-viewing outside.

Li Bi sat at his writing-desk; he took up a scroll of the Dengzhen yinjue and read a few lines, but his mind was restless and unsettled, and those subtle, profound words would not go in. So he simply picked up his fly-whisk and slowly stroked the fine, smooth horsehair strands with his fingertips.

Zhang Xiaojing and the others had gone to Yining Ward and were slow to report back. From the watchtowers everywhere, too, for a little while now, no news at all had come in. He had sent a runner off with a dispatch to press them, and had had no answer yet. Even Xu Bin had vanished no one knew where.

Li Bi disliked this feeling intensely; it made him feel the whole situation was slipping out of his control.

The matter of the Türk Wolf Guards, the matter of the Que-le Huo-duo, the matter of the traitor within the Jing’an Bureau, the matter of Zhang Xiaojing’s deceptions, the matter of the Right Minister and the heir apparent—not one of them had settled like dust and been sealed under a stamp. Countless threads crossed and tangled into an intricate net that drew tight across Li Bi’s chest.

The bronze water-clock in the corner of the hall struck off another quarter-hour, and still no word came back from Yining Ward. Li Bi decided to send a runner once more to hurry them, in a sterner tone this time. Having given the order, he glanced again at the water-clock—and found that Cui Qi was no longer standing where he had been.

What was going on? Li Bi suddenly felt that something was not quite right.

From beyond the hall came a rush of hurried footsteps; first a shout of rebuke rang out, then it turned to a cry of alarm, and the cry of alarm turned at once into a scream. The fingers stroking the whisk went taut in an instant; a sharp light burst from Li Bi’s eyes as he looked toward the entrance of the great hall.

Dozens of black-clad, masked men vaulted savagely over the hall’s threshold; ten-odd crossbows fired at once, felling with deadly accuracy the ten-odd armored guards and buliang men within. Then half of them reloaded while the other half drew blades and hacked at the nearest clerks. Those soft, bookish clerks, caught wholly off guard, had no strength to resist, and in a moment blood sprayed everywhere.

These cutthroats were like a violent gale blowing into the hall.

The blow fell far too fast; the rest of those in the great hall made no response at all, only stared dumbly as it all unfolded. A single buliang man who had dodged the first wave drew an iron ruler-baton and charged fiercely back at them. With a wet thud a crossbow bolt drove into his eye-socket, the soft eyeball bursting on the instant, blood and white matter spattering all over a young menial beside him. The menial clawed frantically at his clothes, shrieking wildly—and then the shriek stopped short, a gleaming black bolt lodged in his throat too.

Long Bo stepped over the threshold of the hall, chewing his mint leaves, and with an easy air tossed his two spent crossbows aside.

Only now did the people of the Jing’an Bureau wake as from a dream. Screams rose suddenly on every side; people bent to hide or ran for the doors, colliding with one another among the desks, and the scene fell into utter chaos. But every door of the hall was already held, and whoever ran for the outside was either driven back by a blade or shot dead by a crossbow.

“Hush and get down, and you live!” Long Bo’s shrill voice rang through the great hall. There was a heavy irony in the words, for this was the very phrase the Lüben Guards used in carrying out their duties—now turned upon the Jing’an Bureau itself.

Most of those here were civil clerks, with no power to resist brute force. At Long Bo’s shout the terrified men crouched down one by one, not daring to draw a loud breath, until in the whole hall only one person still kept to his feet.

With the situation subdued, Long Bo came step by step from the doorway into the middle of the hall, looking about him with keen interest as he went. So this was the fabled Jing’an Bureau—the heart and hinge of Chang’an’s defenses, able to command every garrison force in the city save the Palace Armies. A pity that, like a heart, it was in itself only a soft, frail lump of flesh: let a sword pierce into the chest cavity, and it could not withstand a single blow.

Long Bo walked past row upon row of wooden desks, his oxhide boots crushing without mercy the scrolls fallen to the floor, which snapped with the dry, grating sound of splitting bamboo. He paused a moment before the great sand-table, and out of curiosity even broke off a length of ward-wall and brought it up to his eye to study, clucking in praise: “Exquisite. If the Türks could see this, I daresay they would be sick with envy.”

An old clerk looked up and sighed with regret. Long Bo glanced at him: “Grieved, are you? This is only a sand-table. If the whole of Chang’an came to look like this, would you not feel it far worse?” He sighed regretfully, and a thin blade slid out into his hand and swept across the old clerk’s neck. The old man slumped forward onto the sand-table, and the streets of Chang’an were dyed a sheet of blood-red.

A fresh wave of terror ran through the crowd, until the masked men shouted them into silence. Long Bo declared aloud: “Let it be known to you all—we are the Pifu, and we have come today to give this great tree of the Jing’an Bureau a shake.”

People looked at one another blankly; none had ever heard of such an organization.

Long Bo strolled around behind the sand-table, where a row of screens enclosed a small half-separate space, its floor raised on planks so that one could look down over the whole hall. On it stood a young man in a green robe, a fly-whisk in his hand, his eyes fixed on Long Bo, his expression perfectly calm.

“Deputy Director Li—I have long admired you.” Long Bo made a show of bowing with clasped hands and mounted the platform step by step.

“Who are you? What do you want?” Li Bi did not deign to trade words with him at all; it was pointless.

“The Pifu—did I not just tell you?”

“I am asking your real name.”

“A pity—but it is not you who gives the orders now.” Long Bo snatched the fly-whisk from Li Bi’s hand and snapped it in two, his hooked nose almost jabbing into Li Bi’s cheek.

The clerks below the platform let out low cries of alarm, fearing for their chief. But Li Bi showed not the least sign of dread, his sword-straight brows knit to the utmost.

“Messages pass in and out of the Jing’an Bureau every moment of every hour. How long do you imagine you can keep this hidden?”

Li Bi was not bluffing; he spoke the truth. The Jing’an Bureau was very tightly linked to the world outside, and in less than a quarter-hour the garrison beyond would sense that something was wrong. The Jingzhao Prefecture was right next door, and the main force of the Lüben Guards was quartered in Jiahui Ward not far to the south; let a single warning go out, and reinforcements would come streaming in without end. Elite though these few men were, they could never hold out against that.

Even the taking of hostages was out of the question. The Tang code laid it down expressly: those who seize hostages are to be struck down together with the hostages—no allowance whatever being made for the hostages’ lives.

“The Deputy Director need not trouble himself. We Pifu do our work in far less time than that.”

Long Bo raised a hand, and his men took the squirt-pumps from their backs and began spraying all about. What came out of the pumps was not water but a viscous, ink-like liquid with an acrid reek. As they sprayed they made no distinction at all between people and things, but drenched everything alike. The clerks, sprayed black from head to foot, could only tremble. The sand-table got especial attention: nearly the whole of Chang’an was buried under the black ooze.

“Yanzhou rock-oil.” Li Bi forced the words out through his teeth, the corners of his eyes almost splitting.

“The scraps and leavings from refining—I hope the Deputy Director will not think them beneath him.” Long Bo said it with a smile, drawing a fire-steel from his belt and tossing it up and down in his hand. Everyone in the hall watched the thing in fear and trembling, their hearts leaping high and low with it.

A masked man hurried into the hall and raised his right hand, signaling that the right side-hall had been subdued.

Long Bo glanced at the water-clock in the corner of the hall, well satisfied with the pace. Now only word from the left side-hall was wanting.

***

The masked men’s assault on the left side-hall had gone very smoothly; a great mass of files was kept there, with almost no guard on it. They left not one man alive, and ten-odd clerks’ corpses lay sprawled every which way on the floor.

The party’s leader made a few hand-signs and set his men to spraying with the squirt-pumps, then sent the deputy squad-leader to lead a party toward the rear hall. One task remained to them: the rear-hall prison had not yet been cleared.

The deputy squad-leader took five men and went along the corridor beside the left side-hall toward the rear hall.

From the left side-hall to the rear hall one had to pass through a small moon-gate, behind which lay a little garden scene; then, turning along a stretch of ornamental wall, one reached the rear-hall prison—there was no branching path.

The assault so far had gone all too smoothly; the illustrious Jing’an Bureau had shown next to no power to strike back. Every one of the men was at his ease—this rear hall held only a few cells, and clearing it out would not take more than a few finger-snaps.

They passed through the moon-gate, and the space before them suddenly opened out. The former owner had set in the middle of this little courtyard a jagged artificial mountain, carved with the name ‘Penglai,’ complete with tiny pavilions, thatched huts, plank walkways, and green pines and cypresses. In a winding fold of its belly was a grotto, over which a plaque read ‘the Immortals’ Cave’—a whole mountain’s contours caught within a few square feet, and in the darkness it made a scene all its own.

The deputy squad-leader had no taste for admiring it; the party formed a long single file and passed one by one along the side of the rockery.

Just as the last man at the tail of the file was passing the rockery, a barrier-knife suddenly thrust out from the Immortals’ Cave within it and stabbed one man in the chest. The man cried out and fell to the ground. The other five spun about at once and, without a word, raised their crossbows and fired, riddling the rockery in an instant until it bristled like a hedgehog.

Their volley spent, they came to look, and found that the Immortals’ Cave ran clear through at both ends: the attacker had long since slipped out the other side and fallen back to the rear hall.

This was an unlooked-for turn indeed. The deputy squad-leader pressed his palm down in vexation, ordering that they go forward cautiously from here on.

So the remaining four fell into a triangular formation—one in front, three behind—arms crooked with crossbows leveled, legs bent, moving warily along the foot of the ornamental wall toward the rear hall.

At the end of this stretch of wall was a great corner; around it ran a long straight passage, at the end of which lay the prison. Cui Qi and Yao Runeng stood now with their backs pressed to the passage wall, cold sweat streaming, terror in both their eyes.

A moment before, Cui Qi had hidden himself in the Immortals’ Cave, meaning to listen out what was happening beyond, and had chanced upon those five men passing by. He had probed at their strength—and never expected the counterstroke to be so decisive and sharp; half a beat slower, and he would have been shot into a sieve.

These men reacted faster even than the hundred-times-tempered Lüben Guards; and the crossbows they carried had force enough to drive a bolt into solid rock.

“Where in the world did these monsters come from…” Cui Qi licked his parched lips, his heart pounding with dread. Yao Runeng poked his head a little way out past the wall, and a crossbow bolt came splitting the air at once. Cui Qi yanked him back in a hurry; the bolt-head grazed a long streak of blood across the young man’s cheek.

Yao Runeng, snatched back from the jaws of death, went deathly pale, his legs trembling beyond his control. He had not thought their shooting could be so precise even in the dark.

“Fool! They’re in a search-and-advance formation now, every crossbow drawn taut—poke your head out rashly and you’re asking to die!” Cui Qi cursed him as he would a raw recruit. Yao Runeng had no time to retort: “What do we do next?”

Cui Qi thought a moment: “This straight passage gives no cover at all. Once they come round the corner, we’re finished. Fall back to the prison first and hold them off at the door.”

With a great enemy before him, the commander’s bearing that Cui Qi had cultivated in the Long Mountains seemed to have come back to him.

Yao Runeng roused his spirits afresh: “Right! We only have to hold out until the great hall sends men to relieve us! Not one of these jail-breaking villains will get away.” Cui Qi gave a bitter laugh and, on the point of speaking, held his tongue; he was nowhere near so hopeful.

A jail-break? The great watchtower on high had gone dark, and that was the Jing’an Bureau’s very nerve-center of communications—what mere jail-break was ever so brazen? By the enemy’s numbers and quality, Cui Qi reckoned things looked grim for the great hall as well. He knew the Bureau’s inner security all too well; it came to just four words: strong without, weak within.

The general feeling was that this was the very heart of Chang’an, and the office in charge of catching thieves besides—who would dare tweak the tiger’s whiskers in its own den? And so even a man as shrewd as Li Bi had spent little thought on the matter.

And yet someone had gone and done just that, and on no small scale.

If he could help it, he had not the slightest wish to be buried alongside the Jing’an Bureau—but just now there was nowhere to flee. Cui Qi had to gather himself and see how he might get through this calamity.

“Damn it, I’m not even one of the Jing’an Bureau’s men anymore—I can’t die here!” he cursed inwardly, feeling his luck was truly wretched.

The two turned and ran back to the prison. This prison was in fact a holding-room made over from a firewood shed, only three cramped compartments, its window-lattices all of wood. The main door had no reinforcement of any kind, and its two short bronze pivots would break at a single kick.

Cui Qi called over the three jailers and briefly explained the present situation. The jailers were all Lüben Guard soldiers by origin; though they knew Cui Qi had turned traitor, their best choice for the moment was to obey their old commander. The five of them set to at once, hauling a wooden cabinet, a long table, and a bamboo chest behind the door to brace it, then lashing them together with chains. Cui Qi also brought out a jar of wine the jailers had hidden away and splashed it over the wooden bars of the window.

Yao Runeng took out a smoke pellet and threw it. The thing worked poorly at night, but having it was better than not.

The enemy was close at hand, and in such haste this was the best they could do.

When Yao Runeng had finished all this, he opened the cell behind him. Wen Ran was sitting in the straw; she had washed her face with water and combed her hair after a fashion, coiling it up on her head, and looked a little better than before. Yao Runeng said apologetically: “It’ll have to be a bit later before we can question you—there’s some trouble just now…”

Wen Ran trusted Yao Runeng, and she lifted her face: “Trouble? Has it to do with my benefactor?” For a moment Yao Runeng did not know what to say, and could only shake his head and answer that he did not know. Wen Ran’s gaze went past his shoulder to the men outside, busy barricading the door.

“Your voice is shaking. I thought the Jing’an Bureau would be quite safe…” Half a day of torment had sharpened Wen Ran’s senses somewhat, and she knew this situation was rather bad.

Yao Runeng comforted her with a wry smile: “Don’t dwell on it. In a while, move deeper into the cell—don’t stay too near the outside. Here, take this.” And he handed her a finely-made dagger with a horn hilt. It had been passed down in his family, and he had always carried it close.

Wen Ran hesitated, then took the dagger. She often cut spices with a small knife, and the feel of the thing was not unfamiliar to her. Outside, Cui Qi gave a shout, and Yao Runeng rose quickly and went over.

“Ah—that is—you…” Wen Ran did not know his name, and could only call him ‘you.’ Yao Runeng turned back, and she said: “Can I help you?”

“What?”

“One more person is always better, isn’t it? If something happens to you, I won’t be spared either.” Wen Ran turned the dagger over in her hand, her tone firm. “My benefactor said it: your life is something you win for yourself.”

“Come now, the Jing’an Bureau leaning on a woman to take the field—what would that look like? Set your mind at ease; the great hall will send relief soon.” Yao Runeng clenched his fist, not knowing whether he was comforting her or himself.

Wen Ran closed her mouth in disappointment; Yao Runeng had no time to go on soothing her, and turned to come to the door.

Cui Qi looked out through the crack of the door; it was pitch dark outside, and he could just make out several men, far off, moving this way. One in front, three behind—and there seemed to be one more following at the rear.

Every crossbow was trained forward; no one watched the rear. That flaw made Cui Qi’s heart sink—it was no flaw at all, but a sign that they had nothing to fear behind them: the left side-hall must already have been taken.

The design of these men seemed larger even than he had imagined.

“Damn it—if I had a pocket crossbow, I could at least throw their formation into disorder,” Cui Qi thought bitterly. His own crossbow had been confiscated when he re-entered the Jing’an Bureau—a surveillance detail had no need of the thing.

Yao Runeng raised his head, only for Cui Qi to press it down: “Before they rush us, they’ll loose a volley of bolts at the window—do you want to get yourself killed?” Yao Runeng ducked back behind the barricade and said in a low voice: “Commander Cui… er, thank you.”

“I’m saving my own skin.” Cui Qi stared at the crack of the door, his face blank. Yao Runeng knew it was the truth, yet he no longer resented him so much just now. He rummaged and rummaged, and drew from his breast a jade xiezhi: “If I die, could you send this back to my family?”

“A jade xiezhi? You don’t see many of those. Isn’t it usually a pixiu or a qilin people carry?” a jailer beside him asked curiously.

“The xiezhi can tell crooked from straight, and gores the lawless with its horn. A family of the courts through and through—even its sacred beast is unlike other men’s.” Cui Qi saw the origin at a glance, then pushed it back, mocking himself: “Don’t give it to me. I’m a traitor; I’m afraid it would gore me with that horn.”

In the dark Cui Qi’s face could not be seen. Yao Runeng was about to say something more when Cui Qi barked low: “Here they come!”

The enemy had closed to within crossbow range. The point-man at the head rolled along the ground and pressed swiftly up to the door. The four behind him trained their weapons on the window of the prison. If anyone dared show his head, it would be blown apart on the instant.

The point-man pushed at the door; it did not give, as expected. The four behind him each loosed a bolt at the window at once, then rushed together to the door. Hiding behind it, Yao Runeng and Cui Qi soon caught an acrid smell they both knew all too well—the Yanzhou rock-oil that had nearly loosed catastrophe on Chang’an.

“This is bad! They never meant to break the door down at all!” Cui Qi’s face changed. “They mean to burn this whole place to ashes!”

Once the stuff caught, it would not rest until the whole firewood shed had burned to nothing. The enemy did this to force the defenders to open the door themselves. Yao Runeng and Cui Qi exchanged a glance; there was nothing for it but to fight their way out.

They and the jailers hauled the barricade aside again, and the main door was suddenly kicked open from outside with a bang. A black-clad man at the front burst in like a wolf, like a tiger, knocking the jailer and Yao Runeng at the door flat to the ground. The black-clad man set down his crossbow to draw his blade.

The switch of weapons left only a momentary gap, and the seasoned Cui Qi had been waiting all along for just this chance; he sprang like a tiger.

The barrier-knife in his hand was already thrust straight out, and in one stroke he ran the black-clad man clean through, not forgetting to give the hilt a twist. By now a second man had rushed in; with no room to pull his blade free, Cui Qi simply drove his head into him. The black-clad man, thrown off by Cui Qi’s life-be-damned way of fighting, was forced to give a step.

Cui Qi did not hesitate, but pressed in close and threw his fists. There was no method to his boxing, yet the will behind it was full-blooded and unstinting. Under the utmost pressure, his fighting cast off the layers of misgiving he had carried in Chang’an and recovered the bold, headlong joy of his old days in the Long Mountains.

“Cui Qi of the Long Mountains! Cui Qi of the Long Mountains!” Low at first, his voice grew louder the more he fought, until at the end he was roaring it out, fierce as a maddened tiger. The second man could not hold him off and was beaten sheer to the ground. Cui Qi stamped down with all his force, and with a crack his sole crushed the man’s chest.

Only now did a third black-clad man charge in, and Cui Qi tangled with him doggedly right at the main door. The prison door was narrow, and with this block the black-clad men behind could not get past their comrade to reach Cui Qi.

Yao Runeng and the other three jailers scrambled up in the meantime and joined in the attack, briefly making it four against one.

Just then, with a wet thud, a crossbow went off. The one who fell was not on the prison’s side, but the black-clad man standing in the doorway. The deputy squad-leader outside, seeing that this man could neither force his way in nor would pull back out, had simply fired his crossbow—and the bolt ran clean through his own comrade and Cui Qi together.

No one had dreamed they would deal so cold-blooded a stroke against their own comrade, and no one had time to react. Cui Qi gave a roar of fury and fell to the ground together with the black-clad man.

In that instant there was no cover left between the jailers, Yao Runeng, and the black-clad men outside. The deputy squad-leader and another black-clad man at once fell back, opening the distance. Cui Qi, fallen on the ground, hastily lifted his head and cried out a warning—it was a repeating crossbow!

But it was already too late.

With the prison’s cover gone and the distance opened, they could not have withstood the enemy’s weapons even at twice their number. Bolts flew, and the three jailers were struck down one after another. Yao Runeng gritted his teeth and tried to rush in, only to be pinned through the left shoulder by a bolt and fall slantwise against the threshold. Cui Qi, wounded though he was, could still move his upper body; clenching his teeth, he picked up a blade from the ground and hurled it with all his strength. The deputy squad-leader knocked the blade aside with his crossbow, then kicked Cui Qi flying.

The prison’s counterattack ended there. Three dead and two wounded—they had wholly lost the power to fight.

Under his mask the deputy squad-leader’s face was very dark. It was nothing but a shabby little prison across from him, yet it had cost him no fewer than three of his elite fighters. He had his one remaining subordinate drag Yao Runeng and Cui Qi into the room and dump them in front of the cells, then drew his blade.

“You’ll wish you had died fighting just now.” the deputy squad-leader said savagely.

Thud.

The sound of a steel blade entering flesh.

The deputy squad-leader was baffled—he had not yet made his move, so how could there be such a sound? He looked again at Yao Runeng and Cui Qi; there was nothing amiss with either. Startled, he turned his head quickly, only to see his one remaining subordinate standing where he was, trembling all over, a bloody blade-point protruding from his chest.

Only then did the deputy squad-leader realize that this subordinate had been standing with his back to the cells—and they had never troubled to check whether anyone was inside.

The blade-point slowly withdrew, and with a heavy thud the black-clad man crumpled limply to his knees, revealing behind him a bewildered Wen Ran. Through the bars, she gripped in her hand the small knife handed down in Yao Runeng’s family.

The blow had taken everyone by surprise. Yao Runeng’s pupils shrank, and he shouted for her to get back at once.

But it was too late; the deputy squad-leader rushed over in great strides and clamped down hard on Wen Ran’s wrist. Wen Ran shrieked with pain, and the little knife fell clattering onto the flagstones. Yao Runeng, holding back his agony, gritted his teeth and made to charge in, but the deputy squad-leader kicked him sprawling to the ground and roared: “Don’t be in such a hurry—not one of you will get an easy death!”

The deputy squad-leader drew a leather strap from his waist and bound Wen Ran to the bars of the cell, then bent to fetch a squirt-pump from the body of one of his comrades. With a few soft, sputtering pushes, all three of them were sprayed over with the gluey rock-oil.

When all was ready, the deputy squad-leader, grinning cruelly, took out a fire-steel and struck at it, click after click, to make fire.

Yao Runeng knew what dreadful thing was about to happen, but he was powerless to stop it. He looked in despair toward Wen Ran, who was still blankly unaware; then he looked toward Cui Qi, whose face was all smeared with blood, his expression unreadable.

Yao Runeng stared blankly up at the sky for a moment, then his eyes hardened; turning to one side, he said softly to Cui Qi: “Commander Cui, the moment the fire starts, I’ll throw myself forward and hold him fast—you seize the chance and get away.”

Cui Qi opened his eyes and looked at him.

“You’re not one of the Jing’an Bureau’s men; there’s no need for you to lose your life for it. But I hope you’ll get this girl out—she’s innocent.”

Cui Qi let out a snort of a laugh through his nostrils. Yao Runeng did not know what he was laughing at, but he did not ask. The young man, resolved to die, forced back the fierce pain in his shoulder and drew up his left leg, hoping that at the instant the flames engulfed him he would have the strength to spring forward.

His hands were trembling, his jaw was trembling, and liquid ran uncontrollably from the corners of his eyes. Cui Qi stretched out an arm and laid it on Yao Runeng’s shoulder: “Your legs are still sound; you have a chance to run for it. Why go to such lengths?”

“Every man must answer for his own choices.” Yao Runeng did not so much as turn his head. At the words, Cui Qi’s shoulder gave a slight shudder.

By now the deputy squad-leader had at last struck a light: the wad of charred mugwort tinder in his hand had kindled into a small, blue-bright flame. He swept his gaze over the three blackened quarries and said, with venom and cruelty:

“Come, dance me a Sogdian Whirl there in the fire—you’ve a long time to be dying anyway.”

To keep clear of the blaze, the deputy squad-leader backed off a few steps until his back was against another cell. Judging the distance safe enough, he raised his arm to fling the tinder out.

A long, slender hand suddenly reached out from between the bars of the cell behind him, gently snatched the tinder away, and dropped it into the water-hole of a squirt-pump.

There was still most of a tube of rock-oil in the squirt-pump, and the moment the burning tinder went in, there came a whoosh and a dazzling flame surged up out of the pump, engulfing the deputy squad-leader from head to foot in an instant.

The deputy squad-leader turned into a living torch, lighting the once-dark prison bright as day. He screamed shrilly, but the scorching heat soon seared his vocal cords, and only his two legs went on kicking in despair—for all the world as though dancing a Sogdian Whirl. Before long the deputy squad-leader toppled to the ground with a thud, his body charred to a cinder, the flames still blazing fiercely on.

“Have you all forgotten me—Cen Shen of Xianzhou?”

a young man shouted furiously from inside the cell.

Only now did Yao Runeng remember that there was still a prisoner in the cell. This fellow named Cen Shen, who had wrecked one of the Jing’an Bureau’s plans in Huaiyuan Ward, had been hauled back and shut up here ever since, all but forgotten. He had kept himself curled in the deepest corner of the cell, and with the darkness besides, no one—the deputy squad-leader included—had noticed that such a person was there.

Who would have thought that the one to save them in the end would be this luckless wretch?

With that, all five intruders had been finished off. Yao Runeng, snatched from death, let out a great breath and turned to Cui Qi with delight: “Commander Cui, it’s safe here for the moment—let’s hurry to the great hall!”

“Things look grim over at the great hall, I fear; I won’t be going.” Cui Qi said coldly. Yao Runeng was somewhat annoyed—the man had been fighting shoulder to shoulder with him only moments ago; how had he slipped back into his old ways now?

“If it’s awkwardness you fear, I’ll explain to the Deputy Director that you did not flinch or shirk the fight,” Yao Runeng said.

But Cui Qi gave no answer, only a faint, bitter smile. He moved his hand away from his lower belly, revealing the shaft of a crossbow bolt with only its tail-end left, the blood already soaking the whole of his lower robe.
