## Mari of Kōga
But Maeda Gen'i was not so soft a man as Kazama Gohei had supposed.
Gohei believed he had sounded out Gen'i's true mind; in fact it was Gen'i who had seen through him. An Iga man, when all is said, has no honest, dogged loyalty in him. Use him for all he is worth, and then there is nothing for it but to stop his breath—there is no other way to handle such a one. All this Gen'i drew, keenly, from behind Gohei's glib talk.
(That one—I must not let down my guard.)
Taking a breath in an inner room, Gen'i turned over the steps that would end in Gohei's death. That he would set Gohei to uncover the whole shape of the affair went without saying. But his pressing need, meanwhile, was to decide whom to set watching Gohei's every move. That man would have to cut Gohei down the moment it was required. Was there anyone of such caliber to be had?—
For that, of course, Gen'i was not unprovided.
Ringing a bell, Hōin Gen'i called an attendant to him in haste.
"The other day—did you send a messenger to that place?"
"The one in Ōmi…?"
"Just so."
"When he calls, show him straight in here."
"He should reach the capital by about this evening."
When the retainer had gone, Gen'i rose alone, went into a small sutra-hall beside the family chapel of the mansion, took out a single scroll of scripture, and came back to the room.
He laid it on a heavy sutra-desk of vermilion lacquer, said to be of Nigatsu-dō make, and the aged gold-brocade wrapping of the scroll made, in that dim room plastered close on three sides, a still space of quiet, holy light.
The book was a sutra copied out in gold ink on indigo ground.
Its title was one that not even a cleric of the Esoteric schools could have called familiar.
—The Dhāraṇī Sutra of Marishiten.
The script, at least, could be read.
He took up the scroll, bowed lightly over it like the official in priest's robes that he was, and murmured under his breath something wholly other than the sutra his eyes were reading.
"Is that fellow Mari keeping well, I wonder. Nine years now," he muttered, and in the same breath intoned the mantra: Nōmaku samanda bodanan on marishiei sowaka.
There was a fondness for the man in his voice.
Marishiten, guardian deity of the Buddhist way, means in Sanskrit the shimmer of heat. The haze that quivers up over the spring fields cannot be seen for what it is, nor taken in the hand.
"There is a deva, and her name is Marishi. Ever she goes before the sun, yet the sun does not see her; she alone can see the sun." So runs a passage of the Flower-Garland Sutra of the Deva Marishi, a passage that calls up the lovely imagination of the ancient Indians, who gave the heat-shimmer the shape of a living god.
Her form is that of a heavenly maiden: on her head a crown of jewelled strands, in her left hand a celestial fan, her right hand let down with the palm turned outward and the five fingers spread in the mudra that grants all wishes—and for all that, her figure is invisible to men. Marishiten alone can see men. Pray to her, they say, and you go unseen and unknown by others, bound by no one; you are spared every evil—the peril of kings and of thieves, of the road, of flood and fire, of blade and army and battle-line, of poison and savage beast and venomous insect, of every hateful crowd and wicked man; and in the trackless waste this deva keeps you and does not forsake you, and without fail delivers you out of every extremity.
A guardian deity fit indeed for the ninja of Iga and Kōga.
Naturally, she was widely revered among them.
The man Gen'i was waiting for went by the byname of Marishiten. Among the Iga there was nothing to match it; it was used only within the fellowship of the Kōga, and even there it was withheld from any middling adept. One district, one generation, one man—such was the unwritten rule, so that the name seems to have been a kind of title the fellowship conferred on none but a practitioner of surpassing skill. Unseen and untouchable as the heat-shimmer: Marishiten, in Sanskrit, is the heat-shimmer itself. The byname came, as has been said, from the belief that the man commanded, like the deity, an uncanny power to sweep aside all perils.
As Gen'i sat waiting for the man, for some while now an unbearable drowsiness had been settling over him. He held himself straight and bent to his reading, fighting for all he was worth against the weight of his eyelids; and yet this drowsiness carried a kind of foreboding. Perhaps the man had already come. Gen'i strained his eyes at the text.
(An insolent creature. At this rate he has come into the house already, without so much as asking to be announced.)
He twisted his lips and made a deliberate effort to smile. By that much—by the mere act of smiling—he wanted to prove to himself that some margin of waking sense was still left him.
And then, as if in answer, a voice rang out—
"Tokuzen'in-dono."
Gen'i's eyes flew open.
"Out of pure fondness I've come in unannounced. Though of course, if a ninja went in and out by the front gate each time, no number of lives would see him through."
"Hm."
For all his composure he could not hide his alarm, and looked about him—but on the ceiling, behind the folding screen, in the shadow of the sliding doors, nowhere was there so much as the shadow of a crawling thing.
(Insolence.)
The blood rose to Gen'i's face. He rapped the desk in vexation and called for a retainer.
"Someone, there!"
"Sir."
"It seems an intruder has crept in."
"An intruder? There's no sign of one, my lord."
"The man I gave orders about a while ago."
"As for him—"
"What of him?"
"He is already here."
"Wh—where?"
"Before you, my lord."
The retainer who had been answering face-down to the floor suddenly lifted his head. His face cracked open in a bright peal of laughter—
"It's me, Tokuzen'in-dono. Mari Dōgen, of Kōga. —No—"
He set his hands to the floor again.
"It has been a long while. Good of you to send for me this time. What is your business?"
He came striding forward and sat cross-legged in front of Gen'i.
Gen'i could not quite master his displeasure, and said only—
"And since then—have you kept well?"
That was all he said.
"Well enough. Only, we've both grown old, the pair of us. Looking at you, for a moment I took you for someone else."
Gen'i and Mari Dōgen were no ordinary acquaintances. On the second day of the sixth month of Tenshō 10, when a detachment under Akechi Mitsuhide surrounded Oda Nobutada's lodging in the capital, Nobutada charged Gen'i with the guarding of the young lord Sanbōshi, and gave him, to see him out, a ninja he happened to have in his personal service—Mari Dōgen of Kōga.
The bond between them began there.
Dōgen cut a bold path through the flashing blades and got as far as the Shirakawa gate; but finding the enemy soldiers thick on the road here too, he turned back to Gen'i—
"It's past helping now. I'm a ninja—I can hide myself in heaven or earth—but with your whole party in tow there's nothing I can do. Open your belly here. I'll take your head for you."
"And what will you do?"
"Need you ask? I'm a ninja, with no man in all Japan for a master. To die wedged between the quarrels of samurai would be a disgrace. I'll run."
"W-wait. Then I can't carry out my lord's last command. Somehow—take me with you, get me clear."
"Ah—down!"
Dōgen shoved Gen'i and the others, clinging to him, down into the cover of the long grass, waited for the Akechi soldiers to pass, and then—
"Say fifty kan a year. How's that?"
In a low voice, Dōgen said a curious thing.
"Hm. —Well, so be it."
In return for the rescue, he pledged to deliver fifty kanmon each year to Dōgen's house in the Kōga district, for as long as Mari Dōgen should live.
Dōgen lashed Gen'i to his back, held the little Sanbōshi to his chest, and—Marishiten to the life—raced over hill and field until he had delivered them all the way to Gifu castle.
By the time they reached the castle, even the redoubtable Dōgen was drawing breath with difficulty, the grazes all over his body gone to pus; yet he would not stay in the castle a moment. He shook off Gen'i's hand where it caught at his sleeve—
"There's a woman waiting for me in Kōga. It's been too long since I held her."
He laughed, easy and bright. Gen'i watched him go with open admiration, thinking that in skill of arms and in wits alike here was a man wasted on the rappa's trade.
"Won't you think again? Stay on here, take your place among the samurai, and you've the makings of a man who could hold a small castle or two."
"You talk nonsense. Do you still not see it? You kept your life just now because I was a ninja. Had I been an ordinary samurai, your body would be lying in some field by now. A ninja has that much in him—but there's a cast of mind in us that will never mix with the common run of samurai. Cripples, you might call us. And above all, I couldn't bear the dreariness of it—taking a master, keeping retainers. Look at the roof of that turret. Those sparrows flocking there—if that's what you samurai are—"
"—then what am I?"
"An owl."
Dōgen laughed, wide and bright, the red of his open mouth showing, and then—
"A ninja, like the owl, lives in the hollows of men, in the shadow of the five elements, keeping to no flock, alone. Do you think a creature like that could live an ordinary samurai's life? —"
"Rather than fret yourself over such needless things—look here—"
He clapped Gen'i on the shoulder—
"Don't forget the promise—fifty kanmon. With that money I can keep one more woman, and that's my pleasure in life. So it won't do unless you rise in the world. I'll pray for it, from the shadows."
With that parting word, Mari Dōgen took his battered body and vanished from the castle.
As the days went by, Gen'i came to think back with warmth on the man who had pulled him through the crisis of his life. The fifty kanmon he sent every year thereafter, once the harvest was gathered from his fief in autumn, setting aside all else to do it. In the lean years, when his stipend was small, it was a sum that cost him dear; and that he never once let it lapse was a measure of how thorough a villain Gen'i was.
Gen'i's character was by no means a good one. He knew it himself. He rather prided himself on it. But a true villain is more scrupulous about a debt than any honest man, and shows, at times, an uncanny fidelity. Had the other party to Mari Dōgen's spoken promise that day been some half-hearted good man, the pledge would have lapsed after a handful of payments and come to nothing.
With a fidelity that bordered on obsession, Gen'i went on sending the fifty kanmon. And Mari Dōgen, each time, gave no receipt and sent no word of thanks, but only went on taking the money in silence.
Out of the habit there grew, of itself, a kind of unspoken pact between the two of them alone, sealed in wordless understanding: that if ever the day came again when Gen'i had need of him, Dōgen would rise to it though it cost him his life.
"Dōgen. It's this, and nothing else."
Gen'i said it, settling his knees afresh.
"Of late, for some reason, rappa have got into the capital and are running riot as they please. By one account there's even a plot of treason against His Highness the Kampaku afoot. What do you make of it?"
"Hm. The Iga men."
"You can tell?"
"It's not Kōga, at any rate. Ever since the Udaifu wiped the Iga men out, they've nursed a fierce grudge against the world. Men like that will want to help themselves to a little plunder, too. And looking on the gilded halls of the capital, they'll be itching to set them alight as well—there's not a Kōga man but feels for them in that."
"Is that all? Are the Iga men moving out of their own grudge alone?"
"Now there's a clever man. You've grasped it well. The world has turned from the Udaifu to the Kampaku, and you've poled your boat neatly against the current to come this far—but the season's near, I fancy, when you'll have to change boats. Reckon the Kampaku's age will last forever, and you'll have made a grave mistake."
"Wait."
Gen'i had risen and started for the paper screen, but Dōgen checked him—
"No one's listening. I've a genin of mine crouched in the garden thicket, keeping watch."
"You think of everything."
"The thing to do is count the wrinkles on the Kampaku's face. He's gone clean back to his dotage. This Korean campaign is only one sign of it. Ever since the child the daughter of the lord of Odani bore him died, they say his wits have wandered; and a man so soft over a child—if another son is born to him, his derangement will know no bounds. A realm can't hold long for a man with no heir. It's a one-generation dominion at best. Every daimyō alive, sharp as they are, is making ready to slip out from under the Kampaku's world. Tōdō, the same; Hosokawa, Chōsokabe, Kanamori, Fukushima, Katō—there's no end to the naming of them. Every one of them is secretly on good terms with the Naifu—with Ieyasu. Might it not be that some hot-blooded man among them is egging the Iga on, to hasten the collapse of the realm? Or else—"
"The Naifu?"
"Just so. If the house of Tokugawa stands behind the Iga men, this is no small matter. Kyoto magistrate though you are, Tokuzen'in-dono, move rashly and you'll spoil the whole thing."
"Hm. —Can you find that out?"
"A weighty task, that."
"I'll pay you handsomely."
"I don't want it. This is for you, and no other. Just cover my expenses in gold and silver. But tell me—have you hired no other rappa?"
"There's one Iga man. Kazama Gohei, the name was, I think."
"Kazama. …So that's where he's burrowed in. I'd heard he betrayed Iga and bolted off somewhere."
"You know him?"
"Not to know him. But Iga and Kōga, for all the two names, are near enough one country, joined along the same hills. Talk among the ninja gets about, great and small."
"Can you do it?"
"Hm. A man of that much skill, were he in Kōga, could bear the name of Mari. But among his fellows there was one greater than he. What's become of that one now, I couldn't say."
Beyond that, Mari Dōgen would not speak of the doings of the ninja fellowship. However much the man before him was Gen'i, to talk of his own kind to an outsider was a thing a rappa felt bound, at heart, to hold back from.
"So I'm to keep watch on what the Iga men get up to. …There's not much art in that, either."
Muttering it inwardly, Dōgen left the mansion of Maeda Hōin Gen'i. A wicker pannier on his back, dirty cotton hakama cut off at the shin, his half-grey hair carelessly bound up: he had made himself over into a travel-worn seller of plasters.
Dōgen was a Kōga man, and had his pride in it. To dog the Iga man Kazama Gohei's every step and note his every move was galling to him, and at the same time so much wasted labor.
(Who were the Kōga men out on work in the capital, now?)
He thought of getting from such men the inside threads of the rappa's rampage in the capital. But it seemed not one of them was out earning his keep.
Since Hideyoshi's Odawara campaign there had been, for the time being, an end to war in every province, and so the ninja's usefulness had dwindled.
Drifting along the house-rows of Horikawa, idly hawking his wares from eave to eave, Dōgen struck all at once on an old memory, and unawares shortened his stride.
(That's it. …There was Mochizuki Gyōbuzaemon.)
The surname that had come to Dōgen was, in the Kōga district of Ōmi, a house of no small renown.
A man of the Mochizuki, Gyōbuzaemon Shigehisa by name, had in his old age, as though on a sudden whim, left the land and settled in the capital. Some five or six years back, it must have been. He was dead now, though, these several years.
(What had he stolen into the capital to do?)
Dōgen cocked his head. But there was no way to know. Only one thread of memory remained to him.
When Gyōbuzaemon left Kōga, so it was said, he had taken a single girl with him. He had never, as far as anyone knew, married in all his life, so she was most likely a child left in his keeping by some house of standing.
(Since Gyōbuzaemon's death, what has become of the girl? Even if she was fourteen or fifteen then, she'd be well grown by now.)
As soon as he reached his inn in Kiyamachi, Dōgen summoned two of his genin. Ever since leaving Kōga he had kept more than ten of them hidden about him, before and behind; each in his own disguise, so that to an ordinary eye they were no followers of his at all.
"Back to Kōga at once. Find out—to your own full satisfaction—what Mochizuki Gyōbuzaemon was about in the capital."
"Understood."
To the other genin—
"You—go straight to Iga and find me the names of those who are away from the province just now. Understand?"
"Well, now…"
"Lost your nerve? Let them learn you're a Kōga man, and you'll likely not cross the border alive. And yet, without spying out how the Iga move, this work won't go forward half a step. At this rate it may well turn into a fight between Kōga and Iga. It's an order. Go and find out."
"Sir."
Before dawn the next day the two genin left the inn, each for his own destination.
The Kōga district lies in the south of Ōmi province, mountain heaped on mountain. It is scarcely a separate country from Iga; the highlands join the two, north to south.
Where the Kōga temper differs from the Iga is here: against Iga's fierce independence, the Kōga served a master, and served him well. The loyalty with which they served the Sasaki, protectors of Ōmi, down to that house's very fall, is proof of it beyond anything else. When, in the first year of Chōkyō, the ninth Ashikaga shogun, Yoshihisa, led an army in person against Sasaki Takayori, it was the Kōga shinobi who fought as Takayori's vanguard, who fell by night on Yoshihisa's headquarters at Magari-no-sato in Ōmi and raised the name of the Kōga shinobi high across the land. So skillfully did they come and go with the thickening and thinning of the mist that the capital's forces, it is said, came to dread them: "They work sorcery," the cry went, "throwing up a wall of haze to blind us."
The fifty-three houses of Kōga, they say, in a single phrase. Among the shinobi it is the collective name for the leading families of the district. That they are counted by a number is, no doubt, because—where the Iga shinobi are almost all of the one Taira surname, distant clans gathered under a single name—in Kōga the houses were split among many lineages, Minamoto and Taira, Fujiwara and Tachibana, each holding its own ground. And so no one house stood at their head. The great affairs of the district were settled by the council of the fifty-three houses. In that, they were no different from Iga.
For interest's sake, let us set down the roll of the fifty-three houses of Kōga in the early years of the Warring States, as recorded in the diary of the Akutagawa house of the Kōga men, kept in the Shōtoku era.
Yamanaka Jūrō; Saga Echizen-no-kami; Miyajima Kamon-no-suke; Kuraji Ukon-no-suke; Hirako Tonomo-no-suke; Katsuragi Tango-no-kami; Sugitani Yotōji; Tsuchiyama Shikanosuke; Mochizuki Izumo-no-kami; Hari Izumi-no-kami; Minobe Gengo; Ukai Genhachirō; Ogawa Magojūrō; Yamagami Tōshichirō; Hatta Kansuke; Jinbo Hyōnai; Aoki Chikugo-no-kami; Koizumi Geki; Torii Hyōnai; Sugiyama Hachirō; Natsumi Daigaku; Tarao Shirōbei; Mikumo Shinkurando; Ōhara Genzaburō; Wada Iga-no-kami; Makimura Umanosuke; Ikeda Shōemon; Hattori Tōdayū; Ōkawara Genta; Ōkubo Gennai; Saji Kawachi-no-kami; Ueno Shuzen-no-shō; Aeba Kawachi-no-kami; Takano Bingo-no-kami; Oki Ukon-dayū; Tongū Shihō-no-suke; Ueda Shinhachirō; Iwamuro Daigaku-no-suke; Nakayama Minbu-no-jō; Takayama Gentazaemon; Ban Sakyō-no-suke; Akutagawa Sakyō-no-suke; Uda Tōnai; Ueda Mikawa-no-kami; Nagano Gyōbu-no-jō; Taki Kanhachirō; Noda Gorō; Naiki Iga-no-kami; Ōno Kunai-no-shō; Iwane Nagato-no-kami; Kurokawa Bunnai; Takamine Kurando; Shinjō Echigo-no-kami.
Besides these, each house had its branch families and its genin, so that the number of those called Kōga ninja can hardly have fallen short of three hundred. As much may be gathered from the record that in the fifth year of Eiroku, when Ieyasu attacked Gamagōri, a branch castle of the Imagawa, he took two hundred Kōga men into his hire—a fact set down later in the "Petition of the Old Warriors of Kōga," drawn up by the Kōga men who had become retainers of the shogunate.
In that same battle, it is said, they slipped by night into Gamagōri castle, held by Udono Nagamochi, cut off Nagamochi's head, and, each setting fires here and there within the walls, brought the castle down at a single stroke.
Mari Dōgen of Kōga was of the Ban, one of the fifty-three houses—of a branch of it, born Ban Tōnai—and, taking his father's name, called himself Dōgen after he retired from the world.
The Mochizuki Gyōbuzaemon who, as we have said, died in the capital was of a branch of the Mochizuki; the main house, generation upon generation, took to itself the style of Izumo-no-kami.
By Kōga custom this main Mochizuki house had always been treated as first among the families of the district. This was because it came down in the direct line from Kōga Saburō, founding ancestor of the Kōga school of ninjutsu.
Kōga Saburō is a monstrous figure out of Kōga legend, said to have truly lived in the Heian age: betrayed by his elder brother, he fell down a hole that led to hell, went on falling for seven days and nights, crawled out again, and this time, changed into the body of a serpent, took his revenge. He is, no doubt, the Kōga counterpart of Oiro Tayuya, the spy of Prince Shōtoku whom the Iga ninja revere as their own first ancestor.
Now, the genin who had gone to look into the circumstances of the said Mochizuki Gyōbuzaemon's death came back to the Kiyamachi inn where Mari Dōgen of Kōga was lodging only after ten days had passed.
"Did you learn anything?"
"Sir. I turned up a strange thing. The place where Gyōbuzaemon died was—of all places—the Kyoto mansion of Ishida Jibu-no-shōyū Mitsunari."
"Oh-ho. Killed by a curious man, then."
"Nothing of the sort. He was kept in comfort at the Ishida mansion and, they say, died a peaceful death."
"Jibu-no-shōyū and Gyōbuzaemon. Now where could the two of them have crossed? Mitsunari was once a temple boy at Kannon-ji in Gōshū, they say; but even within the one province of Gōshū, surely he can have no tie to the Mochizuki of the Kōga district."
"Even if there's no tie between the two, there's a man who joined them. Why, the lord Hakkansai himself."
"The lord Sasaki?"
At the name, Dōgen, in a way quite unlike him, drew himself suddenly upright. Hakkansai—Sasaki, or Rokkaku, Yoshikata—was the old lord whom once all the ninja of the Kōga district had looked to as master.
At the old lord's name even a man like Dōgen sat up straight. An Iga man, ninja though he is too, lives by a haughty creed that sells his soul to nothing on earth.
In that one thing the Kōga and the Iga differ. The Sasaki house of Ōmi, be it said in passing, was from Sadatsuna—a retainer of Yoritomo—on through the Kamakura and the Muromachi ages a bulwark house of the Minamoto line; and in Yoshikata's generation it fought Nobunaga, was brought to its knees at Kannon-ji castle in Ōmi, and so the noble line came to an end.
Yoshikata later fled to the Kōga district, took the tonsure, changed his name to the lay-priest Jōtei, and nursed his old age in a corner of the Mochizuki house, once his own vassals.
The tale goes back a way—to the eleventh year of Eiroku.
The lay-priest Jōtei was by then close to fifty; and what was pitiless, in a beaten man, was that his body was still hale. He had a local woman keep house for him, got her with child, and she bore a daughter.
But once the daughter was born, he had no land to leave her. Having no choice, before she was well grown, he made the child, so it is told, an adopted daughter of Mochizuki Gyōbuzaemon.
"Then the girl Gyōbuzaemon took with him—she's the lord Hakkansai's child?"
"Just so."
"And what has that to do with Ishida Jibu-no-shōyū?"
"Don't make a show of it—out with it."
"Ah—that's just the point."
"The lord Hakkansai, in his last years, took his ease in the capital among the pleasures of poetry and nature; but the one thing that weighed on him was the single child he had left behind in Kōga. In passing, no doubt, he asked Ishida Jibu-no-shōyū—then the coming man—to see the child settled. Jibu-no-shōyū's father, the lord Masatsugu, was once, they tell me, a retainer of the Kyōgoku house of Ōmi. Kyōgoku and Rokkaku differ in name, but at root they are the one Sasaki line. Might it not be that tie the lord Hakkansai called upon?"
"Hm. A shrewd reading, for the likes of you. And what did Jibu-no-shōyū do with the child?"
"He arranged for her to be taken in as adopted daughter of the lord Sōkyū of Sakai, the Ōkurakyō and Hōin. And Sōkyū, as they say, is a merchant so rich a daimyō of a million koku couldn't touch him. Her fine living, surely, is hers for the asking."
"And what is the girl's name?"
"Kohagi, I heard it was."
"Kohagi…"
Dōgen shut his eyes and murmured the name over, as if fixing it in memory. After a while he opened his mouth again—
"And what of Gyōbuzaemon?"
"He was kept at the lord Sōkyū's charge, and ended his days in comfort, in a teahouse-style retreat at Imadegawa in the capital, they say. …But here's the grave part."
The genin leaned forward as he spoke.
"Well? This grave matter of yours. You're making a great face over it."
"This lady Kohagi, the lord Hakkansai's daughter—to her, Gyōbuzaemon taught the whole art of the district, down to its last particular."
"…What?"
Dōgen's eyes went wide.
"You're telling me he gave her the licence of the Kōga school?"
"Just so."
The genin nodded, deeply.
"You mean she's one of our own?"
"Just so."
"And knowing it, Jibu-no-shōyū placed her with Sōkyū the Hōin as an adopted daughter?"
"Ah, as to that—you'd best ask Jibu-no-shōyū himself."
"Don't talk rot. —But this is turning interesting. This business of the capital's thief—there may be threads I never dreamed of, hidden all over the place. I set out at once."
"Where will you go?"
By the time the genin hurried after him, Dōgen was already sitting on the earthen floor of the inn, lacing his straw sandals.
"To Sakai."
Dōgen murmured a word in the genin's ear, and then sprang out into the darkness of the road, his body melting away in the blink of an eye.
The genin who had watched from the road as his master set off like a gust of wind went back, with no caution at all, to the eaves of the inn—the Ōmiya—and slipped in at the earthen door.
There was a man who had been watching that figure, unmoving, out of the dark.
Dōgen's genin, like his master, was got up as a peddler. And the man in the dark who watched his back was likewise done up in the style of a capital merchant.
(That travelling trader is no common man. The way he carries himself, the way his eyes move—there's the smell of a ninja about him.)
When one ninja looks on another, however good the disguise, some instinct stirs. All the more with this watcher: he was a genin of Tsuzura Jūzō—Kuroami, the Iga man.
(A Kōga man.)
Seeing it, he gave up his night's work. He would stay on this road till daybreak, he decided, and make out what was afoot. As luck had it, there was an inn across from the Ōmiya.
Kuroami quickly got up his travelling clothes, passed himself off as a wayfarer, and took a second-floor room of the inn that faced the road.
(What has brought a Kōga man out to the capital?)
But this was a thing Kuroami had no need to ask himself. It went straight home to him. Beyond doubt it was a last, hard shift the rulers of the capital had hit upon, to set someone against the rampage of Kuroami's own band of Iga men.
(Make an enemy of Kōga, and there's trouble.)
Kuroami felt a trembling rise from the depths of his body, and saw it with his own eyes. It was fear. But he made no forced effort to master it. A ninja, by long custom, lets his own cowardice run as instinct wills.
All night long, the paper screen cracked open, Kuroami watched the road and the Ōmiya, trembling in small fits the while.
At the hour the first cock crows, with time yet before dawn, a black shadow ran across the great roof of the Ōmiya that Kuroami was watching. It was drawn straight over to the far side and gone; but Kuroami's fear only grew the greater.
(That ninja is no ordinary man.)
The foreboding cast an honest shadow across his instinct for fear.
It was several days before Kuroami, lodging at that inn, made out that the man staying at the Ōmiya was, past all doubt, Mari Dōgen of Kōga.
There was, to be sure, something familiar in Dōgen's rough-hewn build; and he had seen the genin's face before as well. Once he was certain of it, Kuroami hastily quit his lodging and went back to the annex of Iseya Kahei, behind the Hōkō-ji.
"Where've you been? Haven't seen you these few days."
Jūzō said it idly, paring his nails on the edge of the veranda.
"Easygoing as ever, I see."
Kuroami, too, never tired of his fussy, nagging little jabs.
"Is paring my nails so easygoing? I was thinking of shaving my beard after, at that."
"Off to see a woman, are you? A bad streak—you've taken it from the old master."
"Was my father such a one for women?"
"Everywhere he went he came to grief over some woman, and this Kuroami had no end of trouble for it."
"Set your mind at rest. I'm no such womanizer as you make me."
He grinned—
"What's that face for?"
Jūzō looked again, closely, at Kuroami's small eyes stretched wide, the fear not yet gone out of his face.
"Master Jūzō. It seems a Kōga man has cut into our game."
"The magistrate's office has hired him, most likely."
"And the leader is Mari of Kōga."
"…"
Jūzō fell silent. A faint tautness ran across his shut mouth.
"You're sure it's Dōgen?"
"No mistake."
Kuroami's teeth were faintly chattering. A man who could show his fear as honestly as this was a rare thing.
Pressing his point—
"This will be no easy matter."
"Hm. No easy matter."
Even as he said it, Jūzō had already shaken off that instant's tension, and laughed as if to tease Kuroami's earnest, staring eyes.
"No cause to fret. But we'll have to spy out the enemy's moves, at least. Learn his aim and his movements, and then we simply turn them against him."
"And is that all it will take?"
"No, it's not. Dōgen's a real nuisance, there's no denying. We'll have to make an opening and cut him down."
"Can you cut him, Master Jūzō?"
"You doubt it?"
He laughed aloud—
"A man's a hard thing to cut, when it comes to it. Ever since I idled ten years away at Otogi Pass, I've come to feel, like anyone else, a pity for anything that lives and moves. My hand's not gone soft, I think—but the ferocity's left my heart."
"That's just grumbling."
"To be honest, it is grumbling. But that aside—if it's Maeda Gen'i who hired Dōgen, then I've a scheme. Kuroami—or rather, Iseya Kahei—do you have the run of the Maeda house as well?"
"Hardly that. But I do a little trade with the magistrate's retainers, at least."
"Let a word be dropped, quietly, in their ears—that this thief is acting on the orders of a certain great personage. You may say the Naifu—Ieyasu—or you may say Hidetsugu, the Kampaku's adopted heir. It may tell better than you'd think. And meanwhile we too will lie quiet a while and watch how things go."
About the time Jūzō learned that the leader of the Kōga men newly come to the capital was Mari Dōgen, at nearly the same hour Dōgen too learned that the leader of the Iga was Tsuzura Jūzō. He had guessed it, no doubt, from the report of the genin he had sent into Iga.
"At Otogi Pass there should be a hermitage that Jūzō's grandfather, Tsuzura Ukyō-dayū, built. Did you look there too?"
"I did. But the storm-shutters were nailed fast, and the summer weeds, dead where they'd grown, had climbed clear over the rain-veranda."
"Proof enough. It's Jūzō and no other. Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon would be too old by now to start anything fresh."
"Then this is a heavy business."
"How so?"
"Tsuzura Jūzō, they say, is by now a master you might call the first in all Iga."
"To my eyes, a stripling."
Dōgen pulled his collar open. The skin of his chest, thick with coarse hair, had flushed a little red. Ranging the sake-flasks up in a row before his raised right knee—
"All the same—this girl they call the lord Hakkansai's orphan—"
"Oh—you met her?"
The genin said it as he poured for Dōgen's cup.
"I went to Sakai, and nosing about I found she was living at the Komatsudani villa in the capital; so I slipped in there at once and had a look at her."
"Did you speak with her?"
"I saw her; she never knew it. Sound asleep, she was. A ripe, toothsome-looking woman."
"You didn't get up to any mischief, I hope."
"I'll have to, one of these days. But at a first meeting, with her asleep at that—to carry on strangely would shame the name of Dōgen."
"Once you've shared a bed, you're only a woman and a man. No call to think so stiffly about it."
"And you drew off, just like that?"
"I left a Kōga letter behind, that's all."
"She is the daughter of the old lord of all Kōga."
A Kōga letter is no letter at all. It is a single Sanskrit character, drawn in a special hand. When a Kōga ninja takes work in another's territory, where a fellow is already at a job, he pastes a small printed slip bearing the character in the shadow of a pillar of the ninja's dwelling, as a greeting to say he has newly come to the district. The character is but one glyph, and since each ninja keeps to his own, it tells at once who has come. In greeting Kohagi not as Hakkansai's child, nor as Imai Sōkyū's adopted daughter, but as a fellow of the Kōga, Dōgen showed the depth of his design.
"Long lashes, she has."
Still in that mock-idle tone, Dōgen went on about Kohagi's charms.
"Her black hair spilling from the pillow, pooling out over the matting—there was no telling it for beauty."
For all the jokes he cracked to amuse the genin, Dōgen's eyes shone from far back in his head, telling plainly that his mind was nowhere near the things his mouth let fall. What was in Dōgen's head was not Kohagi's black hair, but the dark, looming image of an Iga man—Tsuzura Jūzō.
(An Iga man's swordwork is an eerie thing. And Jūzō, they say, has the edge on the blade even more than at stealth. Just how good is he, I wonder? If I judge him the better man, the winning move is never to face him at all. If I see he can be brought down, I'll force my way in and cripple him. Either way, this has turned into a trial of skill between Kōga and Iga.)
The year ended. And then half a year went by.
Jūzō, Kuroami, and the rappa under them—whether they had all gone to ground in the earth or vanished into the sky—made in the end not the least sound. And so the peace of the capital returned, to all seeming, to its old quiet.
Maeda Hōin Gen'i, though he could make nothing certain of it, put the question to Dōgen with a vaguely easier look.
"Well now—have they quit the district, do you suppose?"
The rampage of the unknown rappa had already reached the ears of the Kampaku Hideyoshi, and Gen'i stood under stern orders to have them taken. But once they had left the capital, they were outside his jurisdiction. He had no thought of slackening the pursuit; and yet, for all that, he breathed easier.
"In the end they may have been common thieves after all. Once they'd picked the place near clean, they'd no more use for it, I daresay."
At Gen'i's words Dōgen gave a grin that rubbed against the grain of them—
"For an inquisitor, Tokuzen'in-dono, you're too good-hearted—but then, you are a man of the cloth."
Gen'i, hearing it, made a sour face.
"If you've something in mind, say it."
"It's a ninja's instinct. Telling you would get you no nearer."
"Why not?"
"And that's the very opening the Iga men look for."
Dōgen would not be drawn, and went on shaving flakes off a strange black lump with his kozuka and laying them on his tongue. From his fingertips there drifted a faint, rank stink. Gen'i, frowning at Dōgen's coarseness and at the reek of the thing in his hand—
"What is that?"
"Boar's gall, dried and pressed hard."
"Do Kōga men eat such stuff?"
"Some do, some don't. Once you start on it, it gets to be a habit—there's no telling how fine the bitterness is. How about it—a sliver?"
"No."
"As you please. But pay it no mind. This is my supper, you see."
The place was Gen'i's study. In the northeast corner of the grounds a great ginkgo towered up, and at its foot stood a shrine of no small size. Dōgen had taken up residence in a row-house within the grounds, on the pretext of being a shrine-keeper hired to tend it. His days there were, needless to say, tedium itself.
That said, Dōgen was far from easy in his mind. Ever since his own coming to the capital, the Iga men's movements had stopped, and he watched the fact with something close to dread. First, there was this: the Iga men lurking in the capital knew he had gone over to Gen'i. And then one thing more. That they could hold to so long a silence was, Dōgen thought, no ordinary matter. When a ninja works to some end, to keep constantly in motion is easy enough; but to lie idle and mute for a fixed stretch of time is next to impossible. This unseen adversary of his took no short, small stroke aimed at some petty gain; he held his silence exactly as long as he needed, and might then move in a way to catch one wholly off guard—and to show such ease, such margin, could as well be proof that behind him stood the master of some vast conspiracy indeed.
(This Tsuzura Jūzō, whoever he is—he's good.)
Dōgen found himself admiring, in a curious way, an enemy who did precisely nothing.
But in this Dōgen was most likely reading too much in. Jūzō's reason lay somewhere far simpler.
The Kampaku Hideyoshi had gone from Kyoto and Ōsaka.
On the twenty-sixth day of the third month, in the first year of Bunroku—Tenshō 20—Hideyoshi left the capital and went west, to enter Nagoya castle in Kyūshū, the base of operations for the Korean war.
But twenty days before Hideyoshi left, the warships bearing Konishi Yukinaga's forces and the rest had already passed the waters off Iki island, bound for Korea. The opening act of the Korean war, the thing Imai Sōkyū loathed above all, had already been raised.
The expeditionary army moved with breathtaking speed. By the time Hideyoshi's train had reached Hiroshima, Konishi's force had already taken Pusan castle; the next day it stormed Tongnae, then pushed on to Yangsan and Miryang, and the din of it filled the sky and earth of southern Korea.
The road from the capital to Nagoya took a month. About the time Hideyoshi entered Nagoya castle in his gorgeous procession, in Korea the Kuroda force had thrust ahead and taken Kimhae castle, and the Katō force had stormed Kyŏngju. The Korean army had no muskets. The victories of the war's first days were dazzling. Before a Japanese army armed with firearms, they could not stand for a moment.
News of these victories reached Tsuzura Jūzō day by day, as he lay idling in the annex of Iseya Kahei, behind the Hōkō-ji in the capital.
The townsfolk of the capital took it, as they took the wars at home, for an affair the warrior houses alone waged, that decided the warrior houses' fate alone, a thing altogether outside their own lives; and yet, to Jūzō's eye, the town seemed somehow astir.
The townsfolk of the capital have always had, toward the rise and fall of the powerful, a cool look and a shrewd instinct all their own.
They had lately marked that Hideyoshi's regime had passed at last into its afternoon; but this victory overseas served, if only for a while, to wipe that impression away.
At such a moment, to put it about that Hideyoshi's regime was in decline would plainly cut against the townsfolk's mood, and so against oneself. And besides—whatever one judged of the regime's staying power—Hideyoshi's own name, among the common people, strangely never lost its shine.
"The man has a rare gift for being loved."
Even Jūzō thought so. The sending of troops to Korea had, among the Sakai merchants like Sōkyū, lowered the regime in men's eyes; but Hideyoshi's own popularity, on the contrary, rose with the victories among the common people.
"At a time like this, there's nothing for it but to lie low."
A crash was plainly on its way. Already, in truth, prices had begun to climb with the vast buying-up of army rice. The day when cries of resentment would fill the land was not far off. And the hour that boiled over would be the hour the power over the realm changed hands.
Jūzō settled himself to wait for that coming season of upheaval.
"With Hideyoshi away, there's no work to be done in any case."
To stir about to no purpose at such a time, he thought, would only end in making easy prey of himself for the Kōga men in Maeda Gen'i's hand.
"Kuroami, for a year or two the thing to do is to take our ease and eat our idle rice. I've sent word to that effect to the Hōin in Sakai as well."
"Shall we take up the tea ceremony, then?"
Kuroami chimed in readily. His own view was the same.
"We could even slip clean out of the capital for a while, go back to Otogi Pass in Iga, and there wait out the age."
"A wise notion. With the Kōga men, and Master Gohei, and the lord Jirōzaemon, and this girl they call Kohagi—so many getting in the way, the work goes hard; and clearing out would throw their schemes off the scent besides. But I'll stay. It wouldn't do to cut the thread that ties me to the capital."
"Ho—then we'll be hard put to cook our rice. Shall I carry off some fine woman to keep house?"
To Jūzō, saying it to tease him—
"You wicked bug."
Kuroami made a face like a man who has bitten down on a bitter beetle.
