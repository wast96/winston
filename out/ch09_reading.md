## Chapter Nine

The next day was the famous "May Thirtieth Memorial," only two days short of the old-calendar Dragon-Boat Festival. Shanghai folk of Feng Yunqing's stamp were busy enough, to be sure, scraping together the money to keep the festival, busy with their "immortal's leaps" and their crawlings through dog-holes; but there were many others busy about something wholly different: the May Thirtieth commemorative demonstration! For some days past, on the telegraph-poles of every road in Shanghai, and on the compound walls of the great mansions and foreign villas, slogans of every kind had been written up in profusion, and the place of the demonstration was openly proclaimed: Nanjing Road, that street of historic meaning.

The military and police authorities of the three jurisdictions—the Chinese, the French, and the International Settlement—had held a joint-defence conference beforehand. On the morning of "May Thirtieth," toward nine o'clock, along Nanjing Road, along the Bund, and as far as the far end of North Sichuan Road—a good five miles of thoroughfare—the Municipal Police of the International Settlement had thrown out a close net of pickets; armed constables, and patrols of motor-bicycles mounted with light machine-guns, were never for a moment out of one another's sight. At the important points, moreover, tall armoured cars squatted in the middle of the street, the muzzles of their machine-guns trained on the crowded crossroads where the passers-by thronged thick.

At the western end of Nanjing Road, the quarter commonly called Nichengqiao, the great tall horses of the mounted patrols pawed and tossed their manes among the vehicles and the foot-passengers, and now and then flecked white foam from their mouths.

Just then, on the footway of Xizang Road, on the side nearest the Racecourse, two men and a woman, none of them above twenty or so, were walking slowly northward; as they walked they glanced this way and that, and from time to time exchanged a word or two. The two men both wore Western dress; one of them was in pale grey, very much the gentleman, the creases of his trousers flat and straight; the other, in a dark navy serge, was by no means so presentable, his trouser-legs crumpled into the shape of sausages; the woman wore a long gown of peacock-green voile lined with white Indian silk. In that place, at that hour, and being besides three young people whose clothes did not match, they were—it goes without saying—a little conspicuous.

They came to the great gate of the Xin Shijie Hotel and there stopped. A mounted patrol, three to a file, was just then passing before them, and the morning sunlight, striking the carbine-barrels slung aslant on the patrolmen's shoulders, gave off a bluish glint. The three young people stood there watching the backs of the mounted men until they were out of sight. Suddenly the girl of the three, with something of an impatient air, said:

"Which way are we to go? Up and down this same road—this is the third time already! How—dull it is! Let us stand and wait in one spot; but there again you, Boqing, say it won't do. And besides, it is close on half past nine now, and there's not a sign of anything stirring. The constables are on such strict guard! It looks as though today's demonstration will come to nothing, doesn't it?"

"Don't cry out so loud, Susu! There are three-stripes coming across the way."

"Hmph! Zhisheng, if you're so timid, what need had you to come out! But—Mr. Bo, are you quite sure you haven't got the time and the place wrong?"

"There's no mistake! Little Cai told me plainly enough—it starts from Nichengqiao, drives straight down Nanjing Road, on to the Bund, then turns into North Sichuan Road, and breaks up at the park rifle-range. The time is ten o'clock. Don't fret, Miss Zhang; there's still half an hour to go!"

It was the young man with the sausage trouser-legs who answered. His name was Boqing, and he was a fellow-student of Wu Zhisheng's. They had by now been standing more than five minutes in that spot when two big fellows of the plain-clothes-detective sort sidled up beside them, their dark shifty eyes fixed on them all the while. Zhang Susu was the first to feel it; she gave a tug at Boqing's sleeve, turned, and walked a few steps west, and as she neared the side gate of the racecourse she turned her head and said to Wu Zhisheng and Boqing, who had followed her:

"See them? Those two in the long black gowns. For all the world as if they'd been printed off the same block as the bodyguards at Sunfu's house."

As she spoke she could not help bursting into a laugh. Wearied as she was of ordinary life, she found the business before her rather good sport; and the boredom that had risen in her from pacing three times up and down the road without seeing anything out of the way now dispersed as well. Yesterday afternoon, when she had heard Wu Zhisheng tell of a certain Boqing who was pulling him in to join the demonstration, she had promised herself no end of excitement, no end of ardour; she had hardly slept a wink all night, and this morning had run round early to Zhisheng's college to hurry them out; and in that fervour of hers not only was Wu Zhisheng left far behind—even Boqing seemed hard put to keep up.

Wu Zhisheng and the others turned to look; the two fellows in the long black gowns had already vanished, but a great motor-car, all painted red, something like the money-vans that the banks use, was now drawn up in that place. In a moment this red car too drove off. The sound of its horn was strangely hideous, like the hooting of an owl.

"That's the car they've got ready for taking people up!"

Boqing told Zhang Susu, and as he spoke a graver look came over his face. Zhang Susu smiled and did not answer, but watched most intently the passers-by coming and going at the crossing of Nanjing Road and Xizang Road; it seemed to her that among these hurrying people there were many who had come on purpose to demonstrate, come to this starting-point to await the signal. A wave of heat spread little by little from within her breast, and her face was a little flushed.

Wu Zhisheng too was looking about him. He wondered secretly why he saw not one fellow-student he knew. He glanced at the great clock on the high tower of the Racecourse away to the west—it was still only nine-forty. All at once he felt hungry, and turned to look at Boqing, very much wishing to say, "Suppose we go and get a bite to eat first?"; but the words, just at the tip of his tongue, were pressed back, and he substituted on the instant:

"How goes the fighting at the front? Have you had any letter from home?"

"They say it's give-and-take. My home's been battered to bits by the guns, and the whole family have fled to Bengbu. This wicked, wicked warlords' war—"

Boqing had got so far when his eyes gave a glare, and what came after was lost; a bus had stopped in front of them, seven or eight people got down, and the several people who had been standing near them got aboard too, and the bus drove off again, leaving only the three of them there once more. An Indian constable came up, waved them on, and, tapping Boqing lightly on the shoulder with one end of his wooden baton, said, "Go! Go!" So they went east, back to the gate of the Xin Shijie Hotel, and then on southward along Xizang Road.

Now the state of things on this road was very different from before! Four mounted patrolmen were ranged in a line across the middle of the road; the men, firm in the saddle, gazed about on every side, seeming ready, the moment they spied a disturbance anywhere, to charge upon it. From south to north, moreover, came three files of mounted patrols, two to a file, ten-odd zhang apart, driving in wherever the crowd was thickest along the way. A motor-bicycle, with two Western constables aboard, went tearing madly down the road. Then came an armoured car, majestic and terrible, its horn braying like a devil's voice, sounding without cease as it came. Yet the crowds along this road too kept gathering and growing. At the mouths of the five roads that met Xizang Road at right angles were clusters upon clusters of people, forming and scattering, forming and scattering. The Chinese, Western, and Indian constables patrolling to and fro along the road wheeled this way and that, driving the people off with their batons, making show of their pistols. Within the cordon disorder had already begun!

The three of them could not stand still any longer now—stand still and someone came to move them on—so there was nothing for it but to go south. As they neared the door of a leather-goods company, a man of about thirty in Western dress came running from the opposite side, threw out a hand, seized Wu Zhisheng by the shoulder, and cried:

"Ah! Old Zhi! Don't run south! It's dangerous!"

This man's name was Ke Zhongmou; he was a friend of the lawyer Qiu Sun, at present a newspaper reporter, and a familiar caller too at the Wu house.

Before Wu Zhisheng could answer, Zhang Susu had already pushed forward to ask:

"How is it up ahead? Have they taken anyone up?"

"Ha, Miss Zhang, so you've come too? Is it to join the demonstration, or to look on at the fun? If it's to look on at the fun, Miss Zhang, my advice to you is to go home again!"

"That I don't understand at all!"

"And yet I know you understand it very well. A demonstration like this—either one opposes it, or one throws oneself ardently into it and takes the lead. If one comes with a mind merely to look on at the excitement, then better not come at all. Miss Zhang, I'll speak plainly: even if you don't oppose it, you're not likely to have much heart for it either—"

"Then, Mr. Ke, what have you come to do?"

Zhang Susu again cut in with her retort, and her face had changed colour. That way Ke Zhongmou had of taking her for a creature too dainty and timid to bear anything roused her resentment to the last degree! But Ke Zhongmou, unhurried, lifted the snapshot camera he held and flourished it before Zhang Susu's face, and only then answered, smiling:

"I? I am a newspaper reporter; my calling is a free calling, and my standpoint too is the standpoint of liberalism!"

This said, he gave a nod, and, waving his snapshot camera, made his way across the road.

At this Zhang Susu gave a cold laugh, looked at Wu Zhisheng, and looked again at Boqing, as much as to say, "So you too make light of me? Very well—wait, and I'll do something!" At that very moment a disturbance broke out in a knot of people across the road, and a shrill police-whistle rent the air. Zhang Susu started from head to foot, and without so much as a word to her two companions ran off as if she flew, straight across the road, straight toward that turbulent crowd. But before she reached it, the knot of people suddenly parted, disclosing two constables who had raised their batons and were looking for someone on whom to vent their power. Zhang Susu checked her steps in spite of herself and stood there wavering, craning her neck to look. All at once, not far off, a firecracker went off. This was the signal! A great shout of voices came hard upon it, at first, it seemed, from no great number, but at once answered on every side. Zhang Susu felt all the blood in her body come surging up, and her heart pounded. Instinctively she ran forward a few steps, and in her agitation did not know what she ought to do. Then of a sudden she heard a thunder of hooves come charging up from behind like a hurricane; she dodged quickly to one side, saw many people running in confusion, saw the galloping file of mounted patrols scatter a knot of the crowd a little way ahead—yet the crowd gathered again and came pressing straight this way. It was a mixed column of students and workers, scattering handbills as it came, shouting its slogans like a peal of thunder. Zhang Susu's heart all but leapt into her throat; her whole face crimson, her mouth wide open, she could only laugh. All at once, behind her head, there rose a wild roar:

"Down with the warlords' war!—Overthrow—"

Zhang Susu turned her head sharply to look; it was Boqing after all. He shot her one glance, and without a word ran forward and mingled with the ranks of the crowd. By now the crowd had run past before Zhang Susu, with a great body of constables coming up behind, and, further behind still, the armoured cars and the mounted patrols; and the people who were with Zhang Susu all surged northward too. But ahead of them, also, were constables striking out with their batons. At this the whole group broke and scattered in confusion. In the confusion someone caught Zhang Susu by the hand and led her across the road. It was Wu Zhisheng; his face was very ugly to see, yet there was a smile at the corners of his mouth. When the two of them reached the front of the Sun Sun Company, they saw that the main body of the demonstration had already burst through the Nanjing Road–Zhejiang Road crossing and divided into many small columns. Zhang Susu let out a breath, and felt that her heart had ceased its pounding, but was sinking, heavy as lead. She could laugh no longer, and her fingertips were ice-cold. Yet the demonstrators still came on without a break, seven or eight to a column, shouting slogans all along the stretch of the three great department stores on Nanjing Road. Before the Sun Sun Company where Zhang Susu and Wu Zhisheng stood, no small crowd had in a moment gathered again. Out from the direction of Yunnan Road there charged a red car for taking people up, and five or six constables leapt down from it, meaning to round up those gathered before the Sun Sun Company. In her alarm Zhang Susu turned to run into the Sun Sun Company, but the company's clerks bawled out, "Don't come in!" and shut the iron grille as they cried it. By now Wu Zhisheng had leapt out into the middle of the road, and Zhang Susu, hardening her heart, ran across after him; and when, on the footway of the south side of the road, she caught Wu Zhisheng's hand again, both her hands were shaking, and were all over cold sweat.

Handbills lay scattered thick over the ground here, and Wu Zhisheng and Zhang Susu hurried along treading upon them. The police-whistles shrilled one after another. The clamour of voices was so confused that one could not make out what was being cried. The faces of the two of them had wholly changed. Happily, just ahead was the Da San Yuan restaurant, its door still open. Zhang Susu and Wu Zhisheng, stumbling and staggering, dived quickly into the Da San Yuan, and at that moment a great shouting of slogans burst out once more on Nanjing Road. Zhang Susu did not so much as turn her head, but ran straight up to the second floor of the Da San Yuan.

The private rooms were all full of guests. Zhang Susu and her companion were much disappointed. They had meant only to take shelter for a while, but once inside, their appetites were roused. The two of them stood facing each other and knitting their brows. Happily the waiter hit on a plan, and asked them to share a table with a single guest. This guest had come nearly half an hour before, had a room to himself, had eaten scarcely anything, and did nothing but read the newspapers. At first the guest was, it seemed, a little unwilling; but when Zhang Susu edged up to the low door of the room and peeped in, the guest suddenly threw down his newspaper and stood up with a great laugh; it was, after all, none other than Fan Bowen.

With a cry of surprise, Zhang Susu asked, smiling:

"Is it you? All alone!—And what are you hiding in here for?"

"Let me guess: you're not waiting for anyone, nor have you come to settle the question of your stomach—you must have come to gather stuff for poetry—the May Thirtieth commemorative demonstration!"

put in Wu Zhisheng, sitting down below Fan Bowen; and he snatched up the newspapers to read—all of them that day's tabloids, and a more complete set even than the full run one buys on the trains.

Fan Bowen rolled up his eyes and fixed Wu Zhisheng with a stare, then suddenly heaved a sigh, and, turning to Zhang Susu, said:

"A very fine subject, but the performers are no good at all! I've read it, mind you, from beginning to end—would you say this room is badly placed? From Nichengqiao in the west to the Risheng Lou in the east, half a li of demonstration taken in at a glance! And yet, in a poet's name, I say it again: the performers are no good at all! Am I to write only of the constables like monkeys, the armoured cars like tortoises? Of course not! I am no such false poet, to fawn upon power and place! One must, naturally, write of the other side too. When Homer of old wrote that deathless epic the Iliad, he did indeed exert himself to extol the divine valour of the Greek host, yet he did not forget to praise the heroism of Hector; only, in today's affair, the demonstrators' side is no good at all!—But, Susu, my real object in coming here was not this at all; I came for another matter—another matter, and one that has spoiled my spirits too!"

"Does that also belong to your stuff for poetry?"

Zhang Susu, idly pointing here and there with her little finger at the dim-sum list for the waiter to see, asked it offhand. But Fan Bowen at once turned red, sighed a second sigh, gave a reluctant nod, and made no answer. With Fan Bowen this meant, "Ask me again, and I'll tell you!"—but Zhang Susu did not take it so. Following the ordinary usage of polite intercourse, she let the unanswered topic drop and meant to talk again of the demonstration, the demonstration she had herself "taken part" in. But Wu Zhisheng, who knew Fan Bowen's character best of all, suddenly let go of the newspaper, gave Fan Bowen a smart slap on the shoulder, and said, as if in threat:

"Poet, tell the honest truth! What are you doing, hiding in here all furtive and alone?"

Fan Bowen shrugged his shoulders and smiled a wry smile, looking exceedingly put out. Zhang Susu laughed, but felt a little sorry for him too, and was just about to turn the talk aside with some remark, when suddenly, on the partition-wall that connected with the next room, someone came tapping, and a woman's stifled, giggling voice, half laughing, half questioning, said:

"Is that Susu?"

It was plainly Lin Peishan's voice. Fan Bowen's face grew redder still, and Wu Zhisheng laughed aloud.

Zhang Susu, who seemed to have grasped the secret in it too, slid her glance across Fan Bowen's face and ran out; and after a little while she came in hand in hand with Lin Peishan, and behind them followed a man—it was Du Xintuo, his walking-stick hung over his arm, his straw hat in his hand.

The moment she was in, Lin Peishan, as though soft and languid and weak, leaned on Zhang Susu's shoulder, and, eyeing Fan Bowen aslant from behind Zhang Susu's loose black hair, said:

"Bowen! I mean to make you a present of a box of visiting-cards, with the title printed on them: Pastoral Poet and Detective-Novelist! Well?"

As she spoke, she gave a coquettish, giggling laugh. And they all fell to laughing too, Fan Bowen himself among them. All at once his spirits rose again; first he set his right palm flat and upright against his breast and made a slight bow toward Lin Peishan, after the fashion of the monks at their salutations, then shook hands with Du Xintuo and asked, smiling:

"And you, Old Tuo? What do you give me?"

"I—give you a copy of Love's Labour's Lost, that masterpiece of Shakespeare's."

Du Xintuo answered with a great air of ease, and a cold, subtle smile to go with it. He had changed today into Chinese clothes: over his spare, slender frame he wore a lined long gown of navy-blue mao-ge silk, with much the manner of a well-born idler of the old school. Fan Bowen knitted his brows a little, but then, with a look as of gratitude, gave a smile and said:

"I hope that in this masquerade of ours I shall not mistake the partner of my heart."

"That would be well. But I may as well tell you—I am a newcomer, and cannot yet be reckoned to have joined that masked ball of yours!"

So saying, Du Xintuo and Fan Bowen both burst out laughing, as if at some understanding between them. Just then Lin Peishan and Zhang Susu were talking together with extraordinary animation. Wu Zhisheng sat facing the two of them and nodded now and again. Zhang Susu was giving a full account of how she had come to join the demonstration, and how she had escaped danger. Though at the moment, being in the thick of it, she had not only had her spell of "not knowing what she ought to do," but had had both hands shaking and had broken out in cold sweat, yet now, calling it to mind, she remembered only how, at the moment when she saw that the file of mounted patrols could not scatter the main body of the demonstration, and that the main body had on the contrary burst through the cordon and driven straight to Nanjing Road, she had been moved, how her blood had boiled, and how she had laughed wildly, and how she had taken no heed at all of the mounted patrol charging and sweeping past like mad things at her very side. Her face flushed again, her eyes flashed with an excited light, and her speech was quick and keen and full of spirit. Lin Peishan opened her eyes wide, laid her hand on Zhang Susu's, broke in all at once on Susu's account, and cried in a shrill voice:

"Aiyo! Su, it's too dreadful! Was it one of those great tall horses ridden by a red-turbaned Ah-San that came charging up behind you? Oh, oh, oh—Zhisheng, did you see the horse's head graze the top of Su's head, and all but trample her down? Ai, Su—oh!"

Wu Zhisheng nodded, and laughed too, greatly excited.

Zhang Susu, however, did not laugh; her face was very grave. She took the printed silk kerchief that Lin Peishan wore as an ornament at her breast, wiped her own forehead with it, and was just about to go on, when Lin Peishan cut in again with a question, gripping Zhang Susu's two hands the tighter as she did so:

"Su! Your companion just shouted out a slogan like that! Tsk, tsk! Did the constables chase you as far as the door of the Sun Sun Company? And your companion was taken up there and then?"

As she spoke, Lin Peishan turned her eyes again to Wu Zhisheng's face. Wu Zhisheng had not truly heard what it was, and nodded as before. Zhang Susu, not knowing the rights of it, saw Wu Zhisheng confirm Boqing's arrest, and of a sudden cried out, sprang up, threw her arms round Lin Peishan's head and shook it wildly, crying again and again:

"One sacrificed! One sacrificed! Counting only those we saw with our own eyes, those we knew, there's one already! Ai, how grand! How heroic! To have burst through the constables, the mounted patrols, the armoured cars, the close-laid cordon layer upon layer! Ai, I shall never, never forget today!"

"I too saw two or three people taken up! And of these there was one whom I dare swear was a mere passer-by, no way concerned in it."

This was Fan Bowen speaking to Du Xintuo across the table, with a sigh for no cause. Du Xintuo nodded coldly and did not open his mouth. Fan Bowen turned his head to glance at Zhang Susu, and, seeing this young lady worked up beyond all measure by her own ardent recollections, could not forbear sighing again, and said in a loud voice:

"Everything has fallen into decay! Even the mass movement has decayed past all believing. I took part in person in the famous May Thirtieth movement of five years ago, and in those days—ai, 'The world is world, and man is man!' Ai—in those days, in those days, the crowds held Nanjing Road the livelong day! That was what could be called a demonstration! But today, they merely charge through it! 'Having once known the sea, one can make nothing of lesser waters'—to tell the honest truth, I find today's demonstration too flat and stale!"

Zhang Susu and Lin Peishan both turned their faces to gaze at Fan Bowen, dumbfounded. The two of them had come into the world a little too late, and had never lived to see the grandeur and heroism of that time; hearing Fan Bowen's tall talk, they were half in doubt and half in belief, and could not get a word out. Fan Bowen was the more pleased with himself, his eyes fixed on the sky beyond the window, as if dazzled and intoxicated by the heroic grandeur of his recollections; when all at once a man beside him let out a few cold laughs—it was Wu Zhisheng, silent this good while, come now to cross swords with Fan Bowen:

"Bowen, I sympathize with you—everything has indeed fallen into decay! And one proof of it is yourself!—Five years ago you took part in a demonstration, but today you sit up here on high on the second floor of the Da San Yuan restaurant, hoping to follow in the footsteps of the Emperor Nero, and take his refined pleasure in climbing to a height to watch the burning of Rome!"

Fan Bowen slowly turned his face back, gave Wu Zhisheng a faint, unconcerned smile, but gazed the more eagerly at Zhang Susu and Lin Peishan, as if to ask, "Can it be that you too hold this view?" The two young women looked at each other and smiled, and neither made a sound. At this Fan Bowen was somewhat put out of countenance. Happily Du Xintuo now joined in the talk:

"Even to hold Nanjing Road the livelong day would be no such great matter! A thing like this happens often enough abroad. The people of the great cities are restless by nature, and love a rumpus—"

"You call it a rumpus? Ai!—"

Zhang Susu demanded indignantly, and shook Lin Peishan's shoulder hard again. But Du Xintuo answered coldly and with decision:

"Yes—I hold it to be nothing but a rumpus. Turn over the whole history of every land, ancient and modern, and there is not one country that ever grew rich and strong by these so-called demonstrations. Such doings—the gathering of a mob to make disturbance—are plainly no more than the momentary impulse of an uneducated people! More than enough to spoil a business, and not enough to bring one off!"

"Then, Mr. Tuo, how do you think one should set about it, to have enough to bring a business off and not enough to spoil it?"

Wu Zhisheng got in ahead of Zhang Susu, and gave a hard pull at Zhang Susu's wrist. Du Xintuo smiled and did not answer, but only pursed his lips and went whistling, low, the tune of the Marseillaise. Fan Bowen stared in astonishment. Lin Peishan laughed to herself on one side. Zhang Susu puffed out her small cheeks, turned her face to Wu Zhisheng, and said:

"What need to ask him any more! His remedy is bound to be the same as their Old Sixth's—Xueshi's precious 'iron hand' policy. It's bound to be that!"

"Just wrongly guessed, Miss Zhang. My settled conviction is that for a country like China there is fundamentally no remedy at all."

So said Du Xintuo, still smiling. No sooner were the words out of his mouth than they drew a great outcry from both Zhang Susu and Wu Zhisheng. But Fan Bowen reached over and gave Du Xintuo a pat on the shoulder, and flourished a raised thumb before his face. At that very moment the waiter brought in the dim-sum, and, all unawares, Fan Bowen's hand swept outward and all but knocked the whole lot to the floor. Lin Peishan could hold her laughter in no longer; she laughed aloud, steadying herself against the chair with her left hand and rubbing her stomach with her right.

"Bowen, you—"

Zhang Susu glared at Fan Bowen and cried out. But the next thing Fan Bowen said to Du Xintuo turned Zhang Susu's anger into laughter again:

"Old Tuo, you and your uncle Xueshi, Old Sixth, are just such a pair as one seldom meets with. He is too hot, you are too cold; the one cold, the one hot, and both come out of your honourable house!"

"Many thanks for the compliment. It's summer already now; a little cold is the better thing.—Do have some dim-sum! This, on the other hand, is a thing one should take while it's hot."

So saying, Du Xintuo gave a dry laugh, sat down, and fell to eating the dim-sum. Zhang Susu seemed to have turned the whole of her pent-up anger upon the dim-sum; she snatched up a steamed bun, took a fierce bite out of it, then threw it down again, and asked Fan Bowen in high dudgeon:

"And you? Neither cold nor hot, I suppose?"

"To him it's all nothing but stuff for poetry. Cold, hot, people taken up, blood shed—it's all stuff for poetry!"

Wu Zhisheng, seeing his chance, made game of Fan Bowen once more. It was true that he was still more out of sorts with Du Xintuo; but he supposed that to mock Fan Bowen directly was to strike at Du Xintuo indirectly; he supposed that between Du and Fan there was but a difference of degree. From what time this view of his had arisen he did not himself know; but ever since the fact that the two of them, Du and Fan, were rivals for Lin Peishan had grown day by day more plain, this fixed prejudice of his had thickened by degrees. Having just now given Fan Bowen his prick, he shifted his eyes at once from Du Xintuo's face to Lin Peishan. Du Xintuo still did not change countenance, but chewed carefully, head to one side, at the dim-sum in his mouth; while Lin Peishan, her slender waist slightly bent, leaned against the back of the chair on which Zhang Susu sat, lost by herself in a brown study.

Fan Bowen paid no heed to Wu Zhisheng's gibe, but sat down close beside Zhang Susu, and suddenly sighed again and said in a low voice:

"I am one who, seeing heat, grows hot, but who, seeing cold, does not necessarily grow cold. I am fond of dropping a few witty sayings, but within, my heart is uncommonly grave; I often long to do some serious, earnest thing, I crave some happening to give me a stir! Why did you not come this morning to call me to go along with you? Can it be you had decided that I would not go and demonstrate together with you?—Er, that companion of yours, taken up perhaps—I should very much like to know him."

Zhang Susu laughed, and, taking up a dumpling to eat instead, answered:

"Now that's rightly said. If you didn't say so earlier, who was to know you meant to come along too! But there's one thing—"

Coming to a stop on these words, Zhang Susu suddenly lifted her face to look at Lin Peishan, who leaned wearily against the chair-back with fixed, dreaming eyes, and smiled a queer smile; and just as some sly words were about to come out, Lin Peishan had already turned red. Zhang Susu laughed the louder. All at once Du Xintuo took up his chopsticks and tapped lightly on the table, a cold, faint smile floating at the corners of his mouth, and began to chant aloud an old Chinese poem:

"A face is like a flash of lightning, the seasons like a gusting wind;

the grass turns green, then frost turns white; the sun goes west, the moon comes east;

fine temples cannot bear the autumn, and in a rush turn to withered fleabane!……

The gentleman is changed to gibbon and to crane, the mean man to sand and to insect—"

Zhang Susu, hearing it, drew her brows together and gave a soft snort down her nose. Just then the low door of the room swung suddenly open, and a man stood framed in it, on either side of his big nose a pair of near-sighted spectacles like the thick bottoms of glass tumblers jutting out upon a head thrust forward, a most ludicrous figure. This man was Li Yuting. He seemed not yet to have made out how many people were in the room, nor who they were. Zhang Susu, caught off guard by Li Yuting, felt somewhat ill at ease. Du Xintuo, chanting his poem, saw him too, laid down his chopsticks, and rose to greet him, throwing Zhang Susu a grinning glance the while as he asked Li Yuting:

"Professor Li, how come you here too? When did you arrive? You've been newly taking Fan Bowen for a master, I suppose, and learning to write detective stories!"

"Old Tuo, for that you deserve a slap on the mouth!"

Seeing Zhang Susu change colour of a sudden, Fan Bowen made haste to put in the word first, and shot Du Xintuo another glare. Li Yuting did not perceive that there were barbs in their talk, and made no answer; he edged a cautious, timid step forward, and, heaping his whole face with smiles, said:

"Ah, all five of you! Have you taken refuge in here too? The streets are so full of people, and the constables are past reason, and my eyes being none too good besides, it was truly most dangerous just now—"

"What! Has the demonstration not broken up yet?"

Wu Zhisheng asked in a great hurry, still chewing dim-sum in his mouth.

"It has not broken up. As my carriage went past Dongxinqiao, I ran into a column of two or three hundred, with foreign bottles and stones for weapons, in a set-to with the constables. At some point or other, someone flung a handful of handbills into my carriage. My one thought then was to tell the coachman to hurry on; how was I to know that, just as we neared Daxin Street, we should run into the constables again, chasing the demonstrators—ai, that sheaf of handbills in the carriage brought disaster on me! I produced my card, but still the constables would not let me go. I went and spoke to the three-stripes on patrol, but that was no use either. In the end they took my hired coachman and the carriage too into the police station. I must own they treated me with quite exceptional favour, and did not detain me. Nanjing Road is still tense now, the crowds forming and scattering everywhere, and all the big shops have shut their iron grilles—"

Li Yuting had got so far when he was suddenly cut short; Fan Bowen threw back his head with a great laugh, pointed with one hand at Wu Zhisheng and with the other at Zhang Susu, and was just about to report on their behalf how they too had "met with danger," having besides some most ingenious witticisms already got ready, when all at once a great uproar of shouting rose from the road beyond the window, followed by a confused trampling of feet that broke out through all the private rooms on this second floor of the Da San Yuan, and in a moment came surging to the head of the stairs. Fan Bowen's heart failed him, his face changed, and the words would not come out; he ducked down, and, without knowing it, was minded actually to crawl under the table; by this time Zhang Susu had already run to the window to peer out, Wu Zhisheng following behind. Li Yuting stood there in his agitation wringing his hands. Lin Peishan shrank into the corner of the room, her eyes stretched wide, her mouth half open, wishing to speak but unable to get a word out.

Du Xintuo alone seemed still able to keep his usual bearing; though his face had turned a livid white, a wry smile still forced itself out at the corners of his lips.

"Devil take it! It's nothing. The people have all scattered."

Zhang Susu ran back and said it, as if much disappointed. She turned her face, saw the look on Lin Peishan, and could not help laughing. Peishan craned her neck and asked:

"Whatever was it! Su—aren't you afraid of stopping a stray bullet!"

Zhang Susu shook her head; and no one could make out whether this shake of the head meant that she was not afraid of stray bullets, or that she did not know what the nature of the uproar in the street really was. Uneasy still, Lin Peishan sent her eyes to question Du Xintuo; she had noticed just now that Du Xintuo seemed the most composed of them all, and the first to reckon that no trouble would come of it.

"Whatever it is, let it be! At any rate there'll be no trouble. I have faith in the foreigners' power to keep order! And I even think the Settlement authorities are in too great a fluster; guarding it so strictly only stirs up alarm in people's minds."

Du Xintuo said it looking straight at Lin Peishan and Zhang Susu, putting on an air of caring for nothing at all.

Li Yuting, listening, only shook his head. He had always held Du Xintuo to be a pleasure-loving young gentleman who knew nothing of the gravity of things, and now he was the surer of it. He could not forbear stepping forward and saying to Du Xintuo, very gravely:

"Don't be too sanguine. Shanghai just now is beset with hidden perils too. Consider—the price of rice has shot up to over twenty dollars a picul, and everything is dear; and since March, the tram-men, the bus-men, the cotton-mill hands, one strike has followed hard upon another. The Communists have a plan for a general uprising in May—"

"And has it come off, then? Today is the thirtieth of May!"

"True, May may be said to be over, but the peril is not over! On the two railways, the Longhai and the Pinghan, the fighting grows fiercer and fiercer; the Zhang-Gui armies too have already moved out toward Hunan; young Zhang's attitude is unclear, and the whole of China is to be drawn into the mêlée. On the borders of Jiangsu and Zhejiang, in the Wenzhou–Taizhou region of Zhejiang, in the Ningbo–Shaoxing country even, in the two Hu, in Jiangxi, in Fujian, everywhere the peasants are in tumult, and the bands of brigands great and small that have raised the Communist flag are past all counting. Along the Yangtze, from Wuxue to Shashi, red flags cover the hills and the wilds—and a few days ago your own honourable district too broke into disorder, when two companies of a garrison battalion mutinied and made common cause with the Communist bandits. Let the fighting not cease for a single day, and the Communists' activity spreads wider by a day. June, July—this greatest peril of all is still to come—"

"And yet Shanghai—"

"Oh, even in Shanghai the peril deepens with every day. Within these last few days it has come to light that Communists have got in among the troops near Shanghai, that Communist handbills and small cells have been found in the garrison stationed in Shanghai itself, and that a large part of them, it is said, is very unsteady. Among the arsenal workers, too, there is secret organizing. Today, May Thirtieth, the Settlement side guards so strictly, and yet still there is a demonstration, and the constables' cordon has been burst through by them—and you still say the Settlement authorities are in too great a fluster?"

Li Yuting's voice sank lower and lower as he spoke, yet to those who heard it the words rang the louder and shriller in their ears. Du Xintuo's brows drew slowly together, and he said no more; a red flush rose over Zhang Susu's face, and her eyes flashed, as though her ardour were on the leap. Wu Zhisheng gave a tug at Fan Bowen's sleeve, and said, in what seemed still a mocking tone, and yet seemed to be said in earnest:

"Just wait, Bowen! There'll be a theme for your poetry now!"

Fan Bowen, however, actually nodded gravely, turned his face to fix his gaze on Li Yuting, and was about to say something, when Lin Peishan had already got in ahead:

"Shanghai, surely, is safe enough? With the Settlement—"

Before Li Yuting could answer, Du Xintuo across from him put in:

"Safe enough! At the least, tomorrow, the day after, next week, next month, the month after that, will all still be safe enough! And not Shanghai only—at the least Tianjin, Hankou, Guangzhou, Macau, several great commercial ports, in the next several-times-over months, will all still be safe enough! And failing that, Japan, France, America—surely those, at any rate, cannot come to be unsafe! The places left us for our leisure and our pleasure are many yet—safe enough!"

Lin Peishan gave a giggling laugh, and her heart was set at ease. She was a lively, pleasure-loving girl, and with the intoxicating loveliness of spring before her, how should she bring herself to bear needless fright over some unknowable danger in the future? But the cares of the others were of another kind. Li Yuting gazed at Du Xintuo a while in astonishment, then looked again at Wu Zhisheng and Fan Bowen, as if seeking someone with whom he might talk in earnest. At last he sighed softly and said:

"Hm—at this rate of fighting, fighting, fighting on; at this rate, when front and rear alike, and government, and trade, and the schools, are all split into parties and factions—then the day of the general collapse, I fear, cannot be very far off! The White Russians lost their state power, but still had a place to flee to as exiles; when it comes our turn, I fear it will not do! And by that time, the whole world in revolution, the whole world's bourgeoisie—"

He could say no more, but hung his head and brooded. He was much grieved at the internal treachery and strife between the party-and-government authorities and the magnates of society; and he himself bore just now a mission—he had been dispatched by Wu Sunfu to seek out Zhao Botao and negotiate over a certain matter, a certain dispute over the rights of the two sides. Ever since he had seen the demonstrators just now at Dongxinqiao, he had kept turning over in his mind that one old saw: fear not that the enemy is strong, fear only that a rift should open in your own line. And now, in his pessimism, he felt that this very rift was widening and deepening in step with the enemy's advance!

All at once a wild laugh startled Li Yuting out of his musing. It was Du Xintuo, who, his back against the door-frame, laughed coldly and murmured softly to himself:

"Then let us take our pleasure, and ask nothing of tomorrow: rich wine and lovely women—to drown ourselves deep in the fine wine, and lose our souls in the soft, warm embrace!"

Then all at once he lifted his voice and cried:

"Look, all of you, at this bewitching weather! Is it not too great a waste of the fair scene to moulder in here! I know of some White Russian exiles who have newly opened a pleasure-garden, called the Liwalida, where there is fine wine, and music, and princesses and duchesses and noble consorts and famous beauties of old Russia hurrying to wait upon one; where the green shade of great trees is like a canopy, and the fragrant grass like a carpet! Where there is a bay of green water, and pleasure-boats!—Ai, snow-white bosoms, snow-white legs—I am put in mind of the joys beside the Seine, I am put in mind of the fire-like ardour of the girls of France!"

As he spoke, he turned and took his straw hat and walking-stick from the clothes-hook on the partition; seeing that his proposal met with no answer, he seemed for a moment taken aback, but at once smiled coldly, went up to Lin Peishan, put out his hand, and, with a slight bow from the waist, said:

"Miss Lin, if you would like to go home, I'll ask Miss Zhang to keep you company—"

Lin Peishan smiled a bewildered smile, threw another rapid, sidelong glance at the four of them—Zhang Susu and the others—then, as though making up her mind, nodded her head, and, leaning on Du Xintuo's arm, went off.

At this Wu Zhisheng gave Fan Bowen a wink. But Fan Bowen, to the general surprise, gave a complacent smile, turned, and, looking at Li Yuting, said:

"Yuting, one cannot but say that this university-professor talk of yours is so much rubbish! Your dire warnings and honest remonstrances cannot make Xintuo take thought for danger in the day of ease; on the contrary, they have made him resolve to seize the pleasure of the hour—wine tonight, and tonight let us be drunk! A poor return for all your long sighing and your weeping bitter tears!"

"Rot! What's the use of talking of it! Let's go to North Sichuan Road. Zhisheng, didn't Boqing say they were to break up at North Sichuan Road?"

Zhang Susu cried it out, glanced at the dishes on the table, took out a banknote and tossed it into a dish, and turned to go. Wu Zhisheng followed her out. Fan Bowen hesitated a moment, then cried out "Wait a bit, wait a bit," gave Li Yuting another smile, and went flying downstairs too.

Li Yuting leaned at the window and gazed out with all the strength of his eyes. The people in the road had thinned a little; Wu Zhisheng and Fan Bowen, one on either side of Zhang Susu, had gone off eastward, gesticulating as they went. A doubt hovered in his mind a good while: what were these three going to North Sichuan Road to do?… Though he had not caught Zhang Susu's last words clearly, yet the look on her face was plain to see; and besides, he had had experience of her temper and her turn of thought. "Is this not the inevitable splitting-apart of the present age?" he brooded gloomily, and felt his heart grow heavier by degrees. At last he shook his head, as though putting it all from him, looked down once more at the state of things in the street, and himself left the Da San Yuan restaurant.

He went westward. At the door of the Hua'an Building he looked at the watch on his wrist—it was half past ten already—and went in, and took the lift straight up to the fifth floor. In the corridor he took out his own visiting-card, wrote a few words on it, and gave it to an attendant. After a long while the white-clad attendant came at last to usher him into an elegant guest-room facing full upon the Racecourse—an inner and an outer room, a suite of two, with a bathroom attached besides.

The door leading to the bathroom stood half open, and steam laden with a heavy perfume filled this inner-and-outer suite, so that a film rose at once over the thick glass of Li Yuting's near-sighted spectacles, and all was a blur of white through which he could see nothing clearly. He seemed to see a snow-white, furred human shape flash before him and dart into the room on the right that served as a bedchamber; and as the shape went past there floated from it a brain-piercing perfume and a giggling, seductive laugh. Li Yuting, at a loss, put up his hand to wipe his spectacles, steadied himself, and looked again. Seated in the sofa before him was none other than Zhao Botao, in a bathrobe of coarse-rice colour, buried sideways in the sofa in the shape of a silver ingot, his face turned aside, his two legs cocked up over the arm of the sofa, showing the coarse black hair thick upon them; needless to say, he too had but just come from his bath.

Zhao Botao did not rise, but gave Li Yuting a careless nod, put out his right hand a little way by way of greeting, and then, turning his face toward the bedroom door, called:

"Yuying!—Come out! Come and meet this Mr. Li here. He is near-sighted, and just now can't have seen you clearly.—Eh, no need to dress yourself up; come out just as you are!"

Li Yuting opened his mouth wide in astonishment, not understanding the drift of this behaviour of Zhao Botao's. But the woman with the strange perfume all about her had already come out, smiling and swaying her waist. A great sheet of snow-white towelling was draped over her like the cassock of a monk; she threw out her bosom and came skipping over, her uncommonly high breasts leaping beneath the towelling. She had a small round face, and lips of so vivid a red that even when she was angry they seemed to be smiling. Zhao Botao, smiling a little, shot Li Yuting a sharp sidelong glance, put out his hand, and gave the woman's plump buttock a pinch.

"Ai-yo…"

The woman gave a coquettish, affected cry. Zhao Botao laughed aloud, and taking advantage of it pushed and turned her lower body, making her turn round gracefully in a circle, and another circle, then gave her a hard shove and said, as if in command:

"Enough! Off with you! Go and dress yourself—and shut the door!"

As though, having shown off a precious jewel before others, he then wrapped it away safe in its many folds again, Zhao Botao only now turned his face to Li Yuting and said:

"Well, Yuting? Ha, go and look at yourself in the glass—you've turned red in the face! Ha ha, you truly make a wonder of what's a small matter! People say I, old Zhao, am fond of my sport; quite true, I like this style of thing. In whatever I do I like to do it straight out. I don't care to have people guess this and guess that, and take me for some monster with I-don't-know-how-many secrets. Just now, the moment you came in, you saw there was a woman here. Your eyes are bad, you didn't see clearly. In your heart you were making your guesses. I know it. Now you've seen clearly, eh? Perhaps you even know her—wouldn't you say she's not bad? The skin and the figure of a Western woman!"

Breaking off all at once, Zhao Botao shook himself and stood up, took a cigar from the cigar-box and set it between his lips, then pushed the box toward Li Yuting with a gesture of "Pray, help yourself," and buried himself in the sofa once more, cocked up his legs, and slowly struck a match and lit the cigar. His manner was as of a man without a care in the world, given up wholly to his ease and comfort. Li Yuting did not smoke, but with his hand resting on the edge of the cigar-box he fingered it softly and mechanically a while, much in two minds within as to how he might not fail the mission Wu Sunfu had charged him with, and yet not give offence to old Zhao. He waited for old Zhao to speak first. He felt it best not to set himself up at the outset in the character of "special envoy for the negotiation," not to make himself out plainly of "the Wu party." But Zhao Botao only went on smoking, saying not a word, nor did his glance rest much on Li Yuting's face. About five minutes passed, and Li Yuting could hold out no longer, and resolved to venture a few probing words first:

"Elder Bo, did you see Sunfu yesterday?"

Zhao Botao shook his head, took the cigar from his lips, and seemed about to speak. But he put out his hand, flicked off the ash, and set it between his lips again.

"Sunfu's native place has suffered a bandit calamity, and he has taken some losses by it, so that his spirits are low, and in some matters he is close upon rash and hasty; for instance, in the two matters he disputes with you, Elder Bo—the accounts of the bond settlement, and Zhu Yinqiu's mortgage-loan—these were, from the first—"

Li Yuting dragged out this word "were," watching Zhao Botao's expression closely; his first thought had been to say "were, from the first, small matters," but on the instant he felt that would not do, and meant to change it to "were, from the first, sure to admit of some way of compromise"; but in the very moment of this hesitation his words were cut short by Zhao Botao.

"Oh, oh, is it those two matters that make Sunfu feel put out? Ah, easily settled! But, Yuting, are you here today to negotiate with me bearing Sunfu's terms, or to feel out my drift?"

Taken off guard by this "straight-out way," Li Yuting was somewhat put out; he had indeed come bearing terms, and was charged too with the task of feeling out the drift, but now that Zhao Botao had blurted it all out in a breath, this made things awkward, and besides, placed as he was between the two great ones, he must, for his own interest's sake, in the end be round and smooth on both sides. So he gave a smile and made haste to answer:

"Not—so. You and Sunfu, Elder Bo, are old friends; whatever there is to say, you may very well say face to face; what need to put me in between—"

"Just so! Then, Yuting, you must certainly have come to feel out my drift! Very well, I'll tell you honestly. This person that I am, I like to do my business straight out!"

Zhao Botao cut short Li Yuting's words again, his glittering eyes shooting straight into Li Yuting's face.

"For you to be so straight out, Elder Bo, could not be bettered."

Driven to the point where he simply could not turn round, Li Yuting could only say this, half complaining within that Zhao Botao would make so little allowance for a man, and half feeling too, of himself, that to think to play it clever before old Zhao was a great and grievous blunder. He ought at once to change his tactics! But Zhao Botao, as though he had seen clean through Li Yuting's mind, suddenly threw back his head with a great laugh, stood up, clapped Li Yuting on the shoulder, and said:

"Yuting, we too are old friends—whatever there is to say, out with it. I have no secrets. It's just as with women—if Sunfu had a woman he was fond of, he wouldn't necessarily be willing to show her to all the world. Ai, Yuting, do you want to look at her again? Have a look at her when she's dressed and got up!—Diu na ma, you old rogue! You know I've little liking for another man's cast-off woman, but this is the exception; she is no mortal, she's a bewitching sprite that steals a man's soul away!"

"You are famed for taking in all comers and rejecting none. That too is a style one cannot do without!"

Li Yuting felt he could not but say this to humour him, though within he was anxious again, in dread lest Zhao Botao slip past the serious business once more; happily it was not so, for Zhao Botao gave an approving smile, went back to his sofa, and himself took up the matter of the "dispute" between him and Sunfu, and his own attitude in it:

"All the past business you understand—we'll not speak of it; my say now is in a few plain words: on the bond side, the dividing of the accounts, I'll go by Zhuzhai's first proposal, and pass it over easily enough; only on Zhu Yinqiu's side, the mortgage-loan—I've given him my word by mouth already, and cannot alter it, unless Zhu Yinqiu of his own will consents to cancel the former agreement."

Li Yuting watched Zhao Botao's face, weighing the weight of his every word, and felt at the same time that the present negotiation was exceedingly thorny. The one point on which Zhao Botao stood firm was the very focus on which Wu Sunfu would not give ground. Wu Sunfu, who had taken sundry losses in the peasant rising of his native place, meant not only to swallow up Zhu Yinqiu's filature cheap by way of compensation, but to seize Zhu Yinqiu's great store of cocoons cheaper still, and hurry the reeling of the futures-silk he had sold off, in the hope of profiting yet, in the very tumult of the filature-silk's falling price: all this Li Yuting understood very well. But Zhao Botao's glittering eyes seemed to have seen clean through to the crux of it too. He had gripped Wu Sunfu by his vital spot; he would sooner lose twenty or thirty thousand on the "bond-account division"! Li Yuting brooded a while, and only then, with a soft breath, answered:

"But what Sunfu's side is set upon is precisely Zhu Yinqiu's mortgage-loan; Elder Bo, permit me to put in a little of a third party's opinion—"

"Ha, I know why Sunfu sets such store by Zhu Yinqiu's mortgage-loan—I know there are a few phrases in that loan-contract of theirs that speak of Zhu Yinqiu's great store of dried cocoons!"

Zhao Botao cut short Li Yuting's speech, slapped his thigh, and laughed aloud.

Li Yuting started, and a film of cold sweat actually broke out down his spine; he was anxious for Wu Sunfu, and pessimistic besides over his own mission. Yet this very start shook him free of his hemming-and-hawing manner, and with a wry smile he turned his words about and asked:

"Of course—what business could be hid from that pair of eyes of yours! But there's still a thing I don't quite understand—eh, Elder Bo, what use do you want those dried cocoons for? We're all of one household. What need have you, Elder Bo, to make sport of Sunfu? If he can't get hold of Zhu Yinqiu's dried cocoons, he'll be somewhat hard put to it—"

Li Yuting's words had to break off half-way again; he heard Zhao Botao give a dry laugh, and saw him throw back his head and puff out a mouthful of cigar-smoke, the puffy flesh on that triangular face of his twitching lightly once. Then came an answer hard as steel, that made Li Yuting's hair stand on end:

"You don't understand? What a joke!—In my business I love to do things straight out; deal with me open and above-board, and I too am open and above-board. Yuting, you are today Sunfu's representative, so I may as well put forward a plan, and see whether Sunfu and his people can agree to it: I'll bring Shang Zhongli in to be general manager of that Yizhong Trust Company of Sunfu's and his."

"Ah, this—I hear it was settled long ago to elect a certain man surnamed Tang."

"My reports here also say it's a man surnamed Tang, and one of the Wang faction besides."

Hearing this answer of Zhao Botao's, Li Yuting's heart gave a leap; he understood it fully now: that the quarrel between Zhao Botao and Wu Sunfu was, after all, no matter of plain commerce; and he felt the more that a compromise between the two sides was hopeless. He opened his eyes wide, gazed at Zhao Botao, and, as if in entreaty, ventured one more question:

"Have you any other view, Elder Bo?—Suppose, suppose the general managership of the Yizhong were given instead to Du Zhuzhai? Zhuzhai stands above the fray!"

Zhao Botao gave a slight smile, and answered at once:

"Old man Shang stands above the fray too!"

Li Yuting laughed too, and at the same time awoke with a start to the fact that his own attitude had already gone beyond what befitted a third party, and that he must trim his sails in a hurry. He gave Zhao Botao a glance, and was just about to declare that his own standpoint was, from first to last, a willingness to serve every side loyally and well, when Zhao Botao stretched himself lazily and of a sudden shifted his tone:

"As for Sunfu's skill and daring in business, I admire them too; the pity is only that he has one fault—he trusts himself too much! That plan of his for the Yizhong Company is very good, but he did not consult me first. I, now, whenever I have a plan, always call him in—as with this bond operation of late. In bringing Shang Zhongli into the Yizhong, I am doing nothing but seeking to work together with him. Yuting, whatever I have, I say; if Sunfu will insist on hugging his prejudices, then let it drop. I hope he may weather one hard pass after another, and, one day, stand me a cup of wine at his wedding-feast of success—would not that be finer still!"

Coming to this last sentence, Zhao Botao rose to his feet with a great laugh, flexed his two arms in the air several times, and made to go and open that bedroom door. Li Yuting, knowing he was about to loose that "bewitching treasure" once more, hastily stood up too and cried:

"Elder Bo—"

Zhao Botao turned about and looked at Li Yuting as if much out of patience. Li Yuting pressed a step forward, put on an ingratiating smile, and said:

"Tonight I'll play the host—I'll invite the two of them, Sunfu and Zhuzhai, and ask you besides, Elder Bo, to grace us; and you shall talk it over face to face. How would that do?"

Zhao Botao's glance went wheeling several times over Li Yuting's face, and only then did he answer, between a smile and no smile:

"If Sunfu has no mind to give up his prejudices, then there's no call for this extra bother either!"

"I think there's a very great likelihood of it; he will see soon enough that a one-man show is not so good as getting up a company."

Li Yuting said it with great assurance, though what he feared within was the very opposite; he reckoned that eight or nine times out of ten Sunfu would not bow.

Zhao Botao laughed wildly, gave Li Yuting a hard clap on the shoulder, first let fall a phrase of Cantonese, and then, in Mandarin, cried out loud:

"What? You say soon enough! Yuting, in front of me, old Zhao, tell no lies. Not unless you reckon half a year, six months, as 'soon enough.' Of Sunfu's dispositions on every side I know a little; since he has made up his mind to run this Yizhong Trust Company, he has at the least six months' working power laid by; but after three months, I fear, he'll find the load too heavy and be unable to shift his forces about—I mean on the money side, he can't turn it round. By that time, let the banking-and-native-bank trade tighten on him ever so little, and he'll not be able to bear it! Just now he's riding the crest, and it's for others to give way to him. Ha—as for his giving way to others, look again after three months! Perhaps not even three months!"

"Oh—you write it, Elder Bo, in the large; I am thinking in the small. For instance, if Zhu Yinqiu's dried-cocoon mortgage-loan cannot be settled as Sunfu hopes, then he's at once in a bad way. Without cocoons he can't set the works going, and if he can't set the works going he'll—"

Zhao Botao shrugged his shoulders and grinned savagely. But Li Yuting doggedly went on:

"—he'll swell the ranks of the workless workers. Elder Bo, from the first month till now, the Shanghai labour troubles have grown fiercer and fiercer, and become a great problem of public order. It would seem that, for the sake of the general situation, Sunfu's side must, to be sure, give a little ground—but best of all if you too, Elder Bo, would be a little easy, and, as to Zhu Yinqiu's mortgage-loan, let it rest a while and not meddle with it."

Having finished, Li Yuting felt his heart lighten; he had done his duty, striven for the general good, played the peacemaker and not the poker that stirs the fire. He fixed his eyes on Zhao Botao's triangular face, hoping to find on it some look of "approval." But there was none! Zhao Botao gave a scornful shake of his head, sat down in the sofa again, cocked up his legs, and said only, coldly, four words:

"You overstate the case."

On the instant a red flush shot over Li Yuting's face, and he felt it harder to bear than a beating. And because it was a whole heart's loyalty flung back at him, so that, over and above the bitterest sense of wrong, there was added the lonely indignation of one who has not found his right master. Yet he wished to press his honest counsel once more. He threw out his chest, and made ready to bring forth all the statecraft he had won from reading ten thousand volumes to win Zhao Botao's ear; when, all unlooked-for, the bedroom door over yonder suddenly opened first a crack, and the small round red lips sent a crisp, clear voice through the gap:

"You want me? You whistle!"

After this voice, a dark, shifty eye took its place at the crack of the door. Zhao Botao smiled and beckoned. The door opened, and the woman came drifting over light as a lotus-flower, first tilting her head in a smile at Zhao Botao, then turning her face aside to give Li Yuting the slightest of nods. Zhao Botao put out his hand and pinched the woman's snow-white forearm, then cried out abruptly:

"Yuying, this Mr. Li here says the Communists are coming—aren't you afraid?—"

"Oh, you mean those little red devils who do nothing but write up slogans? The night before last, riding past Changbang Road, I saw one. For all the world like a rat: the moment it sees anyone coming, one whisk and it's gone without a shadow."

"But catch you off guard, and at one turn they've become tigers; in Hunan, Hubei, Jiangxi, there are just such tigers. In Jiangsu and Zhejiang too!"

Li Yuting made haste to catch up the thread, glad at heart that he still had a chance to press his "dire warnings" once more. But at once he was disappointed again, for the woman curled her lips in a smile and murmured softly, as if to make a show of her wit:

"Tsk, tsk, tigers again. Tell that to the children!—Where there's a tiger, there'll be a Wu Song to beat the tiger!"

Zhao Botao turned his head to give Li Yuting a glance, and suddenly said gravely:

"Yuting, you may as well go back and tell Sunfu my meaning. I hope he'll consider it with a calm mind, and then give me his answer.—Let the tiger run mad, and I'll guard strictly against it; but I can never, because there's a tiger yonder, give ground till the thing's past all reason! Have you time tomorrow evening? I ask you to the Majestic, to dine and watch the dancing."

As he spoke, he stood up, shook hands with Li Yuting, and, most courteously, saw him to the door of the room.

When Li Yuting was in the street again, he craned his neck and let out a breath, and walked eastward. He chewed over Zhao Botao's talk, and bethought him too that he must go to the Louza police station and negotiate the bailing-out of his coachman and the carriage. The guard all along Nanjing Road was strict still, and handbills lay everywhere by the roadside. A motor-car went speeding past, rolling up a gust of wind, and the handbills went whirling and dancing over the road; suddenly one flew up very high and actually blew into Li Yuting's bosom. Li Yuting caught it offhand, gave it a glance, and a few startling lines drove straight into his heart:

"…the warlords, the bureaucrats, the local despots and gentry, the landlords, the compradors and the bourgeoisie, under the command of imperialism, join in an assault upon the revolutionary forces, seeking to root out the revolution of China altogether; yet the contradictions within imperialism, and within China's own ruling class, deepen day by day, and this present unprecedented great war among the warlords of North and South is the very surfacing of the clash of their contradictions, and the revolutionary masses of China must, in this time, press the harder—"

Li Yuting hastily threw the paper away and, gathering his spirit in one rush, ran forward a few steps, as though a ghost were at his back. It seemed to him that all was black before his eyes, and there rose up a strange and monstrous picture: Wu Sunfu with his grip on Zhu Yinqiu's throat, and Zhao Botao, from behind, seizing Wu Sunfu by the hair, the two of them locked in a fight to the death, heedless of one who stood at their side, knife in hand, biding his chance.

"This is the last day come—come at last!"

Li Yuting cried out in his heart in his misery, all the sinews of his body as though come loose, and his heart, heavy as lead, sinking down.
