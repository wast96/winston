### False Politeness

A sham, hollow politeness is likewise a Chinese specialty, and the dwellers on the Shanghai seaboard above all make a regular study of those three words, "false politeness." Suppose three men—A, B, and C—have arranged that on a certain day A shall play host and stand the treat. When the eating is done and the bill is to be settled, B and C are sure to press forward, both crying "Let me! Let me!"—the words of false politeness; and though their pockets be swept as bare as a washed dish, a hand must still go groping into the pocket as if to fish out money. Yet since it was settled beforehand that A should be host, why should not B and C in all honesty let him feast them, and what need at the last of this "Let me! Let me!" and all the false politeness of it?

I have often watched, in the wine-and-food houses, a party of diners who have eaten their fill and soaked themselves in wine till their faces are as red as Lord Guan's, and who, when they walk, cannot command their own two legs, and reek so of liquor that the smell of them turns the stomach. When the bill comes, four or five of them will fall to snatching at it all at once, without a word agreed, hemming the waiter into the middle of them so that he cannot take it from any one of them. Some, groping out their silver jiao pieces, will let them spill and scatter across the floor with a fine ringing and clinking and jingling; to a bystander it is a little painful on their behalf—but there they are, in high self-satisfaction, putting their whole strength into those three words, "false politeness," and how should they leave this last flourish out? The other tricks of false politeness are many more; but from this one instance the rest may be inferred, and it will stand for them all.
