### Hauling Foreigners

Say a Chinese and a foreigner hail a rickshaw at the same moment: the puller will as often as not choose to pull the foreigner and take no notice at all of the Chinese. And why should this be?

In the puller's mind every foreigner is a great rich man, a very god of wealth: when he pays his fare he spends foreign silver as though it were coppers and a gold quarter-piece as though it were silver dimes, and what is more he does not haggle over the price when he hires the rickshaw, so there is a deal of bother saved—unlike the Chinese, who will wrangle over a single copper and jabber on without end. That is why the puller would sooner give up the one for the other.

And when a drunken foreign soldier hails a rickshaw, they press forward the more desperately to take him, for it is said these gentleman soldiers, in paying the fare, will often fish out a gold pound to settle it; so a man bent on making his fortune cannot but risk his neck to hail them. Yet there are times when, so far from getting any gold pound or silver pound, one comes away instead having tasted an imported ham and five foreign cigars—and that too is a common enough thing!
