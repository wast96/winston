Chapter 20. An Audience with Zhou Enlai
Chen Zhigao had been hearing slanderous rumors and gossip—that he was squandering the nation’s property to entertain the high officials and notables passing through. And because during my time in Hong Kong I had frequently come and gone at Du Yuesheng’s mansion, people made me out to be Du’s female disciple…. So Zhigao felt that we, working ourselves to the bone under such hard conditions on the Party’s instructions and commission, should still have to suffer such wrongs and slights, and he was much aggrieved and no longer wished to carry on at Qujiang. As it happened, his classmate from Aurora University in Shanghai, Xu Jingwei, was head of the planning department at the Bank of Communications and wanted to invite Zhigao to be his deputy; and the bank’s chairman, Qian Xinzhi, was also someone we had known back in Shanghai. After turning it over and over, Zhigao thought of tendering his resignation to the Central Relief Commission, and to have me take the two girls to Chongqing, under the pretext of visiting relatives, to sound out whether it might be possible. My mother and I had not seen each other for many years, and to have this chance to travel a thousand li to visit her, I was of course overjoyed and gladly agreed. So from Qujiang I went by train by way of Guilin, then changed to a plane and reached the “provisional capital,” Chongqing. Zhigao had arranged in advance that in Chongqing I would lodge at the mansion of Qu Yingguang, vice-chairman of the Central Relief Commission. That very evening I received a telephone call from Elder Sister Deng Yingchao: “Mulan, you’ve arrived; tomorrow morning Wu Hao wants to see you. We’ll send a car to fetch you—come and have lunch here with us.” I asked whether I might bring the two children along, and she said yes, and told me to wait for the car the next morning at the gate of the airfield. That night I lay in bed tossing and turning almost until dawn: counting on my fingers, it had already been eleven years since I had last listened in person to the kindly guidance of the two leaders, Enlai and Yingchao. My two daughters, too, would have the honor of an audience in my train; yet with the orphans of Wan and He I did not know what day we might be reunited. Over all these years I had endured all manner of misunderstanding and grievance—ought I, tomorrow, to pour out my troubles before my old leaders?… Early the next morning I got up in haste, took the two girls, and, following Elder Sister Deng’s instructions on the telephone, walked to the vicinity of the airfield (telling Qu Yingguang that I was off to see a friend I had not met in years); and there indeed, with the license number already given me, was a small car reserved for the Eighth Route Army’s Chongqing Office, waiting. The driver comrade was courteous and considerate, and warmly saw my daughters and me into the car and drove straight to the “Eighth Route Office” at Zengjiayan. There I met Comrade Zhou Enlai in his military uniform; his face was as radiant as ever, but after eleven years of ceaseless toil and travel for the revolutionary cause his looks were no longer what they had been, and he now wore a beard. Elder Sister Deng and a secretary took the two children off to play elsewhere, and Comrade Enlai and I talked alone in his study, which doubled as his bedroom. I first gave a brief report of my work over these years. He said: “Liu Shaowen has already reported all of this. Since 1931 you have kept in contact, one after another, with Pan Hannian, Wu Defeng, and Liu Bochui and his wife, and everything you have done in accordance with the Party’s instructions and requirements we have long known. The Central Committee knows that your work has achieved great results. Work behind enemy lines is very hard, and that under such difficult conditions you could do your duty, keep secrets, and still be creative—and moreover establish and develop the Party’s underground liaison work, rescue and welcome so many well-known patriots of every circle back to the rear to take part in the resistance, and carry out the relief of great numbers of ordinary refugees so admirably—is no easy thing. On behalf of the Central Committee I express our appreciation of your labors; and still more must I praise and thank Chen Zhigao for the important contribution he has made!” Hearing Comrade Enlai offer, on behalf of the Party organization, such warm and kindly words of comfort, I could not keep back my tears, and felt in my heart an incomparable consolation.
Comrade Enlai went on, with the same solicitude: “The Central Committee knows that in your heart you worry about the orphans left by the two martyrs, Wan and He. You have been to Macao and have seen the orphan of the martyr He Chang, being raised by the Lu family; I hear that Comrade Lu Biao and his household love the child dearly, that the child is very bright and studies hard, so you may set your mind at rest. As for the child of the martyr Wan Xiyan, Old Dong (Dong Biwu) once asked Zhang Dulun, an old comrade-in-arms from the Northern Expedition, to inquire of the child’s uncle, and to convey the Central Committee’s thought of sending him to the Soviet Union to study. The uncle, Wan Xikan, said that the Soviet Union is now waging its patriotic war of resistance against the fascist German invasion, that life there is even harder than ours, that the child is still young, and that the matter of going to the Soviet Union had best be left for some later time. He is now studying in Chongqing, is very clever, and has kin nearby to look after him; so although this time you may not be able to meet him, you may rest easy all the same. We must all remember the dying words of the ‘Earth Leopard’: ‘While the Xiongnu are yet undestroyed, what use is a home?’ Our Chinese war of resistance has now entered its hardest, stalemate phase. Qujiang is an important transport hub linking the southeast coastal region with the great rear of the southwest. You are now working in fine harmony with Li Hanhun, chairman of the Guangdong provincial government, and his wife, and have made a positive contribution to upholding Kuomintang–Communist cooperation and the resistance. The people’s anti-Japanese armed forces led by our Party have already grown greatly, and have established fourteen anti-Japanese base areas behind enemy lines throughout the country, only awaiting the moment for the counter-offensive, when victory in the war of resistance will surely be won. I hear that Chen Zhigao has had you come to Chongqing this time to stay at Qu Yingguang’s, meaning to resign from his relief-commission work at Qujiang and move to a post at the Bank of Communications. Our view is that it would be better for him not to resign. Chen Zhigao’s present post is extremely important. For one of our people to hold such a post is no easy thing.” He then spoke of some concrete tasks he hoped Zhigao would carry out, using his official, lawful standing to give secret support to the East River guerrilla column—material support of every kind possible, including through the Central Relief Commission’s special relief funds. I said that the chief reason for Zhigao’s wish to resign was that he had heard so much gossip and rumor about us, and felt that he was working hard and getting no thanks, with no way to clear himself, and was much aggrieved. At this Comrade Enlai laughed aloud and said: “You should urge Chen Zhigao that, for the sake of the whole nation’s first priority—winning the war of resistance at the earliest day—he must swallow humiliation and bear his burden, force himself to the hard task, keep on striving, and hold this important post without complaint. Work in the white areas is at once hard, complicated, and very dangerous, and transferring cadres is no easy matter; why should he take to heart the gossip spread by people who do not know the true situation? We hope Chen Zhigao will, at this important post at Qujiang, give still fuller play to his talent for serving the country, and that on no account will he beat a retreat and quit at a few idle words. We must learn the spirit of the Great Yu, who bowed when he heard good counsel—keep an open, humble heart, correct a fault if we have it and guard against it if we have not, bear what others cannot bear and tolerate what others cannot tolerate. Has not our Party and our army grown ever stronger amid the enemy’s countless slanders, revilings, massacres, and ‘encirclement campaigns’?”
I said: “After the Southern Anhui Incident, when the four patriotic democratic figures Song Qingling, He Xiangning, Liu Yazi, and Peng Zemin were about to issue a joint declaration opposing the second anti-Communist high tide, there were even people who said that I, siding with Du Yuesheng, meant to go and stop them from publishing it.” Comrade Enlai said: “When Song Qingling was still in Hong Kong, Xiao Chao (Elder Sister Deng) went there to thank the friends who supported our Party’s stand for unity and resistance, and Du Yuesheng gave a dinner for her too—you were among those who attended as fellow guests at the time. So long as these secret-society bosses hold firm to the resistance, they too are objects we work to unite. You are the head of the rescue department of the Mutual Aid Society; when Song Qingling and the others wished to issue a statement bitterly denouncing the anti-Communist outrages of the Kuomintang die-hards, calling for the rescue of the New Fourth Army’s commander Ye Ting, and offering comfort to the families of the suffering comrades, how could you have gone to obstruct them? All of this we know perfectly well; what need have you to believe these rumors and grow angry over them? Several times you have asked to go to Yan’an, and each time we have not agreed—is it, then, only by being transferred to Yan’an that one does revolutionary work? Working in the white areas is, if anything, harder and more dangerous than in Yan’an. On the Qujiang side we do not wish to send more cadres; with you there, you can already hold the fort single-handed. As for Li Hanhun and his wife, we have already sent someone specially to work there; you should go on cooperating in unity with them in your present manner of work, and not expose your true identity. You must put the larger situation first: even if you personally feel wronged, you must force yourself to the difficult task and return to Qujiang as soon as possible—within our own ranks, to convey the Party’s commendation and praise, to encourage our comrades, strengthen unity, and work hard; and toward those outside, you must persuade Chen Zhigao to give up any thought of resigning and to hold firm to his present post. The Bank of Communications may not necessarily let his talents show, and I reckon that Xu Shiying and Qu Yingguang of the Central Relief Commission will not agree to his resigning either. A post our people have already taken must never be lightly given up like this—would that not be as good as turning deserter? Your present work position is so important; who could take your place? To subordinate everything to the needs of the work—this is the Party’s discipline, as you understand. The Central Committee trusts you without the least doubt, and is forever encouraging and praising you; why then are you still not content to work in the white areas?”
Hesitating, starting to speak and then faltering, I mumbled: “My wish to go to Yan’an is not only to leave the white areas and change my working environment; it is also because I want to leave the Chen family—I feel it would be better, after all, to part from Chen Zhigao.” Comrade Enlai asked with concern: “Has not Chen Zhigao done a great deal of work for our Party and acquitted himself very well? Why do you want to leave him?” Weeping, I said: “It is true that during this national crisis of the resistance the various members of that large Chen household have been able to set aside their differences of political view and unite in concord under the banner of patriotism and resistance. But over these years I have never made my true identity public in his home, and my heart has been uneasy. I do not ask that Chen Zhigao love me and me alone….” After Comrade Enlai pressed me as to what exactly I meant by this, I finally stammered out the rumor that Chen Zhigao was on rather intimate terms with a certain lady. Comrade Enlai said: “You must not lightly believe the idle talk that others pass around.” And, smiling, he patted me on the shoulder and said: “My dear lady, surely you too are not going to be jealous over such a thing? We Communists must be a little more large-hearted, and look a little higher and farther. Even if a democratic personage has had such a romance, at times it is perhaps out of the needs of social work, mere playing to the occasion; in this respect one should not be too hard on people outside the Party. You have all along worked independently at a special post; Chen Zhigao respects you greatly, does as you say, and has never interfered in your activities—what more can you ask of him? You must broaden your mind, put the larger situation first in everything, and not take to heart these small matters of daily life.” Under Comrade Enlai’s warm, patient, earnest teaching, I was in the end brought to heartfelt acceptance.
Cai Shuhou was originally a Communist Party member under the Comintern’s intelligence system; I had known his identity back in Shanghai. When he was doing white-area work in Shanghai together with Liu Shaowen, Li Nanxiang, and others, he knew that Liu Shaowen and I could report matters to Comrade Zhou Enlai through a single-line channel. Once, in casual talk, he told me that he was a member of the International Section and wished to transfer to membership in the Chinese Section. Meeting Comrade Enlai now at Zengjiayan, I had the chance to convey this wish of his. Comrade Enlai said: for the present it is better to keep things as they are and maintain the single-line contact. Speaking of Cai Shuhou’s problem, I thought of my own: apart from the few senior leading comrades above me, my Chinese Communist Party membership was not public, and on top of that there was so much gossip and rumor—might my Party membership and my length of Party standing become a problem in the future? So I spoke this misgiving in my heart. Comrade Enlai answered me at once, clearly: “After the Chinese revolution led by our Party has won victory, I will take it on myself to certify it for you!” Hearing this guarantee of his, the knot of doubt that had long revolved in my heart also vanished like mist and smoke.
There was much to talk about, and much that Comrade Enlai wanted to learn; I marveled at how clear and detailed his memory was of so many things. Time spent in gladness always passes especially fast, and the whole morning was soon gone. At noon we ate in the small dining room, which held only a single eight-immortals table and was as plain and simple as his study-cum-bedroom, yet neat and dignified, without a single needless ornament. Comrade Liao Mengxing was also at the table to keep us company. Among the dishes was a large carp, and Elder Sister Deng said: “Mulan, this was got ready specially for you today! You know, in Chongqing this is something we can seldom eat.” Comrade Enlai kindly had the children call him Uncle, and, stroking Yunzhong’s head, said: “Ah! A pair of big black eyes—when she grows up she is sure to be very clever. Mulan, your stock is of the finest; the children you bear are sure to be clever, every one.” The fruit after the meal was watermelon, and when Uncle Enlai saw how the children loved it, he had a big watermelon brought for them to take home. And he said to me, drolly: “Mulan, mind you don’t drop the watermelon to pick up sesame seeds!” I knew this was a play on words, reminding me not to give up an important post over personal grievances, and I hastened to thank him. My second daughter, Dazhong, was then still small and can no longer remember the scene clearly; but Yunzhong to this day retains a very deep impression of that honored audience. During the meal Liao Mengxing thanked me again and again for our having gone to rescue her mother and bring her back to the great rear. I said: “This is only my bounden duty; is your mother not my mother too?” From that time on I addressed the venerable He Xiangning respectfully as Mama Liao.
Coming back from the “Eighth Route Office” at Zengjiayan, my mind was in a great tumult of thought. How many times had I put to the organization my request to go and fight at Yan’an, the holy land of revolution I had so long yearned for—only to be refused each time, each time persuaded to settle down and do underground work in the white areas. After Comrade Zhou Enlai’s heartfelt, weighty talk, I came to a fuller understanding of the Party’s instructions. As Comrade Zhou Enlai had kindly enlightened me: “Is it, then, only by being transferred to Yan’an that one does revolutionary work?” To work in the white areas is, just like working in Yan’an, to work under the Party’s leadership; and because the posts differ, all the more is each called on to play its own irreplaceable, special part. That the Party organization had placed me at such a special post to carry on work independently meant the burden on my shoulders was very heavy, and the task very glorious. I did not fear the hardships and dangers of underground work—why, then, should I reckon up personal gains and losses? In doing rescue work, intelligence work, and united-front work on the hidden front, the persons one dealt with were of the widest range; especially during the second period of Kuomintang–Communist cooperation, the two parties had contacts with each other, each with its people inside the other, and the patriotic democratic movement was on the rise. We Communists, in order to isolate the enemy and shield revolutionary activity, had to be good at mobilizing every positive factor to unite for the resistance, had to combine open activity and secret work skillfully, and complete the tasks the Party assigned with flexibility and quick wit amid an intricate, complicated situation. Given this special situation, as for a certain amount of gossip and rumor, and the misunderstanding of some people, comrades included, so long as I knew well that I was working in the faith of loyalty to the Party and in a spirit of courageous responsibility, I would have a clear conscience, would not take it to heart, would from the height of putting the whole situation first endure and stand fast, and swallow my personal grievances in silence. Once I had thought this through, I felt that my spirit of dedication as a Communist had been sublimated.
I put aside the notion I had come with, and after taking the two girls back to Qu Yingguang’s house I of course said not a word about going to Zengjiayan. I told Qu Yingguang of Zhigao’s wish to resign and go to work at the Bank of Communications. Qu said: “The two of you have done your work at Qujiang very well; Chairman Xu Shiying is even preparing to hold a celebration of your merits! Why should Chen Zhigao come running to Chongqing to be a mere deputy department head at the Bank of Communications? Chongqing, too, is all day under the threat of enemy bombing, with everyone on edge. Here it is by no means certain his talents will show better than at Qujiang!” A few days later came Xu Shiying’s sixtieth birthday, and I went with Qu Yingguang to Gele Mountain to attend the banquet celebrating it. Xu Shiying, having already learned from Qu Yingguang of Zhigao’s wish to resign, made a point of publicly praising Zhigao’s achievements at Qujiang, and made clear that he did not agree to his resigning and hoped I would go back and persuade him. It fell out exactly as Comrade Enlai had foreseen.
I had come to Chongqing ostensibly to visit my mother, and so, my official business done, I had good reason to bring her to Nanwenquan for a few days’ stay. When my mother and I met, I told the old lady of our original plan and of Comrade Zhou Enlai’s instructions, saying that I had thought that if I could be transferred to work in Chongqing I might often see her and fulfill in some small measure a daughter’s duty of filial care, but that my fond calculation had come to nothing and I would soon be returning to Qujiang. My mother anxiously asked after my eldest younger brother and his wife at Qujiang, and, learning that all was well with them, was reassured. She too urged us to put the larger situation first, to go back to Qujiang, and together with Zhigao to hold our posts and share in the nation’s ordeal; and she said that Chongqing, forever suffering the Japanese planes’ wild and reckless bombing, was very unsafe, and that she too was going to move with my third brother to Yibin, and that even the historical research institute where my fourth brother worked was preparing to move there. My mother, like Old Madame Chen, was a devout Buddhist, chanting the sutras all day long, praying that the Bodhisattva Guanyin would keep her living kin safe, delivering the souls of the dead, and praying for peace in the world. My father had no brothers or sisters, and so my mother, unlike Old Madame Chen, had not to wear herself out managing a great household. My mother was frugal all her life, and the money the family saved was all spent on schooling for several of my younger brothers and sisters. After she settled in Chongqing, several of them were near at hand to look after her, and though I had not seen her for many years, I was somewhat reassured. After taking leave of my mother, we flew from Chongqing to Guilin and then by another route returned to Qujiang. This was in the summer of 1942.
After I returned to Qujiang and conveyed to Zhigao Comrade Enlai’s encouragement and comfort, his earnest charge, and his high hopes for him, Zhigao felt deeply Comrade Enlai’s discernment of men and the depth of his trust, and reacted with a strong, eager wish to repay it. At the time many comrades of the work teams were very fond of my two daughters, and were delighted to see them back from their long journey, asking one after another whether Chongqing was nice, whom they had seen, and so on. The children answered innocently: “Chongqing is very nice—even the bathwater is hot (it was a hot spring, after all!). We saw Grandma and Uncle, and Uncle even treated us to watermelon!” That Comrade Enlai had the children call him Uncle was truly thoughtful: first, to let me work with an easy mind in the white areas, the Party being forever my own natal family; and second, since children speak without restraint, the term “Uncle” also guarded against letting slip a secret.
At this time Yang Huimin was still, at great risk, engaged in Hong Kong in the secret rescue and transport of trapped patriots of every circle, escorting batch after batch of people and supplies from Hong Kong back to the great rear. In October, Zhigao, fearing that if she stayed active in Hong Kong too long she could hardly escape the danger of exposure and arrest, sent her a telegram secretly instructing her to come back at once, meaning to dispatch another work team to take over from her. But she took the attitude that “a general in the field is not bound by his sovereign’s orders,” and insisted on staying to carry on her risky work in Hong Kong, wishing to escort the famous “Queen of the Screen” Hu Die and her husband and their party, together with the great quantity of luggage they carried, from Hong Kong to Qujiang and thence by another route to Chongqing. In November, Hu Die and her husband Pan Yousheng safely reached Qujiang. One day she suddenly came to find us, saying that the large lot of “luggage” she had entrusted to Yang Huimin to escort for her had gone missing on the way, and that Chen Zhigao must be held responsible to make it good. How had this come about? It turned out that the “luggage” Hu Die had handed to Yang Huimin to escort amounted to no fewer than twenty-one great trunks and bundles. How, on a journey of flight, would a husband and wife carry so much luggage? In fact, of all this “luggage” only a few pieces were truly luggage; the overwhelming bulk of it was high-grade smuggled goods that the wealthy wives of gang bosses and the concubines of high Kuomintang officials had entrusted to Hu Die to carry along—things like the imported Western medicines and cosmetics that were scarce and dear in the rear—which cost little to buy in Hong Kong but, if they could be shipped to Chongqing and sold, were highly welcome, fast-selling wares from which huge profits could be reaped. Seeing that there was too much luggage to escort, Yang Huimin, to ensure the safety of both people and goods, decided to send the people and the luggage separately; the result was that although the people arrived safe, this lot of “luggage,” being too conspicuous, was seized by a band of bandits. At the time, Hong Kong and Kowloon and the Pearl River Delta had already fallen, and just at the three-forked crossroads on the boundary between the occupied zone and the Guangdong rear, the Japanese army often came to harry, and there were also, active in the area, the East River guerrillas led by our Party, and, more than that, bandits and desperadoes of every stripe who specialized in robbing the refugees of their money and goods along this stretch. It was at this three-forked crossroads that this lot of “luggage” was robbed. Before Hu Die’s visit we had already heard the report of the escorting staff, and Zhigao had long since sent out a telegram asking the relevant people to try to trace it along the way. Hu Die, perhaps because the goods others had entrusted to her had been lost en route, leaving her no way to answer to them, was in her anxiety very ungracious in her speech, and insisted flatly that Zhigao must make good the loss. Zhigao then answered her, equally bluntly: “The staff of our relief commission, at the risk of their lives, escorted you people from the occupied zone to the great rear as a matter of duty—we are not running an insurance company. Your people have all been escorted through safely; the luggage was robbed by bandits on the road—how can you make us pay for it? Speaking of legal liability, we have only the duty to trace it on your behalf; to have us make good the loss is simply out of the question.” The two of them fell to quarreling, sword-point against spear-point, and there and then reached deadlock. Seeing that she could not have her way, Hu Die went off in a huff. Some days later the desperadoes who had robbed the luggage shipped the goods to Guilin to dispose of them, and sold them openly on the market. Those smuggled Western medicines and the like could not be traced to their owners. But among them was a mink coat that Hu Die herself wore, which was recognized as her property, so that it was known to be stolen goods; yet the fence, once he had his money, had long since slipped away. How could that lot of stolen smuggled goods ever be recovered? The story spread far and wide in society: that she, her consigned goods robbed, had lost great face before those wealthy wives, and had actually turned her anger on us. After Hu Die reached Chongqing, she lodged a complaint with Dai Li, the head of the Military Statistics Bureau’s secret agents, saying that we had shielded Yang Huimin and “colluded with the Communists’ East River guerrillas” to rob her “luggage.” Thereupon Yang Huimin was arrested and thrown into prison. This was truly a calamity out of a clear sky, a wrong as vast as heaven; and from it they went on to “trace” the matter to Zhigao and me—a disaster all the more out of the blue. Yet, thinking it over afterward, it was not so strange after all: it must be that Zhigao and I, doing at Qujiang, in the name of the relief commission, so much work favorable to strengthening Kuomintang–Communist unity in the resistance, had long since drawn the notice and the hatred of the Kuomintang’s die-hard anti-Communist faction, and of the Military Statistics agents in particular. My trip to Zengjiayan in Chongqing had naturally deepened their suspicion; only, in the political climate of the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation, they could find no pretext, could get no handle on us, and were hard put to make a move. Now, availing themselves of Hu Die’s complaint, they could “move their troops in a just cause” and lay hands on us. So, on the face of it, we were implicated through Yang Huimin; but in truth the Military Statistics side was a case of “the drinker’s heart is not in the cup”—striking at us, slandering the Communist Party, and wrecking the united resistance. Yang Huimin’s wrongful case came of Hu Die’s false accusation, and perhaps she was also dragged in on account of our affair—a wrong upon a wrong indeed! As for this prison calamity of ours, on the part of the Military Statistics agents it was, as the saying goes, malicious slander and the shifting of blame by rumor-mongering, seized on to make their move, an old trick to sow discord and split the Kuomintang–Communist cooperation.
