## 12

Every year, once Qingming had passed, the town of Xietang held a grand temple fair, and even in these years since the Japanese soldiers came it was no different. Machine guns stood mounted at both ends of the long street, and still the country people from the villages all around carried the temple's bodhisattva out in a sedan chair. The procession ran from morning to dusk, and through all the gongs and drums and piping reed-flutes the one thing missing was the crack of firecrackers going up into the sky. The Japanese forbade the setting off of firecrackers utterly, at any hour and on any occasion. Let a single firecracker sound, and the machine guns they had mounted would sound after it.

Zhongliang's little cigarette shop stood at the far end of the long street. From behind the counter he could see the bridge he had once pictured to himself, and beneath it the ginkgo tree just breaking into bud. This had once been his mother's home; now it was his shop, where besides cigarettes and matches he sold sweets and coarse paper as well. Su Lina, too, would sometimes buy up country hens and eggs from the villages, selling them mostly to the mess officer at the Japanese barracks.

Once, after Zhongliang had gone with the Japanese mess officer to carry eggs into the barracks, he came back and said the devils in there were really conscripts hauled over from Korea. Su Lina was crouched at the stove-mouth boiling water; she laughed and asked whether he meant to turn them. But the moment the words were out, the smile left her face.

Without meaning to, Su Lina thought of Zhou Chukang — thought of the very first task she had been given, which was to get close to him at any cost, to draw intelligence out of him, and in the end to win him over, to make him one of us, one of our own agents. When Mr. Pan set her these tasks, Su Lina had just turned twenty-one, two days short of her graduation ceremony at St. Mary's Hall.

On the cargo boat out of Shanghai, with her mouth close to Zhongliang's ear, Su Lina told the story of her life for the first time — of her parents, who had died in one of Yuan Shikai's prisons, and of the two men she had known. They lay in the boat's cramped false bottom, packed as if into a lightless coffin, and pressed close against them was the raw opium the boat's owner was smuggling. When she had said it all, Su Lina broke down and wept, sunk so deep in her own past that she seemed not even to feel that Zhongliang had already gathered her in. She clutched at the cloth of his back the way a drowning woman clings to a floating door-plank.

But when Zhongliang's lips groped through the dark and found hers, she came to herself all at once, turned her face aside, and shut her eyes tight in the darkness. Su Lina went rigid as a corpse, as though even her breathing had stopped.

The boat put in at a wharf on the far side of the Yangtze; this was the last stage of Chen Taining's escort. He stood on the bank, pointed off in one direction, and said, “Go north and you're on your own side's ground.”

Su Lina nodded. When she had watched Chen Taining board again and go, she worked a bangle off her wrist, pressed it into Zhongliang's hand, and said, “Let's go our separate ways.”

“Where will you go?”

Su Lina did not answer. She gave Zhongliang one last look, then turned and went off into the town along a snow-covered path, took a room at an inn, and promptly fell into a fever. For three days and three nights Su Lina lay in the inn bed, going back over her whole life from beginning to end, and the conclusion she reached was this: there was no longer any place in this world that would hold her.

On the morning of the fourth day Zhongliang knocked at the inn-room door. He stood in the doorway and looked at the haggard Su Lina. He said not a word, only stood there stock-still and looked at her. His eyes held all the weathered care and dread of a grown man.

The truth was that Zhongliang had kept watch the whole time in the teahouse across from the inn. The three days Su Lina lay in bed, he sat three days at the teahouse window. In those three days his eyes never left the inn's front gate.

A few days later a weary-looking man and woman appeared in a small town called Xietang. They walked along the long street by the river to the foot of a bridge and stood beneath an ancient ginkgo tree. Zhongliang looked a while at the bamboo shop across the way, then took Su Lina by the hand and led her inside. Xu-sao knew her son at a glance. She rose from the low stool where she sat, a bamboo knife still in her hand. Her mouth opened and her eyes went moist. But when she saw Su Lina standing behind her son, her gaze slowly froze; she turned to the old bamboo-worker, who stood there grinning with a mouthful of black teeth, and said, “Look at that. He's made more of himself than that father of his ever did.”

The old bamboo-worker was a quick man. He said not a word, wiped his two big hands on his apron, and was soon back from the street with a cut of pork.

Over the meal the old bamboo-worker talked as though he had known Zhongliang for years, nephew this and nephew that, on and on, from the boy's dead grandfather down to his grandmother's burial. “I saw to every bit of it myself,” the old bamboo-worker said. “I was like half a son to them.” Xu-sao never said a word; when she had unhurriedly eaten her bowl clean, she got up and went out to the shop in front. Zhongliang knew his mother had something to say, and followed her out. He stood before her and watched her strip a length of bamboo from the stalk as one skins a hide. Without raising her head, Xu-sao said evenly, “Whose concubine is she? Or some dance-hall girl you took up with?”

“She's my wife,” Zhongliang said calmly. “Your daughter-in-law.”

Xu-sao raised her face and looked at her son — and at the same time saw Su Lina, standing in the doorway of the inner room. Xu-sao's eyes leapt between the two faces; all at once she got to her feet and said, “Then have the wedding done. Do it, and it's settled.”

With that she threw the bamboo knife to the ground, brushed off the front of her jacket, and went into the inner room.

Zhongliang, for his part, could not work it out; even on their wedding night he was still asking Su Lina how his mother had known they were not married.

Su Lina did not answer. In the candlelight she gazed at this man a full seven years younger than herself and said, “If the day ever comes when you regret it, you must tell me.”

Zhongliang shook his head. After a long silence he took Su Lina's face in his hands and asked her, “Do you know why it is we've come to this day?” He did not wait for an answer, but said at once, “It was because of you that I set out on this road.”

Su Lina said, “Without me, some other woman would have married you.”

“That's not it.” Zhongliang thought a moment. “If I'd never met you, I think I would have been a postman on the streets of Shanghai all my life.” “And now you're nothing at all,” Su Lina said.

“I've become your husband.” Zhongliang laughed, reached out and drew her into his arms, and held her hard, as if afraid she might leave. On his wedding night Zhongliang thought again of the first time he had seen Su Lina, outside the door of No. 203, Siming Apartments. She had worn a sleeveless chiffon nightdress, one hand on the door-frame, the look on her face languid and indifferent.

The moment the news of Japan's surrender came, the old bamboo-worker's first act was to come running over from the bamboo shop. “You'll have to lay in some fireworks,” he told Zhongliang. “Eight years, and not a soul in this town has set off a single cracker.”

But the next day Zhongliang went the length of the county town and found no shop selling fireworks; the whole county was busy bringing down traitors, and he could only come back with half a sack of lotus-root starch on his shoulder. That same day a company of Nationalist troops came into the town and took over the Japanese barracks. The company commander was a young man of smart bearing; no sooner had he pitched camp than he gathered the townsfolk beneath the old ginkgo tree. Standing on the steps of the bridge, he raised his fist like some hot-blooded youth: “We have won this war, and now is the time to rebuild our homes.” The country people looked at one another, and not one of them raised a fist with him. A little let down, the company commander let his arm fall and went on: his was the government's army, and his soldiers were everyone's own brothers. If ever the townsfolk needed help, they had only to come to him at the barracks; and if any soldier of his made trouble in the town, they had only to come to him at the barracks too, and he would punish the man without mercy. To that end he had his men set up two letter boxes, one at each end of the long street, so that anyone with a proposal or a grievance who found it awkward to speak to his face might simply put it in a letter — though the main thing was to denounce the traitors hiding among them. When he had finished, the company commander turned to the newly appointed headman: “Sir, will you appoint the people a letter-carrier?”

The new headman stroked the little tuft of grizzled whiskers on his chin, hesitating. Better, he said, to call for volunteers: whoever put his name forward, the town would allow him half a silver dollar for every household on his round. Again the country people looked at one another. Then, out of the crowd, Zhongliang spoke up: “I'll do it. I've been a postman.”

But Zhongliang had no sooner drawn his one silver dollar than his mission was over — for the plain reason that no one wrote the company commander any letters at all. Instead the young company commander came through the street on patrol every day, a still younger orderly at his heels. He seemed particularly fond of resting at Zhongliang's cigarette shop, coming in almost every time to lean a while against the counter, sometimes buying a pack of cigarettes and, as he smoked, making idle talk with Su Lina for something to say.

The company commander said he had once been a history student at Nanhua University, and that after throwing down his pen for the army he had fought in the Xuefeng Mountain campaign at Zhijiang, in Hunan. His dream was to stay on at the university as a history teacher; it was the Japanese devils who had forced him into this uniform. Every time the company commander spoke, the way he looked at Su Lina put Zhongliang in mind of his own younger self.

Once, when the company commander was telling of passing through Guangxi on the march, Su Lina could not help asking whether he had ever heard of the 88th Division. The company commander thought and said he had not only heard of it but come across it; afterward it had gone to Burma to fight the devils. “Have you kin there?” he asked.

Su Lina shook her head, lit a cigarette, and sat behind the counter drawing on it slowly, mouthful by mouthful.

Watching the way she smoked, the company commander said suddenly, “You're nothing at all like the people of this town.”

Su Lina laughed and asked him, “Then where would you say I'm from?”

Looking at her pale, slender fingers, the company commander shook his head and said, “You're certainly not from this town.”

“My husband's family is here,” Su Lina said, smiling.

“And your own family — where is that?”

Su Lina thought a moment, then said, “Shanghai.”

The company commander nodded; seeing Zhongliang come out from the inner room, he nodded to him as well and went off with his orderly.

Watching the company commander's back as he climbed the bridge, Zhongliang said, “He's taken a liking to you.”

“To my eyes he's still a boy.”

“To your eyes I'm a boy too.”

“You were, once.” Su Lina looked at him. “Now you're my husband.”

Zhongliang laughed. This was the most peaceful stretch of days they ever had. But such days did not last. One day the company commander walked into the shop in a brand-new major's uniform. He had just been promoted to battalion commander, and his soldiers were out in the waste ground beyond the town, digging trenches and building pillboxes.

The battalion commander bought a pack of Three Castles, but he had really come to say something. He told Su Lina to go as far away as she could; to stay here was only to be cannon fodder along with the rest of them. Su Lina said, “You know you'll be cannon fodder, and you still fight?”

The battalion commander gave a slight smile. “A soldier's trade is fighting.”

“Even so, a man ought to know what he's fighting for.” It was the first time Zhongliang had ever broken in while the battalion commander was talking with his wife.

The battalion commander was taken aback. He stared at him a while, then said to Su Lina, “Go, and the sooner the better.”

With that the battalion commander gave Zhongliang another glance, took his cigarettes from the counter, and turned and left.

A fortnight later the battalion commander and every one of his soldiers were killed, and the little town of Xietang was destroyed along with them. The fire in the long street burned for three whole days and nights, and only when the entire street had burned to ashes did the rain come down in torrents. The old bamboo-worker and Xu-sao perished together in the flames. Nothing would move them to go with Zhongliang to Shanghai, still less to go off to the countryside with the old bamboo-worker's two daughters. They meant to keep watch over their property, their home. Grinning, the old bamboo-worker told Zhongliang that he had taken the measure of the Japanese — was he to be afraid of his own countrymen? The two of them saw Zhongliang and his wife all the way onto the boat. Waving, the old bamboo-worker called, “Come back when the fighting's done. Your mother and I will be waiting.”

Xu-sao never said a word; the look she gave her son was like a last farewell.
