## Revolutionary Work Under Gu Zhuxuan's Cover

{d} By Gu Shuping

During the War of Resistance and the War of Liberation, following the Party's instructions, I made use of my connection with my fourth uncle, Gu Zhuxuan, and of the Green Gang's organization, to carry out a number of revolutionary assignments with relative ease.

Gu Zhuxuan, styled Rumao, was called Gu the Fourth because he was the fourth child in his family. He was born on the fourteenth day of the third lunar month of 1885, a native of Yancheng in Jiangsu. At sixteen he left his village for Shanghai, where he kept himself alive pulling a rickshaw for the Xieji Company at the foot of Xizang Road. At twenty he took the Green Gang boss Liu Dengjie as his master, and later gathered several hundred disciples and pupils of his own, with several thousand disciples-of-disciples beneath them. Before the liberation of Shanghai he opened the Tianchan Stage in turn on Nanjing Road and Fuzhou Road, and held such posts as commander of the Zhabei Defense Corps, director of the Zhabei Merchant Corps, chairman of the Jianghuai Native-Place Association in Shanghai, deputy head of the Subei Native-Place Federation in Shanghai, deputy head of the Subei Refugee Relief Committee, and trustee of the Chinese Red Cross; and several times he gave relief to the famine-stricken of his home district. During the First Revolutionary Civil War he once rescued Jiang Weixin, then leader of the Shanghai labor-movement column — the younger brother of Jiang Weichu, husband of Gu Zhuxuan's niece — and was commended for it by Comrade Zhou Enlai. Later, comrades such as Zhou Wenwen, now with the People's Procuratorate of this city's Hongkou District, Yang Jin, now with the Municipal Bureau of Industry and Commerce, and Zhu Junxin, former vice-chairman of the Shanghai Federation of Trade Unions, all carried on underground work under Gu Zhuxuan's cover. From the time of the War of Resistance I too, under his cover and with his support, carried out such underground work as escorting and rescuing the Party's leading comrades.

After my home county of Jianyang in Jiangsu was liberated at the end of 1940, I held in turn such posts as standing member of the district consultative council, county councillor, and deputy head of the district and county armed-forces committees. Because I was related to Gu Zhuxuan, the organization often had me take charge of liaison, escorting leading comrades safely through Shanghai.

In the spring of 1943, the CCP Yanfu District Committee sent Tang Junzhao, magistrate of Jianyang County, to confer with me about escorting Comrade Yu Ping and his wife, Comrade Li Feng, from northern Jiangsu through Shanghai to Yan'an. Yu Ping was then head of the Enemy-Work Department of the CCP Yanfu District Committee, and Li Feng was secretary of the Huai'an County Committee; on notice from the organization they were to be transferred to work in Yan'an. From northern Jiangsu to Yan'an, the overland route was tightly blockaded by the Japanese and their puppets and full of danger; the water route — first by boat to Dalian, then up through the Northeast to Yan'an — was dangerous too. After deliberation the organization decided on the water route by way of Shanghai, since there my fourth uncle Gu Zhuxuan's connections could furnish every kind of convenience and meet any emergency. Tang Junzhao's instructions to me were that along the way Yu Ping should pass as my teacher; one comrade would first help me get him as far as Dongtai, after which I alone would escort him to Shanghai, where I was to arrange, through the Japanese-puppet offices, a pass to Lan County in Shanxi, and so go on from there to Yan'an. He advised further that the true purpose of the journey and the couple's identities could be told to Gu Zhuxuan frankly — that to keep them from him would do more harm than good.

The four of us set out from the base area by boat, carrying passes for Taizhou. But no sooner had the boat put in at the Dongtai wharf than trouble came. The puppet troops of Wang Jianzhai's unit of the 36th Division, stationed at Dongtai, searched travelers closely on the wharf; Li Feng, the other escorting comrade, and I all escaped inspection, but because Yu Ping had once been imprisoned and had had his feet shackled, he could not walk quite normally, and the moment the puppet soldiers saw him they fixed on him, gave him a slap on the thigh, and led him away.

Just then I remembered that in Dongtai there was one Cao Xiaohong, who kept a grain shop; his father, Cao Runzhi, was a man of great standing and a sworn brother of Gu Zhuxuan, and both the other escorting comrade and I knew the family well. We left the wharf at once and went to Cao Xiaohong's house. I told him, "My teacher and his wife are on their way back to their old home in Shanxi, and my teacher has just been seized on the wharf." At this Cao Xiaohong went off immediately to intervene, and within the hour the puppet troops delivered Yu Ping to the Cao house.

By the original arrangement, the other escorting comrade now turned back, and I went on by boat from Dongtai to Taizhou with Yu Ping and his wife. Thanks to the help of my second elder brother, Gu Yuebo, who ran a transport firm in Taizhou, we avoided trouble on the way and obtained passes for Shanghai. Once in Shanghai, I felt it would not do to go straight to my fourth uncle's house, so I first took the couple to my third uncle's; my third uncle then went to consult the fourth, who at once agreed to give his support and help. The two of them, reckoning that the Japanese, being Buddhists, did not as a rule search temples, and that the abbot of the Dizang Temple on Guling Road (today's Jiangyin Road) was on close terms with both of them, and that with many people coming to perform Buddhist services there the place made good cover, lodged the couple in the temple. I then told my fourth uncle the true state of things — that Yu Ping and his wife were cadres of the Communist Party who hoped, under his cover and with his help, to reach Yan'an — and he said there would be no problem. A few days later, at midday, Gu Zhuxuan received the couple at his home (No. 15 Yingchun Fang, Hubei Road) and gave a banquet for them, and they went over the route to Yan'an and the arrangements for their safety. Gu Zhuxuan spent the equivalent of ten or twenty piculs of rice to buy off a Chinese working in the Japanese office that issued passes, and, standing surety himself, secured for the couple a pass to Taiyuan in Shanxi. Against any mishap on the road he also wrote a letter to the famous Peking-opera actor Li Guichun (stage name Xiao Dazi), asking him to help the couple out of any difficulty, and gave it to them to keep in reserve for the journey; for Li Guichun often performed in the large and middling cities of the north, was well known and widely acquainted and could be of use, and Gu Zhuxuan was very close to him — his son, Li Shaochun, being Gu's adopted son. Everything being ready, Gu Zhuxuan bought the tickets and saw the couple off. After I received word that they had reached Lan County in Shanxi safely, I returned to northern Jiangsu and reported to Tang Junzhao, who told me the organization had already had a telegram: they had arrived safely in Yan'an.

In March 1945, Comrade Lin Li, wife of Ma Bin, secretary of the Sheyang County Committee, developed a goiter that had to be removed by surgery. Comrade Huang Kecheng gave instructions that treatment must be arranged somehow. Through Tang Junzhao the organization again came to me, asking me to escort Lin Li to Shanghai for hospital treatment. So Lin Li and I hired a civilian boat and came by water to Shanghai.

Shanghai was still under Japanese-puppet rule at the time, and the enemy hunted Communists closely. For safety's sake, Lin Li first stayed at Gu Zhuxuan's home (now the third floor above the front of the Labor Theater). Gu Zhuxuan then arranged, through his connections, for her to be admitted for treatment to the Red Cross Hospital (today's Huashan Hospital); and after her discharge she convalesced at his home for a further ten days and more.

Having completed my task, I returned to northern Jiangsu together with Lin Li, and at Gu Zhuxuan's request took his youngest son, Gu Naijin, to the base area to join the revolution. (Gu Naijin was then fifteen; he is now deputy director of the Culture Bureau of this city's Huangpu District, and a member of the Communist Party.)

In August 1945 the organization decided to send me, along with Tian Yunqiao, Fang Si, Zou Fanyang, and Wu Jie — five of us in all — to do underground work in Shanghai, under the direct leadership of Zhang Zhiyi, head of the City Work Department of the CCP Shanghai Bureau, and of Tian Yunqiao. In 1946, in order to turn the strength of the gangs against the enemy, the underground Party set up a Gang Work Committee, whose policy was to unite, educate, and rely on the masses, to wage struggle, to build up the revolutionary forces, to break down the enemy, and to make ready for the liberation of Shanghai. Tian Yunqiao was secretary of the committee, with Jin Longzhang and myself as members; Jin was responsible for work among the Hong Gang, I for work among the Green Gang. I told Gu Zhuxuan that the organization had sent me to do underground work in Shanghai, and he said he was willing to help. So at first our meetings were all held in Gu Zhuxuan's manager's office on the second floor of the Tianchan Stage, which became the safest place for us to gather. Relying, too, on his standing in society and on the Green Gang's organization, I secured a legal identity, gained a firm footing, built up our forces, and carried on the struggle against the enemy.

At that time the organization instructed me to use my connection with Gu Zhuxuan to stand for deputy head of Yulin District, so as to cover the Party's underground work. I put the request to Gu Zhuxuan, and he nominated me and, in several rounds, invited the influential people of Yulin District, along with the bao heads and district representatives, to gatherings — men who were in fact his own disciples and their disciples, or members of the Subei Native-Place Association in Shanghai. Over the meal Gu Zhuxuan hinted to them that they should exert themselves on my behalf in the election for deputy district head. In the end I won, and received my commission from the Nationalist municipal government.

To make still better use of the various social forces and gang connections, on Gu Zhuxuan's introduction I took as my teacher Hu Jing'an, who had once tutored Chiang Kai-shek, and became sworn brothers with Wu Zhongda (then head of Jiangning District), Ma Shuli (then director of the Frontline Daily, and later secretary-general of the Nationalist Party's Central Committee in Taiwan), and others; I also took on more than three hundred young men as pupils. Not a few of these young men rallied round me and served, in varying degrees, the underground work.

At the end of 1946, soon after the Nationalists set up the Yulin District Office, a "struggle against Meng Xinmin" was launched under the leadership of the underground Party, aimed at exposing the collusion of the Nationalists with the Wang puppet regime, at striking the enemy, and at educating the people.

Meng Xinmin was the newly appointed Nationalist head of Yulin District, a creature planted there by Fang Zhi, Pan Gongzhan, Qian Dajun, and their like. Under the Wang puppet regime he had served in Yizheng County, Jiangsu, as military judge of a Japanese land-reclamation garrison, and had put anti-Japanese patriots to death. After victory in the War of Resistance he slipped into Shanghai, transformed himself at a stroke into a district head, and set about oppressing the people once more. His crimes as a traitor had to be thoroughly exposed if the people were to be roused.

A worker in the district office at the time, Gu Xuanwu, was a young man eager to better himself and on close terms with me. He was a fellow townsman of Meng Xinmin's and had suffered much bullying at Meng's hands, and at my prompting he resolved to come forward and expose Meng's record. Using the connections of the Subei Native-Place Association in Shanghai, he went to Yizheng County in Jiangsu and gathered the original evidence of Meng Xinmin's collaboration — among it files in which Meng had personally authorized killings in his own hand — which, together with Meng's extortion, his violation of women, and his other unlawful acts over two years in Yulin District, were drawn up as ten major charges and laid before the municipal procuratorate and the local court, with a demand that he be punished according to law.

At the trial, Meng Xinmin, faced with the hard evidence marshaled by Gu Xuanwu and the others, could not deny it; his old swagger was swept away and he was reduced to abject confusion. In the end the court ordered him released on bail, to the unanimous satisfaction of the district representatives in the gallery. The next day the Zhonghua Times carried the story. From then on Meng Xinmin no longer dared to oppress the people openly, which served to protect them; but the greater gain was that the collusion of the Nationalists with the Wang puppets had been laid bare, so that the people saw it plainly for what it was.

After I became deputy head of Yulin District, I regularly attended the relevant meetings of the Nationalist municipal government, so that I not only kept abreast of the political tendencies of the Shanghai Nationalists and reported them to the organization in good time, but was able to obtain internal Nationalist materials directly and gathered a good deal of important intelligence. Once, for instance, at a congress of the Shanghai Nationalist Party, a register of the delegates from the municipal and district party headquarters and the special party branches was handed out; I kept that register right up to the liberation of Shanghai and gave it to the Municipal Public Security Bureau, where it served as a certain basis of verification for the 1951 campaign to suppress counterrevolutionaries. Again, seeing in the district a confidential document sent to the district office which said that underground Communists were at work among the street peddlers and were to be watched for, I at once reported it to the organization.

The young men who rallied round me in those days did no little service to the intelligence work as well. Comrade Jiang Hairong, for instance — whom I introduced into the Party after Liberation, and who now heads the Dahu Hotel — worked at the filling station of the municipal police bureau; using the chance the refueling gave him, he made out the numbers and license plates of the bureau's vehicles, and struck up acquaintance with the drivers, chatting with them whenever a car came in for fuel and asking what missions they were on, and so obtained important news of Nationalist arrests — he even traced the movements of the spy chief Mao Sen, head of the municipal police bureau. Again, on the eve of Shanghai's liberation, when the Nationalists, bent on holding the city, were throwing up defense works in the outskirts, not a few of the pupils who had taken me as their master were pedicab men, and under cover of pedaling their cabs they went out to the outskirts to observe the positions and numbers of the enemy's pillboxes and reported them to me. All this intelligence I passed on promptly to Tian Yunqiao, in preparation for the liberation of Shanghai.

On the eve of Shanghai's liberation, the Nationalists issued a notice for the distribution of citizens' identity cards and sealed off the household registers, meaning to hamper our Party's activities and persecute the revolutionary people. Reckoning that this would bring serious difficulties to the Party's underground work, I resolved to get hold of one of the official seals used for issuing the cards, so as to be prepared and to foil the enemy's scheme. As it happened, the clerk of the Twelfth Bao in the district, Zheng Delin, was an upright man, related to me, and one whom we had helped to plant in that bao; over two years he had done a good deal at my request, and I now charged him with the task. Taking the occasion when the cards had all been issued and the seals were to be destroyed on the spot, he secretly kept one seal back and chopped all the rest to bits, handing them to the issuing inspector, Zhao Zhiqing. When Zhao asked why they had been chopped so fine that they could not be counted, he pleaded orders from above — that they were to be smashed and destroyed on the spot to stop any abuse — and the district representatives standing by spoke up for him too, so that Zhao pressed the matter no further; and thus the seal passed, right under the enemy's nose, into my hands. At the same time Zheng Delin brought me some blank identity cards, and I later gathered more blank cards elsewhere, to be used by the underground comrades.

When the War of Liberation reached the outskirts of Shanghai, the Party wanted to send a body of comrades into the city to serve as an inside force for its liberation; but the enemy in the city checked citizens' identity cards strictly, and those without cards were either arrested or detained, so that for a time the comrades could not get in. Zhang Zhiyi and Tian Yunqiao were much alarmed and came to me to work out a plan, and in the end that one seal and the blank identity cards did their work and shattered the enemy's scheme.

Before Liberation, then, I carried out a number of revolutionary activities with Gu Zhuxuan's help and under the cover of the Green Gang's organization. The Party and the government do not forget any of those outside the Party who have contributed to the revolutionary cause. In August 1949, when Shanghai convened its First All-Circles People's Representative Conference, Gu Zhuxuan attended as a specially invited delegate. In July 1956 he died of dropsy, at the age of seventy-five.
