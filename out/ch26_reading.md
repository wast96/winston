## Chapter 26. The White Cloud May Be Slain but Not Detained

A cold autumn; a chill in the light.

The moon showed its ghastly-white face with an eerie air, stroking the earth with a cold hand.

In a temple long fallen into disrepair, at the foot of great round pillars whose red lacquer the wind and rain had eaten away, a few people gathered, their faces grave, their hearts grieving; silently they stood in the empty, icy inner hall, sending off a comrade fallen in battle.

Ronghua's memorial had no spirit-tablet, no ashes, no likeness of the dead, no gravestone. The members of the Central Special Branch's "Red Spear Squad" buried their tears and their grief-stricken resolve deep in their hearts, and the "sparks and tinder" of vengeance spread with the mournful wind into wildfire.

The wind beat its rhythm against the tiled eaves…

Zhong Yundi, deputy leader of the Red Spear Squad, listened coldly to the wind, his eyes holding a deep tenderness at parting from a comrade, and said in a low voice: "Comrade Timely Rain—go well on your road. Comrade Drifting Wind has come, too, to see you off."

Startled, the others looked outside; there was nothing there, only the wind still, the mournful wind howling.

Everyone understood, and bowed their heads in silent mourning.

"For the struggle to free the toiling millions! The soul of the revolutionary is unquenchable, generation on generation!!" Zhong Yundi spoke with fervor and passion. "Comrade Timely Rain—rest in peace." The company drew their pistols; unable to fire a salute, they raised the guns instead toward the sky in salutation, and so completed the whole rite of mourning.

"Comrades, through a traitor's betrayal the Central Special Branch now stands in grave peril. For this the Central Special Branch has restarted its newest plan and woken the hibernating snake, that he may take over Timely Rain's work directly. In other words: with Comrade Timely Rain fallen, a new Timely Rain is already in place. Comrade Timely Rain has issued us a first-order warning—that within the Central Special Branch there lies hidden an agent of Juntong, codename the Rivet." As Zhong Yundi reached this point, the crow's-feet at the corners of his eyes sank deep, and his face was like autumn frost.

"Comrade Wu Hao, leader of the Central Special Branch, orders us!"

The whole company stood grave, and came to attention.

"First, root out the traitor within; second, protect the special envoy; third, sternly punish the turncoats. To clear away obstacles for the smooth convening of the enlarged national work conference of the Party's special committees—to guard it and see it safely through."

"We pledge to complete the task," the company answered with one voice.

Night's curtain hung low; the last of the daylight fled in panic into the black-shrouded hills.

In the great compound of the Rong mansion, the Third Madam's room was very warm; the maid Xing'er, charged specially by Rong Sheng, tended with painstaking care to the Third Madam's meals and daily comforts.

The Third Madam did not know that Ronghua had gone off with the wind and rain. High and low, the whole Rong household kept this dreadful grief buried between their brows and behind their eyes, and were toward the Third Madam more meek and obliging than ever. Out of pity for the Third Madam, the First Madam had gone along with her son's wishes and kept the news of Ronghua's death sealed up airtight.

The Third Madam knew few characters and did not care to read the newspapers; the Third Madam stood on ceremony and did not care to go out shopping in person—whatever she wanted to buy, she would mostly have the shopkeeper send a clerk round with it. All these habits, for the time being, played into Rong Sheng's anxious design.

Xing'er, forcing a smile, burned incense for the Third Madam; she carried the kingfisher-green cage of it to the Third Madam's side, and a piercing fragrance shot up, driving straight into the vitals—yet the Third Madam suddenly caught in it the smell of decay.

"Xing'er." The Third Madam stretched her legs lazily and said, "I've been poorly these last few days—how is it your Second Miss hasn't come back to see me? Didn't you have the Eldest Young Master telephone her?"

"We did." Xing'er's tone was as crisp and clean as ever. "The Second Miss is busy—her bookshop is to open a branch in Nanjing, and she's gone to Nanjing, run off her feet. A little ailment like yours—don't disturb the Second Miss at her money-making. What, are you saying I don't wait on you well enough? Trying to find fault with me on the sly?"

"My child." The Third Madam reached out affectionately and pinched Xing'er's cheek. "Sharp of tooth and quick of tongue. We'll see who dares to marry you by and by."

"I won't marry at all—I'll wait on you my whole life, how about that?"

"Silly talk." The Third Madam sat up. "I'm not one of your black-hearted women who buys a maidservant only to order her about and grind her down; we're all our fathers' and mothers' children. Rest easy—your whole future rests on me. No pauper's son, no country bumpkin, no cart-driver: none of them shall you marry. Wait till our Ronghua is married off to some great house as its young mistress, and I'll send you along in her dowry as a second wife."

"There, there—as for me, I don't hanker after it." Xing'er turned her back, and there were tears glittering in her eyes.

"What's the matter?"

"The incense got in my eyes," Xing'er said, smiling.

The Third Madam laughed.

"The First Madam says that a few days back she went to the silk shop and had several outfits made up for the Second Miss; they came today. The First Madam asks you to look them over and see whether they'll do." Even as she spoke, calm and self-possessed, Xing'er spread out the newly made "burial clothes" and let the Third Madam choose them with her own hands.

The Third Madam knit her brows and said, "The colors aren't bright. Why, out of a clear sky, make clothes for her at all?"

"They say the Second Miss's business is doing well and has made money for the household. The First Madam's having a few clothes made for her—it's a kind of reward, you might say."

"Hmph. As for doing business, who's shrewder than our Ronghua?" The Third Madam warmed to the subject. "Never mind that the First Madam manages the family fortune now; in time, can we look to the Eldest Young Master? We cannot! We must still rely on our Ronghua." Carefully, in earnest, she chose among the clothes. "A qipao—it wants kingfisher-green trim and touches of gold to look well, opulent, in the fashion…"

Xing'er bent her head; a gust of wind stole in and blew the burial clothes cold as ice.

In the Liyun Pavilion the lamplight was dim. Rong Sheng, holding Ronghua's portrait in his hands, sat dazed in a carved wooden chair, his eyes deep-sunken, his hair disheveled, his face gray with sorrow. Lishui kept the First Madam company, sighing and wiping away tears.

"Your son means to bury Second Sister tomorrow night," Rong Sheng said, his face haggard. "To lay her right beside Eldest Sister, so the two of them can look after each other…" Rong Sheng could not go on for grief.

The First Madam's heart ached. However much she despised the Third Madam, however surely she and the Third Madam would forever sail in different channels, still, as a mother and as a woman, she felt deep pity for the Third Madam's misfortune.

"You keep it from her now; but when she comes to know it later—that she never even saw her own daughter off on her last road—how will she feel then?" The First Madam thought far ahead.

"To tell her now is to kill her now," Rong Sheng said. "I think Second Sister, in the world below, would not wish to see her own mother in an hour of such anguish and despair."

"And by and by, what's to be done?" the First Madam said. "You may keep it hidden for a while, but not for a lifetime."

Rong Sheng could give no answer; he bowed his head low, until his forehead touched the cold picture-frame, and the tears welling from the corners of his eyes fell scattering upon the portrait.

"I have a plan," Lishui said. "Isn't Second Sister a very left-leaning, very modern sort of person? Nowadays a great many university students are running off to Yan'an. Let us just say that Second Sister has gone to Yan'an."

"Gone to Yan'an?" The First Madam was full of doubt.

"Isn't the government hunting Communists, and hunting them hard? Anyone caught is to be shot! On that ground alone the Third Madam won't dare make a fuss; if everyone holds their tongue, this lie can be told on and on."

The First Madam and Rong Sheng looked at each other, and said almost at the same moment, "Will it work?"

"It will!" Lishui thumped her chest.

"Let it be so, then. So long as a person has hope, she will go on living." The First Madam said to Rong Sheng, with meaning in her words. "And how have matters gone with the bookshop?"

"Director Han says someone set the fire deliberately," Rong Sheng said. "The losses are grievous. Your son means to rebuild the shop from the ground up—as a way of remembering Second Sister."

"That will take a great sum of money." In her heart the First Madam reckoned up the cost of rebuilding the shop. "And once the shop is open, it will need a reliable person to run it."

"Your son thought…" Rong Sheng raised his eyes and glanced at his mother. "Let Rong Gui do it—he's sure to run it well. We, mother and son, ought to do something for him. What do you say?"

The First Madam's face grew very dark.

"As you please," the First Madam said; and at last her eyelids drooped, which was to say she agreed.

In the early morning Yashu woke from her warm bed; on the pillow-cover beside her lay strands of A-Chu's hair, and Yashu was certain that this lovely night had been no gorgeous spring dream but a true and trustworthy dream come real.

She threw on a robe and got up; outside she heard the crisp sound of cups and dishes being set down, and, of course, her lover's footsteps.

Her heart was full and content.

Her gaze reached out through the crack of the door and dwelt on the leather shoes of her lover, pacing to and fro.

The door was pushed gently open, and Yashu, her toilet complete, appeared before A-Chu with a graceful, dignified air, a faint delicate fragrance about her—loose sleeves and a short jacket, a wide robe drawn in at a slender waist, her feet in high heels a thing of allure, her eyes brimming with tenderness—and came lightly toward A-Chu.

"Good morning," A-Chu said.

"Good morning," Yashu said.

"I've made breakfast." A-Chu smiled as he drew out a chair for Yashu, attentively seating her. "I don't know whether it's to your taste."

Yashu's eyes did not see the good things on the table—only a happy daze.

A-Chu's caring eyes, limpid as autumn water, rippled through Yashu's heart; after so many years of waiting, all the memories of a love forced to break off in mid-journey came winding back before her eyes, pressing on her fragile, sensitive nerves.

"What's wrong?" A-Chu asked with concern.

"Thank you." Feeling she had let herself go, Yashu gave her lover once again her sweetest smile.

"You're very lovely when you smile." A-Chu sat down across from her and poured her the better part of a glass of milk. "Do you know, a woman as elegant and gentle as you is very hard to find."

"Elegant and gentle—perhaps that's only the surface," Yashu said. "There are many fine girls in society now; they dress splendidly, take up Western fashions, and know how to seize hold of life's happiness."

"The winds and rains of Europe and America have indeed made a whole crop of new women in society. But a great many proud girls have learned nothing of the advanced thinking—only the packaging," A-Chu said. "They wear name brands, put on their makeup, study their deportment, and now and then can even counterfeit the rest. The one thing they cannot do is open their mouths; and the moment they open their mouths, the high and the low, the refined and the vulgar, show up on the instant."

"What sort of woman do you like?" Yashu asked.

"'In advancing, no joy; in retreating, no grief,'" A-Chu answered.

"That doesn't seem to be my type," Yashu said, looking A-Chu level in the eye, not the least equivocal.

"I admire your discernment," A-Chu said calmly. "In truth, you don't necessarily know yourself as well as you think. Do you know—whether your family stood in its eminence or fell into decline, you managed all the same to take no joy in advancing and no grief in retreating. It's only that you haven't yet realized it yourself." A-Chu set down the tableware in his hand and said, "You are very strong."

"Strong—that seems to be your patent," Yashu said.

"Do you like to pay me back in my own words?" A-Chu laughed.

The telephone rang. Yashu was much surprised; she turned her head to look, and on the wall of the sitting room an old-style wall telephone was vibrating.

"I never imagined…" Yashu said.

"Never imagined what?" A-Chu went over and answered it.

Yashu wiped her mouth with her napkin and followed him over. "I'd always thought that wall set was an ornament."

"A work of art," A-Chu said. "A very serviceable one, though." He lifted the receiver. "Hello, who is it?"

"Sir, it's me." Han Zhengqi's voice came through the receiver.

"What is it?"

"It concerns the Second Master."

"Where are you?"

"At the Police Bureau."

"In fifteen minutes, at the little restaurant by the crossroads outside the Police Bureau." A-Chu hung up.

"Do you have to go out?" Yashu asked.

"Yes."

Yashu went to the coat-stand to fetch A-Chu's overcoat; standing at the door, she made A-Chu feel, for the first time, the warmth of a "home." He could not help wishing to embrace her, yet he did not turn the wish into deed; he only took the coat, and murmured softly by her ear, "I will be good to you."

Yashu's blood rose once again; she nodded and smiled, and this smile came from the heart, without the least trace of embellishment left in it.

A-Chu went out.

Behind him, the ground was full of sunlight.

Less than half an hour after A-Chu had left Plum Blossom Lane, Li Qinhong received a detailed report from the agents she had set to watch it. They gave a full account of the dwelling where A-Chu had lingered the whole night, and of the woman, particulars and all.

When these materials were laid one by one before Li Qinhong's desk, she made, almost in the first instant, the clearest of judgments: this elder brother of A-Ci's could never be a Communist.

For if he were a Communist, he would never place his own lover in such danger; if he were a Communist, could he, after all that had happened, come and go freely at Plum Blossom Lane as though nothing were amiss? He would not shrink from even a hair's breadth of suspicion—and there could be but one reason for that: he simply did not know that the very house next door to his lover's was a Communist liaison point.

So the two Yang brothers' suspicion of being Communists ought to be ruled out. Then who was that mysterious man who had answered the telephone?

Li Qinhong was extremely sensitive by nature; like a keen-nosed hound, she was steeped in the game of "hunting and killing" the Communist Special Branch. Why had she not gone to search the Rong mansion? For one thing, Ronghua could never have kept a radio set or other confidential apparatus at home; the burning of the Huamei Bookstore had in fact already borne out her deduction. For another, to allow that Ronghua's car crash had been an accident rather than deliberate sabotage would serve to lull the Communist Special Branch, to make them believe that Plum Blossom Lane, rented by Ronghua, could still be used. She could pay out the line and land a big fish. What was more, her "Rivet" was already driven firm and deep into the enemy's very heart; she believed that so long as the Communists did not give up this enlarged national conference of their special committees, she would surely have her chance to net the whole shoal at a single haul.

In a most unremarkable little eatery sat a man in a large top hat, a scarf wound about his neck, muffling half his face.

Zhong Yundi was waiting here—waiting for the third man he was to speak with to appear.

The man came, dressed in a loose silk gown, got up like a merchant, and sat down across from Zhong Yundi.

"Been here long?" the man said in greeting.

"Yes."

"Calling me out in such a hurry—has something special happened?"

"The organization has decided to requisition your dwelling temporarily, as a temporary liaison point, to handle the special committees' group discussions."

"My home is on Fourth Avenue—is that suitable?"

Fourth Avenue in Shanghai was extremely lively, its crowds thick and mixed, its ground complicated.

"Stillness at the heart of the din; safety at the heart of the peril," Zhong Yundi said.

"Very well. When?"

"Today."

"Today?"

"Yes. Now."

"I have a wife and children at home." The man plainly had his misgivings.

"It won't be long."

"Why not use Plum Blossom Lane?"

"Plum Blossom Lane is to be the formal venue of the Central Special Committee's conference," Zhong Yundi said. "Is there anything else you don't understand?"

"No. Only…"

"What?"

"That business at Hengji Li the other day—I suspect there is a problem inside our own ranks." He hesitated a moment. "Of course, I myself was the recording secretary of the meeting, and the last man to leave Hengji Li that day; the suspicion falls heaviest on me…"

"The organization sees to the very tip of the autumn hair," Zhong Yundi said. "You needn't carry that burden in your mind. Trust the organization; wait for the inquiry to clear it up. The truth will very soon come to light."

"I trust the organization!" His manner was much moved; he put out his hand and gripped Zhong Yundi's tight, saying, "Take care." He left the little eatery quickly.

Zhong Yundi glanced at his watch, marked that all around was without anything amiss, and went on waiting—waiting for the fourth man he must speak with. Ever since the Central Special Branch had been broken into, the organization had resolved to comb through, one by one, all the members who had taken part in the preparatory meetings for the "enlarged conference of the special committees"—and above all to mark as chief suspects the Central Secretariat personnel who had gone in and out of Hengji Li on the day of the incident. Zhong Yundi held special interviews with them, speaking the same words to each, observing their differing behavior. They believed it in earnest; and of course the "Rivet" hidden within would let down his guard as well. So long as the "Rivet" judged that "Plum Blossom Lane" was of the highest value to exploit, then the Comintern member who had for the moment lost contact with the organization—if he risked a rash approach to "Plum Blossom Lane"—would at least not be arrested on the spot. Among these several interviewed men, whoever's dwelling suddenly saw "agents" appear nearby, or a sudden crop of persons of unknown identity, peddlers among them included—that man would be the "Rivet," plain to see at a glance.

After Zhong Yundi had waited a quarter of an hour, a cloud of doubt rose in his heart.

Why had the fourth man never appeared?

Just as he was resolving to leave, he caught sight of the woman courier Tian Xiuyun hurrying up. Zhong Yundi rose and walked outside; Tian Xiuyun was selling cigarettes at the door of the little eatery. Zhong Yundi took out money to buy a pack, and in the instant of paying and taking the cigarettes, they held a brief exchange.

"Snow Wolf can't come."

"Why?" Zhong Yundi asked.

"He's been bitten fast by a whole pack of hounds."

"Can the hounds be made to loose their grip?"

"We can try. I've already sent A-Chun out."

A-Chun was Tian Xiuyun's husband, a peripheral member of the Special Branch, whose cover was that of a "police runner"; he had long knocked about the Racecourse quarter of the Shanghai Bund, and had brothers he knew among the Concession patrol house and the Police Bureau, so that whenever a Special Branch comrade was arrested by mishap, it was mostly A-Chun who stepped forward to bail him out.

Snow Wolf was one of the secretaries of the Central Secretariat, and the fourth man for whom Zhong Yundi waited; at Snow Wolf's unlooked-for arrest, Zhong Yundi was very much on the alert.

He wondered: why arrested at this particular moment? And what was the reason for the arrest?

Tian Xiuyun gave him the answer. "The murder of the amah at Hengji Li, blown up by all the big papers, has put the Police Bureau's criminal section under great pressure; they're casting their net everywhere, hoping to run the murderer to earth as fast as they can. Because Snow Wolf had frequently gone in and out of Hengji Li, a neighbor picked him out, and the Police Bureau arrested him at once on a charge of murder."

"Can he be bailed out without trouble?"

"Not clear."

"All right, I understand."

Zhong Yundi and Tian Xiuyun soon went their separate ways.

The sky darkened all at once, dark clouds flying low; Zhong Yundi circled through several side lanes, and having made sure no one was tailing him, went into a clothing shop. A quarter of an hour later Zhong Yundi came out in a Western suit and leather shoes, a document case in his hand, threaded street and alley, and at last went into a small, exquisite-looking café.

By the method arranged beforehand, he sat down at the second seat by the window—the seat he had reserved, on which a newspaper already lay. The waiter brought his coffee; he deliberately glanced left and right, and suddenly noticed that in the seat back to back with his own sat a woman, tilting her head slightly and purposely back, the fragrance of her hair drifting brazenly by Zhong Yundi's earlobe.

"Don't turn round," the woman spoke.

Zhong Yundi did not turn; with his back to her, he was perfectly composed.

"You asked me here—is there business?"

"You're late," the woman said.

"Snow Wolf has fallen into the trap." Zhong Yundi spread the newspaper open in his hands, screening his face.

"Then you must root out the Rivet as fast as you can."

"Our inquiry has entered its substantive stage."

"Any discoveries?"

"It needs time."

"There is no time. The convening of the special-committee conference is upon us." The woman took a sip of coffee; Zhong Yundi heard clearly the silver spoon stirring it—plainly the new "Timely Rain" was in a state of unusual anxiety.

"When we investigate the traitor within, we can't help but base ourselves on members inside the organization, and we often overlook certain outside factors," the woman said.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean that in the way we're considering the problem now, we need to break out of the fixed grooves of thought. The Rivet is not necessarily someone in our core departments. But we cannot guarantee that every comrade working in a core department has social ties that are flawless and reliable."

"Cast the net too wide and it works against us. We cannot keep every core member's social ties under surveillance. To do so would not only fail to mend matters, it would set every man in fear for himself."

"Create an atmosphere of tension, and the Rivet will be more sensitive than anyone; at the moment when both attack and defense are exhausted, he will lose the power of correct judgment."

"How is it to be done?"

"Use Plum Blossom Lane as bait, to draw the agents' attention."

"But we've been unable all along to make contact with the Comintern's special envoy. What if he—?"

"Fish and bear's paw cannot both be had." The woman seemed to have made up her mind. "On the third page of the newspaper in your hand, there's a piece of news: the Yang Industrial Company has successfully bought out the Xianghe Cotton Mill."

"I've seen it."

"The boss of the Yang Industrial Company is Drifting Wind's elder brother."

Zhong Yundi was taken aback. "Ronghua never mentioned it before."

"I only learned it myself just now. This time, thanks to his stepping in, Drifting Wind came safely through his crisis. He can use a hospital to save his own younger brother; we too can use a hospital as cover to accomplish our mission."

"Will he cooperate?"

"He must cooperate." The woman answered with great confidence. "I'll leave you an envelope; look at it after I've gone." The woman stood up and walked toward the door; a careless flick of her hand, and an envelope dropped precisely at the tip of Zhong Yundi's toes. The woman left swiftly. Zhong Yundi picked up the envelope; inside was a sheet of very old, very thin paper.

When Zhong Yundi saw clearly what was written on it, he could not help a great start of shock.

It was a traitor's "recantation," and it ran: In 1927 the Communist bandit-woman Bai Yun, arrested in Zhabei District, and moved by the government's call, resolved to give herself up, of her own will withdrew from the Party, and would actively assist the government in seizing her Communist accomplices, that she might earn merit to atone for her former offenses, and so forth.

The recantation was written in great disorder; there were old bloodstains on the paper, and beneath it was appended a photograph of Bai Yun.

Bai Yun was Tian Xiuyun's alias!!

In the second-floor office block of the Police Bureau many policemen came and went, among them the family members who had come to the Bureau to plead for a prisoner's bail; A-Chun was just then furnishing "Snow Wolf" with the bond-guarantor's certificate required for the bail.

In the room next door A-Chu was sunk in deep anxiety. Only twenty minutes before, with Han Zhengqi's help, he had listened in beneath the window of the interrogation room while the police questioned the murder suspect in the Hengji Li amah case; A-Chu was disappointed—this man's voice proved, beyond all doubt, that he was not the one he was hunting.

Ever since A-Chu had conceived the notion of acknowledging his brother, he had lived in unusual hardship. There he was, with no gain in prospect, unconsciously—no, one should say deliberately—sharing the dangers of A-Ci.

He knew very little of the cause to which A-Ci devoted himself, yet at critical moments he had to step in and lend a hand; nearly every day A-Chu faced a "challenge to survival," and, to speak in all conscience, he was baffled to find himself so tenderly guarding a younger brother with whom he shared blood but no feeling.

So long as the man who did the voice-matching over the telephone remained unfound for a single day, A-Ci was in that much more danger for a day.

With A-Ci's life without the least guarantee, the "vengeance" plan A-Chu had labored so carefully to concoct would once more run aground. Link fitted close to link; not one link could go wrong.

"I need him! From every angle, I need him," A-Chu said.

"You say you need," Han Zhengqi said.

"Yes."

"You mean the Second Master."

"Yes."

"Sir, you could perfectly well take another course—move him, and then send him abroad."

A-Chu gave a faint smile. "Do you think he would fall in with any arrangement of mine? No, he would not. He would sooner die for his cause than take flight, because flight would make him a coward. Do you know—I was wrong too. From the very first I cared about his weal and woe, his glory and disgrace; and the upshot? At every turn, everywhere, I am helping him save himself! Saving himself, over and over." A-Chu let out a long breath. "He forces me to look far and plan ahead, to tread the mines and lay the road with him, to fight with our backs to the river."

"Sir, I will find that man," Han Zhengqi said, plainly to comfort A-Chu. In the great city of Shanghai, to find a man hidden away in the dark was for all the world like fishing a needle out of the sea.

"Reporting," a policeman called from outside the door.

"Come in." Han Zhengqi sat back in his place.

A young policeman came in. "Someone has come to bail out the murder suspect in the Hengji Li case."

"Who is the guarantor?"

"A police runner from the patrol house."

"Are the papers in order?"

"In order."

"Then do it by the rules."

"Wait." A-Chu cut in. "I'd like to listen once more."

A-Chu would not give up.

The light was very dim; A-Chu met with "Snow Wolf" alone.

"Snow Wolf's" cover was that of a professional accountant at a trading firm; his features were mild, his cultivation good; he wore a black Western suit, and his face was shadowed and calm.

"You aren't the least bit alarmed," A-Chu said, his gaze roving over him.

"I—I've been wronged."

"Everyone who comes in here says as much. Can't you try some other way of putting it?"

"You don't look like a policeman."

"Good eyes."

"Since you're not a policeman, you have no right to interrogate me here."

"I know what kind of man you are." A-Chu lowered his voice. "Your surname is 'Communist,' isn't it, elder brother?"

"Snow Wolf" laughed. "You have a rich imagination."

"You've been through more than fifty hours of confinement and questioning, and you're not the least bit thrown into confusion; you answer with ground to stand on, your bearing calm—which shows your training and cultivation all the more. However you look at it, you're no accountant at a trading firm."

"How do you think I ought to behave? A terror that doesn't know which way to turn? Or begging for mercy with a runny nose and streaming eyes?"

"What was your relation to that amah?" A-Chu launched suddenly at the heart of it.

"That of a client." "Snow Wolf" answered fast and mechanically.

"You had employed her?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Two years ago."

"When the thing happened, why were you there?"

"I had gone especially to find her. There is an invalid in my house, and I wanted to ask her to come and help."

"How did you know her home?"

"We had kept in touch all along. Sir, I must correct one thing you said. No. 1141, Hengji Li, is not her home, but the home of the new client she served."

A seamless pretext. A-Chu smiled. "You've prepared very well. But I didn't come to watch you perform; I came to hear your voice. Your voice is very clean—you are indeed not the man I'm hunting. Set your mind at ease; you'll be bailed out very soon."

"Thank you." Though "Snow Wolf" had not entirely understood A-Chu's words, he read in A-Chu's eyes the two characters for "goodwill," and so thanked A-Chu politely.

A-Chu walked out of the interrogation room, head bowed. Han Zhengqi and Liu A-Si both stood waiting for him at the door; there was no expression on his face, and Han Zhengqi knew that A-Chu was disappointed.

"Release him as soon as you can," A-Chu said.

Han Zhengqi nodded, and they walked toward the corridor. Han Zhengqi meant to see A-Chu out; and just as they neared the door of the criminal-investigation section, someone came bowing and scraping out of it.

The newcomer happened to block the way in front of A-Chu and the others.

"Thank you, thank you." A-Chun had already got the bail papers and, coming out with the policeman, was thanking him with great attentiveness. "I've put you brothers to trouble."

"From now on, don't take on so much of this business," said a policeman who had come out after him. "How much are they paying you? These people run deep."

"Yes, yes. Your little brother is only doing a job entrusted to him, seeing it through for another…" A-Chun was still speaking when he came face to face with A-Chu.

This was truly a case of: you wear out iron shoes tramping in search of it, and then find it without the least effort.

"You—?" A-Chun was somewhat puzzled.

The policeman came to attention.

Han Zhengqi waved the policeman away.

A-Chu's spirits soared at that moment, as though the clouds and mist had parted to show clear blue sky. "Do you still remember my voice?" A-Chu asked.

A-Chun's face changed utterly; he turned on his heel to run. Liu A-Si, like a wild horse slipping its halter, flew down the corridor and jammed himself fast across A-Chun's path of flight. A-Chu's feeling swelled up all at once; Han Zhengqi could see that A-Chu meant to kill again.

A long-disused lumber room in the Police Bureau became the "Rivet's" last spot on earth.

"Let's not go round and round," A-Chu said. "You must know who I am."

"Adjutant Yang, we've no feud from a former life, no grievance in this one."

"But you nearly took my life."

"That was a misunderstanding."

"No misunderstanding. I am no Adjutant Yang."

"But—your voice?"

"So you really do have a keen ear for voices." A-Chu felt that this man must on no account be left alive.

"Adjutant Yang, I never entrapped you. Be magnanimous, sir." The "Rivet" pleaded with him. "I'm a member of the special-intelligence group, a man of merit in cracking the Xiang bandit case; Section-head Li can bear witness for me."

"I'll ask you one question only. On the day of the incident, did you go to No. 1141, Hengji Li?"

"I did."

"To do what?"

"To win the Detective Division time for its operation, I eliminated a Communist bandit-woman…"

"That is, the old amah."

"The amah was her cover; she was a Communist bandit. She sent the special committees of the Party a warning to withdraw; after I eliminated her, I switched the warning for a safe-signal. Had it not been for that sudden car crash, we would long since have netted the Communist committees at a single haul—and I could have gone back to my unit. A pity; the work fell short by one basketful of earth." The "Rivet" seized A-Chu's hand and said, "Believe me, every word I've said is true. As for the telephone voice-matching, that was Section-head Li's arrangement. With so great a thing having happened, an internal screening is the usual practice, Adjutant Yang."

"I told you—I am no Adjutant Yang." A-Chu shook off his hand and said coldly, "I am Adjutant Yang's elder brother."

"What?" The "Rivet" was as if struck a stunning blow over the head.

"You're a clever man; you know why I've been so anxious to find you, don't you? So long as you live, my brother's life is forfeit."

"No wonder… no wonder, that voice? No—you can't kill me. They know I came in here." This was the "Rivet's" last hope. "If I vanish without a clear account, none of you will escape suspicion."

"Only suspicion," A-Chu said. "But if you were to get out—suspicion would harden into iron fact."

The "Rivet" began to tremble.

"Set your mind at ease. I'll let you go without a sound," A-Chu said coldly.

Time seemed on the instant to congeal.

A-Chu gazed at him long and long; a chill rose little by little from the soles of the man's feet, and he felt his whole body shudder, his eyes brimming with terror. "I'll keep my mouth shut tight as a jar," he said.

"No use now," A-Chu said. "In this trade, you should long since have reckoned on the day it all comes to light. I trust you have prepared your mind enough to meet death."

The "Rivet" lost all control; his eyeballs all but burst from their sockets, for the cord bound him too tight, and struggle as he might it availed nothing. Liu A-Si sealed his eyes and mouth with a black strip of cloth; his face began to writhe—perhaps from terror, his color turned ghastly beyond words.

"In your next life, be reborn a good man." A-Chu made to Liu A-Si the sign for "kill at once."
