## Afterword II

The earliest idea for this novel grew out of a question somebody put on Zhihu: If you were to write the storyline for Assassin’s Creed, where would you set it?

Assassin’s Creed is a sandbox video game in which the hero moves about through a city of ancient or of more recent times, carrying out assassinations of every kind; I am very fond of playing it.

When I saw that question, the first thing that rose up in my mind was the Chang’an of the Tang.

Tang Chang’an is, to me, a place out of dream. It was a great city, ordered and magnificent, where people of every station and from every corner of the land gathered together, the elegance of letters and the awe of arms crossing and recrossing, its life splendid and many-colored, its temper open and manifold. There anything at all might happen; it is truly the fittest stage a maker of stories could ever conceive.

Picture the figure of an assassin leaping down from the Wild Goose Pagoda under the full moon, the fiery-red lanterns that hunt him stretching from the Vermilion Bird Avenue all the way to Qujiang Pool, startling up the countless roosting birds on the Leyou Plateau … here is a fragment brimming with pictures, mystery and grandeur tangled together in one; and could it but be written out, what a delightful thing that would be.

So I dashed off a few thousand words, meaning at first no more than to indulge a flight of fancy; but, to my surprise, the more I wrote the more excited I grew, and a full-length story quietly took shape of itself. As the tale went on, my ambitions swelled. I tried to make its pace quicker, its structure more intricate and complex, the character of every player closer to the way a modern reader thinks. To put it plainly, what I hoped to bring forth was no longer a costume adventure of an assassin, but a modern story set in a great international metropolis—one that merely happened, by chance, to take place in ancient times.

As luck would have it, Chang’an is precisely such a city, one whose temper transcends both time and place: it can hold the classical and the modern at once, with never a jar to the sense of it. And so this story shifted, little by little, from a slow-paced costume romance of chivalry into a fast-paced, lone-hero drama of counter-terror set in ancient days. To make this quality plainer still, I even cut the plot up afresh—after the fashion of the American series 24, with its division into episodes—one chapter to each half-shichen, twenty-four chapters in all, exactly the span of a single day.

In writing a work of this kind, the greatest challenge is not the weaving of the story nor the molding of the characters, but the exact depiction of the living detail of that age. To set the reader down within it, to let him feel a living, breathing Chang’an, the author must have the whole of that stretch of history at his fingertips: how they drank their tea, how they took their meals, where they relieved themselves, how they rode in a carriage; what a woman wore upon her head when she went abroad, how a man laid out his money away from home; from the statutes and institutions of the court above, down to the prices of food and goods below—nay, even which way the sewers of Chang’an ran, and of what pattern were the railings that held back the water, and so on and on. What must be depicted is, in truth, a whole entire world; and however minutely one writes it, it is never too much.

To this end I pored, in fear and trembling, over a great mass of materials; monographs and archaeological reports alone I read by the heap, and I went besides to Xi’an several times to see the ground for myself, in the hope of drawing a little nearer to that true Chang’an.

Here I owe especial thanks for the strong support of several friends—Professor Yu Gengzhe, Jinghong, Saoshu Mao, Senlin Lu, Hei Dupi Xiaoti, and others—who furnished me with much precious material, and who spared the time, besides, to read my poor work with care and offer me all manner of comment on the wording and the plot. Such thoughtfulness I have kept graven ever since upon my heart. For an amateur lover of history and letters, to have come to know friends so deeply learned and yet so ready to share what they know—it is truly a fine thing.
