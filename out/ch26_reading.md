## Itō Kashitarō

By the way: in the days when the present writer and his fellows were sitting the higher-school entrance examinations, there was among the cram-books for English-to-Japanese translation one, known familiarly as the "Ono-Kei," which stayed a best-seller, most likely, for a good twenty years and more.

Its author was Mr. Ono Keijirō, a name that must be a fond one to anyone now aged thirty to fifty. Mr. Ono was born in the second year of Meiji, the son of a physician of Chinese medicine in Fukushima prefecture, came out of the Tokyo Higher Normal School into the world of English teaching, ended as a professor at the Matsuyama Higher Commercial School, and died in November of the twenty-seventh year of Shōwa, the day after the present Crown Prince's investiture, at the age of eighty-four.

The obituaries ran in all the papers at the time. As a man long dear to the world of examinees, he was given a fairly full obituary in every paper; but that his father-in-law had been the Shinsengumi man Suzuki Mikisaburō, and that his uncle by marriage had been Itō Kashitarō — that, of course, went unwritten.

Mr. Ono had a rare and devoted purpose. The royalties he earned from the "Ono-Kei" cram-books he poured into the study of his father Ono Ryōi, and of Suzuki Mikisaburō and Itō Kashitarō; and in the fifteenth year of Shōwa he brought out, gathering it all together, a single not-for-sale volume. It ranks now, even in the world of secondhand books, among the rarities.

That Itō Kashitarō.

He was the son of a rōnin of Shizuku in Hitachi. A slender, handsome man, every inch of the clever, quick-witted stamp.

Because in his youth he had left home and studied first at Mito, learning the martial and the scholarly arts there, he took the baptism of the Mito brand of revere-the-Emperor, expel-the-barbarian thought. He was on close terms even with Takeda Iga-no-kami, chief of the Mito loyalist party (a karō; later he took the name Kōunsai, raised a volunteer barbarian-expelling army, and was put to death for it) — so Itō's own loyalism must have been a fairly extreme thing.

Itō was now in Edo.

He kept a dōjō at Sagachō in Fukagawa.

His pupils came to a hundred or so, which is a large dōjō as dōjō go.

It was just after the Hamaguri Gate affair that Toshizō heard the tale that this Itō Kashitarō had a mind to join the Shinsengumi — bringing with him a good many comrades and pupils — and would not mind doing so.

Toshizō heard it from Kondō.

"Is it true?"

Toshizō too had heard the man's fame.

"It's true. Heisuke reported it, so, by letter."

{j} and he showed Toshizō a letter from the jokin Tōdō Heisuke, who was just then down in Edo.

Toshizō gave it a glance.

"Well now — Itō Kashitarō,"

{j} he said doubtfully.

"Is the man to be relied on?"

"He's reliable enough."

Kondō was of a believing nature.

And it was a time when hands were wanted. What with the great works that had come one on another of late — the Ikedaya affair, the Hamaguri Gate affair, the seizing of the Chōshū residence at Osaka — the men had dwindled, through death in battle, through wounds, through desertion, to somewhere around sixty. If Itō would join, bringing a crowd of his pupils with him, the commander Kondō was in a humor to weep for joy and welcome him.

"I wonder, though,"

{j} said Toshizō, staring at Kondō's great jaw.

"You don't like it, Toshi?"

"This man's a scholar, isn't he?"

"And what's wrong with that? The Shinsengumi is a gathering of nothing but swordsmen; the ones with an eye for the Four Books and Five Classics or the works on war, who can turn out a piece of writing, come to no more than Yamanami Keisuke, Takeda Kanryūsai, and Ogata Shuntarō."

"None of them worth much."

Where their tails were fastened on, how their spines were set — of such fellows Toshizō could make nothing. Learning was well enough. But no kind of man was more useless to an iron-hard body like the Shinsengumi than one with too much power of thought turned on his own circumstances. So he believed. Toshizō meant, come what might, to forge the Shinsengumi into a military organization hard as steel.

But Kondō was otherwise. He was fond of scholars. That he kept a man like Takeda Kanryūsai — a spineless, flattering word-monger of a samurai if ever anyone saw one — in high employ as jokin and secretary was one sign of it. Scholars and men of argument were just now the ornament Kondō most wanted.

For Kondō had his outward dealings. He was now on terms of direct discussion even with Matsudaira Katamori, the Kyoto Protector, who stood in the capital as the shogunate's deputy.

He talked, too, with the senior men of the domains, on a footing of equality and better. In their company he set famous debaters against himself and argued the affairs and business of the day. Kondō was now no mere swordsman, but one of the men of political weight in Kyoto.

For that he needed at his side an intellectual bodyguard. Men of Takeda's or Ogata's grade were no longer enough.

And into this, dropping as if out of the sky, came the matter of Itō Kashitarō.

That Kondō leapt at it was no wonder.

"For one thing, Itō Kashitarō means the Hokushin Ittō-ryū, doesn't it?"

"Hm — one of the great schools of the realm."

Not a potato-patch school like the Tennen Rishin-ryū.

"And yet,"

{j} Toshizō did not like it.

The Hokushin Ittō-ryū (its founder Chiba Shūsaku, its dōjō at Otamagaike in Kanda, Edo) had the Mito Tokugawa house for its greatest patron, and naturally out of this gate came a great many Mito-school revere-the-Emperor, expel-the-barbarian men. Count them on the fingers and there rose to Toshizō's mind such names as Kaiho Hanpei, Chiba Jūtarō, Kiyokawa Hachirō, Sakamoto Ryōma.

They were anti-shogunal men. Some were outright topple-the-shogunate men. In short, no different at all from the Chōshū sort of revere-the-Emperor, expel-the-barbarian man.

—Is Itō to be relied on?

The place where Toshizō said this was here.

—Reliable enough.

What Kondō meant was Itō's learning and his swordsmanship. He could handle a blade. He could handle one terrifyingly well.

The school Itō Kashitarō had first mastered at Mito was the Shintō Munen-ryū, but after coming to Edo he studied the Hokushin Ittō-ryū wholly under Itō Seiichi of Sagachō in Fukagawa. He reached its inmost secrets in no time, was granted the rank of deputy master, took Seiichi's daughter Umeko (later divorced) to wife and married into the house, succeeded to the Itō name, and, on Seiichi's death by illness, succeeded to the dōjō as well.

After he had the dōjō he taught not swordsmanship only but hung out a signboard reading

—Instruction in Letters and Arms,

and expounded Mito learning along with it, so that a great many loyalist activists gathered under him.

Itō further kept up a lively acquaintance with the national-minded scholars within the city of Edo, so that his name rose high among the expel-the-barbarian men, and the rōnin who came up to Edo from every province,

"One must hear the fine discourse of Itō-sensei,"

{j} came thronging one after another to visit his gate.

***

"Kondō-san, this is like taking a landmine to your breast,"

{j} said Toshizō.

"Toshi, your likes and dislikes run too strong. Why do you hate the Hokushin Ittō-ryū so?"

"It's not the sword I hate. But that school has too many topple-the-shogunate men in it. And they're forming, as it were, a faction across the whole realm just now."

"You make too much of it."

"Not so much, either. They say blood is thicker than water, but a fencing school is like blood too. Men bound by a school are a fearful thing."

Put it in the terms of today, and it is like an academic clique. The old-school-tie feeling.

Among the Shinsengumi's officers, the Hokushin Ittō-ryū meant two men: the general secretary Yamanami Keisuke and the jokin Tōdō Heisuke. Both had been hangers-on at Kondō's dōjō in Edo, comrades since the first raising of the flag.

And yet, to the eyes of the Tennen Rishin-ryū breed — Kondō, Hijikata, Okita, Inoue, comrades since that same first raising — there was somewhere no blood-tie with them, some difference of skin. To put it large, it was the difference between the man of intellect and the peasant, or, in the cant of the present day, between a graduate of a famous Tokyo university and one from a nameless provincial college.

For at the founding —

that is, when Kiyokawa Hachirō (Hokushin Ittō-ryū), talking round the men of weight in the shogunate to raise a government band of rōnin, sent his summons flying to the various dōjō of Edo and the near provinces, no summons even came round to Kondō's Tennen Rishin-ryū.

It was only because the two hangers-on of the Hokushin Ittō-ryū (Yamanami and Tōdō) got wind of this movement from other dōjō of their own school and put it to Kondō — "How about it?" — that the whole company decided to answer the call.

Yamanami and Tōdō, being of a great school, naturally had many connections along its lines. They had a face in the world.

Toshizō could not stomach the breadth of such worldly connections in a Hokushin Ittō-ryū man. This, to be sure, was no matter of reason but of pique.

"Come, don't set your eyes flashing so,"

{j} said Kondō.

"It's hard on Heisuke — Tōdō — who's gone down to Edo alone to open the ground for recruiting."

"Heisuke's a fine fellow, though."

"He's a good one."

"But I can't stomach his school. If Heisuke comes back leading Itō Kashitarō and a crowd of other Hokushin Ittō-ryū men, the Shinsengumi's as good as handed over to that school."

The general secretary Yamanami Keisuke would be glad of it.

A man of the same school, Itō, comes. Naturally they join hands. In the way of things, where does that lead?

"The Shinsengumi turns revere-the-Emperor and topple-the-shogunate."

"There, there,"

{j} Kondō raised a hand.

"Don't say it. Even if Itō is a poison, it's my art to use poison for medicine."

"I wonder,"

{j} Toshizō, with a look not at all reassured, smiled quietly.

***

Tōdō Heisuke was down in Edo, on business half private and half the official business of recruiting men.

He called on Itō Kashitarō, of the same school, at the dōjō in Sagachō, Fukagawa.

The young man Tōdō Heisuke — the writer may have introduced him in a line or two before — was a bright fellow who styled himself,

"A by-blow of the Lord Tōdō of Ise,"

{j} and did nothing but joke. At the Ikedaya raid he had taken a cut to the head and was thought done for, but a few stitches and he mended by leaps, and at the Hamaguri Gate affair he had worked with even more courage than before.

Kondō loved Heisuke. He was an old familiar, and his plain, cheerful, gallant streak suited Kondō's taste. Though it need not be Kondō; a youth like Heisuke would find his way into anyone's liking.

But Tōdō Heisuke, old familiar though he was, had not been raised in Kondō's dōjō. His blood-tie ran to the Hokushin Ittō-ryū that Toshizō misdoubted. And Heisuke, it seems, had been troubled.

"Heisuke is troubled,"

{j} say it, and anyone in the corps would laugh. But Heisuke, though a man who could not put a reason into words, had Mito learning at the bottom of his thought. It was the mark of his fencing lineage — his blood, one might say.

(The Shinsengumi has become the shogunate's running dog. This throws away the whole purpose — that at the time of Kiyokawa's recruiting we were to be the vanguard of expelling the barbarian.)

Thrown away, and worse: they had cut down at the Ikedaya the very radical rōnin of Chōshū and Tosa who were that vanguard, and at the Hamaguri Gate affair had fought them face to face.

(This is not the bargain.)

So Tōdō thought. Yet the man never let slip a hair's breadth of that sort of discontent within the corps. Let it slip, and he would simply be cut down by Toshizō.

When, after the Hamaguri Gate affair, the shortage of men began to grow pressing, Kondō said,

"I'll go down to Edo myself and try recruiting. I've other official business there as well."

{j} he let fall.

Tōdō spoke as if leaping up.

"Let me clear the way ahead for you. I'll go down to Edo a step in front and settle things with the dōjō."

Kondō gladly consented.

Tōdō went down to Edo. Threading, no doubt, from one old acquaintance of the school to the next, he made his approach to Itō Kashitarō at Sagachō in Fukagawa.

(Itō was formerly of the surname Suzuki. He had once gone by the name Suzuki Daizō. By the time Tōdō called he was already an Itō — Itō Daizō. When he went up to Kyoto, this year falling on the kinoe-ne of the first year of Genji, he changed his name to Kashitarō. But it is a nuisance, so here, for convenience, we shall keep to the name Itō Kashitarō throughout.)

Tōdō Heisuke, calling on Itō, said a most grave thing.

"Kondō and Hijikata are traitors,"

{j} he said. Itō was startled.

"How do you mean?"

"Why — some years back they bound themselves with us and swore to give their all to the loyalist cause; yet Kondō and Hijikata do nothing but run about as the shogunate's fangs and claws, and there's no knowing when the aim they first proclaimed, of loyal service to the country, is ever to be reached, so that many among the comrades are indignant." (This is drawn from such sources as the Reminiscences of the aged Nagakura Shinpachi of the Shinsengumi.)

"And so,"

{j} Tōdō said a thing scarcely to be believed of this cheerful young man,

"taking the chance of Kondō's coming up to Edo, I would assassinate him, and, setting you — always deep in loyalist purpose — over us as captain, remake the Shinsengumi into a pure loyalist party; and it is to be a step ahead of Kondō that I have come up."

"Ho."

Itō was smiling. To sit smiling in silence was the only posture open to a man before a matter of such moment.

"Me for captain?"

"Just so."

"With Kondō assassinated?"

"Indeed."

Tōdō nodded.

"………"

Itō looked at the fresh-colored boy's face of Tōdō Heisuke and could not think this childlike swordsman a glib schemer. Itō too had an eye for men. He trusted Tōdō's character.

"But, Tōdō-kun. It is all so sudden, and the matter far too grave. If you press me to decide my course this instant, I can only decline."

"No — you must decide. I too say this with my life laid down. If it leaks, I cannot escape a death sentence. If you will not decide on the spot, then here I either cut my belly, or—"

"Or cut down this Itō?"

"Just so, just so."

Tōdō laughed. But his face would not come all the way open, and set halfway into a stiffness.

He gazed steadily at Itō.

"Well?"

"Tōdō-kun,"

{j} and Itō drew his own long sword to him. Tōdō gave a start.

"I strike blades with you."

With a sharp click of the guard,

"I too am a warrior. Your words I will let out to no one. I keep them graven in my breast alone. But whether it lies in my power to change the Shinsengumi into a loyalist party — that is another thing."

"Itō-sensei can do it."

"For the present, I will promise only to join. The work comes after. But before that, I must meet Kondō-kun and talk with him fully."

"Of what?"

"I would first have Kondō-kun's true heart, his first purpose, told me. Then I shall state my own opinion, and even if we cannot come together over loyalism, if we agree on the single matter of expelling the barbarian at least, I will join."

Itō was, far past loyalism, a topple-the-shogunate man. But hiding for the time the thought of toppling the shogunate, he meant to enter as a plain expel-the-barbarian man.

"And there is the matter of terms. For myself it is no matter, but among my pupils and comrades are many of real ability. It will not do to have them mere newly-signed troopers."

"Of course. In point of talent and of numbers alike, this must be a merger of equals between the Shinsengumi and the Itō dōjō."

"If you will have it so, I shall be grateful. And the later work you speak of will go the easier."

"A real pleasure."

After that, it came to drinking.

Over the cups Itō, of a sudden,

{j} "This Hijikata-kun, now — he was the vice-commander, I think. What manner of man is he?"

{j} he asked.

Tōdō's eyes took on, all at once, a light unlike any before. Itō did not miss the dread of that name showing in his face.

"Ho-ho — so grave a man as that?"

"No, sensei."

Tōdō set down his cup.

"A fool."

"Meaning?"

"A fool — there's no other word for him. He knows not the Emperor's worth to be revered, nor the barbarian's to be feared, nor the urgency of the times; yet neither has he the reasoning to hold that the shogunate is to be honored; in this man's heaven and earth there is only the Shinsengumi, and he thinks of nothing but the strengthening of the corps."

"That,"

{j} Itō cocked his head,

"may be a man truly to be feared. Kondō-kun, being half a poser as a loyalist, there's no telling what he'd do if I worked on him with reason; but this Hijikata does not fall to reason."

"So,"

{j} Tōdō nodded.

"Even for Itō-sensei's fine argument, it would be much like preaching the Law to a stone."

"Tōdō-kun, it's just such a blockhead that's the trouble. Well, one can't tell till one has met him, but this man may, in time, prove too much for my scheming to handle."

"Cut him down,"

{j} Tōdō made the motion with his hand.

It was toward the close of autumn, in the first year of Genji still, that this Itō Kashitarō met face to face the Kondō who came up to Edo not many days after.

Itō promised to join.
