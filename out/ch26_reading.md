## Chapter 26

On the way back, in Colonel Arita's car, Helen sat between Bai Ping and me. Bai Ping said not a word; utterly worn out, she sat in the corner, her eyes half closed, not a trace of a smile on her face, with no wish to talk at all.

Helen was in much better spirits than she had been at Arita's house, but because of Bai Ping's silence, several times she swallowed back the words she meant to say.

Half past eleven, and the streets of North Sichuan Road were already very still; but the Japanese teahouses still had their lamps lit, and the White Russian bar-rooms were still noisy with voices. Cars drove off down the road, and now and then one caught, faintly, a Western song, and now and then a Japanese night-ballad. There was no one else about; in the dark corners were the occasional Japanese sentries, and by twos and threes the Japanese soldiers came and went from the bars. In such an air my heart trembled with hatred; beside me Helen, perhaps because of the fright just now, gazed out of the car tense and grave. We had no wish for sleep at all; we felt only emptiness and desolation. Only Bai Ping sat there, settled and weary, and though her eyes gazed from time to time far out of the window, I believed she no longer felt anything: her nerves were slack, and it seemed that all thought and feeling in her had likewise come to a stop.

When we had gone the length of North Sichuan Road and crossed the bridge, the streets seemed stiller still; here there were no more bars or teahouses, the light was dimmer, the sounds fainter, the figures of people scarcer. But then, little by little, it grew lively again: I saw little stalls and peddlers, their oil-lamps lit at the mouths of the lanes, blowing on their hands, and an indescribable feeling of warmth floated up into my heart. Helen's face too took on a glow; she looked back at Bai Ping, who still sat there just the same, and gently drew Bai Ping's hand and said tenderly:

"Bai Ping, are you tired?"

"..." Bai Ping did not say a single word, but opened her drowsy, weary eyes wide and smiled at Helen. Helen seemed to have found her chance, and at last brought up the thing she had long wanted to bring up and had not; bashfully, haltingly, she said:

"If it hadn't been for you just now, I..." Suddenly her tone changed, and sobbing she said: "Bai Ping, I shall be grateful to you forever."

"It was his doing." Bai Ping smiled serenely, and taking Helen's hand, raised it a little to point at me.

"No," I said. "I only found it out; all the credit is Bai Ping's."

"..." Helen fell silent all at once out of shyness; though she had found out that I too knew of the matter, she did not thank me, but only leaned against Bai Ping, like a child nestling against its mother, her eyelids drooping, so full of a tender innocence that it brought back to me how she had looked when I first met her, the year before, at Stephen's house.

The car had driven out onto Nanjing Road now; I saw the moon above the racecourse, its light shining straight into the car. Helen in her white dress made me think of a water-lily on the water; I watched her, and many strange questions rose up in me at once, but I had no way to open my mouth. The driver suddenly asked us where to go first, and I asked Bai Ping:

"Shall we take Helen home first?"

"No." Helen gripped Bai Ping's arm tightly: "Aren't you tired?"

"No, I'm not sleepy," said Bai Ping. "Of course we'll see you home first."

"No. I still want to talk with you."

"Then will you come and stay a night at my place?"

Helen nodded with a smile, and so I told the driver to drive to Route Prosper Paris.

As we were nearly there, Helen said to me:

"Will you come in and talk with us too?"

"Of course," I said, "so long as I'm not in the way of your talk."

So the three of us went up to Bai Ping's rooms; Bai Ping led us into the study and went into the bedroom herself.

Helen seemed to be in this study for the first time, and observed and asked about everything with curiosity; but I could think only of my various concerns, and now that there were just the two of us here, I broke in on her words and said:

"How did you come to be at Yamao's?" For fear of shaming her, I kept my eyes turned elsewhere.

"I never imagined Yamao was that kind of man."

"How long have you known him?"

"Two weeks."

"Did you come to know him through your work?"

"Through society."

"Then your work is socializing?" I said, smiling.

"What a thing to say," she said.

"Really, I still don't even know where you work."

"You don't know?" She was surprised, but then, as if it dawned on her that she had never told me, said: "I'm at the Hailin Broadcasting Station."

"Singing?"

"Mostly singing, but a little English news reading too."

"It's a Japanese station..."

"I suppose there must be some connection." She covered over her own manner and added: "One has to live!"

"Is the pay good?"

"Not bad."

"Was it Mei Yingzi who got you in?"

"Yes," she said. "Did she tell you?"

"I guessed," I said, probing. "Hasn't she given you anything else to do?"

"Such as?"

In my suspicion, she must surely have some other task like mine; but her expression seemed utterly at a loss, as though it had not stirred the least surprise in her heart. Could she really be feigning so well as this?

Bai Ping came in; she had changed into a padded cloth gown and soft slippers. My talk broke off. Bai Ping said:

"Why haven't you turned on the electric heater?"

So I turned on the heater; Helen wanted to telephone home, and Bai Ping went out with her, and I sat alone before the heater.

Since the outbreak of the Pacific war I had scarcely once listened to the wireless news reports; if I turned on the wireless at all, it was always to find a broadcast of classical records, and of late, with the busyness of life, I had not listened to the wireless for a long while, so that I had not even known of Helen's broadcasting. Since the work was Mei Yingzi's recommendation, was it a pure matter of helping her make a living? or was there some other, political meaning in it?

I had meant to question her closely when Helen came back, but Bai Ping came in first; with slow movements and a weary air, like a snake, she opened the door just wide enough for her body and softly slid in.

After her came Jimi, that Persian cat, who, as if imitating her movements, followed silently at her heels; and when she sat down on a sofa, it leapt quite naturally onto Bai Ping's knees, sought a comfortable posture, and lay coiled there. Bai Ping then lowered her eyelids, and with fingers painted a bright crimson stroked Jimi; then she lifted her gaze and looked at me wearily, and, as though it were of no importance, said in a mocking tone:

"Do you really not know the profession of our all-the-rage broadcasting girl?"

"I truly only found out just now."

"Then do you blame me?" she said, lowering her eyelids. "For not telling you sooner."

"As if whether I knew or not could matter so much to you."

"..." Bai Ping smiled, glancing at me and glancing at Jimi.

"I hear it was Mei Yingzi who arranged it."

"Naturally." Bai Ping did not look at me; she said coolly: "Where the sunlight falls, stars will naturally appear."

The door opened, and Helen came in; her powder and rouge had come off now, and she had thrown on one of Bai Ping's brown-figured yellow woolen dressing-gowns, which made a natural harmony with her golden hair.

"A star," I thought, looking at Helen. "Helen truly is a star now, but is she the stuff of a star? She is clever, beautiful, but not lively enough, not quick enough; her nature is too deep, her thoughts too tangled..."

Helen sat down beside Bai Ping; everyone was silent. I wanted to sound Helen out, but had no way to begin; several phrases that might have served as an opening I held back for fear of rousing Bai Ping's suspicion; at last I could not but say something to break the stillness of the air:

"Do you still read often?"

"Now and then," said Helen.

"You'd do better to socialize a little less from now on."

"I don't want to socialize," said Helen. "But it's become a part of my work now."

Bai Ping said nothing all the while, sitting there serene and grave; she commanded the whole air of the room, so that our talk could go no further, and again there was a dead silence, and we let the night pass away outside the dark window. At last I rose to take my leave, and said to Helen:

"In a day or two I'll come and call on your mother."

Bai Ping did not press me to stay, nor did Helen say anything; she only saw me to the door with an affectionate look.

I walked out into the street; the night was far spent, and a bleak wind and a wan white moonlight went with me along the lonely road. I was not in the least tired, nor did I feel the cold; my eyes on the ground, my hands thrust in my pockets, over the empty waste of my heart there churned scattered, tangled thoughts, and in one breath I walked all the way home.

The next day was my moving day. In an apartment house on Weihaiwei Road I had found two rooms with a bathroom attached; the two rooms had only one door, while the bathroom had a door opening onto the other side of the corridor: it was very quiet and clean. I had taken it on Mei Yingzi's instructions, and, as she had also instructed, had told neither Bai Ping nor Helen.

Since my life had come to be so often tangled up with the Japanese, I had long since become, in the society of my relatives and friends, an odd and lonely man. At first a few of my nearest and dearest had given me sincere advice, but now they had all grown distant, and when we met would only make perfunctory small talk; I could imagine how, behind my back, they pitied me and cursed me, but since I had no way to clear myself to them, I could only avoid them as much as I could. Rising early and coming home late, I still could not escape having to meet these unbearable faces, and this had lately been my keenest suffering; for this reason, my moving was in fact a kind of release.

When everything was arranged, I turned on the lamp, drew the curtains close, and sat alone on the sofa smoking a cigarette; I felt an indescribable ease, feeling that I had escaped the world of suffering.

Someone knocked at the door; this, of course, was the servant come to see to something. Without thinking, without even looking, I said:

"Come in."

After the sound of the door came a waft of scent.

Mei Yingzi? I turned round in astonishment, and it was indeed that wondrously beautiful figure, resplendent in gorgeous dress, in white leather gloves, holding out a bunch of dew-beaded roses.

"You?"

"Do you mean to say someone besides me already knows your address?"

"Of course," I said. "The landlord here."

"And the boy," she said. "But the one they know is not the one I know."

True enough — here I had changed my name to Chen Ji; and so I fell silent, and in my silence I felt that suffering follows a man wherever he goes, and a kind of proud feeling rose up in my heart.

Mei Yingzi laughed; and now I felt her laugh was terrible, for I thought of Helen, and I was sure that all that had befallen Helen had crumbled to ruin within that smile of hers. I meant at once to take her to task, but Mei Yingzi set down her handbag and carried the flowers into the bathroom, which checked my question; but she came straight out again, took off her coat and gloves, and then carried a flower-vase back in. As I hung up her coat, I said:

"You honor me so greatly?"

"Don't you believe I am still a woman?" she said from within.

"Did you foresee that I would not have bought flowers to set out in a vase?"

"Do you mean to say you never knew that I bought this vase myself yesterday and set it here?" It struck me that I had indeed not noticed, when I last looked over the rooms, that there was no vase; and so I said:

"Ten thousand thanks to you."

"To serve our hero," she said, "is all honor to me."

Ever since the theft and the returning of Bai Ping's document, when Mei Yingzi and I were alone she would often use these two words, "the hero," to praise me; but each time I heard them I felt wretched, as if I were being made anew to consider whether what I had done was in any way good or fine. Now she used the two words again, and I felt a heavy oppression, and said nothing.

Mei Yingzi carried the vase out — a white porcelain openwork basket-shaped vase, set with pure white roses and jade-green leaves — and the room was at once touched into life. I felt at once a warmth and a tenderness; whether or not it was these flowers that worked on my mood, I had a clear and lucid reason, and, considering the question I had meant to press her with about Helen, I changed my whole manner into another mode, and when Mei Yingzi had sat down I said in a humorous tone:

"Last night, at Yamao's, I met our broadcasting star."

"Helen?" she answered serenely.

"Who else but Helen do you think worth my calling a star?"

"Then you're jealous?"

"Jealous of Yamao?" I laughed. "To be frank with you, Helen came home with me."

"And is that worth being proud of?" Mei Yingzi smiled a cool, indifferent smile. "Helen's socializing has gone deep into the very heart of the Japanese navy now; every night there's someone to see her home."

"Is Yamao a naval major?"

"Of course not." Mei Yingzi laughed in triumph. "Let the army and the navy vie for Helen."

"This too, of course, is your masterpiece," I said; but Mei Yingzi suddenly said tensely:

"You didn't talk of anything with Helen, did you?"

"Talk of what?"

"Perhaps you asked her about the work I'd given her?"

"Isn't that a very natural thing?"

"No, no," she said. "That would be a grave mistake."

"How so?"

"She's still naive; I can't assign her work," said Mei Yingzi gravely. "I must wait for the proper time, wait until she has grown naturally familiar with the enemy, and use her when I come to have need."

"Then for now you are only making use of her, having her do your bidding without her knowing why."

"I ask you," she said sternly. "Did you, in the end, talk of anything with her?"

"My empress," I said. "Set your mind at ease; don't you know yet that I am the most obedient and the most discreet of men?"

"Thank you." Mei Yingzi at once put on a sweet, comforted smile, and in a wholly feminine tone said: "But this really did give me a dreadful fright."

"But I do not approve of such methods of yours."

"I am only faithful to the work."

"But Helen is an innocent child."

"And what harm does this do her?"

"Her music, her future, her character, her beauty — are these not all to be ruined by it?"

"For victory!" said Mei Yingzi.

"Your own work is worthy of respect; to make use of an ignorant child is shameful."

"My work is to mobilize the suitable personnel."

"But Helen has a genius for music, a future beyond the reach of most; for art, for culture, ought we to blight such a bud?"

"Has her brother not a gift for music? He's at the front. Have you not your gift? It's in the work. How many geniuses there are in the world, how many heroes, how many philosophers, artists, scientists yet to be, bleeding at the front, dying in the trenches, groaning in the hospitals for the wounded; and what is it for? For victory, for freedom, for love..." she said, clear and strong, grave and calm.

"I understand, I understand," I cut her short. "But surely she ought to be let know it herself."

"It is the work," said Mei Yingzi. "One must have regard to the efficiency of the whole. You know she is naive; then her naivety would make her weak and wavering, and she would fail, if she were forever conscious of her mission."

"But," I said, "if she is sacrificed, will it have helped the work or not?"

"That is fate," said Mei Yingzi sternly. "Soldiers who die before ever reaching the front are common enough too."

"..." I thought a while, then said: "I don't understand your intention: in her socializing with Japanese officers, how much good does it do the work, in the end?"

"To be frank with you, I now know which of the naval officers and which of the army officers are certain to be at odds."

"And it is for just this much good that Helen is to be sacrificed?"

"It cannot be measured out by the foot, my friend," said Mei Yingzi, firm and cold. "And besides, later on, whenever I have need, I can call Helen into use at any time..."

"But by that time," I said, "do you suppose the Japanese won't have called Helen into use first?"

"That is a matter of technique," she laughed complacently. "When Helen, in her beautiful and innocent guise, socializes with the Japanese soldiers, the upshot is that she comes to loathe them."

Mei Yingzi's words were hard as iron and cold as ice, and set every one of my nerves trembling. I thought of the black shadow on the window last night, thought of Yamao's face over the gambling — then were all those things things Mei Yingzi had foreseen? First she would have Helen suffer, then have Helen hate, and so Helen would become the strongest of weapons. I said:

"Then all this socializing of hers is at your direction?"

"That is only natural," said Mei Yingzi ironically. "When Helen becomes a star, those who worship her will not be Japanese soldiers alone."

"You mean?"

"I mean that you are in love with her," she said, letting out a beautiful, cold smile. "You love her already more than you love yourself."

"That's absurd," I said. "Even if I love her, what I love is her gift, her soul, and not her title and bearing as a 'star.'"

"Remember," Mei Yingzi laughed, "you too are, after all, a man."

"You simply know the vanity of men too well!" All at once I thought of her purpose in getting Helen the job, and said: "So you meant all along to make her this kind of star."

"Of course," she said triumphantly. "The concert was the first step of my plan, the broadcasting the second."

I fell silent; a foot away was this beautiful Mei Yingzi, but I saw only her sinister cruelty and her grandeur! A kind of awe, a kind of contempt, a kind of dark and hidden sorrow came stealing upon me from all around, and rose up from within me.

Mei Yingzi turned into a demon's shadow, the white roses into poison-toadstools, the whole room like a graveyard. I was suffocating, I was in anguish; numberless philosophical concepts rose up in my mind! Love and hatred, life and nation, war and its means, beauty and ugliness, humanity and cruelty, the great and the sublime, and space and time, heaven and hell... These concepts melted into a cocoon, and I bound myself up into a chrysalis.

"The concert," said Mei Yingzi, seeming to wake from her own reflection and murmuring to herself, "would in fact be even easier to hold now!"

I said nothing; but an unspeakable anguish blurred my sight, and teardrops itched their way down my cheeks. I stood up, and quietly withdrew into the bathroom.
