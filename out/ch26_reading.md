### Conclusion

From the winter of 1927 to 1931, under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership, the Central Special Section played a major role in the struggle against the enemy; the organs of the Party Center in Shanghai were closely protected and suffered no great damage. As Chen Yun put it: "The Special Section was a fighting fortress of the Party under Comrade Zhou Enlai's direct leadership. It was a fighting underground organization of the Party in the White areas."

That the Central Special Section's intelligence and security work could win, one after another, so many startling and major victories in a Shanghai controlled by an enemy hundreds of times its strength was owing not only to these Red fighters' loyalty to the Party's cause, their deep hatred of the class enemy, and the daring and flexibility of their struggle; it was inseparable, too, from the Party's close ties with the masses and from the support of the broad laboring people and progressive figures. And Zhou Enlai's foresight and his resourceful gift for leadership were a still more important factor in these shining victories. At a critical hour in the Chinese revolution he faced the bloody White Terror without a trace of fear, pressing forward and holding to the fight. As in the many fields of struggle he led for the Party, where his achievements stood out, so in leading the Party's clandestine struggle under the Central Special Committee Zhou Enlai showed outstanding gifts. He had not only worked hard to study and grasp Marxism-Leninism; through long revolutionary practice he had gained rich experience of struggle and an astonishingly detailed knowledge of developments in every field in China. Farsighted and shrewd in decision, he could always seize the favorable moment in time and act, so that the Party in its clandestine struggle turned danger into safety again and again and stood on unbeaten ground. The great breadth of spirit with which he bore up the whole situation under the most difficult conditions; his tireless way of working day and night; his down-to-earth, hands-on, conscientious style; his warm and thoughtful care for his comrades: all of it left the deepest impression on everyone, and won the comrades' profound reverence and heartfelt love.

Chen Yangshan, who joined the Central Special Section's intelligence work in Shanghai early and served in it longest, recalled those stirring years: Comrade Zhou Enlai at that time carried leadership work of many kinds in the Party Center. In the thick of it he founded the Central Special Section himself, and constantly decided its various major policies and measures. Practice has proved that the Central Special Section made great contributions to protecting the Party Center and other Party organizations and to striking at the enemy's plots. At the same time we should also point out: the years from 1927 to 1931 were precisely the period when the "Left" opportunist lines of Qu Qiubai, Li Lisan, and Wang Ming held sway over the whole Party one after another. Heedless of objective conditions, they were forever staging so-called "general strikes" in the cities, calling for uprisings everywhere, for workers to rise in the cities, for town and country to combine and seize the great cities; they spoke of "winning first victory in one province or in several," directed the Red Army to attack Changsha, Nanchang, Wuhan, and other great cities, and staked on a single throw the fine situation and the revolutionary strength that had been built up afresh after the failure of the Great Revolution. In the cities, above all in Shanghai, on every revolutionary anniversary, such as May First, May Third, May Fourth, May Seventh, May Ninth, and May Thirtieth, and the other so-called Red May commemorations, they called on all comrades to go and demonstrate on Nanjing Road; in workers' districts and when the factories let out, they were forever holding open propaganda and flash rallies. In this way the Party's organization was gravely exposed, comrades were arrested and jailed in large numbers, and some even lost their lives. Even so, from 1927 to 1931 the Shanghai Party Center and its other leading organs suffered no great damage. Set this beside the situation after 1934, when the Party's leading organs and grass-roots organizations in Shanghai were all but netted in a single haul, and one sees the more clearly what a great contribution the Central Special Section, under Comrade Zhou Enlai's leadership, made to protecting and consolidating the Party. What a glorious chapter in the history of the Chinese Communist Party!

So it was. The great victories the Party Center's intelligence and security work won in Shanghai were the success of policies Zhou Enlai had drawn up with his own hand. From the founding of the Central Special Section until Zhou Enlai left Shanghai at the end of 1931 for the central revolutionary base, the clandestine struggle the Party waged in the White areas went forward smoothly under his direct leadership throughout. He was the Party's outstanding commander on the hidden front; he gave the work of the Central Special Section his heart and soul, spending enormous energy on it. He laid down a whole set of principles for the underground Party's work and drew up a series of systems, rules of discipline, and methods of operation, all of which played a decisive part in the Party's victorious growth. In organization, for instance, he required a lean, well-hidden structure and strict discipline, with no dealings between people who had no working connection, so that damage in one place could not bring losses on several. Above all he guarded strictly against traitors within, and took many effective measures. In their activity, members were to become as professional and as woven into society as possible, using an open identity to cover secret work; and everyone was reminded at the same time to keep close ties with the masses and to get on well with all quarters, above all with the neighbors around them, so as not to arouse suspicion, invite damage, and bring losses to the work. Turning the terrorist actions Gu Shunzhang had been so keen on into hidden political struggle, turning the use of individual informers into the building of a whole counter-intelligence system, turning showy display into painstaking investigation and research: all this was the fruit of a long, unremitting struggle waged by Chen Geng, Li Qiang, and many nameless heroes of the hidden front, loyal and selfless toward the Party, who in their concrete work firmly carried out Zhou Enlai's instructions and fought against the mistaken tendencies of the days when Gu Shunzhang ran the Central Special Section's daily work.

Through the half-century of turbulence that began then, Zhou Enlai laid his plans and poured great effort into the growth of the Party's intelligence work and its radio communications. Under his guidance the Party's intelligence work developed swiftly, on a broad field and a deep united-front foundation. The sources and channels of intelligence widened; its quantity and quality kept rising; and not only did it open an entirely new phase in intelligence work, but there gradually took shape a distinctive style that joined the Chinese Communists' intelligence work to united-front work. The Party's intelligence work made a special contribution to the victory of the Chinese revolution, and showed the tremendous power of intelligence work with Chinese characteristics.

During the War of Resistance, the intelligence work Zhou Enlai led also made a major contribution to the international anti-fascist war. Yan Baohang, a secret Party member whom Zhou Enlai personally approved for membership and arranged to plant inside the Kuomintang, obtained intelligence that Germany would launch a surprise attack on the Soviet Union around June 20, 1941, within the week. To lose no time, Zhou Enlai drafted an urgent telegram in Russian himself and had the Yan'an headquarters relay it to Moscow at once. Dimitrov, then secretary-general of the Comintern, recorded in his diary of June 21, 1941: in a telegram from Zhou Enlai in Chongqing to Yan'an (Mao Zedong), it had been pointed out that Chiang Kai-shek firmly declared Germany would attack the Soviet Union, and even named the date, June 21, 1941. Afterward Stalin wired Mao Zedong that this telegram had let the Soviet Red Army enter combat readiness twenty-four hours early, and expressed deep thanks for the timely, accurate intelligence the Party Center had provided. This was a major contribution of Zhou Enlai's to the international anti-fascist war. The Pan Hannian intelligence system he directly led also obtained the strategic intelligence that Japan's guiding strategic thought after the outbreak of the Soviet-German war was to "go south," not to "go north," which the Party Center passed to the Soviet side. Only on the basis of intelligence of this kind did the Soviet Union dare resolve to draw troops from its eastern-front defense forces to join the defense of Moscow. Zhou Enlai also had the Southern Bureau photograph, send to Yan'an, and report to the Soviet Union the original written materials Yan Baohang had obtained on the detailed dispositions of the Japanese Kwantung Army in the Northeast; so that after the Soviet Union entered the war against Japan on August 8, 1945, it broke through the defense works the Kwantung Army had long labored to build, following the map with the force of splitting bamboo, and utterly shattered Japan's fantasy of using the Northeast as a base for a last-ditch stand. Fifty years later, Rogachev, the Russian ambassador to China, on the commission of Russian President Yeltsin, on November 1, 1995, conferred on Yan Baohang (posthumously) and on the underground workers he had led during the War of Resistance, Yan Mingshi, Li Zhengwen, and others, the commemorative medal for the "50th Anniversary of Victory in the Great Patriotic War."

The outstanding achievements of the Pan Hannian intelligence system in the occupied areas and the Kuomintang-ruled areas, praised again and again by the Party Center and Mao Zedong, are common knowledge.

Xiong Xianghui, the "idle chess piece, cold stone" Zhou Enlai had planted at Hu Zongnan's side at the end of 1932, won Hu Zongnan's trust and became his aide-de-camp and confidential secretary. Together with Chen Zhongjing and Shen Jian, he steadily supplied the Center with swift, accurate military intelligence. Xiong Xianghui reported to the Central Intelligence Department, in advance, Chiang Kai-shek's every move in his secret telegram to Hu Zongnan ordering a lightning strike on Yan'an: the timing, the aim of the operation, the line of attack, the disposition of forces. As Zhou Enlai put it: "On the northwest battlefield there was usable intelligence every day"; "Chiang Kai-shek's operational orders had not yet reached the army commanders before Chairman Mao had seen them." That Mao Zedong, campaigning back and forth in northern Shaanxi, could anticipate everything like a god and lead Hu Zongnan by the nose at will, owed much to this swift, accurate intelligence. The outstanding achievements of Xiong Xianghui, Chen Zhongjing, and Shen Jian may be set beside the model deeds of the "Three Heroes of Longtan," who in those years had penetrated the Kuomintang's highest secret-service organ. In June 1949, when Zhou Enlai met Xiong Xianghui in Beijing, he said with great pleasure that the intelligence work in Xi'an had been very successful. He said: "In our Party's intelligence work, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di may be called the 'Three Earlier Heroes,' and you three may be called the 'Three Later Heroes,' so called because all of you made contributions to protecting the Party Center. Chairman Mao once thought that if medals were given, they should be given to you too."

Shen Anna, sent by Zhou Enlai to penetrate the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters, worked there in the confidential office as a confidential stenographer from the autumn of 1938 to the spring of 1949. At the Kuomintang's successive central plenary sessions, central standing-committee meetings, supreme national-defense council, and military-and-political conferences she was in charge of shorthand, and recorded the reports of Chiang Kai-shek, He Yingqin, Bai Chongxi, and Chen Cheng, and of the Kuomintang's Juntong and Zhongtong secret-service organs. Over ten years she obtained a great quantity of very valuable intelligence. At a critical turning point in China's situation, she copied out in full and reported to the Center the minutes of two military conferences Chiang Kai-shek convened in November 1945 to discuss the military dispositions for attacking the Liberated Areas, the deployment and transfer of forces, the division of war zones, the appointment and removal of commanders, and the estimates of our forces and their characteristics, so that the Center could move its own forces to match the enemy's dispositions and order of attack. Afterward, in Chongqing, Zhou Enlai praised the intelligence of the Wu Kejian system to which she belonged, in this period, as "swift and accurate." This was another successful example of intelligence work.

Zhou Enlai was the direct leader and commander of the secret work of turning Kuomintang troops.

Over the years his work of secret contact with Kuomintang generals never ceased, and he sent people through various channels to move them to forsake the dark for the light. In the three years of the War of Liberation that decided China's future and fate, the officers and men of Kuomintang forces who rose in revolt, accepted peaceful reorganization, or came over numbered more than 1,770,000 in all, and the generals who revolted or came over more than 1,400, among them 153 full army divisions. The direct leader and commander of this work was Zhou Enlai. He also guided and set in motion the revolt of the Kuomintang's largest cruiser, the Chongqing, and of the famous "Two Airlines" (the China National Aviation Corporation and the Central Air Transport Corporation), arranging even the details of signals and liaison himself. General Zhang Kexia, a secret Party member who had joined the Chinese Communist Party in Shanghai through the Central Special Section in July 1929, had all along worked in the Northwest Army system of Feng Yuxiang's forces. Zhou Enlai had met him several times in Wuhan and Nanjing to hear his reports and give instructions on the work of turning the enemy. In the summer of 1946, at the critical moment when Chiang Kai-shek was launching all-out civil war, Zhou Enlai met Zhang Kexia in Nanjing. Many years later Zhang Kexia recalled the scene of that meeting, which showed Zhou Enlai's quick-witted care. At the time Zhou Enlai was living at Meiyuan in Nanjing, negotiating with the Kuomintang. Zhang Kexia said: Not long after I reached Nanjing, Comrade Zhou Enlai arranged to meet me in person. Since the matter was of great weight and going to Meiyuan would be awkward in many ways, it was agreed that toward dusk the next day we would meet outside a park in a secluded spot near Feng Yuxiang's residence; at the appointed time Comrade Zhou Enlai would pass by in a black car and ask me to get in at once, and the talk would take place in the car. At this key moment, when China was about to fight out the decisive battle between two roads and two fates, that I could report in person to the Party organization, once again hear the teaching of the beloved Comrade Zhou Enlai, and receive the Party's concrete instructions directly: how precious this was, how fortunate it made me feel!

I waited in a state of excitement until dusk, then, in field uniform, met Comrade Wang Yeqiu (who then worked at Feng Yuxiang's) and, pretending to stroll, walked toward the appointed spot. It was very quiet there, scarcely a soul about. When the appointed time came, a black car pulled up on the dot and stopped before us. The door opened, and I saw the long-missed Comrade Zhou Enlai, in a Zhongshan suit, sitting calmly in the back seat, nodding to me. No sooner had I climbed in and sat beside him than the car sped off. As we drove on, Comrade Zhou Enlai looked carefully out the rear window, and seeing no car tailing us, greeted me warmly. In the car, besides the driver, a guard sat beside him. I reported at once on the situation in the Xuzhou area. Comrade Zhou Enlai watched me now with bright, keen eyes, now nodding in thought. After I had spoken to him about future work and the question of organizing an uprising, he instructed me: now you must explain our Party's policy to more of Chiang's officers and men, to the senior generals and those who command troops, and point out the way ahead for them. Chiang Kai-shek is bound to fight the civil war. If he fights, we fight too. We will hit back at them hard on the battlefield, and strike at the diehards from inside the enemy as well. We must win over and set in motion the revolts of senior generals and large units; in this way we can build a greater momentum and dissolve the enemy's morale. I said: the unit I am in has been with me a long time, we know and trust one another, and in battle they will surely obey my orders. Most of the officers and men now see there is no future in following the Kuomintang, and the conditions for revolt exist. Once the Party gives the order, I can guarantee a revolt at any time. I told Comrade Zhou Enlai that the struggle within the unit was very complicated, with not a few reactionary Kuomintang officers and a number of secret-service men as well; to guard against enemy sabotage and ensure the revolt went smoothly, I asked that before the attack the People's Liberation Army send liaison personnel to me, the better to coordinate. Comrade Zhou Enlai nodded his agreement. He thought a moment, looked around, glanced at his watch, and then suddenly gripped my hand and shook it hard. I understood that the talk had to end. On the shoulders of this outstanding Party leader before me lay countless heavy burdens, countless matters awaiting his urgent attention. He handled a myriad affairs, great and small; and now, just when the Kuomintang reactionaries' arrogance was at its height, he had, undaunted by the risk, on the streets of Nanjing, the Kuomintang capital, calmly given his instructions for striking at the enemy. What an astonishing, admirable feat!

The car stopped at a secluded spot, and when I had quickly got out, it sped off at once, vanishing in a moment into the oppressive night of old China.

Two years later, on November 6, 1948, the Chinese People's Liberation Army launched the Huaihai Campaign. On November 8, Zhang Kexia, together with He Jifeng (a secret Party member), led more than 23,000 men in revolt in the Tai'erzhuang and Zaozhuang area.

From the time he led Li Qiang in Shanghai to develop the first radio transmitter-receiver and set up the first radio station, Zhou Enlai went on leading, effectively, our Party's and our army's radio-communications work through every historical stage of the Chinese revolution that followed. In the War of Resistance and the War of Liberation, our Party's and army's radio communications developed all around. Zhou Enlai worked hard to lead the hidden struggle in the air between the enemy and ourselves, and personally arranged the setting up of dozens of secret radio stations in the enemy-occupied and Kuomintang-ruled areas, spread across the great cities and strategic points, ensuring the Party Center's timely, effective leadership of the "Second Front."

In the period of the nationwide War of Liberation, domestic intelligence work unfolded on all fronts and won outstanding achievements; one may call it the high point of its development. Mao Zedong once appraised it with satisfaction: in this period "our intelligence work was the most successful." The Party Center, too, praised the intelligence work of this period. The Central Committee's Decision on Intelligence Work of November 1949 said that in this period the Party's intelligence work "played an important part in winning the victory of the Chinese revolution nationwide." This great historical merit should be credited first of all to the commander of the hidden front, Zhou Enlai.

Not long after New China was founded, Zhou Enlai, looking back on this history in a speech, said:

Intelligence work developed in the period when the Party stepped onto the political stage in 1927, not after the failure of the Great Revolution. At first the Party did not yet understand how to organize intelligence, and met defeat as soon as the enemy struck; so a special-work department was set up in Wuhan, attached to the Military Commission. But because the whole political line was mistaken, we had intelligence and yet stayed numb to it. On the main questions we all had intelligence, and the result was that we watched ourselves go down to defeat with perfect clarity; political immaturity brought on such a result. Today, under the leadership of the Party and Chairman Mao, the revolution has won, and that has given intelligence work the chance to develop; the work must be developed boldly and without restraint. Intelligence work is by its nature a venture; to hit the mark every time is impossible, there are bound to be some failures, and one must be prepared for things to go wrong and for losses. But the failures must not be too many. Too many failures are recklessness, to be avoided; and when things go wrong they must never go so badly wrong as to become a fatal blow. When the head of Shanghai intelligence work, Gu Shunzhang, turned traitor in 1931, the Center was nearly netted in a single haul, and it was only because Comrade Li Kenong and others ran the risk of coming to Shanghai to report that disaster was narrowly escaped; that kind of blow was a fatal one. Gu Shunzhang's betrayal was no accident: the Li Lisan line of the time and the Fourth Plenary Session had both deepened his wavering, and we had not been on guard beforehand. A fatal blow can be avoided, and that calls for strengthening education, for strictness within, for keeping inside and outside clearly apart, and for avoiding lateral links.

Here Zhou Enlai set out the history of the development of our Party's intelligence and security work, and pointed in particular to the lessons to be drawn from Gu Shunzhang and similar cases of betrayal: a weighty summing-up of experience in our Party's and our army's struggle against the enemy on the hidden front.
