### Charity Sharks Grown Rich on Refugees

In reason, a man who runs a charity ought to keep himself clean and be as pure as water, that he may deserve to be called a good man, and may answer to his conscience and hold his head up without shame. But once one begins to speak of the inside workings of Shanghai's charitable world, it is enough to make a man weep bitter tears and burn with an unending indignation.

Charity is by nature a losing business—how should a man grow rich by it? Yet among the charitable bodies of Shanghai there are not a few good men who, on the strength of the two words "good works," have made a great fortune and built up an estate; to point them out one by one would be past all reckoning. Such creatures are truly the sort who kill without minding the smell of blood, who fall upon the refugees and the poor and gobble them up like wolves and tigers, thinking only of stuffing their own pockets—muchee-muchee—and giving not a thought to the scorn and reproach of the rest of society. Good men they are called; charity sharks is all they are.

The men of the charitable world, as the author knows them, fall into some three sorts. The first sort truly hold to the creed that all men are their brothers and the people their children; they come forward to do the work, take not a jot for themselves and are scrupulous to a hair, and, not content with giving of their spirit, will dig into their own pockets besides—such men deserve the name of "good men," but their number is very small. The second sort, under the gilt signboard of some hall or society, spread the word everywhere, advertise in the papers, or send men round to solicit; and once the donations are in hand, they first keep the greater part for their own use and hand over only a small part to the charity's needs. The third sort are an out-and-out swindle. Whenever some flood, drought, plague of locusts, or other calamity befalls, they rent a room or two on the spur of the moment, hang out the great signboard of some "association" or "benevolent hall," have someone draw up an appeal all pity for Heaven and grief for man, tears in every line, and print it off in hundreds of thousands of copies, while they respectfully invite a few men of name and note to serve as directors. The formalities done, they send men out on every side to solicit and advertise far and wide; and their band of solicitors must further pick out the smooth-tongued and brazen-faced masters of society, so that the takings, thus managed, can hardly fail to set a new record. Nor do they carry on their soliciting in this port alone; they send agents out to every place and every port to strive at the soliciting and strive at the collecting, and at times must lay out advertising money to solicit in the papers besides. In a word, when it comes to the two words "soliciting funds," one may say there is no hole they will not bore into, no crack they will not slip through. On the subscription-books and receipts they send out, all are printed, in bright vermilion, the eight great characters, "May he who handles this and fattens himself be struck by lightning and burnt by fire"; but the Old Man of Heaven is large and forgiving, and who is to come and call them to account over such a trifling reckoning? So swear the oath as they may, they fatten themselves still. Once the calamity has passed, every one of them has grubbed his fill and may take his ease for a lifetime; the rented rooms are given up, the gilt signboard taken down, and the manager, the deputy manager, the clerk-in-charge and the staff great and small all slip away in a puff of smoke, and so the show is closed for the time. And should some fresh calamity befall thereafter, that they go through the old routine once more is a common thing enough.

The first sort of men I spoke of truly deserve the two words "good man" or "worthy"; the second sort can only be called hypocrites; and the last are truly the charity sharks of the charitable world, the vermin of society. The scandal that broke out last year over the investigation into the funds subscribed for the Northeast Volunteer Army made a stir over the whole city and set the tongues wagging like a sky full of stars; whether there was any peculation in it, we outsiders cannot know. But should we, unluckily, tread once more in the charity sharks' tracks and turn up a band of "righteousness sharks," would it not set the foreigners laughing their teeth loose at us?
