## Chapter 16

The next day I went to Mrs. Stephen's, and Mrs. Stephen, sure enough, wished to see me about the matter of Helen. She said she did not know whether Helen's giving up singing was because she had grown interested in philosophy, or because she had grown interested in me; but if it was philosophy that had caught her interest, she felt I ought to find some way to turn her back. She was against Mrs. Manfield's attitude, though—that of marrying her daughter off to singing, as it were—and held firmly that in matters of love there is no stopping; and if it was truly a matter of love, she hoped I would give up my bachelor's creed, build a good home, and that the two of us would spur each other on in our work.

Mrs. Stephen's kindness touched me deeply. She probed again and again to learn whether I was in love with Helen; but the truth is that I had never once thought of it myself, nor felt it. My dealings with Helen were purely a friendship of the best kind, and were it to turn into some troublesome thing, I did not wish to weigh it or to think it over. My way of life was the single life, most free and beautiful, and I had not yet the resolve to give it up.

I told her the true state of my feeling for Helen: in point of friendship it was of course very fine, but there was no defined love in it. At her age she might, in her sensitiveness, suppose herself in love with a man, when in fact she could have the same feeling on coming close to any man at all. And so I had settled it with Mei Yingzi that for the time being I would not keep company with Helen.

"Very good." When Mrs. Stephen had heard out my honest confession, a comforted smile came to her face. "But what if she comes looking for you?"

"…" I could say nothing, and began to see that in yesterday's hurry I had not thought of this. Mrs. Stephen sat at her ease and said:

"You need only avoid any occasion of being alone, the two of you. When she comes to find you, you can very well ask several friends along and all make merry together."

"That is easily done," I said.

After lunch I came home, resolved to act on this decision from the next day on.

But nothing turns out as easy as one would have it. Helen, it seemed, was a girl turned very much inward, unwilling to meet strangers, and the upshot was that I found myself once more together with Mei Yingzi—and afterward with Stephen and Bai Ping too.

This kind of life brought back my old hidden ache and remorse, but it did indeed multiply Helen's smiles. At first she would often sit silent at our gatherings, but later she came to laugh and talk with perfect ease; at first it was always Mei Yingzi who called us together, but later Helen too would take it on herself to invite us; at first Helen was always the first to want to go home, but later she often wished to draw the gatherings out. She grew used to every scene of wild revelry and romance, learned to idle away long hours in cafés, learned to dance like a mad thing, and learned, too, a small, clever wit at teasing. At the table our searching talk of books of philosophy dwindled away to nothing at all; yet neither did she speak of any effort at her singing, and the days were spent away without aim, without plan, without an ideal.

Mei Yingzi and Helen seemed to have some special bond; I had not thought she would show such warmth and patience as the centre of our circle. Whenever we telephoned she would come on the minute, and often she and Helen were together first, and would ring up to summon me, and Stephen and Bai Ping as well.

Stephen seemed to make nothing of it—enjoying life all the same, and finding it, if anything, the more amusing to be with us.

Bai Ping was of course glad enough of such gaiety. But I am sure we told upon her earnings, though on certain occasions Mei Yingzi, Stephen and I would all often contrive to help her out in secret.

Only I—on the one hand I ran a great deficit in my purse, and on the other I bore an anguish of spirit past telling. Though my spirit and my time were not wholly consumed in these gatherings, what time was left could no longer let me gather my mind to my scholarly work. My first aim had been to bring Helen back to singing, but this had borne no great fruit, while my own life was wrecked entirely. Almost every night in bed I repented of the day's living, and yet, come day, I lived on as before. Once, Bai Ping had suspected I loved Mei Yingzi, and bade me see less of Mei Yingzi; once, Mei Yingzi had suspected I loved Bai Ping, and bade me see less of Bai Ping. Not long since, they had both suspected I loved Helen, and bade me not be alone with her; and now no one spoke of any of these things, but only lived, day upon day, this preposterous life.

In this way something more than a month went by, and I plainly saw a violent change in Helen. Her dreamy smile had turned bright and open, her gentle manner had grown emphatic, her slow ways had turned quick. Her hair was permed to the fashion, her dress grew day by day more brilliant, and the once silent child was now very fond of talking. In the beginning she would often keep coldly to herself in company, liking to speak to me alone of her thoughts on life and her questions of the mind; but now she loved to hold forth in company on her opinions of philosophy and of literature and art, so that everyone at the table turned to attend to her. At a dinner-dance Mrs. Stephen gave in early September this showed itself more plainly still, and I felt her another person altogether.

For it was at Mrs. Stephen's last dinner-dance that I had come to know her, when she was still a bashful child, dressed with quiet decorum, wearing very little powder or rouge. With her still, quiet manner, she dared not look anyone full in the face, and had a tender, feeling softness about her; in that gathering she had been no more than a little girl no one noticed. But today, in the same place, in the same air, when she came in with her mother she drew the notice of the whole room.

She wore an evening gown faintly touched with red, cut low at breast and back, a string of pearls at her throat; her hair was beautifully waved, her powder and rouge laid on thick, showing her girl's beauty to the full, and her eyes flashed with a quick, lively light. Coming in, she at once looked all about her, and no longer clung to her mother through the introductions; then she was sisterly and warm with Mei Yingzi and Bai Ping, and mixed on equal, familiar terms with Stephen and me, and at the last let out one of those laughs in common social use, breaking off just as it had drawn the notice of the men nearby. It was the first time I had heard her laugh so—when she had learned it I could not tell—and when she had done laughing she went with a most exquisite air to talk before Mrs. Stephen, and in talking had a thousand graces of manner that I could not but attend to.

She was beautiful—after Mei Yingzi, hers was the beauty of the room, and Mei Yingzi had not her youth. At the dinner-table she talked and laughed with an easy grace, and now and then drew the talk round to matters of the mind. In her singer's voice she recited me a few lines of a dialogue of Plato's, all most aptly placed; and seeing I did not answer, she turned to talk of Wagner with someone else, carrying it off most beautifully. After dinner, when the music began, a crowd of young men gathered to ask her to dance. Her steps Stephen and I had long since brought to a finished ease, and today she had besides an unlooked-for coquetry that tinged them with a rare charm.

Mrs. Manfield seemed hugely pleased, her spirits high, her eyes forever on her lovely daughter, and beneath her tongue she held a full measure of praise. When I went over to ask her to dance she was still watching Helen. As I rose to dance with her I said:

"Helen is really too beautiful today."

"Ah," said she, her plump face smiling most innocently, "that is all your doing, Xu—yours, all of you; you have changed her whole view of life. No more is she in torment, no more downcast; no more does she pore all day over philosophy and brood all day on empty questions. See—she has grown so beautiful." And with that she waved a hand toward Helen, as though she must have me look, and of course I turned my head to look a moment.

"Beautiful indeed!" I said. "But what of the singing?"

"Ah," and Mrs. Manfield's plump cheeks smiled more innocently still, "this week past she has practised every day—for Mrs. Stephen has asked her to sing today."

"…" I could find no more to say, but Mrs. Manfield went on:

"Mei Yingzi has already arranged it with Professor Paci—at Christmas they are to get up a concert for her."

"…" Still I could find nothing to say. Mrs. Manfield said again:

"But don't tell her now—she might not like it; I mean to wait until after she has performed today and gone home, and then talk it over with her carefully, and have her practise every day."

"Yes, yes."

Though I said "Yes," I did not do as Mrs. Manfield wished. For the Helen of now was long since no longer the daughter her mother feared for; she had indeed lost her emptiness and loneliness, but what filled that emptiness and lifted that loneliness was neither an infatuation with philosophy nor a drowning in song. She lived no more for thought or for art; she would, by the handiest and craftiest of means, take the lustre of thought and of art to adorn the brilliance of her own life. And so, as I danced with Helen to the second piece of music, I said:

"Helen, your mother tells me someone has already arranged it with Professor Paci to hold a concert for you at Christmas. I wish you success in advance."

"Really? Why did she not tell me sooner, so that I might hurry and practise?"

Her eagerness went quite beyond what I had looked for; she was thrilled as a rose just opening.

"…" I could find nothing to say, for I thought of the water-lily's shadow, I thought of the white flowers set about my room the day I reasoned with her, and all at once I thought of the bright-red roses she had brought.

After a dozen or so pieces, Miss Helen sang. Everyone applauded warmly; Helen held back a moment, then walked easy and graceful to the piano, faced her audience, gave a little smile, took a singer's pose with both hands, and sang to the piano's accompaniment. It made me think of that tea-party here, the day I came late, and how shy, how timid her manner in singing had been then—how far apart the two of them were!

Her voice was full and sweet—she was indeed a born singer—but today her greatest success lay still in her bearing and her beauty. Everyone applauded together, and Mrs. Manfield most warmly of all; and when Helen made a thirty-degree bow and a sweet smile, sweeping the whole company with a fluent glance, and came down beautifully, trailing her evening gown, to her mother's side, every eye was upon her, and I saw the tears of joy run down Mrs. Manfield's face.

Everyone crowded round to take Helen's hand and wish her joy of her success. I was the last to shake her hand, and I said in a low voice:

"Splendid, Helen."

"I'm so out of practice," she said politely; "I must practise hard from now on."

"I wish you a boundless future," I said.

Mei Yingzi stood by, smiling—a smile of triumph; and all at once I felt her sorcery: she had indeed created Helen. Her smile seemed to me an insult and a mockery, and afterward, as I danced with her, she said:

"Well, Xu? Helen is quite herself again."

"It is your sorcery," I said.

"And is it not your success as well?"

"No," I said; "it is my failure."

"Because she has given up philosophy?"

"But she has not gone back to art."

"Do you still not believe she will work hard at her singing from now on?"

"No," I said; "from now on she will work forever for vanity."

"Grieved, my friend?" she said. "Yes—never again will she be tempted by your philosophy."

"Forever tempted by vanity."

"Perhaps this, after all, is a woman's world," she said with a sweet smile; "but your feeling is nothing more than a kind of jealousy."
