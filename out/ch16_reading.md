## Chapter Sixteen. The Hour of the Ox, First Half (1 a.m.)

{v} Li Bi silently lowered himself down until only half his head showed above the water. The sound of the water-wheels would cover most of the noise he made. From this dark vantage, the places lit by torchlight stood out with peculiar clarity.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fifteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Ox, first half.

{d} Chang’an; the Xingqing Palace.

The lantern-float pageant of the fourth watch, at the hour of the Ox, second half, was now but half a shichen away. Around the edges of the plaza several hundred great pine-oil torches, wrapped in colored cloth, were kindled one after another, until they lit the whole place bright as day. The Longwu Army began, in good order, to open the passages on every side and let the common folk stream into the plaza a little at a time.

The south plaza before the Xingqing Palace was broad and open, and had been marked out beforehand into block after block of separate zones with powdered lime. By whichever entrance the people had come in, in that zone alone they were made to stay. Cross the line, and at the least you were cursed at, at the worst clubbed with staves. For safety’s sake the Longwu Army would not in the slightest mind beating a few men to death.

Besides the spectators’ zones, in the very middle of the plaza there were some twenty-odd great blocks more. Here the gorgeous, imposing lantern-float carts, their night-long fight to the finish now behind them, came driving in amid the press of their partisans and were set down. Every one of them was a victor of the outer rounds of the lantern-float contest, each having beaten at least a ten-odd rivals, and all of them were riding high.

Here these embroidered lantern-float carts would await the final contest at the hour of the Ox, second half, and at a single stroke carry off the lantern-floats’ crowning honor.

The performers, however, were far from idle. They knew that up on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, not far off, most of the officials and great nobles had eaten and drunk their fill, and had risen from the spring banquet to stand at the tower’s edge, looking down over the whole plaza. If a man could but catch the fancy of one or two of them now, he would have nothing to worry about for years to come. And so the performers went on straining every art they had, throwing themselves into the show, driving the fever of the crowd higher still.

Under their urging, both the Xingqing Palace plaza and the Qinzheng Wuben Tower fell into a riot of merrymaking. The commoners flung their hands aloft, heads bobbing thick as a tide, cheers mingling with the gongs and drums of the music-bands; fiery trees and silver blossoms, song and dance clamoring to the sky, the whole field of vision one bursting brocade of flowers — a sight as splendid, at its very height, as the fortunes of Great Tang itself.

In all this merriment, only the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower kept still dark and silent. Yet no one was troubled by it; every one of them waited in expectation for the moment the hour of the Ox, second half, should come, when it would astonish the world at a single cry.

The minds of the men inside the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower at this hour, however, were altogether unlike those without.

Once Li Bi had gone, Zhang Xiaojing was plainly much more at ease. As though he had set down a weight from his heart, he began of his own accord to ask after certain details, and Xiao Gui, every last doubt of his old comrade now gone, naturally told him all he knew.

But with the hour drawing near, and the Pifu setting the qilin-arms in place more slowly than he had reckoned, Xiao Gui began to grow restless.

No plan ever ran as smoothly as one imagined it would, and for that Xiao Gui had long been prepared. But the qilin-arm was unlike other things: what was poured into it was heated rock-oil, and once the hour was past and the temperature fell, it would lose its bursting force. So Xiao Gui was obliged to go and stand over the places where the work lagged, and watch them himself.

Seeing their chief standing behind them, his face as black as the bottom of a pot, the Pifu grew tense in their turn. All at once one of them, careless, let a qilin-arm slip from his hands and drop beneath the hanging bridge. The bamboo tube tumbled away into the darkness underfoot, and after a good long while there came up from the ground below a single “crack.”

Xiao Gui, without ceremony, gouged a savage cut across the man’s face, and the blood sprayed. The man let out a scream of pain, yet dared not flinch away. In a grim, chilling voice Xiao Gui said, “Your two hands are left you so as not to hold up the work. Make one more mistake, and it won’t be only a bamboo tube that goes over the edge.” The man bowed and cringed his yes-yeses, took up another qilin-arm, and set about mounting it once more.

Zhang Xiaojing drew Xiao Gui aside. “Is there no quicker way to make the switch?”

Xiao Gui shook his head. “This is Master Mao Shun’s own design. Who could better him at it?”

“And if Master Mao Shun has held something back, I’m afraid no one would catch it either...” Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his single eye and put in the reminder: “He is not doing this of his own free will.”

At his words Xiao Gui fell to thinking. Mao Shun was no man of the Pifu; if he had chosen to work with them, it was wholly because a steel blade lay across the throats of his family. It was not out of the question, then, that he had been playing some quiet trick or two along the way.

“On the technical side, only Mao Shun understands it. If he has deliberately withheld a better way of making the switch, we should be hard put to it to find out. That way he shows a cooperative face, so no harm comes to his family, and at the same time, without turning a hair, he obstructs our business.” Zhang Xiaojing had already begun to say “our” of the Pifu.

Xiao Gui nodded and turned his head to look toward the Tianshu. Mao Shun was still crouched there, motionless, and nothing of joy or anger could be read in the old man’s hunched back. Xiao Gui was about to go over when Zhang Xiaojing pressed a hand on his shoulder. “Let me do it.”

Xiao Gui was a little taken aback. Zhang Xiaojing gave him a grin. “Nine years a buliang chief in Chang’an teaches a man far more than ten years a soldier in the Western Regions.” Xiao Gui laughed too, and thumped his shoulder. “Then I’ll leave it to you, Big-Head.”

Zhang Xiaojing walked up to Mao Shun and, without more ado, seized him by the back of his collar and hauled him up. Wholly unprepared, Mao Shun started in fright at the sudden move. Saying nothing, Zhang Xiaojing dragged him all the way to the lantern-tower’s edge, threw back the brocade skin stretched over the outside, and shoved Mao Shun toward the open air.

The guards looking on gave startled shouts and moved by instinct to stop him, but Xiao Gui held them back and signaled them to be easy. They saw Zhang Xiaojing stretch out a leg over the edge and plant a foot on a bamboo strut set at a slant, then twist a fistful of Mao Shun’s hem and, just in time, catch back the old man as he pitched outward.

Now both their bodies leaned out beyond the lantern-tower, thrust into the night air, their whole balance hanging on Zhang Xiaojing’s one leg for a fulcrum. Let his hand loosen, or his leg draw back, and Mao Shun would drop from the tower and be dashed into a smear of pulp.

Mao Shun struggled a few times in his terror, only to find it did no good at all. His head was a great deal cleverer than Zhang Xiaojing’s, but his strength fell far, far short.

“What — what do you mean to do?” Mao Shun cried, his white hair whipping wildly in the night wind.

Zhang Xiaojing fixed him with a stare and said loudly, “How can the qilin-arms be set in faster?”

“I’ve already told you!” Mao Shun said furiously.

“What I want to know is the faster way.”

“There isn’t one — this is the fastest!”

“Oh — so you mean you’re of no more use?” Zhang Xiaojing let his hand slip, tilting Mao Shun’s body further down, and the old man’s terrified scream rang out through the whole Tianshu tier. Someone asked in alarm what was to be done if Mao Shun should happen to die, but Xiao Gui waved a hand and told them to wait and watch.

Zhang Xiaojing drew his arm in and hauled Mao Shun up a little. “Do you remember one now?” Mao Shun, gasping for breath, shook his head in despair. Zhang Xiaojing bore down slightly with his foot, and the bamboo strut gave a splintering crack, crack, as if about to break beneath the tread. Mao Shun’s pupils shrank all at once, and he shouted, “Don’t step on that! It’ll collapse.” For he had not the least wish to die crushed beneath his own creation.

“Then let’s move to a livelier spot — perhaps that will jog your memory.” Zhang Xiaojing’s tone was full of malice. He hauled Mao Shun back up and, along the hanging bridge, walked him into an outer lantern-chamber close by.

This lantern-chamber happened to be “Wuwei,” next door to “Tangdi.” Its theme within was Duke Li of Wei breaking the Yin Mountains, and so the craftsmen had fashioned out of rawhide a little hill in the shape of the Yin Mountains, upon which stood two mounted lantern-figures, Duke Li of Wei and Jieli Khagan — the one riding forward with lance upraised, the other fleeing in defeat with his head turned back. Once set going, Duke Li of Wei would swing his lance up and down of itself, while Jieli Khagan turned his head again and again, to show the backward glance of a man in a rout. Inside the rawhide, moreover, were set row upon row of little flags, so that when the lamps were lit, from far off the whole sky and earth seemed thick with the banners of the Tang army.

Zhang Xiaojing dragged Mao Shun into the lantern-chamber and glanced back. Between the chamber and the tower hung a straw curtain for a partition, which served nicely to block the others’ line of sight. He hauled Mao Shun to the chamber’s edge, pressed down on his head and shoved him outward, bending the old man’s upper body out into the air in a posture of menace — then, close by his ear, murmured, “Don’t be afraid. I’ve come to save you.”

Mao Shun was in no way ready to believe him — taking it for another trap — and shook his head in anger. Zhang Xiaojing, by main force, gripped his jaw hard to keep him from making a sound. “Listen. I am Zhang Xiaojing, Commander of the Jing’an Bureau. I worked my way in among the Pifu to stop their plot.”

The suspicion had not left Mao Shun’s eyes, but he struggled a good deal more weakly now — after all, Zhang Xiaojing had no need to lie. Lowering his voice, Zhang Xiaojing said, “I know your family has been seized by the Pifu, and that you have no choice. I will do all I can to keep you and your family safe — but you must work with me.”

Mao Shun made a few muffled sounds. Zhang Xiaojing said, “In a moment I’ll let go of your mouth, slowly. First give a scream of pain, so they hear it; I’ll keep hold of you like this, to save any suspicion.” Then his hand drew slowly away from the jaw, Mao Shun’s body gave a wrench, and out of his throat came a shrill, piteous wail. At the same instant Zhang Xiaojing pressed down hard with his arm, thrusting Mao Shun a little further out.

“Good, good,” Zhang Xiaojing soothed him under his breath. “Now, you must tell me one thing.”

“What...?” Mao Shun asked back warily, never quite daring to be wholly at ease.

“How can the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower be stopped from working? The quickest way.”

This was to draw the firewood from beneath the cauldron: so long as the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower did not work, the Pifu’s plot could not be brought off. Zhang Xiaojing pressed for the quickest way because the hour of the launching was close upon them, and he was but one man.

Mao Shun wavered a moment — this was as much as to kill his own child with his own hands. Zhang Xiaojing said coldly, “There’s little time left. You don’t want to send the whole court of Great Tang up to heaven with your own handiwork, do you?”

Mao Shun shuddered — this would be a nightmare beyond doubt. At last he spoke. “All the power of the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower comes from the water-wheels of the underground hall. At the third mark of the hour of the Ox, first half, men will couple the water-wheels to the turning-gear, and set the main axle driving. Should anything go wrong with the turning-gear, the lantern-tower is like water with no source — it can stir no further, not by a hair.”

“Where is the turning-gear? How is it wrecked?” This was all Zhang Xiaojing cared for.

“The turning-gear is at the crown of Mystic Abbey’s roof. Because it must take up the force of the turning, it is forged of fine steel. In a hurry like this, there’s no destroying it.” Mao Shun turned his head and glanced at Zhang Xiaojing. “But I have to tell you — this can only stop the lantern-tower turning; it cannot keep the fierce-fire thunder inside the Tianshu from bursting.”

Zhang Xiaojing grew a little vexed. These craftsmen never came straight to the point; they must ramble on over cause and consequence for an age. His tone turned rough. “Then what do you say we do?”

“There’s only one way.” Mao Shun drew a deep breath and shut his eyes in pain. “The meshing dimensions between the turning-gear and the works above and below it were all reckoned out in advance. If the turning-gear could be tilted to a certain angle, the transmission of force would be thrown askew, and given time it would wring the Tianshu apart. The rock-oil inside would leak out, and could at the very most start a fire — with no danger of an explosion at all.”

“Is it like making furniture — let the mortise and tenon slip out of place, and not only does the frame not bear the strain, it falls apart instead?”

“Near enough.”

“Then how is it to be tilted?”

Mao Shun said, “When I designed the lantern-tower, the thing I feared most was an uneven transmission of force wringing the Tianshu to pieces. So to guard against it, I fixed the turning-gear itself firm to the whole eaves-crown of Mystic Abbey, so that the entire roof is its mounting-frame. The roof does not move, and so the turning-gear does not move. Ai — this is hard, very hard...” His voice sank away, and he fell into thought.

Zhang Xiaojing said mildly, “Then wreck the roof along with it, and be done.” Mao Shun choked. All his thinking had been fixed on the turning-gear itself; he had never dreamed this coarse, brawny fellow would put forward so brutal a method.

“The roof is brick and stone. How is it to be wrecked?”

Zhang Xiaojing was silent a moment, then cast his eyes up above the lantern-chamber. There, section upon section, ran the transmission-rods that linked the tower to the chamber within, and the most conspicuous section of them all was the qilin-arm that had just been set in place.

Mao Shun was startled at first, thinking it too absurd. But turning it over, he saw it truly was a way of breaking cunning with brute force. What was packed inside the qilin-arm was likewise heated, sealed rock-oil; once touched off, it might not destroy the roof outright, but it would be enough to tilt the turning-gear. He ran a swift reckoning in his head, and nodded to show it could be done.

“Good.” Zhang Xiaojing pulled Mao Shun back in from the edge. “Then let me ask one more question. Is there really no quicker way to set the qilin-arms? I have to get something out of you, to win their trust.”

Mao Shun was silent a good while, then sighed. “There is... but if they get them mounted in time, the Que-le Huo-duo will come to pass, and there’ll be no redeeming it, ever.”

“If I fail, that is what there’ll be no redeeming.”

Xiao Gui saw Zhang Xiaojing come out of the “Wuwei” chamber with Mao Shun in his grip, the old man shivering all over, his face ashen.

“Got it out of him — the fellow was holding something back, sure enough,” said Zhang Xiaojing, and thrust Mao Shun forward. Mao Shun sprawled on the floor and, trembling, gave out the method of mounting. Beside them a Pifu who knew the craft muttered a few words to Xiao Gui, confirming that the way would indeed serve.

Once laid bare, the trick was very simple — it merely left out a few steps. But who, save a master craftsman of Mao Shun’s standing, would dare to alter the procedure on his own authority?

“Big-Head, when people called you Zhang the Yama, I didn’t even believe it.” Xiao Gui stuck up a thumb, then gave Mao Shun a spiteful kick. “This old thing — had he spoken up sooner, we’d never have been driven to such a rush!”

Mao Shun lay sprawled on the floor, trembling all the while, with nothing left of a master’s dignity.

“Since we all know it now, you’re of no further use.” The killing air rose in Xiao Gui again. Zhang Xiaojing hastily stopped him. “I promised to spare his life.” Xiao Gui looked at him. “Big-Head — how is it your heart goes soft just now? That won’t do.”

“Don’t make me break my word.”

Xiao Gui glanced at him, and seeing how earnest his face was, could only draw back his foot in vexation. “Business first; the rest we’ll settle when the time comes.” He looked at the hour, and gave orders for the new method of mounting to be passed to the Pifu at every lantern-chamber, to be seen to with all speed.

At once the lantern-tower was all bustle again. Zhang Xiaojing looked about him, reckoning things over in his mind. There were so many qilin-arms; the Pifu must surely be keeping a reserve — set down, most likely, in the little cauldrons of Mystic Abbey. He ought to find some pretext quickly to go down, get hold of a qilin-arm, and set it in place.

Once he had a qilin-arm and had blown the turning-gear askew, the greatest danger would be counted lifted. As for whether the lantern-tower could be saved, whether the Son of Heaven lost face — that was no concern of Zhang Xiaojing’s.

He was deep in thought when Xiao Gui came over again. “Big-Head — there’s a surprise for you in a little while.”

“Oh?”

“Once the qilin-arms in the tower are all set, you’ll pull out of it with me and go down to the Hydraulic Hall. There are thirty crack veterans waiting there now, making ready for a great stroke; you and I will lead them, and do something that’ll do the heart good.”

“Thirty crack veterans? In the Hydraulic Hall?” Zhang Xiaojing gave a start.

“Of course. The surprises of this night are hardly the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower alone.” Xiao Gui laughed, not noticing the twitch of Zhang Xiaojing’s brow.

***

Li Bi stood in the dark of the Hydraulic Hall, somewhat at a loss.

He had done away with the guard smoothly enough, but he did not know what to do next. On every side here were sealed earthen walls, and overhead ran cross-shaped bracing posts, set this way and that like those used in a mine-pit. In the whole space there was but one stairway leading up above — and up there were nothing but enemies, where he could on no account go.

Zhang Xiaojing might have some brilliant plan, but the two of them had never once had a chance to speak alone. To have got those two words across to him was already the very limit of what could be done without rousing others’ suspicion.

Li Bi had no candle at his side, and could only feel his way forward in the dark, soft-footed. After going round twice, he made certain at last that there was here neither any enemy nor any other way out. He felt himself caught within a riddle, its answer close at hand yet not to be found. He reckoned it up: it was now the hour of the Ox, first half, and only half a shichen remained before the lantern-floats.

A weary thought stole over him.

“Why not simply hide here, and wait until it’s all over?”

The notion seemed reasonable enough. As he was now, there was nothing much he could do; it was enough to preserve his life as best he might, and be no burden to others. The Hydraulic Hall was very stoutly built; even should the world above be blown to pieces, it would not reach down here.

But Li Bi hesitated only a single finger-snap before driving the temptation off with a cold snort.

A Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau, no less — how could he, like some cur, scrabble only to save his own skin? To have been kidnapped was disgrace enough; and if now he lost heart and sat waiting for others to rescue him, then what face would he, Li Bi, Li Changyuan, have to face the heir apparent? Besides, Zhang Xiaojing was still up there risking his life — was Li Bi to prove less to be relied on than a condemned convict?

At the thought of that man, feelings of the utmost complexity welled up in Li Bi’s heart. In the Lingguan Loft, those words Zhang Xiaojing had roared at him had seemed not wholly feigned. Li Bi could tell that they had been a true cry of rage from the heart — and for that very reason the more chilling.

Zhang Big-Head who had fought soaked in blood with the Eighth Company; the buliang chief who had cut down a county commandant and been flung into the death cells; the traitor taken by the Right Xiao Guard; the condemned convict hunted through the whole city; an old soldier come to demand of Chang’an a reckoning of justice!

Every one of these was true, and yet Zhang Xiaojing had not turned traitor after all — and that was what Li Bi found so chilling. He suddenly perceived that he had never seen to the bottom of Zhang Xiaojing, and that the reason was not that the man was too complex, but that he was too simple. Beneath that fierce, savage face and those rough dealings, what manner of contradictory heart was there?

Li Bi believed that when Zhang Xiaojing had raised the crossbow and leveled it at him a while ago, a true intent to kill had risen in him. Only so could he win Xiao Gui’s trust. To save the greater number, even at the cost of the innocent, Zhang Xiaojing would strike without a moment’s hesitation — and so would Li Bi.

They had once talked over just this question: a ferryboat is caught in a storm, and one man must be killed as a sacrifice to the river-god to save a hundred — to kill, or not to kill? Zhang Xiaojing’s answer and Li Bi’s had been exactly the same: kill. Yet Zhang Xiaojing had not been satisfied with that answer; he had said it was the inevitable choice, but that did not mean it was the right one.

In that answer one could glimpse the deep source of all the contradictions between what Zhang Xiaojing was and what he did. There were times when Zhang Xiaojing was simpler than anyone alive, Li Bi thought.

Casting these tangled thoughts aside, Li Bi knit his brows and searched once more through this cramped darkness.

The whole outer ring was Longwu Army. Long Bo had been able to slip in under a craftsman’s guise, but Zhang Xiaojing surely could not have. He must have some other way in — and this Hydraulic Hall must be where the answer lay hidden.

Wait — water-power?

Li Bi cast his eyes once more upon the six great wheels. The water pushed and the wheels turned — but where did the water come from? His eyes lit up; with a splash he leapt into the channel and, against the current, waded to the wall, where sure enough he found a conduit-mouth.

The rim of the conduit was quite new, carefully faced with a course of brick, about the size of a man, and the water within had risen almost to the top of the hole. Li Bi was sure that, following this channel upstream against the flow, he must come out at some open watercourse aboveground. He was no great swimmer, but he took the measure of it: so long as he thrust his nose above the surface, there was, barely, a thread of room to breathe.

Joy blossomed in Li Bi’s heart. Only let him get out, and he would go at once to have the Longwu Army throw a cordon round the tower, and take the Pifu in a single net.

He drew a deep breath and had just crouched low, about to slip inside, when all at once he heard a stir of noise. Dreading that the enemy might have marked this place and be tracing the sound, Li Bi hastily froze where he was, soaking there in the water.

Soon he saw first a few torches, and then a company of twenty or thirty men entering the Hydraulic Hall. They were fully armed, and several of them were familiar — the very band that had stormed the Jing’an Bureau.

Once inside, they set the torches in a ring, spread out about the place, and began to check over the gear on their bodies. It was fortunate that Li Bi had thrown the guard’s corpse down beside the body of the maintenance craftsman; the men gave it a passing glance and marked nothing amiss.

Li Bi silently lowered himself down until only half his head showed above the water. The sound of the water-wheels would cover most of the noise he made. From this dark vantage, the places lit by torchlight stood out with peculiar clarity.

These Pifu had likely come down here too, to keep clear of the blast? No, that was wrong... Li Bi suddenly realized that what these men carried was all weaponry, with the whole air of a party about to sally out — hardly so simple as sheltering from an explosion. But if they meant to give battle, why come into the Hydraulic Hall at all? Could it be that they too meant to leave by the passage of the channel-mouth?

Just then Li Bi saw one of them throw open a chest and take out a heap of pale-grey sharkskin diving-suits, dealing one out to every man. The act seemed to bear out his guess.

Without a sound Li Bi sank his body a little deeper and drew back toward the passage of the channel-mouth. He could wait no longer; he must leave at once, or these men would be into the water in a moment and catch him red-handed.

Carefully Li Bi moved his body, going against the current, working slowly into the passage of the channel-mouth. Halfway along he stopped short, sketching swiftly in his mind the layout of this quarter of Chang’an. All at once he recalled the uncanny thing Xiao Gui had done a while ago, making him stand up on the lantern-chamber, and a dreadful surmise began, little by little, to take shape in his mind.

He stood in the pitch-black passage and looked back in horror, his heart on the sudden colder than the channel-water.

The Hydraulic Hall’s channel had an entrance, and so it must have an outlet too. The entrance lay to the south; then the outlet lay to the north.

Directly above the Hydraulic Hall stood the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower, and to the north of the tower there was only one place.

The park of the Xingqing Palace.

Yuan Zai pressed on toward the Xingqing Palace with his soldiers of the Lüben Guards, but all along the way the lantern-watchers were thick and the going badly jammed. He stood on no courtesy: crying “Business of the Jing’an Bureau!” he set staves and scabbards to clearing a road. The commoners ahead were thrashed soundly and senselessly, and under cover of that storm of curses the men bulled their way through like charging boars, and soon reached the front of the Xingqing Palace.

All the way, the guard-corporal of the Lüben who headed the party kept asking where exactly they were going, and to do what. He was a soldier of the regular sort, with an inborn resistance to vague orders. Unluckily Yuan Zai himself could give no answer, and when pressed hard, bore the man down with the weight of his office.

When they came up near the Xingqing Palace plaza, the first thing Yuan Zai marked was not the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower soaring into the clouds, but the Qinzheng Wuben Tower beside it. The glazed owl-tail finials at either end of its roof-ridge, the gilt harness-bells hanging from its up-flung eaves, a corner of rainbow gauze fluttering out from its cloud-screen, the bracket-sets carved and lacquered and painted — every luxurious detail set Yuan Zai’s spirit swaying, and filled him with a boundless longing for the feast being held up there.

The tower blazed with light just then, and faint strains of music and drifts of fragrance came floating over to steal into his ears and nostrils. Yuan Zai twitched his nose and made out the scents of storax and Linyi borneol — rare treasures a man seldom met with in the common way, yet up on that tower no more than seasoning to lend the banquet savor.

“Who knows when I too shall be fit to drink and make merry up there,” Yuan Zai thought enviously. He sighed over it a while, then hauled his wandering thoughts by main force back into place, and only then moved his gaze to the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower.

The moment he saw that looming black monster of a thing, a strong presentiment burst upon Yuan Zai: the place Zhang Xiaojing had spoken of was there.

By the condemned convict’s account, the Pifu were very likely hidden within this tower. If it were truly so, then it bore out the old saying, “the greatest hermit hides in the marketplace” — they had hidden themselves, of all places, right under the Son of Heaven’s nose.

Still, Zhang Xiaojing’s word was not to be wholly trusted; he must first look into the matter and make certain. Yuan Zai swept his eyes about, and found that the first problem to be solved was how to draw near the tower.

The watch here was kept by the Longwu Palace Guard. Unlike the ordinary sentry-troops, they stood for the majesty of the imperial house, and wherever they were posted was forbidden ground. At Yuan Zai’s back was a pack of Lüben soldiers bearing weapons; to go rushing over so rashly — never mind striking them, but merely to lay a finger on one of them — would be reckoned rebellion.

And besides, even if the Longwu Army let them pass, the plaza within was already packed with the people, so that a man could not stir a step. In this ground Yuan Zai dared no longer take up a scabbard to flog anyone; once a panic and a stampede got up, he feared he might not escape with his own life.

A few tall war-horses passed slowly across the front of the plaza, and by the firelight Yuan Zai made out the man among them: Chen Xuanli, Grand General of the Longwu Army. In his present standing Yuan Zai should have no great trouble in gaining an audience with Chen Xuanli; he had only to lay the whole matter out plainly, and it was not beyond hope that he might win the man’s cooperation.

But — would that not be to hand a share of the credit to another for nothing?

To Yuan Zai’s way of thinking, credit was a scarce and finite treasure, not to be lightly lent to anyone. His instinct told him that this was, very likely, a prize even greater than the wresting of the Jing’an Bureau — and all the less, therefore, to be shared out with another.

Better to go it alone, if go it alone he could.

From his high vantage he watched carefully a while, then directed his Lüben soldiers to skirt round by the outside to the southeast corner of the plaza. This was the angle between the plaza, Daozheng Ward, and the Chunming Gate, where the crowd was thinnest and, at the same time, the great lantern-tower nearest.

In the streets hereabouts the road-surface bore many wheel-ruts, some new, some old, and all deep — a sign that a great many freight-carts had passed. Yuan Zai studied them and concluded that this must be the way by which materials for the building of the great lantern-tower had gone in and out. The people of Chang’an were for the most part superstitious, and so a building-site commonly set its materials-gate to the southeast, in the same quarter as the privy, holding it a “foul gate” through which no other party might mingle its traffic.

About a foul gate the commoners were fewer, the road clear, and it stood but fifty-odd paces from Mystic Abbey. Yet along that stretch the Longwu Army had set three lines of cordon in all, stretching spiked barriers across the road, their guard close and strict. The Lüben went as far as the corner and no further, that they might not provoke the Palace Guard too far.

“Are we to force our way in?” the corporal asked rashly.

“Wait,” Yuan Zai answered.

He leaned against a torch-post, lifted his head, and gazed at the huge structure before him. If nothing at all befell the great lantern-tower, then at the very worst he had made a wasted journey; but if something did change there, this would be the spot from which one could respond the fastest.

What Yuan Zai needed was only a little patience — and luck.

***

Xiao Gui’s words left Zhang Xiaojing deeply shaken.

For one thing, he had not reckoned that, besides the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower, the Pifu had yet another plan; for another, the mustering-place of that band of crack veterans should be, of all places, the Hydraulic Hall — where, be it remembered, Li Bi was. If Li Bi had done away with the guard, the veterans would find it out at once, and that would be as good as exposing himself as well.

More awkward still, by Xiao Gui’s meaning, Zhang Xiaojing was to go along with him. That way he would have no chance at all to steal a qilin-arm at Mystic Abbey, and there could be no question of blowing the turning-gear to ruin.

He must contrive some chance to act alone.

“Big-Head, what are you standing there in a daze thinking about?” Xiao Gui patted him.

“Oh — oh, it’s nothing, nothing...”

“I know your head’s still in a bit of a muddle, that you haven’t got it all straight yet. But believe me — we held out at the beacon-fort; what’s a little trouble like this?” Xiao Gui crooked a finger at him. “And don’t forget — you still owe me a few mint leaves.”

“Then you’ll have to wait till I dig them out of a dead man’s mouth,” Zhang Xiaojing answered.

Xiao Gui roared with laughter — it was talk that belonged to the beacon-fort of long ago, and to it alone. His laughter done, Xiao Gui laid a hand on Zhang Xiaojing’s shoulder and, of a sudden, said gravely, “Big-Head — you and I never once betrayed each other under the Türks’ siege, and I trust you won’t this time either. See you don’t fail me, and fail the whole of the Eighth Company.”

Zhang Xiaojing hardly dared look straight into those eyes, and could only give a vague nod of his head.

“So I want you in on the operation at the Hydraulic Hall — that way I’ll have something to show my men.” Xiao Gui winked. “Set your mind at ease; this one won’t put you in any hard spot. It’ll be a rare pleasure — very much to your taste, I promise you.”

“Then what exactly is it?”

“You’ll know soon enough. It isn’t the moment yet — no sense in alarming the Longwu Palace Guard outside.” Xiao Gui held back the secret. At those words Zhang Xiaojing’s mind turned like lightning, and all at once a splendid pretext came to him. “It’s the Longwu Palace Guard outside?”

“Of course. With the Son of Heaven on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, naturally it must be they who stand the guard.” Xiao Gui found it very odd that Zhang Xiaojing should ask so elementary a question.

“What I mean is — the outer guarding of the great lantern-tower, is that the Longwu Army’s charge too? Not the Left Xiao Guard? Not the Qianniu Guard, or the Wanqi?”

Xiao Gui said it was certainly the Longwu Army; when their convoy had come into the plaza it had passed inspection at post after post, and one had only to see the tiger-guard blazon on the sentries’ shoulder-helms to know it. He could not understand why Zhang Xiaojing was fretting over the point.

Zhang Xiaojing’s face turned grave. “If it’s the Longwu Army, then we may run into trouble.”

“Oh?”

“The Grand General of the Longwu Palace Guard is called Chen Xuanli. When I was buliang chief of Wannian County I had dealings with him a few times. The man is exceedingly thorough in his work; he sees to everything in person. So important an installation as the great lantern-tower — before the lamps are lit, he will most certainly come to inspect it. Have you made ready to deal with that?”

Xiao Gui grasped at once where Zhang Xiaojing’s misgiving lay.

He had not, beforehand, been without a thought that someone might come into the tower and see through the truth of it, and so had left a few quick-witted men in Mystic Abbey, disguised as petty clerks and guards of the Forestry and Crafts Bureau. These had been instructed to their faces: whoever should try to burst in and inspect, they were to bar him, one and all, and the reason was but one — “Delay the lighting of the lantern-tower, and I fear the Son of Heaven’s wrath” — at hearing which, the other would most likely give it up.

But if it were truly as Zhang Xiaojing said, and the one who came to inspect was Chen Xuanli, then those few men would hardly be able to bar him. In fact Zhang Xiaojing had no clear idea whether Chen Xuanli would come in person; but this was the one pretext to hand at present, and he had to make a fifty-fifty chance out to be a certainty.

Xiao Gui knit his brows. “Then what’s to be done?”

“There is only one man who can bar Chen Xuanli.”

“Who?”

Zhang Xiaojing cast his glance over that way, to where Mao Shun had just clambered up from the floor and was rubbing painfully at his back.

Understanding lit Xiao Gui’s eyes at once. Weak as Mao Shun was in character, in matters of craft he carried a supreme authority. Should he refuse outsiders entry on the ground that it endangered the works, then even Chen Xuanli, most likely, could do nothing about it.

Seeing Xiao Gui already drawn into his rhythm, Zhang Xiaojing spoke up at once. “In any case I’ve nothing to do up here; you might as well let me take Master Mao down and stand ready in Mystic Abbey against any mischance. When you’ve finished the mounting, come down and join me, and then we’ll go on to the Hydraulic Hall.”

Xiao Gui thought it over a moment, judged the proposal a good one, and nodded. Then he called up two guards to escort Zhang Xiaojing and Mao Shun down. This arrangement showed that Xiao Gui’s suspicion was not yet wholly gone. Zhang Xiaojing thought to himself: Xiao Gui would not, sure enough, set his mind at ease and let a man who had only just surrendered go off with a craftsman deep in the secret — not even a man who was his old comrade-in-arms.

He made a show, deliberately, of being unconcerned, and went of his own accord over to Mao Shun, to give Xiao Gui the chance to charge the two guards. Mao Shun had not yet grasped what was happening; Zhang Xiaojing hauled him up roughly, then leaned to his ear and said, “Do everything as I say.”

Mao Shun hastily nodded, eased his body, and let Zhang Xiaojing steer him where he would. Over there Xiao Gui had finished his charge; the two guards came up, one before and one behind, guarding the pair of them as they went down out of the tower. Xiao Gui, for his part, turned back to go on urging the craftsmen through the last of the mounting.

To come down from the lantern-tower to Mystic Abbey was no easy matter either. The gaps between the hanging bridges were wide, and the scant candlelight lit only a ring about oneself. They had to circle warily round and round along the tower’s edge; one careless step into the void, and a man would drop straight to the pitch-black bottom of the tower.

Through the dim space the four of them wove up and down, the hanging bridges and bamboo frames giving out creaks now and again, as though they might snap at any instant, so that from afar the party seemed like ghosts afloat in the air. The clamorous song and dance without came through the tower’s stretched skin in waves, working a strange sounding-effect within this grim, hollow tower. The feeling of it was as though a crack had been prized open between the two worlds of light and shade, and a little of the warm breath of the living had seeped through from the world of men.

“Where are you from?” Zhang Xiaojing asked suddenly. The guard leading the way did not take it in at first, and only when he felt a tap on his shoulder did he understand that he was being spoken to.

“I’m a tuanjie militiaman of Yuezhou — a son of a Pillar of State.”

“Oh?” Zhang Xiaojing was mildly surprised. The tuanjie militia were all local garrisons, guarding none but their own home district; yet if a man’s father or grandfather had been granted the honorary title of “Pillar of State,” his standing was quite another thing — at the least it might have won him a commander’s post.

An officer of that rank, and yet following Xiao Gui in a head-forfeiting trade like this? Zhang Xiaojing mused to himself, and tossed his head back. “And you?” The guard behind hastened to answer, “I come of the frontier levy of Yingzhou.”

In the border prefectures there were always able-bodied men set to guard the frontier, and the local garrison offices recruited stout fellows, Hu and Han alike, from among them. “Oh?” said Zhang Xiaojing. “Over Hebei way, then. I recall your parts produced a military commissioner of Pinglu?”

“That’s right — An Lushan, Commissioner An; he’s an Yingzhou man.” The guard answered respectfully. “I was a yueqi horseman under his command.”

At that name Zhang Xiaojing looked the man over a little more closely by the candlelight, and sure enough there was some Hu blood in this guard. “Then how does a man come all the way here from the Pinglu army?”

The guard gave a bitter smile. “My commanding officer helped himself to the army’s grain and lined his own pockets. The Transport Commissioner sent an accountant to look into it, and the officer, for his pains, burned the man to death along with the whole granary in a single blaze. Because I had crossed him before, he named me the one who set the fire. I had no way to clear myself, and could only flee.”

“Hah — and where is it not so? The crows of all the world are black alike.” The guard in front put in; no doubt he too had met with some grievance of his own. The one behind offered a word in his defense: “Commissioner An, now, he’s a good man — a man of honor. A pity there are far too few officials of his sort.”

Zhang Xiaojing had done no more than open the subject, and the two guards fell of themselves to pouring out their bitterness. It seemed the men Xiao Gui had found were much of a kind in their histories — all of them elite soldiers who had suffered some great wrong.

“And how did you come to know Commander Long Bo?” one of the guards asked suddenly, out of curiosity.

“Heh — that’s a long story.” Zhang Xiaojing told out what he and Xiao Gui had gone through at the beacon-fort, and the two guards listened with gasps of wonder, their eyes glinting with admiration and pity.

They had never thought that this one-eyed fellow before them should be a survivor of the same battle to the death as Xiao Gui — no wonder the two were on such easy terms. For men who had once gone into battle side by side and killed the foe together, they felt an inborn liking and trust.

Zhang Xiaojing went on to tell of his return to Chang’an as a buliang chief, of what had befallen at the Wen family’s incense shop, and of all the wrongs he had borne in the Jing’an Bureau — very frankly, with nothing added to spice the tale. The two guards were all but dumbfounded to hear it: an hour before, this man had been their most dangerous enemy, and now he was the chief’s good friend; yet, thinking it over, the reason for his change of side was only too easy to understand. Drive a man to such a pass — how could he not turn traitor?

By the end of this stretch of the way the two guards were wholly won over by Zhang Xiaojing, and would tell him anything. Without much trouble he drew out of them the charge Xiao Gui had laid on them: “So long as Zhang Xiaojing and Mao Shun do not, of their own accord, leave Mystic Abbey and go outside, let them be.”

Not to go outside meant one could carry no word to the outside. In other words, to move about freely within the tower and Mystic Abbey was no trouble at all.

Having felt out Xiao Gui’s bottom line, Zhang Xiaojing knew where he stood, and of a sudden he threw out a question. “Do you hate the court?”

The two guards answered as with one voice, “We do.”

“If you had a chance to bring down the court of Great Tang, but it would cost the lives of a great many innocent people — would you do it?” Zhang Xiaojing’s voice came in the dark, unhurried and even.

“Of course we would.” Again as with one voice. Soon one of them asked, faintly, “How many is a great many?”

“Fifty.”

“We’d do it!”

“And if this vengeance of yours upon the court cost five hundred innocent commoners their lives?”

“We would... I suppose?” This time the answer was markedly feebler.

“And five thousand, then? Fifty thousand? Just how many of the people must die before you would call off this operation of yours?”

“This time we’re aiming only at the court; we’d never lift a hand against the people.” One of the guards came to himself at last.

Zhang Xiaojing halted, drew back the stretched skin, and looked out. “Come and see for yourself. Gathered on the plaza right now are getting on for fifty thousand of Chang’an’s own people. If the tower goes up, the Qinzheng Wuben Tower will not be spared, true enough — but these fifty thousand will become wronged ghosts as well.”

The two guards looked out in turn, and their breathing plainly quickened. Outside the heads bobbed thick as a tide, so that scarcely a patch of the plaza’s ground could be seen — fifty thousand lives was, if anything, to put it low. Even for cruel men who believed in no Buddha and revered no Dao, to kill so many at a single stroke could not but set the heart quaking.

The guard registered in Yingzhou asked in doubt, “Do you mean you don’t approve of this operation?” Zhang Xiaojing gave the man’s blade a glance, and without a flicker said, “It isn’t that I don’t approve; it’s that one must mend the roof before the rain. I once heard a Daoist master of the Qingyun Abbey say that when a man dies on another’s account, he turns into a wronged ghost, a vengeful revenant, and clings to one without end — even the wheel of rebirth cannot wipe away the sin of it. One man wrongfully dead counts for one kalpa; the death of fifty thousand — reckon it up, how long a man must seethe in hell for that?”

The men of the Tang were much given to the worship of gods, and believed devoutly in cause and consequence. Hearing this, the two guards both showed unease upon their faces. “Then what do you say we should do?”

“When I came up just now, I saw a crown-loft beside the eaves of Mystic Abbey’s roof, with a True Lord enshrined within. I’m thinking that if we offer up a prayer of expiation here, it may wipe away a little of the guilt.” Zhang Xiaojing put it as a matter for discussion, but his tone brooked no objection.

“But we’re supposed to be going to Mystic Abbey...”

Zhang Xiaojing gave him a glance and said mildly, “This won’t take much time. It’s settled.”

After that stretch of talk, Zhang Xiaojing loomed rather large in the two guards’ minds, and there was, without their marking it, a powerful compelling force in his word. The move did not strike them as abrupt. The two guards conferred in low voices and, judging that this request did not go against Xiao Gui’s charge, saw no reason it should not be allowed.

“Give me the eight characters of your birth-dates, the two of you. I know a little of the Daoist arts, and while I make the expiation I can clear away some of your karmic hindrance into the bargain.”

The two guards, naturally, thanked him a thousand times over.

The crown-loft of Mystic Abbey was a square, lofty chamber; over its head was the very bottom of the lantern-tower, and below it lay the whole of Mystic Abbey and, underground, the Hydraulic Hall. This loft might be called the vital linchpin joining the upper and lower parts together.

Zhang Xiaojing pushed open the door and went in, to find nothing at all within: the pillars roughly lacquered, the window-lattices crudely made — plainly never meant to be lived in. In the very center of the room stood a great millstone-form cast of fine bronze, its substance bright and clear, faint whorls upon whorls still to be seen on its surface, though it bore no ornament. This millstone was divided into three tiers in all, each a full three chi high, meshing above and below; and at the narrowest point on top was a mechanism, thrusting right up into the tail of the Tianshu — this object must be the turning-gear that Mao Shun had spoken of.

Zhang Xiaojing looked it over carefully. The rim of the turning-gear was fixed into the floor of Mystic Abbey by an inset method, the two made all of a piece, exceedingly firm. It seemed that without the fierce-fire thunder there would truly be no shaking it.

Zhang Xiaojing came out, and the guards were much surprised — how had he come out so soon? Zhang Xiaojing said, “There’s not even a lamp in there; there’s no worshiping the gods. Let’s go down first.”

The four of them left the crown-loft and went down by the stairway to the great hall of Mystic Abbey. The six little cauldrons were still blazing away behind the hall, but most of the qilin-arms had already been sent up, and few bamboo tubes were left in the cauldrons — no more than ten, by the look of them.

Zhang Xiaojing shot Mao Shun a look. Mao Shun went over at once, fished one up out of a cauldron, stroked it over from end to end, and said to the keeper, “They need one more up above.” The keeper reached out hastily to carry it up, but Mao Shun stopped him. “The hour’s getting on, and that position is a rather particular one — best I go myself.” So saying, he gathered up the qilin-arm, turned, and went on up.

Odd as the keeper found it, who would dare question Master Mao’s word on a matter of craft?

At the same time Zhang Xiaojing asked the fire-tender for a flint, some mugwort tinder, and a few bundles of green incense-sticks, and waved them before the guards’ eyes. “I’ll go up and set a stick of incense; I’ll be down soon.” The two guards moved at once to follow, but Zhang Xiaojing said, “There’s no telling when someone may come bursting in from outside; you keep watch here. I’ll be back in no time.”

Zhang Xiaojing was only going to make an offering to the gods, and was not leaving Mystic Abbey. So the two, glad enough to climb a few flights the fewer, rested there in the hall and waited for him to come back.

Rid of the two guards, Zhang Xiaojing went back up to the crown-loft alone. Mao Shun was already surveying the turning-gear’s position, putting out his fingers now and again to take a measure, muttering a reckoning-formula under his breath. Zhang Xiaojing asked how the calculation was coming, and Mao Shun returned a single line: “It can’t be hurried.” At that Zhang Xiaojing dared not press him, and could only wait patiently at his side.

Mao Shun at his work was altogether other than his usual self. At ordinary times he was no more than a frail, timid old man; but the moment his own field of skill was touched, he became on the instant a very grand master in his bearing, as though to say, “Who but I?” No wonder Chao Fen praised him without end.

To stop the explosion, the turning-gear had to be hurt but not destroyed. Only with the gear’s angle thrown askew would the turning slowly wring the Tianshu apart, as one crushes a stick of sugarcane. Break open but one spot, let the rock-oil pour out and lose its inner force, and there would be no danger of an explosion. To bring that off, the placing of the qilin-arm had to be of the utmost nicety — work that no one but Mao Shun could do.

The crown-loft was silent past all telling, with only the din of the world outside coming faintly through. After a spell of reckoning, Mao Shun undid the loops at the front of his robe, drew from his breast a piece of soapstone, and bending low, drew a few lines on the stone plinth below the turning-gear; then, hesitating a little, he set the qilin-arm gently against them, and took its measure there.

Zhang Xiaojing let out a long breath, thinking that surely this was near enough done? But Mao Shun, working away, suddenly went weak at both knees, flung the qilin-arm clattering down onto the floor, and said with a sob in his voice, “I can’t... I can’t. This is the whole labor of my life — I cannot destroy it!”

Zhang Xiaojing barked at him in a low voice, “If you don’t destroy it now, it’ll be destroyed by villains in a moment! Is it not all one?”

“But it is so beautiful, so exquisite. Destroy it this once, and there’ll never be a second chance to build it again...” Mao Shun slumped to the floor as though broken. Whatever coercion and wrong he had borne before, at the very moment of striking, the craftsman’s heart had won out in him at last. In this, Chao Fen would have understood him very well.

“Do you mean you’ll disregard your own family’s lives as well?” Zhang Xiaojing had no mind to marvel at such aesthetics.

The few words struck Mao Shun. He suddenly raised his head, threw his arms about Zhang Xiaojing’s thigh, and pleaded bitterly, “Don’t blow this up — let me find a way to get you out, and go and report it to the authorities, how would that be?”

“There’s no time!” Zhang Xiaojing kicked him into a corner of the crown-loft and then, like a fierce lion, clamped a grip on his throat. “Get it mounted, quickly! Or you’ll die before the tower does, and I promise you your family will die a wretched death too!”

“You... are you not a man of the government?”

“The story I told those two guards just now — you heard it too; every word of it is true.”

The keen light of that single eye all but cut Mao Shun to pieces by inches. Mao Shun, after all, was no Chao Fen; he could not reach that state of self-forgetting where a man knows neither kith nor kin. Under the crushing pressure, Mao Shun could only, with every unwillingness, take up the qilin-arm again and wedge it toward the lines he had drawn.

Just then, a faint laugh came from within the crown-loft.

Zhang Xiaojing’s brows knit on the instant, and he snatched out the crossbow at his waist. Mao Shun asked in alarm what was the matter. Zhang Xiaojing bade him mind his work, then half-straightened up and looked to left and right. The four corners of the crown-loft’s ceiling were plastered white right into the angles; there could be no place of concealment there at all.

Then it came to him: above this crown-loft was the main frame of the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower, so the roof could not be thick. If someone were lying atop it and listening, he might very well have caught the whole of the talk before. Zhang Xiaojing quietly raised the crossbow and edged, bit by bit, nearer. All at once he heard soft footsteps again, and without a word loosed two bolts straight up at the ceiling, then followed with one more each to the front and rear.

The ceiling was indeed no more than a flimsy makeshift of boards, and all four bolts drove clean through. By the sound, it seemed one of them had struck something. Zhang Xiaojing was about to look up through a bolt-hole when a grim, chilling voice came down first:

“Zhang Xiaojing — you have a treacherous heart after all.”

It was Yuchang!

So the fellow had not gone far off at all, but had been dogging them the whole way. A wringing pain shot through Zhang Xiaojing’s belly. The situation now could be called as bad as it could possibly be: the truth found out by the most troublesome enemy of all, and little hope, he feared, of retrieving it.

He pricked up his ears again, but the stir on the ceiling had ceased; Yuchang had already gone away. With the fellow’s skill and the tangled maze of the tower, there was no possibility of Zhang Xiaojing overtaking him to silence him.

Once word reached Xiao Gui’s ears, then he, and Li Bi, and Mao Shun too, would all be done for in an instant, he feared.

Somewhat at a loss, Zhang Xiaojing looked at the four holes in the ceiling. Was there truly not the least chance left?

No — there was a chance yet!

A stubborn resolve rose in his breast. Gritting his teeth, Zhang Xiaojing turned and roared at Mao Shun, “Take hold of the flint and the tinder! Light the fuse this instant!” Once the turning-gear was blown askew, then even if Xiao Gui got wind of it, there would be no time to mend it.

Mao Shun’s hand shook. Set it off now? Then the two of them would have no time to withdraw.

“Don’t blow it now and there’ll be no chance at all!” Zhang Xiaojing knew the consequences too, but this was the one chance there was. Mao Shun was struck still for a moment; he had never thought this fellow would care nothing whatever for saving his own life.

From above came a press of footsteps, and the creak, creak of the wooden bridges and bamboo beams. Little time was left them. He turned and thrust the flint and tinder into Mao Shun’s hands, and bade him strike a light. Mao Shun huddled by the turning-gear’s stone plinth, striking the flint blow upon blow, but his hands shook so badly that for the longest while no spark would come.

“Die for your country resisting the foe, or die by your own hand as a traitor to it — choose one for your family.” Zhang Xiaojing flung the words down coldly.

Blow up the turning-gear and die, and it would count as a glorious death for the country — his family would at least be commended and honored. Fail to blow it up, and when the tower went off, the whole world would know it for Mao Shun’s own handiwork; his death would end his part of it, but what would become of his family could well be imagined.

Mao Shun’s spirit was near the breaking point.

By now the footsteps had drawn close to the crown-loft, and Zhang Xiaojing knew the last moment had come. With no leisure to wait for Mao Shun to declare himself, he stepped forward to stand in the doorway of the crown-loft, felt out four bolts from his waist, and loaded the crossbow.

He reckoned it up: holding this doorway, he could stall them at least a ten-odd finger-snaps more — barely enough for Mao Shun to touch off the qilin-arm.

The footsteps came nearer and nearer, and no small number of them. Crossbow in hand, his back against the loft door, Zhang Xiaojing fixed his single eye hard on the outside, sweat running from his brow. There was little light in the loft now, while the men outside all carried lanterns — the enemy in the light, himself in the dark. How the Pifu would storm the loft, he had to judge in advance.

All at once the loft door was flung carelessly open with a swish, and Xiao Gui’s head poked in.

This was wholly beyond Zhang Xiaojing’s expectation. He had pictured the enemy breaking down the door, or through the ceiling, or simply loosing arrows and bolts from the doorway; he had never imagined Xiao Gui would come pushing in alone, wholly off his guard. And so for one instant Zhang Xiaojing’s movement froze.

“Big-Head? How is it you’ve come up here?” Xiao Gui asked.

Bounded by the light, his eyes could make out no more than Zhang Xiaojing’s face. Zhang Xiaojing was on the very point of pulling the trigger-lever when, hearing these words, he could not help but start. Swiftly he hid the crossbow away, his expression stiff, at a loss what to say. Xiao Gui looked him over suspiciously. “Weren’t you supposed to be waiting down below?”

Yuchang had not told him about us?

That was Zhang Xiaojing’s first surmise — but how could that be?

“Oh — I came up to worship the gods.” Zhang Xiaojing answered vaguely, on his guard within lest the other were lulling him on purpose, to seize the chance for a sudden strike.

Xiao Gui’s look was not that of a man dissembling; he laughed with a click of his tongue. “You believe in that sort of thing? It’s an empty shell in there — there’s no god to worship at all.”

Zhang Xiaojing suddenly noticed that Xiao Gui had said “you,” and not “you two.” The loft was bright without and dark within, and the place where Mao Shun was mounting the qilin-arm was on the far side of the turning-gear, its tall stone plinth screening Mao Shun’s figure from view, so that Xiao Gui had not marked his presence at all — most likely still supposing Mao Shun to be in the great hall of Mystic Abbey.

Having settled on his course, Zhang Xiaojing turned his body round to block the doorway, quietly slipped the crossbow back, and managed a smile. “That’s just why I’m getting ready to go down, isn’t it?”

Xiao Gui felt something amiss somewhere; he stared at Zhang Xiaojing a while, then looked past his shoulder at the turning-gear. Suddenly he threw up a hand, and Zhang Xiaojing’s heart all but skipped a beat.

“Don’t dawdle about up here for nothing — get down.” Xiao Gui said. “It’s all done up above; the works will start any moment. Let’s get down to the Hydraulic Hall and muster, as fast as we can.” He paused, then stressed with relish, “And then we sit tight, and wait to hear the biggest firecracker in all Chang’an.”

Zhang Xiaojing was sure at last that Yuchang could not yet have told Xiao Gui, or Xiao Gui would never be wasting so many words on him. At this unlooked-for stroke of luck he let out a long breath in secret.

Zhang Xiaojing shot a glance at the dark corner by the turning-gear, and made deliberately for the loft door, saying loudly as he went, “This time we’d best seize our chance well, or we’ll regret it all our lives.” Xiao Gui gave a few grunts of assent, looking thoroughly pleased with himself.

The far side of the turning-platform had kept silent throughout — a sign that Mao Shun, huddled there, had heard it all as well.

Outside the loft, Zhang Xiaojing saw many men standing in the long passage — all the craftsmen who had been busy above just now. They had finished the task of the switch on time, thrown down the tools they no longer needed, and were withdrawing below together. This meant that the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower had now become, wholly and utterly, the Que-le Huo-duo.

The decisive hour of the Ox, second half, was about to come. And its fate would be settled by the one who had created it.

His mind full of misgiving, Zhang Xiaojing left the crown-loft with Xiao Gui and went down, the craftsmen following silently behind. As if in passing, Zhang Xiaojing asked, “And Yuchang?”

“Heh-heh — afraid he’ll take his revenge on you?” Xiao Gui gave him a mischievous glance.

“Yes.”

“Set your mind at ease — he’ll trouble you no more from now on.” Xiao Gui reached to the sash at his waist and gave it a shake; on it hung a red cord, and the cord was bare and empty, without a single copper coin.

This was what Yuchang had given over to Xiao Gui: ten copper coins, for ten things done.

“The starting of the Que-le Huo-duo wants a man to light the fire at close range. So the last thing I charged him with was to stay in the tower, and light it the moment it starts. His footwork is very good — he’s the only man who could get clear before the fierce-fire thunder goes off... so long as he gets clear in time.”

Zhang Xiaojing looked at Xiao Gui, understanding breaking over him all at once. “You never meant to let him leave alive?”

“A dangerous, unmanageable fellow like that — how could his life be left him?” Xiao Gui tilted his head back, winding the end of the red cord about his finger.

It seemed Xiao Gui and Yuchang had each kept a wary guard against the other all along; and it was thanks to that alone that Zhang Xiaojing had won himself a road out of death into life.

The cheering outside rose wave upon wave, each higher than the last. Out on the plaza the lantern-float performers had brought their contest with one another to a white heat. The final “lantern-crown red tally” was about to be decided; he or she who won it would have the honor of mounting the Qinzheng Wuben Tower and, before the Son of Heaven, the assembled court, and the envoys of every nation, lighting the lamps of the Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower.

“Ah — I do envy the folk down there. To pass so happy a stretch of time before they die — it’s a blessing earned over several lifetimes, truly.” Xiao Gui lifted a flap of the stretched skin, and made the remark coldly.

Zhang Xiaojing gazed at him. “As I recall, you were not this sort of man in the old days.”

“A man always changes — and so does a court.” Xiao Gui answered darkly.

Soon they reached Mystic Abbey. The two guards had been waiting on tenterhooks, and were relieved to see Zhang Xiaojing come down together with Xiao Gui. Xiao Gui looked round about. “And Master Mao?”

The keeper of the little cauldrons said, “Master Mao took up a qilin-arm and went up again.” “Gone where?” Xiao Gui asked, knitting his brows. The keeper said he did not know. Xiao Gui looked to Zhang Xiaojing. “Big-Head — wasn’t he with you? How is it he’s gone off on his own again?”

“Master Mao said he’d thought of an oversight he had to correct, and would go back whatever anyone said. Seeing that he wasn’t going outside to inform on us, I thought, and let him have his way.” Zhang Xiaojing added, feeling his way, “Shall I go up and look for him again?”

He glanced up instinctively; the crown-loft was still without a stir, and there was no telling what Mao Shun was about up there after all.

Xiao Gui stood where he was, somewhat vexed. Any other man would have been one thing, but Master Mao was the designer of this lantern-tower; should he try to work some mischief with a qilin-arm in hand, he could all too easily endanger the whole plan.

But the hour of the Ox, second half, was upon them now, the tower would in a moment become the most dangerous place of all, and there was besides a more important operation at the Hydraulic Hall waiting to be led. For the moment Xiao Gui was in something of a dilemma. Zhang Xiaojing spoke up of his own accord. “This is my oversight; I’ll go back for him. You go down first — don’t wait for me.” At this Xiao Gui at once refused. “No. Once the tower turns it’ll become a sea of fire in an instant; to go up is a road straight to death.”

“The twenty-four lantern-chambers burn in order, and only last does it reach the Tianshu — there’s a little time yet before the explosion. I think I can get out.” Zhang Xiaojing said. “We held out at the beacon-fort; is our Eighth Company to be afraid of a small scene like this?”

Xiao Gui turned his head and barked at the two guards, “Set to watch a man, and you couldn’t even do that! You go too — see that Xiaojing has someone to back him up!” Unwilling as the two guards were, they could only murmur their assent.

“Kill Mao Shun, and get down again as fast as you can. Once you’re at the Hydraulic Hall, you’ll know where to find us next.” Xiao Gui charged him, his tone full of worry.

If before he had still harbored some doubt of Zhang Xiaojing, now his mind was wholly at ease. No mole would volunteer of his own will to go to his death; only a comrade who had shared life and death with a man would make such a choice.

After the fashion of those old days Zhang Xiaojing and Xiao Gui embraced each other once, and then, with the two guards, he wheeled hastily about and went up. The men beside him urged Xiao Gui to get down to the Hydraulic Hall quickly, but Xiao Gui did not stir; he kept gazing at the stair-head where Zhang Xiaojing had vanished, his eyes flickering.

Not long after they had gone, a vast wave of cheering suddenly rose outside the tower, like startled breakers dashing on the shore, and in a moment swept through the whole tower and would not die away. It seemed the lantern-float red tally of this year’s Lantern Festival had at last been decided.

Dense and rolling, the watch-drums came booming from every side. The hour of the Ox, second half, had come.

Xiao Gui let out a long sigh, snapped his fingers, and gave the last command: “Open the tower!” Then he turned and went down to the Hydraulic Hall.

In the mechanism-room to the side, a ten-odd stout men bore down together on several iron bars, and that force, through a long train of intricate works, let the roof of the Hydraulic Hall sink slowly down. With a few clicks and clacks, the horse-mouth couplings of the hall’s roof engaged with the six great water-wheels and meshed to perfection. The mighty power gathered of the six wheels climbed up along the roof’s horse-mouths, passed through the keel, turned the rudder-levers, and was at last passed to that turning-gear cast of fine steel, driving the Tianshu slowly round.

The moment the Tianshu moved, the whole Taishang Xuanyuan Grand Lantern-Tower gave out a low, drawn-out hum; its body shivered a little, and awoke at last.
