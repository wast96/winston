## Chapter 16. The Hills Wind, the Road Turns, and I Meet You Again

“The sunlight pours down over the earth, and the clouds go soaring in freedom across the deep-blue vault of heaven. That is their domain; up on the canopy of the sky the rosy clouds and the light of day are playing out a stirring love affair.” Yang Muci intoned the words under his breath, but the gun in his hand never missed; behind him he could hear the querulous cursing of the man he had just “shot.”

“No one can halt the march of my advance…” Alert, Yang Muci wheeled and brought his hand back around; the muzzle came to bear on Xin Lili, who was coming up the stairs toward him, and he snatched the gun back at once.

“Could you show a little consideration for my personal feelings?” Xin Lili said, pressed flat against the foot of the wall. “I’ve been hit.”

“Is it serious?”

“The bullet caught me in the belly.” Xin Lili held up a hollow shell casing. “Thank God! Lucky it wasn’t a real battlefield.”

“An exercise and a battlefield are no different,” said Yang Muci.

This was the last “live-combat exercise” examination Yang Muci would sit at the school. If today he could plant, within the set time, the flag that stood for his action team on the roof of the main teaching building, and at the same moment “wipe out” the defenders and take out the “enemy” command post, he would graduate with the highest possible marks. To put it another way, he would have bought himself a handsome pass, and could stage a triumphant, aboveboard jailbreak. The half year of brutal massed training had been no less than a spell trapped in hell, the heart shut up in a cell; now, with the first light almost at hand, he could allow himself not the slightest slackening, not a hair’s carelessness. Otherwise everything he had achieved would be thrown away.

“Do you know why I picked you for my partner?” Muci said. “Look out!” Quicker than the words could come, Lili caught sight of someone overhead; before she could make a sound, Muci settled the crisis with a single shot. Their faces were close, cheek to cheek, each feeling the other’s slightly labored breath. “Thank you,” Lili said.

“A trap hidden in the dark is nothing to fear. What is to be feared is a trap that moves.”

A figure came flying along a steel wire, swinging through the air. “Unluckily for us, you’ve called it!” Xin Lili loosed two shots in succession and saved Muci, who had been left exposed at the muzzle’s end. The steel cable dropped into Muci’s hand. “Perfect harmony,” Muci said with a smile. “That is exactly why I chose you.”

Yang Muci lashed the wire around his waist, sprang into the air, and flew up a whole story, his movements savage, unstoppable as a blade splitting bamboo. With a corkscrewing kick of both legs he flung his opponents over. Xin Lili led her people charging straight up, dropping one man with each shot.

“Nobody move!” A violent roar came down the corridor. “Move, and I shoot the hostage!”

Yang Muci and the others darted behind the wall, taking it for cover, and in an instant broke off the assault.

The team member at the southeast corner signed to Muci in hand-language: he clamped a hand around his own throat, then with his forefinger traced a closed rectangle in the air, up, right, down, and back to the left.

“The hostage is below the window,” Muci told Lili.

“Doesn’t it strike you as too quiet?” Lili said. The narrow, featureless passage looked all the more like a death trap. “This is a warning without words. We can’t just bull ahead. Think it over again.”

“I don’t need you to map out a line of reasoning. What I need is a result you can see the instant you raise the pole,” Muci said, and then let his eyes travel, with deliberate intent, over the hills and hollows of Xin Lili’s chest.

“Dream on, or I’ll knock your eyeballs loose.” Curse him though she did, there was a preening satisfaction in Xin Lili’s tone.

Muci let his body slide along the wall and, naturally enough, drew close against Lili, murmuring, “At the decisive moment, general and minister had best be of one heart.”

“Using me like this—aren’t you afraid I’ll turn my coat in the thick of it?” Xin Lili’s gun swung to point at Muci.

“That would only make it more interesting.” Muci took up a magazine, raised it level with the top of his head, and swung it slowly left and right. The classmates coming up behind at once checked their ammunition—all of it the hollow drill rounds.

The check done, Yang Muci crooked his elbow, forearm angled to the ground, fingers pressed together, and swung it from behind him out toward the front. On the order they all pushed forward at full speed.

“On one, two, three!” The words had scarcely left Muci’s mouth when Xin Lili shot out like an arrow with a whoosh, gave a cry—“Aiya!”—and rolled into the middle of the corridor. “Don’t shoot!” she said. “I’m unarmed. I’ve come to trade for the hostage.”

“Want to trade for the hostage? Fine. Take off your clothes and walk over.”

“All right.” Xin Lili agreed with brisk good cheer; there seemed to come through the air the sound of cloth being torn, and then, with a soft phut, the instructor playing the enemy saw only a blur before his eyes and took a round in the chest. Yang Muci directed his team in seizing the commanding heights of the whole building. The hostage was freed—the one playing the hostage was the instructor Yu Xiaojiang.

“I’ll take you to the command post,” said Yu Xiaojiang.

Yang Muci walked on without so much as turning his head.

“This way you can save time and finish the mission,” Yu Xiaojiang said, keeping close on Muci’s heels.

“You—shut it!” Muci barked her down.

“If you want to win by surprise, you’ll do as I say. Otherwise you—”

Yang Muci fired a shot back over his hand and caught Yu Xiaojiang in a vital spot.

“Are you mad?” Xin Lili shrieked. “You’ve killed the hostage—they’ll dock us points.”

“Why did you do that?” Yu Xiaojiang demanded of Muci.

“Teacher, you’re already dead. The dead shouldn’t be any trouble.” Coolly, Yang Muci put a second round into Yu Xiaojiang. Caught off guard, she took the jolt of the casing without warning and slid to the floor. “Move.” Yang Muci stepped over her body and led the way on.

“Why did you kill the hostage? She could have led us by a shortcut.” Xin Lili hurried to match Muci’s stride. Muci stopped short; they had come again to a crossing of the ways.

“Doesn’t it strike you as against all common sense?” Muci said.

“What does?”

“The nearer we get to the finish, the clearer the road becomes. Knowing the teacher as I do, he would never let us stride so easily right across the ground under his very nose.”

“What is it you’re actually trying to tell me?”

“I’ve worked out a truth of my own: if you’re going to trust no one, then you mustn’t even trust yourself.”

“What do you mean?” Lili was on edge.

“We’ve breached every pass and cut down every guard, struck the camps and taken the strongholds—it’s all gone too smoothly.”

“You mean—we took the wrong road from the very start?”

In Muci’s mind a lightning-flash lit everything up, and all at once it came clear. “You’ve got it. The command post isn’t inside the building—it’s outside. It should be right—right under our noses, over there!” Muci’s keen gaze shot to the infirmary that adjoined the teaching block. “Move…” He turned and ordered the action team split into two squads. One squad would go straight for the roof to plant the flag; he would take the other and drive in depth toward the roof of the infirmary.

“Keep your head,” said Xin Lili.

“My head is uncommonly cool.”

“You’re breaking the rules of the exercise.”

“They’re the unspoken rules, not the truth.”

“Without compass and square you can draw neither circle nor line. Order in a war-game is a must.”

“Disorder is the most effective means of breaking order.” Muci was making ready to give the order to attack.

“And if you’re wrong? What then?”

Muci gave Lili a glance and, feigning deep feeling, declared, “Then I, Yang Muci, shall walk into the sea to make amends to Xin Lili!”

“Better than walking into the sea to atone would be to give me your own self.” Xin Lili slipped aside to let Yang Muci by; Muci raised a hand above his head, crooked his elbow, and laid his palm over the crown of his skull.

“Mind the cover,” Xin Lili commanded her squad, covering Muci’s advance.

Muci, using the steel cable, went down the face of the wall, his limbs spreading open in the wind so that he came at the half-shut window in the attitude of flight. Bracing his feet at a slant against the wall, he peered inside, and he saw Du Luning. Yang Muci turned side-on and, laying his forefinger, middle finger, and ring finger out straight and side by side across his other arm, told his fellow trainees that the commander was right before them.

Du Luning was here, directing his troops.

“Everything on the line—go!” Almost roughly, Xin Lili sprang up all at once and led the squad rushing down from the rooftop. In an instant every gun of the squad was trained on the door of the command post.

Muci plunged in violently from outside the window, and the sheer force of his charge swept through the whole command post. Shattered glass sprayed across the floor for yards.

There was no transition of any kind, not half a second’s hesitation; Yang Muci did not leave Du Luning so much as the space of a blink. In the very moment Du Luning steadied himself, Muci brought the butt of his gun to bear on Du Luning’s face and dealt him one hard, powerful blow.

For a trainee to knock a commander to the ground before every watching eye was, at the school, the first case of its kind. Du Luning heard clearly the mechanical clack of a bolt being drawn, but he could not move at all, could not get back up.

Du Luning had never once lost this regulation kind of exercise, because the infirmary lay outside the bounds of the war-game. And so the command post had no defending force, still less any relief; only a handful of soldiers were on hand, and the squad that had broken in through the door had already shot them into sieves.

Yang Muci had won.

On a still afternoon steeped in the cloying, vulgar sweetness of osmanthus, in a quiet and secretive study, Young Tang was giving Rong Chu a lesson, the gist of it being how a man goes about conquering the love of women. Outside the study, beneath the trellis, light and shadow flickered wondrously over two figures: in the gentle, mild glow Yang Muchu and Xia Yuechun strolled and talked, quietly and at length.

Dust-dreams and cloud-smoke: it was as if, just now, they had not all dispersed but were crowding in thick and fast, like the scent of osmanthus, the cloying deepening the longer it lasted. Xia Yuechun found it quite unexpected that Yang Muchu should engage Young Tang as Rong Chu’s private tutor; and though he neither understood it nor pressed into it, he did wonder how, given Young Tang’s temper, the man had ever agreed to so “peculiar” a request from A-Chu.

“How did you manage it?” Xia Yuechun asked.

“You mean Young Tang? I played him at show-hand, and he lost.”

Just then, from the study window, came Young Tang’s voice, mannered and prettified. “To seek love is the fairest pursuit in the spiritual world of mankind, brimming over, haloed in a sacred light. To seek desire is a need of the body’s own instinct. Sweet and harmonious love-making can steep body and soul in rapture, a bliss close to death. As a man, one must know how to cultivate those rare, fresh flowers and grasses of passion that carry the fragrance of a virgin. Herein lies the very essence of desire.”

Through the window, Yang Muchu smiled at Xia Yuechun and said, “Young Tang’s greatest gift is that he can take the foulest, most unlistenable filth and tint it, cleanse it into the plain, natural colors of honest simplicity, and then raise it higher still, into something civilized and refined, flawless as unblemished jade.”

“And here I always thought the two of you were sworn enemies,” said Xia Yuechun. “What makes Young Tang Young Tang is that he isn’t merely a scoundrel skilled at toying with women—he passes, too, for a lover of the multitude.”

“There are many kinds of women, too,” said Young Tang. “One kind is intoxicated by the wealth and power a man possesses—though, granted, some also admire his talent, or his looks; they’ll fall for some noble young gentleman, some film star, and so on and so forth. Those we can make use of. We use their vanity and their itch to possess, to draw off their flowerlike youth and their watery tenderness, and we needn’t shoulder the least responsibility for it.” Young Tang drew a deep breath; his craving was coming on.

“But isn’t that utterly shameless?” said Rong Chu.

“Shameless, you say? Perhaps you’re right. Still, the blending of money and beauty is no union of soul and flesh—it is not, absolutely not.” Young Tang blew his gushing nose and went on: “Mind your bearing toward a woman. Neither too attentive nor cold as ice; you must be just so, exactly so—now near, now far. What a woman likes best is to hear a man praise her looks; the pity is that a woman of brocade wit and embroidered speech is, more often than not, plain of face, while a beautiful woman, more often than not, never wins a man’s true love.”

“Why is that?”

“Because men are wanting in confidence.” Young Tang’s smile was sly. “Remember this: a man always carries something of the beast in him, and a woman, as a rule, something of the demon.”

“That much I know.” Rong Chu had at last found an opening to air his learning.

“And what do you know?”

Rong Chu began to hum, low: “It was that one turn of her autumn eyes as she went that drove Scholar Zhang out of his wits.”

“What’s that you’re singing?”

“The Romance of the Western Chamber—the tanci, of course. Ow!” Young Tang had, it seemed, just given Rong Chu a “lesson.”

“How many times have I told you—not tanci at every turn of your mouth! You are a ‘new aristocrat, raised in the winds of Europe and the rains of America’! Every word you speak invokes Greece, every verse you quote comes from Shakespeare! You are a seed of universal love—understand?”

“Are you so universal in your love, then? Might your love lead to the tragedy of one seduced and then cast off?” Rong Chu asked.

“Such things do happen, in point of fact.”

Yang Muchu and Xia Yuechun could not help a laugh.

“He does not, at least, want for a man’s dash,” said Xia Yuechun.

“Well then, is there some old love you can’t forget?” Rong Chu pressed on.

“An old love? None. If one must be heartless—since ancient times it is the heartless who move us most.”

“Those are words spoken against his own heart,” said Xia Yuechun, with something clearly in mind.

“A wild goose’s claw leaves its mark in the snow—I dare say there is one,” said Yang Muchu.

Xia Yuechun sighed with feeling. “Young Tang has soaked his whole life in women, in old books, in the gambling table. His love spreads so wide it spills into wantonness, into no love at all. I don’t understand—what is it you want your nephew to learn from such a man?”

“In a short time I mean to train him into an aristocrat—privileged, respected, well-bred, civilized, and pleasing to women,” said Yang Muchu. “Let us go and sit in the drawing room.”

Side by side they walked on along the secluded path paved with pebbles.

“Do you know, you’ve become a figure like a ‘riddle’ on the Shanghai Bund now,” said Xia Yuechun.

“Is that so?” Yang Muchu smiled faintly. “There’s a Western proverb: there is nothing new under the sun. I have no wish to pin on myself the label of one who plays at ghosts and gods; only a man fearful or faint of heart at the core does that. I can hardly wait to step out and stand in the sunlight! I long for the day when I will tell all the world who I am.”

In Shanghai, at the door of a bustling department store, amid the ceaseless stream of carriages and horses, Ronghua came out from her shopping in a lotus-root-colored qipao. At the entrance she bought an English-language copy of the Shanghai Current Affairs Daily, then boarded a tram and was gone.

On the tram, Ronghua leafed idly through the paper; turning to the advertisements, she tilted her head back with a touch of disappointment—the classifieds gave her, as ever, a pale, mocking smile.

Just then, in the Crown Hotel in Hangzhou, an immaculately dressed Yang Muci ran a finger down the “newspaper rack” set out free for the guests, then carelessly drew out an English-language Shanghai Current Affairs Daily that scarcely anyone ever gave a glance. Humming a popular little tune, he made for the golden staircase.

Yang Muci and Xin Lili had checked into this hotel the evening before. After half a year of brutal training, they had at last graduated with distinction.

When they left the school, each was handed a large envelope; Yu Xiaojiang told them it held an important document, and that they were to deliver it, on time, to the address written on the envelope. On handing it over, they would each receive a new work permit and a graduation certificate.

The envelope was to be carried on the person at all times, neither lost nor opened; otherwise they would be dealt with under military law.

Since the addresses on the envelopes were the same, Yang Muci and Xin Lili had agreed to travel together. They had taken, respectively, Room 26 and Room 15 of the hotel—positioned at either end, answering each other head to tail.

Free of his shackles, Yang Muci, light in body and spirit, had come back to a world that was his own.

He went into his room and drew back the curtains. All at once he sensed something amiss in the air of the place. It was pure sixth sense.

He heard water pouring down in the washroom…

He shoved the door open.

Xin Lili was bathing in the half-translucent shower, the streams of water sliding down into beautiful spiraling curves that wound about the twin peaks; all unknowing, Yang Muci had blundered into a world where water and milk ran together. He gave an involuntary cry, muffled in his throat. Xin Lili’s loud shriek masked the complicated smile mixed into her face.

“Sorry.” Muci pulled the door shut at once.

But there was no shutting in a heart so full of spring: the door was pulled open again by Xin Lili.

In truth, from the instant Yang Muci had opened the door, a delicate, gorgeous flower had risen up out of the mist; the dripping spring waters flaunted a “softness” over her whole body, temptation rippling out an ambiguous savor of feeling. That his partner should “unexpectedly” bestow such a favor upon him meant that something, surely, was afoot.

She stood there, and Muci felt danger lying in wait on every side.

Lust and the will to kill flowed together and merged.

Muci quickly drew the curtains.

The sensual blood seethed and burned in Muci’s veins; yet Muci knew, clear-headed, that if he did not act now his body would soon be boiled down into the thin gruel of “carnal lust.”

“Lili, restrain yourself a little.”

“You’re telling me? To restrain myself?”

“Yes—and me too, of course; both of us, restrain ourselves a little.” Backing away without meaning to, Muci warned her: “You don’t need to put on an act. I know someone put you up to this. We’ll come to grief.”

“Of course something’s going to ‘happen’—it’s not as if we’re breaking any vows, so what are you afraid of? We’ve been lonely the better part of a year; oughtn’t we let ourselves go, body and soul, and enjoy a little of life? You’re not going to tell me you’ve never once accepted a woman’s love?” Tenderly, Lili came toward him.

“Wait a moment,” said Yang Muci.

“I’m waiting.” Lili smiled, all radiance.

“It’s wrong. Lili, we’ve both got it wrong. It isn’t one person’s mistake. Both of us have it entirely wrong.” Understanding came to Muci all at once. “This is a trick he never tires of playing.”

“Who do you mean? What are you saying?”

“We’ve walked straight into a snare, and the man who set it is Du Luning. Neither of us has graduated—we’re still under examination. Tell me honestly: what happened? Did someone telephone you? Or bring you fresh orders?”

“How did you know?”

“From your face. Your own expression tells me you don’t in the least want to ‘do it’ just now. Look at yourself in the mirror—it was the unlit weariness on your face that gave me the grounds for my doubt, and the answer to it.” Muci tossed her a garment, then turned his back. “Put your clothes on. ‘Love’ should come out of love, not out of orders.”

Xin Lili dressed and put on her shoes.

“Ready?” Muci asked.

“Ready,” Lili said. “And here I thought my body held no attraction for you at all.”

“Enough of that. I’m a man.” Muci turned back and opened the curtains a sliver. “A moment ago—were you meaning to ‘kill’ me? And once you’d ‘killed’ me, you thought you’d graduate free and clear? Foolish!”

“Five minutes ago someone telephoned me and ordered me to ‘seduce’ you and then ‘finish you off’; then I’d graduate and be on my way, and you’d be sent back to the school to train all over again.”

“You’ve done right by me, I suppose.”

“How on earth did you see through my act?”

“Your passion wasn’t enough.”

“You’re deliberately running down my skills, is that it?”

“I’ll tell you—I saw through your disguise thanks to knowing you as I do, day in and day out. Had it been anyone else today, you’d certainly have succeeded.”

“Comforting me?”

“Complimenting you.”

Muci took the envelope from inside his clothes—the so-called “Party-State secret.” He made ready to open it.

“You’re mad.” Lili moved to stop him. “We’ll be dealt with under military law,” she said.

“The whole direction of our thinking has been wrong from the start. Think: if it’s one secret document, why issue two envelopes? And if we’ve been ordered to go and collect our certificates, why shadow us the whole way, and then arrange for you to ‘kill’ me? If we’ve already graduated, why go on testing us? Wrong! They laid the trap in advance, and I’ll wager that whether or not you’d succeeded today, the two of us would have been marched back and made to ‘take the lesson over again.’ Our success in the exercise, against the ‘rules,’ was only one facet of the wit you and I were showing off. It was no more than a catalyst to build up our absolute self-confidence—nothing more. Understand? And Du Luning is simply waiting for us to lose our heads with triumph and dig our own graves.”

“Perhaps your judgment is right. Through this stretch of testing we’ve won victory after unbroken victory. Yet the path we walked was broken all along—we’ve toiled forward through suspicion, through one snare after another.”

“Those broken paths have already offered us some constructive counsel.” Without the least hesitation Yang Muci tore the letter open, and his face went instantly iron-gray. “Beyond all doubt!”

Xin Lili took the letter and read; a single line stood on it in small characters: On November 2nd, at two thirty in the afternoon, report to my office promptly to collect your graduation certificate. Fail to arrive in time, and the consequences are yours to bear. Du Luning.

The two of them looked at their watches at the same moment.

It was now November 2nd, eleven twenty in the morning.

What were they to do?

From the city of Hangzhou to the school out in the countryside, the drive alone took more than three hours—to say nothing of a stretch of mountain to climb on foot. If they gave up now and conceded defeat, the better part of a year’s special training would go for nothing.

“Run!” Muci seized Lili and tore out the door.

The two of them swept downward like the wind.

In the car park of the Crown Hotel in Hangzhou, Xin Lili stood seductively at the door of the “attendant’s booth,” laughing and chatting with the guard. Muci, crouching low and turning sideways, slipped in among the cars ranged side by side.

He heard a faint clicking at a lock—it was a “car thief,” and his sudden appearance gave the “thief” no small fright. “You—?”

Muci laid a forefinger to his lips. “Shh…”

The “car thief” grinned. “In the same line of work, eh?”

“An amateur.” Muci made a show of modesty. He fished a hairpin from his pocket.

“What good is that? Here, borrow my tools.” The “car thief” was eager to lend a hand.

Without a word, Muci slid the hairpin into the keyhole, opened the door with the greatest ease, and swiftly slipped inside and started the engine.

The “car thief” was floored with admiration. He sidled over in a rush and asked, “How’d you do that?”

“A professional!” Muci said with a laugh, and drove the car away.

Seeing the car come from a distance, Xin Lili hurriedly blew the guard a “kiss” and dashed off; without the engine ever cutting out, the door swung open, Lili flung herself in like a shot, the door slammed to, and they raced away.

The impression A-Chu made on Han Zhengqi was of a man of few words.

Especially at the dinner table.

Most of the time A-Chu did not speak; when now and then he was in good humor, it was only to force out a few words of courtesy. Yet Han Zhengqi knew very well that A-Chu harbored a myriad ravines and gorges in his breast, and that the silent mountains could not mask the naked edge that showed on his face.

“There are those who, failing to kill me, would like to drive me out.” A-Chu tossed a copy of the Evening News onto the table.

Han Zhengqi glanced without thinking at the headlines: A COUPLE, RUINED SPECULATING IN SHARES, LEAP INTO THE SEA; THE ROSE BALLROOM CROWNS ITS MOST POPULAR DANCE HOSTESS; RONG FAMILY’S BASTARD SON LENDS AT USURY, BRINGING DISASTER AND DEATH UPON HIS FOSTER-MOTHER; and so on.

Rong Chu reached out and drew the paper in front of himself, nearly upsetting the soup bowl. A-Chu set down the silver spoon he had been eating with and fixed him with a cool stare, and Rong Chu, cowering, gently put the paper back where it had lain.

“Which company has the Yang family’s bank worked with longest? Most closely?” A-Chu asked.

Han Zhengqi answered, “The Dongyang Company.”

“How strong is this company?”

“Very strong. The Dongyang Company’s total annual sales run extremely high.”

“How much?”

“Around seven or eight hundred million fabi.”

“The Dongyang Company? Run by the Japanese?”

“Yes.”

“Traitors! Thieves within the house!” A-Chu struck the table with all his strength; plates, dishes, knives and forks, the soup spoons, all shook in rhythm, and both Han Zhengqi and Rong Chu stopped eating.

A-Chu leaned back against the broad rosewood chair. “Japan is poor in resources; even a fool can see how they covet the heart of our China. To work hand in glove with them for years on end—if that is not treason, what is it? The Yangs would sell their own ancestors!” His eyes threw off sparks, and the sparks fell hushing the whole room to gravity. “Ever since the September 18th Incident, the Japanese have occupied our Three Eastern Provinces; the nation’s will to resist runs high, and the clamor to boycott Japanese goods has never subsided—so how is it that the Dongyang Company still turns in such returns?”

“They bribe the men who matter in government, corner the market, and when goods are scarce and prized they unload their shares at high prices; by every trick under the sun they win men’s hearts, until in the end all the rivers run to the sea and they work their economic miracle.”

“I don’t understand economics,” said A-Chu. “But those who hold the lifeblood of the economy in their grip—these speculating merchants, these muddled officials, these comprador capitalists—they don’t necessarily understand it either. The national economy above all, and the international one.”

“And your meaning, Xiansheng?” Han Zhengqi probed at A-Chu’s intent.

“Much as I disapprove of grasping for quick gain, I want too badly to build my own economic kingdom in a short time. Analyzing the enemy matters, no doubt; but getting into the real fight as soon as we can matters more.”

“On that point, Xiansheng, you and I are of one mind without prior counsel.”

“The return on investment in industry, commerce, and finance is too low just now, and the rate of capital formation is low as well; to look to investment for our first bucket of gold would be, beyond question, a tale out of the Arabian Nights. We must recombine a golden triangle. You must understand—even with capital and men all to hand, a poor combination serves no purpose. To gain twice the result for half the effort, one must take the shortcut. I need a ‘visible man,’ standing under the mercury-vapor lamps, to hide the ‘invisible hand’ behind the scenes.”

“I am that ‘visible man’ under the mercury lamps,” Rong Chu volunteered.

A-Chu gave a faint, scornful smile and said to Han Zhengqi, “You see? The fish has leapt the dragon gate and fancies itself worth a hundredfold.” His unhurried manner only left Rong Chu somewhat embarrassed and afraid; the boy stole a flustered glance at A-Chu’s expression, found something unkind in it, and dared not make any rash move.

“He is too green. To make him my right arm and left is not realistic.”

“Then—what of me?” Han Zhengqi asked, plainly hoping to smooth the mood a little.

“You tell me.” Not only did A-Chu refuse the overture, his manner turned severe. Han Zhengqi, a man of guarded depths, gave a small smile, and A-Chu softened somewhat and added, “A man content to serve out hard labor for the sake of an ‘old love’?”

Han Zhengqi’s very soul trembled in secret; he had felt all along that there was more in A-Chu’s words than the words. And sure enough, A-Chu came to the point: “I hear Han Yu has been transferred to the customs house?”

One of the chopsticks fell from Han Zhengqi’s hand; but he did not move, did not stoop to pick it up.

“Are you not perhaps fretting too much?” A-Chu lowered his head and wiped his hands on a spare napkin, not so much as looking at Han Zhengqi’s face—which chilled Han Zhengqi’s heart all the more.

“Should our society split and fall apart over some clash of persons, I would not be in the least surprised. The society’s interests must be safeguarded and built up; what you need is faith in me, not wariness of me. And I, for my part, need to know what is in your hearts, not to guess at it—I have neither the time nor the strength to waste on keeping up relations between people. Of course, if you have acted so because you sense some coming danger that will fall on your children and grandchildren, and you are frightened, and at a loss—then I can understand you.” A-Chu’s voice was uncommonly gentle, and yet Han Zhengqi’s heart was filled with dread.

“I have only this one child, Xiansheng,” he said, his words pale and utterly without force.

“I know,” said A-Chu.

“If—if anything should happen, I beg you, Xiansheng, spare him.”

“What could happen? While I am here, I will let nothing come to pass that works against us!” A-Chu rose to his feet.

“Some things a man does because he has no choice.”

“I too do what I do because I have no choice.” A-Chu let fall this weighted remark and walked out of the drawing room.

He left Han Zhengqi and Rong Chu each nursing his own thoughts. Han Zhengqi felt wave upon wave of bewilderment and scorching anxiety gnawing at his heart; he needed courage to face the errors of the past, and needed still more to bear the torment of conscience and press stubbornly on.

He prayed to the spirit in heaven of the woman he had once loved to leave one side of the net open for him; they had, after all, once loved each other.

“To cherish one’s child—how does that make a man less a man?” A-Chu said, gazing at the bird’s nest beyond the laboratory window.

“Muttering away to yourself—what are you droning on about? Come and look at this,” said Xia Yuechun. “It’s TNT.”

“TNT?” A-Chu reached out and rubbed the powder on the table between his fingers. It was all evidence he had gathered from the site of the explosion; Xia Yuechun had gone to the trouble of engaging a doctor of chemistry, lately returned from study in England, to make the analysis. A-Chu had no wish to wait for the police bureau’s test report; he trusted only himself, and saw to his own affairs himself.

“The Germans invented it,” said Xia Yuechun. “TNT is a high explosive; its substance is trinitrotoluene, a pale-yellow powder got by acting on toluene with concentrated nitric and sulfuric acid. Need I go on explaining?”

“As I recall, there was no cardboard box or anything of the sort in my consulting room—so where could the explosive have been placed?”

“It takes up little space—a package the size of a medicine bundle would be enough. Set off by a single detonator, in one hundred-thousandth of a second it can swell to tens of thousands of times its own volume. The instant it explodes, TNT can generate several hundred thousand atmospheres of pressure, enough to shatter mountain rock and the stoutest roof-beams.”

“Made by the Germans? As I recall, ‘gunpowder’ was first invented by us Chinese.”

“Just so. Our forefathers invented gunpowder back in the Han dynasty—some two thousand years ago and more. In the Song it passed to Europe. Foreigners refined it with great care and turned the technology to war; they invented firearms and munitions. With stout ships and heavy guns, it was on ‘science and technology’ that the Eight-Nation Alliance stormed and took Peking—the barbarous plunder, the bloody slaughter… while all we knew to do with it was make firecrackers.”

“Western Europe was no better, you know. Do you know what they first invented the steam engine for? They used it to work song-and-dance amusements.”

The two of them laughed together.

“Still, an explosive of this kind should be a rare thing among ordinary people,” A-Chu concluded.

“Right—it’s mostly for military use.”

The military? A-Chu turned it over again.

“And the police bureau still hasn’t turned up any lead worth having?”

“Han Zhengqi has a guilty secret,” A-Chu said.

“Surely not? Aren’t the two of you working together? If the man can’t be relied on, better you part ways sooner than later.” Xia Yuechun turned on the tap and washed his hands.

A-Chu was fiddling with the miniature rockery and pinks on the windowsill; small as the potted landscape was, it too was a work finely carved and painstakingly wrought.

“Feilai Peak is the work of nature, and the pyramids the work of men. Yet in one thing they are alike: both are raised up out of heaped stone. Each has its own wonder, each its proper place, each its own achievement—it all depends on how you set the stones,” said A-Chu.

“However you set them, if the Way is not the same, men take no counsel together.” Xia Yuechun plainly disapproved of the risk A-Chu was running.

“No—when the Way is not the same and yet men take counsel together, that is where the excitement lies. Just like these ‘cold hills and lean stones.’ Whether it comes out the uncanny work of ghosts and gods or the singular design of a master craftsman depends still on our own skill in patching and boring through.”

“I can’t out-talk you,” said Xia Yuechun.

“It isn’t that you can’t out-talk me—it’s that the wonder of it is hard to tell to another.” A-Chu laughed, well pleased with himself.

“There’s too much innuendo in that. Surely you don’t—?” Xia Yuechun grew suddenly tense. “You’re not thinking of using Han Yu, are you? I warn you—one thing is one thing and another is another; be a little more decent in your dealings.”

“What are you getting so worked up about?”

“Han Yu is my friend.”

“So I see. If even a friend feels so, how much more the bond of nature between father and son!”

On the rugged mountain road, Yang Muci and Xin Lili were forced to abandon the car, for the way ahead was too treacherous. They dared not risk cutting through the woods, and so chose to go forward along the plank gallery. Their pace was swift as the wind; the two of them sprang and leapt along the planks, adjusting each other’s bearings, working in unspoken accord, their movements clean.

The branching trunks that grew out aslant from the plank gallery gave off a bitter, hidden fragrance; the moist air soaked through to the very spirits of the two of them. They did not speak to each other, only repeated the same dull motions over and over, until at last the two of them climbed up onto a sheer cliff.

To snatch back time, they had chosen the one shortcut there was, and it was every bit as perilous. Climb across the sheer cliff, and on the far side lay the school’s parade ground; yet victory, near enough to touch, also bred the cold dread of a single misstep into a bottomless abyss.

It was a natural barrier all but impossible to cross.

Yang Muci made ready for the climb: with a strip of cloth he bound the haft of a bayonet to his hand, then loosed from his waist a three-pronged steel grapnel. Fixed below the grapnel was a rope plaited from strands of hair—a specially treated hair-rope that could bear a load of five hundred catties. Muci took aim at a hard, sturdy tree at the top of the cliff, stepped back a few paces, and with a whoosh planted the three-pronged grapnel firmly in the unyielding trunk.

Muci looped the rope around himself and jumped in place to warm up; when all was ready, he asked Lili, “Care to bet?”

Just then Lili suddenly crouched down, looking at the lay of the land, looking at Muci.

“Make your choice,” Muci said. “There’s no time left.”

Lili stood and pressed herself close against Muci’s chest. “My life is yours,” she said.

“Come on!” Muci heaved his whole body upward; Lili’s hands and feet locked in a death-grip on his shoulders and waist, temple pressed to temple, and the bodies of the two of them hung suspended in a cleft in the sheer rock face. Muci’s bayonet bit fast into the crevice, and by its leverage he rose. Muci’s eyes looked up, looked ahead; Lili’s eyes looked down, looked below. Endlessly adjusting their posture, they struggled upward.

Lili’s face and Muci’s face drew closer and closer, until she could all but count the beads of sweat seeping out at the edge of his brow; unable to help herself, she put her lips to Muci’s ear and said, “I love you.”

Her smile carried the sweetness of first love, and her feeling, in a subtle stir of emotion, quietly overflowed its banks. Having spoken those three words, she felt her whole body grow light, as though in an instant she had set down some enormous burden of the spirit; she felt herself a free and lovely little bird, resting now in the embrace of a great eagle.

Setting his foot on a solid seam of rock, Muci said, “Love must be willed on both sides.”

“No—love is my own affair, and mine alone to decide,” said Lili.

Muci smiled and neither agreed nor refused.

“I’ll take that as a yes,” Lili said.

“Let’s get up there alive first, then talk.” Muci’s climb had plainly quickened now; Lili, wrapped tight around her “love,” wheeled with him back and forth across the sheer precipice, drifting slowly through the air. Her head against the chest of the man she loved, she held her breath and listened to his uneven breathing, and felt in that moment that happiness had already come down upon her. Not only did Lili look on the bottomless abyss below the cliff as though it were nothing; she even imagined that she and Muci were holding a romantic rite of courtship high in the air.

Before the god of love, the god of death must in the end give ground. And lovers on whom the god of love has looked with favor will never die!

Du Luning had opened the window; a light breeze came in and gave him a slight chill.

Yu Xiaojiang sat at his desk, tapping at a German typewriter. Now and then she stole a glance at the expression on Du Luning’s face. She knew that Du Luning, standing at the window, was not there merely for the view—in that country of barren hills and rough waters, there was nothing to see but a natural screen of blue-green peaks. What Du Luning was watching for was people—his students. His mood just now must be at once anxious and exhilarated.

Whether this towering natural barrier could be turned into a window onto the road to success depended on whether his students’ will held firm and their judgment ran true.

They were both on edge.

For neither of them knew whether, at this very moment, his two finest pupils would come down safe and turn danger into deliverance.

Knowing them too well only made the worry the greater.

Could they graduate cleanly? Or rather—should he give them a pass and let them graduate? In truth, Du Luning too had hesitated again and again. There were two main reasons. The first was that Muci and Lili were, without the least exaggeration, the very elite of intelligence talent. A course others could not master in a year, they grasped the essentials of in two or three months and wielded with perfect ease. To keep them shut up in the school for long would be a waste of resources. What was more, the two of them were of the overbrimming-energy sort, forever running some experiment or other at the school; once they had nearly blown the school library to pieces. The second reason was that the two of them were too strongly attractive to the senses, their looks too pleasing to the eye. In the spy school the boys were forever finding excuses to visit Lili’s room, and the girls clung and fussed to get close to Muci; carried on long enough, Muci and Lili would leave too deep an impression on everyone—no good thing for their later work. As a discipline, intelligence work is in fact something whose true marrow you might not grasp though you spent your whole life at it; it has no fixed, scientifically defined form, and so the form of graduation was kept simple too.

Just then the clock pointed to twenty-four minutes past two in the afternoon.

Du Luning said, “One minute left on the clock…”

At that moment, heavy footsteps sounded in the corridor.

“They’re here.” A happy glow broke over Yu Xiaojiang’s face.

“You’re very pleased,” Du Luning teased.

Two twenty-five. The door was flung violently open…

Spent to the last of their strength, the two of them fell flat on their backs to the floor. Muci’s sweat had soaked through his clothes, and Lili’s hair too was dripping wet; Du Luning scarcely knew whether to laugh or cry.

“Congratulations, congratulations to you both—you’ve crawled back at last.” As Du Luning spoke, he reached out a hand to pull Muci up; Muci rose with the pull, and Lili too, panting, steadied herself on her feet and combed her hair with her fingers.

“Where did you come up from?” Du Luning asked.

“Down out of the sky,” said Muci.

“What a pity. Lili, I really can’t tell whether your charms fell short or his self-command was simply too strong.” Du Luning regarded his two favorite pupils with keen amusement.

“Teacher, how do you know we didn’t do anything?” Muci began, provoking him as was his habit.

“Well then—what did you do?”

“Everything that ought to be done, we did.”

“Oh? Then where was the time to rush back?”

“Haven’t you heard, Teacher, of a swift fight for a swift finish?” Muci said, standing to attention.

At Yang Muci’s faintly ironic challenge, Yu Xiaojiang could not hold back a laugh.

“Not to abandon, not to doubt, to pledge one’s very life—that is rare and precious, to be sure. But if—and I only say if—it should cause you to let slip the moment for battle, then the loss would outweigh the gain,” said Du Luning.

“We won, Teacher. That’s the point that matters.”

“You beat your teacher, did you? Is that it?” Du Luning began to needle.

“I didn’t say that. The teacher said it himself.”

“In this life one must look far and high, not have the short sight of a mouse. This time, count yourselves lucky. From here on, we’ll see whether your luck holds so good. Congratulations—you have formally graduated.” The luster came back into Du Luning’s face. “In days to come, on the field of battle, you will gallop free and wide, going only where your will inclines and as your will directs. Forget everything here as fast as you can; from this moment on, you have no further tie to this place. And we have never met—understood?”

“Yes, Teacher.” Muci and Lili answered aloud.

Before long they had their graduation certificates in hand.

Around three o’clock in the afternoon they formally left the school. No one saw them off; the two of them walked out through the iron gate in silence, and hearing the heavy clang of the iron chain lowered behind them, both felt a strange thing stir in their hearts. Muci was to report at once to the Central Shanghai Garrison Command, and Lili would go to the “White Rose” ballroom to work as a dance hostess.

A military car was waiting for Muci; in his trim uniform, Muci said his farewell to Lili.

“I’m off,” said Muci.

“Don’t go.” Lili suddenly rushed at him and threw her arms around his head.

“We’ll meet again.” Muci had no wish to provoke her.

“Will you come to the ballroom to see me?” Lili asked.

“I will! I certainly will!” Muci said. “Only you’ll have to give me a discount on the dance tickets—I haven’t got that kind of money.”

Lili laughed.

“So you really do take me for a dance hostess.”

“A dance hostess is a fine thing to be. When a man is low, he can go there and seek some comfort for his spirit—at least he won’t be keeping a mistress behind his wife’s back. And a ballroom, as an environment, belongs to the smokeless industries: it props up morality and rescues the economy at once.”

“Oh, get away with you!”

The military car’s horn blared on.

“I’m off.” Muci waved to Lili with an easy grace; Lili chased after him and kissed him hard. Her movement was too quick, without the least warning. Muci’s chest shook, and even as his hands made a gesture of refusal he passively received Lili’s kiss. Their warm breath, rippling and swaying, melted open at the very tips of their two hearts and turned to a tenderness soft as water. The hem of Lili’s dress fluttered up in the wind, and Muci’s cap fell away down into the valley below…

The military car’s horn blared with all its might.

On March 20th, 1932, an inconspicuous notice of a missing person ran in the advertisements of the English-language Shanghai Current Affairs Daily, the gist of it as follows:

On the 19th, a small boy went missing along the walk of the “Rose Garden” in the French Park, wearing a white jacket and black leather shoes. Anyone knowing the boy’s whereabouts is asked to contact Mr. Lin Tan at once; a handsome reward is offered.

In the French Park the green pressed vivid on the eye, and the breath of flowers came at one in waves. On a bench along the walk of the “Rose Garden,” Ronghua sat waiting for the coming of “Drifting Wind.”

A little girl got up like a foreign doll came running over; she wore a flowered skirt and a butterfly bow in her hair, and carried a rose in her hand, a rose that smelled very sweet. She ran up and sat down beside Ronghua.

She pinched at the flower’s stem with her fingers.

“Little one, mind—the flower has thorns,” said Ronghua.

With her plump little hands the girl plucked the petals off, cupped them in her palm, and, pursing her small mouth, blew across her hand; the petals rose lazily into the air, and the little girl went chasing after them, laughing merrily.

The little girl was very like Rongrong in those years gone by.

To Ronghua these fluttering scraps of petal seemed like the gaudy, lovely years of her life drifting past the strands of her hair. As it happened, one fallen petal, holding a cool fragrance, came to rest on the toe of her high-heeled shoe, breaking off short her nostalgic reverie—as though some sixth sense were telling her in advance that the one she waited for had already come.

And the man had indeed come.

In a white Western suit and black leather shoes, an English-language Shanghai Current Affairs Daily in his hand, Yang Muci came slowly up the flowered path. He was for all the world some idle young gentleman of leisure, loafing as he waited for his little sweetheart.

Muci’s cover identity now was that of a major and adjutant in the Detective Division of the Central Shanghai Garrison Command.

Ronghua and Muci caught sight of each other at almost the same instant—for each held a newspaper in hand.

Suddenly seeing “A-Chu,” Ronghua felt at once that something was “amiss.” She could neither stay nor go. A-Ci, meanwhile, his manner composed, was watching her from a distance.

Muci pretended to watch a butterfly flitting over the flowers, then in a flash shifted his gaze back to Ronghua, a faint trace of a smile about him, waiting to see what she would do next.

But Ronghua did not want “A-Chu” to catch sight of “her own comrade” and interfere, later on, with her work. So she rose, folded the newspaper in her hand, put it into her small shoulder bag, and left the bench.

Her behavior left Muci dumbfounded. What was this about? Could there be a tail behind him? On edge, Muci swept the surroundings quickly, and once he had made sure no one was following, he sat down on the bench to wait for “Timely Rain.”

Watching from a hidden spot, Ronghua saw “A-Chu” stubbornly refuse to leave, and had no choice but to “give up” the rendezvous.

On March 25th, 1932, a small item ran in the advertisements of the English-language Shanghai Current Affairs Daily, the gist of it as follows:

The Forest Skating Club will hold a birthday celebration for Mr. Tan on March 26th at the “Milan” Café on Yufo Temple Road; all good friends are asked to attend. Signed: the Forest Skating Club.

The doors and windows of the “Milan” Café were all set with single sheets of plate glass. Ronghua arrived very early and laid that day’s Shanghai Current Affairs Daily on the table. One hand propping her cheek, she ran her eyes swiftly left and right.

Ronghua sat by the window; the great spotless panes had a fine transparency to them, so that the street opposite and the passersby coming and going showed clear through the glass at a single glance.

Again she saw “A-Chu”; and this time she was full of alarmed doubt, and went dazed, and sat without stirring.

Gripping the Shanghai Current Affairs Daily in his hand, Muci pushed through the door with a light step; once again he saw Ronghua, and, of course, he saw the newspaper too.

He gave Ronghua a courteous smile and inclined his head; almost mechanically Ronghua responded to Muci. She had the feeling that this man was not A-Chu—had A-Chu seen her, he would never have kept up so reserved a manner. A-Chu’s manner was gentle and mild.

Through the café drifted a low, languorous love song.

He and she—their eyes held each other, locked, for several seconds.

He was certain: this was the person he was looking for.

She was certain: this person was, “beyond all real doubt,” not A-Chu.

The two of them, perfect strangers, seemed in an instant to have become a man and a woman about to “talk of love”; and everything turned lovely and still.

A waiter came up and asked, “Sir, have you reserved a table?”

Half joking, Yang Muci said, “Reserved or not depends on whether the young lady is in a good mood.” He walked over toward her; Ronghua still did not move.

Calm and self-possessed, he sat down across from her and said, “Are you a friend of Mr. Lin Tan’s?”

“Lao Yu asked me to send you his regards.” A trace of a smile spread across Ronghua’s face. “Welcome home.” Ronghua’s voice was lovely, giving off faintly a quiet, graceful charm, so full of warmth that Muci felt it uncommonly dear and near.

They began to come to know each other, in the very season when the rain of blossoms flew everywhere.
