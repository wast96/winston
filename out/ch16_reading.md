## Mount Kannabi

Over the upper reaches of the Kizu-gawa hung a new moon, thin as a paring of wood. It was past the hour of the sixth bell, most likely; the wind had gone entirely dead.

"That sunset a while back was a sparrow, surely?"

Someone in the band, pushing forward through the grass, whispered it to the man beside him.

"No. Indigo, that was."

The answer came sour. And it was true: for so clear a day, the sunset had not stained the sky red. So the evening air had never gone sparrow-brown; the indigo had only thickened, deepening toward black, and slid on into night. A ninja loves the sparrow-brown of dusk and holds indigo for an ill sign. Sparrow or indigo, the difference was no more than a matter of moisture in the air, most likely. But they knew, from long years of it, that this same difference could sharpen or blunt the wariness of the ordinary folk now settling to their night's rest.

"Never mind. Tonight it's no mansion we break into. We're bound for a dead village without so much as a stray kitten in it. Sparrow or indigo, it'll make no odds."

said another, to ease him.

The band turned south, away from the windless bank of the Kizu-gawa, threading the little paths between the oak groves that stood scattered here and there, making for the hamlet of Kiwano under Mount Kannabi. Kazama Gohei walked at their head; Kisaru kept close at his side; and strung out behind her, in a loose file, came the Shimotsuge genin.

"Halt."

said Gohei.

"Look ahead there, through the trees."

Through a gap in the trees the hill of Kannabi bulked black against the sky. For once, sounding like the jōnin he was, Gohei went on, unhurried.

"From here the road climbs, gently. Follow it up two chō and you come out at the dead village of Kiwano. Look on beyond that for a level rise: that will be the site of the old Hachiman shrine. There is no one there. Still, we take no chances. — You. Iba no Yokoashi, was it? Run ahead and scout it."

"At your word."

The man called Iba no Yokoashi slipped out of the trees. Gohei ordered the rest to strip off their travelling clothes and change into ninja garb; and when he had swiftly finished dressing himself, he

"Kisaru,"

called.

"What?"

Kisaru lifted her pale chin.

"You'll stay here. The men can see to the work."

"Why?"

"Right now there's a woman's scent rising off you."

Gohei said it without looking at her, his eyes on the darkness of Mount Kannabi ahead.

"I don't expect a soul in those ruins, but there's always the one chance in ten thousand. It would be a sorry thing to have us scented out. You never thought to kill your own smell beforehand — that was careless. Better you don't come."

"But —"

Kisaru tucked her chin against her breast and sniffed at herself, like a puppy, and then:

"I don't smell of anything."

"You'd be the last to know it. You've a strong scent to you. The day's sweat on the road has it hanging thick about you. Why hadn't you the wit to wash when we forded the Kizu-gawa? That kind of care is what being a shinobi means."

"That's odd."

"What is?"

"It's only to you I smell, I'll wager. Once this work is done, you said, there's to be a wedding — a first, provisional one. You'll take me to your bed. That's what has you in such a hurry."

"Fool. This is no game."

"Say what you like. I'm only coming along."

Kisaru turned her face away with a flounce. Above the peak of Kannabi the stars glittered, heaped close as dust blown into a drift.

"Do as you please, then."

As Gohei said it, the man Iba no Yokoashi came back.

"A dead village, sure enough. Not one lamp, not one soul to be seen."

"Good."

Gohei nodded.

"We go one at a time. We meet by the stone torii. Kisaru — you keep with me."

They set out one by one, at intervals. Last of all, Gohei took Kisaru with him and left the trees at an easy pace.

"Do I smell?"

"Mm."

"Then hold me and find out."

Even Gohei frowned at the girl's sheer outrageousness. He would not rise to her. They had gone half the way in silence when he stayed the walking Kisaru with a hand at her breast, pointed to the grass at the roadside, and, slipping quietly into it himself, sank into a crouch.

In the darkness before him, Kisaru's scent seemed to stand there in the shape of a woman.

"Are you going to hold me?"

"Still on about that."

"When you want to, all the same."

"Be quiet a while."

Gohei, not looking at her, was straining his ears into the dark. There was something wary in the way he held himself, and Kisaru fell silent of her own accord. Presently, in a low voice, Gohei said:

"You don't hear it?"

"Hear what?"

"A sound."

"Why?"

"Listen well — whether those men of ours haven't been killed."

"Killed? What do you mean?"

Kisaru's eyes went wide. Gohei took it with a cold face, and:

"This too is a guard against the one chance in ten thousand. The man we deal with is Sōkyū of Sakai. He'll never suppose the leak in his plot can be stopped up with gold alone. There is no telling what he may be contriving in a place like this. That is the very reason I asked for the loan of Iga genin."

"I don't follow."

"Suppose Sōkyū means to wipe us out to the last man. Those genin of ours may be dying at this very moment. If so, we would surely catch some sound of it — a ring of blades, the twang of a bowstring, one note at least. That is what I mean when I tell you to strain your ears for a sound."

"I hear nothing."

"Then all is well."

"What a frightening man you are. So when you told me to bring the genin along, it was to use them for live bait?"

"That is what it is to be a shinobi. A heart without pity — that alone is the very life of the shinobi's art, and it was your own father who taught me so. His blood runs in you; a callous heart is yours by birth. Well? You'll not tell me I'm wrong."

Abruptly Gohei reached to take Kisaru where she crouched beside him, but she slipped out of his arm like water, and

"Let's go on."

she said, standing up ahead of him on the dark field-path.

By the time they reached the mouth of the hamlet of Kiwano the moon had already hidden itself among the clouds. A great cypress towered at the entrance to the village, and on its eastern side stood a house of which only the walls and the stone footing had barely survived the ruin. The two passed before it and went in among the houses. Wrecks of rough plaster wall, all that wind and rain had left standing, rose here and there like grave-markers, and from some tree or other, now and again, came the call of an owl.

The two of them turned onto the little path that ran up to the Hachiman shrine. Grass had overgrown the way. The night dew soaked their shinobi tabi.

They came before the torii. The whiteness of its stone was cold in the dark, cold as dead bone.

Kisaru at last spoke the doubt that had been rising in her for some while.

"They are none of them here. What can have happened?"

At that instant, without a word, Gohei shoved her aside.

"Down! Run! We've been played. I'll see you in the capital."

Arrows came slicing through the dark from every quarter, biting into the ground, glancing off the legs of the torii and leaping away.

Flat to the earth, Gohei drew his sword with a flash.

"Run!"

Gohei shouted it once more to Kisaru, who lay sprawled in the grass four or five ken off. At the cry she sprang up and made to flee toward the hillside — and in that instant a black dog burst from the thicket and threw itself onto her shoulders.

Gohei slipped the kozuka from his hilt and sent it flying, driving it deep into the flank of the dog as it went for Kisaru's throat. It came almost in the same breath as his own frantic roll along the ground to dodge a blade that had fallen at him. And so the distance between the two of them naturally widened.

Kisaru flung the dog off and dived into the nearest thicket. But there in the dark a naked blade was waiting, wicked and cold.

"...A woman, is it. She smells sweet."

So the man in black muttered, and swept his shinobi sword across in a flat sidelong stroke. The severed tips of grass flew up like a scatter of powder. But by then Kisaru's body had already drawn back a full two ken, and she raised a bright, silken voice, almost a song.

"Fool, fool. With an arm like that you think you can cut me?"

For Kisaru, no doubt, the cry was meant to plant her position in the enemy's mind. The proof was that her small body had vanished from the spot, as though it had run straight up into the sky. Behind it hung the strong scent of her. The several men in black who rushed the place of the voice on the instant looked at one another. A scent rose there, sure enough, as if a person stood in it. But no one was there.

Gohei cut down one of his attackers, and knew from the man's swordwork that he had Kōga men against him. Not one of them, in sheer skill, was anything near his match. Yet if their blades were the poorer, their coordination as a pack was faultless. Gohei sprang right, ran left, hunting some pool of shadow to lose himself in — and wherever he went, two ninja lay in wait for him without fail. The ambushers had a set way of it: they struck from left and right at once, and one of the pair would raise his blade high and come down in a slanting cut across the body. Against Gohei's single sword it was a cunning thing, one there was no guarding against but flight.

But let him once slip that first pair of strokes, and the win was in his hands. In the shadow-pool of one thicket, resigned to being cut, Gohei drove into the gap between two blades, slashed one man across the wrist and the other across the belly, broke clean through, and ran for all he was worth.

"After him."

said one of them. Their night-eyes seemed as keen as any Iga man's; several of them came at Gohei's back without so much as troubling the sound of their own feet.

As he ran, Gohei hurled the sword in his hand high into the night sky. For a few seconds it hung against the heavens, then arced over and fell to earth at a point altogether elsewhere.

"Ah — over there, is he?"

For a moment his pursuers, their ears drawn off toward the black grass beyond, checked and stopped.

"You lot, get over there."

And so Gohei bought himself time. But it was the only trick left to him: in his hands now, beyond an armor-piercer and a few star-shaped shinobi shuriken, no weapon of defense remained. As a rule he never carried a wakizashi, holding the short sword a useless thing.

Just then, from off to one side: "Ah — Gohei!"

With that low cry a shadow came bursting out.

Gohei knew in an instant it was Kisaru, and, still running, thought: this is bad.

Gohei flung himself flat. In a few seconds more his pursuers would be swarming over the top of him.

But the truth was that in the very instant Kisaru cried "Gohei!" and came bursting out, Gohei had already sold her to his pursuers. She stopped, in a gesture as if she could not trust her own eyes. Gohei had wiped his own figure from her sight. From that moment Kisaru had become his stand-in. And the pursuers, swarming toward her, caught sight of her and —

"Here he is!"

— it was the natural end of it that they should bay like dogs. Gohei had reckoned that far in a heartbeat. Kisaru's body, so to speak, now filled the very office the flung sword had filled a moment before.

That Gohei, hearing his name called by Kisaru, took just that measure to save himself, was the shinobi reflex bred into his body; into it entered no honor, no human feeling, not even a will of his own. It was not his wit. Far from wit — it was only the long-drilled reflex of self-preservation that made Gohei do as he did.

"Don't take her alive. Cut her down, cut her down."

The voices welled up all around Kisaru. She darted this way and that inside the ring of them. Ninja though they were, they could scarcely make out her shape with any clearness; in the dark their eyes strained to hold a small smudge of a thing that drifted softly to and fro. At times it vanished from their sight altogether. But by her ill luck, Kisaru had a scent. The ninja's uncanny sense of smell fixed where she was more surely than any eye.

"There — behind that tree."

From the cover of the grass Gohei's eyes watched, unmoving, as the ring of pursuers drew tighter and tighter around Kisaru.

Kisaru carried no sword. In her palm, gripped hard and gone damp with sweat, lay a single shinobi shuriken, and nothing more.

Now, thought Gohei. Choosing the low ground, he crawled, not stirring a single blade of grass, and inch by inch drew himself away from the pack of butchers gathered by the tree.

"Cut her down."

It was when Gohei heard the band's commander shout the order that he kicked off from the ground and rose.

And without a backward look, he went running down off the high ground of the dead village.

Kisaru, meanwhile, ringed round by their numbers, showed no fear on her pale face. If anything, her lips were parted a little and she looked almost merry. She had settled on one resolve. When you were driven into a corner like this, with no way out, there was a single way of escape. It was a thing her father, Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, had taught her as a child. Kisaru was trying to call it back exactly. And she had it. It was this: to let yourself be cut.

"When it comes to that, let them cut you."

So her father taught. You were fated to be cut in any case. If, by taking the cut deliberately, you happened to lose your life — well, you had lost nothing you would not have lost anyway: such was Jirōzaemon's reasoning. First you fixed your resolve and stilled your mind; then you worked out the manner of the cut. Which part of the body to give them, and how. After that you planned what to do once the blade had landed. In the enemy, the relief of having struck home opens a small gap of an instant. Whether you got out alive, Jirōzaemon taught, was decided by how you used that gap.

Lips just parted, breathing out, Kisaru counted the black shapes that ringed her, one, two. When she had counted to six, that was the last of them.

"Haaah!"

There was no telling which of them gave the cry. As though to crush the shadow of her between them, the six rushed in at once. Kisaru judged it in a flash. She chose one of the Kōga men, the one bearing down on her from the front. She raised her pale left arm. And in the same instant, without a moment's hesitation, she ran toward the blade above her head, like a small child running.

To the enemy her move must have been the last thing looked for. For a heartbeat the man flinched and sprang back, then at once brought his sword up overhead and cut straight down at the pale thing fluttering before his eyes. A wrist flew; blood sprayed. But the very instant of that downstroke became the Kōga man's hell. He dropped his sword, and clapped a hand to his face.

"Ugh —"

He doubled backward and went down. The man had indeed cut off Kisaru's left arm — but he had not been able to stop the shuriken that came flying from her right hand.

The band froze where they stood. Kisaru's reckoning had fallen out exactly. By the time they came to themselves, she was gone.

It was a good quarter of an hour later that Kisaru, at the foot of the Kizu-gawa embankment where the night mist was rising, washed her wound.

Wound was a mild word for it. Her left hand was gone. The bleeding kept trying to carry her senses off with it. She had roused a farmhouse and got a share of their shōchū to wash the wound, then bound it hard with a dry cloth smeared thick with a cut-salve. All the while a pain like a saw grinding through the bone raced round her whole body, and Kisaru, biting her lip, fought to keep down the sobs that kept forcing their way up. When she had finished tending it, she tried to stand. But the blood-loss must already have reached the limit past which she could act; the strength to rise had left her. Kisaru crumpled down among the reeds and, breathing in the damp sweetness of the earth, began to weep, thin and low.

The wound hurt. It was the pain of it, more than any grudge against Gohei, that made her cry like an infant. From that one moment she had known that Kazama Gohei had thrown her away. Yet, strangely, no hatred of him rose in her; rather she felt, hard and pressing, the foolishness of the whole shinobi trade, in a feeling that took in herself and Gohei together. Gohei too, one day, will come to this — the thought had begun to stir somewhere at the back of her mind. So long as he went on as a ninja, his body too was fated, in the end, to become what hers was now. There was no saying the day would never come when he too was hacked to pieces.

(But what of Jūzō…?)

Kisaru broke off her sobbing all at once and opened her eyes. And that man — what of him? Jūzō was a shinobi too. For him as well the day would come, sooner or later, when his hands and feet would be lopped away. But what set him apart from her and from Gohei was this: he was a man who seemed to live for the sake of that very day. A man who had bought, in exchange for his own body, the one object of a lifetime's passion. This same agony ravaging her now would be, for him, even the goal he lived toward — and as Kisaru thought it, the image of Tsuzura Jūzō behind her eyelids began, all at once, to turn hollow and cold. It carried even a touch of loathing. This pain, Jūzō would never understand. The same pain, for that man, would turn to a rapture he offered up to his own god. But Gohei — Gohei would weep, helplessly, just as she was weeping now. It was true that Gohei had betrayed not only his comrades but the woman who loved him. And yet, for that very reason, one might say that Gohei carried the same nerve of pain as she did. Fallen there in the night mist, Kisaru noticed a strange thing: whenever she called up Gohei's face the agony took on an odd sweetness, and whenever she called up Jūzō's, an unpleasant pain shot through her, as if a hand were stroking the open wound.

"But I can never go back now — never back into the world where those two live."

That much at least is certain, Kisaru murmured aloud. For her, there was a thoughtful, quiet weight in the words. It was as if she were telling herself over that this one thing, at least, was sure. Kisaru had been left maimed. As the pain of the wound eased, her resolve about what to do with herself had begun to harden.

"I'll go home."

Kisaru said it to herself. Home to Iga. She would wipe herself out of this world where she had run herself ragged pining after a man.

"— I'll just vanish."

At what, there was no knowing, but Kisaru laughed, a low chuckling laugh. It was an innocent smile, astonishingly free of care. She did not want to show a man her maimed body. Only the pain of the wound at her wrist stayed with her. But apart from that hurting, Kisaru's heart had cleared, bright and blown wide open, like the night sky over the Kizu-gawa. ...Kisaru rose to her feet among the reeds. And when she had crawled up onto the embankment, she set off, trudging step by weary step, toward the Shimotsuge district of Iga.
