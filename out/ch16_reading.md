## Chapter 16. Struggle on the Solitary Island

In November 1937, the Chinese army withdrew from Shanghai along the whole line, and the "Chinese sections" outside the French Concession and the Anglo-American International Settlement, along with the various suburban counties, all became zones occupied by the Japanese army. The two concessions, surrounded on all sides by the Japanese-occupied areas, thus became a "Solitary Island" after the Japanese invaders had seized the great stretch of southeastern coastal China; and only when the Pacific War broke out in December 1941, and the Japanese army marched into and occupied the International Settlement and the French Concession, did the "Solitary Island" at last fall. In this "Solitary Island period" of more than four years, among China, Japan, Britain, the United States, and France, and among the Nationalist, Communist, Japanese, and puppet sides, the contests and struggles of many forces were interwoven, and the Solitary Island's political situation was extremely complex and tangled; yet it was precisely in such a special environment that, under the Party's leadership and influence, the resistance-and-salvation movement of every trade and profession in Shanghai rose to an even greater tide.

In January 1938, the Party Central and Comrade Zhou Enlai sent Liu Shaowen to Shanghai in the capacity of secretary-general of the Eighth Route Army's Shanghai Liaison Office; he conveyed to me that my request to go to Yan'an was not agreed to, and that I was to make full use of my legal identity as Chen Zhigao's wife and as managing director and deputy general manager of the Tongyi Trust Company, and of the advantageous position of the upper-level united-front connections already opened up, to carry on and hold fast to my work in Shanghai, and to bring my own strength and role into play in advancing the surging resistance-and-salvation movement of every circle in Shanghai. And so I could only obey and remain in Shanghai.

After Liu Shaowen came to Shanghai, in his open capacity as secretary-general of the resident "Eighth Route Office," he met with several members of the World and China Society—Li Nanxiang, Chen Gaoyong, and others—and disclosed to them my identity as head of the rescue department, as well as the three achievements, in 1931–1932, of the members of the World and China Society, myself and Chen Zhigao among them: rescuing Comrade Guan Xiangying and getting him released from prison; obtaining information by a stroke of luck and promptly notifying Pan Hannian of Xiang Zhongfa's defection, thereby safeguarding the security of the Party Central's leaders then in Shanghai; and, through Chen Zhigao's commissioning of the lawyer Baho to place openly in *Shen Bao* the "Zhou Shaoshan notice," refuting the rumor on Comrade Zhou Enlai's behalf and shattering the enemy's despicable plot to slander the Party Central's leaders. He also specially, on behalf of Comrade Enlai, thanked and saluted Chen Zhigao! He further brought a letter in Comrade Enlai's own hand. The letter was not long, only a single sheet; its content praised the underground work we had carried on in the white area of Shanghai, and especially the achievements of the rescue work, and encouraged us to hold fast to the position already opened up and to go on working hard for the Party. This letter was passed around and read among the comrades who worked with me and the non-Party friends who cooperated closely with us. On its account, Comrade Cao Liang changed his name to Rubi, expressing that he treasured this handwritten instruction as a precious jade disc.

When Guo Moruo left Shanghai, he handed over to A Ying many of the affairs of the resistance-and-salvation work of Shanghai's cultural world, and even entrusted his own diary to A Ying's keeping. At that time A Ying, in the name of the "Wind and Rain Bookroom," was responsible for editing the *Documents* monthly, a digest-type periodical launched by the "Eighth Route Office" that gathered resistance-and-salvation materials. The masthead title of this *Documents* was in my own handwriting. The periodical brought out its inaugural issue in October 1938, and put out eight issues in all; in its pages it reprinted in great quantity the documents of the Chinese Communist Party and the speeches and reports of the Party Central's leaders, as well as the documents and reports of the Soviet party and government, and it also selected for publication some of the Kuomintang side's statements and activities regarding the resistance, reported regularly each month on the fighting on the various fronts of the resistance and on the anti-Japanese activities of the masses in Wuhan, Chongqing, Guilin, northern Shaanxi, and elsewhere, and comprehensively analyzed and expounded the international situation and the course of the world anti-fascist struggle. This was a weighty resistance-and-salvation periodical that drew much attention from every circle, and was naturally hated by the Japanese and puppet forces. To avoid being wrecked by enemy agents, A Ying and I agreed to hide the "Wind and Rain Bookroom" inside the Tongyi Trust Company. The reason we chose the name "Wind and Rain Bookroom" was that Zhigao had hung in A Ying's room a couplet written by his ancestor, the Grand Secretary Chen of Haining—"On the wind-swept bed I unroll the scrolls; by the rain-swept railing I lie among the flowers"—and had arranged it like the study of an old-style man of letters, so it was called the "Wind and Rain Bookroom," which further conveyed the sense of sharing hardship in the same boat, weathering wind and rain together. Besides publishing *Documents*, the "Wind and Rain Bookroom" had these as its main work:

It printed Mao Zedong's *On Protracted War*.

It published Huang Zhen's paintings made during the Long March, Sketches of the Westward Journey.

It collected materials from all quarters and published A Record of the Major Events of the War of Resistance.

It published various new works by progressive writers, including A Ying's own anti-Japanese play Flower of Noble Blood (which, to cope with censorship, also went under such titles as Sorrow at the Fall of the Ming and Ge Nenniang). Because A Ying was in charge of the "Wind and Rain Bookroom," Yu Ling, drawing on the sense of the phrase "wind and rain, dark as nightfall," devised for A Ying the pen name Wei Ruhui. And A Ying's more popular works, such as The Legend of Fengdong Mountain, were used as cover.

It compiled the Collected Works of Qu Qiubai. Before it could be printed, the materials were kept in the Tongyi Company's storage vault, and it was published after the founding of New China.

It put in order the Diary of Fang Zhimin. Before the editing could be finished, the materials were preserved.

In accordance with a book-purchase list written in Chairman Mao's own hand, it bought a good many thread-bound books, including The Art of War by Sunzi. These were later shipped, together with *On Protracted War*, by way of Chongqing to Yan'an.

Besides being distributed within the Shanghai concessions, the *Documents* monthly was also secretly distributed to the New Fourth Army. Zhigao's fifth-branch niece Chen Yiyu and Li Yi were the underground couriers responsible for taking *Documents* to the New Fourth Army. Later, when A Ying renamed Sketches of the Westward Journey as the Long March Album and republished it, he mentioned the two of them by name in the preface he wrote. Chen Yiyu did outstanding work in the New Fourth Army and later became a political commissar at the army level; sadly, she gave her life in the War of Liberation. The external distribution work of the "Wind and Rain Bookroom" was done by Chen Zhigao, while I was responsible for the related economic expenses, mobilizing the Tongyi Company's manpower, materials, and money to support it—for as deputy general manager of the company I had that authority. Not a few of the company's colleagues were progressive people, such as Guo Yushang, and they all actively supported the enterprise of the "Wind and Rain Bookroom," none of them coming forward openly to claim credit, but making their contributions in silence. During this period A Ying wrote a great many resistance-and-salvation plays, and also kept up a regular correspondence with Mr. Liu Yazi, right up until May 1939, when *Documents*, the Daily Translation Herald, and several other resistance-and-salvation papers and periodicals were forced to cease publication one after another.

At that time, Chinese-language newspapers published within the Shanghai concessions had to submit to the Japanese army's news censorship. In December 1937, in order to evade the Japanese army's news censorship, the Cultural Committee of the CCP Jiangsu Provincial Committee founded a Translation Herald, in the charge of two comrades—Xia Yan and Mei Yu (the Chinese translator of *How the Steel Was Tempered*, who after the founding of New China changed his name to Mei Yi, and served as director of the Central Broadcasting Administration, secretary of the Party group of the Academy of Social Sciences, editor-in-chief of the Encyclopedia of China Publishing House, and a member of the Central Advisory Commission)—which specially translated and published the writings of foreign news agencies and foreign-language papers, reporting news of the international situation and of China's resistance. After the Translation Herald appeared it sold very well, its daily print run soon reaching ten thousand copies; but it put out only twelve issues before being forced to announce its closure on the 20th of that month. There were also several foreign-run newspapers within the concessions that were exempt from the Japanese army's news censorship. So Zhao Banghong—former manager of the Shanghai Evening Post and Mercury, an old friend of Xia Yan's, and manager of the Translation Herald—proposed finding a foreigner to be manager and running another open newspaper. The Cultural Committee then decided, on the basis of the former Translation Herald, to run a Daily Translation Herald with a foreign merchant as publisher; the preparatory work was chiefly in the charge of Comrade Mei Yi, and the main editorial board members were Mei Yi, Yang Fan, Wang Renshu, Dai Pingwan, Jiang Chunfang, Yun Yiqun, Cao Diqiu, Wei Que, and others, many of the editors being Communist Party members. On Zhao Banghong's introduction, they found the principals of the British-owned University Book Company registered in Hong Kong, Sundius Fees and Norman Bonner, to serve as publishers of the Daily Translation Herald, which was brought out in the name of British merchants; but the British merchants were only nominal, taking no part in any of the paper's work and bearing no responsibility for its profits or losses, merely drawing two hundred dollars a month from the paper and coming forward to cope whenever the Japanese and puppet side tried to make trouble with their censorship. To give the paper greater legality, they specially engaged Qian Nashui, a former editor of *Shen Bao* with a certain influence in society, as editor-in-chief. Qian Nashui had also formerly been a Communist Party member; though he later left the Party and held differing views on some of our Party's positions and practices, he was still a patriotic intellectual with a sense of justice. He had once been head of propaganda of the Mutual Aid General Association, and when I was head of the rescue department of the Mutual Aid General Association we had been in contact with each other, so we were also acquaintances. When the Daily Translation Herald opened, it had only 1,350 dollars in cash, while the paper's capital stock was set at ten thousand dollars, so the source of funds became a great problem. So Zhao Banghong, Qian Nashui, and Mei Yi came together to see Zhigao and me, wanting me to become chairman of the board and to help solve the funding problem. Although Zhigao had quite a reputation in Shanghai, he was by no means a big capitalist boss, and having already gone to considerable trouble to support the expenses of the "Wind and Rain Bookroom," he truly had no strength to shoulder alone the further heavy burden of supporting the Daily Translation Herald; so I declined and would not accept. Later Liu Shaowen talked with me and said that this matter was of great significance, and that as for the difficulty of raising funds, it might well be submitted to the "Tuesday Dining Club" for discussion and solution. And so I took on the heavy responsibility of chairman of the board of the Daily Translation Herald, in charge of raising funds.

The Shanghai "Tuesday Dining Club" came into being in 1937, at the time when the bloody "August Thirteenth" resistance was raging like a fire and the "Seven Gentlemen" had already walked free with honor. Pan Hannian, in his capacity as head of the "Eighth Route Office," had openly met with Chen Lifu. The Ten Great Principles for Resisting Japan and Saving the Nation put forward by the Party Central were also made public in the newspapers. Pan Hannian paid separate calls on Song Qingling, He Xiangning, and the "Seven Gentlemen"—the National Salvation Association leaders headed by Shen Junru—to congratulate them on the victory of their struggle for democracy and justice; he also paid separate calls on well-known figures of the financial, religious, and educational worlds, asking them to face the national calamity together and resist Japan for national salvation.

Luo Shuzhang was a Communist Party member who had joined the Party in 1934; hidden within the National Salvation Association, she, together with Zhang Zonglin, Hu Yongqi, Yang Huaisheng, and others, invited Dr. Liu Zhan'en, president of the University of Shanghai and a Christian, to give a speech at an all-circles rally welcoming the triumphant return of the "Seven Gentlemen." Dr. Liu's impassioned speech struck a chord among warm-hearted people, and with Luo Shuzhang's linking-up, the twenty-five-member "Tuesday Dining Club" was founded. Its members were: from the educational world, Liu Zhan'en, Zhang Zonglin, Wei Que, Chen Heqin, and Shen Tilan; from the industrial and commercial world, Chen Sisheng, Wang Jihua, Hu Yongqi, and Yang Yanxiu; from the news world, Qian Nashui, Gu Zhizhong, Mei Yi, and Wu Dakun; from the financial world, Sun Ruihuang, Wang Zhixin, and Huang Dinghui; the Christians Wu Yaozong, Yang Huaisheng, Wang Kuisheng, and Xiao Zongjun; the Buddhist figure Zhao Puchu; from the publishing world, Lu Gaoyi; from the Local Association, Yao Huiquan; from the women's world, Luo Shuzhang; and the accountant Li Wenjie, among others. Luo Shuzhang and Zhang Zonglin were the conveners. Zhang Zonglin was a veteran Party member who had joined the Communist Party as early as 1929, a disciple of Tao Xingzhi, and, together with Hu Yuzhi, a member of the Fu She. Li Wenjie and Yang Yanxiu joined the "Tuesday Club" after me. Before Luo Shuzhang left Shanghai with the "Seven Gentlemen," she came to see me, saying that Pan Hannian had bidden her come; they already knew that I had agreed to sustain the Women's Comfort Association after the venerable He Xiangning, and also knew that I had indicated to the venerable He that, since the Women's Comfort Association could no longer operate openly, a change of name to the Women's Salvation Association might be considered, and that if I too left Shanghai, Elder Sister Xu Guangping should be asked to take over. The venerable He and Comrade Liao Mengxing both thought this excellent. Since I was not, for the time being, leaving Shanghai, Shuzhang entrusted me with the two units she was responsible for: first, the "Tuesday Dining Club," and second, the Chinese Women's Vocational School. To both I readily consented. At the first meeting I attended, Luo Shuzhang introduced me to everyone, and Liu Zhan'en at once expressed his welcome, saying that he had already talked it over with Shuzhang and that Elder Sister Huang was asked to take over from Shuzhang and serve, together with Zonglin, as convener of the weekly meetings. The membership roll was kept by Yang Huaisheng, general secretary of the Young Men's Christian Association. The meetings were held in the directors' room on the ninth floor of the YMCA at Baxianqiao; on Tuesday evenings no notice was sent out, and everyone came, rain or shine, each paying a dinner fee of one dollar at his own expense; there was no chairman and no minutes were taken. Each time a topic was prepared and two or three central speakers designated, and everyone would talk freely around the topic. The topic discussed each time was determined in advance by Liu Shaowen, Zhang Zonglin, and me. The books and papers the members read were mostly supplied by the "Wind and Rain Bookroom"; while such papers and periodicals as *Documents* and the Daily Translation Herald were provided by each person himself. The participants were all patriotic, progressive senior intellectuals and social notables, and after the founding of New China many of them were members of the National Committee of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference. The members of the "Tuesday Dining Club" cared about the current situation; when they discussed Chairman Mao's book *On Protracted War*, they spoke very warmly, demolishing the theory of quick victory, opposing the theory of capitulation, and strengthening the conviction that a protracted war must be won. We also invited the American journalist Snow, author of *Red Star Over China*, and his wife to take part in the discussion; that a foreigner should be so devoted to Yan'an drew not a few patriotic youths to hurry off to Yan'an. The members of the "Tuesday Club" supported the charity performances and charity sales then being held in Shanghai to comfort the front—not only taking an active part each time themselves, but also mobilizing the bodies they represented to take an active part. For instance, when the Shanghai Drama Circles' Salvation Association held three days of charity performances of Peking opera, Kunqu, and spoken drama at the Lyceum Theater, every one of our members warmly sold tickets for the charity performances. All the money raised from the charity performances and charity sales was used to buy raincoats, rubber shoes, and other comfort goods, and representatives were chosen to take them to the New Fourth Army units to give comfort—the underground lines of communication protecting their safe passage there and back. At the first anniversary of the War of Resistance, the Shanghai All-Circles People's Comfort Delegation, with Gu Zhizhong and Wang Jihua of the "Tuesday Dining Club" as leader and deputy leader, went to southern Anhui to comfort the New Fourth Army. Zhu Libo, one of the comfort delegates of those years, kept all his life a photograph taken as a memento when the New Fourth Army leaders Xiang Ying, Li Yimang, and others received the comfort delegates. In the spring of 1939 a second comfort mission was organized, with Wu Dakun as leader. As the comfort delegation was on its way back to Shanghai, it was discovered by Gu Zhutong, who detained Xu Dixin, Wu Dakun, and their party and sent them to the Shangrao concentration camp. Shaowen had me discuss at the "Tuesday Dining Club" how to rescue them, and at the meeting the religious representative Wu Yaozong (a member of the Anti-Imperialist Grand Alliance) at once responded, immediately telegraphing Song Qingling to appeal for a rescue. Yao Huiquan also warmly telegraphed Mr. Huang Yanpei. Later, through a joint telegram from Huang Yanpei and Shen Junru to Gu Zhutong of the Third War Zone, Gu was asked to order their release. In short, the "Tuesday Club" was a progressive body that brought together the anti-Japanese forces of every quarter.

It was not until 1939, when the "Wind and Rain Bookroom" and the Daily Translation Herald were wrecked on the same day, that I handed over the affairs of the "Tuesday Club" to Elder Sister Xu Guangping. She sustained the "Tuesday Club," which grew to forty-five members, right up until the Japanese army invaded the concessions. The Party History Office of the CCP Shanghai Municipal Committee once asked me to write a memoir of the "Tuesday Dining Club," and I asked Li Wenjie to write it, for not only did he hold the posts of lawyer and accountant, but he had kept a diary day in and day out for decades, so his memory would be more accurate.

After the National Government moved its capital to Chongqing, a "Friday Dining Club" came into being, directly led by the Chongqing-based "Eighth Route Office." In the "Friday Dining Club" and the Shanghai "Wednesday" and "Thursday" nine-person core groups, as well as in the friendship associations of every trade and profession, Communist comrades all played a core role. I remember that when I returned to Shanghai after the victory of the War of Resistance, the "Banking Union" had already become a huge organization. Liu Shaowen once said to me that, among all the various mass bodies, the Shanghai "Tuesday Dining Club" was a pioneering experiment of the "three-thirds system" of the united front in the white areas. When it was spread throughout the country, all sorts of talents naturally sprang up among the various mass bodies—that is, the political foundation for the growth and development of the eight democratic parties. When Chiang Kai-shek ordered the dissolution of the Democratic League, and the Nanjing "National Assembly" openly declared a renewed "bandit extermination," greatly losing the people's hearts, this prompted the eight democratic parties to oppose Chiang and support the Communists openly in Hong Kong. Of the "Tuesday Dining Club" and the Daily Translation Herald, old comrades-in-arms still survive—in a flourishing age of revival, the long-lived are many!

Following Liu Shaowen's suggestion, I raised at the "Tuesday Dining Club" the matter of supporting the publication funds of the Daily Translation Herald, and won the members' full support; on the spot each subscribed for fifty dollars' worth of shares, raising more than a thousand dollars. The rest of the money was advanced, one sum after another, through the Tongyi Company by me, who had been made chairman of the board of the paper, to the amount of eight thousand dollars, and so the difficulty of the opening expenses at the founding of the Daily Translation Herald was, after all, solved.

After the paper came out, it regularly published news—from the foreign-language papers Liu Shaowen brought—of the victories of the Eighth Route Army and the New Fourth Army in striking at the Japanese aggressor troops, of the Kuomintang forces' resistance at the front, and of the international anti-fascist united front, and it was very popular with readers and sold very well, almost forming, with the old-established *Shen Bao* and *News Daily*, a gratifying situation of three legs of a tripod standing together. But the greater the sales, the greater the difficulties. A precondition of running a paper was to have enough paper to print it on. Fortunately Zhigao's schoolmate Wang Weizhen was head of general affairs at the *News Daily* office, and Zhigao talked it over with him and asked Wang to allocate, at cost price, the large stock of paper in the *News Daily*'s storehouse for the Daily Translation Herald's use. We opened a Zhengda Transport Company within the Tongyi Company, and, using this company's name, opened a current overdraft account, settling accounts with the *News Daily* office once a week; I would write out the Tongyi Company's checks to keep the funds of the Daily Translation Herald and the Wind and Rain Bookroom turning over. After the paper's sales greatly increased, there were not enough hands for the work, and the newsboys' school run by Mr. Chen Heqin, head of the education department of the Municipal Council, helped solve many difficulties. Mr. Chen Heqin was a famous educator of children; he served as chairman of the board of the newsboys' school, and I served as deputy chairman. But the greatest difficulty was still the problem of funds to support the paper over the long term; the Tongyi Company had already advanced large sums for it, and we could not solicit donations from the dining-club members again and again—how were we to go on like this?

When the Tongyi Company was mediating its resumption of business, the Central Bank, the Central Trust Bureau, the Bank of Communications, and the Farmers' Bank had promised the company investment support of 640,000 dollars, but the funds never came through. At that time the company's directors and supervisors—Wang Xiaolai, Du Yuesheng, and others—were all in Hong Kong, and to solve the company's difficulty in turning over funds, the managing director Xu Jiqing proposed that I, in my capacity as managing director and deputy general manager, make a trip to Hong Kong and, through Du Yuesheng and others, press those big banks to fulfill their earlier promise of investment support.

Qian Nashui had a fellow townsman and schoolmate, Hu Egong (style name Nanhu), an early member of the Chinese Communist Party who was at that time a counselor of the Executive Yuan of the Nanjing government (of which Kong Xiangxi was president); learning of the Daily Translation Herald's financial difficulties, he volunteered that he could mobilize Kong Xiangxi's son Kong Lingkan to donate funds in aid. A Ying was unwilling to seek help from anyone connected with Kong Xiangxi, considering that this matter touched on too wide a range; nor could I decide on my own authority, so I reported to Liu Shaowen and asked whether Kong Lingkan might be allowed to donate. Shaowen said: now that it is a matter of Kuomintang–Communist cooperation and united resistance, according to the spirit of the Central Committee's instructions, so long as it is money to support the resistance work, it may be accepted no matter which side it comes from, and there is no harm in trying. But the donation must carry no attached conditions; they must not be allowed to reach in and interfere with the personnel or editorial policy of the management or editorial departments; the independence and autonomy of the Party's propaganda position must be maintained. So Zhigao and I accepted an invitation to a banquet given by Hu Egong, at which Hu warmly praised the Daily Translation Herald for holding fast to anti-Japanese propaganda and winning the people's hearts, and expressed his admiration for Zhigao's warm support of resistance-and-salvation work in every way, and it was settled that he would come forward to win over young Kong in Hong Kong. Shaowen further bade me submit the proposed Hong Kong fundraising trip to the "Tuesday Dining Club" for discussion, but without needing to mention Hu Egong's suggestion. At the meeting Yao Huiquan, who took part in the "Tuesday Dining Club" representing Du Yuesheng's Local Association, at once said that he could telegraph Du Yuesheng in Hong Kong to ask for a donation. Before long, Yao told me that he had received Du's reply telegram, expressing his welcome for Shanghai to send someone. And so I set out for Hong Kong to raise funds—representing the Tongyi Company, the Daily Translation Herald, and the Shanghai Women's Comfort Association all at once.

When I reached Hong Kong in the autumn of 1938, Du Yuesheng's chief disciple Zhu Xuefan (a labor-union leader of the Kuomintang system, who after the founding of New China served as Minister of Posts and Telecommunications, vice-chairman of the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress, and chairman of the Central Committee of the Revolutionary Committee of the Kuomintang) and Du Yuesheng's eldest son Du Weifan came to the wharf to meet me. Because Hu Egong and Yao Huiquan had already sent word and made contact in advance, and because Du Yuesheng's wife Yao Yulan accompanied me, we went together to a family banquet given by Kong Xiangxi's wife Song Ailing. At the banquet it was settled that Kong and Du would each subscribe for 25,000 dollars' worth of shares, so the fundraising went very smoothly—one might say it succeeded at the first stroke. As for pressing the four big banks, the Central Bank among them, for the money owed to the Tongyi Company, that came to nothing.

Before I left Shanghai for Hong Kong, Liu Shaowen, sensing that I was not quite content to remain in Shanghai doing salvation work behind enemy lines, bade me, after reaching Hong Kong, write directly through the Party organization there to Comrade Zhou Enlai in the great rear to ask for instructions. In Hong Kong I lodged at the home of my old Northern Expedition comrades-in-arms, the couple Li Fang and Wang Huazhen, at No. 2 Kimberley Road; the two of them had seen Comrade Enlai's reply in his own hand, written to me from Chongqing, which both commended the results of my work and ordered me still to obey the larger cause and return to Shanghai to hold fast to my work.

My other task in going to Hong Kong was, in the name of the Shanghai Women's Salvation Association, to visit the women's anti-Japanese bodies there and exchange work experience. First I went to call on the venerable elder He Xiangning and reported to her on how the Shanghai Women's Comfort Association had been renamed the Women's Salvation Association, at which she was very glad. At that time a patriotic old mother from the Northeast, Old Madame Zhao, had come to Hong Kong together with Comrade Deng Yingchao; all four of this Old Madame Zhao's sons had given their lives on the anti-Japanese battlefield, and she had sent her youngest son too to the anti-Japanese front. Such a spirit of pressing forward to fight and kill the enemy over the fallen was a great inspiration and a moving, admirable example to the women of the whole country. When she was interviewed by reporters, because she did not understand Cantonese, I acted as her interpreter. The Hong Kong women's welcome for Old Madame Zhao was held at the Sohao Restaurant, and was presided over by Sir Robert Hotung's beloved daughter, as the representative of Zhang Manyi, wife of T.V. Soong. Afterward we took a group photograph as a memento: she stood behind Old Madame Zhao; Old Madame Zhao and He Xiangning sat in the front center; Deng Yingchao sat beside Old Madame Zhao; and I sat beside the venerable He, who held her cane (I was then carrying my second daughter, Dazhong, so I placed my handbag in front to cover my big belly); Comrade Liao Mengxing stood on the left; Hu Mulan, daughter of Hu Hanmin and head of the Hong Kong Women's Comfort Association, stood in the second row. There were also Wu Jufang, wife of General Li Hanhun and leader of the northbound comfort delegation, the wives of senior generals of the Nineteenth Route Army, and representatives of the Young Women's Christian Association—many of whom had taken part in the Women's Comfort Association in Shanghai, so it was truly a joyous reunion in a strange land. After I returned to Shanghai, I wrote a report on this event, published in the magazine *Shanghai Women*, and the editor made this group photograph the cover of that issue. This photograph was chosen for display at the exhibition commemorating the 45th anniversary of the victory of the War of Resistance Against Japan, enlarged to life size, and after the exhibition it was collected in the commemorative album North and South of the Great River. This meaningful group photograph was also used in the television special commemorating Comrade Deng Yingchao.

While in Hong Kong, Pan Hannian wrote me a note saying that He Chang's orphaned son He Ping was being fostered at the home of Lu Biao in Macao, and that I might quietly go to see him once, but must not reveal our true identities as birth mother and son—we could only address each other as aunt and nephew—and might go accompanied by Ke Lin, then director of the Kiang Wu Hospital in Macao. He (Lu) Ping was then already nine years old and looked very much like He Chang; his adoptive parents, the couple Lu Biao, cherished him with the utmost care, and the Lu family grandmother in particular doted on him as on a pearl in the palm. I remember that when I parted from He Chang, he had spurred me on with the line "While the Xiongnu are not yet destroyed, what use is a home?" Now, though he had given his life, seeing the child he left behind so clever and lively and safely growing up, I too felt somewhat comforted and at ease.

After I returned to Shanghai from the Hong Kong fundraising, I handed the fifty-thousand-dollar check given by Kong and Du to Zhang Zonglin. We agreed to give the Daily Translation Herald ten thousand dollars first, to expand the paper's format to a full folio sheet. After this the paper was even more popular with readers, its daily sales exceeding thirty thousand copies. The paper's editorials of the time came mostly from Mei Yi's hand. When he married, I gave him a lyric to the tune Langtaosha, which contained the lines "the pen that sweeps away a thousand troops chills the enemy's courage; the will of the multitude is a fortress-wall." The Daily Translation Herald once serialized in full Mao Zedong's *On Protracted War*, the Sixth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee's Letter to Compatriots Throughout the Country, Zhou Enlai's On the New Stage of the War of Resistance and the Aggressor's New Policy delivered at the headquarters of the New Fourth Army, and other important speeches and related documents, further expanding the Party's influence among the masses and heartening people's fighting spirit against Japan. With the money raised in Hong Kong we also founded a Defend-the-Nation Publishing Company, with me concurrently as chairman of the board and Zhang Zonglin concurrently as secretary-general, to unify the left-wing cultural publishing and supply work in Shanghai—I in charge of allocation, Zhang Zonglin in charge of auditing the expenses. Some held that our acceptance of Kong's and Du's donations was a capitulation to the big bourgeoisie; others comforted me by saying that Marx, in the stage of the German democratic revolution, had also accepted donations from the big bourgeoisie when publishing the *Rheinische Zeitung*. The facts proved that we, having sought instructions, had correctly carried out the Party's policy of the anti-Japanese national united front, and had brought into play the effect of "those with money give money, those with strength give strength, uniting to resist Japan and facing the enemy as one." In those years the colleagues of the Daily Translation Herald and the members of the "Tuesday Dining Club" all with one voice praised me as a "people's diplomatic envoy" returning in victory. In 1999, when A History of the Chinese Communist Party in Shanghai, compiled by the Party History Research Office of the CCP Shanghai Municipal Committee, was published, it too pointed out objectively and fairly: "The Tongyi Trust Company where the chairman Huang Dinghui worked, along with the Chinese Vocational Education Society and the Shanghai Local Association, contributed no little effort in the matter of the paper's funds."

At that time, Elder Sister Xu Guangping, with the support of the Shanghai underground Party's Cultural Committee and of the progressive cultural world, presided over the editing of the *Complete Works of Lu Xun*, the publishing work being likewise in the charge of Zhang Zonglin. Under conditions of a shortage of funds, the printing costs of the Complete Works were raised under the unified charge of Hu Yuzhi; the members of the "Tuesday Dining Club" also gave their full support, each member first paying out fifty dollars to subscribe in advance for a hardbound set of the *Complete Works of Lu Xun* as a memento, and helping to distribute it. Mr. Mao—Shen Yanbing—in an article published in October 1977 in Research Materials on the *Complete Works of Lu Xun*, mentioned that in the autumn of 1938, in order to pay the printing costs of the *Complete Works of Lu Xun* in Hong Kong, it was solved only with my help in opening an unlimited overdraft account for him at a bank. In Mao Dun's autobiographical memoir, he described in still more detail the arduous course of his search for a publishing house across Shanghai, Hong Kong, and Shanghai again, saying that in the end it was I who helped solve it in Shanghai. Many of the details I no longer remember well. In 1979, Mr. Mao, in the presence of my eldest son Wan Changjie, further praised me as one who "sought no fame, yet fame came of itself." In truth I was no celebrity of the cultural world, but held myself to be merely a nobody who loved literature and art, doing a little bit for the progressive cultural world of resistance and salvation, that is all.

After I came back from Hong Kong, the International Shelter held a ball game to raise funds for the resistance, and when I presented the prizes to the winners we took a group photograph as a memento. In those days I never wore makeup and dressed very plainly. This is my favorite and best-taken photograph of my whole life, and I treasure it still. (See color plates 6–7, figure 3.) The Shanghai correspondent of *L'Humanité*, the central organ of the French Communist Party, wrote a report on this and sent it back to London (France was by then occupied by the German army) to be published in *L'Humanité*, praising me in it as the "Queen of the Refugees." This honor I truly did not deserve and dared not accept.

From 1937 to 1938, the National Government moved from Nanjing to Wuhan, and then to Chongqing; although it too left a portion of its personnel in the Shanghai concessions, these people never dared come forward openly to engage in anti-Japanese activities. Once, the Kuomintang Central sent Chen Xiaocen to Shanghai to find out about the situation of anti-Japanese activities there; unable to gather any information about salvation work from the Kuomintang's Shanghai personnel, he came to call on me, and, not finding me, ran into Chen Zhigao instead. Zhigao did not tell Chen Xiaocen that I was his wife, but arranged a meeting for me with him at the Bankers' Club. When we met, I told him about the refugee relief work carried on by the Charity Federation led by Zhao Puchu, but as for our opening the International No. 1 Shelter through Father Rao and carrying on Party-building and army-recruitment work among the refugees, I did not say a single word. Among the cadres of the Charity Federation were Communist comrades, who relied on Zhao Puchu for cover and loyal cooperation—this was the origin of his becoming, after the founding of New China, deputy head of the personnel department of the East China Military and Political Committee and a leading figure in relief and welfare circles. Zhao Puchu's maternal uncle Huang Hanzhi was a head of the Charity Federation, and Zhao Puchu worked in the Charity Federation all along. His study of Buddhism, his cultural cultivation, and his modest and diligent attitude allowed the Party's underground work to develop in safety, and in the work of the Charity Federation he also trained the cadres and the refugee compatriots in a style of sharing weal and woe. People praised him as a Marxist-Leninist hidden within the Buddhist world. Under his influence, the magazine Awakened Sentience published in the early days of liberation, and the magazine Buddhist Culture of today, both preach a purifying of life that links theory with practice, not a passive life. The remembrance of the venerable Zhao Puchu will forever remain in the hearts of upright people.

The Chinese Women's Vocational School that Elder Sister Luo Shuzhang entrusted to my care was jointly founded in the early days of the War of Resistance by four democratic big sisters—Shi Liang, Luo Shuzhang, Shen Zijiu, and Hu Ziying—together with Madame Dai, wife of Wu Yunchu, the owner of the Tianchu Monosodium Glutamate Factory, and Gao Jingyi, wife of Wu Gengmei, a member of the World and China Society. The school trained a group of nurses, who went to the hospitals to take part in the work of rescuing the wounded from the front; it also trained some students in sewing skills, to make cotton vests and first-aid kits for the officers and men resisting Japan at the front. The leadership of the school was in the charge of Elder Sister Luo Shuzhang. Also hidden within the school were the underground Party members Wang Renshu, Guo Zhenyi, Qian Yishi, Cai Xiaying, and others, who played a core leading role. After Shanghai was reduced to the Solitary Island, the school's leadership was reelected, with Wang Renshu succeeding as chairman of the board of school directors and Xu Guangping taking over as principal; I served as a managing school director, in charge of the school's financial management. This went on until the autumn of 1939, with me bearing all the school's economic expenses. In the summer of 1939, after Zhigao had gone to Hong Kong (this matter is related separately below), Li Nanxiang, Chen Gaoyong, Jin Xuecheng, Cai Shuhou, and others consulted with Liu Shaowen and Wang Renshu and felt that, with the Japanese and puppet forces by then increasingly penetrating the concessions, and the political color of the school's leaders such as Xu Guangping and Wang Renshu being too "red," the school could hardly go on being run as before. Just at this time Hou Wailu returned from abroad; he was engaged in international intelligence work, had made contact with Pan Hannian and Liu Shaowen, and was living at Chen Gaoyong's home, while Li Nanxiang's younger brother Li Huanzhi had also just come from Chen Lifu in Chongqing. Through many talks and consultations, these several people decided on cooperation between the Nationalist and Communist sides to run an intelligence-personnel training class in the Shanghai concessions, hanging out the signboard of an accounting training class to the outside world, but not recruiting from outside—taking in only students internally recommended by the two sides (both men and women). The two sides had even worked out the rules and regulations of the training class; all that was lacking was a building, and so it was proposed to turn the Chinese Women's Vocational School into an organ where the Kuomintang and the Communist Party would jointly train intelligence personnel to counter the Japanese and puppet spies and secret agents. The decision on this matter reached "the top" on both sides: on the Kuomintang side it was agreed to by Chiang Kai-shek, and on the Communist side it was agreed to after Liu Shaowen sought instructions from above. So the school's board of directors was reelected: Wang Renshu resigned his post as chairman of the board of school directors, letting Chen Zhigao, who had already gone to Hong Kong, hold the chairmanship nominally; Xu Guangping resigned her post as principal, and was succeeded by Li Nanxiang, then chairman of the Kuomintang's Jiangsu Provincial Party Headquarters and later head of the Shanghai district of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang-Anhui Itinerant Education Corps. When the two sides handed over their posts, a contract was signed, which specially stipulated: first, the school was renamed the Chinese Vocational School, taking in both male and female students recommended by the two cooperating sides; second, the original teaching and administrative staff were all to be retained, and could not be dismissed at will; third, all the school's internal and external debts were to be paid off by the new principal, Li Nanxiang. From then on, on every front there were our comrades who had broken in; this was our new creation and contribution on the intelligence front amid the special environment of the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation and the Shanghai concessions. Xu Guangping did not understand the inside story at the time, so she deliberately kept this contract, in order to clarify her responsibility later on. But after the founding of New China, for various reasons, Pan Hannian and Liu Shaowen were unable to explain the truth of the matter to Xu Guangping and the other democratic big sisters, so that they long harbored a misunderstanding of me. On this account I too suffered under a false charge for many years. Not until after the Party's Third Plenum of the Eleventh Central Committee, when Luo Shuzhang had her Party membership restored and learned the truth, did she, in the memoir she wrote, make a self-criticism on this matter and explain to her student (the Party secretary of Shanghai's light-industry system) the ins and outs of it. Her student came to the East China Hospital to see me, made a careful record of this part of my recollections, and entered me into the alumni directory of the Chinese Women's Vocational School. Gao Jingyi too had for a time gone along with some people's misunderstanding of me, but later she also realized she had wronged me. When her son came to Shanghai to see me, he too expressed his apologies to me. I feel that comrades engaged in underground work, bound by the Party's discipline of secrecy, cannot explain everything to everyone in every place, and it is very hard to avoid being misunderstood and suffering some wrong in certain matters; it is enough that one's own conscience is clear. Of course we could not avoid having shortcomings and errors in our way of working either, but happily, since our aims were the same, it was easy to communicate with and forgive one another.

After the "July Seventh" Incident, from North and Central China all the way to East and South China, great stretches of the homeland inside the Great Wall were seized by the Japanese army, and everywhere there were multitudes of exiled students unwilling to be the slaves of a conquered nation who came to the Shanghai concessions. To help them continue their studies and make a living afterward, the Shanghai Local Association of Du Yuesheng's system and the Chinese Vocational Education Society led by Huang Yanpei became the main forces in this regard, running one after another fourteen remedial schools that specially enrolled exiled students, the teaching staff being mostly progressive people. In addition, Wang Kuisheng founded a further-study remedial school, and the Banking and Money Trades Friendship Association also ran a night school, and so on. All these schools asked me to serve concurrently as a school director. When the Young Women's Christian Association held a fundraising rally in aid of the resistance, it too asked me to be a team leader (the general secretary was Xie Zuyi, the deputy general secretary Gong Pusheng), and asked me to give a mobilization address. I saw clearly that, in the upsurge of the nationwide resistance-and-salvation movement, as the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation became open and legal, the objects of our work grew ever broader; and in order to unite all the forces that could be united to resist Japan together, I actively went and did the work. During the Shanghai Solitary Island period, whatever the trade or circle, in every resistance-and-salvation activity that asked for my participation and support, I did my utmost, fulfilling my duty to give a ray of light for every ray I had, and a measure of warmth for every measure I had.

To sum up all the above, in this period my concurrent posts were many; though they all centered on one thing—resistance and national salvation—the threads of the work were tangled and manifold, and I was truly busy day and night, not slackening my work in the slightest even while pregnant. The main focuses of my work were: the Women's Salvation Association, the No. 1 Refugee Shelter, the Tongyi Trust Company, and the Daily Translation Herald. Fortunately I had the support of my family and no worries at the back of my mind.

On 5 June 1996, page 15 of the *People's Daily* published a long article by Luo Qingchang and Chai Chengwen commemorating Liu Shaowen, "A Nameless Hero, a Model for the Ages—Commemorating the Ninetieth Anniversary of the Birth of Comrade Liu Shaowen" (see the appendix), from which the reader can clearly see how the Party brought into play the power of the united front and broadly mobilized every stratum of society to unite as one and resist Japan together. I was fortunate to become a soldier the Party posted to the white areas, pressing bravely forward under the Party's unified leadership.
