## Chapter Sixteen

As day was breaking, Zhu Guiying's mother, lying on the broken bamboo couch, grew slowly quiet. A whole night of weeping and cursing, of searching the shanty quarter for her daughter like a woman gone mad, of trying again and again to burst into the mill and fight "Tu the Chamber-pot" to the death — by now the old woman was too spent to stir. Yet she was not asleep; she lay with her bloodshot old eyes wide, staring into the void. The blaze of her fury had died down, and a cold, cold terror was creeping up over her heart.

The kerosene lamp on the plank table burned its last drop of oil and went dark, dark, and out. Outside the bamboo door the sky slowly paled to a fish-belly white. The old woman felt a ghostly hand press down on her breast and tear her heart to shreds; again she heard the bamboo door rattle, again she saw her daughter's head come rolling, dripping with blood, to the edge of the couch! She sprang bolt upright. But it was not her daughter's head; it was two men standing before her. In the gloom she made out her son Xiao Sanzi and the next-door neighbour Jin Heshang; and, as though her heart had eased a little, she cried out at once:

"Have you found out? Where have they locked her up? That thing that just rolled in — wasn't it A-Ying's head?"

"What head! No! — Some say she's been taken off to the Public Security Bureau, some say she's still locked up in the mill: three men and six different tales! Damn it all!"

Jin Heshang answered through clenched teeth. Bang! Xiao Sanzi kicked over a broken stool and gave a savage snort. The old woman was dumbstruck for a moment, then fell again to beating her breast and stamping her feet and cursing through her tears.

The shanty quarter was stirring now with the sound of voices. The siren of the Yuhua mill blared out its imperious hoot. Hurried, jumbled footsteps went running past outside, and among them loud shouts, laughing curses, and the foul banter of the loafers.

All at once a thin, tall, coquettish woman came running in. Xiao Sanzi knew her for Yao Jinfeng, and his eyes flew wide open; he was on the point of cursing her. Just then another came in at her heels — it was Lu Xiaobao, who pulled Xiao Sanzi aside to the bamboo door and said in a low voice:

"I've found it all out for you. Elder Sister Guiying is still in the mill. Go and beg Mr. Tu, and she can be let out."

Before Xiao Sanzi could answer, he heard Yao Jinfeng over the other side saying loudly, with a laugh:

"Blame this, blame that — there's no one to blame but herself! Mr. Tu thought well of her to begin with, and she wouldn't be lifted up when the chance was hers! Never fear! I'll go and put in a word for her. Mr. Tu is a soft-hearted, good man! Only Guiying too must have a change of heart —"

Yao Jinfeng had not finished when Xiao Sanzi sprang across and seized hold of her, glaring, and swore:

"I'll beat you, you slut! Who wants your sly toadying here!"

The two of them grappled together in a knot. Jin Heshang dragged Xiao Sanzi off, and Lu Xiaobao too hauled Yao Jinfeng away. The old woman came after them, cursing venomously:

"You are all in league to do her harm! You want to curry favour with Tu the Chamber-pot — go and be his concubines, then, the pair of you! You two stinking sluts! You dust-carts!"

Cursing as she went, the old woman slammed the bamboo door to, came back, clamped her mouth shut, and wept no more. Of a sudden her grief was gone, and her whole breast was filled with a hatred you could have hacked with a knife; she hated Tu the Chamber-pot and Yao Jinfeng and all their sort to the death, and hated to the death every last one of the women who had gone in to work. And this simple hatred drew her on to a dim sort of pride: her daughter was no running dog!

Xiao Sanzi and Jin Heshang seemed to share this same feeling, and they were laying plans now for another matter. It was Jin Heshang who spoke first:

"It's getting late! Yesterday we all agreed the whole gang would go and raise a rumpus at that dog-bred Zhou fellow's place. Are you coming or not?"

"Coming! Why wouldn't I come! That damned 'Red-Headed Match' wants to shut the works down — we'll turn his 'red head' black! Smash his dog-kennel to bits!"

"Only I'm afraid he'll have slipped off, and set a constable at the door of his kennel!"

"Hah! And didn't we all settle that too? If he slips off, we'll camp in his kennel and not budge!"

Xiao Sanzi shouted in a fury and brought his fist down on the broken plank table. The old woman, listening at their side, understood now what it was all about, and all at once she stamped her feet and cried aloud:

"I'll go too! If you're all to be carted off one by one by the constables, then I, old woman that I am, don't care to live either! I'll go along with you!"

And as she cried out she caught hold of her son. Caught hold of him! The old woman herself hardly knew whether this "catching hold" was to go along with them or to keep her son from going. But hold him she did, wailing and weeping, till Jin Heshang was at his wits' end. Xiao Sanzi, his face flushed crimson, hopped about and shouted wildly:

"Ma! You've lost your senses! We don't want you, old woman, coming along! What fun is there in it!"

Xiao Sanzi shoved the old woman off with all his strength, and, pulling Jin Heshang along, was gone.

By the time Jin Heshang and his band of fifty or sixty match-workers reached the neighbourhood of their employer Zhou Zhongwei's house, the sun was already three rods high. This house of Zhou's was tucked back in a narrow lane, but at its mouth stood a gatekeeping constable. The fifty or sixty workers had no choice but to elect eight delegates to go in and treat with him. The greater part of them waited at the lane-mouth, sitting on the cement, lifting the hems of their coats to wipe away the sweat and then using those same hems for fans.

Xiao Sanzi was one of the delegates. When the eight of them had come inside the lane, sure enough the master's front gate was fast shut. The eight delegates made an uproar outside it for a good while, but from within the house came never an answer, as though it were an empty shell. Xiao Sanzi, beside himself, raised his fist and beat again on the black-lacquered gate till it thundered, and, as if his lungs would burst, roared:

"You think hiding in there settles it? We'll set your mother a fire going, and see if you don't come out!"

"That's it! We'll set the place ablaze! Ablaze!"

The other seven delegates raised the cry with him; and one of them did in fact fish out a box of matches. All at once, from the balcony of the wing-room upstairs, came a burst of wild laughter. The eight delegates knew that laugh; they looked quickly up — and was it not Zhou Zhongwei standing there! With a short jacket of Indian silk thrown over him and his feet bare, he stood looking down and laughing at the eight below. Was this a challenge? The eight delegates leapt this way and that, shouting curses. But Zhou Zhongwei only laughed. Then, of a sudden, wagging his head and rising on his toes, he leaned his short, fat body over the balcony rail and called down in a loud voice:

"You want to set a fire? Splendid! Why, I'll thank you for putting thirty thousand taels of fire-insurance money into my hands! The house isn't mine — set your fire and welcome! But there's this one thing: the mistress lies sick in her bed, so you must first come and help carry the mistress out!"

With that Zhou Zhongwei laughed his loud "ha, ha" again till his face went red with laughing. The eight delegates could do nothing with him, and only opened their throats and cursed him foully. Zhou Zhongwei took no offence; the more venomously they cursed below, the more wildly he laughed above; then, of a sudden, quite gravely, he called down to the delegates:

"Hey there, hey there, old friends! Let me teach you a trick! Go and burn my factory! That's insured against fire for eighty thousand in silver, and in another half-month the policy runs out! If you mean to burn it, you'd best be quick about it! The insurance house is run by foreigners; and foreigners' money — why, we're only too glad to use it! If you make me that eighty-thousand-tael windfall, I'll thank you in good earnest — a spread of wine and food at the Hongyun Lou; I'm not lying!"

The eight delegates were fit to burst with rage. Their throats had gone hoarse with cursing, and against this brazen, shameless Zhou Zhongwei they were simply helpless. And there were but eight of them; even had they hit on a plan, they could not have carried it through. So after a word together they ran back to find their comrades at the lane-mouth.

Standing on the balcony, Zhou Zhongwei laughed his "ha, ha" and saw the eight of them off with his eyes until they were out of sight; only then did he go back into the house, still laughing. This "mansion" of his was no more than a house of three bays above and three below; ever since his match factory had begun to lose money he had cleared out half the wing-rooms, meaning to let them, and had dismissed a cook and two wet-nurses besides. The "hard times" had in truth long since filled his mansion through and through, the more so as his wife's consumption had reached its third stage, so that this year she could not so much as leave her bed even in the summer heat; yet Zhou Zhongwei could still laugh, and often. Sprung from a pauper's beginnings and risen by way of the comprador's trade, he had always been an empty frame of a man, and his particular gift was that it cost him little to "swagger up" and little to "flop down" again; be he never so hard pressed, he could laugh.

Having thus, like Zhuge Liang in the "Empty City Stratagem," laughed off the eight delegates, Zhou Zhongwei ran down to the wing-room below to play again with his set of "little ornaments." On two eight-immortals tables set end to end there was arranged, all neat and complete, the whole old-fashioned pomp of a birthday celebration. In the eighth month of next year he meant to hold a grand feast for his own fortieth birthday, on his turning forty. He loved the old style of the former Qing, with a great deal of noise and bustle; and having nothing to do this morning, he had brought out his treasured "little ornaments" to rehearse it in advance. Just when he was gazing at it in high self-satisfaction, the eight worker-delegates outside had raised too fierce an uproar, and he had been obliged to run up to the balcony and play out that little comedy. Now, looking again at his "little ornaments," he suddenly bethought him that his wife's "great matter" might well fall due before his own birthday feast; whereupon he cancelled the birthday pomp and changed it, for the sport of it, into an old-style "laying-out" of mourning. He set up mourning-curtains three inches high, and changed all the little double-backed chairs of ebony, each no bigger than a matchbox, for little seat-covers of white satin; and he arranged it item by item with far more zeal, in truth, than he ever brought to the running of that match factory of his — and far more method too!

He had just set in place a pair of wind-proof jar-lamps the size of oranges, and was about to raise up the east camp-gate and the west, when two visitors came running in, and this great work of his could go no further.

The two visitors were Zhu Yinqiu and Chen Junyi, who, taking a look at the little playthings on the two eight-immortals tables, could not help laughing outright. Zhou Zhongwei, seemingly well pleased, rubbed his hands and laughed his loud "ha, ha" too. Zhu Yinqiu clapped him on the shoulder and said:

"Elder Zhong, I admire you — what composure you have! Aren't those your factory's workers petitioning outside? The lane-mouth is packed with them, wrangling with the constable!"

"Why! Is there really such a thing afoot? I knew nothing of it at all. Forgive me, I must leave you a moment — I'll go out and take a look."

Zhou Zhongwei said it with a show of alarm, and for once did not laugh; he did up the buttons of his short jacket and made as if to hurry out. Chen Junyi caught him fast.

"Don't go out! Let them make their racket! Elder Zhong, a brave man does not court the blow before his eyes; you must not show your face just now!"

"Elder Jun is quite right! The day before yesterday Wu Sunfu was all but battered to pieces, motor-car and all! The workers' insolence is beyond all words! — But, Elder Zhong, that a business like yours should be brought to losses and a shut-down — it truly passes belief! You are not as we are; yours is a trade that no household can do without, one of the seven things they must see to the moment the door is opened of a morning, is it not? Your street-corner good-for-nothing may go without his dinner, but his cigarette-end he must and will smoke — and for that he needs a box of your matches!"

So Zhu Yinqiu put in as well, picking up from the table one of the orange-sized wind-proof jar-lamps to glance at it, with a faint smile.

But Zhou Zhongwei made no answer; all at once he laughed his "ha, ha" again and, hopping like a toad, sprang to a writing-desk in the back half of the wing-room, where he scrabbled and clawed through a heap of old letters; and at last, pinching out a mimeographed sheet between his fat fingers, he handed it to the two of them, Zhu Yinqiu and Chen Junyi, and said:

"Just look, the two of you, at what this is, and you'll see that my business is as good as any in the world!"

It was a circular letter from the All-China Match Manufacturers' Association giving notice to its members, with, appended, a copy of a memorial submitted to the Ministry of Industry and Commerce by the Guangdong Match Trade Guild. The circular ran as follows:

"We beg to state: This Association has repeatedly received letters from the Guangdong Native-Match Trade Guild reporting that, according to what is put about in the newspapers of that province and of Hong Kong, the Swedish firm the Sino-Swedish Match Company is to make a loan to our country, on the condition, among others, that Swedish matches be granted a monopoly in China for a term of some years; that the match-merchants are in the greatest alarm, and pray that inquiry be made and answer given, so as to set the general doubt at rest; and there was enclosed therewith a draft memorial to the Ministry of Industry and Commerce. We have further received a letter from the Match Trade Federation of the Three Eastern Provinces reporting that, according to the word of the Japanese match-merchants, it is said that the Consul of that country resident in Shanghai has declared that the Ministry of Finance of our Government has an arrangement to borrow from the Swedish Match Company, tacitly conceding it various rights; whether this be true or no they pray may be ascertained and made known. Upon this: the rumour that the Swedish firm is treating with the Government for a loan was noted by this Association so early as the sixth month of the present year; and upon repeated inquiry thereafter it was learned that this rumour had not become fact; yet, the reports being many and confused, and the members hardly to be kept free of misgiving without some definite pronouncement from the side of the Government, this Association has accordingly laid the matter before the Ministry of Industry and Commerce and begged a plain answer; and so soon as an instruction shall be received, further notice will of course be given. We herewith append, severally transcribed, the draft of this Association's memorial and the draft of the memorial of the Guangdong Native-Match Trade Guild, and trust they will receive your attention!"

Rising on his toes and standing at Zhu Yinqiu's back, Zhou Zhongwei read the notice through with him; then, panting, he declaimed aloud the telling passage in the memorial of the Guangdong Match Trade Guild: "'Yet our country, with the ravages of war year upon year, has seen its commerce wither to the last degree; while the Government, at this very season when the treasury is strangely bare, heaps tax upon tax and levy upon levy on the raw material for imported matches, and, with public loans and treasury notes, burden upon burden, has sunk us into a pit past all redemption. And now this Swedish Match Trust, judging that its hour to crush the native match of our country is come, turns to account the light import-duty on matches from abroad, and ships them in unceasingly at cut prices, so that the native matches of our country, which cost more to make, can find no sale, and the goods pile up mountain-high, and must be sold off at a loss below cost, borne with clenched teeth in the mere hope of keeping the money turning. But the native match-merchants of our country, slender in capital, are no match for the Swedish Match Trust, that rich and mighty tyrant over the whole globe; and thus the match-houses of our country, failing one after another, are come well-nigh to five-tenths and more!'" — Zhou Zhongwei shook his head and, of a sudden, laughed his loud "ha, ha" again and said:

"There, you see! Elder Yin, Elder Jun, my business is truly as good as any in the world! Were it not good, would the Swedish Match Trust ever have turned its thoughts this way?"

Chen Junyi and Zhu Yinqiu looked at each other and knitted their brows. To the two of them, outsiders, there seemed something not unlike weeping in that "ha, ha" laughter of Zhou Zhongwei's; yet with Zhou Zhongwei it was genuine, full-weight laughter, true goods at a true price. He could laugh loud and long at any time. Had it been otherwise, he could never have grown so fat.

Just then Zhou Zhongwei's rickshaw-man came running in in a fluster to report that the workers had elected ten delegates who were about to come into the hall. Zhu Yinqiu gave a tug at Chen Junyi's coat-hem and stood up to go. But Zhou Zhongwei barred the way and would not let them, crying loudly:

"Sit a while longer. I have a few serious words to talk over with the two of you! What is there to fear in ten delegates!"

"That's not the way of it! Elder Zhong, you must after all open your parley with the workers' delegates, and there's no sense in Elder Jun and me, idle bystanders, being caught in the thick of the bustle. Whatever serious words you have, we can talk them over this afternoon — isn't it all the same?"

"Why, no, that won't do! Brother Zhu, forgive me; since you have come, sit a while longer, and let me trouble the two of you to serve as my bodyguards for the moment! Never fear! The workers of my factory are very civilised, and I treat them very civilly too! Should you two be startled in the least, the fault shall be mine to answer for."

Zhou Zhongwei's face too had flushed red; as he spoke he cupped his hands and bowed, and then, flinging his arms wide, he headed Zhu Yinqiu and Chen Junyi back into their chairs and would have them, willy-nilly, sit down. The two could not fathom what game this "Red-Headed Match" was playing, and could not help laughing; and it was in the very midst of that laughter that they heard, from outside, a thunderous pounding, bang upon bang, on the pair of black-lacquered gates, whereupon the two men's laughing faces turned on the instant to the shape of weeping. The workers' delegates were shouting and cursing outside the gate. "Dog of a boss! Thief of a boss!" — every phrase of it grated on the ear. Chen Junyi and Zhu Yinqiu felt wretched too, and went red to the roots of their ears; but Zhou Zhongwei, smiling still, slapped his chest and, looking into their faces, said:

"Didn't I say they were civilised? Civilised to the last degree! A few curses do no harm to the digestion. Elder Jun, in the old days when we were compradors, if things fell out amiss and the taipan flew into one of his foreign rages, the cursing was sometimes viler by far; yet these workers are, when all's said, Chinese, and we too are Chinese, so that in cursing us they do but curse themselves."

"Elder Zhong! Your powers of composure are excellent indeed! I dare say if they gave you a box on the ear you wouldn't be angry either!"

Chen Junyi said it in mockery, yet he could not raise a laugh. Zhu Yinqiu, at his side, knitted his brows. Zhou Zhongwei at once gave his head a wag and answered very gravely:

"Just so! There was once a taipan of some foreign firm or other — a Flowery-Flag man, or else a German, I can't now recall; no matter which, a foreigner, at all events; and he said to me: 'You Chinese are truly a wonderful set of treasures — knocked down flat on the ground by another, you find lying there more comfortable than standing up; you've no more use for your legs to walk on, so you just roll about all over the ground!' Elder Jun, tell me, is that true or not? He had the Chinese temper off to a hair, give him his due. The Chinese were always the greatest hands at taking their ease!"

The clamour outside the gate grew fiercer now. All at once two human heads climbed up outside the iron grating of the south-facing window of the wing-room and peered in. Zhu Yinqiu, turning his face sharply and seeing them, could not keep his heart from a leap. The heads dropped down again, and there followed a burst of clamour and cursing more urgent and more deafening than before. Within the wing-room one could scarcely hear a word spoken face to face. Zhu Yinqiu let out a breath and said to Zhou Zhongwei:

"All the same, Elder Zhong, don't take it too easily! You had better telephone to the police station and have constables come to drive them off!"

"Just so; that is my mind too. And besides, your good lady being gravely ill, a fright like this is scarcely fitting for her!"

"No matter! My wife is very deaf. And, to make a joke of it, my wife's life-insurance policy runs out the day after tomorrow, so that if some mishap really did befall today, it would only be Heaven not failing a man who has taken pains. Ha, ha! — But they have been making their din this good while, and their throats have gone hoarse; out of pity for them I'll let them off and send them home first. And for that I must lean on a word from you two, Elder Yin and Elder Jun! We are all old friends — lend me a hand this once!"

"Elder Zhong! What game is it you're playing, after all? To make jokes in front of the workers — that is a very dangerous thing!"

Chen Junyi said it hurriedly and got to his feet. Zhu Yinqiu did likewise. The clamour outside the gate died suddenly down.

"I'll go bail that not half a hair of you two shall be harmed! Only, whatever I say, do you two but agree to it, and I shall be grateful past all telling!"

Zhou Zhongwei, still unwilling to speak out plainly, laughed his "ha, ha" and went himself to open the great gate, calling out again and again:

"No uproar! No uproar! Eat more, and talk less: don't you know that old, old saying? Now there'll be rice for everyone to eat!"

But outside the gate, among the ten workers' delegates, there was now one man more. It was an armed constable, keeping order there. When the ten delegates saw Zhou Zhongwei come out, they surged forward in a mass and hemmed him round, all clamouring at once in a babble of tongues. Old hand though Zhou Zhongwei was, who had come through many a great set-to, this time even he lost his nerve; in his haste the sweat ran down his whole head and his face went scarlet, and he could not for the life of him think what to say first. He would have fled, too, but now there was no way out.

"No wrangling now! Hear what Boss Zhou has to say, and then you may speak! Have you not a scrap of manners?"

The armed constable too shouldered his way into the ring of the ten delegates, bawling aloud. Zhou Zhongwei took a little heart at once; he put up a hand and wiped a fistful of sweat from his brow, swallowed a mouthful of spittle, and then, raising his voice, cried out:

"Listen, all of you! I, your boss, am a Chinese, and you too are Chinese, and Chinese must help Chinese! What have you come for? You want me to start the works! Just so — if the works don't start, you will starve, and I, your boss, will starve too! Make no more disturbance, and I too will start the works. Thanks be to Heaven and the Bodhisattvas, I, your boss, have this very moment engaged two Gods of Wealth — there, they are the ones sitting in the wing-room! I, your boss, have borrowed the money, and tomorrow the works shall start!"

Zhou Zhongwei could not help laughing his "ha, ha" once more; but, having spoken too fast, he was short of breath, and after no more than a few peals he opened his mouth wide and gasped, his eyes bulging. Some among the delegates still had their faces set fierce as tigers, yet said nothing. A few ran in at the gate to see whether there really were any Gods of Wealth in the wing-room. Zhou Zhongwei caught sight of them and hastily backed in at the gate too, and, gasping still though he was, called toward the wing-room:

"President Chen, Manager Zhu, be pleased to step this way and meet the workers' delegates of my humble factory!"

Zhu Yinqiu, holding back a laugh, paced slowly out into the reception hall and stood facing outward, knitting his brows. Chen Junyi came out after him, but wearing a smile.

The ten delegates all fell silent of a sudden. Among themselves they signalled with their eyes, as though debating whether these two were real Gods of Wealth.

"There now, there now; Boss Zhou has promised to start the works — go home, all of you! Brawling and bawling is against the regulations! Make more of a row, and it's off to the station with you!"

So the armed constable bawled sternly outside the gate. But Zhou Zhongwei, on the contrary, held the constable back, and, smiling, cupped his hands to the ten delegates and said:

"I truly must thank you! Had it not been for that racket of yours, President Chen and Manager Zhu would not have lent me the money! Now all is well — tomorrow, for certain, the works shall start. I, your boss, am a man whose every word is as good as said and done!"

"There's no hiding from us, wherever you go!"

As the ten delegates withdrew, Xiao Sanzi, walking last, flung out this curse and spat, full at the great gate of the Zhou mansion.

The three bosses went back into the wing-room and laughed with one voice; Zhou Zhongwei, as though he really had raised a sum of money, wagged his fat head and paced to and fro in high content. He had all along two roads in mind by which he might borrow; and now, in his elation, his taste for "play-acting" broke out afresh; with a glance at Zhu Yinqiu he thought within himself, "This one we'll make the Eastern-Ocean taipan," and could not help laughing his "ha, ha" once more. But before his laughter had ceased, Chen Junyi said, of a sudden and very gravely:

"Elder Zhong, you must after all think of some plan. Today you have made a joke of it and coaxed them away; but tomorrow they'll come and make their racket again — won't that be a plague!"

"Quite so. When they come again tomorrow, they'll surely not be as civilised as just now — Elder Zhong, you must guard against it beforehand!"

So Zhu Yinqiu put in, knitting his brows. But Zhou Zhongwei felt that Zhu Yinqiu, knitting his brows so, was the more like that Eastern-Ocean taipan, and could not help crying out with a laugh:

"A plan, you say? Ah! — The plan rests with you two!"

Chen Junyi and Zhu Yinqiu were both taken aback — the more so as Zhou Zhongwei's look was not that of a man joking. And Zhou Zhongwei, setting on his most solemn face, went on:

"I reckoned it all up long ago: I've had my fill of being a boss, and whoever wants this broken-down factory, I'll make it over to him; the pity is only that the Swedish Match Trust has no mind to run a factory in China — otherwise I'd be glad enough to work with them. Just now I told you two I had a few serious words to talk over; well, here come the serious words. For the present I've hit on two roads: one road is a certain Eastern-Ocean taipan I've long been acquainted with, who is willing to help; the other road is the Yizhong Company. I am a Chinese, and whenever I see any good thing going I'd always sooner draw it toward my own folk; and besides, Wang Hefu, Sun Jiren, Wu Sunfu, the three of them, are old friends, and a good turn should be sold to a face one knows. That is my thought — only I don't know how it stands with them. Ah, Elder Yin, Elder Jun, you two work very well together with the Yizhong Company: how does it look to you — will they take my account, or no?"

"Oh — so this is the move Elder Zhong means to make? Is it that you'd let the factory, or sell it outright? For they'll take no mortgage!"

Chen Junyi answered slowly, with a glance at Zhu Yinqiu. But these words of Zhou Zhongwei's had stirred up Zhu Yinqiu's grievances; and Zhu Yinqiu, suspicious by nature, took it moreover that Zhou Zhongwei was mocking him on purpose, so that he only knitted his brows and heaved a sigh and said nothing.

"Either will do! Either will do! We're all of us old acquaintance in any case, and easy to deal with!"

Zhou Zhongwei cried out again and again, as though Chen Junyi were the very representative of the Yizhong Company, and this idle chat of theirs a formal negotiation. Chen Junyi smiled, thinking Zhou Zhongwei too impatient by half, yet pitying him too with all his heart; and so he said again, very earnestly:

"Elder Zhong, you must surely know that all the real power in the Yizhong Company lies in Wu Sunfu's hands? How shrewd this Wu the Third is, how high he holds his eye! A piece of business you bring to his own door, he'll pick the more holes in! And if he takes a fancy to your factory and means to work you, then it's another matter altogether; he'll turn his ruthless hand to pressing you, and drive you to your wits' end, till at the last you must go and beg of him! Elder Yin here has suffered his high-handedness —"

"You had far better go to the Eastern-Ocean taipan! To treat with Wu the Third is downright like begging meat from a tiger's mouth!"

Zhu Yinqiu broke in ahead of him, and heaved a bitter sigh.

Zhou Zhongwei's whole bellyful of fond calculations was upset at a stroke. His face flushed red, and his two eyes opened as wide as brass bells. He had, in fact, treated with that Eastern-Ocean taipan first, and it was only because the terms were too harsh that he had bethought him of the Yizhong Company; and now, hearing this tune from Chen Junyi and Zhu Yinqiu, he was thrown into no small dismay. For the first time in his born days he was unable to laugh his "ha, ha"! Yet he did not despair. So long as there was the least profit in it for him, he would not mind swallowing a little insult. He wiped a fistful of sweat from his brow and, his face all woebegone, asked again in a fluster:

"But, Elder Jun! This letting of a factory — how is it done? Are both your own factories let out in that way?"

"Just so, both of us have let out. Elder Yin handed his factory over and troubles himself with nothing at all, drawing a rent of five hundred taels a month. As for me, I manage the factory's affairs as before, under the style of general manager, and they pay me a salary; to the outside world I pass for the boss still, but in fact I must ask Wang Hefu's leave in every last thing — and that's nothing to speak of, for Wang Hefu is a courteous fellow, and a good friend! My factory buildings and machinery are reckoned at no rent; that goes another way: for each lot of goods the factory turns out, I may draw, on the value, ten parts in a thousand, as depreciation on the buildings, the machinery and the plant. This was all their notion — you see how shrewd they are!"

"That way of letting out of yours — I'm twelve parts in favour of it, in favour!"

Zhou Zhongwei sprang up with a shout; his hopes had come alive again, and again he could laugh. But Zhu Yinqiu, at his side, coldly poured a ladle of cold water over Zhou Zhongwei's fit of joy; he said:

"I'm afraid you'll be out of favour with it again in a moment, Elder Zhong! Guess what salary Elder Jun draws? Two hundred and fifty dollars! The general manager who runs a silk-weaving mill of a bare 300 hands draws a salary of no more than 250! Boss Wu and his people really have the face to name such a figure! Elder Jun, you are a very '250' yourself — I, now, wouldn't do it!"

"There's no help for it! Shut the factory up, leave the machines unused, and they rust; that's good machinery thrown clean away! I have my own hard case, and can only let them get a little the better of me! And besides, with myself minding things inside, one's mind is easier after all. Ah, Elder Zhong, don't you say so?"

Zhou Zhongwei gave a nod, but said nothing; over his fat face there gathered, for once, a look of gravity — he was turning it over in his mind. This way of Chen Junyi's, of letting out the silk mill, had greatly moved the heart of our Boss Zhou. Above all that point of remaining general manager as before, and passing to the outside world for the boss still — that Zhou Zhongwei mightily envied. Nor was it a matter of vanity alone; there was a great weight of economic meaning in it too. That Zhou Zhongwei's empty frame had been able these several years to hold itself up at all was owing, half of it, to the standing of a match-factory boss that was a name without a substance; and were he once to lose even this empty signboard, then, with every sort of debt closing in on him at once, Zhou Zhongwei would be truly finished, and could never laugh again.

And so, then and there, Zhou Zhongwei made up his mind to try his luck with the Yizhong Company, fully resolved to become "a second Chen Junyi"!

He sprang up all at once, clapping his hands, and cried to Chen Junyi:

"What you say is exactly right — leave the machines standing idle and they rust! Doesn't the Guangdong match-trade say it most feelingly in that memorial: that in these last years the match factories of the Chinese have failed by five-tenths and more! I am a Chinese, and I ought to protect China's home-industry factories! The Eastern-Ocean taipan would buy me out at a heavy price — and though he's an Eastern-Ocean man, and China and Japan have ever been on friendly terms, of the same script and the same stock, no high-nosed Swedish Match King or the like — yet how could I bring myself to it? This profit of mine I would sooner make a present of to the Yizhong Company: Chinese should look after Chinese! Enough — I mean to go this instant and have a talk with Wu Sunfu!"

"Why put yourself to it, Elder Zhong! I can tell you without casting the lots: this errand of yours will come to nothing, and you'll only get a bellyful of vexation for your pains!"

Once more Zhu Yinqiu coldly poured a ladleful of water over Zhou Zhongwei's fit of joy. Zhou Zhongwei gave a start of dismay, and his face flushed red. Chen Junyi hastened to put in:

"It's worth going to try. The Yizhong has lately taken in eight small factories at one gulp — this is their line of trade! Only, Elder Zhong, I'd advise you not to go to Wu the Third; better treat with Wang Hefu instead, for Wang Hefu is the easier man to talk to. And he is the general manager of the Yizhong Company besides."

Zhou Zhongwei let out a breath and nodded again and again. His whole heart being set on becoming "a second Chen Junyi," he found Chen Junyi's every word pleasant to the ear and full of reason. But one like Zhu Yinqiu, a black-mouthed old crow that opened its beak only to croak ill-luck — that Zhou Zhongwei found truly stifling to hear. He shot a glance at Zhu Yinqiu, and of a sudden could not help laughing once more, saying to himself within: "The more I look, the more he's the very image of that Eastern-Ocean taipan! An Eastern-Ocean man! A bad lot!"

At one o'clock in the afternoon Zhou Zhongwei, big with hope, met Wang Hefu in the manager's room on the second floor of the Yizhong Company. Before the Chinese typewriter by the window sat a young typist, the machine clacking away at an even beat. Wang Hefu's look was somewhat harassed; his ear on Zhou Zhongwei's statement, his eyes slid again and again toward the typist, as though he grudged him the slowness of his work. All at once the telephone bell in the confidential room next door rang faintly, and thereupon a clerk came up to Wang Hefu, stood to attention, gave him a fixed-eye salute, and said:

"Will the general manager take the telephone!"

"Forgive me, Elder Zhong — I'll go and answer the telephone, and then we'll talk on."

Heedless that Zhou Zhongwei was just at the crucial point of his speech, Wang Hefu withdrew and was gone, and the door of the confidential room shut with a bang.

Zhou Zhongwei let out a breath, wiped the sweat from his brow, and took up his tea for a sip. He found the room uncommonly hot; from the moment he came in it had been like being shut in a steamer-basket, and his fat body did nothing but sweat, so that speaking cost him the more effort. The wind of the electric fan, too, blew hot and fretful. He stood up and took a turn round the room, and came at last to stand behind the typist, looking on idly. A notice was already half typed out; and though Zhou Zhongwei had at first no mind to read it close, one line in the middle of it leapt of a sudden to his eye; he stared at it fixedly a while, and the ball of hope within him shrank little by little, till it was all but snuffed out. What the notice declared was this: that the eight factories should for a time run but half a day's work, and cut down production.

Back in his seat again, Zhou Zhongwei found the sweat on his brow thicker yet; but that merry heart of his was as though frozen stiff, all its life gone out of it. Mechanically he wiped the sweat away, his eyes stretched wide and nailed to the little door of the confidential room over yonder, longing for Wang Hefu to come out quickly.

Five minutes went by, and ten minutes went by; Wang Hefu did not appear. Patient as Zhou Zhongwei was, he too began now to taste what it is to sit out in the cold. The typist had finished the work in hand, and, with a lazy stretch, poked his head out at the window to watch the modish girls and the big-bellied merchants passing in the street.

Zhou Zhongwei could bear it no longer, and was moreover in a fluster with the heat, and so had a mind to go and call Wang Hefu out; but in his haste he took the wrong way, and, hurrying to the spring-door that led out of the manager's room to the outside, put up his hand and gave it a push — only then did he perceive his mistake, and could not help laughing his "ha, ha" to himself. And that spring-door was, if you please, laughed open by him. A thick perfume struck his nostrils! Two smiling faces, a man's and a woman's, arm in arm — both of them known to Zhou Zhongwei: the man was Staff Officer Lei, the woman Xu Manli.

"Why! Staff Officer Lei! When did you get back to Shanghai? This is quite unlooked for!"

Zhou Zhongwei hailed them with a loud laugh, and all his bellyful of troubles was gone. Without waiting for Staff Officer Lei to answer, he made haste to greet Xu Manli too. In an instant that heart of his that had seemed frozen stiff was brimming with life once more, able to hatch a plan, able to burrow into every crack and cranny in search of a way out. From Xu Manli his thought flew at once to Zhao Botao, and to the story noised abroad that old Zhao had lately made another killing in the bonds; and, most important of all, flew at once to that long-current rumour that old Zhao was getting up some trust or other and buying up factories! The flame of hope blazed up warm and bright in Zhou Zhongwei's heart again. He blamed himself for being such a muddlehead as not to have thought sooner of this, the real God of Wealth!

By this time Wang Hefu had come out too, and after a word or two of civility drew Staff Officer Lei aside for a whispered talk, head to head. Zhou Zhongwei, his mind all a-turning with the new notion, seized the chance and threw himself into paying court to Xu Manli. His laughter startled the four walls. Xu Manli, with a pursed-lip smile, said:

"Mister Zhou, you do the honours for the host and entertain me — 'the Red-Headed Match,' and no false fame!"

Zhou Zhongwei laughed the harder for it; then of a sudden he put away his laughter and said very gravely:

"Miss Xu! There's a small matter I'd beg of you! No one but you can do it! I must and will ask your help — it's a very small thing."

"Oh — and what thing is that?"

"Ha, a very little matter. That match factory of mine has of late felt the pinch of the war, and I can't keep the money turning; —"

"Oh, oh! With a war on one's hands, a factory man has little to be merry about! But, Mister Zhou, you are the famous 'Red-Headed Match,' a face well known in the market — what have you to fear!"

"But this year is an exception, a real exception! The public loans and treasury notes have drained the market dry of hard silver, and word is that when a factory man wants to raise a hundred thousand or more, everyone only shakes his head. I'm truly a little stuck for the turning of it! My figure is not large. Fifty thousand would be best; and if not that, then twenty or thirty thousand would serve to patch things over. Miss Xu! Do, do help me!"

"Aiyoyo! Take counsel with me? You're joking! Eh?"

"Not at all, not at all — before you I'll not tell half a lie! I know that Zhao Botao is willing to lend money; the pity is only that my style of 'the Red-Headed Match' is an empty name, and that with this God of Wealth I have not so much as a nodding acquaintance! Today, by what luck I cannot tell, I've run into you, Miss Xu — it is the merit laid up by my ancestors! Do, then, put in a word to make us known to each other! One word from you is more potent than an imperial decree; let old Zhao but give a nod, and I, Zhou Zhongwei, am saved!"

Before Zhou Zhongwei had finished, the colour changed all at once on Xu Manli's rosy, pretty face. She threw him a sharp, cold look, as much as to say, "Why, you are simply making game of me!" and turned her head away, giving the upper half of her body a twist or two. Zhou Zhongwei saw that something was amiss, but could make neither head nor tail of it, and, putting out his tongue, dared say no more. After a moment Xu Manli turned her face back and, with a smile that was no smile, refused him:

"That scoundrel Zhao Botao! I'll have nothing to do with him! If you want to worm your way in with him, you must look elsewhere for a cleverer hand!"

At the words Zhou Zhongwei's heart gave a leap. A brand-new hope had suddenly burst too. That heart of his was as though stiffened again, at its wits' end. Xu Manli, swaying her slender waist, rose lightly to her feet and, as if in mockery, cast another sidelong glance at Zhou Zhongwei. Zhou Zhongwei, in a fluster, sprang up too, minded to make one last effort; but Xu Manli had already tripped gracefully away, and Wang Hefu came over, clapped Zhou Zhongwei on the shoulder, and said:

"Elder Zhong! We broke off but halfway in our talk just now, yet your errand I understand entirely. The founding aim of this company at the outset — the very thing we all fell to speaking of by chance that day of the funeral at the Wu mansion, as Elder Zhong well knows — was that we meant to lay the groundwork of an industrial bank, so that we of the industrial world might all have some means of accommodation among us. But things fell out, unlooked for, against our wish, and this present scope of ours is a very small one, that will not stretch to oblige a friend! Last month we took in eight factories; but at this moment, the war being still unfinished, with sales along the Yangtze at a standstill, and here in our own street the competition of the Eastern-Ocean mills to face, there was no help for it, and we have had to draw in our bounds and go over to half-day working. So that when Elder Zhong comes to us today, our heart is long indeed but our strength is short — we are sorry past all measure!"

"Ah! Chinese industry has indeed fallen a thousand fathoms at a drop! This half-year past, the flour trade of Tianjin was reckoned, at least, to be powerful and well-founded, seated in the first chair of all China; and yet at this moment, of the eight great mills of Tianjin, seven stand idle, and the one that is left shuts down every second or third day!"

Staff Officer Lei paced over to Zhou Zhongwei's side and put in his word. Zhou Zhongwei, the sweat soaking through him from head to foot, could not get his words out; the few he wrung out by main force sounded, even to his own ears, as though another had spoken them. Over and over he set forth that what he asked was no great matter, and that the market for his factory's goods was, on the contrary, a steady one, unaffected by the war.

"Elder Zhong, we are all of us factory men, like one family, and we know each other's sweets and bitters through and through. The truth is that our capital was never fully paid in, while our undertakings have been spread wide; the company is in no case to make any further loan on security."

"Then, Elder He, even the leasing arrangement, such as Elder Jun has for his silk mill — that too won't do?"

"Elder Zhong, had you come to talk of this a month ago, we should certainly have done your bidding; but now I must only beg you to forgive us!"

Wang Hefu refused him flat and clean, and looked at Zhou Zhongwei's sweating face with a wry smile.

Hope now utterly snuffed out, Zhou Zhongwei burst all at once into his loud "ha, ha," and, pointing with one hand at Staff Officer Lei and with the other at Wang Hefu, cried aloud:

"Hey there, hey there, do you remember? That day of old Mr. Wu's funeral! And Miss Xu Manli too! Remember? The dance on the billiard-table! Miss Xu lost her high-heeled satin shoe! Ha, ha! Now that was a play indeed, a dream indeed! — But, Hefu, what's all this of forgiving and not forgiving; we are old friends, what need of ceremony between us! I'll tell you an honest word: the factories of the Chinese must sooner or later all turn to walking corpses, and only an injection of a little foreign blood can bring them to life! Staff Officer Lei, don't you believe it? You wait and see! Ha, ha, Miss Xu, the big dining-table here is smooth enough still — come, dance us another round; make merry a day while the day is ours!"

Staff Officer Lei and Xu Manli both laughed, but Wang Hefu knitted his brows and changed colour. Truly, from that day of old Mr. Wu's funeral until now it had all been one great dream! Their one great dream of building up an enterprise! And now the waking from the dream was near at hand, was it not?

"It's getting late — be quick! Sunfu appointed two o'clock!"

So Xu Manli said to Wang Hefu and Staff Officer Lei with a pucker of her brows, casting, by design or none, another sidelong glance at Zhou Zhongwei. Zhou Zhongwei had no notion that she and the others had some secret business of their own afoot; but those three syllables, "two o'clock," struck upon his eardrum with peculiar force. He sprang up all at once, said a word of "farewell," and hurried off. On the staircase he was still laughing his "ha, ha" to himself. Before ever he was out of the Yizhong Company's gate he had made up his mind to go to that Eastern-Ocean taipan and beg of him an "injection of Eastern-Ocean blood"! Once more he was all elation. Seated in his rickshaw, he ran on thus in his thoughts: China and Japan have ever been on friendly terms, of the same script and the same stock, and are better after all than the high-noses; there's no road left for patriotism, so what's to be done! And besides, he is not the only one, either, to be hand-in-glove with the foreign merchants!

A motor-car, running at the top of a speed that set a new record for the year 1930, came up on him from behind, and in the twinkling of an eye was straight past and away ahead.

Zhou Zhongwei saw the three persons in that motor-car: Staff Officer Lei in the middle, Xu Manli on the left, Wang Hefu on the right. That these three should be tangled together in one place — there was likely some serious business toward, was there not? — such a notion flashed once more across Zhou Zhongwei's mind; but on the instant the Eastern-Ocean taipan reoccupied the whole of his consciousness. Smiling and nodding to himself, he settled on his final policy: that in anything whatever he might give ground, only the boss's title he must at all costs keep; for without that empty signboard, every debt would come pressing in at once, and he would be undone as before!

The next day, sure enough, Zhou Zhongwei's match factory started work once more. A brand-new set of factory rules, freshly promulgated and harsher than before, Zhou Zhongwei had copied out fair through the night; and two short men who could not speak much Shanghainese, newly added as technician and overseer, came along with Zhou Zhongwei too.

Zhou Zhongwei, his whole face wreathed in delight, hopped about like a toad, leading the two newcomers to take over the affairs of each department. And at the last he called together the whole factory's fifty or sixty workers and made them a speech:

"Yesterday I, your boss, promised you the works would start, and today they have started! I, your boss, am a man whose every word is as good as said and done! The factory runs at a loss, but carry it on I will, come what may; and why? First, because with the factory shut you have no rice to eat; you are Chinese, and I, your boss, am a Chinese too, and a Chinese boss must help Chinese workers! Second, because there are far too many foreign matches of the imported sort in the market, and the silver dollars of us Chinese go running into foreigners' pockets — several hundred million of them in a year! We are a home-industry factory, and you are Chinese, and now that you turn out native goods, Chinese workers too must help their Chinese boss! When the cost of making runs high, the goods won't sell; and your helping me means only that you draw a few coppers' less in wages, and when this factory of ours makes money, we'll all be merry together! A Chinese boss, though he runs at a loss, will not shut his factory, but will help the Chinese worker; and Chinese workers too must work for dear life to bring the cost down and help the Chinese boss! There now — long live the home-industry factory, long live, ten thousand times ten thousand years!"

By the last few sentences of his speech this fat Zhou Zhongwei was already sorely out of breath, and could scarcely bring it to a close; the last cry of "long live, ten thousand times ten thousand years," which he forced out to the end, had something of a wail in it. On his flushed fat face, fat as it was, the blue veins stood out corded; and sweat-beads big as soya-beans rolled down from his brow.

The fifty or sixty workers, like so many stone statues, showed neither expression nor sound. Zhou Zhongwei, panting, forced a wry smile, waved his hand, and dismissed his "temporary lecture-meeting." Before long the motors were droning, and on the machines the steel belts bore the match-sticks along, ranged in little bundles, close and trim, exactly like cartridge-belts, rolling and rolling with a rumble. And Zhou Zhongwei's thoughts went rolling too, far away. The whole of his past life came rolling, one scene after another, before his eyes: first a comprador, then an independent boss in his own right, then a comprador once more — a comprador in altered guise, a boss in name only from this day on! One dream; one turn of the wheel!

Zhou Zhongwei suddenly laughed aloud, "ho, ho." Come what might, he could always laugh, and often.
