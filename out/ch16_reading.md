## Reminiscences of the Du Household

{d} Narrated by Huang Guodong; set down by Luo Liquan

### I

I was twenty-four the year I entered Du Yuesheng's household as its chief accountant, and that is now several decades ago. That I could get into the Du residence at all I owed to my father.

My father, Huang Wenxiang, styled Wenxiang, came up in the fruit trade. In those days my grandfather kept a fruit firm, the Yuanyu, on Lima Road in Nanshi, Shanghai — at the northern end of what used to be called the Great Wharf, now named the Yangjiadu Wharf — and through a go-between he had my father taken on as an apprentice at the Zhang Hengda fruit firm. The "dangshou" — the manager — of the Zhang Hengda at that time was Du Yuesheng's uncle, and it was to him that my father bound himself as master.

The fruit firms that ran up from the Great Wharf in those years were the Zhang Hengda, the Tang Hengtai, the Zhang Jinji, the Pan Yuansheng, the Yuanyu, and others; near Shiliupu, on Lima Road, were the Baoda, the Liang Dongsheng, the Yida, and others; and within the old French Concession bounds at Shiliupu were the Tongmao, the Xiangmao, the Hecheng, the Yuan Xiangmao, the Wu Shunfa, and others. My father rose at the Zhang Hengda from apprentice to junior book-keeper. It was then that Du Yuesheng too came to the Zhang Hengda as an apprentice; but he lasted no great while, for he had no heart for learning the trade. He was forever off in the streets idling; he would eat in the restaurants and, when he could not pay, run up a tab, and when the restaurants came to the firm to collect, the firm had to settle for him. When a rickshaw carrying a woman passed on the road before the shop, Du would pelt it with rotten fruit, insulting and pawing at her, and some of the women would come to the Zhang Hengda and raise a great row. All this made his uncle thoroughly displeased, and he got Du placed as an apprentice at the Baoda fruit firm, under the Baoda manager as master. Before long, his old faults unmended, the Baoda manager put him off too.

After losing his place, Du Yuesheng drifted about Shiliupu. Shiliupu was then one of Shanghai's chief commercial quarters and a water wharf, its streets thronged and its idlers many. Du first took as his "old man" a certain Chen Shichang, who ran a tally-game and sold sweet rice-wine; later he came to know Huang Jinrong, a detective in the French Concession police. Huang had Du go out and levy wharf-money from the junks moored at the Shiliupu docks, and collect the monthly "regulation money" from the low brothels — the "flower-and-smoke rooms" — the opium dens (the "swallows' nests"), the gambling tables and the like nearby. Everything he took in he handed over to Huang Jinrong, who split off a share for Du; and in this way Du became Huang Jinrong's right-hand man.

About this time Huang was running gambling tables in the French Concession and dealing in opium besides, and in all of it he took Du in as a partner, or had Du front for him; Du ran gambling tables and opium of his own as well, and little by little grew rich, and after that opened the Zhonghui Bank.

Once Du had money, the first thing he did was to raise the Du family ancestral shrine at Gaoqiao in Pudong. When the shrine was completed in 1931, the leading men of every circle in Shanghai went to offer their congratulations, and my father among them. Du Yuesheng, catching sight of my father, had a trusted houseboy quietly pass him word to come a week later to his residence on Rue Wagner (today's Ninghai West Road), as there was a matter to discuss. My father went as bidden, and Du had Yang Yusheng, who had kept the accounts till then, hand over all the keys to my father and told him to take over as chief accountant at once; and my father took up the post. Later, when my father died of a stroke, Du wanted me to carry on his work, and I did so, right up to the liberation of Shanghai.

### II

In those years Du Yuesheng had two residences in Shanghai, one on Rue Wagner and one on Route Doumer (today's Donghu Road). The Route Doumer house had been built for him by Jin Tingsun out of the profits of his franchise to issue the aviation lottery, and made him a present of; Du never lived in it himself, and after the victory over Japan sold it to the American consulate for six hundred thousand US dollars. The Rue Wagner house was a gift from Huang Jinrong, who had grown rich peddling opium and the like; on Rue Wagner he built two houses, each of three bays and two depths — the front depth a two-story Chinese shikumen, the rear a three-bay, three-story Western villa — and gave the eastern one, No. 212, to Zhang Xiaolin, and the western one, No. 216, to Du Yuesheng.

Du Yuesheng had lived before at Minguo Li on Minguo Road; on moving into Rue Wagner he threw the three ground-floor rooms of the front, Chinese-style house together into one great hall, and lodged his principal wife, née Shen, in the three rooms above. The ground floor of the Western house behind he divided into three: the front of the west wing was the secretaries' room and the back the antique room, where Du would discuss weighty matters; the middle was the parlor; and the east wing was the Western-style dining room, fitted with Du's own writing desk, an opium couch, sofas and the like, where he received ordinary callers and where the chief accountant's office, the meals and the telephone room were as well. The whole second floor was given over to his second wife, Chen Guoying (the Fifth), and the whole third floor to his third wife, Sun Peihao (the Seventh). His principal wife, née Shen, a Suzhou woman, had been married to him by Huang Jinrong, and bore him the son Weifan (milk-name Linbao). Chen Guoying, also a Suzhou woman, bore three sons — Weiyuan (milk-name Genfa), Weihan (milk-name Ertu), and Weining (milk-name Santu). Sun Peihao, a niece of the Suzhou-ballad singer Xiao Guisu and likewise a Suzhou woman, bore two sons, Weiping and Weixin. His fourth wife, Yao Yulan, was a Beijing woman: her mother was Xiao Lanying, who sang the old-man roles in Peking opera, and her father a clown-role player known as "Seven Lamps." Yao Yulan had herself been a Peking-opera singer, and after marrying Du bore two sons and two daughters — Weishan and Weisong, Meiru and Meixia.

After the victory over Japan, Yao Yulan went further and brought Meng Xiaodong to Shanghai to live with Du. Seeing that all Du's other wives were Suzhou women and she the only northerner, isolated and without a faction, Yao had meant to draw Meng in as an ally; but once Meng came to Shanghai she won Du Yuesheng's favor, and instead pushed Yao Yulan aside, to Yao's bottomless regret.

I have seen a book, a life of Du Yuesheng, that says his fourth concubine had several secret trysts with a cousin come up from her home district; that someone informed on her to Du; and that Du sent one of his Four Guardians to hack the cousin's legs off and lock the woman up. There is nothing to this. Yao Yulan's conduct was proper, she had no cousin in Shanghai, and she was never once confined. There is a passage, too, in the pingtan ballad "The Death of Xu Dangui," which has it that Du Yuesheng's third concubine carried on loosely outside, that Du had the man's hands and feet cut off and turned him into a "living winter-melon," and that Zhang Chunfan took Qi Zaiyu as his master and Qi Zaiyu took Du Yuesheng as his, so that the teller had the thing at first hand. This too is untrue. The third concubine, Sun Peihao, was always perfectly correct; she has lived to a great age and is now in America, and her two sons are both in business there, each with a certain standing in society. Nor was Qi Zaiyu ever a disciple of Du's, for neither before the victory nor after was he yet of the standing to take Du as his master.

Besides these masters of the house, the Du residence kept a great many hired hands. The accounts office, myself apart, had Yang Yunxin, Qiu Cengshou and Zhao Qinbo — four of us in all. I was responsible for drawing money from the banks and paying out all the household's expenses, for handling the incoming and outgoing correspondence, and for receiving important callers. Yang Yunxin dealt with the cards of every wedding, funeral and celebration that came in, sent gifts and letters everywhere at the proper seasons, wrote up the reply-register, saw to the telephone, water and electric repairs and installations and to the various newspapers, disbursed the petty cash and the year's-end and festival gratuities, kept the great hall clean, and looked after the callers' chauffeurs and guards. Qiu Cengshou kept the food accounts, handled the hiring and transfer of the kitchen staff, and saw to the paying out each month of the "relief" sums with which Du Yuesheng relieved the destitute old and alone. (An old solitary, once introduced and granted a "relief token," could draw against it each month a relief payment of anywhere from ten to fifty yuan; these solitaries were for the most part the families of detectives and constables who had died in the two Concessions, the British and the French.) He also wrote up the reply-register when letters went out, and so on. Zhao Qinbo had charge of taking the "young masters" out to amuse themselves, and looked after the telephone and utility bills and all the clocks, great and small.

There was, besides, one Wan Molin, in charge of the houseboys, the chauffeurs, the cooks, the gatemen, the guard and the rest. Every telephone call from outside to Du was taken first by Wan and then passed to Du, and when Du telephoned out it was Wan who got the connection and then handed him the line. Wan Molin was illiterate, but his memory was extraordinary: he could hold in his head the 100-odd telephone numbers of Du's relatives and friends, his disciples, offices and enterprises, so that Du had only to bid him ring some place and he could dial the number offhand, without ever opening the telephone book.

Wan Molin's original name was Mulin. He had come to work in the Du household in place of his elder brother, Wan Zhaotang (styled Duxin). Wan Zhaotang had been a disciple in my father's fruit firm and later went to work in the Du household; after some years, having laid a little money by, he took to opium, and finding it more than he could bear to work in the Du house day and night, asked Du to place him in the opium company and let his younger brother Wan Mulin take his place. Everyone thought the name "Mulin" ill-sounding, and later Yang Du changed the character "mu" to "mo."

The Du secretariat had four secretaries in all. Weng Zuoqing (styled Zuoqing) was a disciple of Zhang Xiaolin's, introduced to Du by Zhang. Qiu Fangmo was introduced by Chen Qun. Wang Youtang — who had once been a secretary in the Songhu Police Bureau — was introduced by Liu Chunpu. Hu Xuwu was introduced by Huang Yanpei; he had formerly been a secretary at the Shanghai Local Association.

Du's close bodyguards when he went out also numbered four. Lu Guicai was a disciple of Zhang Xiaolin's, had been an officer in the old army, and was known in society as a Yangzhou man-about-town called Lu the Pockmarked; he took disciples on a great scale, 1,000 or 2,000 of them, lived at Nanyang Bridge, and ran the Weiyang Grand Stage, the Rongguixiang cigarette-wholesaling house and the like. Chen Qinhe was a Taizhou man-about-town, likewise with no few disciples, who ran on the side the Hengya story-house and the Hengya theater in Hengmao Li on Xizang Road, the First Hotel at Baxianqiao, the Hengya teahouse on East Zilaihuo Street, the Second Hotel at Baxianqiao, and the Tongle theater and Tongle inn on Shunchang Road. Chen Jifan was the more educated of them and could speak French; he had been introduced by Zhang Yishu, a Chinese member of the board at the French Concession consulate. Du Yuesheng thought him the more honest, and after the war broke out, once he had gone to Hong Kong, sent for him to come. Gao Huaili was a northerner, had been a plainclothes man in the French Concession police, and had held such posts as inspector in the Songhu Police Bureau.

The Du family had many motorcars and still more drivers. Du's own chauffeur was Wang Baoyu (milk-name Aju), a Wuxi man, who had charge of all the drivers in the Du house and had two assistants under him. The four wives' chauffeurs numbered eight, the children's four, and the odd-job cars two, so that there were more than ten drivers in all.

The kitchen cooks were of several groups: two of the Suzhou group, two of the Yangzhou group, three of the local group, two of the Beijing group, and three under-cooks — more than ten in all here too.

There were, besides, four night-watch guards, six gatemen, and two rear-lane road-patrol guards. The tea-servers who received Du's guests in the great hall (called the head tea-servers) were four, and there was one man who did nothing but prepare the opium pipes. The opium-preparer was named Yu Fangxun (styled Yongfu); he had come up from a fruit stall at Shiliupu and was a noted man-about-town. Tattooed all over, he took disciples on a great scale and had held such posts as head inspector of the New City God Temple and head inspector of the Shanghai Cotton Goods Exchange. He had been longest of all in the Du house, and Du Yuesheng reckoned it a mark of great face that he could keep a man of this kind to prepare his opium. His manner was habitually free and easy, careless of ceremony: even when Huang Jinrong or Zhang Xiaolin came he would grin and banter without the least restraint, and would often crack jokes when he saw the daughters of the house, and Du let it pass. He had two assistants who helped him prepare the pipes. There were odd-job men besides — two to boil water and sprinkle the courtyards, two to tend the cooling plant, tea-servers to sweep the light-well and the great hall and to carry messages; three garden hands; and more than twenty women servants, under the charge of Du's widowed elder cousin. Each wife's quarters had two menservants besides, to mop the floors and wipe the windows. The domestic and odd-job staff of the Du house were, in truth, a huge army.

The guests who came regularly to the Du house included Huang Yanpei, Zhang Yishu, Zhang Shizhao, and Chen Qun — this last, when Yang Hu was Songhu Garrison Commander, had been head of his political department, and was afterward principal of the Zhengshi Middle School that Du Yuesheng ran (Du was the board chairman and put several thousand yuan a month into the school); during the War of Resistance Chen Qun served as the puppet governor of Jiangsu, and killed himself after the victory. Then there were Liu Chunpu, Yang Du, and the Hongmen big brother Gao Shikui (who came by car to the Du house every day, his whole living met by Du); the lawyers Qin Liankui, Jiang Yiping, Wang Yintai, Lu Tongdong, Zhu Wende, Wang Sizeng and the rest; from the business and political worlds, Wen Lanting, Qian Xinzhi, Wang Xiaolai, Xi Qiaqing, Liu Hongsheng, Pan Gongzhan, Xu Jiqing, Wu Kaixian, Yang Guanbei, Jin Tingsun, Yang Zhixiong and others; and, with Du's disciples Zhu Xuefan, Lu Jingshi, Tang Shichang, Zhang Rongchu, Xu Maotang, Luo Qinghua, Wang Xianqing, Shao Ziying and the rest, no fewer than one or two hundred a day. Add the callers' guards and drivers, and the place was in a truly ceaseless bustle.

With so vast a staff to keep and so many guests to entertain, the Du household's outlays were staggering. The secretaries Weng Zuoqing, Wang Youtang and Qiu Fangmo drew three hundred yuan a month each; the honorary secretaries Yang Du, Zhang Shizhao, Huang Yanpei, Chen Qun, Liu Chunpu and the like were sent five hundred a month; a senior chauffeur got fifty a month and a junior thirty; the bodyguard Lu Guicai got 150 a month, Chen Qinhe a hundred, and the others, Chen Jifan and the rest, eighty each; gatemen, ordinary tea-servers and plumbing-and-heating men got sixty a month, the dining-room tea-servers a hundred, the head cook eighty, and the rest fifty all round; the women servants twenty a month all round; the night-watch guards eighty a month. I too was given two hundred a month, Yang Yunxin one hundred, and Qiu Cengshou and Zhao Qinbo eighty each; the crew of the Yuebao launch drew wages of some five hundred a month. And the caretaking and service staff at the Du shrine at Gaoqiao, the Yuebao villa on Moganshan, the Du villa at Hangzhou, the Suzhou villa and other such places called for great outlays besides.

As the War of Resistance grew daily more tense and Du prepared to leave Shanghai, he had me settle up the bank accounts. In all he owed more than 5,700,000 yuan — of which 2,400,000 was borrowed from the Bank of China and the Bank of Communications; 300,000 from Mrs. Jian, née Chen Jichen, the daughter-in-law of Jian Zhaonan, boss of the Nanyang Brothers Tobacco Company; and 2,000,000-odd owed to the Zhonghui, the Tongshang, the Minhua and other banks — while his stocks, securities, real estate and the like were reckoned at under 2,000,000. These debts were not cleared in full until Du returned to Shanghai after the victory, when the change in the currency wiped them out. During his time in Chongqing, Du, through his contact with Xu Caicheng in Shanghai, had cotton goods and other supplies carried by roundabout routes from the occupied zone into the rear, and made a great fortune; and it was then that he truly became a rich and powerful boss.

### III

Du's rise had to do with the secret societies. As I understand it, the Hongmen was of no great strength in Shanghai; most of its members were northerners, for the brotherhood had first flourished in the north, and in Shanghai the greater part of them were the proprietors and hands of barbershops, restaurants, bathhouses and teahouses. The Green Gang's power in Shanghai was the greater: from high officials and great merchants down to peddlers and porters, all had their Green Gang men. The most famous were the "three big bosses" — Huang Jinrong, Du Yuesheng and Zhang Xiaolin. Below them were such men as Jin Tingsun (proprietor of the Qiamao Air-Conditioning Company, the Huangjin Grand Theater and the Xiaoyaochi bathhouse); Ma Xiangsheng (proprietor of the Jinmen Grand Theater); Gao Xinbao (proprietor of the Lido Ballroom); Xie Baosheng (proprietor of Ciro's Ballroom, the Grand Stage, the Carter bathhouse and the like); Rui Qingrong (proprietor of the Xinguang Grand Theater, who made a point of taking arts-world figures as his disciples, men like Shi Chunxuan); Yang Shunquan (nicknamed "Fish-monger Little Shun," who kept a fish shop at Shiliupu, was the fish tyrant of the day, and ran the Ximen bathhouse at Laoximen besides); Fan Kaitai (nicknamed "Ebony Kaitai," who kept a timber firm); Fan Huichun (proprietor of the Shengyang Film Company and manager of the Far East Stadium — these last two being Nanshi men, reckoned elders in the Green Gang, who had come up at the City God Temple, and whom Huang Jinrong had known when he was an apprentice at a picture-mounting shop there); Gu Jiatang (also called "Garden Agen," once a flower-gardener at the Hardoon Garden, who later grew rich on Du Yuesheng's coattails, built a great garden villa on Hart Road called "Jialu," owned 100-odd lane-houses, and put up the Gu Family Garden on Great Western Road); Fan Hengde (also called "Ahu-lang," who kept a wine firm and co-ran the Grand Stage with Xie Baosheng); Xu Desheng (a Yangzhou man, called "the Yangzhou Du Yuesheng," who took disciples on a great scale in Yangzhou); Yuan Baoshan (who had once run the Dongnan Grand Theater, now the Nanshi Cinema); Ye Zhuoshan (a rich young man-about-town, fond of riding, who kept three horses at the racecourse); Dai Buxiang (manager of the Great China Hotel); and Huang Zhenshi (who kept a fish shop, was a magnate of the fresh-fish trade, and ran the Hongyunlou restaurant on East Jinling Road).

Among Huang Jinrong's famous pupils were Ding Yongchang (milk-name "Pheasant Ada," manager of the Great World); Gu Zhuxuan (proprietor of the Tianchan Stage); Tang Jiapeng and Chen Rongsheng (the two of whom together ran the Dalichun restaurant beside the Great World gate, until, falling out over the profits, Chen Rongsheng had men beat Tang Jiapeng to death); Fan Liangbo (who lived entirely off taking disciples); and Xia Lianliang (manager of the Xinguang Grand Theater).

Among Du Yuesheng's famous disciples were Jiang Zhaoming (nicknamed "the Xuantong Emperor," who ran the gambling house at No. 181 Avenue Foch and took all manner of men-about-town as disciples, a great many of them); Pu Xianyuan (also called "Little Aben," who took arts-world people as disciples, most of all the players of one-man comic opera); and Tang Shichang (manager of the Shen Bao and the Xinwen Bao, who took the reporters of every paper as his disciples and controlled the Shanghai news business). He had pupils besides in banking, in the customs, among famous Peking-opera actors, in the transport departments and elsewhere.

That Du Yuesheng could force his way in as one of the Green Gang's three big bosses, and even come from behind to overtop Huang Jinrong, had a turning point behind it. He had begun as Huang Jinrong's assistant, and his standing in the gang was far below Huang's. In the 1920s, Lu Xiaojia, son of the Zhejiang military governor Lu Yongxiang, went to the Gongwutai to see Lu Lanchun perform, and when something displeased him let out a jeer, for which Huang Jinrong dealt him a slap. Two days later the Songhu Defense Commissioner's office sent troops from Minguo Road into the French Concession, surrounded the Gongwutai, seized Huang Jinrong and locked him up in the Defense Commissioner's office. This move took the French Concession authorities by surprise; everyone reckoned that for Chinese troops to dare break the rules and enter the Concession to seize a man showed great resolve — that they meant to put Huang Jinrong in his grave — so that not a soul dared come forward to get him out. It was then that Du Yuesheng stepped boldly forth, raised a huge sum, dealt personally with the Defense Commissioner He Fenglin, and after much toing and froing at last got Huang Jinrong out on bail. This affair greatly raised Du's standing within the gang, and Huang Jinrong, out of gratitude, did all he could to push Du up as well. At the same time Du struck up a friendship with He Fenglin, through whom he came to know a number of warlords, raised his own price, and opened a new channel for peddling opium besides, greatly swelling his income. At that time the opium deals he did in a single year with just one northern warlord came to as much as several million yuan.

Once he had money, Du set about cultivating men in politics, striving to reach the upper reaches of society, and began to run banks, factories and other enterprises. After the fighting of January 28, when the new French consul took up his post, the French comprador Zhu Baosan and his son-in-law Wei Tingrong, out of spite toward Du, spoke ill of him before the new consul and egged him into ordering every gambling house and opium shop in the French Concession closed. Stung by the blow, Du had his disciple Shao Ziying (chief interpreter of the French water-and-electric company) organize a total strike of the workers at the French bus, electric-light and waterworks. With the Concession's public vehicles halted, its water not running and its lamps dark, all was thrown into confusion. The French consul, at his wits' end, had the Chinese board members Li Yingsheng and Zhang Yishu accompany him to call on Du Yuesheng at his residence and ask him face to face to help settle it. Du agreed on the spot, and the next day the French utilities and buses all went back to work; but the closed gambling houses and opium shops he did not mean to reopen, so that his chief source of income was cut off, and the office fees and carriage allowances from here and there were not enough to meet his outlays. Yet for the sake of face the old scale had to be kept up, and what money fell short he could only borrow from the banks. By now his social standing and his power in the gang were alike consolidated and immense, and he had no fear of anyone pressing him for a debt.

### IV

The day before he left Shanghai, Du sent word to the four of us — Wan Molin, Zhu Wende, Yang Yusheng and me — to come to Rue Wagner for dinner the next evening, as he had things to say to us. That night Du told us that the War of Resistance had grown tenser still, that he reckoned the Japanese would make some move against him, and that he had no choice but to leave. He bade us be bold but careful and go on acting by his instructions; and he laid it down that Zhu Wende was to write the letters and send the telegrams for Wan Molin, that I, besides assisting Wan Molin, was to take the reports and telegrams in Shanghai to the telegraph office and contrive to get them out, and that Yang Yusheng was to handle nothing but the Relief Committee's monthly relief payments. These so-called relief payments were in fact subsidies the Nationalist government paid to social notables, to keep them from being tempted, by financial straits, into the Japanese camp as traitors.

Having given us his instructions, Du left for Hong Kong. Qian Yongming (Xinzhi) went with him, and he took along the two secretaries Weng Zuoqing and Wang Youtang, the tea-server Ma Zhenxing (Little Ah-Wu) and the driver Tang Erbao. After reaching Hong Kong, Du telegraphed the four of us again, charging us to handle everything as he had said the night before, and, if anyone came to Shanghai to find us bearing his letter of introduction, to receive them well and carry out to the letter whatever they asked.

Those who kept in touch with us in Shanghai included, on the Nationalist side, such men as Jiang Bocheng, Wu Kaixian, Wu Shaoshu, Wang Xianqing and Wang Manyun (who later turned traitor); on the side of the democratic parties, Wang Minxiu (who at the time went by the alias Li Dacheng, sent by Huang Yanpei to do liaison work in Shanghai); and two disciples from Zhu Xuefan's newspaper. Others besides came to us bearing Du's letters of introduction, wanting to rent houses, buy cars, take out car licenses, or have letters carried to Hong Kong; some asked us to buy them boat tickets to Hong Kong; and some who had tickets needed me to charter besides a small launch out to the liner, for at the Gonghexiang Wharf, where one boarded, Japanese sentries stood guard and searched people bodily.

In this way not quite half a month passed, and the first of the four of us to vanish, all at once, was Yang Yusheng. He first took refuge in the house of a woman whose address no one knew, and then fled to Ningbo. Du Yuesheng, in Hong Kong, having had no word from Yang, wrote to ask after him, and we told him the truth. Yang Yusheng had started an obscure nobody, but after becoming the Du household's accountant had gradually come into money: he had 300,000-odd in cash, more than 200 mu of land in Ningbo, five two-depth houses and a garden. Du had trusted him greatly and made him assistant manager of the Zhonghui Bank. Yang had spoken ill, in Du's hearing, of the manager Fu Pinggui; and when the new Zhonghui building was finished and they moved in, Du shifted Fu to general auditor and appointed as manager Xu Maotang, son of Xu Qingyun, whom Yang had introduced. For this reason Fu Pinggui bore Yang a grudge. After Shanghai fell, Fu Pinggui's father Fu Xiao'an became mayor of the puppet Shanghai Dadao Municipal Government, and Fu Pinggui, catching sight of Yang Yusheng one day at the Zhonghui Bank, threatened him: "The Japanese already know all about you — you had best watch out; once the Japanese gendarmes take a man, there is no coming back alive." Yang, timid to begin with and, now that he had money, all the more afraid to die, took to hiding at home and dared not go to the bank; and because he was so ill-liked — the Zhonghui staff and the Du household, Du's own sons included, all got on badly with him — even at home there were people who telephoned to frighten him, so that he dared not even stay there. Du, learning that he had gone to Ningbo, telegraphed him to return to Shanghai and work as before. On getting the telegram Yang not only did not return to Shanghai but went instead to Hong Kong; and Du, at the sight of him, flew into a rage, ordered him out of Hong Kong at once, and cut off all dealings with him. Yang knelt on the floor and begged piteously, but Du would not heed him. Later, on the pleading of Qian Xinzhi and Huang Yanpei, Yang was allowed to remain in Hong Kong, but from then on he was cut off from the Du family.

After Yang Yusheng left, Du Yuesheng wrote to have the Relief Committee work put in my charge; each month Xu Caicheng drew the funds from the Bank of Communications and handed them to me to distribute. Yang had been paying only nineteen people; after I took over I gradually widened it to more than two hundred, and it went on until the Pearl Harbor affair, after which the work was stopped.

Three or four months later Wan Molin was betrayed by men under Wu Kaixian and seized by No. 76, the Wang puppets' secret service. The man made an appointment to meet Wan at the Hua'an Hotel (now the Overseas Chinese Hotel) on West Nanjing Road; Wan went as agreed, and the instant he stepped from the car was surrounded by a dozen men lying in wait at the hotel door and carried off. Wan's driver Shao Dedu saw it and came straight back to tell me. I made inquiries and learned it was the doing of Wu Shibao of No. 76, that after his arrest Wan was cruelly tortured, and that Wu had men extort money from Wan's wife besides, more than 200,000 in all.

Two months after Wan Molin's arrest, I too was taken in by No. 76. I was on Caishi Street when six or seven Wang-puppet agents with pistols leapt from a car parked at the roadside and bundled me into it. After it happened, Gu Wenda (Gu Qi), Zhang Chunbao, Pan Sansheng and Tang Shengming (younger brother of Tang Shengzhi) all went to Wu Shibao to plead for me, and that same night Wu had me driven home.

A little over a month after this, Gu Qi and Zhang Chunbao, the two of them, came to my house and told me that Wu Shibao had built a new residence on Jessfield Road and would be giving a housewarming banquet on such-and-such a day, and that I was to attend. I told them I would have to ask for instructions first, and when I did, I was told I must send a handsome gift. These people then gave me money, with which I bought some paintings and calligraphy and had them sent ahead; and, seeing no way out of it, I screwed up my courage and went to the banquet for Wu Shibao's new house. Many came that evening, most of them Japanese; among the Chinese were Wen Lanting, Yuan Lüdeng and Lin Kanghou (then called the three notables of Shanghai), Tang Shengming, Zhang Chunbao, Ji Zengshou, Geng Jiaji, Pan Sansheng, Gu Wenda and Lu Wenying. My heart was in my mouth, and the moment the meal was over I thanked Wu Shibao and took my leave.

A few months later Wu Shibao sent Gu Wenda to tell me that the Japanese were watching me and that I should leave Shanghai and lie low for a while. I bought at once a ticket on the liner Empress of Japan and set out for Hong Kong. When the ship reached Hong Kong, the Du household's tea-server Ah Tao and Du's disciple Shao Ziying came out to the liner in a launch to meet me. I asked how they had known I was coming to Hong Kong; they said Boss Du had gone to Chongqing and had telegraphed word for them to come and meet me, and had also told Du Weifan to lay on a welcome dinner for me. That very evening Zhang Shizhao's concubine, Kuai Dezhen, and Du's fourth wife, Yao Yulan, joined the banquet as well; and afterward I lodged in Shao Ziying's room.

Three days later Du Yuesheng came back from Chongqing, and the moment we met showed me the warmest feeling; I thanked him for wiring specially to have someone meet me at the wharf. Du said, "You have had a hard time of it in Shanghai — it was no more than your due." He asked at once how the matters people had come to me about, bearing his letters of introduction, had turned out; I said I had done everything according to the instructions in his letters, and that those who came were well satisfied. Du was very pleased to hear it. The next day Du talked with me again, gave me 5,000 Hong Kong dollars in pocket money, and went on to speak of Yang Yusheng. Yang Yusheng, he said, had no conscience — the moment he came into money he feared for his life; he agreed to my face in Shanghai to see to the things I entrusted to him, and no sooner had I left than he shirked and did nothing, so that every quarter suffered for it; and he still had the face to come to Hong Kong to see me. The sight of him made Du angry: to have used such a man, he said, he bitterly regretted. At this point he turned the talk to me: "This time you have come to Hong Kong, stay a week or ten days and then go back. Now that Wan Molin has been taken in Shanghai, there is no one to front the work, and people on every side in Shanghai are put out; it does the anti-Japanese work no good. Take my advice and go back!" Just then a guest arrived, and before I could say where I stood the talk broke off. That evening Du had a number of his close friends and disciples each invite me to dinner, so that nearly every evening someone kept me company at the restaurants — a great show of face for me.

I stayed more than twenty days, and seeing that Du's face was none too pleased, decided to stay a few more and then go back to Shanghai. Five weeks passed, and Du summoned me to talk again; the moment I saw him I said first that, however dangerous Shanghai might be, I had decided to go back, and he said, well pleased, "That's good — the sooner you go back, the easier my mind." A few days later I took my leave of Du, and he had already had Ah Tao buy me a ticket for a first-class cabin. The night before I left, Du laid on a banquet to see me off, with Zhang Yishu, Zhang Shizhao, Guo Lanshi, Lu Jingshi, Zhu Xuefan, Du Weifan, Gu Jiatang, Shao Ziying and others to keep me company; Du poured me a glass of wine with his own hand and said, "A safe journey, and all good fortune when you reach Shanghai," and charged me to be bold but careful and to mind myself in everything. And so I returned to Shanghai.

In Shanghai I saw to all my affairs as before, and many friends came to see me. One evening Wang Xianqing and Zhu Wende came to call, and told me quietly that Wan Molin had been moved to the police station on Fourth Avenue, and that the two of them, through Liu Shaokui, Lu Dagong, Liu Junqing and others, had a plan to break him out that very night; they wanted me not to stay at home, lest I be dragged in. But early the next morning Wang Xianqing came alone to find me and told me the escape had not come off: because Wan Molin could not get out by the main gate but would have had to be let down from the fourth floor, and if anyone below had seen it and opened fire the danger would have been great, they had called it off. As he left, Wang too told me to be careful at every turn. I went to the central station on Fourth Avenue and, through Liu Shaokui, saw Wan Molin. A few days later Wan Molin was moved again to the Japanese gendarmerie — this being the outcome of negotiations between Xu Caicheng, Jin Dingxun and the Japanese General Sakata — and before long he was released from the Japanese gendarmerie headquarters. I was very glad to see it, and my nerves eased somewhat; but within three months I was suddenly seized by the Japanese gendarmerie headquarters, who said I was an anti-Japanese element. They questioned me for a dozen days, and, through Xu Caicheng's contriving my rescue, I was let go and went to recuperate at the Nanyang Hospital. Jiang Bocheng sent a representative that very night to console me, and afterward, several times, in the dead of night, a few young men came with fresh flowers to console me — men I neither knew nor could place, though they knew my name. A few days later Du Yuesheng sent his man Old Yu from Hong Kong with a letter of condolence, who said to me: "The Old Master was greatly moved and sent me specially to console you on his behalf; he hopes you will soon be well. And he hopes you will still hold on carefully with the affairs in Shanghai." I nodded in silence.

About three months later I was seized again, this time by the gendarmerie on Beidang Road (today's Hengshan Road). This time it was Ding Rusong, who kept a fish shop at Gaoqiao, who led the Japanese to take me. The cause was that Wan Molin had run a secret radio station, which Zhu Wende had fronted for and installed in Ding Rusong's house at Gaoqiao; the Japanese army had discovered it and wanted Ding to lead them to Zhu Wende, but Zhu had long since gone into hiding and could not be taken, and Ding Rusong, unable to bear the torture, had had to lead the Japanese gendarmes to seize me instead. I was put into the Beidang Road gendarmerie, and at the first interrogation I told the Japanese: first, that I did not know where Zhu Wende was now living; and second, that as to what sort of man I was, they could go and inquire at the gendarmerie headquarters on North Sichuan Road, where they all knew me. This did some good: after that I was neither interrogated nor tortured, and, having been held more than forty days, I was bailed out by a Taiwanese, Cai Qingqi, whom my brother had engaged.

When I told the various parties concerned of this arrest and release, someone pointed out that I ought to give Cai a good dinner and a present. I was making ready to do so when, to my surprise, this Cai invited me one time after another to eat at the International Hotel and the Xinya, the guests at his tables being all of them noted traitors and the gendarmerie captains of the four Shanghai districts where the Japanese were stationed. I laid the case before the various parties and before Xu Caicheng, asking whether I should go on meeting these people and joining their banquets; and the general view was that, since I had to come out into the open to do my work, I could not but humor them — so long as I made no political slip and let no secret out, I might have careful, guarded dealings with them.

Cai Qingqi treated me to six or seven meals, and at last came out with his request: he was preparing to hold his fiftieth birthday, with a day of benefit Peking opera at the Jincheng Grand Theater, and wanted me to come forward and invite the noted actors and amateurs of Shanghai to take part, and to invite a few Shanghai notables to be sponsors of the celebration. I had no choice but to do as he wished: I invited the noted actors Ma Lianliang and Wang Yinqiu, and the noted amateurs Zhang Zhongyuan, Sun Lanting, Gai Jianfei, Zhang Tuosheng, Sun Junshi and others, to dine at the Nanguo restaurant, asked them to help with the performance, and introduced Cai to them; happily, every one agreed to take part. For sponsors I further found him Wen Lanting, Yuan Lüdeng, Lin Kanghou, Zhang Weiru (chairman and general manager of the securities exchange during the puppet period), Ma Xiangsheng, Xie Baosheng, Gu Zhuxuan and others, and helped him sell the tickets and send out the "autumn-wind" cards.

Afterward Cai Qingqi wanted me to serve as adviser to the Huanghou Grand Theater, its manager, Zhang Jinshou, being Cai's adopted son. Before long the Huanghou was reorganized, and Cai wanted me to take a share in it; but seeing that the several shareholding proprietors of the theater were all traitors, I felt I must think carefully, and consulted Jiang Bocheng, Wu Kaixian, Xu Caicheng and others. Later Xu Caicheng notified me that I was permitted to take a share, the money to be handed to me by Xu; and so I took part in the Huanghou Grand Theater and became its general manager, right up to the liberation.

After the victory in the War of Resistance in 1945, Du Yuesheng returned to Shanghai from Chongqing by way of western Zhejiang, and Wan Molin and I and others went to the station to meet him. The moment he saw me, he called me over and asked how the debts he had run up before stood. I told him that, what with the appreciation of the stocks and the currency reform and the like, the more than 5,000,000 in debts had all been paid off; at which he broke into a smile, showed his pleasure, and pulled me into his own car to ride back to the Du house together.

After Du Yuesheng came back from Chongqing there was a great change in him. Where before he had spent as the whim took him, money coming in by one hand and out by the other without the least grudging, he now told me the expenses must be tightened, and the household staff was cut down somewhat. The secretary Qiu Fangmo, for instance, having served under Chen Qun as head of the puppet Shanghai income-tax bureau, was no longer engaged; the secretary Wang Youtang died old and sick; the secretary Weng Zuoqing found himself a post as secretary at the Commercial Bank and left the Du house; Wan Molin and Zhu Wende had enterprises of their own and no longer came often to the Du house; and the opium-preparer Yu Fangxun became head inspector at the securities exchange — so that only I was left to help keep the household running.

By this time Du's children were all grown, and Chen Guoying proposed to hold the weddings of her three sons, Du Weiyuan, Du Weihan and Du Weining. Du agreed at once, telling Chen Guoying to see to it in consultation with me. Later Sun Peihao proposed to hold weddings for her two sons, Du Weiping and Du Weixin, and this too was left to me to manage. After that Yao Yulan's second daughter married. Following the six weddings came Du's own sixtieth birthday.

All six weddings were held at the Lido Garden; to save money, only tea and refreshments were served, yet the guests each time numbered more than 5,000, and the cost was still very great. Several of the gifts were of high value: the Zhonghui Bank manager Xu Maotang gave each time a gold ingot weighing more than twenty taels; the disciple Huang Zhendong gave each time a gold ingot of more than ten taels; and Sun Zhifei, manager of the China Merchants Electric Company, and Zhang Rongchu, manager of the Qingfeng Cotton Mill, gave each time ten taels of gold. When Du's daughters married, the four of them together gave a gold "table service" — chopsticks, wine cups, cup-mats, soup spoons, spittle dishes and the like, all worked in gold. Later, at Du's own sixtieth birthday, these four together gave the Three Stars of Fortune, Rank and Longevity worked in gold, weighing more than ten catties. These were among the more striking instances of the time.

The celebration of Du's sixtieth birthday was held at the Lido Garden on the fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month, with more than 6,000 guests. Because Du had asked for economy, each table was served only six cold vegetarian dishes and one great pot of plain noodles; the drink was Coca-Cola, presented by the factory that made it, and the beer was presented by the China Brewery and the Shanghai Brewery. That day, besides the many cars and the many guests, there were several thousand beggars come for a meal. A few days later there was a three-day benefit performance at the Chinese Grand Theater on Niuzhuang Road, the whole proceeds given to charitable bodies and the costs borne by Du; and the noted Peking-opera actors of north and south whom he had invited all gave their services free, Du presenting each with a gold pen and a gold watch as a keepsake.

By this time Meng Xiaodong had come to Shanghai from Beijing and was staying at the Du house, and she took part in the performance too. A few days before it, Meng Xiaodong asked me to arrange for people to send flower-baskets, and I agreed. Du Yuesheng, standing by, said the flower-baskets should be reckoned at fifty yuan each, to be given afterward to charity; and I proposed that for each basket received, its cash value should be posted on a red paper on the wall outside the Chinese Grand Theater, naming who had sent it and to whom — to which both Du and Meng agreed. On the day of the performance, those who sent flower-baskets mostly sent ten (500 yuan); only the securities exchange and Du's disciple Huang Zhendong each sent 200 baskets (10,000 yuan). The red papers on the wall, all reading "Miss Meng Linghui takes the stage — congratulations from So-and-so," covered the wall outside the Chinese Grand Theater; the baskets came to more than 2,500 in all, worth over 125,000 yuan.

In 1948 Du Yuesheng met with a bitter blow: his son Du Weiping was arrested by Chiang Ching-kuo. When the Central Daily News carried a photograph of Du Weiping in handcuffs and it was set before him, he felt the loss of face too grievous, and was cut to the quick. Du Weiping was a broker at the Shanghai Securities Exchange, dealing in securities for clients, and at the same time deputy general manager of the Shanghai Dadong Book Company; Chiang Ching-kuo held him on a charge of speculation and profiteering and disturbing the market, a charge that could be made much of or little — one might be shot for it, or held a few days and released. Du Yuesheng at once telephoned Qian Xinzhi, Zhang Shizhao, Xu Jiqing, Lu Jingshi and others, invited them to the Du house, and talked with them long and earnestly. The next day he further invited Huang Yanpei, Sheng Pihua, Liu Hongsheng and Xu Caicheng to consult, and they talked the whole morning through. Outside, I heard them saying that Chiang Ching-kuo had at first meant to arrest H. H. Kung's son Kong Lingkan as well, since Kong Lingkan and Du Yuesheng ran the Yangtze Company together and that too counted as speculation and profiteering; but afterward Kong's mother Song Ailing and his aunt Song Meiling both put in a word with Chiang Ching-kuo, and so he did not lay hands on him. But there was already a rumor in the market that Du Yuesheng too had been detained.

What plan Du Yuesheng and all these guests worked out over those days to rescue Du Weiping, I do not know. I know only that a few days later a wave of panic buying broke out over the whole city: everything sold at the official price — whether to wear, to use or to eat, from gold to grain — was snapped up bare, the black-market prices changed three times a day and still would not keep within the price limits, and so the gold yuan certificate fell straight downward and the prestige of the Chiang family's regime was swept to the ground. Before long, Chiang Ching-kuo quietly left Shanghai, and Du Weiping was released. There was talk then in the market that this wave of panic buying had been got up by Du Yuesheng in collusion with the various quarters connected to him; but how the truth of it stood, I do not know.

Though I was decades in the Du household, there are many things I truly do not understand even now. Take this: after Du returned to Shanghai, he sold off the Route Doumer house — yet at that time his Shanghai debts of more than five million had all been cleared, he still had money in the bank, and he was in no hurry for cash to spend. Why sell the house? And, having sold it, he then rented several rooms on the seventh floor of the "Eighteen-Story Building" (the Maoming Apartments) for himself, Meng Xiaodong and Yao Yulan to live in, at a very high rent — a thing hard to make sense of.

Or take this: among the callers at the Du house after the victory was a man whose name we did not know, who often came at night to see Du Yuesheng. He gave no name when he came, and called himself the abbot of the Longshou Temple; but this "abbot" was sometimes in Chinese dress, a tweed gown, and sometimes in Western suit and leather shoes, without a trace of the monk about him. Every time he came, Du received him, and their talks ran on rather long. Later I learned that this Longshou Temple stood opposite the Nanjing Cinema (now the Shanghai Concert Hall) and had been built after the War of Resistance; the people thereabouts said the monks of this temple were disorderly — often going out in Western clothes at night, and sometimes with women coming and going by night as well. After liberation the temple was closed at once. What this abbot-monk was really about, and what he had to do with Du, I never did make out.

Or take this: Du had taken on in Chongqing a new disciple surnamed Zhou (I cannot now recall the name he then went by), and after coming to Shanghai had him introduced to Zhou Xiangsheng and set to work at the Xiangsheng Taxi Company. When Zhou Xiangsheng himself came, Du would often not receive him; but when this Zhou came, Du received him at once, and told me and the tea-server Xu Taosheng that whenever the man surnamed Zhou came he was to be let in, and not stopped. We all thought it strange, and did not understand it until after liberation. In the early days after liberation, at the door of the No. 1 Department Store on Nanjing Road, I saw this Zhou riding in a car that was turning north from Xizang Road; he saw me too, stopped by the side of Xizang Road, got out, shook hands with me and talked, thanked me for the care I had shown him in the past, and told me that his original name was Zhou Enting, and that he was a younger cousin of Premier Zhou.

Ever since the price-control affair, Du had lived in the Eighteen-Story Building, keeping close and going out little, and received fewer guests. A little more than two months before the liberation of Shanghai, Chiang Kai-shek telephoned to have him come to Fuxing Island for a talk. On his return Du made arrangements to leave Shanghai, wanting me to stay behind and telling the secretary Hu Xuwu to go to Hong Kong. I would not comply, and pleaded my case to Du: I had managed the six weddings of his household and his sixtieth birthday, a great many people knew me, and, on top of that, I had held certain nominal posts in Nationalist organs, so that to stay had grave risks; whereas Hu Xuwu was just the opposite — with wives senior and junior and heavy family ties, he was unwilling to go to Hong Kong. Seeing us balk at his arrangements, Du was much displeased, and at once had the tea-server Xu Taosheng telephone for Xu Caicheng to come and talk us round. Xu said to us: "The question of your going or staying Mr. Du settled long ago; you must not wrangle over it — wrangling will do no good." And there and then he brought out a sum of settling-in money for Hu Xuwu. At this the two of us could raise no further objection. After Xu Caicheng had reported back to Du, Du called us in and said, all smiles, "That you can heed my words is very good; I am well pleased." He brought out a large gold bar (ten taels of gold) and handed it to Hu Xuwu, saying, "Take this and set your family affairs in good order. Off you go!" — and Hu took his leave. Du then said to me: "That you heed my words sets my mind at rest. I will tell you plainly now: because Chiang Kai-shek has summoned me to talk, I have no choice but to go and live a while in Hong Kong, but I shall be coming back to Shanghai. Friends on the Communist side have spoken to me of wanting me to join the new Political Consultative Conference after liberation, so you will have no trouble in Shanghai. Mr. Xu is not going to Hong Kong either, but staying in Shanghai; if you meet with any difficulty, you can talk it over with him."

After we had talked a while, and Xu Caicheng too had gone, leaving only the two of us, Du said to me further: "When the People's Liberation Army enters Shanghai, they are regular troops and will make no trouble; after liberation you will still be able to see many old acquaintances." So saying, he gave me three letters and said, "If anything happens, you can go to these three men." I saw that the envelopes were addressed to Liao Chengzhi, Sheng Pihua and Pan Hannian. After liberation, seeing Pan Hannian on the list of Shanghai's mayor and deputy mayors in the newspaper, I went to the municipal government to ask to see Deputy Mayor Pan; Pan received me, and I handed him all the letters.

A little over a month before the liberation of Shanghai, one evening Du summoned me to the Eighteen-Story Building to talk, and told me that the next evening he would be giving a dinner for guests; for the sake of quiet, the next afternoon, apart from the invited guests, no other callers were to be seen, and after three in the afternoon the gatemen, doormen and tea-servers of the house were all to go home. He had me go myself the next morning to the Dexing Restaurant at Shiliupu to buy some cold dishes, and in the afternoon to the Xinya restaurant to buy stir-fried dishes; the household cook was to prepare in the morning a great earthen pot of chicken, duck, ham and pork knuckle, and then be sent home in the afternoon. When the food came, Mrs. Yao was to heat it and I was to carry it in.

The next day I did everything as Du had instructed. Before five in the afternoon Xu Caicheng came, and Du had him receive the guests at the ground-floor gate and had me wait inside the door of Room 705. At half past five there came, one after another, Qian Xinzhi, Zhang Shizhao and Huang Yanpei, and also a white-haired old man with a long beard, helped in on Xu Caicheng's arm; this guest I was seeing for the first time (only after liberation, seeing his photograph in the paper, did I learn he was Zhang Lan, vice-chairman of the Central People's Government). After him came Sheng Pihua, Sha Qianli and Shi Liang. When all the guests had come, nine in all went into the dining room and shut the door to eat, the heated dishes being carried by me to the door, where Xu Caicheng opened it and took them in. What was said within I could not hear a word of. It was nine o'clock before the party broke up, and Du himself helped the long-bearded old man down to the ground floor and into a car. This was the strangest banquet ever held at the Du house; and not long after, Du left Shanghai, and not long after that Shanghai was liberated.
