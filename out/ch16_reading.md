## Chapter 6. Vile Treachery, Illusions Undone

### (1) We Do Not Flinch from Owning Our Failure

The "Hanoi work," through mistaking the target of the action and striking Zeng Zhongming in error, had ended in failure; and for matters to come to this pass was, in truth, an utter wreck. There is a common saying: "Success is allowed; failure is not." Applied here it means this: had we succeeded, we should have produced the looked-for effect and rooted out a source of calamity for good; having failed, we had but bred a tiger to leave trouble behind—worse than had we done nothing at all.

Worse to what degree? The record of what happened stands there for all to see, and the compass of it is far too wide. This account still follows on from what went before, setting down our own experience and that part of it which is most to the practical point.

On the twenty-first I did not sleep the whole night through; and when day broke I took the drafted telegram-text I had prepared, meaning to send it over to Brother Bingxi's place and ask him to dispatch it. No sooner had I stepped out the door than I discovered that the car in which we had gone circling about near the scene in the dead of night was standing there, plain as day, outside the door—and had not even had its headlamps put out. It was our good luck that the local inspectors were so weak and incompetent; had they but paid the least heed, could they not have followed the picture to the horse, seized the whole lot of us, and let not one man escape? To think of it now makes my face burn.

From Brother Bingxi I learned that the three men taken were named Yuan Boxun, Sun Yadong, and Yang Weihe; while those of ours who had not come back to report were Yu Jiansheng, Zhang Fengyi, and Chen Bangguo. Naturally it did occur to me that every one of them carried a passport—though what names were used on those passports I had never once seen, and whatever the names might be, that it was these three men was beyond all doubt. What could not be settled was still this: of Yuan Boxun and the other two, which was which.

Brother Bingxi told me that he had already sent, in his own name, a brief report, much the same in substance as the one I had drafted, and had asked that the frequency of reporting be increased, the better to keep close contact. I asked Brother Bingxi to send my report off at once, and at the same time to arrange by telephone a time with Mr. Xu, that we might meet—first to hear any further news, and then to talk over how to conduct the case at law for the men taken. Mr. Xu replied that he hoped I would make allowance for his position: better to wait a day or two before arranging a meeting; anything needful could be talked over by telephone.

This meeting with Brother Bingxi moved me as I shall never forget: it was the solace he gave to one who had failed. What was precious lay not in the words, but in the sincere friendship that showed through them, which I could both see and feel. Beyond shame, what else could I show?

I drove the car to the repair shop we often used, and told them that if it was not I who came for it, Mr. Wei coming would be all the same. Then I hired a pedicab to go find Wei Chunfeng. Wei was not in; I went again to find Miss Nguyen, and she too was out—no doubt they had both gone off gadding about; but why had they not telephoned? There was nothing for it but to go back. On the way I turned it over, too: should I go and meet Yu Lexing and the others? On second thoughts, there was nothing much to talk over; better to wait until the higher authorities had given instructions, and speak of it when the next step was to be decided.

Back at our quarters, Buyun and Yingjie were asleep, and Luqiao was drinking alone, pouring for himself; when he saw me come in, he put the bottle aside. He pulled me upstairs, saying he had a notion he wished to talk over with me. In truth, had he not said it, I could have guessed—for he was a man who would never own himself beaten; he held that, the task having failed and the duty being unfulfilled, we must surely try once more. Once we fell to talking, so it proved. His meaning was: "We still have five men fit for action, and all the gear we need is on hand. Let me go in alone; whatever it takes, I mean to find that man. I ask only that the other comrades cover me from outside—that will be enough." I was much of the same mind—truly it was a meeting of minds unsought; only one thing: we had first to have some grasp of the new situation after the change, or it would be sheer recklessness.

An understanding of the new situation still depended on what Mr. Xu could furnish; relying on our own strength alone, we could not meet the practical need. As for the working connections taken on locally through Wei Chunfeng's hand, until we had got in touch with Wei there was no knowing whether they could still be brought to bear. Because we were hemmed in by all the conditions set out above, we had no choice but to let things cool, to wait a while: this was a case of circumstance being stronger than men. And if we would not give up, what then? What use was mere bravado?

The mood of that time was just this up-and-down unease.

Luqiao and I were together day and night, sharing hardship and peril, and may be said to have formed a friendship. I was not taking his work-report; nor was he laying his account of the operation before me. When the two of us, in private, "chatted idly," we had, more than once, talked our hearts out around this matter of having struck Zeng Zhongming by mistake, baring our very livers and gall. We both understood: since the thing had not succeeded, no further words would win us understanding or sympathy—though in truth we wanted none of these; what the two of us sought to get to the bottom of was only this: how the error had come about.

The writer has, in truth, no wish to vaunt how able Wang Luqiao was; yet whatever one says, one must own him a man of courage and experience in action work. For one thing, he had already set the record of having, single-handed, cut down with his own hand the most notorious of "professional killers," Wang So-and-so; for another, after he had entered the Wang house, he was able to linger, unhurried, downstairs and up, for a good many tens of minutes—and this alone is enough to show his coolness and self-possession. One who has not himself been through such a pass cannot, of course, feel what it is like within; to charge him with being "flustered and at a loss" would be somewhat too harsh.

In examining the chief cause that led to the error, it still comes down to this: that the reconnaissance work was not carried deep enough—we had not even made out that the two houses had been knocked through and made one, and the rest may be imagined from that. As to whether Wang Jingwei himself had originally lodged in this very room where Zeng Zhongming was wounded—that is to say, whether the man Wang had originally been in that room on the third floor, to the right and near the main street, and had afterward, for some reason, changed to another, and so escaped by luck—of the truth of this Luqiao and I dared affirm nothing.

Because we wholly trusted Tang Yingjie's repeated reconnaissance reports, the whereabouts of the man Wang's bedroom had been settled; and so in Luqiao's whole conception this mark was deeply graven. Such a case has, in psychology, a name for it—the "law of primacy," that is, the first impression, which brooks no doubt nor change. It was under this influence that, once Luqiao had entered the Wang house, he went, without the least hesitation, straight up to the third floor; and when he could not get the room-door open, he called to the men below to pass up the axe; and when he had hewn the door open and looked in, there was a man lying prone under the bed—this, of course, was surely the man Wang. This whole string of actions came wholly of instinct: in the mind it was a thing foreordained, a matter of course, with simply not the least other thought.

As to the particulars, Luqiao gave a description too, and what I still keep in memory is this:

Before we had entered the Wang house, we knew only that there were a good many people in the household; as to just how many, and who they were, we were "not very clear." Our attention was fixed wholly on their guarding capacity—which took in the two heads of men and weapons; as to whether the local police were posted to protect the place, that we had not reckoned in.

After entering by the back door, we found only that someone poked out a head and peered; we met with no resistance whatever. Still, one cannot, because the other side offered no resistance, be sure they had no weapons. By the intelligence we had got, and by the judgment of Luqiao, myself, and the rest, the Wang house might have had one or two small pistols, in the hands of the attendants downstairs, for guard duty. Then why did they not return fire? Luqiao and I held that they were cowed by the shots Chen Bangguo fired first and by the incalculable force of our onset; for we had come prepared, and had they opened fire in return, it must have brought on a gun-battle of grossly unequal strength, which, as may be foreseen, would have caused yet more dead and wounded.

To speak truly, we never did know on which floor the Wang house telephone was fitted, nor where; nor had we planned beforehand to cut the telephone line after going in, to guard against word being passed—this was a great loophole in the carrying-out of the case, and it may serve too to show that the action work of forty-odd years ago had not yet the scientific cast of mind of today. Then why did the people of the Wang house wait until the men had gone before telephoning the alarm? Our explanation is that they were frightened at the time; whether it is right or not, I cannot say.

There is one more point that is an insoluble riddle: that the electric lights, from the bottom of the house to the top, throughout the whole building, were all lit. By then it was already midnight, when, one would suppose, the lamps had been put out for sleep; had the people of the Wang house been startled awake by the sound of the door being struck and the shots that followed on it, and so turned on the lights? Or had the lights never been put out at all? We did not know where the main switch of the power supply was. Had it been pitch-dark after Luqiao and the others went in, what followed might perhaps have been different. They would have used a flashlight, or might have pressed at random a switch on the wall—but they would on no account have wasted time groping about to find the main switch. In such a case, to use a flashlight is a great taboo, for its beam betrays one's own position and offers the other side a mark, so that one is easily attacked, and may be caught off guard. I asked Luqiao to think again: had he taken any notice of the lights being on? He said that from going in to coming out it had seemed like broad day, and that the thing had simply never crossed his mind.

Since No. 27 and No. 25 were two houses knocked through, by rights every floor must have had a connecting way between them; but because none of our men had this notion in their heads, even seeing a door they would not have given it a push. Luqiao said that only if, on reaching the room he had in mind on the third floor and opening the door, there had been not a soul, or no one within view, would they then have searched about everywhere—and in searching would have found the way through. But there proved to be so many coincidences in this world: all his attention was drawn off by the false show set before his eyes. No wonder that one error followed on another and led to the rout.

Recalling how he hewed the door open with the axe at the time, Luqiao said: "My first thought was—thank goodness this tightly shut door is made of wood; had it been an iron door, I should not have known what to do. Still, though a wooden door, it was very stout; swinging the axe and striking, one felt at once how hard it was. So it took a good many blows before I got a hole hewn in it. The hole was not neat—jagged and irregular, like the teeth of a saw. Who would have thought that at one glance I should see a man burrowing under the bed? He had been frightened, I suppose, by the door-hewing; with no place to hide in his haste, and wanting some cover, he could only burrow under the bed—which is instinct and common sense alike."

Luqiao said again: "At the time, could he not get under? or had he burrowed halfway and stopped? or, just at the moment I hewed the door open and saw him, had he by chance burrowed only halfway in? These too are past making out. It was just because the man's lower body was left exposed outside while his upper body was hidden under the bed that I took him for Wang Jingwei. Had the man been sitting or standing, I should of course have been able to tell—for, you know, I knew Zeng Zhongming, and would not then have fired on him."

"What is more, the hole hewn in the door was not large, and the field of view was not broad either; in the room a bedside lamp was lit—not dark, but not very bright either, only dim enough to see by. In such a case one could of course not be wholly certain that the man lying prone under the bed was Wang Jingwei; but 'first come, first master': because, by our reconnaissance report, we held this man to be Wang Jingwei without a doubt, I fired the moment I could. To say, after the event, that this was a little rash would not be unfair; but to charge that I grew flustered from fear would be too great a wrong. In fact, with men on watch outside and men guarding my back, even had the Wang house guards meant to resist, I had nothing to fear. It was true enough that, having gone at it three times running in one day and each time botched it—a tiger's head, a snake's tail—my temper had been drawn up like a flame, and I had grown a little impatient; that much is so."

Luqiao grew more and more worked up as he spoke, and at the last poured out his heart: "Mr. Chen, you understand my nature. Let me now say one more word after the fact: had I known on the spot that I had hit the wrong man, then though the sky should fall I would have had to find that fellow named Wang. But now that it has come to this pass, what more is there to say? Still, I hope you will find me another chance, that I may vent this breath!"

The passage set down above is just the true face of that irresistibly valiant patriot Wang Luqiao. Men have their fortune and their misfortune; a pity that, through one false move, he was unable, in this historic performance, to make his name at a single stroke—which is a great matter for regret.

I also talked with Tang Yingjie alone. There were mainly two doubtful points I hoped to have cleared up. First: every reconnaissance report had said that the man Wang lodged in the very room where Zeng Zhongming was wounded; why did it afterward prove that the man Wang was not in that room that night? Was there some mistake? Tang Yingjie insisted he had not been mistaken in the least: the man Wang most certainly was in that room; a man is a living thing, who walks and moves—if he had changed rooms for the moment, could that be called impossible? Besides, in the records we already had, Zeng Zhongming did not lodge at No. 27; why he should suddenly appear that night in the front room on the third floor of No. 27 is also a thing hard to fathom. Might it not be that, the situation that day being unusual, with many suspicious incidents one after another, he had stayed on for the moment to confer on how to meet the emergency? Tang Yingjie's imagination was quite rich; he made not a few suppositions—but by now all is past and gone, and where is one to go to verify it!

This account of Tang Yingjie's happens, strangely enough, to tally with a passage of record on page 203 of the eleventh volume of the full translation of The Secret Records of President Chiang, published several decades later—which is itself an inconceivable thing. The original text of that passage runs: "On the twenty-first of March, Wang Zhaoming's secretary Zeng Zhongming was slain by patriots in Wang's secret residence on Gao Lang Street in Hanoi. Because it happened that on that very night Wang Zhaoming and Zeng Zhongming had by chance changed bedrooms to sleep, the assassins mistook Zeng Zhongming for Wang himself, and fired several shots into the room."

I have always felt this account hard to credit; even granting such a thing happened, it cannot escape the suspicion of being far-fetched and self-serving. Yet since there is such a statement, it can hardly be wind from an empty cave; so from what source came the material? Even I myself am much puzzled. Was it that Tang Yingjie, after returning to Chongqing, put in a report, and the content of the report was written just so; and that, once filed away, it lay sealed in dust and untouched for decades? Later, when the Wang case was no longer kept secret but made public, Tang's report came alive again in the files, and, after being put in order, recast, and adopted, became original material of reliable provenance. This is the writer's own conjecture; how it in fact stands, I believe no one will come forward to make plain.

Second: I asked Tang Yingjie—having gone to reconnoiter so many times, why he had not even discovered that the two buildings, No. 27 and No. 25, had been knocked through in the middle and used as one; what was the reason? Tang Yingjie said, with much show of reason: "I did not go in. They knocked a hole in the wall inside and fitted a door; how was I to see it from outside? Even when I hung head-down from the rooftop and peered in for a moment, it was but a matter of a few seconds; and what is more, from the window one could see only into the room. As for what stirred in the corridors and passages, I have no X-ray eyes—how could I see that?"

Tang Yingjie went on, in a rather displeased tone: "Did you not yourself, Mr. Chen, speak of the want of inside intelligence that runs clear to the bottom? For a thing like this, one need only place a man anywhere in their household, and he would know it all clear as day, and no such error would come about." What he said was true enough; but as for "placing a man anywhere," where is there anything so easy as that?

I felt the man was past reasoning with—let him be!

In the dead of night—it was already the small hours of the twenty-third—Brother Bingxi telephoned to wake me, saying he was coming; I thought again some important matter had arisen. Before long he arrived, and handed me a folded slip of paper; opening it, I saw it was a telegram from Dai Yunong, calling me alone to return first to Chongqing, and requiring me to set off the very day the telegram reached me. Of any other instruction there was not a word—the meaning being that I need not even hand over my duties.

I asked Brother Bingxi: "Is there any other instruction for you?"

Brother Bingxi did not answer directly, but told me only, in a friend's tone: "Set your mind at ease and go; the affairs here I will take charge of and see to." I pressed him again with a question: "Then Brother Lexing and Brother Jiazhuo, and Cao Shi'ang and Tan Tianqian, and the newly come Comrade Zhang and the rest—are they none of them to be notified either?" Brother Bingxi thought a moment and said: "I think there is no need. As to the matter of those several comrades, Mr. Dai is sure to have another disposition for them—perhaps each will have his own task; there is no telling."

These words, as I heard them, gave me a real qualm: could it be that I was being recalled to be handed over to court-martial? Though I thought so, this time I had no wish whatever to shirk; come what might, I ought to obey and act, and even knowing well that after returning I should meet with severe punishment, I had nothing to complain of. Thinking of this, I felt quite at ease.

What was troublesome was the question of how to leave the country. These few days the Annamese police were still searching for others in the case; the airport and the docks were naturally the places they especially watched; but leave I must, by whatever means, so I had to think out some cover—which was truly a great vexation.

That morning, Wei Chunfeng, who had been out of touch, telephoned at last; how could he know how badly I needed him! I asked him to meet me at once if he could; he agreed to wait for me, ten minutes later, at the roadside outside the lane where I lodged. He said there would be a black Citroën sedan—that was it.

I had a few minutes to wash and dress; and when I strolled out to the mouth of the lane, I already saw the car. In it were two young ladies besides: one was Wei Chunfeng's girlfriend—that same Miss Nguyen who helped us in our work; the other did not look like an Annamese girl. I gave her but one glance—she was truly dazzlingly beautiful; put it this way, she was the loveliest woman I have seen in my whole life. Chunfeng had me sit in the car beside him, and drove slowly on. I asked him low: "I have things to say; can that young lady understand?" Chunfeng said: "She cannot understand a word; say what you will, it does not matter. She is Miss Nguyen's little friend; they grew up together." With Chunfeng's explanation, we talked on without reserve.

I asked Chunfeng to find a way to buy me a boat ticket to Hong Kong, and hoped, too, that he could see me aboard; if he used this car to carry me to Haiphong, and asked these two young ladies to keep me company on the hard trip as cover, so much the better. Chunfeng said all this was no problem; he would make the arrangements for me. As to whether a ticket could be bought at once, he must go to Haiphong to see. For now he would first take me back; he meant to ask Miss Nguyen and the beautiful young lady to talk it over, to ask whether she had any other business, and whether she would be willing to lend us the car again; if it were settled, he would go straight to Haiphong.

From Hanoi to Haiphong is like Taipei to Keelung—only an hour's journey; and Wei Chunfeng had word back before noon. He said there was no ship weighing anchor for Hong Kong that day; the soonest would be the next forenoon. So on his own decision he had bought a ticket on a passenger-cargo vessel with only six berths; and as for the matter of getting safely aboard, we need only do as we had just now planned.

That I had been ordered off first could not be kept from the several comrades who lodged with me either; we did no more than shake hands and part. Little did I think that this parting would be the last—that I should never again meet Tang Yingjie or Chen Buyun. As for the matter of Wang Luqiao's being taken, let it wait for later telling.

It was on the twenty-fourth of March that I left Hanoi. Wei Chunfeng, this young fellow, was truly too good. That morning, about seven o'clock, he first telephoned, bidding me meet at the old place where I had got into the car before; then he saw me to Haiphong and aboard the ship.

This time it was the beautiful young lady who drove, with Chunfeng sitting at her side, and I was put in the back seat beside Miss Nguyen. On the way Chunfeng made the introductions for us, and only then did I learn that the beautiful young lady was called Dana. Chunfeng went on to charge me, one thing after another: "Give me your passport; the ticket is on me; after the customs formalities are done, I will give it back to you." He said too: "When we go aboard, the three of us will go into the cabin with you; say nothing, mind nothing, only carry yourself as easily as usual—that will do."

Chunfeng patted Dana on the shoulder and told me further: "She and Miss Nguyen are good friends; today she took half a day's leave especially to see you off on your business; when you have the chance in future, you must thank her well." In my heart I was indeed endlessly grateful, but clumsy as I am, I could not get out a single word. Chunfeng went on: "Dana's father is a Frenchman, her mother an Annamese—what is here called a 'métisse'; they now reside at Marseille, and come back once or twice a year; the family circumstances are quite good. She herself works at a company; only there are too many who court her, so that at times she finds it a bother."

All the way we talked and laughed, and however heavy the cares on my mind, they were washed thin. When the car reached Haiphong, they crowded round me and went aboard together; I met with not the least trouble, and so, on the loneliest of journeys, there was left with me one most beautiful memory.

### (2) Zeng Zhongming Served Wang in Loyalty; Though He Died Unjustly, He Has No Cause for Grievance

It is both a duty and a responsibility to bear witness for history; and here I solemnly set forth this statement: the sanction order we received was confined to the sanctioning of Wang Jingwei alone, and did not include Zeng Zhongming. Wang's so-called words—"Mr. Zeng's views on the affairs of state were the same as mine; and because our views were the same, he did not this time escape death"—are of the kind that says, "if I must die, I will drag along a man to cushion my fall"; or else they are a form of self-advertisement. Everyone knows that Zeng was loyal to Wang; as to whether he too advocated a surrender-style peace, the facts show that at most he stood in no more than a subordinate, following place. Since chief and follower are distinct, we must of course keep to reason.

It has been said, again and again, in the foregoing, that the shooting and wounding of Zeng Zhongming came of an accident, and was a grave blunder besides. Afterward, the few of us who took part in the actual work exchanged views informally, and toward this Mr. Zeng Zhongming, who had suffered the mischance of the fish in the pond, we all felt the greatest contrition and regret. But when we called to mind that he too was one of the principal figures of this treasonous action, we grew somewhat easier.

In other materials besides, and in the "An Account of the Conduct of Mr. Zeng Zhongming" that Wang Jingwei wrote, it is stated that, at the moment Zeng Zhongming was shot, his wife, Madame Fang Junbi, was also in the room, and, "braving all to save her lord," took three shots. Of the news that Madame Fang Junbi was wounded we at the time knew nothing whatever; nor was there any such report in the local papers; nor had Mr. Xu, who regularly furnished us with high-grade intelligence, heard any such rumor from the local police authorities. By the oral report Wang Luqiao made to me afterward, he mentioned only that on hewing the door open he found a man lying prone under the bed, and had not seen a second person, or any other.

The hole hewn in the door was not large, and within the field of view one could of course not see the whole of the room. Was Madame Fang Junbi in the room at the time? Where was she standing? Of this we knew nothing at all. If it be really so that Madame Fang Junbi was wounded, and took three shots, then it is strange. For Wang Luqiao used a No. 2 revolver, which holds only five rounds; he fired three shots, all into the waist and belly of the man under the bed. Unless Madame Fang Junbi too was hiding under the bed and was hurt by a "ricochet," it is well-nigh impossible. At the same time, one thing we can guarantee: that we would on no account have fired on a woman who was not a target and had no power to resist.

There is no material to show how grave Madame Fang's wounds were; perhaps they were a knock or a graze. If so, it may also prove that they were by no means caused by a direct hit.

Of Zeng Zhongming's state after he was wounded, what we know is also limited. According to page 205 of the eleventh volume of The Secret Records of President Chiang: "At this time, the money Wang Zhaoming carried was all deposited in the bank in Zeng's name; and so Zeng Zhongming, gravely wounded and in mortal danger, propped himself up, and on his sickbed signed every one of the checks—whereby he lost too much blood and died."

In "The Rise and Fall of the Wang Regime," written by Jin Xiongbai under the pen name Zhu Zijia, from page forty-five to page forty-seven of the fifth volume, there is also a somewhat fuller description. Whether it is all fact one dare not say for certain, for he too had it at second hand; I copy it out below for reference.

"When Zeng Zhongming and his wife were examined at the hospital, Zhongming's belly was riddled with shot, truly a mass of a hundred holes and a thousand wounds. The doctors cut open his belly to operate, and actually removed a length of intestine over a foot long. And because he had lost too much blood, a transfusion was needed. While they were at Nanjing, owing to the ceaseless bombing by Japanese aircraft, the whole family, against any mishap, had had their blood tested; He Wenjie (Wang's son-in-law) proved of the same blood type as Zeng Zhongming, and so it was Wenjie who gave blood. At this point the doctors declared the wounds too grave, and the case past hope. As for Zhongming's wife, Madame Fang Junbi, the two shots in the arm and the leg were happily without great harm; but of the one shot in the chest, the ball had struck at the tip of the right lung—a hair's breadth from death, one may say, for had it gone but a little lower, she might have died on the spot. Happily her constitution was sound, and after long treatment she did not become, with Zeng Zhongming, a pair of mandarin ducks sharing one fate—a great good fortune within misfortune. For the military hospital at Hanoi was not only poorly equipped; the doctors' skill was too little, and they lacked experience in treating gunshot wounds. When the doctors were transfusing blood into Zeng Zhongming, owing to the poorness of the instruments, Wenjie's blood could not be conveyed straight into Zhongming's body, but dripped, drop by drop, onto the floor; and Zhongming, seeing this, still knitted his brows and said to Wenjie: 'To have wasted so much of your precious blood—truly what a pity!' Madame Zeng—Madame Fang Junbi—having undergone her operation, was sent back to the ward, when Madame Wang Wenxing (Wang's daughter) suddenly found that a great wound was still exposed on her back, the blood and fluid still flowing without cease; it turned out that, by an oversight, it had never been bound up for her.

"At two o'clock that afternoon, Wang, hearing the report that Zeng Zhongming's wounds were past hope, resolved to go in person to the hospital to visit him. But Hanoi was still, for him, beset with peril on every side; the assailants showed that they had a strong backing, and might strike at any moment in the street. So when the car drove from Gao Lang Street to the hospital, He Wenjie, Madame Wang Wenxing and her husband, and Chen Guoqi (Wang's nephew), the three of them, sat in the carriage, while Wang lay hidden at their feet in front, covered over with clothing, in the hope that people would not suspect Wang was within. When Wang reached the hospital, there was already less than two hours left before Zhongming's death.

"The scene of Wang's visit was bitter enough to draw tears. Zhongming of course knew himself past all hope of living, yet his mind was, as it chanced, still perfectly clear. Wang faced this dying man—who from his boyhood had followed ever at his side, the orphaned kin of a revolutionary martyr, whom he had come to regard as his own flesh and blood, his most faithful comrade, and, for many years, his right hand. Today, sacrificed for him, his life hanging by a breath, the two, unwilling to wound each other's heart, still put on smiles to comfort each other. In fact the two said nothing at all; Wang, his eyes brimming with hot tears, gazed a few times in boundless grief, and in the end had no choice but to leave him and go.

"Zhongming, who at ordinary times could not bear even a little pain, yet, having taken this mortal and enormous injury, showed on the contrary an uncommon calm and strength. He suddenly bethought him of Wang's finances, which had all along been in his charge—the cash deposited in the bank, and the checks that had all along been signed by him; that if once he died, Wang might be thrown at once into straits. He resolutely asked to be allowed to sign a blank check, against any mishap. The others could only raise him up from his sickbed, and with trembling hand, spending his last strength, he signed the check at last. The first he signed all askew; he gritted his teeth—the pain of the wound past his enduring, the beads of sweat already standing on his brow—and managed at last, again with his final strength, to sign a second check; then he sank down again, and gasped without cease.

"Not long after Wang left the hospital, Zhongming's condition worsened by degrees … and, dragging on to four o'clock in the afternoon, he closed his eyes at last for good. It was Zhongming's death that spurred on the appearance of the Wang regime; and, turned about, one may also say that Zeng Zhongming's death was, in truth, the first sacrifice offered up for the Wang regime."

This passage's account of Zeng Zhongming's signing of the checks differs a little from what is said in The Secret Records of President Chiang. Reasoning by common sense: if they were not checks with a fixed limit, one signature would have been enough, and there would have been no need to "sign every one of the checks." So the writer, for one, thinks Zhu Zijia's account nearer to reason.

There is a sentence in the passage above—"it was Zhongming's death that spurred on the appearance of the Wang regime"—which is an overstatement; for the man Wang, set upon treason, had, as it turned out, long had his settled plan. Let me ask: before Zeng Zhongming's death, had not the man Wang already been planning to set up a "regime," the better to represent a "new China" and talk "peace" with Japan?

The man Wang, over Zeng Zhongming's grave wounding and death which he could not cure, felt something of the keen anguish of "giving his own body in the other's stead," and so with his own hand composed for Mr. Zeng Zhongming an "Account of Conduct," the original text of which is as follows.

An Account of the Conduct of Mr. Zeng Zhongming

Alas! I truly never thought that today I should take up the brush to write an account of the conduct of Zhongming! On the first of November of the twenty-fourth year, when I was shot at by an assailant in the Central Party Headquarters at Nanjing, and sat in a pool of blood, he came to see me, in great sorrow, and I comforted him with words; that scene is before my eyes even now—and now it is he who lies in a pool of blood, and comforts me with words. That day, though I was on the brink of death, I yet did not die; and now it is he who has closed his eyes, never to look again. The affairs of state having come to this, the dead are gone indeed; the living old man ought to follow them in death—whether it will avail the country or not, there is no knowing! And even should it avail, the boundless grief of him who dies after—when shall it ever end!

He was born on the twenty-eighth day of the second month, in the year bingshen, the sixteenth year before the era of the Republic of China, in Min County of Fujian. Orphaned young, his mother was most worthy. Among his brothers and sisters he was the youngest. His elder sister, Xing, married into the Fang family; widowed young, she brought her orphaned son Xianshu, together with her husband's younger sister Junying, and her own younger brothers Shengtao and Shengdong, to study together in Japan, where one after another they joined the Chinese Tongmenghui, and followed Mr. Sun in devotion to the revolution. In the year gengxu, together with Junying, and with Li Zhongshi, Yu Yunji, Huang Fusheng, Chen Bijun, and Zhaoming, he plotted to assassinate the Qing Prince Regent; the plot failed, and Fusheng and Zhaoming were seized. Again, with Junying and the rest, he took part in the Canton rising of the twenty-ninth of the third month in the xinhai year, in which Yunji and Shengdong fell in battle. In the first year of the Republic, with Junying, Bijun, and the others, he won a government-funded place to study in France, each bringing his younger brothers and sisters along, and stretching what three or four earned to keep six or seven.

At this time he was fifteen. Junbi, Junying's younger sister, was two years his junior; from childhood she had heard her sister's teaching, and knew the meaning of pledging oneself to one's country. Once she entered the Montargis lycée, she gave her whole mind to study, diligent and unremitting; and, mindful too that she was young and far from home, she would, at every school holiday, turn her hours of leisure to the making-up of Chinese learning, pressing forward on both fronts. Her learning grew day by day; and, schooled in diligence and thrift, firm in will and integrity, she nurtured here that pure and frugal conduct with which, in later days, she was to serve her country.

Since the first year of the Republic the affairs of state had known no rest, and Zhaoming was ever on the move; his wish to study abroad, though he had the will, he could not attain. But Zhongming gave himself deep and single-minded; graduating from the lycée, he went on to university, taking first chemistry and also letters, and in turn gained a bachelor's degree in chemistry at the University of Bordeaux in France, and a doctorate in letters at the University of Lyon—brilliant alike in name and in substance. He then held the post of secretary-general at the Franco-Chinese Institute at Lyon, and did much for Franco-Chinese education, keeping his mind, too, on political affairs at home; his political convictions were fixed at this time. He and Junbi had been fellow students from childhood, and were of a kindred bent; once made husband and wife, their union was of the happiest. Junbi gave herself to painting, and won a name at home and abroad. In the fourteenth year they returned home together, both to be professors at Sun Yat-sen University in Canton. When, on the first of July, the National Government was established, he was appointed a secretary—and this was the beginning of his devoting himself utterly to the affairs of state. From that time on, over several years, as China's progress and its turmoil rose and fell by turns, he and Zhaoming followed each other through hardship and peril; steady in judgment and calm in spirit, yet, having tasted every danger and come to know the true from the false, his largeness of measure grew with each passing day.

In December of the twentieth year, at the Fourth National Congress of the Chinese Nationalist Party, he was elected an alternate member of the Central Executive Committee. On the twenty-eighth of January of the twenty-first year, he was appointed secretary-general of the Executive Yuan, and soon transferred to be vice-minister of Railways. At that time the Northeast was already lost, and Shanghai too was under arms; the whole nation was in peril, and made the saving of the state from ruin its business, while the Communist Party, seizing the chance, grew ever more rampant in Jiangxi, plotting to overthrow the Republic of China. The Central authorities thereupon resolved that, within, all must rest upon the Three Principles of the People, to complete the building of the Republic of China; and most needful of all was to enrich the people's strength and develop the national strength, to make the people's livelihood ample and the national defense firm, sweeping away every obstacle whatsoever. Without, they must, by peace and justice, seek the world's sympathy and aid, and look for Japan's final awakening. All these resolves probed deep into the sense of root and branch, and fixed the course and the steps for saving the state from ruin. The great plan once settled, it was proclaimed to the whole nation, to be carried out with one accord. The Chairman of the Military Affairs Commission, Chiang Kai-shek, took command of the forces at Nanchang, and bore the charge of suppressing the bandits; the other organs of administration, too, all worked with one heart to realize the affairs of state. He, in the Ministry of Railways, aided the minister, Gu Mengyu, in improving the railways; and though the coffers were desperately short and debts had piled up, he spared no effort in planning and contriving. In turn he set up the Jinpu train-ferry, extended the Longhai Railway, and completed the Canton–Hankou Railway—this last a work that, since the closing years of the former Qing, the whole nation had looked toward but never been able to finish; and now at last one could go straight from Canton to Wuhan, linking with the Beiping–Hankou line, a great contribution to the national defense and the people's livelihood. In November of the twenty-fourth year, at the Fifth National Congress of the Chinese Nationalist Party, he was again elected an alternate member of the Central Executive Committee, and soon resigned his post as vice-minister of Railways. In February of the following year he went abroad with Zhaoming, and returned upon the Xi'an Incident that December.

In February of the twenty-sixth year he took office as deputy secretary-general of the Central Political Committee. In August, the Central Political Committee, on the outbreak of the war of resistance, specially set up the Supreme National Defense Council, with him as director of its secretariat. At that time the Central authorities resolved to throw the whole nation's strength into the resistance, while the mediation of peace still ran alongside, not at odds with it. When, on the seventh of July, the Marco Polo Bridge Incident broke out, the Central authorities still declared their willingness to take up every means of international mediation and reconciliation, to still the war. When, after the thirteenth of August, the fighting spread to Shanghai, and the powers of the Nine-Power Treaty met in conference at Brussels and proposed mediation, the Central authorities still accepted it. And in early December, as Nanjing was on the point of falling, the German ambassador, by his government's command, conveyed Japan's peace conditions, which the Central authorities agreed to take as the basis of a peace negotiation. In September of the twenty-seventh year, when the League of Nations met, the Central authorities again instructed their delegate to ask that Article 17 of the Covenant be applied—this too a peaceful means of settling the dispute. All these facts are plain to all at home and abroad; but their hidden and intricate turns he, from his part in the confidential business, knew deeper and more nearly than any.

Now the great question of war and peace bears on the state's life and death, its safety and its peril; to fight when one must fight, and to make peace when peace is possible—this is the constant rule of statecraft. Moreover, since China took up the resistance, the whole land has been under arms; the ground lost stretches to nine provinces; the officers and men dead and wounded number over a million; the people are ground to dust and mire, in numbers more than manyfold that. If the peace conditions do no harm to the state's survival and independence, then to end the war and seek to make good the losses is what one loyal to the state's counsels ought most to propose. But the Communists have no fatherland in their hearts: at the first they sought, by the Shanghai fighting, to pin down the national army so that they might hold Jiangxi; and when, after years of being suppressed, they had fled from the southeast to the northwest, and were at their last extremity, they again used the Xi'an Incident, took the name of resistance, and turned men's eyes away. Once the resistance had begun, they seized the moment when the whole nation hung between life and death to expand yet further their political organization and their armies, that they might in the end accomplish their plot to overthrow the Republic of China. Knowing that if a peace were made it must be against their designs, they set all their strength to wreck it, luring and turning this way and that, so that in provocation, estrangement, incitement, and slander there was nothing they left undone. Zhaoming, grieved that the affairs of state were being thus deranged, and fearful that the state's great plan, held hostage by base men, would not escape ruin, spoke of it again and again in the Supreme National Defense Council. On the ninth of December, the Chairman of the Military Affairs Commission, Chiang Kai-shek, came to Chongqing, and he spoke of it again in the sharpest terms; but in the end it was not taken up. So on the eighteenth he left Chongqing, on the nineteenth reached Hanoi—Zhongming going with him—and on the twenty-ninth made his memorandum public to the world.

In the small hours before dawn on the twenty-first of March of the twenty-eighth year, at the hour of the ox, before day had broken, several assailants, bearing arms, burst into the residence and fired several tens of rounds, wounding five persons; he was the most gravely hurt, and died that day at the hour of the monkey. His wife, Junbi, braving her body to save him, also took three shots; the other three were wounded, in varying degree; and of the assailants, three were taken. The next day, the French papers all marked it, in large characters, as the work of the Blue Shirts; and, by the assailants' own confession, the aim of the murder was in truth Zhaoming, and so forth.

In his life he wrote much in letters, but in politics he laid weight on action and spoke little; and, being placed in confidential matters, he made discretion the more his study. Yet it was just because he was placed in confidential matters that he saw clearly into the course of the Central authorities' decisions, and the reasons for their stumblings and changes. His care for the state being deep, and while he yet lived, he sought some way to save it; his sincerity long stored, he resolved at last to do what his heart found peace in, careless alike of the world's idle praise and blame, and of his own life and death, weal and woe. Alas! This may be called both benevolent and brave!

From his wounding to his death his mind was clear; he said to kin and friends: "For the affairs of state there is Mr. Wang; for the affairs of the house there is my wife; there is nothing I am not easy about!" His wife, Junbi, taking three wounds in her body, watched him meet his end, and, enduring her pain, said: "In such an age as this, one may die for the resistance, and may die too for the pursuit of peace; we ought, with our own single deaths, to buy the survival of the nation and the race." When he died, his three sons were all young. The two houses of Fang and Zeng, from the closing years of the former Qing, took part in the revolution, down to this day—some dying for the affairs of state, some laboring to the end without cease. Zhaoming and he consorted the more closely, being joined by public duty and private friendship alike; and at his death, he grieved for the nation, and grieved for the two houses. Written in haste, this cannot do justice to his life; I set down only the greatest of his aims and deeds, to tell his comrades, that they may know how to carry on the tale. (The sixth of April of the twenty-eighth year.)

### (3) By Sophistry, "To Cite One Instance" Pleads in Defense of Suing for Surrender

The single blow having missed, and Zeng Zhongming being hurt while Wang got off by luck, Wang, unwilling to keep silent, made besides a piece of pleading, entitled "To Cite One Instance," and, biting back at us, said: "The advocacy of peace was a view discussed and jointly decided by the highest organ."

This thing was got up truly without skill; its places of sophistry are too many; it may hoodwink a few, but it can hardly win the belief and sympathy of the multitude.

The piece was drafted on the twenty-seventh of March—that is, six days after the "Hanoi affair"—and was not made public until the ninth of April.

I have here found a copy of the original text of "To Cite One Instance," which I ask the reader first to peruse; after which there are related documents to offer for reference. The text runs as follows:

To Cite One Instance

When Mr. Zeng Zhongming lay dying, he spoke two grave and simple sentences: "For the affairs of state there is Mr. Wang; for the affairs of the house there is my wife; there is nothing I am uneasy about." Mr. Zeng's views on the affairs of state were the same as mine; and because our views were the same, he did not this time escape death.

Mr. Zeng's death was a death for the country, a death for his views on the affairs of state. At his death, because there yet remained I, who held the same views on the affairs of state, he took comfort. While I have one breath left, to comfort the friend who thought ever of me as I think ever of him, I ought the more to spend my utmost effort, that the view may be realized—the more so as the realizing of this view is bound up with the survival of the nation and the race.

Because I issued the Yan Telegram, I was reckoned an advocate of peace; and advocacy of peace is my view on the affairs of state. Is it my view alone? It is not! It is a view discussed and jointly decided by the highest organ. Is there evidence for this? The evidence is not to be numbered in hundreds and thousands; but let me now cite one instance!

The Fifty-fourth Standing Committee Meeting of the Supreme National Defense Council. Time: nine a.m. on the sixth of December of the twenty-sixth year. Place: the Central Bank at Hankou. Present: Yu Youren, Ju Zheng, Kong Xiangxi;

In attendance: Chen Guofu, Chen Bulei, Xu Kan, Xu Mo, Weng Wenhao, Shao Lizi, Chen Lifu, Dong Xianguang;

Chairman: Vice-Chairman Wang. Secretary-General: Zhang Qun. Director of the Secretariat: Zeng Zhongming.

Vice-Minister Xu (Mo) reported: "The German ambassador to China, Trautmann, on the twenty-eighth of last month received instructions from the German government, and came to see President Kong (Xiangxi); on the forenoon of the twenty-ninth he saw Minister Wang (Chonghui) again, and stated: 'He was instructed by his government as follows: the German ambassador to Japan had, at Tokyo, held talks with the two Japanese ministers of the Army and of Foreign Affairs, inquiring whether Japan wished to bring the present situation to an end, and asking under what conditions the Japanese government, wishing to end the present situation, could bring it to an end. The Japanese government thereupon put forward several conditions, and charged Germany to convey them to the Chinese authorities. The conditions were: (1) autonomy for Inner Mongolia; (2) the demilitarized zone in North China to be enlarged, but the administrative power in North China still to belong wholly to the Central government—only it was hoped that in future no man hostile to Japan should be sent as North China's supreme head. If the matter could be ended now, it would be done thus; if in future a new regime should be established in North China, it should be let stand—but, up to the present day, the Japanese side had no intention of setting up a regime in North China. As for the mineral development now under negotiation, it should still be carried on; (3) the Shanghai truce zone to be enlarged—as to how it should be enlarged, Japan did not mention, but the administrative power at Shanghai to remain as before; (4) on the anti-Japanese question, it was hoped this would be handled in the spirit that Minister Zhang Qun and Kawagoe had shown last year; the detailed method was a technical matter; (5) on the anti-Communist question, the Japanese side hoped there might be some fitting arrangement; (6) the question of tariff improvement; (7) China to respect the rights of foreigners in China'—and so forth. After the ambassador had seen President Kong and Minister Wang, he expressed the wish that he might go to see Generalissimo Chiang, and at once wired to ask leave; the Generalissimo at once said the ambassador might come for a talk. On the thirtieth I accordingly accompanied the ambassador to Nanjing, and in the boat had a private talk with him, in which he said: 'China, in resisting Japan until now, has already shown its spirit of resistance; and now the time to end it has come. In the European war, Germany had several chances to make peace, but in the end trusted its own strength and would not; and only when the Treaty of Versailles was signed, and men set what conditions they would, could Germany not but accept them.' The ambassador cited too Hitler's opinion, hoping China would consider it; and said that, as he saw it, Japan's conditions were not harsh. On the second of December we reached the capital, and I first saw the Generalissimo. The Generalissimo, having weighed what I set forth, said he must confer with the various generals in the capital. At four in the afternoon I went again; those present were now Gu Mo-san (Zhutong), Bai Jiansheng (Chongxi), Tang Meng-xiao (Shengzhi), and Xu Ci-chen (Yongchang). The Generalissimo bade me report on the ambassador's mission to the capital; when I had done, each asked whether there were other conditions, and whether there was any limit on our armaments. I answered that, by the ambassador's account, there were only the conditions now put forward, with no other appendix; and that if we could agree, the fighting could cease. The Generalissimo first asked Meng-xiao's opinion; Tang did not answer at once. He asked again what Jiansheng thought; Bai said that if these were all the conditions, then why were we fighting. I answered that what the ambassador had put forward were only these several conditions. The Generalissimo asked again: what was Ci-chen's opinion? Xu answered: if these were all the conditions, they might be agreed to. He asked again Mo-san; Gu answered that they might be agreed to. He asked once more Meng-xiao; Tang too voiced agreement with the others' opinions. The Generalissimo thereupon said: 'This mediation of Germany's should not be refused,' and said: 'Even so, these are not yet terms of national ruin. (2) The North China regime must be preserved.'"

At five in the afternoon, the ambassador saw the Generalissimo, I acting at his side as interpreter. What the ambassador said to the Generalissimo was the same as what he had said at Hankou to President Kong and Minister Wang, but with one sentence added: that if it were not agreed to now, and the fighting went on, the future conditions might not be thus. The Generalissimo stated: (1) He dared not trust Japan—Japan might not keep its word on the conditions; but Germany was a good friend, and had put forth such effort in mediating; and because he trusted Germany and was grateful for Germany's kind mediation, the several conditions might be taken as the basis and compass of a negotiation. But there were still two points he must ask the ambassador to report to the German government: (1) that in our negotiation with Japan, Germany must be the mediator throughout—that is, Germany must play the go-between to the end; (2) that the administrative sovereignty of North China must be maintained to the end. Within this compass, the conditions might be taken as the basis of a negotiation—only Japan must not regard itself as a victor, or take these conditions for an ultimatum. The ambassador then asked whether he might add a sentence. The Generalissimo said he might. The ambassador said: "In the negotiation, the Chinese government should adopt an attitude of forbearance." The Generalissimo said: "It is the same for both sides." The Generalissimo said further: "In fighting so urgent as this, there is no mediating or negotiating; I hope Germany will tell Japan to cease fire first." The ambassador said: "The two points the Generalissimo has raised I can convey on his behalf. If Germany is willing to mediate, and Japan is willing too, then Führer Hitler may propose that China and Japan first cease fire." The Generalissimo said: "If Japan regards itself as a victor, and first spreads it about that China has agreed to the several conditions, then the negotiation can go no further." On the way back, the ambassador said that this talk, in his view, gave ground for hope. On reaching the capital, the ambassador also said to the Generalissimo: "These conditions are not an ultimatum." The ambassador, in the boat, at once wired Tokyo and Berlin, but up to now there has been no reply. How things will develop hereafter is not yet to be known.

Is there any other evidence besides? Not to be numbered in hundreds and thousands! But its nature is not yet a thing of the past; and for the state's weal and woe there is need to keep it strictly secret. The affair of the ambassador's mediation, however, is already a thing of the past, and so it may be brought forward to cite as one instance. From this there arise the following three questions. First: comparing what the ambassador said at the time with the Konoe cabinet's declaration of the twenty-second of December last year—if what the ambassador said could be a basis for peace negotiation, why can the Konoe declaration not be a basis for negotiation?

Second: when the ambassador was busy mediating, Nanjing had not yet fallen, and it was already held that a peace negotiation might proceed. Why, when the Konoe declaration came, with Nanjing, Jinan, Xuzhou, Kaifeng, Anqing, Jiujiang, Canton, and Wuhan all fallen one after another—Changsha not yet fallen, but already burned clean by our own hand—why then can a peace negotiation, on the contrary, not proceed?

Third: when the ambassador was busy mediating, the men of the Supreme National Defense Council, whether at Nanjing or at Wuhan, were already of one view. Why, when the Konoe declaration came, should the views differ again? Nay, even the man whose view differed must be defamed; and defaming not sufficing, they must take his life besides, that he might no longer serve the country.

To these three questions I do not wish to reply. But on the great question of war and peace, I cannot but speak once more for the people.

Someone says: "Having advocated war, one ought not again advocate peace." This will not do! The aim of the state is survival and independence; war and peace are but means to this aim. When one must fight, one fights; when one may make peace, then whether one may make peace is decided by the conditions. If the conditions harm the state's survival and independence, then one may not make peace; if the conditions do not harm the state's survival and independence, then one may make peace. "Even so, these are not yet terms of national ruin"—the words are still in our ears; let me ask, what is wrong with advocating peace? Someone says: "China has gained unity through the resistance; if peace is advocated, then the state of unity will fall apart again." This I absolutely oppose. From of old to now, one who bears responsibility for the state should only, for the sake of repelling the foreign foe, settle the interior; he should on no account, for the sake of settling the interior, repel the foreign foe. What sort of thing is a foreign war, to be used as a means of internal unity? China is seeking the state's survival and independence, and does not resist for the sake of internal unity. To use the resistance as a means of internal unity—this I absolutely oppose! The more so as, in today's affair, to advocate peace does not harm unity, and not to advocate peace will not keep the country from splitting.

Someone says: "If peace is advocated, the Communist Party will at once make trouble." I hold that the Communist Party has trouble-making for its very nature: advocate war, and they make trouble; advocate peace, and they make trouble. Should the Communists' trouble-making come into the open at a time of advocating peace, it can only be better, not worse, than the present state of things, in which they manipulate and hold sway, provoke and estrange.

Someone says: "The world does not wish us to make peace." I hold that peace and war are bound up with the survival of the nation and the race, and should be decided by ourselves, standing in the active place, using diplomacy so that the international situation may favor us; we should on no account bow and rise at another's bidding. The more so as, at present, apart from the Third International, no other country opposes our making peace.

From what has been set out above, it is already clear. There is this too to declare in earnest: after the defeat in the war of jiawu there was a humiliating peace; after the defeat in the year gengzi there was a humiliating peace; these are things one grieves to speak of, and I would not have this peace be such. After the Franco-Prussian War, France had a humiliating peace, and only in the Great War drew breath at last; after the Great War, Germany had a humiliating peace, and only in these days has drawn breath; these are things one speaks of with pride, and I would not have this peace be such either. For such a cycle of retaliation, without end, is by no means the way to lasting peace and order. What I seek with all sincerity is a plan for East Asia to last a hundred years. I have seen it clearly through, and have concluded, that between China and Japan it is plain as day: war means the wounding of both; peace means the survival of both. If the two countries, in peace, will but strive together, they can surely lay the foundation of a lasting order in East Asia. Otherwise, there is only mutual ruin, both perishing together. Of this view the people of both countries have their doubters; yet they have too their firm believers—the more so after twenty months of bitter fighting, in which Japan's expenditure has not been small, and China's sacrifice not light. The one road of mutual ruin, both perishing together; and the other road of common survival and common development—both lie plain before us. Can it be that the men of resolve in the two countries, awed by the momentary weal and woe, praise and blame, will pace and hesitate, and dare not resolutely make their choice? I hope all will press on in the spirit of independence, unbending and unyielding! The first to give himself in sacrifice for the peaceful founding of the nation—Mr. Zeng Zhongming—has already, with his own blood, lit up for us this great road of common survival and common development, and gone forward on it!

Last of all, I have a few more words. In mid-February, Chongqing sent a Central Committee member, a certain gentleman, to bring me a passport, that I might leave the country. I asked him to convey a few sentences. First: had I not left Chongqing, the Yan Telegram could not have been issued; yet at such a time of peril, to leave Chongqing was already grievous enough—how much more to leave the country! The reason I was willing to leave the country was only to show that, if the view were but adopted, the individual was no question. Second: I hear the National Government is striving to bring about international mediation; this is well. Yet at the least, international mediation and direct negotiation should proceed together. Thus I, in a private station, might lend help from the side—which would not be without avail. Third: if the National Government in the end will not make up its mind, but lets this state of things drift on, then, though I have left the country, I will come back. These few sentences are surely what made up the cause of the affair of the twenty-first of March. What is to be lamented is that Mr. Zeng Zhongming, younger than I, carried his aspiration to the grave, and died before me.

After this piece of mine is published, may my countrymen take pains to read this piece of mine, and understand my view—that it is the essential road to China's survival and independence, and at the same time the essential road to the lasting peace and order of the world and of East Asia. Though my view cannot for the time be adopted by the Chongqing side, one day it will be adopted by the whole people, and even by the peoples of both China and Japan; and then I can be without regret.

(The twenty-seventh of March of the twenty-eighth year.) After the man Wang's "To Cite One Instance" was published at Hanoi and Hong Kong simultaneously on the ninth of April, on the eleventh of April the Generalissimo, Mr. Chiang, set down his feelings: "Many a false and treacherous man have I seen, but never one so base as Wang."

This was the first time, since Wang Jingwei issued the "Yan Telegram," that the Generalissimo, Mr. Chiang, put to paper the severest rebuke of the man Wang. The "falseness" and the "baseness" pointed to in the diary must have had their concrete facts; but men of our level, the middle-grade comrades at the work, would not have known them clearly. Lately, as it chanced, I came upon a copy of the full text of "A Further Reading of Wang Jingwei's 'To Cite One Instance,'" from the brush of Central Supervisory Committee member Wu Jingheng (Zhihui); this piece mocks Wang Jingwei to the very dregs, and where it describes the "falseness and baseness" it cuts, as it were, into the very wood three-tenths of an inch deep. The whole runs to nine thousand words, and I leave it for the next section to copy out entire, for the reader's perusal.

On the seventeenth of April, the Generalissimo, Mr. Chiang, received the Chinese and foreign correspondents, and in a statement declared: that unless Japan's warlords awakened thoroughly, gave up aggression of their own accord, and rooted out their dream, there could be no talk of peace in East Asia ever; he stressed the whole strategy and the consistent policy of trading space for time, of massing small victories into a great one, which must surely deal the Japanese army a mortal blow; and he exposed the enemy state's plot of "building a New Order in East Asia," sternly refuting the traitors' and state-thieves' country-selling fallacies. Though the statement did not plainly name Wang Jingwei, it was aimed precisely at the many pleas in "To Cite One Instance."

The full text of the Generalissimo's questions and answers with the correspondents runs as follows:

(Correspondent's question): There are still those who say that Konoe's declaration of building a New Order in East Asia can serve as a basis for a Sino-Japanese peace, and do not regard it as terms of national ruin. How does the Generalissimo hold this should be dealt with?

The Generalissimo's answer: The doctrine of building a New Order in East Asia is Japan's new name for swallowing up China. In my speech of the twenty-sixth of December last year, I already analyzed the enemy state's aggressive ambition in full detail, and there is no need to labor it. If Konoe's declaration is to be construed as the word "peace" in "terms one may make peace on," then in the dictionary one need no longer keep the word "surrender." This is a joke of the most shameless kind; not only will there be no such thing today, there will never be such a thing at all. The aim of China's resistance is to guard the survival, independence, and freedom of the whole race; and while this aim cannot for one day be attained, our struggle cannot for one day cease. Now the longer the fighting drags on, the more Japan's weak points are exposed, and the more evident its ambition—which is what is meant by the map unrolling to reveal the dagger. Looking at the doings of Japan's warlords, anyone can know that they mean to seize the very life of our whole race, and make us forever their slaves. Konoe's so-called building of a New Order in East Asia is just Japan's design to lord it alone over East Asia—not only to wipe out China, but to wipe out the power of every nation in the Pacific. Japan's lust for aggression grows with the times; and China's recognition of the advance of Japan's ambition grows ever clearer, and so our will to resist grows ever firmer. In such a case there is absolutely no room for peace, and absolutely nothing that any cunning, false, hypocritical theory of surrender can do to shake the resolve of our whole nation by one jot. Toward the men who advocate such a thing of the most shameless kind, I hold that, by the reason, the morals, and the conscience of us Chinese, the force of a sanction is greater than the force of a sanction in law; such men have, in spirit, already pronounced their own death sentence, and are truly not worth mentioning; as for the state's punishment, that must be fixed by how they act hereafter.

(Correspondent's question): May I ask, how in the end can the Sino-Japanese war be resolved?

The Generalissimo's answer: This question depends on how the facts unfold hereafter. Now our resistance has lasted only twenty-two months, not yet two full years, and Japan's international standing as a first-class power has already been pressed down, by our resistance, to the standing of a second-class power. If we press the resistance harder, then—judging by past facts—I believe that within no long time we shall make even its present second-class standing untenable; so that on the one hand its national strength will be the more exhausted and its standing the lower, and on the other we shall enrich our own strength, until, reaching a favorable time and a favorable ground, we deal it a mortal blow, and make it truly recognize the strength of China's resistance—and only then is there hope of peace being born. I believe this is not only possible; if only we strive, the coming of this time is sure to be very soon. Apart from this, if one would have peace restored between China and Japan, then it must be that Japan's warlords awaken thoroughly, give up aggression of their own accord, and change their traditional policy at the root. If they do not give up their policy of aggression at the root, and cannot root out their so-called dream of a New Order in East Asia, then not only is there absolutely no possibility of the Sino-Japanese war ending, but East Asia too will have no time of peace ever. Today, any son of the Yellow Emperor with the least heart in him, however low his degree of knowledge, will surely never say that Konoe's New Order in East Asia is not terms of national ruin for swallowing up China. The enemy's ambition and the Chinese people's recognition have grown plain to this degree; if there is still anyone who pleads that the Konoe declaration is not terms for the destruction of China, and holds that peace is yet possible—what is this but suing for surrender and selling the country? To speak plainly, such a man not only does not shrink from setting himself up as a traitor; he is simply willing to be Japan's slave.

(Correspondent's question): How will the war situation evolve hereafter—will China still carry through, to the end, its original strategy of long-term resistance?

The Generalissimo's answer: Five years ago I gave a speech on resisting the foreign insult and reviving the race, discussing the strategy of our resistance should war break out between China and Japan; you gentlemen need only read that speech to know that the strategy we use today was already decided five years ago. We will by no means change the whole strategy and consistent policy of trading space for time and massing small victories into a great one. We resolved then: not to fight was one thing, but once we fought we must carry it through to the end; and even should the whole country everywhere fall utterly, we have the assurance and the confidence to win it back again. At that time, thinking from the least favorable situation, we had already made our final resolve; and our estimate then was that the enemy would need eighteen months to press into our eighteen provinces. Now our resistance has already lasted twenty-two months, and we still hold many whole provinces intact; even in the provinces already occupied, only a few provincial towns and strongpoints have fallen, and in those provinces all the land and people are still within the grasp of our armies' sphere of power. The enemy has not only gained nothing at all; he is, moreover, sinking, just as our plan foresaw, into the mire, and treading the road to collapse—so that hereafter, everywhere and at all times, he will take from us the final mortal blow. Therefore, speaking of the whole situation of the resistance today, we can by no means gauge the final success or failure of the resistance by the gain or loss of a few provincial towns and strongpoints. When all understand our consistent policy and whole strategy, they will no more be deceived by those shameless traitor theories that do the enemy's work.

### (4) A Great Traitor All Along, Who Stole the Sky and Changed the Sun, and Won Favor by Flattery

Mr. Wu Jingheng (Zhihui), Central Supervisory Committee member of the Chinese Nationalist Party, on the eleventh of April, aimed at the "To Cite One Instance" Wang Jingwei had issued, and himself published at Chongqing a piece, "A Further Reading of Wang Jingwei's 'To Cite One Instance.'" This piece is rare and precious, such as was never had before, and may well rank as one of the important documents in the history of the resistance. It is not only rich in content; it embraces many secret passages of history; and of the man Wang's cunning and shiftiness, his endless fickleness, it makes concrete exposure. Besides, there are not a few places of raillery in it—judgment hidden beneath the surface, the grave and the droll mingled—every one cutting into the very wood three-tenths of an inch deep, so that one reads it with a glad heart and a keen delight, unable to hold back a laugh, and stirred to sympathy. And it is only this elder statesman of the nation, the venerable Wu Zhihui, famed for his humor, who can speak so; from another's brush it would not have been quite the thing, and would surely never have had this seasoned, sure, and forceful touch.

"A Further Reading of Wang Jingwei's 'To Cite One Instance'" runs in full as follows.

"For long I heard nothing of Wang Jingwei's whereabouts; then came the startling news in the papers that Mr. Zeng Zhongming had been cruelly slain by patriotic overseas Chinese; and hard upon it a friend in Hong Kong sent me besides a poem and an essay—the poem being Wang's, whose despondency ran beyond its true occasion, to which I have already replied, matching its rhymes and setting him right by way of parable; the essay being what was issued after Mr. Zeng's death, its main drift being the fear that, once fallen under the frost and dew of a morning, he should be forever branded with the traitor's name, and that men should not credit the pains of his advocacy of peace—so, to speak for himself, and to cry Mr. Zeng's wrong. Alas! Wang's plight is a sorry one, and grievous enough! But I would that, from now on, Wang have only the one great blunder of the Yan Telegram, that reckless indiscretion, and never play out to the end a scene of selling the country to win glory; then the gentleman's fault would be like an eclipse of sun or moon, which all men see. Wang's splendid history would then not in the end be cast into the privy, and it would also greatly comfort our friend Mr. Zeng below the earth. But since Wang's essay appeared once in the Nanhua Ribao, the news at home and abroad has come thick and fast—how Gao Zongwu and the rest did thus and so; how there was a welcome at Tokyo, secret parleys at Hanoi, and thus and so again. I must now, giving ground, allow that all this may be the oversensitiveness of newsmen's heads; yet for a man of weight to loose his words lightly is well-nigh, like the Yan Telegram, to invite trouble. Looking back to Wang's sudden southward journey last winter, when the Yan Telegram had not yet been issued, I myself fell into a fog five li deep, and could make nothing of it. Wang Jingwei—the Party Leader's earliest disciple, the party's Vice-Director-General—ought to have shared the party's very existence; he ought not, when the savage foe was just planning to bomb Chongqing madly, to have fled before the battle and hidden himself in a place of safety. Had one bidden me then guess that he might have some other design, how should I have dared, with a mean man's belly, to gauge a gentleman's heart? But to take it simply as too great a weariness, a wish for a little rest, seemed still not of a kind. So, bethinking me that his wife, Chen Bijun, was a heroine among women, and seemed the abler head, I sent off a quick letter to Hanoi. The text of it ran thus: 'To Madame Bijun: Mr. Jingwei has of late been with me at Chongqing; I saw him presiding at meetings by the ten in a week, and giving addresses again by the ten; and besides, in his cares over telegrams and dispatches, and his attendance on colleagues and guests, he was scarce at rest day or night; I reckoned he would fall ill, and now indeed he is ill. To be ill and take a little rest, and after a short recovery to shoulder again the great and hard—this is what one greatly hopes. Only, the doings of a man of weight are easily turned by the enemy's spies and by slanderers. At this time, we are but at the beginning of the resistance—with the weak facing the strong, we mass our main force, and wait till the enemy is worn deep before we destroy him: this is the whole nation's common faith. That is, he profits by "a swift war and a quick decision," and we profit by "long-term resistance"—a national policy fixed by four hundred and fifty million souls together. Therefore, on the night we left the capital the year before last, it was arranged to draw the enemy into the mountain country: you, Madame, and Mr. Jingwei, together with all who attended the Supreme Council, took counsel on this policy—that we should take a great loss, and so let him get his feet stuck in the mire, greedy to lure us to peace, and marshal his forces to come again. This is like the sumo wrestler, at the moment when his strength is spent and his voice grown hoarse, when the taiji boxer is just about to lay on the finishing hand; and to let him, instead, take his rest—is this not the anguish of Yue the loyal and martial and the twelve gold tablets? It would set the whole globe laughing and ten thousand ages cursing. Since of old there is no state that does not fall, and no man that does not die; but a man has his character, a state has its character. For a man to die of being lured, and a state to fall of being lured—both are the shame of the race. You, Madame, aided Mr. Jingwei in the attempt to strike Zaifeng at Beiping; for thirty years, you and Mr. Jingwei, like Liang Hong and Meng Guang, have shone bright as the stars of the Dipper, looked up to by the whole nation. "I bare the blade and laugh, true to the boy I was"—all men believe that an elder statesman's face is more splendid still than a young man's head. Mr. Jingwei, in his illness, knows nothing of outside affairs; I would that you, Madame, quickly clear away this stain, that the true man may forever appear again in the sky—as you, Madame, will surely have done already. I humbly count myself among your distant friends, and make bold to add this one word; and I hope Mr. Jingwei may soon recover. Your younger brother Wu Jingheng bows his head. The twenty-fifth of December.' I know not that, by the time this letter reached Hanoi, Wang had already sent the Yan Telegram to Hong Kong. And what is stranger yet: on the twenty-sixth, at the memorial meeting, Generalissimo Chiang had here already refuted in detail the fallacies Konoe issued on the twenty-second, and it was printed at home and abroad—how could Wang not know? Why then, on the twenty-ninth, did he publish his Yan Telegram besides? Except that he was set on speculating in the savage foe and shaking men's hearts, what other explanation is there? Well might the whole nation be enraged, and all men grieved and hateful. I grew anxious for him, and said: Wang is brewing his own bitter draught. Even to reckon by the small circle, martyrs like Wen Shengcai, I fear, are today the more numerous outside the nation's gates. And so it proved: Lin Baisheng was struck before, Mr. Zeng Zhongming was slain after—needless sacrifices, all brought on by an incautious word or deed. Afterward he strove hard to make amends, and out came 'To Cite One Instance' again; yet the more he speaks, the muddier he grows. As the ancients said: 'A word once out, four horses cannot catch it'—is it not so? O Wang! The gentleman's fault is like an eclipse of sun or moon, which all men see; be but never a traitor to the end, and all men will look up to him; and let it be left to filial sons and loving grandsons to explain it, saying: it was but one word held unwise; his heart, it is proved, had nothing else in it. Wang cites one instance; I have all along a further reading. First—Wang insists that the advocacy of peace was not his alone, and so lays bare the record of the National Defense meeting: this is to leak diplomatic and military secrets, for which the law has a clear penalty; and, what is more, he was himself the chairman of that meeting, and to use his own office and station to leak secrets is a heavier crime still. Wang this time himself knows too: 'for the state's weal and woe there is need to keep it strictly secret.' Now, thanks to the leaking of the secret, the talk of that time is, in effect, his own proclaiming of his own indictment. The course of the ambassador's mediation at that time, and the diplomatic secret—to this day the enemy dares not use it for propaganda, nor has the ambassador let out a word of it abroad; yet Wang has taken the Yan Telegram of his own advocacy, admitting that at Chongqing he was not free—but, having left Chongqing and being in a place of freedom, why did he not send a secret telegram to the Central authorities, as a private proposal? Instead he first published it at Hong Kong; so what your countrymen hate in you is not that they are keen to criticize your private view, but that they despise you for leaking state secrets, seeking the enemy's trust, wrecking the interest of the resistance, and making a propaganda to sate a private greed. At this urgent pass of the resistance, at the moment when the state hangs between life and death, when the officers and men at the front, and the whole nation's countrymen, are risking their lives to fight the enemy and win the final victory, then, whatever view any individual may have, not to offer it to the government but to publish it openly—this is a thing neither the law of the state nor human feeling can allow; how much more when you were the chairman at the time? So Wang's insisting on whether he advocated peace or not is all mere wrangling, and forgets the crime of his open counter-propaganda. I make bold to say: except for speculating in surrender to the enemy, wishing to be a puppet, and shaking men's hearts to reach the aim of ruining the state, what other explanation is there? Second—on the matter of peace or no peace, Wang has all along been wrong in his observation. Is there not, in the secret diplomatic talk he laid bare, a passage of Mr. Chiang's, clear-sighted as one seeing fire? Namely: 'What the Generalissimo stated to the ambassador—that he dared not trust Japan; that Japan could tear up a treaty, and its word could still less be counted on.' And there was, in the conditions the ambassador conveyed at the time, already plainly this back-stage sentence: 'If in future a new regime should be established in North China, it should be let stand'—brazenly slipped in. Had not the Generalissimo, the year before last, on the contrary put forward two demanded conditions, meeting this proposal with a strict and upright bearing, then, even had the ambassador's good intent been realized, it would have been no more than Prime Minister Chamberlain being taken in (at the Munich Conference), persuading Czechoslovakia to cede the Sudetenland (to Germany): in less than half a year, Czechoslovakia was bound hand and foot, and handed over the whole country. What drug of stupefaction Wang has drunk, I know not, that this time, in 'To Cite One Instance,' he should say: 'The Japanese conditions the ambassador related were not so clearly drawn, and were harsher than these.' Does he then mean the many lies with which Konoe cheated the rustic and the idiot—no indemnity, no cession of land, abolition of the concessions, and so on? Even without reading Mr. Chiang's detailed refutation, every schoolboy understands that the so-called 'economic cooperation' means China's money is all his money; the so-called 'mixed residence in the interior' means the whole country becomes a concession, and China's land is his land too; why then should he need you to pay indemnity? why need you to cede land? why need you to grant concessions? Why, such 'terms of national ruin' as even a schoolboy understands, does Wang alone pretend not to understand? Pinching up red dog's dung and blowing on it as a firelighter—if I were to defend Wang and say this was not a case of greed blinding the wits, who would believe it? So my Hong Kong friend wrote to tell me again: The heroine among women you worship, Madame Wang—I had the luck to slip in among her 'comrades' and hear her secret address. She analyzed it thus: 'We do not grudge stirring up an internal disorder to topple him. Once peace is made, we let Mr. Wang come out and organize a "Central Government," and the state will not fall.' I fear this frenzy of a lost mind, husband singing and wife following, is simply standing bolt upright to dream. Wang further enumerates: 'When the Konoe declaration came, Nanjing, Jinan, Xuzhou, Kaifeng, Anqing, Jiujiang, Canton, and Wuhan had all fallen; Changsha had not yet fallen, but had already burned itself clean. A peace negotiation, on the contrary, could not proceed.' At that time I saw Wang, after Canton fell, wailing and stamping as though he had lost his parents. I took it that Wang, having such a hatred of the enemy state unavenged, had the air of one ashamed to face his ancestors. How was I to know it was rather that he repented of kneeling too late! The changes of a great man are truly beyond what we lowly fools can fathom. Perhaps he firmly believes that Czechoslovakia, after ceding the Sudetenland, must enjoy lasting peace and order. So he takes the national policy he too shared the knowledge of—that we China, of necessity, to gain the final victory, made of Nanjing and Wuhan and many key points a painful exchange for the enemy's exhaustion, driving them to be greedy to lure us to peace, and to wish us to cease fire and rest with them; while our officers and men at the front, and the whole nation's countrymen, are seeking survival out of death, carrying out the consistent policy of long-term resistance, just at the moment when the turn is reached—and, unmindful, Wang has forgotten it, and holds on the contrary that the more of our cities the enemy occupies, and the more fiercely he butchers our countrymen, the faster we should surrender to the enemy, and the harsher the conditions we should accept. In talking such wild talk, are you doing the enemy's work, or haggling on the enemy's behalf against your own country? With this cowering fear of the fierce foe, no wonder that, dreading the enemy's coming to bomb Chongqing, you must fly to the safe zone of Hanoi abroad, and hasten to echo the enemy premier Konoe's 'terms of national ruin' for building a 'New Order in East Asia.' You take the heavy price we paid with the nation's life—the chance bought of the enemy's exhaustion—and would, on the contrary, offer it up to sue the enemy for peace, and let him breathe his fill; so that after the truce, when the enemy has rested half a year, and our men's hearts are scattered and our forces slack, he comes again to sweep our southwest—is it not ten times easier than today? After half a year thus, is not the whole of China turned into Czechoslovakia? Even had you organized a 'Central Government,' are you not become a Hácha, the ruined president of Czechoslovakia? This too every schoolboy understands, and Wang and his wife again pretend not to understand. If one would have men explain the reason, it is no more than that they envy Puyi, Yin Rugeng, Hácha, and the rest, who, though puppets in name, are yet second-class slaves! The people of the Three Eastern Provinces, men of spirit, would rather drift to this day north and south—knowing that to be the docile subject of a protectorate is harder still than to be the slave of a fallen country! So then, Wang and his wife would urge the merchant and industrial circles of Hong Kong, and the young students, all our countrymen, to surrender; but I believe our Hong Kong countrymen would rather sell matches in Hong Kong than be willing to follow Wang to Nanjing and be docile subjects. For he need only meet a countryman fled from the Three Eastern Provinces or the occupied regions, and hear a little of the small savor of 'bowing' and 'slaps in the face,' and he cannot but hope to seize the enemy's exhaustion, and, fighting to the last, seek his escape out of death. Third—in Wang's 'One Instance' there are three questions. The first two, answered by the explanation above, are already enough. His third question is put thus: 'When the ambassador was busy mediating, the men of the Supreme National Defense Council, whether at Nanjing or at Wuhan, were of one view. Why, when the Konoe declaration came, should the views differ again?' I never thought that a man of Wang's peerless cleverness should ask so foolish and stupid a question. Before I answer you straight, let me first ask you: how is it that the selfsame Wu Zhihui has all along wished to respect you and think highly of you? When, on the first, second, and third days of the fourth month of the sixteenth year, together with Mr. Li Shizeng and the rest, I begged you day and night to be prudent, and on the fourth you suddenly, with the then Communist leader Mr. Chen Duxiu, issued a joint declaration—then I railed at you without ceremony. Afterward you did, after all, correct your own error, and to this day you hold it a great mistake. Whether a great mistake or not—perhaps it is still your nerves' oversensitiveness. But as for the error of that time, your own awareness of it is truly not wrong, and so I respected you as before. By December of that year, you again fell into a strange notion, and I again, with Li Shizeng, Zhang Puquan, and the rest, urged you to be prudent. Mr. Zhang even knelt and begged you. Again you would not listen. When, on the twelfth, came the burning and slaughter at Canton, I could not but rail at you again, and you went abroad with tears streaming. Afterward you felt again: 'Though I did not kill Boren, Boren died because of me'; and then, for the sake of party and state, you came back east. So I respected you, down to this day. I never thought you were truly what the Communists dubbed you—a 'prostitute politician' and a 'wavering element'—and out came another Yan Telegram. Naturally, how could we ever approve this suing-for-surrender view of yours? Mr. Zhang even repented that he ought not, before the Central Party Headquarters at Nanjing, to have risked himself to fling his arms round the assassin, and save so worthless a man. Answering you with the history above—that men were formerly of one view with you, and now again are not of one view with you—is all quite natural. If you really go and be a puppet, I fear even your promising sons and daughters will refuse to own you, the old traitor, for a parent! So your confidential secretary, your most trusted intimate comrade, because of the view his conscience held, would go so far as to leak your secret. We truly grieve for Comrade Zeng Zhongming, for we have lost a truly faithful comrade who was for the public and not for the private. Let me answer you straight again. In a rogue's tone, by a hypnotist's art, you falsely charge men with having said: 'Having advocated war, one ought not again advocate peace.' Who says that advocating war forbids advocating peace? Who says that war and peace are not the state's great concern? But Wang Jingwei must know: what you call peace is a peace sought under the conditions of Konoe's 'building a New Order in East Asia' of the twenty-second of December last year, which you accept. Do you not know that 'the New Order in East Asia' is just the 'Japan–Manchukuo–China bloc'? Is it not just the new name for 'Japan's swallowing up of China'? Are not these conditions 'terms of national ruin'? Advocating peace thus—is it not surrender? Can it still be called negotiating a peace? Such a surrender—can it still be called the state's great concern? Such a peace-making—is it not selling the country? With a view so surrendering, so country-selling, can you still blame men for differing from you? Such reasons of right and wrong, gain and loss, of war and peace, survival and ruin, even a schoolboy can discern clearly. A man of your cleverness—can he truly not know? Yet there you are, still distinguishing peace from no peace, prating a fool's dream—if this is not from some other design, what is it? To come to the main text, you must understand: had you, meaning the state well, by proper procedure—such as the Supreme National Defense Council—secretly offered the question of war and peace, men would of course have discussed it with you. Finding it improper, they might also vote it down. As with the Nanjing peace-talk, which was not raised again, so it was.

"Had your Yan Telegram been laid by yourself before the several members at the Party Headquarters for discussion, they would at most have urged you not to dream a fool's dream, and voted it down, and there an end. But you went abroad openly, published it openly, for a malicious propaganda. You have already committed a capital crime, and moreover sought to enter the ranks of traitors. Men who are clean, loyal and loving to their own country—who would approve your treasonous scheme? You raise the question: 'Why should the views again differ?' Is it not the height of folly? The further reading above of mine may be called saying much in few words. But I would have Wang rein in his horse at the cliff's edge; and there are yet a few small questions on which I must give faithful counsel. (a) Some say that Wang's issuing this 'To Cite One Instance' this time has two aims. (1) It is that our friend, hearing that day Madame Wang's address to the 'comrades,' in which she said: 'To reach our aim, we must first bring it about that the question of war and peace can be openly discussed—at the least, that the question of peace may be freely discussed by all, on the same footing as the question of war. Now the time has come.' Therefore Wang fired the first great cannon, hoping to stir up approval, or refutation, and make a hopeless muddle, in the design of shaking men's hearts and wrecking the resistance, so that the enemy might cry 'ha, ha' with delight. But Wang and his wife, daring to commit so great a crime of selling the country, have not only lost their character utterly, but must rouse boundless popular wrath. I hold that, propagandize as you will, apart from your fellows of the Wang and Liang sort, what Chinese is left to believe these country-selling, enemy-surrendering propagandas of yours, and be shaken by you? You do but proclaim your own bankruptcy of character! As for playing the lute to the ox by way of refutation, I know all are unwilling to muck out a foul privy. I, counting myself among your distant friends, ought to make one last contribution. Mr. Wang, I hold that you have trusted the enemy and not trusted your friends. You take it for true that the enemy state will not own the National Government for its opposite party; and so you believe the enemy's word—that only you, the man Wang, need come to be their party in peace-making—to be true; and so, to this day, you still say on the surface that you only advise Japan to make peace with the National Government, and that you, in a private station, may help from the side. These polite words—let me ask, if you were not in league with the enemy, what standing have you to say you can advise the enemy state to make peace with your own country's government? I believe you are, to this day, still dreaming this dream, not yet waking from it, still taking it as true—and not as a deceit of theirs—that the enemy owns only you for its opposite party; and so you are still, at your ease and full of grace, very politely and very modestly, urging the enemy state to make the National Government its opposite party. But ask yourself of your own content: what drug, in the end, is that which you are selling in your gourd? Plainly, without ceremony, let me strip off your peep-show for you and say it! Your content is this: that, when the fit time comes, you will, without ceremony, come out and say: 'Now the state has been made a wreck by you pack of irresponsible, insincere, utterly unseeing menials; you should hurry and clear off, and let me do it!' On the one side you come out again with words that feign to grieve for Heaven and pity men: 'Since Japan will not have the National Government come out as its opposite party, and the National Government is too ashamed to come and sue Japan for peace, then at the last there is only I, Wang Jingwei, driven of necessity, to come out and save the country, and be Japan's opposite party.' Under Konoe's so-called 'New Order in East Asia,' the two Messrs. '-wei' together accomplish the great work of the Japan–Manchukuo–China 'bloc.' Is not this the plan of you and your wife? This my friend does not say idly, still less do I say it in jest. Your statement of the ninth, against what the Ta Kung Pao published on the fifth—the news that you are in league with the enemy and selling the country, playing the tiger's cat's-paw and drawing the wolf into the house—you still defend yourself against, and will not own. But the evidence I have got is fuller and surer than what the Ta Kung Pao published. If it comes to a lawsuit, I will go into court and bring the true proofs to prove it. By then you will surely have nothing to say! (2) It is to lay bare the record of the National Defense meeting, to have the Wang faction at Hong Kong and elsewhere spread a rumor, saying: 'The Wang family's Yan Telegram and the government are playing a duet'—that there is evidence for it. But now that the record is laid bare, it just makes men know that the Nanjing meeting and the Wang family's Yan Telegram have not the least causal link; one sees only that Wang himself is a creature undecided between war and peace, turning his back on the cold and chasing the warm. And to speak of a duet: Wang has cocked his little cap awry, smeared white powder on his face, and plays his hand-dance and foot-play on the stage front, while Konoe, behind his backside, loudly sings the 'New Order,' and Wang's hands and feet keep string and beat, no less than if it came from his own mouth. Then, of a sudden, Mr. Chiang takes Konoe's place behind him, and bitterly refutes the 'New Order'; and Wang, on the stage front, stands dumb as a wooden chicken, hands and feet all stiff and unmoving. This one may set the little brother watching the duet to judge: with whom did Wang play his duet? The little brother must cry out: I saw it in the playbill—it is called the 'Duet of the Two -weis,' a joint Sino-Japanese performance. This is a small matter; I advise the great man Wang not to mind it; to drag it, by committing the great crime of leaking secret documents, into a single droll remark, is even less worth it. (b) To leak secret documents in the charge of one's own office and station is crime enough; and to alter and forge public documents at will, to suit one's own meaning, is the less what one who would lead the multitude should do. For instance, as Wang cites in his essay, the ambassador saw Minister Wang again, 'stating: the German ambassador to Japan, at Tokyo, held talks with the two Japanese ministers of the Army and of Foreign Affairs, inquiring whether Japan wished to end the present situation; and asking under what conditions the Japanese government, wishing to end the present situation, could end it.' This the record has not; and you, for the enemy, cover up the point that the enemy wished the German government to come and make peace!—to spare the enemy state's resentment. And on the anti-Japanese question, 'the detailed method is a technical matter'—the record has this not either. And the talk of Tang Meng-xiao, Bai Jiansheng, Xu Ci-chen, Gu Mo-san, and the rest—the record has none of it. And the ambassador's saying: 'China, in resisting Japan until now, has already shown its spirit of resistance; and now the time to end it has come. In the European war there were several good chances to seek peace, but in the end, trusting its own strength, Germany would not make peace, until, at the signing of the Treaty of Versailles, men set what conditions they would, and it could not but accept them … As he saw it, Japan's conditions were not harsh'—of this the record again has not one word. This the more ought to have been declared on behalf of the German ambassador Trautmann, to spare the ambassador the censure of his government in Germany, and the loss of our Chinese diplomatic credit. Of such small tamperings there are many more; though all of no great moment, they are just such as could stir a peace-talk into being. Yet for a man of the central councils to be able to forge so freely—even were it truly a moment's carelessness, greed blinding the wits, to go and be a puppet—you who can so fail in fidelity to your office: would not even the little imps, seeing you, be afraid? Would they not want your life? You must take care; I think that, in due time, there is sure to be an end like Yin Rugeng's given you. Moreover, now that you and your wife would rally your 'comrades' and brew a small disorder, how will you keep your own subordinates in hand? By nothing but hawking a sham. Su Xiwen was plotted against by his own guards; I bow and hope Wang will take care. (c) Wang blows his own trumpet: 'What I seek with all sincerity is a plan for East Asia to last a hundred years. I have seen it clearly through, and concluded, that between China and Japan it is plain as day: war means the wounding of both, peace means the survival of both.' And again: 'Plain as day, it lies before us; can it be that the men of resolve in the two countries, awed by a momentary weal and woe, praise and blame, pace and hesitate, and dare not openly make their choice?' Wang, sure enough, with an air of the greatest solemnity—revolutionary youth, party chief, orator, poet, traitor—with one shake of the body turns into an old missionary. And your heroine among women, Madame Chen, has of late too sighed often over how the people suffer in war, forgetting that she helped you set the fires at Hankou and Beiping, for all the world a 'world-worn old granny' (that is, a compassionate old dame). A pity that you and your wife, in secret, are again there egging on the 'comrades' not to spare a small disorder, preparing to organize a 'Central Government,' in the design of stealing the political power, as your stepping-stone to advancement with the enemy. At this men cannot but see through your false masks, and know that you are shedding cat's tears over the mouse—which is truly laughable; better to counsel you to keep well the young man's head that 'bares the blade and laughs,' and be honest to the end. (d) But what Wang least believes in is honesty; his ambition would soar to the utmost height, so that, near enough, were there a God in the universe, he would still think to ride above God. In truth, however well he can learn Sun Wukong's arts of change, he can never change that quick tail; men who look at him see only a beast. And what, in the end, is his tail? It is a callow, sallow youth (one who does not age); it is an orator who knows not what logic is; it is a bookman culling chapters and plucking phrases; and it is a common fellow who loves a few stinking coins. That formerly I called him a hypocrite was because I was taken in by others; whatever party chief or traitor—these are all titles he takes for a passing lodging, in the end to be kicked aside by men, when, lifting his tail, he changes again: from the far left he changes to the far right, from the highest to the lowest, from the most fragrant to the most foul—it is all one to him. He thinks himself to have 'seen it clearly through,' and changes on the instant. He saw clearly through the left side of the revolution, and felt he must at the least be one of a trinity with Lenin and Trotsky, and that Stalin was by no means his match; he saw clearly through the plan for East Asia to last a hundred years, and thought that at the least Hitler, Mussolini, and the young military men must all take his direction. Men say Wang Jingwei long ago joined a certain clique, and has become a hard-cast traitor, by no means so simple a matter as Qin Hui or Yi Wan-yong. So for the Qin Hui and his wife on West Lake, cast in white iron; for this couple one must at the least prepare tungsten steel, to match their weight. What is to come I know not; wait and see what he changes into before we speak. What I sigh over today is this: since he knows that 'advocating peace will not harm unity, and not advocating peace will not bring a split,' can others not see it too? What men answer you is no more than that advocating war too will not bring a split; who is there borrowing the resistance for unity? What are you opposing? Your raising that word of opposition is but a set purpose to incite your 'comrades' to raise a small disorder for the enemy. To stir up a small disorder within the country now—can you stomach this shape-shifting change? Without the tungsten steel to wait for judgment and reward you a hundred years hence, what else would serve? 'China has gained unity through the resistance' is a new phrase that all say glibly with one voice. No one coined this phrase and then resisted. Ask of the content of this phrase, and it is just to say: apart from a very few brute-beast traitors, the whole people of China are patriots. I too cite one instance: the People's Political Council, over which you once presided, is just the representatives of every party and faction, united and gathered together through the resistance. All, thinking of how the former splits weakened the national strength, once united will not split again. So you too can believe that 'advocating peace will not harm unity, and not advocating peace will not bring a split.' Within that unity there comes too the Communist Party's submitting to the Three Principles of the People, upholding the National Government, and resisting in common. You hold that 'the Communist Party has trouble-making for its very nature.' You say again the Communist Party can 'come into the open.' But down to now, one sees only your 'open' suing for surrender, and has not yet seen the Communist Party's 'open' trouble-making; the Communist Party can come into the open, and yet you cannot come even to the surface. It is you who would propagandize your own private peace-talk, incite an internal disorder, plot to sell the country, furnish the enemy's interest, and sate a private greed. The ancients said: 'When a man cleanses himself to advance, approve his cleansing, not his retreating.' We know only that they are Chinese who submit to the Three Principles of the People, uphold the National Government, and come to resist in common—and that is all. England, which the Communist Party would first of all overthrow, may yet, through the democratic bloc, have England and Russia cooperate. And the men of the nation who advocate democracy and have submitted to the Three Principles of the People—should they, on the contrary, be discriminated against? How can that be? You, who all your life have preached democracy, have yet seen clearly through the fortunes of the anti-democratic bloc, and would jump ship on the instant. Your talk of Communist or not-Communist is but a wish to babble: 'China's favoring Communism should be for the aggressor states, who take anti-Communism as their signboard, to "chastise"!' You say: 'Should the Communists' trouble-making come into the open at a time of advocating peace, it can only be better than the present state of things, in which they manipulate and hold sway, provoke and estrange.' This is just your own plan proclaimed plainly: it is that you hope to gain the Communists' trouble-making—so you first play at manipulating and holding sway, provoking and estranging; and if the Communists fall into your trap and come to make trouble, your chance comes again. What is it? It is that you may then, in the name of seeking foreign aid, borrow Japanese soldiers to put down the internal disorder! Is this design of yours not that of Wu Sangui of the Ming, of Yi Wan-yong of Korea? You act so against nature and reason, to this degree—have you a shred of conscience left? You sat in the Central Party Headquarters more than a day; who did not, with you, urge with all earnestness our Communist friends to come naturally into the open, and build together a state of the Three Principles of the People? Why do you now openly make special mention of it to the enemy—what is in your heart? You who advocate a country-selling peace—have you still the power to make them come into the open? When all of us, with them, love not the country in common, there is no way to make them come into the open.

"Well then, please, you who do not take trouble-making for your nature, lay your hand on your conscience, see a little less clearly through, and honestly be the young man whose head 'bares the blade and laughs.' If I have offended you, I am willing hereafter to take your punishment. Last of all, I too return you a 'To Cite One Instance.' You will remember that, in the twenty-third or twenty-fourth year of the Republic, when you were president of the Executive Yuan and concurrently minister of Foreign Affairs, did not the Japanese warlords demand the removal of Song Zheyuan, the Chahar chairman? On receiving the Japanese warlords' demanded conditions, you asked not which one it was, nor how, still less what fault Chairman Song Zheyuan had, that he should be removed; you took your spirit of sincere and responsible diplomacy, your surpassing insight, your unfathomably clever means, and hastened that very night to give the order, and forthwith removed Chairman Song; you bowed your head and dropped your ears to the Japanese warlords, and reverently, punctiliously, carried it out. In less than twenty-four hours, you handed the whole sovereignty of our Chahar over to the Japanese warlords. And the Japanese warlords, without spending a single bullet or shedding a drop of blood, peacefully took Chahar. Look—what difference is there between this and Hácha's making a present of Czechoslovakia last month? Reckoning the time, your clever and swift diplomatic means, in making a present of Chahar, were even quicker than the making-present of Czechoslovakia. Of other such instances there is no knowing how many; I cite but one instance, and it is enough to show your lofty vision, keen insight, and clever, wise diplomatic genius—your being able, responsibly, to lose sovereignty to the enemy, and, very sincerely, to disgrace the country. Mr. Wang, so responsible, so sincere toward the Japanese warlords—how should the Japanese warlords be willing to let go of you! Among the four hundred and fifty million of China, save for you, no second man is to be found. So, having found Wang Kemin and Liang Hongzhi, and the two of them not being enough, they must say to you that only you can be their opposite party in peace-making. This is no wonder in them. For they are not of set purpose come to seduce you, or of set purpose come to harm you; it is but that it profits themselves, and makes it easy for them to destroy China! And you, in truth, are also not of set purpose come to harm Japan. But their seeing you make a present of Chahar in such a way, and so swiftly, could not but make the Japanese warlords' ambition to destroy China wax the higher, and their spirit of insulting China rise especially high. And so, getting an inch and pressing for a foot, they made the Japanese warlords lose all sense, forget themselves, and even leave their own traditional policy and highest military strategy out of account! Right down to the outbreak of war on the seventh of July at the Marco Polo Bridge, they still thought the Chinese would take only the hard and not the soft. So they sent great armies more into the pass, burned and slew Tianjin against all law and Heaven, occupied Beiping; and still fondly dreamed that our National Government, like you in your day as president of the Executive Yuan in the twenty-third and twenty-fourth years, need only be overawed and cajoled by force of arms, and would sign and submit, and North China would be got with folded hands. Not only that; they must spend their pains besides, sending troops to Shanghai, attacking Nanjing, right down to occupying Canton and Wuhan. Mr. Wang, do you know what this is? I fear you still do not understand! This is just their waiting for you to come out and be their opposite party on behalf of the National Government! But when they reached Wuhan, you still would not come out to be their opposite party. Do you really mean to have them fight all the way to Xi'an and Nanning before you will come out to topple the National Government and be its opposite party? If they reach Xi'an and Nanning, having lost many soldiers and spent much military funds, till their whole year's war budget of six hundred million is spent clean; and if by then they cannot take Xi'an and Nanning—or, having taken them, you still do not come out to topple the National Government and be its opposite party in dealing with them—then you have truly harmed them, truly done them wrong. By then, your good friends the Japanese warlords will surely question you: 'The words in your "To Cite One Instance," of "laying the foundation of a lasting order of a hundred years in East Asia" and of "the other road of common survival and common development"—are they not a cheat?' When you, at that time, fear you cannot answer, I will think for you: you need only say: 'I am a Chinese; in our fighting, no deceit is too much for war. My being thus now means you are beaten. Yet this is just what deserves to be called "a lasting order of a hundred years in East Asia," "the other road of common survival and common development"!' Answering them thus, are you not become a great statesman famous in history? If it truly comes to such an end, then on our future history of the Sino-Japanese war, on the day the Republic of China gains its independence, freedom, and the wholeness of its territory, sovereignty, and administration—would not your first merit be set down on the first page? What then have you to fear of a wrong that cannot be washed clean? I think that, if you would serve the country and win a name, and leave a fragrance for a hundred generations, this is the only way; apart from this, every road is a road of death. Take care, take care, and above all do not take the wrong turning! Should it come to the time of 'a single false step, the regret of a thousand ages,' then repentance too will be past reach. Farewell!"

Thereafter, Wang's fawning-on-Japan activities grew more unbridled by the day, while the Juntong's work against Wang was never for a moment relaxed. (End of Chapter Six.)

The single blow at Hanoi having failed, the writer was soon ordered back, flying to Chongqing by way of Hong Kong. And our comrades left at Hanoi still strove on without cease, to complete the unfinished work. But the man Wang was now like a bird startled by the bow, guarding himself most strictly; and to accomplish the aim of sanctioning him would be harder still.

The man Wang did not, because of this one blow, draw in his horns even a little, but grew more unbridled, writing with his own hand to Long Yun, the chairman of Yunnan Province, inciting Long Yun to break from the ranks of the resistance, and planning to slip away to Kunming to take shelter with Long Yun, and there set up a rival court against the Central authorities. At that time, as the situation of the resistance evolved, how weighty was Yunnan's position; had there been the least stirring of wind and grass, it would have been past bearing. Happily this letter did not fall into Long Yun's hands, and so a hidden trouble of the greatest gravity was, unseen, dissolved.

The man Wang, of course, did not give up his hope for all this; and before long, with the help of Japanese military and government officials, he was at last escorted from Hanoi to Shanghai; and a puppet regime that sold the nation's interests was there being brewed up, ever more busily, and made ready to play its part.

To go on: after the writer was recalled to Chongqing, I met with a coldness such as I had never known since I took up the work; and in my own feeling, I held this to be a severe "punishment of the spirit" dealt me by my superiors. After a month, I was appointed acting chief of the Third Section of the Bureau headquarters, and, having achieved nothing there either, was transferred to the Central Training Corps' Party-and-Government Training Class for a course. On the evening of the graduation, Mr. Dai honored me with a banquet; and when the wine and the food were done, I was led alone into a side room, where, in a spirit of letting bygones be bygones, he gave me a new task. This time it was to send me to Shanghai—first to deal urgently with the problems that had arisen in our own organization, and then to carry on the work of sanctioning Wang Jingwei. Though this errand could be covered under the foreign concessions, that was, after all, a Japanese-occupied area; a road full of hardships was to be foreseen.

In the middle of the seventh month of that year, before I had reached Shanghai, the work units of the Shanghai area were suddenly wrecked; and amid alarms on every hand and crisis heaped on crisis, still under the threat of the enemy-and-puppet forces, all the work and activity was already paralyzed; while Brother Wang Luqiao, who had been transferred to Shanghai before me, was by this time already under arrest, and the whole working environment was extraordinarily perilous.

One may say that, from the very moment I set one foot on the "Shanghai Bund," I had already entered a state of tension. I warned myself: hereafter, I must deal warily indeed with them for a while.
