## The Bank

On the morning of the first day of the New Year, the doors of the Zhonghui Trust Bank on Tianjin Road stood open. Under the government's rules promoting the new calendar, the banks worked as usual on the first day of the abolished-calendar New Year; but customers were hardly likely to come at such an hour, everyone being busy paying New Year calls all over town.

A little before ten in the morning a rickshaw stopped at the bank's door, and Chen Qianli got down. The first fare of the New Year: he handed the puller a five-jiao coin, then turned and lifted a small suitcase off the seat. Today he wore a gray fur-lined robe of figured satin with a marten-paw lining, over it a black tussore-silk riding jacket, and on his head a little sable cap.

This get-up he had rented from a secondhand-clothes shop. The shopkeeper thought nothing of it: of late a fair number of people had come to him wanting an outfit to play the part of a great rich house.

For sheer presence this customer scarcely needed the outfit at all. And he truly knew his goods: he had turned over this one and eyed that one, taken to none of them, and pressed the man into bringing out two genuinely costly articles, and of those two had chosen this, the one whose lining was somewhat the more worn — and that was the right choice.

Marten-paw fur is no common sable; it is the little strip of skin below the claws of the marten's paw, light, soft and warm, and to make one such robe might cost the lives of some hundreds of martens. Never mind the martens: to sew those small scraps of skin into a robe with never a stitch-mark to be seen — you could not find a craftsman nowadays to take on the work.

Chen Qianli stood at the roadside, set down the suitcase, and fished a packet of cigarettes from inside his robe. He did not usually smoke, but now he drew two puffs, then threw the half-smoked cigarette away and turned to mount the steps.

Not ten minutes after Chen Qianli had gone into the bank, Ye Qinian came out of the Cathay Hotel. He had Secretary Ma drive the car on ahead; on the first day of the New Year he meant to walk a little. Straight along Renji Road, in ten minutes or so one could reach Tianjin Road. All the way he was thinking of Chen Qianli — could he manage to see him today, he wondered? That was his mortal, undying enemy. He would not, of course, finish Chen Qianli off with a single shot; he would torment him slowly, and ask him why, in those years, he had turned so faithless and treacherous. He even thought, not without a sneer, that if Chen Qianli were like Cui Wentai and came running up to him with an “I want to see your big boss,” would he really lead the man off to see Mr. Lifu? He hoped Chen Qianli would go on being just that stubborn and unyielding, so that he might have the chance to take his revenge to the full, and gladly.

The sun was fine, and for an instant an emptiness rose all at once in his heart. The Special Operations Headquarters, the Party and the state, the Communists' secret plan — these words haunted his mind day and night, yet in a single moment they all lost their meaning, and even his teeth-gritting hatred came to seem very far off. If in those years Chen Qianli had not been so headstrong, his head turned by some ABC of Communism, some Letters from Afar, then, given time, perhaps he too would not have stood so unyielding over the matter of Ye Tao. Had it been so, then today, the first day of the New Year, with the family gathered, the two of them might have shared a cup. At the thought he all but let his tears fall.

Tianjin Road was the street of the banks; roundabout it was all banks and native banks, and even on New Year's Day motorcars and rickshaws lined half the road. Secretary Ma had long since parked the car in Fucheng Li and was not in it himself. Ye Qinian walked on a few steps, saw the Yuji native bank, and turned in. The Special Operations Headquarters had put up half the Yuji's capital, and its proprietor was one of their own. Headquarters had planted men in every trade and calling, and by now had long since formed a secret network of communication, so that whatever happened, in any corner of the Party and the state, they would know of it a step ahead of anyone.

At midnight the night before, “Xi Shi” had telephoned. The moment he had the intelligence, he had settled his scheme lying in his bed at the Cathay. Now the Yuji became a provisional operations command post — Fucheng Li stood slantwise across from the Zhonghui Trust Bank, and the window of the native bank's rear upper floor was the very best observation point. Secretary Ma and You Tianxiao had both already arrived.

You Tianxiao's men this time were all crack hands; in under ten minutes they had the operation set, he explained to Ye Qinian.

“A little slower does no harm; but if word leaks from your quarter, I'll have no choice but to hand you over to the internal investigation room for family discipline.”

“It absolutely will not, Teacher.”

The native bank was full of the detective squad's subordinates, men who did not know that the unit truly directing this operation was the Special Operations Headquarters. In such a case he had to avoid addressing the other man as Director Ye.

“What's the situation in the bank?”

“The men are all deployed to their posts. There's no movement in the bank for now.”

“No one is to be seized without my order.”

“Yes, Teacher.”

Ye Qinian went up to the second floor. The proprietor of the Yuji native bank, knowing that Director Ye of Headquarters was coming to direct things on the spot, had set out a low table and tea things upstairs early. Ye Qinian sat down by the window and looked toward the Zhonghui Trust Bank building.

The bank building stood at the crossroads, five stories in all. Below ground was one more floor, where the vault lay; the four walls of the strongroom were cast of steel plate, and the plate of the vault door was fully forty centimeters thick.

When the building went up, the corner had been deliberately designed as a slanting face, and the bank's main door too opened this way — no doubt, Ye Qinian thought, they had consulted some master of geomancy at the time.

You Tianxiao came upstairs to tell Ye Qinian: “They've come.”

“How many?”

“One car, three people. There's Lin Shi — that's ‘Old Kai’ — and Ling Wen, and our ‘Xi Shi’ is in the car too.”

Chen Qianli had not come after all, and a faint disappointment stirred in Ye Qinian's heart.

The night before, New Year's Eve, Cui Wentai had not slept a wink all night, for all the world as if he were sitting up to see the old year out. Chen Qianli had said that the comrades taking part in the bank operation the next day were not to go home that night but would all sleep in the clinic's sickrooms. And so he had no way to see Director Ye face to face. No one had foreseen this — not he, not Captain You, not Director Ye.

Outside the clinic, true enough, there were two furtive-looking fellows, most likely plainclothesmen of the detective squad; but he could hardly just hand important intelligence over to them, could he? Even if they really were Captain You's men, Captain You had given them no word about all this, and they'd not lightly believe him, Cui Wentai, would they! And even if they did believe he was one of their own, if by some slip they took a couple of drinks afterward, they might well forget the whole thing.

Besides, even if they did carry it off, whose credit would it be? He, Cui Wentai, was not doing this for the Party and the state. And to take it a step further: even supposing he cared nothing for reward and was willing to pass the intelligence to them, he would still have to manage it just now — and how? The clinic was full of people, all suspecting an agent within; for him to slip out in the dead of night and slip back, and be seen at it, would land him in no end of trouble.

He held out till the middle of the night; and seeing the others all sound asleep, he made up his mind at last, crept to the consulting room, and put through a call to the Cathay Hotel.

He had on only the single cotton jacket he slept in; the woolen overcoat the driver wore hung on the hook behind the door, and he dared not put it on, fearing to make too much of a stir. Yet even so, cradling the telephone, he was trembling and breaking into a cold sweat, and in a moment his back was a patch of wet.

He dared not wait for the Cathay's switchboard girl to wake Director Ye, but only had her pass on two lines. First: “Old Kai” is Lin Shi. Second: tomorrow at half past ten they go to the bank to open the safe-deposit box.

Coming out from the call, he had another fright. The clinic had been a lane house; the wing rooms of the rear upper floor served as sickrooms, and from the consulting room to the sickrooms ran a short passage, a stairway on one side of it and the sickrooms on the other. No light was on in the passage; just as he was tiptoeing, meaning to slip into his room, he ran head-on into Yi Junnian, who was smoking at the head of the stairs.

Yi Junnian said not a word, but watched him coldly, and did not even ask him to explain why he was up in the dead of night and had gone outside. Of course, he could always say he had gone to the lavatory.

It was only in the morning that he found several of them had left. At the table there were only Lin Shi, Ling Wen, Doctor Qin and himself — even Liang Shichao, who lodged at the clinic, was nowhere to be seen.

Back in his room, Cui Wentai grew more and more uneasy the more he thought. If by any chance Director Ye had not got the message and had let Lin Shi and the gold slip away, that was a responsibility he could never bear. He had sold himself to them, and sold himself by halves at that — a bad bargain all round. Let Director Ye once lose his temper, and he might call him a real Communist and a sham defector, and make an end of him.

He went to the consulting room and told Qin Chuan'an he wanted to telephone the car firm: he had not driven the car back to the firm the night before, and if he did not report in this morning the firm's manager would be fuming and cursing.

Qin Chuan'an was busy writing out prescriptions. He knew he might well have to withdraw at any moment, and there were many patients who had long been under his treatment and got their medicines from him, for whom he wanted to prepare prescriptions in advance. For several of them he even meant to recommend doctors at other clinics. He had all these patients' addresses; he had only to put the finished prescriptions into envelopes, and in a sudden withdrawal he could stuff the sheaf of letters into a postbox and be done. He pointed his pen toward the telephone and went on writing, head bent.

The call went through; Cui Wentai gave the room number, then looked up at Qin Chuan'an and saw him wholly absorbed in his work. The room's bell rang twice, and someone lifted the receiver: it was Ye Qinian. Cui Wentai had thought he could make up a few coded phrases to get his meaning across to Director Ye, but on the spot the words failed him, and he held the telephone stopped at his lips.

Cui Wentai stole a glance from the corner of his eye at Qin Chuan'an and saw him still bent over the desk, seeming not to be minding him at all.

“Boss, a customer's booked the car for this morning, so I've gone straight out on business. I'll come back once this job's done.”

After a good while the other end said: “Understood.”

Cui Wentai hung there irresolute, holding the telephone in a daze; the other end waited a moment, then, a little impatient: “Understood — you telephoned last night.”

The other end hung up.

Setting down the telephone, Cui Wentai felt no easier in his heart; on the contrary he grew more and more distraught.

Taking his chance while Cui Wentai was gone, Lin Shi said to Ling Wen: “I met Comrade Long Dong, in Guangzhou.”

Half an hour still remained before they set out — usually the most unsettled hour of all, the more so as Chen Qianli had said that this time they would carry out the task under the enemy's close watch. She was struggling to master herself, and had meant to rise and clear away the bowls, when Lin Shi came out with this, and for a moment she could not take it in.

From the lane came a burst of firecrackers; on New Year's morning the Shanghai households open their doors and set off a string of “door-opening” crackers.

“I heard from Old Yi that your husband, Comrade Long Dong, gave his life at Guangzhou — when did that happen?”

“After the Guangzhou Uprising he was arrested at the Soviet consulate and killed by the enemy.”

Lin Shi nodded: “If that's how it was, he is very likely still alive.”

Having said the words, Lin Shi felt a strange sensation rise in his own heart. The two of them — one Long Dong's wife, the other a man who had once counted Long Dong a friend to the death — now sat at the table discussing Long Dong's life and death in a tone all but flat. He is very likely still alive: as though whether a man had given his life or was living were both facts one could accept, and the only thing worth discussing was which of the two was the likelier. But after the failure of the Great Revolution, such cases were only too common.

Once the underground Party was broken up by the enemy, the single-line links of the organization were severed; when an upper contact was arrested and killed, the lower contact who reported to him vanished at the same stroke, with no document to prove who they were or where they had gone. When a local organization was broken up, some comrades gave their lives, some vanished, and those who remained, if they still had any means of reaching the Party, were transferred elsewhere to go on working — but they too must often change their names and identities.

In the most tangled cases, it often happened that a comrade thought to have given his life was found in the end to be alive, while a comrade supposed to be living had in truth long since died.

There were forever tales of every kind: some sprang from comrades' feeling for one another, always insisting the fallen were alive; some came of the enemy's stupidity and scheming, who, to claim credit or to lay a trap, would put about news that could be neither proved true nor false.

“In the second year after the Guangzhou Uprising failed, about the fifth month, the organization transferred me to Guangzhou to work with Comrade Long Dong. In that year in Guangzhou, Long Dong and I talked a great deal, and he showed me a photograph of the two of you. Your knitted cap came down over your ears, you had a big shawl across your shoulders, you wore a pleated skirt, your hands tucked in its pockets. He was alive then, alive and well —” Even to Lin Shi's own ear it still sounded strange to speak so flatly.

“In Guangzhou, but for him, I might have been caught by the enemy a dozen times over. I truly could never learn to talk like the local people. It was too dangerous, and in the end it was for that reason the higher-ups had to transfer me out.”

Lin Shi remembered a day in the eighth month when Long Dong's spirits seemed especially low; the two of them sat under the arcade eating congee, and amid thunder and lightning a downpour soon came on. Long Dong told him that the enemy had burst into the secret station before there was time to warn Ling Wen.

Ling Wen's response came very slowly; it was only when Lin Shi spoke of that photograph that she grew, little by little, stirred.

Lin Shi became aware of the change in Ling Wen's mood; she was all but on the point of tears. He had only meant to say a few things unconnected with the operation, to put her at her ease.

But the hour to set out had come. Ling Wen rose and asked Lin Shi: “Your wound — you'll manage the walking all right?”

“Comrade Lin Shi, you must surely be a big leader?” Cui Wentai braked, sounded the horn, gave it the gas, squeezed out of the tangle of rickshaws heaped up at the crossing, and cocked his head toward the back seat. “Five big yellow croakers — my word, no small matter. It's got to be a big mission. In half a lifetime I've never laid eyes on that much money.”

No one in the back seat answered him. Ling Wen sat with her head bowed over her cares; Lin Shi lifted a corner of the window curtain and watched the street outside.

“Still, this car of mine has carried gold bars before, though I never saw them myself.”

Cui Wentai laughed a few times. “Last spring I went to Seward Road to pick up a fare — Ningbo people. One old master and two servants. Struck me as very queer: the servants were empty-handed, and there was a bundle done up in a tablecloth that the master himself hugged to his chest, and he said he was going to the police station.

“Once they were in the car I saw something was off, and I asked a casual question, and sure enough — they were going to report a case. So I asked, report what case? They said the servant, cooking the midday meal, had found seven gold bars under the rice crock. Sitting at home, gold bars dropping from heaven into your rice crock — isn't that a fine thing? Why report it? I asked. The servant said the young master had been missing for several days. Oho, I thought, now this gets interesting.

“Do you know why? At that time all Shanghai was buzzing with the Wang Jinzhi murder case. Have you heard of it? Wang Jinzhi, head steward of a Taikoo steamer, had worked the Yangtze boats for thirty years, a man of the utmost good faith, so that the native banks and gold shops entrusted him to carry gold bars from Shanghai to Wuhan; in all those decades he never once slipped, you could give him any number of extra bars and it was no matter, Wuhan was sure to receive them. But this time something went wrong — he was killed, and when he died there were only a few silver coins on him, and of the gold bars not a single one was left.”

“The stolen gold bars were the ones in the rice crock?” Lin Shi asked.

Cui Wentai nodded. “That's what they reckoned, which was why they were going to report it at the police station. And I reckoned the same — it had to be that lot. So I asked, why did they think the gold in the rice crock had anything to do with the Wang Jinzhi case? They said, because their young master had gone missing.

“So that was it — then I understood; then there truly was a connection. The young master of that house was surely no good sort; outsiders wouldn't know, but the family would. Whether your own son is a good sort — the master of the house knows that.

“Those gold bars in the rice crock had to be tied to the Wang Jinzhi case. The master of that house was a timid man, and once he'd guessed as much, off he must go at once to report it at the police station. They were that sure, and thinking it over I was that sure too, and the police station, well, they were that sure straightaway as well.”

“A few days on, I looked at the newspaper, and there it was, printed sure enough. Guess how it turned out?” Cui Wentai spun out the suspense, waiting till the car had passed the crossing and turned onto Tianjin Road before going on. “Guess how it turned out? The paper said that the moment the police station had the gold bars, they fetched the men from the native banks and gold shops to look — the bars they cast themselves were all marked, and one look told them it was the very lot that had been stolen. The police station said at once: the number of bars is short, half of them are missing. Since half the loot's in your rice crock, you're answerable for handing over the other half too. You say you knew nothing of it — maybe it was your son; then hand your son over. And if you can produce neither the gold nor the son, then into the police station we put you first. That master of the house cried what an injustice — he'd only meant to do a good deed, and had gone and landed himself in court before anyone else.”

The car stopped at the door of the Zhonghui Trust Bank. As they went in through the bank's main door, Ling Wen said: “This Cui Wentai — how is it he's so full of talk today?”
