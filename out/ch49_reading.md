## Chapter 49

The clerk at the firm said Honsa Jiro had not come in that afternoon, but had telephoned to say that anyone with business should call him. I knew he was at home, so of course there was no need to telephone; I went straight to his house.

As I stepped into his doorway I heard voices in the drawing room. I had the servant announce me, and Honsa came out to greet me and ushered me in, saying that everyone inside was a friend.

True enough, everyone inside was a familiar face — that is, they were the very strangers I had met the day before. What startled me most was that Miyama Yoshiko was among them, while Sophie, whom I knew best, was not; which meant there was not a single Chinese in the room, and I was the great exception.

After greeting everyone I took a seat diagonally across from Miyama Yoshiko. The night before I had been careless in the attention I paid her; today, naturally, I fixed my attention on her with particular care.

My first impression had been of a face childlike and lively; afterward I had noticed the softness of her chin and the compassion at the corners of her mouth; now I felt that neither observation had been wrong, only that her unbroken calm and gravity reconciled the two different kinds of beauty in that face. That is to say: a face like hers, with too much smiling and play of feature, would inevitably have seemed frivolous; while a face that was not like hers would have found its calm and gravity turning wooden. I now felt that the age I had guessed for her was quite right. Judging by the face I might have put her a few years younger, but judging by that calm and gravity a few years older, and the figure I now settled on was precisely the reconciling of the two: I made her twenty-two. Today she wore a kimono again, which I thought made her look younger than in an evening gown.

In the midst of our idle talk my eyes met hers. She looked away. I found that the distinctive thing in her face was, after all, the eyes: they were narrow and long, seeming almost too small, but her lashes were thick and tilted slightly upward, so that all the mystery she possessed lay, I was sure, in there — and this lent her face, out of nowhere, an added measure of nobility. The ripple I had seen cross her face at the dinner table the night before did not show at all today, while the compassion I had noticed at the corners of her mouth seemed to surface whenever she assented to some remark.

Honsa seemed to feel I was paying Miyama Yoshiko too much attention. He said:

"What are you thinking about?"

"I was thinking," I said, and, looking at Miyama Yoshiko again, asked: "Miss Miyama, it has just come to me that yesterday, behind our masks, we danced together more than a few times."

"Do you think so?" Miyama tilted her eyes up a fraction and countered. Her talk was often as clipped as this, giving me no way to draw any closer to her. So I looked at Honsa and said, boldly:

"From Miss Miyama's chin I can imagine the charm she had behind her mask."

"And what has the one to do with the other?" said Honsa, laughing.

"I was only thinking how ill-suited Miss Miyama's face is to photography, and yet what a perfect type it is for a painter to seize on for its character."

Honsa laughed. Everyone was attending to what I said, and the Japanese, who did not follow much Mandarin, looked to Honsa to interpret; Honsa rendered it for them.

Miyama Yoshiko looked at me and suddenly betrayed a fresh ripple across her face — the first all day — and again, tilting her eyes up with that noble little movement, said:

"You put too much faith in your own opinions."

After that our talk broke off. The guests exchanged remarks in Japanese, and I hated myself bitterly for not knowing the language, for having no way to command the drift of the conversation. Then a plan came to me all at once, and I said quietly to Honsa:

"Might I have a word with you?"

So Honsa took me into another room. I sat down and said:

"Do you find me strange today?"

"How so?"

"I hope you'll forgive me."

"Forgive you for what?"

"For the attention I've been paying Miyama Yoshiko."

"And that wants my forgiveness?"

"The fact is — I won't hide it from you — I came to call on you today entirely on her account."

"What? Have you lost your heart to her?"

"Perhaps. In any case I want to know her a little better, to get a little closer to her."

"You mean to say…"

"Need I spell it out? I'm sorry now that I dined here yesterday. You know how hard it is for me to take an interest in a woman."

"But surely that's your own affair. She's here at my house today — a fine chance for you."

"But I've no wish to court a married woman, or a girl who already has a sweetheart."

"I can vouch there's nothing of the sort. She's been here from Tokyo only a few months."

"Not come to join a lover?"

"She's simply here to see the sights, that's all. Her uncle is a chief of the information bureau."

"And she lives at her uncle's?"

"Yes."

"Whereabouts?"

"Yuyuan Road."

"Then I'll ask a favor of you," I said. "Will you let me see her home today?"

Honsa hesitated a moment, then said:

"I can only drop a hint from the side; the rest of the effort is up to you, and whether she's willing is up to her."

"Of course," I said. "Thank you."

"This will cost you a proper dinner," said Honsa, laughing.

Then we went back to the drawing room. At half past five someone rose to leave, and Miyama Yoshiko stood up too. Honsa was in the corridor fetching people's coats; I was ahead of Miyama, and Honsa quite naturally handed me hers — a black woolen cloak with a fox-fur collar. I took it and helped Miyama into it, saying in a low voice:

"Might I have the honor of seeing you home, Miss Miyama?"

But Miyama's reply came out loudly — on purpose, I was sure, so that Honsa would hear:

"Would it be any trouble, sir?"

Honsa, who was just then by the coat rack, said:

"Splendid. If your car's free, may I trouble you to run Miss Miyama home."

"It will be an honor to be charged with it," I said.

Miss Miyama raised no objection and said not a second word; she took her leave of the others and, head lowered, walked out behind the guests who had already gone ahead. Coat over my arm, I made my hurried farewells and followed behind her, with Honsa behind me, seeing us out.

I opened the car door for Miyama and she got in. I shut it, went round and took the seat to her right, tossed the coat onto the back seat, and started the car.

I drove very slowly, casting about for something to say to Miyama, but nothing came, until we had turned out onto a main road, and I said:

"Straight to your house?"

"Thank you," she said. "Oh — you know my home is on Yuyuan Road?"

"If we follow Eastern custom," I said, "would it be presumptuous of me to ask you to dinner just now?"

"I never dine so early," she said, "and besides, we had tea and cakes at Mr. Honsa's today."

"Then might I take you somewhere to sit and talk a while, and see you home only after dinner?"

"Is this your Chinese courtesy?"

"I should say it is merely a personal request of mine to you."

"In that case, I'm sorry," she said. "By my own habit, every engagement must first have the consent of the head of my household."

"Forgive me," I said. "In our China, when a lady of quality deals with a man's invitation she gives only a yes or a no; for if she uses some evasion, a foolish man will often misread it — as, for instance, I might now, when I say I hope you'll be so good as to telephone home."

"Then let me tell you this: if it will prove that I am not refusing your kindness, I can accept your engagement tomorrow afternoon."

"Thank you," I said. "Then I'll call for you tomorrow afternoon at four o'clock."

"How would five o'clock be?"

"It's the same honor to me either way," I said.

So I drove all the way to Yuyuan Road, and at the corner of Edinburgh Road she told me to stop. As she got out, she said:

"Number 1470A, flat two. I'll expect you at five tomorrow."

I watched her disappear into the lane beside a flower shop. Then I drove home. I was well satisfied with the day's gains; I thought I would have an hour or two of sleep before dinner, and after dinner go round to Bai Ping's.

On the way back I still could not put my finger on what was strange in the impression Miyama Yoshiko had left me. Her talk in the car today had again been in less than pure Mandarin, everywhere slowed or elided to cover the clumsiness of her Chinese. If she had Asamura Toshimiko's command of Mandarin, then such a performance would indeed be a marvel: to feign a pure "cannot" out of a pure "can" is easy enough, but to feign a half-can, half-cannot truly astonished me. Beyond that, I did not feel she had any special magic. I seemed to have this adversary well in hand, and took comfort in my own confidence. Back at my lodgings, I had a very good hour and a half's sleep.

At nine o'clock I was at Bai Ping's. Neither Mei Yingzi nor Bai Ping had come; Ah Mei was outside, and I sat alone, while all manner of strange, restless thoughts rose in me. They were thoroughly tangled: I thought of the plan to go to Beiping, I thought of Helen, I thought of this whole war; from myself I passed to the whole world, and from the whole world to every corner of it, and from every corner of the world to our own particular corner, and so to our work, to Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi, to Miyama Yoshiko. The speed of a man's thought must be the swiftest motion in the world; light and electricity seem sluggish beside it. In a spell of sleeplessness, or of sitting still, every man has known his thoughts galloping free, drawing boundless space and bottomless time into a single point — the freest happiness, and a torment too. And so I sat, savoring that happiness and that torment.

Suddenly I thought of the previous night's conference, and it struck me as strange that I had never reported the details of what I had met with while stealing the documents, and that they had never asked me. What, after all, had Miyama Yoshiko meant by swapping a bomb for the documents? And what did she want with the documents she had taken?

If she was, beyond doubt, an agent of the enemy, then in planting the bomb she must have been working for us. That is: she must have heard beforehand that someone meant to steal the documents, and had set a trap for the thief. And since I had caught her at her work, it meant either that her bomb had come to nothing, or that the report of someone stealing the documents had proved false — so that although our operation the night before had failed, on her side too the outcome fell short of her reckoning, and victory and defeat were not, after all, a thing one could weigh. Next: whether what she had planted was even a bomb remained a question; and whether she belonged to the Japanese side was a question too — for if she were of the Japanese side, why go planting a bomb by stealth? In short, Miyama Yoshiko's identity, her work, and her aims were all in question, and not one of my suppositions had been borne out. I even entertained the absurd notion: might she be an agent of the British? And might our present suspicion of her be, like Bai Ping's suspicion of Mei Yingzi at the start, and Mei Yingzi's of Bai Ping, a fearful and grievous misunderstanding?

In any case, since Miyama Yoshiko was all questions, everything I had thought of must have occurred to Bai Ping and Mei Yingzi as well; yet at the conference the night before none of it had been raised for discussion at all, which was truly a strange thing.

Was it, then, that what I had thought of was the mischief of my old schooling — this craving after logical satisfaction of every kind — which in espionage one ought never to demand? Or else that the credit I had earned by the night's work had made them jealous, so that they would not bring it up for fear of feeding my conceit?

So I resolved that today I would have them give me an answer to these questions, some logical satisfaction; but just then my thoughts slid off again onto the difference in meaning between theory and fact. I thought of my own research, of Helen's music, of art and culture —

In the midst of my scattered thoughts I heard someone come back outside. It was Mei Yingzi. She was plainly dressed, with hardly any powder on her face, and greeted me with a weary smile. Shedding her coat as she came in, she handed it to Ah Mei and dropped onto the sofa, her handbag still in her hand, resting wearily on her knee. I began to question her:

"Did you come away with anything?"

She nodded, and for a good while said nothing. Growing impatient, I blurted out the questions I had been meaning to put, saying:

"Why on earth did Miyama Yoshiko take the documents out? Why did she set the bomb? I don't understand. And whether what she set was even a bomb is itself a question."

Mei Yingzi looked at me wearily and made no answer. So I went on:

"I'm still uneasy about who Miyama Yoshiko is. Why would she steal the documents? If she's an enemy agent meaning to kill whoever stole them, then she must have got wind that someone was going to — and who was it she suspected? Could it be the guests at the ball that day? Could it be us?"

Mei Yingzi said nothing, only went on looking at me wearily. Out of all patience, I said:

"As long as these questions go unresolved, my work has no direction, no purpose, no meaning — it's all wasted effort."

"But," said Mei Yingzi in a low voice, "our work now is precisely to seek out the direction, the purpose, and the meaning of the work."

"I don't understand!" I said.

"All these questions of yours," said Mei Yingzi with a weary smile, "haven't they arisen because your own work came away with nothing?"

"Yes, I came away with nothing — but I've met Miyama Yoshiko, I've begun to cultivate her, and for tomorrow evening I already have a dinner engagement with her," I said proudly.

"Then won't all your questions be answered through your cultivating her?"

"Nonsense!" I said. "It's only on the strength of our present judgment of who she is that my cultivating her has any meaning at all."

"And do you suppose our present judgment can be correct?"

"At least we have some inkling," I said. "Haven't we material to go on?"

"Material?" she said.

"Even though I didn't get the documents last night, everything I ran into and came away with can at least serve as material for our judgment," I said. "And yet none of you seems to care about any of it, or even to want to know."

"That's because I've no wish to build a judgment on material so thin," said Mei Yingzi. "Since you want to judge who Miyama Yoshiko is in advance, and since all the material is your own experience, you may as well form your judgment yourself and then go and cultivate her."

Somewhat nettled, I said nothing. In the silence I smoked a cigarette. Then Mei Yingzi said, suddenly gentle:

"Forgive me — I'm really so tired just now, a little unwell. Feel whether I'm feverish?" And she held out her hand to me. I took it, then reached over to touch her brow. She sat quite still, and for a moment I felt what a tender, girlish creature she was; an unspeakable warmth welled up in me that I longed to offer her, but I had no way to show it, and when I lowered my hand I said:

"I can't feel any fever."

She closed her eyes and gave a faint sigh, and as I returned to my seat an inexplicable shame rose from the bottom of my heart. I went back over all I had just said, and it was nothing but crowing over my own deserts — no lofty aim in it, only a pitiable pride and a mean self-importance.

So I sat in silence across from her, watching her weary lashes tremble with the closed lids.

And in that bleak, still silence I heard the sound of Bai Ping coming back. From her brisk, nimble footsteps and the sound of her trading words with Ah Mei, I could imagine what vitality and life she was bringing with her. Mei Yingzi still sat with her eyes wearily closed, and I did not know whether she had heard. My frozen mood thawed a little then, but even so I did not go out to greet her.

In an instant, wearing the smile of a lily just opening, Bai Ping appeared in the doorway like a rainbow. It was as though a wave of living, quickening spirit had swept our gloomy air apart. She was in full paint and powder, her face made up like a translucent autumn moon, at her ears white jade drops swaying like shooting stars; a blue-gray weave of small checks was thrown over a cheongsam of flowered brocade, the sweater's front left open, and with both hands in its pockets she looked us over, easy and content. In the lamplight the scattered flowers of the brocade were like carved fretwork, a gravity showing through their loveliness. I marveled at Bai Ping's air that night — unexpectedly radiant, unexpectedly fresh. I said:

"Bai Ping — is it some news of a triumph that makes you glow all over with this strange light?"

"I hold that one should lift one's spirits through dress and bearing," she said, and sat down beside Mei Yingzi, and looking at her said:

"What's the matter, Mei — a little downcast?"

"It's nothing," said Mei Yingzi with a wan smile. "I'm just a little worn out."

"For my part I hold that the lift of one's dress and bearing depends, after all, on the spirit." As I said it a strange feeling came over me. The two friends before me seemed forever to wax and wane like sun and moon: whenever Bai Ping was at her most brilliant, Mei Yingzi appeared dim and forlorn. Except in the early days of my acquaintance with them, I had seldom weighed the beauty of the one against the other; but at the back of my mind I had always felt Mei Yingzi to be the loveliest woman in our world, with no one to compare to her — and tonight, as she sat in her downcast posture beside a radiant Bai Ping, I actually found that Bai Ping had indeed grown fresher and more beautiful than she. How much of the body's beauty rests, in the end, on how we adorn ourselves, and how much on the rousing of our spirit?

"What?" Bai Ping paid no heed to what I said; she only turned to Mei Yingzi: "Have you had a fright?"

Mei Yingzi did not answer, but smiled and nodded. The smile was gentle and sweet, and it washed away all the proud cast her smiles had carried before. It came to me now that, of these two friends, whenever one was radiant and the other downcast, beauty might belong to the radiant one — while my sympathy always went to the downcast side. The cause was perhaps tenderness; but in Mei Yingzi's smile I discovered that she had always hidden away the very charm that ordinary people are forever displaying. I thought of the iron heroes of history: in the instant of defeat, the charm they let show must be precisely the charm they had always lacked and that ordinary people always have — and in their lives it is the most precious, the deepest, the most human and lovable charm of all, for in that beauty the whole truth and goodness of human nature stands revealed.

The smile at the corners of Mei Yingzi's mouth was slow to fade. She bowed her head, opened the handbag in her hand, and carefully drew out two folded sheets of paper, which she gently handed to Bai Ping.

Bai Ping took them, and at first bent her head and read in silence; then she leaned back on the sofa, knitting her brows, and seemed to read them through once, and then once again.

I had waited to the end of my patience, watching Bai Ping in silence. At last she raised her head and realized I had been waiting — as if she had assumed I had read them long since, and only now found that I had not — and she passed the papers in her hand to me.

The first was a thin sheet of typing paper, on which the following lines in English had been scrawled carelessly in pen:

"Miyama Yoshiko, alias Lang Diyi, followed Kawashima Yoshiko for many years and was active in Manchukuo and North China, often disguising herself as a man and ranging through military and political circles under the name Akiu Saburo. Dashing and unconventional, vigorous and quick. Will stake a thousand in gold at a sweep without turning a hair. Open-handed with her friends, whom all follow. Once returned to her own country, then went on to Nanjing, and lately has come to Shanghai; her mission unknown. R.S. 1041"

"Since coming to Shanghai, Miyama Yoshiko at once grew suspicious of Mei Yingzi, and on Mei's account has clashed several times with Umetake. R.S. 5518"

The second was a thick sheet of letter paper, on which a neat letter in English had been typed:

"Y: The matter of Miyama Yoshiko has now been fully looked into. She came to Shanghai before the outbreak of the Pacific war and fixed her attention on you very early, urging her view on Umetake several times, though he would never credit it. A few days before the masked ball she quarreled with Umetake once again; he half humored her and half resented her, and kept much from her, which is why that day Miyama Yoshiko laid this trap on her own. Her intent, beyond ensnaring you, was to prove to Umetake that her judgment had been right. That you did not fall into it is a great mercy — but hereafter you would do well to keep somewhat out of sight. That night, as the ball broke up, Miyama Yoshiko passed a note to Umetake, saying, no doubt, all that she had arranged; and the next day Umetake told her proudly over the telephone: 'Now you can believe me at last.' Naturally, for Miyama Yoshiko too, that day was a great defeat.

"Enclosed are the RW and RS items, for your reference.

"As for XECM, all is well; set your mind at rest. Your friends are deeply concerned for your health; do take care of yourself and rest quietly. With every good wish.

"S.V"
