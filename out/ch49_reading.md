## To Edo

"Husband and wife."

{j} Even to call it that: at first it had the air of a stage-play, but when two people of a single mind throw themselves in earnest into the same part, it seems they truly become what they act.

And with Oyuki and Toshizō, so it was.

They had passed but one night together, and already it felt as though they had piled up a thousand.

The second day at the Saishō-an.

Both Oyuki and Toshizō had settled so fully into their parts that, at the least little thing, at the very same moment,

"………"

{j} they would smile at one another. To laugh in the same instant is a thing that cannot come to pass unless two sensibilities have drawn together until at last they grow alike.

That afternoon Toshizō,

{j} (Will there be a sunset to see today as well?)

{j} opened the west shōji and stood gazing out past the town of Naniwa toward the ranked hills of Kita-Settsu and the lines of roofs; and though he was sure he had not spoken a word aloud, Oyuki,

{j} "With the sky patterned so with cloud, I wonder how it will be,"

{j} she said. And sure enough, the clouds had risen.

"I should so like to see it, today as well,"

"They speak of famous spots for viewing the moon, but a famous spot for the setting sun is a rare thing. This house is called Saishō-an, the Hut of the Western Glow, so I suppose the evening sun is its one stock-in-trade,"

"But the shō they use means a soft radiance, not the shō of blazing light,"

"The blazing one would glare too harshly, perhaps, and so they chose the softer. For all its red-gold brightness it carries a stillness, the hushed jakkō-light of the Buddha, and that is the very thing of a setting sun,"

"Master Hōgyoku, indeed!—"

Oyuki stifled a laugh and teased him. It was only to be looked for: a man who turned out haiku would have a honed ear for the feel of the characters, too.

At the Saishō-an there was a tea room.

Afterward Oyuki laid the hearth and the rest of it and called Toshizō in. He seated himself before the sunken hearth.

"I have no skill at tea,"

"You have only to drink it, and that will do,"

"And this sweet?"

"It is matsukaze, from Kameya Mutsu in Kyoto,"

(Kyoto. …)

{j} At only the word, a pressing, close-crowding rush of feeling welled up in him. It was not that he loved the city of Kyoto. It was rather that the years he had buried in its streets were too raw yet to be remembered.

Oyuki seemed to sense it at once and hastened to turn the talk aside, but there was no drawing back a Toshizō who had once sunk into silence.

Without a word he took up the bowl, drank a mouthful, thought a while, and then tipped it back and drained it at a draught.

"How did you find it?"

"Hm?"

He raised his face as though waking from a dream,

{j} "It was splendid,"

{j} and wiped the pale-green froth from his lips with a paper from his breast-fold.

"Do you mean to go on living in Kyoto after this, Oyuki, for your training in painting?"

"That is my intention. Even were I to return to Edo, I have no house to go back to.—If the world grows quiet,"

Oyuki had let slip a thing forbidden between the two of them.

"If it grows quiet, might I live together with you, Toshizō-sama?"

"The future is past knowing. As they say in the way of tea, to deepen the bond of this one meeting is the only happiness left to such as us, I think. Whether I go on from here fighting a war half like a wanderer's exile, or whether at a single stroke we turn the world back to the old Tokugawa days, the future cannot be known. I am sorry for you, that you have bound your fate to a man like this,"

"No. Oyuki thinks there is no happiness greater than her own, just now,"

Oyuki, who carried an unhappy marriage in her past, believed that live as she might for years in that other way, none of it would come near the memory of these two days and two nights.

"—Only,"

The words failed her, and Oyuki bowed her face. Her shoulders were weeping. She must have wished, just then, the impossible wish, that these two days and two nights might go on ten thousand years.

"Is it near the hour?"

Toshizō drew the watch from his breast. They were waiting for the sunset. The time was already close upon them.

"Let us go to the west veranda. I shall come once I have cleared this away, so please, go on ahead,"

He stood out on the west veranda.

But the cloud had sunk lower and lower, and only a faint vermilion bled across the western sky.

"Oyuki, it looks to be no good,"

{j} he called in a loud voice toward the inner room.

"The sunset?"

{j} said Oyuki, coming out.

"Ah, no good after all. But then, we saw so beautiful a sunset yesterday,"

"Yes. And that is well enough. The world of men is not so made that yesterday's sunset can be seen again today,"

When the sun went down, the room grew of a sudden dim, and cold.

"Shall we lie down?"

{j} said Toshizō, carelessly, and then looked at Oyuki.

Her ears had flushed red.

***

The next morning Toshizō had the people of the Saishō-an call a palanquin, and hastily put his things in order.

Presently the household came to tell him the palanquin had come. Thrusting his short sword through his sash, Toshizō,

{j} "Oyuki, I am off,"

He did not say the word for parting. And Oyuki, cradling Toshizō's Izumi-no-kami Kanesada against her sleeve, came out to the entrance to see him off, feeling for all the world as though she were his wife.

Toshizō stepped down from the entrance-board, slipped his feet into white-thonged sandals, and turned back toward it.

Oyuki held out the sword to him.

"Well, then.—"

And with that, and no more, he went out. When his figure had vanished beyond the garden shrubbery, Oyuki went back to the room where she had spent those two days with Toshizō.

"Might I stay on here a few more days?"

{j} Oyuki asked the mistress of the Saishō-an.

"Please, as many days as you like,"

The mistress too must have guessed at something. She spoke in a tone of deep sympathy for Oyuki.

For some days after, she passed the time shut up in that room, grinding her mineral pigments, ranging her brushes, covering sheet after sheet of gasenshi and throwing them away.

She meant to see once more the sunset of that day. She would paint the town of Naniwa seen from the plateau of the Saishō-an, the hills of Kita-Settsu curved like moth-eyebrows, the Bay of Osaka that flashed now and then with light, and that splendid evening sun going down into it.

Landscape was not Oyuki's strength. But she felt she must set it down. She made sketch after sketch, and when at last she spread out the silk, she saw again that sunset she had watched with Toshizō, going down.

It was on the twelfth of the First Month that the shogunal warship Fujiyama-maru, bearing Toshizō and the other forty-four survivors of the Shinsengumi, put out from the anchorage off Tenpōzan at Osaka.

The anchor must have come up at about the hour when Oyuki, at the Saishō-an, was setting to work on her first sketch.

On the first day, as the ship came into the Kitan Strait, one of the war-wounded, Yamazaki Susumu, breathed his last. He was a rōnin of Osaka.

He had joined in the first recruitment, just after the founding of the Shinsengumi, and had served throughout as an inspector; at the Ikedaya affair he had disguised himself as a druggist, lodged on the ground floor, and carried on a bold work of espionage.

At the Senbon-matsu on the Yodo embankment, charging into the Satsuma position, he had taken three balls in his body and lived on still, so stout was his spirit; but from about the time he came aboard the wound had festered badly, and he died in a high fever.

"Dead, then,"

By the sudden chill of the hand he held, Yamazaki's, Toshizō knew that what lay before his eyes was Yamazaki no longer.

The funeral was carried out as a burial at sea, after the custom of the Western-style navy.

Yamazaki's body was wound round and round in cloth, an anchor made fast to it, and over it was laid the national flag, the Hinomaru (ever since Perry's coming in the sixth year of Kaei the shogunate had made this the general ensign of Japan; at Toba-Fushimi too the shogunal army had raised the sun-disc flag, and the shogunal navy used it for its naval colors. It was in the first month of the third year of Meiji that the Restoration government took it over anew as the national flag); and on the deck the captain, the ship's officers below him, and the musketeers drew up in ranks.

"So the navy will give Yamazaki his funeral, will they,"

{j} said Kondō, who had lain sick and bedbound in the officers' room until now, and he came out onto the deck in crested formal dress and Sendai-hira.

His face was pale.

When he moved, the bone of his shoulder seemed to pain him still.

Okita Sōji, who lay in the same room as Kondō, had wasted so far that he could scarcely walk unaided; yet,

{j} "Hijikata-san, I'll come out too,"

{j} he said, and got down from the cot. They tried to stop him, but the young man only smiled, put on haori and hakama, leaned on his sword for a staff, gripped the handrail, and made to climb the stair.

When Toshizō made to take him under the right arm,

{j} "I'd rather you didn't,"

{j} he refused. That it should be said of Okita Sōji of the Shinsengumi that he had grown so weak he walked leaning on another man's shoulder, for this vain, showy Sōji that was not to be borne.

"The doctor tells me to stay lying down, so I do as I'm bidden, but truly, I'm perfectly well,"

"Is that so,"

Toshizō looked at the young man's smile, grown to a translucent beauty, as one looks at something to be feared.

"The stairs on a warship are steep, aren't they,"

He said it to cover his gasping for breath.

It was too much for him. For the work of breathing, half the use of Okita's lungs had already begun to fail.

Of the forty-three men of the Shinsengumi, all but three too badly hurt to move were drawn up on the deck. Kondō of course among them, and nearly all of them bore wounds, greater or lesser.

"You're about the only one, Hijikata-san, standing there without a scratch,"

{j} said Okita, with a low chuckle.

"Talking will tire you,"

"It won't tire me. I'm admiring you. Look them over how you like, and it's only you, Hijikata-san, who's as hale as a demon,"

"Be still,"

Presently the naval officer commanding the musket-party drew his sword.

He gave the command.

Rat-a-tat-tat, ba-boom, the funeral volley rang out across the Kitan Strait, and the body of Yamazaki Susumu, inspector and vice-commander's jokin, slid from the ship's side into the sea.

All the while a bugle was sounding.

When the funeral was over, Kondō, who seemed much moved by this rite the navy had given, caught hold of the captain, Hida Hamagorō (a man well versed in engines; after the Restoration the new government begged his service, and he rose to such posts as Inspector-General of Naval Engineering, the equivalent of a rear admiral; on the twenty-seventh day of the fourth month in the twenty-second year of Meiji, while away on official business, he fell from the platform at Fujieda station in Shizuoka and was struck and killed by a train just then coming in), and,

{j} "You have my thanks, my thanks,"

{j} he said, thanking him over and over. Commander of a corps though Kondō was, worn down as he was by his wound, the sight of him bent low before Captain Hida had the look of some old countryman.

Even after they had withdrawn to the officers' room,

{j} "Toshi, since the corps was founded more men have died than a man can count, but not one of them was given a funeral like Yamazaki's. He worked hard, and in dying he was blessed, too,"

He was full of admiration.

"Kondō-san, a funeral's no thing to be admiring over. There's a saying: the resolute man never forgets that he may lie in a ditch; the brave man never forgets that he may lose his head. It means you never forget the fate of it, your corpse flung in a gutter, your head passing into the enemy's hand. A man, to my mind, is a thing that dies a death without a burial,"

"Toshi, you're too contrary by half. Yamazaki was a brave man, and he was buried besides. That's what I'm glad of,"

And yet, Kondō, Hijikata, Okita: in their own last hours, would they be buried or no?

The ship, her boilers stoked to the full, ran eastward, hugging the Pacific coast.

The Fujiyama-maru was a great ship, wooden-hulled, three-masted, of a thousand tons.

Twelve guns aboard.

One hundred and eighty horsepower.

She had been ordered from the United States by the shogunate and received in the first year of Keiō, a battle-tried ship that had taken part in the bombardment of Shimonoseki during the Chōshū expedition.

A wooden ship of a thousand tons should be large indeed; but with more than a thousand shogunal troops packed aboard this single hull, the misery of life inside her was beyond words.

The galley alone could not cook for them all, so they hauled great cauldrons out onto the deck to boil rice and simmer soup, and about these the men were jammed solid, with no room even to lie down, each hugging his knees for what little ease he could find.

The New Year sea ran rough with wind and wave, and nearly all of them, sick as invalids from the pitching, and few there were who could eat the food they were given down to the last grain.

The rice, too, was bad. Much of what had been loaded at Osaka was old rice out of the stores of Osaka Castle, and boiled up it gave off a foul smell.

Kondō, in his Edo days, had been neither the sort nor in the circumstances to make anything of his food; but having come to Kyoto and grown used to good eating,

{j} "Toshi, this, a man can't eat,"

{j} he said, at a loss.

Toshizō ate almost nothing. Sooner than eat bad food, he held, a man had better die.

But that Okita Sōji would not take a single grain into his mouth, this had both Kondō and Toshizō at their wits' end.

"Sōji, eat now,"

{j} they scolded, but he only smiled weakly. Not to eat could only be bad for his sickness, that was certain. Kondō spoke as though bellowing:

"Sōji, a samurai is not a man to say whether the field-rations of a battlefield taste good or ill,"

"It's only, I'm seasick,"

Sōji's face was deathly pale.

"If I force it down, I bring it up again. And bringing it up takes strength, and then I'm the worse for it after,"

Toshizō picked out Nomura Risaburō, an ordinary trooper who was oddly proof against the sea, and set him to nursing Okita. Nomura was an attentive man; he had the galley make a broth of simmered fish and a thin rice gruel, and got Okita to drink them. That much, and only that, would go down his throat.

Four days at sea.

Before dawn on the fifteenth, as they came off Shinagawa, Toshizō had come out on deck and was retching over the side when of a sudden he caught sight of lights on land, and,

{j} "What place is that?"

{j} he asked a sailor.

"That is Shinagawa-juku, sir,"

{j} the sailor answered, in the accent of Iyo-Shiwaku.

(Shinagawa, then, we had best go ashore here.)

{j} He put it to the captain, Hida Hamagorō, and Hamagorō agreed at once, saying something quickly through a laugh. When Toshizō thought it over afterward, it seemed to have been,

"Even the Shinsengumi, it appears, cannot win against seasickness,"

{j} that he had said.

Before dawn they dropped anchor, three cutters were lowered, and it was settled that the forty-three of the Shinsengumi, and they alone, would go ashore.

At Shinagawa,

{j} "the Kamaya,"

{j} an inn by that name, they lodged; but Kondō and Okita alone did not put up there, hiring a fishing boat straight from the beach, going on ahead into Edo, and it was arranged that they should take their treatment at the shogunal Igakusho, the medical school by Izumibashi in Kanda.

At the entrance of the Kamaya, Toshizō,

{j} "Shinsengumi Lodging,"

{j} had a name-board set out. This Kamaya at Shinagawa may be called the first quarters of the Shinsengumi after they had left Kyoto and Osaka.

"Gentlemen, the war begins from here. We will stay some days, so recover from the weakness the seasickness has left in you,"

{j} he told them, and for himself took a room at the back with a view of the sea, pulled a single kaimaki over him, and lay down with a flop.

(What is Oyuki doing about now, I wonder.)

Strangely, he could not shake the feeling that Oyuki was still there at the Saishō-an.

It was likely because on that day of parting Oyuki had sat so quietly on the entrance-board, seeing him off as though from a home of her own.

He slept soundly until evening.

He woke.

About that same hour Oyuki, in that room at the Saishō-an, was spreading out her painting-silk and musing vaguely on which color she should use to catch the red of the sun that was, at that very moment, sinking behind the hills of Kita-Settsu.
