## 9

Yanzhi settled at Fei Family Village, ten li beyond Xietang; she had decided it all at once on the road home. In her ragged clothes, her daughter in her arms, she was like a young widow whose family had been destroyed in the war, and the one who took her in was an aged widow. For five silver dollars Yanzhi became her niece, come from far away at the end of her rope, and each day she learned to weave bamboo baskets in the yard, though she never went with the old woman to hawk them in the town. Yanzhi resolved never to set foot in Xietang again, and to live out her whole life in this little courtyard walled with rammed earth.

Time soon made Yanzhi into a countrywoman; her skin grew rougher by the day, and the bamboo splits left her ten fingers thick with calluses. She buried the bundle she had brought from the boat under the bed: when her daughter was grown, she would use the money in it to raise a house, and then to bring in a live-in son-in-law for the girl. For now, Yanzhi wished only that her daughter would grow up faster, day by day.

But Yanzhi went into the town after all. Word of victory in the War of Resistance came from the mouth of a traveling peddler, though the villagers showed little enough excitement at it. It was the children who were excited, shouting and chasing the peddler all the way out of the village. It was toward dusk that Yanzhi found her daughter gone. First she searched everywhere alone, frantic as a madwoman, then let out a scream and sank down in a heap at the mouth of the village. The whole village turned out that night; with torches they beat through every thicket, every well, every creek-inlet around the village. In the small hours people came back one after another, and the old widow laid a garment over Yanzhi's shoulders and said the peddler must surely have carried the girl off. "A thing like this happens in the village every year," the old widow said.

Before dawn Yanzhi set out for Xietang. The peddler had come up from Xietang, so he would surely leave the same way. All along the road Yanzhi asked after him at every ferry crossing, but people seemed inured to this kind of kidnapping and only shook their heads blankly: didn't know, hadn't seen.

It was under the stone memorial arch at the entrance to Xietang that Yanzhi caught sight of Young Master Tang. His hands were bound behind him, and gripped between two soldiers he was all but dragged along. Behind him came the owner of the pharmacy, the proprietor of the Xietang Inn, the chairman of the dockworkers' union; these once-respectable men were shrunken past bearing now, and not one of them could walk on his own legs. They were about to be shot as traitors, as drug-dealers, as traffickers in human beings, right below the Laifeng Bridge outside the arch. The townsfolk trailed after a squad of armed military police, streaming past Yanzhi in a jostling crowd, and no one recognized in this glassy-eyed country woman the most beautiful woman the town had once had.

As if a gust of wind had swept through, the people in the street thinned all at once. Yanzhi searched every wharf, every landing, every boat in the town, and asked after them of everyone, but no one had seen a peddler with a carrying-pole, and no one had seen a small deaf-mute girl. Just then gunshots sounded far off, and Yanzhi gave a soft cry, as though every one of those bullets had bored at once into her breast. She raised her head slowly, looked toward the rear window of the tailor shop across the river, and slowly toppled onto the stone steps.

But Yanzhi soon came round, as though she had dozed off and dreamed a nightmare. She pushed through the milling boatmen and, a hand pressed to her heart, crossed the long street swaying, and came back to Fei Family Village as if sleepwalking. Yanzhi knew she would never see her own daughter again. And close on that came the old widow's death, on the way back from the town. The ferry-boat at the crossing capsized, and her body floated up two days later at the place where she had gone under.

A year later Yanzhi pulled down the rammed-earth wall and raised a house of two courtyards. She bought five mu of land in the village besides, and a water buffalo, and hired two day-laborers.

Yanzhi turned away every matchmaker who came to her door, and each day she sewed cheongsams alone in the house, partly to pass the time. She would finish a cheongsam and unpick it, sew it up again, forever changing the style, and often sewed a brand-new garment down into an old one. At first the villagers guessed endlessly about her money; later they all decided the woman's mind had gone wrong. Not until one night, when a band of Nationalist troops routed from the front broke into the village, did people learn that this woman who never stirred from her gate had once been the lady chieftain of the Xiangfu marsh, a terror in her day.

The Nationalist soldiers plundered house by house; they robbed not only grain and money but stripped the clothes off the villagers to wear themselves. Out of their uniforms, the soldiers were nothing but bandits. They broke down Yanzhi's door, and as they were turning the place upside down someone recognized Yanzhi. The man told the others to stop and, a little abashed, called out to Yanzhi, "Boss."

Yanzhi said, "You've mistaken me for someone else."

The man said, "There's no mistake. I'm Scarface Qiang." And with that he turned his head and put the knife-scar on his left cheek toward Yanzhi. "I'm Old Mo's nephew," he said. "Scarface Qiang."

Yanzhi remembered a face like that. "You've certainly come up in the world," she said.

Scarface Qiang hung his head. "My uncle's dead," he said.

Old Mo had died three days before, in a clash with the People's Liberation Army. He had been press-ganged into the ranks while taking his pleasure upstairs at the Xingchun Lou in the county town. That day Old Mo, drunk, had fallen to squabbling over a woman, whipped out his pistol and slapped it down on the table. "You think that prick of yours can stand up harder than my gun-barrel?" he said.

The young man before him, done up in a Western suit and leather shoes, went white with fright and turned to leave. Old Mo roared with laughter and told the whore in his lap that a wet noodle like that would be a soft egg with his trousers off too. But the young man was soon back. This time he wore an American-style uniform and carried a revolver, and with him came a squad of Nationalist soldiers shouldering carbines.

The water bandits of the Xiangfu marsh were reorganized into a single Type-B company, and Old Mo, once he had put on a uniform, became a first-lieutenant company commander, and they marched off to the bank of the Yangtze. "But we were only going there to be cannon-fodder." By the end Scarface Qiang was streaming snot and tears, and he said in a voice torn ragged that in a matter of days the brothers who had come out of the marsh were dead but for the ten-odd of them left.

Yanzhi said nothing, and as a crowd of old scenes flashed past before her eyes, someone spoke up all at once: "Boss, why not lead us again? This time the brothers will do whatever you say."

Several voices took up the cry, begging Yanzhi to lead them back into the Xiangfu marsh. Yanzhi sat down in a chair and looked at them without a word.

"Or you could speak to Mr. Qin again, and vouch for us to go over to the Communist forces." Scarface Qiang said, "This will all be Communist country any day now."

Yanzhi reached out and pointed around the room. "Take whatever's inside, all of it," she said, "and once you've taken it, get out of my sight."

Scarface Qiang said, "And where else can we go?"

Yanzhi said, "Back where you came from."

Scarface Qiang said, "I'm afraid there's a dead end waiting for us wherever we go."

"Then do a little good for your own souls," Yanzhi said.

The soldier-bandits left that same night. The next day Yanzhi opened her storehouse and made the villagers good with the grain inside it. The people of Fei Family Village wept with gratitude toward Yanzhi, and were full of awe of her besides. Yet no sooner had the People's Liberation Army's work team taken up quarters in the village than someone informed on her. Yanzhi was shut up in her own storehouse, and she thought the whole night through and still could not fathom how the villagers could turn on her, as thankless as bandits themselves.

Yanzhi was soon marched off to Xietang and shut in the rear yard of the town office. It had become the Military Affairs Commission of the People's Liberation Army now, and every day uniformed soldiers came and went across the yard to interrogate the people held in each of the rooms. The pair who questioned Yanzhi each time were a man and a woman, and of the two the man was the somewhat gentler. He always held his pencil the way a man holds a cigarette. "Take your time," he would tell Yanzhi. "There's no hurry. We have all the time in the world."

Yanzhi sat on a bench and gave her account point by point. It was the first time in her life she had looked back on herself so closely, and much of the past, once spoken aloud, she could scarcely believe herself. When she came to driving the knife into Pockmarked Liu's chest, it was as though her two hands were still smeared with blood. Yanzhi rubbed her palms against her thighs without stopping, lifted her head to look at the two before her, and all at once did not know what more to say. There were tears in her eyes.

The male soldier said, "All right. That's enough for today."

It was on an afternoon some days later that Yanzhi spoke of Qin Shuji. "If he had waited that day at the Fenshui Pavilion," she said, "I would surely be wearing the same clothes as you now."

The male soldier suddenly slapped the table. "Do you know what you're saying?" he said loudly.

Yanzhi said, "I do."

The male soldier asked, "Then do you know what kind of man Qin Shuji was?"

Yanzhi said, "I do. He was one of yours."

The male soldier asked again, "And what else?"

Yanzhi licked her lips, looked at his knitted brows, and shook her head.

Half a month later Yanzhi was taken to the prison in the county town, where they held bandits, secret agents, counter-revolutionaries, and Nationalist officers, but very few women. Each day at the exercise hour, as she passed down the long corridor, many eyes watched her in surprise from behind the iron bars. Yanzhi was shut in a cramped single cell on the second floor, and apart from two meals a day, no one came to question her again. Outside the cell window lay the drill-ground, where the prisoners drilled and walked.

Winter came on quickly, and snowflakes drifted in at the window and settled in Yanzhi's icy palm, and never melted.
