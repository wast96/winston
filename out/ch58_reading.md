## Chapter 58

Night.

Mrs. Manfield sat facing me. I said:

"As you yourself put it: 'Each time I try to talk her round, I only end by being talked round by her.' So today I did not try; and I mean not to try again."

"You mean you are going to take her away with you?"

"No," I said. "This afternoon I finished getting my homeward pass. Tomorrow morning I send the luggage on ahead, and the day after I leave. I want to set off early, before Helen knows. The clothes that have not yet come I will not bother to take."

"But what has moving up your departure to do with keeping it from Helen?"

"Because each time I see her again is one more thread that binds me."

Mrs. Manfield looked at me with a boundless pity; after a while she said:

"Poor child! I shall be grateful to you always."

I said nothing. Mrs. Manfield watched me as though she would go and did not, would speak and did not; at last she leaned back against the sofa and said, in earnest:

"Young man, one who has tasted the bitterness of love will taste its happiness too; is the victory not there before you? This door will always be open to you."

Then she rose, came to me, and smoothed my hair with her hand, saying nothing for a long while; at last she gave a small, soft sigh and went quietly out.

"Sleep early now. Good night," she said tenderly, and softly closed the door; and at the sound of that door the tears that had filled my heart came welling up like a spring.

I could not sleep; a thousand griefs churned in me. I began to pack my few belongings, setting the newly made clothes together with the suitcase I had brought from Dr. Philip's. In that case there were only two Western suits, a few pieces of underclothing, five or six books, some pages of Bai Ping's diary I had copied out while in the hospital and the letters Helen had written me long before, and, besides these, the morning-gown Mei Yingzi had given me, the one Bai Ping's bullet had pierced and my own blood had stained again and again; the rest was unimportant letters and scraps of paper. Apart from the five or six books and a few letters I no longer wanted, most of it was the most precious keepsakes of my life. I took Bai Ping's portrait from its frame, and together with those copied diary pages and Helen's letters, and the photograph of Helen that I also drew from its frame, I laid them all in the case's secret lining. The case I had had made to order the day before yesterday; last of all I moved into it the new clothes and toilet things and everything from the bottom of the old case. This was my only trunk; besides it I had one simple travelling bag, in which all the newly bought bedding had lain untouched, for what I slept under was always the Manfields' own.

My packing done, I had a thousand things I longed to pour out to Helen, and I resolved to leave a letter for her at my going. I found paper and pen and sat down at the desk to write; but I could not find where to begin. I wrote a sheet and tore it up, wrote another and tore it up, and after seven or eight of them I forced myself, at last, to go on.

The letter was very long. As I recall it now, it ran more or less like this:

"Helen:

"You said: '—I want life now, I want a soul, I want music, I want the world. And so I need a love like yours—' But the love of a confirmed bachelor? It belongs to the spirit, and is not fixed on one; it is abstract, and empty; it gives forever without reckoning what it receives; it belongs to the whole of mankind and of history, it is made one with great Nature, it is the equal of the bosom of God.

"That, of course, is only my ideal, my own gloss upon it; I have naturally never lived up to it, and perhaps never shall; but until quite lately I was always striving toward it.

"What makes mankind precious is that we have ideals, and the ideal belongs to God; to strive toward the ideal is to draw near to the true, the beautiful, and the good.

"Yet mankind has never reached the ideal, and is not permitted to reach it; for all the striving of how many generations, the ideal has come no nearer to us. How empty, then, how remote is this love of the confirmed bachelor that I spoke of?

"For I am a man; I am a man born of a mother; I have every failing that belongs to mankind. I cannot make my bosom the equal of God's.

"In my proud, unceasing giving, I had forgotten that all the while I was ceaselessly receiving too; and only when that receiving was cut off altogether did I discover that I could not go on living upon giving alone.

"When I was heartening others and consoling them, we were all of us leaning, hour by hour, on the heartening and the consolation of others; and I never knew it—and not to know that is not to know the warmth and the meaning of the human world.

"And now that I know it, and have set my heart on being an ordinary man, I find how much I need the love of my fellow men! ..."

I could write no further there. I put the letter away and lay down on the bed; after some two hours of fitful dozing I got up.

At seven o'clock I put the now-empty picture frame into the drawer and slipped out with my luggage. I took it to the travel bureau, had it weighed and paid the charge, and went alone to a noodle shop for a bite to eat.

All at once the sorrow of parting seemed to have banked itself high in my heart, and every taste of separation in life and in death rose in me anew: I thought of Stephen, of Bai Ping, of Mei Yingzi, of Helen. Last I thought of Mrs. Stephen, and suddenly felt I must see her one more time. All the other friends and kin? Those we should surely meet again; but she—she it might well be a parting forever, for who could say her end would not be Stephen's, or Bai Ping's, or Mei Yingzi's?

Thinking so, I came out of the noodle shop and took the tram to Route Lafayette to see Mrs. Stephen.

Mrs. Stephen's garden was as tranquil as ever; the winter jasmine and the cannas were in bloom. I rang the bell.

A servant I did not know opened the door. I asked:

"Is Mrs. Stephen at home?"

"Whom shall I say?"

I gave her a card; she took it away, and coming back said:

"She has just risen. Please wait a moment in the drawing room."

I went into the drawing room and sat down on the sofa, and a brightness and ease about it brought the old days floating up in me.

It was here that I first met the dazzling, radiant Mei Yingzi; it was here that I first met Manfield's mother; it was here that I heard Helen sing, twice, in two wholly different ways; it was here that I broke into the strangest of societies and took up the most secret of work; it was here...

The door opened, and two red-haired English hounds came in and nuzzled at my clothes and shoes; then the tall, elegant, serene, and noble Mrs. Stephen came in, with a smile of welcome, and said:

"Good morning."

"Good morning," I said.

"Not gone yet?" she said, sitting down across from me.

"Tomorrow morning."

"And Helen?" she asked. "When does she leave for Beiping?"

"She says she is not going."

"Not going?" This took her by surprise, but after a moment's thought a mischievous smile came over her: "Is it because you are not going?"

"She wants to come with me to the interior instead."

"Is that not a challenge flung at your bachelorhood?" she laughed.

"When I came to feel that even a confirmed bachelor must keep the balance of his feelings by the feelings of friends, of society, of the human world, then this bachelorhood of mine seemed to me very remote and empty."

"So you have surrendered, then—very good," she said. "So you mean to take her to the interior."

"But no," I said. "When I was cast off by separation and death and left utterly alone, every woman who cherished me seemed like a mother."

"All women are mothers at bottom."

"If it is that motherliness one should honor, then all the more should I honor Mrs. Manfield's feelings," I said. "And besides—do you know how different the life I am going to in the interior is from the life she ought to unfold?"

"You are right," said Mrs. Stephen. "She is still young; we should treasure the gifts she was born with."

"And so tomorrow I shall slip away without a word to her," I said. "I hope you, too, will be willing to help her, to hearten her and comfort her."

"That is well," she said. "I hope that when our work is done, the two of you may be made husband and wife. I shall be praying for you always."

"I had not thought so far," I said. "Of the future I no longer dare to think. Stephen is dead, Bai Ping is dead—both beyond anything I could have foreseen."

"But both live on in our hearts."

"And Mei Yingzi, say—you, we—shall we ever meet again?"

"The world is a single whole; mankind has but one pulse, we have but one soul; however great the distance, still we are together."

"And you think that can console you?"

"But besides this," she said, "what have we with which to console ourselves?"

I fell into a mournful silence.

The telephone rang, and I rose to take my leave. Mrs. Stephen gave me her hand and said:

"Our friendship will warm forever the most desolate and lonely places of my heart."

The servant was answering the telephone; she pressed my hand and said:

"Tell Helen to come and see me."

"Goodbye," I said.

"Goodbye—we are together always," she said, and went to take the call, watching me with the eyes of one loath to part.

Suddenly I thought of Mei Yingzi. I said:

"May I not see Mei Yingzi one more time?"

She had just picked up the receiver; she covered the mouthpiece again with her hand and said softly:

"No one yet knows where she is. You must be a little stronger."

I had nothing to say. I made a hurried farewell and came away, back to Route Prosper Paris. I told Helen I had been calling on Mrs. Stephen, and told her, too, that Mrs. Stephen very much hoped she would visit.

From that hour on, that day, I stayed always at Helen's side. There was much in my heart I could not find a way to say, and could not say; I did my best, with an effort, to find abstract and empty things to talk of, and whenever she drew toward the real question I would put her off. But at last she seized an opening and said, straight out:

"It seems we ought still to talk over what came to nothing the other day."

"Has that not already been settled?"

"You mean...?"

"We are agreed on everything; the only trouble is your mother, and I do not wish to wound her," I said. "I hope you can win her consent."

"And if she does not consent?"

"Then the day after tomorrow we will find a chance to talk her round."

"And when do we go, then?"

"The day after tomorrow, I think; I still have a few clothes that may be sent round," I said, "and then we can make ready to set off."

She was silent, and I turned the talk aside once more.

That night, pleading a few letters to write, I went to my own room and wrote on at the letter I meant to leave for Helen:

"—Now that I feel myself unworthy to speak of the love of the confirmed bachelor, it seems to me that your love for me is, after all, a single person's love (though not a creed of the single life): because you want life, want a soul, want music, want the world, therefore you love me—how strange a saying that is!

"If our love is of the spirit, of the ideal, of that bachelorhood I spoke of, then (and here I used Mrs. Stephen's very words) the world is a single whole, mankind has but one pulse, we have but one soul; however great the distance, still we are together.

"If we must be together, then it seems that, apart from what is called the meaning and the form of marriage, mankind has no other meaning and no other form for it; and yet this is the most human, and the most instinctual, love of all.

"If we were conscious that we loved only in this instinctual way, should we not have felt as much long ago? And your feeling now does not seem to be of that kind. As for me, I still cannot believe that my love is only this. I cannot tell now; but the strange solace and warmth I feel at your side come wholly of the weariness, the loneliness, the desolation left when Bai Ping was dead and Mei Yingzi gone, when the tension slackened and the fellowship dispersed, when the bright bustle turned cold and the solid thing crumbled—just as an abandoned infant will feel a mother in every arms that hold it.—"

Just here, of a sudden, someone knocked at the door.

"Who is it?" I said, putting the letter away.

"The tailor has brought the clothes." It was Ah Mei's voice.

I went out and saw a man cradling a white parcel, standing outside the drawing-room door, lit from the side by the lamplight within; and my heart began all at once to pound wildly.

How could it be he? I thought.

But looking again, it was indeed he.

It was not the tailor who had lately been making my clothes, but the proprietor of the tailor's shop that Cishan's Third Uncle had taken us to—short of stature, fair-skinned, his plump face wearing a smile.

"Come this way," I said calmly.

He came over unhurried, walked into my room quite naturally, smiling, without a single word, and opened the white parcel.

Ah—it was the overcoat and jacket I had left behind on Cishan's Third Uncle's boat.

He laid the clothes on the bed. Then, from the innermost fold of his gown, he drew out a letter; there was no writing on the envelope, and something seemed to be enclosed inside. Then he said:

"That is all, then."

"Is there no other word?" I asked softly.

"Goodbye." His smile deepened as he said it.

I saw him to the outer door and thanked him and took my leave. I came back and tore the letter open in haste. Inside was a ring—a red diamond set square within a cross of white diamonds. The letter bore no signature, but it was Mei Yingzi's beyond a doubt. She had written:

"My telephone call and your footsteps crossed, one just behind the other, in that drawing room where we first met; is not everything in life as though foreordained? In truth, even had we met, there would have been nothing to say, and so I did not send them after you to bring you back. It is well: all that was left unsaid we understand in our hearts, and all that had to be said has already been said.

"Now—beautiful, noble, faithful, devout—any crown you set upon our friendship, I shall feel no shame to wear. No parting in life or in death has ever divided, nor ever will divide, our laughter shared, our weeping shared, our sighing and our trembling shared.

"Since we parted I have wanted to come and see you every day, but have had not a moment free—as you can easily imagine. For now, I fear there will be no occasion for us to meet.

"Let us each seek our haven. I shall come before the world henceforth in the character of Cishan's Third Aunt.

"I have always felt that nothing man ever made could be more beautiful than this ring of mine, and so I give it to you; you are sure to love it, and you will never, I think, forget me."

I read it twice and sat blankly on the sofa a long while.

Afterward I thought it must be near the hour when Mrs. Manfield and her daughter went to bed, and I wanted to go and see them one last time.

I stepped quietly into the drawing room. Helen was reading; her mother was putting things in order. Helen said:

"Is the letter finished?"

"Yes," I said. "Are you not yet ready for bed?"

"We are just waiting for you to have some tea together," said Mrs. Manfield, and went out to fetch it.

Helen laid down her book and looked at me. She said:

"Your face is very strange today."

"I am tired, I expect," I said. Then all at once she said with a smile:

"Just now Mother seemed not so set against it."

"Against...?"

"Against my going to the interior with you."

I nodded as though gratified, thinking in my heart that it was only that your mother, knowing I leave tomorrow, no longer needed to hold out against you. And of a sudden I felt a great pity for Helen, so deceived, and felt ashamed, and grieved, and yet could show nothing of it. I thought of Mrs. Stephen's words, and felt sure she would explain to her and hearten her, and so I said:

"Tomorrow or the next day you ought to go and see Mrs. Stephen; she misses you very much."

Ah Mei and Mrs. Manfield came in with the tea and cut short Helen's reply. Over the tea I found Helen trying, again and again, to bring up the matter of the interior. To me every mention meant more lying words to speak, and that was a torment; after I had turned her aside several times, I asked her to play me a piece on the piano. She did not refuse. An Italian serenade, I think it was—plaintive and sorrowful, so that it seemed to me the very music of parting; and by the time it ended I could hold back my grief no longer, and forcing myself to bear up I said:

"It is late, and I am very weary." And with that I rose.

"Good night," said Mrs. Manfield.

"Good night," I said. "Good night, Helen."

I went back to my room, rested a while, and then wrote on at that letter I was leaving for Helen:

"If this is how it stands, then what worth has my love? Set beside your mother's love for you, how shamed I am by the comparison! Shall a need of mine such as this cost a mother her nobler, holier need?

"So when we spoke of going together to Beiping, it was only that we each alike needed a change of scene—that we were, say, fellow travelers on one road. Now, in plain fact, I must go to the interior, and for a time I do not even mean to do my scholar's work; and so our roads have already parted.

"Now, if I went with you to Beiping, what I should sacrifice is the life of the body; and if you went with me to the interior, what you would sacrifice is the life of the spirit.

"—

"Now, by the time you read this letter, I shall already be gone.

"Hereafter, let us each weigh well which kind of love ours truly is.

"I do not understand this single person's love you speak of. To me there are only two kinds of love in the world:

"The love of the ideal, of the spirit: then we are everywhere present, present in every place; the world is a single whole, our soul is but one; I shall dwell forever in your singing and the sound of your piano, just as Bai Ping dwells in every word I speak.

"And the love of the human, the instinctual: then, between us, being neither mother and son nor brother and sister, there seems to be but one form for it, and that is man and wife.

"Now the work I go to in the interior belongs to the war, to the nation; and your work belongs to peace, to the human world. But mine is for a time, and yours is forever; and when my work of the moment is done, if we both then feel that our love is of the latter kind, then, and only then, may we be together.

"And for now, we should still weigh and reflect. Often, in the midst of our work, we shall find our love raised to a higher plane; sometimes we shall feel we share one bosom with God; in art we may feel the very same; and yet this and our instinctual, human love, for all their contradiction, are still in harmony.

"In a word, my view is the exact opposite of yours: if not-marrying were the rule, then we should have no reason to be together—so that this letter is, on the contrary, a proposal of marriage to you.

"I take your photograph away with me; through all our meetings and partings it will be a keepsake, and I think you will allow me this.

"Make up your mind to go to Beiping. Mrs. Stephen will give you every help."

Such, more or less, was the letter—failing to say what it meant, its words all out of order; but truly I could write no better then, and in any case this disorder and confusion may stand for the state of my heart at parting. I sealed it and wrote Helen's name. I put on my hand the ring Mei Yingzi had sent, and began to make ready for bed.

Suddenly, again someone knocked at the door.

"Who is it?"

"It is I."

"Come in, please."

It was Mrs. Manfield who came in. I supposed she had come to make her last farewell; but she shut the door, came softly to me, and said in a tone of eager sincerity:

"I have decided now to let Helen go to the interior with you. You need not leave tomorrow."

"What do you mean by this?" I was startled.

"For your happiness and Helen's."

"But what of you?"

"So long as she is happy, I shall not suffer."

"No, Mrs. Manfield—please sit down." When she had sat, I went on: "Happiness cannot be had upon a supposition. Happiness must be created; it takes striving; and the more we create and strive, the firmer the ground and the surer the safeguard of our happiness."

"You mean you will still go alone."

"Yes," I said. "I have made up my mind."

To my surprise, Mrs. Manfield of a sudden began to sob again.

And so I reasoned with her. I painted the hardship of her living alone in such a Shanghai as this, without Helen; I painted the perils of the road to the interior; I said she would surely come to regret it, and that Helen might fall ill upon the journey, and that then it would be too late to turn back. Just as you yourself said, I told her, war is always for a time; the victory and the peace are there before us; and then, if Helen loves me, of course I will come back at once.

This at last moved her. As she left she wore a look of deep gratitude and of clinging reluctance, and through her tears she blessed me over and over; and my heart was utterly melted by her.

After she had gone I sat alone a long while, and felt that her change of heart today, and her keeping me back, were in no way at Helen's asking, but wholly out of the tenderness of her own leave-taking of me. And so upon the envelope of the letter I was leaving for Helen I wrote:

"I shall be praying always for your mother, the noblest and purest of mothers."

Last of all I thought of Ah Mei. I left two thousand dollars on the desk and wrote, again, on the envelope:

"Two thousand dollars for Ah Mei, in thanks from me."

I had three hours' rest.

At five o'clock, dressed in a gown, with the Western overcoat that the ever-smiling proprietor had brought me tucked under my arm (I left the jacket behind), I set out upon my journey under a wide and dusky sky.

There was wind; I saw white cloud and gray cloud streaming up in the east.

(The End)
