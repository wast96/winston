## Dice

The fifteenth of the last month of the year; only ten days or so remained until New Year's Eve.

At about nine thirty-five, Wei Dafu came up to the front of the Zhejiang Grand Theater. Across the way stood the Fourth Avenue market.

The Municipal Council permitted vehicles to park along this stretch of Zhejiang Road, and ordinarily it was jammed past bearing: besides the motorcars there were rickshaws, the pushcarts of peddlers, the flatbed carts that hauled in the vegetables, and anyone going in or out of the market had to thread the gaps between them.

Something about the day struck Wei Dafu as off. On either side of the market entrance everything was in good order. A row of motorcars stood at the curb as always, but the wheelbarrows and the flatbed carts, the ones the carters dragged with the tow-rope biting into their shoulders, had all vanished, as though someone were hiding around the corner turning them back.

He watched for a while. He noticed that the moment a rickshaw pulled to the curb and its mistress stepped down, the puller would hurry off with his empty rickshaw, as if some warning hung in the air around them; drenched in sweat and gasping, they still knew better than to linger even a moment in the forbidden zone. Perhaps his nerves were getting the better of him, Wei Dafu thought. Then again, it was common enough in the Settlement for the patrolmen to take a sudden notion and come out onto the street driving off idlers and hangers-on with a will. He had probably been too much on edge these last days, he told himself.

A film poster was pasted up by the theater door: today's opening was Cuckoo Soul Abroad, starring Jin Yan and Ziluolan. Not worth seeing, most likely, he decided—a picture with three principal characters, all three of them dead by the end. And the timing was wrong besides; the first show was not until three in the afternoon. His mind wandered as he turned it over.

Nine-forty in the morning, the roof garden of the World Hotel.

The amusement ground had a forlorn look. Winter sunlight fell on the carousel, where a few wooden horses drooped their heads, the flaked patches of paint especially stark. The skating rink and the billiard room stood empty, the storytelling hall had not opened, and only one or two customers sat in the open-air tearoom.

Yi Junnian walked to one corner of the garden and stood at the parapet, looking out. The building across the road had a market on its bottom two floors; housewives and servants were packed at the entrance, and this was the busiest hour of all. The windows of the upper two floors were shut. They were top-hung, and could only be pushed open from below.

“Who did you see this morning?” Ling Wen asked behind him. By their arrangement, Yi Junnian was to have gone first to Ling Wen's home that morning and come to the market together with her. But he had not come; instead he had sent a clerk from his painting-and-calligraphy shop with a note, asking her to meet him at the roof garden of the World Hotel. Ling Wen had been here once before with Yi Junnian, and got in and up the elevator easily enough.

“A driver at the Nanshi police station—one of our own, placed there.”

“Meeting him in such a hurry—has something gone wrong?”

Yi Junnian, his back to her, shook his head, still gazing down at the road below. He thought a moment, then said abruptly: “The detective squad at Baiyunguan mustered a crowd of men in the middle of the night. Said they had business to do inside the Settlement.”

Yi Junnian was Ling Wen's superior, and by rights he should not have told her any of this; but she had worked in the group longer than anyone, she was highly capable, and she had always handled its internal liaison, so he kept almost nothing from her.

“Should we warn Old Fang?” Ling Wen said, suddenly anxious.

“It may have nothing to do with us—and there's no time to warn anyone now.”

Qin Chuan'an did not go in by the market entrance. There was a side door on the north face of the building, and he went in there and took the elevator straight up to the third floor. The moment the doors opened he heard Schubert; he recognized it as the Unfinished Symphony.

He passed down a dim corridor floored with patterned tiles, a pale-green ground worked with a saw-toothed lattice whose true color he could not make out. Of the rooms on both sides of the corridor, one had its door open; the folding chairs stacked inside were thick with dust.

Qin Chuan'an went straight to the end of the passage and pushed through a pair of double doors into a spacious hall set with several rows of folding chairs, and there at the front, astonishingly, was a whole orchestra. He found a chair close against a pillar and sat down. He had often come to watch the orchestra rehearse; he loved music, and kept a gramophone in his own clinic. Whenever the orchestra had a concert at the Town Hall or the Lyceum Theatre, he would usually come early to watch them rehearse. He liked to hear them run a passage over and over, even a single phrase.

After listening a while he glanced at his watch. By the seventh run-through it was nine-fifty. Qin Chuan'an left the rehearsal hall, but instead of going back the way he had come to the elevator, he went up the stairs on the far side of the corridor. The meeting was to be held in the mezzanine between the third floor and the fourth.

At the far end of Tongchun Lane rose a high wall, and behind it stood the Gezhi Public School, run by the Municipal Council, though its gate opened onto the other side of the block. Tian Fei took this road every day on his way to work.

He had studied at the Gezhi school for nine years altogether, from the primary grades up through the upper forms. It was an English-style public school that took only boys, and the winter holiday was to begin today; now and then a knot of young men came out at the gate. Cold as it was, they wore padded gowns, but every one of them was neatly turned out, an Indanthrene-blue cloth gown thrown over the wadding, a round billed cap of soft felt on the head, the school's badge embroidered in yellow upon it.

Tian Fei paced up and down along the wall between the school's main gate and its side gate. Most of the passersby went with their hands clasped behind them, crowding through the intersection; seen from behind they were one great field of round felt hats and plush padded caps. In no time they were swallowed up in the crowd crossing the road.

He worked at the library, and it was he who had discovered that room behind the stacks—a hidden chamber formed of its own accord over long years, a space between two floors so large that hardly anyone outside knew it was there. This was the reserve stacks. The innermost cubicles, the row of them at the end of the corridor, held books either badly damaged or discarded because too many copies of a newer edition had come in. Not even the librarians themselves went into those cubicles; only Tian Fei looked in now and then, to rescue a few volumes from the dust-laden shelves.

A little over a month before, he had shifted aside a heap of books that would not fit on the shelves and had had to be stacked in a corner, and found there a door, its lock rusted past all use. He pried it open and discovered this fine place, thick with dust and giving off a smell of mold.

In fact Tian Fei should have arrived a few minutes earlier, since it was his job to open up. He felt in his pocket—the key was there; at the time he had not troubled to hunt for the room's key, but had simply taken off the old lock and fitted a new one. He felt again in his right pocket—the dominoes were there too.

Yi Junnian watched Ling Wen step into the elevator going down. Her instincts were always good; he ought to be more careful. Old Fang had told him the meeting was highly secret, that those coming to it had been carefully chosen, that they came from different places and, once they had joined the action group, had to break entirely with whatever work they had done before. Yi Junnian stood where he was a while longer. Around the market entrance there was no sign of anything; he turned his gaze to the other side.

Old Wei was standing on the raised sidewalk, a cigarette pack in his hand as though about to tear it open. Then he stopped, lifted his head, and stared straight ahead, as if he had suddenly seen something.

Following Wei Dafu's line of sight, Yi Junnian found Ling Wen out in the middle of the road. Plainly Wei Dafu had recognized her—his memory really was good. The two of them had indeed met once: on an urgent occasion Yi Junnian had had to send Ling Wen running to that teahouse to tell Wei Dafu the meeting-place had changed.

Wei Dafu had bought his cigarettes at the tobacconist's beside the Zhejiang Grand Theater. Crossing the road, just as he was about to tear the pack open and light one, he looked up and saw a woman—a good-looking one, he thought approvingly—no, wait. He looked harder, and made sure he was not mistaken: he had certainly seen her somewhere before. But he could not for the life of him remember where, or on what business.

This part of the market's second floor was all noodle stalls and cook-shops, and the morning trade was in full swing. Cui Wentai had meant to make do with a bowl of soybean milk and a hunk of fried flatbread, but the moment he got up here a whim took him and he found himself set on a bowl of pork-offal soup. The pig innards sold at the Fourth Avenue market were the freshest and most famous in all Shanghai, brought in every morning by wooden boat down Suzhou Creek, the baskets still steaming as they were unloaded.

He was a driver for a car-hire firm. That morning he had gone out of his way to take a job carrying a passenger to the Jinliyuan Wharf. He had reckoned the time and could get to the market exactly on schedule; once his business here was done he would go back and check the car in, and no one would be any the wiser. Doing secret work in Shanghai, one sometimes badly needed a motorcar, which was why the organization had gone to the trouble of placing him with the hire firm. It had taken no small effort to bring off, and he meant to hold on to the post.

For some reason Cui Wentai wanted that bowl of pork-offal soup badly just then—a few slices of tomato in it, a heavy dose of pepper he shook in himself, and two sesame cakes besides. A bowl of the fragrant, peppery soup, still a little too hot, went down and settled him at once. Draining the last of it, a mouthful of sesame cake still between his teeth, he checked his pocket watch: not yet nine-fifty. He rose unhurriedly and looked toward the elevator.

Five minutes to ten.

On the east side of the market ran an alley of the narrowest kind. On its right was the market's back wall; on its left a bamboo fence, from whose gaps drifted, now and again, a strange smell of spices. Behind the wall were shadowy figures, a good many of them, every one odd of feature, robed in white and wearing white caps. Lin Shi looked up at the top of the building, marking the positions of the windows and the fire escape. He glanced at his watch again and hurried across the road.

On the fourth floor of the library, by the open shelves where readers picked their own books, Lin Shi took up a position a little nearer the door. A few steps out and to the right was the staircase; at the turn where it bent downward stood a door, and behind it a corridor that led to the meeting-place.

Close on ten o'clock a motorcar drew up at a corner diagonally across from the market. A man leaned in at the window, said a few low words to those inside, and at once walked briskly away. The two men in the back seat talked on and off; they too were waiting for the moment to come.

“What's the World Hotel like?” one of them asked.

The guard-like man in the front passenger seat turned around. “The roof garden's amusing—all sorts of games at night. If Captain You cares to enjoy himself, on the Municipal Police's own ground, he need only say the word. That hotel might as well be run by our station; even the tea-boys report to us on schedule.”

In the back seat, Detective Yao of the Central Police Station did not like his subordinates talking too much, but he only went on evenly: “The rooms aren't bad. What do you say—for the New Year we book Captain You a room, let him soak in the bath and play a few hands of cards?”

You Tianxiao shook his head and glanced at the building opposite. “If a man were standing in the roof garden of the World Hotel, and anything stirred at the mouth of the market, he'd take it all in at a glance.”

“The captain is too cautious,” Detective Yao laughed. “When the Municipal Police make an arrest in the Settlement, they could pack the rooftops with people—what could that lot do about it?”

Though You Tianxiao held the rank of captain of the detective squad in the Judge Advocate's office of the Longhua Garrison Command, he had never had dealings with the Settlement's Municipal Police. The foreigners in the force, from the commissioner down to the inspectors, had long looked down on the Longhua detective squad, who barged about the Chinese-administered districts as they pleased; when the squad's men worked inside the Settlement, a single slip could see them hauled into a station and locked up for a few days. But the higher-ups were on good terms now. The Nationalists had stopped bawling for the overthrow of imperialism, agreements had been signed on dealing with the Communists, sharing intelligence, and extraditing prisoners—and so the men who did the actual work had naturally grown cordial. You Tianxiao was well acquainted with several of the Chinese detectives in the International Settlement's police, and his friendship with Detective Yao was closer still.

“This China Merchants embezzlement case—did that man Du in the Settlement have a hand in it or not?” You Tianxiao changed the subject. He meant the great scandal of the previous autumn and winter, the one that had become common knowledge.

“Li Guojie—the man's a prize sucker. The whole thing was a set-up from the start. They say his granduncle had something going with the Empress Dowager Cixi, and when Grand Secretary Li heard of it he was so frightened he couldn't sleep for nights on end, and in the end decided to make the good-for-nothing younger brother swallow a packet of poison and be done with it.”

Detective Yao always talked this way, like a game of Chinese checkers, a piece here, a piece there.

“This much is plain: Chen Fumu took the money, hung up his seal of office, and ran. The man had made his preparations well in advance. What's not known is whether the tycoon Du was the one who started it all.”

“He's said to have had a hand in it.” Even the loose-mouthed Detective Yao grew a little careful at the mention of the man Du. “The Settlement papers reacted so fast—whoever staged it had no ordinary reach. The story goes that Li Guojie had the Anhui Hatchet Gang assassinate the general manager of China Merchants and swapped out several of the captains, and so laid a hand on the tiger's backside. Du went up to Mount Lu himself to pour out his grievances to the Generalissimo—”

Someone came hurrying across the road to the car. Seeing who it was, You Tianxiao pushed the door open at once and got out, heard the man's report, and turned to Detective Yao, who had climbed out after him. “That subordinate of yours—the one you didn't catch this morning—he's going to spoil things after all, just as I said.”

“What's happened?”

You Tianxiao half wanted to curse, but it was no one else's fault. A police station was a place where fish and dragons mingled from the first; if blame was to be laid, it could only be laid on the muddle of his own side's intelligence work. By the time he had gone to the station's Political Branch and squared things with their deputy commissioner, and the men and their assignments had all been handed out, word came that there was a traitor in the station—and that he was among the very officers taking part in the operation. But why, instead of fleeing for his life, had the man come running here? To carry a warning? He truly cared nothing for his own life.

Around ten o'clock the people coming to the meeting entered the room one after another. A long table stood in the middle, its green baize cloth marked with grease stains and holes burned by cigarettes. Each of them drew a few dominoes from a pocket and set them on the table.

Yi Junnian stood at the table and squared up the dominoes the others had laid down at random, glanced at the tiles, and said: “Not everyone's here yet—” He raised his head and studied the people in the room one by one. Besides Ling Wen, Wei Dafu, and Tian Fei, there were seven other strangers, but Old Fang was nowhere to be seen. Old Fang had called them all to this urgent meeting; why had he not appeared himself? A sudden unease took Yi Junnian, a feeling that something might go wrong today.

He looked at his watch again: a quarter past ten already. Wei Dafu said abruptly: “Whatever it is, out with it—let's get on with the meeting and break up the moment it's done.”

Another of You Tianxiao's men came with word: they had already moved inside the market. A man who did not care whether he lived could get in anywhere. First he had ingratiated himself with the station colleagues who knew nothing of the matter and slipped through the cordon set at the market's side door. Unable to get into the passenger elevator, he had forced his way in and gone up to the third floor by the freight elevator the market used for its cold store. Blocked on the third floor, he was now raising an uproar in the rehearsal hall, had wounded a plainclothes man of the detective squad, and had sent a whole crowd of musicians fleeing this way and that through the building; then he had fallen back to the freight elevator and gone up to the fourth.

You Tianxiao lit a cigarette, then remembered himself and handed one to Detective Yao as well. He took a few draws and threw the half-smoked cigarette on the ground. “We can't wait for them to hold their meeting. Just take them.”

Two muffled reports sounded far off down the corridor, and everyone in the mezzanine room froze. Yi Junnian darted to the door, listened, then opened it: the passage was quiet, and the door to the stairwell was still shut. He turned back, shook his head at them all, and laid a finger to his lips. Everyone fell silent and watched him.

Yi Junnian shot Wei Dafu a look and went back to the table.

But once again, just as he was about to speak—the sound came from the ceiling. Now everyone was sure it was gunfire; many people were screaming, and above the floorboards came the sound of feet scattering in flight, and then, outside the window—when someone had come in earlier and disliked the damp, moldy smell of the room, they had opened the window.

There was a clang: first a steel casement fell from the fourth floor, and then a man, who struck the ground with a muffled thud. Tian Fei rushed to the window and craned his head out to look. Someone had snapped the hinges and fallen from the fourth floor together with the window, man and casement at once.

Had the man chosen to jump from here in order to raise the alarm? There was no time to think. Yi Junnian said in a lowered voice: “Go, quickly—out the back door!”

The back door opened onto another corridor that led to the stairs.

“Remember!” Yi Junnian warned them again. “Downstairs, don't rush straight out into the street—get into the market crowd first.”

Wei Dafu was first out the door. He ran down the corridor, shouldered open the fire door, and bounded down the stairs, several of the others from the meeting behind him. Before the rest could reach the stairhead, the patrolmen pouring in from the far end of the corridor loosed a volley their way; Lin Shi had only just pushed the fire door open when a bullet struck him in the leg.

The door to the stairwell was sealed off. Yi Junnian led them back toward the front corridor, the way they had come in—but the door at its end stood wide open, and in the doorway stood several patrolmen with guns leveled.

Yi Junnian went back into the room and sat down before the pai gow tiles. A pair of dice had appeared on the table; he picked them up, put them in his pocket, steadied himself, and was just about to speak when the door was slammed open.

“Ho—quite a crowd. And what are you all doing hiding in here?”

You Tianxiao strode into the room, went straight to the long table, and clapped his hands. The patrolmen rushed in, each with a rifle held ready, and ringed the people in the room about. A few plainclothesmen lounged idly by the door—men of the Longhua detective squad, whom You Tianxiao had brought himself. He shot them a glance, as though not quite satisfied with their showing.

Yi Junnian looked coldly at this swaggering fellow, then turned his eyes to the tabletop and said, all at once with a smile: “Such a show of force—and we were only gambling for money.”

“Gambling for money?” You Tianxiao came up to Yi Junnian, drew a pair of dice from his pocket, aligned the two of them at six and six, and set them down side by side beside the pai gow tiles on the table. “Come along with us. We'll play somewhere else.”

At the sight of You Tianxiao producing a pair of dice, they were all stunned. Something lurched in Yi Junnian: this was the agreed sign for making contact—the man sent down from above to pass on the assignment would produce a pair of dice—but how could this man know of it?

“Take the lot of them!” You Tianxiao ordered.

Cui Wentai had been running behind Wei Dafu earlier, but he had gone down only one flight when, in the turning, the man who had been clamoring to get the meeting started was already gone, and he could only turn right into a corridor. He had lost his bearings and only knew to run for all he was worth. Behind one door he saw an elevator and dashed in—but came out into the ground-floor cold store; it had been the freight elevator after all. He snatched up a piece of sacking to drape over his shoulder and hoisted a side of pork onto it.

A black police van from the station stood outside the door, and a knot of patrolmen watched the exit. Cui Wentai buried his face beneath the raw pork and ran out of the market in the press of the crowd.

The man who had jumped lay curled in the middle of the road. The patrolmen had roped off a ring around him; one went over with a camera to take photographs, another crouched at his side to see whether he had stopped breathing. Across the road a crowd of onlookers had gathered; the patrolmen went to drive them off, but the crowd would not disperse—this city held far too many people of a prying, meddlesome turn. Cui Wentai did not dare look closely; he turned and ran for the intersection.

He had just rounded the corner when another police van came straight at him, and he ducked hastily into a lane—only to have a hand clap him on the back. His heart gave a jolt; before he could turn his head he was dragged into the shadows.

“Old Fang!” Cui Wentai came round from his fright.

“Where are the others?”

“Scattered, all of them!” Cui Wentai panted.

Old Fang took stock of the situation on the road, where some patrolmen were beginning to seal off the intersection. “This lane runs through to the road behind—we go separately!” He put on the hat in his hand, slipped out of the lane, edged off into the distance with the scattering crowd, and in a moment was gone.

Cui Wentai then ran off deep into the lane; he had to circle back to fetch his car. Reaching the far end, it struck him all at once: surely Old Fang wouldn't think he'd made off with a side of pork in the confusion?
