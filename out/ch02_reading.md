## Chapter 2

It was before the outbreak of the Pacific War. Shanghai had fallen early, yet the foreign concessions still kept their peculiar standing. Order within them was then maintained by British, American, French and Italian garrisons; and though each of these had its own sector, off duty, on their days of leave, one met the men easily enough in the bars and dance halls — where, given the state of the war abroad and the politics of it, clashes and brawls were forever breaking out.

I remember it was the start of the summer of 1939, one o'clock at night, when I came out from a friend's place. The street was already very still, not a passer-by in sight; but as I was crossing the road, someone at the corner called out to me:

"Excuse me, sir."

It was an American officer, and he seemed scarcely able to walk.

"Yes?" I stopped.

"Could you call me a cab?"

All at once I caught sight of the streak of blood on his calf, and started:

"You're hurt?"

"I am." And with that he leaned against the wall.

"Wait here, then." I hurried off to the Venus dance hall nearby, meaning to telephone from inside; but American and Italian soldiers were quarrelling and making an uproar in there, and armed police were massed at the doors within and without, so that I could not get in and had to look elsewhere. The shops along the street were mostly shut, and there was nowhere else with a telephone; in the end I ran all the way to a cab-stand and brought a car back to where the American officer was waiting for me.

As I helped him into the car he shook my hand with great gratitude; and moved half by sympathy and half by curiosity, I said:

"Shall I come with you to the hospital?"

"If it is not putting you to too much trouble."

So I got in beside him, and said:

"To Renji Hospital?"

"No," he told the driver: "to Bubbling Well Road, Medhurst Road."

It passed for Chinese, but was not pure enough, so I said it over again for him; yet privately I wondered — could there be a hospital in there too?

I did not ask, though, for a more curious question was leaping in my mind, and I said:

"Was it at the Venus you were hurt?"

"It was," he said. "One of our own party's pistols went off among us."

"Did no one come out with you?"

"No," he said. "We are already too short of hands."

"Then no one even knows you're hurt?"

"At the time I took it for a slight wound myself — who would have thought it none too slight."

His pain seemed to be mounting; I let down the little folding seat in front so that he could rest his leg.

When we reached Bubbling Well Road he directed the driver to stop before a large apartment house and had me help him down. I paid the fare and went with him into the building, into the lift, and he directed it to stop at the third floor. I supposed this must be his home; but stepping out of the lift, at a doorway, as he turned the key, I saw the brass plate: "Dr. Philip, Specialist in Surgery and Neurology."

He brought me inside and switched on the lights: a spacious, spotless surgeon's clinic, the outer room a waiting room, but not a soul within. We went in, and just as I was about to put my question, he said:

"Now I mean to perform this operation myself. Can you lend me a hand?" — smiling in a way that ill became a wounded man.

"You think I can help you?"

"So long as you're willing." He sat down in a chair, cigarette in hand, and passed me one, then went on: "You can stay out tonight?"

"Of course." I laid the cigarette on the table and did not light it.

"Truly? Then I won't send for Dr. Philip."

"You think me equal to it?" I said.

"Naturally I'm only asking you to assist." He laughed: "I'm a very capable surgeon, you know." He drew on his cigarette and added: "You're not smoking?"

"I'd rather do something for you first."

"You've no wife?"

"I'm a confirmed bachelor."

"Splendid — then we're comrades." With that he rose and led me further in, to an operating room of matchless cleanliness. He had me help him off with his uniform and into a white gown that hung on the wall, then bade me put one on too; we washed our hands together, and soaked them again in disinfectant. He had me plug in the sterilizing stove, while he himself went over the surgical instruments in the glass cabinet and passed them to me to sterilize. I watched him lay out the drugs in order on a silver tray beside the operating couch; then he directed me to wash my hands once more in the disinfectant, to set sterile gauze on another silver tray, to lift the instruments from the sterilizer with forceps and lay them on the gauze, and at last to carry the tray over.

By then he had taken off his shoes and socks, swabbed the bullet wound with spirit and cauterized its edges with iodine, and was beginning to inject the anaesthetic.

Blood ran from the wound; he had me bring the table lamp and train its light on it, and after examining it a while, said:

"Not bad."

"Well?"

"The bullet went in on a slant, not deep."

"It's still inside?"

"I believe so."

Then I watched him work with knife and forceps and gauze, and in about a quarter of an hour he drew the bullet out. He had me set the lamp back; I watched him spread ointment on a dressing, and at last he began to bandage.

The thing was done at last. He rested on the operating couch and had me sterilize and put away the instruments and return the drugs and gauze to their places, and said:

"I'm deeply grateful to you. When Dr. Philip comes tomorrow, we can let him know nothing of this."

About twenty minutes later, when I had cleared everything away, I took the cigarette he had given me and sat down on the sofa to smoke it. I said:

"So you're an officer and a surgeon both."

"That is what's called a medical officer." He sat up, began to smoke, and with a look of contentment said: "Good friend — now we can talk."

—

This was the beginning of my friendship with Stephen.
