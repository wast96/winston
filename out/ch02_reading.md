## 2

A year later, Lin Nansheng had all but given up the work of turning the enemy, and put more of his energy into gathering and analyzing intelligence. The concession had always been a place where dragons and fish mingled; almost every intelligence service in the world kept an office there, along with countless informers of the police stations and the ears and eyes of the gangs, and after Japan joined the Axis these people seemed busier than ever. Sometimes, looking down from his office window, Lin Nansheng felt that every hurrying figure below carried some errand of its own.

Lin Nansheng's public identity now was that of a business assistant at the Huaxing Trading Company. This firm, which exported silk and tea, was in fact the Juntong's intelligence relay station in Shanghai. For it Gu Shenyan had rented the whole top floor of the Xianghu Building, in the busiest stretch of Nanjing Road. This was the commercial heart of Shanghai, and the intelligence clearinghouse of the western Pacific. Every day, information of every kind flew in like snow through its many channels, and, once sifted, analyzed, and sorted, flew out again like snow.

Lin Nansheng was run nearly off his feet. Yet however busy he was, there was one thing he never forgot each week: to go to a bar called the Alise and see a man who was half Russian by blood.

The man was the bartender there, and also a Communist agent left behind in Shanghai. Sitting at the bar, Lin Nansheng drank the cocktails he mixed, but came still more for the intelligence that might be traded. When Gu Shenyan had given him this task he had said: intelligence work is you within me and me within you. And he had reminded Lin Nansheng too that in the world of intelligence there are no permanent enemies, and still less any permanent friends.

And yet, often enough, drinking those mixed drinks whose names he could not have told, Lin Nansheng found that between himself and the bartender across the counter an understanding had grown. That night, breaking his own rule, the bartender stood him a vodka, then turned to look at one corner of the bar and said, Tomorrow night the man who takes my place will be sitting there, a Bloody Mary on the table before him.

Lin Nansheng said, And you?

It is time I went, the bartender said. I have stayed too long in one place.

The next evening, when Lin Nansheng came to the bar again, he found that the one making contact with him was, of all people, Zhu Yizhen. In the nearly six years since he had last seen her, her greatest change was the head of hair — once bobbed like a child's, now permed into deep waves.

In those days Lin Nansheng had still been an English teaching assistant at the University of Shanghai, and Zhu Yizhen's first love. Their affair, teacher and student, had been hidden from the eyes of the whole school, but not from Zhu Yizhen's mother. One morning she burst into the principal's office and said that for such a thing to happen at a mission school was a disgrace before God. Before she left, she gave the young principal two choices: dismiss the indecent English assistant, or the next day she would bring in the newspapermen.

On the eve of his leaving the school, Lin Nansheng waited until daybreak in the little chapel behind the sports ground. He sat in the cramped, pitch-dark prayer stall where they had trysted times without number. There they had embraced, kissed, and made love, under the very eye of God. Lin Nansheng remembered her saying: once I am yours, I am yours for life. But that night Zhu Yizhen did not come. Her mother had shut her up at home, where she knelt before her father's memorial portrait and repented until dawn.

When the two of them had left the bar, Zhu Yizhen stood in the street and said, If you ask for someone else, I can put it to my superiors.

Lin Nansheng said mildly, I only fear this was your superiors' meaning from the start.

Zhu Yizhen was taken aback, and looked down at the tips of her shoes.

Chongqing is under air raids every day now, Lin Nansheng said. We need every movement of the Japanese air force.

And you should know what we need. With that Zhu Yizhen put out a hand and hailed a rickshaw. She did not look at Lin Nansheng again, but had the puller take her winding through several streets before she changed to another cab and went home. Ji Zhongyuan was sitting under the lamp, cutting a seal. He had once been a seal-engraver at Duoyunxuan; now he kept a scroll-mounting shop in an out-of-the-way corner of Fuyou Road, its signboard advertising that he also cut seals.

This was their home, and also their intelligence station, for the sending and receiving of reports.

Zhu Yizhen went into the inner room, changed into a sweater, and came out to sit at the edge of Ji Zhongyuan's table; she watched until he raised his head, and only then said, So this is why you had me take the bartender's place.

Ji Zhongyuan nodded.

Zhu Yizhen glanced at the locked drawer of the dressing table and said, You have been reading my diary.

Your photograph album too, Ji Zhongyuan said calmly. You should not have kept these things.

I did not keep them for you to pry into.

I need to know you, Ji Zhongyuan said. We are husband and wife.

Zhu Yizhen gave a cold laugh and said, So you would have me rekindle an old flame with a Juntong agent?

The light in Ji Zhongyuan's eyes began to dim. I know only that this man matters greatly to us, he said.

And I?

You are an intelligence agent, Ji Zhongyuan said. You must understand: intelligence comes before all else.

After a long silence Zhu Yizhen said, I request that the present situation be reported to our superiors.

That is your right, Ji Zhongyuan said. But until an answer comes down from above, you will obey my orders. After a long moment he pointed at the dressing-table drawer and added, Those diaries — better dealt with sooner than late.

Lin Nansheng's second meeting with Zhu Yizhen was at the DDS Café.

The place was Lin Nansheng's choosing; he remembered that Zhu Yizhen liked the hot chocolate here. But this time she ordered a black coffee, no sugar.

Your taste has changed, Lin Nansheng said with a smile.

Zhu Yizhen seemed not to hear. She laid a copy of The Young Companion pictorial on the table and said, These are the garrison dispositions of the Japanese Third Air Division at Hankou; you should find them of use.

Lin Nansheng likewise seemed not to hear. Looking at the mark a ring had left on her fourth finger, he said, Why did you take it off? Zhu Yizhen curled her hand shut and said, You ought to give me something in return, no?

Are the two of you truly husband and wife? Lin Nansheng shook his head as though it were nothing and said, I do not believe you would marry a seal-engraver who keeps a mounting shop. As he spoke, seeing Zhu Yizhen about to rise, he seized that hand of hers.

Let go, Zhu Yizhen said.

He is your superior. Lin Nansheng put away the smile and, fixing his eyes on hers, said, Your assignment is not only to trade intelligence. Zhu Yizhen said, Please let go of my hand.

Lin Nansheng loosened his grip by degrees, leaned back in his chair, and said in earnest, Zhenzhen, this trade is no work for a woman.

Zhu Yizhen was taken aback, and said, It is you who have no right to this trade; you have broken the rules between us.

With that she rose and left the café without looking back.

This time Zhu Yizhen did not go roundabout, but returned straight to the mounting shop on Fuyou Road. The moment she was in the inner room she said to Ji Zhongyuan, in a fury, Damn it — he has followed me, and now he has your measure too.

That was to be expected, Ji Zhongyuan said, smiling. Have we not followed and looked into him ourselves?

That is not the same, Zhu Yizhen said. He will bring danger down on us.

And we can bring danger down on him just the same. Still smiling, Ji Zhongyuan said with certainty, He understands as much.

You put rather too much faith in a Nationalist Juntong agent. Zhu Yizhen's tone turned hard. Do not forget the New Fourth Army Incident, she said.

Ji Zhongyuan sat down on a chair, looked up into Zhu Yizhen's face a good while, and said suddenly, Yizhen — the two of you were lovers once; you loved each other.

Zhu Yizhen started, but said at once, That is past.

And now? Do you trust me? Having said it, Ji Zhongyuan went on watching her without stirring, watched until, little by little, she lowered her eyes and said not another word.

Early that morning Ji Zhongyuan took out a folding fan of mottled bamboo and gave it to Zhu Yizhen, sending her to carry it to Zhenru Temple outside the city; it was already afternoon when she returned. Carrying a box of the temple's vegetarian pan-fried buns, Zhu Yizhen had gone only a little way along Fuyou Road when she heard an explosion from the direction of the mounting shop. Her heart leapt into her throat. When the second blast sounded, she all but broke into a run toward home.

Zhu Yizhen was seized head-on in someone's arms. The man wore a long gown and a felt hat, and without a word of explanation bundled her into a rickshaw waiting at the roadside; only then did she make out, beneath the brim of the hat, Lin Nansheng's face. She said, Let me down.

Lin Nansheng seemed not to hear. He said to the puller, Go, quickly.

Let go of me. Zhu Yizhen went on struggling, went on demanding to be let down, until Lin Nansheng drew a pistol and pressed it to her waist; then all at once her eyes went round and she glared at him. Both their breaths came a little fast, breaking against each other's faces.

After a good while Lin Nansheng drew the pistol back and said in her ear, You must keep calm.

But Zhu Yizhen could not grow calm; again and again there rose before her eyes the grenade hidden at home. She remembered that it was a standard Japanese Type 97 infantry grenade. When Ji Zhongyuan had set it into the hidden compartment where the wireless transmitter was kept, he had said that its force was enough to blow the whole room apart. He had said, too, that it was meant for himself.

Lin Nansheng kept his arm tight around Zhu Yizhen's shoulders all the way until they were inside his flat and the door was shut, and only then let go. He told her that it was not only the mounting shop on Fuyou Road that had been struck that day, but the rice dealer's at Baxianqiao, the teahouse at Shiliupu, the pawnshop at Xiaodongmen — bombed, or else raked with gunfire. These places must all have been your contact points, he said. And at the last he said, The trouble is with your leadership.

Zhu Yizhen stood stunned a good while, then looked him straight in the eye and said, Then how did you come to be there?

I got word that No. 76 had thrown a cordon over Fuyou Road, and hurried to warn you, Lin Nansheng said. It was lucky you were not inside.

Zhu Yizhen said not another word. She sat on in the sofa until the sky had gone black, then rose all at once and made for the door. Lin Nansheng caught her back and asked, Where are you going? She would not answer. He pressed her down into the sofa and said again, Right now there is nowhere you can go. Zhu Yizhen clenched her teeth and strained with all her strength to rise. Lin Nansheng bore down on her the harder and said, You are going to your death — they have the sack held open, waiting for you.

Then let me go to my death. Zhu Yizhen suddenly broke out in a scream.
