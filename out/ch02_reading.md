## The Rain-Soaked Buddha

Jūzō left the pass once the sun was high, following the road down first to Tarao in Shigaraki of Kōga, and from there threading the woodcutters' paths, meaning to come out at Kasagi.

From Kasagi, if you follow the current of the Kizu, Nara is close. The road from Tarao to Dōsenbō felt like walking through a cavern of green.

Brush covered the mountainside and let no sun through, and perhaps because the trees bore so many kinds of nut and berry, small creatures scurried out from under Jūzō's feet almost every tenth step along the mossed path.

One of them, a squirrel, flashed the fine gloss of its back and stopped short a few paces ahead of Jūzō, then all at once turned to face him.

"Master Jūzō."

It truly seemed the squirrel had called out. Jūzō swept his eyes over the ground around him. There was no one. A patch of blue sky showed through the thick of the branches, pouring a band of green light onto the moss where he stood.

"Ho-ho-ho. Master Jūzō."

The squirrel was already gone. To his right lay a wet shelf of rock, a spring welling up before it. Out of the rank smell of the dokudami crowding its rim, a bright splash of color rose into view.

"What—it's you."

"Oh. 'It's you'—that is a hard way to put it."

"If you mean to play tricks, spare me."

"I have played no trick at all. I have only been here at the edge of the spring, waiting all this while for you to come. Ha—it is you who are the strange one, Master Jūzō, letting a mere squirrel lead you off."

The woman who had stepped into Jūzō's path, trailing the smell of dokudami, wore a showy small-sleeved robe in the latest style; but she had hitched it short at the hem with a studied carelessness, and stood there light as a sparrow dropped down to peck at seed, her small feet set easily apart on the moss.

"Well now. I hardly knew you. You've grown handsome."

She said nothing, only laughed, a glitter in the one part of her face that shone like an animal's—her eyes.

"And where are you bound, Mistress Kisaru?"

"The capital."

"After the Kaza fellow, then."

"Hm-hm."

"Find him, and if he has turned traitor, run him through. A weighty errand indeed."

"Who told you that?"

"Your father told me."

Jūzō said it, and laughed as if the whole thing amused him.

"And you, Master Jūzō—where are you bound?"

"Who's to say."

"Nara, I'd guess?"

"Ho. And who told you that?"

"My father. While I look for Gohei, he said, I was to help you as well. So—we are companions. You and I, Master Jūzō."

"A nuisance."

"Why?"

"I am a man. Traveling paired with a woman won't do."

"Because Kisaru is a woman, you'd say her craft falls short?"

"You say frightening things. That skill of yours just now was enough to blind me. Only—a woman is a woman, and I am a man, and there are times a man wants to take her."

"Then do not hold back. You may have me, if it is you, Master Jūzō."

"That's steep. Perhaps I'll take you now, then. I've less patience for such things than you'd think."

"Tch."

She laughed, and sprang to a rock four or five ken off.

Jūzō was laughing too. It was nothing but banter, of course; yet in a corner of his mind he found himself thinking of Gohei and Kisaru.

(Betrothed or not, perhaps nothing ever passed between her and Gohei. It may be that Jirōzaemon reproached Gohei as though something had, and so made him his son-in-law by force. Gohei, loath to marry into a landless gōshi's house, seized the chance of a errand to the capital and vanished. Most likely that was the way of it. …And by the look of her, past all doubt, she is a maiden still.)

Kisaru leapt down from the rock and set off through the trees, running like a squirrel.

Jūzō quickened his own pace without meaning to. But for the short half-hour of the descent to Kasagi, he lost sight of her altogether.

Where the woods gave out and the slope turned steep, below him he saw the white riverbed of the Kizu, carrying a single thread of deep blue current westward.

Standing in the shade of an oak at the top of the bluff to catch his breath, Jūzō sensed all at once that Kisaru was hiding in the tree above him.

(She means to spring some trick on me.)

Jūzō caught himself in the mood of waiting for it, and watched his own mind with a flicker of amusement.

(Do I like this woman, I wonder?)

Just then a robe came fluttering down and Jūzō stepped aside. Into the space he had cleared dropped Kisaru's body next.

"No! Don't dodge!"

Almost naked, she rode up onto Jūzō's shoulders as if for a piggyback and covered his eyes with both palms.

"From here I go to the capital."

"Mm."

"One day I will give this body to you, Master Jūzō."

She said it, her breath coming quick.

"And Gohei?"

"The truth is, I hate him. Father wants him for a son-in-law, so he put me up to it."

"Then why go to the capital to look for Gohei?"

"That, even I do not understand."

"Enough."

Prying her fingers from his eyes one by one, Jūzō said:

"A woman's fickleness is a nuisance to a man. Your first task, and single-minded, is to find Kazama Gohei. Even a rappa's daughter must keep her honor. In your bad points, it seems, you take after your father."

Keeping his eyes deliberately shut for her sake, Jūzō took the curve of her waist in his two hands and set the small, tautly muscled body down on the ground. The firm flesh, strange and springing, seemed to smoke and melt away in his palms, and a sudden giddiness rose behind his eyes.

(One day I will have this woman.)

Without looking at her—rather turning over the movements of his own heart with a keen, curious interest—Jūzō set his back to her and started his feet down the slope.

"Master Jūzō."

"Dally with you, and the day will be gone."

Jūzō's feet, all but falling, sped their pace toward the bed of the Kizu below.

Dusk had not quite faded outside when Jūzō found an inn at Aburazaka and took a room.

"Won't you have a woman?"

The innkeeper spoke from outside the threshold, no doubt taking the hint from his guest's flashy turnout. Jūzō had gone to some trouble over it: he had let his hair grow and swept it up, bound it back richly with a white cord, and wore a moss-green Iga hakama with the hems caught by little gold clasps.

Jūzō sat a while thinking, working the clasps loose, and at last said:

"Send one in. But bring a little sake first."

On entering another domain, a ninja's custom was to buy a woman before anything else. To learn the local accent, of course, and to sound out how things stood, was the nominal purpose—but.

Presently the woman came in.

She was on the large side, and could not have been called a beauty. But she had narrow, clean eyes and full cheeks. She seemed a very silent sort: in a far corner of the room she sat, hands folded on her thick knees, gazing at the weave of the matting.

Jūzō picked back and forth between two dishes—wild yam and dried fish—and tipped the flask, and after a while opened his mouth.

"Come nearer, here."

"…………"

"Sit off there like an idol on its stand, and the sake goes flat."

"…………"

Without a word she reached for the flask and made to pour.

"No. You'll take some too."

Jūzō took the flask from her and passed her a cup in its place. She accepted it obediently.

Pouring, Jūzō said:

"What is your name?"

"They call me Kohagi."

"A good name. Nara-born, I take it?"

"Yes."

"Born here as well?"

"…yes."

"Ha—a silent one. This being the old Buddha-capital, its women too must take after the Buddha. Tonight, then, let us make an offering of sake to the Buddha."

For the first time she gave a faint smile. When she smiled, the flesh about her eyes clouded like a spring half-moon, and her face turned unexpectedly beautiful.

Each time he filled it, she drained the cup at a breath. Jūzō refilled it steadily. Before long the line of her knee gave way, and a white calf caught his eye.

"You're drunk."

She took it with a smile at her eyes.

"But you—you have not taken a single drop all this while,"

she said, artlessly.

The woman was laughing.

Inwardly Jūzō felt a stab of alarm. He had never had a taste for drink, and a ninja shuns the smell of it from the start—in the dark, the scent will betray his presence. He had only feigned the cups with skill, and never before had anyone marked his hand at it.

"It's only that I like to make others an offering of sake."

Telling himself it was a clumsy thing to have said, he stretched his legs and rose for the bedchamber.

Two sets of bedding were laid in the next room. Jūzō shed his clothes, set his sword by the pillow, and stretched his legs into the bed with its strong smell of dye.

The woman came in with a hand-candle. It shook because her own step was unsteady. Even so she folded Jūzō's scattered clothes neatly, then with a sigh put out the light.

In the dark there was only the sound of silk sliding. It had a crisp, clipped cadence. Listening to the sound alone, the woman standing in the dark seemed another person entirely from the somewhat heavy, slow-witted one of a moment before.

She brought her fingers together in a slight bow at Jūzō's pillow, and slipped into the bedding. Then, quietly, she made her body rigid.

In Jūzō's breast a certain question toward this woman had begun to bud.

Staring into the dark at the ceiling, Jūzō had begun to think that the woman lying beside him was no common courtesan.

Perhaps a rappa, he thought.

But in Iga he had never seen a face like hers.

(A Kōga woman, then—)

If so, why she had come near him, he could not begin to reckon.

With the same probing fingers Jūzō drew her suddenly to him and searched beneath the red silk clinging to her lower body. She seemed to flinch. A hard tension ran through her, and she drew her knees close.

Jūzō explored on, quietly, and reached the depth of her. Even in the dark he could tell how desperately she was steadying a breath that wanted to break. At last, as though her strength had given out, she opened her body to him. From then on she was only a body.

She gave a small cry and clung to Jūzō like another woman altogether.

Jūzō gave the faintest laugh.

(A common courtesan after all. And new to the trade at that—not many days in it…)

Even thinking so, Jūzō went on feeling an enemy moving within her.

She threw herself into it wildly. Even if she were a Kōga agent sent against Jūzō with some purpose, in this one instant she was no more than a white body.

Presently she slept.

Then Jūzō thought he had heard some small sound deep in his own mind. It spread in ragged waves, and in the woman's sleeping breath he sensed a pity that seemed to soak all the way through. What kind of pity it was he himself could not have said; but of all that had stirred in his heart since the evening began, he knew this one thing came nearest to the truth.

The Hour of the Ox was drawing near.

Jūzō slid quietly from the bed and put himself in order in the middle of the room.

The woman breathed the small breaths of sleep.

Breathing so, she had softly opened her eyes and was watching Jūzō's movements.

Jūzō felt that faint gaze on his back.

(A Kōga woman after all.)

Taking no precaution whatever, Jūzō slid the door open with a sound.

Thinking herself found out, perhaps, the woman gave a start under the bedding.

Fifteen chō east of Aburazaka, out toward the old Kyōgoku quarter of Nara, the houses thin away. Climbing to the top of Kumoizaka and coming out before the Muryō-in, Jūzō felt a small shock. Ahead of him lay the wood of the Tamukeyama Hachiman, with the Kasuga and Wakakusa hills crouched behind. In the gathering night-mist of rain, a faint light that flickered off toward the Nigatsu-dō served as its halo, and against it a strange shape filled the dark between earth and sky. It was the Great Buddha.

But there was no hall over it.

Straining his eyes, he saw the huge Vairocana set upon the ground in a ruin so hideous that surely no one on earth had ever conceived a form so monstrous. The whole body was seared to a molten wreck, studded with countless swellings and hollows, and the head no longer held any shape at all. That the one who had heaped such shame on this Buddha was Matsunaga Danjō Hisahide, Jūzō had heard tell.

From the spring to the autumn of Eiroku 10, Hisahide fought the Miyoshi Triumvirate at Nara. Hisahide held the Kaidan-in; the Miyoshi forces made a fortress of the Great Buddha's cloister. The fighting burned the Hannya-ji, the Monju-dō, the Busshō-dō, the Myōkō-in, the Kannon-in, the Jukai-dō, the North Water Gate and the South Water Gate, the Senju-dō, and at last the Kaidan-in itself; and on the tenth day of the tenth month Hisahide set fire to the Great Buddha Hall where the enemy was entrenched.

"At the Hour of the Snake, the Great Buddha Hall burned to the ground.—Fierce flames filled the heavens, for all the world like thunder and lightning. The image of Shakya, the Great Buddha, was melted quite away as into hot water. It is beyond all words; pitiful, pitiful, a thing past the reach of thought."

So a monk of the Tamon-in set it down. Twenty years had passed since then. Still the restoring was not done, and the Buddha went on baring this wretched countenance to the light of day.

Gazing at the giant's carcass, sitting rigid in the dark, Jūzō thought suddenly of the strangeness in the spirit of that merchant, Imai Sōkyū, who had chosen the front of this corpse to arrange a plot to murder Hideyoshi, the ruler of the age.

Soundlessly the rain had begun to wet the field at the foot of Tamukeyama.

Wetting his hems in the grass, Jūzō walked straight to the Buddha's pedestal and sat. Looking up, he saw white threads of rain dripping without pause from the four fingers of the Buddha's left hand, thrust forward in a mudra.

Then he sensed black shapes moving around the Buddha, walking softly, whispering. One seemed to have come as far as the fingers: Jūzō heard the legs of the rain spatter loudly against a raised umbrella.

"Who's there?"

As he spoke, Jūzō shifted his place. His left hand had already loosened the sword in its scabbard.

The shape came on in silence. It entered Jūzō's field of sight, and halted.

Jūzō fixed his eyes on the white face, and let the strength ease a little from his shoulders.

(The courtesan of a while ago…)

The woman held the umbrella's handle in her left hand and stood with her eyes lowered, her feet demurely together.

Still seated, Jūzō said:

"You wanted something?"

"Your shoulders will get wet."

She came close and held the umbrella over him.

"Leave it. I am enjoying the mood of the rain here."

"Ho-ho-ho, how refined of you."

She broke suddenly into a bright peal of laughter.

"Let me go along with you. I am the one sent to meet you—Master Tsuzura Jūzō of Iga."

Jūzō rose without a word. The woman put out a white arm and raised the umbrella high. He had half guessed as much; and yet that this woman should be Imai Sōkyū's messenger was more than he had bargained for.

Walking beside her, Jūzō said:

"Are you Kōga?"

"…………"

"Sōkyū's rappa?"

"…I would not know."

The eyes that glanced up at him were laughing.

"Then why did you give yourself to me a while ago?"

"Ah. That was not the reason."

She spoke as they reached the three-forked road climbing to the Nigatsu-dō. Jūzō's feet had been carrying him idly toward the Nigatsu-dō's light, but her finger pointed the opposite way, to the wood of the Jihō-in. She passed on by the Ryūzō-in, the Hōgon-in, the Ryūshō-in, and at length took the road up the hill.

The rain still fell. A darkness like a wash of black ink lay thick over everything.

Watching her in it—her hem lightly pinched up, her step as easy as if she crossed an open field at noon—Jūzō thought her no ordinary rappa.

The drum of rain on the umbrella eased a little. Under it, the woman was laughing softly.

"Does it amuse you?"

"What amuses me is myself.—All I needed to do was play the guide. And yet I gave myself to you—that was more than was asked. I only wanted to know what sort of man the famous Tsuzura Jūzō of Iga might be."

"…………"

Jūzō, of course, took this for a lie.

Once Jūzō knew her for a rappa, there was no need to fix her nature any further. Hired by Sōkyū, she would seem to stand in the relation of an ally to him; but among rappa there had never really been such a thing as an ally. He knew more cases than he had the heart to count of one spy killing another at a paymaster's single whim.

(Will she not kill me? This woman—someday.)

Jūzō laughed without a sound.

Jūzō trusted such presentiments of his own. His trade, and his life, held together only by that trust. And with the thought, for no reason he could trace, an unaccountable desire for the woman walking beside him rose suddenly from deep in his body.

Her shoulder was there below Jūzō's eyes. The white nape gave off a scent in the rain that wetted the dark.

Another self within him made a show of quiet surprise at this sudden desire for a woman who might kill him. These several years he had been utterly bored with living; only now, as if for the first time, he remembered that. As the sharp, quickening thrill of danger came alive again in his body, it carried with it desire for the woman. Jūzō savored the crossing of the two.

"Woman—"

"Call me Kohagi, if you please."

"Is it far?"

"No."

She halted and pointed to the grove of asebi ahead.

"Beyond that shade there is a ruined cloister of Tōdai-ji. There."

"A pity. With a little more road, I might have taken you."

"What—in this rain?"

A blue desire kindled in the eyes she raised to him. Perhaps she had painted herself some strange picture, of being pressed to lie down in the rain. A strange woman, Jūzō thought. But the moment he set his foot on the stone steps thick with moss, he had already forgotten her. He had come to the foot of the gate. With a glance at the rotted gate, he passed on into the grounds.

Even by night the priests' quarters showed badly gone to ruin. Dust grated under his hand on the lattice of a screen, and told him the temple had long stood untenanted.

"Ah—not the quarters…"

The woman came after him at a trot.

"Where, then?"

"The Aizen-dō you can see over there."

Sure enough, that hall alone held a faint light among the trees of the compound.

Jūzō started forward. The woman stood at the eaves of the quarters. When he looked back, she gave a slight bow of the head. She meant, most likely, to slip off somewhere as she was. It struck Jūzō then that she might not be of Imai Sōkyū's party at all, but of his enemy's. And yet somewhere in his heart he took a pleasure in the thought. It was only his own intuition. (If that is so, then all the more, one day, I and that woman…)

Jūzō drew in his neck, alone, and—

(I'll be killed.)

An oddly buoyant smile rose to his lips.

The intuition proved right.

When he slid the door of the Aizen-dō rattling open, the two shapes inside started up and swiftly caught up their swords.

"—is it?"

"No. Tsuzura Jūzō of Iga."

"Then the man who guided you?"

"A man?"

"Just so. He was to be got up as a monk."

"My guide was a woman."

"A woman."

The two looked at each other in the light and shadow. One flashed a glance to the other, and that one seized his sword and ran from the hall—meaning, no doubt, to cut her down on sight. After the man had gone, wind blew into the hall. The candle guttered, throwing an eerie shadow into the eye-sockets of the man who remained.

He was gaunt. Past fifty as well. An old scar ran from his right temple across the cheek, as though it laid bare the very design of the man's savage life.

He watched Jūzō steadily from the depths of his dark eye-sockets. Each time the light struck his eyes, a greedy wetness lodged in them, like a damned soul in a hell-scroll. Behind him stood an altar-cabinet. In it stood a strange, sinister Aizen Myō-ō.

Standing before the cabinet, narrowing his eyes, Jūzō said:

"I am a guest. And I have named myself. Why do you not name yourself?"

"Rappa."

The man twitched his cheek where he sat. The scar cast a shadow that made him look as if he were weeping.

"You forget yourself. I am the principal here."

"I am asking your name."

"A retainer of the fallen Shibata house. Matsukura Kurando."

"Well chosen."

Jūzō laughed and sat cross-legged before the man.

"A broken bandit from about Mino, most likely. Your accent gives you away."

The man's small eyes flew open and he seized his sword. Checking him with a smile, Jūzō went on:

"'Kurando'—well pinned on. It has the look of some court rank about it. 'A rōnin of the Shibata house'—cleverly thought out too. The pathos of a man with no place in Toyotomi's world is just the thing for a part in treason."

"Have you come to pick a quarrel?"

"I have come to do a job."

Jūzō took his own sword and shoved it clattering away, off to one side.

"But with the likes of you, no job could be done."

"Give me your reason."

"You'll know it soon enough."

Jūzō drew out a sheet of tissue paper, tore a strip from the edge, and twisted it carefully into a cord to scrape the rainwater from the hollow of his ear.

The other man came back, drenched with rain.

This one was still young. He wore an overlong field-sword thrust through his belt, but on shoulders too sturdy for the rest of him sat the good-natured face you often see on a wandering country balladeer.

"H-he's dead."

He rushed in, dropped down before Matsukura Kurando, and worked his lips in a trembling shudder.

"Give me the particulars."

"There are none. Under the—the Great Buddha, run through once from behind, dead…"

"Who killed him?"

"I-I couldn't say."

The man stole a glance at Jūzō, who was scraping his ear. Matsukura Kurando too eyed Jūzō's face closely.

"This Iga man, most likely."

"It was not I."

Jūzō dropped the paper cord on his knee.

"To change the subject—Matsukura Kurando, was it? How many men do you have?"

"Twenty in the capital."

"Besides?"

"Fifty in Mino."

"And this one?"

"He is called Kumobei."

"The dead one?"

"A man I raised from a boy—Kakesu-no-Jirō. A rappa."

"What of it?"

"You too, from tonight, are under me."

"That is unfortunate. But then the job cannot be done. You will have to die."

The head of Matsukura Kurando, that had gone on talking until now, seemed to shut its mouth—and rolled forward off his shoulders.

Somehow, in Jūzō's hand, Kumobei's field-sword was already bared and gripped.

"Kumobei."

"Eeh!"

"From tonight, you follow me. I showed this man no mercy, but for the sake of finishing the work it could not be helped. From the very fact that a woman rappa fastened herself to me, the secret is already out. The blame for the leak I judged to lie with this man. Great matters cannot be carried through by a loose-tongued bandit. That great matter—did this man leak it to you?"

"Y-yes. Only to me and Kakesu-no-Jirō…"

"Jirō is luckily dead. From this moment you are to forget it. Forget it not, and I cut you down as well."

"Aah."

"No need to be afraid. The Iga have their own way—the Iga man's way—of running a job. I killed a man for the work. In Iga we do not call that inhuman, as the world does. So long as you follow my running of it, I'll not put a mark on your allotted span."

Saying it, Jūzō drew two pieces of silver from his breast and tossed them by Kumobei's knee.

Kumobei crawled to gather them, and looked up at Jūzō. Watching the corpse, Jūzō said:

"Were you and this man long acquainted?"

"N-no. Only half a year ago, at an inn near the Tō-ji in the capital, this Master Kurando said he'd make a samurai of me if I joined the band…"

"What did you do before?"

"I was a drifter of Sakai."

"With those rope-calluses on your hands—you've been a sailor too."

"As you discern; I am put to shame. I sailed on a bahan ship as far as Luzon and Korea."

"Very bold. But the work I set you will not be so bold."

"I am a coward to the root."

"That cowardice will serve. Lie low in the capital a while."

"Where in the capital?"

"Wherever you are, my messenger will find you out."

Jūzō stood.

From this hour he meant to ride through the dawn to Imai Sōkyū's mansion in Sakai.

"This Buddha here—bury him in a corner of the grounds."

For the first time Jūzō's face took on a pained look. And with that he left the corpse and Kumobei, and went out of the temple.

Outside it had already begun to lighten. Crossing Tobuhino westward, he watched the dawn sky.

The faint tiredness left somewhere in his body was perhaps a feeling near to regret—regret at why he had not killed that woman.

He had cut down not an enemy but an ally. Over this Tsuzura Jūzō, who lived by the Iga custom, felt no contradiction. In Iga the sense of friend and foe was thin from the start. They loved only their trade and their band of the trade. Split for a time into enemy and ally, that consciousness and that organization still sprang from a single source. As one proof of it, there is a recorded case in which Iga men in the pay of Takeda Katsuyori of Kai, and Iga men in the pay of Tokugawa Ieyasu of Mikawa, kept up their professional contact the whole time, deep in the mountains of Suruga. It is out of a wariness of just this, they say, that the commanders of the Warring States would not, as a rule, put their weightiest secrets into Iga hands.
