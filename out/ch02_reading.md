## Chapter 1. The Man with Four Eyebrows

Dusk. Just past dusk.

It was the busiest hour at the Dragon-Soaring Inn. Every table in the downstairs hall had guests, and the waiter Little Beijing was in a lather, his voice half hoarse from calling orders. Upstairs, all four-by-six of the twenty-four rooms were taken as well. Most of the guests were jianghu men with blades at their hips, and none of them knew why this ordinarily quiet place had come suddenly alive.

Then hooves rang out, and two horses came galloping in at the front gate. The horses in the yard shrieked and the hall broke into shouting; but the two big men in blue riding in sat their carved saddles without a flinch. A pair of silver hooks hung glinting from one saddle, and their owner was a purple-faced man with a broad beard, his eyes as bright and edged as the hooks themselves.

His gaze swept the hall and fixed on Little Beijing. "Where is he?"

"Still upstairs, sir. Room Heaven," said Little Beijing.

"And Miss Nine?" the purple-faced man asked again.

"Upstairs as well, sir," said Little Beijing. "Clinging to him."

The purple-faced man said no more. A press of his heels, a pull on the reins, and his horse shot up the stairs like an arrow.

The other rider was no slower. Half his left ear was gone, and a knife-scar cut across his face from that ear to the far corner of his mouth, so that his iron-grey skin looked more monstrous still. His horse hit the landing and he was already out of the saddle, two somersaults in the air, then a kick — bang — that flung open the door of Room Heaven by the stair-head. He was through it in the next breath, a pair of tempered-steel judge's pens in his hands.

Then he froze. There was only one person in the room — a woman.

Naked to the last inch: snow-white skin, a full bosom, long, taut legs. A woman a man's mind would go straight from to a bed — except that this one was up on the rafter. The beam sat high, and she perched squarely on it, wearing the look of a cat in heat on a hot tin roof. She was not yowling, only because her mouth had been stopped.

The purple-faced man flicked his whip. The tip snaked up and drew a knot of red silk out from between her teeth.

"Where is he?" the scarred man was already asking.

The woman on the beam fought a few breaths back. "Gone. He'd worked out who I was long before we got here."

"Which way?" the scarred man pressed.

"By the hoofbeats, north — toward Huangshi Town." She rushed on, "Get me down first. I'll ride with you."

"Nobody's holding you up there," said the scarred man coldly. "Can't you get down yourself?" He was already turning back through the air as he said it.

"I can't!" she cried, more frantic still. "That wangba sealed the pressure-point in my thigh."

But by then the two of them were out through the window. Below, another pair of horses stood ready, held on tight rein. They lit in the saddles, and the horses shot away northward like arrows.

Hearing the hooves fade, the woman on the beam went white to the lips. She beat her fist against the wood. "Wangba! Every damned one of them a wangba —" The door stood open. She looked down at her bare legs and bit her lip. "And now which wangba gets first crack at me?"

"This wangba, that's who." Little Beijing came grinning through the door, eyes narrowed on those firm white legs. Then the door swung shut.

***

Huangshi Town was a good-sized place, and this street its busiest. But the hour was late now, a hook of new moon on the flagstones, and when the two riders came up it at speed, they saw no one on it.

The scarred man reined in and looked about him. "Think he'll bed down here for the night?"

"He will," said the purple-faced man.

He was a man like any other, and every man had to sleep by night — only, everyone knew he had a certain failing where sleep was concerned.

"If he's put in somewhere," the scarred man asked, "where would it be?"

"The Yingchun Pavilion," said the purple-faced man without a moment's thought.

The Yingchun Pavilion was the house with the most beautiful women in Huangshi Town, and *he* could not sleep without a woman by him. That was the failing. But then, doesn't every man have his?

The lantern at the door of the Yingchun Pavilion still burned. Its rose-red glow was inviting any comer in to a rose-red night.

The door stood half open. A tug on the rein and a snort from the horse, and the purple-faced man rode straight in. In the courtyard a sallow, thin-faced man sat drowsing in a rattan chair.

The whip snaked around his neck. "A young man in a bright red cape — has one come by tonight?"

The whip choked off any answer; the man could only nod, again and again.

The whip loosened. "Still here?"

He gasped a breath and nodded again.

"Where?" said the purple-faced man.

"He was in the Peach Blossom Hall until just now, sir, drinking with four other gentlemen. They took turns pouring, and in the end they got him drunk."

"What four?" said the scarred man, sharpening.

"Four rough-looking men, if you ask me, sir. But mannerly enough to *him*."

"And where are they now?"

"I saw them help him back to his room. Still in there with him, as far as I know."

The purple-faced man was already wheeling his horse in through a stand of peach trees to the left. In the little grove the lamps of the Peach Blossom Hall were still burning, and inside, a wreck of cups and dishes lay on the table, three or four wine jars stood empty.

The scarred man went through the air in a somersault, cleared the doorway in a bound, and kicked open the door at the back of the hall. Then he froze.

Four men were in the room, and only four. They knelt in a stiff row facing the door; their faces, drained bloodless already, went at the sight of him a furious red. Their clothes were fine and cut to the pattern of grand men — but somebody had made a shambles of their faces.

On the first man's brow was drawn a turtle, and four characters spread across his cheeks: *I am a turtle.*

On the second's, a wangba: *I am a wangba.*

The third: *I am a live pig.*

The fourth: *I am a country cur.*

The scarred man stood in the doorway and looked; and looked again — at the drawings, at the words — and then all at once he burst out laughing, bent double at the waist, as if in all his life he had never seen anything half so funny.

The four gnashed their teeth. The hatred in their eyes said they would have leapt up and bitten out his throat, if they could have. But there they knelt, stiff as posts. They could not leap. They could not so much as stir.

The scarred man howled with laughter. "The mighty Four Heroes of Jiangdong — since when did they turn into turtles and wangbas, live pigs and country curs? Now, *there's* a strange thing."

The purple-faced man went laughing out into the yard, clapping his hands. "Roll up, roll up! Come and see the celebrated Four Heroes of Jiangdong in all their present glory — nine taels of silver for any man who steps in for a look!"

On the floor the four faces went from red to a bloodless white shot with green, and the cold sweat came off them in a rain.

"That fellow's a wangba himself," said the scarred man, still laughing, "but by heaven he's a first-rate wangba."

"Not a wasted ride, after all," said the purple-faced man.

The two of them broke off their laughter. Someone was coming in from outside, head lowered.

A girl of fourteen or fifteen at the most. Her hair was heavy with pearls and jade, her face heavy with paint; but through it all shone a childish look, both piteous and endearing.

She kept her head down. "Would you two gentlemen," she said softly, "be here for the Young Master Lu?"

The scarred man's face darkened. "How would you know that?"

She stammered. "Just now, the Young Master seemed almost dead-drunk — I was sitting beside him, and I drank two of his cups for him on the sly."

"Popular with the ladies, is he," sneered the scarred man.

The girl went scarlet. "Only, all at once he came back to himself. He said I had a kind heart, and gave me a thing to sell to you."

"What did he give you?" the purple-faced man asked at once.

"A... a sentence."

A frown. "A sentence. What sentence?"

"He said the sentence is worth no less than three hundred taels of silver, not a cash less. And he said that you two must pay first, before I may speak it." She seemed to know how absurd it was, and blushed deeper still before she was done.

But without a moment's hesitation the purple-faced man drew out three notes of a hundred taels each and tossed them onto the table before her. "Done. I'll have your sentence."

She stared at the three notes with round eyes, as though she could not credit that the world held such a man — a man who would truly lay down three hundred taels of silver for one sentence.

"Come here. Say it softly, into my ear — and mind those four brutes back there don't hear."

She hesitated, then came to him and whispered by his ear. "His sentence is eight words. *If you want me, find the Boss's Wife first.*"

The purple-faced man frowned. He honestly could not make head or tail of it. There were countless Boss's Wives in the world — every shop had one — how was he to find *the* one?

The girl added, "He said also — if you couldn't make it out, he'd throw in another for you. The Boss's Wife, he said, is the most beautiful woman in the world."

The purple-faced man stared a moment longer, asked no more, waved to his companion, and strode out.

The scarred man was already following. Then he turned, caught up one of the empty jars, and lobbed it back over his shoulder. As luck would have it the jar came to rest on the second man's head. The jar was green.

The scarred man laughed loud. "Now *there's* a wangba out and out."

There were plenty of pretty Boss's Wives in the world. Which one was the most beautiful?

"Does the fool mean us to go shop by shop," said the scarred man with a frown, "and haul out every Boss's Wife in the town to look her over?"

"No need," said the purple-faced man.

"You've another way?" said the scarred man.

The purple-faced man was musing. "I think I may have made out his meaning."

"And which is?" said the scarred man.

"Have you forgotten," said the purple-faced man with a small smile, "what they call Zhu Ting?"

The scarred man laughed again. "Then I ought to have found a jar to fit *his* head as well."

Zhu Ting had never done a day's trade in his life. He had never kept a shop. He held that any trade a man might take up, any shop he might keep, would sooner or later run into losses; and he was not one to take the risk. In truth there was a better reason still for his idleness — he had never had the capital. And yet everyone called him the Boss.

***

Zhu Ting was a man who understood how to enjoy himself, and took a broad view of most things; and between the two, his person grew a little more each day. A stout man has always the look of good fortune about him, and only a man of good fortune could be a boss — so a great many people called him the Boss. And in point of fact he *was* a fortunate man.

His own looks were not much to boast of, but his wife was a great beauty. He had never done a single stroke of honest work, and yet always contrived to live in the softest house, wear the choicest clothes, and drink the best of wine.

He had one thing besides in which he took a private pride: he held himself lazier even than Lu Xiaofeng. Once you had seen him settle into that big cushioned mandarin's chair of his, there were few things in the world that could get him up. For whatever he had a mind to do he first had to *stop* and think it over; and once he had thought it over, there was scarcely a thing in the world that had to be done at all.

If his days went on in comfort even so, it was because his two hands were wonderfully clever, and could fashion all manner of strange and marvellous things. Whatever a man could think of, Zhu Ting could make.

He had once wagered another that he could make a wooden figure that would walk. He won fifty tables' worth of shark's-fin banquet and fifty jars of aged wine into the bargain — which put another five catties on him at least. His present study was a great kite that would carry a man into the sky. Once he had wanted to go down and look under the earth. Now he wanted to go up.

He was about that when he heard hooves and horses in the street, and saw the two blue-robed men come in.

This time the scarred man did not kick the door. It was already open.

He came in glaring. "Where's the Boss's Wife?"

"If it's a Boss's Wife you want," said Zhu Ting mildly, "you'd best try the general store across the way. That's where they keep one."

"There's one here as well. Everyone calls you Boss. That makes your wife a Boss's Wife."

Zhu Ting smiled. "The Boss's Wife here would be greatly honoured, I'm sure, to hear that the men of the Blue-Robe Tower have come specially to see her."

He knew who they were. The Blue-Robe Tower was not one tower. There were a hundred and eight of them, and in each tower a hundred and eight men — an organisation of formidable weight. Not only were their numbers great; their discipline was tight, so that what they set out to do was seldom left undone. These two were among the men whose portraits hung on the wall of the First Tower. Where the First Tower stood no one knew, and no one had seen those hundred and eight likenesses with his own eyes; but every man in the jianghu knew that to hang your portrait among them was to ride roughshod through the world.

The one with the scar was called the Iron-Faced Judge — a name earned, so it was said, when a knife-stroke to his face notched the blade itself. Those two words, *Iron Face*, came out of that stroke.

The other was called Soul-Hook, and the pair of silver hooks he carried had truly hooked away many a soul.

"It's a great pity," Zhu Ting went on mildly, "but she happens to be much occupied just now, and hasn't the time to see you."

"Occupied with what?" asked the Iron-Faced Judge.

"She is sitting with a friend over wine," said Zhu Ting. "And isn't sitting with a friend over wine the most important business in the world?"

"This friend of hers," said the Iron-Faced Judge, "is his surname Lu?"

Zhu Ting's face darkened. "Best listen carefully. The gentleman named Lu is *her* friend. Not mine."

"Where are they drinking?"

"At that inn where the young dog puts up, I believe," said Zhu Ting. "The Green Cloud."

The Iron-Faced Judge looked him up and down, and a nasty little smile spread across his face. "Your wife's at an inn drinking with a notorious lecher, and *you* sit here quiet as this?"

"A child wants to pee, a wife wants to stray — nobody has ever stopped either one," Zhu Ting said easily. "What am I to do if I *can't* sit here? Turn cartwheels on the roof? Crawl about the floor?"

The Iron-Faced Judge burst out laughing. "You take a broad view, my friend. I do admire it."

He laughed often, for he knew that his laughter unnerved men more than his silence: when he laughed, the scar on his face twisted along with him, and the effect was worse than any evil spirit painted on the wall of some derelict temple.

Zhu Ting had been watching him. "Have you a wife?"

"None," said the Iron-Faced Judge.

Zhu Ting smiled, unhurried. "If you had a wife as handsome as mine, you'd take a broad view too."

***

Lu Xiaofeng lay on the bed with a full cup of wine standing on his chest. None of it had spilled, and the reason was that he did not move; you might have taken him for a corpse. His eyes were closed and had not opened once. His brows were thick and his lashes long; on his upper lip he wore a moustache trimmed to a neat pair of upswept points.

The Boss's Wife sat opposite him, looking at that moustache. She was indeed a very beautiful woman: curved brows, wide bright eyes; her mouth delicate and full, like a ripe peach — the sort of mouth a man could not help wanting to take a bite of. But what was most winning about her was neither her face nor her figure. It was the ripeness of her, the settled charm of a grown woman. Any man alive would take an interest in a woman like her.

Just now, though, it was Lu Xiaofeng's moustache that had her attention. She had studied it a good while, then broke into a small giggle. "Those two ends of yours really are the twins of your eyebrows. No wonder they call you the four-eyebrowed man." She laughed with her whole body, as though a branch of flowers were shaking. "Anyone who hasn't seen you would never guess you kept a second pair of eyebrows on your lip."

Lu Xiaofeng had not stirred. Then he drew a long, deep breath, and the cup on his chest came to him. The wine ran up in a single gulp — down it went. He let the breath out, and the empty cup was back where it had been.

The Boss's Wife laughed again. "Is that drinking, or a conjuring trick?"

Eyes still closed, Lu Xiaofeng said nothing. He only put out a hand and pointed to the empty cup. She had no choice but to fill it again for him. "Look here — you asked me to sit with you and drink. Why lie there like a dead man and not so much as glance at me?"

Lu Xiaofeng, at last: "I don't dare look at you."

"Why not?" said the Boss's Wife.

"I'm afraid you'll seduce me," said Lu Xiaofeng.

She bit her lip. "You take pains to have half the world think we've something dirty going, but you're afraid *I'll* seduce *you*. What's the sense in that?"

"For your husband's sake," said Lu Xiaofeng.

The Boss's Wife stared. "For him? You think he *likes* being a live wangba?"

"Better a live wangba than a dead one," said Lu Xiaofeng.

He gave her no room to answer. "A man in his line of work — anyone might chop his head off at any turn. He knows too many people, and too many of their secrets." She could not deny it: Zhu Ting *had* made many strange, secret things for many strange men. They trusted his mouth to be shut — but a dead man's mouth is tighter still. To kill in order to shut a mouth, and then to make away with the body, was work such men undertook without a qualm.

"If he were dead," said Lu Xiaofeng, "and you kept widow's mourning for him a whole year, I'd change my name from Lu."

The Boss's Wife raised her brows and sneered. "What do you take me for? Pan Jinlian?"

"Even if you were Pan Jinlian," said Lu Xiaofeng, easy as ever, "I'm no Ximen Qing."

The Boss's Wife glared at him, stood up, and turned to go. Lu Xiaofeng still had not stirred, and made no move at all to stop her.

But she was scarcely through the door before she wheeled back in and planted herself at the head of the bed, hands on her hips. "Do you truly think I can't see what you're up to? That I'm a fool?"

"Aren't you?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"You've had a falling-out with him, but you're afraid someone will do him in — so you make a show of my having something going with you. Then, to prove myself clean and to keep out of widow's weeds, I'll come begging *you* to protect him. And anyone who really meant to kill him has to stop and think twice."

Her voice was rising with her temper. "But have you spared a thought for *me*? Why should I carry a black pot on my back with never a word said?"

"For your husband's sake," Lu Xiaofeng said again.

She could not answer. For a wife to give up something for her husband — was that not, by every rule of heaven and earth, exactly what a wife was for?

"So," said Lu Xiaofeng mildly, "so long as your husband trusts you, what other people think never has to trouble you."

The Boss's Wife bit her lip and stared a while, and could not help asking, "Do you think he truly would trust me?"

"He isn't stupid," said Lu Xiaofeng.

The Boss's Wife glared at him. "And is he certain to trust *you*?"

Lu Xiaofeng sighed lazily. "That's a thing you'd best ask him."

He drew in another breath, took down the cupful on his chest, and murmured, "The Blue-Robe Tower men, if they're not too stupid either, should be nearly here by now. You'd best be going."

A worry showed in her eyes. "They really do want you, then? What for?"

"That," said Lu Xiaofeng, "is precisely what I mean to ask them. Or I wouldn't have let them find me here."

Zhu Ting sat in his mandarin's chair, staring into space. Heaven knew what wild notions were passing through his head — but that was how the strange contraptions came about.

The Boss's Wife came sauntering in, a small handkerchief pinched between two fingers, swaying her hips as she passed him twice. Zhu Ting appeared not to notice.

The Boss's Wife could bear it no longer. "I'm back."

"So I see," said Zhu Ting.

The Boss's Wife put on a mysterious look. "I've just been drinking a great many cups with Xiaofeng in his room. My head still spins."

"I know," said Zhu Ting.

The Boss's Wife rolled her eyes. "But we did nothing else, only drink."

"I know," Zhu Ting said again.

"You know a fart!" cried the Boss's Wife.

"That much I really don't know," said Zhu Ting.

Her temper flared again. "I go and drink with another man in his room for half a day, and not only are you not jealous, you sit here mooning over some foolery?"

"It's *because* I've no foolish thoughts," said Zhu Ting, "that I'm not jealous."

Her hands went back to her hips. "A man like that, a woman like me, shut up together in one small room — you really believe we'd sit there prim and proper the whole afternoon over wine?" She sneered. "What do you take him for? A saint? Liu Xiahui?"

Zhu Ting laughed. "I know he's a great rogue. And I trust him."

The Boss's Wife flared hotter. "You're not jealous only because you trust him — not because you trust *me*?"

"Of course I trust you as well," said Zhu Ting.

"But you trust him more," said the Boss's Wife.

"Don't forget," said Zhu Ting, "we've known each other since we wore split-crotch pants."

"If you're friends of twenty or thirty years' standing," said the Boss's Wife with a sneer, "why have you two suddenly turned into enemies who won't so much as speak?"

"Because he's a great rogue," said Zhu Ting. "And so am I."

She looked at him, and at last she could not help letting out a snorted laugh. She shook her head. "The things you two great rogues get up to — I don't understand any of it, and I grow more baffled by the day."

"Naturally you don't understand what great rogues get up to," said Zhu Ting. "You are no rogue yourself."

"There," she said, glowing, "now you've said something that sounds like a person."

Zhu Ting smiled at ease. "The most you are is a little rogue. A very, very little rogue."

***

Lu Xiaofeng lay where he was, eyes still closed, a full cup of wine on his chest. She had filled it for him on her way out. He was hardly going to stand up merely to pour himself another. The bed was soft and comfortable, and there could not be many people in the world who could get him off it now.

His red cape hung on the stand by the bed. There was no telling why — spring, summer, autumn, or winter, wherever he went, he always carried a red cape with him. Wherever that cape was, the man himself would not be far off.

The Iron-Faced Judge and Soul-Hook had seen it. They had seen it through the window.

In the next breath they were through the window and standing at the head of the bed, glaring down at Lu Xiaofeng.

Lu Xiaofeng lay like a dead man, no reaction, no sign even of breathing.

"You would be Lu Xiaofeng?" the Iron-Faced Judge said sharply.

Still no answer from the man on the bed.

Soul-Hook frowned. "Perhaps the fellow's dead."

"Perhaps," the Iron-Faced Judge sneered. "Men like this don't last long as a rule."

Lu Xiaofeng's eyes came open, glanced at them, closed again. "How strange," he murmured. "I thought for a moment I saw two people in the room."

"There *are* two people in it," the Iron-Faced Judge said loudly.

"If people had actually come in," said Lu Xiaofeng, "why didn't I hear anyone knock?"

"Because we didn't knock," said Soul-Hook.

Lu Xiaofeng opened his eyes again, took one more look. "You're sure you're people?"

"What else would we be?" said the Iron-Faced Judge. "Ghosts?"

"I don't believe it," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"Believe what?" said Soul-Hook.

"A man coming into my room knocks first," said Lu Xiaofeng mildly. "Only a wild dog jumps in through the window without a second thought."

Soul-Hook's colour changed, and a whip lashed down at him. He was one of the four masters of the double hooks east of the Pass, and no less accomplished with this whip he had braided from snakeskin: it was said he could crack a walnut set on three cubes of bean-curd without disturbing the curd. Lu Xiaofeng was a good deal larger than a walnut, and he lay before him as if dead. The stroke was as sure a thing as could be.

But Lu Xiaofeng put out a hand and pinched the whip's flicking tip lightly between two fingers, the way an old beggar pinches a bedbug. He had not learned this trick from Hua Manlou. Hua Manlou had learned it from *him*.

Soul-Hook's face turned as Cui Yidong's had turned when his blade was pinched — green, then white, then red. For all the strength he put into it, he could not draw the whip out from between the two fingers; and Lu Xiaofeng lay there as comfortably as ever, and not half a drop had gone from the cup on his chest.

The Iron-Faced Judge, watching from the side, showed astonishment in his eyes. Then he laughed loudly. "Fine — fine work! Lu Xiaofeng lives up to his name."

Soul-Hook broke into a laugh of his own and let the whip drop. "There now — I have tested at last whether this Lu Xiaofeng is the real Lu Xiaofeng."

"The times are ill, and men are not what they were," said the Iron-Faced Judge. "Impostors turn up in the jianghu every day. Friend Lu will not, I trust, hold our discourtesy against us."

The two of them cast about between them for a step down, while Lu Xiaofeng seemed to have gone back to sleep.

Soul-Hook's laughter died away. He coughed twice. "Friend Lu will of course have known who we are all along." As though to remind Lu Xiaofeng that the Blue-Robe Tower was no one to cross.

"We come this time only under orders," said the Iron-Faced Judge. "We ask Friend Lu to take the trouble of returning with us. We answer for his travel there and back, and we guarantee not one hair of him shall be touched."

Lu Xiaofeng sighed lazily at last. "And what would I go back with you for? It's not as if *your* Boss's Wife would sleep with me."

The Iron-Faced Judge's face darkened. "We have no Boss up there. You have one here."

Lu Xiaofeng's own face darkened. "Then since you know that much, you'd best hurry back and tell that Wei fellow of yours upstairs to keep his hands off Zhu Ting. Or I'll put a torch to all one hundred and eight of your Blue-Robe Towers."

"If we did kill Zhu Ting," sneered the Iron-Faced Judge, "wouldn't that suit you as well?"

"You never heard, then," said Lu Xiaofeng mildly, "that I've no taste for widows?"

"Come along with us just this once, and I'll guarantee the Boss's Wife is not a widow."

He had scarcely got the words out when a knock sounded. Not from without — whoever was knocking had come in unnoticed. And he was not knocking with a hand. He had no hand to knock with.

It was dusk again. The last of the sun came in at the window and struck the knocker's face full on — a thing that could scarcely be called a face any more.

The left side had been sliced clean off, and the healed wound had shrunk and puckered until it dragged nose and eye askew — no whole nose, only half; no pair of eyes, only one. Where the right eye had been was a black, deep socket. A great cross had been cut with a blade into his brow. Both hands had been struck off at the wrist; fitted now to the right stump was a cold-gleaming iron hook, to the left an iron ball larger than a man's head.

Beside this man the Iron-Faced Judge looked like a handsome fair-cheeked youth.

He stood just inside the doorway, tapping the door lightly with the iron hook. "I am a man," he said coldly, "not a wild dog. When I go into another's room, I always knock."

As he spoke, the ruined half of his face twitched without cease, so that he seemed at times to weep and at times to laugh.

At the sight of him even the Iron-Faced Judge could not help a shudder. He had not seen the man come in. Soul-Hook had already stepped back two paces. "Liu Yuhen?" he blurted.

A rasping little laugh came out of the man's throat, like a blade dragged over rust. "So there is still someone in the world who knows me. Rare — rare."

The Iron-Faced Judge was stiff with dismay. "You are the — the Jade-Faced Gentleman, Liu Yuhen?"

*This* man was called the Jade-Faced Gentleman?

He nodded, sorrow on him. "*Love, from of old, leaves only regret behind; the past is smoke, unbearable to speak of.* The Jade-Faced Gentleman died long ago. The pity is that Liu Yuhen is still alive."

The Iron-Faced Judge went pale. "What — what have you come here for?" He seemed to hold this man in a dread he could not name; his very voice had changed.

"Ten years ago," said Liu Yuhen coldly, "Liu Yuhen already wished himself dead. For all that, he is still alive. I have come only in search of a death."

"Why should *I* want you dead?" said the Iron-Faced Judge.

"Because if you do not want me dead," said Liu Yuhen, "I will want *you* dead."

The Iron-Faced Judge froze. Soul-Hook's face had gone green.

At that moment there came another knock.

This one from outside. And before another breath had passed the man was through the door — without having opened it. The heavy plank door might as well have been a sheet of paper. He had struck it with nothing, kicked it with nothing; he had merely walked forward, and the door had shattered before him.

And yet he had nothing of the strong-arm about him. He looked more like some soft-mannered scholar: a clean, fair face on which there was always a small smile.

Now he was smiling still. "I too am a man. And I too knock."

The Iron-Faced Judge saw all at once that even when this man smiled, his eyes held a killing edge, like the edge of a blade.

Soul-Hook had backed two more paces. "Xiao Qiuyu!" he blurted.

The man smiled. "Ah, sir — you have the knowledge, and the eye."

The Iron-Faced Judge, stricken again, said, "Not — the Heartbreak Swordsman, Xiao Qiuyu?"

He nodded, and sighed long. "*Autumn wind, autumn rain — enough to sicken a man to death.* Whenever it comes to killing, I am always a little sorrowful."

"Sorrowful about what?" the Iron-Faced Judge could not help asking.

"What I am sorrowful about just now," said Xiao Qiuyu quietly, "is whether it should be I who kill you, or whether I should leave it to Brother Liu."

The Iron-Faced Judge laughed suddenly again, but the laughter caught in his throat, so that even to his own ears it sounded a little like weeping.

Soul-Hook was quite unstrung, looking here and there for a way out.

Then a voice at his side laughed. "What are you looking for? Not your pair of silver hooks, by any chance?"

The man stood in the window: dark-faced and gaunt, short and slight, with a flame of a beard covering his cheeks. In his hands were a pair of silver hooks. Soul-Hook's.

He smiled. "I've brought your hooks along for you. Here — take them."

On the last word he gave a flick of the hand, and the pair of hooks floated slowly across the room toward Soul-Hook — strangely slow, as though an unseen hand held them up from below.

Even the Iron-Faced Judge knew this one. "Dugu Fang," he blurted, "the Solitary Rider of a Thousand Li?"

Dugu Fang nodded. "I seldom enter another's room. But this once is an exception." As he spoke, he was gone.

Then he was at the doorway, tapping the ruined door; the sound had scarcely rung out before he was back at the window, and in through it in the next instant, smiling. "I too am a man. And I too knock." The door was in pieces, and still he had knocked. Having knocked, he had gone back and come in through the window.

Soul-Hook had caught the hooks. "You've come to make trouble for us too?" he cried sharply.

"I don't kill wild dogs," said Dugu Fang. "I watch other men kill."

He fetched a chair and sat down at the window. Outside, the dusk was deepening.

Lu Xiaofeng lay comfortably on. Whatever might happen in the room seemed no concern of his.

Liu Yuhen, Xiao Qiuyu, Dugu Fang — these three he knew. Few in the jianghu would not know them; but fewer still could get Lu Xiaofeng off a bed, and he looked as though he meant to stay on this one. If these three were not the three loneliest and strangest men of the jianghu, they were not far off. That they should be together, and suddenly here — what for?

Soul-Hook's face was green, but he sneered still. "The Blue-Robe Tower has no quarrel with the three of you. Why come today to our door?"

"Because I want to," said Xiao Qiuyu.

He smiled. "I have always killed whoever it pleased me to kill. Today it pleases me to kill you, so here I am."

Soul-Hook glanced at the Iron-Faced Judge. "And if it *didn't* please you?"

"When it doesn't please me," said Xiao Qiuyu, "you could kneel and beg me to kill you, and I wouldn't trouble to lift a hand."

Soul-Hook let out a breath — and in the moment of that breath the Iron-Faced Judge went through the air, his pair of black-iron judge's pens already out, and drove at Liu Yuhen's Sky-Rushing and Welcome-Fragrance points.

There was nothing showy in the stroke. It was accurate, it was quick, and it worked.

But Liu Yuhen seemed not to see the pens at all.

On the contrary, he took a step forward. There came a soft, thick sound, and both pens were buried in his shoulder and his breast.

But the iron ball at his left wrist had already struck the Iron-Faced Judge in the face. The face was suddenly in bloom.

Without a cry, he was going over backwards. Liu Yuhen's iron hook caught him and held him up.

The two pens were still in Liu Yuhen's flesh. They had missed the great points, but they had gone in deep.

Liu Yuhen showed no feeling of them at all. He only looked coldly at the bloody ruin of the Iron-Faced Judge's face. "So it wasn't iron after all."

A jerk of the hook, and the Iron-Faced Judge went out through the window — off to meet the *real* Judge.

In the same breath Soul-Hook's pair of silver hooks flew up and out the window too.

He himself was still in the room, face ashen, hands hanging, blood running from the joints of both arms.

In Xiao Qiuyu's hand a short sword dripped blood.

He smiled at Soul-Hook. "It seems these hands of yours won't be hooking away any more souls."

Soul-Hook's teeth clenched, and still they chattered. He roared out, "Why don't you kill me too?"

"Because just now," said Xiao Qiuyu quietly, "it doesn't please me. Just now I want you to go back and tell your people upstairs to stay upstairs for the next two months. Otherwise it will be hard for the man in question to climb up alive again."

Soul-Hook's face shifted once more. He said nothing, and turned to make for the door.

Dugu Fang was in front of him. "You came in through the window," he said coldly. "You'd best go out through the window."

Soul-Hook glared at him, stamped his foot at last — and the two men who had come in through the window did, both of them, go out through the window.

Liu Yuhen was staring dully at the deepening dark beyond the sill. The pens were still in his flesh.

Xiao Qiuyu went to him and drew them gently out. He watched the blood come from the chest, and in his cold eyes a kind of sorrow seemed to rise.

Liu Yuhen let out a long sigh. "A pity... a pity..."

"A pity you didn't die this time either?" said Xiao Qiuyu.

Liu Yuhen said no more.

Xiao Qiuyu sighed too, long and sadly. "Why do you go on like this?"

Dugu Fang sighed himself. "You break other men's hearts. He breaks his own."

One man dead in the room, the room in ruins — and Lu Xiaofeng lay as if none of it had happened. As if he had seen nothing. Stranger still, these three men seemed to have no notion that anyone lay on the bed at all. The room grew dark. In the dark they stood silent. No one spoke, and no one left.

Then, out of the evening wind, a music came — long-drawn and lovely, as of the immortals.

Dugu Fang seemed to lift at the sound. "She is coming."

***

Who was making such music? Lu Xiaofeng was listening too — it was music no man could help listening to.

He noticed that the room, which a moment before had smelt only of blood, was filling with fragrance — a fragrance beyond the fragrance of any flower, coming in on the wind and with the music, until in the turn of an eye all heaven and earth seemed to hold nothing else.

Then the dark room brightened. Lu Xiaofeng could bear it no longer. He opened his eyes and found the whole room whirling with flowers.

Fresh flowers of every kind drifted in through the window and settled softly on the floor. A carpet of flowers had woven itself along the boards, and the carpet reached to the door and beyond it.

Someone was walking in through the door, slowly.

Lu Xiaofeng had seen many women in his time — the plain ones and the lovely — but he had never seen a woman as beautiful as this. She wore a robe of soft, pure black silk that trailed on the floor, and on the flowers. Her hair fell black on her shoulders. Her face was pale. Her eyes, black too, held a black shining.

She wore no other ornament, no other colour. She stood on the flowers, and all the many colours of the flowers on the floor seemed suddenly to lose their colour before her. Hers was not a beauty of this world. It was past the world's beauty, past belief.

Liu Yuhen, Xiao Qiuyu, and Dugu Fang had drawn back quietly to the corners of the room, deeply respectful.

Lu Xiaofeng's very breath was almost stopping. But he did not stand up.

The girl in black looked at him quietly. Her eyes were as clear as the dew on a rose on a spring morning; her voice, when it came, as soft as a wind — a spring wind that at dusk stirs the water on a distant hill. But her smile was mysterious. Mysterious as the flute-note that comes from far off on a still night, drifting, out of any grasp.

She looked at Lu Xiaofeng. She smiled at him. Then, all at once, she knelt to him — as a single white cloud drifts out of the blue sky and falls into the world of men.

Lu Xiaofeng could not lie on the bed a moment longer. He sprang up. He became a pellet shot from a great bow — through the canopy of the bed, and — bang — through the roof itself. Moonlight fell through the hole he had made. He was gone.

A wide-eyed girl with a well-behaved look about her stood on the flowers behind the girl in black.

Lu Xiaofeng had bolted as though he had seen a ghost, and the young girl was startled too. She could not help whispering, "The Princess did him such courtesy, and he ran? What is he afraid of?"

The girl in black did not answer. She stood up slowly. She touched her own soft hair — hair like drifting cloud. A strange expression passed through her bright eyes, and a long moment went by before she said, softly, "He is a clever man. Very, very clever."
