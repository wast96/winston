## Section One — the Special Section's "Steward"

Of all the branches of the Central Special Section, Section One was formed first. In August 1927, before the Party Central's organs moved back from Wuhan to Shanghai, a "General Office" had already been set up to serve the building of those organs. As the central organs returned to Shanghai one after another, leading bodies of every kind had to be established there; supplies and equipment had to be bought, offices fitted out, and housing rented for the central leadership and the leading comrades. Without a dedicated body to handle all this, the task could hardly have been carried through. Once the Central Special Section was formed, this "General Office" became its Section One.

What set Section One's work apart was its closeness to the Party Central. The work was heavy and varied, and it had to be done with the strictest secrecy and care; the smallest carelessness could put the Party Central's safety directly at risk. It was fitting, then, that the Central Special Section should lead it. The section bore a grave responsibility for the safety of the central organs and the leading comrades. It saw constantly to their food, clothing, housing, and movement, so that none of this would bring trouble down on them. Renting a house meant choosing the right surroundings; buying wooden furniture meant fitting it to a family's supposed station; furnishing a room meant making it blend in. When the Party Central convened a larger meeting, the section's burden grew heavier still: choosing the venue, the interior fittings and the guard, managing supplies, investigating the neighboring houses, patrolling and watching the approaches—all of it had to be prepared in meticulous detail beforehand to keep the meeting absolutely safe. The Party Central held many important meetings and never once came to grief, which was no small feat.

Section One also handled dealings with the outside world, which meant carrying on all sorts of public activity. Renting a house called for a shop's guarantee; furnishing it called for buying furniture. So the section opened businesses of various kinds—a furniture shop, an electrical-goods store—to meet the work's pressing needs. Beyond this there were many special tasks: getting arrested central leaders out of prison, buying guns and ammunition, moving dangerous goods, safekeeping the Party's important supplies and documents, and, when a comrade fell, seeing to the body and caring for the family left behind. These matters look trivial, yet like all the Special Section's work they were full of hair-raising struggle, and much of it had to be closely coordinated with the other branches.

Section One's chief was Hong Yangsheng. Before Section Four was set up, Li Qiang, too, often ran the section's affairs. He once said at a meeting: "Section One was the general-affairs section—it handled everything from birth to death. Chen Geng couldn't speak Shanghainese, so this fell to me as well. A child was born; we sent it to the foundling home. A man died; we bought a coffin and buried him. A lot of it took several branches working together."

Hong Yangsheng later changed his name to Hong Songtao. He joined the Youth League in Shanghai in 1921, and after transferring to Party membership in 1922 he did courier work for the Shanghai Local Committee and for the League's Central Committee. Late in 1925, after training in a military course run by the Central Military Commission, he was sent to the Northeast as a military commissioner, and in the spring of 1927 he was recalled to Shanghai. After the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup he went with the Party Central's organs to Wuhan, where he handled registration work in the Central Organization Department. Soon after the central organs moved back to Shanghai, he was transferred into the Central Special Section as chief of Section One.

Section One of the Central Special Section had heavy and varied work. It came down to three main tasks: setting up secret offices, arranging meeting venues, and rescuing arrested leaders while caring for their families.

### Setting Up the Party's Secret Offices

Setting up offices was the first problem the Party Central's organs faced on their return to Shanghai. Many were needed, each for a different use: some for meetings and work, some for making contact, some to house a radio set, some to keep documents; and the lodging of every comrade had to be arranged and secretly provided by Section One. Hong Yangsheng recalled: "Setting up offices and arranging venues was detailed, painstaking work. Empty houses were plentiful in Shanghai then, and renting was not hard, but you needed a solvent shop to stand guarantor, or nothing could be done. When Special Section One first started, finding a house and a guarantor was very hard. Comrade Zhou Enlai told us to try every means to find social connections and solve the problem. In time we built up three such points. One was the Taihengyuan water-and-electric shop on Lloyd Road (now Liuhe Road); its owner, Zhang Yi'an (also called Xingsheng), was a distant relative of mine, though we had not kept up. For the work's sake I called on him and claimed him as a cousin. Zhang Yi'an trusted me well enough; we asked him to stand guarantor for our rentals, and he would always put his seal to it, and never refused anything we asked. Another was the Sanmin Photo Studio on North Sichuan Road, whose manager was Fan Yuren (also called Mengju, a Shanghai native); he often helped us—buying and storing weapons, passing secret documents, doing cover work. What became of him later I do not know. A third was a cloth shop run by Li Ruisheng, who had once been a shop clerk and had organized a clerks' union. After he opened the shop he stood surety for our rentals, and from 1928 on he brought his whole family to live in an office for the Party. That office, in Yongji Lane on Weihaiwei Road, was a contact point." In those days, if the man living in an office was a bachelor, the Party organization would assign a woman comrade to pose as his "wife." Secret work in Shanghai could not be done without a woman comrade for cover; a bachelor living alone not only drew suspicion but had trouble even renting a house. By the principle that secret work must blend into society, people had to fit their legal identities: a home had to look like a home, a shop like a shop; the woman of the house had to keep house like an ordinary housewife, doing the marketing and the cooking, so that everything held together and nothing gave them away. Most of the fake couples who lived in the offices, after a long time together, grew fond of one another and, with the Party's approval, became real husband and wife. One day in October 1981, recalling his underground years in Shanghai and speaking of "living in an office," Li Qiang, besides the account above, gave an example: the film The Unfailing Radio Waves tells of just such a thing. It is a story film drawn from real people and real events; the man is named Li, the woman is called Huiying, and she is in Shanghai now, a manager in a company under the Ministry of Foreign Trade.

The film's underground radio operator, Li Xia, was modeled on Li Bai, who had been radio-station chief of the First Front Army before he was sent to Shanghai for secret work under the cover name Li Xia. The Party organization assigned Qiu Huiying—a woman Communist who had been a silk-mill worker, originally named Qiu Lanfen—to pose as his "wife." Qiu Huiying, once an indentured mill hand, moved in with him, and, moved by his devotion to the Party and his tireless spirit, she came to love him; in their work love grew between the two of them, and with the Party's approval they became true husband and wife and comrades bound in life and death. They shared hardship and danger, fought side by side, and braved death under the savage White Terror. Arrested more than once, facing the enemy's torture and the threat of death, they held firm and guarded the Party's interests. Li Bai suffered the cruelest tortures and was in the end brutally killed. With his life he composed a stirring battle-song; his revolutionary integrity and his selfless fighting spirit set a shining example for us.

When the Party Central convened a larger, important meeting, Section One would rent a bigger venue and contrive a temporary "family" as cover. For the preparatory meeting of the Congress of the Chinese Soviet Areas, which the Party Central held in Shanghai in May 1930, nearly fifty people took part. Personnel from Sections One, Two, and Three of the Central Special Section were all mobilized to prepare it together. The venue chosen was a row of houses on Burkill Road (now Fengyang Road), behind the Carlton Theatre (now the Yangtze Theatre). Before the meeting it was decided that Li Yimang, who worked in the central organs, would play the master of the house. Besides Li Yimang's wife and their two boys, the Party brought in Zhao Yimin as Li Yimang's younger brother and Li Yichao (also called Li Kuntai) as his younger sister—six people in all, making up a temporary family. The furnishing of the whole house, the food, lodging, and movement of those attending, and the guard inside and out were all arranged by the Central Special Section. Once the meeting ended, the temporary family broke up. The Sichuan girl who had played his sister, Li Yichao, was never heard of again.

More than twenty years later, in the early days after national liberation, Li Yimang saw the film Zhao Yiman and only then realized that Zhao Yiman was that same Li Yichao.

Mainly, though, Section One set up various working offices, liaison points, and contact points for the Party Central. Above the Shengli Hospital, at 447 Yunnan Road next to the Tianchan Stage (now 171–173 Yunnan Central Road), it established an office where the Central Political Bureau met and worked, with the couple Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duansui living in as its keepers. In the spring of 1928, when the Hubei Provincial Committee was broken up and Xiong Jinding moved to Shanghai, the Party Central appointed him accountant of the central organs, in charge of raising funds. He hung out the sign of the "Fuxing Trading Company," dealing in homespun cloth and posing as the proprietor. In truth the place was a meeting-and-working office for the Central Political Bureau, and the central leaders who came and went looked like customers, drawing no suspicion. In his Draft Autobiography Xiong Jinding set down how it came about: "In April 1928, with most of the Hubei Provincial Committee's organs broken up, I moved to Shanghai and made contact with Comrades Li Weihan, Gong Yinbing, Huang Wenrong, and others. I was at once assigned as central accountant, and told to set up a meeting-and-working office for the Central Political Bureau and to prepare secret addresses for sending and receiving mail. I first appeared as a merchant, looking for a suitable house, and found one at the mouth of the Racecourse on Fourth Avenue (now the entrance to People's Square on Fuzhou Road), right behind the Tianchan Stage, reached through a very dirty little alley. The whole upper floor, three large rooms, was just right for the Political Bureau to meet and work in. The second landlord below was a well-known Western-medicine doctor, Zhou Shenglai, who ran a private clinic—the Shengli Hospital. We lived there three or four years, with twenty or thirty people coming and going, and were truly left in peace." Soon after the "Fuxing Trading Company" opened, for cover, Zhou Enlai brought in a nineteen-year-old woman Communist from Hunan, Zhu Duansui, to be the "proprietress" and to live in with "Boss Xiong." The two had known each other earlier in Hunan and had thought well of one another. Living together there for a few months, they came to understand each other and grew deeply attached. On the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival that year, after Zhou Enlai, Li Weihan, Deng Xiaoping, and others had met at the "Fuxing Trading Company," the two of them laid on a banquet at a Sichuan restaurant on Fourth Avenue and invited them to drink and admire the moon. Zhou Enlai, meaning to make a match of it, proposed over the table that they marry; Li Weihan, Deng Xiaoping, and the others all approved, and so the two became revolutionary partners, beginning a lifetime of shared joys and hardships.

Everyone who did underground work in Shanghai in those years, or elsewhere in the territory the Kuomintang ruled, kept an unforgettable impression of these offices. Half a century later, Li Weihan recalled with relish the office the "Boss Xiong" couple had set up: "In October 1927, after the Party Central moved to Shanghai, a good many of the central organs were placed near Avenue Edward VII (now East Yan'an Road). South of the avenue was the French Concession, north of it the International Settlement. When I reached Shanghai I first lived on Danshui Road, north of the avenue, and all around were the Party Central's secret offices. West of my lodging was Qu Qiubai's place, and beyond that Su Zhaozheng's. South of the avenue was the Central Peasant Committee's office, and east of that the Central Secretariat's. Some of the other central leaders lived over toward Avenue Road (now West Beijing Road).

"At first the Standing Committee of the Central Political Bureau often met at Comrade Qiubai's place. That was a whole house—meetings upstairs, and downstairs lived the couple He Jiaxing and He Zhihua (who later turned traitor and sold out Comrade Luo Yinong). Around the first half of 1928 the Party Central set up an office on the upper floor of the house behind the Tianchan Stage on Fourth Avenue (now 171–173 Yunnan Central Road), and the Central Political Bureau met there regularly. Those attending went up a staircase on Yunnan Road, on the west side of the Tianchan Stage, straight into the meeting room. There was a small table by the window for taking minutes. In those days the minutes were kept by the central secretary-general, Comrade Deng Xiaoping (and later by Comrade Huang Wenrong). The keepers of that office were the Xiong Jinding couple. It was after the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, when the Party Central was assigning me work, that Comrade Zhou Enlai talked with me in this very room. Later, probably because Gu Shunzhang was arrested and turned traitor, the office was given up. After liberation, in 1952, when Comrade Mao Zedong was drafting the constitution in Hangzhou, he sent word for Comrade Xiaoping and me to go there and confer. Comrade Xiaoping said to me: 'Luo Mai, let the two of us go to Shanghai and have a look at the old place!' I said, 'Good.' We hurried there and found the room unchanged, though it had become a health station. This was where two successive Central Political Bureaus had worked—the one after the August 7 Conference and the one after the Sixth Congress—and a secret office that had gone a long time without being broken up."

Zhou Enlai, too, could not forget this office. In 1946, in Shanghai again for talks with the Kuomintang, he stayed at the "Zhou Mansion," 107 Rue Massenet (now 73 Sinan Road). To the Xiong Jinding couple, who had also come to Shanghai just then to arrange the New China Daily's distribution there, he said: "You should go and see the old place, and bring back some photographs." In January 1966, on Xiong Jinding's eightieth birthday—by then he was vice-president of the national Red Cross Society—Zhou Enlai came specially, bearing two bottles of Shaoxing aged rice-wine that Deng Xiaoping had given him, to wish him long life. On the eve of the "Cultural Revolution," Zhou Enlai wrote out in his own hand a testimonial for the couple: during the civil-war years, Comrades Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duansui had held the Party Central's most secret work, had braved death for it, had contributed greatly, and were most to be trusted. The revolutionary bond was a moving thing. In 1973, when word came that Xiong Jinding was critically ill, Zhou Enlai, gravely sick himself, went to the hospital to see him anyway. By then Xiong Jinding could no longer speak. Zhou Enlai asked Zhu Duansui whether he had left any last words. She passed over a slip of paper, on which were two lines of parting verse Xiong Jinding had left for Zhou Enlai: "I take my leave now of this land of joy; may you long keep your fighting frame." They spoke his heartfelt reverence for his old comrade-in-arms and his last good wishes.

In 1980 the Shanghai Municipal People's Government designated the old site of the "Fuxing Trading Company," where the Central Political Bureau had once met and worked, a municipal cultural relic under protection.

Hong Yangsheng listed other offices Section One set up:

First, in 1928, on Rue Père Robert in the French Concession (now Ruijin No. 2 Road), the section rented a Western-style house whose second landlord was an old half-Russian woman. Hong Yangsheng and a woman comrade posed as husband and wife and lived on the third floor, using it as the Central Special Section's own internal contact point. Zhou Enlai, the couple Chen Geng and Wang Genying, Gu Shunzhang, and a few others often came here to meet and talk. No one else from the Special Section came, and Hong Yangsheng and the rest did not go to Chen Geng's home either: a good many people from the intelligence system passed through Chen Geng's home, and given the special nature of the work, those whose work was unrelated kept away from one another.

Second, another of the Central Special Section's meeting places was at the mouth of a lane on Tongfu Road (now Shimen No. 2 Road), a shikumen house. Two signboards hung at the door—"Lawyer Fei Guoxi, Correspondence Office" and "Xinyi Agency"—but in fact it was a place for secret contact and for relaying documents. The ground-floor rear rooms were where members of Section One and Section Three (the "Red Squad") lived together. Some weapons were stored there too; it was rather busy, and the Special Section's chief leaders as a rule did not come.

Third, beside the Xieqiao Guild Hall on Seymour Road (now North Shaanxi Road), the section opened an "antique shop" as a secret contact office of the Party Central, where the Standing Committee of the Central Political Bureau also met a few times. A Hunanese, Zhu Jintang, was put in as the shop's "proprietor," and Gu Shunzhang's mother-in-law as the cooking woman. Zhu Jintang had been a cadre of the Anyuan Workers' Club; after the Great Revolution failed he could not hold his ground in Hunan and came to Shanghai to find the Party Central, which set him up to open this "antique shop." During the Sixth Congress, Zhu absconded with the funds, and the "antique shop" closed at once—but the place itself was never broken up.

Fourth, east of Nicheng Bridge the section opened a furniture shop, with Dai Rongxin as clerk and a woman from Songjiang, arranged by Gu Shunzhang, to cook. In those days the leaders moved house often, and when they rented, the furniture shop could provide the guarantee and supply the furniture they needed. After some secret meetings, the house might be given up, but the furniture that had been bought had nowhere to be stored for a time. Once the furniture shop opened, it served both as a contact office and as a furniture depot.

Fifth, at the end of 1928, the section rented a lane house beside the market on Seymour Road. At first it was thought the surroundings were good, well suited to be an important office for contact and talks. But after moving in, they found that the couple of the famous professor Deng Chumin also lived there. Though he was a well-known democratic figure who sympathized with the Communists, and one of his daughters was a Youth League member, he had many friends coming and going, and the people about were rather mixed. For safety's sake, the place was used only as an ordinary office.

Sixth, in 1930 the section rented a three-story Western-style house in a lane on Avenue Road (now West Beijing Road). It stood on its own, with good surroundings—a parlor downstairs and living quarters upstairs. This was made the place where central leaders met the Comintern's representatives. Hong Yangsheng posed as a capitalist and lived there with his whole family, and so did the couple in charge of records and a blind English interpreter. Central leaders and Comintern representatives came and went, and Hong Yangsheng received them all in his guise as a "capitalist."

### Arranging Venues for Party Meetings

Section One's other important task was arranging venues. All the important meetings the Party Central convened were prepared by Section One: from fixing the place, renting the house, and furnishing the room down to seeing to the odd jobs, the whole of it fell to Section One. Once the section had chosen a venue with safety in mind, Section Three would send out the "Red Squad" with weapons to guard it—some patrolling nearby on bicycles, some disguised as small peddlers keeping watch before and behind the house and at the lane mouths, some standing guard at the venue itself. Should danger arise, the outer men would give the alarm and those at the venue would split up: one part to hold off the enemy, another to move the leaders to safety.

Several of the Central Committee's plenums in those days were held at venues arranged by Section One, and some Zhou Enlai arranged himself.

For the Second Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party, held in Shanghai from late June into early July 1929, Zhou Enlai himself arranged for the couple Li Yuchao and Liu Shuqin, and for Luo Dengxian's wife Zhou Xiuzhu, to live in the office.

Li Yuchao and Liu Shuqin both worked in the Records Section, a subordinate body of the Central Secretariat; Li Yuchao ran the section's daily work, Liu Shuqin worked there too, and their office was in Zunyi Lane (now Songshou Lane).

Liu Shuqin recalled how the Second Plenum came about: "In the summer of 1929 I finished my work in the Records Section. Li Yuchao was transferred out of it as well, to work in the military training class. Just then the Party Central was preparing to convene the Second Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, and the meeting was to be covered in the form of a household. Zhou Enlai asked me to live in the office and do cover work; my open identity was the idle mistress of the house, but in fact I did some clerical work for the plenum, listened to reports, and so on; at the same time Gu Weizhen was brought in to cook. I worried I could not manage it, but Zhou Enlai told me that finding someone else would mean having new clothes made, and the Party's funds were tight, and this meeting was very important, and it would be hard just then to find anyone more suitable. So I took the task on. I cannot recall now where the plenum was held—only that the house was in the last row of a lane, very handsome, like a mansion. To make the rooms look like a rich family's, I brought along my satin quilt and good clothes from my wedding. Zhou Enlai was the meeting's overall director, and Gu Shunzhang was in charge of the venue's security and the assembly's administration and finances. Among those attending whom I knew were Zhou Enlai, Guan Xiangying, He Chang, Liu Shaoqi, Xiang Ying, Lu Futan, Li Lisan, Luo Dengxian, and Gu Shunzhang, and perhaps Deng Enming as well. I also saw a foreigner and an interpreter take part. The meeting ran only a week or so, and I did not learn the details. During it, Gu Shunzhang's wife, gaudily dressed, came by the meeting place several times in a motorcar. Luo Dengxian's wife (named Xiuzhu) came to the meeting place too, and lived downstairs with me; everyone else who came for the meeting lived upstairs. Zhou Enlai, Li Lisan, Xiang Ying, and the others did not stay there, and came only when the meeting was in session…"

The Third Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, held in Shanghai from 24 to 28 September 1930, met in a Western-style house on Medhurst Road (now Taixing Road). The meeting ran three days, with several dozen taking part.

During it, the whole of Section Three's "Red Squad" turned out to do the guarding, and Section One sent people to attend to matters. Before this meeting, under Qu Qiubai, Zhou Enlai, and others, and following the Comintern's instructions, some of the "Left" errors had already been corrected. This plenum went further and criticized the "Left" errors represented by Li Lisan, halting the reckless scheme of Li Lisan and the rest to organize a nationwide general uprising and mass the whole Red Army for an attack on the central cities. With this, the rule of the "Left" adventurism represented by Li Lisan came to an end in the Party Central. During the meeting, when some comrades grew heated in denouncing Li Lisan's "Left" errors, their voices carried outside. Zhou Enlai took special care to remind everyone that in a secret-work setting they must mind their safety, and had Section One's people keep order and quiet anyone whose voice rose too high. Though the guards raised the alarm several times, the meeting ended safely in the end.

The Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party, held in Shanghai on 7 January 1931, met at 6 Xiude Lane, Wuding Road, in the French Concession. The meeting ran a whole day, ending deep in the night. The arguments at the venue were fierce; to cover the proceedings, the delegates met upstairs while, downstairs, women comrades of Sections One and Two of the Central Special Section played cards and ran a gramophone. Section Three's "Red Squad" guarded just as strictly, dressed as cooks to protect the venue and ready to go into action at any moment. This plenum, convened on the Comintern's instructions, was attended, besides the 22 Central Committee members and alternates, by delegates of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, the seamen's and railwaymen's unions, the Party groups, the Central Preparatory Committee for the Soviets, and the grassroots Party organizations in the White areas—37 in all. The Comintern's representative, Pavel Mif, attended and constantly used organizational maneuvers to control the proceedings. Through this plenum, Wang Ming and his group came in effect to hold the leadership of the Party Central. From then on the "Left" adventurism represented by Wang Ming ruled the whole Party for four years. The Fourth Plenum and the leadership that followed it, on the one hand, raised a number of "Left" dogmatists and sectarians to leading posts in the center; on the other, they struck excessively at the comrades who had committed Li Lisan's adventurist errors, and wrongly struck at the comrades headed by Qu Qiubai charged with a so-called "conciliationist line," and, under the slogan of "opposing the Right," struck at He Mengxiong, Lin Yunan, Li Qiushi, and others. Soon afterward He Mengxiong and the rest were arrested by the enemy and, holding firm in prison, died heroically.

The preparatory meeting of the Congress of the Chinese Soviet Areas, convened in Shanghai in May 1930 under the Party Central and the Central Executive Committee of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, was held at a venue Section One arranged as well. That meeting drew many people, ran a long time, and made for heavy work, calling out the strength of every branch of the Central Special Section. (Its course is told separately elsewhere in this book.)

### Rescuing Arrested Comrades

Rescuing arrested central leaders, seeing to the last rites of fallen martyrs, and comforting their families were also Section One's tasks.

Under the conditions of the White Terror, comrades of ours were constantly being arrested. As a rule, an ordinary comrade, once taken, was rescued through the China Relief Society; but when a central leader was arrested, the Central Special Section handled the rescue. There were three main methods.

When an arrested comrade's identity had not been exposed, the Central Special Section would openly hire a lawyer to argue the case and rescue him by legal means. Hong Yangsheng recalled: "Section One had a direct line to two lawyers. One was Pan Zhenya, a professor of law who also practiced; he sympathized deeply with the revolution, and whatever we asked he would do, never attaching any condition. The other was the lawyer Fei Guoxi—we would ask him to argue a case, but it still cost some money. This was the method most often used." When Ren Bishi was arrested in Shanghai on 17 November 1929, under the alias Peng Desheng, his identity was not exposed. On 19 November the Mixed Court of the Shanghai International Settlement opened its trial. The Central Special Section had Pan Zhenya argue the case, and he pressed his points until the judge was left without an answer and adjourned. At the second sitting on 22 November, the enemy got nothing out of him and had no evidence, and only on the pretext of "endangering the security of the state" sentenced him to forty days. Three days later, on 25 December, while the concession foreigners were keeping Christmas, Ren Bishi was "released with his sentence reduced."

The second method was to find an inside contact, or get someone to put in a word, or bribe a way through, to win a release. When Ren Bishi went to Anhui on an inspection tour and was arrested for the first time, in Nanling County on 13 October 1928, under the temporary alias Hu Shaofu, his identity was not exposed. Through Chen Congying, He Weidao—a marriage relation of the Ren family and one of Changsha's four great lawyers—was engaged and hurried to Anqing to work for his rescue, moving the case from the special criminal tribunal to the Anhui Provincial Court; the Party Central also ordered the Special Section to carry on rescue work among the Kuomintang's upper ranks, and the International Red Aid put up 8,000 yuan to buy off the officials concerned. In the end he was released on bail without charge and, at the end of 1928, came out of prison and back to Shanghai.

Yun Daiying was arrested in Shanghai on 6 May 1930 and held at the Laozha police station, but he passed himself off as a worker under the alias Wang Zuolin and did not expose his identity. Hong Yangsheng recalled: "At the time, a Central Special Section comrade who had planted himself inside the Kuomintang's Songhu Garrison Command found the Laozha station's detective chief, You Agen, and had a word with him, claiming Yun Daiying was his 'cousin' and asking him to do them a favor. So Yun Daiying was let off with a light sentence of three years and moved to the Suzhou Army Prison." While Yun Daiying was held at Suzhou, Chen Geng, through a connection with a judge of the Jiangsu High Court, was preparing to have him released early. Zhou Enlai sent me to Suzhou to give Yun Daiying money for the road, and to tell him that once out he should not take the train back to Shanghai but go by boat or on foot, to avoid trouble from spies along the way. Had Gu Shunzhang not turned traitor and gone over to the enemy and sold out Yun Daiying, Yun would not in the end have met his death in Nanjing.

The third method was armed rescue. It was used only when the first two had failed and there was no other choice. Armed rescue was among the "Red Squad's" training subjects, and when the "Red Squad" carried out an armed-rescue plan, Section One comrades sometimes helped from the side. After four comrades, Peng Pai among them, were arrested, the Central Special Section had planned to rescue them by force while the enemy was moving them, but a flaw in the arrangements let the moment slip. After Luo Yinong was arrested, the Central Special Section had also meant to "waylay him on the way" or "seize him at the execution ground," but the situation was against them, there was no opening, and in the end the plan had to be given up.

Seeing to the bodies of fallen comrades and comforting and providing for their families was Section One's task as well. Comrades of the other branches took part in this work too, and Li Qiang was often one of them. After the martyr Luo Yinong fell, his coffin was kept at the Anhui Guild Hall. Li Qiang, together with Luo Yinong's wife Li Wenyi, moved Luo Yinong's coffin from the Anhui Guild Hall to Jiangwan Cemetery for burial. He also personally saw to the burial of the martyr Su Zhaozheng and of Deng Xiaoping's late wife Zhang Xiyuan, among others. In a letter to the editors of Shanghai's Party History Materials on 5 March 1982, Li Qiang spoke of the matter. He wrote: "In early 1930, when the spring was warming and the flowers were out, the Party Central gave me the task of burying the martyrs Su Zhaozheng, Luo Yinong, and Zhang Xiyuan. As I recall, Su Zhaozheng and Luo Yinong were buried first, and Zhang Xiyuan a little later, but not long apart. At the burial in the cemetery, Comrade Deng Yingchao and her mother, Zhang Xiyuan's younger sister Zhang Xiaomei, and Comrade Li Wenyi were all present. Under the name 'Zeng Youfan' I set up two stone markers before the three martyrs' graves—'Tomb of Yao Weichang' and 'Tomb of the Zhang née Zhou'—as markers. In the early days after Shanghai's liberation, I found these names in the cemetery registers kept by the municipal civil-affairs bureau, and following that thread I found the coffins of Comrades Su Zhaozheng and Zhang Xiyuan. Because Jiangwan Cemetery was turned into an airfield during the War of Resistance, the coffins were scattered over the waste ground and many were destroyed, and so the martyr Luo Yinong's coffin was never found."

Section One's work also included buying and keeping weapons and certain special items, and setting up all sorts of cover offices to serve the Party Central. To rescue arrested central leaders, the Special Section, under the Central Special Committee, would mobilize great strength and every kind of social connection, spare no expense, and disregard danger to carry the rescue through. To buy weapons, Section One built up many usable connections, dealing directly or indirectly with foreign trading houses in Shanghai, arms dealers, warlords, and men of the underworld. In 1930 the Special Section, through the Central Military Commission, bought many guns from a foreign trading house in Shanghai. The "Sanmin Photo Studio," at the northwest corner where North Sichuan Road met Range Road, was one of the places where Section One stored weapons. Section One also had the task of moving weapons. Sometimes women comrades were sent to carry them, to lessen the suspicion of an enemy who searched. When there were many weapons, women comrades would often dress up as fine ladies and young mistresses and move them in a streamlined sedan. To rent houses and stand guarantee, and to furnish the central organs as needed, Section One set up all kinds of bodies to cover the secret work, in the guise of shopkeepers doing business.

Of Section One's staff, Hong Yangsheng said: "Besides me, they were: Yan Siying, from Nanxi in Sichuan, who lived in an office; Zou Zhishu, from Jiaxing in Zhejiang, who did courier and cover work; Zhou Qing, a Sichuanese who had been a soldier, brought over from the 'Red Squad'; Chen Jiakang, a Cantonese, the Special Section's full-time doctor; Guangdong Ah-si (an alias), a Cantonese who drove the open-topped car Special Section One kept then; Zhu Sheng (an alias), a Hunanese, who was proprietor of the antique shop; Zhang Kecheng (an alias), a northerner, who was proprietor of the furniture shop; Zhang Ah-tao, Gu Shunzhang's father-in-law; Zhang née Lu, Gu Shunzhang's mother-in-law; Zhang Aibao, Gu Shunzhang's sister-in-law; Ye Xiaomei, the daughter of Gu Shunzhang's aunt; Gu Weizhen, Gu Shunzhang's elder brother, who lived in an office as cook; Wu Yunlan, Gu Shunzhang's sister-in-law, who did cover and courier work; Wu Kechang, Wu Yunlan's younger brother, who did cover work; Wu Kechang's wife (name forgotten), who did cover work too; Dai Rongxin, from Yuyao in Zhejiang, clerk at the furniture shop; and the woman from Songjiang (name forgotten), who cooked at the furniture shop."
