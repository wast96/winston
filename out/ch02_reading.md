## Chapter 2. A Warm Current

Sakai is a town of temples. Some are great compounds set about wide gardens; some are crammed into a narrow patch of ground, a fortress town in miniature; and some are so plain and small you would take them for a shut-up private house.

Behind one of the small ones, the Honjuji, stood an outbuilding that might have been a priest's kitchen-quarters or else a barn. There Goemon sat against the wall, Maki gathered to his chest.

It was the close of Tenshō 9, 1581.

"They're all dead now. Every last man who kept me bound — gone, the whole pack of them. Gone."

He rubbed his chin and his cheek against Maki's soft hair, murmuring it over and over, low, half singing.

"Gone. Every one of them, gone."

His hand had gone in at the side of her robe. It moved over the two small firm risings of her breasts, stroking them, kneading them. A Nanban poet, somewhere far off, had once sung of such things: hard as marble, soft as velvet. Goemon knew nothing of that. But his palm knew the feel of them, surely and keenly. At the crown, the bud that should have stood there did not. Only a small dimple in the smooth curve met his fingers. For all his coaxing it slept on, quiet as a little girl.

"A treasure," Goemon breathed. "A treasure."

"What is?" The words came from between Maki's small lips, where the blood showed bright through the thin skin.

"This is." He pressed with his palm.

"That hurts," she breathed.

A faint damp of sweat touched his wrist. Just there, so fine you would have to hunt to find it, grew a little down like the waste of raw silk; he knew it was there. That too was a treasure. His wrist lay in the hollow under her arm.

Was this thing Goemon felt now, this weave of sensation and feeling? Was it lust? Is it a thing a man ought to be shy of setting down?

No. It was not. These feelings were of a piece with the girl herself: with Maki, who for all the hard corner life had thrown her into was still guileless, still unable to suspect a soul. They belonged to her heart and her nature, and they mattered.

In those same years the Kirishitan faith was breaking over Japan like a tidal wave, and it came with its own strict rules for the wiping-out of sin. The sixth commandment: thou shalt not commit lewdness. The ninth: thou shalt not covet another man's wife. Its confessors drove the question home. Even if you never did the thing, did you want it in your heart? Did you turn the thought over with pleasure, and let it stay? And if ever the answer was yes, they charged you to own it plainly. But nowhere did the faith forbid a man to find a woman beautiful, or to love his own wife.

And Goemon, in any case, had never once so much as brushed against such teaching. No commandment bound him.

But the way he was drowning now in this girl, in Maki, was nothing like the many women before her. Take his feeling apart and you could sort it, of course, into the spiritual and the bodily. Yet what he felt for Maki's body was not mere appetite.

Put it this way, perhaps, and it comes clear. These small hard breasts would one day bear a child, and in its service swell heavy and slack, and in the end wither and hang; and Goemon would have loved them at every turn of that change. In the life the two of them led afterward, this was borne out.

So however closely I set down Maki's body, and Goemon's sensations and feelings toward it, there is nothing underhand in it. When a man and a woman stand where these two stood, the flesh and the spirit lock together past prying apart, and each lifts and strengthens the other. That, by the very nature of the creature called man, is the most natural of conditions, and the most to be wished for. Say rather that man is the one animal that has, at long last, contrived to climb so high.

Goemon's days slid by in a warm half-doze. His money, the gold he had lifted in Kyoto, still lay buried, and plenty of it, in the grounds of the Jōfukuji, a chō or so from the house. There was nothing he was obliged to do. He would walk the roads where red-haired foreigners and Chinese came and went, out onto the coast of white sand and green pine, and stand looking over the Chinu Sea as its waves came rolling in their slow, heavy swell.

{p} on the sea of Chinu, my sleeve was drenched;
{p} I wring it out, and still it will not dry.

That this poem stands in the Man'yōshū was a thing Goemon could not have known. But every time he came back and walked down to this shore, he hunted out a few shells for Maki and slipped them into his sleeve. And every time he came home, he gathered Maki to his chest.

"Do you love me, Maki?"

"I do."

"You're mine, Maki. And I'm yours."

"That's right. Tahei belongs to Maki. And Maki belongs to Tahei."

He had told her he was one Tahei, the son of a big landholder in Nabari, over in Iga, cast out after a quarrel with his father. That was the tale he had put in her head.

"Your people must be worried sick, then."

"Mm. I'm the eldest son."

"And if you said you were sorry, they'd still not have you back?"

"Mm. I could try apologizing, I suppose. You said you were Kishū-born, Maki, didn't you?"

"Yes. Saiga."

"So where's Saiga? Down near the sea? There's that Saigazaki pole-line, one rod to a man, and they bring up sea bream and bonito and horse mackerel. Famous for it, they say."

"My own home's far up the Tomobuchi River, a place they call Hosono."

"In the hills, then?"

"Yes."

"That hill country's no good. Nothing but poor farmers."

"That's right. Dirt poor. That's why I couldn't stay at home—"

"Wait. Up around there — your house wouldn't be Ikkō sect, by any chance?"

"Yes. And when I was a little thing, five or six, the Negoro-ji monks were forever coming down on us, knocking us about and burning the house, and we'd run for it."

"So you're one for the Namu Amida Butsu too?"

"They put me out to service when I was small, so I never really learned to pray."

"Do you want to go home?"

"Yes. But even if I went back there'd be nothing to eat. I would like to see my mother and father, though—"

"There, there. One of these days I'll take you back and let you see them. What Maki wants is what I want too."

"—"

Had one certain thing not happened, then whatever Nobunaga did and whatever became of Ieyasu, this married life of theirs would have gone on flowing quietly and warm through the back streets of the town, just as it was.

But one night Goemon went to the grounds of the Jōfukuji, dug at the usual spot, and found the gold gone, jar and all.

He had always been careful about the digging here. So the man who had lifted the jar was beyond doubt a ninja, and no mean one. Someone had come from somewhere and shadowed him.

The old Goemon would have thought nothing of losing it. To him, any daimyō's house and any rich man's house alike stood as good as flung open; he had only to walk in and help himself.

But somehow the will to steal had gone out of the Goemon of now. It was as though, once he did such a thing, he would not be able to look Maki in the face. No one had said a word to him. He simply had no heart for it.

And yet, for the present, there was no way but stealing to keep hunger off.

So one night he broke into a Buddhist-goods shop in Zaimoku-chō.

The place, it seemed, did not only sell altar-fittings; it carved and sold Buddhist images as well. In a broad plank-floored workroom stood household altars and shrine-cabinets, and images great and small in the middle of being cut. The smell of raw wood, of lacquer and hide-glue, hung heavy on the air.

He lit the cloth-torch he carried ready. It was five or six well-dried reeds bound together, wound in cotton cloth, then painted over and over, five times, six, with a tacky wash of camphor and oil, and dried hard.

Goemon remembered the days he had spent up in the tower of the great gate of Nanzen-ji, hemmed round by dry-lacquer Buddhas. He had slipped into any number of temples besides, so images were fairly familiar company to him.

But one of them stopped him.

A beautiful woman, the like of which he had never seen, sat before him. That alone, and she might have been Kisshōten, or Benzaiten. But no: at her breast, on her knees, down her very skirts, babies swarmed and clung, a wriggling knot of them.

Goemon was drawn to the image, some four shaku high, and stood a long while face to face with it.

What were all these babies? The woman's face was Maki's. Yet he had never had anything to do with a brood of infants. He had never lived among children, never stopped to look at one, never once supposed that any of them had the least thread of connection to himself.

And here, now, was Maki, mobbed by babies. A pairing he could never in his life have dreamed.

Goemon, of course, had no way of knowing what she was: the goddess Kariteimo, called by common people Kishimojin. Mother of a thousand children, she had been in the beginning a fiend who seized and devoured the children of others, until, chastened by the Buddha, she turned with her own thousand to the true Law and became the guardian of children, so that the childless who pray to her conceive, and the ailing child who prays is made whole.

He forgot all about stealing. Dumbstruck by this yoking of Maki and children, he stood before the image. Then his eye moved to something set at an angle behind it, and he had to catch his breath again.

There stood what seemed a copy of this Kishimojin. Only, it held a single baby on its knee, and there was no pomegranate in its right hand.

Look closer, and there were differences still. The hair was painted gold, and the black of the eyes was tinted blue. And where Kishimojin only bound her hair up on top, this figure wore a small and beautiful crown.

This face, too, was like Maki's.

Goemon reached out a soft hand and touched the breast, the baby, and then the face.

Of course there was no way he could have known it for what it was: a Santa Maria holding the child Jezus Kirishito, carved by the image-maker Yazaemon on a commission from the Kirishitan church that Takayama Ukon had raised at Takatsuki.

As he lifted his fingertips from the face, a cry all but broke from him. The paint could not have been dry. The red of the lips had smeared, and from cheek to chin the face was one wash of scarlet.

Maki was drenched in blood!

He snatched out a three-shaku hand-cloth and wiped at it. But the white shell-white and the red ran together, and it turned to a hideous ruin, as if the skin had been flayed and the raw flesh laid bare.

A cry came out of Goemon's throat as though wrung from him.

He heard the startled household stirring awake. In haste he killed the cloth-torch and fled.

Home again, he stroked the face of Maki, who slept knowing nothing of it, and only then was his heart quiet.

The thing sat so ill with him that he let two nights pass without stirring out, and did not go until the third.

A sleeping-draught would have made it safe, but he kept no store of such a thing.

If he was going to break in at all, he settled on the mansion of the great merchant Naya Sukezaemon, in Ebisu-machi at the very heart of Sakai.

Sakai was strongly defended. In a letter of 1562, Padre Gaspar Vilela set down that the ways in and out of the town had heavy gates, and that on its three landward sides it was "girt about with a deep moat, ever brimming with water"; and the History of the Western Religion in Japan speaks likewise of "a great moat, a marvel to the eye."

Vilela reported, too, that the city was "governed, like Venice, by consuls."

In Venice, in Italy, the Great Council sat at the base; above it the Council of Ten, which carried on the government of the city; and at the summit the Doge, head of the Venetian Republic, and his counsellors, whose powers were a form and little more.

Sakai, in form, was to be ruled by the Hatakeyama as its shugo. In fact it was run by thirty-six egōshū, chosen from among the townsmen; and the ten strongest of these were called the nayashū.

The nayashū were the merchant princes who kept naya, warehouses, along the shore, and let them out for hire.

These men took on the masterless samurai who came drifting into the town as their own soldiery: half pirate, part landed baron, part clan grandee.

Self-governing the town might be, but not by any common will of its people. The powerful gripped its government and ran it as they pleased. Yet Frois had the right of it when he called Sakai "a kind of commonwealth, built wholly upon the townsmen."

But when Nobunaga made himself master of the realm, he laid on Sakai a land-tax of a harshness without precedent. Ten spokesmen of the wholesale houses came to beg that it be eased; he flung them into prison. Eight of them took their own lives; the two who fled were run down, and their severed heads set up for show at the north gate of the town.

So Sakai passed into Nobunaga's own hands as directly-governed land, with Matsui Yūkan set over it as magistrate; and when the siege of the Ishiyama Honganji broke out soon after, the town served him well as a base.

On Nobunaga's secret order, Kuki Yoshitaka built ten iron ships. Momochi Sandayū had once stared at them from afar and been struck dumb with awe. Those ships swept the Mōri navy from the water, and it was out of Sakai that they did it.

At that, the merchant princes of Sakai bought their survival by making friends with Nobunaga.

Naya Sukezaemon was one of them. He curried favor with gifts of the rare treasures his trade brought in. He was a man of huge nerve. Taking ship himself, he rode out ten thousand leagues of sea, and in 1593, twelve years after Goemon broke into his house, he crossed to Luzon at the head of a hundred-odd rōnin, cowed the lord of that country into suing for peace, and sailed home with his holds crammed full of rare and curious wealth. From that he won the byname Luzon Sukezaemon.

His mansion was for all the world a little castle, ringed with stone ramparts and plastered walls and set with a watchtower. Rather than break into the house itself, packed as it was with servants and rōnin, Goemon set his sights on the storehouse that reared up behind it. With a big shikoro and a big kunai he cut through the earthen wall and slipped in without trouble.

The trade goods were kept in the naya down along the shore. What stood here seemed to be only the rare pieces meant as gifts for men of power, and the treasure the house held for itself. Pelts of strange beasts no one could name; fragrant woods; woven cloth; Portuguese muskets, great swords, spears, suits of armor; bedsteads, tables, chairs; jars of every kind of liquor; cut-glass goblets; bottles of spice and scent. All of it stood packed and ranged tight, one thing hard against the next.

Goemon went round the hoard wide-eyed, but it was cash he was after. He put his eye to a gap in the wall. A man of fifty-five or six, his hair gone half gray, lay with his oily face turned straight up, snoring as though the world held no one but himself. For an old man his color was frighteningly good, and his red mouth hung wide. Three futon were spread side by side, and on either side of him slept a girl of sixteen or seventeen. His right hand held the left arm of the girl on his right, his left hand the right arm of the girl on his left, gripped fast, as if he would not let them get away.

Even asleep, in his face and in his whole shape, the man's stubborn, bullish temper showed plain. Here was nothing like a samurai or a ninja. And this one was sated, content, afraid of no one. There is no easier thing in the world than robbing a man like that.

Goemon moved through the room at his leisure, went through the bedside and the wall-cupboards, and carried off a fat haul of gold-dust bags and bars of gold.

What he took from Naya Sukezaemon kept the two of them fed a year or two.

Had Goemon gone back, then, to his old untroubled life? On the face of it, yes. But two strange things had dropped into his heart, plink, and sunk to the bottom, and at odd moments, without warning, they would bob up again and startle him.

— Maki, with a baby in her arms.

— Maki, drenched in blood.

At such times he would take himself off to the Myōkoku-ji, a great Nichiren temple standing about midway down the east side of Sakai. In its garden grew what is said to be the great sotetsu that the lord Miyoshi Yukiyasu gave when the holy Nikkō founded the temple in Eiroku 5, 1562. It is a plant not native to Japan, carried here from Ming China by way of the Ryūkyūs.

One jō three shaku high, one jō eight shaku round at the root, that strange and teeming shape crouched there in silence under the moon. The sight of it would take Goemon by the chest and drive every other thought out of him. He did not know it for what it was: a voice in his own blood, his far-off ancestors calling; homesickness, in a word.

Nobunaga had heard of this rare sotetsu and would have it, come what may. He had it moved to the garden at Azuchi, called his daimyō together, and feasted the whole day through, gazing at it. But in the dead of night, once everyone had withdrawn, the garden hill shook of a sudden, the pond broke into a chattering roar of waves, and the whole place turned fearful. Out of the dark came a cracked, hoarse voice: "I want to go back to Myōkoku-ji. I want to go back to Myōkoku-ji."

Nobunaga woke. "Who is that! Go and look!" At his order the young pages of the guard searched the garden, but there was no one to be seen. The voice was coming from the trunk of the sotetsu itself.

"A haunted tree! Cut it down!" At the command the young men drew their swords as one and made to close on it — and their whole bodies seized up, and down they went on their backs with a thud.

"Bah! Spineless fools!" Nobunaga swept out his own sword and started forward. On the instant his eyes swam, his arm went dead, and his legs would not carry him. In a rage he made to fling the blade at the sotetsu; it flew not three sun and dropped straight to the ground.

By then the night had fully lifted, and Nobunaga put the matter to the Kyoto diviner Abe Hisanobu. Morning and evening, said the diviner, this sotetsu had listened to the sutras at the temple and been steeped in the savor of the Law; it could not forget the taste, and so it pined for the temple. Best return it to Sakai at once. So Nobunaga, sorely against his will, sent the sotetsu he had gone to such trouble to bring all the way to Azuchi back again to the Myōkoku-ji.

The tale was handed down, and the fame of the Myōkoku-ji sotetsu rose higher than ever, until the Nichiren monks squared their shoulders and made a boast of it. Goemon had heard the story too. In the dead of a deserted midnight, under the winter moon, that tree from the mainland crouched black and tall; and if it carried some will of its own, and gave voice now and then, there was nothing strange in that at all.

Within its swollen trunk, sheathed as though in scales, a secret sap lay stored, and it reached clear out to the tips of the leaves, whose hard massed blades stood cutting the air.

Himself, and Maki, and the sotetsu, and the goddess the sculptor Yazaemon had cut: in Goemon's head they wound about one another, all of them, and breathed—

Now, no event in history can be understood from its surface alone. What went on behind it, and made the visible course of things fall out as it did, cannot be seen from the front. Every event in history keeps its true story hidden on the underside, out of the sight of ordinary people.

Take one instance: the Honnō-ji Incident. In the chapter called "New Waves," the author set down how Nobunaga came to die at the Honnō-ji as it looked from the surface, and looked, moreover, through the eyes of the foreign Kirishitan padres.

But now that Ishikawa Goemon has come on stage, we must look at that affair once more from behind. For there was one man who, at Nobunaga's very last, traded words with him and watched him die in the flames. Nobunaga burned to death at the Honnō-ji; his body was never even found. And that one man was none other than our Ishikawa Goemon.

Then, one day, it happened. The year had turned; it was the second month of Tenshō 10. Goemon was drifting through his days in a haze: cupping Maki's breast, tangling the sotetsu and the foreign goddess together in his head, living on the gold he had lifted from Naya Sukezaemon. And a medicine-peddler came and stood at the door of his house.

"I'm a medicine-peddler out of Aburahi, in the land of Kōga. As your honor knows, ever since the old days of the Nara capital, Aburahi has been famous the length of Japan for its medicines, brought over from Ming China. Whatever the sickness, in any of the five members of the body, I've the very cure for it, all laid out and ready." It was a practiced patter.

Goemon pricked up his ears. Aburahi lay in Kōga, true, but hard against the Iga border, a bare two and a half ri from Ishikawa, the village where Goemon was born.

It was as the peddler said. Back in the days of the Nara court, artisans had been brought over from the mainland and settled there to raise medicinal herbs, and to make gunpowder as well. The powder the ninja used had come, first and last, out of that village. Goemon's own father had learned the making of it there. And later, while he was at work in the powder-shed on the hill behind the Momochi mansion at Ryūkō, Sandayū had set the powder alight and blown him to death.

When the ninja of Kōga and Iga went out into other provinces, they used an art called shichihōde, the seven ways out. They would take on any of seven guises: a priest, a mountain ascetic, a flute-playing komusō, a street conjuror, a sarugaku player, a merchant, or plain everyday dress.

And of course they did not merely alter how they looked. They became the man, wholly. There is a tale about this, called "the ferryman's lore." A certain jōnin up in the Hokuriku, before he would grant one of his genin the full license of the art, set him a test. At a certain crossing there worked an old ferryman, fifty years at the oar, with a shrewd eye for people, who would fall into all manner of talk with those who boarded his boat. The jōnin struck a bargain with him: see through the disguise, and there would be a reward in it. And only to the genin whom even that old man's eye could not pierce did he grant the full mastery.

The medicine-peddler fell under the "merchant" head of the seven ways out, a guise the ninja were fond of; so Goemon had every reason to prick up his ears. And the peddler's speech was pure Kōga, the very tongue of a Kōga man, such as no outsider, however practiced, could ever bring off.

"Aburahi medicine, is it?"

So saying, Goemon drew both hands out from the side of Maki's robe, tucked them into his own breast to keep the warmth in them, and came lumbering out to the door.

A dark-faced man of about forty stood there, with big rolling eyes. A man untrained in ninjutsu would never have caught it, but Goemon knew at a glance what the color of that face was: persimmon tannin, soot, and oil, worked together and rubbed on. Peel the color away in the mind's eye, set a pale skin in its place, and there it was: a face he had seen by night ten years before, in the days when he lived at the Momochi mansion.

In the years when the author was forever being shadowed by detectives, and locked up in police cells, and watching the detectives at their work morning and night, he used to marvel at the memory these men have for a face, and cluck his tongue over it.

For my own part, in that line, I am something of a cripple. I cannot keep even the faces of the actors who have spent seven or eight years in my own theater troupe. Still less, watching the television plays these days, can I ever place the actors who come on as the detective or the culprit.

It is a real torment, this failing. Someone greets me somewhere. Since I plainly ought to know them well, I can hardly say, "And you are...?" So I break into a cold sweat and cast about, in agonies, for some way to carry it off. "Oh, him — you know, So-and-so, who was at our studio four years ago," my wife announces, out loud; and still I cannot for my life place him. After no end of floundering, from some phrase the man has let drop, and by asking my wife about it afterward, I sometimes settle on a guess and grope my way to it: "Ah, so that was who it was!" And nearly every time it turns out to have been someone else altogether.

For a year or so now I have gone back, in odd free hours, to painting in oils, and I find I have no wish to paint anything but the human face. Any face at all, mind, so long as it is a human face, my interest quickens and the itch to paint comes over me. Perhaps this is because, for me, the memory of a face is a thing that melts like foam on water, a matter of the passing instant. Only by working days on end, stroke by stroke, carrying a face over in fine detail onto canvas or board with the brush in my own hand, can I make it lodge at last in my memory. And even then, what stays is not the person's own face, but the face as it has become a painting.

A man like this could never make a detective. To be a detective, you must have the knack of never forgetting a face you have once seen. For a detective needs, on top of such gifts as cruelty, empty-headedness, insidiousness, low cunning, and a brass face, that one power above all: never to forget a face seen once.

Such a man turns every one of these gifts to a single use. Where others are giving themselves up in sacrifice, chasing an ideal, toiling on and on to build something toward the final happiness of mankind, he sidles close, ferrets out the secret of it, and works by any means to pull the thing down, to breed rot in it, to make it fail. And to that end he stops at nothing. He lies, he cheats, he puts about false rumor, he trumps up charges, he baits his hook with money and with women, and he lays on torture, cruel in body and cruel in mind.

This is what we call the spy's nature. And the ninja, too, gives his whole life to the discipline of making that same nature his own.

So there was nothing strange in Goemon's seeing through the disguise to the face of Hattori Hanzō, a face he had laid eyes on just once, ten years before.

"You've placed me, then?" said the peddler, reading the faint change that had crossed Goemon's face.

"Mm."

Goemon nodded and jerked his chin: come up, then. Hanzō slipped off his straw sandals and stepped up into the house. It was a place of only three ken. Maki had already melted out of sight from the inner room.

Now, the Hattori were sprung in the beginning from the Hata, the clan that had come over from the mainland, a house that had carried to Japan the puppetry and the shows of the Western Regions. Even before the age of the Genji and the Heike they had become the head house of the ninjutsu masters, and Hattori Heinai-zaemon had served Taira no Tomomori. With the fall of the Heike they fled to the land of Iga, and there rose to a kind of chieftaincy over the Iga ninja.

The Aekuni Shrine, which opened the earlier novel The Stealthy Ones, already stood in those days. Among its crowd of gods, deities of the naturalized mainland families and many another besides, the Iga ninja singled out two to enshrine as their own: Sukunahikona-no-mikoto and Kanayama-hime. The first of these was a god come over from the mainland: chief of staff, as it were, to Ōkuninushi-no-mikoto: the very Issun-bōshi, the One-Inch Boy, who crossed the sea from beyond in a rice-bowl, with a chopstick for an oar, a god small enough to sit in the palm of a hand. The story is a memory of a small-statured people who came across in dugout canoes, folk who might have sat in a man's hand.

Should you readers, for instance, fall into the fix of catching the pox, or some woman's ailment, with no medicine to touch it, then take a clean sheet of white paper, fold it in two lengthwise, and on the right half write:

{p} May the power of the medicine be fulfilled.
{p} Seiseiri Daimyōjin: heal this present sickness; an august purification.
{p} A body made sound.
{p} (Here, the age, and man or woman.)

and on the left half:

{p} I humbly call upon thee, Sukunahikona-no-mikoto: guard me and grant me blessing, I pray.
{p} Middle slip: Ōanamuchi-no-mikoto.
{p} Aoso Daimyōjin.

Then paste it to the great central pillar of the house and say the purification with a whole heart, and the sickness will be healed, a most marvelous thing, or so they say.

In time the Hattori shifted their seat to a spot not quite twenty chō north of Ueno, and it came to be called the Hattori village. And at the Aekuni Shrine festival held each twelfth month, they took charge of things: leading the ninja, shouldering the palanquins of Sukunahikona-no-mikoto and Kanayama-hime, and bearing them in procession down to the dry bed of the Tsuge River. For it the ninja put on their formal black, and so the festival was called the Kuroudo Matsuri, the Festival of the Black Party.

So for a time they were a sort of head house of the Iga ninja. Around the years of the Ōnin War they served the Ashikaga and took the name Chigachi; and at last, in the generation of Hanzō's father, when the power of Momochi Sandayū and Fujibayashi Nagato-no-kami came to overshadow Iga, they went east and took service with the Tokugawa.

Seen this way, the Hattori were plainly a house that would not rest content, generation on generation, to end their days as independent squires shut away in these hills. They meant to serve some powerful daimyō and make their ambition good; and that ambition, it seems, was to hold the rank of samurai, and in time to become daimyō in their own right.

"A nasty piece of work, and not to be trusted a moment." So Goemon's father used to say of them, knitting his brows, spitting the words out. And when a master powder-maker, no mean ninja himself, spoke so, the man must indeed have been past handling by any common means: nasty, slippery, not to be trusted.

And here before Goemon stood that man's son. It stood to reason that, meeting him ten years back, Goemon had studied him with a wary eye; and no wonder the face had lain graven in his head ever since.

"It cost me no end of trouble, ferreting out where you'd got to," said Hanzō.

"And what did you want, finding me?"

"I'm in the service now of Lord Tokugawa Ieyasu, of Mikawa."

"That much I know."

"Lord Ieyasu says: how does Ishikawa Goemon fare these days? Seek him out and bring him to me. Those were his words."

Even Goemon was taken aback at that.

"What? Lord Ieyasu — bring me to him?"

"Just so. Surprised, are you? Lord Ieyasu knows your name well enough."

"He is not one to go dashing about, our Lord Ieyasu, the way Lord Nobunaga did, or Hideyoshi. He holds still and keeps his mouth shut. And all the while he sees clean to the bottom of things. He'll take the realm yet, one day; that is how I read him."

"Hm. But why me?"

"And so?"

"That lord of mine — this year, when the ceremonies of the first three days of the New Year were done and he was sitting at his ease, turned to me out of nowhere and asked how Goemon was faring. Now, Lord Ieyasu can never have set eyes on you. When I asked how it was he knew of you, he told me that ever since the year you made the name of the great thief your own in Kyoto, he had been gathering up every scrap of talk about you he could come by. It seems he's taken a great liking to you. 'Bring him to me, without fail' — such is his pleasure."

"No. I've no mind to hire myself out and work my ninjutsu for anyone again."

"Don't be so short about it. Lord Ieyasu is not a man to force anyone into his service. Come and see for yourself; if it's not to your liking, you leave. Hamamatsu's a fine place. Come, at the least, and get a close look at the foremost man under heaven, and see what manner of man he is. And if you find yourself won over, why, stay on at Hamamatsu. He'll give you a house in the castle town, so you can bring the good wife along too."

"I'm forever at his side, close as any of his own guard. And you, if you'll only be patient, will soon be where I am."

"—Mind, I've no more taste myself for the neck-or-nothing work."

"There, there. You needn't feel that way. Come as if for an outing, a bit of sightseeing; that will do nicely. I quit of my own will; you were made to quit. That's the difference between us. But either way we're both men struck off the ninja rolls. As for the fussy ninja precepts, I'll have none of them: seven ri off with the lot, and good riddance. Come, let's be going. Let's go."

Goemon brought Maki and went to Hamamatsu. He changed his name to Saeki Dennai, and for the present was set at thirty koku and three rations, and given a residence.

And going along to see for himself, Goemon found it true enough: Hanzō was a great pet of Lord Ieyasu's. To the lord's very face he would come right out with things, blunt as you please, and carry himself with a brazen ease that made a bystander's heart jump.

Hanzō took the tone of an old hand, and instructed him.

"Now mark me. If you want to get into a great man's good graces, it's no use just yes-yessing him, bobbing your head like a grasshopper and spinning about like a mouse in a wheel. Serve him with nothing but blind obedience, give him nothing but single-hearted loyalty, and he'll only despise you. He'll write you off as a man worth just so much and no more. No — every so often you must give him a jolt. Push right up to the edge, to the very line where he hangs between flaring up and holding back. To reckon just how far you can go before he flares: there's an art in that.

"The lord loses his temper for a moment. But there, playing him now hard, now soft, exactly as you please, you come in time to hold his weak points in your hand — or else some secret he'd sooner no one touched. And all the while, on the other side, you let him catch sight of a warm-hearted streak in you, the kind that makes a man think, well, now. That's the trick of it.

"The Goemon of your Sandayū days would have been leaning in by now, tickled past all bearing. A fellow like you: once you got the hang of it, you'd find it grand sport."

Nowadays the pride of Lake Hamana is its eel. Whether there were eels there in those days, no record anywhere tells; but if there were, Goemon would have caught them, cut them into rounds and grilled them, or dropped them into miso soup to boil. Eels aside, he surely landed black porgy and goby, young sea bass, sillago, flathead, and flounder. And suppose he did take an eel and cut it into rounds. Once, in China, the present writer was served a dish chopped into lengths, steamed, and painted along the cut faces with a raw, livid food-crimson; it looked for all the world like the hacked limbs of an infant, and killed my appetite outright.

"Eel again today?" Maki would grumble, wrinkling her nose. So their days ran on.

Then, out of the blue, an order came down through Hanzō, from Ieyasu. Nobunaga had driven into Kai, and the House of Takeda was on the very brink of ruin: go and see that last act through to its end. That was the command.

It was Goemon's first commission; and the death-throes of the Takeda, that great house of Kai, were a thing he would have wanted to witness with his own eyes unbidden. So he set out at once.

But he reached Kofuchū (Kōfu) on the twelfth of the third month, and Takeda Katsuyori, the day before, had already taken his own life at Mount Tenmoku and was dead.

Goemon got the story out of the country people. By their account, it was a bare nine years since Lord Shingen, at whose name the crying child fell silent, had died at Komaba, whether from the musket-ball he took in the earlobe, or from the poison of Kudobei, the foremost poison-maker in all Iga, the man who ended as a heap of maggots up in the rafters of Hōrai-ji. The Takeda had been dreaded across the realm for twenty-eight generations; and now, at their end, all had scattered and fled, and those who followed Katsuyori to Mount Tenmoku numbered a bare forty souls, his lady of nineteen and his heir of sixteen among them. The man who led the enemy to the spot was one Tsuji Yahei, a Takeda soldier until the day before, now gone over to the other side.

It is for just this reason that the Bansenshūkai, the secret manual of Iga ninjutsu, gives over so many pages, in such close detail, to the art of making a traitor inside the enemy's camp: for the blow that finishes a foe, always, is the traitor's.

Goemon meant to seize this chance for another close look at Nobunaga. He had not laid eyes on him since Tenshō 8, the fall of Ishiyama, when he had crouched in a wood near the Yodo River crossing and fired at him with a musket, and missed.

But Nobunaga, treating the campaign as a war already surely won, had left Azuchi on the fifth of the third month, and by the eleventh had got no farther than Iwamura in Mino. From there he came on through the Kiso valley and entered Takatō castle in Shinano on the eighteenth. There he settled and proclaimed the whole disposition of the old Takeda lands; and only on the second of the fourth month did he set foot at last in Kai, reaching Kofuchū at dawn on the third.

But a bare five ri east of there stood a great temple compound, the Kentokusan Erin-ji, revered by the Takeda house for generations.

It is well known that Shingen's war-banner bore, in gold on indigo silk one jō two shaku long, the words: swift as the wind, still as the forest, ravaging as fire, unmoved as the mountain. Those characters had been brushed by the abbot of this very Erin-ji, the priest Kaisen, Shingen's teacher from his boyhood.

Nobunaga's men ferreted out that Sasaki Jirō was hidden in the temple, the man once sent by the Ashikaga shōgun Yoshiaki as an envoy to beg Shingen to march against Nobunaga. Nobunaga threw a company around the temple and demanded that Sasaki be turned out at once. But Kaisen answered that he had no memory of sheltering any such man.

At that very hour Goemon lay hidden beneath the floor of the Unpō-ji at Kofuchū, which Nobunaga had made his headquarters, and heard, right over his head, the boom of Nobunaga's voice. It was a shrill voice, higher than a tenor, and withal sweetish and nasal.

"Into the temple! Rip up the floorboards, tear out the ceiling-boards, but find him!"

It was a voice with no patience in it, flinging its anger out on every side, rushing headlong.

At once some deep voice relayed the command:

"Tsuda Kurōjirō! Hasegawa Yojirō! Seki Kojūrō! Akaza Shichirō-uemon — get over there and hunt him out!"

"Aye!" came the answer, and the troops broke into a run.

Goemon crawled out from under the floor and ran, lost among the soldiers. He was already got up as a foot-soldier.

Erin-ji stood ringed close about with the giant trunks of old cryptomeria. In an instant came shouting, and shrieks, and the noise of things breaking. The soldiers stormed in and gave no quarter.

The temple monks, the boy-acolytes, and the young attendant-samurai, beaten out of the temple, all fled up into the great gate. Goemon, at some point, had hidden himself in the crown of the largest cryptomeria of all, and was watching. Nobunaga too had ridden out in person, and sat his horse a chō or so from the gate.

"Sasaki Jirō, it seems, has already fled. There is no sign of him."

"Hm. Let him slip through, did they? The vile, hateful shaveling! Burn them, gate and all — every last one, and let none live."

At that a warlord came forward before Nobunaga, his face pale, his eyes keen, his chin sharp.

"This temple is the foremost in the province, and the priest Kaisen is a master appointed by imperial decree. And this would be your first act of justice in newly-taken enemy country. A show of clemency, I should think, is much to be wished."

He said it gently, yet with the settled confidence of a man sure of his ground. The words struck even Goemon as just. And for that very reason Nobunaga was already writhing the veins at his brow, glaring at the man out of bloodshot eyes. As he made to speak, the man went on:

"And granting, even, that the chief monks were wicked monks: this temple holds, I am told, sutra-scrolls and ritual vessels, treasures handed down through the Three Countries, and even things belonging to His Majesty the Emperor. My lord, some years back, at Mount Hiei, burned a great store of rare and holy images and relics; and to this day, I hear, there are those who murmur against the deed. It is through Katsuyori's brutal misgovernment that the people's hearts turned from him, and this province has fallen into my lord's hand. I beg you, then, to take thought, and to act in such a way that all men will submit to you willingly. That, I humbly hold, would be the fitting course."

At this Nobunaga flung the whip in his hand full in the man's face, and roared.

"Silence, Mitsuhide! You were a masterless man once, who could scarce scrape together a day's food; it was by my mercy you rose, till now you hold the whole of Tanba, and Sakamoto in Gōshū, a daimyō of five hundred thousand koku and more! Loaded with such favor, you dare, here, before the whole muster of my samurai, to pick and cavil at my rule? It is insolence! It is treachery! Look at them — Hashiba of Chikuzen, Takigawa Sakon, the rest — in feats of arms and in loyalty a hundred times your betters; and not once, not once, has any of them called me unjust. Yet you, who set beside those two are no better than a worm — you! Bah! Hateful wretch!"

He sprang from his horse, strode up, seized Mitsuhide by the scruff with his left hand, and with his right fist beat him about the head, four blows, five.

"Lord Akechi. Get up."

Mori Ranmaru had come to his side, and spoke.

"Ran! Throw him out!"

Mitsuhide bowed his corpse-pale face and went away.

"Fire it..."

Nobunaga, back on his horse, gave the order.

The soldiers heaped firewood and dry grass beneath the great gate and put it to the flame. A wind sprang up just then; the fire leapt up in an instant, and the black smoke stood to heaven like a pillar. Those who at first had cowered together up in the tower: some flung themselves down and died at once, skulls burst; others, past bearing the smoke and the heat, came tumbling out with sobs and screams; some crawled on broken limbs. And the soldiers, waiting for them, ran them down and cut them dead.

Before long the gate burned through and fell, and in a little while more than eighty souls had been slaughtered to the last.

When the fire had died and they raked apart the ashes, "the priest Kaisen was found seated still in the full lotus, majestic, smoldering, in the very posture of a man passing into nirvana"; so the Shinsho Taikōki has it; and the Mikawa Go-Fudoki, too, records that "the abbot of the temple, Kaisen Chishō Kokushi, seated in Zen with his palms joined, passed away of himself." But in the tale as it is commonly told, up in the burning tower the priest Kōzan of Chōzen-ji turned to Kaisen and asked, "And enlightenment — how is it, here in the fire?" And Kaisen left behind that famous gāthā:

{p} For quiet meditation, no need of hills and streams:
{p} blot out the mind, and the fire itself is cool.

and burned to death in perfect calm, or so the story runs. The verse hangs today over the mountain gate of Erin-ji.

But all that Goemon, up in the cryptomeria, could see was the great gate wrapped in crimson-lotus flame and black smoke, and all he could hear was screaming. Like caterpillars scorched off a pole-end and dropping, the people came tumbling down, one and then another, plop, plop, their screams falling with them; and the soldiers would run up and cut them, or run them through. The gate became one pillar of blazing light; then, with a roar, it caved in, and sparks and flame went showering up against the crowns of the cryptomeria all around.

Watching it, Goemon felt the whole of that day a year before, the day Iga was wiped out, come flooding back into his chest. The burning of the Fujibayashi fort and the Momochi fort, the roasting alive of ninja beyond counting: all of it rose again before him. And yet, for Goemon, that day had also been a release from what had bound him hand and foot all his life. That he could live now in quiet happiness with Maki was, in itself, because of that day. Seen so, Nobunaga ought by rights to be reckoned his benefactor.

But this sight, the burning gate, turned his stomach. A single thought came surging up until it filled him: this is a man who must not be left alive. And that, no doubt, was because Goemon had struck his name from the ninja rolls, and, living beside Maki, had won back something like a human heart.

When he had seen the thing through to the end, he rode hard for Hamamatsu, went to Hanzō's house, and made his report. Hanzō heard him out.

"Hoh. Turned your stomach, did it? Well it might. The man is a very devil! Every day the longer he lives, he does that much more evil. Lord Ieyasu can't abide him either. But it's no use standing against a force at the flood, and my lord knows that well. — And yet, mind you. If a man were to cut Nobunaga down—"

"Aye. I saw that same Lord Mitsuhide reviled and beaten before the whole company."

"A man of single-hearted loyalty, but somehow he does not sit well with Nobunaga. Once he was a great favorite — Mitsuhide, Mitsuhide, and made much of — but this past year or so, all at once, Nobunaga has turned against him. Mitsuhide thinks too much of his lord's good, and remonstrates with him to his face. He shows his feeling straight out on his face: his whole look says, own your fault, I beg you, and mend it; and so he presses the admonishment home. The man is too honest by half. Now, a Hashiba of Chikuzen will remonstrate with a smile, turning the thing off onto some other matter, or feigning that he himself has got it wrong. But Lord Mitsuhide has no such artful footwork in him.

"A man as choleric as Nobunaga cannot but be galled by it. Whatever he sees and cannot, try as he will, let pass, he takes for his lord's own good; and because, risen as high as he now has, he holds it his bounden duty to admonish Nobunaga, why, admonish him he does. And so he has dug his own grave. Now, then. Reading the drift of Lord Ieyasu's mind, I have set a kunoichi among Nobunaga's waiting-women."

Goemon looked up, startled.

"Her name is Oyū. Tarao-born. Eighteen years old, but a stout-hearted, sharp-eyed woman. There is a young man too, one Sōda Yoichirō, Tarao-born, in the service of Yomoda Tajima-no-kami, a general under Lord Mitsuhide. He is no ninja; the two were playmates as children. But he is head over heels for Oyū, and so I have her work him. That much I'll tell you for now. And I want you, too, to keep your eyes on Nobunaga and never let go. Lord Ieyasu is a patient man. As for Nobunaga: the Takeda are dealt with, so he'll make his leisurely way home to the west, by one road or another. You may take a little rest awhile."

And sure enough, Nobunaga set out from Kofuchū on the tenth of the fourth month and made his triumphal progress west along the Tōkaidō, a journey of pleasure and sightseeing. The twelfth, Suruga-Ōmiya; the thirteenth, Ejiri castle; the fifteenth, Kakegawa castle in Tōtōmi; and on the sixteenth he reached Hamamatsu castle.

The moment Nobunaga entered Suruga, as the Shinsho Taikōki records, Ieyasu "saw to the roads and to the feasting at the post-stations, sparing nothing, doing all to perfection, heedless of the cost, and gave his whole heart to it." No man, Nobunaga felt, had ever shown such true devotion. Splendid! Should this lord come to Azuchi, he would feast him beyond all compare, and so repay the kindness. So he invited Ieyasu to Azuchi for his pleasure, and gave Mitsuhide the office of host.

That he should entrust him with so weighty a charge suggests Nobunaga did not, after all, hate Mitsuhide so very deeply. Even granting that he felt some guilt over the beating before the gate of Erin-ji, and meant the office for amends, it was too great a charge for that. Mitsuhide, for his part, was moved, his lord trusted him after all, and threw himself into the work with all his might.

Nobunaga came home through the province of Owari and made his triumphal entry into Azuchi on the twenty-first. Mitsuhide returned in his train. But the business lay piled mountain-high. An army was to be raised against Chōsokabe Motochika in Shikoku; reinforcements were to go to Hideyoshi, who was closing on Takamatsu castle in Bitchū. With no knowing when the order might come, Mitsuhide did not go back to his own castle at Sakamoto, but stayed on at Azuchi.

On the shore, in the lee of the embankment, a young man and woman stood close together. Lake Biwa lay smoking with haze; the trees were pushing out their first buds. The woman wore the deep katsugi of a daimyō's waiting-lady drawn over her head. The man, a samurai who looked to be a squad-captain or a little above, had thick brows and full lips, and was handsome enough. It was the hour of dusk.

"Yesterday I overheard Lord Mori Ranmaru speaking to his younger brother, Lord Rikimaru."

"Mm — and?"

"This great office they have given Lord Akechi: just because it is so great a charge, they said, a man who wanted to find fault could find no end of it. And that is exactly what our lord is counting on."

"Hm. So that is the way of it. But you know, Oyū, Lord Mitsuhide is alive to that himself. To give such faithful service and still not be understood, to be hated for it — it is a bitter thing. But the gods and buddhas see all, and every one of his retainers prays with a single heart. This banquet, you'll see, he will carry off to perfection."

"Pray for it — how could I not? It touches your own dear lord. And this time, surely, His Lordship will soften toward him."

Yoichirō watched Oyū's lips, like plum petals, open and close. Lips that said nothing but sweet, happy things: precious lips. He gazed at them, lost. And her plump, soft hand he gathered up in both of his, like a treasure.

Oyū leaned that sweet-scented hair against Yoichirō's chest. And so the two stayed a long while, without stirring.

The next evening, in a corridor of the inner palace of the main keep at Azuchi, Mori Ranmaru came suddenly upon Oyū.

Ranmaru was a page of Nobunaga's, eighteen that year. He and his brothers, Bōmaru of seventeen and Rikimaru of sixteen, all served Nobunaga; and of the three it was Ranmaru whom Nobunaga loved best, who was always in attendance in the bedchamber. His eyes were fiercely long, and slanted a little upward; his nose was fine and high; but his lips were full and fleshy, and gave him a look of manliness. The same contradiction ran through his body. His skin was white, close-grained, moist with a sheen of oil, yet his frame was hard and sinewy, and he stood a good sun taller than Nobunaga.

"My lord Ran," Oyū called, stopping him.

Ranmaru was Nobunaga's catamite, and already a seasoned hand in the love of men; but a woman he had never touched. So now, when this ripe young woman, who plainly knew what love was, turned her brimming smile on him, he caught his breath in spite of himself. Yet for the pride of being Nobunaga's own favorite, he kept his cold face unbroken.

"What do you want?" he asked.

Oyū's face turned all at once grave. She shot a quick glance about her and leaned closer.

The truth was that Ranmaru had, in this way, got from her again and again his intelligence about Mitsuhide, and knew, from all his past dealings, that what she gave him ran true. He knew, too, the channel it came by.

To carry it to Nobunaga's ear he counted as loyal service to his lord. And this woman too, he supposed, who by chance had a childhood friend among Mitsuhide's retainers and got her news from him, must likewise hold it a loyalty to pass it on, through Ranmaru, to her own lord.

A sweet fragrance teased at his senses.

"Lord Akechi, they say, grieves that all his faithful service should go unregarded — if it must be so, then, he thinks — and has begun, in his bitterness, to open a private understanding with Lord Ieyasu. Messengers have already gone three times between him and Hamamatsu."

"Mm. I understand."

With a nod, Ranmaru moved off.

That night, in the wood behind the Seminario at Azuchi, a man in the black garb of a ninja lay in wait. The eyes that showed between the folds of his hood sat deep in their sockets, and the whites of them were a sickly yellow. The face was very long, the cheekbones sharp. At which the sharp-eyed reader will already have known him: Moriichirō, heir of Torii Kihachirō of Ōhara, one of the three young Kōga ninja who had entered the Azuchi Seminario as students. And the forefinger of his right hand was gone at the root.

Oyū came up beside him and passed on the talk she had had with Ranmaru that evening. At once Moriichirō darted away, straight to the east.

On the eleventh of the fifth month, Ieyasu, answering Nobunaga's invitation, set out for the capital with a small retinue, and was to reach Azuchi on the fifteenth.

At the lodging, the Daihōbō, Mitsuhide had shut himself up for days beforehand, preparing with all his soul. He summoned the Ōkusa cooks from Shijō in Kyoto and spared no gold or silver on the ingredients; and for gifts to the lord of Hamamatsu he set out, unstinting, the treasures of the Akechi house: a Kuniyuki great-sword, a Kunitoshi blade, a Yoshimitsu dagger, an unsigned tenmoku bowl brought over in the days of the Song, a Korean tea-bowl, and the like.

On the morning of the very day the guest was to come that evening, Nobunaga appeared suddenly at the Daihōbō, on the pretext of an inspection. As he went about looking, a fury boiled up in him. He summoned Mitsuhide.

"Just as I thought! The lord of Hamamatsu is a Junior Fourth Rank, Upper, Lesser Captain of the Right Guard; I am Minister of the Right, Junior Second Rank. And you lay on this outrageous fuss, as though we awaited the coming of the shōgun himself! What is the meaning of it? Mitsuhide — you nurse a grievance for having crossed me, and you mean to make a friend of Lord Ieyasu and have him at your back some day. So that is your game! Enough! The office of host I give to Niwa Nagahide. Get you to Bitchū, and fight the Mōri under Hideyoshi's command!"

He rattled it all out in one breath, and stamped away.

Mitsuhide went back first to Sakamoto castle in Ōmi, and then, on the twenty-sixth, into Kameyama castle in Tanba, where his wife and children were, and set about preparing to march to Bitchū.

Ieyasu reached Azuchi without mishap. On the nineteenth he was shown the dancing of Kōwaka Hachirō-dayū, on the twentieth the Noh of Umewaka-dayū, and on the twenty-first he set out to see the sights of Kyoto, Osaka, Nara, and Sakai. Nobunaga, meanwhile, on the twenty-ninth, left Azuchi and entered the Honnō-ji in Kyoto, meaning to meet Ieyasu here on his way back from the western tour.

Goemon's mind kept coming back to Mitsuhide's movements.

On the first of the sixth month, in the great hall of Kameyama castle, the Akechi kin and retainers gathered, and a council of war was opened. But nothing came of it such as Goemon had hoped: they drew up the order of battle for the march against the Mōri, made their dispositions, and held the departure feast with kelp and dried chestnuts and cold sake; and at the hour of the cock, six in the evening, the whole force, thirteen thousand in all, poured out through the castle gate.

But when they had gone a bare fifteen chō, Mitsuhide halted the army, and taking with him only five of his elder retainers, Akechi Yaheiji Hidemitsu among them, went into the main hall of a ruined temple at a place called Jōno. Goemon slipped at once beneath its floor. From over his head came Mitsuhide's voice, broken and fragmentary.

"...Lord Nobunaga... is a man there is no relying on... Everything I do, he takes the worst way, always the worst... And if that is how he will have it, then let it come true... In the end, no loyalty of mine... I have made up my mind... whatever you may say, no longer... Now, when Nobunaga has with him only a handful of attendants... a matchless chance, given by Heaven... do you hear... the realm, into our hands... the realm is ours to take —"

The last came loud, taut with resolve. Then, for a while, nothing.

Goemon felt his own chest leap with a fearful pounding. The thing he had half-feared had come at last. The accursed life of Nobunaga was now, by this man's hand, about to be cut off.

Sweat ran streaming down Goemon's brow and under his arms. It was close and airless, without a breath of wind. Clouds crept slowly across the face of the moon.

The Akechi army snapped up its nine banners, bellflower on a blue ground, and began to march to the southeast. The men had been told they were to be reviewed by Nobunaga. At a slow pace the main body went up from Hozu into the hills, passed Mizunoo on its flank, and crossed the Kiyotaki River. Here Mitsuhide halted the columns and told the whole army, for the first time: "The enemy is at the Honnō-ji."

And in the next instant a soundless cry swept over them all. As though struck by a current, the whole army stood rooted a moment. Knowing that the hour to overturn the realm was now upon them, every officer and man shuddered.

After waiting here a while, they pushed on into the streets of Kyoto. Just at that time, at the Nanban-ji, as was told at the opening of the first chapter, Padre Calião was waking to say the early-morning Mass.

A full half-koku before Mitsuhide's army reached the outer wall of the Honnō-ji, Goemon was already up above the ceiling there, moving about, taking the bearings of where Nobunaga slept. And into the space beneath the floor he had carried firewood. Nobunaga's guards, in the sweltering heat, lay sprawled asleep by the cool of the pond; and now, near dawn, when they had only just fallen into a deep sleep, not one of them marked Goemon at his work.

The moment the first war-cry went up outside the wall, and the muskets opened fire, Goemon set light to the firewood beneath the floor.

So Nobunaga, an arrow in his shoulder and a bullet through his thigh, barely got the sliding door shut and staggered into his sleeping-chamber; and Goemon had already gone round above the ceiling, watching him from overhead with scorching eyes.

Nobunaga's corpse was never found, not even among the ashes. No one but Goemon ever saw him after he slid that door shut and fled to the inner room; and Goemon told no one but Maki, and Maki told no one at all. It follows, as it must, that to this day not a soul knows how Nobunaga met his end.

In the second room back from the main hall, Nobunaga tore the sleeve from his white-silk night-robe and bound his thigh with it. And at that, out of the dark above, a great burst of laughter came raining down.

"Wa-ha-ha-ha!"

Nobunaga looked up with a start. One coffer of the high panelled ceiling had gone black, a hole, and the voice was falling out of it. The dim candle-light did not reach so far.

"Who's there!" Nobunaga shouted. But for the deep wound in his thigh, he could not get to his feet.

The voice came back down out of the hole.

"Wa-ha-ha-ha! Your whole wicked, lawless life — and now, at last, here it ends!"

"Who are you? Show yourself!"

"Down you go, into Avīci, the hell of no respite, there to be racked in torment for all eternity! Wa-ha-ha-ha! Now do you understand? Ah — this is sweet."

Just then Nobunaga started. Smoke was jetting up out of the matting of the very room he sat in. A roar came; the heat climbed; already the space beneath the floor must be one sea of fire.

Out of the hole in the ceiling a black shape came floating down and stood bolt upright before Nobunaga's eyes. From the black hood, only the eyes showed.

"Name yourself! Show your face!"

"Name myself, show my face — you would know me for no one. At Mount Hiei you burned a thousand six hundred souls to death, monk and layman alike. At Ise Nagashima you put fourteen thousand to the sword, old and young, man and woman, and left none living. At Erin-ji you burned better than eighty. And beyond these, on battlefields the length of Japan, how many hundreds of thousands of the old and the young, who were no soldiers, have you butchered? And last year, in Iga — what did you do there? What manner of thing were you ever born to be? You buried your captives in the earth and had their necks sawn through with a saw of bamboo; you nailed them up and had them pricked to death by inches; you strung them on the trees and burned them living. How many souls, and how bitterly, do you think, have suffered for your sake? Bah! Now — know it!"

Goemon drew his sword and gave Nobunaga's breast a light thrust.

Nobunaga snatched up the sword at his side. But he could not rise, and so he flung it at the intruder. Goemon twisted aside, and the blade drove deep into the cedar door behind him.

Goemon, with a scornful laugh, slashed clean across Nobunaga's outstretched right arm. From elbow to middle finger the inner side opened in one long clean line, and the blood came spurting out in a single slanting jet and slapped down on the mats. From the seam of the matting the flames came licking up, and lapped at Nobunaga's right knee. He grunted — "Ngh!" — and threw himself over onto his left side; and his right ankle, flung up into the air, was struck away by a swift running stroke of Goemon's sword.

The shriek Nobunaga gave — "Gyaaah!" — came out of a face that was no longer human. It was the face of a beast torn open by terror. At that face Goemon's sword ran slantwise. The thin, sharp ridge of Nobunaga's nose, his pride, was laid open aslant from above the nostrils, and the lower half of his face sagged loose, like the jaw of a mask sprung off its hinge. The two eyes, all that was left whole in that face, turned in that instant to a look of wretchedness, of near-weeping, of a creature begging for pity. Into each of them, one and then the other, a throwing-spike drove home and stuck fast.

Then Goemon stepped back, drew Nobunaga's sword out of the cedar door, and stood leaning on it like a staff, and watched. The flames from below caught in Nobunaga's night-robe. What lay there was no Minister of the Right, no master of the realm's power: it was one creature at the point of death, giving off the reek of its own scorching flesh, spitting its groans out along with its blood, a beast at the edge of death that rolled and thrashed upon the floor.

When even the strength to thrash was gone, the floor burned through and gave way, and, throwing up a great roar of sparks into the room, Nobunaga's shape dropped away into the dark below. In the very instant he vanished, Goemon's right arm flashed, and Nobunaga's trunk was run clean through the middle, on his own sword.

A scream — "Gyaaah!" — broke out of Goemon, though he had meant it for a laugh. On the force of it he ran straight up the wall, reached the transom, sprang from there, and was gone through the hole in the coffered ceiling. The fire had not yet climbed to the high ceiling.

It was at this moment that Mitsuhide's men, Shinshi Rokurō-dayū at their head, came surging toward the room. But the instant they shoved the cedar door open, the flames burst out at them and drove the whole party back.

Day was breaking.

Just at that hour, at the Myōkoku-ji in Sakai, Ieyasu woke, meaning to go back to Kyoto that day. And by the road told of in the earlier chapter he came through the perils of the Iga crossing; and after a brief tryst with Hatsuko on the beach at Shiroko in Ise, he made his escape at last back to Hamamatsu.

As for Goemon: once clear of the Honnō-ji, he changed into a peasant's clothes and, now ambling as though half asleep, now breaking into a hurry, took the road as the day opened wide: through Ōtsu and Minakuchi, over the Suzuka pass, eastward, and ever eastward. The horses of the messengers galloping out from Kyoto in every direction, to carry word of the Honnō-ji affair to the daimyō of the provinces, had not yet caught up with him.

In the face of so vast an event, it was as though, for one moment, the whole world had gone numb.

Goemon went first of all home.

"Wa-ha-ha-ha!"

"What's got into you?"

"Ahh — it feels good! It feels good!"

"Feels good — what does? Look at you, grinning."

"I've seen something worth being glad of."

"Glad of what, then?"

"Nobunaga's been killed..."

Maki was thunderstruck, her eyes gone round.

"What? By whom?"

"By Akechi Mitsuhide."

Hanzō was away with Ieyasu, so he could not be back yet. And at the thought Goemon pulled up short. Ieyasu, off in Sakai: how was he ever to get home? When word came that he had reached Okazaki safe and sound on the seventh, Goemon breathed easier.

A few fretful days went by. Before long Mitsuhide was destroyed by Hideyoshi, and for Ieyasu, for the time being, a spell of peace set in. It was just after this that Hanzō carried off Hatsuko and made her his own.

That summer was uncommonly hot. One evening, when autumn had come at last, Goemon was asked over by Hanzō for a cup, and it was the same old boasting again. How thoroughly he had won Ieyasu over; how, as Ieyasu rose in the world, he would rise with him, hand over hand, without lifting a finger. That was the whole burden of it. Bored, Goemon shut his eyes and made vague noises of assent, mm, mm.

Then all at once his eyes snapped open, and he threw his whole attention one way. He had felt the presence of someone watching him, and was trying to fix where it came from. In that instant he kicked off from the mats, sprang up, thrust up one of the ceiling-boards, and clutched at whoever was there, and came away with a woman's single sleeve, beautifully patterned, in his right hand.

At the same moment Hanzō flung a flustered shout up at the ceiling: "F-fool! Get back in there!"

And at once a shrill peal of laughter broke out overhead. It was, past mistaking, the laughter of a spirited young woman, helpless with delight. Goemon, dumbstruck at a turn he had never dreamed of, stared at the torn sleeve in his hand, and then at the ceiling, where one board's-width now gaped open in a hole.

"Don't come out!"

Even as Hanzō shouted it, a bright, lovely bundle of a thing came drifting softly down through the hole and alighted before Goemon's eyes. And in the same instant the screens between that room and the next flew apart to left and right, and there crouched two monsters, their faces working.

Goemon looked at the laughing face of the gaudy thing before him and was startled nearly off his feet. This Maki, for so she seemed, heedless of Hanzō's fury, opened her mouth, still laughing.

"My lord Saeki Dennai — nay, my lord Ishikawa Goemon."

The voice was different. Sharper than Maki's, higher, with a ring in the nose.

In the Japan of the 1960s there is a pair of girls, The Peanuts, so alike that even their own parents cannot tell them apart. But those two are identical twins. These two girls of the sixteenth century were no identical twins, no twins at all: the one was born at Saiga in Kishū, the other on the beach at Shiroko in Ise, of parents with not a thread of kinship or connection between them.

When he grasped that this was someone altogether other than Maki, Goemon turned his head and looked at the two monsters in the next room.

Why had he taken them, on the instant, for monsters? Fat they were, past the common run; yet not so fat that the like is never seen. What made them monsters was only this: on top of those full-grown, overgrown bodies sat the faces of children. The two had eyes that, in a lean face, might have been round and bright; but for the fat that pressed them above and below, they opened in nothing but narrow slits. And now, as though flung wide by some alarm, those eyes were stretched open as far as ever they would go, and even so it came to no more than a slit.

They were, needless to say, Etegi and Karasumaru.

One look at the earnest, flushed faces of the two, and you saw how utterly they were, in the current slang, "gone" on Hatsuko. Indeed, at any peril to her the two were of a mind to go through fire and water. And this girl so like Maki, ignoring Hanzō's spluttering altogether, spoke up quickly, in a voice brimful of eagerness.

"I've heard tell of you this long while. You can turn a woman's heart whichever way you please, they say — take her very life from her. I've been wild to meet you, just once, to see for myself what manner of man you are. Ah — I've met you at last."

And yet she herself was one of those women who work on a man a kind of hypnotism. I have known two such women in my own time. They lay no plan for it, make no effort; they simply throw into a trance any man they once meet. There are women, in the face, the figure, the voice, the turn of speech, the way they move, the things they do, the whole manner of their living, all of it together, who cast a spell over a man as a hypnotist would. And then the years pass, the spell lifts, and you see she was no peerless beauty after all. Why you were ever so lost in her, so far gone, becomes a wonder even to yourself. Nor had she any gift out of the common run.

But there, the moment it took me, my fever climbed and I did whatever she asked; of that much I am sure. Of the men she meets, a good ninety in the hundred go the same way. What quality in her brings it on, I cannot tell. But it is not that the man works himself into it alone, while she stands indifferent by. She radiates something at a man, and it does its work; and whether she wills it strongly or not at all, it is no different from a trunk so full of sap that it oozes and dribbles without cease.

Hatsuko was such a woman. And so Ieyasu, and Hanzō too, had been thrown into her trance in no time at all.

But Goemon had Maki. Even Goemon, had there been no Maki, would, poor devil, have fallen into the snare then and there, that one meeting his undoing. Face and figure, the two were as like as two halves of a melon; but Maki was the very opposite of the type that takes an eager interest in every man and forever weeps its sap. As like as two melon-halves, and that, precisely, was Goemon's saving.

Hatsuko, smiling, gave a nod toward the two in the next room; and they set both hands to the floor, bowed low and reverent to Hanzō and to Goemon, drew the screens to, and were gone. Knowing there was no danger now, the two went meekly.

Hatsuko started up talking again.

"I'm Hatsuko, you know. Ha for the leaf on the tree; tsu, as in the old saying, 'Ise is held up by Tsu'; and ko for child. You'll have heard about me from Hanzō, I don't doubt?"

Hanzō jabbed her in the shoulder from the side.

"Off with you! Get out of sight! Haven't I told you and told you never to let people see your face? Can't you take it in?"

Jabbed, Hatsuko toppled sideways with a thump. Put out of countenance, perhaps, she flashed Goemon a grin; but the instant her eyes came back to Hanzō's face they slanted sharply upward, and her color turned dead white. Her clenched fists trembled; she drew herself up small and tight and rose. Her eyes blazed, fixed on Hanzō like a snake's on its prey.

This, now, was dangerous: the first warning of a fit of hysterics about to burst. And once it burst, Hanzō knew, it would rage like a whirlwind, and there would be nothing for it but to have Etegi and Karasumaru, sixty-three or four kan of them together, pin her down and wait for her to quiet. Three months and more under one roof had beaten that into his belly.

Hanzō, in a fluster, looked away. Had Goemon not been there, he would have flung himself flat on the instant, ground his brow into the mats, and, past all shame, wailed out his apologies: "I was wrong! Bear with me! O great goddess Hatsuko! Here, look, just so! Mercy, have mercy!" — bawling it so that she should not catch on he was only trying to make her laugh. And then, sure enough, Hatsuko would burst out laughing in spite of herself, and the hysterics would scatter like mist and be gone.

But in front of Goemon he could do no such thing. Working somehow to send his meaning by looks alone, shaking his head, narrowing his eyes, screwing up his mouth, he managed by luck to head the eruption off, and Hatsuko's color came back to her.

Goemon saw it at a glance: aha, so in the bedchamber too the fellow is led entirely by the girl. Where in that small body such formidable energy was stored he could not think; and he stared, running his eyes over Hatsuko up and down. It was a sight, this strapping, handsome man of five shaku eight sun, so cowed before a chit of sixteen.

Hatsuko, feeling herself watched, flushed scarlet, got to her feet, snatched up her torn sleeve, and, swaying her hips as she went, ran off into the next room and shut the screen with a snap.

"Feh. A saucy little baggage, more than a man can manage."

So Hanzō muttered under his breath, all the while trying to read what the girl's coming had done to Goemon; for even to his own eyes she was Maki's very double. But for Goemon there seemed nothing in it beyond plain surprise that two women could be so alike.

"What sort of girl is she?" he asked quietly.

Hanzō made up his mind in an instant.

"She's my wife."

He said it flat out. Even Goemon seemed startled; he had never so much as heard that Hanzō had taken a wife.

"Oh? And that was — when? From where?"

At that Hanzō grinned and edged nearer.

"Goe— no, Dennai. Since it's you, I'll let you into it. But mind, not a word to a soul."

And he told him the whole truth about Hatsuko.

To filch his master's woman: that was a thing Goemon had done himself. But the woman he had taken, his lord had left neglected, cast aside, for twenty-five years. This Hatsuko the master had taken to himself only the other day, a fresh, unspoilt young thing, in whom, plainly, feeling for the master still lingered.

"And suppose Lord Ieyasu were to say, 'Go to Shiroko and fetch Hatsuko back' — what would you do then?"

"When that day comes, I have my answer ready, for I always reckoned he'd say it. 'I had meant to hand her over the moment my lord asked; but by what devil's enchantment I cannot say, the impossible came to pass: she took a fancy to this poor wretch, and swears she must be my wife, and will die if I refuse. To offer Your Lordship a woman of such a temper could bring you no comfort at all; and so I have made her my wife.' That is how I shall put it to him."

"And will Lord Ieyasu take that for the truth?"

"I've a year on him in age, but for looks there's no comparison. Anyone can see it at a glance. And that is the very reason. Hatsuko had believed herself summoned by Lord Ieyasu; and when she found it was I who had slipped into her bed, she minded it not a whit — took to me the more gladly, if anything. So any ordinary man will find nothing strange in the tale.

"Our lord, now — for all that he was packed off as a hostage in boyhood and had his hard years of it, he's a castle-holding daimyō born. Squat and dumpy, a badger just up from its nap; no one would ever take him for the great lover of the realm. Yet he's sure his face holds no end of charm for a woman. That brimful, self-satisfied look that comes into his eye when he weighs one up — you never noticed it? Faugh. It fairly turns my stomach."

Goemon had never yet come on Ieyasu in any such scene, so he only watched Hanzō's face and sipped, little by little, at his cup.

"Then you're in for it, man. He'll come to hate you."

"Aye. 'So — you'd filch my own woman, would you!' He'll hate me for it. And that is just why he'll take my tale for a lie."

"And then, without so much as letting you off the spot, it'll be the saw."

"Aye. An inch at a time. Half an inch at a time."

"And what do you mean to do, then?"

"Ah, but the fellow is Lord Ieyasu, not that devil Nobunaga."

"Mm. Of course."

"Lord Ieyasu, hate you though he may, never lets it out in what he does. He swallows it down and holds it fast in his belly."

"And can you count on that?"

"That I cannot count on. It's a thousand-to-one balance. And that not-knowing is the one thing there's no resisting. The whole relish of a wager is in staking your own head on it. You'd feel the same, I fancy."

"That may be, but—"

Goemon felt, all over again, as though the way this man Hanzō lived had slapped him full across the face. Hanzō made his sharp eyes flash sharper yet; a blue light seemed to stream out of them. His red, thick, manly lips, glistening with spittle, worked hard.

"Some spirit rides me, I think. When it comes to a moment of all or nothing — do it, or don't — the thing tells me: strike him down. And that same spirit, without my knowing, weighs up every last circumstance, and at the crucial moment cries, there's the mark! and shows me where. When it was a question of whether to cut down Lady Tsukiyama or no, the thing said: don't cut. When it was Lord Nobuyasu, whether to cut him or no, again it said: don't cut."

Hanzō shook the emptied sake-jar and clapped his hands. The screen slid open, and Hatsuko, changed into a fresh kimono, came in.

"Not you. Call Etegi."

Hatsuko took no notice, and smiled her sweet smile.

"Sake, is it?"

And with that she quietly withdrew.

"The first night I brought her in here and laid her down in this room, and sat wondering what to do, what to do — the spirit spoke then too: get on with it! To lose myself, to be swept away — such things are not in me. Cool, as you'd say, I wait on the spirit's word."

"Hm. No such fine thing rides me," Goemon murmured, impressed.

"In return, I keep the spirit well tended, day in and day out. So that it can make its reckonings and its cross-checkings easily, I still my head and lay out all the conditions ready. I keep them folded neat in my breast, to take out as the need comes."

"Hm. I see."

"Now, mark. Ieyasu will hate me. But he'll store it away in his belly. He knows well enough that to vent it and be rid of me would in the end be his loss. As the months and the years go by, the hatred wears thinner and thinner. And all the while I make myself useful to him, this way and that. It comes to where he can no longer make light of me. And why? Because it turns into a kind of fear of me. Which is to say — I can put the fear into Ieyasu again whenever I choose. And that is the best footing a retainer can stand on in his lord's service. Once you are there, you can steer your master just as you please."

Foam had gathered at the corners of Hanzō's mouth. Cool as the man was, and though there was no one else by to see, it was a sure sign he had talked himself into a fine humor.

Goemon, who at first had listened dumbstruck, half in admiration, grew steadily sick at heart. He had no wish to talk with such a man, none to keep his company. The old Goemon would have felt nothing of the kind. Some prick of inferiority he might have known, but it would have stayed mere admiration to the last. It was a measure, surely, of how far Goemon had drawn away from the ninja cast of mind. It was not that he feared any harm to himself from the acquaintance. Even a Hanzō would only look kindly on the Goemon he had himself sought out and drawn in; nowhere was there any call to do him an injury. So it seemed.

Just then Hatsuko came in with the sake, in a lilting voice: "Sorry to keep you."

Goemon looked, in spite of himself, at her face, and met Hatsuko's sweetly smiling eyes.

A woman who is the very double of your own wife: even at a first meeting, it is only natural to feel you know her through and through, an intimate, a woman long familiar. But the man who has that woman for a wife: there is something strange in that past telling. A hateful man, a man he shared a secret with; and yet bound to him by a tie that, for all his hating, he could not wholly hate—

Now, here, I must pause and own up to a thing that has been nagging at me: that this business of two things being alike past all telling apart crops up again and again in this novel.

Maki and Hatsuko, born in provinces far apart, yet so alike that Goemon, the husband of the one, takes her for the other. And in the earlier part of this novel, The Stealthy Ones, in the Tenshō Iga War, the ninja Kashii, burning to death in the Momochi fort, clung to the fancy that one of the three faces of the Ashura image at the Kōfuku-ji in Nara was the very likeness of Tamo's. Such things are in no way contrivances got up to make the story more diverting. In that age they were the most natural of happenings.

In that age there was as yet no photography. Nor was the art known of the fine, exact portrait that Leonardo da Vinci and Albrecht Dürer stand for, the art that commands perspective and the play of light and shade. That art, indeed, has not, in its true form, reached Japan even now, four hundred and fifty years on. Da Vinci painted his Mona Lisa between 1503 and 1506 (the eyelashes, called in their day a marvel of fineness, have since, through cleaning after cleaning, wholly disappeared); Dürer finished that self-portrait in which he set down every separate hair in 1500. Techniques, that is, brought to perfection long before 1582, the year Nobunaga burned to death at the Honnō-ji. And yet they never reached Japan.

So the people of that day never saw a human face but the thing itself, before their eyes; a copied likeness of it, never. As you readers will know from your own experience, to stare hard at a face is a rudeness. If we know anyone's face with any exactness, it is because we have checked our memory of it, over and over, against a photograph.

When it comes to the memory of faces, I am next thing to a cripple myself. Even the face of the wife I live beside: let her step out on some errand, and let me try to summon it: what were her eyes like, her mouth? I am startled to find only a vague blur I cannot lay hold of, and hurry to fetch out a photograph and look. Ah, yes, so that was it, and am set at ease.

That we, in our own time, so rarely light on such "two-melon-halves" resemblances about us is owing, first of all, to the rise of photography. A second cause is the advance of lighting. In the old days, once the sun went down, things showed only in a blur. And there was no kerosene then, only fish-oil and rapeseed-oil and the like; not so much as a candle. At night the most you had was a pine-torch or an oil-lamp. There were no spectacles, either. It is not that everyone's eyes were sound; short-sighted they may well have been. Kashii, and Goemon too, may have been short-sighted men. All these causes worked together to give that impression of "two melon-halves"; it is not the author's invention. Absolutely not.

And so the many old tales and plays that turn on two people being "as like as two melon-halves" are not, of necessity, to be dismissed as mere contrivance. Trusting that you readers will grant me the point, I shall carry the story on.

When Hatsuko brought the sake and plumped herself down to stay, Hanzō gave over telling her to get back inside. With a flourish of her white arm she poured, and, bold as brass, struck up talk.

"I say, Dennai — I know that isn't your real name, but I'll go on calling you by it."

Hanzō gave a start. "What are you saying? This gentleman's name is Saeki Dennai."

"But before that, Ishikawa Goemon—"

"You. Who told you?"

"I'm a woman of the ninja, remember. Heh-heh-heh."

"Damn it. Etegi, was it?"

"No matter who. What's out is out; there's no help for it now. They tell me you were quite the lady-killer, Dennai, in your day?"

Goemon gave a wry smile.

"Nothing of the kind."

"They say a woman who so much as meets you falls as if under a spell. But that's no good to you now, is it. However did you come to lose two of your front teeth?"

In those days false teeth were only such as were carved of hard wood and taken out at every meal, and they were not in common use.

"Took a fall and broke them."

"Whoever heard of a ninja breaking his front teeth in a fall?"

"A clumsy ninja, then, I dare say."

"Feh — so you take me for a mere woman, a child!" And Hatsuko arched her brows.

On the instant, in the illusion that he had put Maki out of humor, Goemon said hastily: "No, no — it was for the disguise."

At this Hanzō felt a wish, for once, to egg Hatsuko on. "There, you see? Goemon disguised himself as Dennai, no doubt of it. But to have to knock out his own front teeth for a disguise — there must be some very great matter behind that. Mm; and even I have not yet heard that tale. Some great matter, I'll be bound."

Goemon, judging that anything he said would only be turned against him, held his tongue. And Hanzō, finding it too delicious for words that little Hatsuko should put the great hulking Goemon so out of countenance, narrowed his eyes and sat there grinning.

For Goemon, the affair that had cost him his front teeth already seemed a thing of the distant past.

Momochi Sandayū, from whom it had all sprung, had surely burned to death in the Momochi fort. Goemon had not seen the body with his own eyes; but by the rumor he had picked up, the Oda army had judged a corpse found in what seemed the inner room of the fort, sitting cross-legged, fallen face-down, to be Sandayū's, packed it into a wooden box, carried it to Azuchi, and set it before Nobunaga. And that Momochi Sandayū and Fujibayashi Nagato-no-kami had been one and the same man, Goemon had as yet told no one. He was afraid that, if he told it, it would be flatly denied, though he could think of no ground on which it could be.

In ninjutsu there is no such art as the raising of the dead, for ninjutsu is a practical art. Even in Shugendō, the root of Japanese ninjutsu, there is no spell to call the dead back to life. There is only the art of lengthening life and holding off age.

Every man longs to be deathless. But since the human race began, no man has escaped dying. And so every religion, taking the death of the body for a thing past helping, has no recourse but to cling to one of two ideas: that a man, though he die once, may live again; or that, though the body die, the soul lives on. This springs, at the root, from the way early man, seeing that after the winter solstice the shortened nights grew long again and the cold turned back toward warmth, conceived of the death and the resurrection of the sun. And so many peoples reckoned that, around this solstice, the gods who had died came back to life.

Mithra, the old Persian god who stood at the end of the ancient world, was a sun-god, and his birthday fell on the twenty-fifth of December. In the Julian calendar this day was set as the winter solstice, and held to be "the day of the sun's birth." In old Syria and Egypt, on the solstice, they would cry aloud from the inmost temple, "The Virgin has borne a child! The light waxes!" and carry out the image of an infant, the newborn sun, to show the worshippers.

Christianity, since Scripture says nothing of the day, did not at first keep Christ's birthday; but in time the Christians of Egypt came to reckon it the sixth of January, and the custom spread, so that by the fourth century it was widely kept in the Eastern Church as well. The Western Church, at the start of the fourth century, fixed on the twenty-fifth of December, and this too was carried over, little by little, to the East. Santa Claus, that fixture of Christmas, was no Christian thing to begin with, but an older pagan god who fastened himself on around the day. Priapus of Greece, Freyr of Scandinavia, Krishna of India, and many another mighty god besides, are all born on this twenty-fifth of December.

Japan's own myth of the Heavenly Rock-Cave is a resurrection of the sun; and Hideyoshi, who with Nobunaga's death was now stepping fairly out onto the main stage, put about the tale that at his birth his mother had dreamed the sun entered her bosom.

Now, better than resurrection would be never to die at all; but since that is flatly out of reach, a long life without aging is the next thing to be wished. And that became the supreme aim of the magic arts.

For instance: in the Peacock King rite for the lengthening of life, said to have been founded by En no Gyōja Ozunu, the forefather of our ninjutsu, in the esoteric Shingon teaching, one sets up an image of the Peacock King (who holds a peacock's tail in one of his four hands, sits upon a peacock's back, and wears the mild face of a bodhisattva), forms the mudrā of the peacock's dance, and chants the spell: "Nōmo-botaya, nōmo-taramuya, nōmo-sōkiya, taniyata, go-go-go-go," and the rest.

And the immortal arts of ancient China name a great many drugs of long life. There is one called ōshishi, that grows on sheer mountains: something that has flowed ten thousand years and more, congealed at last into the shapes of birds and beasts; ground to powder and mixed with the juice of a plant called mushinsō, it turns to a liquid, and one shō of it drunk down gives a thousand years of life. There is a lingzhi called sekizōshi, on a high sea-cliff of an island, whose light can be made out on a dark night from three hundred paces off; pounded with the pestle thirty-six thousand strokes, and taken three times a day until one piece is gone, it too wins a thousand-year life. That no one has ever found these and enjoyed an ageless age is only, they say, because no one has yet piled up training enough, ten times over, to gain the power to find them.

Kōbō Daishi, founder of the Shingon sect in our country, four months before he passed away on Kōya-san in the year 835, called his disciples together and left them his testament: "I shall enter the adamantine samādhi, and keep this body of flesh upon this mountain, to guard the Law and the people of the realm. Grieve not, my disciples: though my living body follow the way of samādhi, my Dharma-body shall dwell long in the world. When I have entered samādhi, I shall go up to the Tuṣita Heaven and attend before the Merciful Miroku; and after five billion six hundred million years I shall come forth into the world once more, together with the Bodhisattva Maitreya, and seek out the traces of my former path."

And on the very day he had foretold, he entered his eternal meditation. This was no death, they said, but the entering of dhyāna; so for fifty days they laid no hand on him, and then bestowed him in the stone cave of what is now the Oku-no-in. Ninety-seven years on, at the command of the Emperor Daigo, the Archbishop Kangen opened the door of the tomb-cave; and all was dim, as though cloud and mist stood within it, and the Master could not be seen. The Archbishop wept, and confessed his sins from the depths of him; and when he lifted his eyes again the cloud and mist had cleared away, and, like a full moon rising, the holy countenance was granted to his sight.

The believers who make the pilgrimage to Kōya-san hold, to this day, that in this tomb-cave, in the Oku-no-in, half a ri on from the First Bridge, among the ancient cryptomeria, the Great Master still sits in his eternal meditation, and will one day rise again and come once more into the world. A month or so ago I climbed Kōya-san myself, and saw with my own eyes how the pilgrims, in their unbroken stream, as they neared this tomb-cave, would fix their eyes and feel their hearts pound the harder, and, their lips chanting "Namu Daishi Henjō Kongō," fall to trembling all over: for they believe they are drawing near the deathless presence of the Master. It is much like the Christian belief that Christ did not die but ascended, and sits at Jehovah's side, and will one day sound the trumpet, and lead out the hosts, and come again to the earth, and hold the Last Judgment, and, when he has punished the sinners, set up the thousand-year kingdom.

Now, Goemon, even among the ninja, was of a practical turn, and put no faith in resurrection and the like. The soul was no more than a working of the body; so death was death and nothing more: a man turned, as Kudobei had, into maggots, and went back to the mud. In believing so, he was at one with us materialists. The cleverer among the ninja were mostly so. But a materialist thoroughgoing enough to disbelieve not only in the soul's immortality but in the very existence of gods and buddhas was a great rarity; the most of them still half-believed, vaguely, in such things. Goemon and Hanzō belonged to that rare class. The people of that age drifted, more or less, in a hazy, mystical mood.

The same could be said of Hanzō. He would form the mudrās and chant the spells, just as he had learned them; but he held them for nothing more than a means of gathering the mind. Goemon and Hanzō, driven into a last corner with no way out, could chant a spell and believe that a way out would come of it. And when no spell served, and the enemy's blade struck off their heads, and the blood spurted up like a fountain, in that very instant they could pass into nothingness. Even as the head alone went spinning high into the air, holding the thought "I do not die," in that instant it could pass into nothing.

To have been able, in such an age, to be materialists, realists, as Hanzō and Goemon were, they must, whatever stock they came of, have leaned hard on their own strength, and been men of a powerful will.

Where the two differed was this. Hanzō lived, as it were, oozing his sap as he went (Hatsuko's sap ran full toward the other sex, but Hanzō's flowed toward "work," toward "enterprise"), whereas Goemon, having thrown off Sandayū's yoke, did no more than enjoy his freedom, and that in a merely passive way. Goemon wanted to throw off the yoke of the ninja as well. The one thing that held him to it was Hanzō.

This difference was not a matter between these two men alone. Taking the Tenshō Iga War as the divide, it stood, broadly, for the parting of the Japanese ninja into two ways.

The ninja of that time were not to be found in Iga and Kōga only. There were ninja in Kai, in Shinano, in Echizen, Etchū, Echigo, and Kii. Hōjō Sōun, Takeda Shingen, Uesugi Kenshin and their like all used ninja. But the ninja these three commanders used were of the field-bandit and mountain-robber sort, low-grade men called rappa and nokizaru and the like; and in truth the only school that could name itself first-rate without shame, apart from the two schools of Iga and Kōga, was the Kishū school. Taking the Tenshō Iga War as the divide, the ninja of Japan split and went two ways: the one, Hanzō's; the other, Goemon's.

The rulers who had unified Japan were not going to let so dangerous a thing lie. So the ninja had either to take a stipend from a ruler and become the lowest sort of samurai in his service, or else to give up being ninja altogether and turn to some other trade. Hanzō caught early the way the tide was setting, and pushed himself forward eagerly down the road to becoming a retainer; and by folding as many ninja as he could into the body of retainers, he worked to raise his own standing as their chief. Goemon, for his part, had quit the ninja; but, dragged along by Hanzō, he hung in a middle, in-between state, forever meaning to move on to some other work.

A ninja ought not to drink. Not only because it fuddles him, but because it makes him give off a smell. Yet now, of these two, the one was a crossbreed of ninja and samurai, and the other a man who had lost all sense of himself as a ninja. And on top of it the sap that seeped from Hatsuko poured oil on the flame, so that, as the hour wore on, the pitch of it all rose higher. Before anyone knew it Hatsuko had joined in too, and begun to keep them company in the cup. And when she started up her coquettish squeals, Etegi and Karasumaru appeared in the next room and knelt there with anxious faces. Which only goes to show that these two, also, were no small victims of the warm current of Hatsuko's sap.

Overcome by all these noxious fumes, Goemon went out wobbling, let the night wind blow on him, and made his way home.

Hanzō, for his part, had gone into the bedchamber and flung himself down on the quilts. But all at once he rose, scooped Hatsuko up in his arms (she had only just changed into her night-robe) and, the very moment she went to wind her arms about his neck, threw her down with a thump onto the bedding.
