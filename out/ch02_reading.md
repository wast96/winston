## 2

A year later, Lin Nansheng had all but given up the work of turning the enemy. He put his energy now into gathering intelligence and sifting it. The concession had always been a place where dragons and fish swam together; nearly every intelligence service in the world kept an office there, and the streets were thick with the informers of the police stations and the ears and eyes of the gangs. After Japan joined the Axis they seemed busier than ever. Sometimes, looking down from his office window, Lin Nansheng felt that every hurrying figure below was carrying an errand of its own.

Lin Nansheng's cover now was business assistant at the Huaxing Trading Company. The firm exported silk and tea; in truth it was the Juntong's intelligence relay in Shanghai. For it Gu Shenyan had taken the whole top floor of the Xianghu Building, on the busiest stretch of Nanjing Road. This was the commercial heart of Shanghai, and the intelligence clearinghouse of the western Pacific. Every day, word of every kind blew in through a dozen channels like snow, and, once sifted and sorted, blew out again like snow.

Lin Nansheng was run nearly off his feet. Yet however busy the week, he never forgot one thing: to go to a bar called the Alise and see a man who was half Russian by blood.

The man tended the bar there, and was also a Communist agent left behind in Shanghai. Lin Nansheng would sit and drink the cocktails he mixed, but he came for the intelligence that might change hands. When Gu Shenyan gave him the task he had said, "Intelligence work is you within me, and me within you." And he had reminded him too that in this world there are no permanent enemies, and fewer permanent friends.

Yet often enough, drinking those mixed drinks whose names he could never have told, Lin Nansheng found that an understanding had grown between himself and the man across the counter. That night the bartender broke his own rule and stood him a vodka. Then he looked at one corner of the room. "Tomorrow night the man who takes my place will sit there," he said, "a Bloody Mary on the table in front of him."

"And you?" Lin Nansheng said.

"It's time I went." The bartender's eyes did not move. "I've stayed too long in one place."

The next evening Lin Nansheng came again, and found that his contact was, of all people, Zhu Yizhen. In the nearly six years since he had last seen her, her greatest change was her hair: once bobbed like a child's, now permed into deep waves.

In those days Lin Nansheng had been an English teaching assistant at the University of Shanghai, and Zhu Yizhen's first love. The affair, teacher and student, was hidden from the whole school, but not from her mother. One morning the woman burst into the principal's office and said that such a thing at a mission school was a disgrace before God. She gave the young principal two choices before she left: dismiss the indecent English assistant, or face the newspapermen she would bring the next day.

On the eve of his leaving, Lin Nansheng waited until daybreak in the little chapel behind the sports ground. He sat in the cramped, pitch-dark prayer stall where they had met in secret a hundred times, where they had held each other, and kissed, and made love under the very eye of God. He remembered her words: once I am yours, I am yours for life. But Zhu Yizhen did not come that night. Her mother had shut her up at home, kneeling before her father's portrait, repenting until dawn.

Out in the street, when the two of them had left the bar, Zhu Yizhen said, "If you ask for someone else, I can put it to my superiors."

"I'm afraid," Lin Nansheng said mildly, "that this was your superiors' idea from the start."

Zhu Yizhen was taken aback, and looked down at the tips of her shoes.

"Chongqing is under air raids every day now," Lin Nansheng said. "We need every move the Japanese air force makes."

"And you should know what we need." With that Zhu Yizhen put out a hand and hailed a rickshaw. She did not look at Lin Nansheng again. She had the puller wind her through several streets, changed to another cab, and went home. Ji Zhongyuan sat under the lamp, cutting a seal. He had once engraved seals at Duoyunxuan; now he kept a scroll-mounting shop in a quiet corner of Fuyou Road, its signboard offering seal-engraving on the side.

This was their home. It was also their station, for the sending and receiving of reports.

Zhu Yizhen changed into a sweater in the inner room and came out to sit at the edge of Ji Zhongyuan's table. She watched until he raised his head. "So this is why you had me take the bartender's place."

Ji Zhongyuan nodded.

Zhu Yizhen glanced at the locked drawer of the dressing table. "You've been reading my diary."

"Your photograph album too." Ji Zhongyuan said it calmly. "You shouldn't have kept these things."

"I didn't keep them for you to pry into."

"I need to know you," Ji Zhongyuan said. "We are husband and wife."

Zhu Yizhen gave a cold laugh. "So you'd have me rekindle an old flame with a Juntong agent?"

The light in Ji Zhongyuan's eyes began to go out. "I know only that this man matters a great deal to us," he said.

"And I?"

"You are an intelligence agent," Ji Zhongyuan said. "Understand this: intelligence comes before everything."

After a long silence Zhu Yizhen said, "I want the situation reported to our superiors."

"That's your right," Ji Zhongyuan said. "But until the answer comes down, you'll obey my orders." A long moment passed. He pointed at the dressing-table drawer. "Those diaries. Deal with them, sooner rather than later."

Lin Nansheng's second meeting with Zhu Yizhen was at the DDS Café.

Lin Nansheng had chosen the place; he remembered Zhu Yizhen liked the hot chocolate there. This time she ordered a black coffee, no sugar.

"Your taste has changed," Lin Nansheng said, smiling.

Zhu Yizhen seemed not to hear. She set a copy of The Young Companion on the table. "The garrison dispositions of the Japanese Third Air Division at Hankou. You should find them of use."

Lin Nansheng seemed not to hear either. He was looking at the pale band a ring had left on her fourth finger. "Why did you take it off?" Zhu Yizhen closed her hand. "You ought to give me something in return, no?"

"Are the two of you really husband and wife?" Lin Nansheng shook his head as if it were nothing. "I don't believe you'd marry a seal-engraver who keeps a mounting shop." He saw Zhu Yizhen start to rise, and caught her hand.

"Let go," Zhu Yizhen said.

"He's your superior." The smile was gone now; Lin Nansheng held her eyes. "Your assignment isn't only to trade intelligence." "Let go of my hand," Zhu Yizhen said.

Lin Nansheng loosened his grip by degrees and sat back. "Zhenzhen," he said, in earnest, "this trade is no work for a woman."

"It's you who have no right to it," Zhu Yizhen said. "You've broken the rules between us."

She rose and left the café without looking back.

This time Zhu Yizhen did not go roundabout, but straight back to the shop on Fuyou Road. The moment she was inside she rounded on Ji Zhongyuan. "Damn it. He's followed me, and now he has your measure too."

"That was to be expected." Ji Zhongyuan was smiling. "Haven't we followed him, and looked into him, ourselves?"

"It's not the same," Zhu Yizhen said. "He'll bring danger down on us."

"And we can bring it down on him just the same." Still smiling, Ji Zhongyuan said it with certainty: "He knows that as well as we do."

"You put far too much faith in a Nationalist Juntong agent." Zhu Yizhen's voice had gone hard. "Don't forget the New Fourth Army Incident."

Ji Zhongyuan sat down. For a while he looked up into Zhu Yizhen's face. "Yizhen," he said suddenly, "the two of you were lovers once. You loved each other."

Zhu Yizhen started, but answered at once. "That's past."

"And now? Do you trust me?" Ji Zhongyuan said no more, only watched her, unmoving, until little by little she lowered her eyes and would not say another word.

Early that morning Ji Zhongyuan handed her a folding fan of mottled bamboo and sent her to carry it to Zhenru Temple, outside the city. It was afternoon before Zhu Yizhen came back. She was a little way along Fuyou Road, a box of the temple's vegetarian buns in her hand, when the explosion came from the direction of the shop. Her heart leapt into her throat. At the second blast she was almost running.

A man caught her head-on, in his arms. He wore a long gown and a felt hat, and without a word bundled her into a rickshaw waiting at the curb; only then, under the brim, did she make out Lin Nansheng's face. "Let me down," Zhu Yizhen said.

Lin Nansheng seemed not to hear. "Go. Quickly," he told the puller.

"Let go of me." Zhu Yizhen struggled, and went on demanding to be let down, until Lin Nansheng drew a pistol and pressed it to her waist. Her eyes went round; she glared at him. Both their breaths came fast, breaking against each other's faces.

After a while Lin Nansheng drew the pistol back and said in her ear, "You have to keep calm."

But Zhu Yizhen could not. Again and again the grenade at home rose before her eyes. It was a standard Japanese Type 97 infantry grenade. Setting it into the hidden compartment with the transmitter, Ji Zhongyuan had said its force was enough to bring the whole room down. He had said, too, that it was meant for himself.

Lin Nansheng kept his arm hard around Zhu Yizhen's shoulders all the way into his flat, and let go only when the door was shut. It was not only the shop on Fuyou Road that had been hit that day, he told her, but the rice dealer's at Baxianqiao, the teahouse at Shiliupu, the pawnshop at Xiaodongmen: bombed, or raked with gunfire. "They must all have been your contact points," he said. And then: "The trouble is with your leadership."

Zhu Yizhen stood stunned a while, then looked him in the eye. "Then how did you come to be there?"

"I heard No. 76 had thrown a cordon over Fuyou Road, and hurried to warn you," Lin Nansheng said. "It was luck you weren't inside."

Zhu Yizhen said not another word. She sat in the sofa until the sky went black, then stood all at once and made for the door. Lin Nansheng caught her back. "Where are you going?" She would not answer. He pushed her down into the sofa. "Right now there's nowhere you can go." She clenched her teeth and strained to rise. He bore down harder. "You're going to your death. They have the sack open, waiting for you."

"Then let me die." And Zhu Yizhen broke out in a scream.
