## Preface

{d} Wei Jianyou

The gangs of modern China were a product of the contradictions of a semi-colonial, semi-feudal society, and Shanghai was where those contradictions gathered to a point. To study the Shanghai gangs is thus an indispensable part of studying both the modern history of China and the local history of Shanghai.

The Shanghai gangs rose to prominence after the Opium War; through the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom and the Revolution of 1911 they grew to their height, and from 1911 down to the liberation of Shanghai in 1949 they gradually declined and died away. Their rise and their fall are bound up with the history of modern China's semi-colonial, semi-feudal society. They flourished under the tangled conditions of imperialism's full-scale invasion of China, the slow dissolution of feudal society, and the bureaucratic bourgeoisie that emerged with it; for a time they played an important part on the stage of the age, and then, as the democratic revolution advanced, they slid into decline and at last were gone.

The Shanghai gangs hold a place of some importance in modern Chinese history, and in both respects, the positive and the negative, they told greatly on the course of events. In the struggle against imperialism and feudalism they more than once showed their mettle, as in the Small Sword Society uprising and the recovery of Shanghai in the Revolution of 1911. But they also colluded, again and again, with the reactionary forces of imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism, and did much that harmed the people, above all in their later years: taking part in the counterrevolutionary coup of April 12, serving as the hounds of the foreign concession authorities, conspiring with the secret agents of Chiang Kai-shek and of the puppet Wang Jingwei regime to bring calamity on the country and ruin on its people. Even after New China was founded, when the gangs themselves had passed away, one could still, through the ten years of catastrophe, glimpse their shadow and their poison in the words and deeds of Lin Biao and the "Gang of Four." Not to forget the past is to be guided in what lies ahead; there is real value in probing these matters deeply.

The study of the history of the Shanghai gangs deserves attention enough, but to this day it has remained a weak link. A great mass of source material has been left to scatter and be lost; the old archives lie unsorted, the living witnesses uninterviewed, the old forbidden zones unentered and the new ones unbroken. Little research has been undertaken, and its fruits are as sparse as morning stars. There are only the two books *A Collection of Source Materials on the Shanghai Small Sword Society Uprising* and *The Shanghai Small Sword Society Uprising*; beyond these, works of quality are few. As for the Shanghai gangs of the Republican period, almost no one has taken them up; only in recent years have *A True Life of Du Yuesheng* and a translated *Life of Du Yuesheng* published in Macau appeared. Yet one should remember that the Republican era, though the time of the gangs' decline and death, was also the time of their malignant swelling: many a gang boss took in disciples by the crowd, exploited the Shanghai concessions for years on end, colluded with reactionary forces Chinese and foreign, lent his strength to the tyrant, trafficked in opium on a great scale, ran gambling houses, lorded it over whole neighborhoods, and preyed upon the people, a phenomenon without parallel in the history of the gangs. To leave all this unexamined would be a thing to regret.

The Committee on Cultural and Historical Materials of the Shanghai Municipal CPPCC and its editorial office, answering the call of the National CPPCC's Research Committee on Cultural and Historical Materials, broke with "leftist" notions, freed their thinking, and in a spirit of sifting the false from the true and seeking truth from facts, compiled and edited this special volume of source materials on the Shanghai gangs. In it are the honest recollections of the kin, friends, and disciples of such men as Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng, concerning their origins, their rise, and the chief episodes of their careers; and there are men who were themselves within that world, setting down what they saw and heard of society's dark side. The book lays bare the many foul doings of this evil power, and it also shows how certain gang figures, under particular historical conditions, were guided and put to use by the forces of revolution. With its vivid and abundant contents, the book offers the historian material fuller and more trustworthy than most, and it can help the younger generation understand the manifold ugliness of old Shanghai and the layered darkness behind its gangster underworld, sharpening their vigilance and quickening their love for New China, for a new Shanghai, and for a new life.

That this book has been edited and published opens a path in the study of gang history; it is a rare and precious thing, a footfall in an empty valley that gladdens the ear. Most of what is gathered here rests on what its writers saw and heard; and though the years have passed and there are bound to be gaps and slips, and though wise men will see the matter each in his own way and something may be one-sided or wanting, most of it is firsthand material, well worth consulting. I set down my humble views and place them here at the front.

{d} April 1986
