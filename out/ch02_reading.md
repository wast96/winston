## Chapter Two. The Clean Stay Clean, the Foul Stay Foul

### 1. A Hero's Mettle

*Hero* — a word with a spine in it.

The history of our Party's struggle on the hidden front is full of upright heroes who stood between heaven and earth.

Zong Mengping was one of them; Kuang Yaming was another.

In September 1927, the Yixing County Special Branch of the Party sent Zong Mengping to Shanghai to report and take instructions. The Jiangsu Provincial Committee approved the founding of a Yixing County Committee, with Shi Yaobin as its secretary and Li Yanggu and Zong Mengping as members; it led six branches and thirty-nine Party members.

Zong Mengping — that is, Zong Yishou, and also known as Zong Ying, Wu Danfeng, and Zong Wenbin — was born in 1907 in Jingxi, Jiangsu (today's Yixing). In January 1926 he joined the Kuomintang and worked in the farmers-and-workers department of its Yixing county headquarters. That summer Zong Panlin, a Communist and a student at Wuchang Normal University, came home to visit family and brought Zong Mengping into the Party; the organization sent him to Shanghai University for a short course at its peasant-movement training class. Later, with the Youth League member Zong Daozhang, he opened the county's first peasant night school in Yixing.

On August 7, 1927, the Party's Central Politburo held an emergency conference in Hankou and fixed the general line for the new period: agrarian revolution, and armed resistance to the massacre policy of the Kuomintang reactionaries, with the rousing of the peasants for an Autumn Harvest Uprising as the Party's foremost task.

In October 1927, to carry out the spirit of the Center's August 7 Conference, the Jiangsu Provincial Committee called the heads of the Jiangnan counties together in Shanghai. The meeting resolved that "Yixing shall be the first in Jiangnan to raise an armed insurrection and to found a worker-peasant-soldier soviet," and named Wan Yi and Duan Yanhua provincial commissioners to organize and lead the Yixing rising.

Wan Yi was bold and open by nature, and full of drive. Told by the provincial committee to organize the Yixing rising, he called a meeting of cadres at the Shrine of Chastity and Filial Piety in Yicheng and settled that the rising would rest chiefly on the peasants of the peasant association, with Zong Mengping and Jiang Sanda to arm them and drill a peasant army.

In late October, Kuang Yaming arrived too. The Youth League's Jiangsu Provincial Committee had sent him as an additional commissioner, to help Wan Yi, Duan Yanhua, Shi Yaobin, Zong Mengping, and the rest in their work.

Kuang Yaming — his original name was Jieyu, and he also went by Kuang Shi — was born in 1906 in Danyang, Jiangsu. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1926 and served in turn as Youth League secretary for the Shanghai districts of Hudong, Huxi, and Zhabei, as secretary of the Wuxi Central County Committee, and as an inspector for the Jiangsu Provincial Committee.

Kuang Yaming brought the Jiangsu Provincial Committee's latest instructions, which laid it down that Wan Yi, Duan Yanhua, Kuang Yaming, Zong Mengping, and Shi Yanfen would form an uprising action committee, with Wan Yi as commander-in-chief and Zong Mengping and Shi Yanfen as his deputies.

At twelve noon on 1 November, the Yixing peasants rose, firing the first shot of armed resistance to the Kuomintang reactionaries anywhere in Jiangnan.

Nine days later the peasants of Wuxi rose in the same fury. Shouting "Peasants, rise up, down with the Nanjing government!" and "Peasants, rise up, kill the local despots and the evil gentry and every reactionary!," they "burned and pulled down the houses of the despots and the evil gentry, dug out the land deeds and rent books and put the lot to the torch, and seized and killed the despots and the gentry"; and at an oath-taking rally at Nanshiyuan they raised up Yan Pu as chairman of their peasant committee. Yan Pu at once led five columns in a general assault and took thirteen villages and market towns in a single rush.

Fourteen days later the peasants of Jiangyin, too, "seized a moment when the enemy's guard had slackened, rose at Houcheng, disarmed the public security bureau, burned the gentry's houses and their land deeds, debt bonds, and rent books, and struck several reactionaries dead."

After the Yixing rising failed, Zong Mengping made his way by roundabout paths to Shanghai to find the provincial committee, which posted him to Rugao to head the Relief Society there, and afterward reassigned him as youth commissioner of the Jiangbei Special Committee.

In April 1928 Zong Mengping slipped back into Yixing and called a meeting at the home of Shi Yanfen in Guanlin, where the Party's Yixing County Committee was rebuilt with him as its secretary. Before long he was elected a member of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee and, with Chen Yun and others, formed a farmers' commission to lead the peasant movement across the province.

On September 2, 1929, Chen Yun sat for the first time on the standing committee of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee. On the 9th the standing committee settled the rosters of its military and farmers' commissions: the Peasant Movement Commission would be made up of Chen Yun, Zong Mengping, Ying Ju, Zhao He, and others, with Chen Yun as its secretary. (CCP Central Party Literature Research Office, 2005)

In April 1931, Gu Shunzhang — alternate member of the Sixth Central Politburo and the Central Special Branch's frontline chief — turned traitor and went over to the enemy in Wuhan. The Center resolved that "the organization of the Special Committee itself, from the supreme directing committee on down, shall be reconstituted from top to bottom," and moved Chen Yun onto the Central Special Committee to lead the Central Special Branch. Chen Yun had Zong Mengping and his younger brother Zong Yimao run notices in the papers renouncing the Party, a false front to win the enemy's trust and so bury themselves in the devil's den.

Zong Mengping, too, was a shrewd intelligence hand. A Yixing man, he had come to Shanghai through the organization after the armed rising there collapsed, and was first assigned to trade-union work. Later, by a decision of the Center, he was sent to burrow into the enemy's own ranks and gather intelligence. To that end he was deliberately made to run a notice in the papers breaking with the Communist Party: he had strayed onto the wrong road, it said, in his youthful ignorance; he now cut his ties with the Chinese Communist Party, and henceforth wished to serve the Party and the state. The Kuomintang's secret-service organs were then pressing a campaign of surrenders, and they took Zong Mengping's declaration at face value. The thing was so secret that not even his own family knew of it. Once inside the enemy camp, Zong Mengping would catch wind of the enemy's moves against Communist organs and their planned arrests, and find ways to warn the organization in time. Sometimes the enemy would make an arrest without telling him first; seeing that the agents had taken one of his own people, he would put on a show of temper — How could you arrest him? He is my own man, planted inside the Communist Party. The agents, hearing they had the wrong man, would let the prisoner go at once. Not a few cadres were saved just this way. (Li Kai, 2011)

"Secrets" like these were no game; they carried risk. It was over just such a "secret" that Kuang Yaming was shot by his own side. His own comrades, "taking him for a traitor, opened fire on him and hit him in the front teeth; only because the range was long, and the teeth blunted the force of the bullet, did he escape with his life."

The shooting brought the Shanghai police down on the affair, and once the patrolmen had the truth of it, the Kuomintang's agents tried every trick to lure Kuang Yaming into surrendering. Cornered, he faced them and said, with righteous force: "As for the mistaken shooting — that is an internal matter of our Communist Party. My faith in communism is fixed and cannot be moved. I will not be turned by your provocations, and I will never surrender to you counter-revolutionaries." (Luo Qingchang, 1997)

Wronged and unbowed in heart — there you know the true man.

Our Party, our hidden front — precisely because it had a thousand Zong Mengpings and a thousand Kuang Yamings, men whom riches could not corrupt, poverty could not sway, and force could not bend — could hold firm through the long years and press "ever onward," able to "overwhelm every enemy and never be subdued by any." (Mao Zedong, 1945)

But not Gu Shunzhang.

Gu Shunzhang was a senior figure who had joined the Party early; but he had joined it in organization only. In his thinking he went his own way, and kept a whole set of his own.

Gu Shunzhang reeked of the underworld. He flew into rages and threw his weight about at the least excuse, chafed at discipline, cared nothing for Party principle, and was never, in any real sense, a revolutionary. And so, long before his arrest and his treason, long before he led the Central Special Branch against the enemy — as far back, indeed, as the days when he was mustering the Shanghai workers' pickets into the three armed uprisings — he was already taking in renegades, thick as thieves with them, lording it as a warlord in his own right.

And so, no sooner had the third armed uprising of the Shanghai workers won its victory than Chen Duxiu, Peng Shuzhi, Luo Yinong, Zhao Shiyan, and Wang Shouhua were all of a mind to "weed out the doubtful elements" and "tighten up and reorganize" Gu Shunzhang and his little clique. They could not stomach a picket corps that was "impure, of the most mixed makeup, with every kind of man in it." On no account would they let the likes of Gu Shunzhang swell out of control, run wild as the mood took them, and reckon that a gun made a man king of his own patch.

### 2. The Lumpen Proletariat

At ten in the morning on March 23, 1927, the Shanghai Provisional Municipal Government opened for business. At eleven it convened its committee and declared for all to hear: "Our municipal government is the fruit of our people's long years of struggle and sacrifice, and we will strive on to build a new Shanghai." The Shanghai press reported it far and wide: "An unheard-of thing, a Shanghai citizens' government, has sounded the opening note of the Chinese revolution."

At one o'clock that afternoon, more than a thousand organizations from every walk of Shanghai life, and more than 500,000 people, gathered on the square at Qingyun Road in Zhabei for a "Rally to Uphold the Founding of the Shanghai Provisional Municipal Government and Welcome the Northern Expedition Army to Shanghai." The rally passed a manifesto: it stood firmly behind the Shanghai Provisional Municipal Government, firmly behind the National Government, and firmly demanded that the National Government take back the Shanghai concessions without delay.

At the same time, the leading men of the Party — Chen Duxiu, Peng Shuzhi, Luo Yinong, Zhao Shiyan, Wang Shouhua — were meeting behind closed doors, one session after another, from the morning of 23 March straight through to the small hours of the 24th, until they reached a common view: "The question of the Party's growth is one of the most important tasks before the Party."

"If, within ten days, there are not ten thousand Party members, then this movement will have been a victory for the trade unions alone, and the Party will have failed! We must not now hedge in the least; we must openly propagate the C.P., and must state without the slightest reserve that the moving force behind this insurrection was the C.P." (CCP Shanghai District Committee, 1983)

At that time the Shanghai General Labor Union counted 502 affiliated unions and 820,000 members, but only 3,000 Party members — not even a third of ten thousand. To close that gap in ten days, to make up a shortfall of well over two-thirds, was no easy matter; it was simply an impossible task. And more than that: with the good swept along with the bad and every sort of character mixed in together, even the workers' pickets had yet to be made pure and fit to fight.

In fact, on the morning of 23 March the secretaries of the departments under the Party's Shanghai District Committee held a joint conference; that afternoon, the departments and the industrial-union federation met jointly; and at nine that night the Shanghai District Committee convened its Special Committee. The leading men of the Party — Chen Duxiu, Peng Shuzhi, Luo Yinong, Zhao Shiyan, Wang Shouhua — were all of them uneasy past telling. With one accord they came back to the same worries about the workers' pickets: too few to command them, order very bad, a want of capable men, and could the rank and file be trusted at all.

Luo Yinong said: "The workers' pickets are worn to exhaustion, and Zhou Enlai is at his wit's end."

Chen Duxiu said: "The pickets need a cadre committee. We must bring in a hundred-odd capable men as cadres and forge a core out of them; without it there is no fighting strength."

Luo Yinong said: "As things stand the pickets are impure, of the most mixed makeup, with every kind of man in them. They must be tightly reorganized."

Chen Duxiu said: "You are right. We cannot let a rising won and a victory in hand cover a hundred faults with one fine stroke. First, organize the cadres; second, weed out the doubtful elements — both at once, and both gripped in earnest. On no account may we grow numb to it or take it lightly."

Zhao Shiyan said: "Weeding them out is awkward work. It breeds resentment easily, and it is dangerous; better to pack them off into the Northern Expedition Army."

Chen Duxiu said: "Someone must take charge of this, and quickly. On the one side the unions should look out for men; on the other, open formal talks with Xue Yue — tell him the unions can handle it for him."

Zhao Shiyan said: "Xue Yue told me today he would help all he could. He welcomes workers as soldiers."

Chen Duxiu said: "Good. First, organize the cadres; second, set up the establishment; third, train them; fourth, under Xue Yue's name, carry out the weeding. That is settled, then."

The commander-in-chief of the workers' pickets at that time was Gu Shunzhang. The root of the pickets' "most mixed makeup" lay in Gu Shunzhang himself, a textbook lumpen proletarian. Overbearing by nature and out to best everyone, he built cliques and ran his little gangs, and he had been a foreman at the cigarette works of the Nanyang Brothers Tobacco Company besides. He fit to the life the marvelous portrait Mao Zedong once drew of men of his kind: "They are the most unsettled of all who live. Everywhere they have their secret societies — the Triads in Fujian and Guangdong; the Elder Brothers in Hunan, Hubei, Guizhou, and Sichuan; the Big Sword Society in Anhui, Henan, and Shandong; the Society of the Way in Zhili and the three eastern provinces; the Green Gang in Shanghai and other places — which have all in their time been the mutual-aid bodies of their political and economic struggles. What to do with these people is one of China's hard problems. They can fight bravely enough, but there is destruction in them; led the right way, they can be turned into a revolutionary force." (Mao Zedong, 1925)

When Zhou Enlai made use of Gu Shunzhang, it was exactly this "brave fighting" he was using.

Zhou Enlai hoped, by "leading him the right way," to turn Gu Shunzhang "into a revolutionary force."

But Gu Shunzhang was tyrannical and swaggering, puffed up with his own deserts, and he threw back in Zhou Enlai's face all the pains Zhou had taken with him.

Because Gu Shunzhang was "a child born in the slums of Shanghai," he "hungered to be taken seriously in politics and in society." (John Byron and Robert Pack, 1998)

Wakeman writes that Gu Shunzhang "had the ways of a Shanghai street-corner tough," and that he was "a master of disguise and deceit." (Wakeman, 2011)

Zhang Guotao says that Gu Shunzhang was, "in looks and in habits, something of a Shanghai dandy." (Zhang Guotao, 1991)

Cai Mengjian says Gu Shunzhang was "full of scheming, versed in psychology, fond of his pleasures, and hungry to dominate." (Cai Mengjian, 1980)

Xu Enzeng calls Gu Shunzhang a "homicidal maniac," the very image of "a jackal that eats men when it is hungry and picks fights when it is full." (Xu Enzeng, 1992)

Yang Zhihua — the wife of Qu Qiubai, also known as Xinghua, Wenjun, and Duning; born in 1901 in Xiaoshan, Zhejiang; a Communist since 1924, who served in turn as a member of the Party's Central Women's Department, head of the women's department of the Shanghai District Committee, director of the Central Women's Commission, and head of the women workers' department of the All-China Federation of Labor — said: "The marks of Gu Shunzhang. One: short, wiry, full of stratagems, slippery, brave, and very skilled at conjuring. Two: he said little, and never once told a comrade of his own past or his connections. Three: as a rule he did not read documents, and seldom spoke at meetings. Four: he lived loosely. ... He was an old Party member, and he had carried out the Party's decisions bravely in the past, true — but what was the substance of that bravery? He had grasped neither doctrine nor policy, and all his work was empty of revolutionary consciousness. The common lumpen proletarians of Shanghai are fond of bravery; but precisely because there is no revolutionary consciousness in them, they cannot protect themselves, cannot fight to the end for the interests of the proletariat as a whole, and even turn into traitors to their class. Gu Shunzhang was the very type of the Shanghai lumpen proletarian." (Du Ning, 1981)

The textbook lumpen proletarian Gu Shunzhang, once he had become Zhou Enlai's deputy and taken the real running of the Central Special Branch into his hands, grew more corrupt and debased by the day, and turned at last into his own opposite.

The Central Special Branch had come into being by stages, and this mattered: most of the men of the Third Section — the Action Section, commonly called the "Red Squad" — had been, in the days of the May Thirtieth and above all through the three armed uprisings of the Shanghai workers, the backbone of the workers' pickets and Gu Shunzhang's own subordinates, used to taking his orders. So Gu Shunzhang confounded comrades with hired thugs, the revolutionary fellowship of Party comrades with the gangland loyalty that will take a knife in the ribs for a friend, the Party's armed strength with the muscle of the underworld — and from this got it into his head that the Party needed him, and not he the Party. More and more he looked down on everything and set himself above the Party, forever saying: "No great matter of the Center can be settled without me, Gu Shunzhang."

Zhou Enlai urged Gu Shunzhang to turn back before it was too late, to rein in at the cliff's edge, telling him that such political arrogance ran flat against the Party's rules of organization. But Gu Shunzhang said one thing and meant another, obeyed to his face and defied him behind his back, and went on doing exactly as he pleased. He turned no one away and took in every renegade; for all the world like the Green Gang opening its initiation hall, he swept up the desperadoes of the underworld by the score.

In truth, our Party had never been against "beating the dogs." The Central Special Branch had set up its "Red Squad" precisely to put down traitors and root out enemy agents. But the aim was to kill one and warn a hundred, to catch the ringleader first — never to widen the target at will.

Gu Shunzhang, though, believed assassination could do anything. All he knew was skulking and killing, and nothing about him was like a Communist. He had no ideals and no faith, and dreaded nothing so much as a world at peace. He loved a kidnapping and a knife-fight, the blade going in clean and coming out red. He loved his meat in great chunks and his wine in great bowls: wine today, drunk today, whatever gave the sharpest thrill, whatever spread the most terror. His blind blood-and-iron violence brought the fury of the reactionary authorities crashing down, and in the end it was the revolution that paid.
