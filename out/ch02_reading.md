## Chapter Two

Toward five in the morning a few scattered drops of rain fell. There was a wind. It was far cooler than the night before; the Fahrenheit thermometer had dropped nearly ten degrees. But after nine o'clock the sunlight burned away the murky clouds and hung in mid-sky like a canopy of fire, the mercury climbed to eighty degrees all the same, and people felt the menace of a heat still more unbearable than before.

The ushers of the Wu mansion, each holding a white paper placard lettered "Usher," wore long gowns of coarse black cloth, and about their waists were bound broad, heavy sashes of a single length of white cloth, long and wide. Under the fierce sun they threaded back and forth like shuttles, hurrying from the front gate to the great drawing-room that served as the mourning hall and then back again to the gate to "usher in" each newly arrived condolence-caller, until every one of them was running with sweat. Before half past ten this shift of eight men could still now and then perch a minute or two on the end of the long wooden bench beside the band of musicians at the gate, lift the white sash at their waists to wipe the sweat from their faces, use the "Usher" placard for a fan to catch their breath, and grumble that Third Master Wu would not hire a few more hands; but once the venomous sun was beating straight down on their heads and the mourners came surging in like a tide, and the two bands of drummers and pipers at the gate and before the mourning hall blew and beat without pause for breath, these usher-attendants became simply machines running to and fro, without even the leisure to think of grumbling at Third Master Wu, and at most would glance now and then at the six attendants waiting before the mourning hall and secretly envy them their better luck.

The blare of motor-horns; the flutes, the suona, the little gongs of the band, all mingling in a funeral dirge; the servants shoving this way and that and bawling "Tea over here! Soda-water over there!"; the wrangling at the counter where cab-fares and meal-money were paid out; the shouts of the police and plain-clothes detectives at the gate driving off the idle hangers-on; the acrid reek of cigarettes and the stink of sweating bodies — all of it fused into one and spread through every hall and room of the Wu mansion and through the garden of eight or nine mu that it stood in.

In the great dining-room to the right of the mourning hall a whole roomful of people were packed tight. A blackwood curio-cabinet, curved over like a moon-bridge, straddled the middle of that long, broad dining-room and divided it into a front and a back half. Along the right side of the back half ran a row of windows looking straight out onto the garden; hard against them stood a tall trellis of banksia rose that filled this half of the room with green shade and heavy scent. The left side was wall, but with two doors in it, one forward and one back; outside the rear door was a covered verandah, set now with many little tea-tables and chairs and thronged too with a crowd of mourners holding forth at the top of their voices — a surf of "standard gold," "big silver bars," "cotton and yarn," "so many taels and mace" that deafened the ear, and threaded through it the hiss of servants opening bottles of soda-water. But at the far left end of the verandah, close by one of the doors, a man of nearly thirty in a yellow military uniform and tall riding-boots, three or four cloisonné medals on the left of his chest, sat alone in a rocking-chair slowly drinking soda-water, his eyes fixed from time to time on the door beside him. The door was shut now, but whenever it swung open a crack an intoxicating scent of powder and rouge and a fine patter of laughing talk escaped through the gap.

All at once the man in uniform set down his glass of soda-water and stood up; the steel spurs on the heels of his riding-boots rang out with a chime, and coming to attention he made a bow of sixty degrees to the half-figure of a woman that leaned out through the door.

The woman was the Wu young mistress, and this sudden, ceremonious salute took her aback for a moment. But by the time the man in uniform had straightened again she had recovered herself, and with a smile she nodded and said:

"Why, it is Staff Officer Lei! When did you come? — Thank you, thank you!"

"Not at all, not at all! I had meant to call tomorrow to take my leave, and now it happens to fall on the old master's great occasion, so of course I ought to come and see him coffined. I hear the old master passed away last night; then you, Madam Wu, must be quite worn out."

Staff Officer Lei answered with a modest smile, but his eyes were taking in the young mistress's plain mourning dress: a cheongsam of black gauze, the sleeves drawn tight over the arms and reaching past the elbow, the hem falling to the ankle, setting off with an odd quietness her tall and slender figure. Her face was without powder or rouge; the two arched eyebrows, neither heavy nor faint, were quite natural; the rims of her eyes were faintly red, yet the eyes themselves shone as brightly as ever, forever turning and glancing — and with every glance they gave out an infinite wit, an infinite tenderness. Staff Officer Lei could not help the leap of his heart. Such a fair and lovely "Wu young mistress" he was seeing for the first time; but another fair and lovely shadow, buried five years deep in his heart — one not yet called "the Wu young mistress" but only "Miss Lin Peiyao" — rose of a sudden before his eyes and gnawed at him. This return of the "past," and at just such a moment, was truly too cruel. So, not waiting for the young mistress's answer, Staff Officer Lei bit his lip, bowed once more, and hurried away, threading through the surf of "standard gold" and "cotton yarn" into the back half of the dining-room.

The moment he was through the door two voices hailed him together:

"Ah, Staff Officer Lei! You come in the nick of time — you tell us!"

This cry, uttered by two men as one, worked at once like a spell: the voices arguing over something broke off, many faces turned, and many eyes shot toward Staff Officer Lei where he stood by the door. The lightly powdered image of the young mistress still clinging to his brain vanished on the instant. Smiling faintly, his glance sweeping over the assembled faces, he raised his right hand quickly to touch the brim of his military cap and quickly dropped it again, then went up to the knot of men, clapped a short fat fellow on the shoulder with his left hand and gripped with his right the hand held out to him, and said, as though letting out a breath of relief:

"Surely you are not in here debating standard gold and cotton-and-yarn by the tael and the mace? That sort of thing — I am wholly a layman."

The short fat man raised his eyebrows in disbelief and laughed aloud, but his chance to speak was snatched away by the young fellow who had put out his hand to Staff Officer Lei:

"Not standard gold, not cotton-and-yarn — but not the foxtrot or the tango or the song 'Liwa Lida' you are such a master of, either. We are talking here about the fighting at the front. Sit down first, and then we'll talk."

"Ai! Huang Fen! Nothing good ever comes out of your mouth!"

Putting on an air of protest, and knitting his brows as he spoke, Staff Officer Lei squeezed himself onto the sofa where the young man in Western dress called Huang Fen was sitting. Huang Fen was, like him, a Whampoa man; the two had smelt gunpowder together on the battlefield and were on no bad terms. But of the amusements Staff Officer Lei loved and excelled at Huang Fen was wholly ignorant; and conversely, though Staff Officer Lei could keep the secret of the "work" Huang Fen was fond of doing, whenever they came to talk of it he only shook his head. Of late the two saw each other almost every day, yet not once did they meet without squabbling. Now, in front of all these half-familiar strangers, Huang Fen was in his old vein, and Staff Officer Lei felt thoroughly ill at ease; he would have liked to slip away, but was ashamed to get up and leave at once.

For an instant there was silence. It seemed that, because of the newcomer, everyone was minded to stand on courtesy and none would be first to speak. Just then another group crowded into the front half of the dining-room burst out laughing in the middle of their noisy talk. "Damn him! … Why don't you thrash him?" someone bawled amid the laughter. The voice struck Staff Officer Lei as very familiar, and he turned to look, but the short fat man and another man with a small head and a long neck cut off his view. They were sitting beside a square table, their backs to the moon-bridge curio-cabinet, the table crowded with soda bottles and fruit-dishes. Seeing that Staff Officer Lei's eyes were on the long-necked man, the short fat one supposed he wished to make his acquaintance, and got up quickly to say:

"Let me introduce you. Staff Officer Lei — this is Mr. Sun Jiren, general manager of the Pacific Steamship Company."

Staff Officer Lei smiled and nodded to Sun Jiren; taking the name-card handed to him, he glanced at it hurriedly and made the usual small talk:

"Do you still run the northern Anhui long-distance motor line, Mr. Sun? Transport by land and water both in one hand. I admire it, I admire it."

"Just so! Sun Jiren goes about things with grit and with an eye for the main chance; only it is a pity that with the fighting now begun, northern Anhui happens to lie in the military zone, and his enterprise has had to be suspended for a while. — But, Staff Officer Lei, how is the fighting really going of late?"

It was the short fat man who answered in Sun Jiren's stead. He was famous for his "love of bringing people together" and was the best of hands at puffing others up; among his friends he had earned the nickname "the Red-Headed Match" — not because he owned the Guangda Match Factory, but really because the way he "struck alight" anywhere he went was so like a strike-anywhere match. His real name, Zhou Zhongwei, was for that reason quite eclipsed.

The words were scarcely out of Zhou Zhongwei's mouth when several men cried out together:

"How is the fighting really going? How is it going?"

Staff Officer Lei smiled faintly and gave only a vague answer:

"Much the same, on the whole, as the news in the papers."

"But that is the papers saying every day that the Central Army is winning, while the word about town is all that our side is faring badly. When there is no reliable news in the papers, people only grow the more panic-stricken."

So said a man of forty-odd with a pair of moustaches, in an uncommonly loud, clear voice. Staff Officer Lei knew him for Wang Hefu, general manager of the Daxing Coal Mining Company; two years before, when Staff Officer Lei had been stationed with a regiment in a certain county of Henan, he had met him.

Everyone nodded, in sympathy with Wang Hefu's remark. Sun Jiren now wagged his long neck and spoke.

"The word about town may be overdrawn. But the wounded soldiers coming in this time are truly not few! One of my company's downriver boats was commandeered the day before yesterday at Pukou and carried more than a thousand wounded to be quartered about Changzhou and Wuxi. From what the wounded say, it is simply terrible."

"The Japanese papers even say that a certain person has come to a secret understanding with the North and is about to turn his coat!"

So put in, hastily, a silk-filature owner named Zhu Yinqiu who sat diagonally opposite Sun Jiren; he shot a hostile glance at Staff Officer Lei and nudged with his elbow the man beside him, Chen Junyi, owner of the Wuyun Silk-Weaving Mill, a lean man of nearly forty. But Chen Junyi only smiled.

Staff Officer Lei did not notice how much unfriendliness there was in Zhu Yinqiu's look, nor mark the secret signal that passed between Zhu Yinqiu and Chen Junyi; but he was somewhat put out. As a soldier on active service, he found these questions truly hard to answer. What made him the more uneasy was that at his side sat Huang Fen, who was forever given to "firing off big guns." After pondering a moment, he looked at Sun Jiren and said:

"So it was your company's boat that carried a thousand wounded? The casualties this time are, it seems, not few. Once one fights in earnest, sacrifice cannot be helped; but the enemy's losses are heavier still! Huang Fen, do you remember how it was with us on the Beijing–Hankou line in the fifth month of the sixteenth year? In those days our Fourth Army and Eleventh Army lost more than twenty thousand killed and wounded, and Hankou and Wuchang became a world of wounded men — yet in the end we won the battle."

As he said this a flush came into Staff Officer Lei's face; he cast a glance at the listeners round about to gauge how much his words had told, and at the same time meant to turn the talk in another direction. But to his surprise Huang Fen, with a cold laugh, came out with these few sharp words of rebuttal:

"You speak of the fighting on the Beijing–Hankou line in the fifth month of the sixteenth year? That was a very different thing from now! The dead and wounded were many then because men charged for all they were worth. But now it is likely the very opposite, is it not?"

As though a bomb had gone off beside him, Staff Officer Lei's face suddenly changed. He rose, looked about him, then abruptly sat down again, and forcing a smile said:

"Old Huang, don't talk so recklessly!"

"Recklessly? Whether what I just said is reckless you know well enough. If not, why are you still lingering in the rear even now?"

"The day after tomorrow I go up to the front!"

So Staff Officer Lei answered loudly, forcing a savage grin onto his face. This "proclamation" of a cry moved not only the group before him but affected the people over yonder in the front half of the room as well; the talk there broke off short, and then several men came running over. They had not made out clearly what it was all about; they only saw "the Red-Headed Match," Zhou Zhongwei, wreathe his whole face in smiles, take Staff Officer Lei by the arm, and say, with his eyes on Sun Jiren:

"Jiren, let us give Staff Officer Lei a farewell dinner tomorrow — tomorrow evening?"

Before Sun Jiren could answer, Wang Hefu hastened to show his agreement:

"Staff Officer Lei and I are old acquaintances; let me be the host! — Or else the three of us can go shares, that will do too."

So this little impromptu conclave split into two groups. Zhou Zhongwei, Sun Jiren, Wang Hefu and three or four others sat round the square table, with Staff Officer Lei for their centre, trading the ordinary polite civilities. The other group, eight or nine men — Zhu Yinqiu, Chen Junyi and the rest — gathered before the row of windows on the right, most of them standing, with Huang Fen for their centre, still arguing over victory and defeat at the front. The several who had come running from the front half of the dining-room joined this group. Huang Fen's voice was the loudest; to one Tang Yunshan, newly come among them, he said, bluntly:

"Yunshan, do you know? Lei Ming too is going up to the front! That proves the front really is hard pressed; otherwise they would never have called him up."

"That goes without saying! In the action at Yejigang a few days ago the crack newly-formed First Division was wiped out to a man. German officers to drill them, the very latest German arms — and still it could not stand against the Northwest Army's contempt for death! — But what is Lei Ming going for? Still as a staff officer, I suppose?"

"Most likely he is to be made a brigade commander. The brigade and regimental commanders killed this time are half a dozen at the least!"

"They say a certain great man has been wounded, a certain army commander killed in action — is it false, or true?"

So Zhu Yinqiu suddenly cut in to ask. Tang Yunshan laughed loudly, his glance grazing Huang Fen's face as if to say, "You see! The news spreads wide and fast!" But before his laughter was done, one of the others amplified Zhu Yinqiu's report:

"He is not dead yet. Seriously wounded, it seems. Someone has certainly seen him lying in the French Hospital on Rue Père Robert."

It was Chen Junyi who said this, and as though in dread that the others would not believe his certain news, having used so positive a tone he turned his head to call on the big, tall Dr. Ding to bear him out:

"Dr. Ding, you can surely vouch that this news of mine is no idle talk? Dr. Bo at the French Hospital is a classmate of yours, is he not? You cannot but know."

Every eye now fastened on Dr. Ding. At first he seemed at a loss, not seeing why Chen Junyi should drag him in; but then, as if enlightened, he smiled and said slowly:

"Quite true. The wounded officers are very many. I am a physician; bullet wounds, bayonet wounds, wounds from shell-splinters — I cannot but know them, I can tell them apart plainly enough. But as to who is an army commander, who a brigade or regimental commander, of that I can make nothing whatever. My trade is medicine, and to my eyes the wound on a common soldier and the wound on an army commander are fundamentally no different at all; so turn it as I will, I still do not know whether there was any army commander, or which of them was one!"

Pff! — The men listening all burst out laughing. After the laughter came dissatisfaction. First of all Chen Junyi, shaking his head in great displeasure. The clamouring dispute started up again. But all at once a man came running in from outside, in a white flannel suit, his hair combed to a high gloss; he pushed hurriedly into Dr. Ding's knot of men and, like a bird picking out its food, singled out a middle-aged man in a pale-blue Indian-silk gown with a tuft of "toothbrush moustache" on his lip, clapped him on the shoulder, and cried:

"Zhuangfei, the bonds have dropped again! How stand your one hundred thousand in Troop-Disbandment? There are too many rumours, the market's mood is bearish, and by my reckoning they will fall further yet!"

This shook men's hearts more than any dispatch from the front! Li Zhuangfei of the "toothbrush moustache" changed colour, to be sure, and the group about Zhou Zhongwei and Staff Officer Lei came hurrying over to inquire as well. In these times, of all who had a few coppers in hand, who was not tumbling about in the bond market? Word was that every kind of government bond and treasury note had plunged together, and each man's mind ran its own way: the "bears" laughed with their mouths stretched wide in delight, while the "bulls" swallowed their tears!

"The bonds have dropped again! Trading's halted at the limit!"

Someone stood shouting it by the door that led to the verandah. At once a troop of men came surging in from the verandah — the same crowd who had been bawling "standard gold," "cotton and yarn," "so many taels and mace" out there — all goggle-eyed, craning their necks, peering this way and shoving a step that way, and asking in a hubbub:

"Is it the Customs bonds?"

"Is it the Reorganization bonds?"

"Is it the Troop-Disbandment bonds?"

"Coffin-edge! Everybody's playing old Mr. Wu now!"

This apt bit of wit, struck off on the spot, set even some of the losing speculators, long-faced as mourners, laughing out loud. By now the five or six still left in the front half of the dining-room had been drawn in too by this suddenly whirling vortex of bonds. But they stood a little apart, in the attitude of onlookers. Among them were Fan Bowen and Sunfu's distant clansman-cousin Wu Zhisheng, a university student of sociology. Fan Bowen closed one eye and murmured:

"O the frenzy of speculation! O the frenzy of speculation! You, the flood of gold! Overflow! Overflow! Burst every dyke away! …"

Then he clapped Wu Zhisheng suddenly on the shoulder and asked, aloud:

"Zhisheng, that handsome fellow in the white suit who ran in just now — do you know him? He is a queer creature! Han Mengxiang is his name; he is a broker at the exchange, and yet he can write poetry too — very good poetry! Ah, gold and the poetic spirit have struck up a strange alliance in that man! — Never mind, let us be off; let us go and find young Du and Peishan! The air in the little sitting-room over there is likely not so foul as here, not so reeking of the stink of copper cash!"

Without waiting to learn whether Wu Zhisheng agreed or not, Fan Bowen took hold of him and went. By now the crowd that had thronged the dining-room was drifting apart too, and only five or six were left — industrialists with no great personal stake in the rise and fall of the bonds, and political men of the stamp of Staff Officer Lei, Huang Fen, and Tang Yunshan — drinking soda-water by the quantity and talking a great deal. But the subject of their talk had shifted now from military and political matters to amusements: roulette, the salt-pork houses, the greyhound track, the Pinaud baths, dance-hostesses, film stars; and now Staff Officer Lei felt free enough to speak.

The time was drawing slowly on toward noon. The mourners grew fewer. The two bands of drummers and pipers at the gate and before the mourning hall now blew and beat as though "taking turns." At times both fell silent together, and people felt an odd stillness, as if something had been suddenly plucked away from beside their ears. In such moments the words "Pinaud baths," "dance-hostesses," "film stars," all these bewitching names, rang out the more loudly.

All at once every mouth shut, as though such naked, fleshly loose talk was a little shameful in this abrupt "stillness."

Tang Yunshan raised a hand unconsciously to scratch his bald pate, cast a glance round the company, and suddenly burst out laughing. At that everyone laughed too, as though catching his meaning, and so retrieved the awkwardness that had fallen on them unawares.

When the laughter had passed, Staff Officer Lei looked at Zhou Zhongwei and said, quite gravely:

"Everyone says that dear gold and cheap silver are a fine chance for China to revive her industries and push her home-made goods. How is it really, in fact?"

Zhou Zhongwei shut his eyes and shook his head. After a while he opened them and answered indignantly:

"I have suffered every kind of loss from dear gold and cheap silver! The materials for making matches — the chemicals, the wooden splints, the box-shells — all come from abroad; the moment the gold price climbs, these materials climb with it, so where is the good to me? Buy native materials, then? Very well — raw-material tax, transit duty, likin, laid on layer upon layer, until it comes dearer than the foreign article! And on top of that, Japanese matches and Swedish matches compete for all they are worth, and the Chinese, for their part, have no notion of patriotism and will not use home-made goods …"

But this grand oration of Zhou Zhongwei's in praise of home-made goods had to stop halfway, for out of the corner of his eye he caught sight of the matches on the table, beside the German-silver ashtray — and they were the Swedish Phoenix brand. He gave a few forced coughs, pulled out a handkerchief, and mopped desperately at his fat face. Tang Yunshan smiled, took up that box of Swedish matches to light another Garrick, blew out a thick cloud of smoke, and clapped Zhou Zhongwei hard on the shoulder, saying:

"I beg your pardon, Zhou Zhongwei. To speak the plain truth, your factory's products really do want improving. Your safety matches go without saying; but even your strike-anywhere matches will not 'strike alight at a touch' everywhere, and set against your own elegant nickname they fall a long way short."

Zhou Zhongwei's face flushed red on the instant, truly like a "Red-Headed Match." Luckily Sun Jiren came quickly to the rescue:

"This is no fault of yours, Zhongwei. The workers are too unruly, past all managing. Ever since there have been unions, the products of every factory come out slow and shoddy; eh, Zhu Yinqiu, am I right in what I say?"

"That is just how it is! But you, Jiren, know the half and not the whole! Take our silk trade: at present it is pitiful indeed, beset on every side. The workers demand higher wages; the foreign market is undercut by the competition of Japanese silk; the home taxes are too heavy; and the financiers will not be accommodating over loans! Think of it — costs high, sales poor, capital pinched — what hope is left? The very thought of it disheartens me!"

Zhu Yinqiu had begun to air his grievances too. Before his eyes there rose at once his four great enemies — and above all the financiers, who had him by the throat. The old-calendar Dragon-Boat Festival was almost upon him, and the banks and native banks he dealt with had long since warned him that they could "accommodate" him no further: his mortgage loans must be settled in full when they fell due; but with silk prices sunk low and the foreign-consignment trade slack, with what was he to settle them? He heaved a sigh and went on indignantly:

"Since last year Shanghai has had a glut of hard silver. Money is by no means tight. Yet the financiers know only how to play the bonds and speculate in land — ten million, twenty million, on the grandest scale! But let one of us mill-owners run short of ready money for a time and try to raise a loan of a hundred or eighty thousand, and it is as though you were asking for their very lives; the terms they set are so harsh they make one's blood boil!"

Hearing this put so bluntly, no one cared to say much. Huang Fen seemed to feel much sympathy for Zhu Yinqiu, yet could not help asking:

"What I don't understand is why your 'filature silk' depends wholly on the foreign market. Then what silk do China's own silk-and-satin weaving mills use?"

"Just so — I don't understand it either! Mr. Chen, you can surely answer this question."

So Staff Officer Lei added, turning to look at Chen Junyi, owner of the Wuyun Silk-Weaving Mill.

But this mill-owner said nothing, only smiled where he sat. Zhu Yinqiu answered in his stead:

"They use our second-grade goods. Of late they have used even less of the second grade. They use Japanese raw silk and rayon. Our top-grade goods depend solely on the French and American markets, as they always have. In these last two years the Japanese government has offered bounties on raw-silk exports — on both silk and cocoons, wholly tax-free — and Japanese silk has beaten Chinese silk clean out of the markets of Lyon and New York."

Staff Officer Lei and Huang Fen sprang up crying out that it was monstrous. They looked round at the faces of the company, going over them one by one in hope of finding some sign of "agreement"; but what astonished them the more was that not a face there showed the least surprise — as though it were the most natural thing in the world that China's silk-weaving trade should not use Chinese silk! At this Chen Junyi spoke up, slowly:

"We too mix in some Japanese silk and rayon because we cannot help it. Take Zhu Yinqiu's filature silk: his costs are heavy and the price of the silk is already no small thing, yet by the time it reaches our hands there is still a tax of 65 yuan 6 jiao to be paid on every picul; and the native silk of the various provinces has been climbing in price of late too, with a tax of 111 yuan 6 jiao 9 fen on every picul of it, which again we bear. And this is to speak of the raw material alone. Once it is woven into silks and satins there is a production tax, a sales tax, a transit tax — taxes heaped layer on layer, so that the goods have scarcely stirred before a tax comes after them. Of course the wool comes off the sheep's own back, and the buyer is left to bear it all; but then sales fall off. We mill-owners, to keep our sales up, must find ways to cut our costs, must mix in some cheaper raw material. … Everyone says silks and satins are dear, and yet we mill-owners still get no good of it!"

Then came a moment's silence. The wind carried in the sound of the suona and flutes of the musicians outside, and it seemed uncommonly mournful, as if playing a dirge for China's silk-weaving trade.

Wang Hefu, who had long been silent, suddenly rose to his feet, clapped his hands together, and said, as though in jest:

"Enough! Chen Junyi can at least mix in some Japanese silk and rayon. But Sun Jiren and I? Now that North and South have opened fire, all we can do is sit idle in Shanghai, watch the greyhounds, and make the round of the brothels! To the devil with industry, then! Let us rather think up some amusement and enjoy ourselves a while!"

Before he had finished, a sudden gust of perfume swept in a young woman with bare shoulders and bare arms. Her dress of black gauze — the new 1930 Paris summer fashion — set off the more the lustrous white of her skin and the bright red of her lips. She said not a word, her whole face all smiles; standing at a distance, she only let her soft, alluring eyes glance toward the knot of people on this side.

The first to spy her was Zhou Zhongwei. With a cry of "Aiyo!" the short fat man sprang up, flung both arms flailing in the air, opened his big mouth in a grin, and shouted:

"All rise, and welcome the socialite, Miss Xu Manli!"

The men all turned about in surprise, before they had readied the sort of smiling faces they kept for welcoming a pretty woman; but Miss Xu Manli was already swaying her waist and, a little handkerchief pressed to her lips, giggling without stop. Now Staff Officer Lei rose too, stepped forward, put out his right hand, and said with a smile:

"Manli, why do you come only now? You must pay a forfeit!"

"And how am I to be punished?"

Xu Manli swayed her waist again and, tilting her head, said it as if deliberately holding back a laugh; she had already come up to Staff Officer Lei, and taking his hand she gave it a hard squeeze and then held it lightly for some four or five seconds before flinging it abruptly away and turning to greet Zhou Zhongwei and the rest.

The talk grew lively again, of course, and even Zhu Yinqiu and Chen Junyi, who had been airing their grievances a moment before, were all spring in the face. While Xu Manli was busy with the others, Zhu Yinqiu leaned over and said a few words in Tang Yunshan's ear. Tang Yunshan laughed aloud and kept eyeing Xu Manli. Then Zhu Yinqiu said, deliberately loud:

"Junyi, I have just remembered. The woman who took a room with Zhao Botao at the Cathay Hotel yesterday was —"

Xu Manli whipped her head round and gave Zhu Yinqiu a hard, attentive look, then at once turned her face away again and went on with her practised pleasantries, all the while pricking up her ears to catch every word Zhu Yinqiu said.

But what came next, unexpectedly, was Chen Junyi's voice:

"Zhao Botao? The Zhao Botao who deals in bonds? He is a big bull; he scoops up every kind of bond there is."

"But he scoops up all sorts of women too. The one I saw yesterday looked like some family's widow."

Zhu Yinqiu said this in a deliberately low voice, though he knew for certain that Xu Manli would hear it clearly enough. And he saw, moreover, that the socialite seemed to shudder all over, so that even her laughter trembled oddly.

Staff Officer Lei's whole attention was fixed now on Xu Manli. Little by little it was the two of them who talked most, and most warmly. What he said she could not tell, but of a sudden a flush flew up over Xu Manli's face; with a charming toss of her head she fell to giggling again. Wang Hefu, seated opposite them, saw how things stood, cocked up a thumb, and was about to cry "Bravo!" when all at once Tang Yunshan slipped over from the side, laid a hand on Staff Officer Lei's shoulder, and put a queer question:

"Old Lei! Are you out to 'slaughter the bulls'?"

"What? I never deal in bonds!"

Staff Officer Lei answered, taken aback.

"Then why are you straining so hard to squeeze out what another man has scooped up?"

As he spoke Tang Yunshan could not help laughing himself. Zhu Yinqiu and Chen Junyi actually clapped their hands and laughed at the top of their throats. Xu Manli's powdered face flushed crimson on the instant; feigning not to notice, she called again and again for the servants to bring soda-water. But everyone had guessed more or less how the matter stood, and a burst of laughter filled that long, broad dining-room.

The teasing might have gone further still, had not Du Zhuzhai come hurrying in.

As though suddenly recalling that they had come, after all, to mourn, that the mourning hall was next door, and that this Du Zhuzhai was a near kinsman of the Wu family, the merry company turned grave on the instant, and several of them yawned and yawned.

Du Zhuzhai, all affability as usual, greeted them, and at the same time seemed to say to himself:

"What? Sunfu is not here either!"

"Sunfu has not been here."

So someone answered. Du Zhuzhai knitted his brows, turned about in agitation, and, amid a string of "excuse me's," hurried off. After him Xu Manli and Staff Officer Lei slipped out too, one behind the other. By now everyone was tired of sitting, and several went off to the verandah behind the dining-room to find their acquaintances, leaving only the three of them — Huang Fen, Tang Yunshan, and Sun Jiren — still crowded together on one sofa in close talk; their manner was very serious now, their voices low, and the heart of their talk had turned to the "Enlarged Conference in the North" and the strategy of the Feng-Yan armies.

Since he had not found Wu Sunfu, Du Zhuzhai went out into the garden, cut across a stretch of asphalt path, and climbed the largest of the rockeries. In the hexagonal pavilion at its summit two gentlemen were waiting, out of all patience. One was a man of forty-odd, of middle height, with a triangular face and deep-set black eyes that glittered; he was the very Zhao Botao whom Zhu Yinqiu and the others had spoken of a while before — a devil-king of the bond market. Catching sight first of Du Zhuzhai puffing his way up the rockery, he turned to his companion and said:

"Old Zhong, look — only Du Zhuzhai, and no one else; it seems Sunfu has not taken the bait?"

The so-called "Old Zhong" slowly fingered his whiskers, a good three inches and more in length, but did not answer. He must have been all of sixty, square-faced and big-eared, with narrow eyes and a not undistinguished bearing; had the "Hongxian Emperor" of those years not toppled so hastily from his throne, he — Shang Zhongli — would have had every qualification for a "literary attendant" at court. Now, having "passed from office into trade," he made shift as chairman of a trust company, which was reckoned a sad come-down indeed.

Reaching the pavilion, Du Zhuzhai sat down, took out his handkerchief and wiped away the fine beads of sweat on his face, and only then looked at both Zhao and Shang and said:

"I cannot find Sunfu. He was certainly not before the mourning hall, and the ladies said they did not know either. Upstairs he was not to be found at all. And I could hardly go asking everywhere — did you not warn me to be careful of drawing others' notice? — You had best make the matter plain to me first, and I will talk it over with him afterwards."

"The matter is this — to form a secret company and go long on the bonds, as I said just now; within two days we must raise at least four million in ready cash, and Old Zhong and I have not the strength for it alone. If you and Sunfu will come in, the thing is settled; if not, we all drop it!"

So Zhao Botao said rapidly, in his Cantonese-accented Mandarin. His peculiar glittering gaze shot out from its deep sockets, watching Du Zhuzhai's expression with close attention.

"What I don't understand is why you should still want to go long. These last few days the falling trend in the bonds has indeed come of the war, and later they may be looked for to rise; but the fighting will hardly be over at once, will it? And it is by now an open secret that on the Longhai and the Pinghan lines both the Central Army is sorely pressed. Once the scattered small bulls all come out of their cages together, you may buy in all you can and still not lift the price. Besides, this month's settlement day is not more than ten days off; do you mean to take delivery when it falls due? Why, four million in cash would not be enough for that! —"

"What you say is everyone's view. But there is a subtlety in this yet!"

Zhao Botao cut short Du Zhuzhai's argument, smiling mysteriously. Du Zhuzhai raised his head and shut his eyes, seeming to turn the thing over hard in his mind. He knew Zhao Botao's powers were boundless, that no one was better at "putting it about," and that he had connections in military and government circles too — perhaps he had come by some secret military news? And yet it did not seem so. When Du Zhuzhai opened his eyes again, he caught of a sudden Zhao Botao's sharp, sombre gaze fixed on his own face, and all at once a turn of thought leapt up in his brain: Old Zhao was a big bull to begin with, and with settlement day near and the old-calendar Dragon-Boat Festival falling right across it, he must surely be in a panic — so might not this "bull company" of his be a scheme of "the golden cicada casting its shell"? — But then why should Shang Zhongli be following Old Zhao's lead? Old Shang was no bull! Questioning himself so, Du Zhuzhai could not help stealing a glance at Shang Zhongli.

But Old Shang Zhongli's look was all composure, as he cocked up three fingers and slowly stroked his beard.

"What subtlety?"

Du Zhuzhai asked it offhand, while still reckoning within himself; he had all but decided to fob them off with a few words and go, decided against joining in Zhao Botao's "plot" — but Zhao Botao's answer struck him like a bolt of lightning:

"Old Zhong guarantees it: the Northwest Army is about to retreat, and at once! Before this month's settlement the bonds are certain to rally!"

Though Zhao Botao spoke very low, to Du Zhuzhai it was like a thunderclap from a clear sky, drowning out all the clamour of the garden and the blowing and beating of the two bands; and staring at Shang Zhongli in astonishment, he asked, half believing and half in doubt:

"Oh — Old Zhong can see it so surely?"

"It is not seeing it surely — it is doing it surely!"

So Shang Zhongli answered in a low voice, stroking his beard, and gave Zhao Botao another smiling glance. But Du Zhuzhai still did not understand. There was a subtlety, to be sure, in this word "doing" that Shang Zhongli used, and Zhuzhai had always trusted Shang Zhongli's "guarantees"; but the sums going in and out of this present affair were too great, and he had to have it made plain. A look of wavering indecision rose plainly over Du Zhuzhai's goatish face.

Zhao Botao slapped his thigh and laughed aloud, then leaned close to Du Zhuzhai's ear and said, in earnest:

"That is just why I say there is a subtlety in it! That money spent can win a battle everyone knows. But that money spent can also make another man lose a battle — that few have thought of. — Once a man has the money in hand, why should he not gladly lose a fight?"

Du Zhuzhai could scarcely believe his ears. He thought a moment, then sprang suddenly to his feet, put out his hand, cocked up a thumb and waved it before Shang Zhongli's face, and clucking his tongue praised him without stint:

"Old Zhong, I do admire you — a bellyful of statecraft! This is a subtlety indeed!"

"Then you will surely take a share. And Sunfu? You will sound him out."

Zhao Botao pressed a step closer at once; by his manner he seemed to want the thing settled on the spot.

But Shang Zhongli saw that Du Zhuzhai was still a little hesitant. He knew that Du Zhuzhai, though greedy of gain, was also uncommonly suspicious, far short of Wu Sunfu in daring and force of will. So he deliberately gave a step of slack, and said, on the contrary:

"It is true that there has been a go-between, that the other side has been sounded out once and the terms agreed; but I dare not, after all, call it a sure thing. Dealing with the war-lords carries its measure of danger to begin with; the other side may change its mind at the last. So you had best go and talk it over with Sunfu first, Zhuzhai, and we will speak again afterwards."

"And the terms are fixed?"

"Fixed. Three hundred thousand!"

So Zhao Botao answered quickly, seeming a little impatient.

Du Zhuzhai put out his tongue and gave a tittering laugh.

"A round three hundred thousand! More, and we will not have it; less, and they will not do it. A full ten thousand silver dollars to the li; retreat thirty li, and that comes to three hundred thousand."

So Shang Zhongli said slowly, his shrewd narrow eyes nailed on Du Zhuzhai's goatish face.

There was a short silence. At last a firm, bright light shone in Du Zhuzhai's eyes; he looked at Shang Zhongli, then at Zhao Botao, and all three burst out laughing as one. Then the three heads drew together, and they talked away in eager whispers with the greatest zest.

Just then, across a fish-pond, on the grass in the shade of the willows directly facing that hexagonal pavilion, three young men and two young women were likewise arguing over certain "questions." The young women said little, only sending their laughter over to the edge of the pond, where it startled the white geese drowsing at their noonday nap on the water.

"Enough! Stop your arguing, and I will go and fetch them."

So said a vigorous, cat-faced youth — Du Zhuzhai's youngest brother, Xueshi, a university student of engineering.

"Miss Lin, do you agree?"

So Wu Zhisheng turned to ask Lin Peishan. But Lin Peishan pretended not to have heard, and only went on swinging Zhang Susu's hand back and forth as though on a swing. Fan Bowen, standing beside Lin Peishan, smiled without committing himself.

"No objection, so it is carried!"

So Du Xueshi cried, and went flying off toward the mourning hall. Wu Zhisheng, left behind, paced a few steps with his head down, then suddenly came up beside Fan Bowen and asked, in high spirits:

"There is one more question — do you dare bet me again?"

"Say it first; perhaps it is no question at all."

"It is this — whether the characters of the Fourth Young Lady, Huifang, and the Seventh Young Master, Ah Xuan, will change in time to come or not."

"That one I will not bet you on."

"I'll bet! Zhisheng, give your opinion first — will they change, or not?"

Zhang Susu flung Lin Peishan's hand away, cutting in, and came up to Wu Zhisheng.

"And what shall we bet — a Kiss again?"

"And if I win? I've no wish to Kiss a hobgoblin face like yours!"

Fan Bowen and the others all laughed. But Zhang Susu did not laugh; she cocked up one leg and spun herself round in a hopping turn. She was thinking how the Fourth Young Lady's stiff, bashful ways made one at once angry and full of pity to see; and Ah Xuan — his looks were really not bad, yet his nerves were disordered, so that he was clever one moment and hopelessly muddled the next. All of it was the fruit of old Mr. Wu's "Taishang Ganying Pian education." Thinking so, Zhang Susu felt a queer discomfort in her breast, and quite forgot the wager; just then Du Xueshi came flying back, with two men behind him — one the Wu family's legal adviser, Lawyer Qiu Sun, the other Li Yuting.

At this moment the laughter of Zhao Botao and his two companions — the three of them — came over from the hexagonal pavilion on the rockery opposite. Li Yuting looked up, nudged Qiu Sun's arm, and said in a low voice:

"The three magnates of the financial world! Can you guess what they are up to over there?"

Qiu Sun smiled and was about to answer, but was cut off by Wu Zhisheng's shout:

"Lawyer Qiu, Professor Li — now we want to hear the opinions of you two. And you must not fib! Fan Bowen and I have laid a bet! The question is this: when a man wishes both to safeguard the interests of the nation and to safeguard the interests of his own class, is there, or is there not, a conflict between the two?"

"Speak out your opinions honestly! Zhisheng and Bowen have laid a bet, and no small matter hangs on it!"

Du Xueshi joined in the shouting from the side, but his eyes went to Lin Peishan. Lin Peishan, however, made as if none of it concerned her, and crouching on the grass gathered up rose-petals one by one and laid them out into a great big character "Wen."

Since Qiu Sun shook his head, Li Yuting spoke first:

"That depends on what sort of person, and of what standing."

"Just so. We have already given the example. Take Sunfu, say, and the workers at his mill. Now that filature silk sells slackly, Sunfu says to the workers: 'The costs of our filature silk are too heavy, we cannot compete with Japanese silk, our silk trade is on the verge of ruin; to cut costs we have no choice but to cut wages. For the sake of the nation's interests, the workers must bear the pain a while and take a little less in wages.' But the workers answer: 'The cost of living has risen, we cannot eat our fill as it is, and to cut our wages again is to take our very lives. You who have money to be bosses will never go hungry; if you would safeguard the nation's interests, then bear the pain a while yourselves and make a few coppers less.' — Both sides, it appears, are in the right. And yet the interests of the nation and the interests of class, on the two sides, have come into conflict."

"To go hungry is of course a great matter too —"

Li Yuting said the half of it, then drew back again and raised a hand to scratch his scalp. Zhang Susu gave him a very attentive look, of which he took no notice. All were hushed, waiting for him to go on. From the hexagonal pavilion across the fish-pond came another burst of laughter. Li Yuting gave a start and finished out his opinion:

"But come what may, the capitalist must have his profit! A business that makes no money simply cannot stand at all!"

Wu Zhisheng laughed loudly and turned to Fan Bowen:

"Well? It was I who guessed Professor Li's opinion beforehand and rightly. Poet, you have already lost the half of it! The second question you must expound yourself. — Susu, keep an eye on Peishan, or she will slip away!"

Fan Bowen smiled coldly and still made no sound. So Du Xueshi hastened to speak in his stead:

"The workers demand higher wages; the boss says, then you had best seek another place, I will hire other workers — but the workers flatly refuse to go, and still demand higher wages. Here we must ask the legal adviser."

"Labour and capital stand in a relation of contract; neither can compel the other."

No sooner had Qiu Sun said this than Wu Zhisheng and the rest fell laughing again — Fan Bowen himself among them. Lin Peishan, crouching on the ground and seeming not to have been listening at all, sprang up and made to bolt. But it was already too late; Wu Zhisheng and Zhang Susu barred her way and cried:

"Don't run! The Poet has lost outright, and you must settle the score for him! Or else we will have Lawyer Qiu bring suit on our behalf. Young Du — you stand surety! Will you not answer for it, as surety?"

Lin Peishan only laughed and did not answer; watching her chance, she darted out from under Zhang Susu's arm and ran off to the right along the tiger-skin-patterned gravel path by the fish-pond. "Ah —" cried Zhang Susu, and gave chase after her. But Fan Bowen caught Wu Zhisheng by the shoulder and said:

"Don't be too pleased! Young Du, the surety, has not yet given his ruling!"

"What nonsense! Surety and arbiter both at once! There is no such rule. And besides, it was not stated beforehand."

"It was stated: 'If the words of Lawyer Qiu and Li Yuting should prove doubtful, then Young Du shall arbitrate.' Now I hold that the answers of both Lawyer Qiu and Professor Li are doubtful, and I cannot be flatly ruled to have guessed wrong."

"All irresponsible talk! Empty words that give no reason for anything!"

So Du Xueshi put in too, his cat's face grown all at once uncommonly grave.

At this not only Wu Zhisheng was astonished; Qiu Sun and Li Yuting too were quite at a loss. They all gathered round Du Xueshi and looked at him.

"Nation this, class that, contract between labour and capital — all rubbish! I know only that there is a State. And the helm of the State should be set in a firm, iron hand; what matters is doing, not the talking of empty words! And no one may oppose that iron hand which governs the State! Suppose, say, that Chinese silk cannot compete with Japanese silk: then the iron hand that governs the 'State' should, on the one hand, cut down the workers' wages, and on the other compel the capitalist to sell at the very lowest price, so as at all costs to beat Japanese silk out of the markets of Europe and America! And if the capitalist will not sell at a loss — very well! The 'State' may confiscate his factory!"

Du Xueshi said it all out in one breath, goggled his round eyes, and swayed his body a few times, as though he himself were that "iron hand"!

The four who were listening all smiled, but none of them spoke. The laughter of Zhang Susu and Lin Peishan came through the dense trees to the right of the pond, drawing little by little nearer. Fan Bowen cast a glance toward the laughter, then turned and clapped Du Xueshi hard on the shoulder, and said coldly:

"Fine! Only it is a pity you are neither a capitalist nor a worker, still less that 'iron hand'! And there is this besides: your whole oration is likewise 'empty words that give no reason for anything'! Do not forget — what I wagered with Zhisheng just now was not how anything ought to be done, but which of us had rightly guessed the opinions of Lawyer Qiu and Professor Li! — Enough; let this wager of ours end here, unsettled and done with."

Before the last words were out, Fan Bowen was running off to meet Zhang Susu and Lin Peishan as they came from afar.

"It won't do! Poet, do you think to run away?"

So Wu Zhisheng shouted, giving chase. Li Yuting and Lawyer Qiu laughed loudly behind them.

But just as the two of them, Fan and Wu, were about to overtake Lin Peishan and the others, three men came toward them — none other than Du Zhuzhai, Zhao Botao, and Shang Zhongli, talking in low voices as they walked. They gave the four young people a single glance and then fell silent, going quietly along the tiger-skin-patterned gravel path by the pond to near the willows' shade, and there deliberately fetching a bend to avoid the notice of Li Yuting and Lawyer Qiu, made off toward the mourning hall. But Li Yuting was quick-eyed and had seen it all plainly; he tugged at Lawyer Qiu's sleeve and said in a low voice:

"Did you see? The three magnates of the financial world! Some weighty matter is written on their faces."

"That is because an 'iron hand' has just been born here among us!"

So Qiu Sun answered, and smiled again. Li Yuting laughed too. But Du Xueshi, sunk deep in his own thoughts, had heard nothing and seen nothing.

On the steps before the mourning hall Du Zhuzhai met a newly arrived condolence-caller — a distant relative of the Wu family, Lu Kuangshi, a broker at the exchange and something like an assistant manager at the Daya Securities Trust Company besides. The moment he caught sight of Du Zhuzhai, this Lu Kuangshi, a tumbler in the bond market himself, sprang a step forward, took Du Zhuzhai by the cuff, and whispered in his ear:

"I have come by a secret piece of news: the Central Army's position has turned to its advantage, and the bonds are about to rally at any moment. As yet no one knows it, the public mood is all bearish, and the small bulls on my books are all in a hurry to unload. Why not seize the moment and scoop up a few hundred thousand? You have always dealt only in standard gold; now I'd advise you to try the bonds too while the chance offers, and make a few coppers to wet your beak — it would not be bad at all!"

This speech, on Lu Kuangshi's part, may have been kindly meant; but Du Zhuzhai, who was at that very moment joining a secret bull company, was frightened as if by something and all but changed colour. As he listened, a numberless swarm of doubts came boiling up in his mind: could it be that Shang Zhongli's plan had already leaked out? Could it be that the Central Army had truly turned the tables? Or had Zhao Botao and Shang Zhongli conspired to play some new-fangled trick on him? Or was this Lu Kuangshi merely spreading a rumour on purpose, in hope of some profit? — Du Zhuzhai was almost at his wits' end and could make no answer. Stealthily he threw a look at Zhao Botao beside him. No — he had meant to observe Old Zhao's expression closely, but somehow it turned into a look of greeting instead. Seasoned as he was, he had truly lost his method for the moment.

Luckily a saviour appeared. The servant Gaosheng came hurrying up to Zhuzhai and said:

"Our master is in the study. Will the son-in-law please come at once!"

Du Zhuzhai felt a weight lift from his heart; saying "Very well" offhand, he turned to fob off Lu Kuangshi:

"Excuse me, I must leave you; we will talk again later. Do go and sit a while in the dining-room. Gaosheng, pour tea for Mr. Lu."

Having so got Lu Kuangshi off his hands, Du Zhuzhai led both Zhao and Shang back into the garden, found a secluded spot, and the three heads drew together once more, until little by little a look of gladness spread over all three faces again.

"Then I will go and find Sunfu. Botao, do go to the dining-room and work a little on young Lu; and Old Zhong, go back and come to firm terms with the other side."

So Du Zhuzhai said at the last, and the three of them parted.

But Du Zhuzhai had never dreamed that Wu Sunfu was sitting in his study with his brows knit tight. Even at the moment when old Mr. Wu had breathed his last the night before, Sunfu's face had not worn such a look of care as now. Du Zhuzhai had barely sat down and had not yet opened his mouth when Sunfu tossed a sheet of paper over for him to see.

It was a telegram, a few plain words: "Peasants in the countryside restless, garrison in the town weak, peril imminent; wire your instructions for the emergency with all speed. Fei, the 18th."

Du Zhuzhai changed colour at once. Though he had not, like Sunfu, a great deal of property left in their native place, still it was the ground where "the ancestors' cottages and graves" lay, and he could not but be moved by it, come what might. He laid down the telegram, looked at Sunfu's face, and said just four words:

"What is to be done?"

"There is nothing for it but to do what human effort can and then see. Luckily the old master, and Fourth Sister and Seventh Brother, had come away two days ahead, or it would have been an utter disaster. What is left there for the present is no more than the pawnshop, the native bank, the rice-mill and the like; a considerable sum, to be sure, but after all only so much external property. — What is to be done? I have already wired Fei the little-moustache to see the ready cash safely bestowed first, and to shift the goods of the other shops wherever it can be done … Or it may be only a false alarm, and all pass off in peace still — there is no telling. But the garrison is weak, and that will not do at all; we ought to send a joint wire asking the provincial government to send the security corps at all speed to put it down."

Wu Sunfu too seemed somewhat out of his usual self, running on in a long, disjointed stretch of talk before he came at last to his main purpose. He handed Zhuzhai the draft telegram he had made ready, asking the provincial government for troops, for him to look over, and then went to press the electric bell on the wall behind him.

The study door opened softly. But it was two men who came in: besides the servant Gaosheng, the factory accountant, Mo Gancheng, as well.

The moment Wu Sunfu saw Mo Gancheng come unbidden, his brows knit the tighter, and he called out with great authority:

"Gancheng, I told you there was no need to come here today; the important thing is to look after the mill!"

This rebuke threw the accountant Mo Gancheng into a flustered daze; he answered "Yes, yes" twice over and stood there stiff and rigid.

"Is there nothing amiss at the mill?"

Wu Sunfu smoothed his face and asked it offhand, his thoughts turning again to the menace of the peasant rising in his native place. But to his utter surprise Mo Gancheng, trembling all over, brought out these words:

"It is just that something is wrong at the mill —"

"What! Out with it, quick!"

"Perhaps it is nothing, but, but — the wind sets ill. We have not yet given out notice of the wage-cut, and yet the workers already know of it. They, they — since this morning they have had a look of — a look of going slow, and I have come especially to ask — what is to be done."

Now it was Wu Sunfu's face that suddenly changed; he froze where he sat, motionless and speechless, and the purple pimples on his face seemed each to give off a steam of heat. When this fit had passed, he sprang up all at once and roared like a maddened tiger, cursing the workers and cursing Mo Gancheng and the clerks under him:

"So they go slow first, do they? Insolent creatures! Show them what's what! And what are you there to manage? Coming only now to ask what's to be done? Hmph — all you can do at the mill is fool about, flirt, and carry on with your mistresses! Like as not it was you yourselves who let out word of the wage-cut!"

Mo Gancheng only stood by with his head hanging, seeming not to dare so much as draw a breath. At this useless spectacle Wu Sunfu's fury blazed the hotter; his right hand on his hip, his left clenched into a fist and resting on the edge of that solid-steel desk, his eyes all red light, he glared flashing about him as though seeking something to sink his teeth into. All at once he caught sight of Gaosheng standing stiffly to one side, and he shouted at him in a rage:

"What are you standing here for?"

"The master rang the bell just now, and so I came in."

At that Sunfu remembered the draft telegram, and remembered too that Du Zhuzhai was still sitting in the high-backed sofa across from the desk. Zhuzhai had long since read the draft; a cigar held aslant in his mouth, he sat with his eyes closed, turning over his own private thoughts.

Sunfu took up the draft telegram and handed it to Gaosheng, waving his hand as he said:

"Go and send it at once — the quicker the better!"

That said, Wu Sunfu sat down in his solid-steel swivel-chair, took up a pen and dashed off a line on a sheet of notepaper, then crumpled it in his hand and threw it into the wastebasket, and sat pen in hand, brooding.

Du Zhuzhai opened his eyes, and seeing Sunfu's irresolute manner said softly:

"Sunfu, hard measures are not so good as soft ones here."

"That is my own meaning too —"

So Wu Sunfu answered. His anger was calmed now; he twirled the penholder in his hand twice and turned back to Mo Gancheng:

"Gancheng, sit down, and tell out in full what has happened since this morning."

This accountant, who knew Wu Sunfu's temper thoroughly, understood that he might now speak boldly and need no longer put on that air of trembling, piteous fear. So he sat down easily in a spring-cushioned chair at the end of the desk and said slowly:

"It was about nine o'clock this morning. Overseer number two, Wang Jinzhen, came running to the office to report that Yao Jinfeng of reeling-frame number twelve had broken the rules and would not submit to control; and when overseer number nine, Xue Baozhu, made to summon her to the office, who would have thought it — the women workers of reeling-frame number twelve all stopped their machines and joined Yao Jinfeng in an uproar. When we had heard Wang Jinzhen's report and were about to go and quell it, we heard a great shouting, and Xue Baozhu came dragging Yao Jinfeng along; but by then the women in the workshop had every one of them stopped their machines —"

Wu Sunfu knitted his brows, gave Mo Gancheng a sharp look, and, as if much out of patience, cut short his report to ask:

"Say it plainly and shortly — how far has the trouble gone by now?"

"Of the 520 machines in the workshop, only a small half are still at work now — call it work, though it is really the spoiling of cocoons."

At this last sentence Wu Sunfu gave a roar of rage and sprang up; but in an instant he sat down again and asked, rapidly:

"And the cause of the go-slow is —?"

"They demand that Xue Baozhu be dismissed."

"On what grounds?"

"They say she strikes people. — And besides, they demand a rice-allowance again. They asked for it once before, when the price of rice rose to twenty yuan the picul, and now they ask again."

Wu Sunfu gave a snort through his nose and turned to Du Zhuzhai:

"Zhuzhai — it is truly hard to be a silk-filature owner. When rice is dear, the workers come demanding a rice-allowance; but when the silk price is cheap and I stand to lose, no one gives me a silk-allowance. Very well! Gancheng, go back and tell the workers that if they want a rice-allowance, the owner would sooner shut the mill!"

Mo Gancheng answered "Yes," but his two mousy eyes were on Wu Sunfu's face, and showed a look of great difficulty.

"What else is there?"

"Er, er — I beg the Third Master to judge clearly. If the talk of shutting the mill is put out now, I fear it may raise a riot —"

"What do you mean?"

"This time the workers are of one mind, as if they had taken counsel together beforehand."

"Bah! Are you lot all so many walking corpses? How is it you knew nothing whatever beforehand, and come to me for a remedy only when the thing has happened? Overseer number two, Wang Jinzhen, and the inspector, pockmarked Li, both draw an allowance from me, and yet on ordinary days they take no heed of what the workers are about! Have I so much money, and nowhere to spend it, that I keep these dogs for nothing?"

At this Mo Gancheng suddenly grew bold, and dared to answer the "Third Master" back:

"The two of them do their best, too; they keep watch every moment on the workers' doings! But — it is as though the two words 'running dog' were graven on their faces; they meet a wall everywhere and can worm out not a scrap of news. Third Master! The workers are for all the world like people possessed! Yao Jinfeng was always honest, and this time she has taken the lead. Now the workshop is all one uproar: 'Last time we asked for a rice-allowance you fobbed us off with a pack of lies; this time we mean to fight it out to the death! Do you still mean to dock our wages? We want the rice-allowance, the rice-allowance!' They say things are unsteady at every mill. The workers are all like people possessed!"

"Possessed, are they? Ha, ha! I know this devil! The cost of living is high and they cannot eat their fill! But I know another devil besides, a devil bigger and fiercer than this one: the world's industry lies in ruins, and filature silk has fallen in price! …"

Wu Sunfu of a sudden cried it out aloud with a cold laugh, and an iron-grey anguish and despair rose over the purple-brown skin of his face. But in an instant he recovered his firm, resolute self again. With a forceful sweep of his hand he went on, his face turning to a savage grin:

"Well then, you devil! Are we to fold our hands and wait for death? No! We will yet put up a fight! — But, Gancheng, how did the workers come to know we meant to dock their wages? Someone in the office must surely have let the news slip!"

Mo Gancheng gave a violent start, and a cold sweat broke out down his spine. After hesitating a moment, a scheme suddenly came into his head, and he said furtively:

"I suspect one man. It is Tu Weiyue. Of late the young fellow has lost his head; all day long his thoughts run on Zhu Guiying, the woman worker at reeling-frame number nineteen, and someone has seen him coming and going often at Zhu Guiying's house —"

Just then the study door opened suddenly, and the voice of the Second Young Lady, Fufang, broke in on Mo Gancheng's words. "Third Brother, the people and things from the International Funeral Parlour have all come. But that coffin — it does not look right to me!"

The Second Young Lady stood by the door, and as she spoke her eyes were on her husband.

"Wait a little, I will come directly. Zhuzhai, do you go and look at it first —"

But Du Zhuzhai waved his hand again and again, and said to the Second Young Lady through the thick cigar-smoke: "We are coming, we are coming — there is time enough yet! If it looks wrong we can still go and change it in time."

"Time enough? It is a quarter past twelve, and the meal is already being served outside!"

So saying, the Second Young Lady went off too. Here Wu Sunfu turned and gave Mo Gancheng a glance, and issued his commands with great authority:

"Now go back to the mill at once and give out a notice: because the old master has died, there will be a half-day's holiday this afternoon, wages paid as usual. First get the workers to disperse, so they cannot gather at the mill and stir up a riot. But the rest of you may not rest this afternoon. You must go severally in among the workers and do your work on them, and break their solidarity. I give you till tonight to see the thing settled! On the one hand, have the Public Security Bureau send police to guard the mill; on the other, make a report to the Bureau of Social Affairs. And that Tu Weiyue — tell him to come and see me. Tell him to come this evening. Have you all understood? Go!"

Having sent Mo Gancheng off, Wu Sunfu stood up, sighed softly, and said to himself:

"What is the good of running a mill! It is nothing but vexation! Why did I not start a bank in the first place? With my capital, and my spirit, a bank of mine would surely not have fallen behind the others? The Shanghai Bank, so grand and imposing now, had no more than one hundred thousand dollars when it opened …"

He paused, and put a hand to his chin; but at once his manner turned resolute, and his right hand, clenched into a fist, struck the palm of his left:

"No! I will carry on with it after all! China's national industries are down to a bare handful that one can count on one's fingers! And the silk trade bears more than any on the future of the Chinese nation! — If only the State were like a State, and the government like a government, Chinese industry would surely have its hope! — Zhuzhai, I have a great plan, but there is no time to go into it now; let us go out and look at the coffin the International Funeral Parlour has sent."

"No hurry! I have still a matter to talk over with you."

Du Zhuzhai took the half-cigar from his lips and stood up too; drawing close beside Wu Sunfu, he told over Zhao Botao's "secret plot" from the beginning, and at the end asked:

"Do you see any risk in the thing? If you are unwilling to have a hand in it, then I mean to keep out of it too."

"A million each, five hundred thousand paid down today?"

So Wu Sunfu asked in his turn, without showing his opinion of the matter, his face uncommonly calm.

"That too is Old Zhao's proposal. Old Zhao's plan of steps is this: this afternoon he will sell three million to press the price down further —"

"That is bound to press it down. It may well fall by two or three yuan. And then we buy back in?"

"No! At the first round of tomorrow's morning session we sell another five million, with Zhao Botao coming forward as the seller!"

"Oh! Then the price will fall further yet! Old Zhao is a famous big bull, and once he comes out of his cage the small bulls will panic all the more and struggle desperately to unload; and there are sure to be many new bears besides who will seize the chance to jump in short."

"Just so. That is why we do not begin to buy back in until tomorrow's afternoon session. We buy back slowly, in scattered lots, so as not to draw others' notice, and by four or five days before this month's settlement we must have taken up fifty million at the least —"

"And by that time the good news of the Northwest Army's retreat will be noised abroad on every side!"

"Just so. By then the small holders will come swarming like bees to go long again; and with settlement day near, and the old-calendar Dragon-Boat Festival falling across it, the bears too will be in a hurry to cover, so the rising trend is bound to be fierce!"

"And so our fifty million will be sent out to play the Bodhisattva Guanyin, who saves them from every woe and rescues them from every distress!"

At this Wu Sunfu and Du Zhuzhai laughed together, both their eyes flashing with an excited light.

When the laughter was over, Wu Sunfu said with spirit:

"Good! Let us decide to try it! Only it lets Old Zhao, that big bull, off far too cheaply. Over and above the joint account, we ought to put a small condition to him. Let us go and bargain with him!"

So Wu Sunfu and Du Zhuzhai left the study. And that "great plan" which had long been shaping in Wu Sunfu's mind now closed the more clearly upon his whole consciousness.
