The Central Guard Regiment is a name people know today: the special unit that protects the Party Center directly. The Central Special Branch was a guard of another kind, raised to protect that same Party Center under conditions far more extraordinary.
The members of this tiny, elite "special force" were chosen with great care, each of them a master of some craft, all of them exceptional in nerve and judgment. The Central Special Branch's true history of struggle, thick with the color of legend, was harrowing and full of turns, gripping enough to leave the invented tales of detectives and spies looking pale beside it.
This central guard, formed in the teeth of the white terror, was founded in November 1927. Its creator and its highest authority was Zhou Enlai, who would later become premier of the People's Republic of China.
### 1. A Thunderclap from Clear Skies: The Birth of the Central Special Branch
In 1927 Chiang Kai-shek and Wang Jingwei betrayed the revolution one after the other. The anti-imperialist, anti-feudal revolutionary united front that the Communists had built with the bourgeoisie broke apart entirely, the great revolution went down in defeat, and the revolution passed into a new period of its history, the Second Revolutionary Civil War. Chiang Kai-shek, political representative of the big landlords and the big bourgeoisie and chief of the Kuomintang reactionaries, set up a feudal-fascist regime with imperialist backing and ruled by the most reactionary, the darkest, the most savage means.
That August the Party Center convened the August 7 Conference, which fixed the general line of agrarian revolution and armed uprising. After it, the Autumn Harvest Uprising that Mao Zedong led in Hunan raised the First Division of the Workers' and Peasants' Revolutionary Army, marched resolutely on Jinggangshan in Jiangxi, and opened the Jinggangshan revolutionary base. The provisional Central Political Bureau, headed by Qu Qiubai, stayed on in Kuomintang territory, went underground, rebuilt and enlarged the Party and the other revolutionary organizations, and set a new revolutionary struggle in motion.
After the August 7 Conference the provisional Political Bureau decided to move the central organs from Hankou back to Shanghai. It was a decision reached only after the most careful thought.
Shanghai in those days was a place of extraordinary political and social complexity. It was the country's largest industrial city, with the most numerous, the most militant, the most revolutionary working class; and it was a great city under the brutal rule of the imperialists and the Kuomintang reactionaries both. Here were the imperialist concessions, with their vast garrisons of foreign troops, their police and their prisons for holding the Chinese people down; and here was the Chinese-administered city under Kuomintang rule, thick with soldiers, police, gendarmes, and secret agents, where the white terror held sway. They colluded with the imperialists, used the local gangs and hoodlums as accomplices in the slaughter of revolutionary people, and used shameless traitors to inform, to identify, and to hunt down Communists. But the concession districts of Shanghai were large, Chinese and foreigners lived jumbled together, the enemy was riven by his own quarrels, and there were many openings we could turn to our use: no household checks in ordinary times, lodgings easy to find, a good deal of room to maneuver for secret work, and much else besides. For establishing the Party's leading organs, then, no other city offered conditions so good.
From September 1927 on, the members of the provisional Political Bureau who came back from Wuhan to Shanghai, in ones and twos, were Qu Qiubai, Su Zhaozheng, Luo Yinong, Li Lisan, Li Weihan, Peng Pai, and Ren Bishi; its alternate members were Deng Zhongxia and Gu Shunzhang.
Zhou Enlai was then one of the alternate members of the provisional Political Bureau. He came back to Shanghai from the East River front in early November 1927, was made a member of the provisional Political Bureau Standing Committee, and took charge of the Central Military Commission and of other leading work. This was a major step by the Party Center to strengthen central leadership and to meet the new tasks of struggle the new situation demanded. But in a Shanghai under the shadow of the white terror, the safety of the Party had become the gravest and most pressing problem of all. Without a professional apparatus for intelligence and protective work, without a covert fight against the enemy, the safety of the Party, and above all of the Party Center, had no guarantee whatever. Zhou Enlai proposed such a body to the Party Center at once, and under his planning and leadership, following the special-work office of the Wuhan Military Commission before it, the Central Special Branch was established in Shanghai in November 1927. The Central Special Branch was a fighting stronghold of the Party under Zhou Enlai's direct command, and our Party's first reasonably mature professional apparatus for intelligence and protective work.
The guiding aim Zhou Enlai held to throughout his leadership of the Central Special Branch was this: to protect the safety of the Party's organizations, to fight off the enemy's persecution, to penetrate the enemy's Party, government, police, gendarme, and secret-service organs, to gather intelligence, to root out informers, and to break up the enemy's plots against our Party before they could do their work. In practice the Central Special Branch held to the absolute leadership of the Party, served its line and its policies faithfully, kept its discipline strictly, drew at the same time on every kind of Party strength, and waged a covert fight against the intelligence organs of the Kuomintang and of the imperialists. It carried out Zhou Enlai's principle of making bold use of every usable person within the enemy's own camp: political footing first, material inducement second, working the revolutionary two-faced policy, sending loyal and reliable comrades to plant themselves inside the enemy, kept protected and in place, buried deep for the long term until they had won trust. Because we worked in concealment, in a cause the broad masses supported, the enemy found us hard to guard against; and because we were skilled at turning his every weakness to account and chose our tactics well, we came to know the workings of the Kuomintang's secret services in Nanjing and in Shanghai and built working ties inside the concession police, so that the safety of the Party was secured. In that covert struggle the Central Special Branch won a shining record and rendered the Party and the people a service that can never be effaced.
The Central Special Branch's main tasks were to protect the safety of the central leading organs, to follow and grasp the enemy's movements, to rescue arrested comrades, and to punish traitors and enemy agents.
The Special Branch was organized into three sections: general affairs, intelligence, and protection. The General Affairs Section, the First Section, saw to setting up premises, arranging meeting places, and rescue and relief work; its chief was Hong Yangsheng. The Intelligence Section, the Second, gathered intelligence and built the intelligence networks; its chief was Chen Geng. The Protection Section, the Third, also called the "Red Squad," guarded the organs and eliminated traitors and enemy agents; its chief was Gu Shunzhang, who held the post along with his others. In 1928 a Radio Communications Section, the Fourth, was added, to set up transmitters, train operators, and handle communications with the regions; its chief was Li Qiang. The Special Branch did a great deal of effective work in Shanghai. It built its double-agent relationships, planted Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di, and others inside the enemy's secret services, set up clandestine radio stations, rescued leading cadres such as Ren Bishi and Guan Xiangying, and eliminated the traitors He Jiaxing, Bai Xin, and others, playing a major part in protecting the safety of the Party Center. After Gu Shunzhang's defection in April 1931 the Central Special Branch was reorganized: Chen Yun took overall charge, with Kang Sheng as his deputy; Chen Yun also headed the First Section, Pan Hannian the Second, and Kang Sheng held the Third concurrently, while the work of the old Fourth Section passed to the Center after 1932. After this overhaul the Special Branch changed its methods, worked out its own way of joining united-front work to intelligence work, and rebuilt its network of contacts. When the Party Center left Shanghai in 1933, the Special Branch's work passed in turn to Wu Hejing and others, and in September 1935 it withdrew in stages, leaving an office behind in the city.
The First Section of the Central Special Branch was the earliest to be formed. In August 1927, before the central organs moved from Wuhan back to Shanghai, a "headquarters" had been set up for the sole purpose of preparing the way for them. Once the Central Special Branch came into being, this "headquarters" became its First Section. Hong Yangsheng, the section's chief, recalled: "When the First Section first started work, renting rooms and finding guarantors was terribly hard. Comrade Zhou Enlai told us to try every means to work our social connections and solve the problem somehow. After a while the First Section built up three safe points, one at a time."
In those days, if a man living at one of the premises was a bachelor, the Party organization would assign a woman comrade to pose as his wife. Secret work in Shanghai was impossible without a woman comrade for cover in the role of wife: a man living alone not only drew suspicion but could hardly rent a place at all. Most of the sham couples who kept the premises, having lived side by side long enough, came to feel for each other, and with the Party's blessing became real husband and wife.
When the Party Center held a larger and more important meeting, the First Section would rent a bigger hall for it and arrange a makeshift family as cover. In May 1930, for instance, the Party Center held a preparatory meeting in Shanghai for the Congress of Soviet Areas, with close to fifty people taking part. Beforehand it was decided that Li Yimang, who worked at the central organs, would head a household of six as a makeshift family, with Li Yichao as his sister. The moment the meeting ended, the family broke up. That Sichuan girl who had played his sister, Li Yichao, then vanished, and no one knew where she had gone.
More than twenty years later, in the early days of the country's liberation, Li Yimang saw the film Zhao Yiman and realized only then that Zhao Yiman was that same Li Yichao.
The First Section also set up various offices, liaison points, and contact points for the Party Center. Above the Shengli Hospital at No. 447 Yunnan Road, next door to the Tianchan Theater, for example, it set up premises where the Political Bureau met and worked, kept by the married couple Xiong Jinding and Zhu Duansui.
In the spring of 1928, when the Hubei provincial committee was broken up, Xiong Jinding moved to Shanghai, and the Center appointed him accountant of the central organs, responsible for raising funds. He hung out the shingle of a firm called Fuxing and Company, dealt in native cloth, and made himself the proprietor. In reality the place served the Political Bureau as a spot to meet and work, where the responsible comrades coming and going could pass for customers and draw no notice. Not long after Fuxing and Company opened, Zhou Enlai brought in a nineteen-year-old woman Communist from Hunan, Zhu Duansui, to be the "proprietress" and keep the premises with "Boss Xiong." The two had known each other back in Hunan and thought well of each other, and after a few months together here a deep affection grew between them.
On the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival that year, after a meeting at Fuxing and Company, Zhou Enlai, Li Weihan, Deng Xiaoping, and the others laid on a banquet at a Sichuan restaurant on Fourth Avenue and invited the pair to drink and admire the moon. Meaning to bring the marriage about, Zhou Enlai proposed over the table that they wed; Li Weihan, Deng Xiaoping, and the rest all warmly agreed, and there and then the two became revolutionary partners and began a life shared, for good and ill, to the end of their days.
To counter the Kuomintang secret services and the wrecking of traitors and informers, the Party Center called for men to be planted inside the enemy to master his intentions. On December 31, 1927, the Center issued Central Circular No. 25, which stated: "Where a Party branch so resolves, one or two loyal comrades may be sent into a Kuomintang office or some reactionary organ to do detective and disruptive work. But this must be confined to cases where both of these purposes can be served."
There were two directions from which the enemy's reactionary organs could be entered in Shanghai: one was the Kuomintang police, gendarmerie, and Party offices; the other was the imperialists' concession police. The detectives of these police and gendarme organs and of the concession police all had a great crowd of gangsters and hoodlums feeding them tips and serving as their claws and teeth. At first the Central Special Branch made contact with and bought over a number of such men, using them to reach some of the detectives and get things done for us. Experience proved that, a few aside, most of them were of little use. But it gave the Special Branch its first experience of counterintelligence work; this, one might say, was where the Special Branch's counterintelligence began.
In April 1928 the Central Special Branch set up its Intelligence Section, the Second Section for short, under Chen Geng. From then on the Second Section developed its counterintelligence work step by step and on many fronts, chiefly the work of "getting in" and "drawing out" among the Kuomintang's Party, government, police, and gendarme organs. On the use of men, Zhou Enlai stressed again and again in his instructions: choose with care, use boldly, let each give his best, and test him in the work. The Special Branch sent Communists to plant themselves in the Songhu Garrison Command and in the French Concession police. One of them, Song Zaisheng, planted in the Garrison Command, used his cover as a Garrison Command investigator to build a tie with Fan Guangzhen, a detective chief in the French Concession. Fan Guangzhen's intelligence came partly from traitors, partly from the yellow unions and informers, and was of some use. The man planted directly in the French police, Chen Pengnian, who died on the Long March, had been a Chinese laborer in France and had studied at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow. Using his cover as a French-police plainclothesman, he passed the Party intelligence and blacklists and let our comrades slip away. When our premises were raided he would take it on himself to stand at the door, and when someone came he would call out a greeting: "Hey! Are you a Communist?" "No, clear off!" In this way he did a great deal of work for the Party.
As for "drawing out," it was back in the spring of 1928, on Zhou Enlai's instruction, that the Central Special Branch built its first double-agent relationship, with Bao Junfu. After further effort, in late December 1929 it planted Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di inside the Kuomintang's highest secret service, where they became known as the "Three Heroes of Longtan." In this way, whether from the Kuomintang's Party, government, police, gendarme, and secret-service organs or from the imperialists' concession police, the Central Special Branch could get timely word of the enemy's moves against our Party's organs, so that the safety of the Party Center was secured.
In 1930, under the guidance of Li Lisan's "left" line, the Central Special Branch surveyed the military, political, economic, and general social conditions of the imperialists and the Kuomintang in Shanghai, in preparation for an armed uprising. Among those who took part in the survey were Liu Ding, Ouyang Xin, and Chen Yangshan. Over a stretch of intense and sometimes dangerous work they gathered a mass of material on the imperialist garrisons, the gun positions of the warships, and the Kuomintang's military, police, and secret-service organs, compiled it into a volume, and sent it to Liu Bocheng, chief of staff of the Central Military Commission. After liberation, meeting Chen Geng and Liu Ding once in Beijing, Liu Bocheng remarked, "That set of materials you put together in Shanghai for the Li Lisan line was really something." In March 1931, moreover, on Zhou Enlai's instruction, the Central Special Branch opened the "South China News Agency" in Shanghai. From then on the Special Branch used this open news office as a cover for its work and gained a great deal of important intelligence through it.
Those who worked in the Intelligence Section at one time or another included Chen Yangshan, Chen Geng, Chen Shouchang, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di, Liu Ding, and Ke Lin. It was a section that bred talent, throwing up one hero of brains and courage after another: Chen Yangshan, who drew Bao Junfu out of the enemy's camp to work for us; and Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di, who planted themselves inside the enemy's highest secret service and got at its core secrets. These were model successes of the strategy of "getting in" and "drawing out."
Chen Yangshan was one of those brought in at the very founding of the Central Special Branch's Intelligence Section, and the one who worked longest in intelligence there.
Deng Xiaoping once said, "It was the comrades of the Central Special Branch who protected me." On June 5, 1978, recalling how he had escaped danger under the Special Branch's protection, he said: The Central Special Branch's intelligence work in Shanghai already had a footing by 1928; its main task was to counter the Kuomintang police and secret services and the concession police as they tried to break up our Party, and we had footholds inside all these enemy organs. In those days Zhou Enlai and I shared a room in Shanghai. Once I was out on work when the enemy meant to raid the place we lived and arrest us. The Special Branch comrades had already warned Comrade Zhou Enlai in time to move at once, and my wife had been sent to stay at a hotel. Knowing nothing of this, I finished my business and went back to where we had been living. As I reached the door I heard shouting inside; the sound was wrong, and I knew something had happened, so I hid at once. Just then a man polishing shoes at the mouth of the lane was watching me. Only later did I learn there were our people both inside and out to protect me; otherwise I would surely have been taken by the enemy. In those days the Special Branch's men planted at the enemy's core played a great part.
### 2. A Double-Agent Bond: Chen Yangshan and the Remarkable Bao Junfu
One day in the late 1920s, a high-end Buick swept out of the Nationalists' central police station, went along Fuzhou Road in Shanghai, turned by way of Henan Road into the French Concession, and rolled without hindrance up Route Pichon into the deep shade of its plane trees. The door opened, and out stepped a middle-aged man in a felt hat, a man of considerable style. He was on his way to hand a piece of urgent intelligence, just obtained, to Chen Yangshan, an underground member of the Chinese Communist Party. He was Bao Junfu, then a well-known special commissioner of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department.
Bao Junfu, also called Yang Dengying and Liu Junshan, was born in 1901, a native of Zhongshan county in Guangdong. In 1919, at eighteen, he went to Japan to study, left Meiji Gakuin before completing his course, and on returning home joined the student army for a time. Soon he became an interpreter at the Osaka Japan-China Economic Interpreters' Agency, and later worked at the YMCA that the Japanese ran in Shanghai. He had fluent Japanese and a wide circle of acquaintance.
After Bao Junfu came home from Japan in 1919, he worked at the Japanese-run YMCA and took part in the May Fourth Movement; in 1924 he joined the reorganized Chinese Kuomintang and became a member of its left wing. In the storm of the May Thirtieth Movement, Bao Junfu came to know Yang Jianhong, a man of the Green Gang and head of a foreign-affairs workers' union.
At the time Yang Jianhong held a post at the Evans bookshop in Shanghai, and Bao Junfu, on the pretext of buying books there, kept in touch with him. Yang Jianhong was a Guangdong man, a fellow provincial of Bao Junfu's, a trusted man of Chen Guofu and Chen Lifu, and the Shanghai special commissioner of the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department.
At the end of 1925 Chen Yangshan was working at the Kuomintang Central Transport Bureau, which had been set up secretly in Shanghai; its head was Yun Daiying. The bureau's task was to forward the mass of progressive books and periodicals published in Guangzhou to every part of the country, so that they should not be seized by the post offices that the Northern warlords controlled.
In February 1928 Chiang Kai-shek engineered the Fourth Plenary Session of the Kuomintang's Second Central Executive Committee, and having seized full Party, government, and military power, set about building a secret-service apparatus, establishing within the Central Organization Department a Party Affairs Investigation Section, whose full name was the "Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department." It was the forerunner of the Central Statistics Bureau, and the earliest of the Kuomintang's secret services. Its first chief was Chen Lifu; its second was Zhang Daofan; and it began its clandestine work.
That March, Chen Lifu, Zhang Daofan, Yang Jianhong, and others set about building a detective apparatus in Shanghai. Yang Jianhong, then the director of Party affairs investigation, was hard pressed for men, and men he could safely use were harder still to find. As he cast about in his difficulty, a face flashed into his mind. It was none other than his fellow provincial and old friend, the man he had gone about with often in Shanghai a few years before, Bao Junfu. He decided at once to put Bao Junfu in charge of the practical work of setting the thing up. He found Bao and laid it all out for him; Bao Junfu accepted Yang's offer, and Yang could not wait to bring him to Chen Lifu's house.
Once Bao Junfu and Chen Lifu had met, their temperaments matched and they became friends. They took a room at the Palace Hotel on Route Père Robert in the French Concession of Shanghai and often gathered there to eat, drink, and amuse themselves. Chen Lifu would also take Bao Junfu to a Japanese restaurant to dine. So that he could come and go more freely at Kuomintang central headquarters and see Chen Lifu, Bao Junfu asked Chen Lifu for a pass to the Kuomintang central offices.
Chen Lifu once said to Bao Junfu: a "Communist-purging" apparatus was to be set up in Shanghai, to wipe out the anti-Kuomintang elements within the concessions and restrict their activity, with special attention to the doings of the "Awakened Lion clique," the "Anfu clique," the "Communications clique," the "Reorganization clique," the "Chinese Youth Corps," and other such groups. He wanted Bao Junfu to make wide contact with the various social bodies, offices, and schools, to gather all kinds of material and information, and to report to him promptly.
Listening to their talk, Bao Junfu was seized with misgivings. He wanted to be a Kuomintang official; but to work as a detective against the Communists, and not tell them, would be to fail his friends, and without the Communists' help nothing could be got done, and his very life might be in danger. After much thought he hit on a plan that answered both ends: he would do a little for the Communists and pick up some Communist news, and so he could hold his Kuomintang post and run no risk. So he took up the Kuomintang's task and at the same time resolved to open a relationship with the Communists.
Bao Junfu knew Chen Yangshan was a Communist. The two had met in 1925, when Chen Yangshan, introduced by a mutual acquaintance, came to study Japanese with Bao.
Chen Yangshan was then head of the distribution section at the Kuomintang Central Propaganda Department's Shanghai Transport Bureau, and Bao Junfu often came to the bureau to help him distribute books and papers. Chen would give Bao the books and papers, which pleased him; he said that turning them into Japanese earned him a good fee. Now and then Chen asked Bao to see to something, and Bao was glad to help, and so the two became close friends.
After the Spring Festival of 1928, his attempt to organize a peasant uprising having failed, Chen slipped back to Shanghai from his home district; unable for the moment to make contact with the Party, he took shelter for a time at Bao Junfu's house. Before long he found work at a newspaper to make a living.
One day in mid-April that year, at the newspaper office, Chen Yangshan took a sudden telephone call from Bao Junfu, who asked him out that evening for coffee at a Russian café on Bubbling Well Road and hinted that there was a great matter he needed to talk over with him. Chen Yangshan asked what the great matter was. Bao Junfu paused, then said:
"I can't explain it over the phone, and it wouldn't do to."
As he was about to hang up, Bao Junfu, afraid Chen Yangshan would not come, said again and again:
"Remember now, the Russian café. You mustn't forget to come!"
At seven that evening, when Chen Yangshan came into the café as agreed, Bao Junfu had long been waiting. Chen Yangshan sat down and first apologized:
"Brother Bao must have waited a good while; I'm late, and I'm truly sorry."
"You and I are hardly friends of a day or two. No need to stand on ceremony!"
"May I know what great matter you wished to discuss?"
"It's like this—" Bao Junfu dropped his voice suddenly and, leaning close to Chen Yangshan's ear, told him the whole of it:
"This February, after Chiang Kai-shek took full Party, government, and military power, he set up a Party Affairs Investigation Section under the Organization Department to deal with the opposition of the various factions, and above all the Communists and the progressives. It's run by Chen Lifu and Zhang Daofan. Lately Chen Lifu and Zhang Daofan have had Yang Jianhong build an investigation body in Shanghai under cover of casework, and Yang has asked me to join it. I'd like to hear your view, younger brother."
When he had laid it out, Bao Junfu glanced at Chen Yangshan and said frankly: "I'll be plain with you. This fellow can work for the Kuomintang well enough; but if I'm to be a detective and hunt Communists, and not tell the Communists, then not only do I wrong you, my Communist friend, but without your people's understanding I'm afraid nothing will get done, and this head on my shoulders may not stay there either!" And with that he handed Chen Yangshan a list of names marked for arrest that he had brought with him.
It had all come so suddenly that for a moment Chen Yangshan had not gathered himself, and he could not help asking:
"What exactly does Brother Bao mean to do?"
"I've thought it over and over, and I've decided to help your side in secret too. And of course, at the same time, I can pick up some intelligence from you, true and false. That way I can be the Kuomintang's detective and still be in no danger of my life!"
"This isn't mine to decide. But since Brother Bao thinks so highly of me, I'll get our answer to you as fast as I can."
The truth was that Chen Yangshan too was of two minds. If it really was as Bao Junfu said, and he could feed us intelligence from the very heart of the Kuomintang, would that not be exactly the drawing of a double agent out of the enemy's camp? And if it went wrong, might it not endanger the safety of the Party?
"What to do?" Chen Yangshan asked himself over and over after leaving Bao Junfu. In the end he made up his mind: by the rule of the organization, he would write the matter up that same night and send it, through the Zhabei district committee he belonged to, up to the Party Center to decide.
After studying Chen Yangshan's report closely, Zhou Enlai told Chen Geng to have a talk with Chen Yangshan and learn all he could about Bao Junfu. Afterward, with Chen Yangshan arranging it, Chen Geng met Bao Junfu once in person to learn at first hand about Bao's ties to the Kuomintang. At the meeting Bao Junfu said plainly that he was willing to give the Communists everything he knew.
Having learned the particulars, Chen Geng made a special report to Zhou Enlai and offered his own view: I think Bao Junfu is fairly reliable, for he has always sympathized with the revolution, and even now he gets on well with a number of our Party's comrades. So I think he can serve as our man on the inside.
Zhou Enlai thought for a moment and said: a man like Bao Junfu is not very reliable politically, but in covert struggle, so long as we guide him rightly, he can be put entirely to our use. Whether or not to use Bao Junfu I will decide after talking it over with Comrades Luo Mai, that is, Li Weihan, and Luo Yinong, and then let you know.
Zhou Enlai thought again and said to Chen Geng: given the special nature of Special Branch work, from now on you will be my single-line contact. And I've been thinking, you ought to have a cover name. How does Wang Yong sound?
A few days later Zhou Enlai formally told Chen Geng that the Center had agreed to use Bao Junfu, cautiously. He added: for now you alone will keep the single-line contact with Bao Junfu. As for any requests of his own, you may answer him as seems fit and then report to me. And Comrade Chen Yangshan is yours to use from now on, so that you needn't be a commander with no troops.
Chen Geng then sought Bao Junfu out. At their meeting he gripped Bao Junfu's hands warmly in both of his and told him: Brother Junfu, I have consulted the Center, and it agrees to your being our man on the inside. I hope you will work faithfully for our Party.
Bao Junfu thanked the Communists for their trust in him. Chen Geng told him: from now on we count as one family. Family doesn't talk to family like strangers. I hope you'll use your special standing with Yang Jianhong to get as close as you can to Chen Lifu and Zhang Daofan, and win their trust and favor as fast as you can. And I hope you'll get to work quickly in the near term, getting close to the municipal Party office, the Songhu Garrison Command, the municipal police bureau, and the French Concession police. Whatever you need, we'll do our best to meet it.
"I'll do everything I can! Only, Zhang Daofan is to be married in Shanghai soon, and he means to have me manage it. Just now my own pockets are a bit light."
"Why, that's fine! That's the best chance there is to get close to Zhang Daofan. Don't worry; as for the envelope you're to give Zhang Daofan, I'll have it ready for you the moment you need it!"
As it happened, Zhang Daofan, who had just taken over from Chen Lifu as head of the Party Affairs Investigation Section, was a thorough hypocrite. A native of Panxian in Guizhou, he had studied Western oil painting in Paris in the early 1920s, a fellow pupil, one might say, of Xu Beihong. At this time he was privately pursuing Jiang Biwei, the wife of the great painter Xu Beihong, while announcing in Shanghai his marriage to a Frenchwoman. To make the wedding grand and glittering, he expressly invited that man of Western polish, Bao Junfu, to manage it for him.
Once Bao Junfu had Chen Geng's instruction, he threw himself into decorating Zhang Daofan's bridal chamber and making the wedding arrangements, busy and delighted with it all. On the wedding day the place was packed with distinguished guests, the crowd and the clamor extraordinary. Bao Junfu presented a lavish gift with his own hand: a hundred silver dollars. And bear in mind that a regular Kuomintang agent then drew a salary of only ninety-five silver dollars a month!
When the wedding was over, Zhang Daofan was of course overcome with gratitude to Bao Junfu. Openhandedly he said: "Brother Junfu, from now on go about your work with an easy mind and a free hand. With your gifts, your prospects are boundless. Remember, whatever trouble you run into from now on, just come to me!"
Managing this happy affair for Zhang Daofan left everyone with an impression of Bao Junfu: that he was Zhang Daofan's closest and most devoted man.
Six months later Zhang Daofan rose to be secretary of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department and secretary of the personal staff office, and went on to hold such posts as head of the Central Propaganda Department and chairman of the Central Cultural Movement Committee; during the War of Resistance he even attended the Cairo Conference in Chiang Kai-shek's train. But whether his office rose or not, he never lost his gratitude to Bao Junfu. And so, when Bao Junfu was arrested three years later on account of Gu Shunzhang's defection, he served only half a year before Zhang Daofan bailed him out. That, of course, is getting ahead of the story.
In those days Bao Junfu often accompanied Zhang Daofan and Yang Jianhong in and out of the police bureau and the concession stations, and he needed a car. The Central Special Branch met his request and even assigned him a bodyguard, one Lian Desheng.
Lian Desheng had been a tram-company conductor, brave in the May Thirtieth Movement; later he went to the Soviet Union, and on his return was moved to Special Branch work. A Communist, he was in fact the link between the Party organization and Bao Junfu. From then on Lian Desheng went about with Bao Junfu every day, and Chen Yangshan handed him certain matters to see to. Once Lian Desheng and Bao Junfu were on close terms, Chen Yangshan moved out of Bao Junfu's house on North Sichuan Road.
In mid-1928 Chen Lifu came to Shanghai specially, and on April 15 the front page of the China News carried Chiang Kai-shek's handwritten order: "Yang Dengying is specially deputed to assist in handling important cases in Shanghai and to dispose of them. So ordered." In the winter of 1929 Bao Junfu was also appointed head of the Shanghai office of the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department. On receiving the appointment he moved to No. 44 Hengye Lane in Zhabei.
In the winter of 1929, Chen Geng set up an office for Bao Junfu in an over-the-street building in Shanghai, opposite Dade Lane on North Sichuan Road, where An E, of the Central Special Branch and formerly Tian Han's wife, was in charge of taking the intelligence Bao Junfu gathered, copying it out at the office, and passing it to Chen Geng to handle.
Under the sign of a Kuomintang central special commissioner, Bao Junfu in fact operated independently, under the direction of the Central Special Branch, inside the Kuomintang's military and police organs and the imperialists' detective agencies in Shanghai. He answered to almost no one; the only man he kept close ties with was Chen Lifu. Bao Junfu even sat for the Kuomintang on the concession courts, serving on the jury in major cases. At the same time Xu Enzeng, head of the Investigation Section, sent Gao Zhongxia to assist Bao Junfu in his work. By the end of 1929, so deep was Chen Lifu's trust in him that Xu Enzeng repeatedly turned over to Bao Junfu, in full, the major Shanghai cases Chen Lifu passed down, and through this the Central Special Branch came to understand the movements of Shanghai's enemy detective organs better still.
In May 1928, on Chen Geng's instruction, Bao Junfu, maneuvering skillfully and leaving no trace, built ties with the Songhu Garrison Command, the detective corps, the police bureau, the Municipal Council, the concession police, and other bodies in Shanghai, and came to know such leading Kuomintang figures as Mao Gong, Tan Shaoliang, Qian Dajun, and Xiong Shihui. Being a special commissioner sent out by Kuomintang central headquarters, Bao Junfu held a fairly high position and drew a fairly good income. The Songhu Garrison Command paid him a hundred dollars a month in travel money, and in 1929, when Xu Enzeng became head of the Investigation Section, another five hundred a month. Since Bao Junfu also held a nominal post as inspector at the Shanghai police bureau, he had a further forty silver dollars a month in pay. And from the time Chen Geng gave him his assignment, Chen Geng brought him four hundred dollars a month in person, right up until his arrest.
In July or August 1928, Yang Jianhong killed himself over a corruption case. Chiang Kai-shek formally appointed Bao Junfu special commissioner in Shanghai of the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, making him the head of the Kuomintang's secret service in Shanghai. The cover name he used inside the Kuomintang secret service was Yang Dengying. In a certain sense, the Kuomintang's secret service in Shanghai at that time was staffed for it by the Communist Special Branch.
Once in office, Bao Junfu's first step was to win his superiors' trust. At the start Chen Lifu and the others, though they did not know of Bao's ties to the Communists, were not quite easy about him, and Yang Jianhong had once ordered Bao to take part in a roundup of Communists without telling him beforehand where the roundup was or whom it was after. Bao understood well enough; to trick Yang Jianhong into trusting him, he made a show of hunting Communists eagerly.
After Bao Junfu became special commissioner, the Communist Special Branch on the one hand used his connections to plant men in the Kuomintang police bureau, detective corps, and gendarmerie, and on the other hand did all it could to shore up Bao's position within the Kuomintang. As for certain traitors, the Special Branch let it be known through Bao Junfu that their defections were feigned and that they were still "in league" with the Communists, and so used the Kuomintang's own hand to be rid of them. Sometimes the Special Branch would deliberately set up sham premises for the enemy to raid, so that Bao might win the Kuomintang's trust.
Bao Junfu's reach was wide. Lampson, then head of the political branch of the British concession police, took Bao Junfu for an expert, was on the best of terms with him, told him everything, brought every knotty problem to him for discussion, let him see any material he had, and sometimes let Bao take part when the police made an arrest. Bao passed all the intelligence he came by promptly to the Communist Special Branch, so that the Special Branch had, in the main, a grasp of the doings of the British police. To keep close to Lampson, Bao Junfu, with the Special Branch's consent, made him many gifts, a motorcar among them.
In the first half of 1930 Chen Geng told Bao Junfu that two of our Party's local organs had been exposed, that the concession police and the authorities of the Chinese-administered city meant to raid and search them, and that our Party stood in danger of grave loss. On receiving the task Bao Junfu acted at once, moving the documents and other important material out in time, so that when the enemy searched they came up empty. There was a two-bay shopfront beside the Chinese-administered district near Shanghai's North Railway Station, one of our Party's covert propaganda premises, which a traitor had betrayed. Learning of it, Chen Geng turned the plot to his own account: before the enemy could move, he hurried to shift out all the propaganda material, flags, and the rest, and only then had Bao Junfu telephone the enemy. On the report Bao Junfu led men from the police bureau and the concession station to search, and they turned up nothing but a little propaganda of small value; when this was handed to the police bureau, the chief, none the wiser, was more than pleased, commended Bao Junfu on the spot, and even issued a written order: "In future, when Inspector Bao comes to report news, we are to act at once." At a stroke Bao Junfu's standing rose.
To help Bao Junfu manage and lull the enemy further, Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan let through to him, selectively, some Party documents of no great weight, such as our Party's manifestoes and leaflets and its open journals Red Flag and Bolshevik, and let him see certain documents ahead of others, which Bao Junfu used as capital for getting close to the enemy and winning their trust.
Whenever Chen Lifu or Zhang Daofan came to Shanghai, Bao Junfu played to their tastes and won their favor. What made Chen Lifu think most highly of Bao Junfu was the good relations he had built with the concession police. In the past the Kuomintang Investigation Section had always wanted to be on good terms with the concession police, but the foreigners never gave them the time of day; when the concession authorities tried a major case, they never let a Kuomintang man take part in the trial. The Kuomintang wanted Bao Junfu to build good relations with the concession police so that they would help make arrests at any time; Chen Geng too pressed Bao to build ties with them, but for the sake of intelligence. So from the moment he took office Bao put great effort into this work. Besides building working ties with Lampson, the British police detective chief, and his deputy Tan Shaoliang in the course of cases, he also built a personal warmth with them and became their close friend. Lampson and Tan Shaoliang, taking Bao to be close to the Songhu Garrison Command, wanted to use him to get things done for them. In August 1928 Tan Shaoliang asked Bao Junfu to find out whether two of their men who had gone missing had been locked up by the Songhu Garrison Command. Bao Junfu sought out Qian Dajun to ask, and so it proved. By the time Bao Junfu returned to the concession station, the two had already been released. On the Municipal Council's orders Tan produced two hundred silver dollars for Bao Junfu, and Bao Junfu refused them: you have the wrong man. This left a deep impression on the concession police. Another time, Lampson asked Bao Junfu to get hold of a specimen of the Songhu Garrison Command's seals. Through Mao Naigong, deputy adjutant-general of the Garrison Command, Bao Junfu obtained the seals of the command and of all its subordinate offices, and Lampson treasured them like gold. From then on Bao Junfu had the trust of the British police. In every case tried in the concessions, Bao Junfu was asked to appear; and in handling urgent cases in the concessions, he could dispense with the usual review procedures. Later Lampson recommended Bao as an adviser to the French Concession police as well. From then on Bao Junfu's name rang loud, so that when Yang Jianhong was put to death over a major corruption case, Bao Junfu became, in Chen Lifu's eyes, a second Yang Jianhong and the head of the Kuomintang's secret service in Shanghai. His power greater now, Chen Geng directed him to seize the moment and build ties with the Kuomintang's military, police, gendarme, and secret-service organs and with the concession's Municipal Council and police, and to keep close company with the upper reaches of the Kuomintang, all for the sake of intelligence.
In the stretch from October 1928 to December 1929, besides working to win Bao over politically, Chen Geng sought to raise his social standing, often giving Bao Junfu open journals and sometimes Party documents of little secrecy, so that he might have capital to boast and claim credit with. From Chen Lifu down to Zhang Daofan, Qian Dajun, Ye Xiufeng, and Xu Enzeng, all put their trust in Bao Junfu and did as he said. In this period Bao Junfu learned a great deal of intelligence from the Kuomintang police and gendarme organs, and above all from the concession police, and sent it all promptly to Chen Geng; several of the enemy's roundups came back empty-handed. And to shield Bao from the enemy's suspicion, Chen Geng once deliberately set up premises for the enemy to raid.
The period from December 1929 to April 1931 was the time when Bao Junfu was most favored by Xu Enzeng, head of the Kuomintang Investigation Section. To ingratiate himself with Chen Guofu, Chen Lifu, and Chen Xizeng, chief of the Shanghai municipal police, Bao bought each of them a tax-free car, and drove the two Chens' cars to Nanjing himself. Of the major cases Chen Lifu passed down, all that touched on Shanghai were entrusted, on his word, to Xu Enzeng, who had Bao handle them.
By now Chen Geng found that Bao Junfu's position in Shanghai's enemy detective organs had risen markedly, yet that he handled things for the Communist Special Branch less freely than before, with a certain timidity; Chen guessed he might be afraid the Kuomintang Investigation Section would discover his Communist ties. Meeting this, Chen Geng helped and encouraged him politically, careful both to use him and to protect him, so that he felt the Party organization's care for him. Learning that Xu Enzeng was after intelligence from him, Chen would make up some false intelligence for Bao to pass to Xu Enzeng and win his favor. His misgivings gone, Bao Junfu went on working actively for the Central Special Branch.
Bao Junfu was the first secret intelligence agent the Central Special Branch developed inside the Kuomintang secret service, and under Chen Geng's patient guidance, cultivation, and leadership he did a great deal of useful work for the Chinese Communist Party. From the Kuomintang and concession detective organs he obtained, in good time, intelligence of the enemy's moves against our Party's leading organs, making an important contribution to the safety of the central organs. At the same time, under the direct leadership of Zhou Enlai and Chen Geng, he took part in the rescue of Ren Bishi, a Central Committee member the enemy was hunting; in the rescue of Guan Xiangying, secretary of the Communist Youth League Central Committee; in the attempt to rescue Political Bureau member Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and two other comrades, four in all, which failed for reasons beyond control; in tracking the movements of the traitors Bai Xin, Dai Bingshi, Chen Weinian, and Huang Dihong; and in reporting the defection of the Shandong provincial committee secretary, among other things. In all these he played a very important part, a model of the Special Branch's use of a non-Party agent developed amid the tangled ties of the enemy camp.
The case of Bao Junfu proved how entirely right Comrade Zhou Enlai's directive was, that the Central Special Branch should make bold use of every usable person within the enemy's camp. Besides supplying our Party with intelligence on the plots of the Kuomintang secret service and the imperialist concession police against our Party's organizations, Bao played an important part in rescuing our comrades and clearing out traitors and informers. For example:
On November 17, 1929, Ren Bishi, a member of the Central Political Bureau, went to Jingye Lane on Ward Road to take part in an enlarged meeting of the Communist Youth League's Jiangsu provincial committee. The enemy had found out the meeting place, and the British police had planted plainclothesmen there. Our comrades had no time to give warning; as Ren Bishi climbed the steps and knocked, the door opened halfway to reveal some strangers craning their necks inside. He saw that things were wrong and was about to turn away when a plainclothesman seized his arm, and there was no shaking free. The enemy pressed the woman who lived in the room to give his name; she looked at Ren Bishi once and said, "Don't know him." The enemy did not believe her, and just then the woman's four-year-old child rushed at Ren Bishi; the enemy, giving her no chance to explain, clapped irons on Ren Bishi and pushed him toward a car. Seizing the moment, Ren Bishi quickly slipped the note hidden in his pocket into his mouth and swallowed it.
Having taken Ren Bishi to the station, the enemy found nothing on him but a train ticket, no other proof of anything. Under questioning Ren Bishi said his name was Peng Desheng, an unemployed young man come from Jiangxi to Shanghai to seek out relatives and a livelihood, and that he had been hauled in by mistake for getting a house number wrong. The enemy tortured him cruelly but got no confession whatever, and so held him as a suspect and moved him to prison.
After Ren Bishi's arrest, the Party Center ordered the Central Special Branch to rescue him. Following the Center's instruction, Chen Geng had Bao Junfu find a way. Using his ties with the British police, Bao Junfu told them that the arrested Peng Desheng was one of his own men. And so, for the sake of their relations with Bao Junfu, the British police released Ren Bishi, and Ren Bishi escaped the enemy's cruelty.
In 1931 Guan Xiangying was transferred back to Shanghai to lead the labor-union work. Before long the Shanghai union organization was broken up, and Guan Xiangying was arrested.
The enemy took him to the British concession station, and under questioning Guan Xiangying found that they did not yet know who he was, so he gave the false name Li Shizhen and said he had just come to Shanghai from out of town to make a living, that he had a relative near a soy-sauce shop, and that he had come to ask after a friend's address. Unable to get his identity out of him, the enemy held him for the time being at the British station.
After Guan Xiangying's arrest, Chen Geng had Bao Junfu go to the British station to sound things out. From Lampson, the Western detective chief of the British police political branch, Bao learned that the first hearing had not established who the prisoner was, but that they were very interested in the large chest of documents seized from the soy-sauce shop.
When Chen Geng reported this to Zhou Enlai, Zhou Enlai's instruction was to start from the documents, find a way to snatch the important ones back, and then find a way to rescue Guan Xiangying. So Chen Geng had Bao Junfu go to work on the British police.
Bao heard at the British station that the Kuomintang wanted to take the chest of documents away. The British police would not have it, saying the case was theirs to crack and refusing to hand the chest to the Kuomintang; the two sides were arguing hard over who the documents belonged to. Chen Geng had Bao Junfu tell Lampson that the chest was very important, and offer to help sort out what was in it. Lampson, who wanted just then to pick the important documents out of the chest for himself, said to Bao, "Then you shall be the one to sort them." Bao pretended to be very busy but said he would find a way. So, on Zhou Enlai's instruction, Chen Geng sent Liu Ding to do the "sorting."
Bao introduced Liu Ding to Lampson as an "expert" he had brought in, and led Liu Ding to the room where the document chest was kept, shut the door, and let Liu Ding read through all the documents carefully. Liu Ding drew out the secret documents among them and hid them on his person. As he left he made a point of carrying a few mimeographed sheets in his hand and told the police, "I'll take these back to study." A few days later Chen Geng had Bao Junfu tell Lampson that the prisoner was a scholar and the seized papers all academic research material. Seeing that he was no important political offender, Lampson had him moved to the Songhu Garrison Command.
Since Guan Xiangying never gave himself away, and the Kuomintang had not got hold of the documents, they too took him for no important offender and pursued the matter no further.
Afterward Huang Molan received Pan Hannian's instruction to carry through Zhou Enlai's charge of rescuing Guan Xiangying. Through the noted Shanghai lawyer Chen Zhigao, and Chen's father Chen Qishou, a member of the Tongmenghui, Huang Molan worked on many fronts and got Guan Xiangying out.
In April 1931 came the arrest and defection of Gu Shunzhang, a leading figure of the Chinese Communist Party's Central Special Branch.
After Gu Shunzhang's defection, the underground Party at once sent Chen Yangshan to press on Bao Junfu the lesson of holding firm, telling him: "So long as you keep your mouth shut, the Kuomintang won't dare do anything to you!" And it let Bao Junfu know that "however the situation changes, the Communist Party will not forget you, its friend." And further: "Once arrested, keep your will firm; so long as you don't open your mouth, they can do nothing to you!"
Because the situation was then so pressing, Chen Yangshan could no longer go often to Bao Junfu's house; he could only reach him by telephone, calling him out for secret talks. Several times he met Bao Junfu's wife instead. Besides comforting her, he gave her a sum of money on behalf of the organization and told her, "Whatever you need, whatever your difficulties, come to me any time," and gave her the signal and the place for making contact with him.
At one meeting Chen Yangshan told Bao Junfu's wife: please tell Bao Junfu not to run, and that even if he is seized, he must say nothing. And he analyzed it for her: "This is the best course; nothing much will come of it, and Zhang Daofan will come to help. And please tell Brother Junfu, too, that in the matter of your Bao family, we Chinese Communists will see it through to the end."
After Bao Junfu was seized, it was again Chen Yangshan who met Bao Junfu's wife in secret, telling her: "Give up the big house you rent, quickly, and move in with your mother; it will be safer. Whatever trouble you have, come to me any time." After Bao Junfu's wife moved to her mother's home, Chen Yangshan went to see her again and, on the Party organization's behalf, brought her money.
On Gu Shunzhang's information, Bao Junfu was arrested and held at the Nanjing Gendarmerie Command, and to the end he gave away nothing of the Central Special Branch's work. When the enemy agents questioned him, he always said: "Gu Shunzhang knows all my affairs; I've nothing worth telling." From then on Bao Junfu held his tongue in prison. After the January 28 Incident of 1932, Zhang Daofan, who held high Kuomintang office, worked quietly behind the scenes, and sure enough got Bao Junfu released. Later the Kuomintang secret service deliberately arrested Bao Junfu again, to have him work on captured Communists in prison, urging them to surrender. After Chen Geng was arrested in March 1933, he met Bao Junfu in prison. Bao Junfu, through his connections, looked after Chen Geng there, and Chen Geng, much moved, said to Bao Junfu: "One day the Communist Party will win the country, and when that day comes we will never forget you." Bao Junfu said to Chen Geng: "Though tomorrow they should send me up the mountain of knives and down into the cauldron of oil, I would never regret having made a friend like you."
By this time Zhou Enlai, Chen Geng, and the rest had one after another left Shanghai, some for Tianjin, some for the soviet areas. In 1934 the Kuomintang took Bao Junfu up again, as deputy warden of the Nanjing reformatory; but by now Bao Junfu had broken entirely with the Kuomintang secret-service system, had lost touch with the Communists too, and his life sank into the ordinary.
Times change, and the world's fortunes rise and fall. In 1949, when the People's Liberation Army stormed into the old stronghold of Nanjing, Bao Junfu was already a common man, going by the name Yiwen and keeping a little cigarette stall on the streets of the city for a living.
In October 1950 the Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries opened across the whole country. In January 1951 the Nanjing municipal public security bureau began registering members of reactionary parties, corps, and secret services; on the first day more than 1,500 registered, and by the fifth day more than 4,200, among them senior officials such as a former deputy minister of the Military Affairs Commission's Ministry of Military Administration and a major-general chief of staff of the Northwest Field Headquarters. But a few, full of misgivings, hung back and watched and waited. Bao Junfu was one of these.
Because Bao had once been warden of Kuomintang government reformatories and repentance houses, he was arrested by the municipal public security bureau and soon handed to the court. On review by the responsible court officials, a heavy sentence was in prospect. At this Bao stated that in the days of the great revolution he had worked for the Communist underground: "In 1926 I was brought in by Chen Yangshan to plant myself inside the enemy, and it was Chen who introduced me into the Communist Party. During my time inside the Kuomintang from 1930 to 1931, and while I served as head of the Shanghai office of the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, I held that post with the agreement of Chen Geng, the head of the Communist Party's Central Special Branch. Throughout my time in Shanghai, whenever there was a roundup of Communist Party members, I always warned Chen Geng beforehand so that he could move and hide; later, betrayed by a traitor, Chen Geng and I were both arrested by the Kuomintang, held in turn at the lockup of the Capital Special Criminal Court in Yangpi Lane, Baixia district, Nanjing, and then at the lockup of the Gendarmerie Command on Zhanyuan Road, among other places. In prison I met Chen Geng; I was in a Grade A cell and Chen Geng in a Grade C cell, and I found a way through my connections to have Chen moved to a better Grade A cell. After my release in 1937 I lost touch with the Communists. I never in any way betrayed the Party or sold out the organization. I therefore ask that Comrade Chen Geng bear witness."
Ju Hua, the first president of the municipal court, took the case very seriously. In July 1951 the municipal court twice wrote to Chen Geng, then chairman of the Yunnan Provincial People's Government, hoping he would confirm Bao's political standing in the days of the great revolution. On November 24, 1951, Chen Geng replied to President Ju in his own hand, in these words:
{v} To President Ju of the Nanjing Municipal People's Court:
{v} On the question of whether Bao Junfu's, also known as Yang Dengying, statement is true: so far as I know, Bao did indeed enter into relations with our Party from 1927 on, and before 1931 was in working contact with me, in which period he made no small contribution to the Party. When I was arrested he was in the same prison with me at the Nanjing Gendarmerie Command, and at that time he seemed all right; afterward he served as warden of the puppet Central Repentance House, where, it is said, his conduct was poor. The rest I do not know... It would be best to keep him under watch and control. This in reply.
{v} With respect,
{v} Chen Geng
{v} November 24
Chen Geng's letter affirmed the contribution Bao had made in the days of the great revolution and gave the court important evidence in trying the case, turning Bao Junfu's fortunes. The court then verified matters further, obtained the testimony of a great many senior Party cadres, and on deliberation released Bao, sentencing him to one year's control.
Sentenced to control, Bao Junfu lived at Dashugen in the Xuanwu district of Nanjing, then moved to Suiyuan, with no means of livelihood. In November 1952, learning of this, Chen Yangshan, then vice-minister of justice, wrote at once to the three chiefs of the Nanjing municipal public security bureau, stressing more than once that "in the years after the great revolution Bao Junfu made a great contribution to our Party's protective work, and saved a great many of our Party's responsible comrades..." In the letter he said, "Chen Geng and I are discussing some relief for Bao, or some other way to help him." In March 1956 the Nanjing Military Region sent someone specially to bring Bao Junfu to Beijing, where he was received by Chen Geng, Li Kenong, Chen Yangshan, and others. Chen Geng asked in detail after Bao Junfu's circumstances and charged the Ministry of National Defense with providing for him; he was fitted with false teeth at the Peking Union Medical College Hospital, and had a woolen uniform, leather shoes, and other things made for him, after which Bao Junfu, with a companion assigned to him, stayed three weeks in Beijing. Ever after, whenever Chen Geng came to Nanjing he called at Bao Junfu's home to see him and looked after his welfare.
### 3. Comradeship in Arms: The Legendary General and Chen Yangshan
Chen Geng's was a soldier's life, lived through blood and fire and storm. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1922, entered the first class of the Whampoa Military Academy in 1924, and after graduating stayed on to work there, taking part in the suppression of the Merchant Corps revolt and in the Eastern Expedition against Chen Jiongming. In 1926 he went to study in the Soviet Union, returned the next year to take part in the Nanchang Uprising, and then did intelligence work at the Communist central organs in Shanghai. In the Agrarian Revolutionary War he served in turn as a Red Army regiment commander, division commander, head of an infantry school, and commander of military regions and columns. After the founding of New China he was deputy commander of the Southwest Military Region and concurrently commander of the Yunnan provincial military district; commander and political commissar of the Third Army Group of the Chinese People's Volunteers; deputy commander of the Chinese People's Volunteers; deputy chief of the general staff of the People's Liberation Army and concurrently deputy director of the National Defense Science and Technology Commission; and vice-minister of national defense. In 1955 he was given the rank of general. Not only were his war exploits brilliant; in the Chinese Communist Party's secret struggle, in building up its political power, in united-front work, in founding academies, and in national-defense research he left a shining record on many fronts besides. Yang Dezhi, former chief of the general staff of the People's Liberation Army, once said with deep feeling: "Comrade Chen Geng was a figure of legend, of great influence not only within the Red Army but even in the enemy's own camp."
In 1927, after taking part in the Nanchang Uprising, Chen Geng was wounded in the leg on the march south into Guangdong and was secretly brought back to Shanghai for treatment. Once healed, Chen Geng was picked by Zhou Enlai for the newly formed Central Special Branch. For a long stretch Chen Geng, under the cover name Wang Yong, was chief of the Special Branch's Second Section, the Intelligence Section. In the four years from 1928 to 1931 Chen Geng helped Zhou Enlai lead the whole Central Special Branch and became the finest intelligence worker on our Party's hidden front.
In the legendary career of General Chen Geng there are many legendary tales, but his building, with Chen Yangshan, of the Chinese Communist Party's first double-agent intelligence relationship, and their covert struggle in Tianjin, are the "finishing strokes" that people recount to this day.
From the founding of the Intelligence Section in early 1928, Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan both worked in the Central Special Branch. Chen Geng saw a good deal of Gu Shunzhang in those days; the two had once gone together to the Soviet Union to study intelligence and protective work, and from early 1928 to 1931 they worked together again in the Central Special Branch.
Gu Shunzhang knew Chen Geng's affairs uncommonly well; he even knew a good many of Chen Geng's family, relatives, and friends. It was Chen Geng's wife, Wang Genying, whom Gu Shunzhang led enemy agents to seize at her mother's home in December 1933.
After Gu Shunzhang's defection, Zhou Enlai at once had word sent to Chen Geng to go into hiding immediately, lest the enemy do him some hidden harm.
Chen Yangshan had worked in the Communist Central Special Branch since 1928, had met Gu Shunzhang several times, and Gu Shunzhang knew his circumstances fairly well too. To meet the enemy's sudden strike, Zhou Enlai also had word sent to Chen Yangshan at once, telling him he must withdraw immediately.
At the time Chen Yangshan was living under cover as a reporter for the Shen Bao, in a lane off North Zhejiang Road and Haining Road in Shanghai. The Party sent word to tell him: "Gu Shunzhang has defected and will surely come to Shanghai to make arrests; you must withdraw and hide." He was to leave home at once and stay elsewhere for a week first.
Chen Yangshan made a few necessary arrangements and left home. He then hid with a fellow provincial who was studying at the Shanghai Fine Arts Academy, one Yu Tongliang, who was living at the time in a two-story lodging house near the academy; below was a small shop, and Chen Yangshan and Yu Tongliang both stayed upstairs.
For concealment and safety Chen Yangshan seldom went out, staying home much of the time. Yu Tongliang would buy him little things to eat, such as wontons, steamed dumplings, flatbread, and fried dough sticks. Sometimes, when there were few people about and no one watching, Yu Tongliang would take this fellow provincial and good friend of his into the school canteen to eat.
After about a week of this Chen Yangshan returned to his own lodging, asked around, and, finding his comrades all safe, was at last easy in his mind.
Now the Party organization sent someone to make contact with Chen Yangshan again, telling him to find a place and live with Chen Geng for the time being, and instructing him that "the place you find should be a bit better, like an office suite, so you can pass for merchants; it should have both a front and a back way out, somewhere not easily noticed, and better to spend a little more."
Following the organization's instruction, Chen Yangshan asked around and went about here and there, and at last found a suitable place.
This hideout was on the third floor of a small building behind the Wing On Company on Nanjing Road, beside the old racecourse, all office suites, with the signboards of many firms hung out, very quiet, with both a front and a back way, handy for meeting a sudden enemy strike.
Chen Yangshan rented an office in the little three-story building; the rent was on the high side, but he was fairly satisfied, and he moved in first, reporting the matter to the Party organization in good time.
Three days later Chen Geng moved in too, and was fairly satisfied with the hideout. Except when they were out on work, this was how the two of them lived, in concealment.
In early 1931 the Central Special Branch planned to develop intelligence work in the north. One of the contacts was Hu Egong, who had been a member of parliament, and whose cover name was Wu Nanhu. A member of parliament in the days of the Northern warlords, he had spent a long time in the old officialdom, had dealings with many people of the upper reaches, and knew the Kuomintang's internal factional quarrels well. On the strength of his many social ties he made contact with our Party and often supplied it with important intelligence. The Central Special Branch had him do intelligence work under cover of his social standing and his connections in officialdom, and assigned Yang Xianzhen as his assistant.
In late spring 1931 the Party organizations of Tianjin, Beiping, and the Shun-Zhi provincial committee were mostly broken up. A batch of responsible cadres, among them An Ziwen, Zhou Zhongying, Chen Yuandao, and Liu Yaxiong, were arrested. The court had only just taken up the cases and had not yet decided them. It was an excellent chance for a rescue, and the Party Center gave the task to Hu Egong and Yang Xianzhen, telling them to work every connection to get the arrested comrades out.
Just then Liu Shaobai, a member of parliament, was working to rescue his daughter Liu Yaxiong and his son-in-law Chen Yuandao. Using his standing as a member of parliament, Liu Shaobai had come to know the upper reaches of the Tianjin court, and after much effort had got the court to agree that for five thousand silver dollars this batch of prisoners could be let out.
Learning of this, Hu Egong sought out Liu Shaobai. The two were both members of parliament and had been good friends to begin with, and both were secretly working for our Party. Liu Shaobai told Hu Egong how the rescue stood. Seeing hope of it, Hu went to Shanghai to report the matter to the Party Center.
After Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan moved into the office suite, since everyone around was in business, the enemy agents paid little heed, and the transport was convenient too. The two of them hid by day and went out to work by night, and were safe enough.
But Chen Geng knew too many people in Shanghai, and many of his intelligence contacts had passed through. Afraid something would happen to him, the Party organization decided to send him with Chen Yangshan to Tianjin on a special mission: the Party organizations of Tianjin, Beiping, and the Shun-Zhi provincial committee had been badly broken up more than once, many responsible comrades and Party members had been arrested, and the two were to go and look into the Tianjin situation.
The Party Center's instructions for their Tianjin mission were three. First, to rescue the arrested comrades: to find out where they were held and, together with Hu Egong and Yang Xianzhen, work their release. Second, to see whether a body of the Special Branch kind could be built in Tianjin, to protect the safety of the Party organization. Third, to investigate the traitors: to learn which of them posed a grave threat to the Party and must be dealt with.
Chen Geng, Chen Yangshan, and Wang Genying, Chen Geng's wife, who had just escaped from the enemy, set out from Shanghai on June 16, accompanied by Shao Fumin, a "Red Squad" man who knew Tianjin well. As there was trouble with the Chinese ship, they settled on a British freighter bound for Tanggu. They boarded at the Butterfield and Swire wharf in Shanghai and reached Tanggu after four days and four nights. Early the next morning they took the train on to Tianjin, and Shao Fumin brought them to the Jiaotong Inn in the French Concession.
Shao Fumin, also called Zhang Mazi and Jin Mazi, was a graduate of a Shanghai dental school and had run a small clinic of his own; some of our Party's comrades, when wounded, went secretly to his clinic for treatment. Later Shao Fumin joined the Central Special Branch; thin as he looked, he was tall, and a noted crack shot. This time he was being sent to work in the Northeast. Having settled Chen Geng and the others in Tianjin, he went off to Shenyang.
Chen Yangshan later recalled, in an essay called "The Fight in Tianjin": "We took a British freighter and slept in a big common berth. The ship was very slow; it took four days to reach Tianjin. I remember something that happened aboard: there was a steward serving the passengers their meals, and Comrade Shao Fumin found the man had leprosy and at once put it to the ship that he must not be let do this work, for fear of contagion. Chen Geng and I even joked with Shao Fumin, saying it was lucky we had brought a doctor, or we'd have caught something. When the ship reached Tanggu the wharf was cold and deserted; we went down to look and saw nothing but desolation, nothing at all, very poor, not even a place to sit down and eat. So we went back aboard, and at eight or nine the next morning reached Tianjin. Comrade Shao Fumin took us to the Shijie Hotel by the Four-Faced Clock. Shao knew the hotel keeper well, and told him I and Chen Geng had come to Tianjin to do business. At the hotel Comrade Chen Geng and his family had one room, I had another, and Shao Fumin probably didn't stay; he bought a train ticket and went off to the Northeast."
When Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan set out from Shanghai they were both dressed as merchants; Chen Geng, twenty-eight, wore a gray silk gown, silk trousers, and cloth shoes, and Chen Yangshan too was got up as a merchant.
They told the innkeeper they had come to open a shop in Tianjin, with Chen Geng as the proprietor, Wang Genying as the proprietress, and Chen Yangshan as the bookkeeper.
The breaking-up of the Tianjin Party organization had been exceptionally severe. With the white terror hanging over the whole city, the two of them ran about for more than two weeks before they made contact with the Party. A little later they got in touch with Yang Xianzhen and learned, in a first rough way, where the arrested comrades were held. At the time Yang Xianzhen went by the name "Yang Zhongren" and Chen Yangshan by the name "Old Wang."
After a month or so at the Jiaotong Inn, Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan, afraid that too long a stay would rouse suspicion, moved out to find other lodgings. Chen Geng and his family moved into a rented private house; Chen Yangshan moved to the Datong Apartments to live with Yang Xianzhen and work with him at the rescue.
Early one morning Yang Xianzhen came back with a few of the day's papers, and in the Ta Kung Pao read a piece of news: the night before, a Communist Party organ had been broken up at the Tingzhou Guild Hall. Yang Xianzhen was much alarmed at it, thinking to himself: Yin Jian, secretary of the Shun-Zhi provincial committee, had sent Lai De, party-group secretary of the Hebei provincial committee's Mutual Aid Society, to look for me at the Datong Apartments, and Lai De was lodging at the Tingzhou Guild Hall. Could it be... The more Yang Xianzhen thought, the more worried he grew, and he reported the matter to Chen Yangshan at once.
Chen Yangshan was an intelligence man of great alertness and skill, and he decided on the spot to move house and leave the Datong Apartments at once. But Yang Xianzhen said he had already arranged for Chen Geng to come to the Datong Apartments at eight that morning and go with him to meet Liu Shaobai at the Guomin Hotel, so the new address had to be got to Chen Geng. Chen Yangshan said: "No. Something has happened, and a careless slip could still cause a blunder. We must leave the Datong Apartments at once." So the two of them moved out of the Datong Apartments straightaway.
Chen Yangshan went at once to get in touch with Chen Geng. Then, on his own, he rented a very small room above a flatbread shop and settled in.
Yang Xianzhen went at once to the Guomin Hotel to reach Liu Shaobai. As it happened, Liu Shaobai, having learned of trouble at his home in Beiping, had already left the Guomin Hotel and gone back to Beiping.
One day in July 1931 Hu Egong came from Shanghai back to Tianjin. He told Yang Xianzhen that the Center had agreed to buy the arrested comrades An Ziwen, Liu Yaxiong, and the others out with money, that he had brought the money back, and that Yang Xianzhen was to go and reach Liu Shaobai at once. Yang Xianzhen told Hu Egong of the tangle of changes that had come about, saying that Liu Shaobai had gone back to Beiping and contact was lost. But Hu Egong still insisted that Yang Xianzhen go to Beiping and look for Liu Shaobai at the address he wrote out. At the same time Hu Egong gave Yang Xianzhen an "Outline for Developing Political Intelligence Work in the North" that he had written, telling him to copy it out in five and, in Beiping, hand it secretly to five intelligence contacts. Among these five were the Northwest Army generals Zhang Kexia and He Jifeng.
Reaching Beiping, Yang Xianzhen first delivered the "Outline," and had handed over four copies, when he thought to go and find Liu Shaobai. But he did not know Liu Shaobai's address, so he asked the fourth intelligence contact whether he knew Liu Shaobai; the man said he did, and offered to take Yang Xianzhen to find him. In the end Yang Xianzhen not only failed to find Liu Shaobai but was arrested by Kuomintang gendarmes.
When word of Yang Xianzhen's arrest reached Tianjin, the rescue work was forced to a halt. So Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan bent their efforts to investigating the traitors. One by one they found a number of them and gradually got a grasp of a good deal.
One day the two were walking along the street when a man came toward them, dark-faced, tall, big, and heavy. Chen Geng said quietly to Chen Yangshan: this man is a traitor, keep an eye on him. Then he turned a corner and slipped away.
The man was called Zhang Keyun, alias Zhang Kaiyun, a notorious great traitor of Tianjin. He had studied in the Soviet Union, and after returning defected in Tianjin, selling his own soul and other men's lives with a wild abandon that did the northern Party organizations enormous harm. Chen Geng knew him, and so slipped away at once.
Chen Yangshan watched him for several days and established that this traitor lived at a small hotel in the Chinese-administered city, that he came and went at several hotels in the Chinese city and the French Concession, made contact frequently with traitors and enemy agents, and was extraordinarily brazen in his doings. His very existence was a great threat to the safety of the northern Party organizations, and Chen Geng reported to the Party Center and proposed the traitor be eliminated. Before long Zhang Keyun, that traitor of the blackest guilt, met at last the punishment he deserved.
Chen Geng, for fear of being recognized by Zhang Keyun, kept out of sight for several days. Chen Yangshan went about on his own for several days and found a good many traitors. The French Park in the French Concession was a hub of movement, with many tram lines of the British, Japanese, and French concessions passing through it, and every evening at dusk a number of traitors would be at work there, some making contact with enemy agents, some shadowing revolutionaries. Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan often came here to watch the traitors' doings; being only two, they could take no action, and could only try to learn their faces, shadow them to understand their doings, and study the pattern of their movements, so as to report to the Party Center and gather the force to deal with them.
After some time investigating, Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan gradually got a grasp of the names, marks, lodgings, contacts, and habits of a fair number of traitors. One evening after dinner the two were strolling in the Quanye Bazaar when they suddenly ran into four traitors. As they had met these traitors almost daily of late, they may have roused their suspicion; but the others were many, and, afraid of coming off worse, the two slipped quickly away, and, not knowing the lay of Tianjin well, each wandered three or four hours before he shook off the traitors' tail.
The next day Chen Geng met Chen Yangshan to talk over the work. Both felt that the Tianjin traitors were so brazen, daring to charge about the streets in the open, making contact and shadowing people openly, that they truly threatened the safety of the Party organization. So it was decided that Chen Yangshan should go back to Shanghai at once to report, and propose that the Party Center send a "dog-beating squad" to Tianjin to suppress them.
Having reported, Chen Yangshan waited in Shanghai for orders. Two weeks later the Center sent "Old Man Wang" with him to Tianjin, and they looked over the ground, the traitors' gathering points, and the pattern of their movements, so as to work out a concrete plan for suppressing them. After a spell in Tianjin, "Old Man Wang" went back to Shanghai.
In late August a worker Party member from Tangshan, in the Tianjin Party organization, made contact with Chen Geng. This man was surnamed Ju, had shown himself well in the past, and had a wife and children. Chen Yangshan moved in to live with Old Ju. Chen Yangshan stayed upstairs and Old Ju and his family below; Chen Geng came once every day or two, sometimes to talk over the work, sometimes to go out together.
At two or three one afternoon in early September, Chen Yangshan sent Old Ju out to buy a paper. It should have taken only ten minutes, but Old Ju had not come back by nightfall. As they waited in anxiety, Chen Geng arrived. Judging by the circumstances that something had surely gone wrong, the two left the Ju house at once. Chen Yangshan went back to lodge at the Jiaotong Inn, and the keeper, seeing the "bookkeeper" arrive, greeted him warmly and put him up.
The next day, asking the Tianjin comrades after Old Ju, they learned that he had indeed been arrested. In view of this, it would be dangerous for the two to stay on in Tianjin, and they decided to go back to Shanghai at once and report to the Party Center.
Since so many people knew Chen Geng, going by train risked running into a traitor when they passed through Nanjing; going by ship, there was only one sailing a week from Tianjin to Shanghai; and to stay on any longer was unsafe. So that no traitor or enemy agent should notice, they decided to take the ten o'clock train that night by way of Jinan to Qingdao, and from there go back to Shanghai by ship. The inn and Chen Geng's lodging both lay near the Tianjin main station, but as there were many traitors there and trouble was likely, they changed to the out-of-the-way South Station to board.
That night Chen Geng bound his head in gauze, as if injured; his legs and feet had been hurt before, and now he leaned deliberately on a cane and walked with a limp. The next day they reached Jinan smoothly, by which time it was noon. Off the train, they went to lunch, and in the afternoon took the train again to Qingdao. At Qingdao they changed to a Japanese mail boat, and in a day and a night were in Shanghai. Back in the city, the two lodged at the Qiantai Inn on Tianjin Road, a place where merchants gathered, fairly out of the way. Less than twenty days after they returned to Shanghai came word of the September 18 Incident.
Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan agreed that Tianjin, smaller than Shanghai, left no room to maneuver, was hard to hide in, and would be a hard place to build a body of the Special Branch kind. This trip of theirs to Tianjin had, in the main, carried out the task the Party organization gave them. A few days after returning to Shanghai, Chen Yun came to the Qiantai Inn on the Party's behalf to see them. Chen Geng reported to Chen Yun on what he and Chen Yangshan had learned in Tianjin. Chen Yun told the "two Chens" not to go out on the streets, and Chen Geng above all to keep to the inn as much as he could, since so many people knew him, for fear of danger.
By now the Center had new arrangements for Chen Geng's work, and had him hand it over to other comrades. Two days later the Party Center decided to transfer Chen Geng to the Red Army in the Hubei-Henan-Anhui soviet area. At this Liu Ding came to talk with Chen Yangshan, telling him to stay on in Shanghai and go on with intelligence work in the Central Special Branch.
### 4. Indispensable: The Special Missions of the "Red Squad"
The Red Squad, popularly called the "dog-beating squad," fought hard through the late 1920s and early 1930s to guard the Party Center and the Shanghai underground, to strike at Kuomintang spies and agents, and to put down traitors and enemy agents within, and it brought off one remarkable feat after another. Chen Yangshan wrote of it in a memoir: "The Red Squad came into being over time; it took shape step by step, as the revolution developed and the struggle demanded."
As far back as the great strike that followed the May Thirtieth incident of 1925, the trade-union bodies, to keep order among the strikers and guard against the wrecking of scabs and reactionaries, had begun forming workers' pickets, drawn mostly from the activists in the unions and from Communist Party and Youth League members. Their task was to keep order in the strike at their own mill or firm and to settle disputes within the working class; they answered directly to their own mill's union. In the Third Armed Uprising of the Shanghai workers they seized part of the puppet police force's arms, and later took some weapons and equipment from the warlord troops as well, and so the pickets were gradually armed.
From late June 1925 on, the imperialists and their running dogs pressed the wrecking of the strike harder. On one side they played on the hardship of the workers whose living had grown difficult after the strike, sowing discord to split the workers' ranks; on the other they used hooligans and scabs to attack the strike's leaders, the union officials, and the activists, in organized and deliberate fashion, and some comrades were secretly beaten to death or badly hurt.
Against this the Party organization and the unions worked hard, on one hand, to help the hard-pressed workers with their particular troubles; on the other, they dealt with the hooligans and scabs who wrecked the strike according to the gravity of each case, some with an open warning, some with a firm blow. To deal with the few scabs who wrecked the strike in the most brazen way, deaf to warning and going on with their crimes, a handful of comrades who could shoot were picked from the pickets and charged with putting down the hooligans and scabs. The workers backed the killing of these men who had done so much evil, and society sympathized; the enemy's schemes were broken, and the strike went forward on firmer ground.
From the May Thirtieth period until just before the Third Armed Uprising of the Shanghai workers, the pickets had no firearms. In the Third Uprising they first seized part of the police force's arms, and later some of the warlord troops' weapons, and so the pickets were armed.
After Chiang Kai-shek launched the counterrevolutionary coup of April 12, 1927, and betrayed the revolution, the suppression grew fiercer, and traitors and enemy agents began to appear within the Party too, doing enormous harm. The lesson written in blood convinced the Party Center and the CCP Jiangsu Provincial Committee that a body of comrades had to be drawn out and organized, and trained for the special work of countering the wrecking of Kuomintang agents and traitors. So, on the foundation of the old workers' pickets, the Jiangsu Provincial Committee chose Party members who were politically reliable, good shots, and familiar with Shanghai, and formed them into a small, hard-hitting fighting unit armed with pistols, charged with putting down agents, traitors, and enemy agents within. To set it apart, and in keeping with the color of the Party, League, and army flag and with the name of the Party-led Workers' and Peasants' Red Army, this unit was called the "Red Squad." It was the earliest body the Party built for a mission of this special kind.
When the Party Center was in Wuhan, it had had a unit much like the Jiangsu Committee's Red Squad, and after the Party Center moved from Wuhan to Shanghai it brought that unit with it. So by 1928 there were two Red Squads in Shanghai, one under the Party Center and one under the Jiangsu Provincial Committee. Because intelligence work had not yet fully developed, the two ran into no end of problems in practice and were hard to command as one; the Party Center therefore decided to merge them into a single Red Squad. It told the Jiangsu Committee and the various revolutionary base areas to pick, from among the workers and the Red Army, outstanding League members who were loyal to the Party, strong in Party spirit, upright in conduct, sound in body, quick and brave, dead shots, and seasoned in the struggle with the enemy, and to send them to the Central Special Branch; Zhou Enlai then looked each one over and talked with him before settling the choice. In this way a new "Red Terror Squad" under the CCP Central Committee was formally established. Tan Yubao, just back from study in the Soviet Union, was put in charge of it, under the leadership and command of the Special Branch's Third, or Operations, Section, whose chief was at first Gu Shunzhang and later Chen Geng. Its task was to put down the traitors and enemy agents who appeared within the Party, and the Kuomintang's agents, gendarmes, and military police, and the imperialists' detectives and patrolmen; to guard the safety of the Party Center's offices and its leading comrades; and to rescue arrested leaders.
Once formed, the Red Squad showed its edge at once, concentrating its strength to put down several open traitors in succession, striking such fear into the traitors that they no longer dared act so wildly. This "dog-beating squad" was well known in the Shanghai of those days. For a while at the start, the Kuomintang's agents, police, and gendarmes and the foreign detectives and patrolmen all knew the Communists had "four guns." From their study of one Shanghai case after another they judged that the dog-beating squad's whole armory was four pistols. But those "four guns" could charge where they pleased and fight their way across the city, and they left the enemy badly shaken. In those days the Party's work in public was almost half open; the patrolmen and police dared not touch it, and even when handbills turned up they dared not interfere. Only when someone informed on the Party's activity did the patrolmen set about cracking a case, and when they went out to make arrests they did it with a great deal of shouting, hustling their man away.
But in its early days the Red Squad, with no counterespionage intelligence system yet in place, could not head off the enemy's wrecking beforehand; it could only run down a traitor's doings afterward and put him down. Short on experience at first, it made this or that slip in its operations. Only after the Central Special Branch set up its Intelligence Section in 1928 did the Red Squad's work change in its very nature. Through intelligence work, word of a traitor's or an agent's movements could now be had from inside the enemy in good time, so a traitor could be put down accurately and promptly without much trouble. Sometimes an enemy scheme could even be caught beforehand, the blow struck first and the trouble headed off before it came. Such reliable information gave the Red Squad, as it were, extra eyes and ears; it acted fast, chose its moment well, and struck hard, surer of itself and smoother in carrying out its tasks. The Red Squad brought off many hard assignments in this period without mishap, and that was inseparable from its close coordination with the Intelligence Section's counterespionage work.
By the latter half of 1929 the Red Squad's strength had reached its height: more than forty members in all, their chief weapons Brownings, revolvers, and Mauser pistols, along with a stock of chemical grenades that made the enemy's eyes stream, which everyone called "tear bombs." Once an operation began, if the enemy gave chase the squad threw these to slow him down, wearing special goggles of their own so as not to be affected. At that time the Third Section could also call in firearms from outside at any moment, and could even bring up machine guns (in this period the Special Branch had made contact inside an artillery battalion of the Kuomintang forces stationed at Pudong); it could buy guns from the foreign trading houses too, delivered by the houses' own men. For a major operation, the First Section sent men to a rice shop to buy rice in bulk, loaded the sacks onto a truck as cover for the Red Squad's fire, and made a moving fortress of it, driving it up to the scene to shoot at the enemy. For transport the Special Branch had a few small cars and motorcycles, but relied chiefly on bicycles, all of them unregistered and without plates, to be thrown away when the pinch came.
In early 1930 the CCP Central Committee convened the National Congress of Soviets on Hart Road in the Shanghai International Settlement; it had been planned as a full congress, but with the delegates not all present it was changed to a conference. To keep the meeting safe, the Central Special Branch laid careful plans: on Hart Road it rented a four-story red building and set up a temporary "hospital." The ground floor held the Special Branch's own staff, the second and third floors were "wards" for the delegates to lodge in, and the fourth was the hall for the sessions. On the ground floor the First Section posted men as doorkeeper, bookkeeper, and the like, to receive the odd ordinary patient who wandered in for treatment, to handle such chores as the meter reading and the water and electricity bills, and to keep outsiders out. At the same time Liu Ding, who was in charge of the operation for Chen Geng, took a separate room nearby and set up a post there, a channel for moving the "hospital's patients" out fast: in an emergency they could cross from the roof of the red building to the roof of Liu Ding's lodging, and slip out the front door through his room. To be doubly sure, the Third Section also sent Red Squad men, armed, to stand guard. Some patrolled the neighborhood by bicycle, some kept watch around the building disguised as small tradesmen, some stood guard at the meeting hall, ready, should the enemy get wind of it and patrolmen come to search, to take up their guns and resist at once; and a man was assigned to lead the delegates out through Liu Ding's room. For all this Chen Geng and the others went to the hospital many times to inspect and plan. Meanwhile the First Section had rented some hotels for the delegates and laid in fairly fine clothes to suit every station. As the delegates reached Shanghai they first lodged at the appointed hotels, and after a preliminary screening were sent one by one into the "hospital" as "patients." Once a delegate entered and took his ward, he was cut off from the outside and not allowed out. Before the sessions the place ran exactly like an ordinary hospital, the doctors, nurses, cooks, and staff all going by the book. Only on the day of the meeting did they enter the hall. The conference ran two days in all. When it was over the delegates were sent out of the hospital one by one, to scatter among the various hotels. And so, in the space of a few days, this newly opened hospital vanished like a mirage, its people gone without a trace.
That same year the Central Special Branch ran a short training course on Carter Road just for the Red Squad. Yun Daiying and Zhou Enlai gave the squad's men political talks more than once, and it was just then, hearing them speak so often, that Chen Yangshan grew firmer still in his revolutionary faith. Chen Yangshan took part in the training of the day in the technique and discipline of secret work. The course covered: knowing the enemy's police, gendarme, and agent organs and the hooligan gangs; knowing the names, routes, and layout of Shanghai's streets and lanes and its residences; the art of disguise, so as to hide oneself and to reconnoiter and watch the enemy to real effect; cracking the enemy's ciphers, writing secret letters in invisible ink, picking one's own manacles after arrest, ruining the enemy's cars by mixing sugar into their gasoline, and the like; marksmanship; and more. Each time they trained at shooting they hired a boat out to the mouth of the Yangtze and practiced firing on the open sea. It was the Party's care in every particular that gave the Red Squad its fine political quality and its considerable fighting strength. With the Red Squad as the main force guarding the Party, the Central Special Branch put down traitors and agents without mercy in those days and rescued arrested leading comrades, doing great service to the Party's safety. In a Shanghai held down tight by strong enemies at home and abroad, the squad moved like ghosts; tempered in a long, sharp, tangled struggle, it played out countless stirring scenes and heroic deeds, and left a deep mark in the memory of the Shanghai people, wrapped even now in an air of mystery.
The Central Special Branch's Red Squad grew and hardened in the brave struggle to put down traitors and agents; the Kuomintang's agents, enemy agents, and traitors turned pale at the mere name of the Red Squad and lived in daily dread, and the squad became an important force in guarding the CCP Central Committee. Its members were few and it lasted only seven or eight years, yet under the white terror it did much to preserve the Party's organization, to build revolutionary strength and win the victory of the democratic revolution; and it gave the Party Center valuable lessons for the regular guard force it would build later.
Under the Central Special Branch the Red Squad brought off one feat after another. Many of its cases were cracked and dealt with in good time, striking the enemy hard and guarding the Party's organization and its people.
In his memoir of the Central Special Branch, Chen Yangshan set down several instances of the Red Squad's work in those years.
#### 1. Executing the Traitor He Jiaxing
In late April 1928, Chen Geng led the Red Squad in person to deal with He Jiaxing, the traitor who had sold out Luo Yinong. In Chen Yangshan's account his surname is written with a different character, though it reads the same. Luo Yinong had joined the Socialist Youth League of China in 1920, and after the Chinese Communist Party was founded in 1921 was sent to study at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow. He came home in the spring of 1925 and took charge of important Party work in Guangzhou and Beijing. In January 1926 he was made secretary of the Jiangsu-Zhejiang district committee, with charge over the Shanghai municipal Party as well, and he had helped lead the three armed uprisings of the Shanghai workers. In April 1927, at the Party's Fifth National Congress, Luo Yinong was elected to the Central Committee, and was at once sent to Nanchang as secretary of the Jiangxi Provincial Committee. That November, at the enlarged meeting of the provisional Central Political Bureau, he was made a member of the Political Bureau and director of the Central Organization Bureau. In mid-April 1928 he was transferred from the CCP Hubei Provincial Committee to the Party Center in Shanghai. On the morning of the 15th he went to keep a rendezvous around Avenue Road (now Beijing West Road) and Hart Road, and while waiting was suddenly arrested, taken by the International Settlement police to the Kuomintang's Longhua Garrison Command. As Luo Yinong had long been a leading Party figure with a price on his head, word ran through the Chinese and foreign papers of Shanghai the next day, and the imperialists and Kuomintang reactionaries all rejoiced: "The ringleader is taken; the Communist scourge can be put out." Learning of it, the Party Center had Zhou Enlai organize the Special Branch to try every means of rescue. Before long word came that Luo Yinong was to be handed over to the Kuomintang's Songhu Garrison Command, and a plan was laid for the Red Squad to seize him back by armed force along the way. The workers and townspeople of Shanghai were furious at his arrest and rose to save him too. Fearing an accident on the road, the imperialists and Chiang Kai-shek's men moved the transfer up to April 18, and on the 21st, without any trial, ordered Luo Yinong shot in the western outskirts of Shanghai, so that the rescue never came off.
To learn why Luo Yinong had been taken, Chen Yangshan and his comrades in the Red Squad made a close and thorough inquiry; through Bao Junfu's inside line in the patrol house they soon established that the traitors who had sold him out were the couple He Jiaxing and He Yihua, also called Hao Zhihua. Both had studied at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow, and after returning from the Soviet Union both did secretarial work under Luo Yinong. They knew many of the Party's secrets and held the addresses of many leading comrades.
Chen Yangshan said: in those days each of our Party's workers had a living allowance of only twenty yuan a month, which of course could not meet their taste for pleasure and corruption; they were in thrall to the bourgeois way of life and chased personal comfort, and even broke the Party's underground rules, for which Luo Yinong had sternly criticized them more than once. They came to hate him, and then informed to the imperialists' patrol house, selling out more than a dozen of the CCP Central Committee's offices in Shanghai for a reward of 100,000 yuan. To win the enemy's trust, the first man they sold was Luo Yinong.
To make sure of the hard proof that these two had sold out Luo Yinong, the CCP Special Branch had them lodged for a time at a small hotel. Once the proof was certain, it was decided to put them down. On a still night a party of Red Squad men, with the speed of a thunderclap, burst into the hotel where the traitor couple were staying; before the two knew what was happening, He Jiaxing was shot dead on the bed, and his wife, scrambling in fright under the bed to hide, was badly wounded and lost an eye. While the shots rang out inside, firecrackers were set off outside with an ear-splitting din, covering the Red Squad men, who all got clear of the scene safely once the work was done.
#### 2. Suppressing the Enemy Agent Dai Bingshi
One afternoon in late August 1928, Dai Bingshi staged another "ruse of self-injury," hoping by it to mask the ugly face of a man who had betrayed the revolution and turned enemy agent.
Dai Bingshi was a Shanghai man. Before Chiang Kai-shek's great massacre of April 12 he had worked in the offices of the Shanghai General Labor Union, and after the Great Revolution failed he lay low a long while on orders from above, receiving no word to come out and work. That quiet life, with nothing weighing on him, bred in him little by little a craving for ease, and in the year and more he was out of touch with the organization he prayed to himself, who knows how many times, that the Communists would never find him again.
The Communists had not yet found him, but the Kuomintang came looking first. After April 12, though the Kuomintang reactionaries had cast a dragnet over all Shanghai to take the Communists in one sweep, and in their utter depravity had slaughtered tens of millions of Communists and revolutionary masses, still they lived in dread all day long, as if the shadows of Communists flickered everywhere. To carry Chiang Kai-shek's "party purification" further, the Kuomintang's agent organs racked their brains and made ready to have some who had once worked in the Communists' outer bodies feign eager progress, work their way into the Communist underground, learn more of the CCP's affairs, and bide their time to root the Communists out for good. Dai Bingshi thus became a fit choice for them, for he had made his identity public while in the union, and the extremism and impulsiveness he often showed let the enemy sense a weakness in the man, one they thought could be both baited and used. Under the lure of money and the promise of office, Dai Bingshi came out at last from the quiet waiting he had been savoring, and threw himself again among the workers, cursing at every turn the ferocity of the Kuomintang reactionaries and the dark rule of the Nationalist government, winning the goodwill of the workers around him and the trust of the underground Party as well.
After the Great Revolution failed, the Party's organization had suffered heavy losses, and to restore and open up the work there first had to be people to do it. At just such a time of need for hands, Dai Bingshi was quickly taken into the Party's underground, and before long taken into the CCP as a member.
At a corner of Pingliang Road in the International Settlement there was a shop dealing in paper and stationery, which was in fact a secret liaison post of the CCP Central Committee's offices, where Dai Bingshi's sister-in-law also helped out. Ordinarily the post was used mainly to pass messages; several couriers of the Party Center's offices came and went there, propaganda and Party documents were sent on from there to the provinces, and now and then a meeting was held there. Dai Bingshi was posted here, a shop hand to all appearances but in fact in charge of receiving and sending documents. Because the higher Party organization kept single-line contact with the post, and because Dai Bingshi's particular job kept him from learning more secret CCP matters, the enemy was in no hurry to break the post up, lest a small gain cost a large one and give Dai Bingshi away.
On the Kuomintang agents' side too, Dai Bingshi kept single-line contact straight with his superior, so that even Bao Junfu, who was then supplying our Party with much important intelligence and stood high in the Kuomintang's Shanghai agent organs, knew nothing of it.
One day in August, Bao Junfu was ordered to carry a sealed dispatch from the Shanghai Songhu Garrison Command to the political department of the British patrol house. Tan Shaoliang, the Chinese deputy who held an important post in that political department, opened the dispatch, took one look, and at once asked Bao Junfu to come along with a dozen-odd Chinese and foreign detectives straight to the stationery shop on Pingliang Road to search it. In the attic of the back room and among the packing boxes of stationery and paper in the small storeroom they turned up a great batch of CCP documents not yet carried off, and Tan Shaoliang ordered the detectives to arrest seven people on the spot, Dai Bingshi's sister-in-law among them.
Three days later the seven were handed over to the Songhu Garrison Command for the Chinese side to try, and it fell to Bao Junfu to handle the case. Early on the fifth day Dai Bingshi came, of all things, straight to Bao Junfu, begging him to let his sister-in-law out at once. Only then did Bao learn who Dai Bingshi really was.
Recalling this affair, Chen Yangshan said: "Bao reported it to us at once and had us look into how to deal with Dai Bingshi. Before long Dai Bingshi went to Bao Junfu asking to have his sister-in-law released. After checking with us, Bao agreed to his request."
It turned out that before Bao Junfu was made the Nanjing government's agent-organ commissioner resident in Shanghai, Dai Bingshi had, through the Shanghai agent organs, already made direct contact with Yang Jianhong, the man of real power in the Nanjing government's Kuomintang Party Affairs Investigation Section; knowing full well that Yang Jianhong held his rise and fortune in his hands, Dai Bingshi clung to him for dear life. After more than half a year in the Communists' underground, Dai Bingshi was anxious to make a showing and claim his reward, and to prove his loyalty to his masters, and so he handed Yang Jianhong a plan to break up the secret Party post on Pingliang Road, hoping to gain two ends by it. First, to seize a number of people at once when the couriers from the lower offices came for documents as usual, so that if a breach could be opened among them, more secret courier posts could be broken up, cutting the Communist organization apart, top from bottom, and throwing its command into disorder. Second, that with this post destroyed, Dai Bingshi might be posted to some other, more important office of the CCP Central Committee, to learn more and so help catch more important Communist leaders. To gain the second end, Dai Bingshi said, he himself must not be among those arrested; but to avoid rousing suspicion he thought up a "ruse of self-injury," proposing that when the enemy made the arrests his sister-in-law be taken along too. Yang Jianhong thought very highly of the plan and said at once: the moment the British patrol house handed the prisoners to the Longhua Songhu Garrison Command in Shanghai, his sister-in-law would be released.
None of this had Yang Jianhong told Bao Junfu; he had only said to him, not long before, that in a few days he would hand him a man who had already worked his way inside the Communists and won their trust, and who, when the time was ripe, would supply important intelligence.
Two days after Dai Bingshi's sister-in-law was handed to the Songhu Garrison Command, and still with no sign of her release, Dai Bingshi grew frantic; with his wife weeping and railing at him all day besides, he was driven to the wall, and went straight to Bao Junfu, laid his whole story bare, and begged Bao to make good on Yang Jianhong's promise.
Once Bao Junfu knew all this, that same evening he got in touch with Chen Geng and reported every detail and how it had come about. Chen Geng reported it at once to the Party Center.
The Party Center took the matter very seriously, and soon sent Chen Geng the result of its check: Dai Bingshi was indeed the enemy agent who had wrecked the secret post on Pingliang Road. It ordered the Red Squad to do away with him within a short time.
Just at this moment Dai Bingshi was hiding at home, quietly pleased with himself. On the day the secret post was raided he had found a pretext not to go in; afterward he put on a show of frantic worry, sought out the comrade who was his direct contact, and asked the organization to arrange new work for him at once. The comrade told him: keep out of sight, don't give yourself away, wait for instructions from above.
One morning in early September, Dai Bingshi's wife came home from the market and suddenly found in her basket a little roll of paper no thicker than a chopstick. She hurried to hand it to Dai Bingshi, who took it, opened it, and read:
{v} Deshui:
{v} Someone has come from home. Please come this afternoon at three to Room 3 of the Andong Hotel on Hubei Road and speak with Mr. Zhang.
{v} Your cousin
{v} 2 September, Republic year 17
The "Deshui" of the note was Dai Bingshi's cover name; the "cousin" was the Central-office liaison man who ordinarily kept single-line contact with him; "Mr. Zhang" meant the higher Party leadership.
Note in hand, Dai Bingshi turned it over a long while in agony. Go, and if the Communists had already found him out as an enemy agent, he was going to certain death. Not go, and if it truly was the higher Party organization come to give him more important work, would he not be throwing away a chance to earn merit and reward? At last he set his teeth and said fiercely: "Go!"
To be safe, he had his wife carry a letter in his own hand to Bao Junfu: "I must see Commissioner Yang in person; wait for his reply before coming back." In the letter Dai Bingshi asked Bao to take protective measures for him that afternoon and to lay an ambush at the Andong Hotel beforehand: if anything went wrong, to open fire in the open; if nothing did, to slip quietly away once Dai Bingshi had made his contact with "Mr. Zhang" and gone.
Having read the letter, Bao Junfu said to Dai Bingshi's wife: "Go tell him to set his mind at ease; I'll see to it right away."
Bao Junfu did indeed take protective measures as the letter asked, but the whole operation ran fifteen minutes later than Dai Bingshi had asked, and in those fifteen minutes this sinister enemy agent lost his life.
To carry out the suppression of the enemy agent Dai Bingshi cleanly, the Red Squad drew up two plans. Under the first, Dai Bingshi would be knifed in Room 3 of the Andong Hotel, no firearms used. Under the second, should the enemy agent reach the hotel door, not go in, and turn back, a bullet would end his life.
The Red Squad chose this time and this place mainly for the cover. In the early-autumn Shanghai afternoon, at three o'clock the streets were crowded, so the Red Squad men could pose as tradesmen of every kind, as passersby, as hotel guests, and lie in wait near the Andong Hotel: first, to help protect the comrade carrying out the task in Room 3 and be ready to back him at any moment; second, to carry out the second plan directly, and make sure a last-minute change of heart by Dai Bingshi could not leave the operation empty-handed.
Near three o'clock Dai Bingshi, a summer hat on his head and dark glasses on his nose, came to the south corner of Hubei Road. Catching sight of Bao Junfu lighting a cigarette in the shade of the roadside trees, as if waiting for someone, he felt a rush of gladness, and swaggered through the door of the Andong Hotel with every confidence.
At the door of Room 3 he knocked softly four times. The door stood ajar; he pushed it open little by little and went in. By the windowsill a man stood with his back to the door. Dai Bingshi had got out no more than the one word "Zhang" when the door slammed shut behind him, and before he could turn, a thick, powerful hand had closed on his throat, and a voice, low and slow, word by word, went into his ear: "We'll let you die knowing why, you enemy agent!"
Just then came a clatter of footsteps and voices from the courtyard, closer and closer. The other Red Squad man, who had stood by the window, was already at the door in a few quick steps, and in an instant a cold dagger went into Dai Bingshi's heart. So this vile, sinister enemy agent, bent all his life on rank and riches, met the punishment he had earned.
#### 3. Shooting the Enemy Spy Chen Weinian
One evening in the summer of 1929, Tan Shaoliang, deputy in the political department of the International Settlement patrol house, brought a man to Bao Junfu, a man named Wang Songsheng. Tan Shaoliang told Bao Junfu: this Wang Songsheng knows a good deal about the Communists' affairs and is well worth using.
At the time Wang Songsheng was no more than a hooligan drifting about Shanghai, without a trade and unwilling to do work within his powers, idling his days away, bent on getting rich by peddling political intelligence, seeking a market for his mean and ugly trade in the filthy corners of society and a living for himself.
One day at the end of August, Wang Songsheng found Bao Junfu, took from his trouser pocket a Central Circular he had just come by, and asked whether he wanted it. The Central Circular was a secret document of the CCP Central Committee of the day; where had Wang Songsheng got a thing so secret? Bao Junfu realized that behind Wang Songsheng stood a more dangerous figure, and that he must find a way to draw the important player hiding in the shadows into the open. So Bao Junfu, feigning excitement and great interest, kept the Central Circular and slipped Wang Songsheng some money, urging him again and again: "Be careful in your work, and the moment you have another Communist secret document, bring it straight over!"
That same day Bao Junfu told Chen Yangshan of this top-secret matter overnight, and Chen Yangshan passed word to Chen Geng through the CCP Central Committee's underground liaison post that Bao Junfu had an urgent matter to speak with him face to face.
The next evening, in the lobby of the Lidu Cinema, Chen Geng's wife, Wang Genying, got up like a rich young matron, an arm resting lightly on Chen Geng's, smiled and nodded toward Bao Junfu as if listening to them talk of amusing things. In talk that rose and fell, Bao Junfu gave a full report on Wang Songsheng, and Chen Geng told him he must find out for certain who stood behind him.
Wang Songsheng brought Bao Junfu two more CCP Central Committee documents. Handing him money, Bao Junfu asked whether there were any other secret publications he might go on supplying. Putting on an air of mystery, Wang Songsheng told Bao Junfu: "These documents of mine all come from a certain Communist, absolutely reliable." Bao Junfu, feigning keen interest, said: "Splendid; bring him along one of these days and let us make his acquaintance."
The man who sold documents to Wang Songsheng was called Chen Weinian, and he had worked for more than a year at a secret Party rendezvous at the west-street corner of Xiaoshadu Road. To the outside the post was a barbershop, and Chen Weinian's public identity was that of its odd-job man, while in secret he was in charge of receiving and passing on the Party Center's documents. Three months before, Chen Weinian had quietly, through connections, gone to surrender at the Kuomintang's Longhua Songhu Garrison Command in Shanghai, declaring that he would "repent and turn over a new leaf," "forsake the dark for the light," and thereafter "serve the Party-state" faithfully, willing to do all he could to help wipe the Communists out.
To plant its own eyes and ears inside the Communists, the enemy had Chen Weinian go on working at this secret post, supplying CCP Central Committee documents on schedule as a rule and forbidden to give himself away except in some grave matter. And so Chen Weinian, this shameless traitor, was transformed at a stroke into an agent the Songhu Garrison Command had planted in the Communist underground.
Introducing the case, Chen Yangshan said: "It turned out Chen Weinian was a spy of the Kuomintang garrison command who had already worked his way inside us." Chen Weinian had taken the garrison command's money for selling secret documents, but to earn still more he sold the same intelligence to Wang Songsheng, the running dog kept by the British patrol house. That was how he tripped himself up before Bao Junfu.
Once Bao Junfu had made contact with Chen Weinian, Chen Weinian no longer passed documents through Wang Songsheng. After frequent dealings, Bao Junfu had Chen Weinian's whole story clear, reported it all faithfully to the Central Special Branch, which checked Chen Weinian further by other channels and resolved to execute this dangerous traitor.
One day Chen Weinian came again to deliver the CCP Central Committee's documents. Putting on a show of great satisfaction, Bao Junfu said he would treat Chen Weinian and Wang Songsheng to dinner that evening at the Songhu Restaurant.
The pair of them, traitor and hooligan, were quite overwhelmed by the honor. Over the meal, Chen Weinian and Wang Songsheng vied in flattering Bao Junfu, the great favorite of the Kuomintang's Nanjing agent organs in Shanghai. Bao Junfu promised to bring Wang Songsheng into the "organization" too, and so this hooligan and rascal, who had scraped a living on the scraps thrown him among the British Settlement's patrolmen, was transformed at a stroke into a dog of the Kuomintang's Shanghai agent organs.
After the dinner Chen Weinian, drunk, staggered home swaying, humming a tune all the way, congratulating himself on how brilliantly he had played this move of surrendering and going over to the enemy.
That dinner was in fact a little prelude to the Red Squad's planned suppression: from a signal agreed beforehand, the Red Squad men in disguise had, at Bao Junfu's table, got Chen Weinian's features clear. And they had shadowed him all the way home.
The next day a new stall of illustrated storybooks appeared at the mouth of the lane where Chen Weinian lived; from morning to night there were always children or grown-ups gathered around it, and the lane was suddenly a good deal livelier.
The book stall was another of the Red Squad's plans for killing Chen Weinian. Half a month later the Red Squad had a sure grasp of Chen Weinian's daily habits and of about when he came and went at the lane mouth, and had learned besides the range of his acquaintance and the people he was often in close contact with.
At noon on a bright sunny day, a few children were poring over the storybooks and magazines at the stall, and under the big tree at the lane mouth an old man dozed in a rattan chair. Just then Chen Weinian appeared again at the lane mouth. A man in police uniform flashed past at the mouth of the lane and followed a little way behind Chen Weinian into it; and from the lane's other end an "old man" hawking ice pops along the street came slowly toward Chen Weinian.
A few steps short of Chen Weinian, the ice-pop "old man" suddenly raised his hand with lightning speed; a shot rang out, and Chen Weinian stumbled, turned to run back, and ran full into the "policeman" coming up behind. The "policeman" dodged aside, his hand rose and the blade fell, and this time, after a few struggles, Chen Weinian went down at last. The gunshot wound on his chest bled; two throwing knives stood side by side at his heart, and on his short-sleeved shirt only the two short black hilts showed.
Out on the main road beyond the lane rose the clamor of people and a scatter of running feet. The ice-pop "old man" had already darted out of the lane mouth in a few steps, turned onto another road, and was calling out, "Ice pops! Ice po—"
#### 4. Gunning Down the Traitor Bai Xin
Gunning down the traitor Bai Xin was another of the Central Special Branch's finest operations. This one too Chen Geng organized after getting accurate intelligence through reconnaissance.
Bai Xin was of the first class of the Whampoa Military Academy. In 1927 he had served as Party representative in the training battalion of Ye Ting's 24th Division, and in 1928 took part in the work of the Hai-Lu-Feng revolutionary base in Guangdong. In 1929 he was transferred to the Central Military Commission in Shanghai. Before he turned traitor he was secretary of the Central Military Commission. In those days the Commission often called meetings of the departments concerned, chaired by its secretary, Zhou Enlai, which Bai Xin, as the Commission's secretary, naturally attended. Sometimes it was even Bai Xin who gave notice of the time and place, and the meeting where the disaster fell was at Bai Xin's own home.
On the night of August 24, 1928, five important CCP leaders (Peng Pai, secretary of the CCP Central Peasant Committee and concurrently of the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission; Yang Yin, head of the Central Military Department; Yan Changyi, member of the Central Military Commission; Xing Shizhen, member of the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission; and Zhang Jichun, deputy chief of the Shanghai General Labor Union pickets) were holding a Central Military Commission meeting at Bai Xin's home in Jingyuanli on Xinzha Road in the Huxi district of Shanghai, when several red armored cars suddenly drove up carrying armed Kuomintang troops and agents and ringed the whole building. The agents ran straight upstairs, took out photographs brought for the purpose and checked them one by one, then, without a word, arrested all five.
Chen Yangshan said: "Comrade Zhou Enlai too was to have attended that meeting, but was kept away at the last moment by an urgent matter, and so was spared." To throw people off the scent, the enemy afterward marched Bai Xin off along with the rest.
After it happened, Zhou Enlai at once called an emergency meeting of the heads of the Central Special Branch to work out how to rescue them and learn why they had been taken. On Zhou Enlai's instruction, Chen Geng at once inquired through the relations the Special Branch had planted inside the enemy, and soon learned that the five had been taken on the information of the traitor Bai Xin. Bai Xin was then secretary of the Central Military Commission, and his younger brother Bai Yunshen was director of the reserve bureau in the Ministry of Military Administration of the Nanjing government. Bai Xin had secretly sent his wife to Nanjing to make contact with Bai Yunshen, and through him had soon reached Fan Zhengbo, a standing committee member of the Kuomintang municipal Party and an agent chief. A full month before the five were taken, Bai Xin had secretly surrendered to the Kuomintang municipal Party. After that he passed on to Fan Zhengbo the important intelligence that Zhou Enlai, Peng Pai, and the others would hold a Central Military Commission working meeting at his home, as his political capital for selling himself to the Kuomintang.
Once it was found that the informer was Bai Xin, Chen Geng reported at once to Zhou Enlai. Zhou Enlai swiftly sent word to those concerned that Bai Xin had turned traitor, and told Chen Geng to organize the rescue of Peng Pai and the other five without delay.
Speaking of this fight, Chen Yangshan said: the traitor Bai Xin was guilty of monstrous crimes and a grave threat to us; only by doing away with him could the danger be removed. We soon had his situation clear: after turning traitor he had been living in Fan Zhengbo's home...
After some reconnaissance, Chen Geng and Chen Yangshan learned from Bao Junfu that the enemy would move Peng Pai and the other five on August 28 from the Shanghai Municipal Police lockup to the Longhua Garrison Command.
Chen Geng swiftly reported this to Zhou Enlai. Zhou Enlai at once ordered every man in the Special Branch who could shoot to turn out and lie in wait along the route the prison van would take, ready to stop it and make the rescue.
That day the Red Squad men, together with many others, disguised themselves as a film crew shooting on location, the weapons packed in a leather case that an assigned man delivered to the scene on a motor-fitted bicycle. They had also readied a truck loaded with rice, meaning to block the prison van with it and force the enemy to stop, then strike at once. The operation had been carefully arranged beforehand, and the whole staff of the Central Special Branch turned out. Some disguised as peddlers, some as passersby, they came to the scene and drew up in the array of a "rescue at the execution ground." But because the man bringing the weapons (Fan Mengju, owner of the "Sanmin Photo Studio," who later turned traitor) came late, and because the lubricating grease had not been cleared from the guns beforehand so that they could not be used at once, the rescue never came off. The four comrades Peng Pai, Yang Yin, Yan Changyi, and Xing Shizhen were in the end killed by the enemy.
Word that the four, Peng Pai and the rest, had perished stirred the underground Party to boundless hatred of the traitor Bai Xin. On Zhou Enlai's instruction, Chen Geng pressed the watch on Bai Xin's movements harder. Bao Junfu, then the CCP's important relation inside the enemy, in his role as commissioner resident in Shanghai of the Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, found where Bai Xin was living and talked with him, asking what plans he had and what leads, and drew out of him that he was going to Nanjing to sell more of the Party's secrets to the Kuomintang center, and then to lay a larger plan, meaning to take the whole staff of the Central offices in one net. When Bao Junfu had told him this, Chen Geng had Bao Junfu order his men to keep close watch on Bai Xin and see that the traitor did not leave Shanghai.
To shield the traitor Bai Xin, the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal Party deliberately put up a smokescreen, doing all it could to cover the truth of Bai Xin's informing. On September 14, 1929, the Shanghai Kuomintang organ paper Republican Daily, reporting the shooting of the four (Peng Pai and the rest), deliberately ran a second item saying that Bai Xin had graduated from the Whampoa Academy and had "before this been fooled into wrongly entering the Communist Party; his offenses being lighter, Chairman Chiang has taken responsibility for bailing him out, and he was taken to Nanjing the day before yesterday to redeem his crimes by service, subject to review." But in fact Bai Xin had not left Shanghai at all; he was living in the home of Fan Zhengbo, standing committee member of the Kuomintang municipal Party and an agent chief.
One day Bai Xin, with two bodyguards, came in disguise in secret to the Dasheng Hospital to see a doctor he knew, Ke Dawen. Bai Xin had often come here for treatment, but he did not know that the doctor he trusted, Ke Dawen, was none other than Ke Lin of the Central Special Branch. In those days the Special Branch's people all had lawful identities for cover. Under the cover names Ke Dawen and He Yusheng, Ke Lin and He Cheng had opened the "Dasheng Hospital" on Weihaiwei Road in Shanghai to screen the activity of the Party Center's offices (the Party Center held a meeting there once a month at that time). Ke Lin had known Bai Xin for years; Bai Xin had great faith in his medical skill and often came to his hospital, never knowing who he really was. On learning for certain that Bai Xin had turned traitor, Chen Geng had given Ke Lin special instructions: Bai Xin had lately come down with malaria and might come for treatment or send for a house call, in which case report it at once.
It turned out that Bai Xin, in dread of being killed by the Red Squad, had been hiding here and there ever since he turned traitor, and had at last simply moved into the mansion of the agent chief Fan Zhengbo. Having got a large reward, and with his master's leave, Bai Xin made ready to slip away in secret to Italy to lie low, his departure set for November 11. On getting this intelligence, Chen Geng at once laid careful plans. First he had men rent a place near Fan Zhengbo's mansion to keep secret watch on every move at the house; then he had Bao Junfu go to Fan Zhengbo's home in person to confirm the exact time of Bai Xin's departure. Once all was clear, the men of the Special Branch and the Red Squad set to work at once.
That afternoon the Red Squad men (Shao Dafu, Tan Zhongyu, Wang Deming, Zhao Yifan, Wu Lanfu, Chen Yongjia, Chen Yangshan, Zuo Guangyu, and others) lay in wait in disguise near Fan Zhengbo's mansion at No. 43, Fourth Lane, Hehefang, on Avenue Joffre. At ten that evening Fan Zhengbo's car drove into the lane and stopped at the back gate of the house. Then Fan Zhengbo's bodyguard Han Yunxiu went to the Suzhou Motor Company on Avenue Joffre and hired a car, number 6730. Both cars stood at the back gate, and the house's bodyguards and servants began loading the luggage. Around eleven o'clock Bai Xin, in navy-blue Western trousers, a gray serge robe over them, his black leather shoes polished to a shine, came out beaming from the back gate of No. 43, escorted by the Fan brothers and by Wang Rongchuan, who had come to see him off, in a press of seven bodyguards, and started toward the waiting cars. They had just reached the mouth of Dongwang Lane when the Red Squad men suddenly rushed out of the lane opposite; with one great shout of "Don't move!" they drew and fired at Bai Xin. Hemmed in by the crowd, Bai Xin was not hit. He turned and ran, trying to break clear. The bodyguard Han Yunxiu drew and fired back, but before his shot came he was shot dead by the Red Squad. The rest scrambled to flee. In the wild fire Fan Zhengbo took three bullets and fell badly wounded; his brother Fan Zhengluo, hit by six, died on the spot; Wang Rongchuan, shot in the left foot, dropped and played dead. Of the gatekeeping patrolmen, Lin Hanchen and Wang Baoyuan, one was shot dead on the spot and the other fled with his head in his hands. Bai Xin now ran north for his life, firing back with a pistol as he went. The Red Squad men gave close chase, caught him front and back, and at last shot this monstrous traitor dead below the wall by No. 71 gate, a bullet through his forehead and out the back of his skull, his brains spilling, dead where he fell.
#### 5. The Secret Execution of Huang Dihong
In the early spring of 1924, in Shanghai, a thin young man walked aimlessly along Nanjing Road. He had come from Yangzhou half a year before to study, but still had not found his place; this young man was Huang Dihong. A friend told him to go to Guangzhou; word was that Mr. Sun Yat-sen was going to open a military academy there. Here was a fine chance!
In May 1924 Huang Dihong became a cadet of the first class of the Whampoa Military Academy in Guangzhou, and there he joined the Chinese Communist Party and came to know Zhou Enlai.
After graduating from Whampoa, Huang Dihong was sent by the Party to study in the Soviet Union, where a life of peace, quiet, and the working people as masters gave him too early a taste of the ease of "sitting" on a conquered land. In April 1930 the organization instructed him to return home, to go first to Shanghai to attend the First National Congress of Soviets, and then to northern Jiangsu to build the Party's underground work.
When the congress ended, the Central Special Branch sent a man to see Huang Dihong onto a northbound train. The escort never dreamed that the very next day Huang Dihong was back in Shanghai, and without so much as a word to the Party organization had gone straight to his aunt's house. The life of the great city held every allure for him.
Huang Dihong's uncle, surnamed Cao, had kept a pawnshop in Shanghai and left the family fairly well off. After the uncle died, only the aunt and a girl cousin were left, unable to run so large a shop, so they made over the pawnshop to another and, the two of them with two servants, lived at ease, well-off and free. Only, with so few people in the house, the old lady felt something of a chill loneliness.
On May 25 Huang Dihong knocked at his aunt's door. Seeing that her nephew had made something of himself and grown into a fine-looking man, striding up to her so full of spirit, the old lady was so pleased she could not shut her mouth, and without so much as asking where he had come from or where he was bound, gladly kept him to stay.
Six years before, the cousin Yuxiang had been a slip of a girl who stood before Huang Dihong too shy to say a word, and Huang Dihong then, a thorough vagabond, had counted for little in her eyes. Now Yuxiang had grown graceful and lovely, a girl of nineteen with a good education, poised and easy in her talk and full of refinement before Huang Dihong, and that fair face and youthful figure caught his heart at once. The two fell in love at first sight, went about always in a pair, and were inseparable all day. The aunt, who had no son of her own, was all the more glad to have her nephew for a son-in-law, kin married to kin, and set about with delight to arrange the wedding.
But then, one night deep in July, the aunt drove Huang Dihong out of the house; far from making the happy match, he was a vagabond once more. It turned out that when Huang Dihong had drifted through Shanghai six years before he had made a few friends, and now the old acquaintances found him again, and behind his aunt's back he went to the brothels, the gambling houses, the restaurants. Before long, with no money to keep up such a life of wine and pleasure, Huang Dihong hit on a wicked idea and stole a dozen-odd gold bars from his aunt's house one after another. When the aunt found out she fainted with rage, and the first thing she did on coming to was to order him out of the house at once.
Having left his aunt's house, he still had no thought of going to northern Jiangsu to work, and put up for the time being at a small inn in the Zhabei district. By its dim lamplight Huang Dihong wrote Zhou Enlai a letter, saying that his identity had been exposed once he got to northern Jiangsu, that the enemy was hunting him hard and had left him nowhere to stand, so that he had to come back to Shanghai, and begging Zhou Enlai by all means to find him a post in the Central offices.
Hard-pressed and harried as his work was, Zhou Enlai, mindful of their days together at Whampoa, mindful that Huang Dihong had just returned from the Soviet Union, and thinking above all of consolidating, developing, and strengthening the revolutionary forces, wrote back at once and arranged to meet him face to face.
Could a man who sought comfort and pleasure in life, who would even turn thief to feed his corrupt and lavish wants, still fight bravely and unafraid to give his life for the liberation of the nation? Still more, under the white terror in Shanghai, hardly a day passed but Communists and revolutionary masses were thrown into prison, even cruelly killed. Huang Dihong's revolutionary will had wholly given way; he meant only to make a trump card of what he could learn of the movements of important Communist leaders, a gift to lay before the Kuomintang reactionaries at their first meeting.
The moment he had Zhou Enlai's reply, Huang Dihong wrote another secret letter, this one to Chiang Kai-shek. As a first-class cadet of the Whampoa Military Academy, he told "Headmaster Chiang" that he was much thwarted among the Communists and had important secrets to lay before "Headmaster Chiang" in person.
Chiang Kai-shek, who counted "wiping out the Communists" his first task, was moved not by any fondness for the old "teacher and pupil" tie but by the "important secrets" Huang Dihong spoke of. Having read the letter, Chiang Kai-shek passed it himself to the arch-agent-chief Chen Lifu, who naturally dared not be slack either and at once referred it on, to Xu Enzeng, director of the Party Affairs Investigation Section, to handle Huang Dihong's surrender. Xu Enzeng in turn gave the actual work to Bao Junfu, the Kuomintang center's commissioner resident in Shanghai.
The time, place, and signal for Bao Junfu to meet Huang Dihong were all fixed. At ten in the morning on July 14, Bao Junfu, a briefcase in one hand and that day's Shanghai Daily in the other, came on time to the Nanjing Road post office, sat on the long wooden bench set out for customers in the hall, and read the paper with an idle air. Ten minutes, twenty minutes, half an hour passed; Bao Junfu rose and left the meeting place. Huang Dihong had never shown.
Three days later, at the same time and place and with the same signal, Bao Junfu went again as arranged, and again, after half an hour, came away disappointed: Huang Dihong had missed the meeting once more. After two soundings and some covert watching, Huang Dihong showed himself at last, and three days after that Bao Junfu made contact with him. Bao told Huang: "As the Nanjing commissioner I give you my word your safety will be assured; only, how are you to prove to us that your surrender is genuine?"
Anxious to show his "sincerity," Huang Dihong laid before Bao his relations with Zhou Enlai, gave up that he had already set a time and place to meet Zhou Enlai, and offered besides to bring Zhou Enlai's letter in his own hand to their next meeting. But this soul-selling turncoat did not know that what waited for him next time was a bullet to punish his wickedness.
After parting from Huang Dihong, Bao Junfu felt how grave the matter was, and, allowing no delay, swiftly reported it to Liu Ding of the Central Special Branch. Liu Ding sought out the Special Branch chief Chen Geng that same night and gave a full report of Huang Dihong's surrender to the enemy and his selling out of Zhou Enlai, and Chen Geng passed it on at once to the Center. Zhou Enlai instructed: keep close watch on Huang Dihong's movements, investigate and verify his defection and surrender thoroughly, and the moment it is confirmed, execute him in secret at once.
The Central Special Branch set out on another important coordinated operation.
On July 26, the very day Zhou Enlai had set to talk with Huang Dihong, early in the morning Huang Dihong received a written order from Bao Junfu:
{v} Eleven o'clock. The west-street corner of the City God Temple. Be sure to bring Uncle's letter in his own hand.
{v} Your cousin
The "Uncle's letter in his own hand" on the note meant the letter Zhou Enlai had written Huang Dihong in his own hand; the "cousin" was the signature Bao Junfu had settled on beforehand for himself. Seeing the order, Huang Dihong felt not a flicker of doubt; suddenly certain that his day of rank and riches had come, he made ready in high excitement and set off at the appointed time toward the City God Temple.
Huang Dihong had barely taken his stand in the shade of the roadside trees at the west-street corner of the City God Temple when he saw a file of cyclists come speeding by from east to west; before he could make out the riders' faces, two crisp shots cracked out, and the cold bullets had already gone through his chest. In an instant he lay in a pool of blood, his breathing stopped.
In the street a police whistle shrilled, and soldiers, agents, and patrolmen came hurrying toward where Huang Dihong had fallen. The passersby, in their fright, pressed their backs to the walls facing away from the street, not daring to stir.
Far off, the Red Squad's cyclists left only a trail of dust behind them. Their ghostlike work of rooting out the traitor had guarded Zhou Enlai and the CCP Central Committee, and the scheme of the scheming Kuomintang reactionaries had failed once more.
The effective work of the Central Special Branch left the enemy unable to believe what had happened. Xu Enzeng came to Shanghai in person to inspect the work; sitting face to face with Bao Junfu, he said with anxious foreboding: "It's very strange. How does the Communists' word travel so fast?" Nodding with the same heavy heart, Bao Junfu said as if in thought: "I've been careful and careful again, and still the Communists get a step ahead. Where on earth does the trouble lie?"
Speaking of this case, Chen Yangshan said:
In 1930 there was a first-class Whampoa cadet named Huang Dihong. After returning from Moscow to Shanghai, seeing the Kuomintang trumpeting its "victory over the bandits" everywhere, he grew pessimistic and hopeless about the revolution and resolved to betray the Party and go over to the enemy. He wrote directly to Chiang Kai-shek offering his services, the letter even claiming that on returning to Shanghai he had met Zhou Enlai face to face, and so on. Chiang Kai-shek set great store by his letter of loyalty and handed it straight to the agent chief Xu Enzeng, who gave the matter to Bao Junfu. Having got the letter, Bao handed it at once to our Party.
We too took the intelligence seriously; a check confirmed there was such a person as Huang Dihong, and that he was in direct contact with Comrade Zhou Enlai, a very dangerous enemy. To harm Comrade Zhou Enlai, he had written asking to meet him at the post office beside the Sincere Company; we sent men to wait for him there, but he was very cunning and never dared show his face. Guilty and uneasy, he moved house often; later we pressed the watch harder, found his address at last, and did away with him in time. So we broke the enemy's scheme to harm Comrade Zhou Enlai.
### 5. A News Agency in Chongqing: The "Three Chens" and a Soldiers' Friendship
Chongqing was the center of Kuomintang rule and the hub of its agent and espionage machinery; intelligence organs great and small covered this misty mountain city like a spider's web. Chongqing was also the Kuomintang's anti-Communist base for the southwest, where the head offices of its party, government, army, gendarmerie, police, and secret service all sat. The white terror was exceptionally cruel, the CCP underground suffered heavy blows again and again, the struggle was very harsh, and it was very hard to get any activity going; there was almost none at all.
In the spring of 1935 Chiang Kai-shek's own faction extended its power into Sichuan. Chiang's Chongqing camp, the Chongqing communications section of the Military Affairs Commission's Political Training Department, and Kang Ze's special-operations corps were set up one after another. Liu Xiang, the warlord of eastern Sichuan, to consolidate his own power, formed the "Wude Society" as a way to bind his people together against Chiang's faction.
To keep track of the enemy's movements and open up the intelligence work, in August 1935 Wang Shiying, then head of the Central Special Branch, sent Chen Chang and his wife Liu Qizhen to Chongqing to open an intelligence front.
After Chen Chang and his wife reached Chongqing, through the connection of Yuan Fulu, secretary to the Kuomintang agent chief Kang Ze, they worked their way into the special-operations corps as probationary intelligence agents. But with no cadres and no funds it was very hard to open up a footing, and so they asked the organization to send people.
In September 1935 the Shanghai Provisional Central Bureau sent Chen Yangshan, under the cover name Chen Zhongying, to Chongqing to help Chen Chang open the intelligence front. Chen Yangshan was one of the more senior members of the Central Special Branch; from the founding of the Central Special Branch in 1928 he had been transferred into its Intelligence Section, and had done intelligence work in Shanghai throughout. When the Central Special Branch was wound up in September 1935, it had first been decided to send him to study in the Soviet Union, but as Sichuan was in urgent need of cadres he was sent to Sichuan instead.
After reaching Chongqing, Chen Yangshan looked into the situation with Chen Chang and judged that there were many connections in Chongqing that could be used: the Chongqing field headquarters, the special-operations corps, Liu Xiang's local power, the Wude Society, the disaffected warlords and politicians of Sichuan and Guizhou, and so on. But with no cadres and no funds it was indeed very hard to get the work going. So Chen Yangshan went back to Shanghai at once, reported to the liaison office that had stayed there, and asked that the cadre and funding problems be solved.
After Chen Yangshan got back to Shanghai in late October, he reported to the liaison man Qiu Jifu. Qiu said men could be sent, but funds could not be found. He had Chen Yangshan wait in Shanghai and go back to Chongqing together with the men to be sent. But in early November Qiu Jifu was suddenly arrested, and Chen Yangshan lost touch with his superiors. Only by working every angle did he make contact again in mid-December, and the organization decided to send Chen Kehan back to Chongqing with him.
After Chen Yangshan and Chen Kehan returned to Chongqing, they set with Chen Chang to a careful study of the particular strengths of the various factions in Chongqing and Sichuan. While they were still working out how to open up the work, they got wind that the New Sichuan News Agency, run by the Sichuan power-holder Liu Hangchen, was doing so poorly, with no profit to be had, that it was to be closed down.
In his Shanghai days with the Central Special Branch, Chen Yangshan had run a news agency and understood the value of a news agency as cover for gathering intelligence. In those days the founding of news agencies was much in vogue, and Chen Yangshan often worked in the guise of a news reporter, using open, lawful forms to carry on intelligence work, and had come by a great deal of most important intelligence. His public identity then was that of a reporter for the Shanghai Shen Bao, and he had ties with many Shanghai papers and news agencies. This chance of the New Sichuan News Agency closing was a rare one, not to be let slip. So it was decided to talk with Liu Hangchen about our taking the agency over, and they worked out in detail how to carry on the work.
By much inquiry it was learned that Liu Hangchen had been finance chief of the 21st Army of the National Revolutionary Army under the Sichuan warlord Liu Xiang, and had, by his skill at gathering money, won Liu Xiang's considerable trust; he was now head of the Sichuan provincial finance department and general manager of the Sichuan Provincial Bank, and surely had ties with the military. So it was decided that Chen Chang should find a way, through Kang Ze's connection, to make contact with Liu Hangchen first, and then negotiate with him over our taking the agency over.
Chen Chang was very able in his work and soon made contact with Liu Hangchen. They lost no time in negotiating the takeover of the agency with him.
After several rounds of talks, an agreement was reached on these terms: that we would give priority to relaying their news copy, and they would hand over the New Sichuan News Agency's signboard and seal to us to run, free of charge.
In "Recalling the New Sichuan News Agency," where Chen Yangshan looked back on this stretch of the struggle, he wrote:
After we reached Sichuan, we three Chens (Chen Chang, Chen Yangshan, Chen Kehan) made a careful study of the situation in Chongqing and Sichuan, and while we were still working out how to open up the work, we got wind that Liu Hangchen, a power-holder of Sichuan, had once run a "New Sichuan News Agency" that was to be closed for want of profit. We too wanted to run a news agency, but had not managed it, the registration formalities and the funds and such being hard to settle. This was a rare chance, not to be let slip. So after several rounds of talks, on the terms that we would give priority to relaying their news copy, he handed over the agency's seal and signboard for us to run, free of charge. In this way we could use the agency as cover to carry on activity more widely; it was truly like giving a tiger wings.
Once the agreement was reached, the Special Branch soon set up for him a Shanghai branch of the Nanhua News Agency in Shanghai, and a branch in Wuhan as well. Thereafter the Central Special Branch used this Nanhua News Agency to carry on intelligence work, came by much valuable intelligence, and found in it no small convenience and good cover for its people's activity.
The agency was up and running, with Chen Chang as director, Chen Yangshan as editor-in-chief, and Chen Kehan as reporter. But the funds still could not be found, so Chen Yangshan sold off everything of his that was worth anything to use as the agency's working funds. He was left unable to keep even his basic living, at times getting through a day on a single flatbread, in great hardship. Yet his zeal for the work ran high; he was often editor-in-chief by day and pressman by night, taking the lead in whatever work there was.
Chen Yangshan was strong in Party spirit; however heavy the task, however cruel the struggle, he never wavered, and he was forever teaching everyone to raise their guard and tighten organizational discipline. He often told them: "We must never forget that we are the Party's underground intelligence workers, that our work is work driven into the enemy's very heart, and that a slip will bring losses to the Party's cause."
To find the New Sichuan News Agency a "backer" and solve its funding and its sources of intelligence, the "Three Chens" decided to make contact with the Political Training Department (the intelligence department) of the Kuomintang's Chongqing field headquarters.
The terms agreed were these: Chen Yangshan would serve as a volunteer intelligence agent for the Political Training Department, supplying it with some intelligence; and the Department would give Chen Zhongying a hundred yuan a month as the agency's funds. So the New Sichuan News Agency now had its "backer," and under the signboard of the Chongqing field headquarters' Political Training Department could carry on intelligence work boldly.
To raise the New Sichuan News Agency's standing in society, the "Three Chens" dressed up the news broadcasts of the Kuomintang's Central News Agency as "this agency's exclusive dispatches," and drew as well on Chen Kehan's ties in the Shanghai press to have special contributions or "exclusive dispatches" mailed from Shanghai, making it all seem quite marvelous. Zhejiang men were much in favor then, and both Chen Yangshan and Chen Kehan were from Zhejiang; some took them for men sent to Chongqing on the orders of some important figure to run a news agency and build a base in Sichuan, and so paid them much court, and many papers vied to use the New Sichuan News Agency's copy. The "Three Chens" seized the chance to strengthen their ties, on their own initiative, with the various papers and news agencies. With progressives on the Commercial Daily and the New Shu Daily (Tao Jingzhi, Wen Sixiang, Hou Zhenchang, and others), they built up a rapport, and through them did united-front work. In this way the New Sichuan News Agency soon became a well-regarded rising star of the Chongqing press.
The "Three Chens" also used the frictions among the enemy's factions, fanning the flames to enlarge a quarrel and drive the dog-eat-dog fighting to a fever. When Huang Yingqian, secretary of the Chongqing municipal government (a member of the municipal Party and a Zhongtong man), seized people's houses to build a mansion for the new mayor Li Honghun, of Liu Xiang's faction, it was found out by Yuan Jiapei, commander of the Third Gendarmerie Regiment, and after the New Sichuan News Agency ran the story the Chongqing municipal government and the Third Gendarmerie Regiment fell to fighting each other. Again, when the Juntong Renaissance Society and the Zhongtong CC Clique were struggling for control of the Kuomintang's Chongqing municipal Party, the New Sichuan News Agency ran a story exposing it, and the two sides ended in the farce of a brawl at the municipal Party offices.
In this way, after some months' effort, the "Three Chens" came by a great deal of important military, political, and economic intelligence: on the party, government, and military moves of the Kuomintang center and the local warlords; on the disposition of forces and their struggles with one another; on the scramble for power and profit between the Kuomintang's military and party organs and the various local factions; on the frictions between Chongqing's business world and the Zhejiang capitalists; and all manner of social intelligence besides. This intelligence came chiefly from the Kuomintang's party and government head offices: secret, accurate, and timely, and of high value.
Besides this, the "Three Chens" also learned that Li Jiemin, head of the Chongqing field headquarters' press censorship office and a CCP traitor, had stolen the secret method by which the CCP communicated in chemical writing. They reported it to the Center at once, so that precautions could be taken and the Party organization spared further blows from the enemy.
Apart from running the news agency, the "Three Chens" also secretly led and took part in the anti-Japanese national-salvation work of Chongqing and Chengdu: organizing the Chongqing public to back the Chengdu public's struggle against Japan's setting up a consulate in Chengdu; supporting the anti-Japanese struggle of the people of Suiyuan; and taking part in leading various anti-Japanese national-salvation bodies and the anti-Japanese national-salvation propaganda of the colleges and middle schools.
By then the Red Army had ended the Long March and reached northern Shaanxi, the national anti-Japanese salvation movement was rising, and the southwest had become Chiang Kai-shek's strategic rear; it was against this background of history that the "Three Chens" carried a special fight deep into the enemy's heart. Using open, lawful identities, in the form of running a news agency, they cleverly screened the measures of the hidden struggle, and they succeeded. In this struggle Chen Yangshan played an important part.
In July 1936 Chen Yangshan and Chen Kehan returned to Shanghai to report on the work; by then the Center had sent Feng Xuefeng to Shanghai to take over the contacts. They stayed nearly a month before it was decided to transfer Chen Yangshan to Xi'an and Chen Kehan back to the Party Center in northern Shaanxi, not to return to Chongqing. Chen Chang, for taking an active part in anti-Japanese salvation work, drew the suspicion of the Kuomintang agent chief Kang Ze, and in early 1937 was forced to move from Chongqing to Wanxian. With that the work of the New Sichuan News Agency came to an end.
In August 1987, at the age of eighty-one, Chen Yangshan looked back on these events and said:
It was under such special conditions that we carried a special fight deep into the enemy's heart.
This "New Sichuan News Agency" was in fact paid for by the intelligence department of the Chongqing field headquarters; it was a tool with which we made wide use of the frictions among the Kuomintang's central party and government organs and the local warlords' various factions, drawing in people of every quarter of society and uniting the progressives of the press and cultural worlds, to gather a great deal of intelligence and carry on united-front work. Who could have suspected that a news agency of such tangled political background and such lawful conditions of activity was a Communist intelligence organ?
This was all more than fifty years ago. Of the Three Chens who fought together then, only I am still alive; the others are gone from the world. Kehan died a few years ago; Chen Chang, persecuted by the ultra-Left errors, was tormented to death as early as 1960, and was not fully rehabilitated until 1981. The revolutionary work of these nameless heroes is little known.
