## Chapter Two. The Hour of the Horse, First Half (11 a.m.)

{v} The two horses raced neck and neck through the ward’s streets, lurching to sudden halts and wrenching turns, kicking up enormous clouds of dust. Carts and pedestrians scattered out of their path, setting off still more pandemonium.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Horse, first half. Chang’an; Chang’an County; the West Market.

Trade in the West Market had not thinned in the least for the earlier commotion. With noon approaching, commoners and landed gentry from every ward, white-robed purchasing agents from the great houses, office-seekers lodging about the capital, and literary aspirants from every province in the realm had swarmed in, all hoping to snap up the latest foreign goods to reach the city. Even among the crowds one could spot a good many gentlewomen with spring-victory ornaments in their hair, women who trusted no one else to choose their wares.

Zhang Xiaojing walked the streets at a pace that was nearly a run. Close on his heels trailed a round-faced young man who had not yet shed his boyish look. His name was Yao Runeng. He was a young officer who had only recently joined the Jing’an Bureau, drawn from a background as a constable-catcher in the capital region, and he possessed the gift of total recall. Li Bi had sent him to assist Zhang Xiaojing in his investigation, and, of course, to keep watch on him.

“Commander Zhang, where are you going?” Yao Runeng could not help asking. Zhang Xiaojing’s pace was ferocious, the crowds thick on every side; it took every ounce of effort just to keep up.

Zhang Xiaojing did not slow his stride. “The Jade Purity.”

Yao Runeng had heard the name: the Jade Purity was a shop that dealt exclusively in face-creams and lip-rouges for women. Its shelves held nothing but secret-recipe beauty unguents imported by Arab traders, said to produce extraordinary results, and the place was quite famous among the noblewomen of Chang’an. Its proprietor was one of the West Market’s foremost magnates.

Yao Runeng suddenly stepped ahead and blocked his path. “Would you mind explaining what business we have there?” Zhang Xiaojing scowled. “Do you have any idea what time it is? And you’re standing here prattling!” Yao Runeng answered with stiff formality: “Your status is unusual, Commander. Every move you make should be explained in advance, if only to set Deputy Director Li’s mind at ease.”

“And if I don’t?”

Yao Runeng gripped the hilt at his waist. “I can haul you back at any time.” The words had barely left his mouth before Zhang Xiaojing’s five fingers shot out, seized the sword-guard, and gave a casual twist; the blade was already halfway out of its scabbard. Yao Runeng lunged sideways to grab it back, but Zhang Xiaojing hooked his ankle, and down he went face-first into the dust.

Zhang Xiaojing looked down at him. “If I really wanted to run,” he said coldly, “you’d already be dead several times over.”

With that he turned and walked away. Yao Runeng scrambled up in a cloud of grit, too flustered to brush himself off, and called after him: “Hey! Commander Zhang, if you keep this up, I will file a report!”

Zhang Xiaojing ignored him entirely and strode straight on. Yao Runeng had no choice but to follow, fuming.

The Jade Purity sat in a crooked alley on the north side of the second crossroads in the market’s southeast quarter, just around a bend that screened it neatly from the noise and prying eyes of the main street.

Inside, three pepper-scented clay walls confronted them, bearing nine tiers of shelving wrapped in pink silk gauze and laden with glass vials and porcelain jars of every size. At this hour only a dozen or so women browsed, draped in shawls of every hue, whispering with bowed heads so that their white napes showed above their collars. The soft scent of agalloch incense hung in the air, intoxicating.

The attendant froze at the sight of a man walking in. Zhang Xiaojing flashed his warrant badge and said in a low voice: “Jing’an Bureau business. Take me to the proprietor.” The attendant started to object, but Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his single eye and swept a glance at the women. The attendant, not daring to alarm his customers, offered to go announce him to the master, but Zhang Xiaojing seized his arm and marched straight for the back of the shop. “Matters of military intelligence brook no delay. I’ll go with you!” The attendant tried to struggle, but a tap of a saber pommel to his lower back froze him on the spot.

And so Zhang Xiaojing dragged the trembling attendant along, swaggering toward the rear. Yao Runeng followed close behind; on this point he had no objection. Time was short, and there was none to waste on a leisurely back-and-forth of announcements.

Behind the shop lay a broad open courtyard, where a fat foreign man reclined on a Persian carpet patterned with interlocking medallions. In his left hand he held a high-stemmed goblet, his elbow propped on a round cushion, his left knee drawn up. A young attendant in black boots stood to one side holding a wine-ewer. In the central court a beautiful singing girl circled a plum tree, performing “The Spring Oriole’s Trill,” singing and dancing at once.

The moment Zhang Xiaojing and the others barged in, the performance stopped dead. Two bodyguards moved to block them, but the proprietor wrinkled his brow and waved them back. “You are...?”

“Commander of the Jing’an Bureau, Zhang Xiaojing.” Zhang Xiaojing let go of the attendant, showed his badge, and motioned for Yao Runeng to shut the courtyard gate.

“Oh... would that be Zhang Yama, of Wannian County?” The proprietor had lived in Chang’an for many years and had heard of anyone with the slightest reputation. One-Eyed Zhang of Wannian, called the Five-Faced Yama for the five demons he embodied: vicious, venomous, ruthless, stubborn, and pitiless, a killing god who had kept the toughs of the eastern half in terror. But come to think of it, hadn’t he been arrested months ago and sentenced to hang? How was he out of prison?

Zhang Xiaojing folded his hands in a curt salute, his face blank. “I have a few questions for Your Lordship.”

The proprietor extended his right index finger and drew it slowly along his moustache from the corner of his mouth all the way to the tip of its upturned curl, savoring the gesture before lowering his hand. Zhang Yama must have run short of festival money, he reckoned; the nerve, trying to shake down the Jade Purity! Had the man no idea of this shop’s connections to the palace?

“Someone fetch Master Zhang a bolt of road-silk.”

A standard bolt of plain silk ran forty feet, used in ordinary trade. For long-distance transport, a second forty feet was folded onto it, making what was called “road-silk,” a load only fit for a pack-mule, far too heavy for a man to carry off. The proprietor had deliberately offered road-silk, meaning to humiliate.

You want money? Then play the beast of burden and haul it out yourself.

Zhang Xiaojing stepped forward as if to take it. The proprietor smirked, but before the smirk could leave his face a flash of white steel crossed his vision and a blade was at his throat.

Never mind the proprietor: even Yao Runeng was stunned. He had assumed the convict and the shopkeeper had some acquaintance; he had not expected things to turn violent from the very first move. Yao Runeng whipped out his saber, but found himself unsure whether to back Zhang Xiaojing or to stop him.

Just then a crowd of the shop’s attendants rushed in. Yao Runeng steeled his heart and his blade in the same instant, and barked in imitation of Zhang Xiaojing: “Jing’an Bureau business! Stand back, all of you!” The attendants did not dare come closer.

Zhang Xiaojing’s voice was as cold as ever. “I haven’t asked my questions yet.”

“You lay one finger on me and you’ll be crushed like a bug!” the proprietor snarled, shame curdling into fury.

Zhang Xiaojing lowered his head to the man’s ear. “I’ll be straight with you. I am a death-row convict. If I fail to get the job done, I die anyway when I go back. So what do you think I’ll do?” The proprietor stared into that baleful single eye and felt his stomach tighten. What he feared most were mad dogs who played by no rules. His eyes flickered for several heartbeats before he gave in. “What do you want to know?”

Zhang Xiaojing moved the blade a fraction from his throat. “Have you had any dealings with Türks lately?”

The proprietor was a little taken aback by the question, but answered without hesitation. “No!”

“Have you heard of any merchants who’ve been in contact with the Türks?”

“No. The Türks? It’s been years since anyone saw one in Chang’an.”

The Türks had been broken as early as the Zhenguan era; the Western Türks, too, had splintered after the Xianqing years, leaving only a handful of small tribes on the steppe, sometimes in revolt, sometimes in submission. As for the Türks remaining in Chang’an, they had long since been fully absorbed. Apart from prisoners, envoys, and chieftains come to pay court, the name “Türk” had scarcely been heard in the capital for many years.

“Why not call your people over and ask them? Perhaps they know something.” Zhang Xiaojing pressed.

The proprietor was obliged to summon his attendants and question them one by one about any contact with Türks. The answer, naturally, was no across the board. Zhang Xiaojing waved them away and pressed on: “Then do you know which house in the West Market keeps a ward-map of Chang’an?”

The proprietor shook his head vigorously the moment he heard the question. “Whether anyone else has one I couldn’t say, but I certainly don’t.” He added: “Keeping one violates the laws of the Tang. It would be tantamount to treason. Who would dare?”

Zhang Xiaojing sheathed his blade and stepped back. “I’ll give it to you straight: a group of Türks has infiltrated Chang’an and is planning to cause havoc at the Lantern Festival. What they lack is a ward-map of Chang’an. If you haven’t got one, so much the better. If the court finds out afterward that someone had a private map, the disaster that comes down on that house will be beyond reckoning.”

Only now did the proprietor understand why this officer was in such a tearing hurry: there was something deeper at stake. He sat up straight and arranged his features into an expression of concern. “This humble merchant may be nothing but a trader, yet I, too, wish to serve the court. If you could describe these Türks and their origins, I would be glad to make inquiries on your behalf.”

“That won’t be necessary,” Zhang Xiaojing said coldly. “If you see anyone suspicious, report it to the authorities at once. And one more thing: this is a state secret. You are not to breathe a word of it.”

“Of course, of course.” The proprietor agreed at once and was about to order a servant-girl to bring over a few bottles of their finest balm for the gentlemen’s hands when he looked up and found that both men were already gone. Seeing them off, the proprietor drew in the fleshy folds of his jowls, summoned a trusted errand-boy, and whispered a few words.

Zhang Xiaojing and his party left the Jade Purity and took up a position under a shop banner across from the mouth of the crooked alley. He turned to Yao Runeng. “Did you memorize the faces of every attendant in that shop?”

Yao Runeng nodded.

Zhang Xiaojing said: “Watch the front and back doors of the Jade Purity closely. If anyone suspicious comes out, have the West Market’s buliang men tail them. See which merchant houses they go to, and write down the names.”

Only then did Yao Runeng see the design. Zhang Xiaojing had been beating the mountain to startle the tiger. After the scare he had just given the proprietor, the man was sure to be panicking, rushing to warn every merchant house that kept a private ward-map. All they had to do was follow the Jade Purity’s messengers, and they would know who had the maps. Letting the shopkeeper lead them to the quarry was a great deal more efficient than going door to door.

The method looked brutish, but it was the least wasteful of effort. The way Yao Runeng looked at Zhang Xiaojing changed. No one but a veteran officer of many years could have devised such a trick; the man had gauged every shade and degree to perfection.

“How did you know the Jade Purity was compromised?” Yao Runeng asked, eager to learn.

“I picked it at random,” Zhang Xiaojing answered, his face blank. “Among the big merchants of the West Market, the ones with clean hands are few and far between.”

Yao Runeng hissed through his teeth. “And if you’d guessed wrong?”

“Then the whole city of Chang’an would be finished.”

“...”

Yao Runeng took this for a joke, but not a trace of humor showed on the other man’s face.

Yao Runeng hailed from Qizhou in the capital region. His family had served as thief-catchers for generations; his father and uncle had both died in the line of duty. By imperial grace the court had promoted him, irregularly, to a post in Chang’an. Before he set out he had sworn an oath: he would make himself a terror to every criminal in the capital, and so honor his family name.

Zhang Xiaojing had served nine years as buliang chief, and the whole of Wannian County had danced to his tune. In Yao Runeng’s eyes, the man was the perfect role model. Before he set out, he had privately steeled himself to learn everything he could from this veteran, hoping that one day he too might become a buliang chief or even a county commandant. What he had not expected was that Commander Zhang would be so different from the figure he had imagined.

The veteran thief-catcher of his imagination should have been upright and awe-inspiring, blazing with righteous authority like a two-handed war-blade, the kind of man before whom every criminal quailed. But this Commander Zhang radiated something crooked in everything he said and did. Yao Runeng could not put his finger on what, exactly, was wrong, only that the man carried about him a faint, unsettling air of the dark side. He suddenly recalled Li Bi’s parting admonition: “Observe this man from a distance. Do not get close.” A chill ran through him.

Just then Zhang Xiaojing suddenly asked: “You haven’t been a constable-catcher for long, have you?”

“What? No, I haven’t. Three months and eight days.” Yao Runeng answered.

“Then let me ask you: what should a constable-catcher’s guiding principle be?”

“To hate evil like a mortal enemy, naturally!”

Zhang Xiaojing shook his head with something like pity. “That won’t keep you alive for long in this city.”

Yao Runeng straightened up. “I respect you as my senior, and I admire your methods, but don’t think you can scare me off with that sort of talk. I will continue to perform my duty and assist you, and I will report every suspicious move you make, unless you kill me first.”

Faced with this obstinate young man, Zhang Xiaojing could only shrug. He made a “suit yourself” gesture and said nothing.

By now the buliang men had drifted over and assembled. Yao Runeng issued a few instructions, then a detail occurred to him and he turned back. “Commander Zhang, we’re pressed for time and short-handed. These merchant houses see so many people coming and going every day. How do we decide whom to follow?”

“Follow only the foreign-born. For this kind of business, they won’t trust anyone outside their own.” Zhang Xiaojing answered without hesitation.

In truth, the Tang had never judged a man by bloodline. Chang’an was a city of mingled peoples, and there was no shortage of civil and military officials born outside the Central Plain. Even among the Jing’an Bureau’s own staff there were several foreign-born clerks skilled in mathematics and well versed in commerce. Yet the old discourse about the line between civilized and barbarian would still murmur in the back of someone’s mind from time to time.

“If this involves foreign merchants, shouldn’t we file a report with the West Market Office...” Yao Runeng had barely begun to offer this suggestion before Zhang Xiaojing cut him off without ceremony:

“What I need right now are hands and feet, not another mouth!”

Yao Runeng did not dare delay. He accepted his orders and went. The Jing’an Bureau had no buliang men of its own; they had to be requisitioned on the spot from whichever wards and offices lay nearest, and that took time.

Zhang Xiaojing stood under the shop banner, arms folded, utterly still, his face set like stone. No one could have guessed what was in his mind. By now the sun was nearly at its zenith, and time was rushing past like the waters of the Wei. His single eye rested on the distant watchtower. All was quiet atop it; no flag had been raised.

The other piece of news he was waiting for had yet to come.

***

One ward away from the West Market, the Jing’an Bureau was in the grip of an unprecedented frenzy.

Every clerk was buried among countless scrolls, and the only sound in the hall was the swish of paper being unrolled.

Runners brought in more dossiers without pause, stacking them before the clerks’ desks. To speed things up, they pre-unrolled the scrolls and spread them on simple bamboo display-racks, so that a clerk could begin reading at once instead of wasting time on the unrolling.

Each clerk had been issued three racks: one for loading, one for reading, and one for unloading, ensuring that whenever a clerk looked up there was a fresh scroll ready to hand.

They had to finish a task both simple and formidable within two quarter-hours.

Since the Kaiyuan era, trade between the Türks and the Tang had been at a standstill, but neither side’s needs had vanished. Shrewd Western Region merchants had long since spotted the opportunity and quietly established a transit route. They bought furs and livestock on the steppe, shipped them into Chang’an under the name of Western Region goods, and shipped silk, tea, and salt back out again for the long haul to the steppe. A good many of the big foreign merchant houses in Chang’an had intricate ties to the Türks.

Li Bi had requisitioned the travel passes of every caravan that had entered or left Chang’an in the past five years, concentrating on four categories of goods: sheepskins, ox-sinew, lake salt, and ironware. The first two were steppe specialties; the last two were what the steppe urgently needed. The greater the volume a merchant house had handled, the closer its ties to the Türks, and from the Bureau’s point of view, the more likely it was that Cao Poyan had come knocking at their door.

This was the method Zhang Xiaojing and Li Bi had agreed on before Zhang Xiaojing left.

Under normal circumstances, compiling these figures would have kept the Ministry of Revenue busy for days. But now time was more precious than jade, and these document experts drafted in from every ministry had no choice but to work themselves half to death. They nearly ran out of counting-rods.

Li Bi took no part in the actual work, but with his hands clasped behind his back he paced ceaselessly between the desks, like a senior tutor of the Imperial Academy. After a while he glanced at the water-clock in the corner of the hall, then shook his head in irritation and turned back to the sand-table.

“Tanqi, what do you make of Zhang Xiaojing?” Li Bi asked suddenly.

Tanqi was placing the latest report from the watchtowers on the sand-table. At Li Bi’s question she wrinkled her nose in distaste. “The face reveals the soul. If you ask me, he’s a boorish lecher. I really cannot fathom why you’ve staked your future on a condemned man, young master.”

Tanqi was of mixed Han and foreign blood, with a high-bridged nose and pupils of pale amber. She was Li Bi’s house-born maidservant; her mother had been a woman of Little Balur. Raised in the Li household since childhood, she was clever and perceptive, which was why Li Bi trusted her above all others, and she spoke to him without reserve.

Hearing Tanqi’s remark, Li Bi tapped his finger on the desk. “When Emperor Taizong saved Duke Li of Wei from the execution ground, he pronounced a maxim: ‘It is better to employ the guilty than the meritorious.’ If Taizong could make use of Duke Li, why should I not be able to master this man?”

Tanqi pursed her lips. “He’s hardly in the same league as Duke Li.”

“I noticed he kept stealing glances at you. Don’t go playing Lady Red Sleeve, now.”

“...Ugh.” Tanqi’s cheeks flushed and for once she was lost for words. She shot him a withering glare. Li Bi threw back his head and laughed, and the weariness lifted from him for a moment, but then he let out a soft sigh. “If you knew his history, you would not say that.”

“Don’t tell me he’s a rakshasa reborn?” Tanqi sniffed.

Li Bi said: “In the twenty-third year of Kaiyuan, Suluk Khagan of the Türgesh rose in rebellion and laid siege to the Protectorate of Anxi’s garrison at Balhuan. Thirty li north of Balhuan stood a beacon-fort garrisoned by two hundred and twenty men. They held that fort and withstood the full weight of the Türk army for nine days. By the time the Protector-General of Beiting, Gai Jiayun, arrived with relief, only three men were still alive, but the great standard had never fallen. Zhang Xiaojing was one of the three.”

Tanqi covered her lips with her sleeve in shock. Even from these few plain, unadorned sentences she could smell the bitter reek of blood.

“Zhang Xiaojing came home, his merit was reckoned, and he was awarded the rank of cavalry captain. Had he only endured a few years at the Ministry of War he could have exchanged his commoner’s cloth for an official’s robe, and his future would have been boundless. Unfortunately he quarreled with a superior, and had no choice but to resign his commission, striking his name from the military rolls. He became the buliang chief of Wannian County, and served nine years in the post. Six months ago he was imprisoned for killing his own superior.”

Tanqi drew in a sharp breath. The buliang chief’s superior was the county commandant of Wannian. A subordinate killing his superior, a clerk killing his officer: that was the crime of “want of righteousness,” one of the Ten Abominations under Tang law, a crime that could never be pardoned.

“Why did he kill his own superior?” she asked. But Li Bi only gave a faint shake of his head. Tanqi knew her young master’s ways: what should not be said would not be said. She changed the subject:

“Why did you choose such a dangerous man?”

Li Bi raised his palm and clenched it fiercely in the empty air. “Only the most dangerous man can accomplish the most desperate task. Chang’an is balanced on a knife-edge. Nothing less than the strongest, most violent medicine will serve.”

Tanqi sighed. “Your judgment, young master, Tanqi has never doubted. But what will the people around you think? What will Director He think? And what about the one in the palace... You’ve taken far too much upon yourself for his sake.”

She knew the Tang court all too well. A place like the Jing’an Bureau was a natural target. The slightest slip, and the man at the helm would face a hail of spears from the front and arrows from behind.

Li Bi laid his fly-whisk crosswise in the crook of his arm, his gaze resolute. “Whether it is for him or for the common people, this city of Chang’an must be protected, and someone must do the protecting. Who, if not I, possesses the mind and the courage? I may be a follower of the Way, but I have the will to save the world. This bitter purpose of mine need not be understood by all.”

Just then Xu Bin came running up, clutching a sheet of paper and shouting: “The list is ready!”

Xu Bin and his team had pulled off a modest miracle: they had actually assembled the figures within two quarter-hours. The list bore seven or eight names, all foreign merchants who had handled the largest volumes of the four categories of goods over the past five years, ranked by quantity.

Li Bi glanced at the list and immediately said: “Send by watchtow... no, relay through the watchtower is too slow. Where is Zhang Xiaojing now?” Tanqi saw that her young master had switched into operational mode. She put away her banter and pointed at the sand-table: “At the north alley of the second crossroads in the West Market. Yao Runeng is with him.”

On the sand-table, Zhang Xiaojing was represented by a lone grey figurine, distinct from the vermilion clay figures of the Lüben Guards and the black clay figures of the Türk Wolf Guards.

“Send it by fast horse.” Li Bi ordered.

A fast horse was stabled under the gallery, its rider standing by, dedicated to carrying messages too complex for flag-relay. The list was swiftly rolled into a small fish-tube, the rider tucked it into his sleeve, clamped his heels to the stirrups, and was out the gate in an instant, the beat of hooves fading rapidly into the distance.

At the same moment the booming-voiced herald ran into the hall, brushing past the fast horse as it left.

“Director He has returned!” His lungs were formidable, and his announcement rang through the hall.

Li Bi frowned. How had the old man come back so quickly? That was far from normal. He shot Tanqi a glance; she understood at once and with a flick of her rod swept the grey figurine representing Zhang Xiaojing off the sand-table.

The herald also handed over several other dispatches that had just arrived, all requiring Li Bi’s first perusal and countersignature. He read and signed as he went, but suddenly one eyebrow rose. He plucked out one document, handed it casually to a minor clerk standing nearby, and gave a few low-voiced instructions.

Li Bi had barely finished when the old man himself strode briskly into the hall. His very first words were:

“Changyuan, you have actually appointed a condemned prisoner?”

Wen Ran flicked the candle-drippings from her fingers, straightened her father’s memorial tablet, and sighed softly. “It’s the Lantern Festival today. Do I really have to go?”

There was no one in the room. She was talking to herself.

A short while ago someone had delivered a message, and in the message was a distinctive code. She knew it was from her benefactor.

The message told her to leave Chang’an at once, but said nothing of why. This put Wen Ran in a difficult position. Since her father’s death she had resolutely taken over the family incense shop, gritting her teeth and struggling on alone. Through sheer stubbornness and determination her business had begun to show some life. With the Lantern Festival driving demand for incense everywhere, this was the best time to turn a profit; if she left now, she would lose a good deal of money.

But it was her benefactor’s command, and Wen Ran could not disobey. Were it not for him, the Wen family would have been destroyed last year. Her father had told her again and again before his death that she must obey her benefactor without question.

She sighed again, packed her traveling bundle, and glanced up at the wooden order-tags covering the wall. Every tag represented a weighty order. Wen Ran knew only a few characters and could not keep written accounts; this was the only way she could track her business. She noticed one tag marked with the character “Wang” and twelve pink ink-dots beside it.

That was the eldest daughter of the Wang military commissioner’s household in Anren Ward, who had ordered twelve packets of the finest spirit-calming rue incense, due for delivery today.

Wen Ran’s fine, moth-wing eyebrows drew together. This order was critical to the Wen Incense Shop. The young lady had been delighted with their blended incense and had been longing for more. If Wen Ran could keep her happy, she would make a name for herself among the women of the great houses.

Anren Ward lay to the northeast of Dunyi Ward, three broad avenues apart, not terribly far. Wen Ran thought to herself: at the very least, let me deliver this order first. Leaving the city can wait.

Her mind made up, she turned, fetched the rue incense, set it on a bamboo carrying-rack, and shouldered it out the door. She had meant to hire a mule, but today was a festival day and every beast at the nearby livery stables had been booked, no matter the price. There was nothing for it but to walk the whole way with the rack on her back.

The roads were crowded with travelers. She squeezed through the throng and barely made it to Chongye Ward before she could go no farther. Here stood the Xuandu Abbey, a Daoist temple where the great and powerful came to burn incense, and carriages and ox-carts of every description choked the ward entrance until the road was impassable. Ordinary folk had no choice but to stop and wait.

Wen Ran stood quietly in the crowd, oblivious. From the second floor of a wine-house on the corner of Huaizhen Ward across the broad street, a cold, predatory gaze swept back and forth over her several times.

A middle-aged man in a pale-blue official’s robe withdrew his gaze and slowly raised his wine-cup. His eyes were narrow and close-set, his nose sharp and hooked; when his lips moved they tugged at his nostrils and eyelids, as though a snake were crawling beneath the skin of his face.

“That girl. Do you see her?” He took a sip of wine and asked casually.

Several young men in brocade robes stood at his side. They all nodded.

The middle-aged man spoke with venomous spite: “That business with her and her father last year turned everything upside down, and cost a county commandant his life into the bargain. Running into her today is surely Heaven’s will. If I don’t settle this grudge, people will think Feng Dalun is easy to push around.

“You boys are to give her a proper welcome presently.”

The young men in brocade burst out laughing, their eyes gleaming with vicious lust.

Feng Dalun set down his cup. “Go as far as you like. Zhang Yama is in prison waiting to die; this time no one can protect her.” At the mention of that name a flicker of fear and hatred crossed his eyes. He could not have said himself which feeling was the stronger. To dispel the unpleasant emotion, he waved his hand:

“What are you standing around for? Get to it!”

The brocade youths folded their hands in farewell and clattered down the stairs.

Wen Ran finally squeezed free of the Chongye Ward jam and walked some way down the street. Without noticing how, she became aware that several idle young toughs had appeared around her. Each was rakishly dressed, and where their robe-fronts gaped open one could see dark lines of tattooing below their collarbones.

At first the toughs merely loitered nearby, then one by one they drifted closer, elbowing other pedestrians aside. Slowly they filled the space in front of her, behind her, and on either side. The gaps between them were loose, yet together they formed an unbreakable wall of bodies that penned her in.

Wen Ran sensed something was wrong and tried to break through. The toughs blocked her with sneering grins, shouldering and elbowing her back. She grabbed one of them by the arm and yanked, but instead of pulling him aside she only dragged his robe open, baring two sun-darkened arms.

Tattooed in fierce blue characters down both arms ran two lines: “Alive, I fear not the Jingzhao Prefecture; dead, I fear not King Yama.”

This was the mark of the Bear Fire Gang! That gang was the terror of Wannian County: it kept several hundred idle ruffians in its pay, who at their mildest picked fights and at their worst robbed and killed, swaggering through the streets all day without a care.

Was this the danger her benefactor’s message had warned of? Wen Ran wondered. But she could not understand why the Bear Fire Gang would be coming after her.

Wen Ran was swept along like a leaf caught in a torrent, helpless to resist, as the wall of bodies herded her toward the quieter streets to the north. She clenched her jaw and kept scanning through the gaps. Suddenly her eyes lit up: at the corner of a ward ahead stood a constable’s post, where several constables sat idle outside with their man-catching forks. She surged forward, shoved one of the toughs aside, and ran for the post, crying for help.

The constables heard her cry and snatched up their forks, but when they saw the dozen-odd tattooed toughs strolling up behind the girl, every face changed. The lead youth sauntered over without haste, folded his hands, and said: “The wife’s been playing up. Sorry for the bother, officers.” He unhooked a few strings of coins from his belt and handed them over.

The claim was an obvious lie, but the constables had no wish to make trouble. They took the coins and backed off as one. The toughs hauled the despairing Wen Ran back into their midst, laughing. At the next crossroads stood a hooded carriage, both windows draped in black cloth. Shoving and jostling, they manhandled her into the carriage. Two of them jumped in after her and bolted the door from inside.

The carriage began to move. In the darkness Wen Ran was frantic, but there was nowhere to flee. Before long she heard a great bell-stroke from outside. The sound was distinctive: a grand, resonant boom laced with a crystalline overtone. She knew it at once for the purple-gold Buddha’s Grace Bell of the Jidu Nunnery. Empress Wu Zetian had once taken the veil there, and the bell, cast in purple gold, rang unlike any other in the city.

That bell calmed her in an instant.

Not because of the infinite power of the Buddha, but because she suddenly realized that her moment of utter despair had not yet come.

The Jidu Nunnery stood within Anye Ward, and Wen Ran often delivered incense there; she knew the surrounding streets intimately. The moment she heard the bell she fixed her position: she was on the west side of Anye Ward, very close to her original destination of Anren Ward, with only the Vermilion Bird Avenue between them.

The Vermilion Bird Avenue was the broadest north-south thoroughfare in Chang’an, roughly a hundred paces wide, running straight to the palace. If she could reach the imperial riding-lane, she might yet break free.

Thinking this, Wen Ran braced her back against the wall of the carriage and sat up. Her fingers, groping in the dark, found a loose iron nail in a crack between the floorboards.

She was not the kind to give up easily.

With a scream strangled down to almost nothing, Cao Poyan’s body convulsed forward, his eyes blood-red, the wooden stick between his teeth nearly bitten in two.

The dark stump of a crossbow bolt was pried out of his flesh by the pointed knife in the bamboo-worker’s hand, streaming blood. The man set down his knife and with practiced skill stitched, medicated, and bandaged the wound.

“The bolt had no head; it won’t kill you. But you won’t be able to use that elbow for months,” the bamboo-worker said, rinsing the blood from his hands in a basin. Cao Poyan’s forehead was beaded with sweat. Weakly, he nodded.

Then came footsteps outside the door, and a grim-faced man entered. He had a typical Türk look: a long, narrow, horse-like face criss-crossed with weather-cracks, and two thick white eyebrows. He wore a floor-length plain silk robe in a style neither Chinese nor barbarian, with a bucket-shaped hood draped down the back.

“Your Eminence, the Right Shad.” Cao Poyan and the bamboo-worker bowed together.

“Right Shad” was not a personal name but a Türk title of office. When princes of the royal house were set over the tribes, those governing the east were called the Left Shad and those the west the Right Shad; their authority was immense. That so great a figure should be hiding inside Chang’an would cause an uproar if the court ever found out.

The Right Shad glanced at Cao Poyan’s wounded elbow. “I have just received confirmed word. The fifteen warriors you brought with you have crossed to the next life.” Cao Poyan dropped to his knees with a thud and, burning with shame, snatched up the pointed knife and leveled it at his own heart. “All blame rests with me. I wish to atone with my life.”

The Wolf Guards were the khagan’s most loyal warriors. They had entered Chang’an under orders with no expectation of returning alive to the steppe. But those lives should have been traded for hundreds of times their number in Tang blood to serve the khagan faithfully. To die in a tumbledown warehouse was an unconscionable waste.

The Right Shad gave a cold laugh. “Your life belongs to the khagan. By what right do you decide its end?” He took the knife from Cao Poyan’s hand, sliced off a lock of hair from the top of his head, and wound it around his own wrist. On the steppe this signified the claiming of a guilty man’s soul. From this moment Cao Poyan was utterly dead; there remained only a husk that obeyed.

“You will carry out every one of my orders before you are permitted to die.”

Cao Poyan’s head sank low. He said nothing. This noble Right Shad bore the high blood of the Ashina house and was the supreme commander of the Türk operation in Chang’an, the embodiment of the khagan’s will. His wish was Cao Poyan’s fate.

The Right Shad tossed the knife aside and raised his hand. “Forget about the ward-map; I have sent someone else to obtain it. I have another task for you.”

“Oh?” Cao Poyan looked up.

The Right Shad said: “Word has just come that the family of Wang Zhongsi, the military commissioner of Shuofang, is in the capital at this moment. Go and seize his daughter. Cut off her fingers and send them, joint by joint, to the Tang army’s field camp on the steppe.” As he spoke, a cruel relish crept unbidden to the corner of his mouth.

Wang Zhongsi was the Türks’ nightmare, the very man who would not let them draw breath. The Wolf Guards had come all this way to Chang’an; it would be poor manners not to send a gift.

But Cao Poyan’s brow knotted tight. This operation in Chang’an had been planned for a long time and was on the verge of execution. How could it be altered on a whim? There was something he had never said: that Cui Liulang, too, had been recruited by the Right Shad’s own hand, and had turned out to be a Tang spy. Cao Poyan did not suspect the Right Shad of colluding with the Tang, but the man had not even bothered with the most basic vetting, and as a result more than a dozen elite Wolf Guards had died before they could be put to use, while the blame fell on Cao Poyan.

This noble Right Shad’s temperament was much like that of other Türk princes: too careless, too casual. On the steppe it might pass, but for an operation inside Chang’an, he was ill-suited to command.

Cao Poyan forced these thoughts down, prostrated himself with an air of humility, and said: “After the West Market engagement, the Tang are on their guard. By now they may well have cast their net. I fear that an unplanned diversion will not advance the larger cause and may instead invite disaster.”

The Right Shad’s face darkened. This had been his flash of inspiration, and here it was being questioned by a lowly Wolf Guard.

“Silence!” The Right Shad swept his robe-sleeve in fury. “Wolf Guards do not need mouths. They need only fangs!”

Cao Poyan tried to protest, but the Right Shad raised his leg and kicked him flat. If only he had a whip to hand, he would have given this insolent wretch a thorough lashing.

At this point Cao Poyan had no choice but to close his mouth, pick himself silently off the ground, and knock his head in apology. Yet his fists were clenched, and in his eyes a flame of defiance flickered. A small necklace of colored stones slipped from his collar; it looked as though it had been strung by a child’s hand.

The Right Shad dismissed Cao Poyan and turned, pushing through the door into the outer room.

Outside lay a spacious workshop where several dozen Türks labored at woodworking with furious energy. They were not lean and hard like the Wolf Guards; most had stooped backs and big, calloused hands. Craftsmen like these were treasures of the steppe, yet here they were, hidden in this cramped workshop, bent over their toil. Around them a dozen brawny Wolf Guards paced back and forth on patrol, eyes sharp.

Lengths of raw bamboo had been stripped of leaves and cut into three-foot rods; ten small holes, each half an inch across, had been drilled along both sides, and the rods lay propped in neat rows under the window. Nearby five or six men were loading lanterns onto carts in batches. These lanterns came in every shape: gourds, fairy peaches, bats, auspicious clouds, and more, all roughly the same size. What they shared was a cylindrical void at the center, exactly the right diameter to receive a bamboo tube.

The Right Shad clapped his hands. Every craftsman stopped and looked up at him.

“The khagan, through my eyes, is watching you.” This was his opening. Every craftsman sank to one knee, right hand pressed to left breast, head bowed.

“Many years ago, these people’s cities lay open for us to trample; their women and cattle were ours for the taking. Now we huddle in a corner of the steppe, enslaved by the Tang and the Uyghurs. But this time we will reclaim the glory of our ancestors. From beneath the white-tufted great standard we set forth, through snow and wind, through blades and arrows. Hatred is the finest steed: it alone has carried us a thousand li to Chang’an. Every one of us is a messenger of the khagan’s wrath, a flame of vengeance. Now we have burrowed like serpents into the enemy’s very heart, and from the stones of their own dwellings we shall build their tombs. The sun does not shine forever on the enemy’s pastures; the blizzard will come!”

The Right Shad was a gifted orator. His voice was pitched low, yet every man in the room heard him with perfect clarity. Every one of them was swept up in his fervor.

“I have just inspected your progress, and it is not fast enough! This is no grey-topped tent, no calf-cart. This is the great Que-le Huo-duo! You must redouble your efforts and complete its body. Its soul, too, is already drawing near to Chang’an. By sundown the two will become one, and we shall see it descend upon Chang’an and devour this city’s men, old and young, its women, its children, every last one, from blood to bone, leaving nothing! Your names will stand higher in honor than the khagan’s bravest warriors; your descendants will be blessed by ancestors and heroes alike!”

The last sentence was a roar. The craftsmen and Wolf Guards alike blazed with ferocious excitement, but they dared not cheer aloud. Instead they beat their chests and stamped their feet in rhythm, chanting in low voices, “Que-le Huo-duo! Que-le Huo-duo!” Their boots struck the floorboards in unison, a steady boom-boom-boom like the war-drums of a southward march.

Cao Poyan stayed alone in the inner room, holding the same one-knee salute with hand on heart. But he was far from sharing the outer room’s excitement. He watched the Right Shad’s speech with cold eyes.

Having finished the exhortation, the Right Shad issued a few more instructions and left the shop.

Before the door of the bamboo workshop a narrow lane led out to the main street. The Right Shad walked slowly along it, pulling the hood forward over his head with both hands to hide his Türk features. As the hood covered his face it revealed, on the back of the robe, a cross embroidered in gold thread. He drew out a string of glass prayer-beads and hung them around his neck, pinching the wooden cross at the center between his right fingers.

By the time he stepped onto the main street he had transformed: gentle-browed and kind-eyed, affable and mild, and to every passerby he offered a smile and a prayer, palms pressed together: “May the merciful Lord be with you.”

The fast horse flew past without slowing and tossed the fish-tube toward Zhang Xiaojing. He reached out and caught it firmly in one hand.

At the same time, Yao Runeng’s surveillance of the Jade Purity had yielded its results, and he hurried back. The foreign merchants had been quick to react: the moment Zhang Xiaojing left, the proprietor had dispatched five servants to five different merchant houses, and those five had in turn sent runners to still others. Only thanks to Yao Runeng’s good management had they managed to track down every house that was alerted.

Now Zhang Xiaojing held two lists: one of merchants who kept private ward-maps, and another of merchants with close ties to the Türks. Overlay the two, and the most suspicious houses were plain as day.

That the Jing’an Bureau could produce something like this in so short a time was nothing short of a miracle.

“Deputy Director Li has the makings of a chief minister,” Zhang Xiaojing said, setting down the list with genuine admiration. In all his years as buliang chief, he had cracked countless cases, and he knew that most of the time the truth required no ferreting out of hidden intelligence; it lay in the very documents that anyone could see, if only one knew where to look. This was the art of the “Great Archive.” Li Bi had deliberately gathered a corps of sharp-eyed officers in the Bureau to do nothing but cross-check and collate, exactly what the present crisis called for. The man’s foresight was extraordinary.

Zhang Xiaojing signaled the distant watchtower that the message had been received, then began to assign tasks.

The cross-checked lists had flagged four merchant houses as the most suspicious. All four were in the West Market, but widely scattered. Zhang Xiaojing and Yao Runeng had no choice but to each take a squad and split up.

Before they parted, Yao Runeng respectfully asked for operational guidelines. Zhang Xiaojing balled a fist and jabbed it lightly into the young man’s chest. “Take down anyone who won’t cooperate. That simple.”

Yao Runeng had run into hard men in government service before, but he had never seen anyone work a case as brutally as Zhang Xiaojing. The man was like a whirling iron hammer of a thousand catties, too impatient to fish the gold and silver out of a vase and so he simply smashed it to pieces. Yao Runeng had a strange feeling: even without the pressure of the clock, this man would have done exactly the same.

“Think it’s not the humane way?” Zhang Xiaojing’s tone dripped with mockery. He pointed at the crowds of pedestrians streaming past. “Showing mercy to the enemy is the same as being cruel to these people. Remember that. It’s your first lesson.”

“But we don’t even know yet whether they are the enemy.”

“Anyone who won’t cooperate is the enemy.”

Zhang Xiaojing’s first target was a gold-and-silver shop called the Xifu. Its proprietor was a native of Kang. Although the Xifu’s main trade was in gold and silver wares, it often took part in large-scale commerce through lending, which was why it had ended up on the Bureau’s list.

The travel pass Cao Poyan had used to enter the West Market listed him as coming from Kang and bore a local seal. That kind of document was not easy to obtain without some connection to Kang’s upper echelons, and the fact was that Kang had always been a nation of Türk stock. The two peoples had gone separate ways long ago, but who could vouch for the bonds of blood?

This was not a matter of prejudice, of course. In fact, of the four suspected houses, two were foreign and two were Tang Chinese; there was no bias. The Jing’an Bureau was not the Court of Diplomatic Reception. It had never hesitated to suspect anyone of the very worst.

The Xifu sat on the northwest corner of the third crossroads in the West Market, a prime location where every road converged and trade was at its busiest. The shop-front stood apart from its neighbors: on either side rose great pillars as thick as two men’s arms, lacquered in gleaming black and inlaid with coiling dragon-and-cloud designs. Zhang Xiaojing pushed aside the cloth curtain and stepped inside.

Inside it was quiet, nearly empty of customers. Just inside the door a crescent-shaped wooden counter blocked the way. It stood a head taller than an ordinary man; one could barely see its bare surface, and nothing at all behind it. He shook a bronze bell that hung beside it, and presently a goateed foreign old man put his head above the counter and looked down at him, expressionless.

“Exchanging wares or exchanging coin?” the old man asked dryly, his tone unfriendly.

Zhang Xiaojing tapped the countertop three times lightly with his index and middle fingers and flashed his badge. “Government business. Are you the proprietor?”

The old man nodded.

Zhang Xiaojing came straight to the point. “We suspect the Xifu of keeping a private ward-map of Chang’an and of colluding with Türk remnants. We need to conduct a search.”

The charge was extremely grave, yet the proprietor betrayed no emotion. He answered slowly: “This humble shop deals in gold and silver. We certainly do not keep any private ward-map, nor have we actively colluded with any Türks.” His Tang speech was perfectly fluent, without a trace of accent.

“That will be for me to determine after I’ve searched.”

The old man’s wrinkles twitched. He stared at Zhang Xiaojing. “I know the Prefect of Jingzhao well. You might want to ask him first.”

A gold-and-silver shop of this kind had lending ties to many senior officials at court; it had no shortage of patrons, and ordinary officers would never dare knock on its door. A glint of violence flashed in Zhang Xiaojing’s eye and he was about to resort to force when a buliang man burst in, panicked.

“Commander Zhang, there’s yellow smoke rising outside!” he shouted.

Zhang Xiaojing frowned, turned at once, and pushed through the curtain. Out in the street many pedestrians had stopped and were pointing at the sky to the northwest. He looked up and saw two columns of smoke rising in the distance. One was dense and black, the other a somewhat lighter yellow; the two twisted around each other and spiraled upward, starkly visible against the clear sky.

That direction lay the Yuanlai Trading Post, where Yao Runeng had gone to search. The Yuanlai was a Kashgar merchant’s enterprise, dealing mainly in large-scale livestock, and its ties to the steppe Türks were closer still. It was every bit as suspicious as the Xifu.

The yellow smoke came from a smoke pellet issued by the Jing’an Bureau; seeing it meant an encounter with the enemy, and everyone must rally to the spot at once. Yao Runeng was a capable fighter and had taken seven or eight buliang men with him. If he was putting up yellow smoke, he had certainly run into serious trouble.

Zhang Xiaojing immediately gathered the nearby buliang men and set off running in that direction. One street-crossing later he stopped short. The men behind him could not check their stride in time and nearly collided with him.

A thin thread of doubt flashed through his mind.

He recalled the Xifu proprietor’s words, and the more he thought about them, the more suspicious they seemed. “We certainly do not keep any private ward-map, nor have we *actively* colluded with any Türks.” Not actively. Then passively?

If so, then the old man’s slightly unnatural tone when he mentioned the Prefect of Jingzhao: could that have been a hint to bring the authorities?

Zhang Xiaojing clicked his tongue in disgust and slapped himself hard across the cheek. How long had he been in prison? And already he was this dull? If it had been the old days, he would have spotted it on the spot.

“You all keep going and back up Yao Runeng. I’m going back to take another look.”

Zhang Xiaojing turned on his heel and sprinted back to the Xifu at astonishing speed. At the shop door he drew his pocket crossbow in a single fluid motion, leveled it on his left forearm, kept his right hand on his belt-knife, and ducked inside.

The shop was as quiet as before, but this time the old man did not put his head above the counter. Zhang Xiaojing scanned the room cautiously, then moved to where the high counter met the pillar at its end, kicked open a small side door, and burst through, keeping the pocket crossbow trained on the counter the whole way.

Behind the counter Zhang Xiaojing found the old man slumped against the wooden wall beside a footrest, his head lolling to one side, eyes wide and staring. Zhang Xiaojing crouched, reached out, and felt the neck. The old man had stopped breathing. He turned the body over and saw a deep wound in the small of the back.

It was obvious now: when the old man had been speaking to Zhang Xiaojing, someone else had been standing behind the counter with a blade at his back. He had not dared cry for help, and could only hint. Zhang Xiaojing’s momentary carelessness in not pressing deeper had cost the man his life.

Zhang Xiaojing’s gaze sharpened. He leveled the crossbow and moved toward the back of the shop. From the time he had left to now was less than half a stick of incense. The killer might still be inside.

Behind the high counter was a long, somewhat cluttered room. In the center stood a square worktable, on it several rolls of account books, a small balance, and a pair of shears. Sandalwood shelves of varying height ringed the walls, crammed with gold and silver wares of every description, each piece polished to a gleam. On the floor lay a dozen or more large hide-covered chests with embroidered corners; a few had their lids half open, affording a glimpse of the glittering coins of many nations inside.

Besides dealing in gold and silver wares, the Xifu ran a currency exchange. Gold and silver coins from Daqin, Persia, the Arab lands, and beyond could be converted here into Tang copper cash and silk, and vice versa. That was why the currencies of so many nations converged under one roof.

Several attendants and guards lay dead among the treasure, each stabbed through the heart. The wounds bled little, and the smell of blood would not easily reach the outside.

Zhang Xiaojing picked his way through the carnage and roughly reconstructed the scene: a Türk Wolf Guard had broken in, killed the attendants first, and then Zhang Xiaojing himself had walked in. The Wolf Guard had held the proprietor at knifepoint to bluff his way through the interview. The moment Zhang Xiaojing left, the man was killed.

This Wolf Guard was even more savage than the Bureau had reckoned. He had never intended to negotiate.

Zhang Xiaojing took a deep breath. At the far end of the long room was a door left slightly ajar. A square padlock hung from it, already opened, with an ornamental key still in the keyhole. This must be the shop’s strong-room. He walked to the door, tried pulling: it would not budge, it only opened inward. He gave it a gentle push. He felt a faint resistance, then from inside came a cascade of bright, ringing clinks as metal objects struck one another.

He cursed inwardly and shoved the door open to look. Beyond it lay a flight of stairs descending to a cellar beneath the shop. At the bottom of the steps sprawled a crushed chrysanthemum-petal gold goblet. The intruder was obviously experienced: he had placed a gold vessel just inside the door so that if anyone pushed it open, the goblet would roll and clatter, sounding an instant alarm.

Zhang Xiaojing re-cocked the pocket crossbow and descended step by step. At the bottom a narrow passage stretched before him; ahead, around a bend, a faint candlelight glowed. Pressing his body flat against the wall, he eased the crossbow around the corner first, then leaped through.

No one was there. A single candle burned on the wall. In its dim light Zhang Xiaojing saw that the room was small and held few objects, but every piece was a masterwork, glowing in the candlelight. He looked down and saw, lying overturned on the floor, a gilt tea-sifter embossed with a pattern of immortals riding cranes. Its drawer was half pulled out and empty.

“Damn!” Zhang Xiaojing swore under his breath. It was clear: the proprietor had hidden the ward-map inside the tea-sifter, and the Wolf Guard had found it.

This was very bad news.

At the far end of the room an apsara tapestry hung half loose, revealing behind the wall a dark opening large enough for one person to pass stooped. The proprietor had built himself a secret tunnel; these merchants always had a bolthole ready. The intruder had evidently heard the alarm and fled through it at once.

Zhang Xiaojing charged for the opening, then checked himself. He stripped off his outer robe, balled it up, and tossed it into the tunnel. Almost at the same instant came the snap of a bowstring inside, and a crossbow bolt flew out and struck the robe dead-on. In the fraction of a heartbeat Zhang Xiaojing raised his pocket crossbow, fired one bolt into the tunnel, reloaded with blinding speed, and fired a second.

The man in the tunnel was a meticulous tactician: he had deliberately left the candle burning in the room and positioned himself just inside the mouth of the tunnel. If a pursuer rushed to the opening, he would block the candlelight and make a perfect silhouette. But a crossbow was single-shot. By wasting the man’s bolt with the robe, Zhang Xiaojing had seized the initiative and put two bolts into the tunnel before the other could reload. In a space that narrow it was nearly impossible to dodge.

Hit or miss, Zhang Xiaojing plunged into the tunnel. Ahead in the dark, rapid footsteps receded; if his bolts had struck, they were not fatal. He advanced with crossbow up, re-cocking on the move. But barely a dozen steps in, he felt a stinging prick in the sole of his foot. He lifted it at once and groped the ground: caltrops, a string of them scattered across the floor. Had he been even slightly less cautious he would have driven one through the top of his foot. The delay let the intruder pull farther ahead.

In the span of a few breaths the two men had traded several volleys of attack. Zhang Xiaojing swept the caltrops aside, raised the crossbow, and fired blind, shouting: “Get down and you live!” The only answer was the sound of footsteps, faster now.

The tunnel was not wide but it turned often. Fortunately it ran in one line to the end, with no forks. The intruder ran ahead; Zhang Xiaojing chased behind. The man seemed to carry an inexhaustible supply of caltrops and flung them at random intervals, severely slowing Zhang Xiaojing’s advance. Yet Zhang Xiaojing’s first two bolts had done real damage; the stumbling rhythm of the footsteps ahead told him that much.

The chase went on for several hundred paces. Suddenly Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his eye: up ahead a shaft of daylight came down from above. The exit was near, a vertical shaft going straight up. A figure was climbing a wooden ladder. By the time Zhang Xiaojing reached it the man had already gained the top. He tried to shove the ladder aside, found he could not topple it inside the shaft, and had no time to tear it apart, so he hurled his empty crossbow down instead.

Zhang Xiaojing dodged, raised his own crossbow, and fired, but the bolt grazed the man’s scalp and sailed into the sky. He too threw aside the crossbow, scrambled up the ladder hand over foot, and when he thrust his head out of the opening nearly cracked his skull on a well-winch.

The exit had been disguised as an abandoned well, complete with winch and railing. Zhang Xiaojing hauled himself out and in one motion drew his barrier-knife, holding it flat beside his ear to guard against ambush. The barrier-knife was shorter and lighter than the standard saber, suited for close combat; in a space as tight as a well-mouth it could still be wielded.

But nothing happened. The intruder seemed to have lost faith in ambushes and fled outright.

Judging by the distance and direction of the tunnel, Zhang Xiaojing reckoned this was inside Huaiyuan Ward, south of the West Market. The shop’s proprietor had been no ordinary man: he had actually dug a tunnel that crossed from one ward to another.

Huaiyuan Ward was home to a large foreign population. If the intruder melted into their midst, the trouble would be enormous.

Zhang Xiaojing spotted a trail of footprints on the grass leading into the distance and took off after them. The well stood in the backyard of a small folk shrine, an unregistered temple dedicated to the Lord of Mount Hua, without even a proper wall; its door opened directly onto the ward’s cross-street. With the holiday at hand, a fair number of nearby residents had come to burn a stick of incense in passing, and the altar was doing brisk business.

Zhang Xiaojing circled to the front of the shrine and found a group of bystanders pointing and exclaiming. Two small stalls selling steamed buns and mutton broth had been overturned, their wares scattered everywhere. Beyond them, a young man in a folded-top cap lay sprawled on the ground, horse-whip in hand, swearing furiously in one direction. His mount had plainly been stolen from under him.

Zhang Xiaojing’s face hardened. If the Wolf Guard got away on horseback, all was lost. He shouldered through the crowd, leaped into the street, and intercepted a single-shaft carriage that happened to be passing. The driver, taken by surprise, instinctively raised his whip to lash out, but Zhang Xiaojing kicked him off the box. A woman inside the carriage poked her head out in alarm. Zhang Xiaojing bellowed: “Jing’an Bureau! I’m commandeering your horse!” She clapped her hands to her chest in fright and shrank back inside.

With a single slash Zhang Xiaojing severed the traces between the draft-horse and the carriage, vaulted onto the bare back, clamped his legs, and galloped off in the direction the Türk had fled.

Huaiyuan Ward was densely settled, its streets crowded; no horse could truly run. Zhang Xiaojing soon had the galloping figure ahead in sight. The man’s riding was superb; he crashed through stall after stall, sending up a chain of screams and curses, yet never lost speed.

Unfortunately the horse Zhang Xiaojing had seized was a draft animal, not bred for riding, and without a saddle he had no purchase. No matter how hard he drove it, he could not close to less than three or four lengths behind. He could make out the cloth wrapped around the Türk’s head, but he could get no closer.

The two horses raced neck and neck through the ward’s streets, lurching to sudden halts and wrenching turns, kicking up enormous clouds of dust. Carts and pedestrians scattered out of their path, setting off still more pandemonium. The commotion finally roused the ward’s own guards: two men with wooden horse-forks for stopping runaways lunged at the lead horse from both sides of the street. The Wolf Guard shifted his right leg, wrenched the reins, and the mount reared with a shriek, just clearing the pincers, then he righted himself and galloped on.

But that moment’s obstruction had bought Zhang Xiaojing just enough time. He surged forward several lengths, pulled a smoke pellet from his waist, and hurled it ahead. The pellet contained white phosphorus, sulfur, reed down, pine resin, camphor, and the like; it ignited on the wind and belched smoke, originally for military signaling. The Jing’an Bureau had prepared a supply of its own.

His throw landed the pellet squarely in the saddlebag slung beside the stolen horse. The bag’s owner, the young man whose mount had been taken, was apparently a literary man on his way to present his poems to a patron, and the bag was stuffed with sheaves of verse. The pellet ignited and the paper bundles caught at once. Billowing yellow smoke poured from the bag, as if a streaming war-banner had been raised on the horse’s back.

In an instant the Wolf Guard faced a dilemma. If he ignored the smoke it would mark him for all to see; but the bag was bound to the saddle with leather thongs, and to untie it he would need a free hand, which would cost him speed. The bastard on his tail would not miss the chance.

Instinctively he turned to look back. In the pursuer’s single eye he saw nothing but cold amusement, and a chill ran through him. He recognized that look. It was the gaze of the most dangerous lone wolf on the steppe.

The Wolf Guard gritted his teeth, rode a few more strides, then yanked out a dagger and drove it down through the horse’s ear into its skull. The animal screamed and crashed to the ground. The Wolf Guard used the momentum of the fall to leap into a narrow alley at the roadside. The horse’s massive body blocked the alley-mouth like a barricade. Zhang Xiaojing, arriving a moment later, had to rein in hard and stop.

He was not anxious. The watchtower in Huaiyuan Ward would have seen the yellow smoke and sounded the drum at once; the ward guards would seal both gates immediately. From here on it was a turtle in a jar. He did not believe this Wolf Guard could find a second inter-ward tunnel.

The two ward guards who had tried to stop the horse came running up, panting. Zhang Xiaojing identified himself and asked whether this direction led to the edge of the ward. One guard told him it was a dead end. Zhang Xiaojing then asked whether there was any building on the far side of the alley. The guard hesitated, then said there was.

“What is it?”

“A Zoroastrian temple.” The guard scratched his head with a pained expression.

At the alley’s end the view opened suddenly into a square roughly two hundred paces across. In its center stood a two-story temple. Its walls were white, its tiles red, and it had arched doorways on all four sides, in a style utterly unlike anything in China. Above the doors were carved three camels standing on lotus pedestals, discs balanced on their backs holding sacred flames, with bird-bodied, human-faced priests attending on either side.

The tiles on the temple’s eaves were all the color of cinnabar, shaped like flames. Overlapping one upon another, they made the roof look like a blazing bonfire.

When Zhang Xiaojing and the ward guards burst into the square, it was already in an uproar. Zoroastrianism in Chang’an maintained no full monastery and did not proselytize; this temple served only the faith’s foreign-born worshipers in the capital, so almost everyone in the square was of foreign birth.

They all wore expressions of alarm, gazing toward the temple. Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his single eye and saw the Wolf Guard standing in the doorway, both arms locked around an elderly man. The old man wore a white robe with gold trim and two red sashes crossed over his chest.

The ward guard’s face went white. That, he said, was the temple’s Zoroastrian prelate, a government-appointed official whose station was equivalent to the abbot of a Chinese monastery. If any harm came to him, every worshiper in Huaiyuan Ward would boil over. Zhang Xiaojing gave a slight nod and studied the scene carefully. Only now did he get a clear look at the Wolf Guard’s face. It was not Cao Poyan. This man’s face was wide and flat as a griddle-cake, his eyes narrow slits, and he had a large, pitted nose.

Zoroastrianism had spread widely among the Türks, too. But from the look of this Wolf Guard’s savage ferocity, his loyalty to the khagan probably outweighed any devotion to the gods.

Zhang Xiaojing strode forward to the foot of the temple steps and, to everyone’s surprise, addressed the man in fluent Turkic: “You are surrounded. Release the hostage and surrender, and I can guarantee you the treatment a warrior deserves.”

The Wolf Guard pressed his dagger to the prelate’s throat, his voice hoarse: “Only the khagan has the right to speak a warrior’s name.” Zhang Xiaojing gave a grim laugh. The Wolf Guards chosen for the mission to Chang’an were fanatics to the last. Talking them into surrender was harder than persuading the emperor to sleep alone. A few words would not do it.

But when it came to hostage situations, this former buliang chief had an arsenal of tricks.

Zhang Xiaojing advanced with a cold smile. “You will certainly die. But your name will live on. What we’ll announce to the world is that you confessed every secret of the khagan and his court and personally guided the Tang army to their doorstep. Soon every soul on the steppe will know that this is the man who betrayed his entire people, the man who defiled the honor of the Wolf Guards.”

“Impossible. You cannot know my name!” the Wolf Guard snarled.

“Care to bet on that?”

Zhang Xiaojing pointed the tip of his blade at the man’s groin and traced a slow arc in the air, smiling in silence, a glint of savagery in his single eye. The Wolf Guard felt his throat go dry, and his wrist trembled involuntarily.

The Wolf Guards had an intensely secret ritual. Every warrior initiated into their ranks was attended by a beautiful slave-girl who aroused him to full tumescence, and on the engorged flesh a special name was tattooed. When the flesh was at rest, one read a wolf-name; when aroused, the true name appeared. The Türks believed the organ symbolized the power of life, and that this rite endowed the warrior with an extra wolf-life.

This Wolf Guard did not know how Zhang Xiaojing had learned of the ritual, but he understood that if his body fell into the hands of this one-eyed man, nothing good would come of it.

“Release the hostage and I will let you die fighting like a warrior. Otherwise your name will be passed down in shame forever.”

Zhang Xiaojing stopped five paces from them. He was waiting, waiting for fear to ferment in his enemy’s heart. The Zoroastrian prelate had shut his eyes tight and was murmuring, whether begging for mercy or praying there was no way to tell.

The worshipers around the square watched the standoff in tense silence. Some had dropped to their knees and kindled small fires, casting in spices and oil. Zoroastrians revered fire above all and worshiped the fire-god. Others followed suit, until a dozen little bonfires sprang up around the temple and the sound of prayer rose on every side.

Just then, from across the square, came a ringing shout:

“Give me back my horse!”

A figure darted from the crowd and launched itself at the Wolf Guard. The Türk was already wound to the breaking point; taken by surprise, his wrist jerked reflexively. A line of red flashed across the prelate’s neck. The old man choked and crumpled. Then the figure slammed headfirst into the Wolf Guard, driving him bodily off the steps.

The effect was an explosion. The Zoroastrian worshipers first shrieked in horror, then surged forward as one, engulfing the fallen Wolf Guard in an instant. Curses and the sounds of kicking and punching erupted on all sides. Zhang Xiaojing lunged after them, but the furious crowd was beyond control, a press of heads and shoulders, everyone shoving and jostling. He and the two ward guards tried to push their way in, shouting for people to move, but they were shoved aside again and again.

Then from the alley-mouth charged several dozen men in black tunics. They were not the ward’s own guards but buliang men under the direct command of Chang’an County, led by none other than Yao Runeng. They had seen the yellow smoke and come running. These men carried iron truncheons, and they moved in fast, splitting the crowd into segments, forcing people apart. Any who resisted got a taste of the truncheon, and the situation was quickly brought under control.

But it was only a temporary calm. Most of the crowd refused to leave. They massed around the perimeter, shouting, demanding an explanation from the authorities. A Zoroastrian prelate had been killed in plain view of everyone. This was an event of staggering gravity.

Zhang Xiaojing had no time for the crowd. He strode forward and saw the Wolf Guard lying on the ground, blood streaming from every feature, limbs twisted at wrong angles: the man had been beaten to death. He crouched and searched the body. The color drained from his face.

The ward-map was gone.

Even with nerves as strong as Zhang Xiaojing’s, cold sweat broke over him. During the riot, too many people had crowded around the Wolf Guard. Perhaps some opportunist had taken the chance to steal the man’s pouch, which was the best-case scenario. If a Türk sleeper agent had lifted the map under cover of the chaos... He looked frantically around, but saw only a sea of hostile faces, impossible to tell apart.

Zhang Xiaojing turned back in frustration. The figure who had caused the chaos was now crouching before the fallen prelate, utterly at a loss. Zhang Xiaojing recognized his face: it was the young man whose horse the Wolf Guard had stolen.

“What is your name?” Zhang Xiaojing asked, barely containing his rage.

“Cen Shen, of Xianzhou.” The young man glared right back, not cowed in the least.

“Why did you kill him?”

Cen Shen was so angry he nearly laughed. “He stole my horse in broad daylight! Why shouldn’t I come after him?” His mood suddenly sank and his voice cracked. “Stolen would have been one thing, but why did he have to kill it? Green Brow was the gentlest creature, we’d been together for years, and he just died like that at the mouth of an alley...” His tone shifted again, sharp with indignation. “A dead horse you can pay for in gold, but my poems have all been burned! How do you compensate that?”

Zhang Xiaojing had no time for the tirade. He turned a stony face to Yao Runeng. “Take this fellow and the Wolf Guard’s body back.

“And what happened at the Yuanlai Trading Post? How did yellow smoke end up there?”

“Don’t even ask,” Yao Runeng said with a helpless expression, touching his forehead where a fresh, narrow cut bled. “Over at the Yuanlai, a bunch of unbroken horses suddenly bolted. They had no licenses, and we happened to be showing up just then. The moment we identified ourselves, the people at the post thought we were West Market Office agents cracking down on contraband. Before we could get a word in, a brawl broke out...”

Zhang Xiaojing cocked his head but before he could respond, several sharp drumbeats sounded from the distant watchtower. This was the alert signal: a command from the Jing’an Bureau was incoming. Both men looked toward the tower. After a moment the constable atop it began waving flags. Yao Runeng set about decoding the message at once. As the translation progressed, his face took on an exceedingly strange expression.

“Who sent the order?” Zhang Xiaojing asked. “Deputy Director Li?”

“No. Deputy Director Li is only second-in-command. This order comes directly from Director He.”

“Director He?”

“You didn’t know? He is the Jing’an Bureau’s true head: He Zhizhang.”

At the name, Zhang Xiaojing showed a faint stir of emotion. “What is the order?”

Yao Runeng finished decoding and stood utterly stunned. Fortunately, watchtower commands were always transmitted three times. He scrambled to decode it a second time and confirmed it was correct. He turned to Zhang Xiaojing, at a loss for what to do:

“Commander Zhang Xiaojing of the Jing’an Bureau: relieved of post effective immediately. Escort under arrest back to Bureau headquarters...”
