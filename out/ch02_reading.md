## Chapter 2. Secret War: The Hidden Struggle Within a United Front

After the Xi'an Incident, relations between the Nationalists and the Communists changed fast, and a peace unseen in ten years settled over the country.

On January 13, 1937, the CCP Central Committee entered Yan'an in peace. On February 8, the Nationalists' Central Army entered Xi'an in peace. The next day the Communist representative Zhou Enlai and the Nationalist representative Gu Zhutong opened talks in Xi'an. In late March, Zhou Enlai flew to Hangzhou to negotiate with Chiang Kai-shek face to face. In early April he flew back to Yan'an to report to the Central Committee, and was to set out again, by way of Xi'an, for the south, to negotiate with Chiang once more.

Then one day, that busy and peaceful road between Yan'an and Xi'an erupted in a hold-up that shook the heavens.

### 1. Zhou Enlai in Danger

On April 25, 1937, Zhou Enlai set out by truck from Yan'an's south gate for Xi'an. He rode in the cab with the driver. In the bed of the truck were Zhang Yunyi, deputy chief of staff of the Red Army's Front Headquarters; the staff officer Kong Shiquan; the adjutant Chen Youcai; the cipher clerk Zeng Hongcai; the reporter Wu Tao; four bodyguards; and a squad of soldiers under Chen Guoqiao, deputy platoon leader of the Northwest Security Bureau's special-service detail. These twenty-five men were the traveling party of the vice-chairman of the Central Military Commission.

The great Laoshan, which lies athwart the road between Yan'an and Ganquan, has been ground for soldiering since ancient times. When Qing troops marched to crush a Muslim uprising, they were ambushed here and left a mass grave of a thousand men; when the Northeast Army attacked the Red 15th Army Corps, it too lost two whole regiments here. The soldiers on the truck were all on the alert. They crossed the ridge and started down, and entered the Nationalist-held county of Ganquan. They had just driven into a hollow shaped like a winnowing basket when gunfire broke out. Zhou Enlai ordered everyone off the truck to fan out and fire back. Chen Youcai deliberately drew the enemy's fire onto himself and was the first to fall; Chen Guoqiao led the soldiers on in resistance; and Zhou Enlai, Zhang Yunyi, Kong Shiquan, Wu Tao, and Cao Hongdu, five in all, got away on foot...

Yan'an was thrown into an uproar. Huang Lin, commander of the guard regiment, grabbed his pistol and rushed out the door. Liu Bocheng, the chief of staff, gathered up the horses of every central leader. Mao Zedong charged Huang Lin: "Never mind anything else. Bring the Vice-Chairman back, whatever it takes!" Zhou Xing, head of the Security Bureau, at once sent Cai Shunli to muster the cavalry; Yu Sang, a clerk in the Yan'an municipal police, rode out with a cavalry platoon; Zhou Xing also sent off a feather-dispatch ordering the independent regiment to reinforce; and Zhang Hanwu, secretary of the Yan'an city Party committee, called out the militia and set off. At the Red Army University the students could not sit through class. In the whole of the Long March the Vice-Chairman had never met such danger. Dong Biwu came out to speak and calm the crowd.

Outside Yan'an's south gate a throng of cadres and townspeople gathered of its own accord, and Mao Zedong, Zhang Wentian, Zhu De, and Li Fuchun stood craning their necks toward the horizon...

At the Laoshan site the bullet-riddled truck stood at the bottom of the valley, baggage and papers scattered around it. The dead Red Army soldiers still held their fighting postures; only four, gravely wounded, were not yet dead. The codes were still in the dead cipher clerk's pocket, and Zhou Enlai's wool blanket had been slashed several times.

Zhou Enlai and Huang Lin rode back into Yan'an, and outside the south gate the cheering broke like thunder.

But Zhou Xing could not escape the blame. As the man ultimately responsible for security in Yan'an, he had let the very head of the Party's security work walk into danger, and he took the sharpest criticism of his life.

The safety of Yan'an was the safety of the Central Committee. Yan'an had to be kept safe.

***

Yan'an was no place of lasting peace.

It had always sat on the frontier of war. Its old name was Yanzhou; to pray for safety, someone had deliberately set an *an*, "peace," after the *yan*, and made it Yan'an.

The Yan'an the Communists took over was, in truth, an unquiet place. The Nationalist authorities kept armed security squads garrisoned in the towns. In the countryside stood a great many earth-walled stockades, held by landlord militias. The village lodges of the Elder Brothers Society were of very mixed makeup. Local warlords stirred up bad blood between Hui and Han. Sabotage and murder were common everywhere. Nationalist secret-service organs ran their own outfits too, the "Red-Suppression Societies" and the "Communist-Extermination Volunteer Corps," hard at work; the Suide suppression branch handed out a pamphlet called *Honest Talk*, spreading tales of Communists turning coat in the sing-song of clapper ballads.

There was wavering in the Red Army's own ranks. Ma Zixiang, political commissar of the 5th Red Regiment in Jia County, was captured and turned. Gao Wenrui of the Red 81st Division led more than 90 men and 63 rifles over to the enemy. Jiao Hongpeng, deputy commander of the Huachi guard company, led more than twenty men in mutiny and attacked the county government. The worst was the Sanbian affair, where three guard-company commanders led their men in revolt and killed the special-committee secretary Xie Weijun. Xie Weijun had been one of the "Deng, Mao, Xie, and Gu" of the old Jiangxi Soviet, purged for being a "man of Mao's faction," and only after the Long March brought back into use.

Yan'an's one existing middle school was the Normal School, and it too was a prize both sides fought over. Xu Teli, the Border Region government's education chief, served as its principal himself; Liao Chengzhi and Zhu Guang taught politics, and Ding Ling taught literature. The students pressed one after another to be "progressive," and secret Party and Youth League branches took shape inside the school. But the Nationalists would not let the Communists hold it, and sent in a principal, Ma Zhuojiang, who was a Zhongtong agent. The central government also rotated the Border Region's principals and teachers off to Lushan for training, and some of the teachers came back talking reaction.

Society itself was a tangle: bandits, hoodlums, traitors, and agents all thrown in together. Soon after moving into Yan'an the police arrested several men, among them a merchant named Gao Laoba who preyed on the locals, a street tough named Bi Duanren, and a Henan knife-grinder carrying heroin. Shao Yan, a field intelligence officer of the police, worked undercover out of an inn, and Liang Ji drew up the indictment.

When the Communists took over Yan'an, the Northwest Security Bureau paid close attention to guarding against enemy sabotage. Chen Fusheng, chief of the investigation department, led the investigators Huang Chibo, Long Feihu, Su Yifan, Mu Guanglin, and Wang Huakai in energetic work; but they were all Jiangxi men who could not follow the local speech, so local cadres such as Zhao Cangbi and Zheng Zhuguo were brought in as well. The department found that the owner of a little cloth shop, Gao You, was a traitor: the Japanese had paid him to open the shop in Yan'an and gather intelligence.

There were also two curious monks whose movements centered on the Party Center, the Border Region government, and the security headquarters. Chen Fusheng picked a man from inside the organs, a Helianwan native of Luochuan they called Little Li, and sent him to work as a hand at the temple, where he laid hold of the proof. The two "monks," it emerged, were reconnaissance staff officers under He Shaonan, the Nationalist commissioner for the Suide district. The two men who ran a photo studio and kept in secret touch with them were also agents out of Xi'an. Following the vine to the melon, the department cracked two more cases of the pair's reach into the Red Army. Two more Juntong agents were working under cover in Yan'an's Catholic church, and the Border Security caught them too.

The Nationalist agents confined themselves to gathering intelligence; the direct threat to the Border Region's safety was still the bandits.

***

The great case of Zhou Enlai's brush with death at Laoshan set Yan'an arguing fiercely for a time.

The Border Security held that the Laoshan ambush was plainly a prepared enemy operation: Japanese agents, surely, or the Nationalists' pro-Japan faction, out to assassinate Zhou Enlai and wreck the Nationalist-Communist talks.

Wang Ming turned his eye inward: Trotskyites, he suspected, plotting to kill the Communist Zhou Enlai for his labors toward Nationalist-Communist cooperation.

Others reasoned that the attackers had carried off only valuables and weapons, not the documents and codes, so it was likely plain banditry.

And still others asked: how could a mere band of bandits lay so careful an ambush?

Yan'an, where the Laoshan affair had only just happened on April 25, 1937, passed quickly from shock to composure. Early the next morning Zhou Enlai boarded the little two-seater plane Gu Zhutong had sent to fetch him and flew alone to Xi'an. Whatever the guesses about the previous day's ambush, whatever the dangers still lying ahead, he set off in haste. For a Communist leader, brushing past death was an everyday affair.

The CCP Center never did rule that the Laoshan affair had a political motive behind it. But one thing it proved past doubt: Yan'an's security was in a grave state.

Guard work at the CCP Center's seat had been quite weak. Mao Zedong's first home in Yan'an was a stone cave at the foot of Fenghuangshan; after Japanese planes destroyed it, he moved to Yangjialing, a small hill village three kilometers beyond the city's north gate. Only eight or nine families had lived there; on a level shelf partway up the slope stood cave dwellings faced in gray brick, and Mao lived in the middle one, with Zhou Enlai to his left and Zhu De and Liu Shaoqi to his right. The Northwest Security Bureau handled guard work for the central organs; the leaders' bodyguards were drawn from the bureau's special-service detail, and the guard detachment was furnished by the security regiment. Yangjialing had no strict protection to speak of: the grown-ups and children of the village wandered where they liked, and no one stopped a stranger coming in. After the Laoshan affair the CCP Center resolved to form a Central Guard Battalion, drawing four full-strength companies from the 1st Red Army Corps and posting them to Fenghuangshan to guard the central organs.

And Laoshan, where the ambush had happened, became all the more a focus of Border Security's investigation.

In early 1937, as the Central Committee moved into Yan'an, the Border Region Party committee organized security work and set out to clear the bandits operating in the south of the county. A temporary Bandit-Suppression Headquarters was set up under Wu Tailiang, chief of the Yan'an county Security Bureau, as commander, with Jia Tengyun as commissar. A survey of the ground soon made the picture clear: this band ran on two forces, one open, one hidden. The open one was a gang of habitual bandits under a chief named Li Qingwu; the hidden one was a militia of some eighty men under Ji Yanshou, a local despot of Linzhen; and the hand behind them both was the Nationalist Ganquan county party office, which was tied in turn to the provincial party office in Xi'an. With the Nationalists and Communists then negotiating their alliance, the Border committee told the suppression headquarters to try talks first. The talks dragged without result. The band shifted west, working the road from Ganquan toward Fu County, swelling to more than two hundred men, and on April 25 the Laoshan ambush took place.

The moment it happened, the Border committee decided on a large-scale sweep, again naming Wu Tailiang to command it.

Zhou Xing directed the work himself. Xie Ziqun, chief of investigation for the Border Security, detached a party of investigators to slip secretly into the bandits' country. Zhang Peimou and Li Shubiao disguised themselves as peddlers and went to Jinpenwan, where they quietly seized the "local boss" Li Fengshan and got out of him under questioning that the Laoshan affair was indeed the work of Li Qingwu's band. The suppression troops swung around from the south, several counties closing in together, and the bandit chiefs fell into the net one after another.

Interrogation laid bare what had happened at Laoshan. Li Qingwu and Ji Yanshou had been in league, robbing far and wide for some time. Before Zhou Enlai set out, three trucks had stood two days outside the south gate awaiting orders, and they were spotted by Feng Changdou, a watcher Li Qingwu had planted at Yan'an's south gate. Li Qingwu, guessing the trucks carried valuables, took his men to lie in wait on Laoshan. Zhou Enlai's adjutant Chen Youcai was in a Western suit and a felt hat, and when they went through his pockets they found Zhou Enlai's calling cards. Fearing the Red Army's pursuit, Li Qingwu fled to Shanxi, and not until winter did he creep back to his home village, where the waiting traitor-elimination squad seized him on the spot.

At market towns like Ganguyi, Ershilipu, and Linzhen the Border government shot the bandit chiefs before the crowds: Li Qingwu, Qi Jinquan, Ji Yanshou, Han Lao'er, Jiang Haifu, Hou Zhenguo, and the rest. The public trial of the watcher Feng Changdou drew the greatest stir of all. Ershilipu was a sea of people, and toward this wretch who had tried to murder the Vice-Chairman the crowd itched to crush him with stones and hack him with mattocks.

One would think the case had long since been closed; yet some of the inside story had never come out. The bandit chief Li Qingwu had been taken into the Red Army in 1935, then in 1936 had killed a county soviet chairman and gone over to the Nationalists, and later taken to the hills as a bandit again. Li Qingwu was caught, but some of his band slipped the sweep and went over to the Nationalist 22nd Army; their leader, nicknamed "Cavalry Zhang," took part in the Yulin uprising during the War of Liberation. With a tangled history like this, anyone who did not know the inside of it could easily mistake the case for one with a background of struggle within the Party.

In fact the case not only had nothing to do with struggle within the Party; neither could it be pinned on the Nationalists. Li Qingwu and Ji Yanshou did have Nationalist connections, true, but the Laoshan ambush was for plunder, and no plot to kill the Communist leaders.

Some cases grow more tangled the deeper you dig; some turn out, in truth, to be very simple. But either way, a security service must keep its guard high. The Nationalists had spent ten years killing Communists; would they lightly stop now?

### 2. "Zhongtong" and "Juntong" Take Formal Shape

With the whole nation at war and the Nationalists and Communists cooperating once more, the naive supposed that the Nationalist secret services, built mainly to fight the Communists, would soon be out of work.

Even the spy chief Xu Enzeng was at something of a loss. Was the Party still to be worked against or not? His memoir records: "As the Nationalists and Communists moved toward accommodation, my business gradually came to a halt. When the Japanese invaded in July of the twenty-sixth year of the Republic [1937], and the two parties joined hands again, I gave up the task of fighting the Communists altogether and turned to coordinating operations against Japan. But this change was brief. Before long, as friction between the parties grew, I took up the burden once more. Only the situation had changed, and the struggle against the Communists from then on, in form, in content, and in result, never matched the earlier stage."

At the start of 1928 the Nationalists set up a dedicated intelligence body, the "Party Affairs Investigation Section," inside their Central Organization Department. By then the army had its own "Second Department of the General Staff," handling military espionage and wireless interception; and in 1931 the secret inner core of the "China Renaissance Society," known also as the "Blue Shirts," the "Vigorous Action Society," established a "Special Services Department" under it for intelligence and assassination. In 1932 the Party Affairs Investigation Section grew into a "Special Operations Head Office," and in 1935 was reorganized into the "Party Affairs Investigation Bureau." In April 1937, Xu Enzeng's Party Affairs Investigation Bureau and Dai Li's Vigorous Action Society were merged into the "Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the National Government Military Council," with Chen Lifu as director; the old investigation bureau became the First Section, still under Xu Enzeng, and the old Vigorous Action Society the Second Section, still under Dai Li.

Who could have foreseen it: in April 1938 Chiang Kai-shek raised the whole apparatus in rank.

The First Section of the old "Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the National Government Military Council" was enlarged into the "Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the Nationalist Party Central Executive Committee," with Zhu Jiahua as director and Xu Enzeng as deputy. Placed within the central party system, it was known for short as "Zhongtong."

The Second Section of the old bureau was enlarged into the "Bureau of Investigation and Statistics of the National Government Military Council," its first director Chen Lifu, holding the post concurrently, with Dai Li as deputy. Placed within the army system, it was known for short as "Juntong."

And so Zhongtong and Juntong, the two secret-service organizations whose rampage across China would make every man look askance, stepped onto the stage.

Chairman Chiang led the two bureaus directly, and charged them himself: "Japan is no more than a skin rash; the Communist Party is the disease in our vitals!"

The Nationalists were pressing ahead full tilt. What was the CCP to do?

The Shanghai Special Branch had been destroyed in November 1935. The State Political Security Bureau, after going on the Long March with the army in January 1934, had been renamed the "Northwest Security Bureau." Of the central organs' remaining dedicated bodies, the Fourth Section of the Organization Department had very few people, and the "Security Office" was set up as a local Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia body; none of them could carry the work of directing the whole country. A state cannot go a day without soldiers, nor a party a day without intelligence. Such a state of things could not last.

In December 1937 the CCP formed a "Central Special Work Committee," known outwardly as the "Enemy Area Work Committee," to oversee all the Party's intelligence and security work, with Zhou Enlai as chairman and Zhang Hao as deputy. Soon Zhou Enlai left for the Nationalist areas to negotiate, and Kang Sheng took over as chairman, with Pan Hannian as deputy. A Politburo meeting in the spring of 1938 decided that the Central Special Committee would comprise a War-Zone Department under Du Liqing (Xu Jianguo); a City Department under Pan Hannian, with Wang Jinxiang as deputy; and a Cadre Department under Chen Gang. In 1938 a "Central Security Department" was also formed, under Du Liqing.

The whole country was at war with Japan, the parties were cooperating, and the two parties that commanded armies set about merging their forces. Yet neither side's intelligence and security organs ever let down their guard against the other; each, in fact, was quietly enlarging its own.

The "gun" might still be turned against a common enemy; but the "knife" each side meant to keep gripped tight in its own hand.

***

The CCP Center had reason to strengthen its security. In the first Nationalist-Communist alliance it had trusted its ally naively and paid a heavy price. In this second alliance the Communists had, on the surface, won legal standing and a safer place to live; in reality their position still held hidden danger. Chiang Kai-shek honored the pledge to resist Japan that he had made during the Xi'an Incident, but at the same time he held Zhang Xueliang under detention, forced Yang Hucheng abroad, and moved both men's forces out of Shaanxi. The three-in-one alignment that had been so hard to build in the northwest now rested on the Red Army alone.

With the whole nation united against Japan, the Nationalists could not well start a civil war, and so shifted their strategy against the Communists from "encirclement and suppression" to "blockade."

The chief task of the Nationalists' Zhongtong bureau was to deal with the Communists. Xu Enzeng wrote: "Before the war, every one of the Communist Party's highest secrets was perfectly clear to us; even the secret orders Moscow sent it often reached our hands. In our files we once kept the complete minutes of every Communist Central Committee down the years, all of it seized from inside the Communist central organs..." And Xu Enzeng judged: "So to obtain the Communists' highest secrets, the only way was the old way: to find some means of penetrating their 'central organ.' But the Communist Center then lay far off in Yan'an, and was hard to reach..."

Chiang Kai-shek specially named a trusted general, Jiang Dingwen, as director of the Military Council's Xi'an field headquarters. The field headquarters set up a "Joint Party-Government-Army-Secret-Service Conference," secretly drew up a plan to blockade the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, and carried it out on Chiang's approval and secret order. The plan reached into "politics, military affairs, economy, culture and education, propaganda, mass movements, and secret-service work," and was worked out no less thoroughly than the war against Japan. Its main points came to three: to throw up clusters of blockhouses and form a military line ringing the region on every side; to plant checkpoints choking the roads in and out; and to send in agents for secret penetration.

The heavy work of the military blockade fell to Hu Zongnan. A Whampoa protégé of Chiang's, Hu Zongnan had commanded in turn the First Division and the First Army, the units with which Chiang had made his start, and ranked as a general of the innermost clique. When the war broke out, Hu Zongnan led his troops in the Shanghai and Henan campaigns, then was ordered back to Shaanxi to rest and refit. He had his master's heart: "Even if the war of resistance is lost and we perish before Japan, the nation may yet be restored. But if the war lets the Communists' strength grow until it shakes the foundations of the state, then there will be no day of recovery, ever. For the sake of lasting peace, we must tighten the blockade of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, weaken and squeeze the Communist forces, so that at a favorable moment we may destroy them at a stroke."

The Nationalists poured troops into the northwest, and gave Hu Zongnan a foot in two war zones at once: deputy commander of the First War Zone, covering Shaanxi, Henan, and Shanxi, and acting commander of the Eighth War Zone, covering Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, and Suiyuan. Between them he directed 25 armies, some 500,000 men. The "King of the Northwest," as they called him, held the Tongguan line with a small force to keep the Japanese out of Shaanxi, and turned the bulk of his strength against the Communists. Along the Border Region's frontier the Nationalist troops built blockhouses, joining the Great Wall in the north and the Yellow River in the east, curving from west to south to close a ring; five such lines made a blockaded belt a hundred li deep. On the very first line, the mere eighty li from Luochuan to Zhongbu County (today's Huangling County), due south of Yan'an, there stood 518 blockhouses.

The Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region was ringed on all four sides. To the northwest, in Ningxia, stood the forces of Ma Hongkui and Ma Hongbin. To the north, in southern Suiyuan, northern Shaanxi, and northwestern Shanxi, were the forces of Deng Baoshan, Gao Shuangcheng, and Ma Zhanshan. East, south, and west lay the main body of Hu Zongnan's troops, running west from the Yellow River's west bank, along Qiulin, Luochuan, and Yao County in Shaanxi and Ning County and Xifeng in Gansu, sweeping across the region's southern face, then turning north into Guyuan in Ningxia to outflank its western face and link up with the Ma family armies of the northwest.

By now the Red Army had been reorganized into the Eighth Route Army, and all three of its divisions had marched to the anti-Japanese front in Shanxi, leaving only a small rear guard in the Border Region. For all that the Nationalists and Communists were nominally allied armies, the gap in strength between them was too wide; the balance was lost, and the side grown lopsidedly strong can seldom curb its appetite for attack. Seen this way, Yan'an remained in danger throughout.

***

To the east of Yan'an lay the moat of the Yellow River; to the north, the barrier of the desert. Its roads to the outside world were few.

The eight hundred li of mountain road south to Xi'an were Yan'an's shortest way to the provincial capital and on to the heart of the country; most people, goods, and merchants traveling openly went by the southern road.

To the northeast, by way of Suide, Yulin, and Shenmu, one could reach Inner Mongolia; to the northwest, by way of Bao'an and Dingbian, Ningxia and Inner Mongolia. Both routes ran to the frontier, and both could reach the Soviet Union.

A southwestern route was little known. The "Gan" of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region is Gansu's Qingyang district, southwest of Yan'an; far from the Gansu capital of Lanzhou and near the Shaanxi capital of Xi'an, it had a loose Nationalist grip at the local level, and so became a secret corridor for the Communists.

Three routes brought life to out-of-the-way Yan'an.

In the past, the CCP's central organs had sat first in Shanghai and then in Jiangxi, and every contact with the Comintern went by sea, which meant passing through ports the Nationalists closely controlled. Now the Center's new seat at Yan'an lay to the north, close to the Sino-Soviet border, and could reach the distant powers by land. Inside the Party they called the far-off Soviet Union and the Comintern the "far country." Most of the way there ran through territory held by the Northwest Army and the Shanxi Army, beyond the reach of Chiang Kai-shek's Central Army. At first the traffic moved secretly on foot; after the alliance there were Soviet motorcars, and even an air route was opened. A journey from Moscow that once took half a year could now be counted in days. Zhou Enlai, Ren Bishi, and other Communist leaders all traveled it by plane.

The CCP Center also set about restoring contact with the underground organizations across the country. After the Long March had pulled it out of the Central Soviet, its links with the underground in the White areas had all been severed; local bodies and Party members fought on their own, unable to pull together as one Party. As soon as it settled in Yan'an, the Center had Wu Defeng rebuild the Liaison Bureau, known outwardly as the "Rural Work Committee," to open channels to the outside. Yan'an lay far from the center of Nationalist rule, and the warlords great and small nearby each guarded his own patch; some put no great effort into the blockade, and there were gaps to slip through everywhere. In the spring of 1936, Liu Shaoqi made his secret way from northern Shaanxi to Tianjin and restored the Center's leadership over the Northern Bureau. Feng Xuefeng slipped from northern Shaanxi to Shanghai and restored the Center's link with the Shanghai Central Bureau. So the Party Center regained its contact with the underground in both north and south. Under its guidance, the White-area organizations everywhere quickly corrected their old "Left" line, worked hard to rebuild, and pushed their work forward. Communist activity reached beyond its corner of northern Shaanxi, and a new pattern took shape, in which the legal base area and the secret work in the White areas moved in concert.

Yan'an had become the nerve center from which the Communists worked the whole country and reached out to the wider world, and it gave Chiang Kai-shek a headache. Never for a moment had Chiang been naive enough to let the Communists grow strong; yet to blockade Yan'an during the alliance was a thing without a good name or a good pretext. For the Nationalists themselves, the roads from Xi'an to Suide, Yulin, and Dingbian also passed through Communist-held Yan'an. And so, even as they hesitated, merchants, officials, troops, and common folk kept up an unbroken stream along the roads, and Yan'an had become the traffic hub of northern Shaanxi.

The southern road out of Yan'an was always the focus of the Nationalist blockade. In the spring of 1937, the Eighth Company of the Second Transport Regiment was ordered to carry "assistance grain" to Yan'an. No one had guessed that the man leading this first Nationalist convoy to bring grain and pay to Yan'an, Cao Yi, deputy commander of the Second Battalion, was a Communist who had been out of touch with the organization for seven years. Reaching the Eighth Route Army office at Qixianzhuang in Xi'an, Cao Yi reported at once to Ye Jianying; and in Yan'an, Commander-in-Chief Zhu De received him in person and took him back as a special Party member. From then on Cao Yi carried more than grain and pay: he brought droves of "progressive" young people to Yan'an, and more than once ferried senior Communist cadres.

Yan'an's eastern road ran closest to the Communist-led base areas: Shanxi-Suiyuan, Shanxi-Hebei-Shandong-Henan, Shanxi-Chahar-Hebei, and Shandong. But the Nationalist troops held the Yellow River in the east in an iron grip, choking the throat of that traffic. Northeast of Yan'an lay Yulin, whose garrison commander, Deng Baoshan, was on good terms with the Communists. When the Yulin garrison's supplies passed through Yan'an, the Border government let them by tax-free; when Yan'an went out to buy goods, Yulin let it through as well. Deng Baoshan's troops, drawing military supplies in Xi'an, would bring back medicines, cloth, and other embargoed goods for Yan'an. Deng also gave up two Yellow River crossings, among them Jundu between Suide in northern Shaanxi and Lin County in Shanxi, throwing open the passage among the Communist base areas. Peng Dehuai, Liu Bocheng, Chen Yi, and other Eighth Route Army commanders all traveled this road. Deng Baoshan's help opened an important eastern route for the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region. It was by this road that Mao Zedong, in the War of Liberation, left northern Shaanxi to march into north China and go on to take up his seat in Beijing.

The western route ran through Longdong in Gansu, where the Nationalist watch was relatively slack, and this made the western road the main artery of the Communists' secret traffic. When the Red Army's Western Route Army was destroyed, its commander-in-chief Xu Xiangqian escaped disguised as a merchant on a donkey, and it was by this road that he came into Yan'an. Liu Shaoqi, traveling under the alias Hu Fu to the base areas behind enemy lines in central and north China, did not take the nearer northwestern or southern roads but went around by this longer southwestern one.

In 1940 a convoy of thirty big trucks rolled grandly into Yan'an. The troops aboard were called the "Recruit Battalion," but every one of them was a veteran of a hundred battles.

At the end of the Long March, after the First, Second, and Fourth Front Armies had joined forces, Xu Xiangqian and Chen Changhao led the Western Route Army into the Hexi Corridor, where it ran into the encircling cavalry of the Ma family armies. Cut off and unrelieved, the Western Route Army was wiped out; a bare four hundred survivors, trapped in the snow-choked Qilian Mountains, marched on foot toward Xinjiang under Li Xiannian. The Xinjiang warlord Sheng Shicai was then on friendly terms with the neighboring Soviet Union and also received the Communists' representative. The CCP Center strained every nerve to save the Western Route Army, telling Chen Yun, who was in Xinjiang, to take charge of receiving its remnants. Chen Yun drove to Xingxingxia on the Gansu-Xinjiang border, brought these four hundred-odd officers and men into Xinjiang, and trained them together under the name "Recruit Battalion." There the Red Army men studied modern military skills: artillery, armor, motor vehicles, wireless, aviation. After many turns, 329 of them rode thirty army trucks the long way home. This first crop of modern military technicians the Communists produced would later become commanders in every technical branch of the People's Liberation Army.

***

The Central Committee's arrival made Yan'an, a border town of the northwest, famous across the country. The outbreak of the war with Japan gave this cold and barren place a magnetic charm.

When the war came, hot-blooded young people all over the country rushed to the front against Japan. But Chiang Kai-shek's National Government had withdrawn to the safety of the deep rear at Chongqing, far from the fighting. The Communists, meanwhile, held the banner of resistance high in the north; in Yan'an the leaders were democratic, the cadres clean, the people awake. Look around the whole of China: the greater part of the land had fallen into Japanese hands, the lesser part was the roost of warlords and bureaucrats, and only Yan'an was pure ground for the nation.

And so educated youth everywhere flocked to Yan'an. Between May and August of 1938, the young people sent there through the Eighth Route Army's Xi'an office alone came to 2,288. The fourth session of the Resistance University, opened on April 16, 1938, took in more than a hundred new students a day, and by the year's end graduation had over four thousand educated young people. The fifth session, opening on January 28, 1939, doubled that number again.

Yan'an's beacon effect galled Chiang Kai-shek badly. The Nationalists set up measure after measure to choke the roads in and out.

The Nationalists planted checkpoints at the road-mouths on every highway around Yan'an, inspecting all who came and went and all their goods. The goods forbidden to enter the region were listed: weapons and ammunition, transport and communications equipment, military supplies, grain and oil, cotton yarn and cloth, medicines and sanitary goods, and medical instruments. Any contraband found was confiscated, the vehicles impounded, the people locked up and punished. Crueler still, they detained the young people making for Yan'an and sent them off for forced training, some to the War Cadre Corps in Xi'an, others to the Northwest Cadre Training Corps in Lanzhou.

Hu Zongnan understood well how much the contest for the young mattered. He invited Xiao Zuolin, secretary of the Renaissance Society's Henan branch, to build a Fourth War Cadre Training Corps at Xi'an, which claimed to train high-school graduates but in fact intercepted the young on their way to Yan'an. The special detachment of this corps was later expanded into a Northwest Youth Labor Camp, with sites at Xianyang, Luoyang, and Lanzhou, holding more than eight hundred young people. Beyond Hu Zongnan's military system, Nationalist Zhongtong agents also detained the young at the key roads; between 1939 and 1943 the detentions reported came to more than 2,100.

Under the cover of open inspection, the Nationalist secret services hid illegal secret-service work. The Jiaokou checkpoint held a man out of Yan'an, suspected of being the CCP secretary of Luochuan County, and put him to cruel torture; when he would not talk, Zhongtong buried him alive in secret.

The Communists, for their part, raised a legal banner and kept sending people and supplies into Yan'an. Trade too had its pull. The tighter the Nationalist blockade, the greater the profit in commercial speculation, until even men within the Nationalist ranks were quietly selling goods to Yan'an; so long as there was money in it, they would sell munitions too.

This was a peculiar time. The Nationalists and Communists both cooperated and struggled; the Nationalists meant to blockade Yan'an, yet could not choke it dead. And so officials turned bandit and robbed the roads openly, while "Red bandits" turned Eighth Route men and ran the barriers openly, and along the Yan'an roads there played out scene after scene worthy of the opera *At the Crossroads*.

This was a turn no ordinary person could have foreseen: the new climate of "reform and opening" only drove the hidden contest higher.

### 3. The Defection Incident!

Yan'an's troubles were not only the outer blockade; there were rifts within.

In the spring and summer of 1937, the Anti-Japanese Military and Political University opened a criticism of Zhang Guotao that touched the students of the old Fourth Front Army. Xu Shiyou could not stomach it, and stirred a number of students to plot an escape. A "net member" planted among the students reported it, and the Northwest Security Bureau moved at once, arresting the would-be runaways. That night the bureau's men roamed the hills with lanterns hunting for people, tense as could be.

When Mao Zedong heard, he went to talk with Xu Shiyou himself. Xu Shiyou, under arrest, was still unbowed, and swore that if he had a gun he would shoot old Mao. Before the talk, Mao gave Luo Ruiqing precise orders: no guards were to stand by, and Xu Shiyou was to be handed a gun and issued cartridges. The security men had no choice but to obey, though behind Mao's back they kept watch behind the cave. Mao trusted that a plain peasant general would not turn on the Party, and said to Xu Shiyou warmly that the error was Zhang Guotao's alone and could not be laid on the comrades of the old Fourth Front Army; besides, the students who had tried to slip away were not going over to the enemy but meant to fight guerrilla in southern Shaanxi and go on fighting the Nationalists. Xu Shiyou, who had come in with a gun on him, threw himself to the ground on the spot and swore to be loyal to Marshal Mao from that day. Mao decided to release Xu Shiyou and the others and let them go on studying at the Resistance University. They kept their trust; Xu Shiyou was even sent to Shandong to hold a command of his own.

The tomb of the Yellow Emperor, Xuanyuan, lies in Zhongbu County between Yan'an and Xi'an, and every year at the Qingming festival the government named a high official to hold sacrifices at the mausoleum. The rite of 1938 carried all the more meaning of national unity because of the war, and Mao Zedong wrote the funerary address himself. Just then Zhang Guotao, who had always dragged his feet, turned suddenly eager and asked to go to the mausoleum as the representative of the CCP Center and the Border government. Ever since he had lost his bid for the Party's supreme leadership, Zhang Guotao had been sunk in gloom. The news of the purges that Wang Ming brought back from the Soviet Union made him fear the more for his own safety. Weighing this way and that, he resolved to leave the Border Region and look for another road, and the pilgrimage to the Yellow Emperor's tomb offered exactly a plausible reason to go. Zhang Guotao was then acting chairman of the Border Region government.

Once at Zhongbu County, Zhang Guotao made overtures to Jiang Dingwen, director of the Nationalists' Tianshui field headquarters, and to Gu Zhengding, its political department chief; and Jiang Dingwen, sounding him out, invited him to Xi'an. Zhang Guotao seized the chance on the instant, got into Jiang Dingwen's car, and was gone.

To travel into Nationalist territory without leave, and to shake off the escort the organization had assigned him: Zhang Guotao's sudden move told Yan'an one thing, that this was no simple breach of discipline but something with a more tangled political design behind it.

Leaving Zhongbu County, Zhang Guotao tried to slip out of the sight of the Communists' security organs. In Xi'an he put up at the Xijing Guesthouse and pointedly kept clear of the Eighth Route Army office. Not until April 7, once things were arranged with the Nationalists, did he agree to meet Lin Boqu on the railway platform. With Nationalist agents at his side to see him off, bound for Nationalist-held Wuhan, the train only minutes from leaving, Zhang Guotao supposed the Communists' security men had run out of moves.

But Zhang Guotao, so sure his plan was airtight, had underrated what the Communists' security men could do.

Zhou Enlai, then in Wuhan, received a telegram from Yan'an and sent Li Kenong specially to "collect" Zhang Guotao and bring him to the office. Li Kenong took three men, Tong Xiaopeng, Qiu Nanzhang, and Wu Kejian, and waited three days at the Hankou railway station while eighteen trains came and went. Not until the evening of April 11 did Qiu Nanzhang finally spot Zhang Guotao, in a Western suit and polished shoes, on the train in from Xi'an.

Li Kenong and Wu Kejian boarded at once. Speaking for Wang Ming and Zhou Enlai, they asked Zhang Guotao to come to the Yangtze Bureau, and half coaxing, half propping him up, they forced him into the small car that stood waiting. Only now did the Nationalist agents, waiting for Zhang Guotao to step down, see what had happened, and they moved to stop the car; but Qiu Nanzhang and Wu Kejian, in Nationalist army uniforms, whipped out their pistols, the agents hesitated a moment, and the car had already sped away.

Zhou Enlai argued with him again and again, but Zhang Guotao still would not go back to Yan'an; instead he asked to report to Chiang Kai-shek. On the morning of the 16th, with Zhou Enlai accompanying him, Zhang Guotao met Chiang and told him to his face: "This younger brother has been out in the world, and muddled for a good while." Zhou Enlai rebuked him then and there: "You may be muddled. I'm not."

Encouraged by Chiang, Zhang Guotao said in the afternoon that he wanted to go out to be fitted for glasses and see a dentist, and wandered the streets, but he could never shake off his companion Wu Kejian. He came to the ferry landing and, in the instant the boat was drawing in its gangplank, sprang aboard; yet no sooner had he found a seat than Wu Kejian was at his side again. This unremarkable little man Wu Kejian had once been secretary of the Special Branch and had worked abroad for years; recalled home when the war broke out, he was one of the ablest hands under Zhou Enlai.

On the morning of the 17th, Wang Ming, Zhou Enlai, Bo Gu, and Li Kenong went together to the Pacific Hotel where Zhang Guotao was staying and laid three choices before him: one, correct his error and return to Party work; two, take leave from the Party and rest for a time; three, declare of his own accord that he was quitting, whereupon the Party would announce his expulsion. Zhang Guotao would not agree to return to Party work, and said he would choose between the last two. After Zhou Enlai and the others left, he telephoned Dai Li at once, and that evening armed agents sent a car and took him away.

The day after Zhang Guotao's formal defection, the CCP Center announced his expulsion from the Party. On May 7, Mao Zedong spoke at the North Shaanxi Academy, dealing squarely with the Zhang Guotao affair. He looked back over Zhang Guotao's opportunism through the years and said the man had long since deserted his post in politics. The defection carried off no one; even his own bodyguard, Zhang Hai, hearing that Zhang Guotao had betrayed the Party, would not go with him.

No body of troops is without its deserters and turncoats. The Red Army lost the most deserters not during the hardship of the Long March, when to fall behind was to be cut down by the militia. It was after the Long March reached Gansu that the ranks thinned badly, when the people of Hadapu were looking to take in sons-in-law.

With the whole nation united against Japan and the two parties allied once more, some Communists were bewildered. In the Red 25th Army two regimental commanders resigned and went home: with no White army to fight, they would not soldier at all. The army commander Xu Haidong was so angry he coughed blood, for both were seasoned commanders of his best regiments.

The chief of staff of the Eighth Route Army's 115th Division went to Wuhan to draw the troops' pay, left 30,000 with the organization, and vanished with 20,000 himself. This Zhou Kun had brought up the rear with the Red 9th Army Corps on the Long March, and through the bitterest fighting had never once left the ranks...

The harsh reality reminded the Communists that beneath cooperation there was still struggle.

***

On the Nationalist side, too, they were adapting to the new situation and casting about for new devices.

A blockade was not enough; penetration was added. The "Joint Party-Government-Army-Secret-Service Conference" set up district offices, one at Yusui northeast of Yan'an, one at Luochuan to the south, one at Bin County to the southwest, to direct joint action among the counties of each district. The commissioners were all anti-Communist specialists: Fu Yun at Yulin, He Shaonan at Suide, Liang Ganqiao at Yao County, Jiang Jianren at Dali, Zhao Yuxin at Binzhou, Zhong Jingcheng in Longdong, Ma Jizhou at Pingliang. Through Guo Zijun, secretary-general of the Shaanxi provincial party office, and Peng Zhaoxian, head of the province's civil affairs department, Zhongtong replaced every Nationalist party secretary and every county magistrate in northern Shaanxi with its own key men.

The Nationalists' anti-Communist policy was growing more sophisticated, too.

In 1927 it had been "better to kill a thousand by mistake than let one slip through." By the end of 1929 they promulgated a "Communist Surrender Law" and set up a system of "repentance houses," meant expressly to soften up arrested Communists, and won over no few turncoats. When Gu Shunzhang, head of the Communists' Special Branch, was seized and turned in 1931, it helped the Nationalists crack one Communist underground body after another. Yet for all the sweetness of using Communist turncoats, Xu Enzeng's attitude was still "use his talent, but do not trust his word." Inside the Nationalist secret services these turncoats were looked down on all the same.

When the war broke out, the special needs of anti-Communist work made these turncoats sought-after in the secret services. Chen Qingzhai (Hu Dahai), Du Heng, Chen Jianzhong, Hu Hongtao, Chen Wenzhao, Bu Daoming, Zhou Guangya, and others rose one after another to section-chief and bureau-chief posts and higher at Zhongtong headquarters. Zhongtong also sent a batch of these turncoat-agents to the counties bordering the Border Region to fight the Communists face to face. Every director in turn of Zhongtong's Shaanxi Provincial Investigation and Statistics Office was a turncoat; around the Border Region they included Liang Ganqiao, the Yao County commissioner, Jing Shaoshan and Yang Bo at Suide, Yang Yufeng at Jia County, and Ma Ruisheng at Mizhi. Having scrabbled their way at last into office, these turncoat-agents threw themselves into anti-Communist work with a peculiar relish, and had a peculiar gift for making new turncoats besides.

The Communists hated turncoats above all; one of the main tasks of the old Red Squad had been to root them out. But now the two parties were allied, the turncoats had become officials of the friendly party, and you even had to shake their hands.

### 4. The CCP Central Social Affairs Department

The "gun" might still be turned against a common enemy; but the "knife" each side meant to keep gripped tight in its own hand.

On February 18, 1939, the Central Secretariat issued its "Decision on Establishing a Social Affairs Department." The Central Social Affairs Department was to direct the security and intelligence work of every base area and enemy area of the whole Party, with Kang Sheng as its head. Its structure was lean: a First Department, for investigation, under Xu Jianguo; a Second Department, for intelligence, under Pan Hannian, Kong Yuan, and Zeng Xisheng; and an office and a secretariat. On the Center's decision, Social Affairs Departments were set up one after another across the country, among them that of the CCP Northwest Bureau (the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office), that of the Shanxi-Chahar-Hebei sub-bureau of the Northern Bureau, that of the northwestern Shanxi district Party committee, and the intelligence networks of Pan Hannian and Wang Chaobei.

The Department's tasks were these: "One, to struggle systematically against traitors and enemy spies, to prevent them from working their way into the Party, and to safeguard the carrying out of the Party's political and military tasks and the consolidation of its organization. Two, to send comrades and sympathizers, according to plan, using every opportunity and every possibility, into the enemy's ranks, and to make use of every usable person among the enemy, so that by strengthening our work inside the enemy we protect ourselves. Three, to collect concrete materials and facts on the activities of enemy spies, traitors, and agents, and by them to educate comrades and raise their vigilance. Four, to manage the work of the confidential departments and ensure that secrecy is kept. Five, regularly to select and train cadres fit for this kind of work."

In the past the Communists' intelligence and security apparatus had shifted about a great deal, its work scattered; between intelligence and security, between the Soviet areas and the White areas, between the Center and the localities, no whole and unified system had ever taken shape. Now the founding of Social Affairs Departments at every level, central and local, set in order the relations among the many organs, aspects, and tiers of the Communists' intelligence and security work, and brought it to a systematic, unified, and efficient footing.

Curiously, the name "Social Affairs Department" was not a Communist invention: the National Government's own establishment already held a "Social Affairs Department," with functions close to those of a civil-administration office. For the Communists to give the name "Social Affairs" to an organ in charge of intelligence and security was itself a kind of cover. Even so, the Central Social Affairs Department was not made public; within the circle it was called for short "Zhongshebu," and kept strictly secret.

Kang Sheng was its head from the day it was founded, and ran it for eight years altogether. To speak of the Communists' intelligence and security work, one cannot avoid speaking of this Kang Sheng.

Within the Party, Kang Sheng was addressed with respect as "Elder Kang." Yet consider: in the Yan'an years the only men who could be called "Elder" were the four aged above sixty, Dong Biwu, Lin Boqu, Xie Juezai, and Xu Teli. How was it that Kang Sheng, younger than they, could be called "Elder" too?

The Cultural Revolution showed people the measure of this "Elder Kang." A central leader, he could point at Zhao Jianmin, the Yunnan Party secretary, and shout: "One look at you tells me you're an agent!" His character, too, was contemptible: on his deathbed he denounced Jiang Qing as a turncoat, but where had he been all along? When the Cultural Revolution ended, Kang Sheng was expelled from the Party. People saw that when Jiang Qing went after the old cadres, most of her ammunition had come from Kang Sheng. Only then did they grasp how many he had persecuted in the Yan'an cadre screening and the Rescue Campaign.

Kang Sheng ran intelligence and security work for years; how had he grown so "Left"? Those in the know would say: "This Kang Sheng is a riddle."

Kang Sheng came of a wealthy family in Jiao County, Shandong. He studied in a private school, attended the Lixian Middle School, a mission school in Qingdao, and in 1924 entered Shanghai University. That university, run jointly by the Nationalists and Communists, had Deng Zhongxia and Qu Qiubai among its teachers and trained a great many Communist cadres. In 1925 Kang Sheng joined the Party at Shanghai University, took part in the three armed uprisings of the Shanghai workers under Zhou Enlai, and served as head of the Jiangsu provincial organization department. In 1927 he married a fellow student from Shanghai University, Cao Yi'ou. In April 1931, after Gu Shunzhang, head of the Central Special Branch, was seized and turned, the Center hastily reorganized the Special Branch: the Politburo member Chen Yun took over its leadership, and Zhao Rong, secretary-general of the Central Organization Department, became its second-in-command. Chen Yun held the general-affairs work of the First Section concurrently, the former Jiangsu propaganda chief Pan Hannian ran the intelligence work of the Second Section, and Zhao Rong also oversaw the Red Squad of the Third Section. In 1932, when Chen Yun was transferred to be Party secretary of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, Zhao Rong took over the whole of the Special Branch. This Zhao Rong was Kang Sheng. Kang Sheng was born Zhang Wang and had used several names, Zhang Zongke, Zhang Yuxian, Zhang Yun. So Zhao Rong became one of the Communists' intelligence and security specialists, with the code name "the Boss" inside the system. Before long he was moved to head the provisional Central Organization Department, and the Special Branch passed again to Pan Hannian.

Zhao Rong also had "international experience" that let him look down on his peers at home. In July 1933, Zhao Rong and Cao Yi'ou traveled together to Moscow, where he became deputy head of the CCP delegation to the Comintern. The head of that delegation was none other than the "Left" dogmatist Wang Ming. In 1934, when Kirov, a member of the Soviet Politburo, was assassinated, the Soviet party began a purge of Trotskyites in its own ranks. Wang Ming and Zhao Rong took an active part in the Soviet "anti-Trotskyist purge," and turned on the Chinese comrades studying in Moscow as well. Xiao Shouhuang, a Special Branch man Zhao Rong knew well, was arrested and killed; Wu Hujing, who had taken charge of Special Branch work after the Center left Shanghai in 1933, and who was Zhao Rong's own townsman and colleague, was likewise arrested and killed on a charge of being a turncoat and a spy. Some students who would not join Wang Ming's clique were slandered as a "Jiangsu-Zhejiang Fellow-Townsmen's Society." Wang Ming and Zhao Rong even went over the head of the CCP Center at home and issued instructions directly to Party organizations across China.

On November 29, 1937, a Soviet military plane came down at Yan'an. Mao Zedong, Zhang Wentian, Zhou Enlai, Zhu De, Bo Gu, Zhang Guotao, every Communist leader then in Yan'an, braved a heavy snow to greet it at the airfield.

Wang Ming and Zhao Rong, home from the Soviet Union, and Chen Yun, who had boarded in Xinjiang, stepped down from the plane together. Mao Zedong's welcome overflowed with warmth: welcome to the "immortals" come down from the Kunlun Mountains, welcome to our beloved friends from abroad, welcome to the comrades back from the Soviet Union; this was joy dropping out of the sky.

The CCP Center, only a year settled in northern Shaanxi, was coping with the violent changes that followed the outbreak of war, settling accounts with Zhang Guotao's errors, and reshaping its own leadership, and it badly needed the Comintern's support. At the Comintern's Seventh Congress the Communists had seen Wang Ming, Zhou Enlai, Zhang Guotao, and Mao Zedong elected to the Executive Committee, and Zhao Rong and Bo Gu elected alternate members. Wang Ming was elected besides to the Executive Committee's Presidium and as an alternate secretary of its political secretariat, in charge of the Communist parties of Asia and Latin America; Zhao Rong was elected an alternate member of the Presidium and succeeded Wang Ming as the CCP's representative. In the Comintern, Wang Ming and Kang Sheng stood higher than every Communist leader at home, Mao Zedong included.

Mao's welcome was no mere courtesy. Before long, all three who had come off that plane held important posts at the CCP Center: Wang Ming as head of the Central United Front Work Department, Zhao Rong as head of the Central Social Affairs Department, Chen Yun as head of the Central Organization Department. Soon Zhao Rong changed his name to Kang Sheng.

Why was Kang Sheng chosen to head the Social Affairs Department?

Count the cadres in Yan'an who had held senior posts on the intelligence and security front: Zhou Enlai, Chen Yun, Kang Sheng, Deng Fa, Luo Ruiqing, Li Kenong, Pan Hannian, Zhou Xing. Zhou Enlai was a central leader; Chen Yun had other heavy duties; Luo Ruiqing worked mainly in the Red Army; Deng Fa and Zhou Xing had taken part in the purges; and Pan Hannian and Li Kenong had stood below Kang Sheng in the Special Branch. All told, for Kang Sheng, a member of the Politburo, to head the Social Affairs Department followed naturally enough.

The deeper reason may have gone further. Some old security cadres who had worked under Kang Sheng say that in those days he was forever stressing international experience.

International experience! The Comintern's sway over the CCP in this period is not to be underrated. In organizational terms the CCP was a branch of the Comintern, and the Comintern its higher leadership. In the Party's internal disputes above all, the Comintern played the decisive part of arbiter. When Zhang Guotao set himself up as a rival to the CCP Center, it was Zhang Hao, speaking for the Comintern, whose word finally brought him to accept the Center's leadership. Before Wang Ming and Kang Sheng came home this time, Stalin received them specially. In August the secretariat of the Comintern's Executive Committee studied the Chinese situation as a special question and discussed and adopted Wang Ming's report. That report held that China's central task now was to "build, on the basis of Nationalist-Communist cooperation, a great anti-Japanese union of all the country's anti-Japanese parties and factions," and then to "establish a unified national-defense government for all China and a unified democratic republic for all China."

To turn from Nationalist-Communist hostility to Nationalist-Communist cooperation would demand of the CCP a hundred-and-eighty-degree change of policy, extraordinarily hard to make. The Comintern's general secretary, Dimitrov, decided: "At a time like this we must strengthen the hand of the domestic cadres; we need vigorous people who can find their bearings in the international situation to go and help the CCP Center." The cadres the Comintern picked and sent back for this were, above all, Wang Ming and Kang Sheng.

Kang Sheng had been an alternate member of the Comintern's Presidium, and within the CCP he ranked second only to Wang Ming. At home too he had a solid record, having done grassroots work in Shanghai and served as head of the Jiangsu provincial organization department and of the Central Organization Department. He had, moreover, exactly the intelligence background the Social Affairs post called for, having run the Central Special Branch. And he had taken part in intelligence work within the Comintern, learning no little of the craft of "building special work."

The CCP, then putting its intelligence and security apparatus in order, naturally set store by international experience in this field. Yan'an in those days held a Soviet Red Army intelligence team, quartered at Zaoyuan alongside the CCP Center and the Social Affairs Department, in a brick house built for the purpose, with a radio set of its own. The Center's contact with the Comintern was in Mao Zedong's own hands, sent and received through a station under the cover name "Rural Committee"; Mao's contact with Stalin went entirely through the Soviet team's set. Mao prized his relations with the Soviet intelligence men: when Sun Ping, the head of the team, asked to study the history of the CCP, Mao explained it to him himself. Shi Zhe, who had taken part in GPU work in the Soviet Union, was sent back to Yan'an by the Comintern to look into conditions, and was at once given weighty employment: he served as Ren Bishi's political secretary and, in fact, as Mao Zedong's Russian interpreter, handling every telegram to and from the "far country," and later became director of the office of the Central Secretariat.

Kang Sheng's international experience gave him one more qualification for the Social Affairs post, and he threw himself into founding the department. The training text he wrote, "Building Special Work," had real substance: riding a rickshaw through the Nationalist blockade line in a Shanghai uprising with two pistols hidden in a box of pastries; carrying letters from Mao Zedong and Zhu De to the German Communists through customs when he went to Germany; and on and on, until the young people in the training classes hung on his words. Kang Sheng was rich in the practice of intelligence, and had his foreign experience besides; he was a man of considerable cultivation, and could do calligraphy with either hand; he had wide experience of the world and knew every walk of life. That Kang Sheng could be called an "Elder" in Yan'an had its reasons after all.

An alternate member of the Comintern Presidium, a member of the CCP Politburo, and now armed with the real power of the Social Affairs post, Kang Sheng carried himself grandly in Yan'an. In a black serge uniform, astride a big black horse, a black wolfhound at his heel, he cut a figure that yielded nothing to the chiefs of the Soviet GPU.

***

At its founding the Social Affairs Department had few cadres. Kong Yuan served for a time as a deputy head but was soon moved to the Southern Bureau. Pan Hannian, formerly chief of the Special Branch's intelligence section, was named director of the First Office, in charge of intelligence, and soon rose to deputy head.

Pan Hannian was born into a scholarly family in Yixing, Jiangsu. Quick and studious from childhood, he was publishing poems and essays in the Shanghai press at seventeen; at nineteen he set out alone to make his way in Shanghai, passed into the China National Language School that Li Jinxi ran, and found a post as a proofreader at the Zhonghua Book Company. The Zhonghua Book Company was a base of revolutionary activity in Shanghai, and there Pan Hannian took part in the "May Thirtieth" strike organized by the progressive unions, and in 1926 joined the literary society Creation. Its leaders Guo Moruo and Yu Dafu were figures of great name in Chinese letters; this "young clerk" Pan Hannian showed an uncommon gift for getting things done, and joined the Communist underground.

Pan Hannian was a lively presence in progressive literary circles, running a weekly, writing essays, and making the acquaintance of many notables, so that he earned the playful nickname "Little Boss." When the Northern Expedition began, at the invitation of Guo Moruo, deputy head of the General Political Department, he edited a broadsheet. Back in Shanghai he threw himself into left-wing cultural work, wrote a great deal, and won the notice of the central leadership. When the Jiangsu provincial committee set up the Party's first cultural Party group, its secretary was Pan Hannian. Later the group passed to the Central Propaganda Department, and Pan Hannian became the first secretary of the Central Cultural Committee, taking charge of founding the "League of Left-Wing Writers." In 1930 he saw to combining the left-wing bodies of every field, the "Grand Alliance for Freedom," the "Social Scientists' League," the "Left-Wing Writers' League," the "Left-Wing Dramatists' League," the "Labor League," into a "Grand Anti-Imperialist Alliance of China," and served as its Party-group secretary. At twenty-six the "Little Boss" had a clear road into every circle in Shanghai.

The next year Pan Hannian suddenly dropped out of sight. Gu Shunzhang, head of the Central Special Branch, had defected, and the Center moved Pan Hannian into the Special Branch to take charge of intelligence. From the time he joined the Central Special Branch in 1931, Pan Hannian was always at the front line of intelligence and united-front work. In the Central Soviet he had taken part in the Red Army's negotiations with the Fujian government. On the Long March he was ordered to make his way to the White areas and carry the spirit of the Zunyi Conference to the Party organizations there. In the early days of the Nationalist-Communist alliance he again shuttled between Yan'an and Nanjing, the go-between of the two parties' central leaderships.

In April 1939, Pan Hannian, not long installed as director of the First Office, needed surgery in a big city for an eye ailment, and before he left Kang Sheng arranged that if he could manage to stay on in Hong Kong, he should remain there to run intelligence. In October the CCP Center named him deputy head of the Social Affairs Department, to organize intelligence work behind enemy lines. From then on Pan Hannian worked secretly for years around Hong Kong, Shanghai, and Huainan, matching wits and daring with the intelligence services of Japan, of Wang Jingwei, and of the Nationalists deep in the enemy rear, and won much important strategic intelligence. Pan Hannian was an intelligence prodigy of rare gifts: able to work the front line in person and to direct the organization from behind the scenes, keen in strategic vision, of high cultivation, skilled at making friends in every circle, and adept at analyzing and ordering intelligence.

As a secret intelligence officer, Pan Hannian had passed many times between us and the enemy, and after Liberation he was wrongly branded an "inner traitor," arrested, and imprisoned. When the Cultural Revolution ended and the families of persecuted cadres pressed one after another for their names to be cleared, Pan Hannian and his wife Dong Hui had both died, leaving no children. But his old comrades of the Special Branch never stopped remembering him. The aged Chen Yun put to the Center a request that his case be reviewed and reversed. In 1982, five years after his death, Pan Hannian was rehabilitated.

The Social Affairs Department later named yet another deputy head: Li Kenong, one of the "Three Heroes of Longtan."

In the early years of the war Li Kenong worked all along on intelligence and united-front work in the Nationalist areas, setting up the Eighth Route Army offices in Xi'an, Shanghai, Nanjing, Wuhan, and Guilin one after another and building many intelligence connections in Nationalist territory. In March 1941 he returned to Yan'an as deputy head of the Social Affairs Department. In September the CCP Center decided to establish a Central Intelligence Department; Kang Sheng, head of the Social Affairs Department, held the headship of the Intelligence Department as well, and Li Kenong, deputy head of the Social Affairs Department, was concurrently its deputy head. Before long Kang Sheng turned his main energies to the Rectification and the cadre screening, and Li Kenong took over the day-to-day running of both departments. At the end of 1946, Kang Sheng, his name in ruins over the "Rescue Campaign," had to leave Yan'an for the Shanxi-Suiyuan area to carry out land reform, and both departments passed to Li Kenong. After the victory over Japan, during the Nationalist-Communist peace talks, Li Kenong served as secretary-general of the Communist delegation to the "Military Mediation Executive Headquarters."

After the founding of the People's Republic, Li Kenong served as head of the Military Commission's intelligence department, deputy foreign minister, and deputy chief of the general staff; he led the Korean armistice negotiations and took part in the Geneva Conference, joining intelligence work to many kinds of work besides. All along he was in charge of intelligence, laying out a whole body of guidelines, policies, and methods, and setting the system of intelligence work on its feet.

Li Kenong stood all his life at the pivot of Communist intelligence work, and by common consent he was its outstanding leader.

Others served for a time as deputy head of the Social Affairs Department as well, Kong Yuan, Chen Gang, Liu Shaowen, and Tan Zhengwen among them, all of them elite figures on the Communists' intelligence and security front.

### 5. The Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office

The Party Center's intelligence and security organ was the Social Affairs Department; the army's intelligence organ was the Second Bureau of the Military Commission, and its security organ the traitor-elimination department of the General Political Department; and on the government side there was the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office. The Central Social Affairs Department was called for short "Zhongshebu," and the Border Region Security Office "Bianbao."

The Social Affairs Department, which directed intelligence and security work for the whole Party, was a lean command body; the Border Security, which ran that work in the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, was the arm that carried it out, and the one with real muscle. The region's political system borrowed from the American: not quite a separation of three powers, but what they called "two and a half powers." The government and the assembly were independent, the courts half independent, and the Security Office held the functions of both a public-security organ and a procuratorate. At every level the region's government usually had only three departments, the First (called a "section" in the sub-districts and counties) for civil administration, the Second for finance, the Third for education, and the whole government's numbers were strictly fixed. But the security system was not counted into this order. In the region it was called the Security Office, and below it the security section; its standing at government meetings was raised a rank, so that where other departments sent only their section chiefs, the Security Office's ordinary staff could attend. Where the head of another department had one or two staff under him, the head of security had troops, and two systems at that, one open, one hidden. The open one was the armed security squad and the special commissioners sent to the army and to certain units; the hidden one was the part-time "net members" inside every unit.

Within the Security Office, the First Department (once called a bureau, a department, or a section) handled security; the Second, investigation; the Third, local affairs, later interrogation; and there were a cadre department and an office besides. The First Department was led in turn by Xie Ziqun and Bu Lu. After security and investigation were separated, the First Department was led by Shi Zhe, Zhao Cangbi, and others, the Second by Wang Fan, Li Qiming, and others, the Third by Bai Dongcai, and interrogation by Ye Yungao. In practice the office's work came down to three lines: investigation, intelligence, and interrogation. The men who headed these three longest were Zhao Cangbi in investigation, Li Qiming in intelligence, and Ye Yungao in interrogation.

The security sections in the counties under the Security Office answered to two masters, both the county Party committee and government and the Border Security. The security squad under a security section was also the county government's only armed force, and the detention house the section ran was the county-level jail. The Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region held five sub-prefectures, twenty-two counties, and one city, and security men were everywhere.

Yan'an was a city. Everywhere else the office was called a security branch; only in Yan'an was it called a "public-security bureau," and within the trade it was proudly styled "the number-one bureau under heaven." The first chief of the Yan'an municipal bureau, Liu Huping, did fine work and was sent to Sun Yat-sen University in the Soviet Union for further study. His deputy Wang Zhuochao took over the post and held it for ten years running. After Japan's surrender the chief was Hao Su, a "'38-style' cadre, with a younger local cadre, Kang Shichang, as his deputy. When Yan'an was retaken in the later part of the War of Liberation, Kang Shichang was chief and Liang Ji his deputy.

The Yan'an municipal bureau had four police stations across the city. The head of the First Station, at Qiao'ergou, was Shao Yan, of a bureaucratic family, who at twenty-two had worn a major's insignia and run a telephone office, but had chosen to come to Yan'an and rough it, and became a bureau head in the Ministry of Public Security after 1949. The head of the Second Station was Yang Kaiming; the head of the Third, Zhu Hualong, was an intellectual cadre who served in the Ministry of Petroleum after 1949; the head of the Fourth was named Li, a Long March cadre. As for the bureau proper: the First Section, investigation, had as its chief Gou Liangfa, whose cover in the Nanjing underground had been a Nationalist policeman, and who became president of the Southwest Institute of Political Science and Law after 1949; the Second Section handled public order, household registration, and fire, under Yang Kaiming; the Third Section, justice, took in all the affairs of the police, procuratorate, and courts; the Fourth Section, general affairs. The bureau's cadres were a strong lot: most of the headquarters staff were veteran Red Army men of Jiangxi and Sichuan, with only Liang Ji a Yan'an native. Mao Zedong's bodyguard Chen Changfeng was moved to the bureau after the Long March and served in turn as political instructor of the police force and head of the Second and then the First Station. Zeng Shaodong, political instructor of the mounted patrol, was ranked major general after 1949.

The most conspicuous of all was the police force. In the past the armed units of the Communists' security system had been called guard companies, security companies, special-service companies, and the like, organized on the same lines as the army. The police force now formed at Yan'an was the Communists' first. Its uniform was unlike the army's gray: black from head to foot, with collar tabs specially cast in aluminum alloy bearing the two characters for "Border Police." The cut being the same as the Nationalists' "black dogs," many of the men grumbled at the change of dress, and it took a few days' persuading. At its founding the force numbered only 35, under the commander Zou Lizhi, the political instructor Chen Changfeng, and the deputy commander Zhang Zhili. Before long its three squads were expanded into three district platoons, more than 140 men. This single company was the whole of the public order keeping the Border Region's capital, and the central leaders' bodyguards were often picked from its ranks. Lin Biao's guard Xiang Biao and Dong Biwu's guard Feng Wenbin had both started as Yan'an policemen.

At the main crossroads in Yan'an stood six police posts, charged with keeping order and directing traffic alike. The force was also attached, at set times and unset, to the public-security bureau's public-order section to check household registrations and inspect inns, and to guard the safety of public gatherings. The municipal bureau's mounted patrol had more than a hundred horses, and on great occasions, whenever foreign guests or guests of the central government were received, it was this patrol that rode out to stand watch.

The main military force of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region was the Garrison Corps. The region's highest military command was the Joint Defense Headquarters, its commander Gao Gang, secretary of the Northwest Bureau. There was also the Yan'an Garrison Headquarters, whose commander was drawn from the troops stationed in the region, Wang Zhen and others in turn; the deputy commander's post was held throughout by Zhou Xing, the head of the Border Security, and under Zhou Xing was a well-equipped security regiment. The regiment had grown out of the guard company under the Red Army's Security Bureau, which at first had only a hundred-odd men, about a reinforced company. After reaching Yan'an it was enlarged into three guard battalions, one to guard the Party Center, one the Military Commission headquarters, one the local government. Later the battalion structure was abolished and seven infantry companies placed under direct command, with a machine-gun company and a guard platoon besides, more than 1,500 men in all, armed with light and heavy machine guns and a great many Mauser pistols, but no artillery, established as an internal-guard force. The Yan'an security regiment, one may say, was the forerunner of the later armed-police forces.

The regiment's first commander, Li Wenchang, was a Red Army man of the Ningdu Uprising in Jiangxi; its second, Li Zhizhou, a cadre of the northern Shaanxi independent division; its third was Liu Zhen; the commissar's post was held concurrently by Zou Yan, chief of the Security Office's political department; the first chief of staff was Wang Zhi, the second Xia Fei. The regiment answered to both the Border Security and the Joint Defense Headquarters. The guarding of the Border government and the Northwest Bureau, the training and posting of the leaders' bodyguards, the guard for meetings and for leaders on the move, patrols for public order, the watching of prisoners, the guard for the frontier checkpoints and the road-mouths, the air-defense lookouts and the airfield's defense, the sentry duty at the Yan'an garrison command, all drew their men from the security regiment.

The Social Affairs Department was a Party organ and faced no one outside; but the Security Office bore two signboards, within it the Social Affairs Department of the Northwest Bureau, without it the Security Office of the Border Region government. In Yan'an it was always the Security Office that showed its face for the Communists' security work.

Throughout the war the Communists' intelligence and security work faced powerful adversaries. Imperial Japan had always set great store by espionage; and the Nationalists, the friendly party, kept their secret services at work against the Communists too. Zhongtong had been dedicated to anti-Communism from the days of the single section it grew out of, and Juntong swelled all the faster, carrying the double task of fighting Japan and fighting the Communists.

On the Communist side, the central directing organ, the Social Affairs Department, and the main executing organ, the Security Office, had likewise taken shape as a complete intelligence and security system.

Who would beat whom? Those who knew the trade said: it comes down to the people.

As for the men who led the Border Region Security Office: the head throughout was Zhou Xing; the deputy heads were, in the early period, Du Liqing and Tan Zhengwen; in the middle, Liu Haibin and Liu Bingwen; in the later, Zhao Cangbi and Li Qiming. The one who served longest was Liu Haibin. Liu Haibin had taken the State Political Security Bureau's senior security course in 1932, served as special commissioner of the Second Division on the Long March, and, wounded in the leg, transferred to local work. In the early years of the People's Republic, when Zhou Enlai drew a group of public-security cadres to serve as Party secretaries at universities, Liu Haibin went to the Northwest Polytechnical University, and later became a marriage-relation of the Shaanxi provincial secretary Hu Yaobang, their children wed. The later deputies of the Border Security all came from the local Shaanxi organization.

Among the Border Security's leaders, three had done security work the longest: Zhou Xing, Zhao Cangbi, and Li Qiming. Zhou Xing was a Long March cadre of the Central Red Army, Zhao Cangbi a local cadre of northern Shaanxi, Li Qiming an intellectual come from outside; the three answered exactly to the three great sources of the Border Security's cadres.

Zhou Xing was a heavyweight in the Communists' intelligence and security system. From Ruijin, the capital of the Central Soviet, through his post as deputy head of the 1st Red Army Corps' Security Bureau on the Long March, to his charge of the Border Security in Yan'an, Zhou Xing had worked all along at Mao Zedong's side. In the later part of the War of Liberation he took part in the takeovers of Nanjing and Chongqing, and afterward served as deputy minister of public security, deputy chief procurator of the Supreme People's Procuratorate, governor of Yunnan, and first secretary of the provincial committee.

Zhao Cangbi was a cadre born and bred in northern Shaanxi. At fourteen he entered the Second Higher Primary School of Qingjian, where teachers like Bai Mingshan were among the early Communists of northern Shaanxi, and where the school's village of Gaojie was the very place Mao Zedong would later compose "Snow." A strong student, Zhao Cangbi moved on to the First Higher School in the county town, where Xie Zichang had launched the "Qingjian Uprising" in 1927. Taking in revolutionary ideas at school, Zhao Cangbi went home to teach in a primary school. In the winter of 1932, when Xie Zichang came to open a base area around Qingjian, the eighteen-year-old Zhao Cangbi threw himself into the revolution; in 1934 he was chairman of the soviet of the First District of Yanshui County, and in February 1935 secretary of the county's Security Bureau.

In 1935, hearing that the Party Center had reached northern Shaanxi, Zhao Cangbi set out alone to find it. After a hundred li of wandering he found the Northwest Security Bureau at Bao'an, just in time to join the security training course held that October. Quick and clever, Zhao Cangbi was kept on as a secretary at the bureau after he finished. Before long, in Bao'an County a pack train carrying military supplies was robbed, and Zhao Cangbi led some twenty plainclothesmen secretly into Shunning town and, after a hundred days of investigation, established at last that it was the work of a Nationalist-organized militia. Taking a single man with him, Zhao Cangbi slipped in disguise into the home of the militia chief and hauled out the chief's father for questioning. And so it came out: the militia had planted a shoe-mender as a watcher at the very seat of the Center.

The winding, tangled course of cracking the case fired in Zhao Cangbi a deep interest in the craft of investigation. He served in turn as deputy commander of the Sanbian security command, head of the Suide security office, chief of the plainclothes squad of the Border Region Security Office, and head of the Longdong branch office, and in the War of Liberation as deputy head of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office. After the founding of the People's Republic he worked in the public-security bureaus of Beijing, Nanjing, and Chongqing, and served in turn as deputy head of the Southwest Public Security Department, head of the Sichuan provincial public-security department, president of the Southwest Institute of Political Science and Law, and deputy governor; in 1977 he became minister of public security of the People's Republic of China. From secretary of a county security bureau to the nation's minister of public security, Zhao Cangbi had a complete career in public-security work; this acknowledged "investigation specialist" even wrote, after his retirement, An Outline of Political Case Investigation.

Li Qiming's path onto the intelligence and security front was stranger still: he was hauled in.

Li Qiming was a Shanxi man. In 1929 he went to study at the Taiyuan First Middle School; in 1931 he went to Fenyang to enter the officers' school Feng Yuxiang had set up; in 1932 he secretly joined the Communist Party; and he took part, with Feng Yuxiang's Chahar People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army, in the battle of Gubeikou. When the Allied Army came under the double encirclement of the Japanese and Chiang Kai-shek, underground Communists like Li Qiming were forced to shift to Suiyuan, and in 1933 he went to the Shaanxi Soviet and joined the Red 26th Army.

Li Qiming's gifts were exactly suited to intelligence. Secretary, checkpoint, training class, field work, chief of the intelligence section: having passed through every kind of intelligence post, Li Qiming became the youngest deputy head of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office, and after the founding of the People's Republic the youngest regional minister of public security. The Center was preparing to send Li Qiming abroad as an ambassador, but his father-in-law, Li Kenong, said there was no sense in a man who knew no foreign language going abroad to suffer among foreigners.

Later Li Qiming stayed on in Shaanxi, serving as governor of Shaanxi before the Cultural Revolution and as standing secretary of the Yunnan provincial committee after it.

Look at where these Border Security cadres, Zhou Xing, Zhao Cangbi, Li Qiming, had come from, and one could see the makeup of the Communists' intelligence and security cadres beginning to change.

The Communists had always attached weight to the makeup of the intelligence and security ranks: the senior leaders had been sent to the Soviet Union for training, and the backbone members had been chosen for their worker origins. Deng Fa had begun as a seaman; Zhou Xing, Ouyang Yi, Chen Fusheng, Xie Ziqun, and others had all begun as handicraft workers. Yet a makeup pure and ever purer had not kept out a turncoat like Gu Shunzhang, nor prevented a grave error like Li Shaojiu's.

Chastened by its reverses, the Communists grew shrewder in using people, and drew their new security cadres from many sources: veteran cadres who had walked the 25,000 li of the Long March, and, no less prized, local peasant cadres of northern Shaanxi trained up for the work; worker and peasant cadres from the Red areas, and underground Party cadres brought in to fill out the ranks from the White areas; and, with particular care, intellectuals from outside.
