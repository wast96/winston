# Chapter Seven — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 137–152. Sections 24 ("Two Doubtful Points in the North Station
Killing") and 25 ("Du Yuesheng Finds His Chance to Return the Compliment").*

**Headline finding.** The invented Japanese plot that runs through chapters six
and seven is not invented from nothing. In this chapter it resolves into a
recognizable, if relocated and back-dated, version of a **real conspiracy that
Tanaka Ryūkichi later confessed to** — the murder of Japanese Nichiren monks in
Shanghai, staged to manufacture a pretext for the attack of 28 January 1932.
The author has the right conspirator, the right sect, the right method and the
right consequence, and has moved the crime to the wrong temple, the wrong
victim and the wrong date. That changes how the whole strand should be read.
See "The monk" below.

---

## What happens

### The Hatchet Gang thinks it has won

Wang Yaqiao's gunmen regroup at the rented house by the station and report
success. Wang curses Chang Yuqing — not for competing with him, but for trying
to hang a Japanese consul's murder on the Hatchet Gang — then scatters the
organization: leave Shanghai the same day, no contact between members, and
under arrest admit nothing, above all not the Canton connection. His men
disperse to Wuhu, Hefei, Changzhou, Wuhan and Guangdong.

His wife walks in while the wine is being poured and tells him they killed the
wrong man. The *Shenbao* reports the target as T.V. Soong, Soong reaching the
stationmaster's office alive, and the dead man as his secretary Tang Yulu.

Two details in the report don't fit. The paper counts more casualties than the
Hatchet Gang can account for, and says one attacker was taken alive, badly
wounded. Zheng Baozhen confirms not one of their own men was hurt — so the
prisoner belongs to somebody else. Wang's fear is not identification but that
the captive will name the Hatchet Gang. He, his wife and a few followers
disguise themselves, leave by Zhenru station rather than North Station, and go
on to Hong Kong.

### The other gang thinks it has won too

Chang Yuqing is celebrating a dead Shigemitsu when Tanaka telephones to scream
at him, and then learns one of his own men, Li Xiaosi, was taken at the scene.
Shigemitsu, it emerges, was walking beside Soong the whole time; he grasps only
afterward that he was the target, and that changing out of his usual white suit
that morning saved him.

### Dai Li gets nowhere, then gets lucky

He raids Wang's French Concession house and finds it emptied. A tip sends him
to No. 198 Tianmu Road by the station, but the landlady, shown Wang's
photograph, insists none of her tenants was this man.

Then he interrogates Li Xiaosi — the chapter's best scene. Li Xiaosi has
already told the garrison command that Wang Yaqiao sent him, but cannot produce
one detail: not where or when he was briefed, not where Wang is, and above all
not who he was sent to kill. One moment a Japanese called Shigemitsu, the next
a Chinese. **That is Dai Li's first doubt.**

**The second is the signature.** The grenades left at the scene are marked
"hatchet" in red lacquer. Dai Li reasons that Wang is a meticulous professional
whose jobs are seamless, so why sign one? He concludes the opposite of what the
evidence was planted to suggest — not merely that Wang didn't do it, but that a
*second* assassination organization is working in Shanghai that he knows
nothing about. The dragnet that follows searches women and children at every
station and wharf; the reward climbs from 1,000 through 10,000 and produces
nothing.

Chiang comes secretly to Shanghai on 16 August for the funeral of Soong's
mother on the 18th, summons Du Yuesheng and Huang Jinrong, and asks for help.
Du — with his own account to settle from chapters three and four — names the
Hatchet Gang and produces a real lead: in May one of his men drank with Liu
Gang of Wang's organization, who let slip that "Ninth Master has us on a fine
big piece of business, and it looks like Lushan."

At the funeral, Soong fears a second attempt: his usual British car heads the
procession empty as a decoy while he travels another route, and Dai Li's agents
serve as his bodyguard. Watching the cortège from a darkened upper room near
Jing'an Temple are Tanaka and Chang Yuqing.

Du's man lures Liu Gang — back in Shanghai after lying low in Changzhou — to
dinner at a Sichuan restaurant on North Sichuan Road. Seven or eight men take
him in the corridor. Dai Li interrogates him personally that night, under
torture.

### The monk

Tanaka tells Chang Yuqing that Shigemitsu is finished as a target: he has seven
or eight new guards and will not leave the consulate. The Army Ministry has
reprimanded Tanaka, and military and political figures are now off limits. He
produces a photograph of a new one — an aged, ascetic-looking Japanese who came
to Shanghai around 1920 and took orders as a Buddhist monk. Kill him, Tanaka
says, get it into the papers, and the death of a great Nichiren master will
inflame Japanese feeling no less than the Nakamura affair did in the northeast.
Chang agrees readily: an old monk at Longhua, a secluded temple thick with
pilgrims, is a far softer target than a consul. Tanaka makes him wait until the
hunt for Soong's assassins dies down.

Later, men come over the wall at Longhua by night, find the lit window by the
sound of a wooden fish, wet the paper with a finger to see in, match the face
to the photograph, and force the door.

The Japanese press runs it first — a full page in Tokyo before the Chinese
papers have anything, headlined as the murder of an aged Japanese monk by
Chinese assailants. The Army General Staff follows with a formal statement
calling it a continuation of the Nakamura affair. Before dawn on 28 January
1932 Japanese troops attack Zhabei from the Japanese quarter, and Cai Tingkai's
19th Route Army fights back.

Chiang blames Wang Yaqiao. Dai Li, for once, defends him: the physical traces
at Longhua are nothing like the earlier attacks, and he lays out for Chiang
what he has assembled — that Chang Yuqing was bought by Tanaka to kill
Shigemitsu, that Chang's men were at North Station that morning, and that the
Longhua murder is the same partnership manufacturing the same kind of pretext.
Chiang's response is to order Wang stripped of the volunteer-army command he
has just taken up.

### Wang's patriotic turn

In Hong Kong, Wang learns of the Mukden Incident from a student's newspaper at
the waterfront, where a Northeastern officer in exile has just killed himself.
Shenyang fallen, Zhang Xueliang ordering a general withdrawal. Wang is
gaunt by morning. He resolves to go back to Shanghai although the price on his
head has gone from 10,000 to 30,000: with the country being taken, he says,
what is his head worth?

His wife returns first in late October to test the ground, finds Nanjing no
longer pursuing him with its whole strength after Mukden, and wires him. He
lands in early November off an Italian liner. Within three days he founds the
Iron and Blood Traitor-Purging Corps and takes its command; the book claims
more than twenty thousand join. He raises money for Ma Zhanshan, fighting in
Heilongjiang. Stripped of the volunteer command by Chiang, he laughs and
observes that Chiang can remove his title but cannot stop him fighting the
Japanese, then organizes fifteen combat squads to support the 19th Route Army.

The chapter closes on the death of one of his men, Hu Amao, a driver, in the
Huangpu.

## How it holds up

### The monk murder is a real conspiracy, moved

This is the finding that matters. What Tanaka Ryūkichi actually did — and
admitted to in later testimony — was arrange the attack on **five Japanese
Nichiren monks near the Sanyou towel works in Zhabei on 18 January 1932**, in
which one monk died. Japanese residents rioted, and the affair ran directly
into the attack of 28 January.

Against that, the book has:

| element | documented | the book |
|---|---|---|
| instigator | Tanaka Ryūkichi | **correct** |
| method | pay Chinese to kill Japanese | **correct** |
| victims' sect | Nichiren (日莲宗) | **correct** |
| purpose | manufacture a pretext for Shanghai | **correct** |
| consequence | the 28 January attack | **correct, and correctly dated** |
| victims | five street monks, one killed | a single aged abbot |
| place | Zhabei, by a factory | Longhua Temple |
| date | 18 January 1932 | left vague, months later |
| contractor | Tanaka's own agents | Chang Yuqing's Anqing Gang |

So the strand I called undocumented in chapter six is better described as
**fictionalized around a real event**. The Shigemitsu plot appears to be the
author's invented bridge, running the genuine Tanaka conspiracy back in time so
that it can collide with the North Station shooting. Knowing this, the ch6
notes calling the Japanese subplot unsupported are correct as far as they go
but incomplete, and I would want to revise them.

### Corroborated

- **Dai Li's failure.** Right, and understated. The case was not broken until
  **1934**, roughly three years on, through an assassin who had become a
  security captain in Yancheng, run down by Shen Zui and Cheng Muyi.
- **The funeral.** Ni Guizhen's coffin came from Qingdao to the Soong house on
  Seymour Road; the funeral was in Shanghai on **18 August 1931**, Chiang among
  the family; she was buried that night beside Charlie Soong. The book's dates
  are exact.
- **The Mukden Incident** and Zhang Xueliang's withdrawal order, 18 September
  1931.
- **The 28 January 1932 attack on Zhabei**, and the 19th Route Army's defence
  under Cai Tingkai, with Jiang Guangnai. Both men correctly placed.
- **Ma Zhanshan**, resisting in Heilongjiang that November, and the Shanghai
  fundraising for him.

### Contradicted

- **The case breaks too early.** The book has Liu Gang seized in August 1931
  and tortured into talking. The record puts the break in 1934, and through an
  informer of Dai Li's own rather than through the Green Gang. Du Yuesheng's
  scene — the hinge of the chapter's plot — has no support.
- **Hu Amao.** Real, and famous: a driver for a South City fire-prevention
  society, seized by Japanese troops on **26 February 1932**, forced the next
  morning to drive a truck of ammunition under four-soldier guard, and put the
  truck into the Huangpu, killing himself and all four. He was an ordinary
  conscripted civilian, not a Hatchet Gang member, and he was driving *Japanese*
  munitions under guard, not carrying supplies to the 19th Route Army. The book
  annexes a national martyr into Wang Yaqiao's organization. Note that the same
  pairing circulates in Chinese popular writing, so the book is repeating a
  claim rather than originating it.
- **Wang Yaqiao founding the Iron and Blood corps in November 1931** settles,
  against the book itself, a dating problem I flagged in chapter five. Chapter
  five has the corps already operating in early 1931; here it is founded after
  Mukden. The November 1931 date is the one consistent with the sources. **The
  ch5 note should be revised to say the book contradicts itself, not merely
  that the sources disagree.**

### Unsupported

Li Xiaosi, on whom the first doubt entirely depends; No. 198 Tianmu Road, a
suspiciously precise address with no record behind it; the twenty thousand
recruits in three days; and the Longhua abbot's identity, which the book gives
as a Japanese layman turned monk under a name the scan does not render legibly.

### A caution on sources

Several Chinese popular accounts of the North Station shooting call Wang Yaqiao
a **Green Gang** leader. He was not — he led the Hatchet Gang, and his quarrel
with the Green Gang bosses is the substance of chapters three and four. Any
source making that error should be treated as unchecked on him generally, which
thins the usable pool considerably.

## The structural reading

Chapters six and seven are the book's method in clearest view. It takes a
genuinely strange documented event — two men in matching white suits, the wrong
one shot, an investigation that fails for three years — and splices it to a
genuine conspiracy that happened six months later, moving the second backward
so the two can be one story. The join is the invented Shigemitsu plot.

That is more interesting than straightforward fabrication, and more misleading.
A reader checking any single element — Tanaka, the Nichiren monks, the 28
January attack, Chang Yuqing's collaboration — will find it confirmed, and
conclude the sequence is history. It isn't; the sequence is the author's.
