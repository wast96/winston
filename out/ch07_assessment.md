# Chapter Seven — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 137–152. Sections 24 ("Two Doubtful Points in the North Station
Killing") and 25 ("Du Yuesheng Finds His Chance to Return the Compliment").*

**Coverage note.** The synopsis below covers printed 137–143, which I read off
the scans page by page. Printed 144–152 I have only at OCR level and have not
verified, so I have not summarized them. Nothing here rests on unverified text.

---

## What happens

**The Hatchet Gang thinks it has won.** Wang Yaqiao's gunmen regroup at the
rented house by the station and report success. Wang curses Chang Yuqing —
not for competing with him but for trying to hang a Japanese consul's murder
on the Hatchet Gang — then orders the whole organization to scatter: leave
Shanghai the same day, no contact between members, and under arrest admit
nothing, above all not the Canton connection. His men disperse to Wuhu,
Hefei, Changzhou, Wuhan and Guangdong.

**His wife brings the newspaper.** Wang Yaying walks in while the wine is
being poured and tells him they killed the wrong man. The *Shenbao* reports
that the target was T.V. Soong, that Soong reached the stationmaster's office
alive, and that the dead man was his secretary Tang Yulu.

**Two details in the report don't fit.** The paper counts more casualties than
the Hatchet Gang can account for, and says one of the attackers was taken
alive, badly wounded. Zheng Baozhen confirms that not one of their own men was
hurt. So the captured man belongs to somebody else — and Wang's immediate fear
is not that he will be identified but that the prisoner will name the Hatchet
Gang. Wang, his wife and a few followers disguise themselves, leave by Zhenru
station rather than North Station, and go on to Hong Kong.

**The other gang thinks it has won too.** Chang Yuqing is celebrating a dead
Shigemitsu when Tanaka telephones to scream at him. He then learns that one of
his own men, Li Xiaosi, was captured at the scene. Shigemitsu, it emerges, was
walking beside Soong the whole time; he realizes only afterward that he was the
intended target, and that changing out of his usual white suit that morning is
what saved him. He slips out with the ordinary passengers and drives to the
consulate.

**Dai Li arrives and gets nowhere.** He raids Wang's French Concession house
and finds it emptied. A tip sends him to No. 198 Tianmu Road by the station,
but the landlady, shown Wang's photograph, insists none of her tenants was
this man. Then he interrogates Li Xiaosi — and this is the chapter's good
scene. Li Xiaosi has already told the garrison command that Wang Yaqiao sent
him. But he cannot produce a single detail: not where or when he was briefed,
not where Wang is now, and above all not who he was sent to kill. One moment
it is a Japanese called Shigemitsu, the next a Chinese. **That is Dai Li's
first doubt.**

**The second doubt is the signature.** The grenades left at the scene are
marked "hatchet" in red lacquer. Dai Li's reasoning is the sharpest thing in
the chapter: Wang Yaqiao is a meticulous professional whose jobs are seamless,
so why would he sign one? He concludes the opposite of what the evidence was
planted to suggest — not merely that Wang didn't do it, but that a *second*
assassination organization is operating in Shanghai that he knows nothing
about.

**The dragnet fails.** Checkpoints at every station and wharf, women and
children searched, officials hiring private bodyguards. The reward climbs from
1,000 through 2,000, 3,000, 5,000 to 10,000 and produces nothing.

**Du Yuesheng gets his opening.** Chiang comes secretly to Shanghai on 16
August for the funeral of Soong's mother on the 18th, summons Du Yuesheng and
Huang Jinrong, and asks them to find the killers. Du — who has his own account
to settle with Wang from chapters three and four — names the Hatchet Gang and
offers a real lead: in May one of his men drank with Liu Gang of Wang's
organization, who let slip that "Ninth Master has us on a fine big piece of
business, and it looks like Lushan."

## How it holds up

**CORROBORATED — the frame.** Tang Yulu shot in Soong's place on the morning
of 23 July 1931, Soong reaching shelter alive, the mistaken identity turning on
matching white suits. All documented.

**CORROBORATED — Dai Li's failure, and its length.** The book's insistence that
the investigation stalls completely is right, and understates it if anything.
The case was not broken until **1934**, roughly three years later, when Dai Li
picked up a thread through an assassin who had become a security captain in
Yancheng and sent Shen Zui and Cheng Muyi after him. So the chapter's dragnet,
escalating reward and general humiliation are historically sound.

**CORROBORATED — the funeral.** Ni Guizhen's coffin was brought from Qingdao to
the Soong house on Seymour Road, and the funeral was held in Shanghai on **18
August 1931**, with Chiang among the family present. She was buried that night
beside Charlie Soong at the Hungjao international cemetery. The book's dates
are exact.

**LEGEND, repeated as fact.** Chinese sources widely state that Ni Guizhen, ill
already, was told her son had been shot, and died of the shock. Her death on
23 July 1931 is real and genuinely same-day; the causal link is the part that
circulates without evidence. Worth flagging wherever it appears, since it is
repeated in otherwise reliable-looking places.

**DISCREPANCY — the bodyguards.** The book gives Soong eight plainclothes
escorts; the accounts I reached say six. Minor, but it is the kind of number
the book tends to round upward.

**UNCORROBORATED — Li Xiaosi.** The captured gunman, on whom the chapter's
entire first puzzle depends, appears nowhere I could find. His self-
contradicting interrogation is dramatically excellent and evidentially
unsupported.

**UNCORROBORATED — No. 198 Tianmu Road.** A precise address, which is usually a
sign of a real record behind it. I could not find one.

**UNCORROBORATED — Du Yuesheng's lead.** The scene where Du hands Chiang the
Lushan tip is the hinge of the chapter's plot and has no independent support.
Note also that it makes Du the source of a lead pointing at Wang, which sits
oddly beside the fact that the case actually broke in 1934 through an informer
of Dai Li's own, not through the Green Gang.

**CONTAMINATED SOURCE POOL — a caution.** Several of the Chinese popular
accounts of this shooting describe Wang Yaqiao as head of the *Green Gang*.
He was not; he led the Hatchet Gang, and his relations with the Green Gang
bosses are the subject of chapters three and four. When a source makes that
error, treat everything else it says about him as unchecked.

---

## The structural point

Chapters six and seven together are the book working at its best and its
worst simultaneously. The documented event — two men in white, the wrong one
shot, an investigation that fails for three years — is genuinely strange
enough to carry a chapter. The author's addition, a Japanese false-flag plot
that would have started a war, is what supplies the planted grenades, the
captured prisoner who cannot name his target, and Dai Li's clever inference.
Every one of the chapter's best beats depends on the invented layer. That is
worth knowing before reading it as history.
