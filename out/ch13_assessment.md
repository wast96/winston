# Chapter Thirteen — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 259–280. Sections 44 ("Up Lushan Again"), 45 ("The wind is
bleak, the Yi waters cold" — Jing Ke's line), 46 ("The Bitter News Arrives, and
the Tears Fall Like Rain"). Chapter title: "Sun Fengming, Before and After."*

**This is the chapter where the book is most honest, and it is worth saying so
first.** It does not put Wang Yaqiao in Nanjing. It has him in Hong Kong, at
Deep Water Bay, learning what happened from a newspaper his maid brings in
three days late. That is exactly where he was.

---

## What happens

**Summer 1935, Hong Kong.** Wang has moved from the Peak down to Deep Water Bay
on the south side of the island, partly for the quiet and partly because agents
had come close to killing him at the Peak. He is living as a retired gentleman
and keeping out of sight.

**The backers are impatient.** Chen Mingshu, Chen Jitang and Li Jishen — the
anti-Chiang men who put up the money — keep telephoning to ask whether Hua
Kezhi has moved yet, as though killing Chiang were an errand. The book gives
the funding in detail: several thousand silver dollars each from the principals,
twenty thousand from Wang himself, and **Hu Hanmin personally arranging the
Nanjing business licence and the Party propaganda-department paperwork** for the
news agency once he heard the plan to get at the target under journalist cover.

**Wang wants to go himself**, and is talked out of it. Hua Kezhi comes back from
raising money in Guangxi, embraces him, and says that if Wang insists on coming
to Nanjing it means he does not trust the agency's men — and that Wang showing
his face would draw the agents down on all of them. He describes Sun Fengming's
resolve as iron.

**Sun Fengming's farewell.** He and Cui Zhengyao had agreed to part with smiles
in their eyes, and she has quietly put the parting off and is weeping in the
shadows. He reminds her of the agreement, then of what he told her when they
married: that he had put life and death aside long ago, having watched
countless of his own people die on Japanese bayonets in the northeast. If he
does not go and die, he asks, how many others will have to?

**Then the waiting.** Autumn rain in Hong Kong, Wang unable to sit still after
Hua leaves, watching the papers for word of the Fourth Central Executive
Committee's Sixth Plenum. Nothing on 1 or 2 November. On the morning of the
3rd, a maid brings in the *Sing Tao Daily* with a Shanghai wire: Wang Jingwei
shot at Nanjing.

---

## How it holds up

### The event: corroborated, and correctly named

**1 November 1935**, the opening day of the Sixth Plenum of the Fourth Central
Executive Committee, at Party headquarters in Nanjing. Delegates assembled
outside for a group photograph. Sun Fengming got in on a Chenguang News Agency
press credential and fired **three shots, all of which hit** — left cheekbone,
left upper arm, and the back near the spine. He was shot down and died before
dawn the next morning.

Note that the book gets the meeting's name right. A good deal of Chinese
writing garbles it into the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth CEC; the book does not.

**Chiang was the intended target and was not there.** His own diary for that day
records that he was disgusted by the disorder at the ceremony and went back
inside rather than join the photograph. Contemporaries suspected a pretext, and
the diary is self-reporting on precisely the point where he had most to explain,
so it corroborates his absence rather than his reason.

### The organizer was Hua Kezhi, and he said so at the time

The agency was Hua Kezhi's, run under an assumed identity in Nanjing from 1934.
Wang Yaqiao's authorship of the operation is **not documented**, and it is
contradicted by the best contemporary evidence there is: in the winter of 1936,
from Hong Kong, **Hua Kezhi published an open letter** claiming the operation
for his own group, stating that the intended target had been Chiang, and — by
the account of at least one Chinese source — saying explicitly that it had
nothing to do with Wang Yaqiao. He then posted it to Chiang by registered mail.
That was written while Wang was alive and being hunted for this very thing.

At least three incompatible attributions circulate: Hua's group alone; Wang
Yaqiao; and a version from Zhang Xueliang's own recollections in which the job
was bought by a confidant of Hu Hanmin's, aimed at Cantonese figures. When
three versions compete and none has a document behind it, the honest verdict is
that none is established.

**What is defensible** is exactly what this chapter mostly shows: Wang was
inside the anti-Chiang Hong Kong network around Li Jishen and Chen Mingshu, and
contributed money and possibly weapons to the venture. That level of association
is plausible and widely asserted. Authorship is not.

The book's own instinct here is sound. It shows him funding, worrying and
waiting — not directing.

### The Hu Hanmin detail is interesting

The claim that Hu Hanmin personally handled the agency's registration is
specific enough to be checkable and I could not confirm it. It is worth
flagging, because it converges with the *other* attribution — Zhang Xueliang's,
that the operation was bought by Hu Hanmin's faction. Whether the author knew
he was corroborating a rival account of his own story is impossible to say.

### Cui Zhengyao

That she was arrested and killed after the shooting, along with relatives, is
stated uniformly across the literature. **No source gives a date, a place, or a
manner of death.** That uniform vagueness is a tell that it comes from
recollection rather than record. If the chapter supplies specifics — and its
section titles suggest chapter fourteen turns on a last letter from her — those
specifics are not corroborated by anything I could find.

### One thing the book will get wrong later

Wang Jingwei's back wound is usually said to have carried a bullet that was
never removed and that eventually killed him. **The bullet was removed** — after
he struck the site in a fall in late 1943, it was extracted that December or in
early 1944. He died on **10 November 1944** at Nagoya, of multiple myeloma,
which is not caused by gunshot trauma. His own regime presented the death as
the bullet's work, and that framing has been repeated ever since as fact. If the
book asserts it, it is repeating a piece of wartime martyrology.

## Verdict

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| The shooting: date, occasion, three hits, Sun's death next day | **CORROBORATED** |
| Chiang the target, absent from the photograph | **CORROBORATED** |
| Hua Kezhi and the Chenguang agency as the vehicle | **CORROBORATED** |
| **Wang Yaqiao in Hong Kong, not Nanjing** | **CORROBORATED — the book is right** |
| Wang Yaqiao as organizer | **UNCORROBORATED**, denied by Hua Kezhi in 1936 |
| Wang's financial backing of the agency | **PARTLY** — asserted, contested in detail |
| Hu Hanmin arranging the agency's paperwork | **UNCONFIRMED**, and interesting |
| Sun Fengming's biography | **PARTLY** — one post-1949 commemorative stream |
| Cui Zhengyao killed | **PARTLY** — universally stated, never specified |
