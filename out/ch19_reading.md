## How Du Yuesheng Became Chairman of the Shanghai Flour Exchange

{d} By Huang Bingquan

Before Liberation, Du Yuesheng was not only the number-one magnate of the Shanghai gangs; he was fast coming to stand as a leader of the industrial and commercial world besides. It is said that around the time of the War of Resistance the offices Du held in his single person — in government-and-merchant joint ventures, in private industrial and commercial enterprises, and in the trade associations of every line of business, under such titles as board chairman, chairman of trustees, director, trustee, president, and manager — came to no fewer than 150. The chairmanship of the Shanghai Flour Exchange was one of them. Du Yuesheng had no prior connection whatever with the Shanghai Flour Exchange; how was it, then, that he could vault out of nowhere into its chairmanship? Of the circumstances behind this very few people know; I myself had a hand in the affair at the time, and so set down here a brief account of how it came about.

One evening in May 1936, six or seven friends turned up unexpectedly at my home, among them several brokers of the Shanghai Flour Exchange — Yang Jingcheng, Wang Zhongliang, Zhang Songchun, Gu Zhunian, and others. They told me that the Flour Exchange had lately been caught in the backwash of a sharp surge in cotton-yarn and cloth prices: powerful outside operators, men like Zhu Rushan and Zhong Kecheng, were buying up futures flour of every delivery month in huge quantities, speculating and hoarding, and had touched off a violent run-up. The brokers were beginning to feel the ground shift under them; most had gone short, and if prices climbed any higher they would all be ruined. Their faces were grim as they spoke, and they were sick with worry. It was for this that they had come to me, to work out together some way of coping. At that time, besides running electric-power concerns in the two counties of Nanhui and Chuansha, I also held the concurrent post of warehouse director at the exchange. I had already caught some wind of the matter myself; and hearing them out, I saw at once that if the Flour Exchange were to suffer a short squeeze and suspend trading, not only would many of the brokers who had gone short face the danger of bankruptcy and collapse, but the exchange itself would inevitably have to pay out huge sums and be shaken to its foundations — and my own well-paid second post, my rice bowl, stood a fair chance of being smashed along with it. So I agreed to find them a way through.

The next day I went to the exchange and made a survey, getting a broad grasp of the recent buying and selling on the market, the volume of transactions, the outstanding positions, and the like. Then I went to see Yang Guanbei — at that time manager of the Dada Steamship Company and of the Yangzhou Flour Mill — and told him about it. "What plan have you got?" Yang asked. I said: "Zhu Rushan, Zhong Kecheng, and their sort are always in and out of the Du mansion" — Du Yuesheng's home was then called the Du mansion; it stood on what is today Ninghai West Road, formerly Rue Wagner — "and you are a favored guest there. If you could borrow a single word from him, and in Du's name dump several hundred thousand sacks of flour onto the market, forcing the board price down to the limit that very day, then afterward we watch which way foreign wheat is heading. If foreign wheat rises, we force a settlement and take no loss; if it falls, we cover our shorts on the way down and even turn a profit. This way we can flatten the run-up and rescue many of the brokers from their peril, and at the same time prop up the exchange so that it is not capsized by the great waves. It is a stratagem of fighting poison with poison; the only hard part is whether Du Yuesheng would be willing to let himself be used by us." Yang Guanbei hesitated a moment, then said, "This can very well be done." So we went together to Du Yuesheng's house, and Yang laid the matter before Du to his face. Du said, "If the lot of you can hold firm, I'm willing to help." At that Yang turned to me and said, "Mr. Du has already agreed to help — go ahead and act with a free hand!" And so this fight-poison-with-poison stratagem ripened in a few careless, offhand words.

Early the next morning I gathered the brokers named above and, for the moment, commissioned them to dump onto the market about 500,000 sacks of futures flour of the various months, at the same time quietly putting it about that it was Du Yuesheng who was going short. Contrary to expectation, most of these 500,000 sacks were still swallowed up by Zhu Rushan and his group. The board price fell and then rose again, coming nowhere near the limit-down I had arranged for. So that same afternoon I had no choice but to have them go on dumping in bulk, unloading perhaps another 800,000 sacks; and only then, catching our opponents off guard and unprepared, did we force the board price down to the limit as we had hoped. I recall that the board price that day opened in the morning at about 3.4 yuan a sack and by the afternoon had fallen to about 3.2 yuan a sack, hitting the forced limit-down. Under the rules of the exchange's operating charter, for every 1,000 sacks of flour bought or sold, a margin of 200 yuan — that is, 0.2 yuan a sack — had to be paid in before the opening the next day; and thereafter, whenever the price moved up or down by as much as 0.1 yuan, a further margin of 100 yuan per 1,000 sacks, called the additional margin, had to be collected from whichever side, buyer or seller, stood at a loss. Should the board price take an extraordinary turn, so that within a single day the swing suddenly reached 0.2 yuan, trading had at once to be stopped — that is, halted at the limit — and only after all the margin covering the price difference had been collected in full from buyers or sellers could trading reopen. For every day it was not collected in full, trading stayed shut for another day. But the halt too had a set limit — three days, as I recall; and if by that limit the margin still had not been made good, then, apart from taking such urgent measures as were necessary against the brokers who had not paid up their additional margin, the market had to resume business and trade as usual. I made use of this rule, resolved to force the board price down to the limit. That night Yang Guanbei and I went once more to the Du mansion and explained to Du how we had sold 1,300,000 sacks short to force the market into a halt, and so on; Du neither approved nor objected. At this the exchange's administrative authorities — the two standing directors Zhu Guangcheng and Xu Yongtang — learning that an extraordinary situation had arisen on the market, were beside themselves with alarm, and that very night took a room at the East Asia Hotel and asked me to come and explain. I laid the whole thing out for them: how it had come about, and how it was Du Yuesheng who, to help the exchange strike at the speculators, flatten the run-up, and rescue many brokers from their peril, had come forward to sell short in bulk. I explained to them in detail the measures that ought to be taken from here on and the consequences that might follow, and at last the two men, Zhu and Xu, turned from anger to delight and took their leave.

Nothing more was said that night. The next day I got to the exchange early and hurriedly read the Chicago telegram on American wheat: per bushel — the bushel being the American unit-measure for wheat, each bushel weighing 60 pounds — it had again shot up 5 U.S. cents. The board price on the Flour Exchange generally rose and fell in step with the rise and fall of foreign wheat. Now that foreign wheat had jumped sharply, the exchange side seized on the pretext that the margin had not been collected in full and would not reopen trading. Who would have thought that foreign wheat would rise three days running, unstoppably. I turned the matter over again and again: for this short-selling plan there was no good course but to force a settlement and close out the whole of it. And so, on the third day of the halt, I hurried once more to the Du mansion, set the urgent situation before Du, and, taking a letter of introduction from him, went straight off to the Jiangwan Social Affairs Bureau to ask for its chief, Wu Xingya. Wu was not in at the time, and a certain secretary of the bureau received me. When the secretary saw Du Yuesheng's letter of introduction, he dared not be slow, and asked me how the thing was to be handled. I said, would the bureau please give me a public notice. He asked further how it should be worded. I put forward my suggestion, to the effect that: "Whereas flour bears upon the people's food, no person may be permitted to speculate in it or manipulate it. It is now confidentially reported that outsiders within the Shanghai Flour Exchange are buying up futures flour of every delivery month in large quantities, engaging in speculation and manipulation and hoarding to corner the market. The said exchange is hereby ordered to investigate the matter thoroughly at once and to report." The secretary thought this reasonable enough and saw to it directly. I took the notice, hurried back to the exchange, and posted it on the market floor. In this way the halt, carried out in the name of a thorough investigation, could be kept in force indefinitely and had become perfectly legal.

Two or three days later Yang Guanbei told me that Zhu Rushan, Zhong Kecheng, and the others had been going to the Du mansion day after day, begging Mr. Du to mediate the dispute, and that Du wanted me to come and talk. So I went again with Yang Guanbei to Du's house, and it happened that Zhu and Zhong were there too. Du said to them, "They've come — the two sides of you go and settle it." I then put forward my method: to close out everything at the board price prevailing at the halt. But in the course of the talk Zhu and Zhong seemed to have seen through the secret that it was not really Du Yuesheng who had been selling short, and so they took a hard line, flatly refusing, and said, "Your exchange has broken the law, and we mean to bring suit!" I only answered with a smile and paid them no mind. We stayed deadlocked another two or three days, and after several more rounds of wrangling, Zhu and Zhong, at their wits' end, had in the end no choice but to agree to close out at the halt price with some modest addition. And so this unprecedented storm on the Flour Exchange was at last, under our fight-poison-with-poison contrivance, quietly brought to a satisfactory settlement. Many of the exchange's short-selling brokers thereby escaped loss, and all of them believed that Du Yuesheng had truly delivered them from danger, and were grateful to him.

Two or three years before this, the Association of Interior Flour Mills — organized by Yang Guanbei together with Bian Xiaoqing (manager of the Nanjing Datong Flour Mill) and others — had invited Du Yuesheng to serve as its chairman, and had several times put to the Flour Exchange the demand that "the interior flour mills be permitted to enter into standard trading on the market, to make delivery against their own mills' warehouse receipts, and to be treated on the same footing as the various flour mills of Shanghai and Wuxi." This was because market trading on the exchange had always taken the "Green Warship" brand flour of the Wuxi Maoxin Mill as its standard flour. The flour of every other mill in Shanghai and Wuxi — except for the Red-and-Blue Cart brand of the Shanghai Fufeng Mill, which ranked alongside the Green Warship flour — all had to be marked down in grade against the Green Warship standard. By how much it was marked down was determined by an appraisal committee, made up of the exchange's board of directors together with representatives of the brokers' guild, which fixed the figure once every set period; only the flour produced by the interior mills was not allowed to take part at all. So the mills admitted to the standard could all use their own warehouse receipts, whether they held short positions or not, for spot delivery at month's end, whereas the interior flour mills had to ship their physical stock to Shanghai beforehand, store it in warehouses designated by the exchange, and only then make delivery against the warehouse receipts. In the matter of trading, the former did indeed enjoy many advantages, and the latter were forever aggrieved. And so for a long time there had existed between the two, over standard trading and warehouse-receipt delivery on the Flour Exchange market, an open and covert struggle of squeezing-out and resisting-being-squeezed-out. In the end, because the mills of Shanghai and Wuxi were far greater in strength than the interior mills — and because, moreover, Rong Zongjing and Wang Yuqing of the two mills Wuxi Maoxin and Shanghai Fuxin, and Ning Yuting of the Shanghai Fufeng Mill (succeeded later by Lü Yusun, and finally by Sun Zhongli), all likewise served as directors of the exchange — the moment they raised an objection the board could not uphold fairness and grant the demand of the Association of Interior Flour Mills. Du Yuesheng, Yang Guanbei, Bian Xiaoqing, and the rest, knowing full well that in this quarter their strength was no match, could only swallow their anger and let it be. But they had already come to nurse the wish to bide their time, set foot on the exchange's board, and contend on equal terms with the likes of Rong Zongjing and Wang Yuqing. Through this affair, Du, Yang, Bian, and the others came to regard joining the board as no more than their due; and understanding all the better the principle of striking while the iron is hot, they were hardly going to let slip so rare an opportunity.

Some days later, Yang Guanbei talked the matter over with me in confidence several times, asking me to do the planning that would carry it through. Now I too had my own reckoning in this. For one thing, I was deeply dissatisfied with the exchange's standing director Zhu Guangcheng, who had always kept power in his own hands and acted arbitrarily, obeying in everything the self-serving designs of Rong Zongjing and Wang Yuqing, and had thereby often cost the exchange great sums. I thought to seize this chance to use Du's power to hold them in check, or at the least to keep them from wielding real authority any longer — and this would be to the exchange's good. For another, thinking of myself, if through my mediation the thing could be brought off, then my own relations with Du Yuesheng would draw a step closer, which would be of great help to my future advancement in industry and commerce. So at the time I agreed without the least hesitation to Yang Guanbei's request. I knew that in August of that same year the exchange was to convene its shareholders' meeting and re-elect the directors and supervisors. I knew, too, that about 80 percent of the exchange's shares were held, directly or indirectly, in the hands of the brokers. Just then many of the brokers, grateful to Du Yuesheng and Yang Guanbei for the great service they had done them, were full of goodwill toward the two. I laid hold of these favorable factors and did a thorough piece of work behind the scenes, and in the end got my wish. I still recall that once, when Yang Guanbei was talking it over with me in confidence, he said, "Do you know Mr. Du's temper?" I asked, "What temper?" Yang said, "Mr. Du won't step in unless he means to; but once he has stepped in, he is not the sort to be content below anyone else." I caught his hint and said, "I have a way to make Wang Yiting lose the election and let Mr. Du become chairman." Hearing me out, Yang gave a knowing smile and nodded. The fact was that on the Flour Exchange board there had always been two factions, the power faction and the gentry faction. The power faction, backed by the flour mills, was headed by Rong Zongjing and Wang Yuqing; the gentry faction, drawing on the prestige of its social connections, was made up of Wang Yiting, Gu Xinyi, and men like them. The two factions served each other as the horns of a pincer and made use of one another, and since there was no great conflict between them they had managed to coexist in peace — which was how Wang Yiting had been able to hold the exchange's chairmanship for more than ten years running. Du Yuesheng did not much mind the power faction, but toward the gentry faction he could not help feeling a certain wariness — for they had entered public life and made their names earlier than he, and held a settled social standing, so that Du was obliged to put on a genial face toward them and feign deference. And since there was a difference between Wang Yiting and Gu Xinyi, I reckoned that what Du was wary of lay with Wang, not with Gu. I knew too that Wang Yiting had lost the goodwill of the Flour Exchange, for over the years he had only drawn his money and never attended to affairs, and had all along played the philanthropist at the exchange's expense, to fund his endless "good works." What was more, Wang's second son, Wang Shuxian, had in former years rented a large warehouse of the exchange and opened the Kuangcheng Paper Mill in it, and had run up a heavy debt of unpaid rent, which put Wang Yiting in an awkward position; so that at every shareholders' meeting there were always a good many who opposed him, and the vote by which he was elected director each time was pitifully small. I took aim at Wang's weak points, and secretly, among a set of brokers I knew fairly well, did a piece of canvassing and cultivating to praise Du and disparage Wang, and made sure of a clear majority of the shares. When the shareholders' meeting held its re-election, Wang Yiting did indeed lose. Du Yuesheng, Yang Guanbei, and Hu Yunzhuang (introduced by Du and Yang) — the three of them — were elected directors, and Bian Xiaoqing was elected supervisor. When the directors elected among themselves the chairman and the standing directors, Du was once again elected chairman, and Yang too was elected a standing director. Before long the exchange's internal structure was reorganized, and I as well was promoted by the board to head of the general affairs section.

Du Yuesheng had, besides, a certain sham posture of catching by first letting go, of taking by first giving. When he received the official letter notifying him of his election as chairman, he deliberately declined the post and sent the letter back. The exchange then deputed three directors — Gu Xinyi, Zhu Guangcheng, and Xu Yongtang — as its representatives, and charged me to go with them and press him to take office. Du Yuesheng at first still made a show of refusing, and only after the three representatives had earnestly urged him did he consent to serve. I still remember a few words Du spoke at the time: "I was once a bandit got up as a scholar, and so everyone was afraid of me; now the earthworm has cultivated itself into a dragon, and I've come to have some standing in society. You needn't be afraid of me — from now on, whatever comes up, I'll be sure to help." Du also laid down two conditions on the spot: "First, I won't come to the office to work; second, I won't take a salary." Later events proved that the first — not coming to the office to work — he did indeed keep to; but as for the second, not taking a salary, it was merely an artful trick to deceive people and fish for a good name.
