## Chapter 19

I undressed and went to bed, and chose some dull book, thinking by it to loose my fretted mood; and half an hour later, when my mind had reached upon some fresh matter and drowsiness came stealing over me, I put out the lamp, drew the quilt close, and was just making ready to sleep soundly, when suddenly someone knocked at the door.

"Is it Mei Yingzi?"

"It is Ah Mei."

"Come in." I said, lighting the lamp.

Ah Mei pushed open the door, came to the screen, and I asked:

"What is it?"

"Is there nothing the matter with you?" said Ah Mei.

"Ah." I sat up and asked: "Did Miss Mei come here together with you today?"

"I came first."

"But it was you who told her the address?"

"No."

"Then," I asked again, "she came not long after you had come in?"

"Yes."

"Good." I said. "I understand now. Did you two meet at the hospital, then?"

"I went first," she said; "and then she came."

"When you left, what of her?"

"She was still there."

"Just so," I said. "She followed close upon your heels to come here."

"Is there anything else, Mr. Xu?"

"What did Miss Bai Ping say to you?"

"She said it was nothing serious."

"Did she tell you which quarter she guessed the assassin came from?"

"No." said Ah Mei. "I asked her many things; she seemed not in the least willing to speak of last night's affair."

"Was anyone there?"

"Many people," she said; "but I knew none of them."

"Did Miss Bai Ping give you no message to carry?"

"She only said she would telephone you tonight." I mused a good while, and Ah Mei said:

"Is there nothing else, then?"

"Thank you," I said. But as Ah Mei was going out, I said again:

"Ah Mei, wake me at half past seven tomorrow."

I heard Ah Mei draw the door to, and only then put out the lamp and laid my head down.

—

At half past seven in the morning Ah Mei came to wake me; I rose and washed, and while Mei Yingzi was still fast asleep I threw on my clothes and made ready to go out.

"Will you not take some breakfast?" Ah Mei asked.

"I'll take a bite of something outside."

"Are you going to see Miss Bai Ping now?"

"Yes." I said, and came out; but Ah Mei followed me to the door and told me:

"Last night, when I came out of my room, I heard Miss Mei Yingzi in the young mistress's room, as though rummaging for something."

"…" I mused a while. I could make nothing of it, nor mend it; but half unconsciously I turned back into the room, took my key, locked the door, and said:

"When Miss Mei asks presently, tell her it is my habit to lock the door when I go out."

So saying I came out, took a bite at a little coffee-shop, read a few papers—all with some unimportant news of Bai Ping—and at half past nine, carrying a bunch of fresh flowers, went to Renji Hospital to call on Bai Ping. A nurse asked my name, and led me to first-class ward number twenty; I knocked.

"Come in." It was Bai Ping's own voice.

I went in; Bai Ping was sitting on a sofa set slantwise to the door, in a white satin morning-gown and soft slippers with silvery white fur at the mouth; under the gown she seemed to wear a white cotton patient's dress; her hair was loose, her face unpowdered; her right hand rested on the arm of the sofa, her left held a newspaper, and she seemed to be waiting for me, showing a faint smile, her face lit with a happy radiance as she greeted me.

I gave the flowers to the nurse, went over, and sat down facing her, and said:

"Is it the upper part of the right arm?"

"It is here." she said, pointing to it with her left hand. I moved closer and gently stroked the right arm she had laid on the arm of the sofa; I felt it thickly bandaged within, and said:

"Does it hurt?"

"A little when I move it," she said with a smile. "It is not bad; my fever was gone by last night."

"Is it good here?" The room seemed to me not very spacious, and I said: "Or you might go and stay some days at the Zhongxi Sanatorium."

"No," she said; "the nursing here is very good, and I asked the doctor—he says another day or two and I may leave."

"It would be as well to go home early," I said; "we can have Stephen come and change your dressings and ointment."

"Stephen came yesterday, and told me too to leave tomorrow; he says he can come and see me every day. He is well acquainted with the doctors here, so he too is very attentive." she said. "I think a few days in hospital has its own interest."

"Do you know what the assassin's background is?"

"Who knows," she said; "and I have no wish to know."

"Will you not be in danger hereafter?"

"I am thinking of going to Tianjin for a while."

"To Tianjin?"

"Or perhaps Hong Kong."

"Is it someone who advises you to?"

"It is my own thought."

"For the time being you had best rest a while."

"Naturally."

A nurse brought in a bunch of fresh white roses; on the card was an odd Japanese name—I cannot now recall it, but it was something like Miyama Toshimi.

"A Japanese?" Bai Ping asked.

"I said that you had lain sleepless last night, and slept only after taking a draught this morning."

"Has he gone?"

"He said he would come again in the afternoon."

"Very good." Bai Ping said, and turning her gaze to my face, said with a smile:

"Are you not pleased?"

"Bai Ping, I think you had best go to Hong Kong, to spare yourself the trouble of these Japanese."

"They are no more than a herd of pigs; people say they are toying with me, but I can believe I am toying with them." She laughed: "People say I am their puppet, but I feel that they are mine."

"That is too proud, Bai Ping. Is that not just where the danger lies?"

"No." said Bai Ping firmly, and fell silent in thought.

"Go to Hong Kong, Bai Ping; I will go with you," I said, low and slow.

"Hong Kong?" she laughed. "Do you think the Pacific war will not break out?"

"It will not," I said. "Does Japan yet dare declare war on America?"

"But suppose someone says I am a Japanese spy?"

"Argue it out."

"When the bullet marks me for a spy, with what shall I argue it out?"

I was silent; I could find no words in answer. After a while she patted my shoulder and said: "My friend, be easy. My affairs are all my own. Trust me and forgive me, and you are my best friend."

Still I was silent.

"Tell me, did Mei Yingzi follow close upon Ah Mei?"

"I am sure it was so."

"And slept in my room?"

"Yes." I said. "Ah Mei says she seemed to be rummaging your things in the night."

"She did not sleep in your room, then?" she said teasingly.

"What talk is this?"

"I mean she might well be pleased to sleep in your bed, and send you to sleep in my room."

"What sort of mind is that?"

"Has she not forever some novel fancy?" Bai Ping laughed.

I made no answer; I only felt Bai Ping's manner today was beyond my expectation. She said again:

"Did Mei Yingzi think it strange, finding you at my place?"

"I was as if in a dream, and did not see her astonishment."

"You told her you had not gone back to the country."

"Yes." I said. "But I bade her tell no one, not even Stephen and Helen."

"She promised?"

"Yes; she is to say I hurried back on hearing of your shooting." I said. "But she bids me move out of your place."

"Rightly," said Bai Ping. "I move back home, Stephen comes daily to see me; if you lodge at my place, is that not proof that you did not hurry back on hearing of my shooting?" She sighed faintly: "So I am in no haste to move home."

"Then in a day or two I shall move out of your place."

"Very good," she said lightly.

I came out at half past eleven, with many an unsolved doubt in my heart; Bai Ping, Mei Yingzi—both had become, of a sudden, problems to me, and I loathed their mystery and their guile. I resolved to move back to my own home tomorrow and have as little to do with them as might be; yet at the same time I felt Bai Ping's prospects were truly dark, and though she strove her utmost not to speak of her troubles, in friendship it seemed I could not but help her to a way out. But what political connection could she have, after all? My thoughts wavered in bewilderment.

I returned to Bai Ping's apartment; Mei Yingzi had already gone out.

That night I put my things in order, and the next day moved back home. At ten in the forenoon I telephoned Bai Ping to tell her I had moved back, and bade her telephone me if anything arose. The third day too I did not go to see Bai Ping, nor did I meet Mei Yingzi; but at nine at night I had a telephone call from Bai Ping. She told me she would move home the next morning, and bade me come to dinner at seven in the evening.

The next day at seven in the evening I went to keep Bai Ping's dinner engagement, in a very tranquil frame of mind, meaning to study with her that night the cause of her shooting, the clues to the assassin, and the course of her life to come.

That day my spirits were very good, my mood very serene, and I had even the humour to change into a neater suit and pick out a more becoming necktie; I smoked a cigarette and took a car to Bai Ping's. Springing from the car, I went lightly upstairs. Outside the door I already heard the noisy sound of voices within, and when Ah Mei opened the door I at once heard Mei Yingzi's voice, and said softly to Ah Mei:

"Mei Yingzi?"

Ah Mei laughed, and said:

"They are all come; you alone are wanting."

So it was a dinner-party after all; I handed Ah Mei my overcoat and hat, straightened my tie, and went into the sitting-room.

"Ah, Xu, it is truly a long time since we met." Mei Yingzi, as though after a long parting, was the first to shake my hand; then the Stephens and the Manfields, mother and daughter, exchanged their greetings with me. Helen looked more dazzling than before, and in her smile I could no longer find the low, dreaming grace of her by the water in Jessfield Park. Her mother had grown stouter than before. The Stephens were little changed. As we all sat down, Mei Yingzi looked pointedly at me and said:

"You've grown browner, and seem a little stouter; country life has had the virtue of a tonic on you."

"And Cishan?" Mrs. Stephen asked. "Did you not ask her to come and play in Shanghai?"

"I came in too great a hurry; the moment I had Mei Yingzi's telegram I hastened here." I glanced at Bai Ping; she wore an ample black cotton cheongsam, seemingly newly made on purpose for her wounded arm. I went over and gently pressed her right arm; I felt the bandaging was still very thick, and said:

"Do you still need such bandaging?"

"It saves it from being jarred," said Stephen.

"Stephen bandaged it for me just now." Bai Ping showed a grateful smile.

"We were just saying that Bai Ping looks the lovelier in this dress," said Mei Yingzi.

Bai Ping had indeed a certain other grace today; she wore no rouge, but seemed to have powdered very carefully, and I noticed particularly that her skin could take a heavier touch of powder; in bearing and gesture—whether it was the dress that did it, or that she had changed of set purpose—she seemed unlike the run of city girls. Ever since American films spread widely through China, fashionable girls had all aped the airs of the American film-stars; at first it had seemed a novelty, but with time one grew used to it and thought nothing of it; Bai Ping too, ordinarily, had of course much the same airs; but today she seemed altogether changed. It came to me suddenly that she was like someone, though I could by no means think whom, until at last I saw that she was like the Cishan of my imagining, and could not help laughing.

Before me, Bai Ping always seemed to have some slight reserve toward Mei Yingzi, and Mei Yingzi before me some slight reserve toward Bai Ping; yet when the two were before me together, as on an occasion like today's, their feeling for each other always showed itself as surpassing that for others—and today most plainly of all. Since the day I had seen Bai Ping in the hospital, I knew not what talk had passed between them. Mei Yingzi seemed at every turn to be concerned for Bai Ping's arm, doing the host's offices in Bai Ping's stead; and suddenly it made me suspect that Mei Yingzi's coming that night, and her advising me to move out of the place, were things Bai Ping had known of beforehand—perhaps even at Bai Ping's own prompting; that Bai Ping, ashamed to bid me move away herself, had sent Mei Yingzi to say it. There was in my heart a discomfort past telling.

Ah Mei came to call us to dinner; we went into the dining-room, and I sat beside Helen. Though Helen's manner toward me kept a greater distance than before, she still talked a great deal. She told me happily that her singing had lately much improved, and told me she felt the theory I had once urged, of learning to cross the plateau, was right, and that she now seemed to have crossed that plateau. She bade me come to her home; she would sing for me. She said too, with some conceit, that in Shanghai her singing now had no rival. I mentioned several Chinese girls, also favoured pupils of Professor Paci; she would criticize, without the least reserve, this one's voice as too coarse, that one's throat as not strong enough, another's tone as too devoid of feeling. From first to last she never spoke with me of thought or philosophy. She was now no longer at all the girl she had been.

After dinner I talked much with the Stephens; Mrs. Stephen was forever urging me to give up the single life. She said she was not opposed to the single life, any more than she was opposed to vegetarianism; but if a single man could never forget his interest in girls, that was as absurd as a vegetarian forever hankering after meat and fish; such forced beliefs, she said, were all a sin, and would leave one a painful remorse in the days to come.

At nine o'clock the company scattered. I had many a vexation in my heart. I thought of Mei Yingzi's dissembling today, feigning a reunion after long parting—so lifelike, so beautifully done; I thought of the strange bond between Bai Ping and her; I thought how today's dinner-engagement was unlike what I had imagined before coming; but in last night's talk why had Bai Ping not told me? In sum, gathering it all up, I felt there was no question that Bai Ping's feeling for me had changed, and that Mei Yingzi's bidding me move out was Bai Ping's hint—and this too became, half unconsciously, my settled conclusion.

Therefore, from that day on, I grew comparatively distant from Bai Ping; I seldom went to see her, only now and then telephoning to ask after her. But there was no news of her going to Tianjin or Hong Kong, nor any decision to return to the dance-hall; she only told me she would let me know once she had decided.

Helen came to seek me no more; Mei Yingzi I met more rarely still, only once or twice at Stephen's or at Helen's. I did go to Helen's for dinner; they treated me with much courtesy; I heard Helen's lovely singing, and the success of the Christmas concert was now past question. Stephen, by his wife's account, was very busy; not only did he not come to see me, but whenever I went to his house I never once met him. With Mrs. Stephen I talked the more congenially; her thought and temper were normal and firm, and I became a frequent guest in her drawing-room, where one talk would run very long; this affection was natural, lovely and warm, and it was the first time I had known the friendship the ancients called "insipid as water"—with a deep mutual understanding, a mutual accord, and, most precious of all, the golden distance. This distance of friendship is the same as the distance of beauty; it is like the focus on a camera—a little more is too much, a little less not enough—and it made me see what one may call the art of friendship. I greatly repented my former over-nearness to Helen, and greatly repented moving to lodge at Bai Ping's; it was these that had lost us the fitting distance and spoiled our finest friendship. News of Helen I could still hear now and then at Mrs. Stephen's. News of Bai Ping grew ever more remote, until one day the papers carried word that Bai Ping had returned to the Paramount to dance. I went to her home to see her; she was out, and I talked a while with Ah Mei. Ah Mei told me the cause of Bai Ping's shooting had now been made plain: it was wholly a quarrel over a woman between a Japanese merchant and a Japanese soldier, the soldier having sent someone to shoot the merchant and struck her by mistake; so now there was no trouble at all, and she could return to the dance-hall to dance. I went out and bought a flower-basket to send her, and that night went to the dance-hall to pay the customary duty of a little support. But Bai Ping was extraordinarily busy, and when at last she sat at my table she seemed much put out, saying, in effect, that in my eyes too she was after all only a dancing-girl. I could find no way to answer her, and in less than five minutes I went home. Afterwards too I went to see her, but she was neither at home nor at the dance-hall; when I telephoned her home at night, either she had not come back or was already asleep; and having no particular business, I did not wake her, but only left my regards with Ah Mei. In the amusement columns of the papers I saw news of Bai Ping now and then; her dance-patrons were no longer confined to the Japanese, and all her patrons now respected her freedom—a state, among dancing-girls, that was like a politician standing above all parties. So brilliant and so busy a star I naturally could not, and did not wish to, seek out often.

My life was indeed comparatively tranquil; very serenely I held the reins and ordered my own life as I would.

Yet such a life did not last long. A great event that shook the world broke out. It not only disordered my life; it broke off too the realization of Helen's concert plans, and wrecked the lovely setting and serenity of Mrs. Stephen; it stirred society, and overturned besides history and the map; and since the War of Resistance began, it appraised anew the meaning of our nation's shed blood.
