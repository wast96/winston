## Reunion

In that same year, the third year of Bunkyū, the autumn of Kyoto was deepening.

For Hijikata Toshizō, vice-commander of the Shinsengumi, the days were as crowded as ever; but this man had a curious habit, and there were days when he shut himself in his room for half a day together and let no one else in.

In the corps,

{j} "the vice-commander's gone to ground,"

{j} the men would mutter behind his back. Every one of them found this holing-up unsettling.

(What is he brooding over?)

The unease was that someone else might be about to be purged.

That day a fine drizzle fell from morning on.

Little of the ninth month was left. Some days before, the Shinsengumi had already held the funeral of its commander, Serizawa Kamo. The cause of death, given out both within the corps and to the Aizu clan, was illness. (Among the newer corpsmen there were some who guessed that men of Chōshū had set upon him.) But it was all a thing finished. And a thing finished, in the inner life of the Shinsengumi, was wholly of the past. Not one of the corpsmen had the habit of looking back on yesterday. Every man lived his day for dear life.

That afternoon Okita Sōji came back from patrolling the town and, once he had stepped up onto the entrance-hall floor, stopped on a sudden thought a trainee corpsman attached to the commander's staff,

{j} "Hijikata-san?"

{j} was he in, he was asking. The trainee's face took on a faint shadow.

"He is in, that much he is, but,"

"But? What is it?"

"Well…"

"Give me a plain answer, if you please."

The trainee seemed unable to put it well, but it appeared that Toshizō, from the morning on, had been refusing to let any corpsman into his room.

"Ah, the holing-up."

Okita caught on at last and burst out laughing. A bad hobby, said his face.

Okita alone knew what sort of work Toshizō was about, shut up in his room. Even Kondō had not caught on to this secret.

Okita crossed the corridor and, when he had come to the east side of the inner court, shifted his sword to the other hand and halted. He was before the door of Toshizō's room.

"It's Okita,"

{j} he called through the paper screen. And having called, he set his ear mischievously to it, trying to catch the sounds within.

Just as he had foreseen, there came the sound of something being hurriedly put away. Presently Toshizō's throat-clearing was heard, and,

{j} "Is that you, Sōji?"

{j} he said.

Okita slid the screen open.

"What do you want?"

Toshizō was facing the cusped window. Before the window, a single inkstone-box. In the alcove to the right, a single sword-rack: that was the whole of the furnishing, a bleak set of surroundings altogether like Toshizō.

"As I was patrolling the town today,"

{j} said Okita, seating himself,

"I met a rare person. Try guessing who it was."

"I am busy."

"How splendid for you."

Okita reached his hand slowly toward Toshizō's knee. Toshizō started and made to fend him off, but the thing was already whisked into Okita's hand.

A booklet.

Okita riffled through it. The pages, in Toshizō's crooked, wriggling hand, were crammed close with haiku.

"Master Hōgyoku"—Toshizō's poet-name—"you have been most diligent, I see."

"Idiot."

Toshizō went red.

Okita tittered. This young man knew: it was Toshizō's shameful part, this composing of haiku in secret.

"Sōji, give it back."

"I will not. The great Hijikata Toshizō, vice-commander of the Shinsengumi, once a month takes to it like a bout of the ague and turns back into Master Hōgyoku; and hidden from the corpsmen, at that, he labors over his lines. The men, never dreaming their vice-commander is making haiku, guess at all manner of things and fret. It is not very good, you know, to keep everyone on edge over you."

"Sōji."

Toshizō reached out a hand.

Okita sprang back the width of two tatami and sat peering into the poem-book.

Toshizō's rustic haiku were, for the Hijikata house, a sort of family tradition, along with the Ishida Powder.

His grandfather took the poet-name Sangetsutei Sekiha, and in the Bunka and Bunsei years he was widely known throughout the Hino-juku country of Bushū, keeping elegant company with such famous poets of the day as Natsume Seibi of Asakusa in Edo and the nun Matsubara-an Seifu of Hachiōji-juku.

His late father, Hayato, had no such pastimes; but the eldest brother, Tamesaburō, took the poet-name Sekisui the Blind, and though his name did not carry as far as Edo, in the district round about he was widely known.

Tamesaburō, eldest son though he was, did not succeed to the headship of the Hijikata house; the second son, Kiroku, took it up and bore Hayato, the hereditary name. This was because Tamesaburō was blind; by the law of that day, a disabled man could not inherit. Tamesaburō would say, in the ordinary way, even to Toshizō,

{j} "It's a good thing my eyes went dark. If even one of them could see, I'd not die on the tatami."

So he used to say. He was a bold blind man: in his youth he went to Fuchū-juku to buy a courtesan, and on the way home a downpour breached the Tamagawa embankment and the ferry stopped running. While everyone stood staring blankly at the flood, Tamesaburō stripped himself bare in a trice, tied his clothes atop his head, and,

{j} —A sighted man is the helpless one.

{j} with that he plunged into the muddy torrent and, cutting the water overhand, swam clear to Ishida, where the house stood: such was the tale he left behind.

He was a master of the gidayū chant, too, beyond a gentleman-amateur's skill; but his forte was still haikai, and he made bold, free verses just as his nature was.

Toshizō was under that influence.

And yet, little as it suited this man's temper, the verses he turned out were, most of them, limp and womanish things. Good verses they were not. Rather, even to the amateur eye of Okita, they were frightfully bad, hackneyed one and all.

"Heh."

Okita laughed in the back of his throat.

{p} My palm for an inkstone: the mountains in spring.

(Through what part of that head does a verse this bad come to be born?)

{p} Up through a screen of rape-blossoms climbs the morning sun.

{p} Plum blossoms, seeming no thing of this human world.

{p} A spring night: talk with nothing hard in it.

(Wretched stuff.)

Okita was growing delighted. As Okita saw it, this was the one endearing thing Toshizō had in him. Were Toshizō skillful at his verses too, there would be no saving him.

"Well?"

Toshizō looked bashful, and yet for all that he was hoping for a word of praise from Okita.

"Ah, this one is good."

Okita pointed to a verse.

"Which, which?"

"'Out on the road on the corps's business: a spring moon.' A verse just like the vice-commander of the Shinsengumi."

"Is that so. That's an old one, though. If there's another you take to, say so."

"Yes,"

{j} and having dropped his eyes to the page, he burst out laughing of a sudden.

"'Off to pay the New Year calls; the sky, a black kite, a paper kite.'"

"Ho, that one takes your fancy?"

"Well, more or less."

Okita read on, still holding back his laughter.

(This one's dreadful too.)

{p} A bush warbler, and even the duster's beat falls still.

"Does it take your fancy?"

"Hijikata-san, you're a dear."

Okita, before he knew it, looked earnestly into his face.

"What rot are you talking."

Toshizō hastily stroked his own face.

Okita riffled on and on, and came at last to rest his eyes on the final verse.

To judge by the state of the ink, it was this verse he had been laboring over just now.

(An extraordinary verse, this.)

In earnest, he sat gazing at it.

Toshizō peered over without thinking, and,

{j} "Ah, this won't do,"

{j} and took it up. And no sooner had he taken it than he bundled away brush and inkstone and poem-book, and,

{j} "Sōji, off with you now. I'm busy."

{j} he said. Okita did not stir.

"That verse.—"

{j} and, watching Toshizō's face with care,

{j} "whom did you write into it?"

"I don't know."

{p} Know her, and be lost;

{p} know her not, and not lost: the road of love.

{j} so it stood, written plain in the poem-book.

***

Toshizō left the barracks at once. Where he was going, that much at least he had told to Okita alone.

Never had Toshizō thought the workings of the human heart so strange as in that moment today.

The truth was, in the morning he had thought of Osae. And once he thought of her, it became unbearable.

With Osae, younger sister of Saruwatari, Lord of Sado, the priest of the Rokusha Myōjin at Fuchū in Bushū, he had, in his Kantō days, lain together a number of times.

Women he had "known" in that way there had been several, enough to count on his fingers, in the Toshizō of those years; but love he had never learned. There was Kozakura, the bell-shaking shrine-maiden; and Onobu, daughter of the Senjubō at Hachiōji; and, a history Toshizō would rather not recall, at eleven he had for a time been sent as a shop-boy to the Matsuzakaya, a draper's in Ueno in Edo. About then a maidservant there taught him the ways of man and woman, and when the head clerk found it out he was sent back to his birth-house. But all of it had grown into a faded past.

(Only Osae. …)

She would come back to mind. Though that, too, was only now and then.

(She was a fine woman.)

In Kyoto he had made the rounds, of Shimabara and of Gion alike. But not even the pleasure-women of Kyoto, famed for their skill in bed, had left in Toshizō's body a memory as strong as Osae's.

(Still, it is a thing gone by.)

So he told himself.

And so, knowing—by the custom of the Saruwatari house—that Osae had gone up to Kyoto and entered the service of the Kujō family, he yet made no move to go and see her.

(I am a man who cannot love.)

To the coldness of his own nature he had reconciled himself.

(That is what makes a man.)

So too he had thought. But this morning, in a dawn dream, he had held Osae in his arms. And as he lay savoring the memory of the dream even after waking, of a sudden something people call longing surged up in him and threw the Toshizō rolling in his bedding into confusion.

(So I had such feeling in me after all?)

He rose and dressed and made to take up the corps's business, but everything had grown wearisome to him. This happened to Toshizō now and then.

At such times he would shut himself away and make verses. Not thinking them good even himself, while he composed he let no one near.

A verse came.

That was the verse.

But man and woman are strange things: Okita Sōji, so he said, had run bang into Osae in the town today. The place was Kiyomizu.

Osae, in the dress of one come to worship, was coming down the Kiyomizu slope. Before the temple gate of the Anshō-in she passed Okita and his men. Osae called out to stop them. Okita did not know Osae, but Osae knew him.

{j} —I should like to meet Hijikata-san, just once.

{j} so Osae said, and asked him to send a guide-servant to the barracks later and let Toshizō know of it.

"Okita-sama, you'll undertake it for me, won't you?"

She spoke in the brisk, peremptory way of a Bushū woman. Okita, hearing the speech of a Kantō woman after so long, was glad of it.

"Undertake it I will."

"Be sure of it, now."

Osae went off. Her hair was in the ordinary high Shimada, and her dress, too, was in the samurai style, so Okita said.

The former regent Kujō Hisatada, whom Osae served, had been seen as pro-shogunate over the affair of the Princess Kazunomiya's marriage down to the shogun, and was now shorn and living in seclusion at Kujō village. Toshizō was a little troubled as to what Osae's circumstances might have become along with it.

Before long the guide-servant came to fetch him to the barracks.

Guided by that man, Toshizō set out from Mibu.

As he was leaving, Okita said only one thing.

"Hijikata-san, the Kyoto of these days is a city of monsters."

He meant, no doubt, do not let down your guard. Okita seemed to have some uneasy foreboding.

On and on eastward along Ayanokōji, out as far as Fuyamachi, and there at last up to the north. An alley on the west side of it.

A shabby, aged rented house.

(So this is where she lives?)

He was shown to an inner room and sat down. Looking round at the furnishings, it seemed, somehow, the lone dwelling of a woman.

"It's only coarse tea, but,"

{j} and the servant brought out tea.

"Where is the Lady Osae?"

"Aye, at once,"

{j} he said, and hedged.

"Is this the Lady Osae's dwelling?"

"No; her dwelling, I am told, is a good way further down."

"'I am told,' you say; then you do not know it yourself."

"Aye."

He seemed a menial hired by the day.

In proof of which, before long he vanished off somewhere.

An hour passed.

(Strange.)

The place was beginning to grow dim. Suspicion stirring in him, Toshizō rose and, first of all, tried opening the old clothes-chest.

It was empty.

Going out to the front, he asked the wife of the neighboring house who the landlord hereabouts might be, and she answered, "Aye, it's one Nodaya Tahee of Muromachi."

"Is this house standing empty?"

"Aye, it stood empty a long while, but of late I hear some kuge lord's retainer has taken it."

(Just so: it is monsters that dwell in Kyoto.)

Once more he went inside.

Before long the lattice door opened, and Osae came through the earthen-floored passage, her paper lantern still lit.

"………?"

Toshizō sat on in the pitch-dark room without stirring.

"Hijikata-sama?"

It was, past all mistaking, Osae's voice.

"I am late in coming."

"Well, now."

Toshizō lowered his voice,

{j} "what sort of contrivance is this?"

"Here?"

Osae said brightly,

"when I come away from service, I use this as a place to rest."

"Do you truly use it?"

"Yes."

"For that, the chest is empty. And the matting, too, is somehow musty."

Toshizō rose warily and stepped down onto the earthen floor. He and Osae looked into each other's faces.

"Truly, now,"

{j} and he laid a finger to Osae's chin,

"your face, at least, is past mistaking for the woman they called Osae of the Rokusha Myōjin in Bushū; but since coming up to Kyoto, hasn't a tail sprouted somewhere on you?"

"What hateful things you say."

"Hateful, is it."

Toshizō smiled with his eyes alone.

"The Kyoto of late is a fearsome place. Kantō woman though you are, come to think of it, the Saruwatari are a shrine-house with deep ties to Kyoto, and a house of hereditary scholars of the National Learning besides. And you are in a kuge's service. It's not to be ruled out that you've been dyed with some strange doctrine."

"Dear me."

Osae looked put off.

"And what has that to do with this rented house?"

"That you lured me out and set a trap in this rented house."

"I am going home."

Osae made to turn on her heel.

"I'll not let you go."

Toshizō seized Osae's hand.

"No. I am aghast. I came, I thought, to meet the Toshizō-san of the days when he told me to call him Toshi; and what waited here for me was that outrageous monster, Hijikata Toshizō, vice-commander of the Shinsengumi."

"Don't move."

Toshizō's suspicion cleared in an instant.

He made to draw Osae to him. From her hand the lantern dropped.

Osae twisted her body away.

"No. No, I will not."

"I was wrong,"

{j} is not a thing Toshizō says.

Only, he wished to hasten the taking of her. Once their bodies were joined, this unease would loosen. Toshizō wanted quickly to turn this stranger before his eyes back into his own woman.

"Lie down."

{j} He dragged her up into the room.

"Go back to the night of the Rokusha Myōjin festival. I'm the scoundrel of Ishida, out of Hino-juku,"

{j} he said, as if coaxing her.

"That is all, already—"

"Already?"

"Too late. I will not, now."

Even so, Osae's resistance grew weaker by degrees; but still her body was stiff.

(Strange.)

The doubt he felt at that lingered still, faintly, in the back of Toshizō's mind. Something in Osae's words and manner was coarsened.

Before, she had been a more refined and elegant woman. That was what had pleased the Toshizō of his peasant-swordsman days. Osae had surely changed. Service to a kuge in Kyoto ought to have polished her the more, so what could be the reason for this?

(In time, once I see her body, I shall know.)

The movement of Toshizō's hand grew gentle. Worn out, perhaps, Osae's body settled onto the matting.
