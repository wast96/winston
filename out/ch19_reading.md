## February

Chen Qianli found a room next door and had the comrades go in one by one, to assign each his task alone. To tell the truth, Lin Shi rather disapproved of the way he was going about it. The heart of a mission, Lin Shi felt, was what most needed keeping secret; but that had already been conveyed to them all, and the parceling-out of tasks was a thing that might just as well be raised at a full meeting of the group, for all to talk over together.

Who should be sent to Guangzhou, for one—Lin Shi felt this could perfectly well be put to the meeting, and if someone volunteered, so much the better. A man, after all, knows himself best. For a task like the Guangzhou one, he thought, Liang Shichao would surely step forward of his own accord. Yes—a Red Army commander would certainly call it asking for the front. And Liang Shichao was well suited to it, Lin Shi thought. First, he was a Cantonese; second, as a soldier he was good in action and kept his head in danger; and third, if it could be managed, the organization might, once the task was done, transfer him straight to the Soviet areas—which would be no bad thing, for his experience of soldiering was much needed there.

Chen Qianli, however, did not think Liang Shichao well suited. He had already settled on someone in his own mind—only, he said, he needed to look into it a little more.

There was, Lin Shi felt, a touch of the by-the-book dogmatist about Chen Qianli. Why call them into a little room one at a time to hand out their tasks? He was formidable, no question; his mind could sort things into compartments like the drawers of a cabinet, laying men and matters out in perfect order. Had Chen Qianli not shown himself so quick and decisive in the bank operation, Lin Shi might not have agreed to let him do it this way. The group was a collective; carrying out the task mattered, but so did unity and trust.

Still, let him try it his way. In a crisis such as this, the organization would surely have weighed the matter with care before sending Chen Qianli. His gifts Lin Shi had seen for himself: in half an hour he had thought out the means of moving the gold from the bank's safe-deposit box. There had been flaws in some of the details, but to have devised an effective plan of action at so urgent a moment was no small thing.

Before the other comrades went in, Lin Shi and Chen Qianli talked alone in the little room for a while. When Chen Qianli said to him, “I can't tell you all of what I have in mind yet—some of it is still vague, not yet formed,” he considered a moment and then agreed. Establishing a courier line involved a great deal of fiddling work, above all at its two ends: the danger always lay in the process of making outside contact. Just as with a water pipe, the trouble most often comes at the elbow-joint where two lengths meet. To cover that process well took an enormous amount of work. Lin Shi had been a secret courier for a long time, and had later been responsible for setting up and maintaining courier lines; in this matter he was, beyond any doubt, an expert.

When Chen Qianli spoke with them one by one, Lin Shi at first sat in and listened; at some points he grasped Chen Qianli's intent, at others he could not follow his thinking. But he came gradually to see that Chen Qianli was dividing them into two sets. Some could ignore the agents' surveillance—if they did notice a plainclothesman on their heels, they might pretend to be quite unaware of him. The others were to be most careful in their every move.

In the brief span of these talks Chen Qianli also passed on some methods for spotting and shaking off surveillance—in such things he was an expert too. He reminded each of them to make it a habit, every time they went out on business, to go several times over the motions of losing a tail. Qin Chuan'an said during his talk that for days now he had had the feeling that, closely as these agents seemed to watch, in fact they watched loosely, and shaking off the tail each time he went out was easy enough. But Chen Qianli said to him: if the enemy is plainly watching you and yet watches loosely, letting you slip out of their sight so casually, then somewhere you have not noticed, something must have gone wrong. There was truth in that, and Qin Chuan'an thought of Cui Wentai.

Lin Shi reckoned that Chen Qianli had prepared two schemes, an open line and a hidden one. After listening a while, he pushed open the door and went out into the outer room.

Those who had had their talk with Chen Qianli left the coal yard under cover of darkness, out into the wind and snow. Some of them, in the ten-odd days to come, would fight alone, sparring with the enemy under the enemy's own gaze—a contest not only of wits and nerve but of the mind. Chen Qianli had set out for them several possibilities for the enemy's next move, and devised counters to each; but such reckonings-in-advance often ran up against a great many surprises.

Chen Qianyuan came out of the inner room too. Lin Shi did not know what the two brothers had said in there; he saw only that Chen Qianyuan seemed somewhat stirred. Outside, Dong Huiwen was waiting for him, and the two of them left the yard together.

Wei Dafu went in. Now only Ling Wen and Yi Junnian were left. The day's operation had given Lin Shi a new sense of Ling Wen. This woman comrade had stayed cool throughout, and was very quick to adapt when things arose. If he himself could travel far, to take her with him to Guangzhou might be the best course of all. Why, of all things, had he had to injure his leg? He thought it over, and decided he ought to have another word with Chen Qianli before Chen sent for Ling Wen.

The inner room was small, and it too had a stove, the fire burning bright—a coal yard was never short of coal. Two stools stood by the stove, the kettle steaming hot, and two buns were toasting on the ringed lid of the firebox.

The talk was drawing to a close, and the subject had left the mission behind. Wei Dafu was a little put out. “What do you mean, weak and wavering? Who says so? That I like to grumble now and then—that I can't deny. But to say I waver—when has the Party ever given me a task I didn't carry out to the last jot, never once shortchanging it? Don't go by how I look like a cat curled at the stove most of the time; when it counts, whether I stick my neck out or pull it in, I can play the stout fellow too.”

“There's something in that,” Chen Qianli said with a smile. “A man's nature is what it is; but when the crucial moment truly comes, it depends on whether he is firm within. If his mind is clear and his resolve set, then for all his slackness the rest of the time, at the crucial moment the stove-cat turns into a tiger—and that can work a surprising, marvelous effect.”

“Just you wait—there'll come a day when I'll show you.”

Wei Dafu snatched up a bun toasted golden-brown and took a big bite. “A cold bun—it's only good once it's toasted crisp.” And with that parting shot he went out.

Chen Qianli came out behind Wei Dafu and was just about to speak to Ling Wen when Lin Shi got in first: “Let me have a word with you first.”

Seeing the two of them go into the inner room, Wei Dafu nodded a goodbye to Yi Junnian, pushed open the door, and strode off into the thickening wind and snow.

“I don't think Chen Qianli is telling the truth.” Seeing the room empty, Yi Junnian edged his chair closer to Ling Wen. “That business at the bank this morning can't have been merely to lure Cui Wentai into giving himself away.”

He lit another cigarette and went on: “When Comrade Lin Shi telephoned the bank, he certainly wasn't playacting. So there must have been something important in that safe-deposit box. Can it be he never took it out at all, that the thing is still in the box?”

“The box was given up. Before we left the bank, Comrade Lin Shi settled the rent on it.”

“Then the thing really was carried off by Cui Wentai, and Chen Qianli only said what he did to set everyone's mind at ease. Nothing else happened at the bank?”

“No—” Then Ling Wen suddenly remembered. “Oh, yes—I ran into Miss Tao at the bank. That woman from the Longhua detention house, the one I told you about.”

“How odd that she should turn up there.”

Ling Wen did not tell Yi Junnian that she had not, in fact, gone into the vault. Chen Qianli had told her the night before that from now on, even among members of the group, no one was to share the content of a mission or the details of an operation crosswise. “A ship at sea may always meet a storm or strike a reef, and so its holds are kept sealed off from one another; then even if one place springs a leak, the ship will not go down.”

“This morning, sitting in the café, I watched through the window as you got out and went into the bank. You've grown truly haggard these past days, thinner—” Yi Junnian reached out to touch Ling Wen's cheek, and she pushed his hand away.

Ling Wen knew that this push of hers would very likely hurt Old Yi. A month or two before, she might not have done it, however uncomfortable it made her feel. There were moments when she was even, now and then, moved by the things Old Yi said to her and did for her. To her, Yi Junnian was more than a superior, more than a comrade seasoned in struggle. In her most lost and difficult hour, Yi Junnian had appeared before her.

After the failure of the Great Revolution, the underground network to which she and Long Dong belonged had been dealt a crushing blow. Long Dong had withdrawn in haste amid the crisis, while she herself had been seized by the enemy and held for several months, until a lawyer from the Relief Society bailed her out. But when she got home, all she found was a letter Long Dong had written her before he left.

The house had been searched by the enemy, and every scrap of paper with writing on it had been taken away; but the letter had been left in the false bottom of a flowerpot, and the flowerpot stood on the outside windowsill. That meant Long Dong had come back—of course he would come back.

In the letter Long Dong said he would surely come back for her. But the network had been broken by the enemy, and no further word of Long Dong ever came; she waited, all alone, for close to three years. In the second year someone told her that Long Dong had died after the Guangzhou Uprising. At first she did not believe it; later she came, little by little, to believe. In that time she tried every means to find the Party organization—she went to left-wing bookshops to hear lectures, went to Russian-language classes—but the Party had long since gone underground, and there was no finding the organization through such open activities.

Just as she was on the verge of despair, Yi Junnian appeared before her.

Her first sight of Yi Junnian was in a bookshop, and he did not tell her the truth of the matter. She had just picked up a newly published novel from the Chunchao Book Company; she knew the book—a few days before, she had seen Mr. Lu Xun's notice of it in a magazine. February, she remembered the title. In it there was a widow whose husband had fallen in battle, left with two children. The book was not thick; it cost only eight dime-coins.

She held the book closer and looked carefully at the cover; the woodcut design was of the plainest, but she could not make out what it showed. Someone beside her said, “Can't you tell? It's a river—and floating on it are leaves, and rain, and a great many human faces.”

And so she came to know Yi Junnian. He insisted on taking her next door for coffee, and for some reason she agreed. Only later did she understand how particularly good Old Yi was at talking people round. He saw her home, and all the way he talked—from the novel to woodcuts, from the wandering uncertainty of the young to the antagonism of the classes—without once telling her that he was the man the organization had sent to make contact with her. Afterward she decided he had been taking her measure. Revolution was a great tide washing out the sand, and after the failure of the Great Revolution many had indeed wavered and sunk.

Only months later did she discover that she had found the organization again, that Old Yi had brought her home.

She knew that Yi Junnian's care for her went beyond the friendship of comrades. At times that care could move her—so long as it was not too plainly declared. But the moment Old Yi said certain things, made certain gestures, and laid his feelings out clearly before her, she felt a dim awkwardness, a sense that something, somewhere, was not right.

And sure enough, Lin Shi had brought word that Long Dong was still alive.

As with the earlier news of Long Dong's death, her response to this new word was slow in coming. First she did not believe it, and only later, slowly, believed. But once she believed, it was as though she had become another person, her thinking wholly changed. Just now, for instance, when Yi Junnian said he had seen her through the café window getting out and going into the bank—a while ago that might have softened her a little inside; now she thought instead: how could you possibly have seen? After I got out I had my back to the café, I never once looked round, I went straight into the bank—and there was a car behind me besides. The news Lin Shi had brought seemed to have made her mind cooler, clearer.

Chen Qianli opened the door of the inner room and, seeing only Yi Junnian and Ling Wen outside, asked, “Where's Li Han?”

“He said he was going to make a round of the coal heaps. Said the yard's usually deserted at night, but with all these people coming and going today he wasn't easy about it and wanted to take a look.”

Chen Qianli nodded, and had Yi Junnian and Ling Wen come into the inner room together.

“As things stand, Comrade Lin Shi cannot go to Hong Kong and Guangzhou.” Chen Qianli came straight to the point. “The courier line from Shanghai to Ruijin—this stretch of it is our charge. Making the pickup in Shanghai, seeing them onto the ship, three days aboard, then Hong Kong and Guangzhou—responsible for liaising with the local liaison stations and making sure the contact is safe.”

“Give the task to me,” Ling Wen said at once.

Chen Qianli glanced at Yi Junnian. “Comrade Lin Shi meant at first to have Liang Shichao make the trip in his place. He's a Cantonese, knows the ground, and can speak the dialect.”

“I can go with Comrade Liang Shichao—the two of us can cover for each other.”

Yi Junnian, seated to one side, had said nothing all this while. Only now did he make up his mind. “Let me go with Ling Wen. We know each other well, and are better suited to it than Comrade Liang Shichao. I worked in Guangzhou for some years too, and can speak the dialect.”

The snow had stopped, and from the far bank of the Zhaojiabang the sound of firecrackers slowly rose—at first a scattered popping, then the crackle running together into one, as people began to set off fireworks: nine-dragon shells, meteor-crackers, bursting like flowers over the water. The Concession police strictly forbade the setting-off of such fireworks; but it was New Year now, and who paid them any heed? The several of them went out and stood at the door, heads tilted back, looking up at the sky over the far bank.
