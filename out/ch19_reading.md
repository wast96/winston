## Fushimi Castle

The second-floor room Gohei had ordered from the innkeeper and the room where Jūzō lay hidden in a traveling painter's guise faced each other across a narrow lane, their eaves nearly touching. Gohei had come to the inn dressed as a horse-driver; somewhere along the way he had turned himself into a street conjuror, one of those who peddle their tricks in the open road, and all day he kept the rain-shutters shut, watching Jūzō's room through the narrow seam between the boards.

But he could not see into the room. Through all the muggy heat of this sixth month Jūzō kept his paper screens closed, and all day long would not let in a breath of air.

"A glutton for hardship, this one."

Wiping the sweat from his brow in the pitch-dark room, Gohei muttered it as though spitting the words out. It was caution, a way of guarding one's life, and yet in another sense it seemed the very emblem of how Jūzō lived. What in the world could a man want so badly that he must shut himself up like this, inside a frame of penance he had built with his own hands?

"There were Iga men like that once, in the old days. Men who could lose themselves in their own art. They honed the craft and drank themselves drunk on the using of it, and into that drunkenness they packed a whole life; men who gave no thought to fame or gain, nor to the ordinary comforts of a wife and children. But this is no longer the world of Genki and Tenshō. The era has changed its name to Bunroku, the seeds of war have all been carried off to Korea, and every lord in Japan, great and small, has lost his wits to the tea ceremony. And in such a time that man lets himself be used by a petty merchant and means to be the last Iga man of them all. There is a limit even to folly."

When night came, a light would go up in Jūzō's room. Now and then a shadow crossed the screen, as if the man were moving about at some task. There was nothing strange in the way it moved. And yet, watching it, Gohei felt something press on him from within, a cold and uncanny dread. That the shadow wore a demon's face was no more than his own fancy; but the men of Iga had always held in reverence that piercing, physical certainty of the instincts.

—Jūzō, body and mind, is stretched now to an unnatural pitch. The shadow thrown on the paper screen was nothing out of the ordinary. But once Gohei looked at it in that belief, it seemed to take on an eerie, blood-red cast.

"Tonight, or tomorrow at dawn—"

Jūzō would make his move; of that Gohei was certain. Hideyoshi was at Fushimi now. And if so, the move could only be one thing: an entry into Fushimi Castle.

Gohei put his ninja garb and the tools of the trade into his pack, slung it on his back along with the straw mat that wrapped his sword, and by touch, carefully, went over the cords of his sandals.

All the while he never took his eyes from the gap in the shutters. There was no telling when Jūzō might leave his lodging. That night Gohei's eyes did not leave the shutters until the sky went white.

It was not until the sun stood high the next morning that Jūzō lowered himself onto a baggage-cart in a dim corner of the inn's earthen-floored entry. He was in a painter's dress as before. As Jūzō knotted the cords of his sandals, the innkeeper bent to him with a reluctant, regretful bow and asked,

"So—where will you be bound?"

he asked.

"Who can say."

Jūzō hoisted the great load up onto his shoulder.

"I've fixed on nowhere in particular. If you know of some thriving place, I'd be glad to hear of it."

"Then you'd do well to go to Edo. Ever since the House of Hōjō fell, they say the bustle of Odawara has moved to the new seat at Edo. Carpenters and cabinetmakers are pouring in from Kyoto, Ōsaka, Sakai, and the rest, and every month the houses go up by the hundreds—not the warrior mansions only, but townsmen's dwellings too. There'd be no small call for screen-paintings there, I should think."

"That's good to hear. In newly opened country even a poor painter might be put to use, if only to stop a gap in a hurry."

Laughing, Jūzō stepped out into the road, and from the second floor of the inn across the way Gohei watched him steadily, looking down.

(Leaving in broad daylight, brazen as you please—a careless fellow. The place he's after can't be far from Kyoto, in any case.)

The clear Kyoto weather that had held for half a month had begun, since around midnight, to break at last. Slowly, rolling his shoulders, Jūzō walked off toward the south, and the sky ahead of him was filling with lead-gray cloud that let only a dim, thinned sunlight through.

(This sky won't hold till midnight. By the look of it, Jūzō means to use the wind and rain to get into the castle.)

Gohei too left the inn.

It was an unremarkable scene, nothing more: a shabby street conjuror walking some half a chō behind Jūzō the traveling painter.

But Gohei's heart was far from easy. Just because his quarry was a ninja of great repute, no shadowing had ever cost him such pains. From about the point where they passed Fukakusa the houses thinned and the figures on the highway grew sparse. At times not so much as a stray dog came between him and Jūzō, half a chō ahead. Gohei went on, screening himself neatly behind standing trees, a clump of grass, a wayside shrine. By then the conjuror had in fact already vanished from the road: at a teahouse this side of Fukakusa he had swiftly changed into a peasant's field clothes he had ready, and since he altered even his gait each time, at a distance he looked each time like an altogether different man.

Jūzō trod the dust of the highway with a broad, unhurried stride, for all the world like a wandering painter walking beneath some sky beyond the world. Rain had already begun to spit down. He walked on, letting himself be soaked. By the time the road entered the rows of houses of the town of Fushimi, the shape of Mount Kohata had risen faintly into the last of the light, and here and there in the town the lamps were coming on.

Once in the town, Gohei quickened his stride. With so many crossings and so much foot traffic, there was a danger of losing Jūzō. He had the look of a small tradesman a day out from Kyoto on the road to Ōsaka. And so, before he had gone even half a chō into the town, he saw a dozen and more horse-drivers under the eaves of the houses, squatting on their heels with the air of men waiting for a fare out of the rain. Catching sight of Gohei, they seemed to take him for a fat goose, and two or three of them pressed their horse-reeking bodies close and set to bawling at him one after another.

"Ah, a pity, a pity! The boat down to Ōsaka's only this moment cast off."

"Take a horse, why don't you. Ride through the night and it's a bare thirteen ri. You'll be there easy by morning."

"Even if you put up at Fushimi you'll need your coppers. Hand the coppers to us and we'll carry you clear to Ōsaka with the castle in sight, sleeping the whole way."

"Come now, here's your horse. Up you get, up you get. We'll shove you along from behind!"

"I've no need of a horse."

Gohei lightly brushed away the hand of the driver clinging to his waist and spoke. The driver clapped a hand to his own wrist with a great show of it.

"What's this? What've you gone and done?"

"Ow—it hurts! My bones are screaming with it. My bones are howling!"

In a flash a dozen drivers came swarming and closed in around Gohei.

"Oi, you—a shrine priest, are you?"

"No priest. A tradesman."

"A tradesman, is it? Well, one or the other, it's all one. So you've gone and mistaken the driver for his horse? If it's a beating you want, pay your fare and beat the horse—but thrashing the driver, now, what's the reasoning in that?"

"That was wrong of me. I ask your pardon, so let me by."

Gohei was beside himself, afraid he would lose sight of Jūzō.

"No. You don't pass."

A man with a mat of black hair on his chest planted himself square in the way, legs spread, and gave Gohei's chest a light shove with his right hand.

"You'll not pass if it kills you. Want through? Then ride a horse, or leave us the price of a drink."

"Curse you, you pack of horse-flies. Who do you take me for?"

He would have done better to toss them a coin, but Gohei grudged it. In the very move of trying to cow them and push through showed the bad habit of a man who had drawn a warrior's stipend on the open streets of the world for some years. He had, after all, taken service precisely to climb out of the Iga man's lot, where a man counts for nothing except on the world's underside.

Sure enough, the drivers bristled.

"Who do we take you for, is it? Ho, this is rich. Come, what great personage are you, and out of where? Give us a fine speech and we might just prostrate ourselves, the lot of us. Eh? Well? Out with it. Won't you speak?"

The man in front gave Gohei's chest a hard shove. Gohei knew he had blundered. Waste time here and Jūzō would slip away. Already the man was gone from his sight. He had forgotten the ninja's suppleness. From boyhood he had yearned after the standing and rank of a samurai on the world's open streets, and now, just as he had come by something that looked like rank at last, his own half-baked airs had brought this trifling disaster down on him. Gohei saw his position now with a bitter, biting clarity.

But the regret itself came too late. At the instant his chest was struck, the body-skill bred into Gohei by Iga tradition had worked of its own accord, below thought. The driver, near six shaku tall, was slammed to the ground and crushed flat as a toad.

"Gyah!"

He had bitten his tongue, perhaps; blood ran from his mouth, his right hand clawed a fistful of earth, and then he lay still. It was the other drivers who were startled. For a moment they all shrank back as if to run, but they were plainly no strangers to a brawl, and, barely holding their ground, one of them cried,

"Stones! Give him the stones—smash that face of his to a red pulp!"

They set to snatching up stones, each man his own. As two or three of them began flying about Gohei, he ducked under the throw, dove in among the men, and hurled with a speed like lightning.

Several of them traced an arc through the air, plunged headfirst to the ground, and had their skulls split open.

"Hold—stand where you are!"

Just then a roar went up behind them. At the same moment several spears were driven, butt-end first, hard into the knot of men grappling and packed together.

"Wh—who's there?"

"You'd defy us?"

Before anyone knew it, five or six footsoldiers had ringed them round, and a few paces back stood a small-built samurai, plainly their commander at a glance.

"Will you stop this! Raising a brawl in the castle town of the lord of the realm—what insolent dogs are you? Stop, or shall I have your blood minced on our spear-points?"

At this the ring broke up at last. In the middle of it stood Gohei, not even breathing hard.

The samurai heard the gist of it from one of the drivers' fellows, and flew into a rage.

"Hold your tongues. By what I hear, the fault is yours. Once again you've threatened a traveler, meaning to flay the skin off him and wring a horse-fare out of him. For today I'll overlook it; but work such outrage again and I'll see you punished for it, be sure of that. In this age of peace, and in the very castle town of Fushimi no less, to behave for all the world like brigands stripping a battlefield—it passes all bounds. Now—away with you! Will you not disperse?"

Cowed by his fury, the drivers scattered in a hurry.

The small samurai came up to Gohei, unhurried.

"By your dress you'd seem a tradesman, and yet that was a splendid feat of strength. I confess myself all admiration—quite carried away with the watching. You must surely be a samurai of some name. That it has fallen out so is a kind of providence; will you not tell me your name, if only for the tale's sake?—But I forget myself."

The samurai drew in his chin and inclined his head.

"I am one Ueno Yahei, a mounted-guard officer of the House of Hachisuka. And you, sir?"

He spoke with great courtesy; but this clean-featured, clever-looking samurai by no means meant it in earnest. He watched Gohei's every movement as though to pierce him, and already, it seemed, had marked the unfamiliar shadow in Gohei's face.

This Ueno Yahei was on duty patrolling the town. Fushimi was then just seeing the last of Hideyoshi's castle-building; the mansions of the several lords had been raised, but no magistrate's office to govern the town had yet been established, and the lords policed the town's disorders each in turn, by rotation. So Yahei was, in this case, the officer regularly charged with police duty. This has turned troublesome, Gohei thought.

To keep insisting he was a merchant, a shop-clerk, would only whet Yahei's interest the more. No merchant could own such skill at arms. Better, Gohei thought, to name himself a samurai outright and then talk his way clear; that way it would be easier to keep Yahei's curiosity from pressing. For if his man were a samurai, then, so long as he had done no real wrong, there could be no dogged questioning of him.

"I am no one of such martial exploit or standing that my name is worth the giving. For reasons of my own I go dressed like this at present, but it is all at my lord's direction—a direction that has nothing whatever to do with matters of government. So as to my lord's name, I beg you will most kindly excuse me."

"A pity. You are a samurai yourself, so you will understand. Men of our calling like at least to keep in mind the name of a man of martial honor. If you will not tell me your name, then might you not at least come as far as our guard post and favor me with your story?"

Yahei, this Hachisuka man, with all the worldly-wise bearing of a Kamigata samurai, was trying by a roundabout way to draw Gohei into an interrogation.

"No, as to that—er—"

Gohei was hard put. He composed his face to that very look, pleading that he had business too urgent to brook a moment's delay.

"If a name will do, I'll give it. And once I have, I'll take my leave at once."

"You are too good. It is crude of me, I know, but this too is one of my duties."

And with that Yahei came round at last to his true footing. Gohei thought of giving the name he went by in the Maeda house, but a reluctance stirred against it. He was less the old man's secret agent now than a man working purely for his own advancement. On reflex he settled on giving a false name. False names he had by the dozen. He recalled one he had used for a few days years before, when he had slipped into Mikawa at Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon's contriving. Gohei had been born in the village of Ishikawa, in the Ayama district of Iga, and the surname of that false name, taken from the place, was Ishikawa.

"Ho. And the given name?"

"Goemon.—"

"Ishikawa, now..."

"Your pardon."

Swift as a darting swallow he ran, and was gone into the dark. Yahei stared after him, dumbfounded. A kind of intoxication lingered in him, as though he had met with a specter; and as it wore off, he drew his heavy brows together and cocked his head in doubt.

Jūzō took a room at a small inn in Kagiya-machi and, for the present, undid his wet traveling-clothes. Five chō to the west should bring him out before the Kaga mansion, the daimyo residence set furthest of all from the Fushimi keep.

Seen from the inn's second floor, the castle stood due east. But in the darkness at this hour not even the ridgeline of Mount Kohata was clear. The wind seemed to be blowing toward the northeast, and yet it turned back on itself and beat hard at the rain-shutters of the east window. Now and then, changing its quarter perhaps at the crossing of Masuya-chō before the inn, it came with a heavy, clinging persistence; at this rate it looked to blow up into a real storm by midnight.

By the shadow of the dim lamp Jūzō went over his ninja tools one by one, and last of all drew the Iga-forged sword and gazed into the blue of the blade. It was an unsigned Settsu piece his father had carried, and it cut superbly. Markedly slimmer than a common warrior's sword, the blade measured a bare one shaku nine sun five bu, with no curve to it. To this was fitted an unusually long hilt, and the scabbard, its black lacquer dulled to no shine, had a long cord wound round it.

He slid it quietly home, pushed it with the rest of his gear behind the folding screen, then took out the bedding and spread it. There was still a quarter-hour before the time to move. Jūzō meant to sleep.

The moment he shut his eyes, a dark surge of feeling began to run through his breast with a sound like a whirlpool. His father, killed in the Tenshō Rebellion; his mother; and his sister, who had taken her own life after Oda soldiers violated her—all of it pressed in on him, carrying a hatred that had no outlet. Perhaps there was no direct tie between the facts of that hatred and the Taikō. But they lived side by side within one and the same circle. The Taikō's very existence today stood on a mountain of nameless grave-markers, no different in kind from the tragedy of Jūzō's own blood. Nothing could lay that grievance to rest but the driving of the Taikō himself down among those nameless markers.

It was not, of course, that this act of Jūzō's had its source in that dark spirit of revenge alone. Without carrying it through, his life would scarcely hold together at all. Call it the sorrow of the ninja. For a man whose whole working life went to deceiving himself and others, always in the service of some end set by another's hand, it seemed that only through the one apparently meaningless act of killing the Taikō could Jūzō's own hollow life be lifted, all at once, into something like beauty.

(But that woman—)

Jūzō muttered it without meaning to. No sooner was it on his lips than he blushed, alone in the dark, and hurried to unsay it. What he had been about to think of he could not tell; but at the very least, the image of that woman had no business entering this train of thought. That there could be, in his life as a ninja, any room into which a woman might come—such a thing had never once crossed his mind.

"A pleasant chill."

he murmured softly. When the latter half of the Hour of the Rat had passed, Jūzō threw off the bedding. Having laid out beside his pillow neither more coin nor less than was owed, he eased the shutter open without a sound, slid his body out, ran a little way along the eaves, and dropped to the road. The rain had already stopped. But the wind was wet.

Jūzō's feet were bent toward Ryōgae-machi. The wet dark had wiped the day's heat away, just so. Not hot, and yet not too cool. It is on nights like this, surely, that a man's life will suddenly miss its footing and stray across the border of the seen and the unseen. It was only after he had passed the shopfront of the Kōshū-ya in Ryōgae-machi that Jūzō's shadow vanished all at once from heaven and earth. The shadow runs along the ground. But had anyone witnessed it, he could only have taken it for a black gust of wind that stirred the eyelashes an instant and was gone.

Past the Shimazu mansion, Jūzō went north. Then east. On Mount Kohata before him, the tower of Fushimi Castle reared up rigid and still. His aim was to come out at the rear gate on its far side.

From the Shimazu mansion to Fushimi Castle ran a single narrow path and no other. At the northeast corner of the mansion stood a willow, its roots steeped in the water of the moat. Just after Jūzō had passed, a shadow climbed up out of the moat to the foot of the willow.

"Jūzō.—Just the road I thought you'd take."

A satisfied smile rose on the cheek of the man muttering to himself. It was Gohei, sheathed in ninja black. Keeping just enough distance not to lose sight of Jūzō's shadow, he ran low along the ground. Iga tradition held that to make no sound a man must thread the gaps in the wind. It taught, too, that one should run touching only the big toe to the earth. From the two running shadows came no more sound than a dropped needle.

He crossed the moat, and came out at the inner moat.

(What—?)

It was when he had sunk himself to the very bottom of that inner moat that Jūzō caught the sign of a pursuer. Gripping the weeds on the bed to hold himself still, he strained to catch, within the pressure of the water on his eardrums, some slight change.

(Someone else is here, in this same water—)

When one man puts his body into water, the water raises a fine ripple that carries far off; and yet the ripple here might be no more than his own mistaking of a fish that had stirred.

Surely not, Jūzō thinks. He was moved to laugh at his own suspicion. Quietly he began to crawl along the bed.

Before long Jūzō's palm closed on the slick stone rampart below. Pressing his belly flat to the moss of the stones, he floated up in one motion to the surface, drove a kunai into a gap in the wall, and, climbing like an inchworm, sent his shadow creeping up toward the black sky.

He gained the top of the stone rampart without trouble, but above it a white plaster wall still reared. Jūzō threw a grappling-rake tied to a thin cord over the roof-ridge of the wall, and began slowly to climb.

The instant Jūzō's shadow vanished beyond the wall, a head broke the surface of the water as if it had been waiting: Gohei's. He too drove a kunai into a gap in the stones and hauled his body up, right and left.

Coming down on the inner side of the wall, Jūzō hid among the grass the tools he would need to escape, stripped off his soaked garb, and wrapped it round a stone. Should the castle guards give chase, he would fling the garb into the moat, to make it seem he had leapt into the water himself.

He had a second set ready, wrapped in oiled paper. Quickly he changed, cast off his sandals, and tucked a pair of ninja tabi into his breast. The soles of the tabi were quilted with raw silk floss; the men of Iga put them on just as they are about to enter a house.

Once Jūzō had fixed the layout of the buildings within the castle, he set to threading swiftly from shadow to shadow. A little behind, Gohei came down in the same place. But Jūzō was already nowhere to be seen.

Gohei must have grown anxious. Wringing out his sleeves, he went straight down the stone stair from an embrasure in the wall, half tumbling as he went. If he could, he wanted to reach the front of Hideyoshi's residence one step ahead of Jūzō. The moment Jūzō arrived he would blow his whistle to gather men, then stride forward himself and cut Jūzō down, or take him. To pin the intruder at the very spot, and before every eye—that was the scheme, worthy of an Iga man, that Gohei had. And he would, of course, name himself aloud:

—I am So-and-so, a retainer of Maeda Gen'i, and this man is one Tsuzura Jūzō, who would lay his hand on the life of His Highness the Taikō. So he would cry.

That done, an audience with the Taikō was sure to follow his capture. The Taikō was an easy, approachable man; he would give his word in person and settle the reward on the spot. And that reward, of course, would be a thing of a wholly different order from anything to be had from a Maeda Gen'i of a bare fifty thousand koku.

But Hideyoshi's residence was not so easily found. Naturally enough, Gohei had not studied Fushimi Castle as closely as Jūzō, who was staking his life to break into it.

Gohei made for the great keep. But Jūzō did not go that way.

Wary of assassins, Hideyoshi kept, within the castle's moats, a still smaller ring of moats of his own. Southeast of the keep lay a grove of asebi shrubs, and passing through it one came into a sumptuous garden. A pond girdled it like a moat, and at its center was an island. On that island stood a three-storied tower: Hideyoshi's everyday residence.

To reach it a man had to cross water. Jūzō fitted four water-spiders to his hands and feet. Over the surface he worked himself forward in jerks, for all the world like a real water-spider creeping along.

The rain, which had let up for a while, began again. The wood ringing the pond roared without a pause. The dark had rendered the human eye wholly useless. The Hour of the Ox could not be far off now. For an intruder, one might call a night so favorable a rare thing. Jūzō entered the island's inlet.

An inlet, but only in miniature of the idea. It was narrow enough that with both arms spread he could touch the rock walls of either bank. A small boat lay moored there, and at the slight waves Jūzō raised, its gunwale gave a small slap of water. The sound, of course, was swallowed by the wind and rain that wrapped the pond. Above that floor rose the three-storied tower.

The inlet ran in under the very floor of the residence. Coming up onto land, Jūzō began to walk beneath the floor at his leisure. By the feel of the place he could tell which room above was the watch-chamber. He came to know even the tautness and slackness of its guard.

(Roughly, a hundred men on this first story. On the second, ten at most. The third I take to be empty—but is it?)

Hideyoshi was on the second story. By the plan of it, five or six rooms at most. The third story, he judged, was likely a tea-room.

Now, the way in. To enter by the first story and climb to the second would be the usual course; but that meant hiding himself from a hundred men's nerves.

Better, then, to lift the mats of the empty third story and let himself down into the ceiling of the second. Very likely— He drew out the tube-rake he had ready. It was ten thin bamboo pipes, each about two shaku long, bound together. A hemp cord ran through them, with a hook at the tip. Pull the end of the cord and the pipes drew up into a single bamboo pole, stretching to a height of some two jō.

The tube-rake served the ninja who would climb a building from its outer shell. Jūzō hooked it over a rafter of the residence and left the ground without effort. He lifted off the rain-shutter of the third story and slipped inside. As he had reckoned, it was a tea-room. Raising and lowering the mats time after time, he found it at last, and when he did,

(With this, the Taikō's life is as good as gone—)

Even so, sweat had broken out over his whole body. He put himself through the hole in the boarding and let himself down into the ceiling-space. Crawling along the ceiling, he read the feel of the room below. A quarter-hour or so passed. When his skin had drunk in every last detail of the pattern beneath him and he was sure of it, he went back unhurried to the tea-room. From there he took the stairs down to the floor below.

The corridor ran east and west. A row of tatami edged in scarlet cloth stretched away, and the hanging lamps set here and there along it smoked the gold-leaf sliding screens in a dim, haloed glow.

One by one, Jūzō blew each of the lamps slowly out. It was a provision against the time of his escape.

There lay Hideyoshi's sleeping chamber.

Jūzō put his hand to the metal catch of the screen. That two men of the watch were inside, he could tell by the feel of it. He opened the screen two shaku. To put himself in, to close it again, and to dart like a demon and drive a stunning blow into each of the two guards—all of it was very nearly one motion.

The next room was the dressing chamber. Here there was no one.

Hideyoshi's sleeping chamber lay beyond it. Through the transom a faint light fell on the ceiling of the dressing chamber.

(Damn.—Is there a woman in there?)

Jūzō had, somehow, a foreboding of it. If a woman raised the alarm here, all his pains would come to nothing. But a piece of luck turned Jūzō's way. Meaning perhaps to rise for the privy, a woman slid the screen open and came into the dressing chamber.

Jūzō's palm closed over the woman's mouth as she began to cry out. In the same instant he drove his fist into her side. Without a sound she lost her senses and fell against his chest. Some woman of rank, no doubt, with a court-name of one kind or another—but her name was nothing to Jūzō.

Now, at last, he laid his hand on the final screen. It opened without a sound. There lay Hideyoshi. Asleep.

Jūzō stood at the pillow-side. The room was full of fragrance. Letting his eyes run over it, he saw a sandalwood stand set in the alcove. On it rested a small celadon incense-burner, breathing out its rich perfume.

Jūzō looked in at Hideyoshi's sleeping face. It was no more than the tired face of a sallow old man, sunk without ceremony in sleep. By day he pasted a false mustache beneath his nose to lend himself dignity; by night he took it off. Below the bare nose the lips had fallen a little open, and two yellow front teeth showed through, bleak and cold.

(Shall I kill him?—)

Jūzō laid his hand on the hilt at his back. But when it came to it, something held him. Grip the topknot and cut the throat, and the old man would die as surely as a rabbit. The old man's life and death lay within a single motion of Jūzō's. And yet this man was not the Taikō; so long as he slept, he was no more than a helpless old man. To kill him as the Taikō, must one not first wake him?...

(Shall I kill him?—)

Jūzō stooped to the Taikō's pillow-side.

"Will you not wake?"

he said. The old man slept light. He raised his eyelids, watched Jūzō's face a while, then slowly,

"A face I don't know."

he said. That this man was a dangerous intruder Hideyoshi knew perfectly well, of course, from his dress and his bearing.

"And where are you from?"

Hideyoshi shut his eyes once more as he spoke. A man, too, will sometimes wear the disguise of a beast. He had shut his eyes to show his adversary the breadth of his humanity. In just such contests of the mind Hideyoshi had passed half his life, and gloried in them. Yet silence alone, it seemed, he feared, for he went on,

"What have you come to do?"

he added.

"I?"

It went without asking, and yet Jūzō, sitting cross-legged and silent at the pillow-side, was slow to answer. True enough, he had come to kill this man. And he had made his way in to the very spot from which he could end this little old man's life that instant. And yet—

(A strange thing—)

he thought. A quiet mirth welled up in him. What amused Jūzō was that the ruler of the realm was, after all, furnished with no monstrous or demonic shape. Before him lay nothing but the wrinkled body of a man near sixty. Words he had never expected came out of Jūzō's mouth. In the tone of a man peering at some strange beast, he said,

"Are you truly the man they call Hideyoshi?"

he said. It made for a strangely slack sort of meeting. To Hideyoshi, too, to be asked such a thing over again by a midnight caller seemed to come out of nowhere, and he answered,

"Hideyoshi I am, sure enough."

Up to that point he seemed still in a half-waking daze, as if the dream had run on. Having answered, he was visibly shaken. There was no need to give one's name to an assassin. Hideyoshi's eyes flew open. He started to cry "Guards—!"—and a hand was clapped abruptly over his mouth. The intruder spoke, a laugh in his voice.

"No noise. No—make all the noise you like. If you cry out, it settles the thing for me. I'll take it for my signal and cut you down."

A strange man had come blowing in. Sensing that his visitor did not seem about to do him any immediate harm, Hideyoshi rose onto all fours on the bedding, sat up, and crossed his legs. The man was neither scorning him nor, of course, fawning; this smile was of a kind Hideyoshi had never met before. Plainly, the man was pitying him.

"What are you smiling at?"

"Why, you've grown terribly decrepit."

Hideyoshi stiffened the muscles of his bloodless face. His falling silent was no matter of feeling, of anger. More exactly, one should say the very syntax of his thought had tangled. By what cause this inversion had come, he could not tell; but the man before him now was treating him, from the top down, as an inferior, as though it were the most natural thing in the world. And Hideyoshi half-felt there was some strange power in the man that made such treatment, somehow, only fitting. It was like a mouse that, brought out all at once before a cat, falls into a kind of trance, its thought and movement tangling and stumbling.

"………"

"What have you come to do?"

said Hideyoshi. It was a pained voice, like a man opening his mouth in a dream.

As if it were the most matter-of-fact thing, the intruder said, "To kill you." There was in the voice a resonance like an eerie rumbling of the earth. To Hideyoshi, at least, so it sounded. The illusion that an officer of hell had come to fetch him, warrant in hand, took hold of his mind. As if drawn in, he said,

"For which of my sins?"

"They are past counting."

"Is it for killing Asai's child? That was done at His Lordship the Udaifu's command. I have done other killing besides. But all of it, I tell myself, was killing there was no help for, for the realm's sake."

"You may make your excuses in the netherworld."

"I am ready for that."

"You are a man who rose from a common nobody to take the whole realm; it shows, right enough. But, I am sorry to say—"

Jūzō gave a low chuckle.

"—your hand is shaking."

"That is the night's chill."

Hideyoshi laughed too, since there was nothing else for it. And it was his own laughter that, for the first time, brought Hideyoshi fully awake. The instant he came back to himself, he was aghast.

"Wh—who are you?"

"Ah, awake at last? Ha-ha-ha—I am no messenger of the netherworld, nor anything of the sort. I am an Iga man, one Tsuzura Jūzō."

"A thief, then?"

"I turn thief on occasion. But tonight I have come to relieve you of your life."

"Who set you to it?"

"Guard your tongue."

Jūzō rebuked him under his breath.

"You are my prisoner. Do not forget that with no more than a reach of my hand your life is snuffed out this very instant."

"You—you dare—"

he began to cry. But then, as one would expect, he swallowed his voice and lowered it.

"I—I am the master of the sixty-odd provinces, master of all beneath the shining sun!"

"I know it. But that is a daytime matter, is it not? Your power tells only while your retainers stand ranked about you. Yet in the deep of this night, so long as you sit face to face with me alone, you must submit to my rule."

"Wha—what are you saying?"

"Only listen. Between you and me, the kind of power is different. You bent your whole soul to the moving of men, until at the last you came to command all Japan. I set the one against the other in no way; but I, being an Iga man, poured my heart's blood from boyhood into the moving of one thing only—myself. By now I can work my own body and mind at will. A power of that kind comes to its fullness only in the supreme occasion that tests it. You, to test your power, thought up the conquest of the Great Ming; I wished to steal into this castle you built by moving the men and the wealth of the whole land of Japan, and to stand face to face with you, just the two of us. As it happened, a certain person asked me to kill you. There is no cause for wonder in it. For me it is the Iga man's trade, a thing there is no help for."

"I see."

"Your life is dear to you, no doubt, but you must resign yourself to losing it."

"It is good of you to say so, but I am fond of my life."

"I feel for you, too. Trading words with you, a fellow-feeling has stirred in me. That the Taikō should be so worn-out an old man—that I never once imagined. Leave you be and you have not much of your allotted span left; and to set a blade to you on purpose strikes me, somehow, as cruel."

"Then—will you let me go?"

Hideyoshi let a sly light come into his eyes. The fit of terror had already subsided. Or rather, his face wore even the look of a man relishing this crisis, playing at it together with his oddly-minded intruder. Reading that sly look in Hideyoshi's eye, Jūzō slowly shook his head.

"That I cannot do."

"Why not?"

"I too have my trade. What I have been asked to do, I must carry through."

"In its place I will give you whatever you want—gold, or land, anything you desire."

"What a fool of a man."

"What—?"

"If a man could be moved by gold, he would not take up such dangerous work as this. Were it land I wanted, I should long since have become a proper samurai. There are those in the world who live, not for such greed of gain, but for the joy of their own craft—and it seems that, with one of them here before your very eyes, you still do not see it."

"I understand. But all the same—"

"What is it?"

"Spare me."

Hideyoshi gave a sly grin. A strange sense of nearness had welled up in him even toward this odd intruder. It seemed to him this rogue had a fresh, unspoiled heart, the kind that would understand if you but clapped it on the shoulder.

But Jūzō clicked his tongue, inwardly vexed. For an assassin, trading words with his mark was forbidden. Talk, and feeling passes across. He should have killed without a thought, the way a woodcutter lops a branch from a tree. Jūzō had gone wrong in this from the start—though it was no wonder he had. The man was too human, far too human, to be cut down thoughtlessly and done with.

That very humanity, most likely, was the charm that had carried a man like Hideyoshi up to the mastery of the realm. When Jūzō first saw him, the old man was deep in a greedy sleep. By any reckoning he was no more than a common old man; yet now, looking back, even that had been this man's shrewd kind of appeal. When Jūzō shook him awake, the man, rather than panic, first only blinked stupidly with sleep—and then, at once, turned his own sleep-dazed state to nimble account. That, even to his intruder, had come by degrees to seem an irresistible charm.

"I'll spare you.—But,"

said Jūzō.

"that alone will not content me."

"What would you have me do?"

"Say one word to me: I am glad."

"What?"

Hideyoshi was put out.

"Say it."

said Jūzō, coldly. To turn the man loose just as things stood left him, somehow, unable to settle his own feeling. It was, one might say, a kind of childish petulance in Jūzō. He did not even reckon on getting safely out of the castle himself on the way back. So small a show of thanks was surely no more than the man's due.—Or was it that, standing on his dignity as Taikō, he could not say it to a mere rappa?

"You'll breathe no word of this abroad?"

"I can promise no such thing. I too have a mouth."

"Then I will not say it."

"What?"

Jūzō turned a cold eye on Hideyoshi. He had lost his self-command. The impression of Hideyoshi he had held a moment before was gone, and there came back to him the various tales of arrogance that had gathered about the man since his rise. That in his old age, his self-command lost, Hideyoshi would at times behave with a haughtiness one could only call the conduct of a madman—this was already the common talk of the world. When it struck Jūzō that this was one and the same man as the Hideyoshi cowering small before his eyes, he could no longer stomach the sick surge of disgust that rose in him.

"If you will not say it—"

"What then?"

"Here—come here."

All at once Jūzō seized Hideyoshi by the collar of his nightclothes.

"Wha—what are you doing?"

"I am sorry for you, but keep your eyes shut."

Jūzō ground Hideyoshi's face into the mats. Before his eyes the face flushed dark with blood. Raising his fist, he struck it with all his strength.

"Ungh."

It must have been a fierce shock, for Hideyoshi lost consciousness where he lay.

(Have I overdone it?—)

Looking at the old man lying senseless, both legs gone rigid, Jūzō repented at once. He was only an old man. It was because he had thought of him as a wielder of power that Jūzō's own blood had risen; but looked down on now, he was nothing out of the ordinary—a shriveled creature, age-ugly as his years, fallen with its short limbs stiffened. If anything, dressed as he was in a rank that would make him ashamed of so sorry a sight once he woke, he seemed the more to be pitied.

Jūzō lifted the old man up, set his clothes to rights, and laid him back in the bedding. He was returned to the untroubled figure of the old man as he had been when Jūzō broke in. When he woke, the old man would very likely be at a loss. Might all of it, just possibly, have been a bad dream?

"A wretched farce this has been, Hideyoshi."

Jūzō rose to his feet and gave a soft laugh. It was pitiful and comic at once. There was even a bracing sense of relief, as if some bad blood that had been clotting all this long while somewhere in his body had been scattered and cleared. And yet, when he thought of it, life is full of discontent. It was his own willfulness that struck him as absurd. Let melancholy pile up, and the human spirit will hit on some plausible-seeming purpose to work it off. Jūzō had meant to kill Hideyoshi; but even without the killing, so long as he did some violent act that felt worth killing for, the melancholy would, of itself, scatter like mist.

(—And if so, then it is this man who has drawn the short straw. By killing him I have been able to relish these last few years of my life; but all this man has got for it is a beating out of the blue.)

In the ease of the Jūzō who had laughed softly there was a feeling that mocked the absurdity of the human spirit in just this way. Hideyoshi, made into a small prop of Jūzō's spirit, still lay tumbled in his bedding as before.

But there was, surely, one more pitiable man—in Sakai. Ōkurakyō Hōin Sōkyū. This man had made himself a tool of Jūzō's for his own greed of gain, and it had come to nothing but the paying-out of money. For Hideyoshi's life, against all Sōkyū's hopes, went on breathing still.

(Hideyoshi—we shall not meet again.)

Jūzō vanished from the room. Once more he climbed the stair to the third story, set his foot softly on the rain-wet cypress-bark roof on the northwest side, and from there sprang without a sound toward the pitch-black sky. And by the time he was looking down from the eaves of a corner turret at the moat-water below the stone rampart, a commotion Jūzō had never reckoned on had blown up around the residence.

A little while after Jūzō had slipped out, Gohei had crept into the residence. There the misfortune happened.

"Intruder—!"

As he rounded a corner of the corridor, a man slashed at him without warning. Gohei knew he had blundered, and backed swiftly away down the corridor.

"No intruder am I. I am one come to seize the intruder who has just now stolen into the residence. Do not, by a mistake, live to regret it."

"Bah, I'll not hear it. To arms, every one of you!"

The building fell into an uproar—some running to guard Hideyoshi, some shutting the exits and standing watch, some rushing headlong to seize the intruder himself; and being, after all, men charged with Hideyoshi's own protection, they moved, at the first sign of trouble, with the utmost speed.

Gohei had lost his way out. One man who slashed at him doggedly he cut down at last, for want of any choice, though he had had not the least intention of fighting from the first. His bloodied sword hanging loose from his right hand, lit by a lantern,

"I am sorry to have defiled the residence. I told them I was no intruder, but they would not hear it, and so, against my will, I put a man to the sword. I am one Gero Shōbei, a retainer of Maeda Gen'i."

"Why should a retainer of Lord Maeda have business at Fushimi Castle in the dead of night?"

"For that there is a particular reason. I will gladly tell it you at leisure, later. But while you fritter away the hour tangling with me here, the true intruder will seize on this uproar as a rare chance and slip out of the castle. Mark it well."

"I need no instruction from you. Do you not hear that alarm bell? Every corner of the castle is manned and sealed. In any case, you may make your defense in a fitting place. Throw down your prize."

"Never mind that—is His Highness safe and unharmed?"

"Why do you ask that?"

"Because the intruder came, I'll wager, to cut short His Highness's life."

"By what means do you know this?"

"Just as I have said. I am Lord Maeda Gen'i's secret agent. What use would I be in the office if I did not know it? You gentlemen set aside your own failure in letting the intruder in, and set instead to seizing the magistrate's agent who came to hunt him down. Don't come whining of it later when you understand. There—my sword, I throw it down for you."

Gohei slid the drawn blade back into its scabbard, dropped it at his feet, and with his toe rolled it kicking away. He ran his eye down the line of guards, a cold smile on one cheek, and said,

"I shall look forward to the beaten faces of you fools."

"There—take him!"

The guards piled on and bound Gohei up tenfold and twentyfold.

The next morning, dragged from the cell and set down on the white sand of the court, Gohei was struck with astonishment.

"On inquiry to the House of Maeda, they answer that they have no retainer by the name of Gero Shōbei, still less that they ever sent any secret agent into Fushimi Castle."

The grizzled samurai conducting the examination looked down on Gohei and, without warning, pronounced it coldly, driving it home.

"Now declare your true identity, and keep nothing back."

Gohei was made to see his position plainly. The House of Maeda could hardly own that one of its retainers had stolen into Fushimi Castle, still less shield him.

"I see. So that is how it is."

There was nothing for it now but to lay everything bare: that he was Kazama Gohei, an Iga man serving the Maeda under an altered name; that the man who had broken in was one Tsuzura Jūzō; that he himself, having caught wind of the plot beforehand, had seen in it a spy's once-in-a-lifetime chance for distinction, and, going a little too deep for its sake, had landed under this suspicion; that behind the Iga man Jūzō stood Ōkurakyō Hōin Sōkyū, of Sakai—all this Gohei set forth with a smooth tongue, whereupon it was, this time, the examining samurai who were thrown into dismay.

"Hold."

Judging that, left to talk on, the man might let out anything at all, the samurai went scrambling off to his superior.

For a day or two there was no word at all for Gohei in his cell. Only, in that time, he was subjected twice, from beyond the enclosure of the cell, to a strange kind of identifying inspection.

The first time, an old man, ringed about by a great throng, fixed his eyes steadily on Gohei in the cell. Gohei could not tell that it was Hideyoshi.

The old man said nothing. Only, he let a strange joy show in his face that the man in the cell was not the one who had stolen into his room. Why this was, cannot be said. Perhaps the old man had come, without quite knowing it, to be fond of that night's visitor. Or perhaps it was relief at having escaped an unhappy turn of fate—one in which he would now have had to set his own hand to the very man who had gone off without killing him.

But when the samurai attending him asked, with all courtesy, whether this was the man, the old man—strange to say—answered clearly that it was. That exchange did not reach Gohei's ears.

The next to look in at the cell was the man who, when Gohei had quarreled with the horse-drivers in the castle town of Fushimi, had come between them and broken it up: the Hachisuka patrolman, one Ueno Yahei. This man had felt a suspicion that Gohei, for all his merchant's dress, possessed some unfamiliar skill of body, and, on hearing the next morning that there had been an intruder in the castle, had reported as much. He must have been shown straight to the identification. The moment he set eyes on Gohei, he gave a deep nod to the officer in charge.

"There is no mistake. This is the man. If I recall, he gave his name as Ishikawa Goemon."

he said.

Twenty-fourth day of the Eighth month. Fine weather.—

In a certain court noble's diary, under the year Bunroku the third, there is an entry that begins with just such a date.

"Ten thieves and cutpurses; and one man among them boiled in a cauldron. His nineteen fellows hung upon crosses. The sentence carried out on the dry bed of the river by the Sanjō bridge. High and low thronged to watch."

This is, in all likelihood, the oldest record touching the execution of Goemon.

In the Shoku Honchō Tsugan the fact takes on somewhat more definite shape.

"In recent years there was a man called Ishikawa Goemon. He ceased not from housebreaking and from robbery with violence. Hideyoshi had the Governor of Kyoto, Maeda Gen'i, search everywhere for him, and at length Ishikawa was taken. His mother, moreover, and some twenty of his fellows were bound along with him, and they were boiled to death on the riverbed at Sanjō."

Records of the same tenor appear in the Rekichō Yōki and in the shogunal genealogies too, but this is all there is concerning Goemon, and it goes no further.

It was only after the Edo age had come into its middle years that Goemon was worked up into the figure we all know. A grotesque personage, his shaven crown grown out a whole shaku and his body dressed in damask, appeared anew before the common people. Chikamatsu Monzaemon has a piece called Keisei Yoshioka-zome; and besides it there came out puppet-plays and kabuki texts—The Life of Ishikawa Goemon, Kimmon Gosan no Kiri, Hama no Masago Den Ishikawa, Hade-kurabe Ishikawa-zome, Konoshita Soga Megumi no Masaji, and the rest. Yet who the Goemon who truly existed may have been is, to this day, unknown.

Old theories there are. Reasoning from the surname Ishikawa: among the retainers of the Miyoshi, for one, there was a man named Ishikawa Akashi. His son, of great stature and with the strength of thirty men, broke into his lord's treasury at the age of sixteen. On that occasion, one account runs, he cut down the guard, made off with a gold-mounted long sword, fled, and roamed the provinces working robbery.

There is another account that has him born in Hamamatsu, in Enshū. He called himself Sanada Hachirō at first, they say, but later, having ties to a physician's house—the Yamauchi Kotei of the Ishikawa district in Kawachi province—changed his name to Ishikawa Goemon. And because there is a hamlet called Ishikawa village in the province of Iga, there is a version that makes him a ninja born there, who learned his arts from Momochi Sandayū of that same province and, during his training, seduced Momochi's wife. He is said to have used the wife to steal Sandayū's money, and, when he absconded, to have killed her and thrown her down a well. None of these has the least foundation.

Come to think of it, that so great a thief—a man whose execution, moreover, is set down in the court diaries and the official records of the day—should have left not the smallest trace, neither of his conduct before that execution nor even of his birthplace, can only be called strange. In that strangeness one may catch a certain political odor. There have been many, from of old, who let their conjecture run that way; and theories there are.

That an Iga man named Kazama Gohei, born at Ishikawa in the province of Iga, and a ninja named Tsuzura Jūzō, likewise a country-samurai of Iga, stole into Fushimi Castle in the Bunroku years—this has been handed down from of old as an oral tradition among the priestly houses of Aekuni Shrine, the first shrine of Iga province. There is no strong ground for setting it against the older theories; but, as it happens, Kazama Gohei was born at Ishikawa village, and his break-in came a little before the execution of the man called Goemon. Might it not be that Gohei himself was Ishikawa Goemon?

If, as this story would have it, Kazama Gohei was Ishikawa Goemon, then the crime of working robbery in Kyoto was a wholly false charge. That crime should rightly have been laid to Tsuzura Jūzō—or, more exactly, to Kuroami. No; rather it should be laid to the doings of that whole host of ninja, named and nameless, who had been driven out of Iga and lost their means of living.

By that time Hideyoshi's regime had ceased to be altogether secure. It was a period when even the rumor of treason by Hideyoshi's adopted heir, the Kampaku Hidetsugu, came to light just after this. The uncanny scheming of Imai Sōkyū of Sakai—who afterward went over to the Tokugawa and worked for the destruction of the Toyotomi house—is no more than one small instance, emblematic of the politics of such a time.

One sees that, in such a period, there was only one course open to the governing authorities. Let the thing be made known, and the realm—its foundations already beginning to soften—might well suffer a landslide at some unlooked-for point. And a landslide, by a subtle chain of cause and effect, might not fail, in the end, to bring on some upheaval great enough to shake the realm itself.

And so the affair of Kazama Gohei was given the name of a mere case of robbery. The execution was carried out on a scale out of all proportion to a thief's. Other prisoners Gohei had never set eyes on were added to it, and a whole faction of confederates and kin and underlings was got up. That the execution was pressed with the harshness of autumn frost was, no doubt, because the declining regime of Hideyoshi, by a show of its power to prosecute, aimed at a piece of political advertisement—that the regime was alive and well.

Gohei died. But the man born out of that death, Ishikawa Goemon, lived on into later ages.

And yet the whirlpool that the movements of man against man stir up turns with a bitter irony. Tsuzura Jūzō, the very other self of Ishikawa Goemon, never dreamed that the man executed on the Sanjō riverbed on the twenty-fourth day of the Eighth month of Bunroku the third had been Kazama Gohei—still less had any means of knowing that it was a reflection of his own deed—and, back in his hermitage on Otogi Pass in Iga, he passed the seasons cut off from all dealings with the world.

On a fine spring day, he would sometimes teach his wife, come home with an armful of wild plants, in this fashion:

"That one is good for a sick stomach."

"Its name is Jūō, is it not."

answers Kohagi.

For a ninja gone to ground to make his living, there was no way but to pick out the medicinal plants from among the wild ones, dry them, and sell them to others. Jūzō gathered the herbs, and Kohagi went out to sell them in the villages.

"And what might this be?"

"That is Sanō. Good for the water, but for little else."

Over Jūzō and Kohagi, living so, the years of an ordinary mountain village flowed by. In the fifth year after her husband Jūzō had slipped out of Fushimi Castle, Hideyoshi died. In the autumn of the seventh year, near a place called Sekigahara in Mino, a decisive battle was fought between Hideyoshi's surviving vassal Ishida Mitsunari and Tokugawa Ieyasu, and by its outcome the mastery of the realm passed into Ieyasu's hands—all of this too Kohagi heard by village rumor. But she said nothing of it to her husband. Even had she told him, she saw, there was something growing within him now that no longer seemed likely to stir any interest in him at all.

The wind and frost of the mountain carve away, without mercy, at the rough wild nature around the hut. And in the same way the wind and frost of the pass had, one imagines, begun to carve the heart of Jūzō—withdrawn now from the world of men—along with its fellows of rock and mountainside. Jūzō was beginning to turn into one more feature of the land within the shape of the mountain. And Kohagi too had come to find a joy in the music of the wind as it passed over her husband's face and on.
