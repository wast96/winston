## Chapter Nineteen. The Hour of the Tiger, Second Half (4 a.m.)

He strained to open his single eye and make it out, and found at last that it was a great long string of many-colored gauze.

This too, he supposed, was of Mao Shun's designing: the lamplight of the lantern-chamber, shining through them, could show forth a radiance of richer depth.

The third year of Tianbao, the fifteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Tiger, second half.

Chang'an; Wannian County; the Xingqing Palace.

Xiao Gui led his picked Pifu at a swift pace along the rim of the Longchi. In no more than twenty-odd finger-snaps they had already drawn near the entrance of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower.

Strictly speaking, the Qinzheng Wuben Tower did not stand within the Xingqing Palace at all, but formed part of the palace's southern rampart. Its south face fronted the plaza, while to left and right it joined the high stone walls of the palace city; on these three sides there was no way through. The one stair up to it lay on the north side, within the inner park of the Xingqing Palace, ringed about by the imperial guards. It had been so designed at the first to lessen the risk of an assault—but now that very design had become a nuisance...

By this hour the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was wholly shrouded in dense smoke. The view before them was very poor, everything blurred and shadowy. Now and again sparks flew through the murk, dark red and dull yellow flickering by turns. Xiao Gui and his men had to slacken their pace, threading round the broken wreckage of lantern-frames and the scattered rubble, for fear of hurting their feet.

Xiao Gui walked at the very head of the file, straining to make out what lay ahead, his heart untroubled. The fouler the surroundings, the more it favored them. These twenty-odd Pifu, were they to meet the Longwu Army face to face, would be wiped out to the last man. Only in a setting of confusion and tangle could they win some slender chance of victory.

All at once he halted, tilting his head a little to one side; a broken, fitful din had reached his ears. The sound came not from the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, but from somewhere further south—the shouting of a countless multitude.

On the plaza of the Xingqing Palace tens of thousands were gathered at this hour, packed shoulder to shoulder; a handful of scattered coppers would have been enough to cause a grievous accident, let alone an explosion so terrible as this.

Though the true force of the blast had not in fact been so very great, when had the common folk of Chang'an ever beheld such a sight? By the sound alone Xiao Gui could picture it: those tens of thousands of terror-stricken souls all fleeing at once toward the edges of the plaza, jostling and crushing and trampling one another, churned into a vast and utter whirlpool of chaos—and this was good news, for the loyalist troops rushing in from every side would be caught up and swept along in that huge disordered current, with no leisure to look elsewhere.

Xiao Gui paused only a moment, then ran on ahead; and soon he saw loom up before him two tall and ferocious beast-shaped black shapes, at which his spirits could not help but rise.

The Pifu had beforehand scouted out the ground about the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, and knew that to left and right of the entrance there stood a stone image of a divine beast apiece—the Azure Dragon of the east, the White Tiger of the north—betokening that the Xingqing Palace lay to the northeast of Chang'an.

To sight these two stone images was to know the right entrance had been found. Xiao Gui gathered himself and gave a short, sharp command to the men behind him. One and all they halted, lifted the crossbows slung at their waists, braced them on their forearms and leveled them.

At the entrance of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower there stood, besides the divine beasts, no small number of Longwu Army guards. Chen Xuanli was a fine hand at drilling troops; startled though these guards were by the sudden explosion, not one of them had left his post without leave—rather, they had heightened their watch. Xiao Gui saw that the movable threshold-bar at the entrance had been raised a few notches, forming a half-high wall of wood to keep out intruders.

For a case like this the Pifu had long since laid their plans. The thick smoke was the best of cover; one and all they took up positions of advantage for shooting, and a dozen and more crossbows lifted as one.

'Loose!' Xiao Gui ordered in a low voice.

Thwack! Thwack! Thwack!

The slack twang of loosed bowstrings rose one upon another. These Pifu had every one of them been picked men of the army; to pierce a willow-leaf at a hundred paces was the merest of their skills. The soldiers of the Longwu Army were sheathed in armor, yet that dozen and more of artful bolts drove home precisely into the gaps between the plates, sinking into the vital spots.

In one brief instant the greater half of the guards at the gate went down. The rest reacted with the utmost speed, one and all rolling over and leaping across the threshold-bar to crouch low behind it. But alas, the Pifu had already touched off a few tubes of fierce-fire oil, and flung them in an arc over the wooden bar. In a moment leaping flames rose up on the far side, with scream upon scream of agony.

The Pifu charged with the close work seized the moment to spring across, cutting them down man by man, till every guard was slain. Just then a band of Hu musicians came running up in panic from one side. They were the palace music-troupe kept for the banquet, who had been waiting in a rest-room at the foot of the tower, and had caught up their instruments at the sound of the blast and sought to flee.

The Pifu, needless to say, would not let them off. Konghou or pipa, all alike showed pitifully frail before the keen edge of the blade. In no more than a few finger-snaps these poor musicians had fallen beneath the butcher's knife, strings snapped and pipes broken. When they were done away with, Xiao Gui grasped that survivors from the upper floors of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower would come streaming down without cease. Swiftly he re-spanned his crossbow, sprang over the threshold-bar, and came into the Hall of Diligent Governance on the first floor.

This great hall was exceedingly vast and open, with sixteen great vermilion-lacquered pillars standing ranged within it, coiled about with writhing dragons. Between the pillars were set all manner of rare flowers and curious plants, some richly hued, some faint of fragrance, lush and green, each of a different form, so that the hall was made over into a scene of 'the Dao following its own nature.'

In the very center of the hall, slanting down, hung a broad sky-reaching stair leading to the second floor—no more, in truth, than a wooden stairway some five chi wide, its treads jet-black and gleaming, shaped like the edges of clouds, its balustrade all carved of sandalwood into curving dragon-forms. One who climbed it, resting a hand on the rail, mounted as though treading the azure clouds, as though riding a soaring dragon, turning back and forth again and again, till he reached the banquet hall on the topmost floor. When the Son of Heaven and his many guests mounted the tower, it was by this that they went up.

But this sky-reaching stair had by now taken on a changed aspect. Its main body had been built to hang in air, borne up at each turn only by the tower-pillars so as not to take up room within, at the cost of an unsteady footing. The violent tremor a moment ago had brought the stairway crashing down tier upon tier, the timbers half-ruined. Xiao Gui looked up along the light-well and saw that several lengths of the stair had toppled one against another, tangled into a snarl of confusion.

The height of each floor here was above three zhang; a man who leapt down by main force would only die the faster for it. Which was to say, the upper floors of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower had for the moment been cut off from the world without.

Xiao Gui called to mind the tower's structure a moment, then pointed to the right: 'This way!'

Over here there was a service passage used by the menials, running down to the kitchens and up to the several floors within, for the carrying of dishes and the passing of wine. The main way being blocked, they could only try this one.

The service stair was set in a corner of the tower, its mouth screened by two turns to keep it from the sight of the noble guests. The Pifu passed swiftly through and came to the foot of the stair. This stairway was of course nothing so sumptuous as the sky-reaching stair, and near bare of ornament; but to bear the carrying of heavy loads its underpinning had been built very stout, and so it stood whole and undamaged.

Xiao Gui, without a word, went racing up the stair. Along the way servants and palace maids came fleeing down in panic without cease, and were every one cleanly disposed of. Now and then some lucky wretch dodged the attack and turned to flee shrieking, but the Pifu had no interest in giving chase.

Their goal was one thing only—the Son of Heaven.

At the instant the lantern-tower blew, Chen Xuanli and Yuan Zai had just crossed the roadway at the entrance of the Xingqing Palace, with the Qinzheng Wuben Tower already in distant view.

The sudden vast roar, and the fierce flames and thick smoke that came hard upon it, brought the two of them up short, their faces gone ashen. Their eyes turned as one toward the banquet hall atop the tower; but under the sky-shaking violence of the lantern-tower's bursting, there was no making out at all what had happened there.

Not until the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower came thundering down and crashed with all its weight against the front of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower did the two of them wake as from a dream—though they would sooner it had been an illusion.

That the Son of Heaven of Great Tang should be set upon in the very heart of the capital, his palace-city wrecked—it was nothing short of the most fearful nightmare.

'To the rescue of the sovereign!' Chen Xuanli was the first to come to himself; with a great shout he ran forward.

Yuan Zai followed at his heels, but his movements were somewhat hesitant. To judge by the violence of a moment ago, the Son of Heaven might well already be dead; to hazard forcing his way in now, and put on a show of loyal, dutiful guardianship—was it, after all, worth the while?

Even as he thought it, his steps slowed. Unlooked-for, Chen Xuanli glanced back at him, his tone thick with menace: 'The Xingqing Palace is now sealed on every side; whoever quits his post without leave is to be cut down out of hand!' Yuan Zai's face froze; then he lifted his head and said: 'I, Yuan Zai, bear the charge of the Jing'an; am I a man to cling to life and shrink from death? At so extraordinary an hour, the rescue of the sovereign comes first! The Jing'an Bureau will gladly go before the General as its vanguard!'

In all he said and left unsaid he hinted that the Jing'an Bureau had already given warning of the enemy, and that the Longwu Army must bear the heavier blame. Chen Xuanli gave a cold snort; now was no time for wrangling—first the Son of Heaven must be got down from the tower—if he were yet alive.

They had had three or four guards with them to begin with, and had gathered up along the way a dozen and more soldiers of the inner patrol, forming a small band of no little fighting strength. Chen Xuanli, burning with impatience, urged the party on without cease, and soon reached the entrance of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower.

At the tower gate the first thing they saw was the bodies of Longwu Army soldiers sprawled every which way, and the raised threshold-bar. Chen Xuanli's face went livid to the utmost; this ghastly scene before him told that the matter was worse even than he had feared. The Pifu had not only touched off the lantern-tower, but had stolen soundlessly into the Xingqing Palace besides, in numbers unknown.

For a commander of the imperial guards, this could no longer be called a disgrace; it was a grave dereliction of duty, not to be atoned for by a hundred deaths.

Yuan Zai too saw the gravity of the case. Plainly the Pifu had but one aim—the imperial throne. He reckoned it up in his mind: the guard within the Qinzheng Wuben Tower had likely suffered heavy losses in the attack a moment ago; the plaza now must be in utter chaos, holding the main body of the Longwu Army fast; and as for the Gate Guards who warded the several gates of the Xingqing Palace, their first thought would be to hold the gates close, and the greater the tumult, the less would they dare leave their posts.

The Longwu bodyguard under Chen Xuanli's own direct command might indeed be called on, but they were stationed outside the Jinming Gate, and the Jinming Gate had but now, at Chen Xuanli's own request, been locked and barred. To open it again would take no small time.

Which was to say that, by a cross-grained turn of chance, the only men who could reach the Qinzheng Wuben Tower to rescue the sovereign in any short span were these ten-odd here present. As to how many of the enemy had come, and what weapons they bore, of this they were altogether in the dark.

Full of misgiving, Yuan Zai counseled Chen Xuanli: 'With friend and foe alike unknown, to go lightly into a place of danger will surely fell the commanding general. Better to wait until the Yulin and the Qianniu Guard come up, and then lay our plans.'

The Yulin Army belonged to the Northern Command, the Qianniu Guard to the Southern; both alike were guardsmen of the night-watch who attended the Son of Heaven. The moment the lantern-tower fell, they were bound to set out at once, hastening in from every side to their sovereign's aid.

But this counsel Chen Xuanli flatly refused—was this a jest? It was the Emperor himself now in peril! To sit and wait for other troops to come to the rescue was to pass sentence of death on oneself. In the present pass the numbers of the loyalist troops mattered not at all; what mattered was the hour! The hour! A finger-snap more, a finger-snap less, might make all the difference between heaven and earth.

'We must go in now! This very instant!'

Chen Xuanli drew his belt-blade, casting off his wonted caution. There was no more room for caution now; the tower must be stormed by main force—though every man of them die for it, no harm must be let come to the Son of Heaven.

Their commander having given the order, the Longwu Army soldiers made no demur, but charged without hesitation into the first-floor hall. Soon enough they found the sky-reaching stair half-ruined, and the way impassable.

'Take the service stair alongside!' Chen Xuanli, who knew the layout of the floors well, at once bellowed. The soldiers rushed to the tower corner; but looking up they found the service stair wrapped in raging fire, and that way too was closed. Chen Xuanli narrowed his eyes and made an examination, and discovered on the upper part of the stair the marks of deliberate wrecking.

Those cursed Pifu had climbed the tower by this very way, sure enough, and cut off the road behind them into the bargain! Chen Xuanli brought his fist down hard upon the stair-rail, and actually snapped a length of the hardwood clean through. The white splinters at the break were stained with the blood of this Grand General of the imperial guards.

Both stairways being broken, the Longwu Army soldiers stood in the hall at their wits' end. Yuan Zai turned his neck about, and of a sudden pointed to one side: 'I have a way!'

'Eh?'

'Tread on those flowers and plants! Then one can reach the edge of the second-floor wooden stair.'

At this a savage light broke out in Chen Xuanli's eyes—what a moment to be talking such fucking nonsense! He put out a hand to seize Yuan Zai by the collar. Yuan Zai ducked low, dodging Chen Xuanli's palm, and made off of his own accord toward the flower-thickets between the vermilion-lacquered pillars.

Chen Xuanli was about to give chase when he saw Yuan Zai crouch down and draw out from before him a single block—not a cluster, but a square, four-sided flowerbed—moving it out on its own from that spread of flowers. Upon the flowerbed were purple-jade tulips and yellow-white naga-flowers, but from beneath it came a low rumbling sound.

Only then did Chen Xuanli grasp the fellow's meaning.

These flowers and plants on the ground floor of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower were not truly growing in the earth, but were set in a kind of wood-walled cart called a spring-moving frame. Such carts were commonly kept within the imperial park: soil was banked up in the box of them, seed buried in the soil, and a flower-gardener charged with the watering. Once the leaves grew thick and the blossoms opened on top, these spring-moving frames could be trundled to any place at all, to become a movable prospect of the imperial park.

Yuan Zai had ever been fondest of luxurious things, and to so exquisite and elegant a device he was more alive than any other man. He alone would ever have marked so fine a detail.

Chen Xuanli hastily bade every man come forward and lend a hand; with a scramble of many hands they pushed out those several spring-moving frames, tipped the carts over, and dumped out all the flowers and plants within, soil and all. Pitiful it was that these rare exotic blooms from foreign lands should be trampled under boot-heels into spring mire, with none to grieve for them.

The soldiers stacked the empty carts one atop another, until they came near the ceiling. Then, one by one, they climbed to the top of the carts, where their arms just reached the edge of the broken stair on the second floor, and with a little effort they could haul themselves up.

Before long every man, Yuan Zai among them, had climbed safely to the second floor. On this floor were gathered no small number of servants and maidservants, and here and there a noble in fine dress. Every one of them was begrimed and haggard, slumped weak upon the ground; and seeing rescuers arrive, they cried out for help one and all.

Chen Xuanli could pay them no heed at all, but strode off in great steps for the stair up to the third floor. As luck would have it this stretch of stair stood whole and unhindered, and the party clattered up in a single rush to the third floor—where they were forced to halt.

The third floor of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was an open through-room, thrown open on all four sides, without walls, borne up only by a few rows of pillars. The height of this floor stood just above the walls on either hand, with no building near or far to block the view. Come summer, a cool draught blew through it from every side—a matchless place to take the air, given the fair name of 'the Wind-Wooing Hall.'

This wholly unsheltered layout, meeting head-on a blast on the scale of the lantern-tower's, was as a lamb before a tiger, cruelly savaged. The whole floor entire—bronze mirrors, porcelain vases, mother-of-pearl screens, silken mats, and the living men within it alike—was first rocked reeling by the shock-wave, then washed over by a cloud of fire. Hard upon this the upper part of the lantern-tower came crashing down; its burning spire, striking the outer wall and snapping off, spun and sheared into this floor, carrying with it numberless flying splinters and sparks—a scene of the utmost desolation.

By the time Chen Xuanli and his men rushed onto the third floor, all that met the eye was smoke and dust and ruin; the flooring lay in wrack past all crossing, and not a cry for help nor a groan could be heard—there were, it was to be feared, no survivors at all. In several spots tongues of fire leapt with a roar; left unchecked, they would before long kindle a second conflagration.

Chen Xuanli mastered the horror in his breast, waved away the acrid smoke, and made for the sky-reaching stair leading up to the fourth floor. The Lantern Festival spring banquet was held on the seventh floor, and there too was the Son of Heaven—Chen Xuanli's one and only goal.

Yuan Zai followed close on Chen Xuanli, this scene of raging ruin before his eyes leaving him aghast past speech. Ought he, after all, to go on climbing? Was the risk worth the hazard? For, mark, even had the Son of Heaven not perished in the blast, he might now be in the Pifu's grip. The peril grew ever greater, the profit ever smaller. Yuan Zai's resolve began, despite himself, to waver.

Yet for the moment he could find no pretext at all for leaving. In Chen Xuanli's present temper, let Yuan Zai but breathe the faintest hint of a wish to depart, and he would be cut down on the spot as a deserter.

The floor of this level was strewn with sharp wreckage and a great deal of shattered porcelain, so that running was scarcely possible. Chen Xuanli and all below him picked their way forward warily, hopping from spot to spot. Yuan Zai took the chance to search ceaselessly all about—when suddenly his eyes lit up, and he could not believe what he saw.

At the edge of the floor, ten-odd paces from him, stood a squared eave-bearing pillar, holding up the high-tilted corner of the tower's outer eaves. Against the lower part of this pillar there now leaned a man, his clothes in tatters, seemingly senseless. This man was scorched all over, his features barely to be made out; but that single eye he knew all too well—had once, indeed, wet his own breeches for fear of it.

'Zhang Xiaojing?!'

Yuan Zai was first startled, then delighted; he had never thought to meet this fellow once again in the Qinzheng Wuben Tower. With no leisure for further thought, he shouted to call Chen Xuanli back. Chen Xuanli turned his head and demanded urgently what the matter was.

Yuan Zai pointed at Zhang Xiaojing: 'The arch-culprit who blew up the tower is he. We of the Jing'an Bureau have been hunting him all along.' Chen Xuanli cast a glance that way. He had heard the name before—the man had once, it seemed, been Commander of the Jing'an, and had then, somehow, been put on the city-wide wanted list, only for the warrant to be canceled soon after.

But the name gave Chen Xuanli no more than an instant's pause; he had no interest in cracking cases—the safety of the Son of Heaven was the one thing that mattered. He was about to press on when Yuan Zai called out again: 'This is an important imperial prisoner—go on ahead, General! I shall deal with matters here!'

Chen Xuanli caught the drift: the fellow was making a pretext of not wishing to go on. But the pretext was a high-sounding one, and he could not gainsay it. The man who had blown up the tower could of course not be left unregarded. Having no time for wrangling of words, he could only give a cold snort, and, taking the others with him, rushed hurriedly on toward the fourth floor.

Yuan Zai watched Chen Xuanli and the rest go, then picked his way, one step deep and one step shallow, to stand before Zhang Xiaojing. He bent his head and gave a savoring smile, and drew a blade from his waist.

The blade had belonged to a Longwu guardsman fallen at his post at the entrance, and Chen Xuanli had picked it up with his own hand and given it to Yuan Zai. Unused to the weight of such a keen military weapon, he hefted it several times over before he had it steady.

'When you were lording it so high in Chao Fen's house, you never thought your comeuppance would come so soon, did you?' Yuan Zai said to Zhang Xiaojing, wagging the blade's point, his voice full of venom. That once of wetting his breeches had been an utter and monstrous humiliation; he hated this raging Fifth Yama of a man to the very marrow.

Zhang Xiaojing kept his eyes tight shut, and gave no response at all to Yuan Zai's voice, whether alive or dead none could tell.

Yuan Zai set the blade's point to Zhang Xiaojing and began slowly to press. He had reckoned it all out: that Zhang Xiaojing should die here in the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was the best of outcomes. It was not from spite alone, but from a weighing of his own interests. The scheme he had labored to lay this night would be made wholly secure only by Zhang Xiaojing's death.

Yuan Zai now understood deeply how Feng Dalun had felt: this fellow was far too dangerous; so long as he lived, he was a vast unknown quantity, and unless he died there was truly no setting one's mind at ease.

'The evils you have done are enough to make the court fetch back the ancient penalty of death by slicing. In killing you now, I do you a kindness besides.'

Muttering this idle, pointless stuff, Yuan Zai slowly extended the straight blade. He had never killed a man, and was somewhat on edge, so that his force was not well-judged. The point first flicked open the outer robe, took aim at the heart, then pierced the coarse skin grimed with soot, and at once blood welled out. This gave Yuan Zai a start, and he drew back a little despite himself, then set the blade to it once more.

This time the point was steady; one last thrust of force, and it would drive clean into the heart. At that moment Yuan Zai felt of a sudden a violent pain at the back of his skull; his sight went black, and he fainted dead away on the instant.

'You lecher!'

Tanqi flung aside the bronze ox candlestick in her hand, stepped over Yuan Zai's body, and threw herself toward Zhang Xiaojing.

Of what had happened after he clambered to the summit of the lantern-tower, Zhang Xiaojing's memory was somewhat blurred.

He remembered dimly leaning against the suanni cross-frame, awaiting the coming of the last moment, a riot of color and light before his eyes, dazzling past all telling.

At first Zhang Xiaojing took this for the hallucination that comes upon a man before death; yet ever in his ears a strong voice went on crying out. His reason had given up all thought of flight, but that untamed, unyielding urge within him had never truly owned defeat, and strove all the while to find some chance of living on.

He strained to open his single eye and make it out, and found at last that it was a great long string of many-colored gauze. This too, he supposed, was of Mao Shun's designing: the lamplight of the lantern-chamber, shining through them, could show forth a radiance of richer depth. At this hour the lantern-tower was blazing fiercely, its flames licking the sky, and these gauzes hung suspended in mid-air, dancing without cease upon the rising currents of heat.

How were they made fast to the lantern-tower?

Zhang Xiaojing lifted his head, and saw all at once that above him ten-odd hempen ropes were all made fast to the suanni cross-frame, their lower ends scattered star-wise, drawn off each in a different direction. The gauzes of every color were hung upon these ropes, dangling thick about the lantern-tower on every side, like flower-ornaments on a lady's brow in spring—these were called the spring-drawing cords, though Zhang Xiaojing neither knew this nor cared.

What he cared for was the ropes themselves. After a brief scrutiny he found among them one hempen rope of quite unusual thickness, its head bound at the neck of the suanni, while its other end was drawn off aslant to the edge of the southern rampart of the Xingqing Palace and made fast to a crenel there. Seen from afar, a thick line was slung slantwise between the wall and the tower-top.

A thought of living on rose up thus, out of nowhere.

Yuchang was a most meticulous man, and had surely left himself a line of retreat well in advance, so as to get swiftly away once the last mechanism was set going. This line would not be downward through the tower—there would never be time enough—so his way out could only be above; and then but one means was left:

the spring-drawing cords.

To slide clear of the lantern-tower down this spring-drawing cord—this was the swiftest way to withdraw.

What came next Zhang Xiaojing truly could not recall clearly. He remembered hazily struggling to his feet, climbing onto the cross-frame, seizing by sheer instinct the thickest of the ropes, then slinging his two hands from a silk streamer flying loose in the air; a swing of his body, and in a trice he had slid clear of the lantern-tower's summit.

His body slid swiftly across the night sky of Chang'an, away from the lantern-tower and flying toward the Xingqing Palace.

Just as he was about to reach the southern rampart of the Xingqing Palace, the lantern-tower burst apart of a sudden, and the violent shock-wave set the whole rope swinging fiercely. Hard upon this, the upper half of the lantern-tower toppled over and crashed toward the Xingqing Palace, and this motion wholly changed the rope's line. Zhang Xiaojing, whose feet had all but touched the wall, was jerked abruptly back up into the air, and, amid a great mass of flying debris, went rolling into the third floor...

...Zhang Xiaojing slowly opened his eyes, and saw Tanqi's face.

Tanqi's long black hair hung loose and disordered across her brow, a strand here and a wisp there; her cheeks were smeared all over with grime; that water-blue short skirt was torn past mending, riddled with scorch-holes great and small, baring here and there a scatter of pale white skin.

But she had at this moment not a jot of bashfulness; she leaned forward, cradled Zhang Xiaojing's head, and called his name aloud. Zhang Xiaojing's lips worked, but no word would come. Tanqi looked to left and right, turned up a ewer from among the rubble, and let the few last drops of wine within it fall into his throat. Zhang Xiaojing opened his mouth with all his might and caught them on his tongue; before this, within the lantern-tower, his whole body had been all but baked dry, and now these drops falling into his mouth were as a draught of sweet dew.

Zhang Xiaojing slowly came back to his senses, and asked her how she had come to run here.

Tanqi herself had never thought to meet Zhang Xiaojing again in this place. Earlier she had angered the heir apparent, and been dragged from the Lantern Festival spring banquet by the guards and shut up for the while in a storeroom off the Wind-Wooing Hall on the third floor.

This floor had no walls, and so the storeroom had been built to sink half into the second floor. When the lantern-tower blew, the searing blast-wind swept through the whole Wind-Wooing Hall, and the entire floor was savaged most cruelly—only this storeroom barely came off unscathed. Hearing the chaos outside the storeroom, Tanqi grasped that this was the Que-le Huo-duo breaking loose, and her heart sank into the utmost despair.

When the sounds outside had died down a little, she pushed open the warped and buckled door and stumbled through the drifting smoke and dust, not knowing where she ought to go.

Just at this moment Tanqi saw Yuan Zai making ready to lift his blade and kill. She did not know Yuan Zai, but knew Zhang Xiaojing's face at once. In her extremity she caught up a heavy bronze ox-shaped tall candlestick and smote it down hard upon Yuan Zai—and thus saved Zhang Xiaojing's life.

When Tanqi had told her tale, Zhang Xiaojing turned his neck, his face showing puzzlement: 'Were you not in the Pingkang Quarter? How come you to be in the Qinzheng Wuben Tower?'

Better had he not asked; for at the question, the wall of feeling that Tanqi had held taut all this while by main force of will crumbled at last to pieces. She threw herself upon his breast and wept aloud, repeating over and over: 'I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry...' She felt that she was of no use at all, had done nothing well, and had in the end let the Que-le Huo-duo break loose after all, betraying the trust her young master and the lecher had both placed in her.

'Don't cry—what is it, then?' Zhang Xiaojing's tone was stiff.

Sobbing, Tanqi told over how she had, through Taizhen's hand, stirred up the Son of Heaven. Zhang Xiaojing said, comforted: 'Had you not made such a stir before the imperial presence, and got them to lift the city-wide warrant, I should likely have been shot dead by this fellow at Chao Fen's gate—so your efforts were not spent in vain after all.'

He tried to put out a hand to touch her hair-knot, but the moment he moved his arm, the pull of the muscles gave him a stab of raw pain.

'But the Que-le Huo-duo went off all the same...' Tanqi's tears washed two runnels down her grimy face. The chaos of a moment ago had struck her too hard a blow. The men of the Jing'an Bureau had striven so long, and had in the end failed to stop this attack. A fierce sense of defeat drew Tanqi into a quicksand of self-doubt, from which she could scarcely pull free.

Zhang Xiaojing explained weakly: 'That blast a moment ago would have killed far more people—thanks to you being there. I said long ago you could do things of more worth than fetching tea and pouring water; many a man cannot match you.'

Tanqi forced a smile, taking it only for Zhang Xiaojing's coaxing. His body was all spotted with blood, his garments torn to rags; she could scarcely imagine what a hard pass he must have faced, alone and single-handed, during the time she had been shut up in the Qinzheng Wuben Tower.

Even if the Que-le Huo-duo had truly been weakened, that must surely be the fruit of this man's running to and fro?

Zhang Xiaojing struggled to rise, and Tanqi hastily propped him half-sitting against the pillar. Just then Yuan Zai too came slowly round; rubbing the fiercely aching back of his skull, he raised his head, and finding that it was a maidservant who had struck him, could not help but grow angry: 'Insolent slut, that you dare to set upon a Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau?'

In truth the real Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau was Ji Wen; Yuan Zai spoke thus out of his habit of draping himself in a borrowed tiger's skin. But who would have thought it touched Tanqi on the raw? Her almond eyes glared: 'You great oaf, and are you fit to pass yourself off as a Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau?' She caught up the bronze candlestick and smote him hard once more. This time the force was heavier than before, and struck him on the thigh; Yuan Zai could not help letting out a scream, and fell to the floor yet again.

'Tanqi...' Zhang Xiaojing called her back, and said helplessly, 'He is indeed a man of the Jing'an Bureau.'

At these words Tanqi flung down the candlestick, and her tears came pattering down. If even such a man as this had got into the Jing'an Bureau, did it not mean her young master was surely lost? Yuan Zai, seeing a chance of saving his life, hastily said aloud: 'Between myself and Commander Zhang there may be some misunderstanding!'

Zhang Xiaojing fixed his gaze on this broad-browed bureaucrat; the half of his own sorry plight was owing to this man. He said with a set face: 'I warned you before that there was trouble at the Xingqing Palace—has it not now come true?' Yuan Zai nodded over and over. Having just been beaten sore by this madwoman, he dared no longer put on his official airs.

'And it being so, why then did you still mean to kill me?'

Yuan Zai's wits turned exceedingly fast; knowing that kowtowing for mercy would avail nothing, he simply lifted his chin: 'So many people saw with their own eyes that you, Commander, made ready to blow up the lantern-tower; though I alone believed you, I could never satisfy the multitude.'

These words were most vague, and most artful: they showed at once that he bore no ill will, hinted that his deed had been forced by circumstance, and faintly, in turn, called Zhang Xiaojing's own doings into question. Zhang Xiaojing knew the man was mistaken, but to explain it would cost far too much breath. With the situation now so pressing, he had no time to argue his innocence, and asked outright: 'What is the state of things outside just now?'

Yuan Zai could only rub his thigh and give a brief, plain account: that the Qinzheng Wuben Tower had been broken into, and that Chen Xuanli had led a party to its relief. Zhang Xiaojing knit his brows tight and for a long while said nothing. He knew that, besides the Que-le Huo-duo, Xiao Gui had another plan in hand. What he had not looked for was that this plan should be bolder and more savage even than he had imagined—that it should cut its way, at a single stroke, right up to the imperial presence.

This fellow's strength, though it ranked nowhere at all among the adversaries of Great Tang, was beyond doubt that of the enemy who had come nearest to success.

'I must go up!'

Zhang Xiaojing struggled to get to his feet, but his body lurched to one side and he near failed to stand. The string of fierce fights and flights just past had spent his strength and will alike to the dregs; he was hurt all over, and in the sorriest of states.

Tanqi's eyes went wide; hastily she steadied Zhang Xiaojing's arm and said in a trembling voice: 'Lecher, you have done enough already—don't force yourself any further...' Zhang Xiaojing shook his head and sighed: 'The relief will be at least a hundred finger-snaps in coming; but for Xiao Gui to kill takes only the crook of a finger.'

'But is not General Chen Xuanli there? Surely he is better able than you in your present state?' said Tanqi. For some reason she did not wish to see this man go stake his life once more—not in the least. Though the Son of Heaven above hung in mortal peril, her one wish was that he might lie quietly here.

'Chen Xuanli is a good soldier, but he is no match for Xiao Gui. The only one who can stop him is I.' said Zhang Xiaojing. Once more he clenched his teeth hard and forced himself up—first to a half-kneel, then, with a strong push of his foot, rose at last again to his feet. The look on his face was of the utmost weariness; only the single eye still shone with a fierce and savage light.

Yuan Zai looked at him as at some monster: hurt to such a state as this, and still the fellow would go up the tower to stop that pack of desperate, murderous Pifu? Reckon it how he might, he could not compute where the worth of such an act lay.

Neither did Tanqi understand.

'The road is one I chose; I shall walk it to the end.' A hoarse voice rang out within the Wind-Wooing Hall.

Amid the ruins and the leaping fire, Zhang Xiaojing went swaying and staggering toward the upper floors. His figure was uncommonly weak, yet uncommonly resolute. Only in this moment did Tanqi wholly understand why her young master had chosen him at the first to be Commander of the Jing'an—her young master's eye was never wrong.

At the thought of Li Bi, a pang went through Tanqi's heart, and she could not hold back another sob. This faint sound was caught at once by Zhang Xiaojing. He halted his steps and said, his back to her: 'Oh—yes, here is a piece of good news for you. Your young master is still alive—well, I should say, alive at least when last I saw him.'

Tanqi's eyes flashed, and a thread of glad surprise welled up in her. For some reason she felt strongly that her young master must have been saved by him. But knowing that now was no time to press for details, she hesitantly put out her arms and embraced Zhang Xiaojing from behind; a faint fragrance stole quietly into his nostrils, and made him think, in spite of himself, of that moment of tenderness in the Nestorian confessional.

'Thank you.' Tanqi said in a low voice, pressing her face to that scorch-covered back, and felt the muscles there tighten for an instant.

Li Bi had all but worked a miracle.

From Shengping Ward to Guangde Ward, crossing six wards and running north through four, he had taken less than two marks of time. Given the press of the Lantern Festival traffic, this was well-nigh an impossible feat. Ten-odd people at the least were sent flying by his galloping steed, and he had not even the time to stop and look.

The sudden explosion of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower had wrought the greatest chaos in Wannian County to the west. But further off, the common folk, knowing nothing of the truth of it, took it only for a fine bit of spectacle. In Chang'an County to the east above all, everyone went on viewing the lanterns as before, went on seeking out food to eat as before, wholly unaware that a great disaster was quietly unfolding.

By rights the Jingzhao Prefecture should now have issued an emergency order, sounding the street-drums to break off the lantern-viewing, sending the people back each to his own ward, and locking all the city gates. But the whole central node of the court too was trapped within the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, and for the moment there was not even a man to command from the center. Long years of settled peace had worn away the whole city of Chang'an's wariness and efficiency to nothing.

Only the many watchtowers near the Xingqing Palace still held fast to their posts. The constables signaled frantically for rescue; but lacking the support of the great watchtower, there was simply no one to mark these messages. Those purple lanterns could only flash again and again, in vain.

Li Bi rushed in a single breath to the gate of Guangde Ward, and saw from afar wisps of lingering smoke curling up within it—from the wreckage of the Jing'an Bureau's great hall, not yet burned out. With no leisure to lament, he made to drive his horse straight into the ward.

The soldiers at the ward gate, seeing a startled horse burst upon them, were about to raise their fork-poles to bar the way, when a sharp shout from the rider brought them up short. Was this not... was this not Deputy Director Li? Deputy Director Li, carried off by the villains—come back, of all things, of his own accord?

In the soldiers' moment of blank astonishment, Li Bi leapt on through, and made straight for the Jingzhao Prefecture.

Within the Jingzhao Prefecture and without, they were still dealing in orderly fashion with the aftermath of the Jing'an Bureau's burning, and no one had yet grasped what that distant thunderclap could mean—so dull had the Jing'an Bureau become.

Li Bi rushed up before the prefecture, sprang down from his horse, flung the reins aside, and strode straight in at the main gate. A petty clerk carrying a bundle of files was just coming out; looking up, he was struck dumb on the instant, and with a slap a dozen and more scrolls went rolling to the ground. Beside him a lightly burned man was shuffling toward the gate on a crutch. Catching sight of Li Bi, this wounded man could not help crying out: 'Deputy Director Li!'—and then fell to his knees, weeping aloud.

To the reactions of those about him Li Bi turned a deaf ear. Swinging his arms, he pressed inward with an air of fierce menace. All along the way, from soldiers to officials, none but were struck with amazement; one and all they gave way and opened a path, shrinking from the edge of him.

Li Bi walked on all the way to the main hall before he halted, looked about him, and then seized a petty clerk by the front of his robe: 'Who is in charge now?'

'It is Censor Ji... ah, no—Deputy Director Ji.' the petty clerk answered, quaking with fear, and then pointed toward the tribunal hall.

'Ji Wen?' Li Bi arched a brow. This man had, as it happened, no small connection with the Eastern Palace: he was the brother's-son of the chancellor Ji Xu, and had once been recommended to the imperial presence by Xue Yi, Litterateur to the heir apparent—only for the Son of Heaven to pronounce a single sentence: 'He is a good-for-nothing; I have no use for him.' From that time his career had not prospered. Who would have thought the fellow would go over to Li Linfu, and be content to run before his horse as a lackey, come here to seize power?

At this thought Li Bi gave a cold laugh, let go the petty clerk, and walked to the door of the tribunal hall. Before the door stood a few guards Ji Wen had brought with him; they did not know Li Bi, but, overawed by the mighty presence of him, stood fearful and dared not stir. Li Bi flew up a foot and kicked the inner door open on the instant.

At this moment Ji Wen was within, pouring and drinking to himself, in a mood of blissful ease. His charge was to seize power; as for the other affairs of the Jing'an Bureau, there was Yuan Zai running about outside in any case, and he need not trouble himself over them. So Ji Wen had bidden someone fetch him a hu-measure of grape wine, shut the door, and set to savoring it alone in great contentment.

When Li Bi came bursting in so suddenly, Ji Wen started so that his wrist shook, and the fine wine in his cup went splashing all over the carpet. This grape wine had been brought a thousand li from the Western Regions, at no small cost. Ji Wen, at once grieved and enraged, lifted his eyes and was about to burst out—when all at once an unseen great hand seemed to clutch his throat, and no sound would come.

'Vice-Duan Ji is in a fine humor indeed.' Li Bi's voice was as though steeped in the ice-water of the depth of winter.

Ji Wen was for a moment thrown into no little confusion. Had not this fellow been carried off? How was he back all of a sudden? If he had been rescued, why had Yuan Zai not sent word ahead? What did he mean to do, coming back to seek me out?

A string of questions rose swiftly in Ji Wen's mind, and settled at last into three words: 'Vice-Duan Ji.'—Vice-Duan was the polite by-name of a Palace Censor; that he called me Vice-Duan showed plainly he would not own me as Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau—he was here to seize power! Ji Wen swiftly judged out the crux of the matter; the muscles of his face rearranged themselves in a trice into a stiff smile: 'Changyuan, how is it you have come back?'

Li Bi said, blunt and direct: 'A great matter has befallen before the Xingqing Palace, and Your Honor still sits here at his ease, pouring wine?'

'Eh?' Ji Wen had not thought that the moment he opened his mouth he would put so abrupt a question. 'Before the Xingqing Palace? Are they not at the lantern-floats and the spring banquet?'

Li Bi sighed inwardly. So great a matter, and a Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau wholly unaware of it—to what a depth of incompetence did that reach? He took a step forward and cried sternly: 'The Pifu laid fierce-fire thunder within the lantern-tower; the Xingqing Palace is now one scene of wrack, ruined front and back, and the state of Chang'an is in the extremity of peril!'

Ji Wen's whiskers gave a violent twitch. No wonder he had heard a great crash from the west a moment ago; he had taken it for the first stirring of spring thunder—and it was, in truth, so dire a calamity as this! On the Qinzheng Wuben Tower were the Son of Heaven and the whole company of ministers; had they been caught by fierce-fire thunder, would that not mean... would that not mean... He dared think no further.

'I—I shall muster men as fast as I can, and go to the sovereign's aid...' Ji Wen's voice was dry. But Li Bi cut him off without ceremony, pressing him step by step: 'There is no time! If you have any heart to aid the sovereign, there is but one thing you can do!'

'What?'

'Where is the Right Minister at this hour?'

Ji Wen looked at him in bewilderment: 'The Right Minister—is he not up on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower at the spring banquet?' Li Bi said with a set face: 'He left the Qinzheng Wuben Tower before the explosion—where has he gone?'

Ji Wen's whiskers twitched again. He was no fool, and knew well what it meant for a man to have left at just this juncture. He could not help a wry smile: 'I have been all this while at the Jingzhao Prefecture, clearing up the wreckage—what leisure had I to look elsewhere?'

'You are his man; how should you not know your master's whereabouts?' Li Bi had no mind at all for empty, probing courtesies, but came straight to the point.

Hearing this, Ji Wen composed his features and said: 'There, Changyuan, you are in the wrong. I hold, all unworthy, the posts of Commissioner of the Left Patrol and Palace Censor, to impeach with rigor on the court's behalf and make good its lapses and omissions—am I any man's private servant? Where the Right Minister may be, you had best go ask the Phoenix Pavilion.'

'You truly do not know?'

'Just so!' Ji Wen answered with great firmness, though within he was a little dispirited. He was, when all was said, no intimate of the Right Minister's; whatever plan the latter might have, he would never confide it to him.

Li Bi said: 'Very well! Then I must ask Vice-Duan Ji to remain here a while. When the Jing'an Bureau has ascertained the Right Minister's whereabouts, we shall come to question you again!' Ji Wen thought to himself, so here at last is the meat of the play; he rolled his eyelids: 'Your Honor was seized by the villains, and the Jing'an Bureau was left a dragon-host without a head. It was out of care for the governance of the city of Chang'an that I took it over for the time; I have no wish to cling to the post—yet what I took up was an appointment of the Phoenix Pavilion, and I dare not quit it without cause.'

Put plainly: my appointment was issued by the Secretariat, and if you would take it back, you must first have a warrant of transfer. Ji Wen grasped that, with so great a matter fallen upon the Xingqing Palace and the Right Minister's whereabouts turned doubtful, at so extraordinary an hour he must keep his grip on some office of vital moment if he were to hold the initiative amid the disorder. This authority over the Jing'an Bureau he must on no account let slip.

Li Bi's gaze was keen: 'And if I insist?'

Ji Wen gave a cold laugh and clapped his hands, and the guards outside the door came quickly in. These guards were all men he had brought with him, not old hands of the Jing'an Bureau, and so the surer to use.

'Here, men—help Academician Li away to rest!'

Li Bi's substantive post was Academician-in-Waiting of the Hanlin; in styling him so, Ji Wen had made up his mind not to acknowledge his standing as Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau.

Hearing the order, the guards rushed forward all together and were about to lay hands on him. But Li Bi gave a slight smile and likewise clapped his hands, and a body of Lüben Guards soldiers appeared all at once from without. Those few guards found themselves surrounded instead, and every one showed alarm on his face.

Ji Wen held up his great seal and cried in fury: 'The rightful officer is here—would you rebel?' Li Bi slowly unfastened a seal from his own waist as well, his face cold and stern: 'The rightful officer is here.'

Within the tribunal hall of the Jingzhao Prefecture the two of them displayed their two great seals at once, and stood confronting each other. The seal Ji Wen held up, with its xiezhi-knob and silver cord, was the special seal of the Censorate. Tonight the seizure of power had been got up in haste, and the Secretariat had had no time to cast a new seal; it had merely issued a document, lending this seal the authority to act at discretion in the matter.

As for that bronze seal of the Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau, with its turtle-knob, that Li Bi held—by ordinary reckoning it should carry more force than the Censorate's seal of office. But he had earlier been carried off by the villains, and the document the Secretariat had sent down had expressly noted that, to guard against the villains' turning it to use, this seal was specially annulled—which was to say, the moment Ji Wen took over the Jing'an Bureau, it became a wholly useless dead seal.

Ji Wen laughed aloud: 'Academician Li, such a dead seal as that—better not bring it out to shame yourself!' But Li Bi held the seal high, his expression still unaltered. Ji Wen's laughter broke off short in the middle; his eyes opened wider and wider, and he found that something was not right.

This was no plain turtle-knob bronze seal, but a turtle-knob bronze seal with a rim of gold, and that line of dark gold graven round it looked exceedingly glaring to the eye.

This was not the seal of the Deputy Director of the Jing'an Bureau, but the seal of the Director of the Jing'an Bureau!

He Zhizhang, though he lay gravely ill abed, had yet, in point of law, never given up his office of Director of the Jing'an Bureau.

Li Bi had, in the hour of the Monkey, gone to 'call upon' He Zhizhang in Xuanping Ward, and had taken this true seal away with him as he went. To display it now meant that he held the power to 'act provisionally as Director of the Jing'an Bureau.' Ji Wen discovered, to his horror, that after all this winding about, he had become Li Bi's subordinate instead.

'This—this is a forged warrant! Director He is already fallen sick; he could never have entrusted the seal to you!' Ji Wen was beside himself. Li Bi said: 'It is precisely because Director He lies ill that he made a point of committing this seal to me; if you have any doubt, you may go and ask the venerable gentleman yourself—here, men, take Deputy Director Ji's seal from him for me!'

Only now did he style him 'Deputy Director Ji'—there could be no sharper mockery. The men of the Jing'an Bureau had long looked askance at this superior of theirs, and laid hands on him without the least ceremony, snatching the seal of office clean away. Those few guards dared offer no resistance at all, and were likewise disarmed and shoved aside. Ji Wen's face was ashen; stripped of the Secretariat document's legal shelter, he had no footing whatever in the Jing'an Bureau.

'I demand to see the Right Minister! I demand to see the Right Minister!' Ji Wen suddenly began to shout like a madman.

'If you can get to see him, so much the better—we are looking for him too!'

Li Bi left Ji Wen and his few guards in the tribunal hall, and set men to ward the door—a virtual house-arrest. Then he swiftly gathered together the few surviving recorders and questioned them on the state of things, only then discovering how thorny the matter was.

The Pifu's attack, joined to the great fire, had cost the Jing'an Bureau grievous casualties. Since Ji Wen took over he had done nothing to the purpose, but had rather driven out a number of the staff of Hu descent. From the hour of the Dog until now, near five shichen, the whole Jing'an Bureau had been like a headless fly, and had not even restored the watchtower network. What angered Li Bi the more was that the one decision Ji Wen had made was to arrest Zhang Xiaojing, squandering a mass of resources in that mistaken direction.

It was a thorough-going shambles from first to last.

'Not enough to make a thing succeed, and more than enough to make it fail!' Li Bi gave a heavy snort, his heart full of scorn for this good-for-nothing. A few recorders asked warily: 'Deputy Director Li, what are we to do now?'

'Send men to the Xingqing Palace as fast as may be, to learn how things stand.' Li Bi gave his first order. The safety of the Xingqing Palace—or, to put it more plainly, whether the Son of Heaven lived or died—would bear directly on the whole train of decisions to follow.

'Further—restore the great watchtower with all speed, and give word to the offices everywhere and to the gate guards that the lantern fair is to end early. Reimpose the curfew; let all the people return at once to their wards. Let every city gate be locked and barred, and opened neither by day nor night save on order.'

Hearing this order, the recorders one and all held their breath and hushed. That even the lantern fair was to be called off showed to what a pitch of gravity the matter had come.

'Further—we must find the Right Minister as soon as we can. Every last residence of his that stands on record is to be investigated and made clear.'

A cold glint flashed through Li Bi's eyes. If the whole affair were the chancellor's doing, he must yet have some most dangerous move held in reserve. What had already happened need not be dwelt on; what mattered was how to seize the initiative in the disorder to come. For, mark, in a struggle at this level, it was one or the other of them for the grave. Li Bi must reckon on the worst, and make ready in advance.

Hearing that the Right Minister too was to be investigated, the recorders looked at one another the more, and none dared question deeply. Li Bi lifted his head and sighed faintly: 'The great house has already toppled; there is nothing for it but to do what a man can.' Seeing their superior's look so grave, the several recorders felt a chill of awe, and one after another folded their hands to signify obedience.

Strange to tell, the moment he returned, the very soul of the whole Jing'an Bureau returned with him, and the air of the Jingzhao Prefecture was changed. Even those officials Ji Wen had brought in were caught up by Li Bi's swift, decisive style, and swiftly fell into the rhythm of it. Adjutant Zhao of the Right Xiao Guard, for one, felt the manner of management wholly altered—far better than the old slack and dilatory ways.

The battered and broken Jing'an Bureau, under Li Bi's strong driving, creaked and groaned back into motion once more.

At this a recorder asked once more, warily: 'The Right Minister's residences may not all stand in the name of the Li household; has the Deputy Director any further hint to give?'

The dwellings in the city of Chang'an were far too many; and even had Li Linfu some secret house, he would never have hung out his own name-board over it so boldly. Without some direction to go on, to search thus was no better than dredging the sea for a needle.

Li Bi pondered a moment, and of a sudden a flash of inspiration crossed his mind: 'You may go and look into which of the rich men's mansions in the capital has a Self-Raining Pavilion in it.'

After Li Bi had been kidnapped by the Pifu, he had been taken to a lavish mansion, and had seen with his own eyes their making of a test-explosion of the lantern-tower. The most notable feature of that mansion was a Self-Raining Pavilion with a channel-dike along its eaves. Such pavilions had their origin in Persia, and cost no small sum to build; not just anyone could raise one.

When the Pifu had first seized Li Bi, they had had no thought of leaving him alive, and so had not troubled to hide anything from him. Now that he had come through alive, he could not let slip so conspicuous a clue. Trace that mansion, and who it was that had bankrolled the Pifu from behind the scenes would be plain at a glance.

But the recorders were still full of misgiving: 'The bureau's files have all been burned away. For matters of construction we should have to go to the Forestry and Crafts Bureau to consult the records, and there would hardly be time enough.'

Li Bi looked about him: 'Where is Xu Bin? Did he live?' Xu Bin had a memory of surpassing power; were he yet here, the Jing'an Bureau's searching would take half the effort for twice the result.

An official said that Recorder Xu had been wounded and was resting in the infirmary hall; because Deputy Director Ji reckoned he might be a Pifu mole, he had even set extra men to watch over him. Li Bi was so angered he laughed instead: 'Xu Bin was the very man I sent to hunt out the mole—this Ji Wen is truly blind as a dog!'

He bade a servant lead the way, and went himself to the infirmary hall to see.

Order in the infirmary hall was a little better now than before; the physicians had finished their tending, though the groans of the wounded still fell unbroken on the ear. What human effort could do had been spent; what came next lay with each man's own fortune. Li Bi wrinkled his nose; this smell, of charred human flesh mixed with oil and medicine, made him most uneasy. But this scene was, in great part, of his own responsibility; and so Li Bi could only, in a spirit of atonement, force down the heaving of his stomach.

Xu Bin's resting-place was in a corner of the infirmary hall, a space partitioned off by two screens, with two soldiers keeping faithful guard outside. Li Bi walked over, waved the guards away, and stepped in. Xu Bin lay on his side upon the bed, his face turned outward, his eyes closed and silent, his head still wound round and round with white cloth strips.

Li Bi softened his tread and drew near—when all at once, in a single instant, his pupils shrank sharply, and his whole body froze where it stood.

Xu Bin's body lay curled and turned the wrong way about, facing the inner side of the bed.

Which was to say, his whole head and neck had been wrenched round by main force to face the other way.

As the place of the Son of Heaven's leisure and revelry, the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was decked out in the utmost that lavishness could contrive. Its towers rose like mountains, its beams were carved and its rafters painted; above hung flying eaves with pendent bells, and midway floated colored silks and streaming gauze. The style of it looked exceedingly splendid—but once fire took hold, every part of it was so much tinder. Halls and galleries alike were now shrouded in rolling black smoke, cramming every crevice, as though a madman were flinging thick ink about on every side.

The distance from the third floor to the seventh was no great way; but Zhang Xiaojing's bodily state had sunk to the very bottom, and the wrack strewn all along the way turned this stretch into a thicket of thorns. Gritting his teeth, he skirted the shattered porcelain and broken boards on the floor as best he could, and groped his way toward the stair-mouth.

Along the way he saw many servants and officials great and small lying fallen in every posture, whether alive or dead none could tell; before them the tables lay overturned legs-up, jade dishes and rare viands spilled across the floor, in a wretchedness past all telling. These people, revelling and drinking deep but a moment before, had in the next instant met the shock. Zhang Xiaojing found too some corpses dressed unlike the guests—some of the Pifu, and some of the Longwu Army.

It seemed that Chen Xuanli, after climbing the tower, had met with fierce resistance from the Pifu, yet had held all the while to a forward course.

Zhang Xiaojing rushed in a single breath to the sixth floor, and had to stop to catch his breath a while. He had scarcely eaten all day—only a few plain-oil fritters some shichen before—and now his belly was empty and golden stars swam faintly before his eyes. Lowering his head a little, he saw, beneath a fallen stone screen, the end of a roast leg of mutton showing. The mutton was roasted a crisp golden brown, and at the leg-bone a hand still gripped it.

It seemed that when the blast came, this hapless guest had just taken up the mutton leg, making ready to fall to with relish. Then the tremor struck, and before he could get a single bite he had been crushed beneath the stone screen. Zhang Xiaojing bent and hauled the mutton leg free; the hand did not stir—its owner had, it seemed, come to grief. The irony was that it was the very blaze rising all around that kept this mutton leg warm, so that it had not gone cold and rank and clotted.

Zhang Xiaojing opened his mouth wide and, without ceremony, tore off a strip and chewed it heartily. It was the imperial kitchen's craft, after all: the mutton was roasted crisp and fragrant and tender, seasoned besides with costly spices such as clove and pepper, and drizzled over with apricot syrup. The moment it went down, it turned at once into a warm current spreading through all his limbs and frame, filling back a little of his vital strength.

He was ravenous besides, and ate as he went; in a little while a fat and tender leg of mutton was gnawed down to the bare bone. Zhang Xiaojing felt at last a little better, and, gripping this thigh-bone, came to the stair-mouth leading from the sixth floor up to the seventh. A glance upward, and his eyes turned savage.

On the stair, four or five corpses lay sprawled every which way, the most of them of the Longwu Army—whereby it was plain that Chen Xuanli had met an ambush here. Yuan Zai had said they came but ten-odd men in all; reckoning it thus, the hands left to Chen Xuanli were now few indeed. Even had he broken through by luck, it was at grievous cost.

Yet this too proved, by contraries, that Xiao Gui's men could by no means be many; else Chen Xuanli would be among these corpses.

Zhang Xiaojing thrust the bone into his belt and was about to mount the stair, when a thought struck him of a sudden and he drew his foot back again. Between the sixth floor and the seventh there were but two passages—the guests' and the goods'—which must surely be sternly guarded. To go up rashly would likely be to get himself shot dead outright.

With soft and careful steps he went to the edge of the floor, where the eave-corners hung very low, and the rim turned out into a jutting wooden lip. Zhang Xiaojing hooked his fingers over the wooden lip, set his feet on the railing, and with a strong heave clambered up onto a slanting roof-ridge laid all with black tiles. Trotting up a few steps along the slope of the ridge, and leaping over a carved balustrade, he came to the seventh floor.

The seventh floor of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was called the Star-Plucking Hall, likening its seven floors to the seven stars of the Northern Dipper. It was a long, rectangular hall, wide and open and without pillars; the floor was a little deliberately inclined, and at the highest point, on the north, was the imperial seat of the Son of Heaven, facing south with its back to the north, the other places ranged in order southward and downward, guarding the imperial seat below—this being what is meant by: 'To govern by virtue is to be as the pole-star, which keeps its place, and all the stars turn toward it.'

On the south side of the hall there was besides a little Sky-River Bridge, joining the main body of the hall to a broad platform of level timber without, cloud-towers rising on either hand. Standing upon the platform, one might lean on the rail and gaze afar, looking down upon the myriad people—a view of the finest. The platform stood exceeding close to the lantern-tower; a little while before, when the lantern-tower was first started up, it was from here that the Lantern-Crown Red Tally had flung out its candle-flame and set the tower going.

But alas, for that very reason, when the blast came a moment ago that timber platform was the first thing to collapse, and fell from the city wall together with the luckless wretches standing on it to view the lamps. The Sky-River Bridge too was half wrecked, its remaining half a piteous timber frame half-tilted in the air, like the wail of a broken dragon.

The spot where Zhang Xiaojing clambered onto the seventh floor was, as it fell out, the bridge-head left of the Sky-River Bridge. Swiftly he crouched low and hid beside a pillar-beast, watching narrowly within. The smoke drifting up from below formed a matchless cover.

The hall on this floor was half-enclosed, with a ring of the Xingqing Palace's southern rampart shielding it without besides; and to this was added the great deal of the Que-le Huo-duo's force that Zhang Xiaojing had striven so desperately to bleed away. So the blast and impact just now had not cut to the bone; there was no scene of dead and wounded heaped together, only a somewhat disordered one.

At this hour the Star-Plucking Hall was divided into three sharply distinct groups of people. A hundred and more guests in fine dress were huddled together, quivering like a covey of quail; beside them stood ten-odd Pifu, bearing short crossbows and long blades, ready to loose slaughter at any moment. Further off, toward the south, Chen Xuanli and fewer than ten soldiers of the Longwu Army held their hand-crossbows level, but did not advance—so that a stand-off was formed. All others of no account—the menials, dancing-girls, music-band, and maidservants and the like—had been driven down below.

It seemed the Longwu Army's fighting power was formidable indeed: breaking through defense after defense, they had rushed in one push to the seventh floor. To judge by the stations of the two sides, the Pifu had likely but just now got the situation in hand, and had had no time to accomplish anything further before the Longwu Army came charging up.

But Chen Xuanli could go no step further—for Zhang Xiaojing saw clearly that, at the highest point, Xiao Gui was smilingly leveling his crossbow-bolt at a man robed in a garment of scarlet-yellow, who wore on his head a tongtian crown, about his body a nine-ringed girdle, and on his feet a pair of liuhe boots—none other than Li Longji, Son of Heaven of Great Tang.

No wonder Chen Xuanli dared make no rash move: the life of the Son of Heaven was held in the hand of that old soldier of former days!

The statutes of Great Tang had a provision: one who holds a hostage is to be struck along with the hostage. But before the Son of Heaven this rule lost all meaning.

And upon the many guests were smeared black stains and blotches great and small, like some sticky stuff but newly sprayed on; and scattered across the floor were syringes all of one make. There was no need to look twice: this was surely the Yanzhou rock-oil that takes fire at a touch—which was to say, the Pifu could at any moment, with one small spark, send the whole flower of Great Tang up in flames.

Zhang Xiaojing's head ached a little: the situation before him was far too delicate. Every party stood in a state of the highest tension, and the least change might turn it into the worst of all outcomes. And the hostage was far too precious; not the smallest slip could be allowed.

Nor could time be drawn out; a little longer and countless relief troops would come swarming in, so that Xiao Gui was sure to act as soon as he could.

He could not fight, and he could not delay—this was, in plain truth, a dead game of chess.

But alas, Zhang Xiaojing's bodily state was too poor; he truly had no strength to fight, and could not force the game open. The one way left was... Zhang Xiaojing's great hand gripped the bridge-post of the broken bridge, and all at once clenched it hard, as though he had made within himself a most difficult decision.

He crouched low and stole quietly into the hall from the broken bridge. This Star-Plucking Hall was far too vast, and the people far too many, so that no one marked him at all. Using the overturned tables and stands for cover, Zhang Xiaojing swiftly drew near the very core of the stand-off.

Xiao Gui held the Son of Heaven at his mercy, while Chen Xuanli's crossbow-bolt was trained on Xiao Gui. Zhang Xiaojing judged his moment, and deliberately first kicked a porcelain plate to shards to draw the notice of all, lest anyone, over-strained, loose a bolt. Then he slowly rose to his feet, lifted both hands high, and said aloud: 'Zhang Xiaojing of the Jing'an Bureau, about his duty!'

This voice ringing out in the hall seemed most abrupt. Chen Xuanli could not help turning his head to glance, and, recalling that this Zhang Xiaojing had earlier been on the city-wide wanted list only for the warrant to be withdrawn again, felt some doubt in his mind. Zhang Xiaojing drew a waist-tally from his belt and showed it to the Longwu Army men—Commander of the Jing'an, sure enough. At this the soldiers in the stand-off breathed somewhat more easily: a man of the Jing'an Bureau had come, which meant the relief was not far off.

Xiao Gui's crossbow-bolt still pressed against the Son of Heaven's head, and the look on his face did not change.

Chen Xuanli still watched Xiao Gui with his whole attention, the crossbow-bolt in his hand not stirring a hair. Zhang Xiaojing came to his side and said in a low voice: 'General Chen, the several armies are on their way; I beg you by all means to draw it out a little longer—let the Son of Heaven's life be the one thing that counts.'

This was so much empty talk—did he need telling by the likes of you? Chen Xuanli gave a cold snort. Zhang Xiaojing said again: 'But before that, there is one most urgent thing the General must first be told.'

'Speak!' Chen Xuanli's eyes did not shift.

'I too am one of the Pifu.'

The words scarce out, Zhang Xiaojing struck of a sudden, smiting with that gnawed mutton thigh-bone the short crossbow in Chen Xuanli's hand. As the bow's mouth dipped on the one side, Xiao Gui on the other instantly wheeled and loosed a bolt at Chen Xuanli, driving it clean through his shoulder. Zhang Xiaojing hooked out a foot and tripped him in the same motion, then raised a hand to catch the dagger Xiao Gui had just tossed over, and set it to Chen Xuanli's throat.

This string of movements flowed like drifting cloud and running water, the two men working together in perfect concert, as though they had drilled it a thousand times over. Zhang Xiaojing bestrode Chen Xuanli, feinted a slash with the dagger, and barked at the soldiers about him: 'Lay down your weapons, or General Chen dies!'

At this sudden turn the Longwu Army soldiers looked at one another, not knowing what were best to do. Chen Xuanli raised his head and roared: 'Strike the hostage-taker, and do not waver!' Zhang Xiaojing swept down his palm against the man's neck and struck him clean into a swoon.

The soldiers, a dragon-host without a head, could only fling down their crossbows one and all. A few Pifu rushed swiftly over, bound these soldiers up too, and flung them aside.

Among the guests there was a stir of commotion; when Chen Xuanli had rushed up to the seventh floor a moment before, they had felt some flicker of hope. But it had been thrown into confusion by this unlooked-for fellow, and the situation reversed in an instant. Some had heard him name himself Commander of the Jing'an—so he was a mole after all—and could not even forbear to curse aloud. The Pifu at once set to and put this commotion down.

Zhang Xiaojing turned a deaf ear to the commotion; he straightened up and cast his gaze toward the imperial seat. Xiao Gui, gripping the Son of Heaven's arm, called over this way with satisfaction: 'Big-Head, I knew you would surely come!'

'I am late in coming.' he said briefly.

'Come, come—you have never yet had audience of the Son of Heaven, have you?' Xiao Gui laughed aloud, and dragged the Son of Heaven forward, as one drags a dog, drawing from him a low grunt of displeasure. Xiao Gui gave a cold laugh: 'Your Majesty, the difference in rank between your humble servant and yourself is as that between heaven and earth; yet you and I have one thing in common still—we each of us have but a single life.'

The Son of Heaven, having no help for it, could only shift forward a reluctant step.

Zhang Xiaojing lifted his head, and slowly walked toward him and the Son of Heaven.

The last time he had left Xiao Gui, it had been on the pretext of going to seize Mao Shun. Now Mao Shun, Yuchang, and the two guards were all dead, and Xiao Gui did not know that within the lantern-tower he had all but wrecked the Pifu's great design, but still took him for one of their own. And so, to break this game open, Zhang Xiaojing had no choice but to go on feigning himself a Pifu—and for that he had not scrupled to strike down Chen Xuanli.

Let him but keep Xiao Gui from suspicion, watch his chance to draw near, and subdue him, and the other Pifu would be no threat at all.

The greatest risk in this course was that the least carelessness would breed a colossal misunderstanding from which he could never recover; but he had no other way.

Zhang Xiaojing walked upward step by step, drawing nearer and nearer the imperial seat. This was the first time he had observed the Son of Heaven at close range: a slightly stout old man of sixty, with sword-blade brows and a broad nose, a pointed chin and pendulous ears; to judge by his features, he must in youth have been strikingly handsome and full of spirit. Thirty years and more of reigning over all under heaven had bred in him, as a matter of course, an air of majesty; even now, held hostage by Xiao Gui, he lost nothing of a sovereign's authority. In those somewhat clouded eyes there was not a jot of panic.

It was this man who had raised the whole power of Great Tang to its height, and with painstaking care wrought the flourishing age of the twenty years of Kaiyuan; this man who had stretched the borders of Great Tang to their utmost, his might overawing the four seas. But it was also this man who had, at one remove, created such a monster as the Pifu.

Zhang Xiaojing was yet ten paces from Xiao Gui and the Son of Heaven; a little nearer, and he could launch his surprise attack.

At the eighth step, his muscles tightened a little, straining to wring the last least thread of strength from his very bones, ready to strike of a sudden. At this moment Xiao Gui spoke up: 'Ah, yes—Big-Head, wait a moment.'

Zhang Xiaojing could only halt his steps.

'I have made ready a gift for you—take it!' Xiao Gui made a gesture, and a Pifu rushed in among the guests, seized a man from their midst, and flung him down before Zhang Xiaojing.

Zhang Xiaojing fixed his eyes and looked: the man lying fallen on the ground, quivering, was a noble young lord in a brocade robe and a zheluo kerchief, with a jutting brow and a round nose, his neck ever tilted to one side—none other than Prince Yong, Li Lin.

The two men's three eyes met, and in an instant Zhang Xiaojing was drawn back to that scene of the tenth month of the year before.
