### Averting a Catastrophe — The Defection of Gu Shunzhang

### Gu Shunzhang Escorts Zhang Guotao to the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet

On January 7, 1931, on instructions from the Comintern, the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party opened in Shanghai. Pavel Mif, the Comintern's representative newly arrived in China, attended and used one organizational maneuver after another to control it. Under Mif's direct intervention, Wang Ming (Chen Shaoyu), who had not even been a member of the Central Committee, was co-opted onto it, and soon after the plenum onto the Politburo Standing Committee as well. In this way Wang Ming and his men in effect seized the leadership of the Party Central. From then on the "Left" adventurism that Wang Ming represented ruled the whole Party for four years.

"After this Fourth Plenum, the Party's work in the Kuomintang-ruled areas fell into confusion." In many places the Party organizations were badly damaged, and the work of the Central Special Section was gravely hurt as well, at heavy cost.

"After the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee, Wang Ming's 'Left' adventurism began to be pressed everywhere. The Party Central sent many central representatives, or 'new leading cadres,' out to every part of the country to carry out a so-called 'remaking' of the revolutionary base areas and the local Party organizations in the White areas." That March it sent a whole batch of "imperial commissioners" to the provinces: Xia Xi to Honghu; Zeng Hongyi to northeast Jiangxi; Zhang Guotao, Shen Zemin, and Chen Changhao to the Hubei-Henan-Anhui border…

Zhang Guotao had only lately returned from the Soviet Union, where he had worked on the Chinese Communist Party's delegation to the Comintern; he was a newly elected member of the Politburo Standing Committee from the Fourth Plenum of the Sixth Central Committee. Shen Zemin, newly co-opted onto the Central Committee at the same plenum, was head of the Central Propaganda Department. Chen Changhao had studied at Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow, and after returning home in November 1930 had transferred from the Communist Youth League into the Party (he had joined the League in 1926). (After reaching the Hubei-Henan-Anhui area in April 1931, Chen Changhao became a member of the Party's Central Sub-Bureau there and secretary of the League's Central Sub-Bureau; that September he was made political commissar of the Fourth Army of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army, and in November political commissar of the Fourth Front Army.) It was now decided that Zhang Guotao and Chen Changhao would go by water, escorted by Gu Shunzhang, through Wuhan to Huang'an county; Shen Zemin and his wife Zhang Qinqiu would go by land, slipping into Jinjiazhai by way of Lu'an in Anhui.

Certain "writers," ignorant of the facts, have called this dispatch of central representatives into the base areas — sent by a Party Central under the "Left" adventurism of Wang Ming, to carry out a so-called "remaking" — a mission to "strengthen" the Soviet areas. Ignoring what Zhang Guotao actually did in the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet and what Gu Shunzhang then was, they say that because Zhang Guotao was a "member of the Politburo Standing Committee" and "bore the heavy charge of developing the region north of the Yangtze," "a responsible comrade with experience, familiar with the underground courier line that crossed the Yangtze into the Hubei-Henan-Anhui base," had to be sent to escort him. Some go further still, and even say that "there was no need for Gu Shunzhang himself to take the field on such an errand… When Zhou Enlai sent his own able assistant Gu Shunzhang to escort Zhang Guotao and the others to Wuhan, it was on the one hand to guarantee the absolute safety of Zhang Guotao's party, and on the other to have Gu Shunzhang rebuild Wuhan's underground Party organization along the way." In fact, once Zhang Guotao, that "imperial commissioner," had become secretary of the Party's Hubei-Henan-Anhui Central Sub-Bureau and chairman of its military commission, he pushed "Left" adventurism, and through his mistaken strategic guidance and his high-handed, arbitrary ways the main force of the Red Army was driven out of the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet in October of the second year, and a perfectly sound red base was wrecked. As for Gu Shunzhang, Chen Yangshan, who was then working in the Central Special District, said: "Gu Shunzhang had long since gone soft and corrupt in his living, and Comrade Zhou Enlai had criticized him more than once. Sending him to escort Zhang Guotao to the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet was really a way of moving Gu out of the Special Section."

For Zhang Guotao's journey to Wuhan, Gu Shunzhang made these arrangements: on April 1 Zhang Guotao and Chen Changhao would board a boat at Shanghai and head upriver, accompanied by men of the Red Squad, while Gu Shunzhang himself would go a day ahead, by train from Shanghai to Nanjing and then by boat to Wuhan, so as to reach Wuhan early and prepare the ground. Some writers have invented all sorts of detail about Zhang Guotao's party, painting in vivid colors what passenger liner they took, how they passed themselves off as rich merchants, how lavishly they lived, and so on — all of it fiction that does not fit the facts.

The truth is that Zhang Guotao and Chen Changhao traveled on a nondescript timber boat controlled by the Central Special Section. The boat was a cargo vessel of the Yangtai Timber Company, registered with the Municipal Council of the British Concession in Shanghai by an English merchant; it flew the British flag and hauled timber from Shanghai to Wuhan alone, selling no tickets and taking no passengers. There was a boatman aboard named Chen, a native of Ningbo in Zhejiang, who was a Communist. Many of the Party's responsible comrades often traveled back and forth on this boat as his "friends." Li Qiang, who rode it many times himself, said: "Our comrades — could they take a passenger steamer? On a passenger steamer people would recognize you, and there'd be no getting away. So you went as his friend, on his boat. And his timber boat, you didn't have to buy a ticket for. In Shanghai you'd load a boatful of sawn planks, ship it up to Wuhan, then go back for more. So nobody came asking questions. On his boat we moved a great many important people and important things to Shanghai — we even moved a spittoon (this is no joke, it's the truth) to Shanghai. The helmsman of this boat, that comrade was a Ningbo man, surname Chen; his name I don't know, one wasn't allowed to ask in those days. He did a great deal of work. When you couldn't buy rice in Hankou, he bought rice; past Wuhu he bought a good many crabs. I used it constantly from 1927, and was still using it in 1931. Later, when Gu Shunzhang escorted Zhang Guotao to the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet, he too went on this boat."

On April 4 Zhang Guotao's party reached Wuhan. Zhang Guotao afterward set down this journey in his memoirs, the third volume of My Recollections:

In the middle of March 1931, Shen Zemin, Chen Changhao, and I were preparing to go into the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet. Chen Changhao and I meant to go by way of Hankou to Huang'an; Shen Zemin and his wife Zhang Qinqiu meant to go by way of Hefei in Anhui to Lu'an. At the time we knew very little of the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet. We knew only that there were two small Soviet areas there — one centered on Qiliping in the north of Huang'an county, taking in the four counties of Huang'an, Macheng, Guangshan, and Luoshan and some rural districts of counties along the Henan border; the other centered on Jinjiazhai, taking in the three counties of Lu'an, Huoshan, and Shangcheng and some rural districts of counties along the Henan border. Between these Soviet areas and the Party Central a courier network had been set up, but how they kept in touch with one another only Zhou Enlai knew clearly, and Zhou Enlai had therefore entrusted Gu Shunzhang with arranging every part of this secret journey…

Gu Shunzhang worked hard in my service. He decided to escort me to Hankou himself, and to have his assistant see to Shen Zemin's route. He told me there was a tramp boat that ran between Shanghai and Hankou, with which he had a close connection; this boat would set off for Hankou at the end of March, and he wanted me to take it. He himself would go on ahead to Hankou to line up men to escort me to Huang'an, and he explained to me in detail the steps to be taken at each turn. I decided to act on his plan…

In the full light of dawn the boat put out through the Wusong mouth. It was a cargo boat; the dozen-odd passengers were all "yellow-fish" the sailors were smuggling aboard on the side, and the two of us were "yellow-fish" too — dressed as merchants, and yet not quite like them… On the afternoon of the fourth our boat was nearing Hankou. Gu Shunzhang told the boat's master to put in after six in the evening, for Gu Shunzhang reckoned that a little past six was when the plainclothes men took their meal, and that to land then would be somewhat less dangerous. Sure enough, the boat at once slackened speed and did not moor until after six had passed. As Chen Changhao and I stepped off the wharf, each carrying our simple luggage, dusk was already near. On the road outside the wharf a young man stood holding that day's newspaper, and he flashed us a signal: this was the man Gu Shunzhang had sent to meet us. The young man promptly climbed into a rickshaw, and we too got into rickshaws and rode straight for the Japanese Concession, not far from the wharf.

On a quiet side street in the Japanese Concession we went up to the second floor of a house, where Gu Shunzhang was waiting for us. Once he knew we had met no trouble on the way, he went over the next steps with us. He told us that from here into the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet we could not be escorted by the men of the Hankou courier station; a courier sent out from the Hubei-Henan-Anhui area would have to come and take us in, and it was now known for certain that this reliable courier would need at least three more days to arrive. We would therefore have to stay in Hankou at least three days.

He went on to tell us that this courier post was kept by a reliable married couple, always quite dependable; but according to the latest word it had already drawn the suspicion of the Japanese Concession's plainclothes men, and so he was rather hesitant about whether we could stay there. He told us too of a thing I had never known: he said he was the famous "Hua Guangqi," the great magician, who had performed many times in Hankou and made a great stir; some of Hankou's big merchants and rich men had taken him as their teacher, and a few of them were on close terms with him, wholly unaware that he was Gu Shunzhang. He suggested that if this place would not do, we could move to the home of one of his magic pupils, the household of a big merchant, which would rouse no one's suspicion. I would not agree to this. I told him: if I stayed in a big merchant's house I could not keep out of sight of visitors; many people in Hankou knew me, and not a few of the merchants among them, and should I run into one, it would be worse. This place, though the Japanese Concession's plainclothes men suspected it, was not likely to blow up within these few days. If we hid here and never showed our faces, it might be safer. The upshot of our deliberation was that we all agreed it was better to stay still than to move.

On the two-bedroom second floor the master and mistress lived in the back room, and we in the front. We dared not look out of the window for fear of being seen, and only read books and newspapers in the room. In one Chinese-language paper that day a translated article by a foreigner, "Suppressing the Bandits," praised General Chiang Kai-shek's efforts to wipe out the Communist bandits and told how China's history had repeatedly used a policy of blockade against bandits, with success; now Chiang too was minding the blockade, ringing the bandit areas with blockhouses, clearing the fields and stiffening the walls, so that the bandits could not spread outward, in order to tighten the encirclement bit by bit — a steady and effective method. Having read this piece, I began to think out our countermeasures.

On the evening of the seventh, Gu Shunzhang brought the courier, just come from the Hubei-Henan-Anhui area, to our lodging. This young man who had come to fetch us was short, silent, and taciturn, and looked rather like a seasoned shop clerk. We agreed to set out the next morning, going toward our destination by bus; since it was awkward for Gu Shunzhang to appear at the bus station, he decided to send his assistant to see to things there.

At eight in the morning on the eighth, Chen Changhao and I followed the courier out of the Japanese Concession, through the small streets and lanes of the Chinese quarter to the bus station. Each of us carried a bundle, an umbrella, and the like, walking ahead and behind on our own as if we did not know one another, and buying our tickets separately at the station. Quite smoothly we boarded the bus that ran by way of Huangpi to Lijiaji in Macheng. As it drove into the outskirts it was twice stopped and searched, but we passed both times without trouble…

The courier sent by the Hubei-Henan-Anhui Soviet took Zhang Guotao and Chen Changhao in on April 8; with that, Gu Shunzhang's escort task was done, and he ought properly to have returned to Shanghai "to report." Instead, on the pretext of building up the courier line, he lingered in Hankou, staying at the Deming Hotel in the French Concession, not far from the Dazhimen station, and busied himself with his own affairs. On his own authority he took up the stage name "Hua Guangqi, magician" and gave public magic performances at the New Market amusement hall, plastering the streets with placards to draw a crowd. The sudden appearance of so uncommon a figure in Hankou at once caught the notice of Kuomintang agents. There was at the time in Wuhan a certain Wang Zhuqiao, who had been arrested and turned traitor; the Kuomintang secret service had given him one week to find the Communists, on pain of being shot. But once the local Party organization learned he had turned, the Communists who had had dealings with him were swiftly moved. Day after day this traitor prowled the streets of Wuhan, casting about east and west, sniffing everywhere like a mad dog. He had originally been a worker in a Wuhan textile mill, had once served in a workers' armed picket corps, and had worked in the special-service section of the Central Military Commission in the Wuhan period, so that he knew Gu Shunzhang, who had been in charge of that section then. On April 24, with the deadline the agents had set him almost up and no way to show for it, Wang Zhuqiao came pacing along the riverbank; and at midday, right at the gate of the Jianghan Customs House, he ran straight into Gu Shunzhang. He tailed him in secret all the way to Gu Shunzhang's lodging, and at once reported it to the investigation and arrest office of the Wuhan Field Headquarters, a new secret-service organ Xu Enzeng had set up in Hankou. The enemy heard him out, acted at once, and arrested Gu Shunzhang on the spot.

The head of the investigation and arrest office of the Kuomintang's Wuhan Field Headquarters was Yang Qingshan, a Hongmen boss; his deputy was Cai Mengjian, and Cai Mengjian, whom Xu Enzeng had appointed his special commissioner in Wuhan, was the one who chiefly interrogated Gu Shunzhang. To soften him and coax him into turning, they made a point of "toasting him with wine and tea." At first Gu said not a word. Then Cai said to him: though the two of us have never met, I know you, and you must surely know me; there is no need to say much. Tell me, sir, all you know; otherwise, there is only death. Finally Cai said to him: I have decided to send you to Nanjing; think it over well for yourself, and choose your own future. Gu Shunzhang turned traitor at once, and even said shamelessly to the agents: I was already looking for a chance last year; I am willing to come over. In truth Gu Shunzhang had long harbored his treachery. He had written out a letter of surrender to Chiang Kai-shek and kept it at home against need, and had once charged his wife Zhang Xinghua: if I am arrested and cannot come back, you may give this letter to Chiang Kai-shek. The Party organization later found this letter in his house. In it he flattered Chiang Kai-shek to the utmost, avowing that he had long wished to betray the Party and go over to the enemy — that he was dissatisfied with the Communist Party and had long had "a heart to turn and come back." If Chiang Kai-shek would trust him, he could hand over, one and all, the whole web of organizational ties of the Communist Party, the Third Party, and the Kuomintang Reorganizationists, from the center down to the branch. It is proof enough that the motive for his defection lay not after his arrest but before it.

Cai Mengjian, deputy head of the investigation and arrest office of the Kuomintang's Wuhan Field Headquarters, who arrested and interrogated Gu Shunzhang in Wuhan with his own hands, later described Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection like this:

When Gu Shunzhang was about to be taken to the judge advocate's office for interrogation, he cried out loud: "I want to see Cai Mengjian." Startled inwardly though I was, I at once had the operatives bring him to me. He set himself up as a guest and said to me: I am no ordinary Communist; I am a member of the Communist Party Central Standing Committee, on a footing with Zhou Enlai. Most of the Party's business I manage; every secret organ is arranged entirely by me. I have been trained by the Soviet GPU, and I am chief of the Shanghai Communist Party Central's Red Guard Bureau. As for agents, I am the master of them all. This time I came to Hankou using my conjuring, under cover of the "Hua Guangqi" magic troupe; my chief tasks were: (1) to escort Zhang Guotao to the Hubei-Henan-Anhui border to take up the chairmanship there, and Zhang has already been brought in under my men's cover; (2) to prepare and arrange the transfer of the Communist Party chairman by way of the Guangzhou–Hankou railway to Zhu-Ping and on to Jinggangshan, moving the chairman's seat. Your finding me today may do good for both the Kuomintang and the Communist Party. I cannot say more; I keep back the important matters. Please arrange for me to talk with Chairman Chiang. I said: it would do to have a word with He Chengjun, the field headquarters director here. He said even seeing him would come to no more than what he had already said. I asked him further: how do you know my name, Cai Mengjian? He said: "In any Kuomintang district, the names of the men who direct the anti-Communist work are all known; there is no need to ask much." He did not touch on the matter of the great spy Qian Zhuangfei lying hidden within our Central. Seeing how things stood, I judged it best to be generous, let him go back to his hotel with men set to watch him, and send him to the Nanjing Central for handling, then report to Director He…

The next day — the early morning of April 25 — He Chengjun, director of the Kuomintang's Wuhan Field Headquarters, brought Gu Shunzhang up for questioning. Gu gave away our Party's Wuhan courier organ, the West Hubei Associated-County Soviet government, and the Wuhan liaison office of the Second Front Army of the Red Army; and so these organs were all destroyed and more than ten people arrested. Gu Shunzhang reckoned that, as an alternate member of the Party Central's Politburo who had also headed the concrete work of the Special Section, he knew not only many of the Party's important secrets but the addresses of the Party Central's organs and of many central leading comrades, and knew too that Qian Zhuangfei was at Chiang Kai-shek's very side — a stock of capital with which to claim reward and win favor from Chiang Kai-shek. He therefore put three demands to He Chengjun: first, to be sent to Nanjing at once, saying he had specially urgent secret intelligence to report to Chiang Kai-shek in person; second, that secrecy be kept, and no telegram be sent to Nanjing about his arrest before he reached it, lest word leak out; third, that his safety be guaranteed.

He Chengjun and the Kuomintang agents in Hankou — Huang Kai, Cai Mengjian, and the rest — held the captured Gu Shunzhang a very treasure, and, keen to claim the credit, paid the traitor's terms no heed at all and, ignoring his demands, at once wired Xu Enzeng and Chen Lifu. When Gu learned that He Chengjun and the secret-service organ had already wired Chen Lifu and Xu Enzeng, he stamped his foot and moaned in dismay: "This ruins it — there's no catching Zhou Enlai now!" An agent asked: why? He answered: the Communist Party has a man at Chiang Kai-shek's side, serving as confidential secretary.

### Arrested in Hankou, He Turns Traitor

To return to Gu Shunzhang: arrested in Wuhan on April 24, he turned traitor that same day, and, like beans poured out of a tube, sold off many of the Party Central's highest secrets. Yet he still "kept one card back," letting it be known that certain things he would tell Chiang Kai-shek directly. He pressed Cai Mengjian to "arrange for me to talk with Chairman Chiang," would not open his mouth on those, and said too that he had long sought a chance to "turn," to show his resolve to betray the Party and go over. Cai Mengjian closed his interrogation of Gu Shunzhang and decided to set off for Nanjing at once, "to send him to the Nanjing Central for handling." That night he reported to He Chengjun on the interrogation and on his own plan. Cai Mengjian said: "He Chengjun expressed no objection: 'Send him as you see fit to the Nanjing Central for handling. You may charter a small China Merchants steamer and send a squad of military police to escort him; you had best go by plane to Nanjing first, report the case to the Generalissimo and Mr. Lifu, and act on their instructions.' So that evening I had men charter the upper-deck grand saloon of a certain China Merchants steamer bound for Shanghai, sent my operatives aboard, and arranged with the gendarme regiment for a squad of military police, and had Gu Shunzhang escorted to Nanjing. I myself took the seaplane from Hankou to Nanjing the next morning, and went straight to the Central Party Headquarters to see Secretary-General Chen Lifu, with Xu Enzeng also present. Chen Lifu telephoned Chiang Kai-shek directly to report the true state of things, explaining that Gu Shunzhang asked to be received by Chiang Kai-shek, and Chiang Kai-shek gave this instruction: 'Cai Mengjian may bring Gu to see me at any time.'"

Only on April 27 (a Monday) was Gu Shunzhang brought by steamer to Nanjing under guard. Cai Mengjian went to Xiaguan to meet him off the boat. On Chen Lifu's "instructions" he was first taken to the secret office Xu Enzeng kept on Zhongshan East Road — the very place where Qian Zhuangfei had had his office. Cai Mengjian said: "Only at this point, when Gu Shunzhang got out of the car and looked at the house number, did he say, 'This is our Nanjing office.' The moment he was through the door he said: Xu's confidential secretary Qian Zhuangfei is the core spy the Communist Party planted in the Kuomintang, and he further recommended Li Kenong to succeed him as secretary of the Shanghai Wireless Administration Bureau, setting up another Communist base; every day your Central's important materials for dealing with the Communist Party were sent by men Qian sent to the Shanghai Concessions, for me or Zhou Enlai to check over. In Hankou I knew at once that you were the Wuhan man in charge, because it was Qian who had his son-in-law Liu Qifu serve as courier, carrying every important intelligence item day by day from Nanjing to Shanghai to the Kuomintang Central — down to each successive plan of the central military encirclement, together with the roster of the men in charge of anti-Communist work in every locality — which is how I found out your name. If Qian has already fled to Shanghai and reported to Zhou Enlai, then my whole plan is finished! I told Xu at the time, and telephoned Chen Lifu as well; and just then, on inquiry, Qian was nowhere to be found… My own judgment at the time was that Qian, on deciphering the cable and finding that Gu had been arrested and surrendered, had already fled to Shanghai to give warning. Still I feigned comfort to Gu, telling him Qian had already caught the train for Shanghai and that men had been posted to arrest him all along the Nanjing–Shanghai line. Gu heaved a great sigh."

After a short rest, Cai Mengjian took Gu Shunzhang to Chiang Kai-shek's residence. Cai Mengjian said: "As Gu and I went into Chiang's reception hall together, I found Zhang Daofan, then a secretary under Chen Lifu at the Central, sitting some way off. He nodded to me. There was no need to introduce Gu; I told him that Gu had given away Xu Enzeng's confidential secretary as the Communist Party Central's top spy, now on the run. Daofan then charged me: this must on no account be reported to Chiang Kai-shek — you are young (twenty-three) and should gain experience; do not add great trouble for Mr. Lifu. Presently Chiang Kai-shek came into the reception hall in a long gown and stood throughout. He shook my hand first, and said I had worked hard, very good, very good. I at once presented Gu Shunzhang to Chiang Kai-shek: this is the man who has come over to us. Gu, fancying himself already a guest of honor, put out his hand toward Chiang Kai-shek; Chiang Kai-shek did not shake it, and only said: 'You have come over to the Central, very good, very good; hereafter obey Comrade Xu's arrangements in all things, and serve the country.' Chiang Kai-shek said only this one sentence, standing, then said 'Goodbye.' Gu, who had been sure he must be a guest of honor, found he had become a prisoner at the bar! Chiang Kai-shek suddenly came out again to the inner-room door and charged me to see Gu back, after which I was to return to the residence alone for one more audience. I at once took Gu by car back to Xu Enzeng's Zhongshan East Road office… then went straight back by the same car to Chiang Kai-shek's residence, and Chiang Kai-shek received me at once in the reception hall. He asked what concrete views Gu had voiced, and I told him what Gu had said in the car: that he asked our Central to recognize the Communist Party as a public party and press the Communists to lay down their arms of their own accord… Chiang Kai-shek said with a smile: Gu has become a surrendered anti-Communist traitor; what influence has he left? Still, go on making use of Gu, guide his employment as the moment requires, and report to me from time to time. I then reported to Chiang Kai-shek on the arrest of Yun Daiying as Gu had told it (I hear Chiang at once ordered Yun Daiying found and executed); but on the one matter of Xu Enzeng's sole confidential secretary Qian Zhuangfei being a Communist spy, half from being frightened off by Zhang Daofan, I did not report the truth to Chiang Kai-shek. Wholly out of my private regard for Mr. Chen Lifu's usual bearing, and the more so given his relations with Chiang Kai-shek — since Chen himself, through some carelessness, might bear a share of blame — I did not then dare to disclose it to Chiang Kai-shek. At parting, Chiang Kai-shek charged me to make good use of Gu. When I returned to the place where Gu was held, there was no sign of him: this was Xu Enzeng, in his private jealousy and shame, cutting Gu off from me forever, against all reason. My judgment then was that Xu feared that under my hand Gu would surely be turned to great use and earn Chiang Kai-shek's praise."

Cai Mengjian's account sets down the cringing, brazen face of the traitor Gu Shunzhang, and betrays as well the ugliness of the enemy's own inner scheming, all mutual deceit and jockeying for place.

Xu Enzeng, having spirited Gu Shunzhang off into secret custody first, that same day joined Zhang Chong, chief secretary of the Investigation Section, and Gu Jianzhong, head of the party-affairs group, in a surprise interrogation of Gu Shunzhang. Gu Shunzhang confessed every Party secret he knew: he gave away the addresses of the Party Central's leaders — Zhou Enlai, Qu Qiubai, Li Weihan, Qin Bangxian, Chen Shaoyu, and Xiang Zhongfa — and the whereabouts of the Central Secretariat and the Central Special Section's organs… They at once drew up a plan of action to "net in one sweep" the Party Central's organs and chief leaders. Xu Enzeng himself led Zhang Chong, Gu Jianzhong, and a large body of military and police agents, hurried through the night to Shanghai, and, joining the police stations of the British and French Concessions, carried out this criminal plan, beginning the great dragnet on the morning of the 28th. The enemy afterward boasted: once Gu Shunzhang reached Nanjing, the Zhongtong sent men to Shanghai to seize Zhou Enlai, reaching Shanghai seven hours later. They at once split into two columns: one to collect Gu Shunzhang's family; one to search the Communist Party Central, the Jiangsu provincial committee, and the office of the Comintern's Shanghai representative. Fast though the enemy's action was, our Party's organs had already been safely moved, the central leading comrades had all vanished without a trace, and the enemy's foul plot fell through. It was seen at the time that a radio station, moved out but with men left watching nearby, was carried off by the enemy; and then Zhou Enlai's former lodging was searched too. The facts proved the intelligence Qian Zhuangfei and Li Kenong had provided wholly correct, and the Party Central's countermeasures wise.

By the account of Zhang Wen (also called Zhang Guodong), then an assistant clerk in the Investigation Section under Xu Enzeng: "Shanghai was then the seat of the Communist Party Central, with many units, and once Gu Shunzhang gave away their exact addresses, Xu Enzeng himself led the Investigation Section's chief secretary Zhang Chong and clerk Gu Jianzhong and hurried to Shanghai at once. By what Gu Jianzhong told me afterward, Xu went three days and three nights running without sleep; his mother, his first old concubine, and his daughter were all living in Shanghai then, and though he crossed from Nanjing to Shanghai several times, he did not once go home… Because the Communist Party Central, on learning of Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection, made emergency arrangements on all sides and shifted its every underground secret organ, only a few organs that could not be moved in time — the Red Flag press, the security group's office, the Beisichuan Road contact point, and the like — were destroyed by the Zhongtong agents. The offices Zhou Enlai used to frequent were destroyed as well…" Meng Zhen, once head of the Fourth Section — the section of the "Zhongtong" secret-service organ charged specifically with opposing the Chinese Communist Party — said of this, with deep chagrin: "Gu Shunzhang was arrested in Hankou, a matter of the utmost secrecy. Yet the Communist Party Central in Shanghai got word of it sooner than the Zhongtong in Nanjing. The reason was that at the side of the Zhongtong chief Xu Enzeng lay hidden the Communist underground Party's Qian Zhuangfei." Cai Mengjian, who arrested Gu Shunzhang with his own hands, was angrier still. In The Story of Two Men Who Might Have Rewritten Modern Chinese History, which he wrote half a century later, he railed at Gu Shunzhang for still "lying in ambush" after his arrest, refusing to speak out at once about Qian Zhuangfei, so that the "heaven-sent chance" leaked away and the work fell short of completion by a single basketful, leaving the Kuomintang's secret-service chiefs to rejoice over nothing; and in a tone of contempt he said: "The bandit Qian who lay hidden in our Central fled to Shanghai, so that whatever effect and success Gu might have produced must be counted wholly lost." He held too that the struggle around Gu Shunzhang's defection in that year was a great matter bearing on the future of the Chinese revolution. He said: "The Gu case bore hugely on the fate of the Chinese Communists. Had the Communist spy Qian Zhuangfei not intercepted the cable, then Zhou Enlai and the Communists hidden in Shanghai must have been taken in one net, dealing the Chinese Communists a mortal blow." His meaning is that, had Qian Zhuangfei not been the first to see the secret cable they sent from Wuhan to Xu Enzeng, their plot might have succeeded and modern Chinese history been "rewritten."

"Evil does not overpower good": this is the law of society's development. History is always written by the people, and will never be "rewritten" by reactionaries who go against the tide of history. But one may see from it that the peril of that moment was indeed as fine as a hair, and grave in the extreme.

### The Plot to "Net the Whole CCP Central" Collapses Utterly

At this time the Communist Qian Zhuangfei was indeed the confidential secretary of the Zhongtong chief Xu Enzeng. The moment the cable from He Chengjun and the Wuhan secret service reporting Gu Shunzhang's arrest reached Nanjing, it was already the evening of April 25 — a Saturday — and Xu Enzeng had long since gone off to dance and dally with women.

That night Qian Zhuangfei sat on at No. 5 Zhongshan East Road in Nanjing, the "headquarters" of Xu Enzeng's secret-service organ, and there received one after another six top-secret express cables from Wuhan, each marked "for Xu Enzeng to decipher himself." Qian Zhuangfei thought: what could be so urgent? By then he already held a copy of the cipher book that Xu Enzeng used for cables with high Kuomintang officials, and he quickly deciphered them. Only then did he learn that Gu Shunzhang had been arrested and turned traitor, and meant to collude with the enemy to wreck our whole Party Central apparatus in Shanghai. The situation was desperately urgent, and Qian Zhuangfei's own heart was tense. But he was very steady, read the contents of the cables closely, and considered how to meet this crisis, fine as a hair.

He Chengjun's cables, addressed to the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters for Xu Enzeng to pass to Secretary-General Chen Lifu (Chen was then secretary-general of the Kuomintang standing committee), ran in the main as follows:

The first cable said that Liming (Gu Shunzhang's alias) had been arrested and had already surrendered, and that if he could be brought swiftly to Nanjing, the whole Communist Party Central apparatus could be cleaned out within three days;

The second cable said a warship would be used to bring Liming to Nanjing;

The third cable said a plane would be used instead to send him to Nanjing, because by Liming's account a warship was too slow. This cable said further that on no account must this news be let known to those about Xu Enzeng; were they to learn of it, the plan to net in one sweep the Communist Party Central apparatus in Shanghai would come wholly to nothing…

Qian Zhuangfei, reading these secret cables through, was aghast. But very steadily he noted down their wording and sealed the cables up again. After close thought, he dug out the Beijing–Shanghai railway timetable, took a look, and decided first to send his son-in-law Liu Qifu that same night by express train to Shanghai, to deliver this intelligence to Li Kenong for report to the Central. Having sent Liu Qifu off, he first put the money accounts in order and set them in the cash box; he also went to the Minzhi News Agency to notify a comrade he had placed there, but did not find him. He then took a map from his desk and cut a slit down the middle of it with a knife, sending out the prearranged signal that henceforth contact was to be "cut." That night, though he weighed whether he ought to leave Nanjing himself, the constraints of clandestine work then were such that he did not know Gu Shunzhang's whole situation; and to have the enemy destroy in an instant the state of things he and his comrades had created in Nanjing and Shanghai through bitter toil and every kind of danger was indeed a hard thing to resolve upon. Yet after cool thought and full analysis, he resolved in the end to leave quickly. Early the next morning (April 26) he laid the cables on Xu Enzeng's desk, and left a letter besides, warning the other side that if Xu Enzeng dared to harm his children, he would lay bare every one of Xu Enzeng's secrets to the world. Then he hurried to catch a train back to Shanghai. To guard against mishap he got off at Zhenru and made his way into the city from there.

Liu Qifu reached Shanghai in the small hours of the 26th. Li Kenong was then living at the Phoenix Hotel behind the Sincere Company; Liu Qifu, whose task was courier work on the Nanjing–Shanghai run, found Li Kenong and reported to him on Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection in Wuhan. Seeing him a little tense, Li Kenong did his best to calm him, comforting him: why can't you keep your nerve, why so unsteady!… Li Kenong too felt the gravity of the thing. Anxious as he was for Qian Zhuangfei's safety, he firmly believed Qian Zhuangfei could reach the right judgment and take resolute measures. But should Liu Qifu go back to Nanjing after all? He wavered greatly, his face betraying how hard it was to decide. He had meant to have Liu Qifu simply not go back; but Liu Qifu himself asked to return to Nanjing at once to fetch his wife, Qian Zhuangfei's daughter Qian Jiaojiao, and Li Kenong could only consent. As he left, Li Kenong gave him money for the road and two gold rings besides, and, gripping Liu Qifu's hand tight, said with deep concern: "This trip may hold more ill than good; meet what comes with courage and steadiness!"

That day was a Sunday, not the day Li Kenong and Chen Geng had fixed to meet, and Ouyang Dahan, who served as courier, could not be found. Bearing this desperately urgent intelligence, on which the survival of the whole Party hung, Li Kenong burned with anxiety. In a spirit of the highest responsibility, careless of his own safety, he sought by every means and found the Communist Party's Jiangsu provincial committee, and through it, at an emergency reserve contact station the Central had only just set up, found Chen Geng and Huang Mulan, and He Chang of the Jiangsu provincial committee besides. Li Kenong told Chen Geng the matter was most urgent and must be reported to the Central at once. Chen Geng reported it to Zhou Enlai straightaway.

At this time Hu Di was still in Tianjin. That evening Li Kenong sent Hu Di a cable in cipher: "Hu Di, Ke and Chao gravely ill." This was a signal they had agreed on beforehand: "Ke" meant Li Kenong, "Chao" was Qian Zhuangfei's other name, Qian Chao, and "gravely ill" meant the matter was serious. On receiving the cable Hu Di at once scraped together travel money and left Tianjin by a foreign ship. When he reached Shanghai he could no longer find Li Kenong, but he found Yang Cunren of the Communist Party's Zhabei branch (note: this man betrayed the revolution in 1932) and Hou Rushi; both Hu Di and Li Kenong had once been assigned to this branch. They took Hu Di to the home of a White Russian, said he was a "writer" not to be disturbed, and hid him there for a time.

Facing this perilous situation, Zhou Enlai, with the help of Chen Yun and other comrades, made his decision on the instant. He at once called Chen Geng, Li Qiang, and the others concerned to an emergency meeting and took a series of measures: first, to destroy a great quantity of secret documents, tighten the guard on the Party's chief leaders and move them at once, and swiftly replace with new hands the secretaries of responsible comrades whom Gu Shunzhang could have spied out or knew well; second, to move as fast as could be planned every cadre who might become an object of Gu Shunzhang's spying, to safe ground or away from Shanghai; third, to deal, warily but resolutely, with the important connections in Shanghai that Gu Shunzhang could exploit; fourth, to abolish every method of clandestine work known to Gu Shunzhang, with each department responsible for carrying out an emergency change. That very night the organs of the Communist Party Central, the Jiangsu provincial committee, and the Comintern's Far Eastern Bureau all changed house; at the same time Chen Geng was ordered to lead the whole Intelligence Section in investigating from every side, so as to take measures in time and prepare a counterstroke. To shatter the enemy's plot utterly, forestalling the enemy's own action, the great removal of the leading organs was completed within a single day, and the safety of the Party Central and the Jiangsu provincial committee's organs was secured.

Zhou Enlai's work was always exact and meticulous, minding the safety of every comrade. In handling this emergency he not only bore the charge of directing the whole, but, careless of his own safety, went in person to warn some comrades to move. Not only did he get all the central responsible comrades swiftly out of danger; he also helped other working personnel escape the enemy's snares. By the recollection of Zhu Yueqian, then a worker of the Central Military Commission, that evening she heard a knock, opened the door, and it was Zhou Enlai. Zhou Enlai told her Gu Shunzhang had defected and she must move at once, and discussed with her how to secure the safety of Huo Buqing, a worker of the Central Military Commission and Zhu Yueqian's husband. Huo Buqing had by then already set out from Shanghai by boat for Wuhan, and Wuhan's liaison organ had already been destroyed by the enemy; some way had to be found to get word to him. He was on the road — how was he to be warned? By Huang Jieran's recollection, to catch up with the steamer they had a courier go by plane to Jiujiang, and when the steamer put in at the Jiujiang wharf and the passengers went ashore to look about, the courier went to find him and at last made contact; Huo Buqing got off the boat midway and was kept out of enemy hands.

At this pass, fine as a hair, on which the fate of our Party hung, Zhou Enlai led the comrades in smashing the treacherous scheme of Gu Shunzhang and his master Chiang Kai-shek, and men praised his immortal deed with boundless reverence: "A lifetime's wit, a whole body's daring; Duke Zhou breaks the foe's plot with a laugh." The transcendent contribution of Comrades Qian Zhuangfei, Li Kenong, and Hu Di to the safety of the Party Central won the Central's commendation. More than thirty years later, on May 8, 1968, Zhou Enlai said again at a central meeting: "In 1931, when Gu Shunzhang defected, it was fortunate that Comrade Qian Zhuangfei at Xu Enzeng's Zhongtong got the news the fastest, so that the central organs were spared destruction. Qian Zhuangfei, Li Kenong, and Hu Di were together, and it was for this that they were sent to the Central Soviet. Later Qian Zhuangfei died dodging planes on the Long March; Hu Di was killed when he joined up with Zhang Guotao, because Zhang Guotao was making a speech to the Fourth Front Army and Hu Di said from below the platform that Zhang was like a Fascist, and Zhang Guotao's guard reported it to Zhang, and he was killed for it afterward…" Zhou Enlai's remembrance and tribute showed his boundless deep feeling for them.
