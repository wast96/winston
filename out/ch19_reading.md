## In a Single Night the Plum Blossom Betrays Spring's Craft

Suicide takes hot blood.

Not hot blood only, but courage as well.

It was on the strength of a soldier's hot blood and a man's courage that Han Zhengqi had squeezed the trigger.

His body was drawn bolt upright, his nerves stretched taut as a steel wire about to snap, and in his chest was wrapped a knot of warmth all but gone out. And yet he was alive.

The bullet had not passed through his head.

He remembered checking the magazine before he came; there had been rounds in it.

A-Chu watched him coldly and let fall a single soft sentence: "Have you the courage for a second shot?"

Han Zhengqi's face was ashen, his heart clenched tight. For the least dignity a man could keep, he must fire a second time. But his arm had gone weak and sore, sweat beaded on his brow, and he felt already that it was beyond him. He could not summon back the courage to kill himself.

Before his eyes all was pitch dark.

In that pitch-dark world he saw his own soul wandering alone through the wild waste country.

He saw himself soaked and dripping, all over blood.

He saw himself level the muzzle at A-Chu, and A-Chu's face turn once more into the real Xu Yuzhen's. Xu Yuzhen stared into his eyes, her gaze hollow — the look of the dead.

He truly deserved to go to hell.

Han Zhengqi found that the true root of his anguish of spirit lay in his unrequited love for Xu Yuzhen. One light squeeze of the finger, and he could be delivered; already he could smell the sweetness of the earth.

He fired the second shot; the report rang out, and at the sound he fell.

Liu A-Si and Lu Liangchen opened the door of the tearoom; A-Chu raised a hand to signal them to wait outside. Though Han Zhengqi had fallen mechanically at the sound, though his temple throbbed with pain, he could feel plainly that he was still alive.

It had been a blank cartridge.

"I forgive you," A-Chu said calmly. "I did not mean these two shots to shame you. I want you to know that every sin of the past you have now repaid. Your life has passed through a cycle and come round again. Twice you had the chance to kill me, and twice you let it go. You gave up your life, you took the guilt upon yourself, you won back your good name." A-Chu of his own accord put out his hand to Han Zhengqi. "It is my hope that we can go on working together. There is no obstacle left between us now — I need you! The society needs you!"

It was a gesture.

Han Zhengqi was astonished, and A-Chu's magnanimity woke in him a kind of respect. With mixed feelings he grasped A-Chu's hand. The two of them stepped at the same moment into the sunlight.

"The magazine in your gun — I had one of your own men change it for you." A-Chu drew a fully loaded magazine from his pocket and tossed it onto the tea table. "Han Yu is in room 306, on the third floor of the Park Hotel. He has been given an injection of an anesthetic and by my reckoning is not yet awake; take him to a hospital at once, and his life should not be in danger. Here is the key to room 306." A-Chu tossed the key into Han Zhengqi's hand.

For the first time Han Zhengqi felt, truly and to the bone, how terrible and how cold A-Chu was.

"Thank you, sir." Han Zhengqi pocketed the key and rushed away.

A-Chu, too, walked out of the tearoom. He heard the sound of cars beyond the door, and police sirens, and a clamor of footsteps. Han Zhengqi had taken his men off to the Park Hotel.

Lu Liangchen draped a coat over A-Chu's shoulders, and Xia Yuechun's car pulled up before him.

"How is it you haven't gone?" A-Chu asked.

Xia Yuechun smiled. "Waiting for you, of course."

"Waiting for me? Come off it. You were afraid I'd do away with Han Zhengqi, weren't you?" said A-Chu. "You can set your mind at rest now."

"Get in, get in — tell us in the car," Young Tang bawled from inside.

A-Chu turned back to Lu Liangchen. "You go on back first; I'll go straight to the White Rose Ballroom." With that he got into Xia Yuechun's car.

"Sir — the thing you wanted." Lu Liangchen leaned close to the car window and passed A-Chu a large envelope.

The car drove off . . .

A-Chu opened the envelope. Inside was a series of photographs of A-Ci, taken on the sly by a private detective.

A photograph of Yang Muci in military uniform met A-Chu's eye.

"Your younger brother is very handsome," said Xia Yuechun, with a sidelong glance.

"Handsome, is he? What's so handsome about it — it's not as if we've never seen the face; he's cut of the same rotten stripe as this one," said Young Tang scornfully.

"And what stripe am I?" A-Chu asked.

"Insufferably high and mighty. No — self-satisfied," said Young Tang.

"The two of you are far too alike," said Xia Yuechun.

"Yes — too alike; it gives one a kind of dread," said A-Chu. Suddenly his hand gave a jerk: he had come upon a photograph of A-Ci and Ronghua out walking the streets together, and the shock of it was no small thing. At once he slid the photographs back into the envelope.

So that was it. No wonder Lao Yu had mistaken one man for another — the two of them were of the same camp after all.

"How do you mean to broach it with your brother?" Xia Yuechun asked.

"First I'll ask him to tea," said A-Chu, a little beside the question.

"Here again?"

"No — it's too dreary here, and too far from where he works. Find some quieter tearoom, somewhere nearer the Central Shanghai Garrison Command."

"There's an English tearoom over that way," said Xia Yuechun.

"That'll do nicely — that's the one. Tomorrow at four in the afternoon, I'll ask him to take afternoon tea."

"Only, I hear your esteemed brother is a graduate of a Japanese college of finance — will he be insisting on Japanese tea, do you suppose?" Young Tang began to laugh. "Shall I present you, in advance, with a rhapsody on 'kinsmen turning swords upon kin'?"

"Look at him gloating over another man's troubles — he wants to watch the two brothers cross swords," said Xia Yuechun, who from the car's mirror could see Young Tang's smug, self-delighted face to perfection. "They say your brother is a proud one. Will he bow his head and do as you bid him?" Xia Yuechun asked A-Chu.

A-Chu gave a grunt of assent. "Since ancient times it has been so: the eldest brother stands as a father, and when the father is dead the younger follows the elder. It won't be left to him whether he obeys."

"And if your brother won't take the lesson?" Young Tang asked.

"Then I'll beat him until he does!"

"As fierce as that. Then your brother's in for a wretched time," said Xia Yuechun.

"Drive on — you talk too much." A-Chu pushed the mirror in front down flat, too weary to look at Young Tang's face twisted up with laughter.

The White Rose Ballroom.

Large photographs of the dance hostesses hung at the entrance of the ballroom, and beneath each was set a "flower basket presented by such-and-such young gentleman to such-and-such young lady," a mass of blossom and brocade, gay and thronged with life.

Half of Xin Lili's face wore a bright, radiant smile in the light; the other half was hidden in shadow, so that you could not fathom the sourness folded within the smile. Her forehead, her lovely hair, the smooth and graceful line of her nose fell just so along the border of light and dark, stirring in the beholder a reverie of beauty — something to look back upon, to linger over and be loath to leave, to yearn toward at one's leisure.

A-Chu, with Xia Yuechun and Young Tang, strolled along the long, narrow, brightly lit corridor of the ballroom, glancing idly over the photographs of the dance girls and their little notices.

In the dressing room of the ballroom, He Yashu, as she did every day, opened the lid of her rouge-and-powder box. Dabbing lightly at the rouge with a puff, she touched color into her own palm; intent upon her work, she mixed the shade until the fragrant powder in her palm had bloomed into a flower, dainty and yet not without splendor.

The music from the dance floor came drifting in, as though hurrying Yashu on to her turn.

The thin sediment of desolation banked up in Yashu's heart dispersed little by little into that soft, cloying music, and turned into the thick rouge she smeared upon her lips.

Her red lips were lush enough to drip, brimming with a lustrous sheen, for all the world like rose petals that had drunk by night of the immortal dew of the moon-palace — crystalline, translucent, their color and their fragrance in perfect measure.

Without thinking she straightened up and swayed her waist before the dressing mirror. She wore a white rose-patterned qipao; her bosom was full, her lines pronounced. The silk of the qipao was exceedingly soft and smooth, delicious to the touch.

The gorgeous, graceful figure that showed in the mirror was the other side of the Yashu of former days.

She had become a dance girl.

She did not know whether or not this counted as a fall.

It was time for her to go on.

The White Rose Ballroom. Three months earlier it had already passed into the holdings that stood in Yang Muchu's name. After A-Chu took over the gang he had closed down three finance companies one after another and put an end to all their moneylending business. Every last bit of his limited capital he poured into the catering and entertainment trades, enlarging the scale of the operation, so that members of the society who once could not show their faces in the light now walked out again into the sunshine, dressed to the nines.

A-Chu's principle, in his conduct and in his affairs, was this: be honest, and so profit yourself; keep faith, and so deal with others. The sense of social responsibility rooted deep in his mind guided his every action from first to last. And so, with the men of the society, he chose where to use them, and contrived by every means to keep their rice bowls whole, that they need no longer lick blood from the knife's edge; and at the same time he made sure of their absolute loyalty to him, so that at the first stir of wind in the grass his officers and men would spend their lives at his command.

The floor manager, knowing the proprietor had come with honored guests, came trotting over at a jog and handed each of them a sheaf of dance tickets; then he bowed and withdrew.

A-Chu walked down the middle of the Rose Corridor, and the photographs of this and that young lady — "Black Rose," "Yellow Rose," "Red Rose," and the rest — dimmed one after another under his roving gaze.

All at once A-Chu heard Young Tang give a queer yelp.

"A-Chu, you're finished, you're finished." Young Tang went on hollering.

"What is it?" Even as A-Chu spoke he stopped short, stunned, unable to hide the astonishment on his face. He had seen the large photograph of He Yashu in her qipao, rich in tone, like an oil painting.

Yashu's noble, coldly limpid expression overspread the whole coloring; she was set high above, like a maiden secluded in the courts of heaven suddenly banished down into the red dust of the world. She had not the gift of balancing allure against cold hauteur, so that the dimpling of her smile was stiff. The aristocratic air she had been born with had not yet been washed clean out of her in the muddy dark of these nights; her eyes, quickened by the warm light, looked strangely sensual, and utterly without restraint — enough to wake in a man the desire to peel her bodily out of the picture.

"A-Chu — tell me, if the eldest young master of the Rong house were to see Yashu working as a dance hostess in your ballroom, what would he do?" Young Tang watched A-Chu's face.

"This joke has gone too far," A-Chu said to himself.

"That's just what I mean — I say you're a dead man."

"This young lady must come of noble stock?" said Xia Yuechun, looking at the photograph. "That air of hers is not a thing that can be learned — a pity she has come down in the world."

"The last extremity of 'coming down in the world' is bound to be 'falling into ruin.' To use one's own good looks to rescue one's finances, to fawn on money of one's own free will — perhaps that is the real, the natural her. I wonder whether Brother Xia and Brother Yang would agree?" Young Tang's zest was undiminished.

Xia Yuechun had already guessed eight or nine parts of it in his heart: this white rose in full bloom on the dance floor must stand in some delicate relation to the Young Tang and the A-Chu before him, and it would not do to say so outright, for fear of wounding the two men's dignity. So with a smile he put Young Tang off with a single remark. "What Brother Tang says is well worth savoring. Only, I dare not add a word of assent."

"Hypocrite," Young Tang swore with a laugh.

"It's excusable," said A-Chu.

"You say it's excusable?" Young Tang gave a queer cry, his face working through a whole play of expressions. "You think she'd sooner be a wanton than marry me—" Catching all at once the laughter in Xia Yuechun's eyes, he choked the words back down his throat, blew out a mouthful of foul temper, and said: "—than marry a gentleman of the first water like me: that's excusable, is it?"

"The women of the blue houses are not every one of them wantons; and did not Yu Xuanji, who took refuge in the Buddhist gate, fly her gaudy flag as high as any?" A-Chu answered him back. "To work as a dance girl is one way of staying alive. You suppose everyone is like you — leaning on the shade of his forefathers' merit, whiling away his days playing at opera, smoking opium, racing horses, haunting the dance halls, a parasite upon society."

"When I play at opera, I am furthering the national essence."

"And smoking opium? Is that furthering the national essence too?" A-Chu would not let it go.

"I—opium is a foreign article; when I smoke opium I am—I am—" Young Tang flushed crimson.

Xia Yuechun smoothed it over and came to the rescue. "—assimilating new knowledge," he said.

"Just so — assimilating new knowledge. Do you follow?"

"I don't follow — the idle refinements of you young gentlemen are beyond me. All I know is that for a person to sacrifice her self-respect and earn her bread by selling smiles takes courage too. I feel sorry for Yashu." And it was not sorrow only, but a faint, faint melancholy besides — for the circumstances Yashu must live in; for how low she had stooped from her high estate; for the smiles and the tears that had once been Yashu's.

That Yashu had come to this pass was in part his own doing. He must, therefore, do something for her — not merely for the sake of Rong Sheng's face, but out of his own contrition as well.

"Give me your dance tickets," A-Chu said, holding out his hand to Young Tang.

"What for?" Young Tang was taken aback.

"Give them here." A-Chu all but snatched them. "From this moment she is finished with the work." He tore the tickets to pieces.

"You're abusing your authority," said Young Tang, aggrieved.

"Say that I am, then," said A-Chu.

"Shanghai is a free world," said Young Tang, unwilling to let the matter drop.

"For you to go and ask her to dance would be, beyond doubt, to humiliate her."

"If she was willing to come out and do the work, she must have reckoned on a day like this."

"Do not do to others what you would not have done to yourself."

"I keep no rules of moral order; your golden maxims have no force with me."

"Is it that you want to see her seek her own death once more, right before your eyes?" The words carried full weight; hearing them, Young Tang did indeed draw in his fire, and said, deflated: "Are you threatening me?"

Xia Yuechun of his own accord handed his own dance tickets back to A-Chu, clapped Young Tang on the shoulder, and said: "If you want to dance, we'll go to another house. I'll come with you."

"Everything you spend tonight, I'll pay for," said A-Chu.

Half yielding and half holding back, Young Tang let Xia Yuechun's smooth words give him a way to climb down, and A-Chu had someone see the two of them off to the Paramount, while he himself went on along the corridor and came to the dance floor. The lights at the floor's edge were dim; a dozen-odd couples turned in the dance, and the perfume on the dance girls streamed out beneath the glittering, voluptuous lamps. A-Chu saw Yashu, and all at once his heart felt a stab of pain.

He Yashu wore a high-collared qipao, wrapped close from throat to breast, and yet her snow-white arms were bared, dazzling, under the lights. Her eyes were dreamlike, forlorn and yielding by turns, holding the reserve of fallen blossom, holding a desolate self-respect; treading a dreamlike measure, her weary limbs repeated their mechanical motions, and the dance floor seemed a scented water stained with the raw perfume of flowers, all duckweed adrift and fluff a-whirl, throwing up now and again a spray of sorrowful beauty.

Suddenly A-Chu's eyes and Yashu's met, and in that instant the "rose" on the dance floor began to tremble; A-Chu could all but hear her rustling, shivering heart, and his contrition and her trembling blended in a moment into one plaintive strain of music.

Yashu dropped her lashes sharply, and glistening tears started from her eyes; a dread she had never known before pressed all at once upon her very soul. Then of a sudden she wondered — what expression would be on A-Chu's face? She wanted to know; and so she lifted her head, but in those few seconds she could no longer find A-Chu. He had been like a firefly passing in the night, gone in a single flash — had it been a dream? Yashu wondered, and keeping her inward loneliness secret, she turned on and on as if in the last spinning of a broken dream, and turned on again.

A-Chu had deliberately avoided Yashu's gaze; somewhat distracted, his soul not quite his own, he walked to the corridor of the rear hall and pushed open, at random, a door where the corridor turned. He heard a woman's shriek and a low laugh.

"I thought you'd forgotten me." Xin Lili stood before the dressing mirror, just then putting on her dancing gown, while a little dance girl crouched on the floor arranging the hem for her; her snow-white back was full in A-Chu's line of sight. A brash breach of propriety and a becoming, bashful shyness met and mingled in one, and A-Chu turned away as if by reflex.

"What are you playing the innocent for, you Yang fellow — as if you'd never seen me with my clothes off?" Xin Lili turned her body gracefully, sank into a curtsy where she stood — a pretty European court obeisance — and held out her hand with an elegant ease.

A-Chu took the offered hand and drew her up. He did not kiss the back of it, for he was wearing a Chinese long gown, and it always struck him as neither one thing nor the other.

And besides, it was plain from what Lili had said that she had taken him for someone else.

The little dance girl gathered up her own long skirt, bowed, and withdrew first.

"Do up my zip for me," said Lili.

A-Chu was a little embarrassed, and glanced left and right out of habit.

"What's the matter with you?" Lili asked.

"You are very beautiful — so beautiful that a man dares not make free with you," A-Chu answered.

Lili laughed. "So you've learned to flatter people now, have you?"

A-Chu drew up the zip of her gown; his hand brushed her skin by accident, and he withdrew it with a start. That unmeant flinching only made Lili sense that something was wrong; at once she drew back on her guard, her distrustful gaze taking A-Chu's measure, and in about half a second she had made certain that the man before her was not A-Ci. "Who are you?"

"Whom are you waiting for?" A-Chu returned.

"I'm waiting for a friend of mine."

"I too am a friend of yours."

"And your honorable surname?"

"Guess."

"Mr. Yang?" Lili herself was not sure why she should fall in with his game and guess.

"Clever. Right the first time." A-Chu sat down.

"As far as I can recall, we don't know each other, Mr. Yang."

"Oh? Surely a proprietor needs no appointment in advance to come and look in on the finest of the employees under him. Does he, Miss Lili?"

"Oh?" Lili drew the word out mischievously. "So you are that Mr. Chu one hears of — the divine dragon that shows its head but never its tail — the great proprietor Chu himself? Your humble servant has been remiss."

A-Chu corrected her. "It's Mr. Yang. Yang Muchu."

A flash of Lili's autumn glance; the name made her more curious still.

"Mr. Yang — you're so gentle and refined, you don't look like a man in this line of business."

"The same might be said of you."

"What do you mean?"

"It seems to me you're not one who lives by this trade either." There was an elliptical hint folded into A-Chu's words, and Lili held her tongue and did not answer. A kind of tacit understanding spread through the interstices of their half-probing, half-flirting talk.

"Did you come especially to see me?" Lili asked.

"No. Chance and coincidence."

"Do you smoke?"

"Thank you, no — I don't smoke."

"Is that so?" Lili drew a cigarette from the case. "You won't mind?"

"As you please," said A-Chu.

Xin Lili lit the cigarette with a smile. "Shall we dance? My treat."

"Thank you, but I'm very tired tonight." Then it struck A-Chu, all at once, about the meeting with A-Ci: was there not a ready-made liaison officer standing right before him? "Give him a call," A-Chu said, straight out.

"Give whom a call?"

"Your lover."

"I have more than one lover; which do you mean?" Xin Lili blew out a ring of smoke.

"The one who looks very like me," said A-Chu.

"Looks very like you — like you to what degree?"

"Down to the last hair."

"And you believe that?"

"Didn't you just now take me for him? Why else would you change your clothes in front of me?"

"That was because I wanted to seduce you," Lili said, still smiling.

"You said, 'you Yang fellow — as if you'd never seen me with my clothes off.' It really was the first time I had ever seen you—" A-Chu paused, and said: "—changing your clothes."

"But why do you want to see him?" Lili was full of curiosity. "Don't you dare tell me it's out of jealousy."

"And why not?" A-Chu took a red rose from the vase at hand and offered it to Lili. Their cheeks brushed lightly, and A-Chu said in a low voice: "You are very enchanting."

"Thank you."

A-Chu walked to the door and said: "Tomorrow at two in the afternoon, I'll be waiting for him at the English Tearoom. I'll not leave until he comes."

"You think I'm certain to make this call?"

"Yes. You have no reason to refuse me."

"Just what are you to him, then?"

"His own flesh and blood." A-Chu went out and shut the door.

The clamorous music came rushing at him . . .

A-Chu heard unbridled laughter come from the dance floor; he could not tell whether that laughter came from Yashu's lips, and it made him very uneasy. He summoned the dance-floor manager.

"Sir, what are your orders?"

"That one — He . . . He—?" A-Chu was suddenly a little awkward.

"You mean Miss Yashu?"

"Yes." A-Chu steadied himself. "How long has she worked here?"

"Two months."

"She came to apply of her own accord?"

"Yes."

"And you — how do you and she usually get on?"

"Only so-so."

"Try asking her — there's a securities exchange in want of a member of staff, and the pay is good; ask her whether she'd have any interest in the work. If she's willing, tell me at once."

"Yes, sir." The manager made to go. A-Chu suddenly caught hold of him and said: "Don't tell her the proprietor arranged it. Not a word of it."

"Very good, sir."

"OK." A-Chu let go of him.

A-Chu's personal bodyguard Liu A-Si came over; he had seen the manager leave.

"Is something the matter, sir?"

"Nothing. A-Si — the other day you said you'd looked at a house in Plum Blossom Lane for the young master's friend. Are there any empty houses left in that quarter?"

"There are. Plum Blossom Lane is out of the way, but the air is very good."

"Take me to have a look."

"Now?" Liu A-Si was surprised.

"Come." So saying, A-Chu made straight off ahead. Liu A-Si followed a few steps, then turned back, took A-Chu's trench coat and scarf from the attendant at the door, and went out after him.

Along the broad, long street the yellow lamplight flickered, and Yang Muci drove past in a jeep. He pulled up before the door of the Huamei Bookstore and cut the engine.

A window of the Huamei Bookstore stood half open; Ronghua put her head half out and gave Muci a sign of greeting. Muci got out of the car to wait, and in less than three minutes Ronghua, carrying a piece of luggage, came downstairs with the delegate of the Third Communist International.

Yang Muci took the luggage from Ronghua's hand, and Ronghua made them a brief introduction. "This is Mr. Cong Feng, delegate of the Communist International . . . and this is the one charged with your safety during the Shanghai conference—"

Before Ronghua had finished, Cong Feng had already rushed up and thrown his arms warmly around Muci — and would not let go for a long while, so that Muci was acutely embarrassed. With his eyes he signaled to Ronghua to extricate him, but for the moment she too was at a loss.

"A-Chu, how glad I am to see you," Cong Feng said with feeling.

"You—you've mistaken me for someone else, surely?" said Muci.

"What are you saying? It's me, Cong Feng — look properly." At last Cong Feng let go. "I may be in disguise, but you ought to know me all the same." Cong Feng took off his hat to let Muci recognize him; Muci shook his head, and Cong Feng could not help giving him a punch. "It isn't possible — back little more than a year, and you don't even know me? No wonder Cong Hui says a man's heart is iron. Why, you're far better-looking. Only, you've lost something of your old grace and elegance."

"I—" Muci did not know how to tell him. "I'm very sorry, but I am not this A-Chu you speak of; you truly have mistaken me for another. My name is Yang Muci, and I am the one charged, for this enlarged meeting of the Central Special Committee, with seeing to your safety. As this is our first meeting, I beg you to excuse any want of courtesy." Muci put out his hand.

Much astonished, Cong Feng put out his own hand, and the two of them shook.

Each with feelings of his own.

The three of them soon got into the car.

"For the time being we've arranged for you to stay at Number Five, Plum Blossom Lane. It's a little out of the way, but very convenient for getting about, with roads running in every direction. During the meetings I'll play the part of your wife and see you safely through. We would rather you had no contact or dealings with any friends while the meetings are on — all of it, of course, purely for the sake of your safety. You must cooperate," said Ronghua.

Cong Feng nodded.

Ronghua reached out and patted Muci on the shoulder; Muci, driving with full attention, took a parcel from the seat beside him without turning his head, and passed it to her.

Ronghua opened the parcel; Cong Feng glanced inside. It held American Eveready batteries, Beauty-brand scented toilet soap, a towel, a toothbrush, a cup, and so on.

"I brought my own toilet things."

"But you can't use them — we can't have anyone learn that you're a visitor come from the Soviet Union."

"A washroom is a private domain."

"It's not the ten thousand times that go right you fear, but the one in ten thousand that goes wrong," Muci put in.

"Are you two lovers?" Cong Feng thrust the point home.

"No," Ronghua answered, crisp and short.

"You understand each other very well."

"The work requires it," said Ronghua, and Cong Feng gave a faint smile. He looked out of the car window at a row of last plum trees, their sparse shadows withered and pale, spare and hard, wearing an air of bleak desolation.

The car drove on into Plum Blossom Lane.

Number Seven, Plum Blossom Lane.

Yang Muchu's car stood at the door; he had got out alone to look at the house, telling A-Si to wait outside.

The little courtyard was desolate, overrun with weeds; in the moonlight the grass stirred with the wind, adding to the eerie, forbidding air. But the structure of the house was very fine to look at — beams, pillars, walls all wrought in imitation of the antique, works of art every one. The former master of this courtyard must have set great store by a life of exquisite refinement.

A-Chu thought of renting this courtyard; with a little seeing-to it would make a most leisured and elegant dwelling. If Yashu changed her work, he thought, she might do worse than move in here first.

He crossed a little path and saw a green, glossy pond; amid the croaking of frogs and the chirp of insects, the lamp next door came on.

Number Five, Plum Blossom Lane.

Ronghua saw Muci out.

"He really is a handful," said Muci, getting into the car. "Good luck to you."

"What do you mean?" Ronghua held on to the car door, not letting it shut.

"Don't you think he's like one of those young dandies on the fashionable color-lithograph calendar posters?"

"You're prejudiced against him."

"I've really no prejudice against him at all," said Muci with a laugh. "Only, he's too much a creature of feeling, too temperamental — not suited to this work of ours."

"In this work of ours, it's rare enough to meet a comrade as broadly learned and as warm toward people as he is."

"I've already had a taste of his warmth," said Muci, miming an embrace.

Ronghua laughed. "You can't lay it all to his charge — you should know that I once mistook you myself. Trace the error to its cause, and it is simply that you and A-Chu are indeed too much alike."

"Alike in the face, no more," said Muci.

"No — not merely in the face, but exactly alike; like — like brothers, the twin sort."

"You exaggerate. Still, given the chance, I should rather like to meet this Mr. Chu the two of you speak of, and see just how like him I am." Muci glanced at the sky and said: "Get inside, quick, before he goes wandering all over the courtyard."

Ronghua shut the car door fast for him and watched him drive away.

Cong Feng was much taken with the courtyard in the moonlight; he felt that neither England's classical architecture nor Russia's solid fortresses could match a Chinese courtyard for delicacy and grace.

He halted beneath a low, sunken stretch of wall, listening with great delight to the frogs and insects next door.

"Let's go back inside," Ronghua said, standing by the wall. "The wind is strong out here."

"Let's go over and take a look."

"That's hardly a good idea," Ronghua checked him. "No one has lived next door for a long while. When I was choosing this house I went over there — it's quite desolate."

"It's in desolation that one finds a mournful beauty. Let's go and see," said Cong Feng.

"I must remind you to be careful — just now we are engaged in underground work."

"But you are my wife now, are you not?" Cong Feng laughed. "We must go through some process of getting acquainted — go adventuring a little, come to know each other."

A-Chu stood at the edge of the pond, breathing the clean fragrance of water and trees that hung diffused in the air, and he thought of Cong Hui — so far away, far beyond reach.

Between him and Cong Hui it was utterly over.

For after all he had done, he could never again face Cong Hui, whose pure heart was clear as water.

He pitied Yashu; but who was there to pity him? An inch of sorrowing heart, and nothing in all the world to ease its idleness.

Suddenly A-Chu saw two long slanting shadows fall across the ground; he raised his head — the tree shade dripped green and hid part of his view — the shadows were moving, and soon he saw clearly who had come.
