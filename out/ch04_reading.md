## Chapter Four. The Hour of the Goat, First Half (1 p.m.)

{v} Within Qujiang Pool the channels wound and meandered, towers and halls stood in ranks, flowers ringed it round and willow-shade closed overhead, and little paths threaded in and out among the gardens: in every season of the year it was a superb place to be—whether for a sightseer or for a fugitive.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Goat, first half.

{d} Chang’an; Wannian County; Xiuzheng Ward.

Xiuzheng Ward sat in the southeast corner of the city wall, far from the imperial city, from the East and West Markets, and from such bustling places as Yanshou and Pingkang Wards; but it bordered Qujiang Pool and the Furong Garden, which made it wonderfully convenient for outings, feasting, and the enjoyment of scenery. Though most of the capital’s grandees and nobles did not live here, they all contrived to acquire a detached villa or two, an out-of-the-way lodge, in the ward.

For Long Bo or the Türks to take shelter here was indeed a shrewd choice. In this season few people were living in the mansions of the district; many stood empty, ideal for hiding in.

Time pressed. Zhang Xiaojing and Yao Runeng put whip to horse and galloped all the way from Pingkang Ward to Xiuzheng Ward.

Compared with the packed, crowded buildings of the wards to the north, the mansions of Xiuzheng Ward were laid out far more sparsely—no more than seven or eight households to a street, though each occupied a far greater expanse of ground. The gates were broad, and the enclosing walls to either side ran a good thirty paces and more. The wall-tops were sheathed in a single sweep of blue scale-tiles, and behind them grew pine and bamboo, wisteria and creeper, spacious and pleasing to the eye. Standing back a little, one could even make out the raised terraces and pavilions rising within the courtyards, the very picture of grandeur.

By Tong’er’s account, whenever Long Bo took her out, it was always to the third house along the cross-lane in the southwest corner of Xiuzheng Ward. Set beside its neighbors, this mansion looked rather shabby: the outer skin of its courtyard wall was flaking away, its tiles broken and gap-toothed, like a row of rotten teeth. The beast-ring knockers on the gate were rusted, and no name-board hung above it—a sign that the house was, for the moment, without a master.

The Jing’an Bureau had already pulled the deed: the owner of the house was a wealthy Yangzhou merchant surnamed Jin, who had not shown his face in some years—dead, perhaps, or perhaps he had simply forgotten it. The place had stood derelict and empty all that time; not so much as a house-servant had ever been hired to sweep it. For the Türks to choose it as a safe-house was apt indeed.

Zhang Xiaojing had always believed the Türks must have more than one safe-house in Chang’an—without them they could never mount an operation of any size. Turn the reasoning around, and it followed that if he could only find a safe-house, he might well follow the vine to the melon and run the Türks to ground.

From the outside the empty house showed nothing amiss. But Zhang Xiaojing knew the buildings of Xiuzheng Ward: even the meanest of them ran five or six courtyards deep, and there was no telling what lay inside without slipping in to see. He checked the string and bolt of his pocket crossbow, cinched his trouser-cuffs and sleeves tight, then slipped the scabbard off the knife at his belt and said to Yao Runeng: “I don’t know what’s in there. I’ll go in first and take a look. You keep watch at the gate and stay in touch with the watchtower.”

“Alone?” Yao Runeng asked, startled.

Zhang Xiaojing said coolly, “Right now I don’t dare trust you at my back.”

The corner of Yao Runeng’s mouth twitched. He lowered his head and silently drew back a few paces. After that quarrel in Pingkang Ward, things between the two of them had grown delicate.

A moment earlier Yao Runeng had already reported Zhang Xiaojing’s base conduct up to the Jing’an Bureau by watchtower. The Bureau’s reply, however, had been a rebuke to him: a single informer was nothing set against the safety of all Chang’an, and he was warned not to interfere again with Commander Zhang’s work, nor to use the watchtower to pass along such trifles.

Stubbornly, Yao Runeng held that Zhang Xiaojing must have some scheme of his own, and that it was only his superiors, kept in the dark, who could not see it. Now that the man wanted to go into the house alone—could he be meaning to slip away and escape? Yet if he had a mind to run, he could have knocked Yao out cold earlier and gone; why wait until now?

He stood rooted where he was, his thoughts in a tangle, unsure whether he should follow to keep watch or obey the order and stand by to lend support. Before Yao Runeng could decide, Zhang Xiaojing over there had already clamped the barrier-knife between his teeth, backed off a dozen-odd paces from the wall, then suddenly broke into a run, sprang up to catch the top, and vaulted nimbly over the courtyard wall.

If the Türks were hidden here, they would surely have rigged the main gate and the several corner-gates; going over the wall was the best choice.

The moment he landed he crouched among the shrubs to observe, then moved cautiously inward. There was nothing novel in the layout of the place. Past the spirit-screen stood a flat-eaved central hall, joined to the east and west wing-rooms by a covered gallery that curved and wound about, enclosing an empty court. A pity: the several flower-stands set in the middle had long been shrouded in dust, their earthen pots abandoned. In the soil at the base of the wall grew a few clumps of peony, but the first month was no season for blossom—only the bare, leafless stems reached out, and no one, it seemed, tended them.

The gallery curved round behind the main hall and ran on into a stand of pines, and amid the screening trees there seemed to stand a two-story wooden pavilion.

Zhang Xiaojing hid himself beneath the gallery’s eaves and put his head out to watch for about the time it takes half a stick of incense to burn. Nothing seemed to stir in the courtyard, and his heart sank a little. He had, after all, only been guessing that this might be a Türk safe-house; if the guess proved empty, he would have no usable lead left in hand, and the whole strategy would have to begin again from the start.

He decided to press on a little further, and stepped up onto the gallery, edging forward. Suddenly Zhang Xiaojing twitched his nose: he had caught the faintest trace of powder and rouge—a woman had passed this way just now, and she could not have been gone long. Tong’er had been taken into custody, so it could not be her; who, then, was here? He crouched again and rubbed his fingers over the wooden floorboards of the gallery, and a bluish-white dust clung to the pads of his fingers. This was no ordinary dust: it was stone-grit.

There was no such material anywhere in the house; it must have been tracked in on the soles of an outsider’s shoes.

Beyond doubt, someone had been here. And since they were not in the front hall, could they be hidden away in the two-story pavilion behind?

Zhang Xiaojing was just rising when he felt a rush of wind above his head. His reflexes were lightning-quick: he rolled forward on the spot, dodging the blade’s edge and righting himself in the same motion, and snapped his elbow back to loose a bolt. There was a soft thud—the sound of a crossbow bolt sinking into flesh. Zhang Xiaojing kicked off hard with his left leg and lunged back the way his bolt had flown; there a man had already toppled aslant to the ground. With his right hand like a pair of pincers he clamped the man’s jaw shut so he could make no sound, flung the pocket crossbow aside with his left, drew the barrier-knife and drove it savagely into the man’s belly—three times over, giving the hilt a twist each time.

The man slumped limp to the ground, dead. Only now had Zhang Xiaojing leisure to study his face: another Türk, but wearing the tabard of the Directorate for the Palace Buildings. One side of the gallery was pierced with a straight-latticed moon-window that blocked half the view. This Türk had likely been relieving himself in the thicket behind the window, which was why Zhang Xiaojing had not seen him.

It had been a matter of a hair’s breadth. Had Zhang Xiaojing been a fraction slower to react, the Türk’s blade would have split his skull. And had the man not been greedy for the credit of a stealthy kill, but instead cried out first to warn his fellows, Zhang Xiaojing would very likely have found himself ringed round and slaughtered.

Only one man set to patrol the front hall, rather than two posts, one in the open and one hidden—the enemy’s numbers, it seemed, could not be great either. Zhang Xiaojing was all but certain now: the enemy was in that two-story pavilion behind.

Caught the wolves by the tail at last, Zhang Xiaojing thought, exhilarated.

He could withdraw now and have Yao Runeng notify the Jing’an Bureau; Cui Qi’s Lüben Guards would be here within two quarter-hours. But that scent nagged at Zhang Xiaojing, and he decided to steal a little further forward.

The two-story pavilion behind the central hall bore the name “Zhuxin.” From outside it looked to be built for viewing: the lower story was one great open room for feasting guests, with bamboo stairs leading up to the second floor, which was divided into several rooms for resting or private talk. At the very top was a high kiosk, from which one could lift one’s eyes and gaze far off over Qujiang.

Zhang Xiaojing watched for a while. No figure showed at the windows; these people were careful. He decided to withdraw for the moment—the pavilion’s inner structure was intricate and its spaces cramped, and to blunder in would be too dangerous. But just as he was about to slip quietly away, from one of the rooms on the second floor there suddenly came a woman’s shriek.

At the sound—so familiar—Zhang Xiaojing’s two silkworm brows knotted together. He hesitated a moment, then leveled the pocket crossbow and crept along beneath the lower windows toward the front door. Reaching it, he set his back to the wall, turned side-on to face the doorway, and flung a garden stone he had picked up in the court away in the opposite direction.

As he had foreseen, the man in the pavilion’s main room heard the sound and opened the door to look. Zhang Xiaojing gave the door a violent shove from the side; it slammed hard against the back of the man’s skull, and then his arm cinched savagely around the man’s throat. The fellow’s neck was seized all at once; he struggled desperately, and his right leg kicked over a flower-stand beside him. A fine-veined earthen pot fell to the ground and shattered with a crash into countless shards, ringing out across the whole courtyard.

With a backhand wrench Zhang Xiaojing snapped the man’s neck. But his scheme to slip in unnoticed was now in ruins. Hurried footsteps sounded from the second floor, dust sifted swiftly down from the ceiling, and with it came loud shouting in Turkic. Things having come to this, Zhang Xiaojing wasted no time on regret; he took up the pocket crossbow and charged up the bamboo stairs. The first man to come rushing down he felled with a single bolt, and the body tumbled the rest of the way.

Zhang Xiaojing seized the chance and rushed straight up to the second floor in one breath, ducking behind a handsome embroidered screen that stood facing the stairs. The Türks had hand-crossbows too, and loosed a wild volley—whiss, whiss, whiss—that riddled the screen into a sieve. Zhang Xiaojing deliberately held his fire; then, the moment one man came forward with a raised blade, he loosed a swift bolt that took him square in the knee.

The others dragged their screaming comrade back and dared not close for a while. So the two sides took cover and, each holding one end of the corridor, shot it out. For a moment bolts flew crosswise through the little pavilion like a storm blown indoors.

Arrows and crossbows were forbidden entry into the city, so these Türks’ crossbows were all privately made—inferior to the army’s regulation issue in both rate of fire and accuracy. With the strength of a single crossbow, Zhang Xiaojing could actually pin down three men and three crossbows so they could not raise their heads.

Zhang Xiaojing’s trouble was that the bolts he carried were nearly spent. He guessed the enemy still had at least four men, all shrinking back inside the second-floor rooms and refusing to come out, and a private anxiety gnawed at him.

“Jing’an Bureau business! You are surrounded!” Zhang Xiaojing set his last bolt into the crossbow-groove and shouted it aloud in Turkic.

The shooting in the corridor stopped for the moment, and then came a creaking, scraping sound of something being dragged. A voice cried out: “You, over there—lay down your weapons, or Wang Zhongsi’s daughter dies!”

Wang Zhongsi? At the name Zhang Xiaojing’s movements froze. The man was the linchpin of this whole Tang campaign against the Türks—and the Türks had actually carried off his daughter?

He put half his head out around the corner and saw a burly Türk Wolf Guard standing in the middle of the corridor, a woman trussed up hand and foot hauled against his chest, one hand gripping her by the neck and a sharp knife laid crosswise at her throat. Unluckily the light was behind them, and he could not make out either face.

“I’ll count to three; if you still won’t throw it down, she bleeds.” As he spoke, Ma Ge’er pressed the blade harder against the woman’s tender neck. Her cloud of hair was in disarray, her mouth stuffed with a cloth gag, so that she could only whimper.

At that sound, a flicker of startled doubt crossed Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye. It was not the voice of Wang Zhongsi’s daughter; it sounded more like that girl Wen Ran—but wasn’t she supposed to have taken his warning and left the capital? How had she become tangled up in the Türks’ affairs? And how had she come to be confused with Wang Zhongsi’s daughter?

Ma Ge’er issued his threat a third time, and this time he meant to make good on it. Zhang Xiaojing sucked at his teeth and had no choice but to drop the crossbow to the ground and kick it toward Ma Ge’er. Had it truly been Wang Zhongsi’s daughter, he would not have cared whether she lived or died; but the one held hostage was Wen Ran, and that he could not ignore. These Türks—by sheer blind luck they had hit the mark.

“Your knife too!” Ma Ge’er kept his grip clamped tight on Wen Ran’s neck.

Zhang Xiaojing could only cast the barrier-knife aside as well and step out with both hands raised high.

Two Türks rushed him and pinned him to the floor. His hands pinned and all power of resistance gone, Zhang Xiaojing could only struggle to lift his head, trying to make out the woman’s face—but Ma Ge’er had already shoved her back into the room.

He was still struggling when a great hand yanked him up by the hair and slammed him down against the floor. The brutal impact set stars bursting before his eyes and knocked two streams of blood from his nostrils; then came a second time, and a third, until soon an appalling smear of blood spread across the fine cypress floorboards...

***

Li Bi had by now returned to the Jing’an Bureau. He had gathered Xu Bin and the others and was conferring in low voices before the sand-table. Out beyond them, the clerks, servants, message-runners, soldiers, and attendants were each busy at their tasks; the whole great hall of the Bureau was a bustling press of activity.

Now a petty clerk stood to one side holding a glazed sand-glass; the instant the fine sand ran out of the vial, he would upend its mouth and count aloud: “One leak, two leaks, three leaks...” At every fourth leak, an old man beside him would set down a few crimson paper slips among the wards. Across the whole sand-table there were by now more than thirty crimson slips, laid over a dozen-odd wards and markets in the northern city, joined and clustered together so that, to the eye, the whole spread was a sea of red.

Before long Xu Bin raised a hand to signal a halt to the timing and, cupping his hands to Li Bi, said: “Forty leaks, thirty-seven wards.”

At the figure, the faces of everyone around turned grave.

This was a simulation run on the sand-table, its purpose to work out what in the end the Türks meant to do.

Zhang Xiaojing was out there straining every nerve to trace them, but Li Bi did not like to wait passively; he had resolved to be more active. The Türks had said Chang’an would become the Que-le Huo-duo—yet what the Que-le Huo-duo actually was remained unclear. So Li Bi had assembled a body of clerks well versed in the state of the city and set them a problem: “By what means could one do the greatest harm to Chang’an in the shortest time?”

The clerks soon produced their conclusion: arson.

Other methods were either too complicated or too limited in effect. Arson was simple to plan and cheap to carry out, and provided the time and place were well chosen, a handful of men could touch off a vast catastrophe.

For the Wolf Guards, who had no foothold in Chang’an, it was very nearly their only choice.

But Li Bi was still not satisfied with this answer; he wanted to know more of the particulars—where exactly would the fire best be started? In which direction would it spread? How fast, how slow? So he summoned several veteran clerks from the constable-posts who understood fire intimately, and with this great sand-table ran a fire-simulation.

In the simulation, one full turn of the sand-glass marked one quarter-hour, and one cluster of crimson slips stood for a fire’s spread over three hundred paces around. Xu Bin’s report—“forty leaks, thirty-seven wards”—meant that once fire broke out, within four double-hours it could spread to thirty-seven wards, all of them in the flourishing northern city, the very cream of Chang’an.

And this was only the simulation of a single outbreak. If someone were set on it and lit fires in several places at once, the outcome would likely be several times more harrowing.

Looking at the crimson slips crowded thick across the sand-table, every onlooker saw rise in his mind a horrifying vision of a hell of raging fire. Could this—could this be the true face of the Que-le Huo-duo?

Li Bi knit his brows. “It spreads as fast as that? Have the wards’ fire-precautions been taken into account?”

Xu Bin said: “On an ordinary day, the wards are divided by their walls, and the city is cut across by a lattice of water-channels, so no great harm would come of it—ai, ai, but you must not forget: today is the Lantern Festival. Every ward and every street will hang out lanterns; the candles burning will number in the tens of thousands, the lantern-frames are all bamboo and wood, and at the least mishap the fire leaps from ward to ward. This spring the wind is high and everything is tinder-dry; should a blaze start, it will be a fire sweeping through a chain of camps...”

All at once it dawned on them. No wonder the Türks were so fixed on the ward-map. With the ward-map in hand, one could readily reckon which places lay far from the water-channels; which stood on higher ground where the wind could be borrowed; which bordered the great thoroughfares, where a fire could be made to spread outward on every side at the greatest speed.

Cui Qi spoke up loudly from one side: “We have the watchtowers, don’t we? The moment a fire’s seen to start, send men straight off to put it out—and there’s an end of it, surely?”

Xu Bin smiled ruefully. “Ai, ai, Commander Cui, you make it too simple. Tonight a million soldiers and civilians will be out to view the lanterns, the roads jammed to bursting—how are you to move the constables? And besides, once a great fire starts, the people are bound to panic. With so many trampling and fleeing, are you saving people or fighting the fire?”

Cui Qi fell silent; he of all people knew how terrible a panicked mob could be. The two of them turned their eyes at once to the Deputy Director, but Li Bi only pinched his chin, brooding and saying nothing.

The best answer, of course, would be to cancel the lantern fair and restore the night curfew—utterly impossible; the next best, to search every ward one by one—also impossible. Li Bi shook his head helplessly. Hemmed in on every side, within the Bureau and without, unable to have his way, he was truly like a man dancing the Sogdian whirl in fetters.

There was, in fact, one more method: to have the veteran clerks mark on the sand-table the spots best suited for arson, and send men ahead to lie in wait there. But this was no better than a gamble—let a single one be guessed wrong, and the whole thing collapsed. Li Bi did not care for that kind of trusting to heaven.

Yet if he did not do this, what else could he do? Was he to pin all his hopes on Zhang Xiaojing alone?

Just then a white-bearded old clerk beside him put in: “Better than searching the wards is to search the goods.” Li Bi’s eyes brightened, and he motioned him to go on. The old clerk answered respectfully: “Your servant once worked the land and often burned off the fields. To raise a sky-scorching blaze, you need two things: first, that the fire-head be large; second, that the fire run fierce. The first depends on hemp-oil, the second on firewood. If the Wolf Guards mean to burn the city, these two things they cannot do without, and in no small quantity.”

“You mean that the Wolf Guards, here in Chang’an, must be storing up a great stock of oil and firewood?”

“The Deputy Director sees clearly. In your servant’s humble view, only keep close watch on any bulk store of these two kinds of goods, and something is sure to come of it.”

This was a fresh stroke of thought, and all who heard it nodded inwardly. Li Bi said in praise: “Xun Yue’s Extended Reflections has it: ‘Prevention comes first; remedy comes second.’ This method draws the firewood from under the cauldron—one might say it grasps the very heart of the matter.”

Seeing a colleague win a nod from on high, the others grew bolder too. One said: “Firewood and the like all comes from the hill-folk of the capital region, scattered and piecemeal, hard to trace at short notice; better to concentrate on the oil. That is not easily rendered and pressed—only a great house or a great ward can manage it, so its sources all rest in the hands of a few large merchants, and it can be traced the faster.”

Another petty clerk went on to suggest: “The capital’s oil is mostly hauled in from elsewhere. You need only call up the customs records of the gate guards and see whether, in recent days, any foreign merchants have brought in bulk pork fat, mutton fat, sesame and other oil-stuffs, or rendered oil; then you can follow the map to the mark and find the storehouse...”

“Nonsense—do you suppose men of the Central Plains cannot be bought? When I say search, search the lot!” Li Bi’s face darkened as he set him right. One notion he had always drummed into his subordinates was this: harbor no bias between Han and Hu; both alike are dangerous.

The clerks swiftly copied these suggestions out into a dozen-odd formal dispatches, and Li Bi himself affixed the great seal of the Jing’an Bureau.

“Send these off at once to every office, and let them act on the order with all speed. Within one double-hour I want a full list of every place in Chang’an that stores oil or oil-stuffs.”

The runners took the order and hurried out. The clerks returned to their seats and fell to their work again.

Li Bi went back to his own place and closed his eyes a while. Tanqi came up behind him, and her slender jade fingers pressed to his temples and began gently to knead. Before long, Tanqi suddenly heard a faint sound of snoring.

He had actually fallen asleep.

Tanqi thought about it: the young master had not closed his eyes in a full twenty-four double-hours.

***

Zhang Xiaojing came back to his senses out of the swimming dark to find himself bound to a hall-pillar, his hands lashed high above him. His nose still ached dully, crusted over with a smear of blood. Ma Ge’er came and stood before him, jiggling in his hand that waist-tally stamped “Jing’an ce ping,” with a tent still pitched in his trousers.

Ma Ge’er was in a foul temper just now; the boil on his garlic-bulb nose had swollen larger and larger, until grease was even beginning to seep from it.

On the orders of the noble Right Shad, he had carried these two girls off to this safe-house. His lordship the Right Shad had said only to keep them alive, and had charged him with nothing further, so Ma Ge’er had resolved to enjoy himself thoroughly. Ever since he had come from the steppe to Chang’an, he had kept his head down and swallowed his temper, and the desire pent up inside him had long been near to bursting. He was no cold fish like Cao Poyan: he craved blood, he craved slaughter, he craved a woman’s screams.

Ma Ge’er had it all planned: he would take both women, then keep Wang Zhongsi’s daughter and torture the other to death by the cruelest means he knew, to vent himself thoroughly, and so meet the coming of the Que-le Huo-duo in the fullest vigor. At the thought of that steppe-scourge Wang Zhongsi’s daughter moaning beneath him, Ma Ge’er’s member reared up stiff and high, beyond his mastering.

He had not reckoned that no sooner were his trousers off than an intruder should arrive, which put Ma Ge’er thoroughly out of sorts.

What put him more out of sorts still was that the intruder should carry a waist-tally. Ma Ge’er could not read a character, but from the heavy heft of the thing he knew well enough it was no common object.

Ma Ge’er would have liked nothing better than to make an end of him without a word and go back to his women. But he was a Wolf Guard born, after all, and could not help weighing another possibility: this fellow’s gear was too fine—the tally, the soft armor, the hand-crossbow, all of them high-grade—very likely he belonged to the Jingzhao Prefecture or the Jinwu Guard, or even to the army.

If he could track them to the door, others could too; this safe-house had become exceedingly unsafe.

This was a thing that had to be gotten to the bottom of.

“How did you know we were here?” Ma Ge’er asked in halting Chinese.

Zhang Xiaojing said nothing, only glared coldly at Ma Ge’er out of his single eye. Ma Ge’er found it deeply unpleasant; the look was exactly that of a lone wolf on the steppe. Whether snared in a trap or at the point of death, a lone wolf always watches men with just that cold, sullen stare.

Ma Ge’er gave a cold snort, took up Zhang Xiaojing’s barrier-knife, and with the point lightly pared a sliver of flesh from his throat, so that blood at once welled up like a spring: “Talk, and be quick, or you’ll taste worse yet.”

Zhang Xiaojing’s lips stirred. Ma Ge’er thought he was about to confess, but out came instead a question in return: “Where is the woman you seized?” Ma Ge’er’s brows jumped, and he drove a fist hard into Zhang’s belly, until he could not help retching in great heaves.

“It’s I who am asking the questions here!”

But Zhang Xiaojing already had his answer. A moment before, Ma Ge’er had glanced instinctively at the next room, which meant Wen Ran was in there. That scent of spirit-calming rue incense—he knew it well.

“How did you know we were here?”

Ma Ge’er asked it again, and seeing him still give no response, laid the blade against Zhang Xiaojing’s armpit. The cold touch of the iron made his skin shudder. Ma Ge’er grinned and, deliberately, pushed the edge in slowly, as if peeling a pear, shaving off flat a round patch of bloody skin and flesh from beneath the arm. As the blade lifted the flesh away, Zhang Xiaojing let out a scream he could not hold back.

In Türk country this was called casting flesh-coins, for the flesh spun off came out about the size of a copper cash. Cut from this part of the body, it would not kill, but the pain was extreme; a few flesh-coins cast, and a prisoner would confess to anything.

But Zhang Xiaojing, though his face went ghastly, still held his tongue, that hateful stare fixed on him all the while. All at once it struck Ma Ge’er: the man was playing for time! A large body of men might well be on the road already.

No—they must pull out at once!

Ma Ge’er went to the next room, where his men had already hauled the two women up. He swept his gaze over them, put out a finger, and pointed at Wen Ran: “Bring that one.”

“How can you tell which is Wang Zhongsi’s daughter?” one of his men asked, a little surprised.

Ma Ge’er ran a hand over Wen Ran’s tender neck, lifted it to his nose and drew a breath, and said with a leer: “I found out when I was holding her just now. A great official’s daughter—she smells sweeter. That one’s fragrant too, but the scent’s not so full as this one’s.”

His men all laughed, knowing this one had a strange craving for women and was therefore keenly alert to certain details. On the steppe scent was a rare thing, which only noble ladies could afford.

“And the other one?”

“Throw her next door and kill her along with that spy. We leave at once.” Ma Ge’er drew a hand across his own throat.

The door was shoved open once more with a bang. Zhang Xiaojing looked hard: a woman was being hustled and shoved in by the Türk Wolf Guards.

She was not Wen Ran—only much of a build with her, and dressed in the same foreign robe. But the twisted-silver kingfisher ornaments at her cheeks and the nanmu-wood pin in her coiled hair marked her out as no common birth; what ordinary woman could afford such costly adornments? This, surely, was the true daughter of Wang Zhongsi.

Zhang Xiaojing swiftly reasoned out the truth: the two of them must have been seized by the Türks in the same place, and these coarse Türks, no judges of fine ornaments, had put the cap on the wrong head and confused the two women’s identities.

The Türk Wolf Guard drew his sharp knife and went first for Wang Yunxiu. Her mouth was gagged and she could make no sound; she could only twist her body with all her might—and actually dodged the thrust aimed at her throat, so that the knife slashed her shoulder instead, blood spattering. The Türk, having missed and feeling he had lost face, reached out and dealt Wang Yunxiu a ringing slap to quiet her.

Before he could strike again, there came from outside the window a sudden flurry of beating wings, and several skylarks rose up from the courtyard. Ma Ge’er’s eyes went cold and wary; he signaled to hold off, and strode swiftly to the window to look down over the front court.

The thickets were shaking, footfalls came in a jumble—it seemed a great many people were closing in on the place.

Ma Ge’er turned at once and loudly called his men all inside. He had had seven men to start with; three Zhang Xiaojing had killed, one had taken a bolt in the leg, and only three were left who could still move. Ma Ge’er had no leisure to lament it; he gave a few swift orders in Turkic, and the three took their commands and went out, each his own way.

Ma Ge’er cast a glance at Zhang Xiaojing and Wang Yunxiu, then paid them no further heed and turned to leave as well. From the next room soon came Wen Ran’s panicked cries; it seemed they meant to carry off only this “Lady Wang.”

A bare few snaps of the fingers later, the door of the ground floor of the Zhuxin Pavilion was flung open with a crash, and a dozen-odd men came pouring in all at once. They rushed to the main room and pulled up short. There they saw a Wolf Guard, wounded in the thigh, leaning aslant against a great bronze eared censer, two hand-crossbows raised at the doorway and two more lying on the floor.

The Wolf Guard was no less astonished. He had thought the intruders would be Zhang Xiaojing’s confederates, or at the least soldiers of the imperial guard; but the men before him, each with a strip of flowered cloth slung crosswise and tattooed shoulders bared, were plainly the idle young rakes who drifted about the capital.

The two sides faced off for several breaths. One of the rakes lost his nerve, gave a great shout, and charged with a cudgel raised in his hand. The Wolf Guard, without a word, lifted his arm and shot—square in the youth’s forehead. The rest took fright and scrambled back, and three more bolts came, striking down three men one after another.

“He’s out of bolts!”

Someone or other shouted it, and the rakes charged in again. This time the Wolf Guard had no recourse but to lie flat on the ground and suffer their fists and kicks. These youths plainly had none of the Lüben Guards’ discipline; the moment they saw the Wolf Guard beaten down, they swarmed all in a rush into the main room—a good twenty and more of them.

Their leader, a petty headman, made a circuit of the lower floor, then pointed at the stairs and signed a few men up to the second floor. Word soon came from above: found! He hastened to mount the bamboo stairs, ran along the corridor, and saw in one of the second-floor rooms two people bound: a man lashed to a pillar, and a woman collapsed on the floor, seventeen or eighteen years of age.

The petty headman was delighted: in the whole building there was this one woman and no other, so this time there could be no mistake.

The Bear Fire Gang had abducted a woman today, only for her to escape midway. By the account of the young toughs who gave chase, she had been taken into this mansion by a band of foreigners of unknown origin. The Bear Fire Gang looked on the whole of Wannian County as its private preserve; with one of their own quarry snatched away on their own ground, how could they swallow such an affront? So this petty headman had gathered up a pack of good-for-nothing youths, meaning to seize the woman back.

The petty headman told four men to take the woman away and lose no time; as for the man, he did not know him and there was no need to bother with him.

He watched the escort party go, and his mood turned suddenly fine—this would be a landmark feat in his career with the Bear Fire Gang. The petty headman strolled up to the high kiosk on the second floor and gazed out a while. Off in the distance the brocade splendor of Qujiang lay spread before his eyes, the scenery delightful, and he could not help a rush of feeling: “The rich sure as hell know how to live!” Having enjoyed the view a while, he clasped his hands behind him and, aping the airs of a famous gentleman-scholar, sauntered slowly down.

As he went, the petty headman suddenly felt something odd underfoot. He looked down and found a thick yellowish-brown streamlet running down the stairs to the ground floor, its smell faintly acrid.

He crouched and wiped a finger through it, and judged it must be castor oil, which puzzled him greatly. Wasn’t the house supposed to be empty? How came this stuff here? The petty headman looked up and saw, hung at the four corners of the pavilion’s roof-frame, a good many clay jars, their mouths tilted, pouring oil down without cease; seven or eight turbid streams gathered on the ground floor into one great pool.

All at once his pupils shrank; he bolted for the stairs, shouting as he ran: “Quick! Quick, kill him!” Before the words were out his foot slipped, and he pitched down the stairs, riding the castor oil. The rakes had not heard the warning; instead they pointed at his sorry state and roared with laughter.

At that instant the wounded Wolf Guard, hemmed in and beaten, fished a fire-starter from his breast, blew it hard aglow, and flung it onto the oil. Oil and fire met, and with a whoosh it blazed up; the flames raced along the trails of oil and spread swiftly across the whole ground floor, like a mad dance of golden snakes.

The pavilion was of bamboo-and-wood; walls, gallery-posts, and stairs were kindled in a moment, and fire-mushrooms great and small poked up from the seams of the timber. That once elegant and secluded retreat became in a flash the burning house of the Buddhist scriptures.

The rakes stood aghast and scrambled to flee outdoors. But the men were many and the door was narrow, and in an instant they choked the doorway to bursting. The oil-fire came surging on and swallowed one after another those who had not got out in time, leaving only their shapes writhing in despair.

On the second floor Zhang Xiaojing felt a surge of heat rising under his feet, and hearing the howls of the damned, he knew the intruders had surely walked into the Wolf Guard’s trap.

Having chosen this place to light on, the Wolf Guards would naturally have made their preparations. This bamboo pavilion had been hung all about with jars of castor oil; the moment an irresistible enemy broke in, they would tip the jars over, wait their chance to set them alight, and then make a swift escape. Long Bo’s frequent visits earlier had been just this sort of preparation.

Zhang Xiaojing knew that if he stayed on like this he too would be burned alive. He had all this while been quietly working his wrists, and the ropes had loosened a good deal; a few more chafes and he could slip free. But just then the edge of the floor gave a shrill grinding screech, the whole pavilion shivered slightly, and then every joint of the whole building began to creak and groan.

Zhang Xiaojing swore inwardly. These Wolf Guards were ruthless indeed: not content with the castor oil, they had left several of the mortise joints and support beams between the ground floor and the second floor only loosely fitted. Once a great fire took hold, it would soon bring the whole pavilion crashing down, and those inside, if not burned to death, would be crushed.

His left hand was short a finger and could not undo the ropes at his wrist, so he could only arch his body with all his strength and, using the force of his hips, slam down hard against the floor. This bamboo-and-wood pavilion was built by the bridge-laid method: the second-floor boards were all bamboo planks fitted onto a wooden frame, not sturdy in themselves. Zhang Xiaojing made of himself a great hammer, striking blow on blow at its fragile supports; he had to bring the floor down before the pavilion collapsed entire, if he was to have a sliver of a chance to escape.

Under the ceaseless hammering of Zhang Xiaojing’s hips and the fire pressing up from below, the floor soon gave a wail: one end plunged suddenly down, and then with a roar the main span crashed aslant to the floor below, cutting a sloping slide through the fire.

Unluckily the pillar to which Zhang Xiaojing was bound had not broken; it stuck fast in the middle, holding his body suspended in midair. He struggled a few times, found it no use, and hastily shifted his posture so that the ropes at his wrists were aimed at the flames leaping up.

The rope was woven of Lingnan snake-vine, steeped in oil—tough and springy, but no match for fire. One lick of flame and it caught at once. Zhang Xiaojing bore the searing pain at his wrists, let the rope burn through, then wrenched—once, twice, and at the third it tore apart at last.

But he had no time to rejoice; at once he set foot on the sloping, not-yet-burning boards and ran forward, shielding his face with both elbows as he passed through several walls of fire, and rushed to a blazing window, where he flung himself out with all his might. The burning window-lattice was brittle, and Zhang Xiaojing smashed clean through it. The instant he landed he rolled several times to beat out the fire on his body.

In the next instant the main structure of the pavilion collapsed with a roar, sparks flying on every side, and the little pavilion became once and for all a blazing heap of firewood.

Zhang Xiaojing lay flat on the ground, gulping down great breaths. A good deal of his eyebrows and hair was singed away, both his wrists were burned, and there was a shocking long wound at his waist where a bamboo splinter of the window-frame had raked him as he leapt out.

Before long a confusion of footsteps sounded outside. Thinking there were still enemies, Zhang Xiaojing forced his neck up to take a look, and his shoulders eased despite himself.

Those pouring into the back court were a great body of Lüben Guards soldiers in brown armor—the Jing’an Bureau’s men had come after all. Seeing how fierce the blaze was, the Lüben Guards, without waiting for an officer’s order, spread out of their own accord and began to clear a firebreak around the Zhuxin Pavilion to keep it from spreading.

A burly figure came up before Zhang Xiaojing and helped him to his feet, saying he begged pardon for coming late—though there was not much warmth in it. Zhang Xiaojing looked hard: it was Cui Qi. He spared no thought for his own state, but urgently caught Cui Qi by the arm: “When you came into the house, did you see anyone else?”

Cui Qi, who put little faith in this Mister Zhang, only jerked his chin: “Only saw a few Bear Fire Gang layabouts!”

“The Bear Fire Gang?” At the name, a meaningful glint flashed across Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye.

Cui Qi stepped aside, and Zhang Xiaojing saw, along the courtyard gallery, several of the rakes who had been lucky enough to escape now crouched dejected on the ground, watched over by a few drawn sabers. They had likely just fled out and run headlong into the Lüben Guards.

Zhang Xiaojing barked: “Quick! Sound the Nine-Gate Drum! The Wolf Guards left only just now—they’re close by!”

At the two words “Wolf Guards,” a fierce light burst in Cui Qi’s eyes, and he at once rapped out a string of urgent orders to the aide beside him.

The Jing’an Bureau had a graded system of alarm. When the Nine-Gate Drum sounded on the watchtower, not only did the gates of the ward itself close, but the eight surrounding wards likewise shut their gates, and at the same time, at the sixteen crossroads among these nine wards, chevaux-de-frise and cross-bars were set up.

From the Bear Fire Gang’s breaking into the mansion to the arrival of the Lüben Guards was the space of no more than a single stick of incense. The Wolf Guards, still dragging a Wen Ran along as they withdrew, could not be moving fast. Once the Nine-Gate Drum sounded, a great net would lock down the nine wards fast, leaving them nowhere to hide—and if need be, other wards would sound the Nine-Gate Drum too, sealing the ground off ring by ring.

Cui Qi had much experience in this line; he ordered Xiuzheng Ward to sound the Nine-Gate Drum, and at the same time sent out four troops of Lüben cavalry to search forward in four directions. Only when all this was arranged did Cui Qi crouch down and bid his men fetch some wound-medicine and cloth to bind up Zhang Xiaojing.

“How came you to be here?” Zhang Xiaojing asked Cui Qi.

Yao Runeng slipped out from beside Cui Qi, cupping the wound-medicine in his hands, his face full of shame: “I saw you had gone in so long and not come out, so I ran to the watchtower and told General Cui to come to your rescue—I’m sorry, I didn’t dare go in to save you myself...”

His shame was heartfelt. Not long before he had been righteously questioning Zhang Xiaojing’s motives, and had even meant to kill him; and now Zhang Xiaojing had braved mortal danger alone and all but died of it, while he himself had hung back and watched a man face death without lifting a hand. In Yao Runeng’s own eyes he was nothing but a craven hypocrite.

“Your coming in alone would have mended nothing; calling up reinforcements in good time was the right course. Your judgment was quite sound—there’s no need to belittle yourself.” Zhang Xiaojing offered the assessment coolly, lifting his wrist meanwhile to let Yao dress it.

Cui Qi asked with a frown: “Mister Zhang, what in the world is all this?” His questions were many as a mountain: Türk Wolf Guards had plainly been lurking in the mansion—how had a mob of ruffians come storming in? Why had the two sides opened fire? And how had the Zhuxin Pavilion come to catch fire?

Zhang Xiaojing gave a brief account of what had befallen him: first slipping into the pavilion, then being coerced by the Türks with Wang Zhongsi’s daughter, falling into enemy hands, and then the Bear Fire Gang unaccountably fighting their way in... Cui Qi cut his account short, his face gone green: “You mean to say the military commissioner Wang’s daughter is in Türk hands?”

His voice shook as he spoke. Zhang Xiaojing was on the point of answering when a thought flashed suddenly through his mind.

The one the Türks had carried off was in fact Wen Ran; but if he told the truth, what then? When the Jing’an Bureau hunted the Türks down, it would care nothing whether Wen Ran lived or died.

But he cared about this girl—cared very much.

If in the whole of Chang’an there were but one person he could save, Zhang Xiaojing would surely choose Wen Ran.

In an instant he had made his decision.

Zhang Xiaojing slowly raised his hand, not a tremor in his voice: “That’s right. I saw with my own eyes the Türk Wolf Guards carry her off.”

Cui Qi stood rooted in despair, the world spinning round him.

He had been no more than a soldier of the Long Mountains, who by a little battle-merit and the efforts of his elder brother Cui Liulang had at last won a posting in Chang’an. Before he had grasped so much as a taste of wealth and honor, blow upon heavy blow had fallen on him: first his brother killed, then he himself letting a key Türk figure slip away, and now the kidnapping of a great court minister’s kin dragged in as well.

Cui Qi knew the court’s ways too well. A calamity this size, and the court would be sure to push forward some responsible party to take the punishment. Li Bi’s backing was too strong; Zhang Xiaojing was a condemned man to begin with; which left him, in charge of the operation, as the perfect scapegoat-stuff.

What he had now to mind was no longer how to win merit and make his name, nor how to avenge his brother, but how to keep his own one life.

Zhang Xiaojing gave him a push: “Commander Cui, they’re all waiting on your order.” Cui Qi woke as from a dream, sprang up, and bawled at his men in furious agitation: “What are you standing there like fools for? Stop fighting the fire—go and take the men, now!” Zhang Xiaojing added: “Notify the watchtower; have the Jing’an Bureau send men to the military commissioner Wang’s house to confirm the situation!”

“Yes! Yes! Quick, to the military commissioner Wang’s house to make sure!” Cui Qi had lost all composure, and did whatever Zhang Xiaojing said.

“And... question these men, find out just where they come from.” Zhang Xiaojing turned his gaze on the rakes. In truth he already knew well enough who they were. Wannian County had only so many gangs; they were easy to tell apart—but some things were better left for others to ask.

As it happened, Cui Qi had a bellyful of foul temper with no outlet; he strode fiercely up to the few captured rakes and swung the scabbard of his saber down full on a youth’s head, so that the boy clutched it and toppled to the ground. Not content with that, Cui Qi struck him several more savage blows, until he had beaten him to a bloody pulp before he stopped. The other youths were so terrified they wet their trousers, and without needing to be asked, poured out everything at once like beans from a tipped tube.

It turned out they did not even rank as Bear Fire Gang proper, but were only fringe hangers-on, come along with a petty headman. That headman had heard that a woman some boss had his eye on had run off and was hiding in this derelict house here, and so had come to seize her.

Cui Qi pressed them: who was the woman? One youth said her surname was Wen, the daughter of the owner of the Wen incense shop in Dunyi Ward. Cui Qi raged: “Who asked about her! I mean the other woman—is she the military commissioner Wang’s daughter or not?” The youths gaped, understanding nothing, and could give no answer. Cui Qi laid about him with the scabbard, thrashing them nearly to death, and still got nothing out of them.

Only when a soldier came running up to report on the sealing of the roads did Cui Qi drop them and hurry off in a fever of anxiety to see to the dispositions.

Zhang Xiaojing half-reclined in the gallery and let Yao Runeng see to his wounds. He was hurt badly: the Wolf Guard had shaved a great patch of skin from his armpit, and his wrists and back were burned besides. Yao Runeng carefully washed the wounds first with well-water, then dressed them with golden-wound powder to staunch the blood, and wound them round and round with silk. The fellow’s fingers were long and his touch practiced and fine, no less deft than an embroidering-girl’s.

His body had suffered such cruel torment, and yet he had held out until the relief arrived—hard as iron indeed. Binding the wounds, Yao Runeng thought privately that in Zhang’s place he might not have borne it. Zhang Xiaojing let him tend it, but his eyes stayed fixed on the world beyond the mansion. In his single eye was a worry buried very deep.

This iron-hearted, base-natured fellow—could he actually be worrying over someone else? Yao Runeng thought to himself.

Yao Runeng suddenly noticed that his left hand was one finger short, wrapped in a strip of hemp cloth half-soaked through with blood. He marveled: was this the Türk Wolf Guards’ doing? No—it had been so before that. Casting his mind back, he was certain that before he himself was knocked out, Zhang Xiaojing’s hands had still been whole.

In other words, the wound of the severed finger had come about when Zhang Xiaojing killed the informer. At the thought of his betraying the informer, Yao Runeng’s anger came surging up again. Not without malice, he wondered: could it be that Old Ge had cut the finger off?

“It’s a mark,” Zhang Xiaojing said suddenly, his voice somewhat hoarse.

“What?”

Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye still gazed outward; he seemed less to be explaining to Yao Runeng than to be speaking for some listener in the unseen dark:

“Xiao Yi was the last informer I raised in my time in office in Wannian County. He was of humble birth, but very clever. I still remember: the day before he went to be an informer, the county paid out a bounty. His old mother hid the money away and would not let him fritter it, saying it would be for a wife by and by. Yet Xiao Yi, at the risk of a beating from his mother, secretly dug out half a string of cash and bought me a fine mugwort-tinder flint. He told me, ‘Chief Zhang’s flint is too old, it won’t strike a light—it’s time for a new one.’ And he said that so long as Chief Zhang could still strike a light, he would never lose his way.”

“And yet today you killed him with your own hand,” Yao Runeng returned coldly.

“Let me ask you this: suppose you were aboard a wooden boat, full of travelers, in a raging storm, and one innocent man had to be killed to appease the river-god, or the whole boatload would go down. Would you kill?” Zhang Xiaojing asked abruptly.

Yao Runeng was taken aback, and could not help knitting his brows, caught in the horns of it. The question was cunning in the extreme: to kill the innocent was of course against all humanity, yet to sit and watch a whole boat capsize would likely mean more deaths still. The more he thought, the more his head ached, and for a moment he fell silent.

“Kill one to save a hundred—will you kill, or won’t you?” Zhang Xiaojing pressed.

Yao Runeng retorted, a little flustered: “And how would you choose?” He thought it a truly sly turn of argument.

“Kill.” Zhang Xiaojing said it without the least hesitation, then shifted his tone at once: “It is a thing that ought to be done, but it is a wrong. Because it ought to be done, I did it; even were I to live it over again, I would do the same—but wrong is, in the end, wrong.” At this he lifted the stump of his finger a little. “...And so I cut off a finger of my own. It is the mark of my debt to Xiao Yi. When this business here is done, I will bear the responsibility myself and pay off this blood-guilt.”

Zhang Xiaojing closed his single eye, as if in mourning. A few more creases had come into his face, which made it look the more weathered and bitter.

Yao Runeng kept silent. He found he could not see through this proud, unbending fellow at all. One moment he was like a cold-blooded killer, the next like a brave man full of loving-kindness, the next like a knight-errant whose word was his bond. So many contradictory traits, gathered in one man. It came to Yao Runeng all at once that he had never once wondered what charge it was that had put Zhang Xiaojing in prison.

Zhang Xiaojing slowly opened his eyes: “You’ve been in Chang’an three months now, as I recall?”

Yao Runeng did not see why he had turned the talk this way so suddenly, and could only nod.

Zhang Xiaojing gave a smile that was not quite a smile: “Stay a while longer and you’ll understand. To be an officer of the catch-thief trade in Chang’an is to face just this sort of choice almost every day. What is the wrong thing that ought to be done, and what the right thing that ought not to be? Whether or not to hold to the way of the gentleman—you had best think it through early, or else...”

“Or else?”

“In Chang’an, if you do not become a monster like it, it will devour you.”

With a crack, the pot of salve in Yao Runeng’s hand overturned to the ground, and the dark-brown liquid spilled across the white silk in a spreading stain.

Dum, dum, dum—dum; dum, dum, dum—dum: a rhythmic pounding spread through the whole southeastern corner of Chang’an. It was the Nine-Gate Drum, from Xiuzheng Ward. By the statutes of the Great Tang, the moment the drum began, the street-post constables were to seal off the road-junctions of the eight nearby wards at once.

But today was the Lantern Festival, and everyone’s heart was full of thoughts of pleasure; the constables on duty, too, were bound to be a little slack. Hearing the drum, they were slow to respond, and only after a good while did they go about rousing their colleagues who were sleeping in or playing at double-sixes, their movements rather sluggish.

Luckily Cui Qi had never counted on such fools; he had gone to the trouble of sending a dozen-odd Lüben Guards soldiers with warrant-tallies, each straight to one of the street-posts, to drive them to act with all speed. To make doubly sure, Cui Qi had also flung out five or six troops of crack cavalry to patrol the outer streets back and forth. Even should the Türks slip through the cordon by luck, they would run headlong into this moving wall.

In a moment the nine wards were in an uproar. The constables scrambled to haul out the chevaux-de-frise and thorn-hedges and set up inspection posts at the junctions; the crack cavalry galloped, and countless hawk-and-falcon glances swept again and again over every corner of both sides of the roads. Passers-by stopped in surprise, not knowing what had happened nearby; they could still go on their way, only they were searched at every junction they passed.

A great net settled slowly over the ring around Xiuzheng Ward. And yet Ma Ge’er and his party had vanished without a trace, as though they had ascended bodily to heaven on the spot. Reports came in from every quarter, all with the same content: “Not seen.”

Cui Qi roared at the message-runners: “Impossible! Are they birds? Even birds couldn’t slip past the watchtowers’ eyes!”

Whether Ma Ge’er and the rest went by horse, by cart, or on foot, in so short a time they could not have fled more than two li—the outermost warning-range of the Nine-Gate Drum. Their whereabouts, then, admitted of only two possibilities: one, that they had bought off the checkpoint soldiers and got clean out; two, that they had gone to ground somewhere close by, in one of the wards near Xiuzheng.

Whichever it was, it would develop into an exceedingly awkward situation.

Just at this moment word came from the Wang residence: the military commissioner Wang’s daughter, Wang Yunxiu, had got herself a new Xi cart and gone out alone to try her hand at driving it, and had not yet returned. At the same time, the Jing’an Bureau headquarters relayed another report: near Jingshan Ward there had been a cart-crash, a firewood cart and a Xi cart colliding, but at the scene they had found only the driver and a dozen-odd corpses of constables.

This was surely the Türk Wolf Guards’ doing; only they were so viciously cruel.

When Cui Qi heard the report confirmed, his stomach felt as though a giant hand had seized and wrung it, sick enough to make him retch. Wang Zhongsi was a great minister of the court; if today’s affair went wrong, it would be an earth-shaking calamity.

At his wits’ end, Cui Qi could only go up to Zhang Xiaojing, who was just making ready to set out, and cup his hands: “Commander Zhang, the Türk Wolf Guards have vanished. What’s to be done now?”

Had there been the least alternative, Cui Qi would not have cared to show weakness before this condemned man; but just now he had no other choice. This fellow, single-handed and alone, in less than two double-hours had dug out the Türks’ tail—no ordinary man could have done as much. It came to Cui Qi that only if Zhang Xiaojing worked some prodigy and took the Türk Wolf Guards could he himself escape this great catastrophe—and so even “Mister Zhang” had become “Commander Zhang.”

Zhang Xiaojing saw clean through his mind but had no wish to say so, and gave a snap of his fingers: “Up to the watchtower first.”

The two of them climbed clattering up the watchtower of Xiuzheng Ward and looked out on every side; the sights of the eight surrounding wards were gathered up at a glance. The roads outside the wards ran crisscross, the grey-tiled roofs and high beams within them plain to see, clear as looking at the sand-table. At every junction was massed a dark press of people—the checkpoints doing their work. With good eyes, one could even make out the passers-by’s clothing.

Under so close a watch, the Türks could not have vanished into thin air without a sound.

Cui Qi glared about him wide-eyed, anxious and uneasy, finding everyone he saw suspicious. Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his single eye and slowly swept his gaze around, then halted it on one direction. He raised his arm and pointed southeast: “Qujiang Pool.”

Cui Qi did not understand at first, but following Zhang Xiaojing’s finger he saw, and all at once the light broke on him.

At the southeast corner of Xiuzheng Ward lay Chang’an’s most flourishing scenic spot—Qujiang Pool. Half of the pool lay within the city, taking up the ground of two wards; the other half lay outside, running up against the Shaoling Plain. Within Qujiang Pool the channels wound and meandered, towers and halls stood in ranks, flowers ringed it round and willow-shade closed overhead, and little paths threaded in and out among the gardens: in every season of the year it was a superb place to be—whether for a sightseer or for a fugitive.

Qujiang Pool had its own Shangchi Office to manage it, not under the authority of the wards and their street-posts, and the Nine-Gate Drum had no power to command them. Very likely the Türk Wolf Guards had exploited just this gap: on leaving Xiuzheng Ward, they had gone straight over the roadside railings and slipped into the maze-like gardens of Qujiang Pool.

Chang’an was laid out in straight lines and right angles, but here at the southeast corner Qujiang Pool bulged out beyond them, like a sack of rice with one corner poking up. To keep this stretch of water spanning the city’s inside and outside from being cut in two, no city wall had been ringed about the outer part; only several water-channels had been dug around it. Carts and horses could not pass, but for two or three travelers on foot, getting out of the city was no hard matter.

Seen thus, the Türks’ original choice of Xiuzheng Ward as their lodging had been calculated with great cunning.

Cui Qi said: “You mean they’ve likely gone out of the city through Qujiang?” Inwardly he let out a long breath—this need not be a bad thing. Once out of the city, the Jing’an Bureau need not tie its own hands; it could send crack cavalry to sweep back and forth. The ground around Chang’an was flat and open, with nowhere to hide; to take a few Türks on foot would be mere patient drudgery.

But Zhang Xiaojing’s brows did not unknot for all that. Staring at the misty, boundless face of the Qujiang water, he felt the thing was not so simple. If the Türks meant harm to Chang’an, why run outside the city? Was their aim abduction, or the burning of the city? Zhang Xiaojing spread out the ward-map of Chang’an, crouched, and studied it closely, feeling that these actions contradicted one another and were full of doubtful points.

But Cui Qi was already impatient, and had set to signaling by flag from the watchtower, reporting to the Jing’an Bureau away in Guangde Ward and calling for more men to be sent out of the city in pursuit. When the report reached Li Bi, however, he did not hurry to move the Lüben Guards; his gaze went to the sand-table, and he sank into the same perplexity as Zhang Xiaojing.

The wolf-cubs of the steppe had set them a great riddle indeed.

Cui Qi grew a little anxious; he could not quite fathom why, over a thing so plain, not only Commander Zhang but even Deputy Director Li on his side should be so slow to give the order. For here, every snap of the fingers they dallied, the enemy drew a little further from Chang’an.

The whole cordon had come to a sudden standstill. Cui Qi looked now at the brooding Zhang Xiaojing, now off at the watchtowers nearby, his fingers fretting restlessly at the raised bronze band on his scabbard, reckoning that if no order came he might as well fling a few cavalry troops out first on his own.

But Cui Qi was a soldier after all, and this business of acting first and reporting after did not sit well with him. He was still wavering when Zhang Xiaojing suddenly stood up, shook the map in his hand, his eyes ablaze—and at that very moment the signal-flags on the watchtower waved as well.

The order Li Bi sent and the words that came from Zhang Xiaojing’s mouth were exactly the same:

“This is a feint. The thieves went out by Qujiang; they will come back in by the nearest city gate!”

The city gates nearest Qujiang were the Qixia Gate to the south and the Yanxing Gate to the east, no more than a li away. Slipping out at the southeast corner, the Türk Wolf Guards could re-enter the city bold as brass through these two gates. Out and in again, easily done, and they would leap clear of the Nine-Gate cordon and roam at will.

Cui Qi’s forehead beaded with the sweat of relief. Lucky they had not gone out of the city, or it would have been a case of driving south to reach the north. He hurriedly sent warning by watchtower to the two gates, at the same time lifting the nine-ward blockade on the spot and closing on the two gates with all speed.

But before that, the Jing’an Bureau had wasted too much time deploying in Xiuzheng Ward; the sudden shift was all confusion, and the execution very slow.

The Qixia and Yanxing Gates were vital passages by which the folk of the eastern suburbs came in to view the lanterns, and this was just the peak of the crush. By the time word came back from the two gates, the wily Türks had long since mingled into the great crowd of common people and entered Chang’an once more, gone without a trace. They were a step too late.

The thread was broken off just so, and yet time slid mercilessly by, quarter-hour on quarter-hour.

Cui Qi first hurriedly wrote out a secret dispatch and had it sent by fast horse to the Jing’an Bureau—the matter was too grave to dare conceal the least part of it. Then he looked to Zhang Xiaojing: “Commander Zhang, what do we do?” Without his even noticing it, his manner of addressing Zhang Xiaojing had grown ever more humble, close to pleading.

“Wait a moment.” Zhang Xiaojing lay half-sprawled on the ground, his body tilted forward, the wings of his nose twitching faintly, like a hound.

Cui Qi could not make out what medicine he was selling in that gourd of his, and dared not press him; he could only wait beside him in dread, his breathing heavy.

It was laughable, really. In his days in the Long Mountains, Cui Qi had licked blood from the blade’s edge, gallant and glad in it, never a care in the face of life or death; the soft living of Chang’an had not washed away his fighting strength, but it had corroded his nerve. When a man possesses too much, he can never again hold life and death lightly. Cui Qi realized all at once, to his shame, that all his loud clamor about avenging his brother had been only to cover his own dread of being found guilty.

Was his own future to rest, then, on the shoulders of a condemned man like this? Cui Qi thought, unreconciled.

Zhang Xiaojing suddenly raised his head and asked an unrelated question: “Do you have anyone you know over at the Xuanhui Court?”

Cui Qi was taken aback. The Xuanhui Court belonged to the palace establishment and had nothing whatever to do with the city’s defense; why did Zhang Xiaojing bring it up all of a sudden? Zhang Xiaojing said: “If I remember rightly, the Xuanhui Court has under it the Five Kennels, which rear the eagles, gyrfalcons, hawks, sparrowhawks, and hounds for the Son of Heaven. If we could borrow a few keen-nosed beasts from the Dog Kennel, there might yet be hope for this.”

He raised his hand, took up a fistful of dust and held it to his nose, and drew a deep breath.

The Wen incense shop’s blended incense was of superb quality, lingering for several double-hours without fading, and famed throughout the western capital.
