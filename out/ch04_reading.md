## Chapter 4. War upon War

When he had walked the ground once over, Goemon went back to the inn.

The people were cowed by the unbroken run of war. Tomorrow was the New Year, and there was no life in them at all.

"Come spring, Lord Shibata won't stay quietly holed up."

"Aye. And once Lord Shibata rouses himself out of the north, Lord Mori too, who has patched up his peace with Lord Hashiba for now, is like to bestir himself."

"The lord of Hamamatsu keeps on good terms with Lord Hashiba these days. But there's the question, eh: which side'll he throw in with? A black-hearted one, they say."

Goemon fell in with the fireside whispering and dropped a lazy word of agreement here and there. All the while his heart had run on ahead to Hamamatsu.

Ah, how was Maki faring? Even the New Year rice-cakes would go down poorly, eaten alone.

Nine years had passed since the two of them were joined; how quickly it had gone. Maki, a girl of sixteen then, was a woman of twenty-five now, in her prime.

Since he had come to know Maki, Goemon had lost all interest in any other woman. In all the wide world only the two of them were "his own"; everyone else was one of the "others."

Half-asleep, he lay thinking of her, his pillow in the row beside the pillow-sellers and the eboshi-makers and the journeyman carpenters, and slept.

When he woke it was the first day of the first month of Tenshō 11, the year 1583.

The sky was the same leaden grey, snow ready to fall at any moment. Ducking under a token shimenawa, he went out onto the highroad and gazed off toward Takara-dera castle.

The place was hushed and still; there seemed to be no New Year's rites, nothing at all. Strange, he thought, and then, come afternoon, all at once the people stirred and buzzed, and the air went taut with the sense of something about to break.

Goemon paid his reckoning in haste, shouldered his pilgrim's vest, and stepped out to the front.

Right before his eyes, from the castle gate atop a slope ninety meters high, a lone mounted general burst out and shot away westward like an arrow. Goemon fixed his gaze: no mistaking it, Hideyoshi. Hard behind, not to be outrun, came the corps of pages.

Goemon broke off down a side lane and followed, over field and through wood and along the highroad, close but never too close.

Over Takatsuki, past Ibaraki, through Itami, and before long, off to the left beyond a pine wood, the sea came into view. The sky was leaden, snow on the point of flurrying down, and the wind cut at his cheeks. Straight through it he pressed. At Akashi he made a short halt, only long enough to water the horses, and then, as the day sank into full dark, at last galloped in through the great gate of Himeji castle.

"Twenty-odd ri at a single dash. Damn the man, the bald rat. No sooner's he arrived than he knocks the wits clean out of you."

So Goemon muttered.

Behind him Hideyoshi's retainers came straggling in without a break, one after another. Never dreaming that Goemon lay hidden in the shadow of a great pine, a band of generals passed in front of him, talking under their breath.

"I'd thought this year might come out a little better, and then—"

"Reckonings and loincloths both slip loose from the far end."

"The way things look, this year too will slip loose from the far end."

"Kuwabara, kuwabara."

"At this rate it's shaping up to be another cruelly busy year."

When the second of the month came, out of the castle were carried great stores of sake-casks and delicacies and silver, delivered round to each general's quarters with word to rest and take their ease; and so the whole company came at last into its New Year, and till deep in the night the singing and the revelry never died from any house.

Goemon, though, slipped into the ceiling above Hideyoshi's private room inside the castle. Looking down, he watched Hideyoshi, three secretaries set close at hand, counting out one by one the horses and swords and short robes to be given to some eight hundred and sixty retainers, weighing each against the man's service and having it written down. It was fearfully painstaking work. Begun on the morning of the second, it ran without a break and was not finished until past midnight.

"Bring my morning rice," said Hideyoshi, stretching.

"Sir?" said the startled page.

"Tomorrow I sleep straight through."

And so, having eaten, the moment he withdrew to his bedchamber he fell to snoring, snores so huge you wondered where in that small body they were made, and dropped fast asleep.

Everything that came out of the holes of this man's body, at any rate, was flamboyant. All day, while he worked, he sneezed sneezes that seemed to tear the air of the whole room. He broke wind ceaselessly, in a thousand different notes, and blew his nose in a blast fit to split your ears. He would run to the privy, let go a stream loud as a waterfall, and come running back.

The third he slept clean through. About noon on the fourth he finally hauled himself up and gave out that today and tomorrow he would receive his retainers' New Year greetings. The men had waited and chafed, and now they crowded into the castle each ahead of the last; the audiences before him ran on without a pause. No sooner had the retainers thinned than the next day brought the daimyo of the near provinces, the castle-lords, the monks of temple and shrine, the shrine-servants, all pressing in one behind another. Up in the ceiling Goemon was worn to the bone, and Hideyoshi had not slackened for an instant.

Such was Hideyoshi's New Year.

That year held two first months. On the evening of the fifth of the intercalary first month he mustered his retinue, and early on the morning of the sixth he went up to the palace, his New Year gifts made ready for the imperial house, the court, and the nobles. He paid his respects to the Regent and to all the rest, named Asano Nagamasa Shoshidai of Kyoto and granted him the two castles of Ōtsu and Sakamoto, and on the eighth left Kyoto for Ōtsu, crossed the lake by boat to Azuchi, and there inquired after the well-being of Sanbōshi.

This journey to Azuchi was no mere New Year call on Sanbōshi. There was a larger reason. From here Hideyoshi meant to send a summons to arms out to the generals of the whole realm and, before Katsuie could take the field, to make an end of Takigawa Sakon-shōgen Kazumasu of the Shibata party.

That is: on the tenth of the second month, with a whole army of seventy-five thousand, he set out to subdue Ise. Takigawa held Kuwana, Nagashima, and Kameyama in Ise, a daimyo of some three hundred thousand koku.

Goemon, of course, followed hidden.

The war was Hideyoshi's without effort. Kameyama castle and Kokufu castle fell one after the other; and while he was pressing the attack on the two castles of Seki and Mine as well, urgent word came in that Katsuie had begun to move.

The hour of the decisive battle between the two rivals had come at last. It made no difference to Goemon which of them won, and yet at the news a shiver ran through him.

That winter the north lay especially deep in snow. It was no state for a great army to take the field in, but Katsuie could bear the waiting no longer. On the twenty-eighth of the second month he set out, putting a host of laborers to clearing the snow along the road, forcing through wherever a bare passage opened.

Look at the old wooden image of him in the Saiko-ji at Fukui: a long, high-cheekboned face, and fearful great eyes, glaring, slanting up at the outer corners like an inverted figure of eight, the very face of Enma, the king of hell. Through the Toyotomi and Tokugawa ages Katsuie was fixed in the villain's part, so a likeness made in those ages would have given him a face crueler than the truth. But look at his life, and he is a thoroughbred warrior of the Warring States. Set against a born strategist like Hideyoshi, he is made to seem a man who cannot master his own feelings, all blind headlong charge.

And yet Katsuie too was a creature of that age of ceaseless scheming, a strategist there was no handling by ordinary means, a man you could neither boil nor roast. But against Hideyoshi, the genius of the art, he was outmatched at every turn, and in the end he can only have brought himself down.

Katsuie was sixty that year. Long hardship had whitened more than half his hair, and even his still-sturdy body felt the weight of its armor now. On the frozen mountain tracks his horse kept losing its footing, and each time he had to cling to the reins.

The Shibata vanguard, under Sakuma Morimasa, his younger brother Yasuda Yasumasa, and Shibata Katsumasa, numbered eight thousand five hundred. It was the same road down which, ten years before, Asakura Yoshikage, the man with the blue veins standing out on his brow like snakes, had marched to give Nobunaga battle.

Shaking off his unease, Katsuie pressed on, his golden gohei standard kept always at his side. His heart had known many risings and fallings, and it could not but pound now: at last, against Hideyoshi, the rival of all these years, he would settle which of them was the man.

Small wonder. After Yoshikage fell at Ichijodani, Nobunaga had granted Katsuie the four hundred ninety thousand koku of Echizen, and he had built himself a castle at Kita-no-shō, two ri north of Ichijodani, the present Fukui city, and lived there. Only that time it had been summer, a scorching blaze of heat; now, though it was the third month, a cold wind howled.

On the fifth of the third month Katsuie's van came down out of Yanagase into northern Ōmi and put Kinomoto and Takatsuki to the torch. His main body, twenty thousand under Maeda Toshiie and the rest, entered Ōmi on the ninth, pitched camp on Mount Uchinakao, threw up five forts along its southern face, disposed the companies among them, and settled in for a long war of attrition.

The reports came in an unbroken stream to Hideyoshi at Nagahama.

For the first time Goemon saw the scouts they called kamari. They were no ninja, but professionals of reconnaissance and espionage, who disguised themselves a dozen ways and came and went with quickness. Hideyoshi kept a great many of them.

War, day in and day out: the Sengoku commanders were veterans of it, and they had learned to the bone that a man who was not a thoroughgoing realist could never win one. They put no faith, as Japan's career officers would in the days of the Pacific War, in the aid of Heaven and the gods. They did not let feeling run away with them. They acted always on hard investigation. To march under the banner of Hachiman Daibosatsu was a way to cow the enemy; to visit a shrine on setting out and let it be known that a white dove had risen as a marvelous omen was a trick to hearten their own men. All of it they carried out by cold calculation. If things went ill, they counted it no shame at all to turn their backs and run. They knew the saying well: to lose is to win. They would glower at one another for years, then draw off by mutual consent, or break off and pull back an army that was winning. They fought by the coolest reason.

And of them all Hideyoshi was the realist. Foreseeing that the reckoning with Shibata would fall here in northern Ōmi, he had come out in person in the twelfth month of the year before to survey the ground around Yanagase; and again, in the middle of this year's intercalary first month, he had gone back, raised two ramparts on Mount Shinmei, and given them into the keeping of Ōgane Tōhachirō and Yamaji Masakuni, retainers of Shibata Katsutoyo, and set Niwa Nagahide to watch the front.

From Nagahama Hideyoshi had gone first to Kinomoto, four ri or so north along the eastern shore of Lake Biwa. Right in front of that little post-town the ground climbed steeply: the Shizugatake range crouched there, barring the northward road. With his close attendants Katō, Fukushima, Katagiri and the rest, and six kamari, he crossed the Yogo river and from there went up on foot, along a narrow track between the mulberry fields toward the hills. Snow still lay in the folds and hollows; everything else was black under conifers. The place swarmed with pit-vipers and rat snakes, so the kamari took the lead, thrashing the bamboo-grass about them with switches of bamboo, and when one turned up they would pinch it by the scruff between two fingers and fling it away.

Goemon had grown up in Iga, where snakes are many, and did not spare these a glance; if one got in his way he took it by the neck and pitched it off.

Climbing to the top of the Shizugatake switchbacks, four hundred and twenty meters up, Hideyoshi looked out. Behind him lay Lake Biwa; before him was a small lake he had never seen, two ri or so around. The mountains ringing it left no level margin at their feet, and here and there dropped in sheer cliffs to the water: a black, marshy little lake, as though someone had set it down and forgotten it. This was Lake Yogo. Hidden in the crown of a black pine, Goemon watched. Talking with his attendants, Hideyoshi was setting the kamari to draw a map.

Hideyoshi looked out north, toward Yanagase. Soon, out of that narrow pass, the great army of the north would come welling down. If they wanted the decisive battle, it would surely be fought here, around Lake Yogo. If they came from here, then so; if from there, then so. In his head the phantom of the coming fight already seemed to be drawing itself, move upon move.

His was a fulfilled face. It found the whole worth of living in beating an enemy, threw its every energy in, lost itself and sank body and soul into each battle in its turn. The sight of it made Goemon's chest tighten with a dull ache.

I have Maki now, he thought, and nothing in me wants anything beyond living easy, the two of us together. And somehow that seemed a hollow thing, a paltry thing. Win your battles, close your fist on the realm, and what comes of it? he sneered, and still could do nothing against it: this little man overwhelmed him, and in the end drew him in.

And so now Goemon, got up as a peasant, took to slipping in and out of the very battlefield he had walked three months before at Hideyoshi's heels. It struck him as a strange thing that, before he knew it, he had come to care about this war, and in his heart to wish Hideyoshi the win.

On the seventeenth of the third month Hideyoshi entered Ōmi, pushed up from Shimo-Yogo to near Yanagase, and opened fire; the Shibata army did not answer. So he climbed Mount Fumuro, two kilometers northwest of Lake Yogo, took in the enemy lines from there, and, seeing them settled into a stance for a long siege, raised ramparts of his own along the line of Mount Mano, Mount Doki, Mount Shinmei, and Gongen Slope, and stood facing them. All through this country the hills ran deep, the roads were narrow, valley gave onto valley, so that neither army could easily bring itself to the resolve of a pitched battle.

Only the spies passed thickly to and fro. Goemon mingled among them and ranged over the camps of both sides alike. In ten days and more he had reckoned out their strength: Hideyoshi's army some sixty thousand, Shibata's some thirty thousand. Up to the first half of the year before, Shibata had been the stronger; but in the forces the two had now thrown into this all-or-nothing battle, the case was reversed. Hideyoshi himself had drawn back from the front and lay at Nagahama castle, on the eastern shore of Lake Biwa.

Then, with the fourth month, came an incident that shook the whole army.

Mount Shinmei, at the center of Hideyoshi's line and due north of Lake Yogo, was held by five hundred men under three officers of Shibata Katsutoyo, lord of Nagahama castle: Ōgane Tōhachirō, Kimura Shigekore, and Yamaji Masakuni. And of the three, Yamaji, on the thirteenth of the fourth month, turned traitor without warning.

The ordinary kamari had not seen it coming. Goemon, as one might expect, had. Twice and three times he had watched a man slip secretly in and out of the keep; and one who had the look of a Shibata ninja came knocking at the gate of Kimura Shigekore's quarters within it.

On the night of the twelfth Goemon lay hidden in the grass beside the keep of the Shinmei fort. At the hour of the Rat, midnight, a man came to the gate. "Who goes there?" called the guard. "Urgent business from the field headquarters. Open the gate." The guard withdrew, and after a while came back with a single warrior.

"I am Osaki Uemon-no-jō. I am bid hear the substance of your business."

"In truth my business is not from the headquarters. I am Nomura Katsujirō, a retainer of the Iga-no-kami, Shibata Katsutoyo."

At that Osaki went back in once more and returned with a dozen attendants, took Nomura's long sword and short, closed round him, and led him inside. Goemon, by the art of ten'ton, slid in smoothly with the rest.

In the keep Nomura edged close to Kimura.

"Yamaji Masakuni has made up his mind to turn his coat. Katsuie has promised him Maruoka castle and the hundred and twenty thousand koku around it."

Kimura went pale with a start.

Shibata Katsutoyo was Katsuie's adopted son. When Nagahama passed from Hideyoshi to Katsuie, Katsutoyo had been set there as lord; but he resented how Katsuie doted on his nephew Sakuma Morimasa and slighted him, and in the twelfth month of the year before he had gone over to Hideyoshi. So this was no more than a retainer of what had once been Katsuie's own camp seeking to creep back to the old nest. Which made the rumor of Yamaji's treachery ring all the truer.

Nomura dropped his voice lower still.

"Tomorrow morning he will invite you to tea, close round you, cut you down, and let Katsuie's army into this fort. I too was pressed to take a hand; so I answered that I would, and have come to tell you of it."

Kimura looked hard into Nomura's face. It was square, flat as a round fan, ringed with side-whiskers, the round acorn eyes flung wide: no man, surely, with the wit to work a double cross inside a double cross. So he settled on believing the tale.

Next morning, sure enough, a messenger came with the invitation to tea, and Kimura begged off, saying a sudden gripe had seized his belly.

Yamaji guessed the thing was out. In haste he sent word and had his mother, his wife, and his child, seven of them in all, put aboard a boat from the castle town of Nagahama and rowed away. Then he abandoned them, fired the fort, and fled with nothing but himself to Katsuie's camp. Kimura, who had made ready, seized the boat at once and took the seven.

Goemon saw them crucified on the left wing of Katsuie's front line, at the foot of Mount Nakatani, on the dry bed of the Anegawa.

Five children, the eldest a girl of twelve, were bound to the crosses on the lakeshore in the lukewarm spring wind, screaming and wailing, and run through with spears before their mother's eyes. It tore at the heart to see.

The mothers, the wives, the children of the age of war were only ever these pitiful things, handed over as hostages, made to stand in another's place, offered up as warnings to the rest.

The eldest girl, twelve years old, had taken up her mother's cry and passed it down to the little ones below her: "You are a samurai's children! Do not weep! Do not weep! Do not weep!" But when the long spear came level at last with her own eyes, she loosed a scream, "Kyaaa!", and fought against the ropes.

Goemon could not keep from stopping his ears and covering his eyes.

And the grandmother, the mother, watching it: what must have been in their hearts?

That his own turn would one day come round to just such a pass, Goemon of course did not yet know.

He knew only that he hated, past all bearing, this Yamaji Masakuni, the man who had run such a risk to snatch at the bait of Maruoka castle and its hundred and twenty thousand koku. Goemon meant to look on the man's face and spy out what he did, and he set off at a run for the camp of Sakuma Genba-no-jō Morimasa, the veteran Shibata commander into whose lines Yamaji would have fled.

Morimasa's camp stood six kilometers north of the Shinmei fort, on Mount Gyoichi, at the Echizen border: five hundred and fifty meters high, the tallest peak thereabouts and the most vital ground of all. First Goemon found out that the camp was on Gyoichi; then it cost him four days to work his way there in secret, through a belt of country where more than four hundred kamari of the two armies scurried back and forth. He reached it on the night of the seventeenth.

He slipped through the palisades and the abatis and stole into what seemed to be Morimasa's headquarters. It was a moonless dark. In the wood near the summit, something jet-black dropped on him without warning from overhead. A great serpent, he thought, of the kind that infested those hills, and there would be no fighting free once it wound him in its length; he flung himself backward and rolled, and in that instant a sharp pain went through his right shoulder. A blunder, he thought.

But Goemon, reckoning that this journey led to a battlefield where a stray arrow or a stray bullet might come at any moment, had put on under his peasant clothes the coat of nanban-iron chain mail Hanzo had given him. So the sharpened point that found its way between the links bit no more than a centimeter into the flesh.

The enemy was short, and small. And it moved like an Iga ninja.

Goemon felt no strong compulsion to see tonight's purpose through at any cost; and from the other side, too, came no breath of anything desperate. So a lazy, curious sort of grappling began. Both being ninja, it went quickly, and yet in essence it was a slack, slow-motion affair.

Into the body-arts of ninjutsu had been built the fist-arts that came from China. In China, from the end of the Song seven hundred years ago, they had developed a fighting art of Indian descent. At first it was learned as one discipline of the Buddhist way, beside Zen, for health and for the steeling of body and nerve in self-defense. Then the temples fell into ruin, the monks scattered, and it spread among the common people; specialists arose, and secret societies formed to organize them. An unarmed people prized it as a means to stand against their rulers and the local bandits, and honed it and honed it again; and over the long coming and going with China it passed, by many routes, into Japan as well.

But in Japan it never came easily to its growth as an art of the bare hand: a way of studying the nerves and vessels and joints of the body, of striking their vital points and slipping the enemy's strikes at them, using no weapon at all. What Japan had instead was sumo, which had thrown the meridian- and joint-locks out; and until karate, the empty hand, was brought in, there survived only the little of it built into ninjutsu.

So: the man seized Goemon's collar with his left hand. Goemon caught that hand from below, drew it in to his chest, and with a left knife-hand sealed the hand that would have struck his carotid; then he turned that hand over, laid it atop the opponent's left, and, clamping both, went to work the Rakan-ken move called the single-breast drop. The man tore his left hand free, jabbed for the eyes, and in the same instant drove a right fist for Goemon's suigetsu, the pit of the stomach. Had either lock closed a breath sooner, the other would have gone down senseless; but neither was skilled enough yet, so both slipped, and the thing dragged limply on. At the last Goemon, with the Ryuo-ken move called the wrapping wrist-lock, somehow flung the man to the ground. The man let out a strange snort through his nose, "kukooh," rolled clean over, sank into the grass, and, like a great snake running, went weaving off through it in a single ripple and was gone.

Goemon climbed on then, straight for Morimasa's headquarters at the top of Mount Gyoichi.

Now, the Iga ninja who snorts "kukooh" is none other than Koichi of Higashi-Yubune.

This Koichi had once been hired by Uesugi Kenshin and used as a tool in the suppression of the Ikko risings, stealing into Toyama castle. But in time the faith of the Ikko sect took hold of him, and above all he came under the spell of the character of its chief, Kashiwagi Shirobei. He turned fervent believer, struck his name from the rolls of the Iga ninja, and went of his own free will to the Ishiyama Hongan-ji at Osaka, where he worked the boats that carried Mori's weapons and grain into Ishiyama castle. In the sea-fight at the mouth of the Kii river the Kuki fleet cast him up on the shore, and at the very moment Momochi Sandayū was about to take him, he was saved by the turtle-shaped submersible the Ryūgū-bune of Noshima. In the end, when Ishiyama fell, it was he who set it alight and left Nobunaga nothing but ashes. Readers of the earlier volume, The Stealthy Ones, will know all this.

And this same Koichi of Higashi-Yubune was the man who, at the fall of Toyama, took Kashiwagi Shirobei's orphaned son Karasumaru, then five years old, upon his back and fled to Nagashima in Ise; and when Nagashima too was stormed and wiped out by Nobunaga, carried him overland to Ishiyama castle, cherished him as one cherishes a flower, and by the fall of Ishiyama had raised him into a strapping boy of eleven, nineteen kan in weight; and at that fall brought him along to Iga with their friend Etegi.

Of Koichi since then no word has come; but Karasumaru, at the time of Ieyasu's crossing of Iga, was marked out and taken to Hamamatsu, lodged with Etegi in Hattori Hanzo's house, and, falling captive to the wiles of the self-styled woman ninja Hatsuko, has grown into a heavy fellow of fourteen and twenty-four kan. This too the reader knows.

If, then, the ninja who set upon Goemon just now, on the flank of Mount Gyoichi, gave that nasal snort "kukooh," it can be no one but Koichi. And if so, was he in Shibata Katsuie's hire?

Or had he, by that same free will of his, gone over to Hideyoshi's enemy, Hideyoshi being the heir of the Nobunaga against whom his grievances lay heaped?

But Goemon did not know Koichi. He only cocked his head a little: a strange ninja, that one, with no weight to his blows.

When at last Goemon reached the makeshift hut that seemed to be the headquarters and put his eye to a knothole in the plank wall, a big man stood before the dim fish-oil lamp, beating the floor, tearing at his hair, wailing aloud. His face ran with tears and worked without pause, so that there was no telling what it looked like to begin with. His howling shook the walls of the hut, and at every blow to the floor the boards seemed about to spring loose.

The reader may find something comical in this great bearded man weeping so in his armor. But not one of the five warriors seated there thinks it strange. Grave they might well be, the case being what it was; and yet every face wears nothing but the same stricken grief.

"So — so — so it was all seven! Aiee! The cruelty of it! The cruelty! The cruelty!"

This sinewy, gnarled man, close on six shaku tall, is Yamaji Shōgen himself.

Here a word is needed. A warrior shows no joy, no anger, no grief, no delight in his face; he freezes expression and gesture alike to the last degree, and in the end lets feeling itself run dry.

That, however, belonged to the perfecting of the Tokugawa feudal order. The warriors of the Warring States were far more open and artless; their feeling swung wider, and they were free to show it. For a great bearded man to lift his voice and weep aloud was an ordinary thing, and the writings of the day set down many such scenes, and vividly.

In that age betrayal was ordinary, and what moved these men was only a pure sympathy for one who, by ill luck, had bungled the managing of it. Not a face among the listeners showed a trace of "And after you went and turned on us." Even Goemon, who had watched the crucifixions on the riverbed with his own eyes, felt a sharp sting rise in the back of his nose as he looked on this showy grief.

"Aiee, am I to leave this wrong unavenged!"

With that his feeling came to its verdict, and, his face still streaming, Yamaji set eagerly to laying out Hideyoshi's dispositions. Just then a kamari ran in.

"Tonight Hideyoshi, at the head of twenty thousand, has marched on Mino—"

"Whaaat?" Sakuma Morimasa, seated at the front, sprang to his feet.

Morimasa was twenty-nine. A master breaker of wild horses, his name thundered on both sides. His eyes were small and heavy-lidded but sharp with light; fair-skinned, compact, firm-fleshed, he was a small and handsome man. In an instant his face flushed scarlet and he leapt up.

"No mistake? You're certain?" he pressed.

Yamaji put in a word. "Certain enough. His lordship Sanshichi Nobutaka has always been of one heart with Lord Katsuie. He is not a man to sit content with the single province of Mino that Hideyoshi threw him. If Hideyoshi has marched off into Ōmi, of course Nobutaka will watch for his absence and begin to stir. Just before I slipped out of the Doki-yama fort I heard it for certain: acting in concert with Takigawa Kazumasu, he has raised troops and fired the villages in the lands of Ujiie Naomichi, lord of Ōgaki castle, and of Inaba Ittetsu, lord of Shimizu."

"Hm. Then we strike at Hideyoshi's empty camp! I ride at once to the headquarters on Mount Uchinakao and take Lord Katsuie's orders."

One of the generals at his side laid a hand to check him. "There is no need for the commander to go himself. Let me go."

"Lord Katsuie's an old man. He'll be slow to bestir himself. Unless I go in person and haul him to his feet, he'll never make up his mind. Yamaji, come, let us go."

He made to step out. Just then another kamari came running in.

"Today, at the hour of the Monkey, four in the afternoon, by Hideyoshi's order, at the gate of Azuchi castle, Lord Nobutaka's mother and his daughter were put to the cross."

Sakuma cried "Whaaat!" and sprang up once more. Yamaji too was aghast.

"That hostage his lordship Nobutaka gave up to Hideyoshi last year. You, are you certain of it?"

"Yes. I saw the piteous sight through to the end, and came running straight here by way of Sakamoto."

Within a few days, here too: the crucifixion of innocent hostages, an old woman and a young girl.

Sakuma stamped on the floor, ground his teeth, and groaned. "Not the principal wife, granted, but a lady who had the honor of our lord's own love, was she not! An aged lady, and a girl still in the bud: what crime was theirs? However Lord Nobutaka may have broken his word — aiee! He's a fiend, a thing lower than the beasts! Heaven'll pardon this Hideyoshi no longer!"

Looking for an instant sally, the fort broke into sudden uproar.

Goemon decided to take his ease until they should come back. And the two of them set off at a run, up three and a half kilometers of mountain road, toward Uchinakao.

In great haste the men cooked rice and got a meal down, cleaned the guns, and packed shot and powder each into its own bag.

An hour or so later Sakuma and Yamaji and the rest came back from Uchinakao, fuming and taking it out on all around them. Piecing together the words that reached his ear, Goemon gathered that Katsuie would by no means sanction a decisive battle at this hour.

All the eighteenth, messengers went back and forth to Katsuie's headquarters. Toward evening Morimasa, out of all patience, went off himself once more; and after endless wrangling, only on the morning of the twentieth did he win leave to fall by night, with the Sakuma corps alone, on the Nakagawa fort at Mount Ōiwa.

The choice of Mount Ōiwa was of course Yamaji's counsel. Ōiwa is a mountain on the east shore of Lake Yogo; from the Shibata side it lay across the water, to be reached only by swinging wide around the lake. To go south along the Anegawa was flat going, but there one's movement would be known to the enemy at once, and no surprise was possible. There was nothing for it but to keep to the western hills and sweep wide, round behind Hideyoshi's front line.

Why choose this? Because Katsuie, yielding at last to Morimasa's fierce demand, had allowed the night raid, yet meant no general battle by it. He wanted only to startle Hideyoshi with the surprise, to make him drop Mino and come hurrying back to Ōmi, and so wear out his men and horses; and, at the same time, to lift his own side's spirits.

How it is with you younger readers I cannot say, but for a man of my years the name sends a shiver through him.

Between Lake Biwa and Lake Yogo lies Shizugatake, and running east of it, Mount Ōiwa. Here, for the first time, the name Shizugatake has come up.

In later years Katō Kiyomasa and the men with him would fight their fight here as the Seven Spears of Shizugatake. Kiyomasa is the one who, in the mountains of Korea, faced a raging tiger with a cross-bladed spear and killed it, though the beast bit one of the blades clean off. And three days from now they are set to make their entrance. A scene one has seen a hundred times over, is it not, in the schoolbooks and the picture-books, the magazines and the old illustrated broadsheets.

Hideyoshi had given Shizugatake to Kuwayama Shuri-no-suke Shigeharu to hold, and Mount Ōiwa to Nakagawa Sebei.

But this was not the front line, and so the building of the fort had naturally fallen behind: the earthworks, the abatis, the plank walls, not one of them was finished. And it stood at the center of the second line, in a spot that looked straight down on Hideyoshi's headquarters at Kinomoto.

So both Sakuma Morimasa and Yamaji Shōgen, for all they had promised Katsuie to raid, chill the enemy's blood, and pull straight back, had in truth no such intention. Take this place, they reckoned, and the front line would panic and collapse whole; then they would gallop down the mountain and drive at Hideyoshi's own camp. That was the scheme.

Goemon read it too.

Had he run and told Hideyoshi's army of the plan, the Sakuma force would have gone down in ruin. But Goemon stood now in the place of a bystander. Which side won and which lost was nothing to him. If anything, it would please him a little to see Hideyoshi beaten. No more than that.

So, through the scramble before the sally, he lay in the grass, seizing by the neck the snakes that came crawling to him, throttling the venomous, flinging the rest away, and so passing the time. He had his Iga ration pills by him, and hunger was no worry.

The pills Goemon had made this time were a marvelous compound: thirty monme of cold-dried rice, five monme of buckwheat flour, thirty monme of dried bonito, thirty monme of dried loach, thirty monme of pickled-plum flesh, and the inner bark of fresh pine well steamed. All of it ground to powder, the rest worked bit by bit into the plum flesh and the pine bark, and rolled into balls about five bu across; take two or three a day, and a man can go ten days without touching rice.

And now the hour of the sally came. They bound the horses' tongues, and the men set out with pickled plums held in their mouths.

There is one more thing that may be guessed at in the mind with which Katsuie allowed Morimasa this raid: that from this single blow he could measure how the commanders on both sides would move.

As is ever the way of Sengoku commanders, every man of them takes the side that profits him. Talk as you please of lord and vassal, of the great cause; in the end it is only fine speech laid over a cold weighing of gain and loss. So betrayals of every kind and degree were an everyday matter. By this one blow, then, Katsuie meant to see what each commander, friend and foe, would do; and from that, to plan his order of battle for the reckoning to come.

A civil war, a war of small units with their tangled interests, is a very different thing from a modern war between nations. In the former, betrayal came all too easily, and the strain on a man who held a great force together was no ordinary burden. To put it at its sharpest: he could not trust so much as one of his own captains. In these few days alone, had there not been two men who gave up even mother and wife and child as hostages, then turned traitor and left them to die?

To put it more plainly still, this is how Katsuie reasoned.

If Morimasa wins at Mount Ōiwa, then men will show themselves among the enemy who will turn their coats and come over to us. Yamaoka Kagetaka, say, lord of Seta castle in Ōmi, will screw up his resolve and change sides. And if Morimasa should fall into a hard fight, then within our own camp those who nurse a secret mind to betray, or those with no will to fight, will begin to show their hand. And these he must first put down, or he cannot bring himself to commit to the decisive fight.

So Katsuie brought himself four kilometers south down the highroad to Kitsunezuka, to stand against Hori Hidemasa on Kineyama; and Maeda Toshiie and his son, who seemed to have little heart for the fight, he moved from Bessho-yama to Mount Shige, two kilometers to the south, to set them face to face with the enemy's front line.

To watch the units move and to read the minds of the commanders was, for Goemon, a fascination without end.

Now, Morimasa's six thousand, with Fuwa Katsumitsu, Tokuyama Hidenori, Hara Hikojirō, and Sakuma Yasumasa in the van, came south along the ridge from Mount Gyoichi to Gongen Slope. There he cut off a company under Shibata Katsumasa and sent it straight south against Shizugatake, while he himself, at the head of the main force, swept up along Lake Yogo from south to north and bore down on Mount Ōiwa.

It was already the first part of the hour of the Hare, six in the morning.

Nakagawa Kiyohide, holding Mount Ōiwa, got word of the attack, hurriedly manned the half-built ramparts with his garrison of a thousand, musketeers and spearmen, and fought them off. Again and again he sallied from the fort and charged.

The Shinsho Taikōki writes the fight up in fearful exaggeration, after the manner of the Chinese romances it apes. Sakuma Morimasa, it says, cut about him "like a drunken elephant churning the waves, like a raging tiger grown wings," and "like a serpent climbing the sky and raising the clouds, like a maddened lion bounding rock to rock," and so pressed his attack. And the soldiers of both armies, it says, were "in high guard and low guard all mingled and changing places, chasing and chased, like the red leaves scattered from the ridgetop boughs and swept off by the storm."

Now it is the end, thought Nakagawa Sebei, forty-three that year; and, "swinging and swinging his great blade, a Soshu Sadamune of three shaku two sun," he "clove helmet and skull, split breastplates, cut through flanks and hip-wheeled his men, and, taking whatever came before him, in the time it takes to watch had cut down seventeen or eighteen"; but in the end he fell.

Just then a company of Morimasa's men circled behind the fort, lit torches, and flung them in; the huts caught at once, and flame and black smoke seared the night sky. At the sight, Morimasa's men leapt all together, with a roar, into the fort.

Morimasa, past holding in his joy, ran up onto the roof of a barrack the fire had spared and raised the victory shout. When the roar of it carried across Lake Yogo in the dawn and rang all around, Takayama Shigetomo, who held Mount Iwasaki at the lake's northeast end, on the Hokkoku highroad, took fright. They have got round behind us, he thought, and he abandoned his fort and fell back to the headquarters at Kinomoto.

If even Iwasaki went so, how much more the commanders of Hideyoshi's army in the front-line forts of Doki-yama, Shinmei-yama, and Shige-yama. At the flames rising far to their rear and the shouts of victory they froze, sure the road home was cut, and stood rooted, at a loss what to do.

Bystander though he was, Goemon too was a ninja born to the age of war. To watch a battle with the blood running before his eyes, he could not but be stirred. He ranged the dark field at will, his heart leaping to think what might come of it all.

Had the Shibata force seized the moment and pressed a general assault, with Hideyoshi away, the decisive battle would have ended in Katsuie's victory. Or had Sakuma kept his promise and pulled straight back from Mount Ōiwa, the later battle would have turned in favor of the Shibata force, their spirits running high.

But neither came to pass. Katsuie did not come out, and Morimasa did not withdraw.

From the headquarters at Kinomoto, one panicked courier after another, thick as the teeth of a comb, raced to Hideyoshi at Ōgaki.

Hideyoshi heard the news and leapt for joy. "I've won!" At once he sent runners out to cry through every village and hamlet from Ōgaki to Kinomoto that he would pay ten times over: each house was to cook a sho of rice, and every peasant to ready torches, light them, and stand along the road.

As was his way, he galloped out of Ōgaki alone, in the first hour of the Monkey, four in the afternoon. A few startled attendants spurred after him. Past Tarui, Sekigahara, Fujikawa; the sun went down at Odani, and still he raced on, and at the tail of the hour of the Dog, nine at night, he galloped into Kinomoto. Thirteen ri in five hours.

Why, hearing that Sakuma Morimasa had taken Mount Ōiwa, did Hideyoshi cry "I've won"? Because, most likely, an enemy already the weaker, thrusting out with a part of his force, had opened a gap in his own line of defense; and if that thrust could be beaten down with a strong force of one's own, then, running the routed enemy down, one might shatter the main body as it tried to gather its fleeing men back in.

Riding their victory, Morimasa and his men, meaning to take Shizugatake on the twenty-first, made their camp over the whole stretch from Mount Ōiwa to Shimizu-dani and Hachigamine.

But as the night of the twentieth came on, Goemon woke of a sudden, looked south, and saw a line of torches strung one behind the next like ten thousand lamps, filling the Hokkoku highroad and coming north.

Even Goemon, the bystander to whom it made no difference who won, was startled so. No wonder Morimasa was aghast.

All at once, in the grass where Goemon lay, a rustling started up and moved off toward the northern foot of the hill: Morimasa's kamari, beyond doubt. Goemon ran off mingled among them.

Nearing the edge of the Kinomoto post-town, he found watch-fires already burning everywhere and every house awake, boiling water, washing rice, a bedlam of neighing horses and shouting men. And into it, with the clack of hooves, the units behind came arriving one after another.

Goemon let out a groan. "A-a-a-aah."

He hurried straight back to Morimasa's headquarters and watched. The moment the kamari's reports came in, the leaders put their heads together; and at the tail of the hour of the Boar, eleven at night, they suddenly drew the whole force together, waited for the moon to rise, swung round the western shore of Lake Yogo, and began their retreat as far as Gongen Slope.

Goemon wanted, more than anything, to watch the coming battle from Hideyoshi's side. So, having seen Morimasa's army off in its retreat, he waited a while on Mount Ōiwa; and in scarcely two hours the van of Hideyoshi's army was already climbing the mountain.

No, not the van. Hideyoshi himself.

That metallic voice, with its strange ring, came carrying to him, loud and careless.

"Hm. By the look of these campfire ashes, no more than an hour's passed since they ran. You there, catch Morimasa by the tail at once and give him a little slap. Don't chase him far."

"Sir!" answered several hundred men, and off they ran west along the shore of Lake Yogo in the moonlight.

Reports from the departed unit came in to Hideyoshi without a pause. By these, Morimasa had sent word to his younger brother Shibata Katsumasa, who lay encamped near the cutting some hundred meters northwest of Shizugatake, where it joined Iinoura on Lake Biwa to the southern tip of Lake Yogo: "I am pulling back now; you pull back too." But Katsumasa, to cover his brother's retreat, still held fast to his post at the cutting.

The twenty-first of the fourth month, the eleventh of June by the new calendar, dawned. Watching all this from Chausu-yama, Hideyoshi split his army in two, one part along the lakeside road, the other along the ridge of Hachigamine, closing on Katsumasa's force in a ring. And Hideyoshi himself came forward as far as Shizugatake, which looked down on the cutting. His golden-gourd standard glittered in the morning sun.

"When will Hideyoshi open the attack?"

Goemon watched with all his eyes.

Katsumasa was trying to draw the units left southeast of the cutting into those to the northwest. At the very instant the southeastern units were coming down toward the cutting, Hideyoshi gave the order to charge.

Here occurred the scene they call the Seven Spears of Shizugatake.

Katō Kiyomasa, Fukushima Masanori, Katō Yoshiaki, Wakisaka Yasuharu, Katagiri Katsumoto, Hirano Nagayasu, Kasuya Sukeuemon-no-jō, Sakurai Sakichi, and Ishiko Hyōsuke fought hard here, and Hideyoshi gave them each a letter of commendation: to Masanori, three thousand five hundred koku; to the rest, three thousand koku apiece; only, Hyosuke had fallen, and so a thousand koku went to his younger brother Nagamatsu. The letters to all nine were of one text, and those to Yasuharu, Yoshiaki, and Nagayasu survive today in the original.

Nine men received the letters, yet why "Seven Spears"? Later ages have argued it every which way, and some records and tales have simply cut the roster down; but "seven" has a good ring to it, and the accepted view is that it is only a round number.

As always, the Shinsho Taikōki seizes the moment and lays on with its brush, painting each man's fight in turn. Here, for a sample, let me set down Katō Kiyomasa's part in plainer words.

Yamaji Shōgen Masakuni, meaning to make one stand, brandished his spear, called out his name, and waited for the enemy. There Kiyomasa came running. Tonami Hayato, musket-captain to Haigo Goemon, rushed up and came at Kiyomasa to run him through at a single thrust. Kiyomasa saw him. "You're in my way, off!" and with the word he drove his spear into the joint of Tonami's hip, and Tonami crashed from his horse. Kiyomasa ran up and took his head, then turned now to Yamaji: "Traitor Yamaji Shōgen, you'll not slip me!" and drove at him.

Yamaji gripped a spear a jo long, long in the blade; Kiyomasa had his usual mother-of-pearl-inlaid shaft with the single cross-blade. A while they thrust at each other, but Yamaji was forced onto the defensive at every turn, and, judging he could not win with the spear, he sprang back. "Let us grapple and settle it." "Done," said Kiyomasa, and he threw down his spear and closed with him hard, meaning to take him alive.

I am a traitor, come what may, thought Masakuni; taken alive, there is no knowing what horrors they will put me to. Either way, let this be my end. And he put out all his strength to twist Kiyomasa down.

Masakuni was thirty-eight, a man of great might renowned through the north; Kiyomasa was twenty-three, tall and heavy-boned. Kiyomasa, bent on taking him alive, had the greater strength, and at last wrestled him down and pinned him under his knee.

But Yamaji was strong too. He caught hold of Kiyomasa's kusazuri, the skirt of plates that hangs below the cuirass to cover the hips, and would not be pried loose, and he strained to keep Kiyomasa from drawing a blade. I can never take him alive this way, thought Kiyomasa; then I will have his head. But the shikoro of his helmet, the neck-guard that hangs about it to cover the neck, had caught on an azalea branch and would not come free.

Now, thought Yamaji, and made to throw him off; but he missed his footing and went tumbling over the cliff. His two hands still gripping Kiyomasa's kusazuri, Kiyomasa was dragged after him and rolled twenty or thirty ken down the slope. Kiyomasa cut quickly through the shinobi-no-o, the cord that ties the helmet under the chin; the helmet stayed hooked on the azalea branch and hung there. But in the end he pinned Yamaji, cut off his head, went back and put his helmet on again, and pressed on ahead.

And that, more or less, is how the tale runs.

But the Shinsho Taikōki is a popular novel from first to last, and its author, of course, neither saw the fighting himself nor had it from an eyewitness; small wonder he did not know how it truly went. And in fact Goemon had been watching. And not watching only: it was Goemon himself who took Masakuni's head.

Goemon had wanted to come upon the traitor Masakuni somewhere on the field. Not that he meant to do anything by it; he only wished to see the man's fate through to its end. Coming suddenly round a thicket on the ridge west of the cutting, he ran into a knot of fleeing Shibata warriors, all horseless now and on foot. Among them was a bearded man he knew, a good six shaku and more.

"There he is. What'll he do now?"

Goemon held his breath and dogged the man's steps.

Just then, da, da, daaan, shots broke from a thicket close by. Two or three men reared up out of it and pitched forward at a run. The whole knot of them went down in a clatter. Goemon ran on, mingled among them.

Masakuni was one of them. But a cliff lay before him, and Masakuni went head over heels down over it. Goemon flung himself down at once and got below the cliff ahead of him. There the ground was a grassy slope, still five or six meters above the shore of Lake Yogo.

To the spot where Masakuni had fallen, a Hideyoshi commander came running, by chance. From straight above that commander Masakuni came down on him, and the commander, struck hard on the head, dropped for a moment into a faint. His strange, pointed, eboshi-shaped helmet, its cord snapped by the jolt, came off and rolled away.

The face it bared was pale, the chin thin and sharp: an inverted triangle, a great rat's face where Hideyoshi's was a little rat's. Below the nose a mustache drooped over the mouth in the shape of the character for eight. It was the face of Katō Toranosuke Kiyomasa, whom Goemon had come to know late the year before, when Kiyomasa had come with Hideyoshi to look the ground over.

Rising, and seeing that face, Masakuni sprang back and set himself. But Kiyomasa had not yet come round.

Masakuni knew him too for Kiyomasa, the famed attendant of Hideyoshi. A gift of heaven, this chance. He reached for his waist, but his sword was gone, dropped somewhere. He drew instead the short blade they call the mete-zashi, the armor-piercer, closed in, straddled him, and laid hold of the helmet's throat-guard. Lift it, and the throat lay bare.

This won't do, thought Goemon. His throwing-blades were already out. In that instant his right hand went to his breast and sent five happo-shuriken, drawn as if along a straightened cord, flying one after another at Masakuni's face. Every one struck it full in the middle: thk, thk, thk, thk, thk. The flesh split, and the blood sprayed. As Masakuni reeled back with a cry, Kiyomasa at last came to.

The moment he was up, he sprang and threw himself on Masakuni. The man's face was so masked in blood there was no telling who he was. Kiyomasa clung to him wildly, drew the fallen mete-zashi, and stabbed at the throat beneath him; twice he missed, and the third time drove it home. A terrible gush of blood shot out and caught him full in the face. Shifting his grip on the blade, he sawed at the neck, gyu, gyu, gori, gori, hacking with no method at all.

Catching his breath, he looked closely: three shuriken were still buried in the face. Two, it seemed, had flown loose. Kiyomasa started, and cast his eyes about him. He knew them for Iga-school happo-shuriken.

So it was not he who had felled Masakuni, but an Iga ninja. And Iga and Koga ninja there were beyond counting. He could not claim this head for his own. The face was past knowing in any case; he half thought to leave it and go on; but nowhere about was there anyone who might have thrown the blades.

Never mind, he thought, and, helmet and all, wrapped the head in a cloth and slung it at his waist.

When the fighting was done, at the inspection of the heads, the helmet the head still wore was a rare thing of nanban iron, shaped like the tip of a short-bristled writing-brush, the style they call the "brush-head"; and by that it was known for Masakuni's. No one else came forward to claim it, and so it was counted among Kiyomasa's trophy heads.

And so the Taikōki spins out, on and on, its imaginings and inventions about the valiant fighting of the Seven Spears. But let me show you, by the way, one part more, the work of Fukushima Masanori, the foremost of the seven, this time in the original words. (The kana spelling and the punctuation have been modernized.)

"—There was a warrior fighting to the northwest of Niwato-hama, in armor whose hip-skirt was of sandalwood-burnished tanned leather, laced plate by plate in alternating red and white, and a helmet bearing a heaven-piercer crest (the ornament set upright at the helmet's front). Fukushima Ichimatsu marked him from afar. This is the very Haigo; he shall not escape me. Closing, and seeing him for Haigo Goemon: 'One pass of the spear, have at you!' and from beneath his own cry he thrust; and at the lightning of the spear-point there was no leisure even to blink; and, breath in, breath out, thrusting and closing, they hounded one another; but Masanori, quick as a wink, ran in and took his head. Ah, the pity of it: Haigo Goemon Hisamitsu, thirty-five years of age. Bred and raised in the northern country, he had shown his valor in arms; and though his corpse molders now by the shore of Lake Yogo, his name is handed down to after ages. Masanori, having fought a fight of honor, pressed boldly on and fought yet again—"

Even after he had seen Masakuni's end through, Goemon went on watching, hiding himself now in the crown of a tree over there, now in the shadow of a thicket over here. But what he saw was a fearful thing: packs of beasts, no telling who fought whom, chasing and grappling, roaring, bellowing, shrieking, crying for rescue, all the death-groans kneaded together, a churning horror that covered the western shore of Lake Yogo, water's edge and mountain-foot alike, for three kilometers as far as Gongen Slope, whirling on blind and aimless.

Shibata Katsumasa, fighting and giving ground by turns before the pursuing Hideyoshi army, tried to join Sakuma Morimasa's force at Gongen Slope. Morimasa took his men in, met the pursuers, and struck back.

It was done so finely that Goemon, watching, could not but admire it.

And at that instant one decisive thing happened, the thing that settled the whole battle.

The Hideyoshi army was brought up short, dead in its tracks.

On Mount Shige, close against the north of Lake Yogo, stood the two thousand men of Maeda Toshiie and his son, set there to cover the left flank and rear of Morimasa's army. And these, all at once, abandoned their ground and began to move off to the west.

Goemon, hidden in the crown of a great tree on the summit near Gongen Slope, was sure the Maeda force was setting out to relieve Morimasa's from behind. But instead it marched steadily on, passed clean behind Morimasa's men, came down off the mountain onto the flat of Shiozu-dani, and made away toward Tsuruga, its own province, withdrawing for good.

Goemon shouted. "Wha— wha— they've run! They've turned traitor—"

"Curse you, Toshiie—"

"Coward!"

But no one caught his cry. For Morimasa's and Katsumasa's men, gazing out from Gongen Slope, had all raised the very same cry at once.

"Traitor..." The shouts flew wild, like madness.

But Toshiie's betrayal had been settled already, in the tenth month of the year before. Asked by Katsuie to carry the terms of peace, Toshiie had left Kita-no-shō at the close of the tenth month, reached the Takara-dera castle at Yamazaki on the second of the eleventh, and returned to Kita-no-shō on the eighth. In that stay Hideyoshi had wound him wholly round his finger: he praised him to the skies, gave him handsome gifts, and swore that if he would betray Shibata and come over, his ancestral lands would be confirmed to him. So he won Toshiie's heart. And then, on the twentieth of the fourth month, the moment he reached Kinomoto, he sent a secret envoy: "When the battle opens, I ask you to turn your coat. An open betrayal will be hard for you, I know; so if you will only stand and watch, I shall count it a betrayal all the same." To which Toshiie replied: "Turn traitor outright I cannot; but I promise to keep to a watching stance."

To stand in the thick of battle and not fight beside one's own, but only look on with folded arms: that, it goes without saying, is nothing but betrayal. And Toshiie made good the secret pact more than fully. He quit the field altogether.

In this while, the men under Hideyoshi's banner had swollen by degrees to seven thousand. Hideyoshi kept sending envoys to each of his units, telling them how the fight stood and issuing his orders to dress the line.

Hideyoshi himself took post at the very front, meaning to strike the enemy's right and, riding the wavering there, to send his center and right driving at Katsuie's headquarters. Katsuie, with seven thousand men, had come forward at first light on the twentieth, four kilometers south from his headquarters on Mount Uchinakao to Kitsunezuka.

Facing him, at the Higashino fort, Hori Hidemasa of Hideyoshi's army held five thousand. On the morning of the twenty-first, hearing the din of heavy fighting toward Shizugatake, Hori came down from his fort, gathered in the thousand men of Ogawa Suketada, who held the Hokkoku highroad, and the five hundred of Kinoshita Hanuemon, who held the Doki-yama fort, and showed every sign of falling on Katsuie's headquarters at any moment.

To Katsuie, from the quarter of Shizugatake and Gongen Slope, reports came one on the heels of the next, whipped in on horses lathered with sweat. Some, hit by shot and arrow, barely reached the headquarters, and before they could get out a word of their news toppled from the saddle and died where they fell.

When word came that Toshiie had fled toward Tsuruga, Katsuie ground his teeth till they cracked and his face went white as death. The anguish in that old general's heart through the hour or so that followed is a thing pitiful even to imagine.

He tried again and again, messenger after messenger, to draw Sakuma Morimasa back from Gongen Slope and close him up with the headquarters, for Morimasa by now had the look of a man thrust out too deep among the enemy. But Morimasa, on the contrary, sent word to Katsuie, urging him to bring his guard forward and break through at Kinomoto while he himself held the Hideyoshi army fast. Hideyoshi had indeed pushed too far out to the front. Strike Kinomoto in that gap, and his army, taken front and rear, would be destroyed.

But the enemy in front was not the only strong one: on Kineyama to his left stood a powerful force as well. And besides Toshiie, two or three other commanders were under suspicion of secret dealings with Hideyoshi. He dared not move rashly. Better to stand fast on the line of Kitsunezuka and Shūfukuji Slope and wait his moment. So Katsuie reasoned.

But send as he might, Morimasa would not budge from Gongen Slope.

Meanwhile Katsuie's own men, seeing Toshiie's betrayal before their eyes, began to waver. Toshiie was lord of the whole province of Noto, his son Toshinaga lord of the castle at Fuchū in Echizen; within the Shibata army the two of them ranked, with Morimasa, among its chief pillars. The betrayal struck the army with bitter dismay, and more and more men held that they should gather their forces, go home to their provinces, and wait for another day.

In this while, the five hundred men of Kimura Shigekore and Ōgane Tōhachirō, of Hideyoshi's army, who had been posted on Mount Shinmei, came on behind Toshiie's withdrawal by way of Mount Shige and showed up on the left wing of Morimasa's force at Gongen Slope. Hideyoshi's troops let no such chance slip: throwing in their swelling numbers, they struck Morimasa's army from the front. Assailed on three sides, it broke at last and was driven some two kilometers north to Shūfukuji Slope. Spent and reeling, Morimasa's men fled into the thick forest that covered the ground on every side, flung away weapons and armor, and ran off half-naked.

Padre Frois, in a letter he sent on the eighteenth of December that year, 1583, to the Padre Provincial of the Indies, Alessandro Valignano, tells of this moment thus:

"The Echizen troops fell back to the mountain where they had first lain encamped. Hashiba, seeing this from his castle, suddenly set six thousand men in motion. Shibata's nephew Sakuma, at the sight, turned back in haste to give them battle. The fighting was fierce: both sides fought from daybreak until noon, killing one another with the spear, and until that hour it was unknown to which side the victory would fall. At the last Hashiba sent his whole army out from the castle; and, the victory now turning his way, he fell upon the enemy, already worn out, and broke them. The enemy fled into the dense forest; and because a bare twenty thousand and more, joined into one body, pressed them hard in pursuit, they cast their arms and swords into the road, and, their very clothing grown a burden, threw that off too, to go light and save their lives. And so, all at once, upon the summit appeared fifteen thousand and more half-naked men."

And so it came to noon.

Hideyoshi drew his men together at the foot of Shufuku-ji, in a posture set to strike Katsuie's flank and rear, and his troops before Kitsunezuka were steadily reinforced from behind. Katsuie's camp, meanwhile, could watch the fighting on the ridge far to the southwest as plainly as a thing held in the hand; and as its defeat grew certain, more and more men slipped away, until those who stayed were a bare three thousand.

Seeing the case at its worst, Katsuie resolved at last on retreat. At that, a man named Menju Shosuke pleaded with him: "Let me give out that I am Katsuie and die fighting here; and in that time make your escape, and quickly." Katsuie allowed it, and with a handful of men fled away northward.

Menju raised aloft Katsuie's horse-standard, the gilded gohei, and with a bare three hundred men waited for the enemy at the fort on Hayashidani-yama, a kilometer from Kitsunezuka.

The right wing and the center of Hideyoshi's army bore down on the fort at Hayashidani-yama. Hideyoshi, leading the left wing, came out onto the fort's flank from Shufuku-ji, watched over the attack, and opened up with the guns.

Here was a theme made to order for a popular novel like the Shinsho Taikōki. The author works himself up, wields his brush, and invents the scene that follows.

The melee was on already. The three hundred had come down to thirteen, every man steeped in blood from head to foot, helmets struck off, the sleeves and hip-skirts of their armor cut away; and this, plainly, was the end.

"Menju Shosuke rose up. 'Is that Tsutsui's force now bearing down? He who presses at the very front, I take for Shima Sakon. A worthy foe, matched to his match. From boyhood, they say, he has dwelt in Yamato province and shown his valor time and again. This is the very enemy I have longed for!' And ranging the surviving samurai before him and behind, in a great voice: 'This is Shibata Shuri-no-shin Katsuie! Come, win your reward!' So he named himself; and, charging straight in, he cried, 'Take my head and make your renown!' as he came.

"Shima Sakon, watching him come, reined in his horse and set it. 'The bearing of the dread Lord Shibata, whose name rings through all Japan, I do not find in you. And yet, by your horse-standard, your armor is Lord Shibata's own device. Lord Shibata is near sixty years of age this year, while you who now name yourself Shuri-no-shin are a young man not yet thirty. So, then: to let the Master of Works, Katsuie, get away, you take his name upon you. What gallantry is here! You are Ji Xin of Cathay come again, the Satō Tadanobu, the Murakami Hikoshirō of our own land! I am a man of Yamato province, a samurai of the house of Tsutsui, Shima Sakon by name. The head of so loyal and great a man I shall have!'

"So Sakon called, and called; and Shosuke, sword in hand, faced him, high guard and low, parry and thrust, thrust and close, quick as a lightning-flash, and not a hair's gap to be seen between them. When Shosuke opened and pressed, Sakon slid aside and came in. Back and forth they went, like a raging tiger breaking in, like a lion in fury, each spending to the last the secret arts he had, meeting and parting, parting and closing again; and when it would not be settled, they fretted, and flung down their long blades. 'Come, grapple!' And with an 'Aye!' they closed hard, and, tumbling together between horse and horse, they wrestled; but Sakon won the grip at last, pinned him, and struck off his head. Shosuke, twenty-nine years of age. Seeing it, the samurai of Menju's band, to the last man, died fighting."

And so, by two in the afternoon, the fighting was over. Over the whole face of the land, from the peaks south and west and north of Lake Yogo to Hayashidani-yama, under the scorching pour of the sun from a sky swept cloudless that eleventh of June by the new calendar, the dead and the wounded of both sides lay scattered in wild disorder. The corpses began at once to rot; the wounded writhed and groaned in their pain.

Hideyoshi, seeing it, was all brisk bustle. "Run into the peasants' houses and buy up every reed-mat and sedge-hat you can find, price be damned," he ordered, and so sheltered the wounded from the blazing heat.

Katsuie, with his handful of men, crossed at Yanagase into Echizen, and by way of Imajo came to Fuchū, the present Takefu. This was the castle of Toshiie's son Toshinaga, and Toshiie too had fled here.

And there, at the mouth of the town, still in their armor, Maeda Toshiie and his son came out to meet him: the very men who, a moment since, had betrayed him, and by that betrayal turned the reckoning with his lifelong rival into a crushing rout.

Katsuie looked at these traitors and said, with a gentle face:

"You and your son have used me exceedingly well these many years, and I am truly grateful. Fortune has not favored me; beaten now to the ground in this battle in Ōmi, my part is played out. One thing only I regret: that I can make you no return for your long friendship. From here on, let no old debt to me hold you back. Turn to Hideyoshi with a whole heart. He is a big-hearted man; he will see your house safe, I am sure of it."

This Shibata Katsuie has been cast as Hideyoshi's foil, got up as a hot-tempered, rash boar of a warrior. But in truth he was a brave general with none of Hideyoshi's cunning and craft, a plain, manly man with real feeling in him. He could not have been otherwise; or he could never have carried himself so toward the man who, a breath before, had betrayed him and dropped him into the pit of ruin.

Late that night Katsuie reached Kita-no-shō, the present Fukui, gathered his close retainers, and ordered the castle's defenses made fast. But the men who had come in to the castle were a bare three thousand. There was no help for it: he gave up the outer walls, set men in the second and third baileys only, and sent the wives and children of his soldiers scattering, each to what kin they could find.

At that time Katsuie sent out of the castle the third daughter of Maeda Toshiie, Maa, whom he had kept as a hostage, bidding her go back to her father. The lady Maa was twelve. The year before, she had been betrothed to a young samurai of Katsuie's, one Sakuma Jūzō, then fourteen.

She was a lovely girl, so lovely that Hideyoshi would later lose his heart to her utterly, her skin a translucent white with the faint bloom of a pure, tender richness upon it. Her eyes were large, deep, and clever indeed; held in the steady gaze of those obsidian eyes, the biggest bearded man would feel his whole body go numb.

Into this foul and cruel world a girl so beautiful and so bright had come. And to what end? Poor child, she had been born in the wrong place. Fallen into a world like this, the jewel would soon enough be defiled, pitilessly, and shattered.

Such girls we meet, now and then. And, the pity of it, sure enough, as the years go by, these girls, however content they may feel within themselves, are stained, heartbreakingly, with filth, turn dull and foolish-seeming, and are buried at last in the dust and gone, leaving in our breasts a thing that rails at the world, or curses it.

The lady Maa was such a girl.

Told to be gone in haste, dressed in a plain traveling costume, with no time even to take her leave of her husband-to-be, she was led from the castle by her waiting-woman Achako, hid in the shop of a draper called Iseya who had the run of the place, and had his manservant carry word to Toshiie at Fuchū. But there was no need to go so far as Fuchū. The next day, the twenty-third, Hideyoshi's army bore down on the castle town of Kita-no-shō; and since Maeda Toshiie's own troops rode in its vanguard, the girl was rescued at once and brought safe to Fuchū.

This castle at Kita-no-shō had been raised by Katsuie in the third year of Tenshō, after Asakura Yoshikage fell to Nobunaga at Ichijodani and Katsuie was granted the four hundred ninety thousand koku of Echizen. It was famous for the great intricacy of its ground-plan and for its nine-storied keep.

Hideyoshi's army had brought the men up behind bamboo bundles for shields, kept up a fire of guns, and by the dead of night had pressed them to the very earthworks of the inner keep. Goemon, who had followed the fleeing Shibata army, had somehow slipped into the keep itself.

But these last days he had been in a strange mood. He had stood where it made not a whit of difference to him which side won. And yet this man, who from boyhood had labored at nothing but the training that warps one's own nature, who had learned only the arts of destroying men, who had always held that for a man to be humble, or to love, or to trust was folly and beneath contempt: this same man, tricked by Momochi Sandayū, stripped of his standing as a ninja, and flung to the very bottom, where he had to ply his ninja's craft to steal, had there been joined to a woman named Maki, who knew nothing of distrust. And little by little he had begun to feel the happiness of loving another, of trusting another. It was as spring melts thick ice: quietly, quietly, it was thawing his heart.

That thawing was bringing him back, at last, toward what he had been before the warping, the shape he had worn newborn. And so the fighting of these days that he had watched at close hand, and the human web woven out of it and wrung from it, sank into his heart, vivid, and stained it deep: the workings of men's conduct and heart and feeling that war, in its cornered extremity, threw up so dizzyingly and laid so bare. A strange power drove him, as the strong force of desire drives the young, and he rushed breathless into its every corner. It was that power that held him now, flattened to the ceiling above the great hall of the keep, as the castle came to its fall.

It was already past midnight. The men in the third bailey did no more than loose a shot now and then. The besiegers, meaning perhaps to throw in their general assault at dawn, had eased their hands a while and were catching their breath.

In the great hall, bright as day with its rows of standing candles, more than eighty of Katsuie's close retainers were gathered. In the seat of honor sat Katsuie, his face worn to nothing with these days of strain. And at his side sat a woman more beautiful than any Goemon had beheld in all his life.

It was Oichi, Katsuie's wife.

She was pale, but like a deep lake, deeper by far than the Lake Biwa or the Lake Yogo that Goemon had watched these several days; pale, and still.

Yet she was a mother, with three daughters she had borne and raised asleep in the next room. She had been at his side a bare nine months, the wife of a husband who, rough and clumsy in his ways, had honored and loved her from the bottom of his heart.

Now, with the end of all things before her, something surged and roiled, hidden inside that comeliness, and it was on the very point of breaking through and pouring out.

The jet-black, long-slit eyes gazed steadily at the people in the hall. The small lips were a bright red. High on her forehead, near the hairline, two black lozenges were painted, of a shape hard to name, like silkworm cocoons. Down the back of her white-silk kosode the long jet-black hair hung like a waterfall.

Ten years before, that warped ninja Nejiri had once looked upon Oichi. (Nejiri had become, three years ago, poor wretch, a severed head parted from its trunk, floating down the Yodo; and at that very moment it had drifted up against Goemon, who had just fired at Nobunaga and missed and had hidden himself in the river, and, unknown to him, had been pushed aside and carried off toward the sea.) Oichi was then twenty-seven.

The place had been the keep of Odani castle in northern Ōmi; and there too, just so, the castle had stood on the very brink of falling. Oichi had sat beside her husband, Azai Nagamasa. Nejiri, watching that desperate, beautiful scene, a wife bent on dying with her husband and a husband bent on saving at least his wife, had thought, with a stab of malice: "Aiee, let the two of them burn in the flames, let them writhe and die!"

The woman the ceiling-bound Goemon watches now is thirty-seven.

A properly authenticated portrait of her survives today at the Jimyō-in on Mount Kōya. Her daughter Yodogimi had a painter make it for the memorial rites at the seventeenth anniversary of her first husband Azai Nagamasa's death and the seventh of her own; and the beauty of Oichi, famed from her girlhood as the fairest in the realm, still grips the heart of any who look on it, across three hundred and seventy years and a canvas gone dark with age.

Katsuie quietly began to speak. His was a voice grown thick and rasping and rusted with long years of going up and down the battlefields.

"The battle of Shizugatake is over. As you see, my army is beaten. Yet this gladness I will take: that we lost through no cowardice of mine, nor of any of you. Why we lost, I have things I could say, but I will not; to weigh it over now serves nothing, for the house of Shibata perishes here, and this night. My one thought is how to die. Should the enemy strike off my head, and I and the wives and children and kin of us all be made to bear his insults, it would be a dishonor to the house of Shibata forever. Therefore I shall cut my belly, by the warrior's custom; and so that the enemy shall not find my corpse and paw it about, I mean to burn it in the fire. But I do not wish you all, my retainers, to follow me into death. Take my meaning well: from the bottom of my heart I wish that as many of you as may will keep your lives whole."

At once a great many of them raised their hands together and cried out.

"You wrong us! We will not send our lord on ahead alone. To the other world and beyond, our hearts are fixed to follow you."

"Since you press us so, we have let go those who would only hinder us; but we, with our wives and children, are resolved to share life and death with our lord. Those who would not agree to go are in the castle still."

From Katsuie's up-slanting eyes the tears came streaming down. In a strange, hoarse voice that caught in his throat, he said:

"I see. Your hearts I see well." Their loyalty sank deep to the bottom of his breast. "Only — only — the pity of it is that for the trust you put in me I can make you no return in this world! Forgive me! Forgive me!"

Then: "Bring out the wine and food we have laid by! Spend all there is in the castle! Let us open the feast that sends us off on our journey to the other world!"

And with a sound like a squeal in the back of his throat, for an instant he seemed to weep; but at last he raised his face, his look changed all at once to a bright and merry one, and he clapped his hands, pan, pan.

"Bring out the drums, the flutes, the hand-drums too!"

This last speech of Katsuie's is carefully set down in the letter Padre Frois sent that year, dated the eighteenth of December, to the Padre Provincial of the Indies, Padre Alessandro Valignano. But it is not to be supposed that a single one of the eighty-odd gathered here lived. So it must have been Goemon who told it to someone afterward. Of that there will be occasion to speak further on. And in truth, this speech moved Goemon to the depths.

Katsuie rose to his feet and roared, "The drum, the drum, give me the drum!"; and, lifting his rasping voice, he began to dance, clumsy in the motions of hand and foot.

"Lord Ichinose is young and hot-blooded. He bids me dance, who cannot dance. Then dance I will, and show you my steps."

Cha, cha, chara, karokoro, chara, karokoro, karokoro kara—

Every drop of wine and every dish in the castle was brought out, and shared even to the soldiers at their posts elsewhere. The commanders in the keep, to the last man, sang, and rose, and danced. A strange feeling stormed through every breast. Streaming tears, streaming snot, streaming drool, they threw their arms about one another's shoulders and danced like things gone mad.

Already, straw had been heaped in every room of the castle, and every door and window shut fast. And since not a shot was fired from within, the singing, the laughter, the shouts of joy, the beat of drum and flute and hand-drum that filled the keep echoed up into the night sky; and the besieging army, many of them men who had seen many a castle fall, watched with held breath at a sight they had never before beheld.

Presently Katsuie signed with his eyes to Oichi at his side and left the room. Goemon crawled along the ceiling after him. In a small chamber the husband and wife sat down face to face.

"There, it is enough now. Leave the castle, and quickly. They will treat you well, never doubt it. By the ties of fate I came to be one who could pledge two lifetimes with you; and though it was barely a year, I have lived days so happy that this world can hold no greater happiness. That is enough. My heart is full of gratitude to you, who took so rough a boor as I for a husband. That you should perish along with me, I cannot bear. You were only lent to me a little while. Your three daughters were well used before: when Lord Nobunaga stormed and destroyed Lord Nagamasa at Odani castle, you and the three of them were treated with all care; so this time, surely, they will treat you better still. There, make ready, and be quick."

What, Goemon wondered, would he do himself?

No, no, I would not have it. Were it sickness, there would be no help for it; but to take my own life like this, I could not, unless Maki were with me. How could I go and leave Maki behind, in this world where none but strangers live? And if, by some chance, one does not simply turn to earth and ash but goes on to some other world, then without Maki it would be too lonely to bear.

Goemon watched Katsuie with eyes of wonder.

"No. To live on, that I will not," said Oichi, the tears running as she spoke. "It would be too sad, too forlorn; I could not bear it one moment. Please, please, let me die with you. Ten years ago, reared in a sheltered room and knowing nothing of the world, I gave way to Lord Nagamasa's too-earnest wish and lived shamefully on. And the nine years of loneliness after, until I met you — never, never again would I pass such days. And more: this very enemy now, Hideyoshi, is the hateful man who cruelly ran my only son through upon a spear. No, no more of it. Grant me this. I do not wish to live on in such a world. If you will not let me die with you, then to the end of my life I shall rue that I did not die ten years ago with Lord Nagamasa. The three princesses I will send, as you bid, to Hideyoshi's camp. All three are children no longer; even without a mother, they will make their own way through their lives. I beg you! Let me die with you!"

Katsuie gazed steadily at her face; and when he saw that her resolve was past all changing, he heaved a great sigh and bowed his head.

Then, from somewhere deep down, a joy welled up in him: the joy of being allowed to die together with her. He flung himself forward and caught her by the shoulders, and from his breast broke something like a sob that made no sound.

Oichi's words, "my only son, spitted on a spear," call for a word of explanation. Between the princesses Hatsu-hime and Go-hime there had been a boy, Manpukumaru. At the fall of Odani castle, knowing that a boy would never be spared, they had given him into the keeping of one Kimura Kinainosuke and sent him fleeing to Tsuruga. But Manpukumaru was soon hunted down, and barely a month and a half after the castle fell was put, at Kinomoto, to a death worse even than crucifixion: he was spitted on a spear. It was Hideyoshi who did it, on Nobunaga's order.

Before long Katsuie and his wife each finished a letter, tucked it into the breast of the robe, and passed into the next small room. Bedding had been spread there; and on it, not sleeping, sat the three princesses. Coming along the ceiling to above that room and looking down, Goemon almost fled in spite of himself, so pure and so lovely were the three of them. Never had he seen anything so utterly innocent.

Ten years before, at Odani castle on the brink of its fall, Nejiri had seen these three children. Then they had been only tiny things; now they were girls in the bud, seventeen, fifteen, and thirteen.

"Ochacha. Hatsu-hime. Go-hime—" Oichi called, and looked at each face in turn. "You must not weep. You must hold your hearts strong. You are to be taken now by Nakamura Bunkasai, and leave the castle." She was straining with all her might to keep her composure.

At once Ochacha spoke up. "And you, Mother?"

"I stay in the castle, with your father."

"Then I stay too. You two are to go out with Bunkasai," Ochacha said to her two little sisters.

This Bunkasai was a favorite of Katsuie's. A warrior, yes, but more a master of the tea than of war; an uncle who was forever his tea-companion, and so on close terms with the girls.

The two younger sisters, who did not know what the fall of a castle meant, felt only the strangeness in the air, and fought with all their strength to hold back their tears.

Ochacha had been fully six years younger than Go-hime is now when she met the fall of Odani; and she remembers only, faintly, being woken in the night without warning, being carried on someone's back up the mountain behind the castle, and watching the castle she had lived in until that very moment blaze up, pretty as a picture, spitting showers of sparks.

But now she knew full well what was upon them. Her new father was still someone she could not be easy with, and so she had spent her days clinging to her mother, and to her mother alone. That she should be parted from that mother was beyond all imagining.

Bunkasai came in with two waiting-women. The two younger sisters, soundless, gone white as paper, the tears just streaming down their cheeks, were urged on by the women and went out of the room. Then Bunkasai caught Ochacha's arms from behind, pinned them at her back, took her round the waist, and lifted her lightly off her feet.

"Aah, no!" she cried, kicking her feet; but she was borne away with ease. "Mother! Mother!" The scream trailed off into the distance.

To a samurai named Tominaga Shinrokurō, waiting in the next room, Katsuie handed the two letters. "These are from Oichi and from me, to Hideyoshi, commending our three daughters to his care. Go out of the castle with Bunkasai, put them into Hideyoshi's hands, hear his answer, and come back."

"Sir." And Tominaga hurried off.

In less than an hour Bunkasai and Tominaga were back, and reported that Hideyoshi had said he would by no means use the three princesses slightingly.

Hideyoshi at that hour had his headquarters on Mount Atago, and sat watching the castle. Now Katsuie, his lifelong rival, was about to pass from this world. That was a joy to him beyond all price.

But in his breast a bitter gall lay settled: Oichi, too, would perish along with Katsuie.

He had glimpsed Oichi in her girlhood, from a distance, more than once, and at her beauty, a beauty near to the divine, his head had swum. Hearing that her marriage was of the happiest, and that children came to her one after another, he had lain awake at night for envy. When Nagamasa was destroyed and Oichi was taken into Kiyosu castle, he had managed to see her face a number of times, and the flame in his breast was fanned the higher; but she was his lord's sister, after all, a flower on a peak beyond his reach.

Yet once Nobunaga was dead, there was born in him a hope that to make her his own might not be impossible. But when he thought of the looks they mocked as the "monkey," the "bald rat," the sheer mismatch of it failed his nerve. And there was this too: that even at his lord's command, he had been the man who carried out Manpukumaru's spitting, and so Oichi, surely, must hate him.

In the thick of this torment, Oda Nobutaka, Nobunaga's third son, who was leaguing with Katsuie to hold Hideyoshi down, talked Oichi round and gave her in marriage to Katsuie. And Hideyoshi's enmity toward Katsuie burned the hotter.

But could he say to Oichi, Come out of the castle and I will spare your life? Hideyoshi was pulled this way and that.

Himself at forty-six, Oichi at thirty-six: in years they were well matched. But at a second fall of a castle, would she a second time endure the shame of forsaking her husband and living on? And if she did leave the castle now, she must school herself to become the chattel of the man who had killed Manpukumaru. On top of all which, he was the "monkey," the "bald rat." From the moment of Katsuie's fall he was assured of standing first in all the realm; but that authority was not yet upon him.

No. It was no use. His hand held out to save her would surely be struck aside.

And yet, if he held his peace, a thing of a beauty beyond anything in this world would burn to ruin, to a single lump of ash.

Hideyoshi's heart leaned toward letting her go.

Aiee, serves you right! Taken in by Nobutaka's scheming, you spurned me and flung yourself at a good-for-nothing like Katsuie. This is the punishment for it. Burn to a cinder and die!

He half surrendered himself to such thoughts. And at that moment the three princesses were brought before him. Worn out with weeping, her hair loose and wild, her face cast down; but when the watch-fire's flame lit up the figure of Ochacha, Hideyoshi felt, with a start, as though he had been saved.

The grudge, the whole bitter reckoning he held against Oichi, he could settle to his heart's content on this one.

"She is of the blood of our departed lord. Cherish her, keep her with all care, guard her in a place of safety."

So he ordered; and, his face set hard once more, he turned to the castle.

At the hour of the Tiger, four to five in the morning, Hideyoshi ordered the general assault. The inner keep of this castle had its walls piled high with great stones, and on them was reared the nine-storied tower, strongly defended with pillars of stone and doors of iron. Hideyoshi's men clung to the walls and tried to climb, but arrows and guns were loosed at them in volleys, and many fell dead and wounded.

The Shibata men fought them off with sword and spear; and among them was a boy samurai of just fifteen, wringing out a voice on the point of breaking, laying about him in a wild frenzy. Even Hideyoshi's men, taken in spite of themselves by the pitiful sight, drew back a little.

Toward noon Hideyoshi chose several hundred of his best and sent them at the walls in a headlong, all-or-nothing rush.

"Die! Die!" the boy screamed through his tears, and flailed on, until a stray bullet punched through his chest, and he tumbled from the wall and was trampled under the feet of the melee in a moment. So Maa's betrothed, Sakuma Jūzō, met his end.

At four in the afternoon Katsuie had the ladder of the keep drawn up, gave the order to set it alight, and himself climbed to the topmost, ninth story, and showed himself at the window. From below the keep the flames were already rising, licking fierce and bright.
