## Fragmentary Materials on Using Gang Connections in Revolutionary Work

{d} Wu Chengfang

Back in the period of the First Revolutionary Civil War, when Mao Zedong analyzed the conditions of the various classes of Chinese society, he pointed out: "There is besides a fairly large lumpen-proletariat, made up of peasants who have lost their land and handicraftsmen who have lost their work. They are the most precarious of human beings. In every place they have their secret organizations—the Triad Society in Fujian and Guangdong, the Elder Brothers Society in Hunan, Hubei, Guizhou, and Sichuan, the Big Sword Society in Anhui, Henan, and Shandong, the Rational Society in Zhili and the three northeastern provinces, the Green Gang in Shanghai and elsewhere—all of them once mutual-aid bodies for their political and economic struggles. The handling of these people is one of China's hard problems. They are quite capable of fighting bravely, but they carry a destructiveness; if led aright, they can be turned into a revolutionary force." From early this century the gangs' power reached into every stratum of society, and imperialism and the domestic reactionaries used that power in the service of their reactionary rule. On the other hand, because the gangs were made up chiefly of the vagrant proletariat, there existed, under progressive political influence, the possibility of their sympathizing with the revolution or even lending it their strength. As far as I know, after the founding of the Chinese Communist Party, at every stage of the revolution the Party used gang connections, or worked through people who had ties to the gangs, to carry on revolutionary work. I now recall a few fragments, for the reference of comrades studying the modern revolutionary and social history of China.

From the First Revolutionary Civil War into the Second, for a time I worked in the CCP's Beijing municipal committee. One side of that work was to keep in touch with, and hold together, through Hu Egong (also known as Hu Nanhu, once a vice-minister of education in the Beiyang government), certain men who in earlier years had followed Sun Yat-sen and had done gang work in concert with him—among them Wu Jinglian (for a time speaker of the House of Representatives after the 1911 Revolution) and Zhang Bolie (once deputy speaker of the House). These men held a certain standing in political and social life then, knew fairly well the inner workings of the reactionaries, and were not easily marked by the reactionary authorities. Since the failure of the Revolution of 1911 they had grown pessimistic and disheartened about the state of the times, and they thought us young Communists full of vigor and pinned the revolution's future on us. And so, when we carried on revolutionary activity, they did what they could to give us cover, and from time to time told us how things stood on every side.

Under the influence of our Party's political views, Hu Egong established ties with Li Dazhao, kept up frequent dealings with Karakhan, an official of the Soviet embassy in China, and later joined our Party, where he did well. He was on close private terms with Yang Du, and under his influence Yang Du once met secretly with Li Dazhao; it was Hu who led Yang Du into the Communist Party. In April 1927, Yang Du learned from the Fengtian warlords that Zhang Zuolin, heedless of international usage, meant to seize Li Dazhao, who was lodging in the Soviet embassy in the Legation Quarter; he told Hu Egong at once, and Hu at once informed Li Dazhao. Unfortunately Li set no store by the warning, and so was arrested and gave his life.

In 1929 Hu Egong was sent by the organization to Shanghai, where he took part in the work of the Shanghai Mutual Aid Society headed by the Communist Dai Xiaodong, and later joined the Anti-Imperialist League and became one of its active members, at the same time using the gangs to do a good deal of work. By this time Yang Du was already a Communist, and was in Shanghai too, doing gang work on the Party's instructions as a senior adviser retained by Du Yuesheng. He once thought to use the gangs' strength in the Lake Tai region to organize an armed struggle, but did not succeed. Because Du Yuesheng had many disciples within the French Concession's Council and the International Settlement's Municipal Council, we came to understand the gangs' organization and activities through Yang Du, and at the same time exerted some influence on Du Yuesheng himself.

Yang Du died at the end of 1931, leaving word to his sons Yang Gongpu and Yang Gongzhao to go on doing revolutionary work under our Party's leadership. Afterward Hu Egong, following Yang Du's charge, introduced Yang Gongpu and Yang Gongzhao to us. At that time Yang Gongpu was secretary-general of Tsinghua University, and he introduced to us the president of Tsinghua, Weng Wenhao, creating favorable conditions for the unfolding of the Party's work.

In 1927, in the Rehe-Chahar region, there was a gang boss named Huang Zihe (also called Huang Shouzhong), of fair power thereabouts, who had under him an important lieutenant, Song Lanpo. The Communist Mao Jun (then an engineer at the Beijing Telephone Bureau, and after liberation a deputy director of that bureau) and Zhang Zhutang sent the two comrades Geng Shunqing and Zhang Jigen to make contact with Song Lanpo and Huang Zihe, meaning to use the gang strength they commanded to organize a guerrilla force of one or two thousand. For various reasons it did not come off, but a certain organizational footing was laid in the Rehe-Chahar area. By May 1933, when Feng Yuxiang, under our Party's influence, organized the People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army at Zhangjiakou, this force all joined it, and Huang Shouzhong served as an army commander.

In the 1930s there was in Shanghai a man named Song Qirong, who had ties to the gangs of Shengxian in Zhejiang. He once worked for our Party's underground organs in Shanghai; his open identity was that of a secret agent of the Songhu Garrison Command, and he secretly gave cover to our Party's organs and supplied important intelligence. In 1929, when Li Lisan returned to Shanghai and was recognized by a fellow townsman who went to inform on him, the matter fell into Song's hands, and he reported it to the Party organization in time, so that Comrade Lisan was able to escape danger.

After the August Thirteenth resistance of 1937, when the Japanese army seized Shanghai, just as they were busy clearing the battlefield and had not yet got round to plundering the many stores of goods in Zhabei and Nanshi, we sent Song Qirong to work with the parties concerned and spirit away some of the goods to support the anti-Japanese forces. In 1947, when the former Kuomintang air-force officer Liu Shanben flew his plane over in revolt and went to Yan'an, his family in Shanghai came under close watch by Kuomintang agents and lost their means of living, and we used Song Qirong to relieve the Liu household.

In the Second Revolutionary Civil War and the War of Resistance, the Shanghai underground Party came to understand conditions on every side through many kinds of ties and channels. On the gang side its chief objects were the gangs' upper figures, among whom the more prominent were Gao Hansheng, Wang Cibin, Tan Daonan, Qiu Zipei, and Yao Wencheng. Gao Hansheng, a Hubei man, in his early years followed Sun Yat-sen in Japan in democratic revolutionary activity; in the time of Yuan Shikai's usurpation he served as a member of parliament, and leaned in thought toward anarchism. In Shanghai he had founded the Five Sages Mountain and was a Hong Gang chief. Wang Cibin, an Anhui man, was a sworn brother of Yang Hu; his father had once followed Sun Yat-sen in the democratic revolution. He ran an opium den in Shanghai, and though society generally took him for both anti-Communist and anti-Chiang, he in fact sympathized with the revolution. We made use of his outward color of backwardness, having him appear at certain political activities as cover. Tan Daonan, a Hunan man, was on friendly terms with the Zhili warlord Wu Peifu and had served as director of a railway bureau. During the war he trimmed between the Japanese and the puppets: at times Zhou Fohai sent him living expenses, the chiefs of the "Peace Army" courted him hard, and the agent Wan Lilang of No. 76 Jessfield Road in Shanghai had ties to him—all of them hoping to find, through him, a political line of retreat. Because of these many social connections, people flattered him as the "Doctor of Socializing." After we made contact with him he supplied us with a good deal of information and roused some others to work for us. Qiu Zipei, a Hubei man, ran the Xinchang customs-brokerage firm on Shandong Road in Shanghai; the gangs' upper figures often met there, and Tan Daonan often lodged there, so we used Qiu to carry on some political activity. Yao Wencheng, a Hubei man and an old-style intellectual, had once organized a Hong Gang lodge in Shanghai and, under Gao Hansheng's influence, worked for us. There was also Xu Langxi, a well-known gang figure, who did some cover work for us as well. Because these gang figures had connections in many directions, and could play a special part especially under the sway of the Japanese army and the puppet Wang regime, our political activity could, as need required, reach deep into every quarter.

In the spring of 1935, the Shanghai underground Party, ravaged by the Wang Ming line, was all but wiped out. Comrades Wu Dakun, Mei Dajun, and others, through the ties of Zhao Puchu with the noted Shanghai figures Guan Jiongzhi and the Green Gang man Wen Lanting, came forward to move the Buddhist world to organize a "Dharma Assembly for Peace and the Protection of the Nation," using it to spread anti-Japanese, patriotic propaganda. That winter they further set up the "Shanghai Federation of Charitable Bodies for Disaster Relief" (the "Charity Federation" for short); its chairman was Xu Shiying, head of the Nanjing government's Relief Commission, and its directors included Wang Yiting, Wen Lanting, and Guan Jiongzhi. Wen and Guan, under Zhao Puchu's influence, gave fair support to our Party comrades working within this body, creating certain conditions for the growth of Shanghai's anti-Japanese salvation movement.

In 1937, after the outbreak of the August Thirteenth Songhu resistance, Zhao Puchu at once set about founding, under the Charity Federation, a "Committee for the Relief of War-Zone Refugees," with its offices in the Renji Hall at No. 35 Yunnan Road; Qu Yingguang served as chairman, Guan Jiongzhi and Wen Lanting as deputy chairmen, and Zhao Puchu as head of the reception section. In 1938 Zhao Puchu further promoted the founding of a "Charity Federation Refugee Instruction Committee," its daily affairs entrusted to an education group made up of our own people. Between 1938 and 1940, using the funds and premises Wen and Guan provided, they ran in all some forty or fifty refugee shelters, taking in refugees to the number of five hundred thousand admissions. Later, when part of the gang men under Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng left Shanghai, most of those who stayed on went over to Wen Lanting. We made further use of Wen's social influence to set up a core of refugee education under Zhao Puchu (the Friday Dinner Club), along with a refugee military school and a refugee wireless training class (not a few of the radio operators and signalmen of the New Fourth Army graduated from that class), and so on, carrying refugee work deep and educating and rousing the refugees to join the various anti-Japanese armed forces led by our Party and the revolutionary struggles of Shanghai's factories and wharves. Under the Party's education, many refugees later became a backbone of the New Fourth Army, and some grew into important Party cadres, contributing to the growth of the New Fourth Army and of our Party's cadre ranks.

In 1943, the Japanese and the puppets, mindful of Wen Lanting's influence in commercial and industrial circles, courted him hard into the water, making him chairman of the puppet "General Association for Commercial Control." Under the Control Association the Japanese and the puppets set up some thirty economic bodies, into which our Party sent comrades to work. Moved by our Party's policy, Wen supplied the organization and activities of these bodies, so that our Party gained a fairly detailed and timely grasp of the enemy and puppets' economic doings. Later, when the comrades sent in were arrested, it was Wen who came forward and bailed them out. As the war of resistance neared victory, the New Fourth Army for a time made ready to march into Shanghai, and he expressed his support to the comrades concerned.

At the same time, among the lower ranks of the gangs too we won some over to do useful work for resistance and revolution. There was then on Fuzhou Road a gang headman named Yang Anqing who, under our influence, used his ties on the wharves and in the customs and aboard the steamers to pass along some propaganda material for us and act as a courier. There was also a gang man surnamed Bai who kept a teahouse in the International Settlement, selling beef soup and scallion pancakes on the side, with many disciples under him; within his sphere, when disputes broke out among the vagabonds they came to him to "drink settling tea," and he mediated. Not a few of the clerks of the Wing On and Sincere companies were his men, and whenever the clerks struggled with the capitalists and stirred up a dispute he often came forward to smooth it over, so that both companies retained him as an adviser and paid him regularly. But in secret he sympathized with the revolution, did some work, and at times helped revolutionaries along.

In 1938 the Party sent Comrades Lin Jun, Shao Shangqing, and others to the Chuansha–Jiangyin area to open up revolutionary work and organize the people's armed forces, using the local gang strength; later they organized, in western Shanghai, Pudong, Chongming, Qidong, Jiangyin, Wujiang, and elsewhere, the "East China People's Anti-Japanese Volunteer Army" (also called the such-and-such Anti-Japanese Society, "Armed Resistance" for short). In 1939 the Party further sent Wang Guqin to organize, around Wuxi, an anti-Japanese force of more than three hundred, and the local gang headman Deng Benyin answered the Party's call and led part of his disciples to join. At the same time Shao Shangqing used gang ties to send Mei Guangdi and Zhu Songshou to the Jiangyin–Changzhou area to build two anti-Japanese armed forces, each of more than two hundred, under Mei and Zhu respectively. Later the three forces of Deng, Mei, and Zhu joined to about seven hundred and were reorganized as the "Jiangnan Anti-Japanese Volunteer Army" ("Jiang-kang" for short), with Deng Benyin appointed commander-in-chief and concurrently commander of the first column, and Mei Guangdi and Zhu Songshou each a column commander. The Party then sent Comrade Yang Jin, under the alias Chen Da, to help Deng Benyin in his work. Later, when several chiefs of Deng's own troops broke discipline and were dealt with by the Party, Deng, unused to our tight organizational discipline, left the "Jiang-kang" of his own accord. In the course of 1939 the Party handed the three forces of the Jiang-kang over, one after another, to the military authorities in Jiangnan; reorganized, they were still called the Jiangnan Anti-Japanese Volunteer Army.

At the end of 1938, as the work required, Comrade Ren Tianshi went on the Party's instructions to the Changshu area to organize an anti-Japanese armed force, rousing some gang members to join, and formed the "People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army" ("People's Resistance" for short). At the same time, under the Party's influence, Gu Fusheng organized an anti-Japanese guerrilla force at Qingpu, also called the "People's Resistance," which likewise had some local gang members in it.

Because the gang men were a tangled lot, differing each from each in their attitude toward the revolution, we met different objects with different strategies and methods, winning over the majority and letting the work go forward smoothly. After the victory of the resistance, a good quantity of Du Yuesheng's goods was for a time held up at the customs; Du had his men send gifts to grease the way, but the customs superintendent, Zhang Yongnian, and his subordinates would have none of it. At his wits' end, Du asked for help through our ties. Considering that Du held a certain social influence, we judged we might grant his request, while hoping he would make some political gesture toward us in return, and Du promised he would. Through our work and our smoothing of the way, the goods returned safely to Du's hands; Du was much obliged, and afterward did some things that served our interests. On the other side, toward the die-hard gang men we gave warning or chastisement, and toward the tiny handful of reactionaries we struck resolutely when we had to.

Before liberation, at every stage of the revolution, we used gang connections to carry on revolutionary struggle in different forms, and won some results in organizing armed forces, gathering intelligence, moving materials, and the like. That these results were gained rested, beyond the comrades' own efforts, above all on the correctness of the Party's policy and the depth of its political influence. This work is but one facet of the whole history of the revolutionary struggle; but summed up in earnest, it is of great help to the study of modern history and of Party history.
