## Miss Tao

Ling Wen was woken by a clamor of noise, though she had scarcely fallen asleep before the small hours. First came a shrill bugle-call, cutting through the thin mist of dawn; then, without pause, a repeated clang-clang, and only after a long while did she realize it was the crash of iron doors being opened and shut. She was in a lockup. In Longhua.

The morning before, a black prison van had carried her from the Louza police station to Nanshi; in the afternoon she was put aboard another, and it was not until dusk that she was delivered here, the van full of the people taken at the meeting that day. Once it had crossed Fenglin Bridge, someone in the van said under his breath that it looked to be Longhua. Sure enough, the van drove into the Songhu Garrison Command and stopped before a small building, and someone muttered again: the Judge Advocate's office. At the least sound of talk the military escort began to bellow.

Inside the little building they had stood a long time by the windows, the dusk gathering outside, every one of them heavy-hearted. Only after dark were they pushed one by one into the cells, and she had eaten nothing to this moment.

But she felt no hunger; even with food set before her she could not have swallowed it. She turned much over in her mind without coming to any thread of it, and had no notion of what she would meet with next. One thing, though, she knew for certain: whatever came, she must never yield.

“Now there's a real beauty in the making.”

Sunlight was slanting into the cell, and someone was speaking. Ling Wen turned her head and saw a woman of about thirty sitting on the bed opposite, a little mirror in her hand, tilting her face this way and that before it.

“You're awake?” The woman stood and came to the head of Ling Wen's bed, thrusting a pretty face toward her, talking without cease. “A night's sleep has done wonders for your color. When the two of you came in last night, you looked a fright. My name's Tao—just call me Miss Tao.”

The cell had had a foul smell about it, but with this woman close by came a drift of perfume.

“Company at last. I've been in here months, bored half to death. If three of you had come in it would be better still—we could make up a table of mahjong—” She broke into a giggle. “You don't often get women shut up in here. You two wouldn't be like me, would you—wronged, and locked up for nothing?”

Miss Tao dabbed a little more powder on her face. “Painting myself up day after day, and who is there to see it—You've had nothing to eat all night; aren't you hungry? I've got malted-milk extract—outside you can only get it through someone with connections—shall I make you a cup?”

As she was speaking the cell door opened with a clang, and the warder said with a smirk: “Miss Tao—out you come?”

Miss Tao smoothed her cheongsam, rose, and swayed her way out; at the door she said: “Nice weather today—I mean to have a good long sit in the sun.”

The cell fell quiet, and from beyond the door came the warder's voice to the woman: “Miss Tao, these two aren't like you—they're Communists. Mind you don't go striking up talk with them.”

Ling Wen sat bolt upright and looked about her. There was one other person in the cell, sitting like her on the edge of a bed—a bed that held only a few planks and a straw mat. Miss Tao's bed stood farther in, spread with a thick mattress, a satin-faced quilt rolled up on a sheet patterned with mandarin ducks.

She looked at the other person in the cell; the two of them had met only to be arrested together. She studied her tentatively and met a pair of gentle eyes struggling to smile at her. This young woman wore her hair bobbed to the ears, like a schoolteacher, and for a moment neither knew how to begin.

After a long silence Ling Wen asked: “Are you all right?”

The other nodded. There was a little moss on the brick floor, ants crawling over it in the sun. She raised her head and looked at Ling Wen, her eyes ardent, a little agitated, as though she had a thousand questions to ask and had not yet found voice for them—

“Here, you must be careful what you say,” said Ling Wen.

“I know you. I've read your novel, Winter. Your name is Ling Wen.”

“And you? And that young man in the jacket—the two of you kept close together in the van.”

“My name is Dong Huiwen; he is Chen Qianyuan.” It struck her that this was the first time she had faced such a situation; by rights they should have come to know one another at the meeting.

That morning she and Chen Qianyuan had agreed to reach the Fourth Avenue market before ten, meeting first at the mouth of Tongchun Lane. A straight lane ran to its end at the gate of Minghui Primary School, where she taught. That morning she had had to go to the school first: the winter holiday was about to begin, and she wanted to bid her graduating class goodbye and hand out their certificates of completion, signed and sealed by the principal.

“Are you afraid?” Seeing Dong Huiwen sink into silence, Ling Wen came and sat beside her and reached out to smooth her hair.

The question was blunt, and Dong Huiwen found she did not know how to answer it. Was she afraid? She was not in the least afraid of those men, yet now that she had truly entered this grim place, her heart could not help creeping a little. She had discovered that only when her heart was full of anger did her spirit rise and her fear fall away altogether. She hesitated, then all at once opened her eyes wide at Ling Wen: “The one who jumped that day on Fourth Avenue—who was he?”

Ling Wen shook her head. From yesterday until now she too had kept thinking of the man who had jumped: had he flung himself out of the window so, without a backward glance, simply to tell them the enemy was coming in? She tried to understand him—as though she felt that if she could truly grasp all that passed through such people's minds at the parting of life and death, she would understand Long Dong the better; how green she had been, writing Winter.

Dong Huiwen thought a while, then lifted her head to Ling Wen again: “I'm not afraid. I made up my mind long ago.”

On the barbed wire atop the high wall outside the window a gray pigeon had settled, and silence fell in the cell. Ling Wen looked at the girl before her and felt some anxiety for her. She had done time herself; she knew the hardest hour had not yet come. The men who had taken them still meant to dig out their secrets. The mission—though she too did not know what it was, they had been called together at short notice, and there must be some important task in it. Did Old Yi know what they were to do?

The cell door opened again, and the warder stood in the doorway.

“Time for your hearing.”

Ling Wen looked at Dong Huiwen and rose—

“You. Out.” The warder pointed at Dong Huiwen as he shouted, and put the handcuffs on her at the cell door.

Dong Huiwen was taken into an interrogation room—not the usual place where prisoners were questioned, which was beside the Director's office. She was taken instead to the Western building she had been to the afternoon before, and the man waiting for her inside she dimly recalled having seen at the scene of the arrest. It was You Tianxiao.

Someone loosened her handcuffs, and in that instant the blood surged back into her fingers, the tips of them stinging a little.

“Take them off,” the man said.

The handcuffs came off. Dong Huiwen fought down the unease in her, slowly steadied herself, and waited.

“Miss Dong, do you know why you've been asked here?”

The taut, nervous feeling came over her again. She stared at the man and did not answer. She remembered what Chen Qianyuan had once told her: if you are afraid, you can be angry—anger will drive out fear.

“Miss Dong, would you like some water?” The man jerked his chin at the clerk to one side. “I am the Judge Advocate's office, the detective squad. You Tianxiao.”

Dong Huiwen looked at the glass of water set on the table and said nothing.

“I hadn't thought you'd be so young—” He made a show of looking over the case file. “A young lady like you ought to be dressed up prettily, going to the pictures, strolling the streets—”

“But I was strolling the streets.”

“Were you now? And strolled all the way to a produce market? And those others—were they out strolling the market too, like you?”

Dong Huiwen raised her head and saw the most terrible smile she had ever seen in her life, like a grinning mask of human skin pressed to his face, the expression ice-cold, a red glint at the corners of his eyes.

“The Emergency Law for Crimes Endangering the Republic,” he said after a pause, in a tone of regret. “A charge like that means the firing squad!”

As he came to the two words—shot dead—You Tianxiao's voice rose all at once to a harsh, piercing pitch. The interrogation room fell silent. He lit a cigarette and blew a string of smoke rings in Dong Huiwen's direction.

“Who told you to go to the library upstairs at the market?”

Dong Huiwen was somewhat flustered; she did not know how to meet an interrogation like this. In her imaginings of the revolution no such scene had ever appeared. Nor was the enemy of her imagination anything like the man before her—this fellow who called himself You, whose talk sounded genial but who made her feel that at any moment a savage face might show through. Still she told herself she must set her teeth and hold.

“Let's do it this way, Miss Dong—let's play a game—”

You Tianxiao stubbed out his cigarette and, like a conjurer, drew a sheaf of photographs from the file envelope, squared them, and laid them face down on the table. He slid one out, waggled it in his hand, tilted his head back, and with a great show brought the photograph up before Dong Huiwen's eyes:

“Is this him?”

Dong Huiwen was stunned: she was looking at a photograph of herself.

You Tianxiao drew his hand back, saw that it was Dong Huiwen in the photograph, tossed it down, and changed it for another.

“I don't know this person.”

Dong Huiwen was a little bewildered; she could not guess what sinister scheme lay behind these clownish antics. You Tianxiao was all patience, holding up the photographs one after another—

“I don't know him.”

“Don't know him.”

Outside the window came the sound of a car engine, tires scraping on the brick paving. It seemed to be Miss Tao, laughing and joking, laughing like one of those brazen women in a tanhuang opera. The laughter carried from inside the building to outside it; a car door shut, and the engine turned over once more.

The farce in the interrogation room went on, and Dong Huiwen saw Ling Wen.

“This one I know.”

The hand drew back; he studied the photograph closely.

“Only just met her.”

You Tianxiao let out his breath and changed the photograph again. In it Chen Qianyuan stood with lips pressed tight, glaring, full of fury. A thread of tenderness drifted through Dong Huiwen's heart; she turned her eyes to the glass on the table, feeling she must not look too long at that photograph.

You Tianxiao slowly drew the photograph back, glanced at it, and set it down beside the glass of water.

“You may drink some water.” He held up yet another photograph.

The farce was over at last. Dong Huiwen felt some misgiving: had her expression given anything away? She wanted a drink of water, but once again she caught sight of that photograph. At once she drew her hand back, thinking she must not do as the enemy said—when they tell you to drink, you drink precisely not at all.

“Chen Qianyuan.” You Tianxiao, staring at the photograph beside the glass, spoke the name of the man in it, and said no more.

He opened the file, found a page, looked at it, leaned back in his chair, and drew circles on the page with his finger. “Chen Qianyuan. Reporter.” He looked at Dong Huiwen. “Schoolteacher. Twenty-three—”

You Tianxiao looked at the page again. “Twenty-six.”

He found Dong Huiwen among the sheaf of photographs and set her beside the glass of water too. Now the people in the two photographs stood shoulder to shoulder.

“A fine match, indeed. The pictures, the parks, the department stores—and the library besides.” He fixed his eyes on Dong Huiwen, his face darkening further. “Miss Dong, Longhua is not Nanjing Road. Once you're inside the Judge Advocate's office, if you mean to come out alive you'd do well to use your head. To die is simple enough—there's no counting the lonely, wandering ghosts in the waste ground behind the Command. I can have the two of you shot together, or I can let one watch the other be put to death.”

“On what grounds?” Dong Huiwen drew herself up in the chair, lifted her head, anger rising in her. She glanced at the clerk sitting to one side and cried out: “What proof do you have?”

You Tianxiao waved a hand at the clerk, who rose and left the interrogation room.

“You think everything gets set down on paper? Whether a thing is black or white is for me to say. In the Songhu Garrison Command there is no shortage of ghosts of the wrongfully dead. I'd advise you to think it over well. Miss Dong, the next time I send for you, it will be to a different place.”

“And what of it?”

“Did you see a pair of dice on Chen Qianyuan?”

“Weren't the dice something you produced yourself?” Dong Huiwen shot back.

You Tianxiao lost patience, sprang to his feet, snatched up the glass, and hurled it to the floor; water, and shards of glass, spattered about Dong Huiwen's feet.

“Talk! Where is Haohan hiding?” he roared at her.

Dong Huiwen's eyes went round; she leapt up from the chair and said loudly: “What Haohan? I've never heard of him!”

You Tianxiao rushed at her and drove his fist into her face.

Dong Huiwen opened her eyes to a dazzling white glare beyond the window, and thought: if Chen Qianyuan was the comrade sent down from above, the one she had to protect was still the same person.

At noon the sunlight brought a thread of warmth to the gloomy cell, and from the courtyard came the warder's cursing. Ling Wen stood inside the cell door and saw Dong Huiwen brought back. On the uneven brick path her steps were a little unsteady. Ling Wen stepped back and stood by the bed.

Dong Huiwen stood side-on in the doorway and looked up at the sky. The warder opened the door, unlocked her handcuffs, and pushed her into the cell. “Isn't this better? Speak plainly and you'll spare yourself the suffering.”

Who was that said for? Ling Wen looked at Dong Huiwen and saw her leaning blankly against the cell door, a bruise below the corner of her left eye but no marks of torture on her body. She did not much believe the warder's words, but in the enemy's prison she could not afford a mistake.

Ling Wen helped Dong Huiwen to the bed and sat her down, took out her own handkerchief, wetted it, and pressed it to Dong Huiwen's injured face.

“Did you say anything you shouldn't have?” she asked her.

Dong Huiwen shook her head, gazing blankly at the corner of the wall. For a good while the cell was without a sound. Had she been badly frightened? Had she let something slip without meaning to? For an instant Ling Wen almost doubted her own judgment.

“He said he was from the detective squad, name of You.” Dong Huiwen looked at Ling Wen, and once she began she could not stop, her words growing more and more disordered. “The moment I went into the interrogation room I made up my mind: if they used torture, I'd throw myself against the wall.” She said it loudly, as though in defiance of the military police sitting out there in the guardroom. Ling Wen rose, went to the cell door and looked carefully out, then turned back and signed to Dong Huiwen to speak low.

There was no telling what mental torment the girl had just borne. Even for a seasoned comrade, interrogation was a great trial. Ling Wen thought of some of the stories Long Dong had told her, and a rush of pity rose in her; she had been very frightened herself, her first time in prison.

But Ling Wen still made herself listen closely and observe closely. At first she had not understood why a sheaf of photographs should appear; soon enough she grasped the intent of the fellow named You. This artless girl—she did not know that all she thought in her heart had already shown in her face. Her life had barely begun, and already she must face a situation so tangled and dangerous. Ling Wen could not picture just what expression Dong Huiwen must have let slip, to give the enemy his opening.

But of this she was quite certain: the agent had guessed right—he had set our photographs side by side by the glass. So Dong Huiwen said.

She asked Dong Huiwen: “Besides Chen Qianyuan, was there anyone else in those photographs you knew?”

“Only you, then.” Dong Huiwen looked at Ling Wen, a mischievous smile just breaking out before it vanished again.

“When did you and Chen Qianyuan come to know each other?”

“You've guessed that too—”

Dong Huiwen was silent a while, then sighed and turned her gaze to the bright, sunlit sky beyond the cell door: “There's no knowing where they've locked him up.”

Ling Wen was moved; she put an arm around Dong Huiwen's shoulders and said: “My husband and I came to know each other in the May Thirtieth Movement. When we married, the Northern Expedition army had only just sworn its oath and set out from Guangzhou. But before long the Nationalists began slaughtering our comrades.”

“And where is he?”

“The enemy surrounded the liaison point, and he had to withdraw to Guangzhou; he was killed there some years ago.”

She turned her face abruptly and asked Dong Huiwen in earnest: “Have you betrayed any secret of the Party to the enemy?”

“No. Do you believe me?”

“I believe you.”

“They asked about Comrade Haohan.”

“Comrade Haohan?”

Within the Party everyone knew the leading comrade who went by this working alias; he had often published articles under the name in The Guide Weekly.

“He asked, too, whether I'd seen a pair of dice,” Dong Huiwen said, perplexed.

“Dice?”

Ling Wen had indeed heard Old Yi speak of the dice; he had found it most interesting. Old Fang had said the comrade sent down from above would produce a pair of dice—yet no one had, and it was the agent who brought out a pair instead. So they had learned of the dice. Had Old Yi spoken of the dice to anyone else?

Could Old Yi himself be the comrade sent down from above? She could be sure neither that he was nor that he was not. A man in secret work might run along several lines at once, using a different alias on each. And she, moreover, was Old Yi's subordinate on the line.

“The one who told you about the meeting—was it Old Fang?”

Ling Wen knew she ought not to ask this; by the rules, comrades working on two parallel lines could not inquire of one another, even if they lived under one roof, were one family. Yet on the other hand, had the enemy not burst in on the meeting, once it was over she and Dong Huiwen would most likely have become comrades of one group.

In any case, she had not guessed wrong. Of the eleven comrades who entered the meeting-place, most did not know one another; they had not, in fact, been on the same line of work.

“We must find some way to warn the organization: the enemy is looking for Haohan.”

Ling Wen was still talking quietly with Dong Huiwen when Miss Tao came back. The moment she returned the cell grew noisy, all chatter in her voice; she pressed on the two of them the melon seeds and peanuts she'd brought back, and said she would soon be getting out. She said to Ling Wen too: “So you're a famous writer! I'm very fond of novels myself. You know Xu Zhenya, don't you? He's danced with me.”
