### 1. An Intelligence Chronicle: Chen Yangshan in Jin-Sui
The Jin-Sui Border Region was one of the principal revolutionary base areas our Party led and built during the War of Resistance Against Japan. It stretched east to the Pinggui line (the Datong-Jining section) and the Tongpu Railway, west to the Yellow River, south to the Fen-Li highway, and north to a line running through Baotou, Bailingmiao, Wuchuan, and Taolin in Suiyuan. Vast in extent, its strategic position was of the first importance. Through both the War of Resistance and the War of Liberation it was not only a vital shield for the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region and the Party Center but the strategic pivot of the Northwest and North China; it was the one artery by which the liberated areas of North, Central, and South China kept contact with the Center, and the international route to Outer Mongolia and the Soviet Union. In economic and cultural terms, though, it was a poor and backward place, thinly peopled, some counties holding only thirty-odd thousand souls, and the struggle there was of the harshest.
Luo Qingchang has recalled: "The Jin-Sui region really was a strategic key point, which is why the Center set up a Jin-Sui Sub-bureau there especially. The region had Mongolia and the Soviet Union at its back. In those days, had Stalingrad fallen, the world revolution would have entered a dark time. With the enemy working against us, what were we to do if the situation suddenly turned tense? And tense it certainly was; to safeguard final victory our Party gave correct leadership. Looking to the long term, we built a base in the Daqing Mountains. Following the Center's instructions, and the principle of being ready for whatever change might come, we carried out concrete work, so that right down to the War of Liberation our work played a great part. When Chiang Kai-shek launched his third anti-Communist upsurge, and when Hu Zongnan made his surprise raid on Yan'an in 1947, Jin-Sui had supplied the intelligence in advance."
The Jin-Sui Border Region had a good mass base and lay close to the Party Center, and the Center gave its work there enormous support. The Central Intelligence Department backed intelligence work in Jin-Sui strongly: from the very start it sent cadres again and again to help set it up, kept sending intelligence personnel and shipping radio sets through Jin-Sui into the enemy-occupied areas, and handed a number of important connections to Jin-Sui to manage. The attention of the Center's leaders was the fundamental guarantee of the work's success.
The main directions of intelligence work in the Jin-Sui region were these: at close range, the cities held by the Japanese and their puppets and by Chiang (Kai-shek), Yan (Xishan), and Fu (Zuoyi), namely Taiyuan, Datong, Guisui, Baotou, and Yulin; and at long range, the great cities of Xi'an, Shijiazhuang, Beiping, Tianjin, and the like.
Throughout the revolutionary war years the struggle between ourselves and the enemy in the Jin-Sui region was cruel and complicated, and life there was hard and bitter. It was precisely under such conditions that the Jin-Sui intelligence organs not only made an important contribution to the liberation of the motherland but trained and tempered a staunch and capable corps of intelligence workers. In the struggle against the enemy they were the outposts, the sharp-pointed vanguard; some fell in battle, and some, taken prisoner, held firm and unbending and gave their lives. The noble character of these intelligence workers, and their image of resource and courage, remain forever in the annals of the Party's intelligence work.
In summing up that stretch of Chen Yangshan's revolutionary life given to the struggle against the enemy in the Jin-Sui region, the Party organization wrote: "From the beginning of 1945 until the founding of the People's Republic of China, Comrade Chen Yangshan helped lead the political-legal work of the Jin-Sui liberated area, serving in turn as director of the Investigation Bureau of the CCP Jin-Sui Sub-bureau, deputy director and then director of the Jin-Sui Border Region Public Security Bureau, member of the CCP Northwest Bureau and head of its Social Affairs Department, head of the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region Public Security Office, and director of the Xi'an Municipal Public Security Bureau. In this period he faithfully carried out the general line for winning nationwide victory laid down by the Party's Seventh Congress, led the broad body of political-legal cadres, boldly roused the masses, waged the struggle against the enemy, and helped direct the takeover of large and medium cities, achieving notable results in rooting out bandits and secret agents, defending the border region, and building and consolidating revolutionary power, and accumulating a rich store of experience."
In early 1945 the Central Intelligence Department, acting on the call "to work earnestly and actively in the occupied areas; to strengthen, raise, and expand the work of the armed working teams; to strike at the enemy in an organized and vigorous military way; to strengthen and develop inside-line work; to apply unified leadership, organize the strength of Party, government, army, and people, and wage total war; to develop the militia and mount a demolition campaign; and to keep strengthening work in the border zones," and in light of the state of the hidden-front struggle in Jin-Sui, sent Chen Yangshan, Feng Jiping, Liang Hanbing, and others to strengthen the work of the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau, which was placed under the direct leadership of the CCP Jin-Sui Sub-bureau. The first task was the vetting of cadres, freeing them, and rebuilding the several sections and the intelligence stations. Under director Chen Yangshan (a deputy director, Zhou Quan, was added later) were set up a cadres section, a communications section, a research section, a books-and-press section, and a general-affairs section; a body of core personnel was recruited from the troops and the Jin-Sui Party School to be sent out, communications personnel were actively selected to restore contact with the old inside-line connections, and a route was promptly opened for passing books, press, and intelligence from the several stations to the Investigation Bureau and on through Suide to Yan'an.
Once in office, Chen Yangshan, to strengthen grassroots intelligence work in Jin-Sui, sent a good many cadres down to posts at the base level.
On April 2 the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau carried out a second large dispatch of personnel, sending more than twenty intelligence cadres in all to the great cities marked for capture: some to Tianjin, some to Beiping, some to Shijiazhuang, and some to Taiyuan.
In April, after the Jin-Sui Public Security Bureau issued its directive on strengthening inside-line work, intelligence-dispatch work in the public-security system began to draw attention and was revived. The head bureau set up three intelligence stations: the Hexi station, working mainly in the Yulin area; the Xinguo station, working mainly in the Datong area; and the Wenshui station, working mainly in the Taiyuan area.
On May 19 the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau under Chen Yangshan issued a work report and work plan, reporting to the Central Intelligence Department on the four-odd months of work since operations had resumed at the start of the year, and setting out six lessons, among which it stressed above all that solving the communications problem was the precondition of intelligence work.
On August 1 the Taiyuan station learned of important intelligence about secret talks between the puppet forces and Yan Xishan's representatives, and Chen Yangshan, on receiving it, promptly reported it to the Central Intelligence Department.
In September, after the Shangdang Campaign, Yan Xishan reorganized his Shanxi army; the reorganization plan was obtained and sent out by our Taiyuan intelligence connection, and drew praise from the leadership of the Jin-Sui Military Region.
On December 15, in its directive "The Line for Work in the Liberated Areas in 1946," the CCP Central Committee called on every region to push the "Gao Shuxun Movement" forward vigorously.
The same day, to meet the new situation and strengthen unified leadership, after full deliberation and discussion in the CCP Jin-Sui Sub-bureau and with the Center's consent, the Jin-Sui Public Security Bureau and the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau were merged, with Tan Zhengwen as director and Chen Yangshan as deputy director.
On December 16 the Investigation Bureau sent out its "Letter to the Stations on the Merger," saying that the merger would achieve unified leadership and put "attack and defense" under one hand. Now that intelligence work had a base among the lower ranks of the masses, it would be easier to develop, and a large number of cadres could be drawn off to strengthen the base level. The merger was completed on December 26; the merged Jin-Sui Security Office had under it three offices and ten sections.
On March 2, 1947, He Long and Li Jingquan spoke at a public-security conference. He Long said: "For eight or nine years now, comrades, you have done no small amount of work. In the national war of resistance you took part in the struggle against the enemy, broke the enemy up and squeezed him out, supplied Yan'an with a great many books and papers, and helped the Center greatly in studying the enemy situation and framing policy; and you did not do badly against the Nationalist agents either: the agents in Yulin, for one, drew a stipend from us and gave us intelligence." "As for how to handle the agents and volunteer agents in the interior areas, that is worth studying hard."
On March 18 our forces withdrew from Yan'an, and the central organs moved to Jin-Sui. A large-scale land-reform movement began in the Jin-Sui region.
On April 7, speaking at a Jin-Sui public-security meeting, Li Kenong, head of the Central Intelligence Department, said: "Intelligence work and security work look at things from very different angles. Intelligence work calls for a wide range and a mixed makeup, and its methods differ too; it must be flexible. Security work is otherwise: it has gravity and principle, and demands a clear class standpoint. These two must be brought together, and if that is not clearly grasped in one's thinking, the temptation is to push the responsibility onto someone else."
In March the Jin-Sui Public Security Bureau issued its "Circular on Preparing for the Work of Occupying Cities" and distributed the Center's directive summing up the experience of urban work, pointing out: "The hour of nationwide revolutionary victory is at hand. To deepen our understanding of the enemy-held central cities, so as to coordinate with the final destruction of the enemy, the recovery of the cities, and the work that follows their recovery, has become one of the central tasks of intelligence work. Intelligence personnel should be clear in their minds: we are doing urban work here and now, and we serve as the Party's eyes and ears; from now on we must strengthen the study of urban work, learn the Party's policies, and tie our work more closely to the situation and to the Party's tasks." The main cities named for study at that time were Taiyuan, Yuci, Datong, Guisui, and Baotou.
In 1948 the Taiyuan station sent people into Taiyuan to pull out and smash the Xing County dormant cell of the puppet Juntong's Taiyuan station; the major who led the cell, Cui Yaonan, came over and rose in revolt, the station chief Zhang Shoude was arrested, and the radio set and ciphers were seized and sent to the Social Affairs Department of the North China Bureau, which won commendation.
On April 9 the CCP Central Committee decided: "Jin-Cha-Ji and Jin-Ji-Lu-Yu are to be merged and unified contact established; connections should be sought with people of every stripe, and it is the same with relations up and down the line, situations being reported upward in every kind of form. To take, one must first give. At present our intelligence work cannot open out; we have tied ourselves up too tightly." "Intelligence work ... must nurture cadres below, let them strike root where they are, ... and set more store by cultivating connections than by dispatching people."
During the joint conference of public-security bureau chiefs the Jin-Sui Public Security Bureau, to exchange experience and improve the work, compiled and printed some reference material on inside-line work.
On April 13, 1948, Chen Yangshan gave a talk on intelligence work at the joint conference of Jin-Sui public-security chiefs. He said: after Japan's surrender, in the shift from a struggle that was mainly national to one that was mainly class, my work was disordered and unprepared; I had no groundwork on Yan (Xishan) and Fu (Zuoyi), and lost many chances, and after the peace agreement I grew slack in my thinking, laid too much stress on open and legal struggle, and the work suffered. The North China Bureau, for the North China area, assigned the counties west of the railway, those in the former Taiyue District south of Hongzhao and east of Puzhou along the Tongpu line, to the Jin-Sui liberated area to take over, and assigned the counties near Taiyuan that had belonged to Jin-Sui to the jurisdiction of the North China liberated area.
In May the People's Liberation Army took the key Jinnan town of Linfen, and all of southern Shanxi was liberated. The CCP Jinnan Work Committee was set up as a field organ of the CCP Jin-Sui Sub-bureau, with Ma Mingfang, deputy secretary of the Northwest Bureau, serving concurrently as its secretary.
In June, by decision of the CCP Jin-Sui Sub-bureau, the Jin-Sui Public Security Bureau had director Chen Yangshan and deputy director Li Fushan lead a body of cadres to Linfen to take over public-security work in the Jinnan area.
On June 15, to support the Jinzhong Campaign, the head bureau decided to strengthen the work in Taiyuan. The Taiyuan station met at Shagou in Jiaocheng, where Feng Jinchen conveyed the higher-level instructions, fixing the "three great tasks" of intelligence, demolition, and subversion and the "three great lines of work" of gathering intelligence, dispatching personnel, and communications, and assigning duties to the intelligence workers.
In 1948 the Central Intelligence Department wired Chen Yangshan: "Your smashing this time of a section of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation's organization in southern Shanxi is of great importance for the future struggle against enemy agents in Jin-Sui. Take care to sum up the experience, run down the leads, mind the evidence, and press the investigation deeper."
### 2. New Successes: Fresh Merit Under He Long's Command
In a talk at the panel of the leading group collecting historical materials on Jin-Sui intelligence, Luo Qingchang recalled an assessment He Long had once made of intelligence work and of Chen Yangshan: "Your work is very important. Most people don't understand it, but I do. After the Nanchang Uprising I reached Shanghai, and it was through the Special Branch, through Comrade Chen Yangshan's arrangements, to be exact, that I was hidden in Shanghai and then secretly moved on to Honghu. I know from my own experience: your work is worth every cent spent on it, however much it comes to."
At the end of 1944 the director of the Jin-Sui Military Region's Investigation Bureau, Zhou Yi, fell gravely ill. He Long proposed to the Central Intelligence Department that Chen Yangshan be sent to the Jin-Sui Military Region as director of the Investigation Bureau.
Why did He Long value Chen Yangshan so? There was a reason. He Long had once been commander of the Nationalist Twentieth Army; after he took part in the Party-led Nanchang Uprising on August 1, 1927, the Nationalists put a price of 100,000 silver dollars on his head. In early November 1927 he made his way by a roundabout road to Shanghai. To ensure his safety the Party Center assigned Chen Yangshan to take charge of it and shield him. In mid-January 1928, when He Long left Shanghai for the Hunan-Hubei border to open up a revolutionary base, the Party Center again assigned Chen Yangshan to lay out his travel route. Both times Chen Yangshan carried out the task of shielding him faultlessly, keeping He Long safe throughout his time in Shanghai. It made a deep impression on He Long, who never forgot it and would say with real feeling: intelligence and security work is a remarkable thing, and this man Chen Yangshan is a remarkable man too. And so he asked for Chen Yangshan by name to be transferred to the Jin-Sui Military Region as director of the Investigation Bureau.
On receiving the Center's transfer order, Chen Yangshan quickly handed over his work in the cadres section of the Central Party School and left for the Jin-Sui Border Region.
To strengthen the Party's leadership over intelligence work, in January 1945 the Jin-Sui Military Region transferred the Investigation Bureau to the direct leadership of the CCP Jin-Sui Sub-bureau. Chen Yangshan now set out from Yan'an to take up his post at the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau. On the eve of his departure, Li Kenong, deputy head of the Central Intelligence Department, gave a dinner at the Department in Zaoyuan for Chen Yangshan and the comrades going with him to the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau: Liang Hanbing, Feng Jiping, Nie Yuansu, and others. Over the meal Li Kenong set out their task: on reaching Jin-Sui they were to lose no time in settling the vetting work left over from the "Rescue Movement," adjust the organization promptly, and get operations going; they were also to restore contact as fast as they could with the comrades sent earlier into enemy territory who had been lost from touch, and find ways to send out new people; and they were to take firm hold of collecting enemy and puppet books and papers. Chen Yangshan promised to see it done.
When Chen Yangshan and his party reached the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau in mid-January, the former director Zhou Yi and deputy director Zou Dapeng had already returned to Yan'an. Most of the cadres were under examination in the "Rescue Movement," operations were at a standstill, and the Bureau was for the time being run in trust by the counter-espionage department of the Jin-Sui Military Region's Political Department. The Investigation Bureau existed in name only.
Given this state of things, Chen Yangshan first threw himself wholly into the vetting work. Once it was under way he said again and again: vetting bore on a comrade's political life and on the recovery and growth of the Bureau's work, and it had to be done well; the bounds of policy had to be strictly kept, investigation and study and evidence valued, confessions not lightly believed; and the conclusions had to be reached by seeking truth from facts. He not only heard out the reports of each vetting group in person but did his own investigating, and himself talked with the comrades whose cases were thought more tangled and drew up their conclusions.
In the vetting of Wang Shukai, for instance, Chen Yangshan listened carefully to views from every quarter from first to last and took an earnest part in the discussion. Wang Shukai's problem was mainly that during his time at the Datong station he had, without asking his superiors, gone on his own to Beiping to gather information on Japanese and puppet activity along the Pinggui line; as it happened, the military sub-district he belonged to had just cracked an enemy-agent case, and so suspicion fell on Wang Shukai.
After repeated investigation and verification Chen Yangshan pointed out: Wang Shukai's failure to ask leave before going to Beiping was a lapse in work, but politically no link had been found between him and this agent case; a suspicion with no basis in fact was wrong and should be set right. Wang Shukai, deeply grateful for the organization's trust, laid down the weight on his mind and threw himself wholeheartedly back into his work and into new battles.
To get a clear picture of the struggle against the enemy in the Jin-Sui Border Region, Chen Yangshan, while carrying on the vetting, went about willing to be a pupil, calling on people everywhere to learn. He visited in turn the urban-work department of the Jin-Sui Sub-bureau; the enemy-work department, the counter-espionage department, and the reconnaissance section of the Jin-Sui Military Region; the Jin-Sui Border Region Public Security Bureau; and other offices, to learn how the struggle against the enemy stood in the border region. Pei Zhouyu, then deputy director of the Jin-Sui Border Region Public Security Bureau, has recalled: one day in March 1945 Chen Yangshan asked him to the Investigation Bureau for a long talk about intelligence work, and he gave Chen Yangshan a systematic account of the inside-line intelligence work of the Jin-Sui Military Region's counter-espionage department.
When the talk turned to how the four intelligence stations set up by the counter-espionage department had planted a body of intelligence workers inside the enemy camp, Chen Yangshan not only pressed every question to the bottom but went over each one with him in close analysis. On the case of Pei Zhouyu's slipping into Datong in 1938 and winning over the enemy's Yanbei security commander, Lieutenant General Zhao Jin'ao, to work for us, Chen Yangshan asked him to explain in full the background of the time and the part played by Zhao Jingdi, who had brought him into the city. Then Chen Yangshan gave his view: "Winning over upper-level Japanese and puppet personnel to work for us is the force of our Party's anti-Japanese national-united-front policy. Toward all who are willing to support and join the resistance we must take an attitude of welcome and union; this is greatly to our advantage in carrying intelligence work forward. But there must be a reliable connection to serve as a bridge, or the aim is hard to reach. Your getting into Datong, for instance, was only because you had the connection of this lieutenant general's cousin Zhao Jingdi; it was he who brought you into the city and made it possible. This kind of bridging connection is extremely important."
In the hidden struggle against enemy agents our underground intelligence workers played a specially important part. Through inside-line reports during the War of Resistance, for instance, we came to know in fair detail the organization and movements of the Nationalist Investigation and Statistics office in northwestern Shanxi. Inside-line inquiry gave us a basic grasp of the organization, personnel, and ways of working of the Japanese "Lan County Gendarmerie Special-Work Team" in the Lan County area and of the Japanese agents' "Kawasaki Mansion." The reorganization of Yan Xishan's agent apparatus in Taiyuan, the organization and activity of the Special Police and Gendarmerie Command, and the roster and postings of the twenty agents that Yan's service trained specially at Dianlingqiao were all obtained in good time by our intelligence workers.
When the talk came to how some stations had impure elements and had thrown up traitors, Chen Yangshan said angrily: "Traitors do us the greatest harm. The wrecking of the Party Center in Shanghai was the work of the traitor Gu Shunzhang, who informed on us to the enemy. Toward traitors, and above all toward those who serve the enemy body and soul, we must take resolute measures to strike and suppress them; only so can intelligence work go forward and the fighting will of revolutionary comrades be heartened."
Pei Zhouyu has recalled that this talk ran over two half-days, and that it taught him much and left him with a deep respect and admiration for Chen Yangshan.
One day early in 1945, when Chen Yangshan called at the Jin-Sui Border Region Public Security Bureau, Pei Zhouyu, then its director, was ill and resting; Chen Yangshan asked after his illness and told the comrades about him to find the best doctor to treat him.
When Pei Zhouyu went to the Jin-Sui Sub-bureau and the Military Region to report on his work, he met Chen Yangshan again, and Chen Yangshan asked Pei Zhouyu to the Investigation Bureau for a long talk about intelligence work.
Chen Yangshan first spoke with Pei Zhouyu about the Yan'an Rectification. He said: "In my few years' work at Yan'an, what pleased me most was the good habit of study, everyone earnestly digging into the books of Marxism-Leninism, and above all Chairman Mao's report 'Study and the Current Situation' at the Yan'an conference of senior cadres. Chairman Mao Zedong raised and answered many questions of Party history, and brought everyone's understanding into closer unity." Chen Yangshan then told Pei Zhouyu about the Yan'an Rectification and about how Chairman Mao Zedong had corrected the "Left" errors of the "Rescue Movement."
From what Chen Yangshan said, Pei Zhouyu felt how greatly Chen Yangshan valued the study of political theory and the questions of Party history, and how deeply he respected and loved Chairman Mao.
Thanks to sound measures and methods, the vetting was soon brought to a smooth close. The great majority of cadres were rescued from the "Rescue Movement," laid down the weight on their minds, and took up their working posts; operations quickly got going, and the Bureau's "paralysis" was over.
Many comrades were deeply grateful to Chen Yangshan and admired this responsible, fact-seeking spirit of his. With the cadres freed, Chen Yangshan set about the operational work. He first rebuilt the Bureau's sections, setting up five: a cadres section, a communications section, a research section, an intelligence section, and a general-affairs section. He restored the four intelligence stations at Xin County, Taiyuan, Yangqu, and Fenyang; set up a new Daqing Mountains intelligence office; and opened two shops at Xing County and Lin County as secret liaison points to cover the identities of intelligence workers passing to and from the enemy-occupied areas. The founding of these stations, offices, and points laid the groundwork for reviving and developing intelligence work in the Jin-Sui Border Region.
To open up intelligence work in the great cities of Beiping, Tianjin, Taiyuan, Shijiazhuang, Guisui, and Baotou, Chen Yangshan set out six points of work: (1) to strengthen the work of each intelligence station; (2) to restore and build without delay the secret communication lines to Beiping, Tianjin, Taiyuan, and Datong; (3) to select and send core intelligence personnel actively to each city to restore work; (4) to strengthen greatly the collection of enemy and puppet books and papers; (5) to develop the study of the enemy situation; and (6) to improve the living conditions of the bureau's staff.
On these points Chen Yangshan sent the communications section chief Feng Jiping and deputy chief Feng Jinchen in disguise to Beiping, Tianjin, and Taiyuan to make contact with underground intelligence connections and set up secret communication points, and, in a planned way, sought out, trained, and dispatched twenty-three intelligence cadres to the main cities, all with notable results.
While taking firm hold of operations, Chen Yangshan gave close attention to his staff's living conditions. On the principle that the body is the capital of revolution, he made improving their living one of his points of work. This care for his cadres won everyone's deep gratitude and so fired the whole bureau's zeal, carrying every task smoothly forward.
He Long was well pleased with Chen Yangshan's ability and efficiency. As Chen Yangshan was new to the Jin-Sui Border Region and knew neither the people nor the ground, He Long, to help him get through to the various departments quickly, introduced Chen Yangshan to people on many occasions, so that Chen Yangshan soon became known. This did much to carry intelligence work smoothly forward.
As a delegate to the Party's Seventh Congress, Chen Yangshan was careful at every turn to put the spirit of the Seventh Congress into practice; once in office he went at once to the Jin-Sui intelligence stations to convey the spirit of the Seventh Congress and of the speeches Mao Zedong and other central leaders had made there.
The line the Seventh Congress laid down was to rouse the masses boldly, build up the people's strength, and, under the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party, overthrow Japanese imperialism, liberate the whole people, and build a new-democratic China.
"Unite as one and win victory. This is the line of the 'Seventh Congress,' and we must do our work well in every field, so that the Party Center and Chairman Mao can rest easy." Because Chen Yangshan took the carrying-out of the Seventh Congress spirit in hand so earnestly, along with the vetting the Party had stressed in the Yan'an Rectification, the zeal of the hidden-front workers was roused further, and the Jin-Sui intelligence stations, through the War of Resistance and the War of Liberation, gathered in a planned and secret way a great deal of most important intelligence on the Japanese and puppets and on Chiang, Yan, and Fu, playing an important supporting part in the region's military struggle.
On the eve of Japan's surrender Yan Xishan secretly sent representatives to talks with the Japanese and puppets at Xiaoyi, and in August 1945 he called an emergency military conference, planning to march in secret and take over the city of Taiyuan; again, that Yan Xishan secretly kept 5,800 Japanese troops in his service, and that on March 28, 1946, he held a conference of senior cadres at the East Garden in Taiyuan. Such most important intelligence of the Japanese and Yan making common cause was all reported to the leading organs in good time by our inside-line connections. Before the peaceful uprising in Suiyuan, the whole run of telegrams passing between Chiang Kai-shek and the Twelfth Brigade commander E Yousan was copied out by our inside-line connections. Inside intelligence that Ma Hongkui had sent representatives to secret talks with Dong Qiwu, pressing him to pull his troops west; that Chiang Kai-shek had sent a great many Juntong agents secretly into Suiyuan to wreck the peace talks; and, above all, during the Suiyuan talks, many of Dong's internal meetings and the agents' doings, were all reported to the leading organs promptly and accurately by our inside line. In 1948 intelligence on the political struggle between the "fly-in faction" and the "underground faction" among the Nationalist takeover officials in Beiping and Tianjin, and inside intelligence on the personnel and internal strife of the three special Party headquarters of Beiping, Tianjin, and the Beining Railway, was likewise obtained in good time by our inside line and reported to the departments concerned. At the same time our underground intelligence workers reported promptly on the arrest of our own people and on inside dealings with the enemy: the Daqing Mountains intelligence office, for instance, once cracked the plot of Wang Guohua, civil-affairs section chief of Heqing County, and Zhang Peizhen, head of the Second Sub-district, to stage a revolt.
Besides this, our underground intelligence connections secretly helped our forces recover a number of enemy strongpoints and towns. In July 1946, for instance, our inside line secretly helped take the Yuanping strongpoint. In the spring of 1947 the Taiyuan station's inside-line connections secretly helped local armed forces take the Fengze strongpoint at Qi County, wiping out more than sixty of the enemy. When Xi County town was liberated in 1947, our inside-line connections sent out intelligence of their own accord and covered our forces as they reconnoitered the ground and drew maps, rendering great service to the town's liberation. In the Qingfengdian Campaign our Beiping inside-line connections supplied important intelligence on the Third Army, which played an important part in its destruction.
On the Central Intelligence Department's instructions the Jin-Sui intelligence organs took on the special and important task of collecting enemy and puppet books and papers. Chen Yangshan set great store by this special task the Department had entrusted to them; he set up a books-and-press section within the bureau, and every station put considerable manpower and means into the long work of buying up enemy and puppet books, papers, and materials. A books-and-press transfer station was set up specially at Fangshan County, and two shipping lines were opened from the stations to Yan'an, so that the great quantity of books and papers the stations bought could flow steadily to the Central Intelligence Department at Yan'an. For this the Department sent special commendations more than once. During the War of Resistance the buying stations used every kind of means, through teachers in schools in enemy areas, say, or other inside-line connections, to take out systematic subscriptions to thirteen Japanese-language newspapers and ten Chinese-language ones, along with a great many Japanese and Chinese books, periodicals, and yearbooks; and they organized considerable manpower and set up a special line to ship the material, so as to get it promptly to the Central Intelligence Department at Yan'an. During the War of Liberation our Beiping-Tianjin station and our Xi'an station also found inside-line connections and bought, in a planned way, more than twenty newspapers and more than thirty periodicals from the Nationalist-held areas, and organized a special line to send them promptly to the Central Intelligence Department; for which the Department wired commendation more than once: "Your Investigation Bureau's collection of enemy and puppet newspapers, periodicals, books, and yearbooks of every kind is of great use to us; keep collecting them systematically, and treat the collection of the enemy's publications and the building of book-and-press stations as an important task." "You have been of great help to the Center, especially in the collection of books and papers." These enemy and puppet books and papers were used not only by the Department's Book-and-Press Newsletter agency but were also passed to the central leaders and to the Japanese Communist friends stationed at Yan'an to read. Through his study of the Japanese and puppet press the Japanese Communist friend Okano Susumu (the former general secretary of the Japanese Communist Party, Nosaka Sanzo) put a number of important views before the Party Center. The Book-and-Press Newsletter won Mao Zedong's praise and attention and became an important source for understanding the enemy situation within the Party. The Central Intelligence Department's pamphlet What Is the Pacification Intensification Movement was distributed as reference material on the enemy situation to the central organs. The analysis of the factions among the Nationalists' sixth-term Central Committee members was written up from a Japanese yearbook and did much to make the Nationalist factional struggle intelligible at the time. The enemy's papers and journals, used to study the enemy situation, were the most important open intelligence material, closely and systematically analyzed; some of it became strategic intelligence of great value and gave the Center an important basis for its decisions.
On April 13, 1947, Chen Yangshan gave a talk on intelligence work at the joint conference of Jin-Sui public-security chiefs. He said: "After Japan's surrender, in the shift from a struggle that was mainly national to one that was mainly class, my work was disordered and unprepared; I had no groundwork on Yan and Fu, and lost many chances, and after the peace agreement I grew slack in my thinking, laid too much stress on open and legal struggle, and the work suffered." By this self-criticism Chen Yangshan had everyone measure the gaps, and he also sketched the gains of intelligence work in 1946, praising the work in Suimeng, the Eighth Sub-bureau, Huairen, Shuo County, Guo County, Zhongyang, and elsewhere, and the setting-up of armed small groups in Suimeng, the Fifth Sub-district, Fenyang, Pingjie, and elsewhere. Chen Yangshan also explained "several problems of understanding: the mystifying view, of not going deep among the masses; the purely technical view; the purely money-and-material view; not analyzing intelligence or studying countermeasures; not sorting out one's targets and rushing after quick merit," and the like. Last, Chen Yangshan said: intelligence may be divided into inside-line intelligence and mass intelligence, the former secret, the latter open and to be gathered widely, and the two must be brought together.
On April 25, chaired by Chen Yangshan, the Jin-Sui Public Security Bureau gathered the heads of the intelligence stations and some public-security sub-bureau chiefs for a symposium on inside-line intelligence work, taking up a number of concrete questions: the form of station organization and the cadre question, the question of working methods, the socialization and professionalization of intelligence personnel, intelligence work in the border zones, the question of long-term concealment, the question of the intelligence system, and so on.
Because the problems were analyzed thoroughly, faults were sought on the leadership's side, and the advanced were encouraged and praised, the Jin-Sui intelligence workers stood as one, high and low, united and all-out, and played a specially important part in the hidden struggle against enemy agents. At eleven in the morning on July 18, 1947, our demolition hero Liang Shengyuan successfully blew up the Taiyuan Number Two Powder Works five li north of Taiyuan, destroying 2,000 cases of hand grenades, 2,000 cases of mortar shells, 2,000 cases of rifle cartridges, 700 cases of 200-mm heavy artillery shells, and several hundred landmines; the enemy's losses were heavy and morale shaken. In the spring of 1948 our inside line reported the roster of dormant agents in Baoding, Shimen, Shanhaiguan, Zhangyuan, Beiping, Tianjin, and elsewhere under the North China supervision group of the Second Department of the Ministry of National Defense, and they were all rounded up when Beiping was liberated. On another inside-line report we cracked the case of the former Beiping Bureau of Confidential Investigation commissioner Wu Leiyuan and Fu Juemin, who had wormed into the Association for the Promotion of Democracy to carry on agent work, and we broke in turn the Xing County dormant cell and its radio, the Cheng Dedi dormant cell at Datong, and the dormant organization at Tongliao in the Northeast. Around the liberation of Linfen, of the fifty-six dormant agents of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation in the counties of Hongtong, Daning, Jincheng, Quwo, and others in southern Shanxi, all but a few who fled were arrested. In the Suiyuan-Baotou area, because our intelligence personnel had already wormed into the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics office, the secretariat of the Suiyuan Party-Government-Army Joint Conference, and the Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Suiyuan station, holding such key posts as section chief, cipher clerk, secretary, and cell leader, the doings of the Nationalist agent organizations in the Suiyuan-Baotou area were basically in our inside line's grasp: the organization and personnel of the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics office; the personnel and meetings of the "Secretariat of the Suiyuan Party-Government-Army Joint Conference"; the personnel and doings of the "North China Party-Government-Army Joint Work Steering Committee" and the "Suiyuan Committee for the Suppression of Counter-revolution and Disorder"; the personnel rosters of the several groups under the Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Suiyuan station and the check-in roster of Juntong men who had fled to Beiping, Tianjin, Zhangjiakou, Yanbei, Yulin, and elsewhere; the roster of the agent armed force and the chief dormant elements set up by the Suiyuan station chief Zhao Siwu; the roster of dormant agents laid on by a certain Tian, deputy head of the Bureau's North China region; the dormant positions of ten radio sets; and the state of such counter-revolutionary armed organizations as the puppet "Anti-Communist Self-Defense National-Salvation Army," "Youth League Anti-Communist National-Salvation Army," and "Three-Mountain Border Precaution" of the Jin-Sui-Cha three borders. All of this, in our inside line's grasp and with its help, was netted at a stroke before and after our entry into the cities, so that the peaceful takeover was carried through smoothly and urban order secured. On July 6, 1948, our intelligence workers set off seven hand grenades five li north of Taiyuan; the enemy armored car's front and radiator were wrecked and the car derailed, the driver, his helper, and the flagman each killed, seven or eight wounded. This was a major event in Yan Xishan's very lair; it awed the enemy troops and greatly raised our army's prestige.
Here that Wang Shukai spoken of above deserves a heavier stroke of the brush. In the spring of 1947 the Nationalists changed their all-out offensive against the liberated areas into a concentrated offensive on the two flanks of northern Shaanxi and Shandong. To support Hu Zongnan's assault on Yan'an, the Suiyuan enemy launched the Northwest Shanxi Campaign, and Huairen, Youyu, Qingshuihe, and other places of ours fell one after another. As the fighting grew harsher still, on May 8 Wang Shukai, reconnaissance section chief of the Suimeng Public Security Bureau and concurrently head of the Youyu intelligence station, led the station and a local armed force of thirty-two men deep into the enemy camp, and at Cai'erying in the Dongshan gully east of Youyu they were caught in a sudden attack. Wang Shukai and his comrades fought the enemy tooth and nail, killing many, until he fell. The enemy, who hated Wang Shukai to the bone, cut off his head and displayed it. "Say not that the scholar only argues in the void; where the head is flung down, the blood lies in bright stains." The martyr repaid his country with his own hot blood; Wang Shukai was a hero of the hidden front.
### 3. Turning Circumstance to Advantage: Gathering Strategic Intelligence
On August 15, 1945, the Japanese emperor announced unconditional surrender, and the Chinese people's War of Resistance won its final victory. With the war's victorious end, social relations within China underwent a great change. The principal contradiction in Chinese society shifted from that between the Chinese nation and Japanese imperialism to that between the big bourgeoisie and big landlord class the Nationalists represented and the mass of the people the Chinese Communist Party represented.
After the War of Resistance, the Nationalist reactionaries under Chiang Kai-shek, to keep their monopoly hold on the economy and their dictatorship in politics, sought with American imperialist backing to wipe out the Chinese Communist Party and rule the Chinese people. Facing this basic situation, the CCP Central Committee framed its revolutionary counter-policy: "to meet them point for point, to defeat the American-backed Nationalist reactionaries with a patriotic and just revolutionary war, and to build a new-democratic new China."
Given this situation, the objects of intelligence work had to change, from mainly watching Japanese imperialism to mainly watching the Nationalist reactionaries. So the Central Intelligence Department, following the Center's policy and instructions, sent an urgent directive to intelligence organizations at every level, stating plainly: intelligence work must take as an important task the gathering of Chiang Kai-shek's plots to launch civil war, and go all out to gather the enemy's strategic intelligence.
At this time Chen Yangshan, having attended the Party's Seventh Congress, was about to leave Yan'an for Jin-Sui. Before setting out he went to the Central Intelligence Department to take his leave of its head, Li Kenong, who told him: the object of intelligence has changed; you must turn your various connections toward the Nationalists, open up intelligence work in the Nationalist-ruled areas in a planned way, get intelligence on the enemy's plotting of civil war, and support our army's self-defense struggle.
"The chief's instructions are the direction of our action, and we will see it done!" said Chen Yangshan, ready to act at once. He then put it to Li Kenong that Jin-Sui was short of intelligence cadres and asked that more be sent. Li Kenong said: "You were a cadres section chief at the Center and the Party School, where there are cadres; you can pick some from the Central Party School." With Peng Zhen's consent Chen Yangshan chose a body of cadres from the Central Party School and returned to Jin-Sui without delay.
In October 1945 the Jin-Sui Border Region held an intelligence and reconnaissance work conference. Li Kenong attended and set out the change in the direction of intelligence and reconnaissance work. He stressed: the chance of civil war breaking out was very great, and to support the self-defense war strongly the region's intelligence and reconnaissance work must take the Chiang Kai-shek and Yan Xishan cliques as its main objects, strengthen inside-line work against the enemy in the cities, and back our army's blows against the enemy's civil-war plotting.
After Japan's surrender Yan Xishan reorganized his puppet troops into national troops and, with American imperialist backing, seized Taiyuan, Datong, Linfen, Guisui, Baotou, and other cities and their communication lines, and launched a large-scale offensive against the liberated areas. Civil war was ready to break out at a touch.
Given this state of the struggle, Chen Yangshan held that the scattering of the intelligence apparatus had first to be solved. So, having sought the views of Tan Zhengwen, Pei Zhouyu, and other leading comrades of the Jin-Sui Border Region Public Security Bureau, he wrote a report to the CCP Jin-Sui Sub-bureau proposing that the Sub-bureau's Investigation Bureau be merged with the Border Region Public Security Bureau, to strengthen intelligence and reconnaissance work, unify leadership, and fight in concert. The Sub-bureau studied his report and found the proposal fitted the struggle and the needs of real work. With the Central Intelligence Department's consent, it was decided to merge the Sub-bureau's Investigation Bureau with the Border Region Public Security Bureau into the Jin-Sui Border Region Public Security Bureau, with Tan Zhengwen as director and Chen Yangshan as deputy director in charge of intelligence. In June 1947, after Tan Zhengwen was transferred to the Central Social Affairs Department, Chen Yangshan became director.
After the two bureaus merged at the end of December 1945, the organization was adjusted, and on the old base four new sections were added: dispatch, enemy-work, communications, and research. The intelligence stations of the former Investigation Bureau and of the Public Security Bureau were also adjusted and merged.
In light of the changing enemy situation, Chen Yangshan studied the work of the several stations in the Taiyuan area and merged the Taiyuan, Wenshui, Fenyang, and Yangqu stations under the unified lead of the Taiyuan station, concentrating strength on gathering strategic intelligence, and set up new stations at Beiping-Tianjin, Xi'an, the Northwest, and Guisui. Besides this, the county public-security bureaus everywhere strengthened their inside-line dispatch work. This made the Jin-Sui Border Region's intelligence work a strong support to the military struggle.
Chen Yangshan was good at seizing the moment and using every kind of force to push intelligence work forward. As the situation changed he set out the points of intelligence work in good time, so that the stations could keep close step with events. In January 1946, given the state of things then, when peace was being talked and war fought at once, he stressed again and again that intelligence work must serve the political and military struggle of the moment, strengthening the gathering of secret military intelligence and the work of breaking up the diehard forces. To this end he assigned the stations three key tasks: (1) to work on the families of the diehard troops, holding meetings and getting them to write letters calling husbands and sons home against the civil war, so as to break up the diehard forces; (2) to strengthen the gathering of secret military intelligence, upper-level intelligence, agent intelligence, political intelligence, and strategic intelligence; (3) to seek out and send connections of every kind into the large and medium cities, the key communication routes, and the armies of Yan Xishan and Fu Zuoyi, to win the diehard troops over to revolt and surrender.
Under this plan of Chen Yangshan's, the Taiyuan station alone gathered 112 pieces of intelligence in 1946, of which 21 were of special importance and 64 important. Yan Xishan's military dispositions, his reorganization, and the inside story of his collusion with the Japanese, for instance, were all obtained by our intelligence workers. They also seized the cipher by which an agent lying dormant in the border region's capital at Xing County kept contact with the enemy's Taiyuan gendarmerie, took the dormant agent, and secured the safety of the border region's leading organs. The Datong station obtained such chief intelligence as a copy of the cipher of the enemy Datong leading group's radio, the order of battle, stations, and roster of company-level and higher officers of the puppet Thirty-eighth Division, and a plan of the Datong city defenses. On July 18, 1947, our secret agents also successfully set off the enemy and puppet Number Two Powder Works at Taiyuan, dealing the enemy heavy losses.
After the Party Center issued its directive to mount the "Gao Shuxun Movement," Chen Yangshan set great store by it and pressed its carrying-out hard.
On October 30, 1945, Gao Shuxun, deputy commander-in-chief of the Nationalist Eleventh War Zone, led an army and a column in revolt at the Handan civil-war front, to great effect across the country. To further the work of splitting and breaking up the Nationalist forces and winning them over to revolt, the CCP Central Committee decided to mount a propaganda campaign toward the Nationalist forces, calling on their officers and men to follow the example of Gao Shuxun's troops: to refuse to attack the liberated areas, to strike on the civil-war battlefield, to fraternize with the People's Liberation Army, to rise in revolt, and to come over to the people's side. This campaign was called the "Gao Shuxun Movement." In "The Line for Work in the Liberated Areas in 1946" Mao Zedong called for the "Gao Shuxun Movement" and said: to smash the Nationalist offensive, our Party must work to split every Nationalist force that is preparing to attack or is attacking. On the one hand, our army is to carry on open, broad political propaganda and a political offensive against the Nationalist forces, to break up the fighting will of the Nationalist civil-war troops; on the other, we must prepare and organize revolts from within the Nationalist forces and mount the "Gao Shuxun Movement," so that at the critical hour of battle great numbers of Nationalist troops will follow Gao Shuxun's example, come over to the people's side, oppose civil war, and stand for peace. To carry this work through in earnest and to quick effect, every place must, following the Center's instructions, set up a special department, assign a large body of cadres, and give itself wholly to the work; and the leading organs everywhere must guide it closely.
Answering the call of the "Gao Shuxun Movement," on May 31, 1946, Pan Shuoduan, commander of the Nationalist 184th Division, led his forces in revolt at Haicheng, and on June 8 Gao Shuxun wired his congratulations: "Last year's Handan uprising was meant to save the nation and rescue the people; I rejoice to hear of your division's righteous act, and feel the more that a just cause finds many helpers. Henceforth I hope we may keep in close touch and press our struggle forward." On June 26, 1946, Captain Liu Shanben of the Nationalist air force's Eighth Flying Squadron flew a B-24 bomber in revolt "along Gao Shuxun's road," from Chengdu to Yan'an. His revolt sent another tremor through the Nationalist forces.
At the Spring Festival of 1946 Chen Yangshan directed the stations to mobilize the kin of enemy and puppet soldiers within the base areas to write letters or go in person to visit their soldier relatives, urging the diehard troops to see the situation clearly and forsake the dark for the light; and in the enemy-occupied areas to use every usable intelligence connection to move the diehard troops to turn their coats and rise on the battlefield or make peaceful revolt. Since most of the families of Yan Xishan's soldiers were in the liberated areas, the stations, on Chen Yangshan's plan, from 1946 to 1947 called on the families of Yan's troops to write letters or visit them, and the prefectural and county public-security organs across Jin-Sui broadly carried on the mass work of dispatch and of calling husbands and sons home to break up the enemy forces, to great effect. By the year-end 1946 reports of the Guo County, Xin County, and Ningwu county public-security bureaus alone, 1,143 enemy troops had been broken away, bringing over 6 machine guns, 66 rifles, and 3 pistols. In February 1946, with the secret help of our inside line in the Guo County public-security bureau, more than 500 men of the Guo County self-defense corps were broken away. In this the soldiers' families played an important part. Two years of mobilizing and persuading brought notable results and threw the men of Yan's army and the puppet local armed forces into panic and low morale.
Chen Yangshan was good at reading the situation, and in light of new circumstances adjusted the organization and changed the working methods to fit. On the one hand he took hold of building a sound cadre corps; at the same time he pressed hard on building up intelligence operations, and dispatch work above all. In 1947, with his approval, several dozen able cadres were drawn from the organs and troops and sent into the great cities to carry on intelligence work, so that the stations were both filled out in personnel and strengthened in leadership. In inside-line work Chen Yangshan not only drew able cadres to send into the enemy camp but boldly developed a body of enemy and puppet personnel to work for us. Some of these inside-line connections played an important part in the battles for Beiping, Tianjin, and other cities.
In his working methods Chen Yangshan did not stick to old ruts. When the land-reform movement began in the autumn of 1947, Chen Yangshan pointed out clearly: in the War of Resistance the landlords had been objects to unite with; now, in land reform, the landlords were objects of struggle. The big landlords above all were the backing and the power behind the "homecoming corps," the bandits, and the intelligence squads; the members of those bodies were mostly fled landlord elements, an armed force the landlord class had built, a feudal power that guarded the landlord class's interests, put down the revolutionary masses, and killed our cadres and activists. This land reform was to wipe out those feudal powers, and so we not only could not ally with the big landlords to cover our intelligence work but had to draw a clear line against them. The bullying landlords were to be struck resolutely; the small landlords and rich peasants were to be isolated and won over.
Thanks to Chen Yangshan's timely guidance, the stations' work took no wrong turn during the land-reform movement.
From early 1945 to the spring of 1949, following the instructions of the Party Center and the Jin-Sui Sub-bureau, Chen Yangshan led the public-security bureaus and intelligence stations of the Jin-Sui Border Region chiefly in the following lines of work:
1. Strengthening the collection of military intelligence.
To supply our army with military intelligence, on the base of close ties with the old intelligence connections, new connections were actively developed, and upper-level figures in the enemy forces were won toward us. This work achieved much. The Xi'an station's connections supplied us with the cipher of Hu Zongnan's army's secret telegrams; the Beiping-Tianjin station supplied important military intelligence in the Qingfengdian Campaign; the Taiyuan station, after the Shangdang Campaign, obtained such important intelligence as Yan Xishan's reorganization, military dispositions, and the inside story of his collusion with the Japanese; the Guisui station obtained the order of battle, equipment, and personnel of the 111th, 158th, and 320th Divisions and the 13th Cavalry Brigade and other main forces in the Suiyuan-Baotou area, along with inside intelligence that the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics office and the Bureau of Confidential Investigation were laying on a great many dormant agents in the Suiyuan-Baotou area to wreck the peace talks. Besides this, our intelligence workers obtained the roster of dormant agents in Baoding, Shijiazhuang, Shanhaiguan, Zhangjiakou, Beiping, Tianjin, and elsewhere under the North China supervision line of the Second Department of the Ministry of National Defense, and the inside story of the Beiping Bureau of Confidential Investigation commissioner Wu Leiyuan and Fu Juemin worming into the Association for the Promotion of Democracy, and other important intelligence.
2. Working to break up the enemy forces and set off revolts among them.
On the instructions of the Center and the Jin-Sui Sub-bureau, the "Gao Shuxun Movement" was carried on. Moved by our intelligence connections and inside-line people: in June 1947 Fan Yangbin, a battalion commander of the enemy garrison at Xiangshuibao in northern Shaanxi, led his whole battalion to surrender to our army on the battlefield; in August 1947 Wei Shunshi, deputy commander of a regiment of an enemy Suiyuan unit, laid down arms with more than 400 men in the fighting at Qingshuihe, letting our army wipe out more than 800 of the enemy cleanly; in March 1949 a regimental commander surnamed Wei of the 320th Division of the enemy Suimeng forces led more than 1,400 men in revolt; in the spring of 1949 a battalion commander surnamed Li of an enemy Suiyuan security assault battalion led more than 150 cavalry in surrender; and at the same time our inside-line connections helped the army recover many strongpoints and county towns.
3. Sending large numbers of prisoners and Nationalist officials back to their units to break up the enemy.
After 1948, when the Nationalist forces shifted from the strategic offensive to the strategic defensive and our army from the defensive to the offensive, the Nationalist troops and their military and civil officials grew deeply pessimistic about the future. We used this to send captured Nationalist officers and men back to do the work of breaking up the enemy. Some did counter-subversion work for us; some, when the cities were liberated, helped us wreck fortifications, guard warehouses and factories, seize bad elements, and the like.
4. Moving intelligence connections to contribute to peaceful liberation.
Moved by our intelligence workers, a trusted man of our intelligence connection Dong Qiwu supplied us, accurately and in good time, with important strategic intelligence during the peaceful liberation of Suiyuan. This let us hold the initiative in the peace talks, foil the reactionaries' plot to wreck them, and carry through in victory the peaceful liberation "in the Suiyuan way."
5. Taking part in the takeover of the cities and the management of public order.
When the Nationalists fell back in rout, they left great numbers of agents lying dormant in the cities, and there were besides many stragglers, riffraff, bandits, and remnant counter-revolutionaries left in them. Our intelligence workers had long worked in the enemy-held cities and knew them well; after liberation they were turned to public-security work and took over the cities' public order. This not only built up the strength of the public-security organs but brought many conveniences to rooting out agents and securing the social order of the cities.
Besides this, to support the liberation of the great cities of North China, Chen Yangshan from May 1948 transferred the Jin-Sui Border Region's intelligence stations and workers to the leadership of the North China Bureau Social Affairs Department, the Northwest Bureau Social Affairs Department, and the Central Social Affairs Department, to play their part in the special struggle of taking over Beiping, Tianjin, Xi'an, Suiyuan, Taiyuan, and other cities, rooting out enemy agents, and cracking dormant ones. More than that, he himself organized a large body of cadres to sort out and compile systematic material on the enemy Party, government, army, gendarmerie, and agent organs of Beiping, Tianjin, Xi'an, Taiyuan, Datong, Guisui, and Baotou, together with plans of the city defenses, and had them printed and distributed to our Party, government, and army command organs for reference, winning high marks from every department; for which Liu Shaoqi praised him.
Under Chen Yangshan's firm leadership the Jin-Sui Border Region's intelligence work opened out and achieved notable results, playing a considerable part especially in supporting our army's operations, and so drew the praise of He Long and the Central Intelligence Department.
In his four years' work in the Jin-Sui Border Region, Chen Yangshan played the leading part in reviving and developing the region's intelligence work. He labored day and night for the victory of the War of Resistance and the War of Liberation and for the safety of the Jin-Sui Border Region, and made a major contribution.
### 4. Steel and Sentiment: A Top-Secret Letter from Chen Yangshan

Comrade Xinfu has died a cruel death; and though such a sacrifice was utterly not to be allowed, we still do not know how our Taiyuan connections stand today, whether they have run into danger. We know nothing of it, and we must think of every means to save them; so I ask you to think of every means to learn what Wang Lianzhong and the Zhou brothers have told under questioning, which is most likely at the police and gendarmerie command. I do not know whether Jinchen knows where Elder Zhou (Zhou Jianmin?) lives, or whether Xinfu left any records behind; if you know, how are we to rescue them? My own mind is in a turmoil, and I can think of nothing better. Comrade Jinchen knows the matter better; please offer more ideas to save our connections, so that the loss may not be too great.
{v} The lesson of this is that Comrade Xinfu was timid, and left Taiyuan in too great a fright; the worst of it was that on his second trip to Taiyuan he did not even change his travel pass, but used an expired and altered one. From now on we must mind our travel passes and keep them legal. In the end he thought of no way to cope, such as getting in touch with Lianzhong to find out where he had gone. In short, he could not take the initiative, and lost his head; and I, for not doing my duty in offering him ideas and pressing him to find a way, bear a heavy responsibility in the leadership. As for how to handle it, I have already asked the head bureau.
{v} On the wire about Comrade Li Guangqing's marriage: it cannot be approved. To win the war of self-defense, the Sub-bureau, by the head bureau's order, has for the time being forbidden anyone to marry; please look after him, and urge him that, with a wife at home from whom he has not yet completed a divorce, it would be still less proper.
{v} With respectful regards,
{v} Chen Yangshan
{v} November 20
This was a top-secret letter written by Chen Yangshan, director of the Jin-Sui regional General Investigation Bureau, to his subordinates Wang Qian, Feng Jinchen, and Zheng Xiaoxian. From its contents it may be judged to have been written in 1945.
From this secret letter we can see the daring, the kindness, and the loyalty to the Party's intelligence cause of Chen Yangshan, an elder of the hidden front, and his sincerity and principle toward his comrades-in-arms.
Chen Yangshan was decisive in his dealings yet careful in his thinking, both bold and shrewd. "Let the courage be great and the heart small, the wits round and the conduct square." Such was Chen Yangshan, a hidden-front leader of steel and sentiment.
To win the war of self-defense, "the wire about Comrade Li Guangqing's marriage cannot be approved," "the Sub-bureau, by the head bureau's order, has for the time being forbidden anyone to marry," and one must "urge him that, with a wife at home from whom he has not yet completed a divorce, it would be still less proper." This is just that revolutionary principle of "a needle hidden in cotton."
The matter of "Xinfu" that the letter speaks of leads to a tale that grips the heart.
In early 1941 Wei Jian and the other intelligence workers under Chen Yangshan made contact with the Eighth Route Army's Shanxi liaison office at Qiulin, and under the office head Wang Shiying began underground intelligence work in Yan Xishan's domain, their working contact being the office secretary Cao Yanxing. In August 1942 the Party Central Intelligence Department formally approved their station as an underground intelligence organization. Its main members were Wu Peishen, Zhang Lixian, Kang Li, An Zifeng, Han Bin, and Wei Jian, who followed the Party Center's instruction on urban work: "In organization strive for concealment and a lean, capable body; lie low for the long term, store up strength, and bide your time. As for method: work separately, keep single-line contact, and form no lateral ties." By the conditions of each man's work, three channels of intelligence-gathering took shape: An Zifeng, in the Fenyang and Xiaoyi area, used the collusion of Yan Xishan's Party-Government Committee with the Japanese to gather Japanese and puppet intelligence; Kang Li, in the Xi County and Pu County area, used his administrative post to gather Yan Xishan's political intelligence; Wei Jian and Han Bin, in the Xiangning and Ji County area, used their army posts to gather military intelligence; Wu Peishen, an editor at the National Revolution News Agency, handled the general contact between them and the Qiulin office; and Zhang Lixian, then a reporter at the same agency, ran the communications. From then on, as the Party's underground intelligence workers, they fought on inside the very heart of Yan Xishan's domain.
The intelligence this station gathered then was very wide: anything of the enemy-occupied areas, military, political, economic, or social; they even gathered the books, papers, and periodicals the enemy put out, to help the Party Center study the enemy's strategic movements. Much material was gathered then through that channel of Yan Xishan's Party-Government Committee's ties with the Japanese, sometimes carried to the office on a donkey's back; and the intelligence Yan'an named for gathering they went after by every possible means.
For instance, in April and May 1944, Chiang Kai-shek, to deceive world opinion and mask his face of feigned resistance and real anti-Communism, insisted, when British, American, and other foreign reporters came to inspect our Yan'an, on inserting reporters from the Central News Agency, the Central Daily, the Saodang Bao, and other Nationalist papers, along with CC Clique agents, forming the so-called "Chinese and Foreign Reporters' Northwest Observation Group." The group entered Yan'an by way of Shanxi. To expose Chiang Kai-shek's plot and strike at the enemy in good time, the Party Center directed the intelligence organizations to gather word of the "observation group" as fast as they could. On receiving the order, Zhang Lixian, using his standing as a reporter of the National Revolution News Agency, made a point of getting close to the wife of the agency's head Liang Yanwu, Yan Xishan's fifth sister Yan Huiqing, to learn what he could. The group set out from Chongqing for Kenanpo in Ji County in Yan's domain, and would then go on to Yan'an. In those days Zhang Lixian went to the "Yan mansion" more than once a day, trying to draw word from the "fifth young lady." At first he got nothing of value, until the night before the group, its Kenanpo visit over, was to leave for Yan'an, when Zhang Lixian found a pretext to call on the "fifth young lady" again. She was out, and Zhang Lixian looked through the documents on her desk. There he suddenly came on minutes of a meeting just then being readied for printing and distribution to Yan Xishan's senior cadres; a glance showed them to be the anti-Communist arrangements the Nationalist reactionaries in the observation group and Yan Xishan had lately settled in secret talks. Zhang Lixian decided on the instant, snatched the material, hurried back to his lodging, copied out the several-thousand-word original at top speed, then returned to the "Yan mansion" and, when no one was looking, slipped the original back onto the "fifth young lady's" desk. The copy was passed to Yan'an at top speed. When the "Chinese and Foreign Reporters' Northwest Observation Group" left Ji County and reached Yan'an, the Jiefang Daily printed the document in full; the Nationalist plot was laid bare, and its authors thrown into confusion. The Party Center rated the obtaining and prompt passing-on of this intelligence very highly and warmly praised the work of Jin-Sui.
Wei Jian, for his part, drew on his work inside Yan Xishan's forces, using the contradictions there to win trust, getting into the vital departments, and doing all he could to make chances for intelligence. In the summer of 1941 Yan Xishan, to put his internal organization in order, ran a "summer progressive discussion session" at Kenanpo to train and test cadres in rotation. At this session Yan Xishan's various factions showed their full skill, seizing the chance to pick people for their own faction, draw in cadres, and expand their strength.
The main factions then were Wang Jingguo's Iron Army group, Liang Huazhi's literati group, Meng Jifeng's political-cadre group, Bo Yuxiang's soldier-farmer group, and others. The heads of these groups were all Yan Xishan's senior cadres, sharing a bed but different dreams, outwardly at one and inwardly at odds, each a system of its own, all clawing at each other. At the session they openly sought people out for talks, drawing them in and buying them over. Because Wei Jian was of the army's political-work system, the political-cadre group's man in charge, Lu Xueming, sought Wei Jian out and pressed him to come over and join that faction. To win the enemy's trust Wei Jian agreed. Not long after the "summer progressive discussion session" ended, in early 1942 Wei Jian was transferred to the garrison of the Shanhou strongpoint, promoted to lieutenant colonel, and made "progress-guarantee commissioner." Wei Jian used this to gather important military intelligence on the strongpoint garrisons Yan Xishan was building.
After the Japanese seized Taiyuan and other places, Yan Xishan fell back to the Ji County area, with his headquarters at Kenanpo and his main strength massed roughly in southwestern Shanxi. To hold the seat of his leading organs, garrisons of every kind were set up on the great hills ringing Kenanpo. These hills were covered with fortifications, their trenches linked, tunnels dug through them until the hills were hollowed out, and the troops heavily disposed. Wei Jian worked in a hill strongpoint garrison whose commander was Wang Zhishi. Wei Jian was then the garrison's lieutenant-colonel progress-guarantee commissioner. Wang Zhishi was an upright man, secretly discontented with Chiang and Yan. To split and break up the enemy and enlarge our own strength, after long thought Wei Jian made him an object to be won, approaching and caring for him and sounding and rousing him politically. At first Wang Zhishi did not see clearly what Wei Jian was, but little by little he came to know that Wei Jian ran counter to Chiang and Yan in politics. After several months of earnest and frequent contact Wang Zhishi leaned in thought toward our side. In this time of work on Wang Zhishi, through him Wei Jian got hold of the personnel establishment, troop dispositions, and tunnel layout of Yan Xishan's strongpoint garrisons on the great hills, and quickly passed it to the Eighth Route Army liaison office.
In the summer of 1943 Wei Jian was transferred to the armed command under the Kenanpo Security Committee. The committee had two units under it, the armed command and the inner-guard office, both under the deputy commander-in-chief Yang Aiyuan. Wei Jian served as progress-guarantee commissioner in the armed command. The forces under the armed command were all Yan Xishan's inner-guard troops, comprising the 12th Political Guard Division and the 196th Brigade. Wei Jian took part in the command's business meetings, so that he could keep abreast in good time of the inner-guard troops' dispositions and doings.
In the autumn of 1943 Wei Jian was transferred to the army-organization group of the Work Committee of Yan Xishan's "Comradeship Association." The Work Committee was the supreme leading organ of Yan Xishan's "Comradeship Association." Wei Jian was now promoted to fourth-grade officer of army organization (colonel rank), in charge of the troops' organizational establishment work. Thus he could learn in good time and pass out material on the establishment, personnel, and equipment of Yan Xishan's whole army.
The work went on this way until July or August 1944, when, as Chiang and Yan whipped up their third anti-Communist upsurge, our Eighteenth Group Army's Qiulin liaison office withdrew to Yan'an under the pressure of events; our intelligence organization lost touch with the office, and intelligence work suffered for a time.
Contact with the Qiulin office was cut, but the work did not stop, and this intelligence group's members went on gathering intelligence through every channel. Some intelligence urgently needed sending out, and contact with the Party organization was badly needed, so it was decided that Wu Peishen should go to Yan'an. Yan Xishan blockaded the communication routes tightly then, and passage was hard; as it happened Zhang Lixian had a fellow villager who had settled in the area for years and often went back and forth to the Red areas on business, and knew the roads well. So Wu disguised himself as a peddler and, with this villager looking after him on the road, passed safely through the blockade to Yan'an. Wu Peishen reported the whole of the intelligence organization's work to Li Kenong, then deputy head of the Central Intelligence Department. This was in June or July 1945.
After hearing the report in detail, Li Kenong gave concrete instructions on the group's work: (1) The Japanese are preparing to surrender; our intelligence men are to follow Yan Xishan back to Taiyuan and set up a station there. (2) For ease of contact, the station is to be placed under the Investigation Bureau of the CCP Central Jin-Sui Sub-bureau. (3) On the matter of contact with Wu Zhezhi: cut Wu's tie to the Taiyue Third Prefectural Committee and put him under the station. (Wu Zhezhi was commissioner of Yan Xishan's Fifteenth Prefectural Office, of progressive mind, in touch with the Taiyue Third Prefectural Committee secretary Chai Zemin, and preparing to revolt; Yan Xishan, sensing something, had transferred him back to Xiangning County for review.) (4) After returning to Taiyuan, prepare for peaceful struggle, and try to win a puppet National Assembly seat. (5) A new means of contact was fixed: one, the station was to find a way to buy a radio (Wu brought 400,000 puppet fabi back from Yan'an) for contact by air; two, contact by courier, with specific code names laid down.
At the same time the Central Intelligence Department approved the Party membership of Wei Jian and Wu Peishen.
Wu Peishen came back from Yan'an and conveyed the Center's instructions to Wei Jian, telling him too that his trip to Yan'an had been discovered by the enemy (it was learned to be because of the Drama-Propaganda Second Troupe case, Hao Binnan having been detained and having implicated him), and that the puppet garrison command had ordered his arrest. Wu Peishen could not stay on in Shanxi, so it was decided that Wei Jian should first return to Taiyuan with Yan Xishan's second batch of people to prepare, and that Wu would then slip back to Taiyuan in disguise to arrange the work before leaving Shanxi for Beiping. This was already September 1945.
Soon Wei Jian returned to Taiyuan and lodged at No. 45 Dongjihuying. After entering the city Wu Peishen, under Wei Jian's cover, stayed four or five days at Wei Jian's home and then seven or eight more at the home of Wei Jian's younger brother Zhou Peiji; having made concrete arrangements for the Taiyuan work, he left for Beiping in mid-October.
The Taiyuan station was set up on the instructions of Li Kenong, deputy head of the Central Intelligence Department, and the first thing to be solved was the Jin-Sui head bureau's sending someone to make contact. When Wu Peishen went to Yan'an, two plans of communication had been fixed. One was to make contact through the home of An Zifeng's younger brother An Baozhi in Taiyuan (living at Naishengtang, Taiyuan); the other, should things change, was to place a missing-person notice in Yan Xishan's newspaper under the agreed name (Li Wenfang), naming the contact and address for further contact. After entering Taiyuan, Wu Peishen, weighing An Zifeng's situation, judged that contact at his brother's home was no longer suitable, and so decided that Wei Jian should make contact by the newspaper method.
After old Wu left, Wei Jian, as agreed, ran a missing-person notice for a week in Yan Xishan's Fuxing Daily:
{v} My cousin Li Wenfang and I have long been parted; should he see this notice, let him get in touch at No. 45 Dongjihuying, Taiyuan. Zhou Jianmin, the ___ of ___
In late November the Jin-Sui Public Security Bureau sent Li Wenfang (formerly Zhang Xinfu). That day Wei Jian was at home when he heard someone in the yard calling his name. He came out to find a man in a felt hat and a long gown standing at the door, and his heart tightened at once: was this the man the head bureau had sent? Sure enough, the man spoke the recognition sign we had agreed: "I am a guest from the eastern hills." "There is someone on the western hills to meet you." Wei Jian answered him and warmly welcomed him in. Wei Jian was overjoyed; he had never thought contact with the organization would come so soon.
Li Wenfang was to lead the work in detail and take charge of courier contact. The first problem was the need of an organ to cover him. It was thought best to get a shop for cover; at first he lodged at Wei Jian's home as a relative, and later, through connections, word came that a dim-sum shop with a three-bay front at No. 18 Changjinglou in Taiyuan was to be let. Li Wenfang had an acquaintance at this shop; if it was taken over, its staff could stay on. It needed only some capital and a change of business license to open. This was convenient enough, and it was decided to apply to the Jin-Sui head bureau for two bars of gold.
With Chen Yangshan's approval the head bureau's leaders agreed, but the funds could not be got at once, and the license too had to be settled through connections. Wei Jian thought only Wu Zhezhi (then manager of Yan Xishan's materiel-reserve depot) could solve it. Li agreed. So through Kang Li a loan of two bars of gold was got from Wu Zhezhi, and the license too was settled.
The shop's affairs were still run by the former manager Wang, who was connected with Cui Shou'an of Wei Jian's side. So the Jin-Sui leadership sent Cui Shou'an (formerly Wang Lianzhong) to do courier work and run the shop besides. The staffing was mostly settled by Li Wenfang. Besides him and Cui Shou'an in charge, the business was mainly run by manager Wang. The shop had twelve people in all, of whom only Li Wenfang, Cui Shou'an, and Zhou Peiji (Wei Jian's clan younger brother, also in charge of communications) knew our work; the rest were purely in the business. When all the preparations were ready, it formally opened in February 1946. The shop was named the "Xieyiheng" dim-sum shop.
To meet the need of passing intelligence, Li Wenfang proposed setting up a liaison point outside the New South Gate of Taiyuan in the name of one of the shop's sales points. So at a spot about four or five hundred meters to the west of the New South Gate a wooden building was bought. A peddler's permit was applied for as a shack dweller, and Cui Shou'an ran it. This was got up around March or April 1946.
At the same time Li Wenfang, Cui Shou'an, and Wei Jian, three Party members, formed a Party small group. In April, on the recommendation of Wei Jian and Li Wenfang and with the Jin-Sui head bureau's approval, Kang Li too joined the Party. The four of them formed the Party leading core of the station; by now the station had taken shape as a collective in organization, form, and work.
After Japan's unconditional surrender the situation across the country changed greatly. Zhou Enlai, Marshall, and Zhang Zhizhong formed a three-man mediation group to carry on peace talks. On the surface it looked like Nationalist-Communist cooperation, but in fact Chiang Kai-shek was bent on wiping out the Communist Party and was busily moving troops for civil war. Chiang Kai-shek's chief of the general staff Chen Cheng came to Taiyuan in person early in 1946 to mobilize for civil war. In the "Self-Examination Hall" of Yan Xishan's provincial government he gathered a meeting of more than 3,000, openly clamoring for civil war and boasting that the Eighth Route Army would be "smashed in three months, wiped out in six"; the air of civil war ran high. Yan Xishan, taking over the Japanese and puppet occupied areas and facing the shambles of Shanxi on entering Taiyuan, had to dispose and reorganize the whole and make great personnel changes. To enforce and tighten his rule over Shanxi and meet his civil-war needs, Yan Xishan soon reorganized the various systems of organization, government, army, education, economy, and secret service. The political situation then was most tangled and society in the greatest disorder. The Jin-Sui head bureau's leader Chen Yangshan went to the front many times and gave clear instructions to keep hold of Chiang's and Yan's political movements at each stage, gathering above all military and agent intelligence. Of this group's original intelligence workers, only three could work normally: Wei Jian, Kang Li, and Zhang Lixian.
To meet the needs of the struggle the intelligence workers grew in number too, to fifteen or sixteen. Wei Jian, then in the army-organization group, leaned toward gathering organizational and military intelligence; Kang Li, in the propaganda group of the "Comradeship Association" Work Committee, leaned toward organizational and political intelligence; Zhang Lixian, a reporter at the National Revolution News Agency, leaned toward cultural and educational intelligence; An Zifeng gathered agent intelligence (after An was found out by the enemy in the Drama-Propaganda Second Troupe case, he confessed only what bore on that troupe; the enemy then planted him in the propaganda section of the Special Police and Gendarmerie Command, where he still had ordinary contact with them). Wu Zhezhi, once commissioner of Yan Xishan's southern Shanxi prefectural office and later manager of Yan Xishan's materiel-reserve depot, once placed under our lead, was set, besides trying for a puppet National Assembly seat, to gather economic intelligence as his conditions allowed. Besides, Xue Bomin and He Jiong of the propaganda group of the "Comradeship Association" Work Committee, Yan Handong of the cadre-management group, Wang Zhishi a regimental commander in the 49th Division, Tian Shulan a teacher at Shanxi University, Ren Zhiqing working in the political department of the pacification office, Yan Qi'e a political instructor in a battalion directly under the 43rd Army, and Ma Chen head of the Second District of Yuci, all kept some tie with our side, and much intelligence was gathered through them. This station basically formed a net with a grasp of Yan Xishan's affairs on every side. It quickly passed out, in good time, the dispositions, organization, and personnel changes after Yan Xishan entered Taiyuan, along with all the intelligence that could be gathered on organization, government, army, education, economy, and secret service. The focus of our work then was still military and agent intelligence.
After the Shangdang Campaign, Yan Xishan, his strength drained and Taiyuan's defenses weak, put his army through a great reorganization and readjusted his dispositions across the board. This reorganization plan was got by the military-affairs section chief Zhu Chonglian, and covered the designations, commanders' names, garrison areas, and weaponry of both the old and the newly formed units. Having got the plan, Zhu gave it to Wei Jian to draw up the organizational establishment from. At the end of the afternoon's work Wei Jian found a chance to slip the plan home and copied it into an account book with a writing brush dipped in milk. He began copying at seven or eight in the evening and did not finish until four or five the next morning. Through the night his wife Zhang Ruoling kept watch for him; the windows were curtained dark, an oil lamp used in place of the electric light, and he did not close his eyes the whole night through, feeling no weariness, only a tense excitement. Only when the plan had been returned to the office without a soul the wiser did his heart come to rest. That day Wei Jian tucked this utterly secret and vital intelligence inside the tube of his bicycle, and the courier Cui Shou'an carried it off by bicycle to Chen Yangshan, director of the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau; the deed drew the praise of the Jin-Sui Military Region's leaders.
At the end of 1945, after Yan Xishan entered the city, to fit Chiang Kai-shek's design of false peace and real civil war, he set about forming his southwestern-Shanxi agent organization, the mobile working teams, into a "Special Police and Gendarmerie Command," under Liang Huazhi and Xu Duan; he turned the old political-guard system of the southwestern-Shanxi days into a police system for local security, under police commissioner Yang Zhenji; and his aide-de-camp Liang Jiqing was set to form a staff training corps for the military reconnaissance of the troops. These were Yan Xishan's three great agent systems. It was through these agent organizations that Yan Xishan struck at our underground people; they were a pack of butchers who killed without a blink, diehard counter-revolutionaries. Our conditions for this work were then rather poor, but with effort we gathered a good deal of material on these agent systems' establishment and structure, the names of those in charge, and the organization and doings in each county, and passed it out in good time. In the year and more the station was active, through its workers' labor it gave the Party a picture of Yan Xishan's affairs on every side. After the underground organization was wrecked, Wei Jian returned to the liberated area, and after reporting on the Taiyuan and Yan Xishan situation to Chen Yangshan and other Jin-Sui leaders, he wrote a pamphlet of more than twenty thousand words, A Survey of the Organization, Government, Army, Education, Economy, and Secret Service of Yan Xishan's Domain, of which more than 200 copies were printed and sent to the units concerned, doing its part in the Party's struggle against Yan Xishan.
In late October 1946 this station suffered an unexpected blow and was wrecked. It began with a travel pass.
Li Wenfang had gone to Jin-Sui to report on his work; he had altered the name on the travel pass of one of the shop's clerks and carried it on him, and at the Fen River cement bridge he was stopped by Yan Xishan's sentries, who grew suspicious. Li Wenfang got himself out on bail as the "Xieyiheng" manager and returned to Taiyuan County, and reported the matter to the Jin-Sui head bureau's director Chen Yangshan. Chen Yangshan directed Wei Jian to hurry back to Taiyuan and see how things stood, and if something had gone wrong, to pull all the Taiyuan people out at once. Two days after Li Wenfang left Taiyuan, a policeman came to the shop asking whether manager Li had come back; the clerk said he had gone home and not yet returned.
Two days later the police came asking again. The comrades in the shop grew wary now, and Cui Shou'an came at once to Wei Jian. They agreed to send a letter quickly from Li Wenfang's home in Zhengding County, Hebei, saying he was ill at home, to gain time and wait for word of Li Wenfang. The letter had not yet come back when, on November 1, the Special Police and Gendarmerie Command seized the shop, sent several plainclothes agents, and held Cui Shou'an and Zhou Peiji inside. In the past, for the work's sake, Wei Jian had gone to the shop but rarely, and so knew nothing of what had happened. As luck had it, a clerk came back from Taiyuan County at breakfast time and opened the door to find the place full of plainclothesmen; asked what he wanted, he said hastily that he was looking for someone, for Zhou Peiji, not daring to say he was a clerk of the shop. Told he was not there, he turned and went out. Coming out, he went straight to Wei Jian's home and told him, and only then did Wei Jian know the intelligence point was blown. The case was desperately urgent, and the comrades concerned had first to be warned to get away. Wei Jian told this clerk to leave for a while and not go back to the shop, then went to find Kang Li to withdraw from Taiyuan with him to the liberated area. Kang Li would not agree, saying he had never had dealings with the shop and, if anyone questioned him, would put all the blame on Wei Jian. He would not budge, and Wei Jian had to go and arrange for the other comrades. He found Zhang Lixian and told him to leave Taiyuan at once; from then on Zhang Lixian went back to his native Yuncheng. Last Wei Jian went to find An Zifeng. Though no work had been arranged for him, for his own safety and to keep the other comrades from harm Wei Jian decided to have him withdraw as well. When he got there An was not at home. His wife said: "Two days ago the Special Police and Gendarmerie office called him in, and he has not come back." Wei Jian knew the enemy had already struck. Later, after Wei Jian left, Kang Li too was arrested and turned traitor.
Coming out of An Zifeng's, Wei Jian turned back home to tell his wife Zhang Ruoling to take the children and withdraw with them. For years Zhang Ruoling had been Wei Jian's able helper, running communications, passing intelligence, and hiding material, doing a great deal of work. As Wei Jian neared his own door he saw plainclothesmen watching outside. He hastily told Zhang Ruoling how things stood and had her leave at once to take shelter at the home of Wei Jian's younger brother Zhou Peiji at No. 18 Dadongmen. Then Wei Jian turned back out, slipped down a lane, and came to the place of Zhou Peiyao, squadron leader of the armored-car unit outside the South Gate and a clansman of Wei Jian's, to wait for word. Zhang Ruoling first settled the children at Wei Jian's brother's, then went back for clothes. From outside the house seemed quiet, but plainclothesmen were already inside.
The moment Zhang Ruoling went back she was put under watch. She played the housewife who knew nothing, saying only that her husband had gone to work and not come back and that she was looking for him. The enemy kept watch in the house another whole night, and when Wei Jian did not return, slipped away at first light. Zhang Ruoling got out of the house and went to Yan Handong's home in Dongjia Lane, having Yan's wife Wang Yudi tell Liu Huamin to hurry to the South Gate with word that Wei Jian should go first and she would find a way later. On the word, Wei Jian at once rode his bicycle to Yuci to find the Second District head Ma Chen, a working connection of ours. Ma Chen was out, and Wei Jian found his younger brother Zhou Peiji again. Wei Jian first hid at a villager's home and had him go for word. Just at dusk, Yan Xishan's war-zone political department sent several men of its political battalion to the district office. Zhou Peiji was there at the time, and seeing that things looked wrong slipped out quietly to warn Wei Jian; Wei Jian said, "Let us get away at once, together." They had just run to the crossroads when the enemy came after them from behind. By now it was growing darker and darker, and the enemy called out behind them, "Halt! Halt!" Seeing the danger, Wei Jian said hurriedly, "Quick, into the fields by the roadside!" Zhou Peiji still called, "Don't run!" It was deep winter and nothing grew in the fields, but by good luck the sky had gone wholly dark by now. Wei Jian ran some three or four li at a stretch and ducked into a big water pit in the middle of a field. The enemy, mistaking Zhou Peiji for Wei Jian, trussed him up and hauled him off.
Just as they were taking Zhou Peiji back to the district office, Ma Chen happened to return, and at a glance cursed aloud: "You fools! How could you arrest my village head?" And with that he gave the man of the political battalion a slap in the face. The enemy, seeing they had indeed taken the wrong man, had to let him go.
Later, on September 2, 1947, Zhou Peiji escorted Zhang Ruoling and her children, four in all, out of Taiyuan back to the liberated area, and this roused the enemy's suspicion, and they seized him. On the eve of Taiyuan's liberation Yan Xishan cruelly buried him alive, and Zhou Peiji died a hero's death.
Wei Jian, hidden in the water pit, listened hard for any stir outside. After a long while, when there was no more sound above, he drew his pocket watch from his breast and saw it was past two in the night. That day was the first of the lunar month, and at midnight the moon rose. Wei Jian climbed out of the pit and went east along the railway; at the mouth of Dongzhao village, as day broke, he met a villager driving a cart across the river and rode across with him. Getting his bearings, he crossed the blockade line and the contested zone and returned in triumph to the liberated area. The enemy, having failed to catch Wei Jian, flew into a towering rage; Xu Duan, head of the Special Police and Gendarmerie office, even conducted a self-examination over it, and was said to have been made to kneel a whole night before the portrait of the "Comradeship Association" chairman Yan Xishan in penance. Only on returning to Jin-Sui did Wei Jian hear that Li Wenfang, back in Taiyuan and seeing that the underground organization had come to grief, had felt he had failed the Party and, after returning a second time from Taiyuan to the liberated area, had taken his own life. Among the martyrs of this affair who deserve to be remembered were Cui Shou'an and Lü Lashuang: Cui Shou'an, taken prisoner, held out unbending, gave up nothing, and died under the enemy's torture; and Lü Lashuang was betrayed by a traitor while running communications and cruelly killed.
### 5. Cool and Decisive: Penetrating the Enemy Secret Service to Steal Ciphered Cables
A telegram of 1945 in the files of the Jin-Sui Public Security Bureau, the "Sui-Meng draft cable of the 27th," reads: "Pei [Zhouyu] reports to Chen [Yangshan] a secret cable: 'By reliable word the Guisui Investigation and Statistics office has lately broken several of our organizations.'" The cable told of intelligence men who had stood unbending under the enemy's torture rather than talk, and it warned Director Chen Yangshan in secret that someone had turned traitor. And how was its content obtained? It had been stolen by Zhang He, who had worked his way inside the enemy secret service in Guisui.
Zhang He, who took the name Zhang Youxin after Liberation, was a native of Youyu County in Shanxi. In 1936, at the Zhengfeng private middle school in Guisui, he joined the Party's outer organization, the Chinese National Liberation Vanguard. In 1938 he went to the Eighth Route Army office in Xi'an and was sent to study at the Anwubao youth training class, where he joined the Chinese Communist Party; afterward he did grassroots work for the Sacrifice League in southern Shanxi. In 1941 he was transferred to the police-administration school of the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region Security Office as secretary and teaching clerk. In 1912 the Central Organization Department selected him for the intelligence-work training class at Zaoyuan in Yan'an, where he studied tradecraft, cryptography, and military knowledge.
In February 1943 Zhang He and the radio operator Li Wucai were sent to work in Guisui with a wireless set. They went first to Xingxian in Shanxi, where the director of the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau and Zhang He together fixed the route into Guisui and the recognition signals, and Zhang He was referred on to the Daqingshan intelligence office at Pianguan.
In May 1943 Zhang He and his party set out from Pianguan with Zhang Dazhi's cavalry brigade. They came as far as the base area in the western hills of Youyu County; and since the radio operator Li Wucai had no social ties at all in Guisui, it was decided that Zhang He should go into Guisui first to find cover work, and that Li Wucai and the set would follow once the ground was ready. Early that June, under the alias Zhang Ming and carrying a "good-citizen certificate" from Wuzhai County in Shanxi, Zhang He entered Guisui and took a job as a messenger at the puppet Houhehaote city post office, in charge of the railway-station postal district. Li Wucai, for his part, was placed by Wu Bingzhou, the magistrate of Youping County, hidden as a small trader in the village of Jiucaigou in Youping County, with the set left in the home of a countryman named Wu. But in October the affair took a sudden turn: Wu Bingzhou, who was in on it, was captured and turned, and became an agent of the Japanese gendarmerie at Kouquan near Datong. This threw all the later arrangements into disorder. After the Wu Bingzhou affair Zhang He and Li Wucai withdrew, one after the other, to Yan'an. That was all that came of Zhang He's first entry into Guisui: the wireless-and-reporting plan came to nothing, and he gained only a general picture of the enemy area.
After Japan's surrender Zhang He was again assigned by the Central Organization Department to the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau. During the First Suiyuan Campaign in November 1945 he and Jia Tongfu were ordered to work in the city of Guisui; but the city was by then under siege by our forces, with the two sides locked in a standoff, and the plan to enter could not be carried out. At the end of that year the Jin-Sui Investigation Bureau merged with the Jin-Sui Public Security General Bureau, and Zhang He was assigned to the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau.
In early February 1947 Wang Shukai, head of the Shahukou intelligence station of the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau, passed on the Bureau's decision to send Zhang He to work his way into the enemy secret service in Suiyuan. Wang Shukai and Zhang He drew up the plan for the operation together: (1) fabricate a past that would square with the record of his earlier withdrawal from Guisui; (2) use his fellow townsmen Wen Yuru and Gong Guohua to press for a place inside the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics apparatus, Wen Yuru being then director of the Guisui power plant and part of the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics party-net, and Gong Guohua secretary-general of the Kuomintang municipal party office in Guisui; (3) have Jia Zicheng handle courier liaison on the Guisui-Datong-Huairen line back to the organs, with Hao Ying, under cover as a traveling trader, carrying intelligence to and fro between Guisui and Dongshangou in Youyu; and (4) for cover, once Zhang He was settled, escort his wife Xue Ying into the city to set up house and help with the work.
In late March Zhang He slipped into Guisui a second time by way of Shahukou; once Xue Ying had come into the city, they made their home at No. 12 Goujiatan in the western old town. In October, on the recommendation of Wen Yuru and Gong Guohua, Zhang He got into the "unified-reconstruction" brigade of the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics office, a camp where captured PLA soldiers and arrested Communists were held, and there he took charge of interrogation. During this time the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau put Zhang He in charge of intelligence work in Guisui, with Wang Yanming as his deputy; the two of them, with Xue Ying, made up a three-person underground Party branch. Zhang He passed back in good time the particulars of the intelligence workers the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics office had seized. Pei Zhouyu, then deputy director of the Jin-Sui regional Public Security General Bureau, learned of this and at once wired Chen Yangshan in cipher. The cable said: "By reliable word the Guisui Investigation and Statistics office has lately broken several of our organizations," and it added that many of "the Beijing-Suiyuan Railway intelligence group sent out by the security system of the Jin-Ji district of Jin-Cha-Ji" had been arrested, that some had turned and "given up a number of connections." Chen Yangshan reported this up the line at once and set his people to guard against it, sparing the Party the damage a traitor's information would have done.
In November Hao Ying, a secret courier of the Party, found that enemy agents were following him; later, plainclothesmen kept watch on Zhang He's house as well, and came round to it in Hao Ying's name to look into things. On this intelligence Chen Yangshan at once ordered Zhang He and his wife out of Guisui by the end of the year. Afterward, under Pei Zhouyu and Ji Shukai, Zhang He took charge of liaison with the intelligence connections in Guisui, Jining, and elsewhere. These connections and their trades were: Song Wenlin, who ran more than ten agents in the puppet Mongol army and kept a roast-chicken shop in Jining; Wen Jing, manager of the Hengdeli watch shop in the old town of Guisui; Xu Jun, an accountant with the enemy's Suiyuan provincial government; Hou Weicheng, the resident physician at the Jirentang pharmacy in Guisui; and Zhang Pinshan, a well-known doctor of Chinese medicine in Guisui. Zhao Liang, Zhou Ziyang, Yuan Maosheng, Yang Zengxiang, Lu Jingfu, and others were all secret intelligence men of the Party besides.
Early in 1949 Zhang He was sent into Guisui a third time to help bring over the 959th Regiment, the regiment under Wei Gang, in Ma Fengchen's division of Liu Wanchun's command; the turning was brought off in May, and he came back to the liberated area.
### 6. Deep Behind Enemy Lines: Building the Datong Intelligence Network
Through the Jin-Sui years our people on the intelligence front, following the Party's line of "hide the able few, lie low for the long haul, husband strength, wait for the moment, and shun haste and exposure," took the work of placing agents seriously and gave close care to the choosing and training of the cadres they sent out. The Jin-Sui Intelligence General Bureau under Chen Yangshan sent batch after batch of intelligence cadres into Taiyuan, Xi'an, Suiyuan and Baotou, Beiping and Tianjin, and other great cities to open up intelligence work for the Party. Not a few of them made contributions of the first importance and drew a good number of "special connections" out of the enemy's own organs. Fang Shaoming was one of the outstanding ones. A letter of July 14, 1946, "Pei Zhouyu to Directors Tan [Zhengwen], Chen [Yangshan], and Li [Fushan] of the head bureau," describes him thus: "Fang Shaoming (Wang Yanming) built up many working connections, spread across Datong, Pingwang, Kouquan and other places, and in results he stands alone; after the enemy's surrender he had sole charge of Datong intelligence."
Fang Shaoming was an alias of Wang Yanming, who for the work's sake had also used the names Wang Bozhong and Wang Huaibao. Wang Yanming was a Guizhou man. At the end of 1935 he joined the Red Army in his home district and made the Long March to northern Shaanxi. In August 1938, with the 715th Regiment of the 358th Brigade of the 120th Division of the Eighth Route Army, he set out from Wuzhai County in northwestern Shanxi and pushed into the Daqingshan. In October 1942, when the main body of the Daqingshan cavalry detachment and the leading organs of the Sui-Cha administrative office withdrew to Pianguan, Wang Yanming became an accountant in the supply department of the Daqingshan cavalry detachment. In July 1943, criticized over a love affair and unable for the moment to reconcile himself to it, he left the ranks, and on the way was unluckily captured by the Japanese, who then forced him into the work team at the Japanese strongpoint of Liminbao in Shuoxian. Before long, through Yang Wugong, who had ties with our side, he wrote to Gao Kelin, secretary of the CCP Saibei district Party committee and head of its Social Affairs Department, reporting the error he had made and his capture by the Japanese. On receiving the letter Gao Kelin reported the matter at once to department head Pei Zhouyu, who in turn reported it to deputy director-general Chen Yangshan; with his consent, Gao Kelin sent the courier Li Run to Wang with a letter in his own hand, directing him henceforth to stay in the enemy area and do intelligence work under the enemy-army section of the political department of the Yanbei sub-district. His assigned tasks were: to gather the designations, numbers, and equipment of the Japanese and puppet troops around Datong; to spy out the Japanese and puppet "mopping-up" plans against the liberated area; and, watching his chance, to build up an intelligence organization with care.
Afterward Wang Yanming and Li Run got inside the enemy and puppet apparatus in turn, Wang taking a post in the mine-police office of the Datong colliery; he also recruited two brothers, the Ping-Sui Railway drivers Zou Zonglu and Zou Zengqiao, as intelligence men, and sent the military and political intelligence they gathered back to our side in good time.
After the Japanese surrender Gao Kelin handed Wang Yanming's organizational connection over to the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau, under the Kouquan intelligence station.
In the first days after Japan's surrender the Japanese troops in Datong still held the isolated city and refused to lay down their arms. At this time the mine police of seven submines under the Datong colliery in the Kouquan area, Baidong, Xinzhoutian, Cilaogou, Yongdingzhuang and the rest, banded together some fifty or sixty men with fifty or sixty rifles. The head of this mine-police force was a rabid reactionary; he declared that he was massing his men to wait for the arrival of the National Army to take over, and that if the Eighth Route Army came first, he would take to the hills as a bandit. Wang Yanming decided to deal with this armed band first.
One morning Sun, the intelligence man at Emaokou, reported: "The Eighth Route Army has come to Emaokou; it is a Jin-Cha-Ji unit."
Wang Yanming at once sent Li Run to make contact, instructing him to explain the situation and ask that a force be sent to disarm the band. In the event, a company of the Jin-Cha-Ji troops came, passed themselves off as the National Army, and took the band's rifles without trouble at the Baidong mine.
At the end of 1945 Yan Xishan's 38th Division seized Datong. Wang Yanming seized the moment, while the enemy was recruiting and expanding, to get inside the enemy secret service, becoming a lieutenant-colonel staff officer of the Yanbei Advance Army and captain and chief of the Kouquan police station of the Yanbei office of the Resource Investigation Society. The Resource Investigation Society had originally been a Japanese secret-service organ and was taken over by Yan Xishan after the Japanese surrender. The director of the Yanbei office was Chu Xichun, and its deputy director was a Japanese, Major-General Nakahara. Holding two posts at once during this time gave Wang Yanming great convenience for his work: he built up more than ten intelligence men in the Kouquan area and six in Datong. As "Pei Zhouyu to Directors Tan, Chen, and Li of the head bureau" describes it: "Fang Shaoming (Wang Yanming) built up many working connections, spread across Datong, Pingwang, Kouquan and other places, and in results he stands alone; after the enemy's surrender he had sole charge of Datong intelligence."
In the spring of 1946, while our forces were laying siege to Datong, the Sui-Meng Security Office, the Yanmen urban-work department, the Datong urban-work department, and the enemy-work departments of the besieging troops formed a united-front committee to coordinate intelligence work inside the city, setting up a liaison station at Baishan. During this time Wang Yanming obtained, in succession, the cipher of the wireless set of the enemy's Datong leadership group, and such important intelligence as the establishment, stations, and roster of officers from company level up of the enemy's main-force 38th Division, and the layout maps of Datong's city defenses.
There was a man named Liu Wenzhong who had been an interpreter at the Datong colliery under the Japanese and puppet regime. A former student, young and hot-blooded and loath to wait on the Japanese, he let a national feeling show in his talk, and so grew close to Wang. At the time Liu was a cipher clerk on the wireless of the enemy's 38th Division. He knew Wang Yanming was a Communist, and under Wang Yanming's influence he at last made up his mind to hand over the wireless cipher of the enemy's Datong leadership group.
The guard-platoon leader of Han Buzhou, commander of the enemy's main-force 38th Division at Datong, was one Peng, a Zhangbei man. After the Japanese surrender Peng was watching over the enemy and puppet warehouses, and having made a killing by trafficking in enemy and puppet stores, he wanted to go home and enjoy it in peace. Peng went round through go-betweens to Wang Yanming, asking him to make out a homecoming permit. Wang said: "I can get a travel pass through a friend, but the other side has terms: first, they want the establishment and garrison data of the 38th Division; second, the layout maps of Datong's city defenses." Peng, a plain soldier of simple mind, agreed readily. He first handed Wang the 38th Division's establishment, stations, and roster of officers from company level up. As it turned out, Peng had got the establishment table through the division's captain-clerk and mail handler Ma Zaiwu, an old drinking companion of his.
When Peng asked a second time for the city-defense layout maps, Ma sensed something behind it and refused. With that blocked, Wang Yanming looked into Ma's past. It emerged that the man had once been a militiaman of ours, and after his capture had been sentenced by the Japanese to forced labor. Reckoning that Ma had had some schooling from the Party, Wang boldly decided to approach him directly. After Peng arranged a meeting, the two sides agreed to meet at Kouquan three days later, goods for cash on the spot. A deal that satisfied both sides was struck, and from then on Ma Zaiwu was listed as an agent held and used under control.
In April 1946 the Datong secret service, through its instigation team at Shuoxian, got word that Communist intelligence men had planted themselves inside the Yanbei office of the Resource Investigation Society. The enemy then set about following and watching Wang Yanming and He Dengyuan. He Dengyuan was an intelligence man Wang Yanming had recruited, then deputy chief of the Kouquan police station of the Yanbei office. In the end Wang and He withdrew, one after the other, to our Kouquan intelligence station at Shangwozhai, and their courier Xie Yunguang was arrested and turned traitor. Of this episode, "Pei Zhouyu to Directors Tan, Chen, and Li" (July 14, 1946) says: "The Datong work was largely wrecked in mid-May. The cause was that, just after the Japanese surrender, Comrade Shaoming (Wang Yanming), who led the inside work there, once openly called a meeting to arrange the inside-response work. More than twenty attended, and among them were some opportunists. When our forces failed to liberate Datong quickly and the Yan puppet regime took hold, this part of the opportunists soon went over to the enemy, and as time passed it gradually came to light. The second cause was that too many connections had been built up, all with lateral ties among them; the choice of people was unwise, and even Kuomintang members got mixed in." In this wrecking of our political-work organization at Datong the enemy agents rounded up eight of our intelligence connections in all. Three escaped: besides Wang Yanming, one was the Kouquan group leader He Dengyuan, and one was Peng Ling of the Kuomintang special party office of the enemy's Ping-Sui Railway. Of the eight arrested, seven were got out of prison by the organization through rescue and ransom, and only Sun, the intelligence man at Emaokou, gave his life.
More than forty years later, in March 1985, when the Ministry of State Security set up a leading group, made up of Chen Yangshan, Pei Zhouyu, and Jin Zhaodian, to collect materials on the intelligence history of the Jin-Sui region, the section on the lessons of intelligence work, rewritten by Chen Yangshan himself, said in summing up: the intelligence cadre Comrade Wang Yanming had, in May 1946, called a meeting of more than twenty peripheral intelligence connections to arrange the work, and because one man turned traitor, most of the connections were arrested, and only Wang Yanming and two others escaped back to the liberated area. The lesson of failure here is a deep one.
Wang Yanming was an old comrade tempered in the harsh trials of the Long March, who in 1938 pushed into the Daqingshan and lived through hard years of iron horses and clashing spears, of wind and frost and snow. In 1943, criticized and for the moment carried away by anger, he walked off, and was captured by the Japanese on the way, as witnesses still living attest. Such a rash, willful act was of course a grave political error. Yet his political stumble made the very conditions for getting inside the enemy camp, and for the seven years after it he spent himself utterly, fighting always in the front line of the struggle against the enemy and making rare contributions. Zhang He's judgment of him at the time was: "The fellow came to it late, without any special training, and takes terrible risks in what he does; his strong point is that he is bold and brave." Pei Zhouyu recalled: "He had come to know the enemy area so well that a task no one else could finish, you could send him to and be sure of it." He spoke with a broad southern accent, yet worked in northern cities without ever drawing the enemy service's suspicion. He had only a rough education to start with, but taught himself and made his way, moving among the enemy with perfect ease. In a lifetime of one man's striving, who can be without fault? His whole record shows him worthy to be called a loyal fighter of the Party, or a good Party cadre who had made mistakes.
### 7. Our Man Among the Enemy: Lu Nan's Ingenious Network
In March 1985, summing up the lessons of Jin-Sui intelligence work, Chen Yangshan spoke of the successes too: of how the Suiyuan intelligence cadre Comrade Lu Nan, for instance, had made the fullest use of his legitimate cover as a section chief of Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics, boldly and yet carefully building up, through the open path of making wide friendships, a set of intelligence connections of the first importance inside the vital organs of the enemy's army, police, and secret service; working single-line in the open give-and-take of friendship, choosing his most trustworthy connections and bringing them into his own section to help with the secret work, and choosing the most trustworthy of all for courier and liaison work alone. Because he was skilled at joining the secret work and the open work together, not one of the connections he built up ever came to grief; and around the time of the Suiyuan peaceful uprising, in the struggle to smash the Kuomintang secret service utterly, he made outstanding contributions.
Lu Nan, whose real name was Zhao Xihong, or Zhao Fumin, was a native of Zhaojialihang village in Xintai County, Shandong. As a boy he studied at a mission school, and later apprenticed at the Xiaozhangzhuang printing works in Jinan. Before the July Seventh Incident he left his family and went off with the Kuomintang's An Fifth Brigade. As Li Qiming tells it: "In 1939 Lu Nan came from the Nationalist-held area to Yan'an asking to join the revolution. Having no organizational introduction, he was courteously turned away by the Yan'an reception office, and so went to He Shaonan's security-command headquarters at Suide as a captain-secretary." At the time Li Qiming, of the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region Security Office, was doing intelligence work on He's unit in our Suide Garrison District; Lu Nan sought out the garrison commander Chen Qihan on his own to ask for work, was vetted by Li Qiming, and was developed as an enemy-work agent. Among the intelligence men Lu Nan then developed and led were Cui Jizhou and Gong Zhen. Gong served as head messenger of the Kuomintang Suide prefectural office, and Cui as a warrant-officer special-service master in a company of He's 7th Security Regiment.
Lu Nan built up much in He Shaonan's unit, and made a great contribution to our army's driving He Shaonan out soon afterward and liberating the Suide region.
After the liberation of Suide, Cui Jizhou first withdrew to Yulin with the remnants of He's force. Lu Nan and Gong Zhen stayed behind in the guise of prisoners to await instructions, and were then sent back to the other side under the name of an officers' prisoner detachment. In March 1940 Lu Nan got into the 1st Column of the 1st Guerrilla Army of the Military Affairs Commission under Zhang Lisheng, as secretary of the special-service battalion. There he also developed the battalion's deputy commander Wang Zhixiang and the officer Zhang Yongchang as intelligence connections. That October the column commander Ji Pinzhi questioned Lu Nan about his affairs, letting slip a certain doubt about who he really was. Seeing the wind was ill, Lu Nan fled to the rear, and at the same time sent Cui Jizhou to settle with the security corps of Zhasa Banner in the Ike Zhao League of Suiyuan province. As the Suiyuan Mongolian Political Council and the Kuomintang Mongol-banner party office were then stationed at Zhasa Banner, he sent his man into Suiyuan first, meaning in time to work close to the head organs of the Suiyuan Mongol banners.
In the winter of 1940 Lu Nan and Gong Zhen were sent to Suiyuan by the Suide intelligence station of the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region Security Office. To secure open standing in the Nationalist-held area first, so as to find cover work in Suiyuan, they went first into the wartime mobilization committee of Shenmu County in Shaanxi. It was there that Lu Nan came to know Wei Gang. Wei Gang, a man of Hengshan in northern Shaanxi, was then secretary-general of the Kuomintang in Shenmu County. In March 1941 Lu Nan and Gong Zhen went to the horse farm of the 5th Cavalry Division of Ma Zhanshan's force at Manjin Monastery in the Ike Zhao League. They found Cui Jizhou first, and through Cui came to know Ba Rongchang. Ba Rongchang, a man of Hejian in Hebei, was a graduate of the Harbin Sino-Russian Technical College. In the spring of 1938 he had gone to Yan'an and entered the third class of the Anti-Japanese Military and Political University, and that July had come to the Ike Zhao League to do intelligence work, serving for the time being as a general-affairs clerk of the Suiyuan Mongolian Political Council. At Zhasa Banner, Lu Nan and Ba Rongchang worked together and reported to Yan'an intelligence on the doings of the Suiyuan Mongolian Political Council and of the Investigation and Statistics office of the Kuomintang Mongol-banner party.
At that time Dongsheng County was the political and military center of the Nationalist-held Ike Zhao League. Here were Chen Changjie's Ike Zhao League Garrison Command and the Suiyuan provincial Dongsheng prefectural office, and stationed here too was the Dongsheng inspection station of the anti-smuggling office of the Eighth War Zone, of the Juntong system. And so they fixed on Dongsheng County as their next move. In May 1941 Lu Nan and Gong Zhen went to Dongsheng County, and in July, on someone's recommendation, they got into the Dongsheng Trade Administration Bureau, Lu Nan as secretary and Gong Zhen as a clerk, with Cui Jizhou serving as errand-boy to the bureau's director Zhang Jingshi. Zhang Jingshi and the Dongsheng magistrate, the police chief, and the inspection-station chief Li Heting were old card-players together, who often gambled the night through at Zhang Jingshi's. Waiting on this crowd as an errand-boy, Cui Jizhou set out to win over the Juntong agent Li Heting, and through him became fast friends with the chief of the inspection station's wireless, and so stole a copy of its cipher; Lu Nan, for his part, got hold of a copy of the troop-disposition map of the Ike Zhao League Garrison Command. Having obtained this important intelligence, they planned to send it back to the organs during the temple fair at Jungar Monastery that year. But just as Lu and Cui were arranging to get the intelligence away, in late August the Juntong agent Jia Gengfu, planted in the Ike Zhao League Garrison Command, suddenly arrested Lu Nan and Gong Zhen. By the later account of Li Qiming and Cui Jizhou, the reasons for their arrest, cleared up afterward, were these: first, Gong Zhen loved a good time, played and sang and mixed widely, and their lodgings were like a club, which had early caught Jia Gengfu's notice. Second, as told above, in 1940 Lu Nan had joined Zhang Lisheng's 1st Guerrilla Army. Zhang Lisheng's force was afterward routed and driven into Shaanxi by the Eighth Route Army, and its remnants fled to Dongsheng County in Suiyuan, renamed the Jin-Cha-Sui Border Region Advance Army. Once, Lu Nan was recognized by a battalion commander of those days, who reported his suspicion to the agent Jia Gengfu.
In prison Gong Zhen, weak and wavering, was forced to confess that he was an intelligence man from Yan'an, while Cui Jizhou got himself off by pleading that he was "a rough fellow who waited on people, who took them only for fellow townsmen and cared nothing for any party or faction." But Juntong, to keep a hold on Lu Nan, forced him to pick a courier to go back to Suide first and set up contact, and Lu Nan recommended Cui Jizhou for the duty. When the agent Jia Gengfu came to send Cui Jizhou to Suide with a letter, Cui put on a show, now pleading, now cursing, and refused the task; only when he had won a guarantee of his personal safety in Suiyuan did he go back to Mizhi County and find Lin Ruobing of the Suide intelligence station. From this, and from the coded words in Lu Nan's letter, "I have been ill of late and am very weak," Li Qiming judged that something had gone wrong with him. He also got intelligence from Suiyuan confirming that Lu Nan had indeed been raided by Juntong. It was decided, therefore, to bring Lu Nan back to Yan'an for examination at a suitable time. Cui Jizhou's trip back to Mizhi County smoothly carried to the Juntong man Jia Gengfu a reply arranged by our side. The Suiyuan Juntong, thinking its scheme had succeeded, then had Lu Nan and Gong Zhen released on bail.
After being bailed out, Lu Nan and Gong Zhen opened the Youth Eatery on a street in Dongsheng County. In October 1941 Lu Nan got into the branch office of the "Three People's Principles Youth Corps" in Dongsheng County as secretary. In 1942 Ma Hanshan, head of the anti-smuggling office of the Eighth War Zone and Juntong chief in Suiyuan, transferred Lu Nan and Gong Zhen to Shanba, the seat of the Suiyuan provincial government. Ma Hanshan decided to send Lu Nan back to Yan'an to work for Juntong, but Lu Nan had already had notice from the organization recalling him to report; so he played along, was sent out by Juntong, went first to Yulin, and in April 1942 returned to Yan'an by way of Zhenchuanbao.
Li Qiming said: "Lu Nan was recalled to Yan'an right during the Rectification and cadre-screening, and I was in charge of examining him. At first Lu Nan only laid out the whole course of things clearly. When Juntong raided him he had admitted only to being a Yan'an intelligence connection, and the moment trouble arose he had reported it to the organization. So after a spell of training he was assigned to the book-and-press inspection station of the Border Region Security Office."
After Lu Nan returned to Yan'an, Cui Jizhou stayed on in Suiyuan to wait. Gong Zhen left the revolution for good from then on.
On the eve of the Party's Seventh Congress in 1945, the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region Security Office set up the Sui-Meng intelligence station at Dingbian; and Chen Yangshan, as a delegate to the Seventh Congress, conveyed the spirit of the Congress to the various Jin-Sui intelligence stations, laying down that in the enemy-occupied areas the masses were to be boldly roused and the work carried forward to meet the coming nationwide victory. Under this general guidance the Border Region Security Office drew up a plan for developing intelligence work; with the approval of the Northwest Bureau and the Central Social Affairs Department, the Security Office selected intelligence cadres from the various Yan'an units and sent them out to Shaanxi, Shanxi, Suiyuan, Gansu, Ningxia, Henan, and elsewhere. Lu Nan was one of those chosen to be sent to the Sui-Meng enemy area. Lu Nan had gone back to Yan'an in the first place by pretending to take on a Juntong assignment; when he was sent to Suiyuan again, he drew up at Dingbian a whole scheme for dealing with the enemy service. Its main points were: to exploit the rift between the Central Statistics Bureau and Juntong so as to distance himself from Juntong; to rail at Ma Hansan for, in his hunger for quick results, grabbing innocent anti-Japanese youths and packing them off to Yan'an to throw their lives away; and so on. His specific task was to work for the long term, get into the core of the Central Statistics Bureau, and seize its vital points.
This time Lu Nan settled first in southern Suiyuan, doing secretarial work in the southern-Suiyuan prefectural office headed by Guo Changqing. In February 1946 he went to the branch office of the "Three People's Principles Youth Corps" in Baotou as a section head, editing the Fusheng Weekly. Before coming to Baotou, Lu Nan had already made contact with Cui Jizhou. After the victory over Japan, Cui Jizhou had drifted to Baotou and gone in with others to open an eatery. Afterward, in Baotou, they developed Mi Jingquan and Wang Qi; and Cui Jizhou, in his own name, opened the Wangheyi Inn at Erliban by the Baotou station as a point for meetings and for passing intelligence.
In June 1946 Lu Nan set about opening up intelligence work toward Guisui, the capital of Suiyuan province. It happened that Zhang Qing'en, chief of the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics secret service, had just been named secretary-general of the Kuomintang Suiyuan provincial party office. Zhang Qing'en was busily expanding his secret-service organization and casting wide for talent. Lu Nan decided to defend by attacking, and boldly plant himself in the enemy's vitals. He went straight to Zhang Qing'en, and, following the scheme drawn up by the Sui-Meng intelligence station, loudly cursed Juntong's Ma Hansan for ruining young people, saying that he himself had come back to Suiyuan from the jaws of death only to be cast aside and left unused. Because Lu Nan was shrewd and capable, and because he had a record of having been "sent back" to Yan'an by Juntong, the arrow struck home: Zhang Qing'en took him on as lieutenant-colonel research-section chief of the Suiyuan provincial Special Conference Secretariat. Lu Nan then put Wang Qi forward as his section clerk, with the rank of major.
One mark of the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics secret service was that it penetrated widely into the party, government, army, and police organs at every level. The Suiyuan Special Conference Secretariat was in fact one of the secret-service bodies controlled by the Investigation and Statistics office. It had been set up in 1940, with corresponding bodies in the prefectures, counties, and banners. In May 1947, to fit the all-out civil war about to break out, it was renamed the Suiyuan Party-Government-Military Cadres Joint Conference, its leadership made up of the heads of the Suiyuan party, government, army, police, and courts, with Fu Zuoyi as honorary chairman and Zhang Qing'en, secretary-general of the Kuomintang Suiyuan party office and director of the Investigation and Statistics office, as deputy chairman, and in fact wholly under our control. The provincial Special Conference Secretariat had under it specialized bodies, an intelligence section, a counter-espionage section, a training class for deep-cover agents, and the like, all of which took the party, government, and public-security organs of our Sui-Meng liberated area as their targets.
Once Lu Nan and Wang Qi were inside the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics system, they used their legitimate standing to widen the enemy-work and intelligence work further.
After Lu Nan and the others got into the enemy service's vitals and obtained important intelligence, just as the Suiyuan enemy forces were mounting a great offensive against the liberated area, he sent Cui Jizhou to carry intelligence back to Yan'an in October 1946. Passing through the Jin-Sui Public Security General Bureau, Cui was told by Director Tan Zhengwen: "The situation is so tense now that if we wait for you to get the intelligence back to Yan'an, it will be too late. We will handle all contact with Yan'an." As Li Qiming tells it: "During the Suiyuan war Lu Nan sent men out with intelligence. Comrade He Long, seeing how important it was, wired Yan'an to ask whose man Lu Nan was, sent to Suiyuan by whom, and proposed that the connection be handed over to Jin-Sui." And so the leadership of Lu Nan and the others was transferred from the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region Security Office to the Jin-Sui Public Security General Bureau. When Lu Nan came to the Suiyuan bureau, Chen Yangshan received him, talked with him at length, and set out his specific tasks. Afterward Lu Nan was handed on again from the Jin-Sui Public Security General Bureau to the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau. On November 17, 1946, an introductory document signed by Ouyang Qin of the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region Security Office judged Lu Nan's character and ability thus: "Alert, cool, and able in his dealings, an agent well worth cultivating further . . . he sets store by personal loyalty, keeps faith with his friends, is bold, and has drive. He spends money freely and most detests timidity, yet can also bear hardship well. In eight years of underground life he has drawn very little of the organization's operating funds, keeping himself entirely on money taken from the enemy service."
On this trip back to Jin-Sui, Cui Jizhou, besides bringing intelligence and asking for instructions, also asked how their Party membership was to be settled. The reply then, signed by the two directors Tan Zhengwen and Li Fushan, instructed them on the work ahead: "You should stay on in the secret-service organs, win still deeper trust, and consolidate your position. The line is to lie low for the long haul, get deep into the enemy's heart, obtain important intelligence and every secret, and learn the whole of the enemy service's organization, its policy and plans, and its work (including agents planted inside our own ranks). Press for depth in the work, and shun display. To steal out the wireless ciphers, wavelengths, signals, times, and the like would be a very great contribution, to be attempted when the chance offers." On the question of building the Party organization, the reply directed that recruitment be stopped; as for those already recruited, the notice said: "You and Cui have decided to join the Party; I have already spoken with Cui."
In December 1946 Cui Jizhou returned to Guisui and reported to Lu Nan. But just as their intelligence work was opening up and in full flood, Lu Nan died of illness, on January 8, 1947, at the Guisui Catholic Hospital. According to the report sent back to the liberated area at the time: "Lu Nan's illness was typhus turned to heart disease; penicillin given against pneumonia caused a failure of the lesser circulation, and he died of it." Lu Nan was only thirty-three when he died.
Lu Nan's sudden death not only cut off the political-work connections he had led, but left the work in the enemy area without a leading center. To go on sending out the intelligence Lu Nan had obtained in his lifetime, and to ask the rear to send someone to take charge, Cui Jizhou went back twice in a row to the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau to report between January and February 1947, and it was finally decided that Cui Jizhou and Wang Qi would take over the leadership in the enemy area. At the same time, after Lu Nan's death, the enemy's Special Conference Secretariat also promoted Wang Qi to lieutenant-colonel research-section chief to succeed Lu Nan. Cui Jizhou was placed as head of the plainclothes squad in Wei Gang's 4th Suiyuan Security Regiment. The important intelligence connections still in Guisui at this time were three: Kang Shangchi, Chen Ziheng, and Liu Guoqing. Chen Ziheng was then works-section chief of the Guisui railway district, and Liu Guoqing served in the news agency of the Sui-Meng military academy.
Afterward, for more than a year down to the autumn of 1948, Wang Qi and Cui Jizhou lost contact with the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau. During that time the Bureau sent the courier Hu Quanfu and others three times to Suiyuan to inquire after Cui Jizhou's whereabouts, all without success. The reason they lost touch with the organization was this: in the spring of 1947 the enemy attacked the Yanbei region, and after Zuoyun County fell, the organs of the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau moved repeatedly under guerrilla conditions, while the couriers sent into the enemy area during the land reform of that time stressed only class line and had no means of settling in and surviving.
In mid-October 1948, during the Cha-Sui Campaign, Cui Jizhou was again ordered back to the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau to report. On this trip to the organs, besides bringing back intelligence on the plot of the political commissar of the 16th Regiment of the 2nd Column of the Jin-Cha-Ji forces to defect through contact with Dong Qiwu, his main task was to ask about the way ahead and the whole line. Wang Qi proposed: following Lu Nan's view of lying low for the long haul, that once the Suiyuan city was liberated they should ask leave to withdraw with the enemy.
In this Cha-Sui Campaign the People's Liberation Army for a time liberated the city of Baotou, but only besieged Guisui without attacking it; the aim was to draw the main body of Fu Zuoyi's group west to relieve Suiyuan, so that it could not spare troops to move out through the passes, and thus secure victory in the decisive battle of Liaoshen. After the Liaoshen Campaign our forces moved in through the passes and first wiped out Fu's main-force 35th Army at Xinbao'an, helping to bring about the peaceful liberation of Beiping. With the Party Center's putting forward of the "Suiyuan formula," our army halted its advance into central and western Suiyuan by January 1949.
As the military situation changed, the line of our political work shifted from military and secret-service intelligence as the main thing to political and secret-service intelligence as the main thing. The Lu Nan intelligence system now aimed at grasping the enemy service's deep-cover plans and thereby tracking the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics agents. In December 1948 the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics set up an eastern-suburb work team, transferring Wang Qi to be its leader, stationed in the eastern suburbs of Guisui city facing the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau's Guisui intelligence station across the military demarcation line; Wang Qi and the others, whose special standing made coming and going easy, were led directly by station head Ji Shukai. With the Kuomintang's reactionary regime now in steep decline and its reactionaries wavering and falling apart, Wang Qi, Mi Jingquan, Cui Jizhou, and the rest seized the chance to develop agent cells, forming a second high tide of the Lu Nan system's enemy-work in Suiyuan. In this period they developed more than twenty connections, among them the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics agent Zhang Yuqing and the puppet security-regiment deputy commander Guo Jingbang; and in May 1949 they brought over the 959th Regiment of the enemy's 320th Division, the regiment under Wei Gang, sent intelligence back to the liberated area, and cracked the Juntong deep-cover organization of Cheng Deyan at Datong.
On June 8, 1949, the Suiyuan peace agreement took effect. As word of the Suiyuan peaceful uprising grew ever more pressing, the center of Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics secret-service activity withdrew steadily westward, from Guisui to Baotou and from Baotou to the Hetao. Wang Qi, Zhang Yuqing, and the other enemy-work men were ordered to track the enemy too. That July, Zhang Qing'en, director of the Juntong Suiyuan office, came to Suiyuan with nineteen secret-service cadres; his task in Suiyuan was to obstruct the peaceful uprising, and, following the Investigation and Statistics headquarters' "May First directive letter," to reorganize the North China secret service and lay in deep-cover networks to wage a so-called "guerrilla war behind enemy lines." After Zhang Qing'en fled by air to Taiwan on September 17, 1949, with eleven Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics chiefs, the North China secret-service chief Li Kunsheng, left behind in Suiyuan, set up in the Hetao a secret-service organization, the North China, Northeast, and Northwest Party-Government-Military Joint Work Guidance Committee. After the peaceful liberation of Suiyuan, the intelligence workers who had tracked the Investigation and Statistics deep-cover agents, under the direct command of the Suiyuan Provincial Public Security Department, in the autumn of 1950 broke the North China, Northeast, and Northwest Party-Government-Military Joint Work Guidance Committee at a stroke; and in the great campaign to suppress counterrevolutionaries they netted the whole lot of agents great and small, from Li Kunsheng, Cui Zhengchun, Tian Shumei, and Hu Shangru on down, thus victoriously completing the historic task of destroying the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics organization.
### 8. A Page of History: Legendary Tales of Jin-Sui Intelligence
In the years when Chen Yangshan headed the Jin-Sui Intelligence General Bureau, his comrades and subordinates had a good many legendary stories. Here are a few, drawn from the memoirs of Zheng Xiaoxian, Qu Rixin, and others, for the reader's pleasure.
#### (1) The Outstanding Book-Buyer
In the summer of 1944 the Party Center, to strengthen its strategic research, urgently needed a great quantity of enemy and puppet books, papers, and magazines, and above all such materials as the military and economic yearbooks published in Japan. The leaders of the Jin-Sui Military Region and Chen Yangshan, director of the Jin-Sui Intelligence General Bureau, instructed the various intelligence stations to get these important materials by every possible channel, as quickly and as regularly as they could, and send them straight to the Military Region or the Intelligence General Bureau, whence they would go on to the Party Center at Yan'an.
At the time the Yangqu intelligence station had only lately been merged. When the station head, Zheng Xiaoxian, laid the task of collecting and buying books, papers, and magazines before the others, many comrades made light of it, thinking that things sold openly could hardly count as intelligence. Zheng Xiaoxian set them straight: "War is bound up tightly with politics and economics. From books and papers on open sale we of course cannot find any concrete tactical intelligence like battle plans, but from the whole of the politics, the economy, the state of the country and its strength, we can analyze and judge the shape and trend of the war, and the policies and strategies to be taken in future, and so make our own dispositions to fit. That the Party Center and Chairman Mao are anxious to study this great question of the whole picture shows that the day of the great offensive against the Japanese is not far off."
After talking it over, everyone took the matter to heart and pressed forward to take part. But in fact the book-and-paper work was not as simple as they imagined. To carry out this glorious and arduous task the Party had given them, they first needed people and conditions that could buy books and papers in bulk without arousing suspicion; next, a safe place to pack it all together; and last, safe and reliable routes, methods, and people to bring it out. Buying a few books and magazines at a bookshop, or ordering a few papers from the post office, was easy enough; but to buy in bulk, and to send it out regularly and all at once, was harder. And such books and papers could be had only at the bookshops the Japanese ran in a few big cities of the enemy-occupied areas, Beiping, Tianjin, Shanghai.
After much study, it was agreed that to buy books and papers without being suspected, someone from a cultural or educational unit had best come forward. School principals and teachers suited best, for a school library's needs were large, and books and papers bought in a school's name, however many, would draw no suspicion, and such people had standing ties with the bookshops besides. Among the station's working connections there were not many like this, but they could be sought out and developed. As for the routes of carriage, the border strip around the villages and the horse-inns was of course best; and in carrying it, they would find reliable, fixed courier connections to move it by pack-animal on a regular schedule, under armed escort.
Once the line and route of the work were clear, the station went into action at once, giving separate tasks to a dozen or so working connections in the cultural and educational world of Taiyuan and the county and town strongpoints around it, asking them to collect, buy, and take out long subscriptions to scores of books, papers, and magazines published in the enemy area, and to send the Center at least two mule-loads every week. Among these connections was one outstanding book-buyer, a teacher at the school in Huangzhai town named Liu Zhen. Liu Zhen liked to mix with people and was fairly bold, could speak a few words of Japanese, and, most important of all, had a patriotic heart and a chest full of righteous feeling; he hated the atrocities of the Japanese invaders and firmly upheld the anti-Japanese cause of the Communist Party and the Eighth Route Army, so that the moment he was approached he pledged to do his utmost. He ordered for us such papers and magazines as the Mainichi and the Yomiuri, and sometimes went to the bookshops to buy needed Chinese and Japanese yearbooks besides.
Once, the Central Social Affairs Department directed the Investigation Bureau of the Jin-Sui Military Region to have the Yangqu intelligence station collect Japan's military and economic yearbooks and such books as the collaborator Zhou Fohai's Collected Bygones and Chen Gongbo's My Dealings with the Communist Party, along with other papers and magazines. Books of this kind could not be bought in the towns then, and were not to be found even in the city of Taiyuan. Zheng Xiaoxian talked it over with Liu Zhen, and Liu Zhen suggested they could get them from the big Japanese-run bookshops in Beiping.
But to buy a great many books at the Japanese bookshops in Beiping was no easy thing. After careful inquiry it was found that a Japanese was living in Huangzhai, working at a Japanese-run kabushiki company in the city of Taiyuan. This firm had business ties with Beiping; the man very much wanted to make a trip to Beiping but did not want to spend on the fare. So Liu Zhen, on the station's instructions, set about getting close to him with a purpose, made friends with him by treating and giving gifts, and offered to pay his way and invite him to travel to Beiping together. The man agreed readily enough.
The two went north together. To the outside world it looked only as though Liu Zhen were keeping the man company on a business trip to Beiping, and no flaw could be found. In Beiping, while showing the man the famous sights, Liu Zhen made the pretext of promoting Sino-Japanese friendship to say he wanted to buy some Japanese books for the school library at a Japanese bookshop. The man agreed to help; Liu Zhen put up the money, and the man made the purchases himself. Seeing it was a Japanese buying books, the shop sold to him without the least suspicion. As the books bought came to too great a quantity to carry on his person, which on the road would have meant checkpoint after checkpoint and inevitable suspicion, they turned the shopkeeper's greed for small gain to account, paying to have the shop pack and mail the books to the school in Huangzhai town, which was both safe and sure.
After Liu Zhen got back safely, Zheng Xiaoxian waited two days and then went into Huangzhai town disguised as a peddler to wait for the parcels. In two or three days the parcels did indeed arrive, a few at a time. He and Liu Zhen sorted them out afresh, and filled two whole sacks. To get these things out from under the enemy's nose, past the sentry posts, was bound to carry a certain risk. They laid a little of the day's vegetables over the mouths of the sacks, loaded them onto a donkey led by the reliable courier Ji Xizi, and made it look as though they were coming back from buying goods in town. Zheng Xiaoxian and Liu Zhen walked side by side behind, disguised as teachers out for a stroll after school, keeping cover and watching the movement around them all the while.
It was the height of the summer heat; the sun had gone down in the west, but the heat still pressed close. Zheng Xiaoxian wore a pair of cloth trousers, over his undershirt a single tunic of pongee silk, and a straw hat pulled low; snug against his waist was a Mauser pistol, its magazine full and its hammer cocked. Before they had gone far he felt himself running with sweat. The town market was just breaking up and the farmers were knocking off, and in the surging crowd Ji Xizi mixed cleverly among the passersby and made his way out of the town, while Zheng and Liu, in no hurry, walked behind chatting and laughing. Beside the blockhouse there was a small stream they had to cross, very shallow, with a row of big stones set for people to step over. Just as they were about to cross, four Japanese soldiers coming off watch came straight toward them. It was all so sudden that Zheng Xiaoxian's hand went to his waist without his willing it, and Liu Zhen too was at a loss. At this hair's-breadth moment Zheng Xiaoxian saw Ji Xizi driving the donkey across upstream, forced himself calm at once, and said softly to Liu Zhen: "Don't panic, keep steady, act natural!" And so they went on toward the Japanese, chatting and laughing as before. As the Japanese brushed past them, Liu Zhen even greeted them in Japanese. Seeing they were really teachers from the town school, the Japanese asked nothing and went on by. After crossing the ford and rejoining Ji Xizi, Liu Zhen pointed at Zheng Xiaoxian's waist and said: "That was close, we almost gave it away." Zheng looked down, and sure enough, though the Mauser was tucked inside his tunic, the thin cloth had been pushed out in a bulge, and looking closely one could even make out the dark shape of it.
#### (2) The Excellent Couriers
Courier workers stood in the very front line of the struggle against the enemy, a thorn in the enemy's eye and a splinter in his flesh, in danger of losing their heads at any moment. A courier had to be not only brave and quick-witted but keenly responsible, clearing every difficulty to get the intelligence to its destination in good time.
Over more than ten years of courier work a great many excellent couriers came forward. More than ten comrades among them were arrested and imprisoned in the course of their tasks, and some were even cruelly killed. Their work was done under the most secret conditions, their deeds are little known, and they are nameless heroes in the truest sense.
Li Yuanze (a puppet village head by trade) and Zhao Guilong (a petty trader inside an enemy strongpoint), inside-line couriers of the Lanxian intelligence station, over the three years from 1942 to 1945 went to and from the enemy strongpoints dozens of times and passed along more than a hundred pieces of intelligence. Day or night, in wind or rain, they set out the moment there was a task, crossing hills and ridges and dealing cleverly with the enemy along the way. Once, having learned that the puppet troops meant to make a night raid on the headquarters of the 120th Division stationed near Xingxian, they warned the intelligence station that same night; the station sent a courier by fast horse to reach Xingxian the next morning, ahead of the enemy's move, and got the intelligence to the headquarters, sparing our army a heavy loss.
Li Yuanze's wife Zhao Ruze and Niu Maolin's wife Cheng Rize gave their all to the courier work: not only did they see carefully to the couriers' living, but time and again they helped their husbands hide intelligence, and were called the "strongboxes."
Zhou Gaoming and Liu Tongshan, former inside-line couriers of the intelligence station, were both Communists. They served as base-area couriers in 1942, and as inside-line couriers from 1943 to 1949. Under Yan Xishan's rule, using the cover of petty traders, they went in and out of Taiyuan, Yuci, Shijiazhuang, and other cities, keeping up the link with our inside-line intelligence cadres. Many an important piece of intelligence was carried out by them. They knew the enemy's inner workings well, could cleverly dodge the enemy's checks when escorting intelligence cadres into the enemy area, and would risk their very lives to finish a task. In 1946, when the secret courier Zhang Xinfu took his own life after his identity was exposed and the inside-line intelligence cadres had to be warned urgently to move, Tian Gaoming, without a word, got on his bicycle and rode from Wenshui by way of Qingyuan, Taiyuan County, and Yuci straight to Taiyuan, and after finishing the task turned back at once, more than three hundred li in all, one stretch after another. After the Shangdang Campaign of January 1945, when Yan Xishan's forces had lost thirteen divisions and he put his army through a great reorganization and readjusted the whole defense of Taiyuan, our underground intelligence workers got hold of the Yan army's reorganization plan and copied it with a writing brush dipped in milk into an account book, from seven in the evening to five the next morning; and through our courier at the "Xieyiheng" dim-sum shop in Taiyuan, the material was tucked inside a bicycle tire and delivered to the intelligence station that same day, drawing praise from the leaders of the Intelligence General Bureau.
On August 1, 1945, on the very eve of Japan's surrender, the Shanxi puppet provincial governor, together with Zhao Duan, led a security corps to the city of Xiaoyi for a secret parley with Yan Xishan's representative. The night they set out, the courier had the intelligence to the station; from there it went at once to the Jin-Sui Intelligence General Bureau, so that our side grasped the enemy's movements early.
#### (3) Seizing Nanqi from the Tiger's Den
The station's work called for cooperation among forces of every kind. At the time Li Fang, a reconnaissance staff officer of the Jin-Sui Military Region, led an armed reconnaissance squad of more than twenty able, seasoned scouts, also active in the enemy-occupied area of Lanxian. The station often drew on Li Fang's armed strength to carry out such military tasks as seizures, captures, and raids, and Li Fang for his part often relied on the station's inside-line connections and couriers to pass along intelligence; the two worked together with a fine understanding.
In May 1944 the headquarters of the northwestern-Shanxi military region, to get a clearer picture of the Japanese military dispositions, fortifications, and troop distribution along the Lan-Li highway and to strike the enemy steadily, accurately, and hard, directed the reconnaissance platoon to capture, before long, a "tongue" who could give a full account of the enemy's high-level secrets.
Given the task, Staff Officer Li first fixed on Nanqi as his quarry. Nanqi, also called Nakamura Rijin, had been a Japanese non-commissioned officer, a devil who killed without a blink; his left leg had been crippled by our forces during a "mopping-up," and he had come to Lanxian as a quartermaster in the rear services, director of the coarse-grain company. This creature was forever harming the people, extorting their goods, and doing every evil of rape and robbery, and had once boasted, "If I don't go out of Dongcun, you [meaning the Eighth Route Army] can't get into the strongpoint." The people around Dongcun suffered greatly under him, and Staff Officer Li had long wanted to be rid of this villain.
He made a careful study of Nanqi's situation. First, Nanqi was the enemy's rear-services officer, with a fairly full grasp of the enemy's troop strength and military dispositions along the Lan-Li highway. Second, the coarse-grain company where Nanqi lived stood outside the enemy's inner quarters, lightly guarded, which made it easy for our side to act. Third, seizing Nanqi would rid the people of Lanxian of a great scourge and strike hard at the enemy's overweening arrogance.
On May 20, Staff Officer Li Fang sent Zhao Guilong, a former courier of the station, to Dongcun to make contact with the inside-line connections.
In the morning, Zhao Guilong reached the advance team at Dongcun, found the inside-line connections Ding Haoxin, Xing Shengzeng, Niu Youmu, Niu Chenglin, Cheng Xi, and Li Jisheng, passed on Staff Officer Li's instructions, and asked them to act in concert. Our inside-line connections at once worked out with Zhao Guilong the routes in and out and the concrete plan for acting together, and settled that they could act that very night. Zhao Guilong hurried back to the reconnaissance platoon's quarters and reported to Staff Officer Li, who made detailed dispositions and ordered the whole platoon to stand ready.
In the dead of night, Li Fang came with Li Yuanze and his men to the meeting place by the river at Dongcun, and waited and waited, but no one appeared. Sensing that something had changed, Staff Officer Li ordered an immediate withdrawal.
The next day Staff Officer Li sent Zhao Guilong to Dongcun again to find out what had happened. As it turned out, they had not fixed a time the day before; when the reconnaissance platoon came, the inside-line connections had already been and gone, seeing no one. This time they learned the lesson, studied it over and over, scouted the ground, fixed a recognition signal (two claps of the hands) and a mark for the action (a white cloth or towel wound round the left arm), set the time exactly, agreed to act at two that night, and sent back Li Jisheng's watch with Zhao Guilong.
Late on the night of May 21, black clouds rolled over, the dark so deep one could not see one's own hand, the whole land dead still, with only a few pitiful dog-barks carrying from far off. Then Li Fang, with a squad of men and Li Yuanze as guide, came racing quietly toward Dongcun.
They reached the meeting place on time, where the inside-line connections Niu Chengsen, Li Jisheng, Niu Chenglin, and the rest were already waiting. Li Fang brought out two sets of army uniforms he had readied beforehand, had Niu Chengsen and Li Jisheng disguise themselves as reconnaissance-platoon soldiers to lead the way, and had Niu Chenglin disguise himself and patrol the street; and he divided up the others too: the first group, led by Li Fang, Niu Chengsen, and Li Jisheng, would take Nanqi; the second would stand guard outside the gate; the third would hold the gate of the puppet district office and watch the gendarmerie; the fourth would stay in reserve outside the south wall; and Li Yuanze's task was to break a gap in the foot of the south wall with a pick brought along beforehand, ready for a clean withdrawal once Nanqi was taken. With everything ready, they all moved off quickly to their parts.
Just then Dongcun was utterly still and pitch-dark, with only one or two windows glimmering faintly, where opium fiends and gamblers were no doubt still at their pleasures. On the high gun-tower the enemy soldiers seemed to have lost their souls, heads drooping as they dozed. Staff Officer Li and his men came quietly to the wall outside Nanqi's quarters; Niu Chengsen and Li Jisheng made a human ladder and vaulted the wall, and opened the gate, and Li Fang and three soldiers went into the courtyard. At this the few odd-job men lodging in the south room off the gateway passage, hearing the noise, called out gruffly: "Who's there?"
"Don't move, we've come for Nanqi!" Staff Officer Li barked low.
One fellow would not behave, and was jabbed in the backside by one of our soldiers, after which he lay there meekly, not daring even to breathe hard. Staff Officer Li and the others made straight for Nanqi's room and kicked the door in. Nanqi at that moment lay like a great fat pig, snoring like thunder and dreaming sweet dreams.
Quick as thought, Staff Officer Li sprang up and seized Nanqi by the scruff; the creature started awake and reached under his pillow for his pistol, but Li was quicker of eye and hand and flung the gun to the floor. Nanqi, howling and squalling, wrestled naked with Staff Officer Li, until the other two soldiers rushed in and knocked him down with a rain of fists, bound him fast with rope, and stuffed cotton in his mouth. This overweening wolf, his hands steeped in the blood of our countrymen, was like a punctured ball, taken without a struggle.
The task done, at Staff Officer Li's word our brave reconnaissance soldiers carried Nanqi in one rush out of the strongpoint, joined up with the guards posted here and there, and withdrew without trouble. The inside-line men too took off their uniforms and went back each to his own lodging. By the time the Japanese came to their senses, our heroic reconnaissance platoon and Nanqi with them had long since vanished without a trace.
Li Fang and his party came to Hougou village and joined Zhai Wenhua, the militia leader of Mafang village, who was waiting there in support; they loaded Nanqi onto a donkey readied beforehand and carried him all the way into the base area at Baijiayang in Xingxian, and thence on to the Military Region headquarters.
The next day, when the people of Dongcun heard that Nanqi had been taken, everyone ran to spread the news, clapping their hands for joy, and said as one: "The Eighth Route Army has rid us of a great scourge." The enemy at the Dongcun strongpoint were struck with terror and could not rest easy; come night, they set strict watch and thick sentries as if facing a great army, and the ones with the blackest records of all shrank inside the strongpoint the moment it grew dark, not daring to go out, dreading that one day they might follow in Nanqi's tracks.
After Nanqi was brought back to the Military Region, more than a month of our education and remolding brought from him a great deal of important military intelligence, and he was later released and sent back. The Japanese at Dongcun, because he had given intelligence to us, handed him over to the Taiyuan gendarmerie to be put to death. So did a butcher who had set himself against the people end his evil life.
The Lanxian intelligence station was one of the stations under the Jin-Sui Intelligence General Bureau, and from its founding to its close it lasted a little over three years. Three years is only a fleeting instant in the long river of human history. Yet these three years fell in the stalemate stage of the War of Resistance, when the struggle was uncommonly sharp and complex and life uncommonly hard. Lanxian in particular was then a so-called model county of the Japanese and puppets, who over those years changed the garrison commander several times and kept building up their forces, meaning to make Lanxian a dagger thrust into our northwestern-Shanxi base area. In the face of so cruel a situation, all the station's workers firmly carried out the Party's policy of the anti-Japanese national united front, fully rousing and relying on people of every class, boldly building connections inside the enemy's own camp, and keeping the initiative firmly in their own hands. At the time, whether a man was a landlord, a rich peasant, or a turncoat agent, so long as he was willing to repent and turn over a new leaf and to support the resistance, they trusted him, relied on him, and firmly backed his every progressive act; and thus they drew the forces of every quarter to the fullest into the anti-Japanese side, forming a broad united front, and made their contribution to the final defeat of Japanese imperialism.
Given a correct policy, the cadres are the decisive factor. The Lanxian intelligence station was one of the stations under the Jin-Sui Intelligence General Bureau, and its record proves in full how much the intelligence cadres mattered. The cadres of those days were carefully chosen, and the facts proved these cadres worthy sons and daughters of the Party. Under hard conditions, ill-fed, ill-clothed, and ill-slept, they were loyal and diligent, striving through hardship and never tiring. Because of the enemy's plunder, the mountain region, poor to begin with, was shorter of supplies than ever: a bowl of millet was a rare delicacy, and wild herbs, chaff, and black beans were the everyday fare. Their lodging was poor; deep in the mountains and old forests, they often slept in caves and on the rocks, and even when they slept in a house they dared not take off their shoes or clothes. "We sleep in our shoes and socks, head to foot, and run out at the first alarm" was the true picture of the struggle in the enemy area then.
Was it hard? Hard it was, in truth; but the comrades of the station never once cried hardship or complained of weariness, and year in and year out were always cheerful, and this is revolutionary heroism.
#### (4) Cracking a Network of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation
In 1948 the Central Intelligence Department wired Chen Yangshan: "Your cracking this time of a network of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation in southern Shanxi is of great importance to the struggle against enemy agents in Jin-Sui hereafter; take care to sum up the experience, trace the leads, mind the evidence, and press the investigation further." In this case of cracking an enemy Bureau of Confidential Investigation network, the man of the first merit was Zheng Gui, a subordinate of Pei Zhouyu under the Jin-Sui Public Security General Bureau.
Zheng Gui was a native of Datong, Shanxi. In 1932 he completed his studies at the Datong County rural normal school, and in 1935, having passed the Datong County examination in Chinese medicine, practiced medicine in his home district and now and then taught primary school. He joined the revolution and the Party in 1939, and after 1940 worked in the finance department of the northwestern-Shanxi administrative office and the Jin-Sui administrative office. After Japan's surrender, when the Kuomintang and the Communists were in peace talks and discussing the setting up of a democratic coalition government, the urban-work department of the Jin-Sui Sub-bureau meant to send Zheng Gui back to Datong to seek, as a non-Party figure, a future post in the coalition government and do Datong intelligence work full-time; but for many reasons it did not come off. At the end of 1946, Zheng Gui's connection was transferred from the urban-work department of the Yanmen Party committee to the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau.
In January 1947, on the assignment of Pei Zhouyu and Wang Shukai, Zheng Gui went back to Datong to find cover work, taking a post as a teacher at the Pingcheng primary school in Datong. Before returning to Datong on the task, he made up a false past, saying that through the eight years of the war he had been an army doctor in He Zhuguo's 2nd Cavalry Army in the Northeastern Army, had come home to visit family after the victory, and, his parents being old, wished on the plea of quitting the army for civilian life to take up his old trade in his home district, and so on. But too many people in his home district knew Zheng Gui's real background; Li Bao, education-section chief of Datong County, caught wind of it and set men to watch him, saying: "Zheng Gui's origins are unclear; mind his words and his movements."
The Lu Nan intelligence organization inside the Suiyuan Investigation and Statistics had lost contact with the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau soon after Lu Nan died of illness early in 1947. In March 1948, to open up the courier link with the Lu Nan organization and to "lead the lower-level working connections," Zheng Gui was ordered to go to Guisui first and look into the situation. This time, after entering Guisui and settling in under the surety of a distant kinsman, Jiu Liang, he learned that Jiu Liang's nephew, Xing Shaowen, was a sworn brother of Zheng Gui's from primary-school days, and was then a captain-editor in the intelligence section of the enemy Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Suiyuan station. Having settled in Guisui without trouble and got a general picture, Zheng Gui went back to the organs to report, and Zheng Xiaoxian, intelligence-section chief of the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau, decided to change the original plan, instructing him: "It is now decided that Xing Shaowen is the first target we will attack and win over at the Juntong Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Suiyuan station."
Zheng Xiaoxian said to Zheng Gui: "It is now settled that we win over Xing Shaowen, choosing him as a target for our attack on the Suiyuan Bureau of Confidential Investigation station. Our army is about to set out on a swift strike; you must by every means get Xing back to Guisui first, so that in future he can reach the core again, and, when he wavers, watch your chance to draw him out to work for us."
In June 1948 Zheng Gui took up the task and returned to Guisui, saying to Xing Shaowen: "You know me; my trouble is my own mouth. In Datong I liked to pass judgment on Shanxi's politics, so people suspected me of being a Communist. In fact I have property and a business in Datong, and what property the Communists mean to hold in common I still do not know; but because of it I could not stay on at the Pingcheng primary school. Now I have angrily resigned and go back and forth between Shuoxian and Suiyuan trading in opium. Risky though the trade is, it beats teaching by far." Xing Shaowen, though he did not know Zheng Gui's wartime past, knew his family's wealth well. He believed Zheng Gui could never be a Communist, and moreover meant to use Zheng Gui for gain, letting Zheng Gui lodge at his house and sell opium for him to make money. As it happened, the operating funds Zheng Gui brought back to the Shuoxian office of the Suiyuan Public Security Bureau were all opium, which fit exactly; and the first profit naturally pleased Xing.
That July, Xing Shaowen was transferred to be major group-leader of the custody group of the Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Suiyuan station, and Zheng Gui went along with him. In August, before our army's swift strike at Jining, Zheng Xiaoxian instructed Zheng Gui: "Looking to how to develop things from now on, you must by every means get Xing back to Guisui beforehand, and, when he wavers, watch your chance to draw him out to work for us." And so, as the military situation at Jining grew tenser by the day, Zheng Gui counseled Xing Shaowen: "The Communist army is about to lay siege to Jining, and you wear the rank of a Juntong major; everyone here knows who you are, and if the Communist army takes Jining your position will be very dangerous. The thing to do now is to find a pretext and hurry back to Guisui!" Xing said: "I have long wanted to go back, but without orders from above, how can I leave my post without leave?" Zheng said: "Don't go for the moment; let me take your baggage back first, or when the time comes you'll have trouble getting away." In truth Zheng Gui knew that once the fighting opened Xing Shaowen would surely withdraw to Guisui with the enemy's beaten army, and that when the situation was tense, if Xing could not go along, this lead might well be cut. In the end Zheng Gui, carrying a Juntong courier's papers, went into Guisui ahead of him. Back in Guisui, Zheng Gui took a teaching post at the Tumd primary school. Later he stirred up leading Datong men living in Suiyuan, Jiu Liang, Chai Chuxiang, and others, to raise funds from Datong merchants in Suiyuan, and that winter set up the private Yunzhong primary school in the Datong Guild Hall, with Zheng Gui as dean of studies. Its address was No. 2 Xiaoyu Street, the site of what would later be the Suiyuan Provincial Public Security Department. In mid-September our army liberated Jining, and Xing Shaowen fled with the routed army by way of Huitengliang, Guyang, and Baotou back to Guisui.
By this time the Kuomintang regime was in steep decline. Xing Shaowen, back in Guisui and not yet over his fright, wavered politically. Zheng Gui seized the moment and said: "These days, men in your line are just selling their heads; the officials over you get rich if they hand out the gold-yuan notes even a few days late." Xing said: "I have already made the false step of taking on this dirty work; I'm riding a tiger now, what else can I do!" Zheng offered him a plan: "Last time I went to Shuoxian I ran into Li Mo, who is now a Communist cadre. Li Mo asked me who my schoolfellows in Suiyuan were, and I said you. Li Mo asked me to pass word to you, to ask whether you'd be willing to come out and meet him. To my mind, whatever his party, it's for an old schoolfellow's good; make a connection with that side first, in case the Communists come in and it's a dead end." Xing Shaowen agreed on the spot.
In the autumn of 1948, during the Cha-Sui Campaign, the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau moved into Fengzhen and set up a Guisui intelligence station in the eastern suburbs of Guisui city. Zheng Gui went back to Fengzhen first to report, and Zheng Xiaoxian directed him to bring Xing Shaowen to the station to meet. In September, Zheng Gui led Xing Shaowen to Taoboqi to meet Li Mo, and when they reached the place, Xing found that the one who had arranged to meet him was not Li Mo at all, but Zheng Xiaoxian, intelligence-section chief of the Sui-Meng Public Security Bureau. Only then did he see Zheng Gui's "true face."
Zheng Gui offered Xing Shaowen a plan: "Whether you come over or not makes no difference, for we Communists carry out the Party's policy just the same. We do not want to treat you now as an opponent on the battlefield; we want first to make a friend of you. If you have no objection, then from now on we are comrades."
Xing Shaowen, half startled and half glad, asked back: "Can a man like me be called a comrade too?" Zheng Xiaoxian said:
"Of course. There's no early or late in revolution. We Communists firmly believe that only by liberating all mankind can we at last liberate ourselves, and we take you for a young man who lost his way." Xing Shaowen was a deep-thinking young intellectual who had lost his way late, and so came round quickly. What followed proved that he was genuinely grateful to Zheng Gui for pointing him to a way out, and so he worked hard and did outstanding things.
Xing Shaowen had not been working for us long when Pang Zhongxing, head of the enemy Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Suiyuan station, sent him back into the liberated area at Jining to gather intelligence. Zheng Gui suggested that Xing Shaowen hide for half a month at Datai township in the southern suburbs of Guisui, then fabricate a report and pretend to have come back from the liberated area to make his report; but Pang Zhongxing, as it turned out, had already grasped the inside story, and dismissed Xing on the pretext that he had flinched and drawn back. But by this time the infighting and score-settling inside the Bureau of Confidential Investigation's Suiyuan station was at its fiercest. First, when Pang Zhongxing was promoted to Suiyuan station head in 1946 and, risen overnight, gave himself great airs, Yang Bingren, the Bureau's Suiyuan sub-station chief, who had the advantage of him in both learning and seniority, would have none of it. From friction grew resentment, from resentment enmity, and the two were like fire and water. Then, in March 1948, Pang Zhongxing took delivery of deep-cover funds from Mao Renfeng, ten bars of gold and five hundred silver dollars. He diverted these funds to Niu Xiyuan, manager of the Jinyixi trading house in Guisui, to hoard goods and corner the market for huge profit. To turn this rift among the enemy agents to account and better Xing Shaowen's poor position, Zheng Gui egged Xing Shaowen on to join Yang Bingren's faction in denouncing Pang Zhongxing to the Bureau for corruption. This internal lawsuit came to its result in April 1949: Pang Zhongxing was dismissed, and Zhao Siwu, formerly intelligence-section chief of the Second Department of the North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters, took over as head of the Bureau's Suiyuan station.
When Zhao Siwu came in, the Bureau's Suiyuan station had already moved to Baotou. At this time the Suiyuan Juntong was laying contingency plans and, to wreck the Suiyuan peaceful uprising, was colluding with Liu Wanchun and E Yousan to organize armed agent forces. Zhao Siwu slipped into the army rolls as deputy commander of the Suiyuan provincial 12th Reorganized Cavalry Brigade. The 12th Reorganized Cavalry Brigade set up a Baotou resident office at Baotou, and Zhao gathered most of the Juntong men into it. At the time Liu Xiaoji, deputy chief of the Bureau's Ike Zhao League station, was colonel chief of staff of the brigade's Baotou resident office. Because Liu and Xing Shaowen had been schoolmates at the Zhengfeng middle school in Guisui, Zheng Gui had Xing Shaowen go to Liu Xiaoji to plead his case, and Juntong took Xing Shaowen back on as major secretary of the brigade's Baotou resident office. But in Pang Zhongxing's day the Juntong agent Liu Zhenhuan had secretly reported that Xing Shaowen was consorting with the Zheng Gui of unclear origins, and Zhao Siwu, knowing of it, had once declared: "Sooner or later we shall come to grief through Xing Shaowen." So Zhao Siwu still would not give Xing any real post, and sent him to Zhongtan to collect opium. In view of this, the Suiyuan Provincial Public Security Department instructed Zheng Gui: "Pick a fit target and draw out another." Xing Shaowen sought out and introduced Wu Yumei to Zheng Gui. Wu Yumei, a graduate of the second class of the action course at the Juntong Lanzhou training school, counted as an old hand in the Suiyuan Juntong circle, and during the war had served as a Ping-Sui Railway courier in the behind-the-lines liaison group of the Juntong Wuyuan office. Wu Yumei was then major chief adjutant of the 12th Reorganized Cavalry Brigade and held the Juntong internal files. Deep in seniority and knowing much of the inner workings, he was a heavy bomb our public-security organs had picked out to destroy the Suiyuan Juntong organization and net its members at a stroke. At the time the Suiyuan military and civil authorities had already wired their uprising, and the enemy agents were scattering like monkeys when the tree falls; save for some diehards, most fled for their lives. In this situation, with Xing Shaowen passing word beforehand, Zheng Gui set a banquet and turned him, and Wu Yumei came over at once.
After Wu Yumei came over, he joined Xing Shaowen in winning to our work Wang Shaohua and others of the Juntong deep-cover group in Guisui. In the cracking of cases in the early days after Liberation, our reconnaissance department also developed Liu Xiaoxian, deputy commander of the 12th Cavalry Brigade; and from Jin-Cha-Ji came Zhang Hongbo, then a section chief of the Suiyuan provincial reception office, both of them Juntong stalwarts. So we came to grasp the core secrets of the Suiyuan Juntong utterly, making of them fish in our net and turtles in the jar.
As to the results Zheng Gui obtained after turning the Juntong stalwarts Xing Shaowen and Wu Yumei to the uprising, Wu Yumei's own "Work Summary of the Past Two Years" states: near the time of Liberation, Zhao Siwu ordered him to deal with the files, and he passed part of the Juntong files to our intelligence station. The important ones included: the deployment of deep-cover organizations by wire, of Tian Suobu, deputy chief of the North China region of the Ministry of National Defense Bureau of Confidential Investigation; the course by which Zhao Siwu, head of the Bureau's Suiyuan station, built the armed agent forces, and the roster of the chief figures of the deep-cover units; and the wireless controllers Yang Bingren, the Bureau's Suiyuan sub-station chief, had sent to cover the various uprising units. Part of the important papers of the Bureau's Suiyuan station included the organizational and personnel rosters of its several groups; the check-in roster of Juntong men who had fled to Suiyuan from Beijing, Tianjin, Zhangjiakou, Yanbei, and Hanlin; and the wire traffic between Chiang Kai-shek and E Yousan. They lured the Juntong wireless operator Gao Guangyao to Suiyuan to set up a station, and learned the deep-cover address in Beijing of the Juntong man Zhai Suiping. By the archival record of the former Suiyuan Provincial Public Security Department, by September 19, 1950, a total of 197 deep-cover Juntong men had been identified, of whom 67 had been arrested. Among them were Pang Zhongxing, former head of the Bureau's Suiyuan station; Huang Donglou, Guisui group leader directly under the Second Department of the enemy Ministry of National Defense; Li Qifeng, head of the Bureau's Beijing deep-cover station; Wang Kemo, Datong liaison-group leader; Zhang Fengxian, leader of the Bureau's Guisui deep-cover group; Zhu Hongliang, Baotou deep-cover group leader; Wei Yong'an, Wuyuan deep-cover group leader; and so on.
Besides, from the September 19 Suiyuan uprising down to my formal takeover of the government at the end of 1949, two Juntong wireless sets were also detected and seized through Zheng Gui's hands. First, in the spring of 1949, while Pang Zhongxing was still in place, he had set up a Guisui deep-cover group under Zhang Jie, its wireless operator being Yun Long, a teacher at the Tumd primary school in Guisui. Yun Long's younger brother Yun Hai was a cadre of our Guisui County Public Security Bureau, and our intelligence station sent Yun Hai back to look into and take hold of the set. This case was cracked after Liberation. Second, when Zhao Siwu came in, he made Liu Huanbo the new leader of the Guisui deep-cover group. Liu Huanbo had once been major medical officer of the detention house of the Suiyuan Juntong office, and was then head of the private Tongren Hospital in Guisui. On taking up the deep-cover task, he demanded three catties of gold from Zhao Siwu for funds. Just as they were haggling, Zheng Gui had Wu Yumei take the set away in Zhao Siwu's name and give it to our underground intelligence station.
