## Chapter 4. Pulling Out the Nails: The Covert Struggle to Secure Political Power

An opportunity that comes once in a thousand years is one that anyone would seize and refuse to let go.

With the enemy at the gate, the Chinese nation found a rare unity. Across the country everyone rallied behind the central government's war of resistance, and no warlord clique stood apart in armed division any longer.

Zhou Enlai seized the moment of the Communists' legalization with a decisive hand. He not only rebuilt the Party organization in Nationalist-held China; he got Communists into the ranks of the national army.

Chiang Kai-shek was shrewd too. He used the chance of developing the great rear to march his central-government armies into the southwest and the northwest. In the China of that hour, there was no longer an independent kingdom left to defy Generalissimo Chiang.

Even the "Red areas" that no one had ever managed to enter now had to open their gates to the central government.

### 1. "Dual Regimes"

Mao Zedong had won Yan'an, a place of good fortune; whether he could hold it securely was another matter.

Building a base area is not the same as fighting a battle. In battle the aim is to destroy the enemy's power; in building a base the aim is to settle your own. The Communists controlled the great block of territory that was Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia, and their first task was naturally to keep that power stable. Yet the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region threw up a strange thing: a "dual regime."

The Nationalists held the central authority of China, and with it the right to appoint local governments everywhere in the country. The central government drew the Border Region as a span of 23 counties and insisted that local officials be appointed by the central government. The Communists quite reasonably proposed that the Border Region's governments at every level should be staffed by their own side, but Chiang Kai-shek would not yield an inch of authority. The dispute dragged on, each side appointed its own men, and so every county in the Border Region ended up with a "dual regime."

Yan'an had a Communist "Yan'an County Government." On the Nationalist side, they appointed a "Fushi County Government" of their own. In Longdong, Suide, and other places, where there had been Nationalist prefectural and county offices before the areas were designated recruiting grounds for the Eighth Route Army, the Communists set up their own prefectural and county governments under the name of the "Resist-the-Enemy Support Association." In this way many places in the Border Region came to have two commissioners, two magistrates, two county security squads.

There had been dual regimes in the civil-war years too, but then the lines were clearly drawn and the two sides were sworn enemies. The dual regime of the alliance period shared the same ground, its jurisdictions crossing. Call it "brothers turning their swords on each other," and yet on the surface they still cooperated; call it "sharing a bed and dreaming different dreams," and yet what differed was not only their thoughts but their acts of government; call it "a scramble for power and profit," and yet it blurred right and wrong past telling. In a word, this "dual regime" was a grave hidden danger in the Border Region's politics.

For all the layered defenses the Social Affairs Department and the Border Security had set up around Yan'an, Mao Zedong was not safe.

When Japanese planes bombed Yan'an, a white silk streamer five feet long was found on the wall of a local earth-god shrine, its arrow pointing straight at Phoenix Hill, where Mao lived. The streamer was very likely a Japanese agent's way of marking the target for the aircraft. But through what channel had the Japanese agents worked their way into Yan'an?

Nerves were unsteady inside Yan'an and out. For a spell, crowds suddenly poured into the temples to burn incense. The Security Office looked into it and found a rumor spreading everywhere: that a man and a woman had lain together on a temple altar, and their bodies had grown into one and could not be pulled apart, so that terrified people were flocking to the temples to ward off calamity. The Central Committee at once sent scholars out to give talks debunking the rumor with science, and even brought in foreign doctors to explain the medical facts.

Even the most tightly guarded internal units were not free of serious crimes. A young nurse at the Yan'an Central Hospital vanished, and was later found raped, murdered, and dumped. The infant daughter of Liu Bocheng, commander of the Eighth Route Army's 129th Division, was in the care of the Central Nursery when a killer strangled her and cruelly cut away an organ. Both cases were suspected of having political motives behind them, and neither was ever solved.

In the Border Region's villages a secret group called the "Black Army" appeared, and in the towns a "Star of Heaven Party," gathering intelligence in secret for the Japanese and building networks of collaborators. Deeper investigation found that the core members of these collaborator groups often had Nationalist backgrounds as well.

A meeting was held in the Central Auditorium at Yangjialing, and afterward Mao Zedong and Wang Ming rode home in the same car. Even in that crush a Nationalist agent had slipped aboard. The agent had trailed Mao all the way to Wang Ming's quarters. Just as he drew a dagger to strike, Wang Ming's bodyguard seized him.

Another time, right at the gate of the Security Office, an agent threw a hand grenade at Mao Zedong as he passed.

The tangle of cases made one thing plain: the forces threatening security were not only Japanese agents but Nationalist ones.

The Japanese agents did not know the Communists' inner workings well; to penetrate Yan'an, they had most likely worked through the Nationalist agents.

Whatever else, the Nationalists could not be trusted lightly, and the watch on their agents could not be relaxed.

And yet this was the season of the alliance, and those Nationalist agents with their hidden designs sat in their offices at that very moment, smoking and drinking tea.

The Nationalist county governments and county party branches embedded across the Border Region were like so many nails driven into its limbs and its heart. These open organs of the central government held armed force in the county security squads, and held the post office, the telegraph office, and other arms of government. They worked one hand in the open and one in the dark: setting up baojia units in secret at the village level, building underground Party cells, even steering local bandits to make trouble.

An Eighth Route Army artillery battalion was stationed in Fu County, and the Nationalist magistrate Jiang Longxian secretly sent men to assassinate its commander, Kuang Yumin. On the night the Eighth Route Army was celebrating the anniversary of the October Revolution, the assassins slipped into Kuang Yumin's cave dwelling, mistook the bodyguard for Kuang himself, and stabbed him more than a dozen times.

In the second intelligence-training class at the Security Office's Qilipu school there was a trainee named Zi Jun, who found the class's hard life not to his liking and slipped off on his own to the Yan'an post office to wire home. The Nationalist agents in the post office dangled the bait of help getting home, coaxed Zi Jun into supplying details of the training class, and then threatened him into becoming an agent.

The "dual regime" gravely disrupted the Border Region's government, and the "second armed force" threatened its security. The Communists had entered a mature phase; they were no longer naïve on questions of power and of arms, and now, holding the real power in the Border Region as well, they set about the fight.

First they took control of the "second armed force" inside the Border Region, wresting away the sword-hilt of the Nationalists' local governments. The Border Region government and the Eighth Route Army's rear garrison office issued a joint proclamation placing all security units and local armed forces under the Border Region Security Command; anyone who organized baojia openly or in secret, formed illegal bodies, levied taxes on their own authority, arrested Border Region people at will, inspected travelers under false pretenses, or carried on sabotage propaganda, would be tried as a traitor.

The "dual regime" was harder to solve. The Nationalists' soldiers, police, gendarmes, and agents had a foul name across the country, and the Communists won popular favor by moving to clean up the security squads. But how to move against a Nationalist local government raised a question of legality.

The Nationalist power nailed into Yan'an, the Border Region's very heart, was called the Fushi County Government. The Communists had moved into Yan'an in January 1937, and the notices on the streets all bore the signature of Magistrate Ma Yuzhang. An important task of the Border Security was to deal with this Fushi County Government. Zhang Jiping and Yan Fu, fresh out of the second Qilipu class, took it on: one kept watch, the other tailed, and they found the county government's section chief Chen Heng and its accountant Gao Guanfu suspicious. Watching from outside was not enough, so Zhou Xing sent someone in as well. Cheng Yonghe, under the alias Chen Xin, applied for a post at the county government and, to everyone's surprise, was appointed a county clerk by Magistrate Ma Yuzhang.

For it turned out that this Magistrate Ma Yuzhang, appointed by the Nationalists' Shaanxi provincial government, was in truth an underground Communist.

Fushi County lay deep in the Red heartland, and when the Nationalists picked a magistrate for it, no one dared take the post. Ma Yuzhang, through his connection with Du Bincheng, the secretary-general of the Shaanxi provincial government, volunteered for it and came to Yan'an to serve. When the CCP Central Committee moved into Yan'an, Ma Yuzhang secretly made contact with the Border Security. Zhou Xing kept this important inside man so close that he handled him alone on a single line; not only did the Nationalists not know, but even Yan'an's own security cadres did not know that this Nationalist magistrate was one of their own.

The magistrate's position mattered, and Zhou Xing arranged to strengthen his protection. Xu Jianguo, deputy head of the Border Security, made contact himself and won over Cui Yi, the deputy commander of the county security brigade; this Party member, who had lost his organizational ties, was brought back into the fold. The Border Security also placed its own men in posts in the security brigade, charged with guarding Ma Yuzhang. The head of the Fushi county party branch, Gao Zhongqian, was a Zhongtong agent, and the Border Security deliberately assigned him an orderly named Ma Yinghai. Gao Zhongqian trusted this unlettered lad from northern Shaanxi and made him a Zhongtong courier.

The magistrate was a Communist, the deputy commander of the security brigade was a Communist, the county branch's courier was a Communist: the Nationalists' Fushi county government was in fact under Communist control.

The Communists had won this chance to rise again with great difficulty, and were nursing the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, this foothold of theirs, by every possible means. On no account could they suffer another of old Chiang's covert blows.

### 2. Fighting Over the "Treasure Gourd"

The opportunity was seductive, and Chiang Kai-shek's thinking slowly changed. Now that he had won the allegiance of the whole realm, why keep all these various forces around? Why not unify everything under one hand?

The alliance had barely been reached in early 1938, and by the year's end Chiang's mind had turned. In January 1939 the Nationalists' Fifth Plenum resolved to "restrict the activities of alien parties," settling on a policy of dissolving the Communists by political means.

In Yan'an, Mao Zedong judged the new trend keenly: the Nationalists were compromising abroad and generating friction at home.

"Friction," a term from physics, fitted the Nationalist-Communist relationship of that moment exactly. It was not the smooth "compatibility" of two things at ease, nor the violent "collision" of two things striking; it was the wearing-away of the other, a little at a time, while the two shared the same ground. The word applied to politics may have been Mao Zedong's coinage; the act applied to politics was Chiang Kai-shek's invention.

Through 1939 acts of friction broke out all over. Zhang Yinwu, commander-in-chief of the Hebei People's Militia, seized the moment when the Eighth Route Army in the Shanxi-Chahar-Hebei area was fighting off a Japanese "mopping-up," to attack the Eighth Route Army's rear organs, killing more than 400 of its officers and men at Shen County. Yang Sen's 27th Group Army sent troops to surround the New Fourth Army's rear liaison post at Pingjiang in Hunan, killing six men including the New Fourth Army's colonel-adviser Tu Zhengkun and its major-adjutant Luo Ziming.

On August 1, Yan'an held a "Memorial Meeting for the Martyrs of the Pingjiang Massacre." The CCP Central Committee issued a call for a struggle against friction. Mao Zedong put it this way: "If men do not attack me, I attack no man; if men attack me, I will surely attack them." Liu Bocheng led his troops in wiping out more than 4,500 of Zhang Yinwu's men.

On September 1, Germany invaded Poland. The new war in Europe marked the outbreak of the Second World War. The ever pro-German Chiang Kai-shek took his cue from it and convened the Sixth Plenum of the Nationalists' Fifth Central Committee, settling on a policy of military anti-Communism in place of the earlier one of dissolving the Communists politically.

The focus of the Nationalists' designs was, of course, the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, where the CCP Central Committee sat.

The Border Region's Guanzhong sub-district lay in its south, thrust like a fist toward the Nationalist-ruled Xi'an area; the common people called it the "treasure gourd." Hu Zongnan saw it as a mortal threat to Xi'an and called it the "pouch." He had long wanted to sever this Communist fist, and had set 200,000 crack troops on three sides of the pouch, ready to bring down the blade at any moment.

At this place that any strategist must contest, Xi Zhongxun, secretary of the Guanzhong sub-committee, was put to a great test.

Xi Zhongxun was only twenty-seven, yet already an old revolutionary of the northwest. He had joined the Youth League at thirteen, transferred to the Party at fifteen, launched the Liangdang mutiny at eighteen, and become chairman of the Shaan-Gan Border Soviet at twenty-one. In the mistaken northern-Shaanxi purge he too had been among the principal figures held for interrogation, and had been saved only by the Central Committee. Rich in experience of the struggle against the Nationalists, and a Fuping man who knew the Guanzhong country well, he was sent to take command at the Guanzhong front. Hidden at his sub-district headquarters was also a Shaanxi provincial committee, directing the underground work in the Nationalist-held Xi'an area.

Early in 1939 the Eighth Route Army's veterans' school, the school for the war-disabled that had stood on the bank of the Yellow River, was moved because of pressure at the front to Tuqiao, a town outside the seat of Xunyi County in the Guanzhong area. Hearing of it, the Nationalist magistrate of Xunyi, Zhang Zhongtang, at once mustered two or three thousand security troops to besiege the school. Xi Zhongxun got word of it, warned the school to pull out in time, and moved it to Kanhuagong north of the Xunyi county seat, avoiding an armed clash.

On May 25, a quartermaster of the veterans' school who had gone out to buy provisions was murdered by a Nationalist security squad.

To murder soldiers of the war of resistance was to sabotage the joint resistance of the alliance. When the school's students went into the town to protest at the county government, Magistrate Zhang Zhongtang, without asking the circumstances, mustered his security squad to put them down and beat nine of the disabled soldiers' representatives to death on the spot.

By now there was a Communist Independent Security Battalion No. 1 stationed at the Xunyi county seat, and it would not stand to be provoked. Zhang Zhongtang again mustered the Nationalist security squads of several neighboring counties along with a battalion of the 6th Security Regiment, and attacked the county seat with superior numbers. Independent Security Battalion No. 1, holding the seat with one platoon from the veterans' school, fought them off for seven days and eight nights before being forced to withdraw; the seventeen severely disabled men who fell behind were all killed.

Hard on this, the Nationalists mustered more forces to attack Majiabao, the seat of the Guanzhong sub-district, widening the affair further.

The soldiers and people of the Guanzhong sub-district were furious and clamored for an armed answer; war in the "treasure gourd" was ready to break out at a touch.

At this crucial moment the man in charge, Xi Zhongxun, kept his head throughout. An armed answer would feel good, but it would surely cost them: the Guanzhong sub-district had only three battalions in arms, and the other side had several regiments. Xi Zhongxun reasoned that the enemy wanted precisely to use a Communist resort to arms as a pretext, to manufacture greater friction and seize the "treasure gourd" on the pretext. He talked the troops into keeping their restraint, while working on the Nationalist security regiments one by one to split them apart.

When the Party Central Committee learned of the Guanzhong situation, it acted at once. Xiao Jinguang, commander of the Eighth Route Army's rear garrison corps, sent an urgent telegram from Yan'an protesting to the Shaanxi provincial governor, and telegraphed the whole country protesting the diehards' slaughter of the war-disabled.

The struggle against friction became the pressing task of Guanzhong, and a new problem for the CCP Central Committee. Xi Zhongxun, at the front of the fight, submitted to the Center a special report, "On the Anti-Friction Question in the Guanzhong Sub-district," analyzing the three chief features of the enemy's attacks and proposing a strategy to meet them.

Late that year Hu Zongnan's men shed their army uniforms, disguised themselves as local security regiments, and made a surprise attack on Malan, the seat of the Guanzhong sub-district. The Border Security got word in advance, and the sub-district's Independent Battalion No. 3 and 8th Garrison Regiment broke out successfully at Diantou, then fought at Jingcun, Zhuanjiao, and elsewhere, destroying more than a thousand men under the Nationalist Xunyi county security-regiment commander Guo Xiangtang and the Chunhua county security-brigade commander Li Yangzhi, and retook Malan in victory.

Xi Zhongxun judged that the anti-friction struggle in the Guanzhong sub-district was about to escalate, the Nationalist attacks turning from mainly political to mainly military. Before long the "Chunhua Incident" broke out.

The Chunhua county seat presented the strange spectacle of two armies ruling side by side: the Nationalists held the north gate, the Communists the south, and the north gate, on higher ground, was forever pitching stones at the south to provoke it, the two gates forever probing each other's sentries and trading potshots. In March 1940 Hu Zongnan abruptly moved up a division to besiege Independent Security Battalion No. 2; after two days the battalion was forced to pull out, and the whole of Chunhua fell into Nationalist hands.

The Nationalist army then brazenly attacked Majiabao, the seat of the Guanzhong sub-district. By the fighting of May, Nationalist forces had seized 4 county seats, 5 districts, and more than 40 townships of the Guanzhong sub-district; the Nationalist 2nd Cavalry Division stationed at Luochuan menaced Fu County, only a hundred-odd li from Yan'an.

The Longdong sub-district west of Yan'an was sending up frequent alarms too.

In April 1939 the Nationalist office at Xifeng detained ten Communists, and the Communist 385th Brigade detained a Nationalist district head in warning. The two sides seized men in reprisal without end, and the Nationalist 165th Division moved to lay siege to the seat of Zhenyuan County. Before long the Nationalist magistrate of Ning County, Fang Zhenwu, pressed the Communist garrison to withdraw; when the Communist battalion there ignored him, Fang Zhenwu mustered troops to attack. After two days of hard fighting the battalion had Fang Zhenwu surrounded in a single building in the town, while the Nationalists had the battalion surrounded within the Ning county seat, and neither side could break the other.

On New Year's Day 1940 Zhao Sizhong, deputy commander of the Nationalist Huan County security brigade, led his men into the Hongde district of Huan County, seized three townships, and captured a Security Office cadre whom he sent off to Ma Hongkui. Zhao Sizhong, whose nickname was "Zhao the Fifth," was a notorious Longdong bandit taken into Nationalist service. After harrying the villages, Zhao the Fifth led his men to attack the county seat again. The Longdong sub-district sent the 2nd Garrison Regiment to strike back, killing more than 300 of them; Zhao the Fifth fled in the dead of night with only twelve men.

The Nationalists mounted a great assault on the Longdong area with superior forces, seizing in succession 5 county seats and 6 districts, 43 townships, at Zhenyuan, Ning, Zhengning, Xunyi, Chunhua, and elsewhere.

At harrying the Communists, Chiang Kai-shek had the most experience of all.

Now that it was the alliance, old Chiang would not wear the hat of endangering the resistance himself; he pushed it down the line. For the attacks on the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region the central government always pretended to know nothing, letting local governments take the lead, and the troops it used flew no regular-army colors but were fronted by local security corps.

In this way the Nationalist governments and security corps embedded across the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region became a mortal affliction to the Communists, to be got rid of and quickly.

And yet these too were legally constituted organs, appointed by the central Nationalist government. On what grounds could you drive them out?

### 3. The "Red Sherlock Holmes" Makes His Move

Now it was a matter of method.

In the spring of 1939 a checkpoint in Fu County caught a reporter for the *Central Daily News* coming over from Luochuan. Under a swift secret interrogation the man confessed that his errand was to make the rounds of the Border Region's counties, conveying to the county party branches the anti-Communist policy set at the Nationalists' Fifth Plenum and arranging for Zhongtong agents to strike from within when Hu Zongnan attacked from without.

This reporter-agent was a little past thirty, a graduate of Shanghai Jiaotong University, six years already in the Zhongtong, guarded in his words under interrogation and steady in his replies: not an easy man to handle. To turn him was uncertain, to release him a waste; and after turning it over and over, the Border Security's chief of investigation, Bu Lu, hit on a bold plan.

Bu Lu passed himself off as this agent and strode into a county party branch. The branch secretary was a seasoned Zhongtong agent, yet he could not see any possibility that his visitor was an impostor. Leather jacket, woolen overcoat, an easy and gracious bearing, black leather shoes, gold-rimmed glasses, an air of cultivation: by no reckoning did he look like some rough-hewn Communist.

Bu Lu's real name was Chen Bo. Born in Qionghai County on Hainan Island, he had gone to work as a factory apprentice while still a boy, joined the Communist Party in 1926, and taken part in the Qionghai Worker-Peasant Red Army uprising. After the attack on Bo'ao failed he fled abroad, working in the ethnic-affairs committee of the Malayan Communist Party's central body, in charge of the workers' picket corps, protecting the organization and assassinating traitors. In 1932 he was deported home and worked in the Seamen's General Union. In February 1937 he reached Yan'an and was transferred to the Security Office to take charge of investigation and protection.

Bu Lu, who had knocked about north and south, was broad in his thinking and bold in action, and made a very convincing Nationalist agent. His secretary spoke with a thick Guangxi accent, nothing like the local people of northern Shaanxi: this was the Border Security investigator Zou Yu. From a great landlord family of Guangxi, Zou Yu had journeyed the long road to Yan'an with his elder brother; the brother went into the Social Affairs Department, the younger man into the Security Office. Posing now as a Nationalist agent, he was just what was wanted: an educated young man come from the Nationalist areas.

The tracks of the two "Zhongtong special commissioners" reached every county of the Border Region, and everywhere they went they enjoyed the Nationalist agents' full trust; the branch secretaries reported to them respectfully and handed over their agents' reports.

The evidence Bu Lu gathered proved it: the Nationalist county party branches stationed across the Border Region were all using the methods of secret agents against the Communists. With this handle on the enemy's violation of the alliance, the Communists could now expel these agents with justice and proof on their side.

But Bu Lu's actions, bold and singular, also stirred misgivings among his own colleagues, and someone wrote a letter denouncing him to his superiors. When Kang Sheng, head of the Social Affairs Department, heard of it, he called Bu Lu in for a harsh dressing-down: This is a tactical victory and a strategic defeat. In the season of the alliance, you run disguise operations against a friendly party without a thought for the political fallout?

Whether Boss Kang's criticism was right, the Security Office's cadres had their own views. On Bu Lu's daring to think and daring to do, though, they were of one mind; and Bu Lu earned himself the nickname "Red Sherlock Holmes."

### 4. The Anti-Corruption Storm

Around Yan'an, within the Border Region, there were several prefectures besides, and of them Suide's situation was the most tangled.

The several counties of the Suide area had not been Soviet areas before, but had been handed over to the Red Army by the Northwest Army and the Northeast Army and belonged to the Eighth Route Army's recruiting grounds. The Eighth Route Army had two regiments stationed at Suide, facing the Japanese in Shanxi across the Yellow River. The Communist Suide special committee sheltered within the command's secretariat, working under the name of an open body, the "Resist-the-Enemy Support Association." Every county in Suide had a Communist-organized Resist-the-Enemy Support Association whose chairman was as good as a magistrate, standing on equal terms against the magistrate the Nationalists appointed.

The Nationalists set great store by the Suide area too, and specially appointed He Shaonan "administrative inspection commissioner." He Shaonan was an anti-Communist specialist, and set about grabbing power the moment he arrived. The Suide Resist-the-Enemy Support Association had been founded by the Communists, but for the sake of united resistance they gave up its chairmanship to He Shaonan, and Chen Qihan, the Eighth Route Army's Suide garrison commander, stepped down to vice-chairman. He Shaonan, however, would not rest in an empty seat. On the pretext of local order he brought in more than four hundred security troops, recruited local bandits and toughs, and formed them into five security squads garrisoned across the counties of the Suide area. So the Suide area too now had a second armed force, He Shaonan's own. He Shaonan went in for "friction" in a big way: assassinating Eighth Route soldiers, organizing Elder Brothers Society wharves and a "Black Army," and smuggling opium under the guise of the Eighth Route Army. The soldiers and people of Suide hated him to the bone, and the Border Region government called him "Commissioner Friction." But the Eighth Route Army's forces at Suide were limited, and for a time there was nothing to be done about him.

Before long the Eighth Route Army's main force moved up to Suide. Wang Zhen, commander of the 359th Brigade, took his stand at Suide and served concurrently as garrison commander, and the Communist side was at once greatly strengthened. The Nationalists, for their part, hollered that Wang Zhen was a "general who crossed the border."

The "general who crossed the border" against the "friction expert": let us see whose house wins.

What no ordinary eye would have noticed was that Wang Zhen had, under his command, two young liaison staff officers as well.

To carry on intelligence work in the dual-regime areas, the intelligence cadres the Security Office assigned were generally given cover within the garrison troops. The earliest were Ye Yungao, stationed at Luochuan, and Liu Ziyi and Ye Yinnong, stationed at Suide. In August 1938 Li Qiming brought Lu Lunzhang (Liu Feng) to work at Suide.

Through the "Resist-the-Enemy Bookstore," an intelligence contact point, Li Qiming picked out young people firm in the resistance and sympathetic to the Communists, and recruited the captain-clerk of the Nationalist Suide security command, Lu Nan, the warrant-officer messenger chief of the Suide prefectural office, Gong Zhen, and the major deputy commander of the 7th Security Regiment, Zhang Zhensheng. Mu Qing, a descendant of a revolutionary martyr, working in the Nationalist party branch at Suide, also sought Li Qiming out to pass him intelligence. Mao Peichun, Li Qiming's classmate from the first Qilipu class, came to Suide as well, serving in the cultural-work troupe of the political department of the Second War Zone command.

Through this close-woven intelligence net Li Qiming grasped not only He Shaonan's plans for stirring up friction but also his graft: the more than 100,000 yuan in disaster relief the Center had allocated to Suide, embezzled by He Shaonan.

With justice and proof on his side, Wang Zhen convened a mass meeting of ten thousand at Suide and openly denounced "Commissioner Graft," He Shaonan.

You charge him with stirring up friction, and he slanders you with waging factional strife; you expose his graft, and he has nowhere to hide his face.

His name in ruins, He Shaonan slunk back to Xi'an. And in the Suide area there was never a "dual regime" again.

### 5. "Escorted Out of the Territory"

The Nationalists' friction grew worse and worse; the political struggle had already grown into a military one, and there was no possibility of going on in peaceful coexistence. The Communists resolved to end the "dual regime" in the Border Region and drive out these county lordlings who wrecked its stability.

In February 1940 Mao Zedong drafted a telegram in his own hand and sent it, over the name of Xiao Jinguang, head of the Eighth Route Army's rear garrison office, to Cheng Qian, commander of the Nationalists' First War Zone, demanding that "the Shaanxi provincial government withdraw of its own accord; otherwise we shall escort them out of the territory. This too would be the height of benevolence and the fullness of righteousness."

Seeing that Mao Zedong had offered courtesy before force, Cheng Qian wired back agreeing that the county magistrates of the Border Region might be appointed by the Border Region government.

Seizing power by force was the simplest way, but it would give the other side a handle; the Communists mainly took the path of mobilizing the masses. And now, gaining proof of guilt through covert struggle became the key instrument.

The Ganquan magistrate Yang Lie was plotting a defection from the Eighth Route Army, and the Security Office's intelligence contact at the Ganquan primary school, Shi Zhiwen, got hold of the evidence. In the case of the Fu County magistrate Jiang Longxian's assassination of the Eighth Route artillery-battalion commander Kuang Yumin, too, the proof was supplied by an inside man planted in the security squad.

The Border Region government, openly and in good order, "escorted these Nationalist magistrates out of the territory."

What is amusing is that the Nationalists' own Fushi County Government in Yan'an was expelled too, yet its former magistrate, Ma Yuzhang, stayed on and rose in office: in 1943 he became deputy mayor of the prefectural city of Yan'an, and in 1944 he was promoted to mayor. This secret Communist, whose identity was never exposed, became a very model of the Communists' work in uniting democratic personages.

In January 1941 the Nationalists engineered the New Fourth Army Incident, striking openly and hard at the Communist-led New Fourth Army.

If men do not attack me, I attack no man; if men attack me, I will surely attack them. The Communists opened a great expulsion of every "dual regime" inside the Border Region, and by the spring of 1941 the Nationalist party, government, and army organs embedded across it had all been driven out of the territory.

It was plainly "driving" them out. Why call it "escorting" them out?

That was the art of the struggle: leave the opponent his face, and keep the struggle for one's rights within the bounds of the law.

With every nail pulled from his heart, Mao Zedong could at last give his orders and breathe easy.

Democracy must advance together with the rule of law. To strengthen local security work, at the end of 1941 five security sub-offices were set up under the Border Security. The Border Security assigned a batch of capable cadres to reinforce the work at each. The head of the Guanzhong sub-office was Wang Feng, after the founding of the People's Republic secretary of the Shaanxi provincial committee, head of the Central United Front Work Department, and a vice-chairman of the national CPPCC. His deputy was Yu Sang, later Xi'an public-security chief and a vice-minister of public security. The head of the Longdong sub-office was Zhao Cangbi, later deputy head of the Border Security, and after the founding of the People's Republic Sichuan public-security chief and Minister of Public Security. The head of the Sanbian sub-office was Zhao Wenxian and his deputy Deng Guozhong, later chief of the Shaanxi public-security department. The head of the Suide sub-office was Liu Ziyi, and his deputy Bu Lu, later head of the Social Affairs Department and public-security chief of the Songjiang provincial committee, who took over Guangzhou in the early days of the People's Republic.

Besides the specialized organs, the Border Region set up, high and low, a mass body by which the people voluntarily helped the government carry out the work of rooting out traitors and protecting security, the "Anti-Traitor Committee," which served at the same time as an organ of township government. Beyond the open security bodies, the security system also set up secret "work networks" in every village, street, factory, school, cooperative, office, association, and army unit, to serve as the security organs' "eyes and ears."

In the war of resistance the Nationalist-Communist relationship was tangled as never before. Where before it had been kill or be killed, now it was struggle within cooperation, cooperation within struggle. Mao Zedong once described the Nationalists' anti-Communist friction like this: "Those who plot against us are calculating and unrelenting, trying a hundred schemes at once. Their point-and-line work is deployed within, their armed depredations launched from without; they manufacture rumors, and there are reports by the hundreds and thousands; they carry out sabotage, and there are countless squads of agents."

The Communists' counter to it was to pair covert struggle with political struggle. They used covert means to obtain intelligence that broke the enemy, then used legal means to open the fight and win it. This way of fighting might be called, in a modern coloring, a war of public opinion and a war of law.

As the line shifted and experience grew, the ways of CCP intelligence and security work were shifting too. Where before it had mostly been on the defensive, it turned step by step to meeting the enemy blow for blow. Having driven the Nationalist agents out of the Border Region, the Border Security also began to turn from defense to attack, issuing instructions to penetrate enemy territory and calling for the dispatch of "red spies" and "political detectives."

To penetrate enemy territory meant to penetrate the Japanese-occupied areas.

That secret war was fought in a wholly different way from the open one. Your Red army might fight bravely and well, striking terror at the mere sound of its name. But these Communists, so fierce in their hatred of the enemy, once they entered enemy territory, could they really pass unrecognized?

In the past the opponent had been the Nationalists, and the two sides knew each other inside out; the Communists, half a step ahead in the agent's craft, held some advantage. But now your opponent had gained a foreigner as well.

Could a green "red spy" fresh from the schoolroom outfight the seasoned, cunning Japanese agent?

Liang Ji: former deputy director of the Shanghai Maritime Transport Bureau and concurrently public-security chief; interviewed October 26, 2000. Liang Ji, who had served as head of the office of the Yan'an Public Security Bureau, remembered clearly the important cases of Yan'an in those days.

There are still articles now that say this Magistrate Ma was won over through united-front work. The Ma clan of Yangjiagou was the largest landlord family in Mizhi, northern Shaanxi, and it produced a great many talents besides. Zhang Wentian once carried out investigations here, Mao Zedong once lived here, and to this day international sociology still takes this family as an object of study.

*The Biography of Xi Zhongxun*, Central Party Literature Press. Tu Zhankui: former deputy head of the Qinghai provincial machinery department; interviewed 1999. The Huan County incident shook the Border Region within and without, and the author found this man, who had taken part in the Huan County fighting himself, only with great difficulty.
