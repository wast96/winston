## Chapter Four

On the afternoon of the day old Mr. Wu's body was laid in its coffin, in the town of Shuangqiao, two hundred li and more of waterway from Shanghai, in a great gloomy house, Wu Sunfu's uncle Zeng Canghai lay on his opium couch in a temper. This old country squire, a man past fifty, was the famous "local emperor" of those parts. Ever since, at the age of forty, he had begotten a precious son, that grasping, miserly, mean-spirited nature of his had come into its full flower. The pity of it was that this son of his, though he bore the name "Jiaju" — a fine colt of the house — was in plain truth not the equal of a house-dog; and so Zeng Canghai, who by rights should long since have retired to enjoy his ease, could still not idle his years away, but must give his old mind to the very rice and salt and oil and firewood, and every other trifle besides.

And these last two or three years his luck, too, had been out. When the first flag of the blue sky, the white sun, and the wholly red earth had gone fluttering up over Shuangqiao, and the cry of "Down with the local bullies and the evil gentry" was raised — so loud, so lively, and withal so much in earnest — it had indeed given Zeng Canghai a fright, and, to be quite safe, he had even gone and stopped a while in Shanghai. Later the clamouring young men fled, or were arrested, and Shuangqiao was to all appearances as peaceful and prosperous as ever; but Zeng Canghai's "reign" had been shaken from that time on. Another set of young men, who raised no shout of "Down with the local bullies and the evil gentry," had by now become the "new notables," and step by step had parcelled out among themselves a great many of the "prerogatives" that had once been Zeng Canghai's. By now his standing had sunk to a point he could scarcely credit himself: the "new notables" of Shuangqiao not only sat shoulder to shoulder with him and faced south to rule alongside him, but were forever elbowing him aside besides! "It is true enough — an old man is not worth his keep!" — when the squeeze grew tight, this was all the grumbling Zeng Canghai could muster, while with half an eye he pinned his hopes on that precious son of his, Jiaju.

That afternoon, as Zeng Canghai lay in a temper on the opium couch in his garden hall, it was not that the town's "new notables" had elbowed him aside again, but that the urgent "death-notice" telegram Wu Sunfu had sent was so shamefully late in coming. In the first instant that the wire was put into his hand he had been well pleased; for it came to him that whatever else he might be, he was of an illustrious old stock, and that Wu Sunfu — whose name was forever cropping up in the Shanghai papers — was his own sister's son, and had sent an express telegram at that — which meant, by the look of it, some weighty matter to be talked over; and here, plainly, was root and standing of another order than the town's "new notables," who but yesterday had been snivelling brats. But when he had deciphered the message and found it a mere "death-notice," all that gladness turned to a bellyful of rage. First, it was a plain, unvarnished death-notice and nothing more, no consultation over any great local affair, so that he could not tuck it in his breast and boast of it to all he met; second, the telegram had come outrageously, unconscionably late; and third, that precious nephew Wu Sunfu no longer held his old uncle worth a glance, but had sent this one perfunctory wire to discharge his duty, and had not so much as dispatched a launch on purpose to fetch him. Had Zeng Canghai still commanded the terror of his former days, then in the heat of such a rage the local telegraph-master who had held up an urgent message between the great houses of Zeng and Wu would surely have come to grief; but now the "old man not worth his keep" could do nothing beyond glare and blow out his whiskers.

He scrambled up from the couch, took a few turns about the room, picked up the telegram and carried it to the tall window where the light was better to study it once more, and the more he read the angrier he grew: at the very least, he felt, that telegraph-master who had "trifled with his official duty" must be dealt with. But just then his hired man A Er came in, his head all sweat and his whole body caked with mud. Seeing that Zeng Canghai's face boded no good, this A Er stood off to one side, breathing hoarsely.

"Oh — so you're back, are you? I fancied Qiliqiao had picked up and moved, and you couldn't find it; — I was of a mind to send the police after you! Mind yourself! Go on taking such liberties, and one of these days I'll pack you off to the station-house to have a taste of it!"

So said Zeng Canghai, his head cocked to one side and his eyes fixed on A Er, coldly and menacingly. Such talk came easily to him — whenever A Er stayed out over-long on some errand, Zeng Canghai would trot out this same set speech, and mean nothing by it; but this time it fell just when his temper was up, and, what was more, A Er only stood there mopping his face and gasping, and did not, as the custom always was, report the upshot of his errand the moment he came in — and at that Zeng Canghai's anger turned to the real thing. He pitched his dry, cracked voice higher, and stamping his foot he cursed:

"You brute! Has somebody pasted a seal over that dead mouth of yours? — How much did you get out of them?"

"Not so much as half a copper. — At Qiliqiao today they beat the gong and held a meeting —"

A Er checked himself all at once, lifted the hem of his short blue cotton jacket, and mopped his face again. Before his darkened eyes there swam up again in that instant the great gathering of thousands, the countless hoes and red flags, and the thousands of eyes as red and as fearful; and in his ears there swelled again that instant the huang-huang-huang clangour of the gongs, and the storm of roaring and raging loosed from thousands of throats. His heart seemed to swell within him, and it pounded, thud upon thud, till his whole body was hot.

But of all this Zeng Canghai would never have dreamed. Seeing that A Er said no more, he bellowed at him again in a fury:

"What do I care what damned meeting they hold! You went to dun them for money. Didn't you tell them — settle up in full today, or tomorrow Master Zeng sends the police to haul folk off! Didn't you say as much to those confounded wretches — a damned meeting, indeed!"

"Then send your police! Cut off my head and I'll not go again! The folk of Qiliqiao have every one of them joined the association … they see me, and they know I've come to dun the country debts for you, and they curse me and won't let me come away, and they'd have had me …"

A Er too spoke now in a heat, and had even come to say "you" to his "master." This was no small matter. But Zeng Canghai, his whole mind fixed on the debt that had gone uncollected, let the breach of manners pass unmarked; he cut A Er short, and thumping the table he shouted in a rage:

"A dog-fart of a meeting! Chen Laoba — he's a committee-man of this dog-fart Peasants' Association; and he lends out seal-money himself, so how is it the country folk may welsh on a debt of mine for nothing! I've a mind to go and have it out with Chen Laoba! — Hmph! There's no such law under heaven! It's certain you've been shirking, you dog of a slave, and never went to Qiliqiao at all! Tomorrow, if I find you out, I'll have your dog's life —"

"It's not Chen Laoba's association. It's another one. Only Qiliqiao's own people know of it — folk in the town haven't so much as heard of it yet! Today was the first time they beat the gong and met, thousands of them, all barefoot and in short coats, not a long-gown among them, every one a genuine poor countryman …"

All at once A Er had lost every fear of Zeng Canghai's threats, and had begun, on the contrary, to speak with a rising elation; but of a sudden he held his tongue, for he saw with a glance that Zeng Canghai's face had gone deathly white, his fingers trembling and quivering, and that with a stagger he had sunk down on the opium couch and shut his eyes — that this master, so awe-inspiring on ordinary days, could hang his head and droop like a beaten cock was a thing A Er, in his ten years as a hired man in the Zeng house, was seeing now for the very first time!

Now it was A Er who was at a loss. He was an honest fellow, and at the sight of that "death's look" on Zeng Canghai he thought at once that if he had frightened the old opium-smoker to death the fault would be no light one, and whether the Bodhisattva in heaven might not dock him of his years for it. But his fears were for nothing. Zeng Canghai, lying on the couch, flung his eyes open of a sudden, glaring with a savage red glint, his face gone iron-grey; he sprang up, snatched the opium-pipe to hand, and with a furious roar lunged a step forward and struck out straight at A Er's head, cursing like a madman:

"You dog of a slave! You're no good one either! Would you dare rise in revolt, the pack of you?"

Crack! — at the sound, the ivory opium-pipe snapped in two, but it had not struck A Er's head. A Er threw up an arm hard as bronze and iron to parry it, and dodged aside. The blood in his whole body was driven to a boil by that blow. He stood his ground and opened his eyes wide. Zeng Canghai, brandishing the broken half of the pipe and roaring like thunder, snatched up a pewter candlestick with one hand and hurled it full in A Er's face. The candlestick missed its mark, but as it fell to the floor the brass spike on its top pricked A Er's shin. Blood had been drawn! Rage shot from A Er's eyes. "Beat him to death, the old dog who bleeds the poor white!" — a phrase caught at Qiliqiao rose all at once into A Er's heart. He clenched his fists. Let Zeng Canghai press one step nearer, and A Er would surely do it!

But just then a burst of weeping and railing broke out from behind the garden hall, and hard upon it a bewitching young woman came bursting in, wailing and clamouring together, and flung herself upon Zeng Canghai, all but knocking the old man to the ground.

"What's this? A Jin!"

cried Zeng Canghai, steadying himself against the table, breathless and undone. Just then a second young woman, tall and strongly built, came hurrying in after her! A seething babel of scolding that no ear could pick apart filled the little three-bayed garden hall. Zeng Canghai shook his head, heaved a sigh, and went and lay down on the couch with his eyes shut. For all that he was a past master, famed far and near, at engrossing other men's lawsuits, the quarrel between these two women of his own household — his unofficial concubine and his son's wife — was one he could never settle, and could only meet by hearing nothing and asking nothing.

A Er had gone by now. The two women railed at each other. The wet-nurse, holding Zeng Canghai's grandson, and a coarse-work maidservant besides, stood by the stone steps under the dripping eaves before the garden hall, listening and looking on. Zeng Canghai took up a second pipe and drew at it with a sizzling hiss, grieving in his heart over the old ivory pipe snapped in two, and turning over in his mind, once more, that "meeting" or whatever it was at Qiliqiao. He rather repented now of the "unseemly show" he had just made; and now his old craft and deep calculation set off in this direction: the Communists were stirring up the country folk of Qiliqiao to meet — and their aim, most likely, was no small one? But the town had a battalion of soldiers, and the militia besides; what was there to fear? He might turn the thing to good account and ask the Public Security sub-bureau to seize a few and make an example of them — and every man who welshed on a debt should be reckoned a Communist. … And more: not a soul in the town yet knew of the business, and those several "new notables" who commonly elbowed the old man the hardest were still fast asleep and none the wiser! — At this thought a smile floated up over Zeng Canghai's dark, lean face. He had already hit on a fine way to get back the principal and interest of his usury, and had reckoned besides how he might make a grand exposure of the "new notables'" muddling folly; for they did not so much as know there were Communists at Qiliqiao — and what sort of governing did they call that!

"Good! That's how it shall be done. I'll make the whole lot of them taste your old master's hard hand! Ha, ha!"

Coming in his musings to this gratifying point, Zeng Canghai laid down the pipe and, unable to hold it in, cried the words aloud, and then laughed ha-ha again and again. This dry, cracked laughter went echoing about the garden hall and struck flat and toneless into his own ears, and only then did he grow aware that the two women's wrangling had, at some moment he could not name, come to an unconditional stop. He looked about him in astonishment, and only then made out A Jin, sitting alone at the square table across from the couch, her face buried in a handkerchief, as though she were weeping there.

"A Jin!"

Zeng Canghai called softly. There was no answer. Finding himself in a fix, Zeng Canghai raised himself languidly and was about to go over and smooth things with a word or two, when A Jin of a sudden showed her face and rolled her eyes white at him; she had not been weeping at all, only the rims of her eyes were a little red.

"Tomorrow I'll go back to the country; am I some cheap creature, to hang on here and be cursed and beaten?"

A Jin said this in a shrill voice, and burst all at once into tears — a dry weeping, with no tears in it.

"There, there! What's all the quarrelling about! I've no strength to squabble with a shrew like that; wait till A Ju comes, and I'll have him keep her in order! She's his wife, and it's for him to keep her in order! — I'll have A Ju give her a good beating, to work off your spite for you. There now, there now, A Jin! It's not worth putting yourself on a level with a fool like her. — Go and see whether the bird's-nest gruel is ready. I want to eat before I go out on my business!"

So saying, Zeng Canghai sauntered over to A Jin's side and, with his great sleeve all stained with opium-grime, brushed a few strokes across her face, by way of wiping her tears for her. A Jin gave her head a twist or two, cocked her eyes aslant, and gave a pff of laughter:

"Hmph — and is your word worth anything?"

"Not worth anything, indeed! When I say I'll deal with a man, dealt with he shall be! I, that am the master, need not lift my own hand to it. — Last time, when that man of yours came making a row at the door, did I not promise you he'd be dealt with, and soundly? And was he not, in the end, dealt with by the police? Only one's own daughter-in-law can hardly be sent to the station to be dealt with; that's for the son to do. When A Ju comes by, I'll have him give that hot-tempered baggage a right sound thrashing! My word has always been as good as done."

"Aye, as good as done! Last month you promised me a gold ring, and to this day there's still no —"

"There, there, that's another matter entirely! That's buying a thing, not dealing with a person; — a gold ring, what's the good of it after all? Worn on the hand, it never makes the hand feel easier. If I put the money for the gold ring out at a native bank in your name to breed interest, is that not far better? Now then, off you go and see to the bird's-nest gruel. When I've gone out and come back, I'll give you a native-bank passbook: a hundred dollars! Is that not good?"

It seemed the figure "a hundred" had indeed some magic in it, for A Jin, with a measure of satisfaction in her air, went off. Here Zeng Canghai chuckled secretly to himself, in admiration of his own diplomatic address, and lay down again on the couch to roast up, with vigour redoubled, a great bubble of opium.

But the bubble was scarcely set to the bowl, and he had not drawn half a mouthful, when the wrangling within broke out afresh. This time there was mingled with it a man's railing voice: Zeng Canghai's precious son had come upon the scene. Zeng Canghai seemed not to hear a word of it, but gravely cradling his pipe drew at it with all his might; when, all unlooked-for, out of the boiling clamour of abuse within there leapt a shrill, ringing sentence that bored straight into Zeng Canghai's ear:

"You shameless slut! The old one's not enough to quench your heat, and now you've fixed on the young one too; and am I to give way to you?"

It was the daughter-in-law's voice. Next came the sound of A Jin laughing. Then all at once the son roared like a madman, and the daughter-in-law wept and cursed together. After that it was all one confused din of weeping, railing, and grappling.

Zeng Canghai, his pipe in his hands, forgot to smoke, and dully savoured over that one phrase, "the old one's not enough to quench her heat." True, in such matters, unlike the coming and going of money, he could take a philosophic view; and yet at bottom there was a sour, vinegary discomfort in his heart. And there was another thing besides that greatly dashed his spirits: so his son's readiness to beat his wife was not, after all, a "dutiful obedience to a stern command," but had some other cause of its own.

This further collapse of his authority over his son little by little brought him to a kind of grief.

Presently a heavy tread startled Zeng Canghai out of his brooding. His son Jiaju — a wild-colt of a youth of nineteen, of a face exceedingly ugly — stood before the old man. Flinging the book he had in his hand down onto a chair with a slap, this Zeng Jiaju sat down on the square stool beside the couch, his face toward his father.

"A Ju, old Mr. Wu of the Wu house is dead. Your cousin Sunfu has sent a telegram. You've nothing to do in the town in any case, so tomorrow you shall go up to Shanghai to pay your condolences, and take the chance to ask Sunfu to find you a post."

Without waiting for his son to open his mouth, Zeng Canghai brought out first, slowly, the plan he had just been turning over; but that business of "the old one, the young one, the heat" was still snarled and tangled in his heart.

"I won't go! I've an urgent use for money — give me a few tens of dollars, and at once!"

"What! Come begging for money again! Aiya, you don't know how hard money is to come by! What use? Say plainly first what it's for!"

said Zeng Canghai, startled, and with a lurch he rolled over and sat up. But his son did not answer at once; he fumbled first a while at his waist, and drew out a small square of stiff black paper, which he thrust right up under his father's nose, crying most haughtily:

"What use, indeed! I'm going to give a great feast, that's what! Look — what do you take this to be?"

Zeng Canghai, quick of eye and quick of wit, saw at a glance that the black card was a "Chinese Nationalist Party membership certificate"; and no small was his joy at it. He snatched it from him, rubbed his eyes, and holding it up to the opium-lamp studied it again with care; sure enough, it was no sham! "Party Member's Certificate, such-and-such Province, such-and-such County, such-and-such District, No. 23," with a little likeness of Zeng Jiaju pasted on it besides. — "And No. 23 at that!" the old man said to himself, glowing with delight, and taking his old long-sighted spectacles from the opium-tray he set them on and scanned once more the impress of the party branch's official seal stamped upon the certificate. At the last, with all reverence and respect, he stepped over to his son and handed the certificate back, charging him earnestly, over and over:

"Put it safe away, put it safe away!"

Then he laughed aloud again, ho-ho, and clapping his son on the shoulder he said:

"So now you've come out into the world! What did I always say — no tiger's gate breeds a cur! — A great feast, to be sure! Tonight you'll treat your young friends; a few tens of dollars will hardly be enough, I fancy? I'll give you a hundred by and by. Tomorrow night we must give a feast for our old family friends as well. But wait — there's a great matter yet! — Let me finish this pipe, and then we'll talk."

So the old man clambered eagerly onto the couch and drew at the pipe with a will, huff and puff; Zeng Jiaju, his whole face full of self-conceit, could yet find no words to boast with, and so stretched himself out on the couch as well. He did indeed stow the membership certificate very carefully in an inner pocket of his clothes. But the mind in which he prized the certificate was somewhat other than Zeng Canghai's; he knew that with this thing he could, time and again, squeeze great handfuls of money out of the old man and spend with an open hand.

Zeng Canghai finished a pipe at a breath, took up the teapot from the opium-tray and, mouth to spout, poured down a few gurgling gulps, then set the pot down and said in a low voice:

"A Ju! I've got wind of an important piece of news, and was just meaning to go and report it to the Public Security Bureau. Now I'll send you instead! You've only just joined the party, and it's the very time for you to show your face, do some great thing, and win first honours! — Ha, and the chance is aptly timed too; today two joys have come to our gate at once!"

Hearing that he was to go to the Public Security sub-bureau to see to some business, Zeng Jiaju was struck dumb. He goggled a pair of round eyes and only stared blankly at his father. Plainly he had not the least eagerness for the thing, and had no notion, besides, of how to deal with the Public Security sub-bureau.

"Aye — and a touch of stage-fright besides!"

said Zeng Canghai, half fond and half chiding, and shook his head twice over; then of a sudden he changed his tack and asked:

"A Ju, do you know how many secret opium-lamps there are in the town all told? Which several households of clients does the Third Maid at the front-street general store take? And how much contraband slips in past the barrier-post in a month?"

Again Zeng Jiaju could only gape and make no answer. He was, to be sure, a frequent visitor to the private brothels himself — the Third Maid of the front street, for instance, and her like; but to be asked how many households of clients this or that woman took, or how many secret opium-lamps there were, or how much duty-dodging contraband came in, was a thing he had never so much as dreamed of.

Zeng Canghai slapped his thigh and laughed, ho-ho:

"How's this? A young man never knows, after all, to keep his eyes open at all times and in all places. Aiya, A Ju, you're a Party Excellency now, and you don't know the least thing about how the ground lies hereabouts — how is a Party Excellency like you to carry on! If you won't go worming into the cracks yourself, are you to sit and wait for folk to come and invite you? — But you needn't fret; you've still your old father, an 'old horse that knows the road,' to guide you along, little by little!"

Young Zeng's face reddened now — perhaps out of some shame at his father's "household precepts," or perhaps out of impatience that the one hundred dollars was not yet in his hand. He set his mouth in a sulk and would not speak. Just then his wife came in carrying the small child, ill-humour all over her face, and setting the child down on a chair and steadying it with one arm, she turned her face to look at her husband, as though she had something to say.

But the child would not let her get a word out, and set up a bawling wah-wah; and at the same moment it let go a gush of piss that ran streaming over the whole chair and dripped down onto the floor.

Zeng Jiaju knitted his brows, the slabs of surly flesh on his face standing out ridge by ridge, and with a sudden bound he sat up from the couch and was on the point of berating his wife, when out of the tail of his eye he caught sight of a book beneath the feet of the child that had wet itself — the very one he had brought in a while before, and the child's two feet were kicking and trampling all over its cover.

"Hey! You little brute!"

With a furious roar Zeng Jiaju sprang to the child's side and roughly dragged the book out from under its feet; when he looked at it, it was already wet and torn to tatters, past all recognition. The child's body swayed, and it all but toppled off the chair, but oddly enough it left off its crying instead, and burrowing into its mother's breast only held its little mouth gaping wide.

When he made out, in his son's hands, that the sodden book was none other than the Three Principles of the People, Zeng Canghai's face changed on the instant. He sprang up stamping his feet, and looking into his son's face he cried out in dismay, over and over:

"Ruined! Ruined! This is the very same kind of thing as the Sacred Edict of the old Qing days; it ought by rights to be enshrined in the great hall before the incense-burner on the altar-table — how could you let a child piss on it! If word of this gets out to folk beyond these walls, will that head of yours still sit safe on your shoulders? Damnation, we're ruined!"

By now the child, that had been frightened into silence, burst out too with a wah of weeping. Zeng Jiaju did not rightly follow his father's endless lamenting, but in sum he felt the affair was a bad business, and being much enraged he seized his wife with one hand and set to beating her. The child's and the mother's weeping, and young Zeng's cursing, ran all together into one. Zeng Canghai shook his head and sighed, and gave his mind only to his pipe; then, remembering that there was yet a great matter that called for a trip to the Public Security sub-bureau, he shook out his clothes amid the uproar and went off.

The street was as lively as ever. This town of Shuangqiao had a population of close on one hundred thousand, two or three native banks, pawnshops and silver-shops, and the electric-power plant, the rice-mill and the oil-press that Wu Sunfu ran single-handed besides. All of these had sprung up within the last four or five years.

As he walked, Zeng Canghai looked about him at this newly flourishing market-scene and at all the sorts of citified amusement, and it came to him that the ways of making money now were not to be spoken of in the same breath as in his own day as a "local emperor"; had he not, these last two or three years, been dogged by black luck, then in so thriving a state of things might he not long since have raked in a hundred thousand or eighty thousand? For all that there was hope now of a return in force, he could not help a certain forlornness. His steps grew slower. When he came before the Taibai Lou tavern, the press of people was such that he fairly stopped.

Suddenly, out of the throng, someone caught hold of Zeng Canghai and asked him point-blank:

"Where are you off to at this hour?"

Zeng Canghai turned to look, and knew him for Li Si the earth-peddler; on a certain footing, he and this Li Si had once been familiar intimates who stood on no ceremony, but here, on the open street with all eyes upon them, this Li Si should tug and pull at him and call him plainly "you," as though he were now clean the equal of Zeng Canghai — and to Zeng Canghai, who was forever boasting himself of an "illustrious old stock," this was a thing hard to bear indeed. Yet it would not do to fly out at him. As Shuangqiao grew day by day more citified, this Li Si's hidden power too was swelling from one day to the next. Where there was "earth" there was "wealth," and so there was "muscle" as well: was Zeng Canghai, the old local snake-in-its-lair, likely not to know it? And so, for all his deep displeasure, he mastered himself with an effort, and nodded a greeting instead, answering with a smile:

"I've a little official business at the Public Security Bureau."

"No need to go — today whatever you set out to do gets left hanging!"

said Li Si, with a great air of showing off, and there was a touch of arrogance besides in his tone, as though he himself were the head of the Public Security sub-bureau.

"Why so? Has the sub-bureau chief been changed for another, then?"

Zeng Canghai could truly bear it no longer, and shot back the question coldly, with a measure of mockery in his tone. But the words were scarcely out before he repented of having given offence to this Li Si of the boundless resource.

But as luck would have it, Li Si did not catch the sting in Zeng Canghai's words; he took Zeng Canghai by the arm and drew him to a quieter spot slantwise across from the Taibai Lou, and bringing his mouth close to Zeng Canghai's ear said softly:

"Can it be you've not heard the wind of it?"

"What wind?"

"The Communist bandits have come to Qiliqiao, and tonight they mean to sack the town!"

Zeng Canghai's heart gave a leap and his colour changed too; but this fright of his was not at the news that there were Communist troops at Qiliqiao meaning to sack the town — it was that he grieved to find his own exclusive secret was a "secret" no longer, so that there was now no hope of "first honours" for him or for his son. Still, he was an old hand after all, and after the one leap of his heart he grew calm again at once, and shook his head on purpose, to show that he did not believe it.

"You don't believe it? I'll tell you honestly — as yet there's not a handful of people who know this news. I had it from Battalion Commander He's little establishment. The commander's concubine has already fled to the county town for safety. She hired pockmarked Wang's boat, no less — it's the very truth of truths!"

Li Si went softly on, with every air of the warmest concern.

Now Zeng Canghai's face went wholly ashen! Only now did he grasp that the situation was gravely worse than he had supposed. Earlier, hearing his hired man A Er tell how the country folk of Qiliqiao had beaten the gong and met, he had thought them no more than bare-handed peasants; only now did he understand that there were in truth Communist troops besides, fully furnished with guns and cannon. His terror turned, from the losing of "first honours," to a peril to his very person and household. He asked in haste:

"How many guns have the Communist bandits got?"

"A hundred-odd rifles, they say."

Zeng Canghai's heart eased at that; reflecting that though his scheme to win credit had come to a mere painted cake, there was no danger either, he gave a little laugh, and looking at Li Si's furtive face said, all frank and free:

"A hundred-odd rifles, is it? What's there to fear! The provincial garrison quartered here is a whole battalion strong!"

"A battalion! Hmph! Three months without their pay!"

"And there's the militia besides!"

"In every ten of them there's eleven that are seasoned opium-fiends! — Take my advice, look sharp and get yourself out of the way for a bit; this is no game! The wind's been high these two days past, and it's only you, holed up all day on your opium couch hugging A Jin, that never knew of it. — Maybe nothing will come of it. But one must be careful and watch one's chance. I'll not hide it from you: I've already told my men to load every one of their pieces, and there's to be no sleeping tonight."

So saying, Li Si hurried off.

Zeng Canghai stood pondering a while, unable to fix on what to do. Thinking that any move at all must cost money, he half resolved to run the risk and stay; thinking that if something should really happen his life would be in peril, he half thought too to follow the example of Battalion Commander He's concubine. In the end, turning it over, he reflected that the "reporting for credit" had come to nothing anyway, and that there was no point in going to the Public Security Bureau; so he decided to go home first and settle on a course there.

At home, however, someone was waiting for him. In the dim dusk, the moment Zeng Canghai saw the little tuft of moustache under the man's chin, he knew him for Fei the little-moustache — Fei Xiaosheng, the steward of the Wu house.

"Ah, good, Elder Cang is back. I'd not dare trouble you without cause; it's only that Third Master has sent a letter from Shanghai, bidding me raise one hundred thousand in silver and remit it within three days, so I've no choice but to come and consult with you, Elder Cang."

Fei the little-moustache had come straight to the point and spoken of money at once, and Zeng Canghai could not help being taken aback for a moment. But Fei the little-moustache went smilingly on:

"I've already gone over the accounts. Your share here, Elder Cang, is twelve thousand, all of it native-bank money past its term. I'd not have ventured to raise the matter with you, but Third Master's letter is worded most severely, and I cannot make up the sum, so I must beg you to help me out, Elder Cang — I shall be endlessly grateful."

Zeng Canghai's face fell of a sudden. He had long borne this Fei the little-moustache a deep hatred. By his own common account, Fei the little-moustache had grown fat in his few years as steward of the Wu house. He said, too, that Fei the little-moustache had sown discord between uncle and nephew, so that he, the old uncle, could carry no more than this paltry ten thousand odd in silver on his nephew's native bank. Now, seeing Fei the little-moustache come dunning at his very door under the banner of "Third Master," Zeng Canghai felt he must give him a lesson, and answered coldly on the spot:

"Brother Xiaosheng, you are loyalty itself. I shall be sure to tell Sunfu to hold you in special regard! — It's a thing hard to credit, but my old brother-in-law was no sooner come to Shanghai than he died! An urgent wire has reached me here, bidding me go and preside over the funeral. — I mean to set off this very night. I'll talk over everything face to face with Sun the Third, so there's no need at all for you to trouble yourself!"

"Just so. We too had a telegram of the old master's passing at our end, though we did not know it was to ask Elder Cang to go and preside over the funeral."

said Fei the little-moustache with a smile, speaking no more of money; but in that faint smile of his there lurked a something, as of a man who has seen clean through the workings of Zeng Canghai's heart. He rose and was about to take his leave, when Zeng Canghai of a sudden stopped him:

"No hurry. Sit a while longer; I've a few words more! — Aye, Sun the Third wants one hundred thousand in silver remitted, for some pressing need, no doubt; how much have you raised so far? Give me an account of it, let me hear."

"No more than half. Fifty thousand dollars!"

Fei the little-moustache sat down again and spoke smilingly as before, but there was in his tone a savour of great displeasure at Zeng Canghai's cross-questioning. This Fei the little-moustache was an old fox too, and knew well that Wu Sunfu had long been out of humour with this old uncle. Still, he was Wu Sunfu's own blood-uncle after all, and in point of courtesy Fei the little-moustache dared not slight him; but now, seeing Zeng Canghai go a step further and put on quite the airs of a "master over the master," Fei the little-moustache could not but think the old uncle sadly wanting in a sense of his own place.

Yet Zeng Canghai's "want of tact" had worse in store than this:

"Only fifty thousand still! You've not sent it off yet, I take it? Bring it here! I'll carry it with me tonight!"

Fei the little-moustache's eyebrows gave a jump; he could scarcely believe his own ears. He fingered the little moustache under his chin and eyed Zeng Canghai's lean face.

But Zeng Canghai went resolutely on:

"Go and fetch it this instant and hand it to me. I take all responsibility! — Do you know? The Communist bandits have come to Qiliqiao and mean to sack the town tonight, and these fifty thousand in silver must on no account be left in the town overnight. Sun the Third's affairs are the same to me as my own, and I cannot stand by with my hands in my sleeves."

"Oh — as to that, the wind of it was abroad early this morning, and I've already wired Third Master for his instructions. Should there be the least stir tonight, I have my own arrangements for these fifty thousand in silver. This is a duty that falls within my charge; how should I dare put Elder Cang to the trouble!"

"And if something should go wrong, can you bear that responsibility?"

"I can! Elder Cang's kind intent I take to heart with thanks!"

Fei the little-moustache answered firmly, and rose again to go. But his eyes rolled round, and of a sudden he sat down once more, and turning to look at Zeng Canghai's face, at once so full of hatred and so cast down, he asked:

"Where did Elder Cang come by the news, to know that something is bound to happen tonight?"

"Battalion Commander He told me with his own mouth. He too had only just had secret word of it, and — it seemed Commander He was in something of a fright himself. Do you know whom pockmarked Wang's big boat carried to the county town?"

"It was Battalion Commander He's concubine, gone to the county town to return a call on the magistrate's lady. — Oh, so that is how it is! But Elder Cang perhaps does not yet know that at two o'clock this very day Commander He gave the Chamber of Commerce his guarantee that he would answer wholly for the peace of the town. Only, he said, 'the men have gone three months without pay, and something must be laid on to gild the thing, so as to put heart into them'; and he asked the Chamber of Commerce for a loan of thirty thousand dollars —"

"And did the Chamber of Commerce agree?"

"Agree it did, of course. The money's already been sent over. — Ah, night is coming down, and I've pressing business yet … When does Elder Cang set out? I may not be able to reach the wharf to see you off with due ceremony — your pardon, your pardon!"

So saying, Fei the little-moustache made a bow that swept to the ground, and hurried off in great haste.

Zeng Canghai made a show of seeing him off to the dripping eaves of the great hall, then turned back in a towering rage. He clenched his teeth and did nothing but snort, pacing round and round that great empty, echoing hall. In the three hours gone by he had spent no end of scheming, and now, all unlooked-for, the whole of it had come to nothing. Above all, that last fifty thousand dollars had slipped from his grasp, and he hated Fei the little-moustache with a hatred fit for the murderer of his own father!

His head bent in search of some scheme of revenge, his feet carried him through a long covered walk and brought him before the steps of the garden hall. Within, on the opium couch, a single lamp burned small as a bean, its little flame of pale yellow leaping without cease. He blundered in headlong; suddenly there was a stir, and from the couch two figures started up — and in the dim lamplight he saw plainly enough that the one was his precious son Jiaju, and the other was A Jin.

"You brute!"

Zeng Canghai gave one violent cry, and then all went dark before his eyes, his legs turned to water, and his heart seemed on fire. He caught instinctively at a chair, and sank limp into it. The few sparse hairs of his beard trembled and quivered.

By the time he could make out the things before him clearly again, A Jin was already gone, and only Zeng Jiaju crouched on the couch like a rutting dog, his eyes glittering as he stared at his father.

His son's incest, A Jin's shamelessness, Fei the little-moustache's odiousness, and the menace of the Communist troops at Qiliqiao besides: all churned together at once in Zeng Canghai's brain, and he knew not how to roar and vent his wrath for the best. In the end it was jealousy that won the upper hand. Zeng Canghai loosed his cracked voice and cursed:

"You brute! Even granting your mouth waters for it, if you'd the gumption to go and get yourself a few to play with outside, well and good — but did I bid you eat what's ready to hand at home? Scoundrel! And when her belly swells, is the child to be your brother? Or is it to be your son! A Jin, that slut of a —"

But then — bang, bang, bang, bang! from far off, and thickening on the instant faster and faster. It was gunfire! Like the firecrackers on New Year's Eve. Zeng Canghai gave a great start and began to shout like a madman:

"It's over! It's over! We're ruined! We're ruined! — You little brute! Why don't you run out this instant and see — which quarter is it, how far off from here?"

Zeng Jiaju made no sound, but drew his body tighter together instead. Suddenly someone came running in, weeping and clamouring together, her hair fallen loose all over her face: it was A Jin. Seizing hold of Zeng Canghai, the young woman wound herself about the old man like a snake, weeping and crying:

"It's all the young master's doing, the ruin of me! I wouldn't have it, and he, he — "

With all his strength Zeng Canghai struck A Jin off with a slap of his hand, too choked with rage to get out a word. By now the gunfire had come nearer, and the shouting of voices could be heard as well. Zeng Jiaju's wife came running in too, sobbing and weeping, the small child in her arms, and behind her a whole long string of women: the wet-nurse, the coarse-work maid, the servant-girls, all huddled together in a panic, darting this way and that and crying out in confusion.

Suddenly the gunfire could be heard no more, only the far-off hubbub of voices. On the wutong tree outside the garden hall the old crows beat their wings with a flapping and clapping, and a few even blundered into the hall itself. The knot of women too left off their crying, and only the child still wept. Zeng Canghai felt his heart ease, and catching sight of the child-wetted Three Principles of the People still lying on the couch, he snatched it up with one hand, held it high on the crown of his head, and with a thump went down on his knees, praying in haste:

"Premier on high, spirit of the Premier on high, protect, oh protect this believer in your Three Principles of the People!"

The prayer was not yet done when the gunfire rose deafening, thicker and louder and nearer than before. Rat-tat-tat — the machine-guns had opened up too. Zeng Canghai leapt convulsively to his feet, and the Three Principles of the People fell to the floor. Without a sound the old man ran for his life toward the inner rooms. But just at this moment A Er came running out, and struck him full in the chest, so that Zeng Canghai fell to the ground. A Er took no heed of anything, but only cried, gasping for breath:

"Get you to the back! Lie down in the vegetable plot! Lie flat on the ground! The bullets are wicked! The street's all soldiers now! Front gate and back gate, all soldiers!"

"What? Have the Communist bandits been beaten back?"

With a strength come from he knew not where, Zeng Canghai sprang to his feet and, clutching A Er, asked.

"It's the soldiers and the militia trading fire! Soldiers fighting soldiers now!"

"Rubbish! Get out of my sight!"

At these words, which struck him as all wrong, Zeng Canghai of a sudden put on the master's majesty again. But glancing sharply round, he saw the courtyard lit up a glowing red — somewhere a fire had broken out! Rat-tat-tat — the machine-guns came again after it. Reckoning that all was lost, Zeng Canghai shouted an order to his daughter-in-law and the wet-nurse and the rest to gather up the valuables at once. He himself took up the opium-lamp, ran to a table in the right-hand corner of the garden hall, opened a document-chest, and stuffed great bundles of land-deeds, promissory notes, and passbooks into his pockets. It was only now that Zeng Jiaju, who had crouched on the couch all this while without moving or making a sound, sprang over with a start and thrust his own hand into the document-chest to grope about in it too. Suddenly a burst of shouting seemed to break out at their very feet. Zeng Canghai, in his fright, let everything in his hands fall to the floor. Taking no thought for his son, he turned and ran for the inner rooms, but in the half-dark ran head-on into someone, who seized hold of Zeng Canghai and cried in a shrill voice:

"Master, save me! — "

It was A Jin again. At the same moment a flood of firelight came rushing in from outside as if it flew, and in the glare could be seen seven or eight men, all carrying torches. A Jin knew one of them at once for her own husband, and in her fright her legs gave way; without knowing it she sat down on the ground, clasped her head, and shrank into a ball. Seizing his chance, Zeng Canghai fled for his life without so much as turning his head, and in a twinkling was gone from sight.

"You shameless, heartless baggage — where's the old brute?"

A Jin's husband sprang a step forward and asked in a voice of rage. A Jin only wept. Two others had already laid hold of Zeng Jiaju and pushed him before a young man.

"The old dog's fled to the back!"

"Jinbao! No need to give chase! The men we've posted at the back all know him by sight!"

So several of them cried in confusion. Just then Zeng Jiaju's wife came charging out from within, her hair all loose and streaming, and seeing at a glance that her husband was taken, she flung herself desperately toward him. But someone had already caught her by the hair from behind, and with a violent wrench flung her down, asking sternly:

"What are you about?"

"What am I about! What are you about, seizing my man?"

Zeng Jiaju's wife, sitting on the ground, screamed like a madwoman. Suddenly, turning her head, she saw A Jin crouching beside her, and rolling over where she lay she seized A Jin, bit her hard on the shoulder, and the two of them, grappling in a tangle, fell to fighting.

"It's all this trouble you've brought down on us, you slut! — the old one, the young one, you'd have them all — I'll beat you dead, I'll beat you dead!"

Torches and shouting came again from behind the garden hall. Three men were dragging Zeng Canghai along, and one of them was A Er. Zeng Canghai, all covered in ashes, did nothing but cry for mercy. A Jin's husband ran up and drove his fist straight into the old man's face, saying through clenched teeth:

"You old dog! Do you too set store by your life?"

"Beat him to death! Bite him to death! Zeng the Skinner!"

Rage swept up like a storm. But the young man with the pistol at his belt came over and held the crowd back, crying with great authority:

"No brawling! He must be tried first!"

"Try him! Try him! The old Skinner lends out seal-money, the old Skinner has wrested away our land! — "

"The old dog has seized my wife by force! And had the police beat me!"

"He's had the police seize many of us! We'll bite him to death alive!"

"Ha! So you're a Nationalist into the bargain?"

The young man's voice rang out, clear and ringing, amid the clamour of cursing.

Zeng Canghai's heart gave a leap. He knew not why, but of a sudden he made sure that there was hope for him now; rousing his whole spirit, he looked into the young man's face in the blazing torchlight and said with an effort:

"No, no! I hate the Nationalists above all men! In Sun Chuanfang's day I helped him seize a great many Nationalists, and had a great many shot! If you don't believe me, go and inquire! — A Er here knows it! A Er, A Er — "

"But you're certainly one now! And what is your son?"

The young man cut short Zeng Canghai's self-defence, and turned to look at that wild-colt of a Zeng Jiaju.

"I'm not! I'm not!"

Zeng Jiaju screamed for his life. But before his cry was done, one of the two women who had worn themselves out fighting and left off a while — A Jin — sprang up all at once and screamed like a mad thing:

"You are, you are! Only just now you took out a little black card to frighten me and lure me — you've been the death of me! — Jinbao, spare me! They forced me, they frightened me, their power was too great for me!"

Just then the rat-tat of the machine-guns came again through the air. The young man with the pistol turned his face to glance outside, then drew his pistol, raised his voice, and gave the order:

"Leave two men here on guard. Take Zeng the Skinner and his son away!"

And so the torches and the tread of feet all moved off together toward the outside. Zeng Jiaju's wife, who had been sitting dazed on the ground, suddenly sprang up and, wailing aloud, ran after them. But before the eaves of the garden hall she caught her foot on something and fell. A Er, left on guard, and another peasant ran up and pulled her to her feet, crying at her sternly, as though to comfort her:

"Have you gone mad? This is no affair of yours! Every wrong has its doer, every debt its debtor! Get you to the back! No running about!"

Zeng Canghai and his son were now dragged and shoved out into the main street, where they saw peasants in threes and fives, each with a strip of red cloth bound round his neck and some weapon or other in his hand, running about the street in confusion. A party came toward them, without guns, holding a man too — and it was Li Si. Zeng Canghai was on the point of casting Li Si a glance of greeting when they brushed shoulders and were past each other. Zeng Canghai and his captors went westward, and it was from the west that the thick gunfire came. Every two or three minutes the machine-guns would roar out their rat-tat. All the shops and dwellings had shut their doors, and only a thread of lamplight showed through the chinks.

A hard wind came driving black smoke before it, with a smell of burning on it; somewhere, most likely, another fire had broken out.

They turned a corner, and could go no further. A little way ahead rose the high wall of the Hongchang pawnshop. Zeng Canghai and his son, and the seven or eight men guarding them, were wrapped about in a great motley throng: peasants with weapons of every sort, and soldiers too with red cloth bound round their necks, all crowded into this corner of the street. Suddenly from the high wall of the Hongchang pawnshop a streak of red light shot out, rat-tat-tat — that fuse-like thing sweeping to every quarter, and all at once the "fuse" flicked close to the very street-corner where Zeng Canghai and his son stood!

"Scat—ter!"

A voice shouted in rage from the press of men. The men guarding Zeng Canghai too made haste to take cover under the eaves at the street's edge, and all threw themselves flat on the ground. The crack of rifles rose up all round about them. Zeng Canghai was by now like a dead man, only his eyes still stared wide open. His son gazed in terror and stupor at the flashes of the machine-guns ahead. Just then, from behind the Hongchang pawnshop, a fierce volley of gunfire suddenly rolled up, and a wisp of black smoke shot into the sky beside the pawnshop's watch-tower; then a red glare flashed, and tongues of flame leapt out of the thick smoke. The Hongchang pawnshop was afire! The machine-guns grew a little fainter, but at the same moment a deafening roar of shouting broke out all at once from this side:

"Charge! Charge!"

Countless human shapes sprang up from the ground, from the shelter of the street-corners, from under the eaves of the shops, and rushed forward like a whirlwind.

The several men guarding the Zengs, father and son, rushed forward too. But two of them at once fell back again, and dragging the two Zengs they retreated to the rear; yet before they had gone a bare ten paces and more, the machine-gun on the high wall of the Hongchang pawnshop swept round one last time, and all four men fell. Another mingled band of peasants and soldiers came flying up from behind, trampled over these four bodies, and made straight for the Hongchang pawnshop.

The sound of the machine-guns thinned little by little.

Zeng Jiaju lay flat on the ground, and at first thought himself dead; then, trying to move his hands and feet a little — strange! his hands and feet were sound as ever, and he felt no pain anywhere on his body. He sat up and looked about him. The two peasants neither stirred nor breathed. Zeng Canghai lay with his body curled up, half his face turned upward, his mouth gaping wide, and blood trickling from it. Zeng Jiaju sat stunned a while, then suddenly leapt up and took to his heels.

As he ran in wild confusion into a lonely, out-of-the-way lane, his foot caught on something and he fell. But like a spring he was up again on the instant. Half unaware, he looked back toward the Hongchang pawnshop: the flames were shooting straight up into the sky, and half the heavens were red. The gunfire still sounded off and on, mingled with burst upon burst of shouting. While he was still at a loss what to do, his foot struck again the thing lying across the ground, and he looked at it by instinct: it was a dead man, a strip of red cloth bound round his neck, a pistol still clutched in his hand. A fine idea unfolded of a sudden in Zeng Jiaju's mind. Hastily he untied the red cloth from the dead man's neck and bound it round his own, and fishing the pistol out of the dead man's hand he ran on ahead once more.

By now the gunfire had all but ceased, and only the whoosh of the burning fire and the babel of voices came from far away. But this lane was like something dead; every house had its gate fast shut, and there was not a glimmer of lamplight. As he walked, Zeng Jiaju looked left and right like a stray dog after food. Near the end of the lane he stopped short. A two-storeyed house ahead of him showed a gleam of light. He wavered a while, then went up and knocked at the door, a savage glint shooting from his eyes.

"Is it you come back? Amitabha be praised!"

A young woman's voice came to open the door. But when she saw that it was a stranger, murder written all over his face, levelling a pistol at her, she gave one wild scream and ran within. Zeng Jiaju chased in after her without a word. He pursued her across a courtyard, and before a lamplit room the woman fell. Taking no heed of her, Zeng Jiaju rushed headlong into the room, and full in his face there flashed the frightened, wrinkled features of an old woman, who seemed to cry out an "Aiyo!" besides.

Zeng Jiaju rushed on up the stairs and into a bedroom, this too with a lamp lit, a white cotton bed-curtain hanging low over the bed. Zeng Jiaju lifted the curtain aside with one hand, and saw the rosy little face of a small child peeping out from a lined coverlet of green silk. Like a whirlwind he gave the green silk coverlet a tug, and then, like a whirlwind again, dashed to the wardrobe before the bed, threw open its doors, and thrust in his hand to grope about inside.

"Mama! Mama!"

The child on the bed suddenly began to cry and call out. The sound made Zeng Jiaju start. In wild confusion he raised the pistol and fired at the bed. Crack! — the shot rang all the more ghastly and dreadful in that little room. Zeng Jiaju himself gave a violent start, and the pistol dropped to the floorboards. But the child on the bed only wept the harder. At the same moment feet sounded on the stairs outside the room, and the young woman came running in, weeping and crying together. She flung herself onto the bed, caught up the child and pressed it to her breast, then, like a stone idol, leaned against the little side-table before the bed and stared dully at Zeng Jiaju.

Half unaware, Zeng Jiaju picked up the pistol and levelled it once more at the woman and the child; his face was iron-grey, and his heart pounded and swelled. But just then the old woman came trembling in too, and with a thud fell to her knees on the floorboards, murmuring:

"Great lord and master! Spare our lives! … Spare our lives! The jewels, the money …"

"Bring them! Quick!"

Zeng Jiaju spat out these two words, and seemed himself to grow calmer, letting the muzzle of the pistol point down toward the floorboards.

The child in the young woman's arms cried out aloud again, burrowed its head into her bosom, and called "Mama" in a low voice. Feeling by instinct that her own little darling was still alive, the young woman's ghastly-pale face all at once showed a faint smile of comfort.

Zeng Jiaju's heart gave another leap. From that winsome smile he suddenly knew the woman before him for the mistress of the Jinhua foreign-goods store on the main street — the very woman the sight of whom, time and again, had stirred evil thoughts in him! He looked at the woman, and looked at the pistol in his own hand, took a step forward, and in a flash pressed her down onto the bed and began to tear at her clothes. This unlooked-for assault frightened the woman stiff as a dead thing; but an instant later she resisted with all her might, her eyes glaring straight, fixed upon Zeng Jiaju's cruel and evil face.

"Great king! Great king! Spare our lives! Spare us, spare her! Do a kind deed!"

The old woman cried out desperately in a shaking voice, came stumbling and tottering over, threw her arms round Zeng Jiaju's legs and pulled with all her strength; and a heap of jewels and silver money clattered rattling down onto the floorboards.

"Get off!"

Zeng Jiaju roared in fury and with a violent kick flung the old woman off. And at that very moment the young woman, with a desperate effort, gave a roll of her body and sprang up with a heave, screaming like a mad thing:

"I know you! I know you! You're the son of Zeng the Skinner! I know you!"

Zeng Jiaju's face changed utterly of a sudden. In wild confusion he snatched up the pistol that lay on the edge of the bed, and fired one shot straight at the young woman.
