## Chapter Four. Bloodshed on Avenue Joffre

### 1. The Arrests, as if Foreknown

Nothing galled Lu Xun, reading the newspapers, like coming upon a great white blank, a whole block of it, the way the expurgated Plum in the Golden Vase, or the little empty boxes in the cleaned-up First and Second Collections of Slapping the Table in Amazement, always drop an empty square in behind "in the lamplight's shadow, within the mermaid-silk bed-curtains" or "the two of them afire, they loosed their clothes for bed," to stand in for all the lewdness and villainy the red brush had struck out. Lu Xun said: "'□□' is a home-grown article; the thing is already there in the Tale of King Mu, Son of Heaven, and my teacher taught me to call it a lacuna. But in the old days one met it only in the writings of the ancients, where there was no supplying what was lost; now it turns up in the writings of living men, where to supply it is out of the question. Of late the fashion is turning, rather, to '××.' Slash a few lines of it at random, print it, and you may indeed make the reader admire the writer's ferocity and hate the censor's severity; yet at the moment of submission you also let the censor relish the writer's compliance. So much one dares not say, and one only slashes away the harder for it. Two birds with one stone, and cleverer than merely dotting out a line or two. ... A passage cut from a daily paper, too, may now, it seems, be left blank (the trade calls it opening a skylight)." (Lu Xun, 1981)

But a blank has its uses too. A blank is the best of guides. Let a blank appear in a Chinese paper, and you had only to turn to the foreign-language press to find it: find it every time, find the thing worth reading, a piece of explosive news, some grave and startling event.

And so, on November 12, 1929, every "Chinese paper, the Shen Bao and the China Times among them, opened a skylight, keeping nothing but a single headline" (North China Daily News, November 13, 1929). Anyone with a scrap of English turned at once to the foreign North China Daily News, and in the North China Daily News fixed on a "case of political murder," and within that "case of political murder" fixed on a "reprisal," and within the "reprisal" fixed on one "most important" figure, a man who had "in the last few months laid hands on a great many Communists."

These "many Communists" had been taken at about four in the afternoon of August 24, 1929, at 12 Jingyuanli on Xinzha Road in Shanghai. The place was not far from Wangdeli, at the corner of Avenue Road and Gordon Road, where Luo Yinong had been arrested in April 1928: five or six blocks apart, no more than two thousand meters as the crow flies. Nearer still, of course, was Xinzha Village on Xinzha Road (today Lane 876, Xinzha Road), for the two stood on the same street, one east of Cardan Road (today Shimen No. 2 Road) and one west of it, the one on the south side facing north, the other on the north side facing south, with only Cardan Road, Shanhaiguan Road, Cixi Road, and Datong Road (today Datian Road) between them. It was at 28 Xinzha Village, on New Year's Day 1928, that Luo Yinong and Li Zheshi had held the feast for their wedding.

No. 12 Jingyuanli is generally called the "home" of Bai Xin, "Peng Pai's secretary" or "secretary of the Central Military Commission," "a regular meeting place of the Central Military and Peasant Commissions" (Mu Xin, 2013); or else: "Bai Xin lodged as a tenant at 1015 Jingyuanli, and the place became an important liaison point for the Central Military Commission and the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission" (Party History Research Office of the Jing'an District Committee, Shanghai, 2016). Li Guande, in Jing'an by the Sea (2003), says instead: "No. 12 Jingyuanli on Xinzha Road (today Lane 613, Xinzha Road) is an old-style two-story shikumen lane house built in 1917. ... In February 1929 the Center decided to appoint Peng Pai to the Jiangsu Provincial Standing Committee and as secretary of the provincial Military Commission, and his office was set up here; this too was a seat of the Central Military Commission."

As for where Peng Pai lived once he came to work in Shanghai, the usual account gives "Bailuli on Great Western Road" (Luo Kequn, Xiong Zechu, Qiu Jinrong, and Guo Chengxiang, 1981). Only, Great Western Road lay in the French Concession, its eastern stretch today's Jinling West Road, its middle stretch today's Yan'an Middle Road, its western stretch today's Yan'an West Road; whereas Bailuli was in the old walled city, at what is now Lane 51, Zixia Road, hard by Doushi Street, Huayi Street, Wailang Jiaqiao Street, and Wangjiazuijiao Street. The two have nothing whatever to do with each other.

Li Guande holds that Peng Pai's Shanghai "residence," which was also his "office," was just 12 Jingyuanli on Xinzha Road. There Peng Pai "lived and worked for half a year," "taking one tingzijian of a bare eight square meters for both workroom and lodging, the room holding only a small iron bed, a kerosene stove, a plain table, and two chairs, with the front room for meetings and liaison."

If 12 Jingyuanli was indeed Peng Pai's Shanghai lodging, then it will no longer do to say that "on the afternoon of August 24, 1929, Peng Pai went to Jingyuanli on Xinzha Road to attend a meeting of the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission" (Luo Kequn, Xiong Zechu, Qiu Jinrong, and Guo Chengxiang, 1981). And so Li Guande explains the meeting otherwise. Peng Pai was then a member of the CCP Central Politburo, a member of the Jiangsu Provincial Standing Committee, and secretary of the provincial Military Commission. The Center had decided to "transfer him back to work on the Central Peasant Commission," and he had a handover to make. That was why, on the afternoon of August 24, 1929, he came to 12 Jingyuanli to meet Yang Yin, then a Standing Committee member of the CCP Central Politburo and head of the Central Military Department; Yan Changyi, then a member of the CCP Central Military Commission who also sat on the Jiangsu Provincial Military Commission; and Xing Shizhen and the others, the provincial Military Commission cadre responsible for work among the soldiery.

By Li Weihan's recollection, Peng Pai took up his posts on the Jiangsu Provincial Standing Committee and as secretary of its Military Commission in the latter part of January 1929. The Center had then named Luo Dengxian, Li Weihan, Zhao Rong (Kang Sheng), Xu Xigen, and Peng Pai to form the new Jiangsu Provincial Standing Committee. Peng Pai, whose brief covered both the peasant and the soldiers' work, carried out the Center's line without wavering: he set store by building a united front across the great majority of the countryside, rich peasants included, and pressed the propaganda for land revolution and for soviets. At the same time he worked hard to build up soldiers' organizations within the reactionary armies, to hasten the turn in their thinking.

Zhou Enlai thought highly of Peng Pai's gifts. Zhou was a Standing Committee member of the CCP Central Politburo, secretary of the Central Military Commission, and head of the Central Organization Department, and in drafting the Party Center's circular on the militarization of Party members he took up no small number of Peng Pai's suggestions. Most comrades in the Party knew nothing of military matters and set no store by soldiers' work; against this, Zhou Enlai insisted again and again on how much military work meant to the whole revolutionary struggle, and put it that they should "take the slogan of militarizing Party members as the core of all military work; whether military work as a whole can be done with any force depends entirely on how far Party members are militarized." (Party Literature Research Office of the CCP Central Committee, 2007)

Peng Pai's handover also lends weight to the account that Zhou Enlai, too, was to have come to Jingyuanli that day. Within the Central Standing Committee, Zhou's charge was organizational work, and Peng Pai's "transfer back to the Central Peasant Commission" was a major personnel change; there was reason for him to come in person and explain it to Yang Yin, Yan Changyi, and the rest. But though he said he would come, he did not in fact come. Mu Xin says he "had something come up and did not attend." (Mu Xin, 2013) Wang Guangyuan says he "begged off sick." (Wang Guangyuan, 1999)

Besides Zhou Enlai, there was one other who should have been there that day and was "not": Bai Xin. So Ke Lin says.

Mu Xin says: "Ke Lin, a physician of the first rank, was himself a legendary figure of the hidden front, heavy with mystery." Under the alias "Ke Dawen," together with He Cheng, who went by "He Yusheng," he "opened a Dasheng Hospital in Yan'anli on Sichuan Road in Shanghai," to serve as "a secret organ of the Party Center." But Wang Guangyuan says the "Dasheng Hospital" was on Weihaiwei Road. Li Yimang, for his part, says: "Ke Lin's clinic was at the corner of Range Road on North Sichuan Road, upstairs over the Wuzhou Pharmacy." (Li Yimang, 2001)

I find Li Yimang's account the more credible. For in the Shanghai of those years there was a Yanjili, a Yanlinli, a Yankangli, a Yanzheli, a Yanlingli, and a Yanqingli, but no Yan'anli. And because across from the "Wuzhou Pharmacy" stood the "Sanmin Photo Studio," whose proprietor, Fan Mengju, also had ties to the Central Special Branch: the "Sanmin Photo Studio" was "one of the places" the Special Branch "stored its weapons" (Hongkou District People's Government, Shanghai, 1989). And because, during his years "in practice as a doctor," Ke Lin lodged in Li Yimang's own home, the two of them together morning and night, each knowing the other to the bone.

Ke Lin says: "The meeting had begun, and Bai Xin had not come, but the Kuomintang reactionaries' police did." (Ke Lin, 1992)

Luo Kequn and the others say Bai Xin did come, only that "the hour set for the meeting had arrived" and he "had not yet come." "In a moment Bai Xin came in, and the imperialist Municipal Council's patrolmen came in hard on his heels, and Peng Pai was seized." (Luo Kequn, Xiong Zechu, Qiu Jinrong, and Guo Chengxiang, 1981)

Still others say that, as the meeting opened, Bai Xin, sitting in as always, took his usual place "at the Eight Immortals table in the parlor" and bent to his minutes, his pen flying.

Zhou Enlai, for his part, said: "The arrest of the four comrades Peng Pai, Yang Yin, Yan Changyi, and Xing Shizhen fell at about four in the afternoon of August 24, 1929. At that hour the imperialists' armed patrolmen and the Public Security Bureau's Chinese detectives came roaring up in several red armored cars." "Once they had posted themselves inside the lane and out, they went upstairs and took their men as if they had known in advance, arresting five by name (besides Peng, Yang, Yan, and Xing, there was Comrade Zhang Jichun)." "The five arrested comrades were bundled at once into a motorcar by the detectives and driven straight to the Xinzha police station." (Zhou Enlai, 1930)

The arrest of Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others dealt our Party's military command a ruinous blow. "That same night Zhou Enlai chaired an emergency meeting to work out measures for rescuing Peng Pai and the rest and for punishing the traitor." (Party Literature Research Office of the CCP Central Committee, 2007) Zhou Enlai handed both tasks to Gu Shunzhang and the Central Special Branch.

### 2. The Double Agent

Zhou Enlai gave the task of rescuing Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the rest to Gu Shunzhang, and Gu Shunzhang at once sent Chen Geng, chief of the Central Special Branch's intelligence section, to find Yang Dengying.

Yang Dengying, who also went by Bao Junfu and Liu Junshan, was born in 1901, a native of Zhongshan in Guangdong. He had studied in Japan from boyhood and had fluent Japanese. He came home in 1919, and during the May Thirtieth movement he came to know Yang Jianhong, a Green Gang man and head of the foreign-firm workers' union. Being fellow provincials, the two grew close.

Chen Jianyu, in The Legend of Chen Yangshan, Lucky General of the Hidden Front (2006), says: "In March 1928 Chen Lifu and Zhang Daofan had Yang Jianhong and others set about building an intelligence apparatus in Shanghai. Yang Jianhong, then chief secretary for Party-affairs investigation, was hard pressed for men, and men he could safely use were rarer still; and as he cast about in his difficulty, a figure flashed into his mind. It was none other than his fellow provincial and good friend, the Bao Junfu who a few years before had so often been in his company in Shanghai." Zhang Guodong says Yang Jianhong was "chief secretary of the reporting sub-section," not "chief secretary for Party-affairs investigation."

Zhang Guodong "entered, four months after the 'April 12' coup, the Nanjing Central Party Affairs School of the Kuomintang, of which Chiang Kai-shek was principal. On graduating in June 1928, he was posted straightaway to the Investigation Section as an assistant secretary, and from then on belonged to the Zhongtong intelligence system," rising in the end to secretary at the headquarters of the Zhongtong Bureau, placed among "the highest decision-making level of the Zhongtong Bureau," able "in the director's absence, to act for him in running the bureau's affairs. ... The Investigation Section was at first divided into two sub-sections, Reporting and Compilation, each with a chief secretary and, under him, a number of secretaries and assistant secretaries. The first chief secretary of the Reporting sub-section was Yang Jianhong. Yang was a member of the Shanghai Green Gang; in the autumn of 1928, in a struggle for power within the Green Gang, Yang was driven to suicide." (Zhang Guodong, 1992)

Chen Jianyu says that Yang Jianhong, hard pressed for men, came in person to Yang Dengying for help. He said the Kuomintang's Central Organization Department had set up an Investigation Section that was just short of talent like his, and that he must join; and Yang Dengying agreed. Yang Jianhong then introduced him to Chen Lifu and Zhang Daofan. Chen Lifu and Zhang Daofan booked a private room at the Palace Hotel on Route Père Robert in the Shanghai French Concession and gave a banquet expressly for Yang Dengying, charging him to make wide contact with every kind of society, organ, and school and to gather intelligence, above all on the doings of the Communist Party and its front organizations. They gave Yang Dengying a pass that let him come and go as he pleased at the Kuomintang's Shanghai Municipal Party Headquarters, and told him their goal was to use the anti-Communist purge apparatus they had set up in Shanghai to wipe out the Communist Party, inside the concessions and out, root and branch.

Chen Yangshan had come to know Yang Dengying in the spring of 1926, when someone introduced him and he went to study Japanese under Yang Dengying. After the Spring Festival of 1928, Chen Yangshan, his peasant uprising in Zhejiang having failed, came to Shanghai with nowhere to stay and lodged in Yang Dengying's home. Yang Dengying knew Chen Yangshan was a Communist. At that time Yang Jianhong was forever coming round to press Yang Dengying to work for the Party Affairs Investigation Section of the Kuomintang's Central Organization Department. Yang Dengying asked Chen Yangshan to put it to the organization and hear what his superiors thought. Chen Yangshan then answered to the Jiangsu Provincial Committee, and the provincial committee had him write a report to the Center explaining that here was a connection that could be used. Before long Chen Geng talked with Chen Yangshan, studied Yang Dengying's case in detail, judged the man to be, at bottom, inclined our way and usable, and reported in turn to Zhou Enlai. Zhou Enlai held that Yang Dengying, though not politically very reliable, was genuinely useful in the struggle on the hidden front, and so decided to bring him into play. That May, Chen Geng met with Yang Dengying, settled the intelligence relationship, and set Chen Yangshan to handle him on a single line; and so Chen Yangshan was transferred into the Central Special Branch.

Zhou Enlai's bringing Yang Dengying into play accorded entirely with the Comintern's conception. The Intelligence Department of the Comintern Executive Committee, in its Written Report on the State of the Secret Work of the Communist Parties in the Far Eastern and Near Eastern Countries and on Their Intelligence Work (June 3, 1932), remarked specially that the Central Special Branch "relies in its work on fairly reliable intelligence about the enemy's activities, intelligence obtained either by placing Communists in positions of responsibility within Kuomintang organizations, or by drawing on the personal connections of certain responsible comrades within the Kuomintang camp."

This was concealment within concealment, a mole within the mole. Yang Dengying was the Chinese version of Dusko Popov. Dusko Popov was a Yugoslav patriot. During the Second World War he was at once a British spy and a German spy. He served MI5 and he served MI6, and at the same time swore his loyalty to Adolf Hitler. His codename was "Tricycle." All his pains went to leaving the Germans deceived about the British capacity to resist invasion. The British paid him handsomely for it. The Germans did not stint him either. And he took it all, every bill, with an easy conscience. Not a day of his passed but at some feast or drinking-party. He was a regular at the Moroccan restaurant and the Stork Club. He kept a mansion on the Gold Coast of Long Island, and the women who shared his bed were Hollywood's reigning beauties. He never once thought there was anything wrong in it. His byword was: "Since the money comes so easily, why should I not enjoy myself while I can? This is simply what I am. If I did not live this way I would 'explode' of my own accord, and the Nazi intelligence service would spot at once that something was off with me."

In the same way, Yang Dengying was one servant with two masters: he worked for the Kuomintang and he did the Communist Party's business too. He drew Xu Enzeng's salary, and he used the house, the car, the secretary, and the bodyguard the Central Special Branch gave him.

Yang Dengying's secretary was An E.

An E's original name was Zhang Shiyuan, her alias Zhang Ying. She was born in 1905, a native of Fantan Village in Huolu County, Hebei (today the Chang'an District of Shijiazhuang). In 1923 she passed into the Western Painting Department of the Beijing National Art College. She joined the Party in 1925, and in 1926 went to study in the Soviet Union, entering Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow. She came home in 1929, and the Party placed her at Yang Dengying's side; from then on, against all her habits, she took to dressing in the latest style, eating with a flourish, spending freely, and living in comfort. After the founding of New China, Yang Dengying was still in touch with An E.

In December 1956 Yang Dengying appealed to An E for winter clothes. An E sent him daily necessaries from time to time thereafter, and made a point of sending Yang Dengying's youngest daughter a bicycle.

Yang Dengying's bodyguard was Lian Desheng. Lian Desheng, like An E, was also a Communist, and had also been sent by the Party to study in the Soviet Union, at the University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow (in full, the Communist University of the Toilers of the East). Under cover as a bodyguard, Lian Desheng followed Yang Dengying freely in and out of the police stations of Shanghai's great concessions and the Kuomintang's military, police, and intelligence organs. Whenever Yang Dengying came by urgent intelligence, he always passed it to Lian Desheng, who carried it straight to Chen Yangshan and on to Chen Geng.

Some say Yang Dengying was "an underground worker who lay hidden for years in the very heart of the 'Zhongtong,'" one of "the Four Great Intelligence Heroes of the CCP" (Sang Ye, 2009). The claim is not exact. Yang Dengying was never a formal member of the Central Special Branch's intelligence section; he was only a subject of the Special Branch's work, a double agent in the fullest sense. What he wanted most was to have it both ways, to keep everyone happy: to hold office under the Kuomintang and to serve the Communist Party at the same time, to curry favor on both sides and profit on both, offending no one. Zhang Guodong says: "Yang Dengying really was a man of boundless resource. He could regularly, even issue by issue, get the circulars the CCP Center sent to the various provincial committees into the hands of the Investigation Section, though the actual contents were mostly general lines of work, policy, or analyses of the situation, and not concrete instructions for any single task. But it was enough to win Xu Enzeng's regard, and Xu would at once copy it up to the two Chens and to Chiang Kai-shek, to show his own merit and his fine leadership. More than that, Yang Dengying could also lay hands on some Trotskyist publications to send to the Investigation Section. Every time Yang came to Nanjing with his intelligence 'harvest' in his pocket, Xu Enzeng would send men to wait on him warmly and take Yang to dine at the Anle Restaurant, the World Hotel, and other first-class establishments. Amid the clink of cups Yang would show, again and again, his air of self-satisfaction. I did not know Yang at all well, yet I too was more than once asked along to keep him company. Later, back from my studies abroad, I heard it said that Yang was in fact an intelligence peddler, taking money from both ends: the Investigation Section on the one side and the Communist Party on the other."

But at that time Yang Dengying was not yet, as Chen Jianyu has it, the "Kuomintang Central Organization Department's special commissioner stationed in Shanghai"; still less could he have been "appointed by Chiang Kai-shek," nor could he have "set up an office in the straddle-building across from Dadeli on North Sichuan Road, a place for gathering and distributing intelligence, and formally hung out the signboard of the 'Shanghai Office of the Kuomintang Central Investigation Section'" (Mu Xin, 2013). The "Kuomintang Central Organization Department" never had any "Shanghai office" at all. Zhang Guodong likewise says: "When the Investigation Section was first set up, it had as yet established no directly subordinate lower organs anywhere in the country. By 1930, to widen its range and to get the intelligence it gathered to the Investigation Section for handling in good time, it sent out three special commissioners, furnished with staff and wireless sets, stationed severally at Shanghai, Wuhan, and Kaifeng. The Shanghai commissioner was Yang Dengying, later renamed Bao Junfu ..."

### 3. Ambush at Fenglin Bridge

Chen Geng arranged to meet Yang Dengying at the "Renaissance."

It was a café kept by White Russians. Its regulars were adherents of Imperial Russia or their sympathizers: the princes and grandees, the rich gentry and great merchants of the tsarist age, cast adrift now in Shanghai. Day after day the same familiar faces turned up. Strange faces appeared from time to time as well, but never alone: beside such a man there was always a familiar face, a White Russian woman. These nobles of a vanished day drowned their spirits in memories of the past, to wear away this dreadful present. The great houses and high carriages of St. Petersburg, the splendid dress, the revolution swift as a thunderbolt, the strife of blood and iron, the flight with death for a neighbor, the end in which all came to nothing: their wandering existence rose before them again and again, and stirred in them a grief without bounds. In their hearts there was only the past. They looked back on what had been, they clung to the past, and on the past, too, they lived, for their bread, their herring, and their vodka.

Chen Geng asked Yang Dengying how things stood with Peng Pai and the rest.

Yang Dengying told Chen Geng that August 25 was a Sunday, when the Provisional Court did not sit. The hearing opened on August 26; the judge and the Public Security Bureau's representative went through a show of proceedings, and then the men were remanded to the Public Security Bureau, taken away under armed guard in an armored car to the Public Security Bureau inside the city, and held at the detention house of the detective squad at the Shuixian Temple by the Little North Gate.

Learning that early on August 28 Peng Pai and the others were to be moved from the detention house of the Shuixian Temple detective squad to the Songhu Garrison Command at Longhua, Zhou Enlai at once ordered Gu Shunzhang to muster the whole strength of the Central Special Branch, halt the van on the road, and rescue them by force.

Zhou Enlai said: "After the second hearing, the Public Security Bureau, fearing some mishap, transferred them to the Garrison Command at dawn on the twenty-eighth." (Zhou Enlai, 1930)

There are several versions of the Central Special Branch's armed attempt, at dawn on August 28, 1929, to rescue Peng Pai and the others by Fenglin Bridge near Longhua. One is Li Qiang's recollection. Li Qiang said: "The Central Special Branch's armed operations were usually the charge of the 'Red Squad' under the Third Section. This operation to rescue Peng Pai was on a larger scale, and the 'Red Squad' was short of hands, so Zhou Enlai ordered out every man in the Central Special Branch who could handle a gun. About twenty took part in the rescue. Once they had their orders, they spent the night making ready: they went out and bought some gear they would need in a hurry, tools for striking off the irons and the like. Early on the twenty-eighth, the comrades of the Central Special Branch gathered at the Special Branch organ on Route Tunkadoo, waiting for Fan Mengju to bring the guns up by motorcycle. When the guns arrived and they opened the little leather case, they found every Mauser inside coated with a layer of grease and unusable, so they at once sent someone to buy kerosene, wiped the grease off, and rubbed on hair oil instead—an hour or two gone in the doing. Everything ready at last, they set off by truck. To cover the operation, the comrades of the Central Special Branch set a tripod on the truck and mounted a movie camera on it, as though they were shooting a film on location. The truck drove to a three-way fork called Fenglin Bridge, on the road the prison van was bound to take, and stopped; everyone spread out and made ready. But they waited there a long time and never saw the enemy's van come through. As it turned out, the comrades of the Central Special Branch had missed their chance: the enemy had already taken Peng Pai and the others off to Longhua. The armed attempt on the van failed."

What is hard to understand is why Ziding, the author of the Biography of Li Qiang (2004), should have turned the perfectly sensible sequence—clean the guns first, then set out—into setting out first and cleaning the guns after; that is: "The Special Branch men and women stood ready in force, waiting only for the prison van from the Shuixian Temple detention house to appear on the highway. Sadly, one link in the rescue at the execution ground went wrong: the weapons meant for the rescue arrived late, the anti-rust grease inside them not yet cleaned off and the guns unusable, and the rescue could not succeed." We regret that the Biography of Li Qiang should stray so far from Li Qiang's own recollection.

I have talked this question over seriously with Li Yanming. Li Yanming is Li Qiang's son, and has served in turn as assistant research fellow of the State Council Political Research Office, research officer of the Research Office of the CCP Central Secretariat, director of the Political Systems Research Office and of the Marxism-Leninism Research Office at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, and standing director of the China Society for Historical Materialism, among other posts. Li Yanming says that if the Biography of Li Qiang is ever reprinted, the relevant sections ought to be corrected to match his father's recollection.

On October 16, 2017, I met Li Qiang's other son, Li Xiaoqiang, in Shanghai; speaking of the "rescue at the execution ground" in the Biography of Li Qiang, he too said it should be revised.

There is, besides, a small episode Ke Lin sets down in Remembering Peng Pai (1992). "On the day of the rescue," he and Chen Geng "waited for news at the French Park, and when a long while had passed with never a sound of gunfire, we knew the rescue plan had not come off."

If Ke Lin's memory is sound, this small episode raises two small questions in turn:

First, that Chen Geng was not himself at the scene of the rescue; otherwise he could not have been "waiting for news at the French Park" with Ke Lin.

Second, that the French Park (today Fuxing Park) borders Route Lafayette (today Fuxing Middle Road) and Bai'erbu Road (today Chongqing Middle Road), while Fenglin Bridge lies far off on Xujiahui Road (today Zhaojiabang Road) and Gao'en Road (today Gao'an Road), several kilometers away in a straight line at the very least. Even had the "rescue plan" "come off," Ke Lin and Chen Geng could hardly have "heard the gunfire."

### 4. Bai Xin Turns Traitor

Zhou Enlai said that after Peng Pai and the others were delivered to the Songhu Garrison Command, "without so much as a first hearing, they were simply put in handcuffs and leg-irons, chains and shackles, and kept under the heaviest guard in the Command's guardhouse." Zhou Enlai said further: "Once they had entered the Garrison Command, they knew they must die, and so the letters they got out were mostly in the nature of last testaments. They charged the comrades of the Party not to grieve over their arrest, but to press on and win the growth of the revolution. They charged the Party's leading comrades to husband themselves for the Party's sake. ... Even the last words they left to their own wives were all urgings to strive for the Party."

On the afternoon of August 30, 1929, Peng Pai, Yang Yin, Yan Changyi, Xing Shizhen, and the rest "spoke their last words freely to the soldiers and to the crowd of prisoners within the jail, and singing the Internationale and shouting slogans went out through the prison gate, till the common soldiers and the convicts alike wept aloud, and even the guardhouse warders hid their faces."

As Peng Pai and the others died, "the crowd in the jail faintly heard the shots." They "could not see the valiant bodies of their leaders, yet the valiant deeds of their leaders would be remembered in bright glory forever in every heart, never to be effaced." Zhou Enlai not only wrote these tear-stained words into the Central proclamation, but said to the comrades of the Central Secretariat: "The proclamation must go out at once! It must go out at once!" He was silent a moment, then said: "We must wipe out the enemy! We must finish off the traitor Bai Xin!" (Zhou Enlai, 1930)

Zhou Enlai's naming of Bai Xin as a "traitor" rested on the prison testaments of Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the rest, who stated plainly that their undoing was "Bai's doing." As for the line that "the imperialists' armed patrolmen and the Public Security Bureau's Chinese detectives ... went up and took their men as if they had known in advance, arresting five by name," that was stronger proof still.

Zhou Enlai held that this "arresting by name" was the very key to finding the Judas within the Party.

"Arresting by name" ruled out any blind swoop by the enemy.

Had the enemy not come prepared, they could never have "gone up and taken their men as if they had known in advance."

And the enemy had come prepared because they had got exact intelligence.

The enemy's exact intelligence could only have come from someone in the know about that day's meeting.

After the shots on the Longhua execution ground had died away, of all those in the know about that day's meeting only one remained safe and sound, at large and at his ease.

That man was Bai Xin.

Ke Lin said: "On the matter of Bai Xin's turning traitor, I do not think it was any accident. Bai Xin had been a political instructor in the political instruction corps of Ye Ting's forces; at that time I was chief medical officer at the headquarters of the Fourth Army of the National Revolutionary Army, and as he was often ill and often came to me for treatment, he was well disposed toward me. After taking part in the Guangzhou Uprising he went with the army to Hailufeng, and later came to Shanghai too, and came to me again to be treated. At first he told me he had a relative who had been a fairly senior political officer in the Kuomintang's Wuhan forces, and who later joined the Nanchang Uprising and went south with the army to Hailufeng; when the uprising army was defeated at Chaoshan, the man tried to run, and Peng Pai caught him and had him shot. When Bai Xin told me this, he showed a gnashing, teeth-grinding hatred of Peng Pai. I once spoke of Bai Xin's private grudge against Peng Pai to Yang Yin, but because Yang Yin did not report it to the Party Center in good time, the Party Center sent Bai Xin to the Jiangsu Military Commission, where he became Peng Pai's secretary. It was established afterward, by the Party organization, that once Bai Xin reached Shanghai he entered into close relations with Fan Zhengbo, head of the Kuomintang's Shanghai Municipal Party Headquarters, and was secretly selling out the organization and his comrades. After becoming Peng Pai's secretary, Bai Xin looked all along for a chance to wreck the Jiangsu Provincial Committee organ and help the reactionaries arrest Peng Pai. On August 24, when Bai Xin gave the leaders of the Jiangsu provincial Military Commission notice of the meeting on Xinzha Road, he had already reported the meeting place and the people to Fan Zhengbo in advance. The meeting began; Bai Xin had not come, but the Kuomintang reactionaries' police did, armed to the teeth, as though facing a great foe, and arrested the five leaders, Peng Pai among them, and took them away." (Ke Lin, 1992)

Peng Pai may have been sold by Bai Xin out of a "private grudge," but he could not have been "arrested and taken away" by "the Kuomintang reactionaries' police," "armed to the teeth." For "the Kuomintang reactionaries' police" were in the concessions, where they could never have gone "armed to the teeth" and seized people as they pleased. The men who "came in several red armored cars" and raced to 12 Jingyuanli on Xinzha Road to arrest Peng Pai and the others "by name" could only be, as Zhou Enlai said, "the imperialists' armed patrolmen and the Public Security Bureau's Chinese detectives."

The Central Special Branch soon established the truth: to lay hold of a large reward and win himself capital as a turncoat to the reactionaries, Bai Xin had, more than a month before, secretly surrendered to the Kuomintang's Shanghai Special Municipality Party Headquarters and got in with Fan Zhengbo, an executive committee member of that headquarters and an inspector of the Shanghai Public Security Bureau. Fan Zhengbo was a creature of the brothers Chen Guofu and Chen Lifu, and he took Bai Xin under his wing. Bai Xin repaid the favor by secretly telling Fan Zhengbo that Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the others would be meeting at 12 Jingyuanli on Xinzha Road.

Bai Xin traded the lives of Peng Pai and the rest for high office and rich pay, becoming chief of the Detection Bureau of the Songhu Garrison Command and director of its "anti-Communist" office for the Chinese district.

Bai Xin's shameless betrayal had to be severely punished.

But no sooner had the great net been spread than, on September 14, 1929, the Shanghai Republican Daily carried two reports at once: one saying that Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and the rest had faced death as a homecoming and given their lives for righteousness; the other that Bai Xin "once graduated from the Whampoa Academy, was formerly duped by others into straying into the Communist Party, his offenses being comparatively light, and has been bailed out on the responsibility of Chairman Chiang, taken to Nanjing the day before yesterday, and is to redeem his crime by merit, his conduct to be watched hereafter."

Had Bai Xin got away, then?

Was this a rock-solid fact, or a smokescreen to throw people off?

So Chen Geng went once more to Yang Dengying to find out.

### 5. A Bird Startled by the Bow

Chen Geng met Yang Dengying again at the "Renaissance."

Chen Geng's first question was where Bai Xin had gone.

Yang Dengying said that Bai Xin had indeed gone once to Nanjing, but had not stayed long, and had by now slipped back to Shanghai, where he lay hidden at 43 Lane Four, Hehefang, on Avenue Joffre (today Huaihai Middle Road), in Fan Zhengbo's home.

Hehefang had been built in 1928, fronting Avenue Joffre on the south and running through to Rue Bourgeat (today Changle Road) on the north; catty-corner across the street stood the primary scene of the reprisal Chen Geng and the others had carried out on the He Jiaxing and He Zhihua couple on the morning of April 25, 1928.

Chen Geng asked next about Bai Xin's habits and movements.

Yang Dengying said Bai Xin was a bird startled by the bow: he had drawn a prison round himself, kept close and seldom went out, and was very hard to get near.

At this Chen Geng thought of Ke Lin.

Chen Geng told Ke Lin: you and Bai Xin are on good terms, he often comes to you to be treated; the next time he comes, send word at once, and we will go and take him.

Ke Lin agreed.

Chen Geng had guessed right. Sure enough, "one day Bai Xin telephoned Ke Lin to say he was back from Nanjing and would come round to his house to see him." (Li Yimang, 2001)

Li Yimang said: "Old Ke took fright and hurried back from North Sichuan Road to Seymour Road to talk it over with me. I said we had to report to Zhou Enlai. So he wrote a letter, and I found a way to get it to Zhou very quickly, and very quickly got an answer back: Ke Lin was to invite Bai Xin to his house. When the appointed time came, I was very nervous; looking down from upstairs, I could plainly see several men who had come to act. But we waited a long time, the appointed hour came and went, and Bai Xin never came. That night I received a letter from Zhou Enlai, with fifty dollars in the envelope, telling me to move house at once. Ke Lin, of course, had to hold on."

Bai Xin started at the cry of the wind and the crane, taking every bush and tree for an enemy soldier, and changed the arrangement, asking Ke Lin instead to treat him at the White Palace Hotel.

Ke Lin came to the White Palace Hotel and treated Bai Xin as usual. Bai Xin asked him: "What people I know have come to you for treatment lately?" Ke Lin said: "Very few now." And asked in return: "Do you want to see them?" Bai Xin said: "I do." When the examination was done, Bai Xin gave Ke Lin a good deal of money, and a letter of thanks besides, and Ke Lin tucked them together into his medical bag.

Coming out of the White Palace Hotel, Ke Lin went straight home and waited there until dark. Only when the lamps were lit and everything around had gone black did he take a car to find Gu Shunzhang and Chen Geng and report on his meeting with Bai Xin. Gu Shunzhang and Chen Geng told Ke Lin to keep his nerve, go on treating Bai Xin as before, and report whenever there was anything to report.

Two weeks later, Bai Xin again asked Ke Lin to Fan Zhengbo's house to treat him. Ke Lin took the chance to find out that the brothers Fan Zhengbo and Fan Zhengluo lived on the second floor and Bai Xin on the third. At almost the same time, Yang Dengying, talking with Bai Xin, learned too that Bai Xin would be leaving Shanghai for elsewhere on November 11. The Kuomintang had already given him a large sum of money.

On the strength of Ke Lin's and Yang Dengying's reports, Zhou Enlai decided to act on November 11 and root out the traitor at a stroke.

And so Chen Geng went again, this time to St. Peter's Church on Avenue Road, to find its presiding pastor, Dong Jianwu. Zhou Enlai said that an operation as grave as this, a matter of drawing a tooth from the tiger's mouth, could not do without just such a man, a clergyman by calling.

### 6. A Train Ticket in the Wallet

Wang Guangyuan, in The Red Pastor Dong Jianwu (2001), says: "St. Peter's Church was a famous great church of Shanghai, standing on Avenue Road in the city center; it survives today, under the name 'Grace Church.'" In fact St. Peter's is St. Peter's, and Grace Church is Grace Church. St. Peter's stood at 351 Avenue Road, Grace Church at 375 Seymour Road (today Shaanxi North Road). St. Peter's belonged to the Anglican Church of China, Grace Church to the Chinese Baptist communion. St. Peter's on Avenue Road had its foundation laid in November 1898; Grace Church on Seymour Road was not finished and opened until 1942.

Dong Jianwu, the presiding pastor of St. Peter's, had joined the Party after the failure of the Great Revolution. Chen Geng was his direct superior in the Central Special Branch.

Dong Jianwu's part in dealing with Bai Xin, as The Red Pastor Dong Jianwu tells it, came to two things: he reckoned, and he drew. The "reckoning" meant that he "hired a car specially and drove it from the Songshan Road police station to Hehefang, and worked out the exact time"—worked out "exactly how long it would take the enemy, on hearing the shots, to drive from the Songshan Road police station to Hehefang." The "drawing" meant that he "drew a detailed map and handed it to Chen Geng." But of this reckoning and this drawing there is not a trace in Dong Jianwu's own later notes. Dong Jianwu says only that Chen Geng wanted him to move as soon as possible into "a house he had lately rented east of Songshan Road (near the French Cemetery)," so as to "keep close watch on the doings of the Songshan Road French police station, and telephone Ouyang Xin the moment their arrest van pulled out, to report the time of the police action."

On the afternoon of November 11, 1929, the "Red Squad" men Cai Fei, Shao Dafu, Tan Zhongyu, and the rest slipped in groups into Hehefang on Avenue Joffre. Dong Jianwu, for his part, "watched at every moment the Songshan Road police station and, catty-corner across from his front window, the Wusheng Temple, the place where the Chinese detective-sergeants gathered when something was on"; the moment he saw that "the Chinese detective-sergeants at the Wusheng Temple were gathering for something, and the Chinese and Western detective-sergeants both boarded the red car and drove off west," he "on the one hand telephoned Ouyang Xin to report the time they set out, and at the same time went and asked two detective-sergeants still left at the Wusheng Temple," and learned from them that "a murder by assassination had taken place inside Hehefang to the west."

This "murder by assassination inside Hehefang to the west" meant that Bai Xin's cup of evil was full to the brim, and the "Red Squad" had taken its revenge. Beforehand, Fan Zhengbo's own car, together with a taxi hired from the "Suzhou Motor Company on Avenue Joffre," had drawn up side by side at the rear exit of Hehefang on Rue Bourgeat, and Fan Zhengbo's servant and bodyguard were carrying Bai Xin's luggage out to the taxi piece by piece.

No sooner said than done: just as Bai Xin, in black leather shoes, a serge gown lined with flannel, and navy silk Western trousers, ringed about by the Fan brothers and their bodyguard, slipped out the back door of No. 43 and hurried toward East Lane Five, Shao Dafu, Tan Zhongyu, Hu Lanbo, and the others, who lay in wait next door, sprang out all at once and opened fire, and the great lane filled in an instant with a howling as of ghosts and wolves and a dazzle of flying rounds.

The first hit was Fan Zhengbo's bodyguard. He tried to draw and return fire, but before the bullet had left the barrel his skull had been laid open, and he dropped dead on the spot. Next were Fan Zhengbo and his brother Fan Zhengluo. Both went down at the reports, chest and back, belly and thigh all torn with bullet holes, the flesh turned out at the edges. Bai Xin, seeing how it stood, broke north for his life on the one hand, and on the other drew his pistol and fired back over his shoulder. But it was a cornered beast's last struggle: the "Red Squad" men, hard on his heels and never letting up, drove him at last into a dead corner and shot him dead in front of No. 71.

By the time the police cars from the Songshan Road station came howling up, the "Red Squad" men who had wrought this bloody purge had long since flown, vanished without a trace. On the scene there were only the Chinese and foreign reporters, and the onlookers packed three deep, jostling to see Bai Xin's body curled in a heap. As they looked at the blood and brains still oozing from the back of Bai Xin's head, an old man in the crowd let a thread of a cold smile, not without a bite to it and not easily marked by those around him, flit swiftly across the corner of his mouth. This trembling old man, leaning on his cane, was Dong Jianwu in disguise.

He was a pastor, and often prayed for the dead.

But Bai Xin's sins he would by no means absolve. For Bai Xin had lost the last of his conscience, and death was too good for him. The soul of a devil could not be sent up to heaven.

As he watched a patrolman crouch to search the body and fish a blood-stained wallet from the inside pocket of Bai Xin's coat, Dong Jianwu, looking coldly on, could hardly hold in the laughter at the bottom of his heart.

For at this moment, of all the people there, he alone knew for certain that in that wallet there was a train ticket. A new ticket, not yet put to use. But a new ticket alone could not carry Bai Xin, heavy with the blood-debts of many, onto the coach that would bear him far away.

That evening Dong Jianwu went, of his own accord, to the home of Zhao Zibai, interpreter and detective-chief at the French Concession police station. Dong Jianwu recalled in his later years: "On the night of the Hehefang affair I went to Zhao Zibai's home at 9 Taihe Fang on Chongqing Road, and under cover of taking supper, talking as we ate, I sought to learn the truth of the Hehefang affair. For being at the scene myself I could still get no detailed account, and could not write a proper report for Comrade Chen Geng, so I made the trip specially to detective-chief Zhao Zibai's home to get a clear picture from him. Zhao Zibai said: 'I have been run off my feet today. They say five were shot at; Fan Zhengbo was hit in the neck, the blood pouring out, but he was not killed. He lay in the pool of blood playing dead, and when the shooting stopped he crawled up and dashed inside; his household bound the wound with old clothes, but still could not staunch the bleeding. Just then a big car came from the Garrison Command and took the gravely wounded Fan Zhengbo and the two others, lightly hurt, off to the hospital together for emergency care. But Fan Zhengbo had lost so much blood that whether the hospital could pull him through was another question.'"

Dong Jianwu pressed him: "To what hospital were these people taken for treatment?"

Zhao Zibai shook his head and answered: "That I do not yet know."

Dong Jianwu learned afterward that "once Fan Zhengbo was gravely wounded, the papers trumpeted that the Garrison Command had rushed him to hospital—and that was a smokescreen. Its purpose was to lull people, so that no one watched him any longer. We too went about checking the big hospitals and not the small ones. In fact he had been taken to Huang Jinrong's house at Baxianqiao, and a doctor secretly brought in to operate."

The traitor Bai Xin's death was richly deserved. It raised high the spirits of the revolutionaries and cut deep into the arrogance of the reactionaries. The old Shanghai of wind and flowers, snow and moon, of the peach-blossom face, gave way from that day to a great age of espionage, in which the mountains hid their shapes and the very wind and clouds lost their color. All its strangeness and glitter, all its mutual deceit, played out in schemes beyond counting and counter-schemes, in plots and plots against the plots.

### 7. "Bring His Wife In"

After the founding of New China, Chen Geng gave a written testimonial that Yang Dengying "did indeed enter into relations with our Party from 1927 on, was in working contact with me before 1931, and in that time did the Party no small service." Chen Yangshan too said that Yang Dengying "made a great contribution to our Party's security work after the Great Revolution, and rescued a good many of our Party's leading comrades."

In March 1956 the Nanjing Military Region sent men to bring Yang Dengying to Beijing, where Chen Geng, Chen Yangshan, An E, and others received him with courtesy and met him warmly.

As to how Yang Dengying ended, Zhou Jun and Pan Yingbin, in Yang Dengying: The Special Figure Zhou Enlai Never Ceased to Think Of (2001), say: "In the winter of 1970 Yang Dengying died of illness. Before he died, he said again and again to his children: 'I was no secret agent, no traitor, no turncoat of any kind; what sort of man I was in the end, I cannot say myself, but Zhou Enlai knew me. ...'" "In January 1976, as the whirling auspicious snow ushered in the spring of the Year of the Dragon, Zhou Enlai had already entered his last days. One day he called Luo Qingchang to his side. Luo Qingchang bent over Zhou Enlai, pressed his ear close to Zhou Enlai's mouth, listened quietly, nodded from time to time, and the tears fell in strings. ..." "It is said that Zhou Enlai mentioned some little-known names, Yang Dengying among them. Then Zhou Enlai said: 'These people who helped us in the Party's most perilous hours—never, never forget them. ...'"

Luo Qingchang went to see Zhou Enlai on the morning of December 20, 1975, and not "in January 1976, as the whirling auspicious snow ushered in the spring of the Year of the Dragon." For this historical fact, see the Chronicle of Zhou Enlai (1949–1976) (1997).

{v} On the morning of December 20, his temperature was 38.7 degrees. He summoned Luo Qingchang to discuss the question of work relating to Taiwan, asked after recent conditions in Taiwan and the situation of old friends there, and charged him not to forget those who had done good service to the people. Twice in the course of it he was so wracked by pain that he could not speak, and lapsed into a coma. In the end the talk had to be broken off. This was the last time Zhou Enlai summoned the head of a central department to talk.

In fact, on this "last time Zhou Enlai summoned the head of a central department to talk," Li Ping writes in The Life of Zhou Enlai (2001):

{v} On December 20, 1975, at about ten in the morning, the office of Premier Zhou's medical team telephoned Luo Qingchang, who was in charge of work relating to Taiwan, to say that the Premier, on waking, had several times spoken of wanting to see him. Luo Qingchang hurried to Hospital 305 and found Zhou Enlai with a drip bottle hanging over him, his face very worn. Bearing the pain, Zhou Enlai gripped Luo Qingchang's hand tightly and said: "Qingchang, I am never ill as a rule; I never thought I would fall ill like this, and still be able to see you." Luo Qingchang said: "Cadres in the Party, the government, and the army all care greatly for the Premier's health; may the Premier soon recover." Zhou Enlai said: "I have called you to talk about Taiwan. Those old friends in Taiwan I have often spoken to you of, these people, in the past they did good things for the people; in the future you must never, never forget them—everyone who has ever done good things for the people, none of them must you forget." At this point the pain grew more than Zhou Enlai could bear, and he could only say: "Qingchang, rest for ten minutes; after ten minutes we will talk again." Then Zhou Enlai closed his eyes and passed into a coma, and by past noon he had still not woken, so Luo Qingchang had to leave. Luo Qingchang said: "This was the last time the Premier called me in, our last parting. These charges of the Premier's were, I feel, spoken not only to me, but to the generation after us, to us Chinese, to the people of Taiwan."

Zhang Zuoliang, who "worked at Zhou Enlai's side as his personal health physician for a little over ten years," also writes, in Zhou Enlai's Last Ten Years: A Health Physician's Recollections (1997):

{v} On the morning of December 20, the duty staff, following Zhou Enlai's instructions, sent a car to bring Luo Qingchang, head of the Investigation Department. ... Zhou Enlai talked with him about work relating to Taiwan and asked after some old friends in Taiwan. The two seemed to talk of little before Zhou Enlai sank once more into sleep. The talk had to be broken off; the doctors came at once into the ward to examine and revive him, while Luo Qingchang waited outside in anxious distress. He knew Zhou Enlai's condition was already very grave, and he could not let the Premier tire himself and faint again on his account. Without taking his leave of the Premier, Luo Qingchang quietly left the hospital. Afterward, whenever Luo Qingchang spoke of this, his heart grew heavy and the tears came. He felt the deep regret of never having heard the Premier's important instructions on the Taiwan work; Luo Qingchang was the last department head with whom Zhou Enlai discussed work in his life.

The above shows that what Zhou Enlai talked over with Luo Qingchang was "work relating to Taiwan." What he could not set his mind at rest about was "recent conditions in Taiwan and the situation of old friends there," or the "old friends in Taiwan" he usually "spoke of." His "asking after" them was not likely to have touched on Yang Dengying. But this does not mean he did not care for Yang Dengying, or did not think of him. On the contrary: Li Qiang's Diary (unpublished manuscript) gives us the most precious evidence, quite enough to bear out Zhou Enlai's care and protection for Yang Dengying.

{v} December 11, 1968. At one in the afternoon, went with Yangshan, Ju Shuzhang, and others to Qincheng Prison to question Bao Junfu; by the time we got back to the department it was after hours.

{v} December 17, 1968. In the afternoon, went to Qincheng Prison.

{v} December 22, 1968. At eight in the evening, in the office with Chen Yangshan and others, studied the Bao Junfu question.
{v} December 24, 1968. At two in the afternoon, went with Chen Yangshan to Qincheng Prison; at five returned to the department to deal with documents.

The above proves at least two things:

First, that from December 10, 1968, to March 23, 1969, Yang Dengying was in Beijing the whole time, in Qincheng Prison. This was a most unusual means of protection. In those extraordinary times, it was the only way Zhou Enlai could protect Yang Dengying.

Second, that Zhou Enlai's having Li Qiang, Chen Yangshan, and the others go again and again to Qincheng Prison to visit Yang Dengying, and "study" his "question" over and over, came down in the end to giving him "favorable treatment." That "favorable treatment" went so far as to "agree to bring his wife in."

Whether Yang Dengying, in his lifetime, knew that Zhou Enlai had given such a "telephone" instruction—that Li Qiang, Chen Yangshan, and the others were to "give Bao Junfu favorable treatment, and to agree to bring his wife in"—there is no telling. But I think that so long as Yang Dengying, before his bodily life finally faded, knew for certain that Zhou Enlai had made such a call and given such an instruction, then he could close his eyes in death with no regret left.
