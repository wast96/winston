## Chapter 4

Bai Ping was a dancer at the Paramount. Ever since greater Shanghai fell, it was the Japanese who came and went there most, and so it was a place I greatly disliked; but Stephen liked it — whether to satisfy some appetite for contention I do not know, he was forever fond of crossing the Japanese patrons. In those days the dance-hostesses none of them cared to dance with the Japanese, partly out of loathing, and partly because to dance with a Japanese was to lose one's Chinese custom. And Stephen, whenever he saw a Japanese go to dance with some hostess, would always cut in and carry her off; I would join in behind him, so that in the end the hostesses looked on us as the rescue that broke their sieges — and in fact, apart from us, no one else ever came to break them. Bai Ping too I came to know because Stephen snatched her from a Japanese embrace; yet Bai Ping seemed neither afraid of the Japanese nor disgusted by them. Her face was made bright and open — large eyes, long lashes, full cheeks, thin lips and white teeth — and her smile was a lily just opening. At first sight I liked her very much; but as a knot of Japanese hemmed her round and she talked with them a great deal, the impression she left on me was very bad. It was the second time that Stephen somehow noticed her again — noticed a crowd of Japanese dancing with her — and, without asking my leave, called her off the floor to sit with us, and then took her to the Kaisha.

The moment we sat down I asked Bai Ping. I said:

"It puzzles me — the other girls all detest the Japanese dancing with them; why are you all talk and laughter with them?"

"What of it?" she said, arching her brows. "Dancing is my trade. I earn their money."

"But," I said, "it means no Chinese dares dance with you."

"That can't be helped," she said, lowering her eyes to her own dress. "And things came to this pass long ago."

"You mean the very first time you danced with a Japanese brought it to this?"

"Yes; because I can speak a little Japanese, and after a few times my old regulars stopped coming." Then she changed her tack all at once, fixing me with a barbed look: "The truth is, it's Chinese men who are timid — afraid of the Japanese."

"You mean you want Chinese men to fight the Japanese for you?" I said in jest.

"That's not how I'd put it," she said. "There was a Chinese youth who loved me very much, and he said I ought not to dance with the Japanese. I said this is my trade, I do it for money; I'm not on good terms with them. If you want me, you may take me out, and you may dance with me too. After that he stopped coming near me — and if that isn't timidity, what is it? Ah — unless it was that he did not really care for me."

Stephen had been smoking beside us and listening all the while; now at last he told me that a hostess sitting on the west side seemed to be one I had danced with before, and bade me go and dance first.

I went off to dance; Stephen and Bai Ping were talking away with great animation. Stephen understood Shanghainese well enough but spoke it very badly, and I wondered how they talked so freely — until, my dance done, I learned they had been speaking English. I began to take an interest in Bai Ping: so she knew Japanese, and English too — what a clever girl she was.

After that I often went to amuse myself with Bai Ping. Often, at four or five in the afternoon, sitting idle in a café, I would ring her up and she would come out and wait for us; or she would say she had no time just then and could dine with us only at seven — but never once did she say she had no time today and put us off to tomorrow. I believe she must have turned down many engagements to keep us company, and so she grew the more endearing to me.

But every outing was always the three of us, or, besides the three, other hostesses as well — never just the two; and it was mostly Stephen who paid. Imperceptibly, he and Bai Ping were the leads, while I was no more than a minor supporting player. Until one day, when I had been buying books at a secondhand shop on Yuyuan Road, and, coming back, went to see a friend on Bubbling Well Road and missed him, and being a little hungry stopped for a snack at a Liti café nearby, and idly leafed through the books I had bought. I remember it clearly: among them was Hazlitt's *Table Talk*, and in it an essay on solitude, which said, I think, that for a man to hang his happiness on another is a most painful thing. The saying moved me to sympathy, for I am a man who has forever hung his happiness on others, who often cannot order his own life and has suffered much for it; yet the effect of that essay on me was the very opposite of its intent. I raised my eyes and saw that at every table around me there sat two or three people together; I alone was solitary. All at once loneliness struck me, and in the same instant I thought of Bai Ping. I made a telephone call, and Bai Ping happened to be at home.

"Bai Ping?" I said. "Do you know who this is?"

"My sweetheart, of course."

"No," I said. "Your sweetheart's friend."

"I think it's my friend's sweetheart."

"Say what you like," I said. "I'm at the Liti Café."

"Is anyone else there?"

"Only loneliness, at my side."

"Shall I come and drive it off?" she said. "I'll come at once."

After I hung up I telephoned Stephen, but Stephen was out — and Bai Ping came instead.

It was early autumn. She wore a pale-grey cheongsam with silver buttons, thin-soled silver shoes, a silver flower in her hair, and over it a short cream-coloured coat cut like a man's. Never in my memory had she given me such an impression of beauty. It was as though I were meeting her for the first time. I said:

"Are you as lovely as this?"

"As if you were seeing me for the first time."

"For the first time indeed," I said. "What I used to see was only my friend's sweetheart; what I see today is—"

"Is what?"

"A rose that belongs to no one."

"A camellia that belongs to any man."

"Very well — camellia," I said. "Let's ring Stephen."

"What?" she said, arching her brows. "Can't I drive off your loneliness by myself? Let's not ask him. The two of us have never gone out alone — today would be the first time. Won't you try it and see?"

"Very well." I raised my coffee cup and touched it to hers. "The whole night through."

"The whole night through," she said.

To tell the truth, that day I had meant only to have tea with her — I had not even reckoned on dinner. Whether it was her dress that moved me, or whether only today I discovered her worth, I found myself saying "the whole night through."

"Wild dancing, reckless gambling, and at daybreak I'll walk with you — walk to the cathedral at Xujiahui, to hear the half-past-seven Mass and repent a night's folly." She arched her brows, her eyes flashing with a strange brilliance. For the first time I saw, for the first time I truly knew her — what an extraordinary girl she was.

"Good girl!" I said. "A crime by design, a repentance premeditated."

"Because we are wretched, lonely — and full besides of an emptiness of heart." She fell suddenly low, like a flower meeting flame, her right hand lightly swirling the glass of cold water on the table, her eyes upon it.

I was truly bewildered then: what sort of girl was this, after all? I said nothing; a desolate fellow-feeling came over me, and I began to smoke.

Bai Ping seemed to rise, quietly took up her handbag, and walked out; I neither asked her nor heeded her, my thoughts in the emptiness, my gaze in the emptiness.

After how long I do not know, Bai Ping came back.

"What — can I never take loneliness's place and keep you company?" She was as lively as a little dragon, her two big eyes flashing, all that gloom of a moment ago swept away, her smile a lily just opening.

"It was you who brought this loneliness — don't you know?" I said, after looking at her a long while.

"The bill," she said to the waiter, not sitting down but standing beside the table, taking money from her bag.

The waiter brought the bill; she paid it and said:

"Let's go."

"Where to?"

"Come with me."

I went with her out the door, with her across the street, with her into the Majestic Cinema; there was a crowd at the ticket window, and just as I was about to take my place in it, she said:

"I bought them long ago."

"So that was why she went out just now — to buy the tickets," I thought, and followed her upstairs.

I remember the picture that day was not good. Seeing a film with her was nothing new, but just the two of us was the first time; in the past, her sitting beside me had made me feel nothing at all, whereas today I felt something strange, forever drawing me to be aware of her presence.

At eight o'clock I dined with her at a Cantonese place; at nine o'clock I danced wildly with her at the Lido; at twelve o'clock we were in a car, she nestled against me, and I said:

"Bai Ping, you're tired."

"No." She opened her big eyes wide and looked at me. "Are you still lonely?"

"No," I said. "But there's a feeling I can't put into words."

"Yes," she said. "It's as if, beside a warm stove, I had touched snow."

I did not answer, but gazed quietly ahead and around. The streets were desolate, the car ran unusually fast, its lamps flinging their beams along the road, the shadows of the roadside trees streaming past without pause. I said:

"On a night like this I see autumn at last."

"At your side I always feel it is autumn."

"And at Stephen's side?"

"He is the very sign of spring."

"And how do you feel about yourself?"

"I stand for spring, summer, autumn and winter alike."

"What a grand claim!" I said. "But in the past I felt you were only summer."

"And today?"

"The finest companion of early autumn."

Before a blaze of electric light, the car stopped.

We walked into the roulette den.

That night red came up fourteen rounds running, and in less than an hour we had won more than six thousand dollars — only to lose heavily right after; around three o'clock we won our stake back for a while, but then went on losing. At first the two of us gambled together; later all the chips were in my hands alone, with Bai Ping watching beside me. But just as I was near to losing everything, Bai Ping suddenly vanished; I supposed she had gone to the restaurant to eat, and did not ask. Yet as I laid my last stake, knowing it hopeless, I began to think of where she had gone — and all at once saw her placing bets at the other end of the table. I paid her no heed, watched my last stake go, and rose to sit alone on a sofa nearby and smoke; she paid me no heed either, until, some time past five, she stood up with several thick wads of banknotes in her hands, seven or eight thousand dollars by the look of them. It struck me suddenly: even granting all this money was won, where had her stake come from? When she left me, had she not been left without a cent? I was about to ask her, but she said:

"Let's go and eat something." I rose and went with her to the restaurant, and ordered a little of this and that — eggs, oatmeal. She seemed in high spirits, and talked with me of things that had nothing at all to do with gambling. My own spirits, too, seemed to be roused by her; and when the meal was done and I had lit a cigarette, she said:

"Give me one as well."

I passed her one, and just then noticed that the platinum-set diamond ring was gone from her hand. I had all but asked about it, when somehow it came to me in a flash where the notes in her hand, and her stake when she gambled alone, had come from; I checked the question at once and calmly gave her a light. She let out a breath of smoke, stood up, and said:

"Now we can go to Xujiahui."

"Are we really to walk there?" I asked.

"Wait a moment." She did not answer, but went up to a woman attendant; knowing she meant to go to the cloakroom, I settled to wait. Just as she moved off, I saw that she had left her handbag on the table, and in a sudden start of understanding I opened it.

Sure enough, exactly as I had guessed: a pawn ticket. Without looking at it closely I slipped it out and into my own empty wallet, and waited quietly for her return.

Before the second cigarette was done, Bai Ping was standing before me, her face freshly made up and glowing.
