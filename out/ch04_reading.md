## Fragmentary Materials on Using Gang Connections in Revolutionary Work

{d} Wu Chengfang

Back in the First Revolutionary Civil War, analyzing the classes of Chinese society, Mao Zedong pointed out: "There is besides a fairly large lumpen-proletariat, made up of peasants who have lost their land and handicraftsmen who have lost their work. They are the most unsettled of people. Everywhere they have their secret organizations—the Triad Society in Fujian and Guangdong, the Elder Brothers Society in Hunan, Hubei, Guizhou, and Sichuan, the Big Sword Society in Anhui, Henan, and Shandong, the Rational Society in Zhili and the three northeastern provinces, the Green Gang in Shanghai and elsewhere—all once mutual-aid bodies for their political and economic struggles. Handling these people is one of China's hard problems. They can fight bravely, but there is a destructiveness in them; led the right way, they can be turned into a revolutionary force." From early in this century the gangs' power reached into every layer of society, and imperialism and the reactionaries at home used it to prop up their rule. But because the gangs were made up mostly of drifters, there was also, under progressive influence, a chance of their coming to sympathize with the revolution or even lend it a hand. As far as I know, from the founding of the Chinese Communist Party onward, at every stage of the revolution the Party used gang connections, or worked through people tied to the gangs, to carry on its work. Here I recall a few fragments, for comrades studying the modern revolutionary and social history of China.

From the First Revolutionary Civil War into the Second, I worked for a time on the CCP's Beijing municipal committee. Part of that work was to keep in touch with, and hold together, through Hu Egong (also known as Hu Nanhu, once a vice-minister of education in the Beiyang government), certain men who in earlier years had followed Sun Yat-sen and done gang work alongside him—among them Wu Jinglian (briefly speaker of the House of Representatives after the 1911 Revolution) and Zhang Bolie (once its deputy speaker). These men still held some standing in political and social life, knew the reactionaries' inner workings fairly well, and were not easily noticed by the authorities. Since the 1911 Revolution failed they had grown gloomy about the state of things, and they saw us young Communists as full of energy and pinned the revolution's future on us. So when we carried on our work, they did what they could to cover for us, and from time to time passed us word of how things stood on every side.

Under our Party's influence, Hu Egong built ties with Li Dazhao, kept up regular dealings with Karakhan, an official of the Soviet embassy in China, and later joined the Party, where he did well. He was on close terms with Yang Du, and it was through him that Yang Du once met Li Dazhao in secret; Hu was the man who brought Yang Du into the Communist Party. In April 1927, Yang Du learned from the Fengtian warlords that Zhang Zuolin, heedless of international custom, meant to seize Li Dazhao, who was sheltering in the Soviet embassy in the Legation Quarter. He told Hu Egong at once, and Hu told Li Dazhao at once. Unfortunately Li made too little of the warning, and so was arrested and gave his life.

In 1929 the organization sent Hu Egong to Shanghai, where he joined the work of the Shanghai Mutual Aid Society under the Communist Dai Xiaodong, and later the Anti-Imperialist League, becoming one of its active members while using the gangs to do a good deal of work. By then Yang Du was already a Communist and in Shanghai too, doing gang work on the Party's instructions as a senior adviser retained by Du Yuesheng. He once thought to use the gangs' strength around Lake Tai to organize an armed struggle, but it came to nothing. Since Du Yuesheng had many disciples inside the French Concession Council and the International Settlement's Municipal Council, we came to understand the gangs' setup and doings through Yang Du, and at the same time worked some influence on Du Yuesheng himself.

Yang Du died at the end of 1931, leaving word to his sons Yang Gongpu and Yang Gongzhao to go on with revolutionary work under our Party's lead. Afterward Hu Egong, keeping Yang Du's charge, introduced the two to us. Yang Gongpu was then secretary-general of Tsinghua University, and he introduced us to the university's president, Weng Wenhao—which opened the way for a good deal of the Party's work.

In 1927, in the Rehe-Chahar region, there was a gang boss named Huang Zihe (also called Huang Shouzhong), of some strength thereabouts, who had an important lieutenant under him, Song Lanpo. The Communist Mao Jun (then an engineer at the Beijing Telephone Bureau, and after liberation a deputy director of it) and Zhang Zhutang sent two comrades, Geng Shunqing and Zhang Jigen, to make contact with Song Lanpo and Huang Zihe, meaning to use the gang strength they commanded to form a guerrilla band of one or two thousand. For various reasons it never got off the ground, but it laid a certain organizational footing in the Rehe-Chahar area. By May 1933, when Feng Yuxiang, under our Party's influence, raised the People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army at Zhangjiakou, this force joined it whole, and Huang Shouzhong served as an army commander.

In the 1930s there was in Shanghai a man named Song Qirong, tied to the gangs of Shengxian in Zhejiang. He once worked for our Party's underground organs in the city; his open identity was that of a secret agent of the Songhu Garrison Command, and he quietly covered our organs and passed us important intelligence. In 1929, when Li Lisan came back to Shanghai and a fellow townsman recognized him and went to inform, the matter fell into Song's hands, and he tipped off the Party organization in time, so that Comrade Lisan got clear of the danger.

After the August Thirteenth resistance of 1937, when the Japanese army took Shanghai and was still busy clearing the battlefield, not yet round to plundering the many stores of goods in Zhabei and Nanshi, we sent Song Qirong to work with the parties involved and spirit some of the goods away to supply the anti-Japanese forces. In 1947, when the former Kuomintang air-force officer Liu Shanben flew his plane over in revolt and went to Yan'an, his family in Shanghai came under close watch by Kuomintang agents and lost their income, and we used Song Qirong to keep the household going.

In the Second Revolutionary Civil War and the War of Resistance, the Shanghai underground Party learned how things stood on every side through all sorts of ties and channels. On the gang side its main targets were the upper figures, the more notable of them being Gao Hansheng, Wang Cibin, Tan Daonan, Qiu Zipei, and Yao Wencheng. Gao Hansheng, a Hubei man, had in his early years followed Sun Yat-sen in democratic revolutionary work in Japan; under Yuan Shikai's usurpation he served as a member of parliament, and leaned toward anarchism. In Shanghai he had founded the Five Sages Mountain and was a Hong Gang chief. Wang Cibin, an Anhui man, was a sworn brother of Yang Hu, and his father had once followed Sun Yat-sen in the democratic revolution. He ran an opium den in Shanghai, and though people generally took him for both anti-Communist and anti-Chiang, he in fact leaned toward the revolution. We used his backward reputation, putting him forward at certain political events as cover. Tan Daonan, a Hunan man, was on good terms with the Zhili warlord Wu Peifu and had run a railway bureau. During the war he played both sides between the Japanese and the puppets: Zhou Fohai sometimes sent him living money, the chiefs of the "Peace Army" courted him hard, and Wan Lilang, the agent of No. 76 Jessfield Road, was in touch with him—all of them hoping to find, through him, a political way out. With so many connections, he was flattered as the "Doctor of Socializing." Once we were in touch with him he passed us a good deal of information and brought some others in to work for us. Qiu Zipei, a Hubei man, ran the Xinchang customs-brokerage firm on Shandong Road in Shanghai; the gangs' upper figures often gathered there and Tan Daonan often stayed there, so we used Qiu for some political work. Yao Wencheng, a Hubei man and an old-style scholar, had once set up a Hong Gang lodge in Shanghai and, under Gao Hansheng's influence, worked for us. There was Xu Langxi as well, a well-known gang figure, who did some cover work for us too. Because these men had connections in so many directions, and could be especially useful under the thumb of the Japanese army and the puppet Wang regime, our political work could reach, as needed, deep into every quarter.

In the spring of 1935, the Shanghai underground Party, gutted by the Wang Ming line, was all but wiped out. Comrades Wu Dakun, Mei Dajun, and others, through Zhao Puchu's ties to the noted Shanghai figures Guan Jiongzhi and the Green Gang man Wen Lanting, moved the Buddhist world to set up a "Dharma Assembly for Peace and the Protection of the Nation," using it to spread anti-Japanese, patriotic propaganda. That winter they went on to found the "Shanghai Federation of Charitable Bodies for Disaster Relief" (the "Charity Federation" for short); its chairman was Xu Shiying, head of the Nanjing government's Relief Commission, and its directors included Wang Yiting, Wen Lanting, and Guan Jiongzhi. Wen and Guan, under Zhao Puchu's influence, gave fair support to our Party comrades working inside it, which helped Shanghai's anti-Japanese salvation movement along.

In 1937, after the August Thirteenth fighting broke out around Shanghai, Zhao Puchu at once set about founding, under the Charity Federation, a "Committee for the Relief of War-Zone Refugees," with its office in the Renji Hall at No. 35 Yunnan Road; Qu Yingguang was its chairman, Guan Jiongzhi and Wen Lanting deputy chairmen, and Zhao Puchu head of the reception section. In 1938 Zhao Puchu went on to set up a "Charity Federation Refugee Instruction Committee," its daily business left to an education group made up of our own people. Between 1938 and 1940, on the funds and premises Wen and Guan supplied, they ran some 40 or 50 refugee shelters in all, taking in refugees to the number of 500,000 admissions. Later, when part of the gang men under Huang Jinrong and Du Yuesheng left Shanghai, most of those who stayed went over to Wen Lanting. We drew further on Wen's standing to set up, under Zhao Puchu, a core of refugee education (the Friday Dinner Club), along with a refugee military school and a refugee wireless class (a good number of the New Fourth Army's radio operators and signalmen came out of it), and more—carrying refugee work deep, and teaching and rousing the refugees to join the various anti-Japanese forces our Party led and the revolutionary struggles in Shanghai's factories and on its wharves. Under the Party's teaching many refugees later became a backbone of the New Fourth Army, and some grew into important Party cadres, a real contribution to the growth of the army and of the Party's ranks.

In 1943 the Japanese and the puppets, alive to Wen Lanting's weight in business circles, worked hard to pull him in, making him chairman of the puppet "General Association for Commercial Control." Under it they set up some 30 economic bodies, into which our Party sent comrades to work. Moved by our Party's line, Wen passed on how these bodies were organized and what they did, so that the Party kept a fairly full and timely picture of the enemy and puppets' economic activity. Later, when the comrades sent in were arrested, it was Wen who came forward and bailed them out. As the war neared victory and the New Fourth Army made ready, for a time, to march into Shanghai, he told the comrades concerned that he backed it.

At the same time, among the lower ranks of the gangs, we won some over to useful work for the resistance and the revolution. On Fuzhou Road there was then a gang headman named Yang Anqing who, under our influence, used his ties on the wharves and in the customs and aboard the steamers to carry propaganda for us and serve as a courier. There was also a gang man surnamed Bai who kept a teahouse in the International Settlement, selling beef soup and scallion pancakes on the side, with many disciples under him; within his patch, when the toughs fell to quarreling they came to him to "drink settling tea," and he judged between them. A good many of the clerks at Wing On and Sincere were his men, and whenever the clerks fell out with the capitalists and stirred up a dispute he would step in and smooth it over, so both companies kept him on as an adviser and paid him regularly. But in secret he leaned toward the revolution, did a little work, and now and then helped a revolutionary out.

In 1938 the Party sent Comrades Lin Jun, Shao Shangqing, and others to the Chuansha–Jiangyin area to open up revolutionary work and raise armed forces from the local gangs; later they built, in western Shanghai, Pudong, Chongming, Qidong, Jiangyin, Wujiang, and elsewhere, the "East China People's Anti-Japanese Volunteer Army" (also called the such-and-such Anti-Japanese Society, "Armed Resistance" for short). In 1939 the Party sent Wang Guqin to raise, around Wuxi, an anti-Japanese force of more than 300, and the local gang headman Deng Benyin answered the call and led part of his men to join. At the same time Shao Shangqing used gang ties to send Mei Guangdi and Zhu Songshou to the Jiangyin–Changzhou area to build two anti-Japanese forces, each of more than 200, under Mei and Zhu. Later the three—Deng's, Mei's, and Zhu's—came together at about 700 and were reorganized as the "Jiangnan Anti-Japanese Volunteer Army" ("Jiang-kang" for short), with Deng Benyin as commander-in-chief and concurrently commander of the first column, and Mei Guangdi and Zhu Songshou each a column commander. The Party then sent Comrade Yang Jin, under the alias Chen Da, to help Deng Benyin. When several chiefs of Deng's own troops later broke discipline and were dealt with by the Party, Deng, who had no taste for our tight discipline, walked out of the "Jiang-kang" on his own. In the course of 1939 the Party handed its three units over, one after another, to the military authorities in Jiangnan; reorganized, they kept the name Jiangnan Anti-Japanese Volunteer Army.

At the end of 1938, as the work required, Comrade Ren Tianshi went on the Party's instructions to the Changshu area to raise an anti-Japanese force, brought some gang members in, and formed the "People's Anti-Japanese Allied Army" ("People's Resistance" for short). At the same time, under the Party's influence, Gu Fusheng raised an anti-Japanese guerrilla force at Qingpu, also called the "People's Resistance," which likewise had some local gang members in it.

Because the gang men were a mixed lot, each with his own attitude toward the revolution, we met different men with different tactics, winning over the majority so the work could go smoothly. After the victory, a good stock of Du Yuesheng's goods was held up for a time at the customs; Du had his men send gifts to grease the way, but the customs superintendent, Zhang Yongnian, and his people would have none of it. At a loss, Du asked our help through our ties. Reckoning that Du carried a certain weight in society, we judged we could grant his request, while hoping he would make some political gesture toward us in return—and he promised he would. Through our work and a little smoothing, the goods came safely back to Du's hands; he was grateful, and afterward did a few things that served us. On the other hand, the die-hard gang men we warned or disciplined, and the tiny handful of reactionaries we struck hard when we had to.

Before liberation, at every stage of the revolution, we used gang connections to carry on the struggle in one form or another, and got some results—in raising armed forces, gathering intelligence, moving supplies, and the like. Those results rested, beyond the comrades' own efforts, above all on the soundness of the Party's policy and the reach of its political influence. This is only one side of the whole history of the revolutionary struggle; but taken in earnest, it is a real help to the study of modern history and of Party history.
