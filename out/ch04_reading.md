## Chapter 4. By a Cross-Grained Twist of Fate, Hard to Guard Against

China. Shanghai. The sixteenth of March, 1931.

The clock in the Lanxin Western Restaurant stood at three forty-five in the afternoon.

Yang Muci looked carefully to left and right, then gently pushed open the door of private room 301. A rich fragrance of milk tea met him full in the face.

Lao Yu, seeing him come in, set down the Xinwen Bao in his hand with a smile. “I’d begun to think you weren’t coming.”

“Why wouldn’t I come? It’s not every day an ‘iron rooster’ like you agrees to bleed a little.” Yang Muci sat down against the glass window.

“The young master of Fenghui Bank—I’d best seize the chance to curry a little favor.”

“Then you’d better be quick about it.”

A waiter brought in a large cake, bowed the guests to enjoy it, and then withdrew politely from the room.

Lao Yu passed the fruit knife to Yang Muci. “Happy birthday!”

“Thank you.”

Yang Muci: eldest son of Yang Yubo, magnate of Shanghai’s financial world; a member of the Chinese Communist Party’s underground; an intelligence officer of the Party’s Special Branch, codename “Drifting Wind.” He had studied in Japan and graduated with distinction from the department of financial administration at Tokyo Imperial University, and now worked at an English bank. Lao Yu: a reporter for the Financial News by his public identity; a member of the Party’s underground; a courier of the Party’s Shanghai station, codename “Timely Rain.”

“Why has your family never once held a birthday feast for you?” Lao Yu asked as he ate.

“Is it so important? It’s rather a private matter of mine, I should think.”

“Only curiosity. If you’d rather not answer, you may refuse.”

“I had an elder brother; he and I were twins.” Yang Muci made no secret of his family affairs. “He died.”

“It seems your parents loved this dead brother of yours very much.”

“And so I have never been to their liking. I remember, when I was very, very small, my mother would take me by the hand and, out along the corridor, call me by my brother’s name. ‘A-Chu, A-Chu, come back to your room.’ In those days my mother loved us dearly.”

“And now she doesn’t?”

“I don’t know!” Yang Muci bent his head over the cake.

“What time are you on duty today?”

“Half past four.”

“There may be a storm tonight.”

“What do you mean?” Yang Muci wiped his mouth clean with the napkin.

A shrill wail of a police siren swept across the eardrums of them both.

Yang Muci glanced up out of the window without thinking.

“A birthday present.” Lao Yu laid a letter on the table.

“What is it?”

“Open it and see.” Lao Yu gave a mysterious little smile.

“Let me guess.”

“You’ll likely never guess.”

“Passage to the Soviet areas?… Leaflets that want printing?… A new cipher?… An overdue draft in urgent need of cashing?” As Muci spoke he studied the flickering, restless look in Lao Yu’s eyes, and suddenly he laughed—though his laugh was guarded. “Let me see, then, what the riddle’s answer is.” Muci opened the envelope, drew out a single sheet of paper, and as his eyes fastened on it his smile froze. Plainly this was something he had in no way foreseen. On the sheet was written: Notice of Admission to the Special Police Personnel Training Class of the Central Police Academy.

“What does this mean? Special police personnel?” Muci was very tense, and could not himself say why he reacted so. For he knew that this so-called Special Police Personnel Training Class of the Central Police Academy was in fact a training class for Juntong secret agents. Once inside Juntong, it meant that Muci would lie hidden for the long term, deep among the enemy, in the guise of a Juntong agent, and would thereby lose his chance of reaching the Soviet areas. At the thought of it Muci could scarcely contain himself. “This isn’t quite suitable, Lao Yu—let’s, let’s talk it over again.” Muci pleaded, “I want to go to the front.”

“That is the very front line,” Lao Yu said calmly. “This is a matter the organization has weighed deeply and settled after study. You have been admitted directly through an inside contact, and it is hoped you will report to the academy as soon as you can. Once there, you must abide by its rules and strive to graduate with the highest distinction. The organization hopes that by this road you may march into the enemy’s very heart, lie hidden for the long term within their core departments, and obtain more intelligence, and more accurate intelligence. While at the academy you are to make contact with no one, your own family included. We will forge a document saying you have gone to intern at the head office of an English bank and send it to your parents. So, to avoid any complications, you may not write to them or telephone them. Have you understood?”

“Understood,” Muci answered.

“Have you anything else on your mind?” Lao Yu looked at him with much concern. Muci understood that he wished him to declare himself, and so he said, grave and solemn, “I will see the task through without fail. I’ll strive to graduate and come back soon, and fight at your side.”

Lao Yu smiled, satisfied.

“How do we make contact after I graduate?” Muci asked.

“Place a missing-person advertisement; the passphrase is ‘Mr. Lin Tan.’ When we see the notice, we will make contact with you ourselves. Repeat it back.”

“After graduating, place a missing-person advertisement; the passphrase is ‘Mr. Lin Tan.’”

“Good. A fair wind go with you.” Lao Yu stood up. “I’ll leave first.”

Lao Yu opened the door and closed it behind him as he went.

Muci looked at the shut door, and it was as if he saw himself sinking into a marsh—a marsh without bound or edge, and the worst of it was that there was not a single door by which to break away and flee.

Darkness—there was no light in the dark passage. Muci walked patiently along the narrow, winding black corridor, searching cautiously for some gleam of light; wall upon thick wall stretched away out of his sight, and ring upon ring of strange black shadows closed in on either side of him. He was suffocating, terrified; he longed to break free of all these black shackles. And so he began to run, to run wildly, to cry out, until he hurled himself against a black net. The net was very high and very thick, and he could not pass through it. He called for help, and no one answered; he realized that in this space he alone existed. Terror began to take hold of him. He saw, high overhead, a giant pair of scissors come stabbing viciously down at him through a gap in the net—he screamed, and woke from his dream.

Sweat ran down from his temples.

He had fallen asleep, without meaning to, in the room of a small hotel. Now he remembered: to avoid trouble he had not gone home the day before, but had bought some daily necessities straight off the street and, as instructed, put up at this inconspicuous little hotel to await orders. Worn out in body and mind, he had slept like the dead and dreamed his nightmare.

This will not do. Muci cursed himself fiercely in his heart. It absolutely will not do. He needed to set his frame of mind right, and quickly. Muci got up from the bed, went to the washroom, and splashed cold water on his face to clear his head.

A Juntong agent?

The Special Police Personnel Training Class of the Central Police Academy?

A new battlefield—and the very front line, too.

Muci’s eye came to rest on the calendar block on the tea table: today was the seventeenth of March.

The telephone rang.

Muci took the call; it was the hotel attendant, saying that someone downstairs had left a letter for him. He dressed, hurried down the stairs, took the letter, and returned to his room.

He opened the envelope; inside were a boat ticket to Hangzhou and the address of the Hangzhou police school. He looked at the time on the ticket—there was little time left—and knew he must set out at once.

A quarter of an hour later, Yang Muci, dressed head to foot as a student, left the little hotel.

Standing at the reception desk, the attendant watched through the glass window as Muci left, then put through a telephone call and said, “The merchant vessel has set sail. Requesting escort.”

A steady woman’s voice came over the line. “The escort ship has already left port. All is normal.”

Both parties hung up at the same moment.

On the quay, a steam whistle sounded long.

Yang Muci moved in good order through the bustling stream of passengers. The mouth of the boarding gate was like a tin of stewed meat just opened, the air, for want of any circulation, foul and stifling.

A reckless great fellow came barreling out of the crowd and slammed straight into Muci’s chest. Taken wholly off his guard, Muci was knocked dizzy and flung clear of the throng; the case in his hand dropped to the ground, and his whole weight came down upon another passenger’s legs. The passenger staggered a little, then steadied himself.

“Are you all right?” The passenger he had collided with was a gentleman in Western dress, of about thirty, who was reaching out to help Muci up. Seeing stars, Muci scrambled to his feet, cutting a thoroughly sorry figure.

“Thank you.” Muci picked up his case again; happily it was solid and had not fallen apart.

“Have a look—is anything missing from your person?” the passenger asked with concern.

“Me?” Muci felt at his breast, and his face changed utterly. “My wallet is gone.”

“Was there much money in it?”

“The money doesn’t matter, but there was something very important tucked inside.”

“Your boat ticket?”

“And my identity papers, my registration form…”

“Wait here, and watch my luggage for me.” Not waiting for Muci to agree, the man ran off toward the inspection point at the gate and quickly vanished from Muci’s sight.

Time passed minute by minute, second by second, and now and again Muci took out his pocket watch to look at it…

In the tedium of waiting, Muci began to study the luggage the man had left: a large wooden crate, firmly bound, from which a strong smell of printing ink came wafting out.

By now the ticket gate had become a long, winding dragon, the bent line of people inching forward like a snake. Muci hoisted the heavy crate with an effort and dragged his own case sliding along beside it, keeping his place in the queue and waiting for that good fellow to appear again.

“Is this it?” The man came running out, his head all over sweat, waving a black leather wallet.

Overjoyed, Muci said, “Yes. Thank you.”

The man took the crate back from him. “Sorry to have made you carry my luggage. You could have pushed the case along, you know—it’d have been less work.”

“That won’t do—a mimeograph machine as valuable as this, it would be a shame to damage it,” Muci said with a smile.

The man stopped abruptly. “How did you know it was a mimeograph?”

“One sniff at the smell and you know.”

“You’re a sharp one.” Then the man reminded him, “Won’t you check whether anything’s missing from your wallet?”

“Oh—right.” Muci opened the wallet, and his eyes darkened. Everything was there; the one thing missing was that single sheet—the Notice of Admission to the Special Police Personnel Training Class of the Central Police Academy.

“What is it? Is something important missing?”

“No—nothing.” Muci gave a covering smile. “Where did you find it?”

“I have a fellow townsman who’s a policeman on the platform; I asked him to help.”

“Thank you, then. What is your honorable surname? Where do you make your fortune?”

“My name is Du—Du Luning. I work at a newspaper office. And you?”

“Yang Muci. Out of work.”

“Where are you bound?”

“To Hangzhou, to look for work.”

“What a coincidence—I’m off to Hangzhou on business myself.”

Talking as they walked, the two of them went straight aboard the ship.

Du Luning’s ticket was for a middle-class cabin and Muci’s for a superior one, but Du Luning’s luggage could not be squeezed in at all. Muci offered to trade cabins with him, but Du Luning was too embarrassed to agree. The two of them found a steward aboard, and Muci paid him a little money to move them both into Room 6 of the first-class cabins.

First-class Room 6 was a three-berth cabin, and a young man had already moved in—a strikingly handsome fellow who gave his name as Rong Chu and called himself the young son of the Rong family, Bannermen and the richest house in Shanghai’s medicine trade. His face was ruddy and full of vigor, his speech crisp and clear, and when he laughed he was like a child who has yet to see much of the world. As Du Luning and Muci came in the door, he was already leaping about to lend a hand, for all the world like a boy showing off before company. In his presence Muci felt as though fresh water had seeped into the moist air, and he grew lively. On a short journey such as this, to meet a travelling companion whose talk agreed with one’s own was a pleasant thing indeed.

By evening the ship had put in at Jiaxing, where it was to lie for the night. The three of them went ashore to eat, found a cheap and cheerful little eating house, and sat down; they looked over the newspapers, discussed the state of the times, traded stories and jokes, and let the tasty food digest at its leisure.

“Mr. Du, which newspaper office do you work at?” Rong Chu asked.

“I’m ashamed to say. It’s a small outfit—we put out a magazine called Sunday Luncheon.”

Sunday Luncheon was a magazine rather in the line of pornographic fiction. So when Du Luning named his trade, both Muci and Rong Chu were a little taken aback. Du Luning plainly caught the looks on their faces, and said, “Where have your minds run off to? I’m the ‘master proofreader’ of that periodical—I only cut the stencils; the rest is none of my concern.”

Rong Chu pressed him without letting up. “The ‘master proofreader’ of a ‘lewd’ publication, you mean?”

“That’s an unkind way to put it. You should call it: a quasi-disreputable publication.” Muci spoke up for Du Luning. But Du Luning, of all people, would have none of it. “And what of a disreputable publication? Do you dare say you’ve never looked at one?” Du Luning said, standing his ground with perfect assurance. “These two words, ‘the erotic’—they’re like a flash of lightning off flint, hard to write and hard to paint. The true article springs from life, from life’s own natural charm.”

Rong Chu, who had been reading the paper, suddenly cried out, “What a coincidence—what a coincidence! I tell you, today’s headline is: Made Public Is Not the Same as Transparent. Ha-ha…” As Rong Chu roared with laughter, Muci leaned over to snatch the paper from him, and Du Luning laughed until his eyes narrowed to a slit.

“Hey—what’s this?” Muci’s attention was suddenly caught by another item in the paper. “The eldest son of the Rong family, Bannermen and the richest house in Shanghai’s medicine trade—Rong Sheng—is soon to return to China. According to reliable sources, another, mysterious son of the Rong house, Rong Chu, has already taken a doctorate in medicine in England, and will shortly return home together with his brother. ‘Return home together with his brother’?” The eyes of both Muci and Du Luning turned as one to Rong Chu.

Rong Chu smiled. “It’s no secret.”

“You aren’t going to tell me the Rong house has two young sons both named Rong Chu?” Muci said, half in jest.

Rong Chu threw back his head. “You’ve hit it exactly—there are indeed two young sons both named Rong Chu.”

“Get away with you.” Muci swatted at him with the newspaper for a weapon.

A merry feast broke up amid the din.

Early the next morning was fine and mild, with a gentle breeze.

Muci stood on deck smoking. Du Luning came over; Muci offered him a cigarette and struck the lighter for him. Cupping the flame, Du Luning lit his cigarette from it.

“What are you thinking about?” Du Luning asked.

“Tell me—if a student were to lose his notice of admission, what ought he to do?”

“Is it so important?”

“It’s a thorny business.”

“Then get it back.”

“And if it can’t be found?”

“What do you think?”

Muci gave a sly smile. “With a gold mine right beside you, need you fear running short of money to spend?”

“What do you mean?”

“It’s only a mimeographed sheet. Cutting a stencil should be no trouble for you, Brother Du.”

“You want to forge a notice of admission?”

“Can it be done?”

“It cannot!”

“Why not?”

Du Luning smiled. “I don’t do things that break the law.”

“What’s this matter that’s so serious?” Rong Chu had popped up at some point unnoticed. Du Luning made no reply and walked off toward the stern, and Muci drew near Rong Chu. “Can you keep him occupied for me for half an hour?”

“Why?”

“You don’t need to know the reason.”

“Three hundred dollars,” Rong Chu said.

“Fifty.”

“Two hundred.”

“A hundred, and not a cent more. Do it or don’t; if you won’t, then…”

“Done.” And even as he said it Rong Chu went running off toward the stern. “Old Du! I’ve got something to ask you!”

Muci returned to the cabin as fast as he could and dragged out the big wooden crate that held the mimeograph. The crate was locked. He fished a paper clip from his pocket, worked it into the keyhole, and turned it slowly, but a spring held it back; he pressed harder, without success. He took the clip out of the keyhole and drew out a hairpin instead, worked it into the hole and turned—and this time, easily, the lock sprang open.

When Muci lifted the lid with the greatest care, he was struck dumb.

What sprang into view was no mimeograph at all, but a brand-new American-made wireless transmitter. A thick stack of ink-printed tabloids lay spread over it as cover, still giving off their rich smell of ink…
