## Heroes of the Intelligence Front

Around Chen Geng the intelligence section brought forth talent in abundance. Working alongside comrades in the Central Special Section's other branches, its people carried off one earth-shaking feat after another. Here we tell first where Liu Ding, Ke Lin, Chen Yangshan, and Chen Shouchang came from; the next chapter takes up Qian Zhuangfei, Li Kenong, and Hu Di, the three Zhou Enlai called the "Three Heroes of Longtan."

### Liu Ding, the Weapons Expert

Liu Ding, deputy chief of the Second Section and once Chen Geng's assistant, was an old Communist. He joined the Chinese Socialist Youth League in 1923 and, on the recommendation of Sun Bingwen and Zhu De, crossed over to the Party in Berlin in 1924. He had studied in Germany and the Soviet Union, one of the first Communists our Party sent abroad to learn science and technology, and he went on to become a founder and an outstanding leader of New China's military industry.

Liu Ding (1903-1986) was born Kan Sijun, styled Zunmin, and used at various times the names Kan Zemin, Gan Zuoming, and Dai Liang. He came from a comfortable family in the county town of Nanxi, Sichuan. When he was a boy at higher primary school in his home town, the Yunnan National Protection Army under Zhu De was once quartered there. Its discipline was strict and its battle record brilliant, and Zhu De became a hero in the young Liu Ding's eyes. Another revolutionary elder he looked up to was Sun Bingwen, a learned man of Nanxi. Sun had studied at the Metropolitan University, the forerunner of Beijing University, had joined the Tongmenghui, and had taken part in the struggles against the Qing and against Yuan Shikai; later he was a staff adviser in Zhu De's brigade. Liu Ding used to go to Sun for help with his lessons, and Sun's talk was the boy's first schooling in revolutionary ideas. As it happened, it was these two elders he had revered since childhood who, in Germany in 1924, together sponsored his passage from Youth League member to Communist.

Even in his school days Liu Ding was a restless student. After he entered Jiang'an No. 3 Middle School, the May Fourth Movement and the new-culture currents took hold of him, and he came to prize democracy and freedom and the sanctity of labor. He and his classmate Guo Bohe led the students out to march and make speeches, preaching patriotism, denouncing traitors, and boycotting Japanese goods. Their patriotic doings so enraged a few die-hard teachers that these men incited others to strike, hoping to force the principal to punish the patriotic students. The principal, Qin Jinghuai, was a fairly enlightened man; but to calm things down he publicly expelled eight students, Liu Ding among them, while quietly arranging their transfer to the Xufu Union County Middle School. There the principal was a backward man and most of the teachers old-fashioned. Liu Ding and some of the other progressive students would boycott the die-hard teachers' classes by handing in blank examination papers. In the civics examination, where the teacher taught nothing but the outlook of the reactionary ruling class, Liu Ding handed in a blank sheet. The principal called him into the office and took out a paddle to beat him, and Liu Ding at once shouted, "No corporal punishment!" and "Everyone is equal!" The students crowded outside took up the cry, and in the confusion Liu Ding slipped away; the matter came to nothing. With friends from his middle-school days he led the local progressive students in founding a student union, went out again and again to carry patriotic propaganda to the public, and became a mainstay of the May Fourth new-culture movement in the Yibin region.

After higher middle school Liu Ding came to Shanghai. He failed the entrance examination for Nanyang University, the science-and-engineering school he had long dreamed of, today's Shanghai Jiao Tong University, so he turned instead to Hangzhou and passed into the Zhejiang Provincial Higher Industrial School. The school was well equipped, and most of its teachers had studied in America, Britain, or Japan. The science and engineering courses were taught wholly in English, the textbooks were American and British originals, and homework and laboratory reports all had to be written in English. At first Liu Ding could not follow the lectures, could not read them, could not write them; so he drove himself at the language morning and night until he had caught up in every subject. In his three years at the Hangzhou school he studied seventeen courses in all, among them advanced mathematics, physics, and chemistry. In the holidays he often went up to Shanghai and got back in touch with his middle-school friends, and whenever they met the talk was all of Marxism, of the spread of socialism, and of the workers' movement then surging up. After the summer of 1923, though he was only a year short of graduating from the higher industrial school, he had no heart to return; he resolved to stay in Shanghai, to strive amid the great currents of the age and seek out the right road for Chinese society. In Shanghai he entered Shanghai University, founded jointly by the Kuomintang and the Communists, where many progressive students had gathered; Liu Ding, Guo Bohe, Li Shuoxun, and Li Yimang were among its first students. There he joined the Chinese Socialist Youth League, struck up a correspondence with fellow students on work-study in France, met Cai Hesen and others returned from study there, and, learning how Chinese students lived by half-work and half-study in France, longed to go himself.

Just when Liu Ding was longing to go abroad to work and study, Sun Bingwen, who had gone to Germany with Zhu De, happened to come home on a family visit. In the spring of 1924 Liu Ding followed him by train to Germany. On the way they stopped a month in Moscow, where they were attached for the time being to a students' branch at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East and studied the Communist Manifesto and the ABC of Communism. There Liu Ding also saw Li Dazhao, who had come to Moscow for a Comintern meeting, and heard Stalin deliver his lectures "Concerning Questions of Leninism." That month in Moscow, seeing with his own eyes the happy life of a new society in which the working people were masters, left an impression he never lost. Long afterward Liu Ding said: "From that time on, not for a moment did I stop thinking that China must be freed from the oppression of imperialism and feudalism and take the socialist road."

When Liu Ding reached Berlin, he took Sun Bingwen's advice, gave up his plan to go on to France to work and study, and reported to the Chinese Communist branch in Germany. Before long he went with Sun to the university town of Göttingen to see Zhu De, who was not then openly known as a Communist. Under Sun Bingwen and Zhu De, Liu Ding stayed on among the students in Germany and did much to build the united front. In December 1924, sponsored by Zhu De and Sun Bingwen, he became a full member of the Chinese Communist Party and was made secretary of the Chinese Socialist Youth League branch in Germany. He carried on his revolutionary work and his work-study together. "He spent half a year teaching himself German until he could more or less speak, listen, and read the lecture notes; then at Göttingen University he took whatever engineering courses he liked—wherever a teacher and a course were good, there he went. The school placed few limits, the professors did not care whether a student was enrolled, they were glad of a full lecture room, and they answered anyone's questions. In this 'guerrilla' fashion Liu Ding learned a great deal of what he wanted to learn. But his studies were forever dogged by the struggle to live. He could find no chance to work in a factory, and could only wait tables in a Chinese restaurant, earning very little each day and still setting something by to help comrades in difficulty, so that his life was one of hard want: a meal was often a slice or two of black bread and a cup of weak tea, and he worked and studied often on an empty stomach. For a time, when the Chinese restaurant shut down, he could find no work and had nowhere to eat or sleep, until a Chinese comrade gave him twenty marks and saw him through. The notion he had formed back home, that abroad he could both work and study and have the best of both, was now shattered. 'So work-study went hand in hand with cold and hunger; it meant being tempered under the heartless private-property system of capitalism. This stretch of my life turned me not only from a student into a half-student, half-worker, but into a student who had lost his schooling and a worker who had lost his job. I tasted to the full the cruelty of the private-property system and the hardship of merely staying alive. From then on I felt no attachment to student life at all; my awakening to opposing capitalism and abolishing private property grew very deep, and my hunger to study Marxism and to become a proletarian revolutionary fighter grew keener still.'" (See the recording of Liu Ding's reminiscences.)

Liu Ding took part in revolutionary activity in Germany and was tempered by it. He and the others struggled more than once with the Kuomintang right-wing students in Germany and held their arrogance in check. When news of the May Thirtieth Movement of 1925 reached Berlin, the Chinese students at once launched a campaign of support. They went to the Chinese legation in Germany and demanded that the minister, Wei Chenzu, sign a circular telegram protesting the outrages of British imperialism. The minister refused even to show his face, and the students' anger boiled over. Liu Ding followed Zhu De and the crowd as they forced their way into the legation and at last dragged Wei out of a wardrobe on the third floor, compelling him to sign the protest. Wei then colluded with the German government, which ordered Zhu De, Liu Ding, and the other Chinese students who had made the disturbance expelled from the country, arresting them and holding them three days before releasing them. Before he left Germany, Liu Ding joined Zhu De and the others in a "Red Front" review and field encampment organized in Berlin by the Central Committee of the German Communist Party, and was received by the German Communist leader Thälmann.

Passing through Moscow on his way home, Liu Ding once more entered the Communist University of the Toilers of the East for a short course of study and military training, and was assigned by the Chinese Communist branch in Moscow to help receive Feng Yuxiang's Kuominchün delegation. In 1927 he was transferred to the Soviet Air Force Mechanical Academy, where he studied, taught, and served as an interpreter, learning as he worked. The academy's courses included the construction of weapons, the principles of demolition, radio technology, and telephony and telegraphy. He mastered the theory and technique of the aircraft engine, which laid the foundation for his later career in the arms industry. In the summer of 1929 he was ordered home, but the Sino-Soviet conflict over the Chinese Eastern Railway held him up at Khabarovsk, and he was ordered to join the Far Eastern Chinese guerrilla detachment commanded by Liu Bocheng. Before leaving Khabarovsk he listened to a two-day summing-up report by the Soviet marshal Blyukher, better known as General Galen, on the border war between the Soviet Union and China to which the railway conflict had led.

Back in Shanghai at the end of 1929, Liu Ding took up the post of deputy chief of the Central Special Section's Second Section, helping Chen Geng run the intelligence work. Not long after he joined the section, Chen Geng handed over to him the important intelligence contacts he had been managing directly, Yang Dengying and Yang Du among them. Liu Ding took part in one task after another: the full survey made in preparation for the Li-San Line's planned armed insurrection in Shanghai; the security work for the preparatory meeting of the All-China Congress of Soviet Areas; the rescue of the arrested Guan Xiangying; and, at the detention house of the Songhu Garrison Command, the perilous business of photographing a turncoat Shandong provincial secretary to secure evidence.

During this period, on instructions from the Party Center, Liu Ding began in the summer of 1930 to make use of the fact that the Japanese and the Kuomintang Reorganizationists under Hu Hanmin were colluding to oppose Chiang Kai-shek jointly. He did a good deal of work. At first he gathered intelligence on the Reorganizationists and, through special channels, passed it to the Kuomintang to widen the rift between them. At the same time he helped the Reorganizationists' anti-Chiang activities, helping them scatter leaflets, slogans, and political intelligence everywhere. Later Li Yuchao was brought in to write the reports. For fear the Kuomintang detectives might not see them, they even mailed express dispatches to the Kuomintang secret service. Zhou Enlai, seeing these leaflets, praised the method as a good one.

In his dealings with Yang Du, Liu Ding brought back much important political intelligence, and also got in touch, through Du Yuesheng, that great Shanghai gangster, with channels through which he could keep abreast of the Nanjing ruling clique's movements and of the doings of certain "dignitaries of party and state."

Liu Ding had married a fellow student, Wu Xianqing, at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in the latter half of 1927, and the two returned to China together in the winter of 1929. Wu Xianqing was assigned by the Chinese Communist Party's Jiangsu provincial committee to work among women workers in the Pudong district, and in the summer of 1930 she was transferred into the Central Special Section to do intelligence work under Liu Ding. "Wu Xianqing used her home region's specialty, Huangyan honey tangerines, to open a little fruit stall at the mouth of a lane as cover for secret work. In those days Liu Ding handled the radio links with the various departments, and Wu Xianqing took charge of keeping and delivering the Party's documents and intelligence; husband and wife, one working in the little upstairs room and one 'doing business' at the shopfront, made a fine team. Wu Xianqing had the special ability and adaptability of the secret worker, and she used connections within the enemy camp to advance the work. In 1930, not long after she joined the Central Special Section, her younger brother Wu Quanyuan was released on bail from the Zhejiang Army Prison and came to Shanghai to look for her. In their long talk she learned that his release had been secured by Chen Baohua, a man who held such offices as member of the Kuomintang's Shanghai municipal party committee and commissioner of the Kuomintang's Social Survey Bureau. Chen Baohua had also recommended her brother for the post of general-affairs director on the New Life monthly, published by the reactionary authorities. Wu Xianqing set out to use this connection, and with Chen Geng's consent she set up a secret Party liaison station on the floor above the magazine's distribution office. Through her 'close' dealings with Chen Baohua, she contrived to draw from the important figures around him a number of the Kuomintang's inner secrets and a great deal of important intelligence, and she came to know in detail how various arrested people had conducted themselves in custody. Through other important connections she once obtained a shipment of arms for He Long's forces; but because the Kuomintang had the Huangpu River tightly blockaded and watched, the boat He Long sent to take delivery could not reach the bank, and it came to nothing."

After Liu Ding was arrested in October 1931, Wu Xianqing worked under Pan Hannian and was also charged with keeping in touch with Liu Ding in prison. When she visited him in Nanjing, she used her upper-society connections to pass the Party organization's instructions cleverly to Liu Ding, and it was thanks to this that he was later released on bail. But she never saw Liu Ding again. In 1933, with the approval of the Chinese Communist Party Center, Wu Xianqing was transferred to the Comintern's Far Eastern Intelligence Bureau as head of an espionage group, and she worked for a time in Tokyo. It commands admiration, "the brave spirit with which, knowing no Japanese, she went alone and boldly to Tokyo, a city where she knew neither the place nor a soul." In September 1935 Wu Xianqing went on to Moscow, changed her name to Rosa Lakurova, and entered the Marx-Lenin Institute. Unluckily, in the interval after she finished her course while she awaited an assignment, the Soviet purge widened, and in November 1937 Wu Xianqing was falsely branded a "Japanese spy," wrongfully arrested, and exiled to hard labor in Siberia; from then on there was no further word of her. She was only thirty-three that year.

On July 25, 1986, at the age of eighty-three, Liu Ding died of illness in Beijing. "Liu Ding's Life," published in the People's Daily, praised him as "a loyal Communist fighter, a fine and long-tested member of our Party." It read, in part:

Comrade Liu Ding withstood the harsh tests of the enemy's prisons and courts; he was of noble bearing, unbending and unyielding, and kept the true colors of a Communist. Yet under the influence of "Left" thinking he was many times treated unjustly. During the ten years of catastrophe in particular, he was held in prison for as long as seven years, cruelly framed and persecuted, his body and mind gravely harmed, and still he struggled resolutely against Lin Biao and the "Gang of Four" counter-revolutionary cliques. He believed in the Party and the people to the last, and held firm in his conviction that the road he had traveled in the struggle for the revolutionary cause was the right one. In prison his will did not waver: under the most wretched conditions, using even the paper of tooth-powder packets and coarse toilet paper, he wrote more than two hundred thousand characters of technical summaries and proposals, not one word of which touched on his own rights and wrongs, his own honor or dishonor. After the smashing of the "Gang of Four," Comrade Liu Ding firmly upheld the line, principles, and policies of the Party since the Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee, kept himself in ideological and political step with the Party Center, respected the leadership, and stayed close to the masses. Out of a strong sense of responsibility for the Party's cause, and heedless of his age and frailty, he threw himself into work to speed the development of the aviation industry, at the same time directing the compilation of a history of the "Xi'an Incident." Even while gravely ill in hospital, Comrade Liu Ding never stopped caring about the aviation industry and the national economy; when his life was in danger he still asked, and braced himself, to hear reports on the reform of the system and the progress of the transport aircraft. He gave the whole of his life's strength to the Party and to the cause of socialist construction, showing the lofty revolutionary spirit of a Communist who spends himself utterly and stops only at death. The Party Center cared deeply for Comrade Liu Ding, and thoroughly reversed the verdict on the long, unjust treatment he had suffered, restoring his political good name.

Li Qiang, who had long fought at his side in the Central Special Section, knew Liu Ding's lifelong struggle intimately and felt deep sympathy for the batterings and persecution he suffered again and again from extreme "Left" tendencies. After Liu Ding's death, Li Qiang wrote with feeling in a memorial article: Liu Ding's life had been full of twists and hardships, and, precisely because he had once been arrested and once taken prisoner, in political campaign after political campaign he was always "put through the wringer," and was even fitted with the cap of "traitor." Over this Liu Ding was long unhappy in his heart. During the "Cultural Revolution" he was again shut up in a "cowshed." Not until after the Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee set things right did the original Party group of the Third Ministry of Machine-Building, prompted by Liu Ding's repeated appeals and the help of many old comrades, re-examine his history; on the strength of the powerful testimony written for him by Pan Hannian while Pan was himself in detention, and the testimony of a man taken prisoner together with him, they at last cleared up the whole course of his arrest and captivity, and so utterly overturned the traitor's charge fixed on him for so many years. To help him and to clear up that history, Li Qiang had read the depositions Liu Ding gave in enemy hands. "Those depositions confirmed from many sides that, in the most wicked and tangled circumstances, Comrade Liu Ding feared no danger and waged a resourceful, dogged struggle against the enemy, making a special contribution to the defense of the Party Center and the Party's underground organizations. His great wisdom and great courage the other comrades and I have always deeply admired; he ranks among the few figures of legend in our Party with an extraordinary career and record."

### Ke Lin, the Life-Saving Physician

Ke Lin, a physician of his age, was another figure of legend on the hidden front, one wrapped in a heavy air of mystery. In his middle-school days, together with his close schoolmate Peng Pai, he took part in the struggle to punish Yuan Shikai for proclaiming himself emperor and to restore the Republic; he also beat the local bullies so soundly that Chen Yuebo, a great despot-gentry of Haifeng, rolled up his bedding and fled to Hong Kong. In his long years of secret work he went alone deep into the tiger's den, and time and again, under the very eyes of a savage enemy, brilliantly carried out every task the Party gave him, protecting many of the Party's cadres and revolutionary masses. He was later sent to work in Hong Kong and Macau, where, with a noble physician's ethics and consummate medical skill, he made friends widely, united people of every walk of life, broadened the patriotic united front, and won the deep respect and affection of his compatriots in Hong Kong and Macau.

Ke Lin (1900-1991) was a native of Haifeng county, Guangdong. In middle school he was a classmate of Peng Pai, and, moved by this early leader of the Chinese Communist Party and famous peasant leader, the "Peasant King," he read progressive books and papers, sought after revolutionary truth, and took in advanced revolutionary ideas. After finishing middle school in 1920, he went to Guangzhou and, as a scholarship candidate, passed into Guangdong Public Medical University. The school, formerly the Guangdong Public Medical College, was a medical academy the Americans had founded in China at the time, an aristocratic university. Ke Lin studied hard and came first in the whole school.

In May 1921, Peng Pai, who had studied in Japan, came home after graduating from Waseda University and, on two successive visits to Guangzhou, met Ke Lin there. The Peng Pai of these days was more mature politically. He told Ke Lin that China, beset by troubles within and without, was like a great ship that had lost its rudder, tossed in a raging storm; to save China one had to find the right course, China had to make a revolution, it had to take the road of the Russian revolution. Peng Pai also told Ke Lin about the revolutionary work being carried on in Japan, all manner of strange tales and anecdotes, and the revolutionary activists among the Chinese in Japan and other countries. In these talks with Peng Pai, Ke Lin heard the name of Zhou Enlai for the first time, and he plainly sensed that Zhou Enlai was one of the comrades Peng Pai most respected.

In the spring of 1924, on Peng Pai's introduction, Ke Lin joined the Chinese Socialist Youth League, the first Youth League member at the "Public Medical" school. In 1926, when the Party organization decided that the Youth League members of the Sun Yat-sen University medical school, as the "Public Medical" school had been renamed, should become Communists, Ke Lin was among the school's first members of the Chinese Communist Party.

About this time, the "Public Medical" students raised a school agitation to drive out a toadying foreign teacher, Deng Zhende, and met the high-handed suppression of the principal, Li Shufen, a man forever fawning on foreigners. Acting on instructions from the Party and Youth League organizations, Ke Lin at once roused the school's progressive students to a class boycott for democratic and civil rights. The boycott began in March 1924 and held out until May, when Li Shufen was forced to accept the conditions for a return to class: dismiss Deng Zhende and withdraw the notice expelling the students. Ke Lin then organized and led the "Public Medical" students in joining the Canton-Hong Kong Strike. At the same time the Party organization put forward the demand that the Chinese government take back control of the school, and this at once won the warm support of the progressive teachers and students. The Kuomintang left-wing leader Liao Zhongkai resolutely backed the students' just demand. Ke Lin then called most of the teachers and students together at the school and they resolved on three points: oppose enslaving education and bring down Li Shufen; take back control of "Public Medical"; and merge "Public Medical" into a university under Kuomintang leadership. The teachers and students petitioned the Kuomintang Central Party Headquarters to grant the three demands. On July 1, the National Revolutionary Government set up that very day decided that "Public Medical" should be merged into Guangdong University. The next year, when Guangdong University was renamed Sun Yat-sen University, its medical school was renamed too, becoming the Sun Yat-sen University medical school. Afterward Ke Lin led the progressive teachers and students in the struggle against the Kuomintang right.

In 1927, after the "April 12" coup in Shanghai and the "April 15" coup in Guangzhou, Ke Lin again led the Party organization in rescuing arrested students, and got Xu Binru, the secretary of the Sun Yat-sen University Chinese Communist general branch, whom the enemy was hunting, safely out of Guangzhou. On the organization's arrangement he then went from Guangzhou to Shanghai, and made his way on to Wuhan for the national congress of the Communist Youth League. After the congress he was assigned to medical work in the Fourth Army of the National Revolutionary Army, which was deeply under Communist influence. In the Fourth Army he met Ye Jianying, the army chief of staff, and Li Shuoxun, the party representative of the 25th Division. That September Ke Lin returned to Guangzhou with the Fourth Army and took part in the Guangzhou Uprising of December 11. When the uprising failed, part of the insurgent forces withdrew to Huaxian. Ke Lin, who had received no order to withdraw, hid in Guangzhou for a dozen days and then, in disguise, made his way through Macau to Hong Kong. There he lived in seclusion with Ye Jianying at Tai Po market, enduring nine bitter months. In the summer of 1948, Ke Lin and Ye Jianying sailed together for Shanghai.

Not long after reaching Shanghai, in November 1928, Ke Lin, on Zhou Enlai's instructions and by arrangement of the Central Special Section, joined He Cheng in opening the "Dasheng Hospital" at Yan'an Lane on Sichuan Road in Shanghai. It was in fact a secret organ of the Chinese Communist Party Center, a place where, under the Central Special Section's protection, the Party Center held its regular meetings and Politburo comrades made contact. At the time Ke Lin went by the alias "Ke Dawen" and He Cheng by "He Yusheng," and one character was taken from each of their cover names to make the hospital's name.

He Cheng (1901-1981) was born He Zonglin, styled Runzhi, a native of Shehong county, Sichuan. He joined the Chinese Communist Party at Beijing Medical University in 1925 and worked in the student movement. Like Ke Lin, he took part in the Northern Expedition during the Great Revolution. After "April 12," when the White Terror shrouded the whole country, he led the medical department of the Fourth Army of the National Revolutionary Army into the Guangzhou Uprising though he had lost his Party contacts and was in the most extreme difficulty. When the insurgent army pulled out of Guangzhou and was reorganized at Huaxian into the Red Fourth Division, he found the Party again. Using the medical profession as cover, he moved from place to place doing the Party's underground work, and came to Shanghai in September 1928. On the eve of the Dasheng Hospital's opening that November, to make the work easier, the Party organization approved the marriage of He Cheng and the Communist Zhou Yuehua at the hospital.

Zhou Yuehua was a native of Guangji county, Hubei. Strong-willed by nature, and wanting to break the bonds of a feudal family, she left home alone for Hankou and entered a women's normal school. When the Wuhan Central Military and Political School, run by Deng Yanda and Yun Daiying and formerly a branch of the Whampoa Academy, was founded in April 1927, she passed into its women's company, the earliest women's unit in the history of the Chinese revolution. She had joined the Chinese Communist Party at the military school in November 1926, and afterward took part in the Guangzhou Uprising and in the Red Fourth Division's struggle at Haifeng. When the Red Fourth Division was defeated, she made her way to Hong Kong and elsewhere, and later came to Shanghai to do the Party's underground work.

"At the hospital, Zhou Yuehua was both housewife and nurse. Besides going out on her own to keep up with acquaintances, relatives, and friends and to build secret working ties, she would often put on a cheongsam, paint her lips, and go out on He Cheng's arm. The two of them would pretend to be seeing off or meeting relatives, and at railway stations and wharves they made contact with comrades in the Party, screening the movements of responsible comrades of the Center and of couriers.

"The Dasheng Hospital was a small three-story building, its rooms all connected, with a back door onto another street. The moment leading comrades of the Party Center stepped into the hospital they became its 'patients,' shielded and carefully arranged for a safe move on. He Cheng had every room laid out with care. The room where the Center's leading comrades held their secret talks was hung with an infectious-ward sign, and disinfectant was set out at the door. On the beds all sorts of markers could be hung out at any moment, treatment cards for patients prepared in advance and the like; the whole disguise was so meticulous that not a flaw could be found.

"When the hospital first opened, Comrade Zhou Enlai came in person to brief He Cheng on the work here. He said: at present the Party is hemmed in by a grave White Terror, and our comrades must adapt to this complex and difficult setting, going deep into society and deep among the masses, using every kind of connection to open up the work. Pacing back and forth in the room and pointing at its furnishings, Comrade Zhou Enlai went on to say, the hospital may be small, but the responsibility is great. Your work bears on the safety of the Party Center and of the Politburo comrades. What you do is the Party's security work; you must deal directly with the enemy day in and day out, and become the Party's eyes and ears, its nerves and its bloodstream. Then he added, with a touch of wit, you are a doctor, so of course you know how important the eyes and ears, the nerves and the bloodstream are to the human body. We must get the better of the enemy, stay a move ahead of him, a step ahead of him, and hold the initiative; the Party trusts you, letting you take on so important a task, and you must be bold yet careful, cool and composed, unshaken in danger, and tight-lipped as a sealed bottle. At this Comrade Zhou Enlai laughed, and said, struggle will make our comrades clever."

In late August 1929, betrayed by the turncoat Bai Xin, came the case in which Peng Pai, Yang Yin, and others were arrested and murdered. On Chen Geng's instructions Ke Lin took part in the struggle to hunt down Bai Xin and execute this turncoat, and made an important contribution. In the joy with which the whole Party hailed the victory, Ke Lin felt gladdened to have done his share in avenging Peng Pai and the other murdered comrades.

The suppression of Bai Xin bore on the safety of the Dasheng Hospital too. Ke Lin, who had taken a direct hand in it, was the first to receive word from the Party organization to move house at once. Ke Lin recounted: "After the business [of suppressing Bai Xin on the night of November 11], the Party Center sent Little Sichuan (a Sichuan man; being young, the comrades all called him Little Sichuan, and his actual name is not clear) to my home to tell me to move on at once and leave the hospital. Only two or three hours after I left home, men of the Kuomintang public-security bureau came to arrest me; but by then I had reached the Cai Mengkang Clinic in safety, the seat of a Party Center organ."

Unable to lay hands on Ke Lin, the enemy in a fury seized the neighbors, among them the wife of Wang Ke'ou, who had shared his lodgings. For the safety of Ke Lin, He Cheng, and Zhou Yuehua, the Center decided to close the Dasheng Hospital at once. At this Zhou Enlai came to the hospital with Deng Xiaoping. Zhou Enlai said: the work here has been splendid. But this place, Shanghai, has many eyes and ears of the enemy, and a "business" run too long in one spot easily draws his notice. Deng Xiaoping remarked wittily: with the Dragon King in his little temple, move to another place and you can still call up wind and rain. After the Dasheng Hospital's work ended, He Cheng and Zhou Yuehua opened a clinic of their own on Burkill Road, still a place for Politburo comrades of the Center to meet.

Next, Chen Geng sought out Ke Lin and passed on the Party's instructions. He said: now that we have eliminated the turncoat, the enemy is bound to retaliate. Your position is very dangerous; you must leave Shanghai at once and go to the Northeast, where the Party organization there will arrange your work. Chen Geng ended, weighting his words: "This is Comrade Wu Hao's view."

By November the temperature in Fengtian, today's Shenyang, was already very low. It was Ke Lin's first time in the North, and coming in the season of ice and snow, hard to adjust to, he was in poor health the whole while after his arrival. A month or more later, Liu Shaoqi, secretary of the Chinese Communist Party's Manchuria provincial committee, called Ke Lin in. He had known Liu Shaoqi back in Guangzhou, and was delighted to meet him again here. In their talk Liu Shaoqi said: it is so cold here, you are dressed too thinly, you cannot speak the Northeastern dialect, and the work is hard; I think it would suit better for the Center to assign you work in the South. The next day Ke Lin took the train to Dalian, then sailed back to Shanghai, with Chen Geng meeting him at the wharf. Around New Year's Day, through the eager matchmaking of Lu Dingyi, Yang Hansheng, Guan Xiangying, and others, he was joined for life with Chen Zhiying, a graduate of the Tongde Advanced Midwifery School in Shanghai.

After a short rest in Shanghai, Ke Lin and his wife, on the Party's decision, went to Xiamen and opened a "Tongde Hospital" to serve as a cover organ for the Party.

"The first to come and make contact was Comrade Luo Ming, then secretary of the Party's Fujian provincial committee. Ke Lin had known Luo Ming since their student days, and Luo Ming had even been the one to introduce him into the Youth League. Old friends meeting unexpectedly, they were of course overjoyed. Luo Ming brought him some of the Party Center's documents and briefed him on the Center's recent activities. In their talk Luo Ming disclosed his disagreement with the 'Left' tendency that had appeared at the Center. He held that the revolutionary movement in Fujian was still in the course of recovery and simply could not meet the conditions for launching an urban insurrection at once; for this he had been rebuked by some of the Center's leaders. What was heartening was that Luo Ming told Ke Lin about the survey he had lately made in person of the western Fujian countryside, and the news that the Red Army under Zhu De and Mao Zedong was pushing into western Fujian. He believed China's revolutionary hope lay in the countryside, in the Red Army of Zhu and Mao. A few months later, the provincial committee organ in Xiamen came under enemy surveillance, and some comrades were arrested. After talking it over with Ke Lin, Luo Ming decided to send Ke Lin and his wife to Hong Kong.

"From the failure of the Great Revolution down to 1929, the Party Center's chief leaders and most of its members still put their faith in urban insurrection, and the Party's center of gravity was in the very course of shifting from the cities to the countryside. Because this did not fit China's realities at the time, the Party's work in the cities suffered setback after setback. Ke Lin's experience over these years reflected the same thing. After 1929, the Party's organizations in Shanghai and other great cities were struck by several severe blows in succession; dragons and fish were jumbled together, true and false hard to tell apart, the work harder still and the setting more tangled and more dangerous. In Hong Kong, Ke Lin, rich in experience of underground struggle, set himself a plan of action: not to make contact with the Hong Kong organization for the time being, but first to get a footing in Hong Kong himself, watch how things stood, and then decide. With the help of his younger brother Ke Ping, he opened a little apothecary's shop in Sham Shui Po, the 'Nanhua Pharmacy,' and hung out the sign 'Chinese-medicine physician Ke Lin.' Ke Lin was in fact a Western-trained doctor, but the Hong Kong British authorities ruled that a Western doctor who had not graduated from a Hong Kong medical school could not hang out a Western-medicine sign to practice, which cut his business badly, so that his income barely covered the rent."

In the spring of 1931, Li Shaoshi, whom Ke Lin had known back in the Canton-Hong Kong Strike, came to Hong Kong to organize a Party liaison station. From then until the station was broken up in the summer of 1933, more than two years, Ke Lin, on Li Shaoshi's arrangement, again and again found ways to smuggle to the Soviet areas medical supplies they badly lacked. After the Zunyi Conference of January 1935, Pan Hannian, head of the propaganda department of the Red Army's General Political Department, was ordered to leave the Long March and came to Hong Kong as a special commissioner of the Chinese Communist Party Center, meaning to go on to Moscow to report to the Comintern. A few days before the Mid-Autumn Festival that year, Pan Hannian invited Ke Lin to his own quarters at the Nathan Hotel to meet Ye Ting. Ye Ting had just returned from abroad and settled in Macau. Pan Hannian told Ke Lin that Ye Ting needed someone to keep in regular touch with him, to draw him back into the Party's work and at the same time look after his family. Considering that Ke Lin had too many acquaintances in Hong Kong and might easily run into trouble, and could not hang out a Western-medicine sign, it seemed a move to Macau would serve several ends at once. So on the day of the Mid-Autumn Festival Ke Lin moved his whole household to Macau. From then on, through Ke Lin, Ye Ting built close ties with the Chinese Communist Party Center, and the Party's instructions were all passed on promptly by Ke Lin. Ye Ting got on very well with Macau's upper society and with Li Jishen's old subordinates, and Ke Lin, taking the chance, made friends of these men too, which brought his work much convenience. After the Xi'an Incident, the Chinese Communist Party Center sent Zhang Yunyi to Macau to find Ye Ting, and Ke Lin brought him to Ye Ting's home. Zhang Yunyi conveyed the Center's invitation for Ye Ting to come to Shanghai to discuss jointly the founding of the New Fourth Army. Ye Ting gladly agreed to go, and once asked Ke Lin to go north with him to head the New Fourth Army's medical work. But when Ye Ting inquired of the Party, Pan Hannian still wanted Ke Lin to stay on in Macau.

From 1935 to 1951, Ke Lin worked seventeen years in Macau. On one side he labored, unknown and unsung, for the Party on the hidden front; on the other he rose from an ordinary practicing doctor to become superintendent of Macau's Kiang Wu Hospital. With the standing of a famous physician of the South China of his day, he moved into Macau's upper society and won the trust and praise of every walk of life.

From 1951 on, Ke Lin held the post of president of the Sun Yat-sen Medical College in Guangzhou and secretary of its Party committee. On the medical-education front of New China, with his foresight and insight, cherishing and uniting the intellectuals, governing the school strictly, and encouraging the young, he was hailed as a great master of the medical college. On September 23, 1991, Ke Lin died of illness in Beijing at the age of ninety-one. On September 29 of the same year, the New China News Agency reported from Beijing on his life and deeds, setting a high value on his important contribution to the revolutionary cause and his noble character: "Comrade Ke Lin was in all his life above board and open-hearted, holding to principle and observing Party discipline. He was diligent and uncomplaining, conscientious, bold in advance and pioneering in innovation. Plain and simple in his ways, easy to approach, strict with himself and generous with others, he united his comrades and was upright in conduct. Never counting his own gains and losses, he cared for the masses and cherished the intellectuals, and won the deep respect and affection of the broad body of cadres and masses. His pupils were everywhere; all who knew him admired without exception his noble bearing. For the lofty ideal of communism Comrade Ke Lin selflessly gave the whole of his life to the cause of the Chinese people's liberation and of socialist construction, spending himself utterly and stopping only at death."

### Chen Yangshan, the "Lucky General" of the Hidden Front

Chen Yangshan was one of those brought in at the very founding of the Central Special Section's intelligence section, and the one who did intelligence work longest in that section. "From 1924 to 1940 he fought his way across Wuhan, Shanghai, Tianjin, Chongqing, Chengdu, Xi'an, and other places, through every danger under the savage White Terror; only after 1940 was he transferred back to work in the base areas, where he long took part in leading intelligence and security work. In eighteen full years of underground struggle he was never once arrested by the enemy. The old comrades called him a lucky general of the hidden front. This was no knack of his; it was because he held a firm revolutionary faith, a correct political direction, a supple art of struggle, and a strict sense of organizational discipline." Li Qiang, Chen Yangshan's old comrade-in-arms from the Central Special Section days, praised him as "a hero of the hidden front, a loyal and honest public servant."

Chen Yangshan was born Cheng Yangshan, styled Yingluo, and used at various times the cover names Chen Yingzhou, Chen Deqing, Chen Zhongying, Chen Mingjun, and Gao Junshi. He was born in 1906 in Shangyu county, Zhejiang. His family was poor from the first, and in 1919, at thirteen, he went to Hankou as an apprentice at a native bank. After three years of apprenticeship he became a clerk. The May Fourth Movement that rose in those years and the "February 7" strike on the Beijing-Hankou Railway in 1923 both left a deep mark on his thinking. Reading China Youth and other progressive papers spurred him early on to link his own future with the rise and fall of the nation; he gave up the beliefs he had once held in industry and education as the way to save the country, came to see that only revolution could save the country and the people, and set up a reading circle among the clerks to study Marxist theory. In April 1924 he wrote to Yun Daiying, editor of China Youth, telling of the reading circle they had formed and their doings in social investigation. Yun Daiying wrote back warmly with encouragement and praise, and that July also asked Chen Yangshan to his home, where they talked for six or seven hours, to Chen's great inspiration and instruction. Chen Yangshan said: this meeting was one I could never forget as long as I lived. He kept up a long acquaintance with Yun Daiying afterward, learning from Yun's word and example; Yun Daiying's unshakable revolutionary will and his unstinting spirit of work both left the deepest impression on Chen Yangshan. The article he wrote in memory of Yun Daiying was titled "Yun Daiying, the Man Who Led Me to Revolution."

After this talk, Yun Daiying also introduced the Communist Yu Shisong to make contact with Chen Yangshan; before long Yu Shisong left to study in Moscow, and the contact passed to Liu Baiwen of the Wuchang telephone office. That October, Chen Yangshan went through the initiation ceremony of the Chinese Socialist Youth League. After this their reading group spread quickly and, renamed the "Hankou Youth Self-Improvement Society," became a center for rallying the clerks. In 1925, one day, Chen Tanqiu had a talk with Chen Yangshan and said to him: from now on you are not only a member of the Socialist Youth League, you are also a member of the Chinese Communist Party, and your responsibility is greater.

After Chen Yangshan joined the Party, the Chinese Communist Party's Wuhan district committee made the native bank where he worked into a Party communications station, and from then on so many people came and went to find him that the bank's owner grew suspicious. In May 1925 the organization transferred him to Shanghai as a courier for the Youth League Center, and he took part with Ren Bishi in the May Thirtieth demonstration and in the petition at the Shanghai General Chamber of Commerce. In July he returned to Wuhan as acting secretary of the Wuhan Youth League district committee; in September he became a standing committee member and head of the organization department of the Kuomintang's Hankou municipal party headquarters. Because he had too many acquaintances in Hankou, which made secret work most inconvenient, the Party transferred him back to the Youth League Center. Yun Daiying was then setting up the Shanghai Communications Bureau, and assigned him to work there.

In the winter of 1926 Chen Yangshan was transferred to Zhejiang, doing united-front work at the Kuomintang's Zhejiang provincial party headquarters in Ningbo; in March 1927 he was moved to Hangzhou to build the Party's organizational apparatus. Before long the "April 12" counter-revolutionary coup broke out. With no foothold in Hangzhou, the Party sent him back to Shanghai as head of the propaganda department of the Fa-Nan district committee, then transferred him again to Wuhan to work among the clerks. This time too, because he had too many acquaintances locally and it was unwise for him to work there, he soon returned to Shanghai. For a time Chen Yangshan could not find the organization, had nowhere to settle, and went home to Zhejiang for a while.

In early January 1928, Chen Yangshan and several Party sympathizers planned in Haimen county to get some firearms and raise a peasant insurrection. But someone informed the reactionary government, the Kuomintang sent troops and police to hunt him down, and Chen hid at a relative's for a while, returning to Shanghai after the Spring Festival that year, where he soon joined the Central Special Section's intelligence section.

Chen Yangshan's greatest contribution during his time in the Central Special Section was that he was the first to discover and make contact with Yang Dengying, whose original name was Bao Junfu. This was the first important counter-espionage tie the Central Special Section drew out of the Kuomintang secret service, a model of a man "pulled over" from within the enemy.

Chen Yangshan and Yang Dengying had met in the spring of 1926, when Chen had studied Japanese with him. At that time Chen Yangshan worked at the Shanghai Kuomintang Central Communications Bureau run by Yun Daiying, whose task was to carry the great quantity of progressive books, papers, and magazines published in Guangzhou from Guangzhou to Shanghai and forward them elsewhere, to keep them from being confiscated by the local post offices. These papers were brought to Shanghai by sea under the bureau's own arrangements and stored in an empty house in a Zhabei Road lane, and Yang Dengying often came to help. Chen Yangshan would often bring papers to Yang Dengying, who was delighted, saying that translating these books and papers into Japanese earned him a fairly good fee. The two thus became fairly good friends, and Chen would sometimes ask Yang Dengying to see to a matter for him, which Yang was glad to do. On this return to Shanghai, having nowhere to stay, Chen lodged at Yang Dengying's home. Chen Yangshan said: "Around March or April 1928, Bao Junfu told me that Yang Jianhong, the chief secretary of the Party Affairs Investigation Section of the Kuomintang Central Organization Department, that is, the CC Clique, wanted him to be a detective and do work to wreck the Communist Party. Bao Junfu wanted both to hold office in the Kuomintang and to draw close to the Communist Party, and asked me how he could have it both ways. Considering that he was of the Kuomintang left and had a certain sympathy for our Party, and could be won over and used, I reported the matter to the Party Center. With the Center's approval I was to enter into a special relationship with Bao Junfu, and it sent Comrade Chen Geng to meet Bao Junfu with me to fix his tasks and the means of contact. Before long Yang Jianhong committed suicide over an embezzlement case, and Bao Junfu was promoted to head of the Kuomintang's Shanghai secret service. Because of my special tie with Bao, the Party Center decided to transfer me to the Central Special Section to do intelligence work. From then on I was in the Central Special Section the whole time."

Facing the menace of the White Terror in those days, the underground Party's activities were kept in the strictest secrecy. Chen Yangshan, like many comrades, "went nameless and hid his identity, forever changing his cover and his aliases, keeping contact with other comrades on the Party's orders. He would do a good turn without the other even knowing his true name and real identity, and after many years a chance encounter might be needed before the two knew each other anew; many a dramatic scene came of it." Between Chen Yangshan and He Long it was just so.

After the failure of the Nanchang Uprising in 1927, He Long made his way to Shanghai in early November. As commander of the 20th Army of the National Revolutionary Army and commander-in-chief of the Nanchang Uprising, he was under the Kuomintang's strict order of arrest, with a bounty of 100,000 silver dollars on his head. The Party organization entrusted the sheltering of He Long to Chen Yangshan, who, heedless of danger, gladly took the charge, finding a hiding place and arranging board, lodging, and liaison, handling every matter superbly and keeping He Long safe in Shanghai. Later, when He Long, with the Center's approval, returned with Zhou Yiqun to western Hunan to organize an armed force, wage guerrilla war, and open a base area, the Center had Chen Yangshan lay out the communication line and escort He Long's party smoothly to the Honghu region, once more brilliantly completing the task. From this He Long grasped the importance of intelligence work, and Chen Yangshan left a deep impression on him. But it was more than ten years later, when He Long saw Chen Yangshan in Yan'an, that he learned his true name. In Yan'an He Long said, this work is a marvelous thing, and this Chen Yangshan is a marvelous man too; this work is worth any amount of money. He Long thought highly of Chen Yangshan, and recommended to the Center that Chen Yangshan be transferred to the Shanxi-Suiyuan Border Region as director of the investigation bureau of the Chinese Communist Party's Central Jin-Sui sub-bureau. In January 1945, the Central Intelligence Department, on He Long's proposal, transferred Chen Yangshan to the Shanxi-Suiyuan Border Region as director of the Jin-Sui sub-bureau's investigation bureau.

Between Chen Yangshan and Yang Xianzhen there was a like case. In April 1931, the Central Special Section sent Hu Egong and Yang Xianzhen to Tianjin to rescue the arrested Chen Yuandao, secretary of the Chinese Communist Party's Shun-Zhi (that is, Hebei) provincial committee, and other comrades; that June Chen Yangshan reached Tianjin with Chen Geng. Chen Yangshan then went by the alias "Old Wang," and Chen Geng sent him to the Datong Apartments to share lodgings with Yang Xianzhen and carry on the rescue work together. In late July, when Yang Xianzhen went to Beiping to make contact with a secret Party member, he was unfortunately arrested, and the rescue work came to a halt. Once arrested, Yang Xianzhen was thrown into prison, and before long Chen Yangshan too returned to Shanghai with Chen Geng; the two had no further dealings from then on and knew nothing of each other (at the time Yang Xianzhen used the alias Yang Zhongren). Almost half a century passed, and not until after the Chinese Communist Party's Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee in 1978 did Yang Xianzhen learn that the man once called "Old Wang" was Chen Yangshan, at the time deputy procurator-general of the Supreme People's Procuratorate.

Back in the 1960s, in order to write a biography of General Chen Geng, I had interviewed Comrade Chen Yangshan, who warmly and in detail told me of Chen Geng's struggles in the Central Special Section in those years. The writing of the biography was broken off during the ten years of catastrophe, when all the materials were "confiscated" and destroyed by the "rebel faction." After I came out of the eight years of unjust imprisonment forced on me by the Lin Biao and Jiang Qing counter-revolutionary cliques, I went, from November 2, 1978, onward, again and again to his home at the Hongxia Apartments for talks. He received me as warmly as before, and answered in full every question I put. After several private talks, he also held two discussions with Li Qiang, Liu Ding, Ke Lin, and others who had worked in the Central Special Section. The chapter "General Chen Geng: Into the Tiger's Den," written up from what was said, he read over repeatedly, and separately asked Li Qiang and Liu Ding to join in offering revisions, so that it could be gradually improved and made fuller, and some errors in the account cleared away. This conscientious, responsible attitude and matter-of-fact manner left a deep impression on me. His speech was like the man, honest and plain, unadorned. At every interview he took what he said in earnest. On the more important events he would think back tirelessly, adding and revising without end. Of things he did not know he simply said he did not know, or recommended someone who might, for me to go and interview. "To know is to know, and not to know is not to know." From his lips came no guesswork, no far-fetched surmise, and never a word of anything vague or blurred. Chen Yangshan gave himself to the revolution in his youth, and across sixty-seven long years he stood always at the very front of the fight to defend the Party's interests and to struggle against the enemy. Especially in 1928-1931, during his time in the Central Special Section, he took part in founding the Party's security and intelligence work, and made an outstanding contribution to the struggle to protect the Party Center and its leading comrades. Yet whenever he looked back on that stretch of struggle, he always made light of his own great contribution, passing it off in a phrase, while of Chen Geng's and his other comrades' deeds he spoke vividly and with relish. He was in fact an inward, reticent man of few words. Long years deep in the tiger's den, doing intelligence and security work in a setting of extreme peril, had tempered him all the more into a seasoned, prudent, steady man. In ordinary talk he was always terse and plain. But the moment he touched on those storm-tossed exploits, an irrepressible passion would seize him, his face alight, his spirits soaring. The pitched struggle to suppress the turncoat Bai Xin, the victory in the struggle with the great turncoat Gu Shunzhang, and the other struggles that reached deep into the enemy's secret heart, he would speak of easily, like counting his family treasures. Set beside his everyday bearing, he seemed almost another man. What moved one most of all was that toward Zhou Enlai, who had led the Central Special Section's work in person in those years, and toward Chen Geng, with whom he had long been on close terms and had fought side by side, he would often let show a boundless reverence and an endless longing, fully displaying the sincere friendship peculiar among Communists between comrade and comrade.

### Chen Shouchang, Fallen on the Battlefield

Another fighting general of the intelligence section, Chen Shouchang (1906-1935), also named Chen Xikan and styled Xiulun, was born into a scholarly family in Zhenhai county, Zhejiang. His grandfather was a Qing dynasty provincial graduate, his father a late-Qing licentiate. Studying in his home town under the influence of the May Fourth Movement, he took part in speech-making and propaganda to boycott Japanese goods and the like. At sixteen he left home for Shanghai, where his grandfather, through a friend's introduction, urged him to take a post at the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank. This was a well-paid position that ordinary people envied, but he would not so much as glance at it and courteously declined. Back at thirteen, in a poem he wrote in mourning for an elder brother who had died of illness while taking part in the boycott of Japanese goods at school, there was the bold line, "Should I one day unfurl a cloud-soaring will, I'll see to it that heaven has no stifled thunder," so far-reaching were his ambitions. His grandfather, though an old-style scholar risen through the provincial degree, was fairly enlightened in mind; seeing him thus, he let the matter rest with "each man has his own will, which cannot be forced."

Chen Shouchang passed with distinction into the telegraph office in Shanghai as a clerk. Quick-witted and diligent, he soon mastered telegraph work and the technique of repairing transceivers, and in 1922 he was assigned to the Zhengzhou telegraph office. In the "February 7" railway workers' strike of 1923 he saw the mighty strength of the working class, took an active part in the sympathy strike of the railway workers, and was tempered in his thinking by it. In 1925 Chen Shouchang transferred to the Wuhan telegraph office, and in 1926 he joined the Chinese Communist Party, from then on working in the labor movement under the Party's leadership. That September, when the All-China Federation of Trade Unions set up its Wuhan office, with Li Lisan as director and Liu Shaoqi as secretary-general, Chen Shouchang, under Li Lisan and Liu Shaoqi, organized the workers to support the Northern Expedition of the National Revolutionary Army and took part in the struggle to recover the British Concession. On January 5, 1927, Chen Shouchang joined the mass rally of 300,000 that Li Lisan convened at Hankou, and after it took part in the demonstration, charging with the crowd into the British Concession, tearing down the barricades and blockhouses the colonial authorities had set up, and recovering the British Concession.

After the failure of the Great Revolution, the Party and the trade unions were dealt severe blows. For a time Chen Shouchang lost touch with the Party organization, and in the winter of 1927 he shook off the enemy's pursuit and made his way to Shanghai to find the Party. One day he happened to meet Li Lisan in the street and reconnected with the Party. Soon afterward he was transferred to the Chinese Communist Party Center to do secretarial work.

In the winter of 1928, Chen Shouchang was transferred to the First Section of the Central Special Section to do intelligence work. Under Chen Geng's direct leadership he kept contact with Li Kenong and promptly relayed the intelligence that the "Three Heroes of Longtan"—Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, and Hu Di—obtained from the enemy's highest secret service, and he did much work besides to assist Chen Geng. Alert and brave in his work, at every unexpected caller or ill omen he would shift his lodgings in time, often moving house a dozen or more times a month. Wretched as the conditions were, Chen Shouchang was never once caught off guard by the enemy; he could always dodge the enemy's searches and blows and keep the Party's secret organs safe. His eyes were severely nearsighted, yet his reactions were unusually quick. Every time he went out on a task, the moment he found himself tailed he could adapt to the circumstance, slip the "tail" cleverly, and get clear of danger.

Chen Shouchang also took part in rescue and escort work. While defending the Chinese Communist Party Center's leading organs, he contributed as well to the safety of revolutionary comrades inside and outside the Party. "At the end of 1929, Sano Manabu, general secretary of the Japanese Communist Party, came to Shanghai after a meeting in the Soviet Union. Chiang Kai-shek's secret service learned of it and ordered Sano Manabu hunted down. Zhou Enlai handed the important task of protecting Sano Manabu to Chen Shouchang. Having accepted the task, Chen Shouchang carefully arranged for Sano Manabu to stay in the home of a progressive person in the concessions. Thus a Japanese Communist leader hunted by Chiang Kai-shek's secret service lived safe and sound for one month and five days, and afterward returned safely to Japan."

Later, Chen Shouchang was transferred again, from the Second Section to the Fourth Section, to help the Fourth Section chief Li Qiang run the section, chiefly in charge of setting up and managing the radio stations. As he was expert in radio technology, he used the guise of the owner of a radio-parts firm as cover for his revolutionary work. On December 17, 1930, the radio training class the Fourth Section had opened at Sicheng Lane in Shanghai was broken up by the enemy. After that the radio station was led directly by the Central Secretariat, apart from the Central Special Section. Zeng San served as radio operator, and Li Qiang kept well out of sight. The Center put Chen Shouchang in charge, and every night he saw to the sending and receiving of the telegrams himself.

After Gu Shunzhang's arrest and defection in April 1931, Chen Shouchang, under Zhou Enlai, took part in the emergency measures the Center adopted, safeguarding the Center's organs. The Central Special Section was then cut down in size, and both the radio and the communications work were led by Chen Shouchang. At the end of that year the Center sent him to work in the Central Soviet. Though his aged mother, his two-year-old daughter, and his pregnant wife were all in Shanghai, and there were many difficulties, he put the revolutionary cause first, and at the end of December he sailed from Shanghai, going by way of Xiamen and Shantou to the Central Soviet.

In January 1932, Chen Shouchang became director of the Soviet-area executive bureau of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions. In 1934, when Chiang Kai-shek launched the Fifth Encirclement Campaign, the Chinese Communist Party Central Military Commission sent Chen Shouchang with more than forty men to cross every obstacle and go to the Hunan-Hubei-Jiangxi Soviet to take charge of the provincial committee and the military district. On arrival he directed the cadres of a dozen or more provincial party and government organs, besieged on Xianyuan Mountain in Wanzai, Jiangxi, together with the wounded and sick of the Red 16th Division, in a complete and safe withdrawal to Tonggu county; then he set the ranks in order again and led his troops in guerrilla war among the Luoxiao and Mufu mountain ranges of the Hunan-Hubei-Jiangxi border region, at more than 1,600 meters, in coordination with the strategic transfer of the Central Red Army. In that extremely bitter setting of struggle, Chen Shouchang, like everyone else, filled his belly with roots and bark, sometimes ate but one meal a day, was so starved he vomited yellow bile and his legs and feet were swollen, and still he held to his work. He said to the staff around him, "The common people are hard put too; when you've eaten what's theirs, you still have to pay it back!" The comrades of the provincial political security bureau, unable to bear the sight, took out money they had scraped together and bought him a bowl of noodles, but he flatly refused to eat it. He said to them all, "You are hungry too, we are all the same," and had it taken to the wounded and sick. In the face of a powerful enemy, the Red Army, to preserve its strength, broke up for the time being into scattered parts and sparred with the enemy. Under the masses' cover, Chen Shouchang evaded the enemy's searches time and again; to guard against a sudden strike, he changed his sleeping place almost every night. Once he lay six or seven hours in a ditch under a bridge.

In the sixth and seventh months of that year, Chen Shouchang led the cadres of the provincial-level organs in a withdrawal and was surrounded several times by the enemy, with heavy losses. In one breakout, from long trudging his feet were swollen and festering and walking was very hard, yet he held on directing the fight at the front. For a time, "unable to break out of the enemy's encirclement, he could only go back and hide on the hillside of Gaojian, north of Huangsha in Yifeng. Hidden on the mountain a month and more, Chen Shouchang nursed his foot ailment while he waited for his chance. At the time there was neither medicine nor any medical instrument, and the pus in the wounds on his feet was all scraped off with a sliver of bamboo and the wounds washed with brine, the pain of it bringing the sweat down his forehead in beads. But the enemy was posting notices everywhere offering a reward for Chen Shouchang's capture alive. To escape the enemy's hunt, they sometimes changed place several times a day, taking 'high mountain caves for a house, green hills and leaves for a bed.' In this bitter, dangerous setting Chen Shouchang was still full of revolutionary optimism. 'Not going deep into the mountains, how could one subdue the tiger's might; my remaining years spent in hardship and peril, those who come after may yet bask in the glow.' He chanted the verse to hearten himself and his comrades. His foot ailment, after a short spell of treatment, was healed at last. Then he broke out again and returned to the Huangjindong Soviet in Pingjiang county."

In November 1934, Chen Shouchang read in a Kuomintang newspaper that the Central Red Army had already pushed west out of Hunan. He at once led the Red 16th Division in a drive to the southwest, to coordinate with the strategic transfer of the Central Red Army. In early 1935, the force advanced to Laohudong-Yajian'ao, between Chongyang and Tongcheng, when it suddenly ran into the enemy and fell into a tight encirclement. In the fighting he was struck by a bullet and gravely wounded, and, from losing too much blood, died a hero's death. The Red 16th Division that Chen Shouchang had led fought on until 1937, when it was incorporated into the New Fourth Army and set off for the Anti-Japanese battlefield, to begin a new struggle.
