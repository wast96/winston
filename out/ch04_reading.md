## Kisaru and Gohei

"Look there."

A knot of heads gathered on the Shijō riverbank turned north as one.

"Not there. The northeast, I said."

Told so, the crowd looked that way. There were peddlers among them, and juggler-priests too. In the middle of the packed ring stood a young slip of a girl, her kimono kilted short, pointing to the northeast.

"You fools. Toward that mountain, there—what do they call it—yes, Nyoigatake."

Her eyes alight, she browbeat the crowd down over their heads.

—It was Kisaru.

"Don't look at the mountain. Nothing on the mountain but trees. Look above it."

The crowd's eyes wandered up toward Nyoigatake—a mountain in name only, a swell of ground rising east of the Kamo river.

"See the cloud? That faint one, shaped like a man. Keep watching—it'll grow."

By the time Kisaru said it, the wind had already risen. It blew south, loosening the side-locks of the men who stood facing the mountain. As it strengthened, the man-shaped cloud swelled before their eyes, broke its form southward, and merged with the black cloud that had begun to climb from the hill of the Shōren-in. Yet the real sky over the crowd's heads stayed clear—uncannily clear.

"Don't take your eyes off the cloud, I told you. Watch close—its color's going black, blacker. There, see, Higashiyama's begun to pale."

And whether or not it was fancy, a haze began to settle over the green of Higashiyama, and the wind took on a bleak, rain-laden chill.

"No—don't look away."

So, pinning the crowd's eyes to that one scrap of cloud,

"See—the wind's turned heavy. Above me the pine boughs are creaking. Don't look—I'll look for all of you. There, there, the pine needles are shining. It's beginning to wet them, drop by drop."

A single drop fell to the collar of someone in the crowd. Some winged insect must have lit on the back of his neck, and he took it for a raindrop—

"Ah—it's raining!"

"Not yet, not yet. It's coming. A summer squall—and when it comes down in a rush, keep still, don't move."

In any crowd there is always one weak-nerved soul who makes the rest more of a crowd. One of the juggler-priests, unable to bear the aching suspense of the squall that was coming, suddenly clutched his head—

"It's raining!"

"The squall's here!"

In an instant the crowd broke into uproar.

"A squall! A squall!"

A white rain fell on them without mercy. Some ran; some rushed for the single trunk of a pine and crouched against it. Not one drop, of course, had fallen. And in the eyes of every man who scrambled about, the pupils were blown wide, like a sleepwalker's.

Overhead the sky was blue and clear, absurdly so. The eighth-month sun, even in that moment, was roasting the tiled roofs of the capital without pity. The temple groves, as ever, showed the white of drought under a blue sky that had held for a good half-month now.

Kisaru was no longer there. Tired of her mischief, perhaps, she had already crossed the Shijō bridge and was striding briskly toward the thickets of Kennin-ji.

A samurai stood watching her retreating figure from the shadow of a pine.

Below the woven sedge hat showed a pale chin, hinting at the man's youth and beauty. Faintly he murmured—

"Kisaru…"

—under his breath. The samurai was Kazama Gohei. In no hurry, he set off after her.

In those days Gion was not yet a quarter of teahouses. The road to Kennin-ji ran in a single line past a scatter of farmhouses, through pine woods and vegetable plots and thickets.

Naturally, Gohei following behind was in plain view the moment Kisaru turned her head. If anything, he closed the distance, as though waiting to be noticed.

The road ran into the precincts of Kennin-ji. Pines were scattered across the broad grounds in a fine, measured rhythm. As she reached the foot of the first of them, Kisaru wheeled around. She had reckoned it well, no doubt: where Kazama Gohei now stopped, there was no pine within reach to put at his back if it came to that.

"Who are you?"

She held Gohei full in her glare.

Beneath the hat, the pale chin curved in a smile.

Still hatted, saying nothing, he took a step forward. Kisaru drew back, keeping the pine between them.

"It's me."

The words came, the smile still lingering on his chin.

"'Me'—who?"

Kisaru knew already who he was. From the moment she was followed, she had felt Gohei at her back. She had come up to the capital to find this very Gohei—had haunted the places where people gathered by day, sifting through every likely figure: samurai, merchant, priest, Shinto cleric, down even to the beggars. And now that same Gohei had turned his own steps to come after her.

That being so, the strangeness of her own impulse—to turn and slip away instead—was something even Kisaru could not fathom.

"Sulking, are you?"

Loosening the cords of his hat, Gohei came toward her.

Kisaru stood where she was, glaring at Gohei.

(So I am sulking.)

So Kisaru thought. And yet it was not only that, she felt.

"Gohei."

"Mm?"

"What is it you do now, these days?"

"…………"

"Why did you cut off all word to your home province?—Ah—no you don't!"

Kisaru struck his hand aside and sprang back. Before her was Gohei's face, broken open in a smile. Looking at it, she felt a pang: that a beauty so great, when it laughed, should knot a mean little shadow at the corner of the mouth.

"Why will you not, Gohei—"

"What?"

"—come to take me for your wife?"

"For my wife?"

"Yes."

"In time. I will."

"Liar. It's written on your face."

"Little fool."

Laughing, Gohei reached to take her by the shoulders—

"Ah—"

—and drew his foot back in a flash and clamped his own wrist. A bright red point stood on it. Between Kisaru's fingers a thin needle showed.

"A fierce girl."

Licking his wrist, Gohei said—

"Where is your lodging?"

"I won't say. …In what province was there ever a ninja who told where his own hiding-place was?"

"Let's go to that lodging of yours. There's much to talk over."

Paying her no mind, Gohei set off in the lead. Just as he had reckoned, Kisaru followed, cheeks puffed in a pout.

Leaving the precinct, they came out onto Matsubara road, and Gohei turned east as if idly. Save for the huddle of Rokuhara off to the right, it was open paddy-field as far as the eye could reach. Ahead, out of the green hills, the pagoda of Kiyomizu rose clear and bright.

From the direction of Kiyomizu—back from worship there, most likely—a samurai came down at a quick pace with a single servant at his heel, and, passing before Kazama Gohei, bent slightly at the waist and went on.

When they had let him by, Kisaru looked at Gohei's face with suspicion. She made as if to speak, then held her tongue. She kept close at his heels, step for step.

Gohei walked with no particular destination. In the set of his supple shoulders was the confidence that Kisaru would show him her lodging soon enough. Only, as they went, he questioned her idly, in a rambling sort of way.

"Why were you working that blinding-trick back on the riverbank?"

"I was bored."

"It's well enough you dazzled them cleanly; botch it and you'd be beaten to a pulp."

"What did you come up to the capital to do?"

"Who knows."

"Won't you say?"

"To a man who won't answer what I ask, there's no call for me alone to answer."

"Since when did you turn so stubborn?—Here, now."

So saying, Gohei went to take her hand. There was not a soul in sight. Roughly she shook him off. And yet—

"Ah—not that way."

"This way, then?"

Gohei took the small thicket-path that led in to the Chinnō-in.

"A guest, is it?—"

Suddenly a voice rang out from within the thicket.

Gohei stopped short with a start. On either side of the path stood a grove of green bamboo.

"A guest, is it?"

"Yes. Someone I knew back home—"

Kisaru answered. There was no one to be seen.

"The one you spoke of as your betrothed—him?"

"Heh."

"A fine-looking man, no less."

"All show and no worth."

"Ho—she blushes."

After that a laugh—ah-ha-ha—echoed through the bamboo grove.

"Who is that?"

Gohei stepped close to Kisaru and asked in a low voice.

"The Bamboo Saint."

"The Bamboo Saint?"

"That's what the people here call him."

Gohei stepped off the path and set his feet warily on the dead bamboo leaves. He moved into the grove, working toward the voice.

The bamboo grew thin enough that the daylight showed through from the far side. Had anyone been among it, he would surely have stood in plain view.

"Bamboo Saint, or whatever you are."

"What is it?"

"Show yourself before you speak. This is discourtesy."

"Discourtesy? You set your own blindness on the shelf and talk of that? I've been where I ought to be this good while."

"Where—"

As Gohei spoke, the voice slid away into silence. Puzzled, he took five or six steps forward—and a cold hand coiled around his ankle.

"Ah—"

He leapt back before he knew it.

"Ah-ha-ha—now do you see?"

With the laugh, the ground before Gohei's eyes shivered in small tremors. What he had taken for ground was the thick mat of dead bamboo leaves heaped over it. Under those leaves, buried like a grub with only its eyes showing, was a droll, absurd face. Propped on two elbows, it looked up at Gohei.

"Wh—what are you?"

"Gyōzan, that's who."

"Gyōzan?"

"Abbot of the Chinnō-in—Iten Gyōzan."

"What are you doing down there?"

"Won't you try it with me?"

"Try what?"

"A man might come out a little straighter for it."

"You're looking at the bamboo?"

"That I am. Looking. Long and hard."

"Is there some reason for it?"

"None to speak of. It's like gazing at a woman you're fond of. I'm fond of bamboo—I've gazed at it like this a full twenty years now."

"Twenty years—"

"And no nearer enlightenment for it."

"Why must you go out of your way to lie in the fallen bamboo leaves?"

"Well—that too is a delusion, I suppose."

"A delusion?"

"I fancied that this way I'd come to understand the heart of the bamboo the better."

"And have you understood the heart of the bamboo?"

"No."

"A dull-witted priest."

"But in its place—easy as you please—I go and become the bamboo."

"Hah. A fine tall tale, priest-fashion."

"You call this a tall tale? Ah-ha-ha—the proof is that a man with as clever a face as yours couldn't tell where I was. You came into the bamboo grove and did not see the bamboo, sought only the shape of a man—and that delusion of yours kept you from finding me."

"You prattle."

"But—to change the subject—becoming bamboo has its good and its bad. There are inconveniences."

"…………"

"Bamboo is straight by nature. And when you're straight yourself, you see other men's crookedness all too well—a petty nuisance, past bearing. …For instance."

Gyōzan took his right hand from under his chin and pointed at Gohei's face.

"You have a beautiful face. But it's an evil one. Such things I see clearly."

"Why an evil face?"

"Do you know the followers of the outer path? They discipline themselves as we do. But their direction runs otherwise. It is not to empty the self, but for the self's own gain and greed. It builds up, year on year, and comes out on the face as an evil cast.—Now then: I don't know what name you go by, but you—you are a rappa."

"What!"

"Don't draw. Cut me down, and it will do nothing for that evil face of yours."

Gyōzan rolled over, turning his back, and pillowed his head on his elbow.

"Gohei."

Kisaru came around behind and gave a small tug at Gohei's sleeve.

"Take up with Gyōzan and the day will be gone. Besides, you'll never get the better of him."

"What would you have me do?"

"I'll show you to my lodging. I too—like Gyōzan—have a mind now to get to the bottom of what you really are, Gohei."

Kisaru went ahead of Gohei. In the blue sky above the thicket, the moon still hung.

Where the thicket broke off stood the temple. Burned in some fire—when, there was no telling—half of it was charred black, and half had rotted and fallen in.

"The Chinnō-in. …Why, this is the temple of that bamboo priest just now."

"Yes."

Kisaru went around to the back and stepped into a small outbuilding.

"How did you come to this temple?"

Gohei said it, running his eyes about Kisaru's room—a bare single ken of space.

"On Shijō street Gyōzan caught me by the sleeve."

Astonishingly, the Chinnō-in held no temple lands. It had once belonged to the Shingon sect, but, landless, had long stood without a resident priest, until Gyōzan came to live there. He would go out at times to beg his alms, and at times let lodging to travelers. This Zen monk, it seemed, made a tidy inn of the place and, on the takings, kept up the offerings to its chief image.

"Never mind Gyōzan—"

The young woman's bloom went suddenly out of Kisaru's face, and a hardness came over her, the hardness of a ninja's daughter, and pressed in on Gohei.

"A strange sight I saw just now. A gentleman of rank, with a servant at his heel, seemed to make you a bow."

"A man will bow, meeting someone he knows."

"No. It was not the manner of an ordinary acquaintance. He bore himself as one who took you, Gohei—by your office—for his superior."

"Don't come to me with wild fancies."

"Sit down, Kazama Gohei."

Kisaru said it low and hard.

"In my father Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon's stead, I will ask you.—And, according to how you answer—"

"You'll do what, I wonder?"

"You will not get off so lightly."

"A hard interrogation."

A sneer rose on Gohei's pale face, and he settled cross-legged. His sword he had drawn close to his left side. Blood came up, all at once, about Kisaru's eyes.

"Gohei."

"What?"

"You have broken the Iga law."

"…………"

"I can see it. You always loathed the life of an Iga man. With such skill and gifts as yours, you used to say you'd not spend your whole life a rappa—I remember it."

"Quite true."

"For a full year you cut off all word to Iga. No—I'll not let you talk your way out."

Gohei began to speak, then closed his mouth. A wry smile stayed on it.

"You slipped away from Iga. You took service with some great provincial lord. It must be so. Am I wrong?"

"Well—say out what's on your mind."

"Do you remember Kuranawate-no-Shishiji?"

"Hm."

"This spring he was cut down by his own comrades' hands. Word had reached Iga that he was in the service of Katō Higo-no-kami."

"Kisaru—"

Gohei, too, dropped the honorific. Making a show of shifting his crossed legs, he edged his body forward.

"Have you forgotten? We've not done as man and wife, but you are my betrothed. You'll not have forgotten that."

"If I am your betrothed, why did you leave me a whole year? Depending on how this goes, I will break the tie myself."

"The Master would never allow it."

"My father consents. When I told him I would break the tie and then run the traitor through with my own hand, he said: so be it."

"A child, aren't you."

"What?"

Thrown off, Kisaru opened her mouth a little. White teeth showed between her lips. An innocent, unripe freshness set her long lashes blinking fast.

"Grown people aren't so full of valor as that. Between a man and a woman most of all, it turns strange. Even had I something I ought to tell you, to a child like you I couldn't tell it."

Gohei sprang and seized her in his arms.

"Here and now I'll make a grown woman of you. Become a woman. We'll talk after that."

"No! …Have mercy."

Kisaru struggled. Her hem fell open and a white shin showed. In a flash Gohei reached to her feet and bound her two big toes together with a cord. His left arm held her—breast and both arms at once—clamped so she could not move. His right hand went roving over her.

"M—mercy…"

Even so, Kisaru tried to slip free. She had the bodily skill for it, or should have. But her arms went slack first. She felt the strength dissolving out of her, down the spine and through the marrow. She fought desperately to keep her senses from going numb.

"Have mercy…"

"Hf."

Gohei's hand was inside her body. Her body alone betrayed the child in her. The irony of it drew a wry smile from him.

"I'll call Gyōzan."

"That man is still lying in the bamboo. And even if he came, there's nothing he could hold against a betrothed pair doing what husband and wife do."

As he spoke—with what intent, who could say—Gohei loosed the cord from her toes. Her legs were given their freedom. But that very thing robbed her of freedom instead. Her will had already lost the strength to command her legs to guard her. Into the opened body drove the life of Gohei.

"No."

Her lips alone said it, feebly. From the corner of her eye a single tear fell. But the thing had already begun.

"Kisaru—listen to me."

Gohei whispered gently at her ear. With the look of one drained of all will, Kisaru gave a small nod.

"Now you and I are man and wife past all doubt."

Gohei laid his lips—finer in shape than her own—lightly on Kisaru's bloodless mouth, and—

"The wife of a traitor turns traitor, naturally. Just as you suspected—yes, I did slip away from Iga."

"…………"

"Since then I've served under Maeda Gen'i. My stipend is two hundred koku."

"Do you know Gen'i's office?"

"…………"

"Magistrate of Kyoto. My charge is to hunt out the spies of the provinces who slip into the capital, and men of doubtful business. Depending on my service, my stipend may be raised to a thousand koku."

"And I—am to be that man's wife?"

Kisaru said it. Whether or not she knew it herself, there was a lift in her voice.

Gohei nodded; but on the other side of it, deep in his feeling, a barefaced emptiness spread.

When he took service with the Maeda house, Gohei had hidden that he was a rappa. Still less had he breathed a word of how, on the orders of the Iga man Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, he had met a masterless samurai named Matsukura Kurando in the precincts of Tōdai-ji and taken part for a while in a dangerous scheme.

There could be no question, surely, of taking to wife the only daughter of a full-fledged Iga rappa. …So Gohei was thinking.

He had even changed his name.

Gero Shōbei Yasuji, he called himself now.

There happened to be such a surname among the retainers of the old Ichijō house of Tosa, and he gave himself out as one of their line.

He had cast off Iga.

But his old comrades would not let him cast it off so easily. He would have to guard himself. When the time came, he meant either to cut down whatever messenger Iga sent to call him to account, or to trump up a charge and have the man put to death.

And then, unlooked for, it was Kisaru who came.

He had not been quite without thoughts of Kisaru, even after casting off Iga. But in the matter of taking her to wife, it was less his own will than Jirōzaemon's urging that had ever been at work. And to become Jirōzaemon's son-in-law was to end his whole life a rappa. That would mean the ruin of Gohei's own ambition, such as it was.

…At that moment Kisaru, held beneath Gohei, moved with a sudden shudder, as though something welled up in her.

"What is it?"

"Will you make me your wife?"

"I will."

A hollow look came into Gohei's eyes.

Kisaru did not notice.

She took Gohei's lust for love. For this man's sake she thought she would give up even Iga. And in the instant she thought it, for some reason the face of Tsuzura Jūzō rose before her. To wipe that face away, Kisaru went wild in Gohei's arms.

"Hold me!"

Winding her arms about Gohei's neck, Kisaru tried to blot out Jūzō's face by violently shattering something inside herself. It was almost like prayer. At last the face went out. Kisaru fell limp and emptied.

Whether, in her case, Jūzō's face stood for her Iga comrades, or whether, apart from that, Kisaru secretly loved Jūzō—it was, most likely, both at once. By the time the act with Gohei was over, Kisaru had parted from Iga for good.

"Will you work for me?"

"I will."

"Stay at this lodging for the present."

"And you?"

"I'll be at my official residence on Sanjō. In time I'll send for you."

When Gohei looked down at Kisaru lying on the matting, his face had gone cold again.

"Straighten your hem."

Languidly Kisaru stirred herself and sat up before Gohei.

"Tsuzura Jūzō—do you know what has become of him since?"

At the words, Kisaru lifted her face as if waking for the first time. In that instant the resolute, wild tautness that was hers came back into her eyes.

"You mean Master Jūzō?"

"Hm."

"What has happened to Master Jūzō?"

"Nothing has happened. I merely asked. You'd know something of it, surely. Your father Jirōzaemon is using Jūzō for something, is he not?"

"I don't know."

"It's written on your face."

Gohei laughed aloud.

"You told me only now you'd betray Iga. Are you still not woman enough?"

Gohei put out a hand to draw her in; Kisaru shook it off and—

"Enough.—About Master Jūzō—I know a little."

"Tell it. That, it seems, is the thing that will make me a thousand-koku man."

"A thousand koku…"

Kisaru braced her right hand against the floor to hold herself up. She stared at the matting, thinking.

"The child born between you and me will hold a thousand-koku place of his own in his turn."

"…Master Jūzō is in the capital. That is all I know."

"Best keep it in mind. There'll come a time I must have you work on it."

For some reason Gohei did not press her further, and rose to his feet.

"I'm in haste. No need to see me off."

"As if I would."

Kisaru said it from where she sat, spitting the words out toward the garden. Gohei's back was already moving off, two or three garden-stones beyond.

Kisaru could not shake the sense of having been cheated. A dull ache lingered inside her body. What Gohei had left her, she half felt, was neither love nor sincerity—only an opaque, formless ache.

And yet… Gohei had said a thousand koku. What a thousand-koku samurai's wife carried herself like, in what robes she dressed, Kisaru had no idea—but she found herself seized, all at once, by a wish to become just that. Thinking so, by a strange logic she felt she could believe, after all, in the love of the Gohei who would bring her that good fortune. And at the same time it seemed to her that she herself was beginning to love Gohei. Kisaru raised her dry eyes. In them, beyond the thicket, the sunset-red clouds hung mirrored, blank and vacant.

Under that same sky, Gohei was in the thicket. Gyōzan barred the bamboo path. Opening his red mouth, he said:

"Your coming to call at the temple, too, is some karma of the Buddha. Won't you leave an offering before you go?"

"Absurd."

Gohei shoved Gyōzan aside.

"You're the fool. You walk about with hell on your back, and haven't even the wit, meeting a priest, to make an offering. Your blessings will run out on you before long."

He cursed at Gohei's retreating back, and presently rolled over onto the dead leaves again.

After Gohei had gone, along the path beside where Gyōzan lay, a woman passed—rustling faintly, muffling her footsteps. It was not Kisaru. ……
