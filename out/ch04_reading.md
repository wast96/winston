## Chapter 4. A Convergence of Winds and Clouds

The convergence of winds and clouds at Wuhan during the Northern Expedition—the mighty, surging mass movement above all—was something the like of which had never been seen since the beginning of the world. The event of greatest influence among them was the struggle by which the people of Wuhan recovered the British concession in the first and second months of 1927. At that time the imperialist powers—Britain, France, Japan, Russia, and the rest—all had concessions at Hankou. Between Christmas of 1926 and New Year's Day of 1927, British sailors landed at Hankou and Jiujiang, caroused and got drunk, and molested women workers; when they met with protest they would not admit their fault, and even beat Chinese people to death and wounded others. This roused the righteous indignation of the masses of every circle and set off a mass struggle to recover the concessions and oppose imperialist atrocities. Mobilized by the general labor union, which was led by the Chinese Communists Liu Shaoqi, Li Lisan, and others, tens of thousands of the masses surrounded the British consulate and put forward stern demands: to recover the British concession, to abolish the unequal treaties, to lower the British flag before the consulate, to withdraw the British warships anchored in the Yangzi, and to order the British sailors who had done the killing to apologize and confess their crime to the Chinese people. At first the masses only shouted such not very civil slogans as "British devils, get out," acting on their own; but very soon this developed into a great, organized, led, and orderly anti-imperialist struggle. The angry crowds came pouring like a tide from every direction to surround the British consulate, the sound of their slogans never ceasing. The staff of the British consulate, trapped in the encirclement, could hardly come and go, and the stores of food within were nearly gone; so they quietly sent people out to find the dockworkers surrounding the building, hoping through bribery to get them to help ferry their personnel and luggage secretly away to the British warships anchored in the Yangzi. But the workers refused them sternly, saying: "We must obey the orders of the general labor union! Only lower your British flag [and apologize to the Chinese people], and we will protect your safe withdrawal." At that time the leaders of this struggle adopted very good tactics: although the masses outside had the consulate hemmed in so tightly that not a drop of water could trickle through, they did not cut the consulate's telegraph link with the British government in London. So the British inside the consulate kept sending telegrams to their government, complaining and crying for help. Seeing that things had gotten out of hand, the British government could only wire back, instructing the consulate to apologize and withdraw for the time being. On 9 February the Foreign Ministry of the National Government signed an agreement with the British side, recovering the British concessions at Hankou and Jiujiang. This was the greatest victory the Chinese people had won in the anti-imperialist struggle since the May Fourth Movement, and a glorious page in the history of the great revolution during the Northern Expedition.

In this struggle our women's association too mobilized and organized the women of every circle, taking shifts to surround the British consulate day and night, shouting anti-imperialist slogans, and doing the logistical support work of bringing tea and refreshments to the demonstrating masses, so that women played their part as "half the sky." Although later the Kuomintang right wing "purged the party" against the Communists, the first Kuomintang–Communist cooperation and the great revolution failed, and British imperialism returned to Hankou and restored the British concession, still, under the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party, the Chinese people carried on their heroic, unyielding struggle, and by 1949 at last completely recovered the foreign concessions throughout mainland China and abolished the unequal treaties, and in 1997 washed away a hundred years' national humiliation by recovering sovereignty over Hong Kong, the ceded territory. This was a historic leap! The great victory of this struggle to recover the British concessions at Hankou and Jiujiang gave both the Party and the masses a most profound lesson: they came to appreciate the great power of the mass movement, and that only the people are the makers of world history; and they gained a further understanding of the importance of armed struggle and military work.

Clearly, without the powerful backing of the Northern Expedition's successive victories, British imperialism would not have lowered its flag and withdrawn from the consulate. But at that time, for the whole Party, and especially for the general secretary Chen Duxiu, the understanding of the importance of military work was still very inadequate. Although our Party did excellent propaganda and organizational work at the high tide of the great revolution, it had not yet been able independently to lead and command a revolutionary armed force. Only after passing through the victory of the Northern Expedition and of the struggle to recover the British concession at Hankou, the victory of the Third Armed Uprising of the Shanghai workers, and the counter-revolutionary coup launched by the Kuomintang right that led to the failure of the first Kuomintang–Communist cooperation and of the great revolution—only then, from the lessons of both the positive and the negative side, did we truly and deeply come to appreciate the extreme importance of armed struggle in the Chinese revolution.

After I returned to Wuhan from Shanghai, it happened that Song Qingling wanted to open a national training class for women cadres, and she appointed me to serve concurrently as director of preparations for this class. Much of the concrete practical work, such as finding a house for the class, was done for me with the cooperation and help of Comrades Zhao Wanhua, Liang Jianhua, and others. Zhao Wanhua had a very wide range of social connections in Wuhan and was capable besides, so she very easily found a house in Hankou. What I mainly did was the preparatory work of a director of instruction: drawing up the training plan, arranging the curriculum, and engaging the teachers. All our teachers were people of great renown. Besides Song Qingling, who lectured on the history of the Chinese women's movement, the wife of the Soviet adviser Borodin lectured on the history of the international women's movement, using as textbooks the Chinese translations of Bukharin's *The ABC of Communism* and Bebel's *Woman and Socialism*. Li Lisan lectured on the history of the Chinese workers' movement, and Li Lisan's wife Li Yichun on the history of the women workers' movement. We even specially invited Wan Xiyan to give a lesson opposing "feminism." Why lecture against "feminism"? Because at that time, while men's and women's equality and women's liberation were being advocated, there were also a good many very capable women who fell into a mood of arrogance and self-satisfaction, developing a tendency to talk only of women's own liberation while looking down on male comrades—an extreme swing to the other pole; and in particular some women, out of opposition to the "male chauvinism" of arranged marriage, advocated a doctrine of celibacy and were unwilling to work together with male comrades. Besides this, we also invited a good many notables of the Kuomintang side to lecture—for instance the wife of Xu Qian, and Wang Shijie and others, on subjects such as law and diplomacy. These teachers were people of high standing and rich experience in both the Kuomintang and the Communist Party, yet all were glad to lecture without pay, taking no remuneration.

The students of the Central women-cadres' training class were recommended by the women's associations of every part of the country; they trained for three months, and on graduation received a certificate and returned to work in their own localities. This was of great significance for training the backbone leaders of the women's movement everywhere. Every week the organizers invited famous social activists and theorists to give the class political reports, explaining the political situation at home and abroad, which was also very practical political and ideological education for the many young women cadres who had newly taken up work in the high tide of the great revolution.

On Women's Day—8 March 1927—Wan Xiyan and I, together with Mei Dianlong and Gong Pinjuan, were all married at the same time in Wuhan, and we all lived together in the two-story, three-bay house that my father rented on Youyi Street in the French concession. My father's dozen or so large cases of books were kept on the ground floor, and the second floor was given to the four of us to live in. Pinjuan was three years younger than I, and after joining the Party took the name Bingruo. She was the mother of Mei Xiangming, deputy chairman of the Central Committee of the China Association for Promoting Democracy; after retiring she lived in Beijing and died in 2001. Truly, "many of one's companions wither away," and only friendship endures.

We held no ceremony when we married; it was announced openly by Comrade Dong Biwu at a meeting. At the same time a simple notice was published in the newspaper: "Notice from Wan Xiyan and Huang Mulan: We two, being of one mind and one purpose, have joined as revolutionary companions, and hereby respectfully inform our relatives and friends." For me this was a public repudiation of my former arranged marriage. Comrade Wan Xiyan had a first wife in his own home village, who had already borne a daughter; because it was a family-arranged marriage, Xiyan had long since separated from her. This first wife was unwilling to remarry and remained in the Wan family; after the founding of New China she even received the treatment due to a martyr's dependents, which I think was right. From the May Fourth Movement to the Northern Expedition, young men and women opposing family-arranged marriage and choosing their own partners had become a commonplace tide of the age. Pioneers of the New Culture Movement like Lu Xun and Guo Moruo had originally had wives at home and later chose new companions, but none of them formally divorced the first wife. They were not out to take several wives and concubines like the men of the old society; rather, by their action they opposed the feudal system of arranged marriage. As for putting monogamy into practice, only when New China promulgated its Marriage Law were there clear legal provisions. After our marriage notice appeared in the paper, the comrades who knew us all said the "Empress" had married the "Emperor." Because at that time I represented the women's world at all sorts of mass rallies and often sat on the platform, very active, people in the press and cultural circles jokingly called me the "Empress"; and after Wan Xiyan was transferred to the Central Military Commission he also served concurrently as chairman of the preparatory committee of the National Association of Journalists, and journalists have long been called "uncrowned emperors," so people came up with this congratulatory phrase, praising our brave act of opposing the feudal system of arranged marriage.

Here it is necessary to say something about Comrade Wan Xiyan. Xiyan was born on 10 February 1903, a native of Huangmei in Hubei. His father Wan Zhiying (styled Yaofeng) was a xiucai and an enlightened local gentleman of Huangmei. While Xiyan was studying in Hubei, under the enlightening guidance of progressive teachers, he read many books and journals propagating the new culture and new thought. In 1923 he joined the Chinese Communist Party at Southeastern University in Nanjing and served as the first secretary of its Party branch. Many fellow townsmen and schoolmates from Southeastern University mentioned him in the memoirs they later wrote. Xiyan set out on the revolutionary road under the influence and guidance of the two revolutionary pioneers Dong Biwu and Chen Tanqiu. While Xiyan was studying at Southeastern University, under the influence of Elder Dong and Comrade Tanqiu, progressive young people all over Hubei organized reading groups, read progressive journals such as *New Youth*, *The Guide Weekly*, and *Chinese Youth*, and studied and propagated the revolutionary theory of Marxism-Leninism. Xiyan often returned to his home village in Hubei, and the local Chinese Communist organization in Huangmei was founded by eight comrades, Wan Xiyan among them. Huangmei in the great-revolution period had grown into a great revolutionary force, and was once called "Little Moscow"; a revolutionary memorial hall has now been built there. Later Xiyan took part in the "May Thirtieth" anti-imperialist patriotic movement in Shanghai, and as a member of the Chinese Communist Party was elected a delegate to the Kuomintang's First National Congress, going to Guangzhou for the meeting together with Comrades Dong Biwu, Yuan Puzhi, and others. At that congress he came to know Liu Yazi and other Kuomintang left-wing figures. During the first Kuomintang–Communist cooperation, Liu Yazi was executive committee member and secretary-general of the Kuomintang's Jiangsu Provincial Party Headquarters, and Xiyan was executive committee member and head of the propaganda department of the same provincial headquarters; working together, they formed a deep friendship. After Xiyan fell, Liu Yazi even wrote lines of poetry in grievous mourning for him. In the winter of 1925, Xiyan was transferred back to Wuhan to help Elder Dong edit and publish the *Chuguang Daily*. Xiyan was very good at writing articles and also at public speaking, able to carry on revolutionary propaganda and agitation with fervent, impassioned eloquence. In the winter of 1926, at the Second Congress of the Kuomintang's Wuhan Special Municipal Party Headquarters, Xiyan was again elected executive committee member and head of the propaganda department of the Wuhan Special Municipal Party Headquarters.

Xiyan and I first came to know each other at Wuhan in 1926. At that time, at the *Republican Daily* he was editor-in-chief and I was supplement editor; at the municipal party headquarters he was head of propaganda and I was head of the women's department. Seeing each other morning and evening, we passed from understanding to love. Xiyan supported the movement for women to win their own independence and liberation, but opposed women's embracing a doctrine of celibacy. He encouraged me to break free entirely from the feudal bonds of arranged marriage, and, in the course of transforming society and liberating all mankind, at the same time to transform myself, liberate myself, take my own destiny in hand, and create a new and happy life. This was the ideological basis of our love and marriage. Our union that year, simple though its form, was a formal marriage agreed to and supported by my parents at home, after Comrades Dong Biwu and Qu Qiubai had come to the house for a heartfelt talk with my late father. My father was an old-style democrat who greatly admired Wan Xiyan's talent and praised the editorials he wrote in the *Republican Daily* as very fine. Xiyan too respected my father greatly. When my father was ill, Xiyan engaged a famous doctor and accompanied him across the river to treat my father. So my father not only regarded Xiyan as a fine son-in-law but deeply sympathized with and supported our throwing ourselves into the national revolution. On Youyi Street there were many revolutionary organs and bodies, and when meetings were held, many high-ranking leaders of both the Kuomintang and the Communist Party, notables, and first-rate statesmen would drop in at our home along the way—Dong Biwu and Qu Qiubai, for instance, once had a long talk with my father. Everyone praised my parents for having given birth to so fine a daughter as I, and the two old people were naturally very gratified.

In 1927 Wuhan held, for the first time in its history, a large-scale commemoration of International Women's Day on 8 March, and I was elected director of preparations for the commemorative activities. The preparatory committee was made up of representatives sent by every circle of society (the general labor union, the teachers' federation, the student union, the peasant association, the chamber of commerce...). At every mass rally or commemoration, Xiyan, Geng Dan, and I were all members of the presidium. Geng Dan was deputy commander of the 15th Army of the Northern Expedition and later also head of the youth department of the Kuomintang's Hankou Special Municipal Party Headquarters; introduced into the Communist Party by Comrades Dong Biwu and Wu Yuzhang, he was happily married to Elder Sister Zhang Duhe. I had the great honor of serving as general chair at the rally at which two hundred thousand women and masses of every circle in the three towns of Wuhan commemorated Women's Day. At that time the Hubei Provincial Women's Congress had just been held and had put forward several demands for women's liberation. The Wuhan Municipal Women's Congress had also put forward several demands, and in the name of general chair of the rally I gathered these demands together and presented them in person, in a petition, to Tan Yankai, the then acting central chairman of the National Government at Wuhan, signing it myself; and I sent greetings separately to the women's organizations of the Soviet Union and of the international movement. At the rally I spoke on the brief history of International Women's Day, on why we should strive for women's liberation, and so on, with impassioned fervor and high spirit. My speech was printed in full in the newspapers. This was the most glorious commemorative occasion I felt in my whole life. Even so, I had no mood of arrogance or self-satisfaction. At that time Comrades He Baozhen, Yang Kaihui, and others all worked in the women's association and were organizationally under the leadership of our women's department; and there were also many senior women democrats like Zhang Duhe who, in age and in seniority, all ranked above me. I always managed to keep a modest and prudent manner, learning from the pioneer comrades and respecting the senior women democrats. For the care I received from the Party and the joy I found in the work I have a deep feeling, cherished to this day.

After this Women's Day commemorative rally came a great women's parade. The most worthy of mention among its ranks was the "Red Detachment of Women." At that time the sixth class of the Whampoa Military Academy had opened a branch school in Wuhan and, for the first time, enrolled women students. Dressed in uniform, valiant and full of spirit, they came marching in formation to join the rally's parade, singing all sorts of revolutionary songs at the top of their voices. Their imposing ranks and even step were a fresh and striking sight that made people look on them with new respect. Among these outstanding Whampoa women soldiers were Xie Bingying, author of *Diary of a Woman Soldier*, and Hu Lanqi, author of *In Prison*—both works created out of their own practice. There were also Huang Hao (Jingwen, who in the winter of 1930 served as an officer of the rescue department of the general Relief Society and is now a retired cadre of the Ministry of Health), Luo Jianbing, and others. These women soldiers had all entered the sixth Whampoa class by examination, not by conscription. Because they had to undergo the same hard enlistment training as the male soldiers, they had to pass a physical examination as well as various political and cultural tests, and could be admitted only when they met the requirements in every respect. This Red Detachment of Women not only added luster to the International Women's Day parade but also joined the instructional regiment in the expedition to punish the traitor Xia Douyin. At that time Wan Xiyan had already been transferred to the Central Military Commission as chief secretary of its confidential office and political instructor of the guard regiment. The Military Commission and the women's department saw the expedition off and welcomed its triumphant return; Xiyan and I both spoke in praise of the women soldiers, saying that the Whampoa women students' corps had written a glorious page in the Northern Expedition. It is a pity that soon afterward the Kuomintang and the Communists split and each went its separate way; only at the time of the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation did we join forces again.

It was precisely in this unprecedentedly grand parade that the reactionaries plotted an ugly farce. They had beforehand paid to buy over a group of child prostitutes, twelve or thirteen years old, not yet able to receive clients but already able to sing and entertain, and had them, bare above the waist and wearing only shorts, rush into our parade ranks to make trouble. The moment the prostitutes rushed into the parade, they were spotted by the pickets and seized. But because the reactionaries had plotted it in advance, at the very instant the half-naked prostitutes burst into the parade ranks they snatched photographs of the procession and printed and scattered them everywhere, slandering the women's movement led by the Communist Party as "holding a nude parade." Afterward the public security organ traced the reactionary body that had plotted this farce and dealt with it severely. At the trial, representatives of every circle took part in the joint hearing, and I was one of the judges. To strike back at this plot of the reactionaries, the public security bureau, the women's department, and the women's association held a joint meeting; besides clarifying the truth of the matter and exposing the reactionaries' plot of rumor and slander, they also agreed to ban the brothels within the city of Wuhan. I attended the meeting as a representative of the women's department, and it was decided that the women's department would take on the responsibility of taking in and reforming the prostitutes. Because at that time both the local political power and the military power in Wuhan were in the hands of the revolutionary left, the banning went very smoothly. We closed down the licensed brothels and seized all the open prostitutes and the secret "pheasants," taking them into compulsory custody. A special "Institute for Reforming New People" was set up to take in and reform the prostitutes, with Wang Huazhen of the women's association as its director. The institute treated the prostitutes who had venereal diseases, taught them self-supporting labor skills such as sewing, and posted each one's photograph, name, and age on the wall of the institute's main hall, helping them freely to choose partners, marry, and set up homes. Most of these prostitutes were of poor families, forced to sell themselves to survive, and had not willingly taken up this trade of selling their bodies, so they very quickly accepted education and reform and resolved to seek a new road in life. At that time there were many bachelors in the shop clerks' union and the porters' union who were too poor to afford a wife, and we had them go to the institute to look at the photographs and résumés so that both sides could choose each other. If a man and a woman both took a liking to each other, we helped them freely marry and set up a home. Later, when these families had children, they even acknowledged Wang Huazhen as their "grandmother"! Although after the Kuomintang–Communist split Wuhan restored its brothels, this too was a piece of practical work we did for women's liberation.

I was chair of the committee for the women's foot-unbinding movement. At that time many women in the factories and the countryside still bound their feet into "little feet," and our propaganda work for unbinding went very deep, truly reaching every household: wherever we found a fierce mother-in-law or mother still forcing a daughter-in-law or daughter to bind her feet, we criticized her; and those who stubbornly refused to change, in serious cases, were even fined and could be punished by law. Some great households, seeing that unbinding had become the tide of the age and fearing that in future small-footed women would not find husbands, stopped making their young ladies bind their feet; and the factory owners too felt that letting the women workers unbind their feet was good for labor and production, so that, apart from a few obstinate and conservative families, every circle of society supported the foot-unbinding movement. It may be said that only during the Northern Expedition did the Chinese women's foot-unbinding movement lay a broad foundation everywhere.

Besides this, we also propagated the equality of men and women, advocated monogamy (though there was as yet no legal provision), and forbade men to take concubines again, or to buy bondmaids again or abuse maidservants. But for those households that had already taken concubines or bought bondmaids, the women who had already become second or third concubines could not be placed anywhere, and we could not drag them out by force, so we could only propagate that henceforth no more concubines were to be taken and no more bondmaids kept. This too was a problem the women's liberation movement ran up against in the old society. To truly prohibit concubinage and bondservice and to put monogamy into practice could be achieved only after the founding of New China.

The eighteenth of March was the commemoration day of the student movement, and the municipal party headquarters organized commemorative activities. The singing troupe led by the Youth League organization led everyone in singing "The Song of the Young Vanguards" at the top of their voices: "March forward, ah, the dawn is ahead; comrades, struggle on, with our warm blood and our guns open our own road; brave, forward, struggle on—we are the Young Vanguards of the workers and peasants, we are the Young Vanguards of the workers and peasants!" I remember that on Women's Day I even changed the words of "Down with the powers, wipe out the warlords" in "The National Revolutionary Song" into: "The eighth of March has come, the eighth of March has come, let us sing together, let us sing together, long live women's liberation, long live women's liberation, let us sing together, let us sing together!" These words were widely sung among the women's ranks. In those days at Wuhan, mass activities great and small were held almost every day, revolutionary songs resounded through the skies over the three towns, and it was truly a new scene of seething enthusiasm.

Echoing the vigorous revolutionary mass movement at Wuhan, on 21 March 1927, when the Northern Expedition army had pushed from Zhejiang to the outskirts of Shanghai, the Shanghai workers, under the leadership of a special committee made up of the Communists Chen Duxiu, Zhou Enlai, Luo Yinong, Zhao Shiyan, and others, launched a general strike, which immediately turned into the Third Armed Uprising of the Shanghai workers, routing the Northern Warlords' garrison in Shanghai and occupying all of the city except the concessions. This was a great feat of the Chinese working class in the period of the great revolution.

As the Northern Expedition sent back victory after victory and the mass movement rose like wind and cloud, the swiftly developing victorious situation of the great revolution caused violent alarm among the reactionary forces at home and abroad, who soon gathered around Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang Kai-shek's anti-Communist face was ever more openly exposed: he openly suppressed the worker and peasant revolutionary forces, directed the army and gangs of ruffians to lure and murder Chen Zanxian, deputy chairman of the Jiangxi Provincial General Labor Union and chairman of the Ganzhou General Labor Union, and smashed the left-dominated Kuomintang municipal headquarters and general labor union of Jiujiang and the provincial party headquarters, general labor union, and peasant association of Anhui, killing and wounding many. Guo Moruo, deputy head of the General Political Department of the National Revolutionary Army, who had gone out on the eastern campaign with the army and had learned the truth of these atrocities at first hand at Jiujiang, Anqing, and elsewhere, wrote on 9 April a long essay titled "Look at Chiang Kai-shek Today," powerfully exposing the counter-revolutionary true face of Chiang Kai-shek in betraying Mr. Sun Yat-sen's three great policies and suppressing the workers' movement. Guo Moruo sent an orderly to carry the essay overnight to Wuhan. When the essay arrived, Xiyan was in the hospital with a fever, so it was I who received it, and I immediately presented it to the Central Military Commission. After reading it, the Central Military Commission felt that under the circumstances of the time the essay could not yet be published openly; but to guard against a military threat to Wuhan, the seat of the provisional Central, from Chiang Kai-shek with his heavy forces in hand, it decided to pull back the Northern Expedition units commanded by He Long and Ye Ting from the Henan front as a precaution.

By this time the Northern Expedition army had, in the middle and upper reaches of the Yangzi, defeated the main force of the Zhili-clique warlord Wu Peifu, and in the region of Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, and Jiangxi had routed the "Allied Armies of Five Provinces" of the Anhui-clique warlord Sun Chuanfang, so that the collapse of the Northern Warlords was a foregone conclusion. But Chiang Kai-shek, who had the support of the imperialist powers and the financial backing of the great bourgeoisie of Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Shanghai, had by now grown strong of wing and was resolved to seize the fruits of the Northern Expedition's victory. On 12 April he suddenly launched a counter-revolutionary coup in Shanghai, and under the slogan of "purging the party" against the Communists bloodily suppressed and massacred Communists, worker pickets, and revolutionary masses. The right-wing forces of Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, and other provinces followed suit, in the name of "party purge" hunting down and killing Communists and revolutionary masses on a great scale. The people everywhere angrily denounced the treachery of the Chiang Kai-shek clique, and the Kuomintang Central at Wuhan, on 17 April, also issued an order expelling Chiang from the party and dismissing him from all his posts. But Chen Duxiu, then general secretary of the Chinese Communist Party Central, was still deluded by the Kuomintang leader Wang Jingwei, who posed as a "leftist": on the one hand he continued to echo the decision of the Wuhan National Government headed by Wang Jingwei to push the Northern Expedition against the Fengtian clique (the Fengtian warlords headed by Zhang Zuolin) and opposed an eastern campaign to punish Chiang; on the other hand he followed at the heels of the right-wing forces, accusing the worker and peasant mass movement led by the Communist Party of "going too far," and further opposed developing a revolutionary army directly led by the Communist Party, saying such things as "there is no need at all now to build up armed force" and "if Communists take hold of an army they too will turn into warlords," and even ordering the short wooden clubs carried by the boy scouts to be confiscated as well.

At this critical juncture of a sudden turn in the domestic political situation, in the fourth and fifth months the Party Central convened at Wuhan the Fifth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party. Although the congress criticized Chen Duxiu's Right-opportunist errors, it could produce no way whatever of correcting them, and still elected Chen Duxiu general secretary of the Party Central; it decided to keep on holding to Wang Jingwei and to work upon the forces commanded by Tang Shengzhi and Zhang Fakui (who at the time were very hesitant and wavering)—who had cooperated with our Party and fought bravely in the Northern Expedition—to oppose Chiang together. Although the work of winning over those senior generals in the National Government who were against Chiang (Tang Shengzhi and Zhang Fakui) did not succeed, it still left some political influence, so that at the time of the second Kuomintang–Communist cooperation they were all fairly active. In particular, because Comrade Zhou Enlai had served as head of the Political Department of the Whampoa Military Academy, some of the Whampoa cadets among the senior Kuomintang generals always held him in great reverence.

At the commemoration of International Labor Day on 1 May 1927, an international workers' delegation led by the British worker Thomas was invited to visit China for the first time. The provisional Kuomintang–Communist Central at Wuhan borrowed the Puhaichun Western Restaurant in Hankou and held a banquet to receive and welcome them. Presiding over the banquet was Eugene Chen, foreign minister of the National Revolutionary Government. Song Qingling gave the welcoming address at the meeting, and the whole hall responded with warm applause. Deng Yanda and Guo Moruo, head and deputy head of the General Political Department of the Northern Expedition army; Liu Shaoqi and his wife and Li Lisan and his wife of the All-China General Labor Union; the Soviet adviser Borodin and his wife; and the responsible persons of every circle of society—more than a hundred people in all—attended the grand gathering. Zhao Wanhua and I were in charge of arranging the venue and of the reception. As we were not good at foreign languages, the interpreting was done by Comrade Liu Shaowen. After the grand gathering, Li Fang very warmly invited Guo Moruo and several friends to his home for a late supper and a long talk. It occurred to Xiyan that Comrade Dong Biwu had once charged us to press on with the work of urging Guo Moruo to join the Chinese Communist Party, and to convey to him that the Chinese Communist Party Central set great store by and highly valued the essay "Look at Chiang Kai-shek Today" that he had written, and that the reason it had not been published openly was that Chen Duxiu still cherished illusions about Wang Jingwei, who posed as a "leftist," and had issued a joint statement with Wang, so that to publish the essay might frighten Wang Jingwei off; and that now the Chinese Communist Party Central was carrying on a criticism of Chen Duxiu's Right errors... So we decided to use this occasion of being together for an individual talk with Guo. Xiyan and I both greatly admired his talent, and there was a deep friendship between us. At that time Guo Moruo had come back to Wuhan from the eastern campaign of the Northern Expedition out of opposition to Chiang, and often drowned his sorrows in wine, mocking the doings of the Wuhan National Government as it wavered daily and turned to the right, and jokingly calling Xiyan and me "cross-party elements"—which shows how deeply we knew one another, past all reserve of speech or laughter. Hearing us convey Comrade Dong Biwu's concern for him, Guo Moruo was greatly moved and asked us to convey his gratitude to Elder Dong on his behalf. And so we all raised our cups together and chanted aloud Lin Zexu's famous lines: "If it profits the country, I will give my life; how should I, for fortune or misfortune, flee or seek it?"

International Labor Day, 1 May 1927, was the very time when Xia Douyin at Yichang in Hubei and Xu Kexiang at Changsha in Hunan were stirring restlessly in concert with Chiang Kai-shek, three dark reactionary armed forces plotting to converge and attack Wuhan. It was truly "the mountain rain about to come, and the wind filling the tower"; everyone had a foreboding, and thinking that having met and gathered together today we would soon have to part in haste, there was a strange flavor in our hearts. Moruo loved wine and drank especially much, and, seizing the chance, he even interviewed Zhao Wanhua and Zhang Duhe, earnestly asking how they had come to give themselves to the revolution. "When wine meets a bosom friend, a thousand cups are too few," and with one accord we all felt a hidden worry for the future of the great revolution. Guo's resolve to join the Party being firm, he submitted his application to the Party Central when he took part in the August First Nanchang Uprising. Since ancient times, "a thousand pieces of gold are easy to get, but one good general is hard to find." Comrade Enlai was then in charge of the Nanchang Uprising, and it was right there on the firing line that, on behalf of the Central, he received into the Party two great talents, one civil and one military—the civil Guo Moruo and the military He Long. After the Nanchang Uprising, some of the comrades went up to the Jinggang Mountains, while those scattered in various places carried on the struggle.

On 13 May the Northern Expedition unit of Xia Douyin, formerly stationed at Yichang, mutinied against the Communists; on 21 May Xu Kexiang launched a counter-revolutionary coup at Changsha. Under these circumstances Chen Duxiu still carried on his policy of compromise and retreat, canceling the plan for an armed uprising in Hunan and even disbanding the worker pickets of Wuhan. This further fed the arrogance of the reactionary forces; the Kuomintang Central and the National Government at Wuhan turned more and more to the right, and a counter-revolutionary coup might break out at any moment. By this time discontent within the Party at Chen Duxiu's Right-capitulationist errors was growing ever stronger; preparations began for armed resistance, and the Party and League members who often appeared openly in Wuhan were gradually withdrawn into hiding or moved elsewhere. Xiyan too told me to let my short hair grow out.

Before long we received, conveyed by Chen Tanqiu, the opinion of the Party organization: that we husband and wife were to leave Wuhan for Jiangxi on the night of 14 July on the last steamer.

In accordance with the Party organization's instruction, in mid-June Xiyan and I published a notice in the Wuhan newspapers resigning all our posts, both principal and concurrent. Xiyan had long since handed over his work at the *Republican Daily* to Comrade Shen Yanbing (Mao Dun), and now he also resigned his work at the Central Military Commission, which nominally belonged to the National Government. I handed over the work of the women's department to Comrade Liu Qingyang and resigned all my other concurrent posts.

Before we left, Xiyan and I, on Comrade Dong Biwu's orders, delivered a check for three thousand yuan to Comrade Mao Dun and conveyed Elder Dong's instruction that, the situation being about to change, Comrade Mao Dun should take this check to the Shaodun Company (Cai Shuhou) in Shanghai to cash it, and should "hide himself in his writing, take good care of himself; we shall meet again." At the time we did not know that Cai Shuhou was the liaison officer of the Third International in China.

In mid-July the Chinese Communist Party Central formed a provisional Central Standing Committee of five, including Zhou Enlai, and issued a manifesto strongly condemning the Wuhan Kuomintang Central and National Government headed by Wang Jingwei for "already openly preparing a coup in recent days," and decided to withdraw the Communists who had joined the National Government.

Late on the night of 14 July, after my younger brothers and sisters were all fast asleep, we said goodbye to my parents and, amid the perilous winds and clouds of the great revolution's ebb, began the life of professional revolutionaries. To leave the fountainhead where I had joined the Party and given myself to revolution, to leave my loving parents and my lively, lovable brothers and sisters, to leave the comrades and friends with whom I had fought side by side—the sorrow of parting was hard to hold back. But I still held back my tears and forced a cheerful face, comforting my parents: "We will surely come back—please set your minds at rest, dear Father and Mother!" Then I resolutely boarded the boat and left Wuhan, where "the mountain rain was about to come and the wind filled the tower."

To keep the secret for us, my parents told others that I "had gone abroad to study." In the early summer of 1927 the Wuhan *Republican Daily* had published a special interview with my father, printing his photograph and greatly praising his writings and his contributions to the railways. Fortunately my father was on good terms with people in every quarter, so after we left, in a Wuhan shrouded in white terror, he was, remarkably, not too greatly implicated—he was only demoted, which forced him soon to leave the Canton–Hankou Railway Bureau.

On 15 July Wang Jingwei openly declared at Wuhan a formal break with the Communist Party, and the vigorous great revolution of the Northern Expedition at last miscarried.
