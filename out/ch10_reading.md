## The Sutasuta Monk

The whole party was downstairs at the inn, drinking.

Hijikata Toshizō, on coming into their room, screwed up his face at the reek of unrefined sake. Harada Sanosuke, for one, lay on his side with a five-gō flask balanced on his drum of a belly, lifting his head snake-wise now and then to sip from his cup.

"Harada—is that a warrior's bearing? Sit up,"

{j} he said, with a cold eye. To Toshizō nothing was so distasteful as a man in his cups. Kondō and Hijikata, be it said, were neither of them drinkers; but of the two abstainers Kondō liked a drinking-party and had a fellow-feeling for topers, whereas to Hijikata this smell, like something rotting in the gut, was past bearing. He came to it a little only after going up to Kyoto: when he first drank the fine sake of the imperial city—so this was the liquid they called sake, he thought—and softened somewhat the hatred he had until then borne this fluid.

"You wanted me?"

{j} said Harada.

"I've a word for you all,"

{j} and Toshizō briefly explained that their design was already known at the Hiruma hall, and that the ways about this inn and the key points of the post-town were already held fast by Kōgen Ittō-ryū men.

"Then what do we do?"

"We run."

"I won't have it."

"You're drunk. Hold your tongue."

***

About that time, at the Kōgen Ittō-ryū Hiruma hall in Senninmachi of the Hachiōji station, they had all but mustered every pupil in the neighborhood.

A motley company—farmers, gamblers, Hachiōji Thousand Guardsmen—thirty or forty strong, perhaps. Each carried a wooden sword, a padded practice-spear, or the like, and some had chain-mail undershirts on. Should any complaint come from the intendant's office, they had their excuse ready: it was a field-bout with the Tennen Rishin-ryū. The wit of the deputy master, Shichiri Kennosuke, needless to say.

The hall's own master, Hiruma Hanzō, being a mild man, left the whole command to Shichiri.

The mistress, Osen, had taken to her bed on the plea of a cold. She felt more dead than alive. She had sold the "medicine-man." Thinking she had informed so cleverly, she had let neither husband nor Shichiri guess that she and this peddler had a history from her girlhood. A woman's cunning is a gift of heaven for her own protection. But though her clever plot had gone off well, she, the writer of the piece, had no wish to watch the play itself as it now went forward on the stage.

Shichiri Kennosuke split his men into two bodies. Whichever way the foe should come at them, he meant to seize the chance and break their arms and legs so thoroughly that they would be of no use again as long as they lived.

In that, Shichiri was of a kind. His morbid love of a fight was Toshizō's very likeness. When Shichiri put on his fencing gear and set about the disposition of his men, the very color of his eyes would change.

One body Shichiri stationed at the hall in Senninmachi to guard the master, Hiruma Hanzō. This was the main force, twenty strong.

The other body,

{j} in the Myōjin wood,

{j} he set in ambush.

With this, the five of the Tennen Rishin-ryū, retreat or advance, would be rats in a bag.

—I say it again: there must be no affray on the streets of the post-town. In Jōshū such a thing once happened, and they came near to a decree shutting down all swordsmanship in the district. At all costs draw the foe into the hall, or into the Myōjin wood, and cut them down there.

{j} Shichiri warned his pupils.

"—Surely not,"

{j} said Toshizō, in the inner downstairs room of the Edoya.

"The Hiruma lot won't start anything on the busy streets of the Hachiōji station. Most likely their chance is when we slip out past the edge of the post-town. In short, the danger is after we've crossed the Asa River bridge. A little past the crossing there's a copse on the left; the folk here call it the Myōjin wood. If I were Shichiri Kennosuke, that's where I'd want to bury my men. There's the danger."

"And so?"

{j} said Harada Sanosuke.

"What do we do?"

"I'll tell you now."

{j} Toshizō ran a glaring eye round the company,

{j} "Sōji,"

{j} he said.

"you three—you and Tōdō Heisuke and Nagakura Shinpachi—go on ahead. You three are the dark party. Carry no lanterns."

"Ah—the festival brawl, is it,"

{j} said Okita Sōji—quick on the uptake. In the country outside Hino station, where Toshizō was born, there had long been just such a way of fighting a brawl.

This Three Tama country, ever since Ieyasu's entry into the Kantō, had been made shogunal land, a farming region to feed the great population of Edo; but before that, the farmers hereabouts, when it came to war, had put on armor and shown all the fearsome mettle of the "Bandō warriors," famed for their hardiness ever since the Genpei wars.

Toshizō's own house of Hijikata, come down now to being headmen of farmers, had in the far-off Genpei days sent out a Minamoto warrior, one Hijikata no Jirō (so the Azuma Kagami), and in the Warring States age was one of the "Ten Horsemen of Tama" houses (so the New Gazetteer): Hijikata of Echigo, and of the same house Zenshirō, Heizaemon, and Yahachirō, served the Odawara Hōjō as commanders of their military-farm colonists—as their retainers—and made their valor felt among the neighbors.

All this Three Tama country, being the descendants of such Genpei and Warring-States warriors, was rough in temper, and, farmers though they were, had handed down their forefathers' way of brawling and their tactics of skirmish. The tactics of the later Shinsengumi under Hijikata Toshizō's direction, and their manner of fighting in the Aizu War and the Hakodate War, sprang from the native fighting-lore of the Three Tama.

What Okita Sōji had called

{j} —ah, the festival brawl—

{j} was one such tactic.

"Master Hijikata,"

{j} said Harada Sanosuke, sounding put out,

{j} "my name seems to be missing."

"You're with me. That is, in the festival-brawl tactic, you and I are the lantern party."

"Meaning?"

"Well—just do as I teach you. It's a neat turn, and a merry one."

***

The Hiruma-hall men watching around the Edoya were seven.

These men had but a single task.

—When they come out, report which way.

{j} That alone was Shichiri Kennosuke's order to them. Would Toshizō's party make for Senninmachi and the hall, or run east along the highway and go home to Edo?

(Which will it be?)

{j} so every one of them kept his eyes bright—from the inn's shrubbery, the shadow of the rain-barrel, the earthen floor of the inn across the way—until, after the bell of the Hour of the Dog had sounded, a wind rose. Just then three samurai came out, each in a plaited sedge hat.

Okita, Tōdō, Nagakura, the three. This was Toshizō's dark party, and they carried no lanterns.

—They're out.

{j} the watchers stirred.

The night sky was splendidly clear, the stars crowding and glittering. The three sedge-hats, the moment they left the inn, set off toward Edo. But at once the black shapes of the samurai were lost in the dark of the highway.

—East along the Kōshū Highway, for Edo. Run to Senninmachi at once and tell Master Shichiri so.

{j} someone gave the order, and as the messenger started off along under the eaves, a weird thing came out of the Edoya.

A great monk.

That he wore a bald-pate wig was more than the dark would let them see; but he had a rope headband bound about his head, a sacred straw rope wound at his waist, a straw mat slung slantwise across his back, and a great riding-lantern thrust into his belt.

"Come now, come, good folk, pious men and women all,"

{j} and singing, and dancing too, he set off walking.

This was a drinking-party turn that Harada Sanosuke had learned back in the days when he lay idle in the footmen's quarters of a certain senior retainer of the Matsuyama domain in Iyo (Ehime prefecture).

Even now, in his cups, he would bring out this party-piece; and among the sharper-tongued there were some who,

(Harada says he picked it up in the footmen's quarters, but who knows—maybe that was his real trade all along,)

{j} would say it with a straight face. So thoroughly at home was Sanosuke in the turn.

In his palm was a simple instrument that went clack-clack. Instrument, though—it was but two bamboo clappers, which he pinched and loosed between his fingers as,

{j} "Sutasuta, sutasuta, when the Sutasuta Monk comes by, ..."

{j} he sang.

—What's that, then?

—A Sutasuta Monk.

{j} said one of them.

In the old days beggar-monks of this sort had walked the provinces, but at some point they had died out. Of late, though, they had come welling up again along the highways. This too, perhaps, was a sign of a world grown uneasy with the expel-the-barbarian clamor.

"Sutasuta, sutasuta, when the Sutasuta Monk comes by, at his waist the seven-and-nine sacred rope is strung, on his head a hoop set fast; Dainichi, monk-by-proxy, pilgrim-by-proxy, the penance-and-austerity Sutasuta Monk, who cries 'sutasuta' and finds his peace,"

{j} and Harada Sanosuke, dancing, set off toward Edo. In the straw mat on his back slept the man's own pride, a Hizen-forged Fujiwara Yoshihiro, two shaku four sun.

Behind him walked Toshizō—a plain, crestless riding-lantern likewise at his belt, his face muffled in the dark-blue cloth, in his peddler's dress.

The two crossed the Asa River bridge.

Cross it, and the Hachiōji station ends. Beyond, under the starry sky, the black-earthed Kōshū Highway ran on and on eastward, cutting through the grass and woods of the Musashino plain.

Presently they drew near the Myōjin wood.

The god enshrined in this wood was the tutelary deity of Mount Hiei, which lies on the border of Yamashiro and Ōmi—the Hie Myōjin. In all likelihood, in some far age before the Warring States, there had been a temple-estate of Hiei's Enryaku-ji hereabouts, and to guard that estate the deity had been summoned all the way out to the Bandō country.

The shrine was wrapped in a copse. Zelkova branches grew thick, roofing the highway over, shutting out the starlight, so that below it looked like the mouth of a cave.

"Harada—sing out loud,"

{j} said Toshizō.

"Understood."

An owl was calling.

—Come now, come.

{j} Harada struck up.

—Good folk, pious men and women all. Sutasuta, sutasuta, the Sutasuta Monk's, ...

{j} and just as he got so far, twelve or thirteen men came out of the wood at the side and ringed the two of them round.

Thus far, all was within Toshizō's reckoning.

"Here—monk,"

{j} said one, thrusting a lantern at him,

{j} "where are you bound?"

"Is this a barrier-gate, then?"

{j} said Harada. It was a fighting tone. Toshizō went cold. Here he wanted to come meek, and if possible pass without starting anything. (Have I picked the wrong player for this?) he thought.

"I'll do the asking,"

{j} said Harada Sanosuke, in a deep, rusted voice,

{j} "I hear this country hereabouts is an exclave under the Nirayama intendant of Izu. Has the intendant changed, then, and a barrier-gate gone up on the emperor's open road? Or if you are a pack banded together to throttle the highway at your pleasure and squeeze toll-money out of it, the crime is gibbeting to the ninth degree of your kin—the foremost villainy under heaven. Think well before you answer."

"What's that you're blithering,"

{j} the man flinched; but a knot of them who had come up close to Toshizō,

{j} "and you—are you this begging-monk's man?"

{j} and as they thrust a lantern at him, one of them cried out.

"It's him—the medicine-man!—"

"Where, where—"

Two or three thrust their lanterns at Toshizō's face and began to look him over, licking him with their eyes.

"Here, medicine-man—take off that face-cloth."

It was to see the fencing-mask chafe-marks.

"Aye, sir,"

{j} and Toshizō bent a little at the waist, tucked the straw mat he carried under his left arm, and, making as if to loose the knot at his chin, of a sudden gripped the hilt of the sword within the mat and dropped smoothly into a low stance.

"Hah—what are you—"

As the man sprang back, Toshizō's sword leapt up and, in a drawing cut, sliced the underside of his wrist. An arm, still gripping its lantern, flew clean off.

At that the Sutasuta Monk, Harada, stepped in too and with a shout swept his sword level in a scything blow.

They all leapt back in a scramble.

"Back, get back,"

{j} the leader cried in a fluster,

{j} "widen the ring. There's but two of them."

What was more, Toshizō and Harada had huge lanterns dangling at their belts, as though begging to be cut down by the mark of them.

"Harada—don't strike yet."

"Why not?"

"We wait."

Toshizō was calm. The Kōgen Ittō-ryū men were walking, step by step, into his trap.

This tactic was the very one that, years later in the Aizu War, would sorely plague the imperial army of Satsuma, Chōshū, and Tosa among the mountains.

The truth was that the three of the dark party—Okita, Tōdō, Nagakura—were stealing softly up: Okita from within the copse, Tōdō from the midst of the paddy fields, Nagakura from east along the road.

When the young men of the villages hereabouts fell to fighting men of another village, on a festival night or the like, they mostly went about it in this fashion.

From their several places the three rose up.

"Wah!"

{j} was what they did not cry—it would betray their numbers.

In silence, working hands and feet with a single purpose, from behind, with their wooden swords, they cracked the backs of heads as fast as ever they could.

Tōdō felled three.

Nagakura Shinpachi, striking right cheek and left by turns, laid six men senseless in the twinkling of an eye; and Okita Sōji, wielding a live blade, leapt into the throng and, cutting down their lanterns, made the dark one lantern at a time.

In that confusion Toshizō and Harada Sanosuke cut in from the front, aiming for wrists alone, hewing about them at whatever came to hand.

The Hiruma force broke westward in a rush. A silent surprise-attack out of the dark, and from behind at that, deceives men into thinking the numbers far greater than they are.

"Fall back—"

{j} the Hiruma leader bawled.

They scattered every way and fled; but Toshizō and his men, at the same moment, took to their heels eastward as if on wings. A brawl is a matter of the moment. Dawdle, and the men from Senninmachi would come running, sure as anything.

After that, a month or so went by.

One day, Kondō, home from a teaching round out in the Tama country while the sun was still high, was rinsing his feet at the well behind the hall when,

{j} "Is Toshi about somewhere?"

{j} he called loudly.

Toshizō came out of the hall and stood heavily at his side.

"What is it?"

{j} said Toshizō. Kondō was washing between his toes.

"Over at Satō's place in Hino I heard a rumor come down from Hachiōji."

"What rumor?"

{j} Toshizō was on his guard. He thought the affair might have reached Kondō's ears.

"The word is the Hiruma hall at Hachiōji is shutting its doors for a while. Have you heard?"

"Can't say I have."

"Now that's rich. A man they call sharp-eared Toshizō—"

{j} Kondō laughed, his mouth wide open,

{j} "duller than one would think."

"Dull indeed. But why in the world shut a hall that was, for a time, so full of life?"

"The pupils were of too poor a stamp, they say. Down to the last master that hall had jogged quietly along with none but the Hachiōji Thousand Guardsmen for pupils; but in the present master's time, taking on as deputy some shady fellow drifted down from Jōshū—this Shichiri Kennosuke—broke the hall's good standing. Shichiri, on the plea of keeping the hall solvent, gathered gamblers from all about Hachiōji and off toward Kōshū and set them to fencing, so that in and around the hall there was no end of brawling and bloodshed among the lot. At last Hara Sanzaemon, one of the Hachiōji Thousand-Guard captains, stepped in between, and it was settled to reform the hall's ways."

"And Shichiri?"

"Turned out, I hear."

"Hm?"

{j} A complicated look crossed Toshizō's face.

"By all accounts,"

{j} said Kondō, wiping his feet with a cloth,

{j} "he took two or three of the hall's men and went up to Kyoto. Going about trumpeting that the warrior's future lies in Kyoto, it seems. No doubt he means to turn one of these fashionable expel-the-barbarian rōnin, hoist some court noble on his shoulders like a festival palanquin, and make trouble for the shogunate."

(The warrior's future lies in Kyoto, is it.)

{j} Toshizō fell to thinking.

(The warrior—Kyoto. ...)

But at this moment he had no settled notion in particular.

It was not until the autumn of the next year that this notion suddenly became reality.
