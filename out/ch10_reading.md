## Chapter Ten

The old-calendar Dragon-Boat Festival passed at last amid a general foreboding. The merchants' customary first small settling of accounts for the year had to be rolled forward into the coming "Mid-Autumn": the war had thrown the ordinary courses of life out of their groove.

"To Beiping, to eat moon-cakes there!"—so the military and political authorities too foretold that the fighting would be over by the coming Mid-Autumn at the very latest.

But of that ending there was as yet no sign. On the Longhai line nothing much had come of it; the fronts of the two armies, it was said, stood about where they had stood when the guns first opened. Upriver, though, toward Wuhan, things grew tighter by the day. The allied Zhang-Gui armies had burst suddenly into Changsha!—and that on the second day after the old-calendar Dragon-Boat Festival, the fourth of June by the Western calendar. At once a tremor ran through the Shanghai government-bond market. Rumours came flying in from every side. The speculators of the Chinese Securities Exchange were at once the credulous believers of rumour, and its makers, and its carriers; the whole quarter of San Malu was thick with the air of war, until even the powder-scent and sweat-reek of Zhaojinli close by seemed to take on a whiff of gunpowder.

Then came another piece of terrifying news: the Communist Red Army under Peng Dehuai had occupied Yuezhou!

Li Yuting, who had had the alarm confirmed for him from the Japanese side, felt on the instant a chill run half through his body. He sat dazed a moment, then took off those near-sighted spectacles of his—thick as the bottoms of glass wine-bottles—and wiped and wiped them with his handkerchief, and at last made up his mind to go to Wu Sunfu and offer his earnest counsel one more time. Ever since that day of "May Thirtieth" he had taken good care not to entangle himself again in the quarrel between Wu Sunfu and the rest; yet whenever a chance fell out conveniently, he was always minded to play the peacemaker. He had privately egged Du Zhuzhai on to "put the greater right above kinship," urging Zhuzhai to bring a little pressure to bear on Wu Sunfu, so that a settlement between Wu and Zhao might have some chance of coming about. Wu Sunfu's headstrong self-assurance, he would say, was the root of the disaster.

Hurrying now to the Wu mansion, Li Yuting arrived just as there were guests. In the great parlour stood several men, all holding their breath and standing deferentially aside, watching for Wu Sunfu's every smile and frown. Li Yuting hardly knew these people; only one of them, a little-moustached man of about fifty, he seemed to recall having seen before.

Wu Sunfu stood facing outward, and the colour of his face was not what it commonly was. The instant he caught sight of Li Yuting he beckoned and called:

"Yuting—please, go and sit a while in the small parlour. Forgive me."

In the small parlour there was already a man before him: the lawyer, Qiu Sun. A very large briefcase lay open across his knees, and this Lawyer Qiu sat lost in a brown study, one hand pinching the corner of a sheaf of papers, the other stroking his chin. Li Yuting sat down softly, and did not rouse the lawyer from his musings; but in his heart he asked himself over and again: strangers out yonder, and here the legal adviser—the Third Master had some weighty affair in hand today...

In the great parlour Wu Sunfu paced a few steps like a caged lion, his ferocious glare falling now and again on the face of the little-moustached man of about fifty, and sweeping too across the other three who stood stiffly by. Suddenly Wu Sunfu halted, gave a light snort through his nose, and asked the little-moustache, as though he could not believe it:

"Xiaosheng—you say it is the provincial government's order that the Hongchang pawnshop as well is to keep on trading?"

"It is! And the Tongyuan native bank, the oil-press, the electric plant, the rice-mill—none of them are to be allowed to close. The county's commissioner said to me: the whole market of the town leans on the Third Master's mills and shops; if the Third Master shuts them all down, the market of the town will fall into utter ruin!"

The little-moustached Fei answered with his eyes on the ground. He too, in his heart, hoped those mills and shops would not close—but not for the sake of any "market of the town": for the sake of himself. For well though he knew that, should Wu Sunfu wind up every concern in the town, he would still see to it that Fei Xiaosheng had a bowl of rice—transferred, say, to the Shanghai mill—yet that would be a far cry from the ease and consequence of being steward of the Wu house in the town; and besides, he had boasted roundly before the county commissioner that he could turn "the Third Master" from his purpose.

"Hah! So they too go on about the market of the town, and how it will fare! Can they protect the market, then?"

Wu Sunfu said it with a cold, savage sneer. Yet to hear his townsfolk exalt him as the chief of every trade pleased him a little. The little-moustached Fei, marking how the land lay, hastened to put in:

"The town is very quiet now, very quiet. The newly-posted battalion is a very different thing from Commander He's before it."

"I doubt it! Not a bare li from the edge of the town, and it is the Communist bandits' world. They are lodged all through the country round, and their sentries are pushed up to the western edge of the market. Shuangqiao town may be quiet, but it is ringed round! The one battalion in the town is barely enough to hold that one vital road to the county seat. And I hear, besides, that the soldiers' sentries are forever shouldering their rifles and slipping off. The Communist bandits are twice the number, and twice the guns, that they were!"

Suddenly a man broke in—this was Wu Sunfu's distant nephew, Wu Weicheng, a man of thirty-odd who had come this time along with the little-moustached Fei.

"And they say there is even some sort of Soviet in the countryside now!"

A young man of twenty-odd beside Wu Weicheng added his word too. This was Ma Jingshan, brother-in-law to that Zeng Jiaju who had been lodging at the Wu mansion for the better part of a fortnight; he too had been brought out this time by the little-moustached Fei. Close at his shoulder stood Zeng Jiaju himself, at this moment staring wide-eyed and blank.

Wu Sunfu's face changed of a sudden. He turned and gave Wu Weicheng and the others a look, and gave a single nod. But the little-moustached Fei, watching, felt his heart thump; it seemed to him that this one nod of Wu Sunfu's was worse than any storm of abuse. Hastily he cleared himself:

"Just so, just so—that too there is.—But the province is even now moving troops to hem them in and stamp them out; the town will make no more trouble."

Wu Weicheng gave a cold laugh and was on the point of saying more, when suddenly the blare of a motor-horn came crying in from beyond the front gate; and then, seeing Wu Sunfu wave a hand impatiently, he broke off. Wu Sunfu paced out to the stone steps beyond the great-parlour door and stood gazing. The westering sunlight threw the shadows of a few trees across the steps, and when the wind stirred, a pattern of black and white danced up and down the five stone stairs. Wu Sunfu bent his head and glanced at it, and stamped his foot in vexation.

A motor-car drew up on the asphalt drive within the garden, and the manservant Gaosheng darted forward to open its door. Du Zhuzhai scrambled hurriedly out of the carriage, looked up at Wu Sunfu standing there upon the steps, and knit his brows and shook his head. It was a grave signal. Wu Sunfu's face turned the colour of purple bean-paste, yet he forced a smile.

"It is the strangest thing! It all but hit the limit-up!"

Du Zhuzhai came up the stone steps, panting as he spoke, mopping and mopping at his face with a snow-white ramie handkerchief.

Wu Sunfu only knit his brows and smiled, and said not a word. He gave Du Zhuzhai a look, then turned and went back into the parlour, and of a sudden let his face fall and said to the little-moustached Fei:

"Whether the town is quiet or not quiet, I will not hear of it! The mills, the shops—I set them up, and if I mean to wind them up, then wound up they shall be! I am no philanthropist; whether the town's market thrives or does not thrive is no concern of mine—and whether it is the province or the county that comes to talk to me, this is the whole of my answer!"

"Just so! And so I told them, over and over! And yet, they—Third Master!—"

Wu Sunfu, wrought to the last pitch of impatience, turned all at once to a savage sneer and cut the little-moustached Fei short:

"I know that set piece of theirs by heart! Xiaosheng, you have not yet reported to me how much of the money we put out was got in at this Dragon-Boat Festival. Did you not say last time that six-tenths of it was sure? By my reckoning it should be better than six-tenths! How much, in the end, was got in? Have you brought it all with you?"

"I have not. In the town too the Dragon-Boat accounts have been put off to the Mid-Autumn."

"Hmph! What talk is this!"

Wu Sunfu burst out in a sudden roar of anger. Here was another blow he had never for a moment looked for! The sum came in all to no more than seventy or eighty thousand, it was true; but just now, when he stood in the very want of ready cash, seventy or eighty thousand dollars could do a great deal! He darkened his face, paced a few steps, and looked at Du Zhuzhai, who sat in the sofa taking snuff. And then the news that the bonds had again all but hit the limit-up came bursting anew into Wu Sunfu's swollen head, and his heart grew dark within him.

Du Zhuzhai had drawn a full charge of snuff up both nostrils, and sat now with his eyes shut and his mouth wide, waiting to sneeze.

"If the Third Master winds up the shops at once, and the Tongyuan native bank with them, then even when the Mid-Autumn comes we shall not get our money back."

The little-moustached Fei took a step forward and said it in a low voice. Wu Sunfu shrugged his shoulders, and after a moment, as though he were spitting something out, gave a laugh and said:

"Ha! When the Mid-Autumn comes? By that time, perhaps, I shall not need to have that sum brought up to Shanghai at all!"

"Then—the Third Master fears, perhaps, that just now there is still danger in the town? What Brother Weicheng said a moment ago was a little overdrawn.—The province is truly drafting its best troops to hem them in. And now that both province and county beg the Third Master to have a care for the town's market—it is, after all, the Third Master's own native place; and besides, to close the shops and mills would hardly draw out any ready cash—the Third Master had better grant them this face, and leave it till after the Mid-Autumn. The Hongchang pawnshop is burnt down, and that is another matter."

The little-moustached Fei, seeing his moment come, brought out the idea he had thought out long before, his two eyes rolling ceaselessly.

Wu Sunfu gave a faint, noncommittal smile, and turned and sat down in a chair. He saw clearly now: the bandit-scourge of his native place had not only cost him fifty or sixty thousand, it had pinned down two more lots of fifty or sixty thousand, that he could not draw into his hand to use. He felt, a little, that the affairs of this world could not all fall out as a man would wish. But at the next turn of thought he set it down to this—that it was a matter of the countryside, far off, and of troops that were not wholly at his own command; whereas an enterprise under his own hand had always answered to his direction as he pleased. His Yizhong Trust Company was already going forward now on a well-laid plan; Chen Junyi's silk-weaving mill was about to pass into their hands, and many another small industry too would come to the Yizhong Company to be managed.

So thinking, Wu Sunfu gave the little-moustached Fei his orders in a brisk, decided tone:

"Xiaosheng, what you say is well enough; I must after all do some duty by my native place. Before the Mid-Autumn, saving only the Hongchang pawnshop, which cannot carry on, all the rest of the mills and shops I will keep going by my own strength. But you must strike a firm bargain with that battalion commander in the town: he is to keep his eye on the Communist bandits in the country round."

The little-moustached Fei answered "Yes" several times over, most respectfully, his eyes on the ground. But suddenly he asked again:

"Then the ten thousand and more of silver still lying with the Tongyuan bank—that too is to be left in the town—"

"Left there to turn over the funds of our own several shops. Lent out to other houses—that I will not allow!"

Wu Sunfu said it quickly, waved a hand at the little-moustached Fei, and rose and walked over to Du Zhuzhai. The little-moustached Fei answered "Yes" once more, and knowing his own business was done, was minded to go; but a glance of his eye fell on Wu Weicheng and Ma Jingshan, one on either side, penning in that wild-colt of a Zeng Jiaju, who still stood bolt upright against the wall by the window—and all at once he remembered another matter, and, before Wu Sunfu had begun to talk with Du Zhuzhai, went hurrying after him at his back and cried:

"Third Master! There is one thing more—"

Wu Sunfu turned his face and fixed the little-moustached Fei with a stare, knitting his brows most impatiently.

"It is only Brother Weicheng and Brother Jingshan, these two. They are minded to come and do work for the Third Master. Today they lodge with me at the hotel; tomorrow I go back to the town, and what these two are to do, I beg the Third Master to direct."

The little-moustached Fei said it softly, stealing meanwhile a signal to Wu Weicheng and the other with his eyes. But the two had not yet made a move, when over yonder Du Zhuzhai of a sudden let off a very loud sneeze that made the whole company jump.

"Everyone comes to Shanghai to look for a place—but those who had places in Shanghai to begin with have had their rice-bowls smashed as it is! The banks, the mills, the great companies, all of them, for the badness of the times, are cutting their staff and cutting their pay. Men dismissed by the thousand and the ten thousand are all at their wits' end for a way out. The postal service held an examination, for only sixty places, and there were more than a thousand who sat for it! Folk from inland do not know how it is; they only go on boring their way into Shanghai. I have seven or eight men on my own hands, waiting for places."

Du Zhuzhai, like a man just waked from sleep, rubbed his nose and said it slowly. Wu Sunfu did not open his mouth, only knit his brows and narrowed his eyes savagely, taking the measure of the two newcomers. Set beside Zeng Jiaju, these two seemed a little more presentable. Wu Weicheng's square face gave off an air of shrewd competence, and that Ma Jingshan too seemed no blockhead; both were a great cut above Zeng Jiaju. Perhaps these two were yet worth the making—so the thought stirred in Wu Sunfu's mind.

With a gesture bidding the two come over, Wu Sunfu asked them, in a few plain words, their schooling and their experience of work.

The little-moustached Fei, dancing attendance on Du Zhuzhai, said a few things to this "son-in-law master's" liking, then turned again and drew the dumb-standing Zeng Jiaju out beyond the parlour door and said softly:

"Your good lady bids me bring you word: you are to go home again at once!"

"Little Ma has told me so already. I will not go back. Long ago I asked my cousin Sunfu to find me a post."

"And has he found one? What work do you mean to do? By and by I can carry your lady an answer."

"That is not yet settled. I have a party certificate—I mean to go into some yamen or other and hold a post!"

The little-moustached Fei could not help laughing; this graceless Zeng the Second, he thought, would surely plague Wu Sunfu's head to aching.

Over in the small parlour, meanwhile, it was not so silent either. Lawyer Qiu had put the sheaf of documents in his hand back into the briefcase, lit a cigarette, gave a lazy stretch, and answered Li Yuting:

"You see, the affairs of this world are always the big worm eating the little worm! For all that, as you say, there are certain bankers who have joined hands with the Americans and mean to get the industry of China under their thumb—to turn those owners into so many head-men under their sway—yet mill-owners like Wu Sunfu and the rest are themselves swallowing up sundry smaller mills. Here in this briefcase of mine lies the fate of seven or eight little factories. Tomorrow or the next day I shall shoulder the style of the Yizhong Trust Company's plenipotentiary and treat with the owners of those little mills; I shall have them set their names to these contracts of mine, and their mills will pass into the Yizhong Company's management—which is to say, in plain fact, into the hands of Wu Sunji, or Sun Jiji, or else Wang Heji!—Yuting, I for one put no great faith in all this talk of American capital and its 'trusts.' I rather suspect it a rumour that Wu Sunfu and his people have got up on purpose, to dazzle men's eyes and ears! It is enough for America to ship her manufactures to China and sell them here; why should she come and set up mills in the hurly-burly of China?"

"It is nothing of the sort! Nothing of the sort at all! The clash between Old Zhao and Sunfu—I know it from first to last!"

Li Yuting said it with great assurance. Lawyer Qiu only smiled, drew in a hard pull of smoke, and lifted his eyes to the finely-carved grape-vine tracery on the whitewashed ceiling. Li Yuting followed the lawyer's glance and looked up a moment too, then looked again at the lawyer's face and asked in a low voice:

"Seven or eight little mills at a single stroke? Sunfu and his people have no small daring! And what sort of mills are they?"

"There is everything: a lamp-bulb works, a thermos works, a glass-works, a rubber-works, a parasol-works, a soap-works, a celluloid-works—none of them on any great scale."

"And all bought up cheap at a knock-down, I suppose?"

Li Yuting pressed the question quickly. But Lawyer Qiu would not answer. For all that Li Yuting too was an intimate of the Wu house, the lawyer held it a matter of course to keep his client's business secrets; he gave another easy smile, and turned the talk aside:

"It always takes the absence of civil strife for the mills to prosper."

Having said it, Lawyer Qiu tucked his briefcase under his arm and walked out of the small parlour, and pulled the door to behind him.

At the bang of the closing door Li Yuting felt his heart give a sudden jump. He looked at his watch: it was but five o'clock. He had, then, sat in this small parlour no more than ten minutes or so; yet already it had seemed to him a very long while. Now that he alone was left, sitting idle here as though awaiting a summons from his superior, he felt the more the tedium of it. He rose and looked at the kesi tapestry on the wall, "The Lady Ming Goes Out to the Frontier," then sidled to the window and looked out at the trees in the garden. The motor-car standing on the asphalt drive he knew for Du Zhuzhai's, and of a sudden he grew the more uneasy: strangers out there in the great parlour; the legal adviser here a moment ago, and now gone too; Du Zhuzhai's car standing in the garden—did not all of this prove that Wu Sunfu had some weighty affair on hand? And he, Li Yuting, having come by chance at an unlucky hour, was set to warm a cold bench here—was it not plain that his host had conceived some wariness of him? And yet Li Yuting asked himself: he was still the Li Yuting of before, and nothing was changed in him. It was no more than that, a few days since, he had eaten a supper at Zhao Botao's, and that time there had been no other guest, only he and Old Zhao, the two of them, and they had indeed said a good deal that touched on Wu Sunfu—no more than that!

Li Yuting felt a cold creeping down his spine. To be groundlessly suspected—he thought of it now with fear, and now with a sense of wrong. He could only lay the blame on his own over-eagerness, his over-anxiousness for the general good, his whole heart set on a peaceable compromise between those two "giants." It might well be that his kindly-meant words urging Du Zhuzhai to hold Wu Sunfu's wilful course in check had been carried by Du Zhuzhai to Sunfu after all! It might well be that they had already set him down for a mean fellow sowing discord between kinsmen! that they had set him down for Old Zhao's running-dog and spy, and were therefore so on their guard against him!

This small parlour had another, side door giving on the garden. Li Yuting had half a mind to slip quietly away and have done. But at a second thought he felt it would not do to leave without a word of farewell either. Suddenly a burst of laughter came from without. It was the laughter of the men in the great parlour! and it seemed to carry just this meaning: "Shut up in there, is he—a spy!" Li Yuting's heart went thump-thump, and his finger-tips were ice. Of a sudden he set his teeth and said within himself: "Since they suspect me for a spy, I will play the spy this once!" He went hurriedly to the door that led through to the great parlour, bent his back, and was on the very point of laying his ear to the keyhole to listen—when of a sudden his mind turned again: "What is the good of it? I treat myself as Old Zhao's running-dog, and Old Zhao may not treat me as one at all!" He drew in a sharp breath, straightened up, stepped back a pace, and sank limply into a chair. Just at that moment the door opened, and Wu Sunfu came in smiling, and behind him Du Zhuzhai, rubbing his nose with his right hand and holding the snuff-bottle in his left.

"Yuting, forgive me! A few people up from my native place, a trifling matter."

Wu Sunfu made his excuses, and smiled again. Du Zhuzhai put out a hand, by way of greeting, and then let off another great sneeze.

"Oh—oh—"

Li Yuting smiled with an effort and answered vaguely, twice over; but in his heart he only wanted to weep, for it seemed to him that Wu Sunfu's smile was like a sharp knife. He stole another look at Du Zhuzhai. Du Zhuzhai had the air of a man with much on his mind, twirling his snuff-bottle in the fingers of his left hand.

The three men sat down in a triangle, and talked idly of this and that; and Li Yuting, feeling that Wu Sunfu was after all in his old manner still, grew easy again, and by degrees took his stand once more upon his own ground: a whole heart given to the general good. So when Du Zhuzhai brought up how the inland was thick with bandits as fur is thick, Li Yuting looked into Wu Sunfu's face and said gravely:

"So the fall of Yuezhou was no rumour, but the truth after all!"

"The truth, is it? Why, that too was to be looked for! Changsha is a lone city, hard to hold; the Zhang-Gui armies would of course split off a force to take Yuezhou."

Wu Sunfu answered carelessly, and smiled again. Du Zhuzhai, over yonder, nodded. Li Yuting started, and could not keep back a cry:

"It was not the Zhang-Gui armies that took Yuezhou! It was the Communist Peng Dehuai's Red Army! Sunfu—can it be that you have had no word of this here?"

"A rumour! Fathered on the Communists on purpose!"

Sunfu answered lightly again, and rolled up his eyes to watch the parrot in its cage shelling a peanut.

Li Yuting followed Wu Sunfu's glance and looked at the parrot too, and was quite at a loss in his mind; yet his faith in the news from the Japanese side was exceedingly firm, and he concluded on the instant that Wu Sunfu had been hoodwinked by the propaganda of the other party. He turned his eyes on Du Zhuzhai and said, most stubbornly:

"It is the Red Army for certain! I fear the news Sunfu has had is put about with a purpose. They say that just as the Zhang-Gui armies were pressing on Changsha, the Communists too attacked Yuezhou. The two places fell at very near the same time! Sunfu, in fair truth, the Zhang-Gui armies' striking at Hunan this time is nothing less than making the Communists' opportunity for them. Is it not so, Zhuzhai—let them settle which is cock of the walk on the Longhai line, and let it go at that; why drag in the province of Hunan, where the Communists are thick on the ground?"

Du Zhuzhai nodded, but said nothing. Wu Sunfu still smiled, but his brows were a little knit. Li Yuting, seizing the chance, went on, his manner grown very eager:

"The whole situation now grows more and more tangled. North and south of the great river alike are the fires of war. The Communists of Jiangxi too are stirring there. On the Wuhan side the troops are thin; not sixty li from Hankou there are Communist guerrilla bands! About Shashi and Yichang, the motley levies and the Red Army have come to lodge like cat and mouse in one den—"

"Just so! A few days ago the Pacific line—Sun Jiren's shipping office—had a down-river steamer of theirs seized near Shashi, and to this hour there is no tracing where it is gone; nor does one know whether it was the motley troops that took it, or the Communist bandits!"

Wu Sunfu cut across Li Yuting's talk, rose most impatiently—but only stretched his legs, and sat down again.

"Jiren has run into a black-luck spell in good earnest! His long-distance motor-buses north of the river have been commandeered, and now a river-steamer on the upper Yangtze vanishes—a boat but newly built last year, they say, not six months in the water, and three hundred thousand taels to build!"

Du Zhuzhai took up the word, stroking his chin with his right hand. Yet for all that his mouth said this, and his ears too were on Li Yuting's talk, his mind was running on other things. The shifts of the bond market baffled him. The general situation in such disorder as this, and yet today the bonds had rallied instead—here was a riddle he could not read through. Just then Wu Sunfu rose again, paced a circle round the table in the parlour, and let his eyes slide, as if by chance and yet not by chance, again and again onto Li Yuting's face; but Li Yuting took no notice, and still thought to turn Wu Sunfu's mind to the peril of the general situation, and how all should pull together in accord. But he had no further chance to speak. A servant came to ask Wu Sunfu to the telephone: it was Zhu Yinqiu who was calling. At once Wu Sunfu's eyebrows gave a jump, and he exchanged a look with Du Zhuzhai, wearing an air of the utmost astonishment. Li Yuting saw it would not do to sit on longer, and so took his leave, his whole heart full of an unspeakable, wronged vexation.

Du Zhuzhai, a cigar between his teeth, weighing as he went why Zhu Yinqiu should be telephoning, walked on up the stairs. He knew the women guests were at their mahjong under the awning of the great balcony on the second floor, where the two daughters of the Wu house and the two Lin sisters had made up just one full table of four. Ah Xuan and Du Xintuo looked on from the side. The clack of the tiles came drowsy and desultory, as though it would fall asleep there.

When Mrs. Du saw her husband come in, she called out:

"Zhuzhai, come and take a hand for me!"

Du Zhuzhai smiled and shook his head, slowly took the cigar from his lips, sidled over to the mahjong table, glanced at it, and said:

"Are you tired, then? Have Xintuo take your place! What stakes are you playing for?"

"Papa can't be bothered playing such small stakes!"

Du Xintuo, taking his mother's part, made this soft little sally against his father, and at the same time flashed a glance across at Lin Peishan opposite.

"If the son-in-law master has the humour for it, let him take a hand; he is not like Sunfu, who says mahjong is a stifling pastime, and that if he must gamble, he loves the dice-bowl and the fantan-table!"

The Wu young mistress put in quickly, with a most gentle smile; yet in that smile there was a touch, too, of abstraction. Of late the Wu young mistress had been forever thus abstracted; only a moment since she had missed a "white dragon" she might have taken; in a bare six rounds she had already lost two stakes. This the others did not remark; only Du Xintuo, watching coldly, had seen it—though he too could not tell the reason of it.

Over there Mrs. Du had risen, and Du Xintuo filled the vacant place. He and Lin Peishan were now partners across the table. The Wu young mistress rose too, and catching hold of Ah Xuan beside her, said with a giggling laugh:

"Look at you and the Fourth Sister, two raw hands, going to win the money of those two old hands!"

Scarce had she laughed, when the Wu young mistress once more knit her brows close, gazed blankly a moment at the sky, and drifted lightly away.

Du Zhuzhai and his lady walked to the eastern end of the balcony, far from the mahjong table, and leaned on the stone balustrade with their faces to the outside. Behind them the four at the table were playing now with great spirit, Ah Xuan's voice and Lin Peishan's the loudest of all. Mrs. Du turned her head to look back a moment, then said suddenly in a low voice:

"There is a thing I must talk over with you. A while ago Peiyao told me, on the quiet, that our Ah Xin and their Peishan seem to be quite taken with each other; whenever Ah Xin comes here, he is always off out to play with Peishan!"

"Oh! Let them be. It is the common way of things nowadays."

"Aiya, aiya! See how muddle-headed you are! Have you forgotten the two are of the wrong generation to each other? Peishan is a whole generation the elder!"

Du Zhuzhai's brows drew tight. He reached a hand out over the rail, knocked the ash from his cigar, heaved a sigh, but said nothing. Mrs. Du glanced back at the mahjong table and went on:

"Peiyao is anxious over this too. Someone has asked for Peishan's horoscope-card. He looks, to her eyes, a very fitting match—"

"Which family? Is it Fan Bowen?"

"No. One surnamed Lei. Staff Officer Lei!"

"Oh, oh! Staff Officer Lei! But he is away fighting north of the river just now, and whether he is alive or dead there is no knowing."

"It is said he can be back before long—that too is Peiyao's word."

Du Zhuzhai's whole face wore a look of difficulty. He turned his head aside to glance at the two at their play, and after a while said slowly:

"They are kin, when all is said, and there is no great harm in their going about together a little. But the tone of things is too bad nowadays, and young people take the taint from what they see and hear—and besides, a son grown so big as that, one cannot keep his feet at home. Wife! You had far better not vex yourself with this care!"

"Tsk, tsk! If something were to come of it, it would look ill for the face of both houses!"

"Well—by your telling, what is to be done?"

"By my telling? Long ago I meant to make a match for our Old Sixth, and it was you who scorned the Lin family for having no money—"

"Enough, enough; Wife, do not rake up old scores. I will have a word with Ah Xin by and by. But the main hinge of this business lies with the girl after all. If the girl is firm in her own mind and stands steady, nothing can come of it."

"Her sister says she is a child still, and understands nothing—"

"Hmph!"

Du Zhuzhai shook his head as though he did not believe it, but said no more. Just then the Wu young mistress came up onto the balcony again, and seeing Du Zhuzhai and his wife standing together, seemed to see through it at once that it must be about that very matter, and sent them from afar a bewildered smile. She glanced in passing at the mahjong table, then came swaying over toward Du Zhuzhai and his wife, and was just about to open her mouth, when suddenly from the garden below the manservant Gaosheng bawled up:

"Son-in-law master! The master asks you to speak with him!"

Du Zhuzhai thereupon slipped away. The Wu young mistress, faintly knitting her brows, looked hard at Mrs. Du and asked:

"Second Sister, have you spoken of it?"

Mrs. Du smiled, by way of answer. Then the two, standing close, talked a while more in low tones, and the Wu young mistress broke into a clear laugh. They turned and walked over to the mahjong table to watch the four young people play.

Du Zhuzhai found Wu Sunfu in the study telephoning; from the sound of it, the other party was Tang Yunshan. What they spoke of was something Du Zhuzhai did not well follow—some "word getting out after the Hamburg lot was shipped." At the last, Wu Sunfu said, "Come round, then," and hung up the receiver.

Wu Sunfu, his whole face taut with excitement, sat down face to face with Du Zhuzhai, picked up the daily report-sheet of opening and closing prices for every bond on the exchange that the broker Lu Kuangshi sent round as a matter of course each day, gave it a glance, tossed it aside again, and said:

"Zhuzhai, tomorrow you must scrape together five hundred thousand on your side—five hundred thousand!"

Du Zhuzhai stared at Sunfu in astonishment, and before he had answered, Sunfu went on:

"Yesterday they went up a dollar; today they all but hit the limit-up again; this rising wind is most uncommon strange! I guessed long since it was Old Zhao's doing. Just now Yunshan telephoned—and sure enough: he says Hefu has found it out—Old Zhao and several of the Cantonese crowd are the bulls, and the moment a low price is put out on the market they buy it in, yet they will not take much either, only holding the price up, keeping it at where it stood before the fourth of this month—"

"Then we are in the soup! We ought to have covered yesterday!"

Du Zhuzhai threw down the cigar-butt in his hand and hastened to cut in; fine beads of sweat were starting from his temples.

"Even had we covered yesterday, we should already have been the losers. Now the thing lies plain before us: Wuhan is hard pressed, on the Longhai line there is neither advance nor retreat, and sooner or later the price must fall; we have only to hold it down, not let it rise again, and we need fear nothing. It has become a match of wits now between us and Old Zhao: if they have the stomach to buy in the moment a low price shows, and prop it up right to the month's end, then they have won; but if we are well enough provided—"

"We well enough provided? Ha! We too sell out the moment the price runs up, and prop it too right to the month's end, and then we have won—is that it?"

Du Zhuzhai broke in on Wu Sunfu's words again, and fixed him with a look, with something of disbelief in it.

Wu Sunfu smiled and nodded.

"Why, that is neither more nor less than the gambler's way of doubling at the dice-den! Sunfu! Bonds are a matter of clipping a little interest; to work them your way is far too dangerous!"

Du Zhuzhai could not but set his face against it now, yet his manner was steady still. Wu Sunfu was silent a good while, the whites of his eyes rolling up as though he were reckoning something there; then suddenly he brought his palm down on the corner of the table and said, in a level, deliberate voice:

"There is no danger! Zhuzhai, none at all! You get me together five hundred thousand and hand it to me; tomorrow we press once, and the price must fall back; the small holders will take fright, and on the Changsha side the Zhang-Gui armies are sure to make some new advance these next few days—and so, squeezed from both sides, the market will turn to a selling wind, and let Old Zhao's arts be never so nimble, he shall not wrench it back! Zhuzhai! This is no reckless venture! This is to win by the unlooked-for stroke!"

Du Zhuzhai shut his eyes and shook his head, and said nothing. He was thinking of Sunfu's stubborn self-will that Li Yuting had spoken of. He had made up his mind not to run at Sunfu's heels. And this too he saw plainly: Sunfu was not to be talked round. After a while Du Zhuzhai opened his eyes and said slowly:

"Whether your plan holds danger or no is a lesser matter; but to get you another five hundred thousand—that I cannot do. Since you are so sure of it, and are set on doing it, well and good: the Yizhong has some four or five hundred thousand it can raise—put it all into bonds, then."

"That—won't do! The day before yesterday the board already assigned its use! Just now Lawyer Qiu brought the contracts, and I have set my name to them; those several small mills are bought and settled. The little money the Yizhong has in hand now, I fear, will scarce be enough by and by to turn over those small mills!"

Wu Sunfu said it, and the light of excitement flashed red in his eyes. To have bought up those seven or eight small mills on the most advantageous terms was the Yizhong Trust Company's first great victory since its new founding, and Wu Sunfu's proudest "master-stroke"—and the very thing that sat most ill with Du Zhuzhai. Now Du Zhuzhai, stirred to recall their dispute at the meeting the day before yesterday, felt a fresh touch of anger, and countered at once, coldly:

"Just so! The play is barely opened, and at once there's a famine! If you would work the bonds, then leave the mills alone! And besides, mills that had run at a loss to begin with—we put in fresh capital to enlarge them, with no surety of custom—I truly do not understand what abacus you are all clicking—"

"Zhuzhai—"

Wu Sunfu called, meaning to cut across Du Zhuzhai's complaint; but Du Zhuzhai, for once, would not let Sunfu get a word in:

"Let me speak on! I remember still what you all said that time. You said those several small mills all ran at a loss because their capital was too small, or because they were ill-managed; you said, too, that they already owed the Yizhong a hundred thousand and more, that the old Yizhong was dragged down by just this bad debt, and that in taking this bad debt over from the old Yizhong's hands at but four-tenths on the dollar, we gained our advantage there—so that in truth we bought eight mills, put at three hundred thousand, for a bare fifty or sixty thousand; true enough, we laid out but fifty thousand-odd this time to take in eight mills, and reckoned as it stands, it is cheap indeed—but—"

Here Du Zhuzhai came to a pause after all, and Wu Sunfu burst out laughing; laughing, he hurried in with:

"Zhuzhai, you think that to have to pour in another four or five hundred thousand by degrees makes it no bargain; but if we do not pour it in, our fifty or sixty thousand is thrown away for nothing too! These eight mills are like horses run down in flesh: we must first fatten them up with fodder, and then they will yield. And there is this further point: had we not laid out our fifty thousand-odd to take these mills in, then the four-tenths bad debt we took over from the old Yizhong's hands is thrown away for nothing as well!"

"Good! So, for grudging that four-tenths of bad debt, we throw in ten times as much on top—that is your game of 'buying bean-curd at the price of meat'!"

"Never in this world!"

Wu Sunfu said it with decision, grown somewhat impatient. He sprang up, took a step, and sneered to himself. Never had he looked for it, that failing to coax Du Zhuzhai into the bonds, he should instead raise a side-issue and stir up Zhuzhai's great discontent with the Yizhong. Ever since the dispute arose that day over buying those small mills, Wu Sunfu had seen long since that Du Zhuzhai had no heart for the Yizhong's prospects; and perhaps, when the time came to collect the second call on the shares, Zhuzhai would find some pretext to hang back. This would be most hurtful to the Yizhong. And yet, to keep Du Zhuzhai from wavering, no far-sighted plan of enterprise would serve; only a "get-rich-at-a-stroke" gambler's intrigue—put in your money today and reap the profit tomorrow—could hold him, and barely. At the meeting that day Wang Hefu had likened their buying of the eight small mills, half in jest, to buying up old junk; and Du Zhuzhai, hearing it then, had thought it very just, and only so had left off wrangling. Now Wu Sunfu felt there was nothing for it but to use that same stratagem once more, to hold Du Zhuzhai fast a while. To hold Zhuzhai fast meant, at the least, that the accommodation of funds on the banking side would be a great deal easier. But he must be held the tighter. And so, pacing, Wu Sunfu hit upon a "device." He smiled, turned to the discontented-faced Du Zhuzhai, and said softly:

"Zhuzhai, now we have two matters on our hands—the eight mills the Yizhong has bought, and the one million in bonds we sold short on the third of this month—both of them have got us astride the tiger, with no getting down; there is nothing for it but to set our teeth and drive on to wherever we come out! We are like men pushing a cart up a hill: we can only go on, we cannot go back! It is on just this reasoning that I mean to scrape up five hundred thousand and go 'short' again. It is on just this reasoning that the eight mills the Yizhong bought cannot but be enlarged!"

"Ventures of risk I will not touch!"

Du Zhuzhai answered coldly, shaking his head in vexation. Wu Sunfu's hot, hard words could kindle no daring in Du Zhuzhai; Wu Sunfu knit his brows and pressed a step closer:

"Then the share-capital we have laid in the Yizhong is thrown away for nothing!"

"Draw back in time, and there are still a few tenths to be fished back; I have made up my mind!"

Du Zhuzhai said it in a somewhat altered voice, his face exceedingly grave.

Wu Sunfu could not help a jump of his own heart. But on the instant he laughed wildly, moved a step nearer, clapped Du Zhuzhai on the shoulder, and cried aloud:

"Zhuzhai! Why sink to such faint-heartedness as that! To venture in blind disregard of life and death—no one is willing for that; we have of course our other ways. You surely know that breed of shrewd calculating spirits in Shanghai who take over an old house, lay out capital to whitewash and fit it up, and then pass it on again at a good price. In working these eight mills, we must at the very worst take a lesson from those sharp fellows who deal in nothing but houses! Only we must have a little patience."

"But you must first see who is the good tenant that will come and take this house over, surely?"

"Good tenants there are in plenty! Only let our house be whitewashed and done up to taste, and he will pay a good price for it: and this one is none other than the great and famous Mr. Zhao Botao!"

Wu Sunfu said it with a great laugh, threw back his waist, and strode up and down the study with long steps.

Du Zhuzhai watched Wu Sunfu's long strides with a half-believing, half-doubting eye, and said nothing, but already there was a touch of gladness in his face. He had heard Sunfu tell before now of some "trust" of Zhao Botao's, and he believed Old Zhao was the man to bring off such a stroke; and Old Zhao's not loosing his grip on Zhu Yinqiu's mortgage-loan was proof enough that those rumours had ground under them. And so of a sudden he bethought him that Zhu Yinqiu's telephoning Sunfu a while ago might well have been about that very loan; he was just about to ask, when Wu Sunfu had already paced back and stood before him, saying gladly:

"As for the bonds—for the moment we reckon ourselves twenty thousand and more out of pocket; but, Zhuzhai, there are twenty days and more yet to settlement, and we may very well turn defeat into victory. My reckoning just now cannot go far wrong. Were the Yizhong in funds, then of course we could still let the Yizhong do it, and Wang Hefu and Sun Jiren would surely be for it too; it is only because that money of the Yizhong's is ill to touch that I have come to think of our doing it ourselves, as private men. Here is a thing that serves the public and the private both! Only, more's the pity, I too am cramped in hand of late, having just now been given a wry mouthful by the little-moustached Fei—the muddle-headed wretch! Come, Zhuzhai, let the two of us scrape five hundred thousand together! To sit and watch Old Zhao work the market single-handed is more than a man can stomach!"

Du Zhuzhai shut his eyes and shook his head, and would not speak. The more spirit Wu Sunfu put into his talk, the more afraid Du Zhuzhai grew at heart. He feared that all this of an immediate turn on the Wuhan side was but the empty trumpeting of Tang Yunshan and his sort; and he feared still more this "match of wits" with Old Zhao, for he knew Old Zhao was full of wiles, and of a most formidable dash besides.

Wu Sunfu, who knew Du Zhuzhai's nature through and through, had yet this once one slip in a thousand, and quite failed to see into the secret of Zhuzhai's heart. Again and again, a second time and a third, he plied him, with heartening and with goading; he grew a little angry; but Du Zhuzhai's mind was fixed past breaking, and he only shut his eyes and shook his head and gave never a word. At last Du Zhuzhai, as though in a show of the utmost concession, brought out this one sentence:

"Let it go a few days more, and do it when the market has shown itself clearer; you are in such a hot haste!"

"There is no waiting a few days! The speculator's trade is like sending out troops to battle: one must seize the moment true, and strike quick! And the more so with a fathomless Old Zhao for adversary!"

Wu Sunfu answered most testily, the little pimples on his face reddening and shining one by one. But Du Zhuzhai's face grew paler by the minute. It was as though all the blood of his body had rushed to his heart and pressed there, holding it down, that it should not waver. In truth he was giving but the lesser half of his mind to Wu Sunfu's words; some other matter held the greater half: these were the reckonings of his own interest, tangled and dim of outline, yet forcible bit by bit, till by degrees the scattered thoughts drew to a single point. He had two hundred thousand of capital "laid" in the Yizhong Company. He had taken it at first that the company was to gather in "loose money," to work the bonds a little, and make a mortgage-loan or two; only now did he learn it was nothing of the sort, and that he had been taken in. Then let him draw his capital out while the company had not yet shown its ruin—no matter how much good those eight mills might come to, in a word, "a body that keeps out of the gate of right and wrong"! Hurt feelings? He could not stop for such trifles!—But Sunfu was still nattering on, deafening him with his everlasting bonds! True, by today's closing price, they had lost twenty thousand on the bonds; but that was done in the Yizhong Company's name, split four ways, no more than five thousand a head—reckon it but a few "five hundred" hands lost in eight rounds of mahjong!... And at that Du Zhuzhai could not help smiling to himself. He had settled it: to throw away five thousand for nothing was a far better bargain than to go in daily dread over that hundred and ninety-five thousand! Yet he felt that to bring out his decision on the instant would be too abrupt; he must first make some little disposition. Slowly he stroked his chin, and gazed blankly at Wu Sunfu's face, so full of excitement.

As though something were at strife within his breast, Wu Sunfu's look was one to daunt a man; and had he known the resolve in Du Zhuzhai's heart at that moment, his look would likely have been fouler still. But of that he never dreamed; he was busy plotting how, on his second sister's side, he might drop a word and "throw in a surprise force" to fire up Du Zhuzhai's courage. He felt his own strength could do nothing with a Du Zhuzhai who only shut his eyes and shook his head and would not speak.

But Du Zhuzhai, in the midst of his silence, suddenly stood up, gave a lazy stretch, and, of his own accord, no less, began to speak of "Old Zhao" and "the bonds":

"Sunfu! If you will keep this mind to match wits with Old Zhao to the end, you must beware of one bad fall and the sapping of your vital force! I have seen many a man, and all of them hurt upon this one word 'match'!"

Wu Sunfu's eyebrows shot up, and he began to laugh; he took it, in his error, that Du Zhuzhai's temper had taken some turn for the better. Du Zhuzhai paused a little, then went on:

"And there is another thing. That day, when Li Yuting came to report how he and Old Zhao had treated together, there was one word of his I thought had much reason in it—"

"Which word?"

Wu Sunfu asked hurriedly, and stood up most attentive, and went and planted himself before Du Zhuzhai.

"Just this—that Tang Yunshan has party connections!—True, Old Zhao has them too; but, Sunfu, why should we make trouble for ourselves! Old Zhao's not loosing Zhu Yinqiu's cocoons to you—he takes that very thing for his pretext, and is not your present coil the fruit of it—"

Du Zhuzhai paused again, and, with the air of a man well pleased with himself, drew out his handkerchief and wiped his face. He meant thus, little by little, to come round to his own unwillingness to carry on with the Yizhong Company; but Wu Sunfu of a sudden gave a savage laugh, stamped his foot, and said:

"Enough, Zhuzhai—I forgot to tell you: just now Zhu Yinqiu telephoned, and says he will make over the cocoons and the mill both to me!"

"Is there such a thing? What is the reason of it?"

"As I read it, Old Zhao has likely got wind that I have already bought up a lot of cocoons, and thinks that to keep his hold on Zhu Yinqiu any longer is to no purpose, and so has shifted his ground. And he has this further piece of ill intent: knowing my ready cash is tight, and knowing too that my cocoons are already enough for my need, he thrusts Zhu Yinqiu's cocoons back on me on purpose—meaning to fix me so that on the one hand my ready money is locked up dead, and on the other I am glutted with cocoons past all use! In one word, he has racked his brains to hit on every kind of way to drive me into a corner. Only, that Zhu Yinqiu should want to make over even that mill of his to me—there Old Zhao has reckoned amiss!"

Wu Sunfu said it very calmly, with no great show of chagrin. For though his ready cash was tight just now, the grand ambition of enlarging his enterprises stood yet vigorous and stirring in his heart, and this lightened every other crossing. It was rather Du Zhuzhai whose colour changed a little, in dread on Wu Sunfu's behalf. The more he felt this "match of wits" with Old Zhao a most dangerous thing, and asked hurriedly:

"Then you have made up your mind to take over Zhu Yinqiu's mill?"

"I will talk with him tomorrow and settle it then—"

The sentence was not done, when the study door was suddenly opened, and the manservant Gaosheng, sidling in edgewise, ushered in a man—and it was Tang Yunshan, his whole face declaring plainly the flurry of some grave event afoot. Sunfu and Zhuzhai both took a start.

"The Zhang-Gui armies are pulling out of Changsha!"

Tang Yunshan said just this one sentence, then plumped down into the nearest sofa, gaping wide and scratching his scalp.

The study was still as death. Wu Sunfu narrowed his eyes and looked at Tang Yunshan, and looked again at the report-sheet of the day's opening and closing bond-prices among the litter of papers on the desk. Could the upriver case have turned clean about? Here was the unlooked-for beyond all unlooked-for! Du Zhuzhai let a soft breath go; on the abacus of his heart a few more beads clicked off, one after another: ten thousand—fifteen thousand—twenty thousand; he had reckoned but now on throwing away five thousand, and over five thousand he could keep his heart from aching, but now it might run to twenty thousand, and that was another matter.

After a while Wu Sunfu asked in a hoarse voice, setting his teeth:

"Is this news from without, or from within? This morning you told me, Yunshan, that the Iron Army was marching out toward the Jiangxi border, did you not?"

"Now we know that that was the retreat! Quitting the Wuhan-Changsha line, to spare a useless sacrifice! I had it from Huang Fen's telephone, taken the moment after I had done telephoning you; he had but just got the news himself. The cipher wire the Hankou special agent sent must say so; nine parts in ten it may be trusted!"

"Then no one outside knows of it yet, and there is still a way to save the game."

Wu Sunfu said it in a low voice, as if to himself, and the furrows on his brow eased a little. Du Zhuzhai let another sigh escape; on the abacus of his heart the loss of twenty thousand stood fixed now, and he swallowed a mouthful of spittle and drew out his snuff-bottle by instinct. Wu Sunfu rubbed his hands and hung his head; and then of a sudden he raised it, turned, and looked at Du Zhuzhai and said:

"What man can do, one must not leave undone. Zhuzhai, do you think there is yet a way?—Yunshan's news is a close secret, a matter of their own inner military counsel; just now there are likely still Gui troops in the city of Changsha, and with the Iron Army marching for the Jiangxi border, outsiders will take it that Nanchang is hard pressed. We will lay our dispositions overnight; Zhuzhai, on the banking side you fire off a blank shot—on every bond-mortgage account you must call, one and all, for more security. Let us muddle through tomorrow morning, and in tomorrow's early market we cover in batches—"

"I will warrant that, come the day after, Changsha is still in our hands!"

Tang Yunshan of a sudden put in, with a great air of assurance, and laughed for no cause at all.

Du Zhuzhai nodded, and said nothing. For the sake of his own twenty thousand in and out, he could only, this once more, warm a little to the Yizhong Company's affairs. He did not so much as take his snuff now; he looked at the clock—ten minutes and more short of six—and could not waste a moment of that priceless time. It being settled that Sunfu should see to the broker's side, Du Zhuzhai hurried off. Here Wu Sunfu and Tang Yunshan, the two of them, fell to discussing another matter. Wu Sunfu spoke first:

"Since word of that consignment has leaked, I fear it cannot be shipped to Yantai now; it may be stopped by the navy in the Shandong seas. I have thought it over just now: there is but one road—you make a run to Hong Kong, and there find some means to tranship it elsewhere."

"I too was thinking so. I mean to start tomorrow. The post of general manager in the company I beg you to fill in my stead."

"That will not do! Better ask Wang Hefu."

"Very well. But—ai, these last two weeks nothing has gone smoothly; the motley levies on the upriver side that had been won over went back on their word at the eleventh hour and would all stand watching and stir no foot, so that the Zhang-Gui armies came within an ace of victory and failed—and that is not the worst of it. The worst is that the Shanxi army has to this hour not come out in full, and the Northwest Army, after a hard month's fighting, is too heavily bled, and short of powder and shot besides. Nay, even the veriest trifle—like this shipment of arms, all in good train—will of a sudden spring a leak too!"

Tang Yunshan, somewhat crest-fallen, scratched his scalp, gave Wu Sunfu a look, and gazed out of the window again; a smear of deep-red evening glow hung on the corner of the pavilion by the pool yonder, and the leaves of the trees about it too were touched with a golden yellow.

Wu Sunfu, his left hand on his hip, his right forefinger drawing circles on the writing-desk, bowed his head and pondered. The colour of his face passed by degrees from the arrogance that scorned all things into an uncertain murk, and then out of the murk broke a little flush of excited purple; abruptly he raised his head and asked:

"Yunshan, then the whole prospect of the situation is still one blur? By the month's end there will hardly be a change on the Shandong side?"

"I dare say nothing rash now. Wait for next month's end—ai, it is enough to break a man's heart!"

Tang Yunshan answered with a rueful face.

Wu Sunfu suddenly gave a strange laugh, and leaned his body back against the pure-steel swivel chair, and shut his eyes. The colour of his face turned all at once to an ashen white, and beads of sweat covered his temples. For the first time he felt that he was too small a thing, and that the way before his enterprise was too high with waves; on his two hands alone, snatching and grasping east and west, he could not in truth cope with it all!

Having seen Tang Yunshan off, Wu Sunfu paced up and down in the garden. Now the last streak of sunlight too was gone; the whole garden lay dim and boundless, and the dusk was creeping out of the clumps of trees and spreading abroad. The great parlour, the small parlour, the great dining-room, the second floor—the window-holes of every place shone out with electric light. Wu Sunfu seemed to weary of the sight of those lamps; he paced alone to the little pool's edge, sat down in an idle rattan chair set there, and heaved a heavy breath.

He fell to weighing his enterprise once more. Fell and fierce, the breakers had come rolling in one upon another, and not since today alone; all of them he had passed through unshaken, and had spread his sails and driven on, to come to this unexampled grandeur of scale today. He and Sun Jiren and the rest would jointly command eight mills, all of them makers of daily necessaries! They had made ready, moreover, four hundred thousand and more of capital, planning there to enlarge these eight mills; they would send their lamp-bulbs, their thermoses, their parasols, their soap, their rubber overshoes, out to the poorest and remotest hamlets of all China! They would deal a mortal hurt to those small mills of the same lines newly transplanted from Japan to Shanghai! And Wu Sunfu, further, would take over single-handed Chen Junyi's silk-weaving mill and Zhu Yinqiu's silk filature. All this had been won only through bitter struggle, and only by struggle as bitter could it be held and enlarged. Wind and wave were things to be looked for; as the proverb has it, "let the Way rise one foot, and the Devil rises ten!" He, Wu Sunfu, and his comrades Sun Jiren and the rest—were they not all seasoned veterans of a hundred fights in the world of enterprise? Should they take fright at anything?

Wu Sunfu, so thinking, could not but smile to himself. The water-cool evening breeze stirred the lappet of his gown; he lifted his head and looked about him, and felt that he was not small after all, and by no means alone. He had marked long since that among the old managers of the eight mills they had bought were several he might take for his right hands; he would drill up a band of keen, able subordinates! Only the lower run of clerks was still too weak a stuff. He bethought him of Wu Weicheng and Ma Jingshan, come today to seek a place. It seemed both had a point or two worth having; and though they came not up to Tu Weiyue, they were belike a great deal better than those doddering Mo Ganchengs and the like?

Suddenly he was aware of someone come up behind him, and then a waft of perfume struck into his nostrils; he turned his head sharply, and in the thin dusk knew, by that tall and lightsome figure alone, that it was the young mistress.

"A telegram has come from Staff Officer Lei! Most strange—it is sent from Tianjin."

The Wu young mistress leaned slantwise against the back of Sunfu's rattan chair and said it softly; and the voice shook a little.

"Oh! Tianjin? And what did it say?"

"It says his business will soon be done, and he will be coming back to Shanghai."

The Wu young mistress's voice was plainly strange as she said it, half glad and half afraid. But Wu Sunfu took no heed of it. His quick nerves had of a sudden raised a whole cloud of suspicion out of the two words "Tianjin." Why should Staff Officer Lei have got to Tianjin? He was a serving officer with a brigade at his back! Could the fighting have reached even Tianjin? And then tomorrow's bond market!—In an instant all his ease of heart and mind was fled; Wu Sunfu felt a fever run through his whole body, and the scent on the young mistress turned his stomach. He stood up roughly and said to the young mistress:

"Peiyao, this scent of yours is a queer, outlandish thing!—Ai, go into the house! Has the Second Sister not gone yet?"

And without waiting for the young mistress to answer, Wu Sunfu made off. All the way, his brain seethed with a press of tangled questions and answers of his own: it seemed he ought now to turn "bull"? But how if Zhuzhai would not put up the money? Let him throw those eighty thousand away, and have done with bonds hereafter? Yet no, that would not do—eighty thousand could set up a very fine rubber-works! And besides, without beating Zhao Botao down on the bonds, the Yizhong's business would suffer his wrecking hereafter!...

In the great parlour, Mrs. Du sat talking away with the junior-generation Wu Weicheng. Wu Sunfu walked straight up to Mrs. Du and said with a smile:

"Second Sister, let me have a few words with you!"

Mrs. Du seemed to give a little start, turned her face to glance at Lin Peishan and Du Xintuo, who sat together by the piano turning over a music-book, and nodded with a smile. Wu Sunfu, while he let Mrs. Du go first into the small parlour, said to Wu Weicheng:

"You and Ma Jingshan, the two of you, go tomorrow first to my mill and try your hand a few days; hereafter I will set you to other work!"

"Sunfu, there is Young Master Zeng besides; he has waited a good half-month and more. Let him go along and try too?"

The Wu young mistress, just then come running into the parlour, put in quickly, with a glance aslant at Wu Sunfu. Wu Sunfu's brows knit a moment, but in the end he nodded too. He beckoned the young mistress aside and said in her ear, low:

"We go before the Second Sister to egg Zhuzhai on to a bold stroke at the bonds; you must say that Staff Officer Lei has fought a losing battle and been wounded, taken alive and carried off to Tianjin—ai, you must make it sound like truth, and mind you do not show the cloven hoof!"

The Wu young mistress was struck perfectly dumb, and did not take Sunfu's drift; yet in her heart, for no reason, a wave of sorrow rose, as though she saw already the wounded and captive Staff Officer Lei. Sunfu, though, was smiling faintly, and walked with the young mistress out of the small parlour. But before he pulled the parlour door to, he suddenly bethought him of one thing more, thrust half his body out, and called to the manservant Gaosheng:

"Ring up Master Lu Kuangshi, and ask him to come round about nine o'clock!"
