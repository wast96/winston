### The Red Squad Draws Its Sword

After the Central Special Section had established its first counter-espionage tie, Yang Dengying, inside the Kuomintang's professional anti-Communist organs, it held the initiative in the struggle with the enemy. Though the enemy several times intercepted the addresses and meeting places of our Party's secret organs, in most cases we could get word beforehand through Yang Dengying and escape the enemy's traps. But Yang Dengying's standing in the enemy's agent camp grew only by degrees, and the part he played for the Central Special Section grew only step by step. Even after he was formally appointed the Kuomintang's "Central Special Commissioner Resident in Shanghai" and held great power in the city, the activity of our Party organs and the safety of certain responsible people were still not absolutely secure, never beyond all risk of the enemy's blows. This was because Shanghai's conditions of struggle were extremely complex, its Chinese and foreign police organs a tangle of interlocking roots, hard to unravel; and because the enemy often used money to lure them, so that certain irresolute elements who had wormed into our Party wavered, turned themselves in, and defected. The Special Section therefore could not always avoid running into problems it had not foreseen.

On 15 April 1928, in Shanghai, came the arrest of Luo Yinong, standing committee member of the CCP Provisional Central Political Bureau and director of the CCP Central Organization Bureau, sold out by a traitor.

### British Police Burst Through Luo Yinong's Door

Luo Yinong, also named Shanyang, styled Shenzhai, with the sobriquets Zhengang and the aliases Luo Jue and Yinong, was an early member and leader of the Chinese Communist Party, a brave fighter of the proletariat. He was born in Xiangtan County, Hunan, in 1902. From boyhood he loved to read the old classics such as Water Margin and Romance of the Three Kingdoms, and it is said the heroes in them — men who hated evil like an enemy and stood firm in a righteous cause — won his reverence and shaped his character. His family was well-off; his father had served as a village head, sat in judgment at the local hall, later dealt in grain, and had also gone shares in a general store. Seeing his father mean and grasping, counting every copper, loving money like life, Luo Yinong once pasted a couplet at his own front door to remonstrate with him: "Keep a good conscience, and blessings will come; open your horizons, and build a home and a calling."

In his childhood Luo Yinong studied in his home village. In 1916, at fourteen, he passed into an American-run mission school, the Yizhi School in Xiangtan, where the courses were chiefly English and theology. Unwilling to sit through those absurd theology classes, refusing to attend "worship," disgusted with the school's every rule and restraint, he was forever breaking out to take part in social activities of every kind: anti-Japanese marches and demonstrations, the smashing and burning of Japanese goods, publicity for the army sent to punish Yuan Shikai … To throw off the mental shackles of such a school, he resolutely quit at the end of 1917. In the year that followed he went several times to Changsha seeking a chance to enroll, without success.

In the summer of 1919 he left home for Shanghai, against his family's opposition.

Reaching Shanghai, he entered a middle school, but as his father would not send money to keep him, that winter he left off his studies and, to make his own way, took work as a proofreader at a small newspaper in the French Concession. The pay was low, but at the newspaper office he could read all sorts of books and papers, and the conditions for self-study were good; here he read a great deal of progressive material. In May 1920 the Shanghai Communist group was founded, and through reading New Youth and other journals Luo Yinong took in revolutionary ideas. That year Chen Duxiu moved to Shanghai, and New Youth too moved there to be published, its distribution office close to the newspaper where he worked. Luo Yinong greatly admired Chen Duxiu, and having found out his address, called on him several times to learn from him. Chen Duxiu, for his part, was fond of this ardent, studious young man, and introduced him to the work of the Work-Study Mutual Aid Corps. Later, again on Chen Duxiu's recommendation, he joined the Foreign Language School the Shanghai Communist group ran in the French Concession to prepare students to be sent to study in Soviet Russia, and studied Russian and the fundamentals of proletarian revolution. That August he joined the Shanghai Socialist Youth League, and in early 1921, with Ren Bishi, Liu Shaoqi, Xiao Jingguang, and some twenty others, went to Moscow to study in the Chinese class of the Communist University of the Toilers of the East. That winter he was transferred to full membership in the Chinese Communist Party, and served as a committee member of the CCP's Russia-based branch among the Party and League students of the university's Chinese class.

Returning home in March 1925, he was sent to Guangzhou to take part in the Second National Labor Congress. On 3 May he also attended, as a delegate of the Peasant International, the founding congress of the Guangdong Provincial Peasant Association, and gave the opening address. After the Labor Congress he stayed on as an alternate member of the Central's Provisional Committee Resident in Guangdong and joined the CCP Guangdong District Committee, in charge of propaganda, taking part in the leadership of the May Thirtieth Movement and the Canton–Hong Kong general strike. In October he attended the CCP Central's Second Enlarged Conference in Beijing, and afterward directed the work of the Northern District Committee's Party school. In December he became secretary of the CCP Jiangsu-Zhejiang District Committee. From November 1926 to March 1927 he, together with Zhao Shiyan, Zhou Enlai, and others, organized and led the three armed uprisings of the Shanghai workers. In May 1927, at the Fifth National Congress, he was elected to the Central Committee. He then became secretary of the CCP Jiangxi Provincial Committee. After the "July 15" counter-revolutionary coup, Luo Yinong was moved to Wuhan and made secretary of the CCP Hubei Provincial Committee. At the Party's August 7 Conference he was elected to the Provisional Central Political Bureau, and afterward led the southern Hubei uprising. In September the CCP Central organs moved back to Shanghai. To strengthen the leadership of Party work in the provinces of the middle Yangtze, the Central resolved to set up a Yangtze Bureau, with Luo Yinong as its secretary. In November, at the enlarged meeting of the Provisional Central Political Bureau in Shanghai, he was elected a standing committee member of the Provisional Central Political Bureau. Soon after, he became director of the CCP Central Organization Bureau.

At the end of 1927 Luo Yinong left Wuhan for Shanghai. At that time the CCP Central organs were in a most perilous situation; the comrades mostly lived scattered in the guise of families, and meetings had to be held as the moment allowed. Because his name stood so high in Shanghai, with a bounty of ten thousand yuan long set on his head by the imperialists and the Kuomintang reactionary authorities, agents, detectives, traitors, and constables were forever hunting his trail. On the eve of coming to Shanghai, on 4 November that year, on receiving the CCP Central's order of transfer, he said to Wang Yifei: "Today I set out for Shanghai, my fortune unknown, but the Party's summons is urgent, and this journey bears greatly on much," and so resolved to "go and risk it." It showed that he had long set his own safety aside, meeting every kind of tangled situation with courage and wit.

In February 1928 Luo Yinong was charged by the Central to inspect work in the two Hu provinces. He went to a number of counties and towns in Hunan and Hubei, chiefly to learn how the peasant movement was developing, and gathered a large body of firsthand material for the Central to draw on in setting the country's line of struggle. In mid-April, back in Shanghai from his tour of the two Hu, he again began often to receive comrades come to the city from various places, to hear their reports and discuss the line and tactics of struggle. On the morning of 15 April he came to his office at Wangzhili on Gordon Road (today Jiangning Road), in the British Concession, to talk over work with comrades from Shandong. Not long after he entered, a party of detectives from the British concession police suddenly burst in. The British agent at their head, Locke, exchanged a few words with the traitor and then leveled a pistol at Luo Yinong and snarled: "You are Luo Yinong; I have had my eye on you two or three years now — come with us!" And so they took Luo Yinong away.

The next day many Chinese and foreign papers carried the news, and imperialism and the Kuomintang warlords all doffed their caps in mutual congratulation, saying such things as "the chief ringleader is taken; the Communist scourge can be quenched." Once the news spread, the broad masses of Shanghai's workers and townspeople were filled with indignation, and the point of public censure was turned on the reactionary authorities.

### Hunting the Traitor Who Sold Out Luo Yinong

The Party organization soon learned of Luo Yinong's arrest. When Luo Yinong had just come back to Shanghai from his tour of the two Hu, two out-of-province delegates (one of them from Shandong) had come to make contact with the Central. On the evening of the 14th, Li Weihan went to Luo Yinong's lodging at the Central Propaganda Department organ at Hengchangli, Yuyuan Road, near Jing'an Temple, reported this, and it was agreed: the next day Luo Yinong would go to the CCP Central organ at Wangzhili on Gordon Road to receive the delegates of the Shandong Provincial Committee, while Li Weihan would go to the organ at South Chengdu Road, south of the Hardoon Gardens, to meet the delegate of another province. On the evening of the 15th, Li Weihan went to Luo Yinong's lodging to report on his own contact, and waited there a long time, but Luo Yinong did not come back. Before long came word that Luo Yinong had been arrested.

Learning of all this, the CCP Central took it very gravely. Zhou Enlai at once instructed the Central Special Section to find a way to rescue him and to trace quickly the chief culprit who had sold Luo Yinong out. Through its inside line in the British concession police, the Central Special Section soon learned that the one who had sold Luo Yinong out was a woman, who spoke with a Sichuan accent, was very beautiful, and spoke fluent German. Before long it was further made clear that this woman was He Jiaxing's wife, He Zhihua. It was quickly established: those who had sold Luo Yinong out were He Jiaxing and He Zhihua.

The He Jiaxing couple had both studied at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow, and after returning home both worked under Luo Yinong, assigned to keep the Party's organ at the Gordon Road contact point. Infatuated with a bourgeois way of life, they were forever in and out of restaurants and dance halls, kept no discipline of secret work, sometimes coming home very late, and had been sternly criticized by Luo Yinong more than once. At the time a Party worker's living allowance was only twenty yuan a month, which naturally could not meet their craving for a corrupt and degenerate life. Through their work the two knew many of the Party's secrets and held the addresses of some responsible Central comrades; knowing well that Luo Yinong was a Central leader with a bounty on his head, they treated him as rare merchandise and set about a filthy bargain with the enemy. They went to a deputy at the British concession police station near Jing'an Temple, meaning to trade a batch of secret addresses of Central organs and responsible comrades for twenty thousand American dollars and two passports out of the country, and to flee abroad to a life of dissipation. To win the police's trust, they first sold Luo Yinong out. At ten in the morning of 15 April, after Luo Yinong reached the Gordon Road organ, the He Jiaxing couple, following the method prearranged with the concession police, had the amah carry a letter to the Chinese constable posted at the corner of Gordon Road and Avenue Road, telling him Luo Yinong had come. The British constable-chief Locke at once led several Chinese and foreign detectives to the spot. Locke exchanged a few words in German with He Zhihua and then carried Luo Yinong off on the spot. None of the others present was arrested, and the Party documents left in a drawer were not seized either.

Once the situation was clear, the Central Special Section at once sent men to make contact with the enemy, ready to use a large sum to buy the enemy off and win Luo Yinong's release. But considering afterward that Luo Yinong had been sold out by a traitor and his identity already exposed, so that an open rescue could hardly succeed, it decided instead to use force, planning to snatch him back by armed action on the road when Luo Yinong was handed over by the British Concession's Municipal Police and escorted to the Songhu Garrison Command at Longhua. Gu Shunzhang at the time told this plan to Luo Yinong's wife, Li Wenyi, asking her first to find the He Jiaxing couple and learn when Luo Yinong would be moved from the police station to the Songhu Garrison Command at Longhua. Li Wenyi has said:

The plan to rescue Yinong was told me by Gu Shunzhang; I saw no written material. Gu said at the time: contact had already been made with the enemy, who asked forty thousand yuan; but the enemy being very cunning, it was to be feared that once the money was handed over the man would still not be got out, and we would lose both man and money. So it was decided to put up 20,000 yuan as funds, and when the enemy moved Yinong from the Concession to Longhua, to block the road and snatch him back. The plan then was to buy a coffin and stage a mock funeral, hiding guns inside the coffin, and to have me put on mourning as the dead man's kin and walk behind the coffin with the workers of the mock funeral party; when the prison van passed, they would take the weapons out of the coffin without warning and snatch Yinong away. Gu Shunzhang asked me to find the He Jiaxing couple and learn the time of Yinong's transfer. He said: the British detective Locke knows German, and when Yinong was arrested He Jiaxing's wife had dealt with him in German and had even given him a diamond ring, so she could be sent to find Locke and learn the time of transfer. At the time he did not tell me that it was this very couple who had sold Yinong out.

I went to the He Jiaxing couple's lodging. By then they had moved out of Gordon Road and were staying at a hotel the Party had found for them. I waited there several hours before He Jiaxing's wife came back from outside. She wore a sky-blue long-sleeved velvet dress and a pink gauze shawl, got up especially garish. Seeing me arrive unannounced, she shrugged her shoulders and spread her hands like a foreigner, and before I could open my mouth waved her hand to signal me not to weep. Then, feigning warmth, she pulled me onto the bed and whispered in my ear: they had moved house in secret, and no one must know about the Gordon Road affair. She went on to tell of the circumstances of Yinong's arrest, telling it with great animation, boasting that it was thanks to her that the Shandong Provincial Committee delegates who had come to make contact, and the Party documents, had been protected. I said: you know Locke, you have spoken with him and given him a diamond ring — could you go and find out from him exactly when Yinong is to be transferred? I said a few words, and she fell silent. Knowing I would get no answer for the moment, I could only leave. About a day later, I went to look for her again, and by the time I got there it was past nine in the morning; the He Jiaxing couple were still lying in bed, and only when they saw me come did they get up, lazy and languid. I asked them: "Has any answer come?" They said: "It's not clear; they won't tell us." The He Jiaxing couple had long been in secret league with the enemy — how would they ever tell me the time of Yinong's transfer! In the end this only worked against us, putting the enemy on its guard, and Yinong was very quickly transferred. And so this rescue plan came to nothing.

In the end Luo Yinong was handed over on 18 April to the Kuomintang's Songhu Garrison Command, and because the time of transfer could not be learned beforehand, the plan of armed rescue could not be carried out.

### "What Is a Broken Body Worth, with the Great Enemy at Hand"

After Luo Yinong was handed over to the Songhu Garrison Command at Longhua, he was held in the Longhua detention house. The enemy meant to break him open and through him wreck the CCP Central organs and the local organizations. The Kuomintang garrison commander Qian Dajun, on the one hand wired Chiang Kai-shek for instructions on how to deal with him, on the other set his judge-advocate, a man named Qiu, to interrogate him, and though they questioned him three times running they got nothing of any use to them. Zhang Weizhen, who was then held in the same prison as Luo Yinong, has said:

After Luo Yinong was arrested he was held in the row behind our cell, right across from ours at an angle. I was arrested on 16 February 1928; there were more than twenty in our case, including Zheng Futa (chairman of the Shanghai General Labor Union) and Xu Baihao (organization head of the Shanghai General Labor Union). That time three organs — the Pudong District Committee, the organization department of the Jiangsu Provincial Committee, and the Shanghai General Labor Union — were wrecked at once, on the information of Tang Ruilin. … After we were arrested, Luo Yinong and Gu Shunzhang organized our rescue. At the time we were in touch with the outside and knew the whole situation. In the very midst of the rescue, Luo Yinong was arrested, and the rescue of us broke off. Luo Yinong knew our situation, and so he stood at the window and spoke with us — though of course not much could be said. Seeing him arrested left us far from calm, so when he spoke with us we all came to the window to listen. He told us to write quickly to the outside, to tell the organization he had been arrested and to try to rescue him, and so on. Those were his very words, and they left a deep impression on me. In fact the enemy already knew he was Luo Yinong — how could he be rescued? Even 100,000 yuan would have been no use! But at the time we did not weigh all that, and hurried to write and tell the outside.

That Tang Ruilin had defected we did not know at the time, nor did Luo Yinong. After Luo Yinong was moved to the Longhua Command, the enemy moved Tang from the cell next to ours to the cell where they held him (the others in his cell were all common criminals, kidnappers, bandits, and the like), and only then did we find that Tang was under suspicion as a traitor. About four or five days later Tang was released, and by then Luo Yinong had already been shot. A photograph of Luo Yinong's execution was once shown to us by a warder who had ties with us.

Luo Yinong had long set life and death aside; he stood firm under torture, and every secret the enemy hoped to wring from him it failed to get. On 20 April Qian Dajun received Chiang Kai-shek's wired reply, ordering that Luo Yinong be "executed on the spot." Before his execution, Luo Yinong met death as a homecoming, and wrote a poem of departing life:

{p} Boldly I mount the cart and go; / may we keep, all of us, our one integrity whole.
{p} What is this broken body worth, / with the great enemy right before us?

Before he went to his death, Luo Yinong wrote a letter in cipher to the Party Central, which comrades in the prison passed out and gave to the Party organization. In the last words he wrote to his family he set down: "Study what I have studied, to console me." It showed to the full a revolutionary's utter devotion to the people's cause of liberation and his ardent hopes for those who came after.

On 21 April 1928 the enemy killed Luo Yinong at Longhua. Of how the martyr Luo Yinong met his death, the Shanghai Shen Bao and News Daily of 22 April reported:

On the afternoon of 21 April, the Longhua execution ground in Shanghai was under heavy guard, the reactionary troops and police with loaded weapons as though facing a great foe. Luo Yinong, neatly dressed in a Zhigongni gongni jacket and a grey serge gown, under escort of a platoon of the special-service battalion the enemy garrison command had sent, walked calmly to the execution ground and gave his life bravely.

The day after Luo Yinong gave his life, the Party organization notified Li Wenyi. On 4 July 1980, in a letter to Li Qiang, Li Wenyi looked back on the time: "On the last morning, Gu (Shunzhang) knocked and woke me, worn out as I was, and told me: 'Go quickly to Longhua; at the place where there is a road with the four big characters Wenzhi University, go and look at what is posted on the telegraph pole.' I at once called a taxi, and saw the four square iron plates of Wenzhi University, one character to a plate, hung on a wire strung across the mouth of a road; I got down and looked for the telegraph pole, and saw on the notice posted on it: 'By order of Chiang △△ … the wanted Communist criminal Luo Yinong to be shot at once. Songhu Garrison Commander Qian Dajun. 22 April.' My legs would not move, but I had to find the execution place and Yinong's body, and so I went on along the road. The driver followed behind me. On the right of the road, in a hollow of grass, there was a pool of fresh blood the size of a tabletop, and beside it, tipped over on the ground, a paper marker with the words 'wanted Communist bandit Luo Yinong,' and a clean white handkerchief, neatly folded, that I had given Yinong. Without knowing it I sank to the ground, my right sleeve falling into the pool of blood. The driver pulled me up. My weeping must have drawn no few passersby, and among them a working man in black clothes, a small blue cloth bundle on his back, gave a long sigh and asked me to rest a while at his home, not far off. He told me it had been carried out at two the previous afternoon. I asked him about the execution and where the body was, and he said: 'Before the execution I heard him shout something, "long live" this or that. I couldn't make it out. The body — I don't know whether the charity hall has taken it in. I can take you to look.' I followed him a little way along the same road, and saw on the right a plot of ground where a mound of fresh yellow earth stood, a tuft of green grass planted on its top; in the ceaseless drizzle I saw no marker of any kind. … I stood there stricken a long while, then took the same car back to the hotel to report to Gu Shunzhang and put my request to him; but to my surprise Gu had given up the hotel and gone, and from then on I never saw him again."

After Luo Yinong bravely gave his life, the CCP Central organs came through unharmed and went on fighting hard. Huang Jieran, who worked at the Central Propaganda Department at the time, has said:

In March and April 1928 I was working at the Party journal Bolshevik, and Luo Yinong had come to our organ. … Before long we got the alarm that Luo Yinong was arrested. By the CCP Central's rule, whenever anyone was arrested, the organs he knew were to be moved at once. But ours was an editorial organ, with a great many books — the second floor and the third were all books, in Russian, in French, in Chinese, everything. What were we to do? Everyone believed Luo Yinong would not give us up; they had that faith in him. But by Party discipline we had to move out. So I, Zheng Chaolin, my wife, and the rest had no choice but to hide away. We hid three days, and then the papers carried the news of Luo Yinong's death, printing even a photograph of him after his death. A day or two more passed, and seeing no sign of anything, we went back to the organ. Luo Yinong had been to our organ, and to his death it came through unharmed — which proves how extraordinarily firm he was after his arrest.

The killing of Comrade Luo Yinong was a grave loss to the Party, and stirred the whole Party to shock, grief, and mourning. In the twentieth issue of the journal Bolshevik, published on 30 May 1928, the CCP Central ran an article mourning him with deep sorrow, "In Mourning for Comrade Luo Yinong." The article wrote: "Comrade Yinong has been killed; the Chinese proletariat has lost one of its most ardent leaders, the Chinese Communist Party one of its bravest fighters." The article set a high value on Luo Yinong's revolutionary spirit, saying: "Comrade Luo Yinong's ardent revolutionary spirit may serve as a model for the whole membership of the Chinese Communist Party. Reactionaries such as Wu Zhihui gnash their teeth at the mere three characters of Luo Yinong's name. His death is an immeasurable loss!"

### The Traitor Cut Down Amid Firecrackers

Luo Yinong's death roused in everyone a boundless hatred of the traitors He Jiaxing and He Zhihua. Though it was soon established after the case broke that the two of them had informed to the enemy, the Central was very careful; to obtain hard proof that they had sold Luo Yinong out, it made a thorough arrangement and for the time being placed them in a small hotel on Nanjing Road. Only after firm evidence was in hand, and seeing that this pair of traitors held a great many of the Party's secrets and were a grave threat to the safety of the Party organization, did Li Weihan and Deng Xiaoping, then working in the CCP Central, jointly settle the decision to punish the traitors severely. Li Weihan has said:

At the time we already knew through our inside line that it was the He Jiaxing couple who had informed, but on the surface we pretended not to. Afterward Xiaoping and I jointly resolved to do away with them. I made contact with our Special Section upstairs at the Party Central's secretariat and made the arrangements. To make the traitors believe we had raised no suspicion of them, we sent someone to tell them that the Xinzha Road place (that is, the Gordon Road organ) could no longer be lived in, and that they should move, but before moving should stay a few days at an inn on Nanjing Road. At that time Nanjing Road had many big shops, and between two of them there would be a small lodging house, with a staircase just inside the door and the upstairs connecting the two buildings. The Special Section found an inn of this kind and had them move in first. I arranged to meet them there. Before the meeting the Special Section made a thorough arrangement, posting men upstairs and in the street to watch and to cover; should anything go wrong, I could slip out quietly by the back. At the appointed time I met them there. After we met I said a few things to carry it off. I said: you have had a fright, the old place can no longer be lived in, so you had best find another house and lie low a while. I gave them some more money too, and told them to tell a certain comrade their address after they moved. Seeing that I had come to meet them myself, they believed the organization had raised no suspicion of them, and so, once moved, they quickly sent word of the address to a certain comrade — who was in fact one of the Special Section's own.

After the Central Special Section took on the task and made clear He Jiaxing and He Zhihua's address, it acted swiftly. On 25 April, the fourth day after Luo Yinong gave his life, at dawn, Chen Geng led the Red Squad into the hotel where the He Jiaxing couple were staying, one man guarding the back door and three going upstairs. At the same time, on the pretext of a wedding, ear-splitting firecrackers were set off outside the hotel. Chen Geng and the Red Squad members, swift as a thunderclap that leaves no time to cover the ears, burst into the room where the He Jiaxing couple lodged. He Jiaxing, startled awake, knew things had gone wrong and ran for it; Chen Geng's pistol came up to meet him, there was a crack, and He Jiaxing fell where he stood. He Zhihua was struck by a shot on the forehead that blinded her right eye; shaking all over with fright, she scrambled and rolled under the bed. The Red Squad members thought they had killed her, but in fact she was only badly wounded (it was learned later that when she went for treatment at the Guangci Hospital in Shanghai, the enemy guarded her so closely there was no chance to finish it). Meanwhile the firecrackers went on sounding outside the hotel, covering this light-horse party that had eliminated the traitors as it withdrew smoothly from the scene.

The Red Squad's action in severely punishing the traitors He Jiaxing and He Zhihua stopped the traitors from further criminal betrayal of the Party organization, and guarded the safety of the Central organs.
