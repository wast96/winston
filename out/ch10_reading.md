## My Teacher Yuan Hanyun

{d} Chen Timin

My late teacher Yuan Hanyun, whose given name was Kewen, was the second son of Yuan Shikai. He had many style-names and pen-names, more than one can easily keep track of; only his milk-name, "Baocun," was the one his parents had called him from childhood, and so, whenever a matter touched on his parents, he would sign a piece of his connoisseurship "Baocun" or "Baocen."
Teacher Hanyun was of the "Da" generation of the Green Gang. It was in the fourth lunar month of 1925, in Beijing, that I did obeisance to him as my "old man." Following the late-Qing rule for taking a teacher, I used three red envelopes to hold, separately, the gift for the master, the gift for the banquet, and the gift for the gatehouse (the last being for the gatekeeper), with a fourth red envelope holding the formal ten-column card for doing obeisance to the "old man," setting out three generations of one's family. I handed all four envelopes and the letter of introduction to the gatekeeper, who invited me to sit, and I sat. This was a rule handed down from the Qing: however exalted a pupil's own standing, on coming to his teacher's house he must walk into the gatehouse himself, and the gatekeeper would as a matter of form invite him to sit, and he had no choice but to sit — for not sitting would show contempt for the gatekeeper, and the gatekeeper could tell him to his face that he "didn't know the rules."
I sat in the gatehouse a good while before the gatekeeper came down from upstairs to ask me up. Teacher Hanyun was then living in a small Western-style house; the gatekeeper led me to the door of his smoking room, and one glance in showed me Teacher Hanyun in a long gown (ordinarily in the smoking room he wore only a short jacket) seated on the opium couch waiting for me. I went up to him and performed the "house rite" (the kowtow prescribed by the gang), and he had me sit on the other side of his couch and asked about my schooling and my career, all of which I answered truthfully. At the same time I asked him whether, in the gang, one addressed one's "old man" as "Master" or as "old man"; I felt "Master" too ordinary and "old man" too vulgar, and asked whether I might call him "teacher." He said I might, and so I called Teacher Hanyun my teacher.
Teacher Hanyun was of a very gentle temper, unwilling to give offense, and put on no airs with friends or disciples; he was easy and unceremonious. Whenever anyone came to see him, the gatekeeper would first go in to announce them, and if he saw the master nod on his opium couch, he would show the visitor in; the visitor would greet him, and he would go on smoking and nodding on the couch, never rising. In all my time I saw only two people for whom he rose to receive them and himself saw them to their car.
On his birth-mother's birthday I was ordered to receive the ordinary guests for him, and so I was in the smoking room all day. That afternoon the gatekeeper came rushing in to announce "the Fourth Master." The Fourth Master was his younger brother by the same mother; he rose at once, went down to meet him, and himself accompanied him upstairs to offer birthday greetings (his birth-mother lived upstairs too). When the two brothers came back to the smoking room, Teacher Hanyun introduced me: "This is your Fourth Uncle." I performed the house rite to this Fourth Uncle as the gang rules required, and he returned my kowtow, from which I knew that he was not in the gang. A little later the gatekeeper again came rushing in to announce "the Sixth Master," and Teacher Hanyun again rose to meet him; back in the smoking room he introduced him too: "This is your Sixth Uncle." I performed the house rite to the Sixth Uncle, and the Sixth Uncle likewise returned my kowtow. When these two uncles left, Teacher Hanyun saw each of them to his car in person. Toward evening the gatekeeper came in to say that several gentlemen had arrived and were in the parlor, and Teacher Hanyun told me to go out and receive them for him. At the table, once Teacher Hanyun had come out and offered the toasts himself, he handed me the wine pot and had me play host to the guests.
After the party broke up, I made my report to him and said that the two uncles just now had been too polite — that I, a mere boy, had performed the house rite to them and both had returned my kowtow. He said: "You came a step late today. Had your Senior Uncle" — meaning Yuan Keding — "still been here when you arrived, he'd have sat there without the slightest ceremony and taken your kowtow, and when you rose he'd even have said to you, 'I grant you a seat.' A few days ago Wanhua" — Mei Lanfang's style-name — "came to Tianjin, and because he's always been on close terms with your Senior Uncle, whenever he comes to Tianjin he always calls on your Senior Uncle first and then comes to see me; that's his old habit. The other day he saw me and said, 'His Lordship still hasn't woken from his crown-prince dream.'" Teacher Hanyun then went on: "Over that one phrase, 'I grant you a seat,' your Senior Uncle brought a great disaster on himself, and he still hasn't woken up — a pitiful thing."
I asked how he had brought the disaster on himself. He said: by then I'd already been driven out of Beijing by your Senior Uncle and your Third Uncle — this part I had from someone else. On the very morning Duan Qirui went to Xiaozhan and sent out the circular telegram denouncing Yuan, Duan had kowtowed to your Senior Uncle, and your Senior Uncle sat there without stirring, and when Duan had finished his kowtow and risen, said to him, "I grant you a seat." Duan swallowed it at the time, but once home he made all his arrangements and went to Xiaozhan to raise troops. Now Duan and your grandfather had been the closest of friends; how was he to bear being insulted like that by your Senior Uncle? Yet your Senior Uncle stayed obstinate to the end, refusing to see anyone who would not kowtow to him — no wonder Wanhua said he still hadn't woken from his crown-prince dream.
I asked: "You said just now, teacher, that you'd been driven out of Beijing by your Senior Uncle and your Third Uncle — how did that come about?" He said: "Because in a Japanese-run paper in Beijing, the Shuntian Times, I published an article opposing your grandfather's becoming emperor. At that time your Senior Uncle was set on being crown prince and hoped your grandfather would take the throne. Your Third Uncle said a cripple could not be crown prince — for your Senior Uncle was lame in one leg — and, pointing at me, he added, 'Second Brother doesn't approve of Father becoming emperor, so of course won't want to be crown prince either, which means it falls to me by right.' All this was said in front of your grandfather. When the two of them read my article opposing your grandfather's enthronement, both meant to do away with me. So your grandfather at once sent a secret wire to a seal-carver in his secretariat to cut a rectangular seal of four characters, 'the Sovereign's Second Son,' and had someone quietly urge me to use it immediately, to keep me from danger. I could not bear to slight the old man's kindness, so I had the seal stamped on my books, and that is why part of my library bears this seal. But the two of them still would not forgive me; and your grandfather, learning of it, seized a moment when the two were not present, called me by my milk-name, and said, 'Why not go out of the capital and amuse yourself?' I took his meaning, went home, first put the seal away, made a few arrangements, and that same afternoon went to your grandfather and said, 'I'll do as Father bids.' Your grandfather nodded, and I left the presidential palace alone, went straight to the East Station, took the train to Tianjin, and changed there for Shanghai. In those days each of us brothers had a presidential-palace secretary to keep us company in our reading and our leisure. The moment I left the palace that day, your grandfather sent someone to find me, and learning I had already set out, at once had my secretary Bu Zhangwu (styled Linwu Shanren) follow after me. After Bu and I met in Shanghai, we decided to 'honor the patriarch' before your Grandfather Zhang, opening an incense hall and doing obeisance to an 'old man,' so that your Senior Uncle and Third Uncle could do nothing against me."
He went on: "Once I had honored the patriarch and given the incense-hall banquet, whenever anyone of my own generation asked to exchange cards with me and become my sworn brother, I never refused; so every 'Da'-generation man in Shanghai, whatever his station high or low, exchanged cards with me." At this point he said to me gravely: "Now that you've come in at the gang's gate, you must never look down on the lower orders of society, for though their knowledge is simpler, so long as you don't despise them they can be a great help to us — this is what I learned after honoring the patriarch in Shanghai. From the day I honored the patriarch before your Grandfather Zhang, there were men at every moment and everywhere acting as my temporary bodyguards, all of them sent by first-rate people to protect me. Because I treated them all alike, high and low, they obeyed my every word. One day it suddenly struck me to go and amuse myself at the City God Temple in Nanshi, and I'd no sooner said so than one of these volunteer bodyguards said, 'Let me go and make arrangements, so everyone can be easy.' He went out for a while and came back to tell me, 'It's all arranged; once you've had your fill of the pipe, we'll go along with you.' When I'd smoked, I took them and got down at Xinbeimen, and there I saw someone whispering to the man who'd gone ahead to arrange things; and as I strolled along, sightseeing, I kept careful watch and noted that on every side of me there were men protecting me in secret."
One day I went to Teacher Hanyun's house and found a big-bearded guest already there before me. Teacher Hanyun introduced him: "This is Mr. Xu Langxi," and then wrote out a couplet to give him — the upper line "How much more can it bear of wind and rain," the lower line "Take up the whole of these hills and rivers." That day Mr. Xu and I walked out together as far as the tram stop before we parted ways. The next day I went back to the Yuan house and spoke warmly of the couplet the teacher had given Mr. Xu the day before, and Teacher Hanyun said he would give me one just the same; he had the man who prepared his pipe make ready paper and ink and did indeed write out the same couplet, differing only in the dedication — the one for Xu read "To my sworn elder brother Langxi," the one for me only "To younger brother Ti." Teacher Hanyun said: "In the 1911 uprising at Shanghai, both our Green and Hong gangs contributed — the Green Gang through Li Zhengwu, the Hong Gang through Xu Langxi."
Teacher Hanyun went on: "Speaking of the 1911 Revolution, I once brought on myself a disaster that nearly had the whole imperial house's vengeance fall on my family. At the time your grandfather was Governor-General of Zhili, and I had accepted a commission from Huang Xing; I wrapped the commission in several layers of oiled paper, put it in an iron case, and buried it in the flower bed in the courtyard of my bedroom inside the governor-general's yamen. That was reckless enough, but I also went and pasted revolutionary handbills outside the Zhengyangmen in Beijing myself, and being inexperienced and careless, I was found out, and word reached your grandfather at once. Your grandfather ordered me to hurry back through the night to our home village of Huanshangcun to study, forbidding me to go to Beijing or Tianjin. And if the news had reached your grandfather's ears, it could reach the Empress Dowager Longyu's ears too; luckily the Beiyang army at the time were all your grandfather's trusted men, and the Empress Dowager Longyu must have taken that into account, and so she only retired your grandfather at his existing rank. That was a rash act of my youth."
Teacher Hanyun's household observed the old rites of propriety. The first time I met the senior mistress, Meizhen, for instance, Teacher Hanyun introduced me and I performed the house rite to her; Mistress Meizhen returned my kowtow and called me "Master Chen." Afterward, whenever I came to Teacher Hanyun's and Mistress Meizhen happened to be in his smoking room, the moment the servants lifted the door curtain I would be sure to see Mistress Meizhen already standing perfectly upright there, and on seeing me she would call "Master Chen" and never fail to ask, "And how is Mrs. Chen?" On my grand-mistress's birthday I went to the birthday hall to offer greetings; Teacher Hanyun bade Mistress Meizhen take me upstairs, and the junior mistress, Meiyun, came along to the hall too. I made my bows to the birthday celebrant and, as was proper, offered my congratulations to Mistress Meizhen, who at once returned my kowtow; then I offered my congratulations to the junior mistress Meiyun, and she returned my kowtow just as Mistress Meizhen had.
When Teacher Hanyun and I met, apart from special occasions when we spoke briefly of the people and doings of the Green Gang, we talked of the beauties of nature or of matters of learning. He would often take a foreign dictionary of coins or of stamps and set me a passage to translate aloud for him. Since my French had gone rusty from long disuse, I could not translate fluently, and he said it was enough if I would translate for him the words I did understand. So I translated in fits and starts, and wherever I was stuck I would tell him that below such-and-such a word, above such-and-such another, there were one or a few words I could not make out, and he had only to think a moment before he could supply the words I could not and make sense of the whole. I took him for a man fond of antiquity but wanting in perseverance; he had read very widely, and had dabbled in "old books," "old coins," "Han jades," foreign gold and silver coins, foreign stamps, and the like.
One day I brought along the whole of my collection of Han jades and asked him to judge them. He looked them over and pointed out offhand which were fakes and which genuine but not fine. When I asked how he could tell these few were fakes and those not fine, he heard me out and then had the man who prepared his opium go to his bedroom and fetch two little chests; he unlocked them with a key, drew off the lids, and out came a great many drawers of different sizes. He showed me, drawer by drawer, where a matching fine piece lay, and let me take them out myself to compare, to sharpen my eye. He said the larger chest held medium and small pieces all of the Han, and the smaller chest medium and small pieces all of the Three Dynasties, and that of his Three-Dynasties jades the only recorded piece he still lacked was a "seven-stroke" jade fish carved by a famous Shang hand.
One day I mentioned to Teacher Hanyun that I wanted to set up an Overseas Chinese United News Agency together with the overseas-Chinese assemblyman Huang Bopao, but that the Beijing police office required two shop guarantors paying fourth-class rates or above, whose bonds had to be enclosed with the application, or it would not be accepted; and I was having trouble finding guarantors. Teacher Hanyun scolded me: why hadn't I said so sooner — the "apartment houses" and "carriage yards" of Beijing all pay fourth-class rates, and several of his own servants, the pipe-man included, ran "apartment houses" or "carriage yards" in Beijing. So saying, he told the pipe-man: go outside and get two guarantee bonds for Master Chen to take to Beijing. And so I got two bonds without the least trouble.
Another day I went to Teacher Hanyun's, and had scarcely said a few words when the gatekeeper came in to announce, "Mr. Wang, of the adjutant's office of Superintendent Chu" — that is, Chu Yupu, then superintendent of the whole province's military affairs in Zhili and concurrently governor. Teacher Hanyun said to me: "This man too is a 'twenty-two' (that is, of the twenty-second generation, the 'Tong' rank — my own generation). It's not convenient for me to have him in today; go out for me and speak with him, and if he has anything to say, pass it on. He came a few days ago and said Chu Yupu means to set up a government made up wholly of Green Gang men, and won't use anyone not 'of the household' (not of the Green Gang). He has a batch of friends in a great hurry to be introduced to me, to honor me as patriarch, so as to get posts under Chu Yupu; he may have come today about just that. If he wants to speak with me face to face, put him off till tomorrow." I went out and spoke with Wang, and it was indeed about that, and he hoped the incense-hall ceremony might be held soon; so I arranged for him to come the next day for a talk. The next day this Wang came, and Teacher Hanyun settled with him to open the incense hall on a certain day, and even named the men to serve as "instructing master," "sponsoring master," and "incense-bearer"; all the running about and arranging was left to Wang. Wang further raised the question of the incense-hall banquet, but Teacher Hanyun, holding that none of our group were the sort to want to make a splash in Tianjin, would invite no one beyond those strictly necessary, since the cost had to be shared equally by those "paying the great tribute" that day (formally honoring the patriarch and entering the gang), and Teacher Hanyun, considerate of us, cut the expense down. On the day the incense hall was opened, Teacher Hanyun had me count heads, and counting myself we were twenty in all.
About the time Duan Qirui was to step down, the Overseas Chinese United News Agency was founded, with Huang Bopao as director and me as manager. When we issued our first dispatches, I added a declaration of the "three-nots" principle: the "three nots" were not to spread rumors, not to take a subsidy from any office, and not to abuse people wantonly — a purely fact-respecting, detached line. Before long we had built up a reputation, and the papers of Beijing and Tianjin vied to use the Overseas Chinese Agency's copy. When Zhang Zongchang came to Beijing and Tianjin, it was just when the Beijing and Tianjin papers were competing for our copy; in talking with him I found he much approved of our fact-respecting spirit, and he even brought up Teacher Hanyun with me. I told him there was a little paper in Shanghai called The Crystal, got up by Teacher Hanyun and a few friends, though he held no title in it himself and only helped from behind the scenes. Zhang Zongchang said he hoped Teacher Hanyun would help him run a paper, and asked him to revisit Jinan and its old haunts. I reported all this to Teacher Hanyun.
That autumn I went with Teacher Hanyun to Jinan, where Zhang Zongchang made him a senior adviser and made me a colonel-councillor posted to Shanghai. Making Teacher Hanyun a senior adviser was a surface matter; the real intent was to have him go to Shanghai and run a paper for him, and so he allotted Teacher Hanyun 20,000 yuan as start-up funds. Zhang's secretary-general, Wu Tongyuan, also hung a few nominal posts on me, each paying 300 yuan a month, though because of arrears I could never draw them on time. Wu had long known that Teacher Hanyun kept a great many precious old jades and had wanted to get his hands on them but never had the chance; so, seizing the occasion of Zhang Zongchang's grant of 20,000 yuan, he worked both ends against the middle, breaking the sum into bits, and when Teacher Hanyun was in urgent need of money, he pressed him to hand over jades, remitting 2,000 yuan at a time only once a jade was in hand — with the result that every one of Teacher Hanyun's old jades ended up in Wu Tongyuan's possession.
I went with Teacher Hanyun as far as Pukou, where one of the Yuan disciples, Li Jinbiao, came specially to meet him; Teacher Hanyun asked how he had known he would arrive that day, and he said that The Crystal in Shanghai had been reporting the teacher's movements daily, which was how he knew. When we reached Shanghai we stayed at the Far Eastern Hotel on Xizang Road, in a suite of three rooms — one for Teacher Hanyun and Mistress Meiyun, one my bedroom, and the third for the teacher's attendants. Teacher Hanyun received callers and smoked in my room every day, and since The Crystal had published the date of his arrival in Shanghai and that he was to stay at the Far Eastern Hotel, that very evening a great many guests came; Teacher Hanyun introduced me to each of them, and from then on my room was full of distinguished company every day. On our second evening in Shanghai, Li Jinbiao gave a welcome dinner for Teacher Hanyun at the Grand Atlantic Western restaurant on Fuzhou Road, with some two hundred guests. The seat of honor was of course Teacher Hanyun's, and seated shoulder to shoulder with him was Mei Lanfang. Why did Li Jinbiao do this? Because his object was to get Mei Lanfang to sing a benefit performance for him, and he had Teacher Hanyun ask Mei; Mei, bound by his regard for Teacher Hanyun, of course granted Li Jinbiao's wish. Gu Zhuxuan, boss of the Tianchan Stage, provided a full company gratis — also out of regard for Teacher Hanyun. This was what was then called the way of the "man about town." Because Teacher Hanyun introduced me to each of the guests, most of those who invited him to dinner invited me along too.
Teacher Hanyun came to my room every day and, as usual, smoked while reading the papers. Whenever he saw in the day's paper a kidnapping, a killing of a hostage, or some other murder or larger robbery, he would be sure to ask Li Jinbiao who had done it; and Li Jinbiao, who knew Teacher Hanyun's turn of mind, had the day's cases all figured out beforehand, so that whatever Teacher Hanyun asked, he could answer without a hitch.
While we were at the Far Eastern Hotel, Xu Shiying came specially to call on Teacher Hanyun and asked him, on the auspicious day of his third daughter's marriage to a certain overseas Chinese, to appear in a guest role in the closing piece; Teacher Hanyun agreed then and there to play old Tang in Beheading Tang, and all the props needed — the beard, the hat, the robes — were things Teacher Hanyun wrote out notes for and had gathered up from old friends in the theatrical world such as Xia Yuerun. On the day itself I went in the daytime first to offer my congratulations to old uncle Xu, and in the evening went with Teacher Hanyun so as to see a masterpiece of my teacher's that I had never seen before. Who would have thought that Teacher Hanyun, who lay smoking opium on his couch all day, playing old Tang in Beheading Tang, would have such nimble, lively stage-steps and such clear diction that even the professionals were impressed? They had prepared a home performance that day, but because Teacher Hanyun was singing the closing piece, everyone in the troupe who knew him came to pay their respects, and did not leave until they had watched his Beheading Tang through.
Every day Teacher Hanyun wrote his Hanyun Diary and his letters, and inscribed whatever fans and other small pieces anyone brought him. As usual he lay face-up on the couch, his legs bent, brush in his right hand and the piece to be written in his left. Yu Daxiong of The Crystal often had Teacher Hanyun, between pipes, write a few characters to be made into a zinc plate and printed. Qian Jiechen too often had him write on the couch, and there was besides one Qian Huafo, who could paint Buddhist images and often brought his own work for Teacher Hanyun to inscribe.
On the twenty-fourth or twenty-fifth of the twelfth lunar month that year, Teacher Hanyun took Mistress Meiyun to Tianjin for the New Year. In the first lunar month of 1927 he came alone to Shanghai and stayed at the East Asia Hotel. At the request of Pu Yingxian, who had brought me into the gang, I drafted for him an application to manufacture an opium-cure medicine and asked Teacher Hanyun to correct it; he pointed out the unsuitable places and told me to revise them myself. At the time someone among my fellow disciples said that my asking the teacher to correct a draft about an opium-cure medicine was like cursing the bald monk to a monk's face — an act of betraying master and patriarch. The next day I went to Teacher Hanyun's, where as usual the fellow disciples filled the room; perhaps because my manner that day was odd, Teacher Hanyun asked where I had come from. I said I had heard that someone was saying I had betrayed my master and patriarch, and that I had come to own my fault. Teacher Hanyun said before them all, "Don't listen to them," and then asked whether Pu Yingxian's medicine worked. I said it did. He told me to bring some. Hearing this, I asked anxiously, "Does the teacher mean to quit the pipe?" He said he was preparing to hold off his craving on the train. Some days later Teacher Hanyun sent for me and said that from the day before he had begun quitting; he had first taken a German-made opium-cure medicine, without effect, and then taken the medicine Pu Yingxian had compounded, which indeed worked, and he told me to take him a few dollars to have more made up. I passed his words to Pu Yingxian, who said he would compound the medicine for the teacher free of charge, asking only that the teacher publish his progress in quitting daily in The Crystal and write a shop-sign for him. I told Teacher Hanyun, and he at once wrote the seven-character shop-sign "Puzi's Quick Opium Cure," and published in The Crystal, day by day, the course of his cure; and "Puzi's Quick Opium Cure" was at once thronged with custom.
That year Teacher Hanyun took as a concubine Yu Peiwen, of Yixing. One day I went to Teacher Hanyun's; he happened to be in the front room and I in the little back room, and he asked, "Who's come?" Yu Peiwen said, "Chen Timin." I was much displeased to hear Yu Peiwen address me so, and after that I went to Teacher Hanyun's less often.
In 1927 or 1928, Zhang Zongchang made Teacher Hanyun "Commander of the Advance Army"; the commission, I heard, was fetched by Li Jinbiao, from which one may guess that his becoming commander was Li Jinbiao's doing. A fellow disciple came to me and said: "The teacher is about to become Commander of the Advance Army; you ought to go to Tianjin to lend him your support and serve under him." I said: "It's right to support one's own 'old man,' but first one must consider what the support itself is worth. Once we've given it, will the teacher really gain by our support? Because this bears on the teacher's whole reputation, we disciples ought to weigh it together, and if we judge there is anything amiss, we should not only withhold our support but dissuade the teacher from having anything to do with it." He said: "The thing is settled; the teacher's forces are to be three armies, and our senior fellow-disciple Li Jinbiao and a certain fellow disciple who had been a division commander under Chu Yupu are both to be army commanders." I thought to myself that Li Jinbiao was one of the Shanghai big hoodlums, and that his followers and their followers might well number in the thousands or even tens of thousands, but they were a rabble not to be treated as regular troops; the man was hot for office and wanted to trade on Teacher Hanyun's name to satisfy his own itch to be an army commander, with no regard whatever for the teacher's reputation. After turning it over again and again, I decided to advise Teacher Hanyun against taking the post of Commander of the Advance Army. So I went to Teacher Hanyun's, and though the fellow disciples filled the room, I still whispered to him, laying out for him the points I thought unwise, and pressed him hard not to take the post; but good advice grates on the ear, and in the end he went to Tianjin and set up the headquarters of the Advance Army. The secretariat chief who went with him urged me to come along, but I found a pretext and declined.
Afterward I learned that this Advance Army headquarters was set up in Teacher Hanyun's former residence in Tianjin, and that it even established "eight bureaus," the reception bureau being made over from Teacher Hanyun's old gatehouse, with his former head steward as its director. After Li Jinbiao reached Tianjin, he took all of Teacher Hanyun's stewards, gatekeepers, servants, and pipe-men into his own following, counting them all as Li Jinbiao's disciples. In this way all the menservants of Teacher Hanyun's house became his grand-disciples and took to calling him "Grandfather." Teacher Hanyun was much displeased at this move of Li Jinbiao's, but the rice was cooked and could not be set right.
The headquarters was set up in name, but it never had more than a gate-guard and a few sentries. After some month of muddling along, the Nationalist Army arrived, the Advance Army headquarters was wiped out, and Teacher Hanyun's own residence, having served as its headquarters, was confiscated; he took temporary refuge in the Japanese Concession and soon after died of illness. I heard that Yuan Keding, on receiving a telegram from his mother-in-law Meizhen, came specially to Tianjin to see to Teacher Hanyun's funeral.
Teacher Hanyun had three sons, Jia'gu, Jiazhang, and Jialiu, of whom he said the third was the cleverest. In 1927, after he took a Jiaxing woman as concubine, a fourth son was born, whose name I have forgotten.
