## 10

The cheering that greeted victory in the war had not yet died away when the campaign to punish the traitors began. Day and night the streets of Shanghai rang with police cars tearing past, sirens wailing. Many were dragged from their homes; but often, before a man had so much as reached the prison, his property had already been carved up, and his wife and daughters carved up along with it.

The victors have always celebrated their victory by plunder.

Before Lin Nansheng had even reached Shanghai, his apartment stood ready — in the Edinburgh Building, beside the Jing'an Temple. It was a modest gift from his students and colleagues, in honor of his appointment as deputy of the Shanghai Committee for the Suppression of Traitors. But his weightier task was to help his superiors set up the Shanghai office of the Sino-American Cooperative Organization.

The man who came to meet his plane at Hongqiao was a student from his training class, now a company commander in the Loyal and Patriotic Army. He handed Lin Nansheng into the car but did not drive straight to the Edinburgh Building; instead they went to the small banquet room of the Cathay Hotel, where a welcome dinner had been laid on for him. Yet the instant Lin Nansheng stepped into the octagonal hall, he thought of Miss Lan.

After the dinner Lin Nansheng came out amid the press of them, and still did not return to the Edinburgh apartment; instead he walked on and on down Nanjing Road. To the student who insisted on seeing him home he said, "I know this place better than you. Let me walk a while on my own."

Lin Nansheng wandered the streets of Shanghai the whole night through. As day broke he took a rickshaw across the Suzhou Creek bridge to the little building where he had once lived with Zhu Yizhen, and stood on the far side of the road, gazing up a long time at that attic window.

Now, apart from work, the social rounds, and sleep, Lin Nansheng spent every idle hour poring over the files the Japanese and the puppet regime had left behind — he did not even pass over the medical records at the Renji Hospital — and yet he found no trace whatever of Zhu Yizhen from around the Spring Festival of 1942, nor any of himself.

A month later Lin Nansheng made contact with the Shanghai organization for the first time, at a time and place fixed long before, back in Chongqing. He pushed open a door at the Chunshen Hotel to find a man in black-framed glasses seated by the tea table, watching him with a smile. "You're late," the man said.

Lin Nansheng shut the door. "I had to be sure we were both safe."

"My name is Pan." The man rose and put out his hand. "You can call me Old Pan."

Lin Nansheng took Old Pan's hand. "I know you. You were once Zhu Yizhen's handler." Old Pan started, and before he could speak Lin Nansheng smiled. "I tailed her, in the old days."

"Let the past be." Old Pan poured him a cup of water and began to speak of the peace talks in Chongqing, and of the Nationalist army's reorganization of the puppet forces. He let out a long sigh. "The agreement is signed, but I fear the curtain still won't come down on this war."

Lin Nansheng said lightly, "Political struggle — it's the old men raising their glasses and talking away happily, while the children go at each other with their fists under the table."

"Intelligence work is the same." Old Pan changed the subject and set to laying out the work — their transmission routes, the methods of handover, the fallback plans, every mishap that might arise in the passing of intelligence and how to meet it. When he had gone through them one by one, he put out his hand to Lin Nansheng again and said with a smile, "From today, we're two grasshoppers tied to one string."

But Lin Nansheng kept his seat, with no sign of taking his leave. He looked up at Old Pan. "You have to tell me about Zhu Yizhen."

Old Pan knotted his brows and narrowed his eyes, as though searching his memory for such a person, and thought a long time before he said, "If she's still alive, she should be in the base area in northern Jiangsu." Then he looked at Lin Nansheng. "That's all I know, and all I can tell you." In fact, at that very moment Zhu Yizhen was in Shanghai, living in an apartment with an attic on Middle Zhejiang Road. The man she lived with was Meng Annan, who now went by the name Li Guangwen and worked as an editor at the French Photo Agency. Each day, as he left for work with his briefcase, Zhu Yizhen would climb to the attic. It had become her embroidery room, hung all over with colored threads and needlework, and in a hidden compartment below the windowsill sat a wireless transmitter.

Beyond being Mrs. Li, Zhu Yizhen had another, more secret identity: she was Meng Annan's radio operator. They had met for the first time the night before they left the northern Jiangsu base, in a thatched hut outside the town of Funing. Across the light of a single hurricane lamp, Meng Annan studied her a while with an examining eye. "You know Shanghai, and you've played at being a married couple before. You're the best choice for this." Seeing Zhu Yizhen keep her head down and say nothing for a long time, he went on, "Of course, you may refuse. I would understand how you feel."

"I obey orders," Zhu Yizhen said, lifting her head.

"Good, then." Meng Annan closed the file in his hands. "Go back and get ready. We leave tomorrow."

"Yes." Zhu Yizhen rose, and at the door she suddenly turned back. "I'd like to know — whose wife am I?"

"Mine," Meng Annan said.

Zhu Yizhen gave him one last look, left the hut, and when she had walked the pitch-dark path to the edge of the village she had no strength left to move another step. She leaned against the rim of a dry well and, little by little, sank to the ground; the place in her chest where a bullet had once passed clean through began again to ache dully.

To this day Zhu Yizhen did not know how she had left Shanghai after she was shot. When she woke she was already in a German clinic in Jiaxing. The one who tended her was an aged Jewish woman, the clinic's proprietress and its only nurse. In the three months and more that followed, Zhu Yizhen asked her more than once, "Who was it that brought me here?" And each time the aged Jewish nurse would shake her ash-gray head and say, in fluent Chinese, "It was God, my child."

So, once healed, Zhu Yizhen became the clinic's second nurse. Then, late one night at the height of summer, she slipped away, took a yarn-hauling boat, and came ashore at the Shiliupu wharf, back in Shanghai at last — like a woman down on her luck who had come in search of kin and found none. Day after day she lodged among the meanest inns of Zhabei, in with the refugees, the fortune-tellers, the kidnappers and confidence men, the petty hawkers. Zhu Yizhen spent every last coin she had on placing notices in search of a missing person; it was her only way of reaching the organization.

At last, after a month and more of waiting and searching, Zhu Yizhen met Old Pan at the back gate of Zhaofeng Park.

But the moment they met, Old Pan said, "By the principles of the organization, you shouldn't be going about looking for me. You've been missing more than half a year."

"I'm not looking for you. I'm looking for the organization." Zhu Yizhen said it with her eyes on a newsstand across the road.

"By the principles of the organization, I shouldn't have come to see you either." Old Pan sighed, drew out a few fabi notes and pressed them into her hand. "Another day we'll meet somewhere else."

Two days later, when they met again, Old Pan listened in silence as she finished telling of this past half-year, then set a manila envelope before her. "Take this money. Once you've left Shanghai, find somewhere to settle properly."

"I don't want money." Zhu Yizhen bowed her head. "I didn't take up this work for money."

"But you know this work has its rules. You've been off the line too long." Old Pan said, "All this past half-year and more, we've looked for you through every channel, and turned up not a single lead."

"So you suspect I betrayed the organization."

"If you had betrayed the organization, it would not be me you were meeting today."

"The traitor-killing squad, you mean?" Zhu Yizhen looked straight into the eyes behind his glasses and said, word by word, "Mission failed, assignment ended, identity exposed, to be withdrawn to the old home — those were the orders the organization gave me before I came to Shanghai."

Old Pan bowed his head, and after a long silence said, "I can arrange for you to go back. But once you reach the base area, you'll face a rigorous investigation." "And only the investigation will prove me clean," she said.

"It could well cost you your life."

Zhu Yizhen faltered. "I'm not afraid. I've died once already."

Old Pan said not one word more. Half a month later, the moment Zhu Yizhen reached the base area she was taken into custody. In an interrogation room made over from a woodshed, she said to the two New Fourth Army cadres examining her, "I can tell you nothing. That is the principle of the organization — unless you are my direct superiors."

"Don't be so pig-headed. This is a campaign. We are here to rescue you."

Zhu Yizhen shook her head, turned her face to the ray of sunlight coming through the window, and said not one word more. She was held until the spring of the next year before they let her go. The superior who came to receive her strode up and gripped her hand, worked his mouth a good while, yet not a single word came out — as though it were he who had been shut away so long.

Zhu Yizhen was strangely calm, only a little weak. In a hoarse voice she said, "Chief, may I rejoin my unit now?"

The superior gave a hard nod. "I've come to bring you back to your unit." When he had said it, he looked at Zhu Yizhen and added, "It's nothing. For the revolution, a little wrong suffered counts for nothing."
