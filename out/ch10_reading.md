## Chapter 10. The Open Scheme: The Secret Contest Between Peace and War

The fourteen bitter years of the War of Resistance had entered the phase of the counter-offensive, and Japan was visibly finished. On the great land of China stood two principal forces, the Nationalists and the Communists, and whether peace or war would come between them was a question that bore on the fate of the nation.

The Chinese Communist Party's Seventh National Congress had lifted every heart. The shadow of the Rescue Campaign had scattered like cloud before wind, the effects of the Yan'an Rectification were quickly telling, and the whole Party was united to seize the victory.

In the same season the Nationalists convened their own Sixth National Congress, and the central task Chiang Kai-shek laid down was to "wipe out the Communist Party."

Nationalists and Communists alike were straining every muscle to grab territory. On the surface they still hailed one another as friendly parties and friendly armies, but under the table their intelligence services were already kicking each other's shins.

### 1. The Secret Front Locks Horns Early

The main task of Communist intelligence work now turned to gathering the Nationalists' anti-Communist military intelligence. The Border Security's own intelligence work, suspended during the campaign, was being restored at a gallop.

Heshui county in Longdong lay close to the Nationalist areas. Chou Zilin, a clerk in the county education section, worked on a guard-detachment soldier to make him turn traitor, and it was only because Yan Fu learned of it in advance that the man was seized on the spot. The Heshui guard-detachment mutiny case shook the Border Region, and everyone saw the truth of it: a new stage had come, and the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region was no longer the rear of the War of Resistance but the front line of the Nationalist-Communist struggle.

The Guanzhong district, always thick with troops, was for the moment left thinly held. The main Nationalist forces stationed there, along with the Border Security's 8th Guards Regiment, had all gone north to contest Suiyuan. Wang Feng of the Guanzhong sub-district Security Office seized the chance, incited a Nationalist security regiment to revolt, and rode the momentum into Chunhua, the county town lost in 1942. The people of the Border Region followed the troops into the town to carry off supplies, and Qin Ping, the field-group chief, fixed his eye on the Nationalist archives and made a rich haul.

The second Chunhua incident broke out. Hu Zongnan, who had long been hunting for a pretext, at once mustered six divisions and struck in three columns, and Yetai Mountain became the focus of the fighting.

The fighting shook Yan'an. The Border Region government organs prepared to move, the Central Military Commission shifted troops and commanders, and the units covered six days' road in three, reaching the Yetai Mountain front to reinforce. After holding for seven days, the Guanzhong sub-district troops pulled back from their positions of their own accord, the Nationalist forces having driven twenty kilometers deep along a hundred-kilometer front.

The Guanzhong sub-office had stirred up trouble, yet it drew no harsh criticism from the Center, for this reason: the Nationalists had already resolved to launch a civil war, and if you did not strike him, he would strike you.

The Party Center wired Chiang Kai-shek and Hu Zongnan to halt the attack and withdraw to their former lines. It also invited the American observer group stationed in Yan'an, escorted by Yang Shangkun and Ma Haide, to inspect Guanzhong, and mounted a propaganda offensive at the same time, publishing the truth of the Nationalist forces' seizure of Border Region ground. Hu Zongnan, fresh from his gains, paid no mind to these theatrics and drove his army on northward. In fact the Communists were preparing theatrics of their own with steel: the 8th Regiment of the 358th Brigade, the 16th Regiment of the New 4th Brigade, and the 1st Battalion of the 3rd Regiment of the 1st Guards Brigade counter-attacked together and took Yetai Mountain in two days, and Hu Zongnan had to cough up all the ground he had just swallowed.

The battle of Yetai Mountain foretold that a general civil war was drawing ever closer. The Border Security placed intelligence work first among all its tasks, put forward a new policy of "bold, open-handed pioneering" and a new working style of "going to the masses, going to society," and filled out and strengthened its intelligence ranks. It set up a separate intelligence section, with Li Qiming, who had run intelligence work since 1942, as its chief, and each sub-office built an intelligence unit of its own.

Following the Center's decision on investigation and study, the Border Security's intelligence section added a department to collect and analyze books, papers, and magazines. Cai Ziwei, once suspected of being a Trotskyist, now ran this press unit. The section also looked to modern technical means and set up a monitoring station. The confidential unit's Huang Bin and He Biao, the radio operator Li Congzhou, the technical-reconnaissance group's Luo Zhen, the code-breaking group's Yu Hong, the technical secretary Chai Jiqun, and more than ten cadres seconded from the Social Affairs Department worked in close concert, plucking out of the air the wireless traffic among Luochuan, Xifeng, Xi'an, and Chongqing. Through inside sources the Border Security had already obtained thirteen kinds of Zhongtong cipher, and had itself worked out four kinds of military cipher used by Hu Zongnan's system, uncovering through decryption a number of hidden agents and secret couriers. Some sub-offices set up stations too; Chen Changhao's son served as a cipher clerk at the Guanzhong station.

Working with a free hand, Li Qiming restored and rebuilt the intelligence groups in Guanzhong, Longdong, Sanbian, Linzhen, and Yanchang that had been pulled back during the cadre-vetting campaign, and the outpost at Fu county, which had been kept up throughout, was reinforced as well.

Bu Lu, chief of the Suide sub-office, was a born detective, and now he had room to show his powers, extending his intelligence feelers into Deng Baoshan's Yulin region, Yan Xishan's Shanxi region, and the Japanese-occupied region of Inner Mongolia. Miao Leshan, a Zhongtong agent, was branch-office secretary-general at Zhenchuanbao in the Nationalist areas, but his old mother and his wife lived back home in Jia county inside the Border Region. Bu Lu called on them in person, arranged for the village to help with the harvest, and Miao Leshan's mother and wife were so grateful that they talked him into gathering intelligence for the Communists. Bu Lu then directed Miao Leshan to cultivate, as opportunity offered, the Nationalist branch-office secretary-general Li Wenfang. Through that connection he not only obtained the Zhongtong intelligence out of Yulin but also watched for chances to set Zhongtong against the 22nd Army.

Li Qiming, chief of the Border Security's intelligence section, meanwhile set to work on the Juntong colonel Wei Liang. He had Song Yangchu use his ties as a fellow townsman to win over Wei Liang's wife, Wang Qiao, and through Wang Qiao he won over Wei Liang. In this way the Border Security came to hold, in the main, the Nationalist secret-service system in Yulin. Zhongtong's and the Military Council's directives on gathering Communist intelligence, the Nationalist Sixth Congress documents, the record of Yan Xishan's secret talks with the Japanese in Taiyuan, Zhongtong's Shaanxi provincial office manual *Methods of Parasitic Work*, Yan Xishan's *Twenty-One Articles on Detecting Communist Elements*, and the *Detailed Rules for Carrying Out Military and Civil Political Security* all fell into the Border Security's net.

Zhongtong had little to show for its espionage against the Border Region and badly needed first-hand knowledge of Yan'an, but no one it might send would dare go. At this point Li Maotang, head of Zhongtong's Shaanxi investigation office, volunteered: I'll make the trip to Yan'an myself!

In the autumn of 1945 Li Maotang was warmly received in Yan'an by the Party Center's Intelligence Department. On examination the Department judged that although Li Maotang had once turned himself in, he had not sold out the organization, and now had rendered outstanding service; it decided to develop him as a "special Party member." Mao Zedong, who approved his admission in person, laughed: "Two directors sponsoring a third director into the Party." Just so. His sponsors, Luo Qingchang, was director of the Social Affairs Department's First Office and Wang Dongxing of its Second Office; the man being brought in, Li Maotang, was director of Zhongtong's Shaanxi provincial office. Three directors, chiefs of the secret services on the two sides of the Nationalist-Communist divide, and all three of them Communist Party members. Kang Sheng, head of the Intelligence Department, gladly announced the appointments too: Wang Chaobei as chief of the Xi'an intelligence station, Li Maotang as his deputy.

Home safe in Xi'an, Li Maotang was again commended by his Zhongtong superiors: into the tiger's den indeed! Little did Xu Enzeng know that this venture into the tiger's den had in fact delivered up a tiger cub.

Such a "special Party member" had to be approved by a high-level leading organ of the Party; once admitted he did not reveal his identity but worked in secret for the Party. Among the Communists' special Party members were some notable figures: Yang Du, one of the "seven gentlemen" of the Chou'an Society who had once backed Yuan Shikai's bid to make himself emperor; the Nationalist elder statesman Wang Kunlun; the famous journalist Fan Changjiang; the Democratic League stalwart Hu Yuzhi. Such special members did intelligence, security, united-front, and liaison work in secret, and could often play a special part beyond the reach of an ordinary member.

The admission of the senior Zhongtong agent Li Maotang set the precedent for taking "secret agents" into the Party. On June 30, 1946, the Party Center's Northwest Bureau issued the *Regulations on Resolving the Party-Membership Question for Field Personnel*. Field personnel then fell into four categories: first, progressives of every class; second, those who had drifted from the Party; third, secret-service men; and fourth, those who had turned themselves in or defected. The document laid down different vetting conditions, probation periods, and admission procedures for each category; the highest grade, the "special Party member," required a recommendation from the Northwest Bureau's Social Affairs Department, review by the Northwest Bureau, and approval by the Center.

On September 7, 1946, by special sanction of the Northwest Bureau, Xue Haoping and Chen Kai sponsored Wu Nanshan into the Party. The Longdong prefectural committee held a swearing-in ceremony for Wu Nanshan, with deputy secretary and organization head Shang Yueming administering the oath. Qi Sanyi, Li Chunmao, Wang Xingwen, and others were likewise approved for membership by the Northwest Bureau.

For those who had thrown themselves into the revolution, nothing mattered more than their political life. For men like Wu Nanshan, Sun Tzu's "lure him with gain" in the use of spies was the lesser thing; the greater was "guide him and release him." That the Party trusted them and let them work with a free hand was worth more than any material reward.

The Border Security made bold use of men from the enemy's secret services who had confessed. The Longdong intelligence group led by Yu Kai (Qin Shenzhi) had more than ten members from Juntong's old Hanzhong class, and men like Qi Sanyi and Li Chunmao, who knew Juntong's ways, proved highly capable. Zhu Langzhou even served as chief of the Yichuan intelligence group. The Longdong field group had two sub-groups under it, at Xihuachi and Mengba, and built a number of inside lines in the Nationalist areas at Xifeng town, Pingliang, and several counties. The secretary of the Zhenyuan county branch office, a Zhongtong operative, the Juntong group leader at Pingliang, and a clerk at the Xifeng commissioner's office all kept contact with the Border Security, and the doings of Zhongtong's Xifeng district office were entirely within its view.

Clear now on policy, the Border Security's intelligence groups also pressed the "drawing out" strategy hard, working to build inside lines within the Nationalist army.

The officer Chen Rujie sought out the Border Security on his own and was posted to the garrison at Luochuan. A second lieutenant clerk in the political department of the 165th Division, Chen Rujie went on to recruit the very man ordered to watch him, the lieutenant staff officer Liu Liangji, and Liu Liangji in turn used old-schoolmate ties to get close to Feng Jiyi, head of Juntong's Luochuan group. Chen Rujie and Liu Liangji secretly reported the whole order of battle of the 165th Division and the minutes of Luochuan's joint Party-government-army briefings, and in the spring of 1946 they sent the Border Security the *Bandit-Suppression Directive* newly issued to the Nationalist army.

The junior Nationalist officer Zheng Lian came over to Yan'an, was trained by the Guanzhong sub-office, and sent back. At the Provisional 59th Division in Baoji, Zheng Lian recruited the staff officers Sun Yuanchang, Zheng Hongfei, Lu Wende, and Chen Limin, along with Sun's wife Chen Yuqin and Zheng's wife Guo Shuxun, forming a secret intelligence group that reported back on enemy dispositions in Baoji.

Lu Nan, who had been pulled back to the Border Region to take part in the Rectification, was sent out again to Suiyuan, where he won the regard of Zhang Qing'en, head of Zhongtong's Suiyuan office, and became chief of the research section in the secretariat of the Suiyuan provincial special conference, gaining a grasp of the whole Nationalist Party-government-army secret-service picture in Suiyuan.

Historians call the Yan'an Rectification the second movement for the liberation of thought in Chinese history. And the liberation of thought liberated Communist intelligence work too. If the Border Security's work had earlier been mainly defensive, one great feature of it in this stage was the turn to the offensive.

The working spirit of "bold, open-handed pioneering" turned into hard results: the Border Security's intelligence strength grew fast, quickly finding solid footing at key points in the Nationalist areas around the rim of the Border Region, then reaching its feelers into the central cities of the Nationalist areas. Xi'an, Lanzhou, Yinchuan, Guisui, Baotou, Wuyuan, Shanxi, all had nails planted by the Border Security's intelligence organs. It sent out secret cadres like Liu Wu to build intelligence outposts at Lintong and Baqiao near Xi'an, and contrived to work its way into the Nationalists' "People's Self-Salvation Army," taking control of part of the force. In that single short year of 1945, the Border Security's intelligence strength grew by 374 percent over the year before.

It also gathered much valuable experience: on organizing its strength, on choosing and using a "bridge," on picking the moment when "drawing out" a man, on joining political means to armed force, on exploiting the enemy's contradictions to deepen his divisions, on directing the counter-espionage struggle, and so on. At the start of the war the Border Security's cadres had still been learning to do intelligence work; eight years on, a whole crop of them, Li Qiming among them, had become intelligence experts.

The War of Resistance was won. Where would China go now?

The whole Party was of one mind. The ambitious Communists would not stop at holding a mountain as their own kingdom; they meant to steer the whole national situation.

The intelligence front was on the offensive, its spirit up. It would no longer be content to bag a few agents; it stood ready at any moment to serve the Party's strategic tasks.

### 2. The Chongqing Negotiations: A Play Performed in Earnest

On August 10, 1945, Japan sent its note suing for surrender. That very night, in Yan'an, Eighth Route Army commander-in-chief Zhu De ordered the forces everywhere to press their attacks on the Japanese and puppet troops, take their surrender, and take over the towns.

On August 11, the Yan'an headquarters issued six orders in a row: seize every city and communications junction that could and must be seized, and the Ji-Re-Liao forces were to march for the Northeast. The situation was fine: the great and middling cities and the communications junctions of north and central China all lay within the encirclement of the Eighth Route and New Fourth Armies, an easy step into any town. Nearest to the Northeast, too, stood the Eighth Route Army, and there a vacuum of power would open.

On August 11, Chongqing issued two orders as well. Chiang Kai-shek called on each war zone to press its operations harder, and ordered the 18th Group Army to hold its ground and await orders. Chiang was anxious: his Central Army forces were huddled in the southwest and northwest, far from the great and middling cities. His plan was to pin down the Communists' 18th Group Army, forbid it to move, and rush the Central Army into position to grab the ground.

Even the Americans could read this posture. President Truman held that "actually Chiang Kai-shek would have great difficulty occupying even South China; to get North China he would have to reach an agreement with the Communists, and if he did not come to terms with the Communists and the Russians, he could forget about getting into the Northeast."

Chiang had a clever stroke of his own. On August 14, Chongqing wired Yan'an: Chiang invited Mao Zedong to come to Chongqing to negotiate. Chongqing at the same time sent Yan'an another telegram, ordering the Nationalist liaison staff officers stationed in Yan'an to sound out Mao Zedong's intentions.

The Party Center judged that Chiang had already resolved on civil war, and that this telegram served only two ends. One was to make Mao's refusal to go to Chongqing the pretext for laying the blame for war on the Communists; and if Mao did go to negotiate, to offer the Communists a few ministerial seats, force them to hand over their army, and then destroy it. The other was to use the talks to stall for time while shifting troops to seize the ground in the occupied areas. Mao Zedong wired Chiang in reply that he would send Zhou Enlai to Chongqing. At the same time Mao received the Nationalist liaison staff officers posted in Yan'an and told them to their faces that for now he was not preparing to go.

The Nationalist liaison staff officers in Yan'an, Zhou Liwu and Luo Bolun, had spent these days poking about the town for Mao Zedong's intentions, and every word they got was that he could not possibly go to Chongqing. So the two sent a secret report to Chongqing: Mao Zedong would not go to negotiate.

This was exactly the intelligence Chiang wanted. He made no preparation whatever for peace talks, and instead moved troops and commanders to snatch the ground of the Northeast and North China.

On August 23, Chiang, who knew perfectly well Mao was not coming, wired Yan'an a third time: "An aircraft now stands ready to receive you; again I wire, urging your swift departure." The sham play was performed in earnest, to a great crash of gongs and drums: the Central Broadcasting Station put it out again and again, the newspapers reprinted it in a rush, and in no time Chiang's goodwill toward peace was known at home and abroad. America and the Soviet Union both called on China to keep the peace, the middle parties within the country were stirred, and the ball of peace was kicked into Yan'an's court.

The same day, Mao Zedong convened an enlarged meeting of the Central Political Bureau, which decided he would go to Chongqing to negotiate, with Liu Shaoqi acting in his place and Chen Yun and Peng Zhen added to the Secretariat. The next day the Communist generals Liu Bocheng, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yi, Lin Biao, Chen Geng, Bo Yibo, and Xiao Jingguang left Yan'an aboard American aircraft, bound for their posts to make ready for war.

On August 28, Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai, and Wang Ruofei, escorted by the American ambassador Hurley and by Zhang Zhizhong, head of the political department of the National Government's Military Council, arrived by air in Chongqing.

In Chongqing, Chiang Kai-shek was thrown into a scramble. Inviting Mao Zedong had been a sham play to begin with, and he had never imagined Mao would perform the sham in earnest. Certain Mao would not come, he had prepared no lines, yet the Communists laid out a whole set of negotiating proposals drafted long before.

And so the Nationalist-Communist talks, proposed by Chiang, unfolded along Mao's script.

The great cause of Chiang's helplessness was that one telegram from the Nationalist liaison staff officers in Yan'an. Chiang had taken the secret wire for inside Communist intelligence and concluded Mao would not come.

He never knew that this telegram too had reached Mao Zedong's hand. Only once he knew Chiang had swallowed the bait did Mao spring his surprise.

Through intelligence means he had carried out a strategic feint. The Communists' political skill had reached a fine polish; never again would they be duped and lose their heads as in the first Nationalist-Communist collaboration. This great success in political struggle was one more outstanding example of intelligence serving strategy.

At the start of the War of Resistance, when the Nationalists and Communists reached their collaboration, the Nationalists posted liaison staff officers to the Communist-led forces. The three Eighth Route Army divisions each got one such officer, all under the direct command of the Nationalist secret-service chief Kang Ze, and their main task was not to help the Communist forces fight Japan but to gather intelligence. In the winter of 1939, when the Nationalists launched their first anti-Communist high tide, the Communists forbade these three to return to the units. So the three liaison staff officers stayed on long-term in Yan'an, lodged at the Border Region government's reception office, and became the Nationalists' open intelligence officers in Yan'an.

The reception office run by Jin Cheng had the job of receiving high-ranking visitors from outside and doing united-front and diplomatic work, and of keeping watch on these plainly labeled agents. The Social Affairs Department was short-handed, and the Security Office came up to help. Wang Zaitian served as secretary of the reception office, the plainclothes-squad leader Liu Jianfu as a clerk, and Yang Huanglin as an instructor in the hospitality section, directing a dozen or so attendants who used their duties as cover to watch the men on the spot. When the three went out, the plainclothes-squad branch secretary Wang Lin shadowed them outside. Under a dense web of watchers, every word and move of the two Nationalist liaison staff officers was within the Border Security's grasp.

The two liaison staff officers often sent wireless messages in secret. The 7th Section, in charge of technical reconnaissance, hid its equipment in Yang Huanglin's cave-dwelling and monitored them from there. But the cipher the officers used was of a very high grade, one the inside sources of the Border Security and the Social Affairs Department had not obtained. The Border Security's attempts at decryption never succeeded, so it tried to get hold of the codebook itself. But the two men were highly wary and always carried the codebook on them when they went out, and the Border Security could get nowhere.

For liaison staff officers used to a life of ease, being stationed in Yan'an was a hard posting indeed. Yet of late this hard life had begun to change: the meals grew better and better, the attendants even helped air their bedding, and Yan'an's dances now invited them along. So the Communists really were treating them as a friendly army? The two officers gradually relaxed, and going out to dance with the thick codebook still stuffed in a pocket began to seem unbecoming.

The reception office made a point of inviting the two out to Dufuchuan in the suburbs for an outing. No sooner were they out the door than Yang Huanglin slipped inside, smartly opened the brass lock, and drew the codebook from the case. Poor Yan'an lacked even a camera. The thick cipher had to be copied out by hand. As the two officers made their way back from the outing, the Border Security's secret sentries relayed word home all along the route like beacon towers, and here at the reception office the copying was not yet done and had to be hurriedly put back as it had been.

It helped to have senior leaders in on it. Eighth Route Army chief of staff Ye Jianying came forward to invite the two to a banquet and a play, and the two liaison staff officers went out in high spirits, while over here the lock was opened and the copying begun at once. Huang Bin, of the third class at Qilipu, had studied wireless work at the Military Commission's Third Bureau and had long wanted to break the officers' cipher; his hands trembled with excitement as he copied. There was so much of it that it took three separate goes to copy it all.

Some of the telegraph traffic between the liaison staff officers and Chongqing went through the Yan'an telegraph office. The intelligence force the Border Security had placed inside the office secretly copied the message drafts and sent them to the Border Security, and the Military Commission's Second Bureau then decrypted them against the cipher. In this way Yan'an held the whole of the secret communications between the Nationalist liaison staff officers and Chongqing: it truly knew the enemy and knew itself.

The Chongqing negotiations were like a modern-day Feast at Hongmen, only this drama was finer than the ancient one.

Chiang Kai-shek had meant to put on a play to deceive, and found himself instead directed by the other side, the agents he had posted turned into buffoons out of the tale of Jiang Gan. Mao Zedong, all passive avoidance on the surface, stepped suddenly onto the stage, with intelligence men at his side as able as Zhou Yu.

### 3. Who Missed the Historic Opportunity?

Thunder for a thousand li, lightning for ten thousand: the news of victory in the War of Resistance came fast. On September 5, 1945, Japan surrendered. Yan'an boiled over. The banks of the Yan River filled with young people leaping and dancing, and into the night the hillsides were still ablaze with torches.

That day and that night, Su Ping ran the whole town, ran Baota Mountain and the banks of the Yan, and still was not tired. Persecuted in the Rescue Campaign, she had been cleared in the screening and was now studying at the Central Party School. In these days the school argued the whole day long. Some said the day marked the turn from war to peace, and a new stage of peaceful, democratic China was at hand. Others said it marked the turn from a national war to a class war, and China's war of liberation was at hand. The two camps argued fiercely, but they shared one point: a time of turning had come for China's future, and the contest between Communists and Nationalists was about to begin.

In Chongqing, Chiang Kai-shek, receiving Mao Zedong, was well pleased with himself.

On September 9, 1945, in the great hall of the Central Military Academy in Nanjing, China's capital, came the surrender ceremony of the Japanese forces in the China theater. General Okamura Yasuji, commander-in-chief of the China Expeditionary Army, bowed deeply before General He Yingqin, minister of military administration and chief of the general staff, China's plenipotentiary for the surrender, and handed over his sword.

This was a moment of glory for the Chinese.

For the first time since the Opium War of 1840, the Chinese nation had won a war against invasion.

And who was commander-in-chief of the China theater? Chiang Kai-shek.

Commander-in-chief Chiang forbade the Eighth Route and New Fourth Armies to take any surrender, and sent his own trusted generals across China to take it instead. Under the international surrender arrangements of the *Potsdam Proclamation*, everything north of the 16th parallel was China's to receive. That zone took in all Chinese territory apart from the Northeast, and also northern Vietnam, Taiwan, and the Ryukyus, all seized by Japan.

The most striking of all was the surrender of Taiwan: since the *Treaty of Shimonoseki* of 1895, Taiwan had been in Japanese hands for fifty years.

In modern times great stretches of Chinese territory had been seized by the powers; this was the first time China had recovered its own land.

The most imposing surrender of all was the one taken abroad.

Long Yun got Chiang Kai-shek's order: the Yunnan Army was to march to Vietnam to take the surrender.

On September 18, 1945, in Hanoi, Vietnam, at the former residence of the French governor-general of Indochina, Lu Han, commander-in-chief of China's First Front Army, received the surrender of the Japanese forces in Vietnam. Beside Lu Han stood American and British generals as Allied representatives; behind them were the guest seats, where Ho Chi Minh and other leaders of the Vietnamese provisional government looked on.

This was the first time a Chinese army had taken a surrender on foreign soil.

The object of the surrender was the once-mighty Japanese imperial army, the army that had been the main force of the Eight-Nation Alliance that invaded China.

Just as the Yunnan Army was rejoicing, Chiang Kai-shek was preparing to move against its home.

On the political stage of China after the War of Resistance stood three forces: the Nationalists, the Communists, and the Democratic League of the democratic parties. In the nationwide war of resistance, Chiang had managed to absorb the warlords of one region after another, and by the time victory came, only two places in the whole country were still not fully his to command. In the northwest was the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, always under Communist leadership, a thorn in old Chiang's side that he ached to be rid of. In the southwest was Yunnan province, where everything from personnel to finance to the garrisoning of troops was decided by the local government. Yunnan, styled a "fortress of democracy," took the line toward the central government of "obeying its proclamations but not its calls": in attitude it supported the Center's resistance to Japan, but when old Chiang summoned Long Yun to Chongqing for meetings he would not come, for fear of being detained. And that place was old Chiang's rear; before striking Yan'an he had to settle Kunming first.

On August 28, Mao Zedong flew into Chongqing. Ambassador Hurley hurried off to Kunming to pull Long Yun to Chongqing as, he said, a third party to the talks. Long Yun would not go; the mission of the governor of Yunnan was to build Yunnan.

The Chongqing negotiations reached a deadlock. On September 10, the Communist base area of Shangdang came under attack by Nationalist troops.

On September 27, Chiang Kai-shek left Mao Zedong in Chongqing and quietly flew to Xichang, near Kunming, to arrange the move against Long Yun. On October 3, Du Yuming led his troops to besiege Wuhua Mountain in Kunming and forced Long Yun out of Yunnan.

The first shot of the civil war had been fired.

Mao Zedong was still in Chongqing awaiting the signing, and the Nationalists and Communists had not yet come openly to blows, and Chiang struck first at a third party.

On October 10 the Nationalist-Communist agreement was reached, and Mao Zedong flew back to Yan'an. Barely three days later, Chiang Kai-shek issued a secret bandit-suppression order, preparing to strike hard at the Communists.

The Yunnan Army stationed in Vietnam faced a hard choice.

Division commander Long Yao was cut to the heart: "If old Chiang dares lay a hand on our own old chief, we fight our way home!"

Some answered, some kept silent. There was no fighting their way back: ten Central Army divisions blocked the Sino-Vietnamese border, holding the advantage over the Yunnan Army's six. And Chiang did his utmost to soothe them: the governorship of Yunnan would go to Lu Han, so it was still Yunnanese governing Yunnan.

Beset within and without, the Yunnan Army in Vietnam ran into an international dispute besides: the French demanded to take over northern Vietnam.

That demand would change the state of things in Vietnam.

Vietnam had been a French colony, then seized by Japan. Two main forces had resisted the Japanese: Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh and the Vietnamese Nationalist Party. The Vietnamese Nationalist Party fostered by China's Nationalists was too weak to amount to anything. The Viet Minh, backed by the Communists, had long kept up the resistance behind enemy lines, and the moment Japan surrendered Ho Chi Minh returned home and formed a provisional government in Hanoi. The new Vietnamese government won wide support; the Vietnamese emperor Bao Dai at once announced his abdication and joined the provisional government as an adviser. Long Yun too supported Ho Chi Minh, and instructed Lu Han to turn over captured Japanese arms to the Viet Minh.

The Chinese occupying forces and the new Vietnamese government cooperated in friendship and successfully stabilized the situation in northern Vietnam, and a fine prospect of independent nationhood opened for Vietnam. Vietnam, so long a lost country, prized this chance at self-determination, and even the opposition Nationalist Party joined with the Viet Minh in opposing the return of France.

But France had the backing of America and Britain and paid no heed at all to the Vietnamese provisional government.

The one hope of blocking a French colonial restoration was the Chinese army stationed in Vietnam. The Yunnan Army was the mainstay of the Chinese garrison, and the mere mention of the French army made its blood boil. In their day you French had colonized Southeast Asia and invaded our Yunnan; now your country is fallen, so what has a government-in-exile to be proud of?

When Japan surrendered, the French major general Alessandri, in exile at Mengzi in Yunnan, went to China's army general headquarters and demanded that the French take the surrender in Vietnam. Chief of the general staff Xiao Yisu answered: the surrender arrangements are decided by the *Potsdam Proclamation*, and the four great powers that attended the Potsdam Conference did not, it seems, include France?

The meaning beneath the words: for taking Vietnam's surrender, the Chinese army was better qualified than the French.

Yet the Chinese government, having already taken the surrender in northern Vietnam, negotiated with the French government under pressure from the powers. On February 28, 1946, a Sino-French agreement was signed: the Chinese army would withdraw in March, and in June the French would relieve it in the defense of northern Vietnam.

Not waiting for the agreed date, on March 6 French warships pushed into Haiphong first and forced a landing.

At this point the Chinese troops had not yet withdrawn, and the defense of Haiphong had not been handed over.

They opened fire. The French army held the Chinese in utter contempt. Its naval guns hit a Japanese depot ashore and set off the ammunition inside, and the whole of Haiphong lay under a pall of smoke.

They fired back. The two mountain-gun battalions of the 184th and 21st Divisions returned fire at once. When the gun-duel was over, the French warships lay crippled in the harbor, and a French rear admiral came ashore under a white flag to parley.

What satisfaction.

Having beaten the Japanese and now driven back the French, Zeng Zesheng, commander of the 60th Army, felt a pleasure he had never known, and had, at last, some sense of a strong army.

Just then came the order to cease fire and withdraw. Letting the French warships go was understandable enough; they were, after all, an anti-fascist ally. But to hand northern Vietnam over to France was hard to understand.

The Yunnan Army in Vietnam received its superiors' order: march to the Northeast to take over its sovereignty.

The pattern of the Japanese surrender after the Second World War now changed utterly.

France's demand to lord it over Vietnam again plainly violated the *Potsdam Proclamation*, yet in the end America and Britain backed France.

Vietnam's surrender was foreign soil, but Hong Kong was Chinese; why was China not allowed to take its surrender? Britain had long since lost Hong Kong to Japan, and the main force that recovered it was the Communists' Dongjiang Column, yet Britain insisted on Hong Kong, and America gave way to Britain.

The Soviets, having sent troops into the Northeast, should count as liberators and had the right to take the surrender there. But Stalin attached conditions to returning the Northeast: China must give up recovering Outer Mongolia, and must guarantee Russia's rights to the railway and to Port Arthur in the Chinese Northeast.

And China's own stance?

After the First World War, in 1919, the powers had insisted on handing China's Shandong from Germany to Japan, setting off the patriotic May Fourth Movement.

Now the Second World War too was to carve out spheres of influence. Leaning on their standing as victors, France and Britain both wanted their old colonies back; then China, a victor no less, had all the more right to reclaim the land it had lost.

Yet Chiang Kai-shek, commander-in-chief of the China theater, reclaimed Taiwan but threw the Ryukyus to America. Later generations sigh that had Chiang been a little broader of mind and reclaimed the Ryukyus along with the rest, there would be no Sino-Japanese dispute over the Diaoyu Islands today.

As a victor, China had the right to garrison Japan. Yet Chiang sent only a few dozen observers, and dragged his feet in dispatching the single division that was to garrison Japan, keeping the strength back to fight the civil war.

China had missed a historic opportunity.

And Chiang Kai-shek had missed a historic opportunity of his own. A leader bold in civil war and timid before the foreign foe could never win the people's support.

The new central leadership set in place by the Communists' Seventh Congress showed its bold sweep at every turn.

When Japan announced its surrender, a vacuum of power opened for a time in the occupied areas. The Nationalist forces were mostly far in the rear, beyond reach; but the Communist-led Eighth Route and New Fourth Armies had all along stood at the front of the resistance, and the ground was theirs for the taking.

The Party Center sent the Social Affairs Department cadre Li Shiying with a radio to the Suiyuan-Mongolia border to make secret contact with the Soviets, hoping to win their support and take over the Northeast first. But the Soviets, wary of their relations with the Nationalist government, were cool. After hearing Li Shiying's report, Mao Zedong sighed: "They don't believe our Chinese Communist Party will win, and in the end liberate all China. That is not to believe in the strength of the Chinese revolution."

The Japanese invading army in central China, facing defeat, secretly asked the New Fourth Army for talks. The Party Center agreed to make contact, and fixed a negotiating line of playing along, buying time, and making ready for the great counter-offensive. Zeng Shan, the New Fourth Army's organization head, was sent as the army's negotiating representative to hold secret talks with the Japanese.

For many years these two secret missions were kept strictly hidden. As work highly specialized and highly secret, intelligence and security work tends to fix its people on the concrete task, and at times to lose sight of the whole. In truth it is work of the highest political weight too, bound close to the entire strategic aim.

In North China the first great city was taken over, Zhangjiakou, with Social Affairs Department head Xu Jianguo as its first police chief. Xu Jianguo then, on the Center's order, prepared to take over Beiping and Tianjin.

In East China the task was to take over Shanghai and Nanjing. On August 12, 1945, the New Fourth Army issued an order appointing Liu Changsheng mayor of the Shanghai special municipality and Zhang Zhiyi deputy mayor. The New Fourth Army's intelligence chief Wang Zhengming led a small party to escort Zhang Zhiyi secretly into Shanghai to arrange an armed uprising.

On August 19, the Central China Bureau reported the Shanghai uprising plan to the Center, with Zhang Zhiyi as secretary of the action committee. On August 20, the Party Center approved the plan.

Barely a day later, on August 21, the Center wired Central China again to cancel the Shanghai uprising. The enemy and puppet forces in Shanghai were fairly strong, Chiang had appointed Shanghai officials and airlifted them in urgently, and in such circumstances the New Fourth Army's main force from eastern Zhejiang risked destruction if it rashly entered the city. On August 22, the Party Center and the Central Military Commission changed their policy on occupying the great cities, so as not to tear the Nationalist-Communist relationship apart.

In a single day the whole strategic line was recast: resolve enough, and fast enough. And that swift strategic turn came, again, from intelligence accurate and quick.

On August 28, Mao Zedong flew to Chongqing. That same day Liu Shaoqi saw off the first group of cadres bound for the Northeast: "You must go and grab it, quickly!"

On September 14, a Soviet aircraft dropped suddenly onto Yan'an, bringing Zeng Kelin, who had entered the Northeast ahead of the rest, to report to the Center. Liu Shaoqi chaired the discussion, and that same day it was decided to establish the Northeast Bureau; the next day the Bureau's secretaries Peng Zhen and Chen Yun flew to the Northeast.

Telegraph traffic ran thick between Yan'an and Chongqing, Mao Zedong and Liu Shaoqi working in the two places at once in perfect understanding. Liu Shaoqi put forward the strategic line of "advance to the north, defend to the south," and Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai wired their agreement at once.

The Party Center urgently moved several hundred thousand cadres and troops out through the passes in a great push to seize the Northeast.

Whoever could seize the opportunity of history could change history.

But to see the opportunity clearly was no easy thing.

The Second World War was also a great intelligence war, and both the Soviet Union and America had erred on intelligence that bore on their nations' safety, while China, by contrast, had led the way in obtaining and judging strategic intelligence. From then on the world's intelligence circles dared not look down on the Chinese.

Within China, the two great intelligence systems belonged to the Nationalists and the Communists. Now these two masters were to take the stage and trade blows.

### 4. Activating the "Cold-Storage Spies"

During the Nationalist-Communist collaboration, Communist intelligence operatives planted deep in the Nationalist camp had suspended all hostile activity and gone into long "hibernation." Such secret assets, buried long-term and not brought out until the crucial moment, were known within the trade as "strategic spies." After the victory over Japan, facing the coming Nationalist-Communist contest, the Communists decided to "activate" these operatives.

On the eve of victory, when Fu Zuoyi's forces prepared to contest the Japanese-held ground with the Eighth Route Army, the time had come to bring out Yan Youwen, buried at Fu Zuoyi's side for eight years. In August 1945, the Border Security leaders Zhou Xing and Li Qiming briefed Wang Yu to their faces, and he made the long journey to Guisui (Hohhot) to restore contact with Yan Youwen.

Cautious of word and diligent in his work within the Nationalist army, Yan Youwen had risen to be aide-de-camp and major-general chief of the press office to Fu Zuoyi, commander of the 12th War Zone; he had been cut off from the Party organization for eight years. Wang Yu, disguised as a fur trader helping Nationalist officers do business, took a long roundabout way before he got near Yan Youwen. Yan Youwen and another underground member, Yang Ziming, had been anxiously awaiting the organization's call.

Yan Youwen supplied the vital intelligence on Fu Zuoyi's move to grab Suiyuan, exactly what the organization urgently needed.

Here in the northwest one man was brought out; over in the Northeast a whole group was.

When the Soviet Red Army struck at the Northeast, the mighty Japanese Kwantung Army proved unable to withstand a single blow. What no one knew was that the Kwantung Army's defensive dispositions had long since been discovered by the Communists' Northeast intelligence group and passed to the Soviets through Yan'an.

The mighty Soviet army, entering Changchun, was nonetheless entirely in the dark, for those deeply guilty senior officials of "Manchukuo" had hidden themselves in the suburbs ahead of time, ready to slip away to Japan. Now the Communists' Northeast intelligence group came forward to help, and Zhang Mengshi was ordered to find the Soviet command. This young master from the "prime minister's" household knew, of course, where his father and his father's subordinates were hiding, and led the Soviets to arrest all the puppet-Manchukuo senior officials, his own father among them. Zhang Mengshi, denouncing his kin for the greater cause, never gave away his secret identity, and went along with these war criminals to the Soviet Union in the guise of a nephew, doing ideological work on Puyi and the others in prison. Later, when the "Manchukuo" war criminals were repatriated, Puyi, in dread of being put to death by the Chinese, meant to kill himself, and no one could talk him out of it; it took Zhang Jinghui's son, "young Zhang," to do it. This "young Zhang," who had shared a cell with the war criminals in the Soviet Union, came home and transformed himself into a Communist cadre at the Fushun War Criminals Management Center.

With the whole Northeast liberated by the Soviet army, the Eighth Route Army's Ji-Re-Liao forces nearby at once pushed out through the passes; the Communist strategy was to seize the Northeast. The Northeast intelligence group came forward to help at once. The Japanese, unwilling to hand their arms to the Eighth Route Army, had scattered their artillery into storage. Now Xian Dong, a puppet-Manchukuo artillery regiment commander, appeared out of nowhere and, knowing just where to look, found the scattered parts and assembled a great batch of guns, and the Eighth Route Army was suddenly a tiger with wings. This Xian Dong turned out to be Kawashima Yoshiko's own younger brother; under the new name Ai Ke he became an artillery brigade commander of the People's Liberation Army.

The most deeply buried operative of all was due to be activated too.

In May 1945 the Nationalists held the First Plenum of their Sixth Congress to discuss postwar anti-Communist strategy. Intelligence this important had to be sent up. As usual Shen Anna hid it away in a drawer. But since her courier had been arrested, the organization had not been in touch with her for three years.

One evening the familiar knock-signal suddenly sounded. Uneasily the couple opened the door, and there stood Wu Kejian. The three fell into one another's arms, laughing and weeping at once, and even the children jumped along with them.

Activated, and put to use. During the sessions of the People's Political Council, the two parties' delegations conferred in secret every night on the next day's negotiating strategy. Shen Anna sat in on the Nationalist delegation's meetings, and that same night passed the minutes to the Communist delegation. The next morning, when Zhou Enlai took his seat at the negotiating table, he already had his plan for dealing with the other side.

With victory, Nationalist officials scrambled for power and place. Some proposed drawing Shen Anna, who moved among the Nationalist upper ranks, up into office. But taking office meant she could no longer serve as a stenographer, no longer gather intelligence for the Party. Shen Anna declined promotion and went on as a lowly clerk.

At this very turning-point between peace and war, the Communists' long-buried intelligence operatives came out one after another, and the Party Center, grasping the other side's every move with precision, won the strategic jump at every turn.

In March 1946 the Second Plenum of the Nationalist Sixth Congress resolved to tear up the Double-Tenth Agreement and go to war. In April the Nationalists held a meeting of the Supreme National Defense Council and arranged the attack on the liberated areas. Afterward the stenographer Shen Anna at once copied out the whole record and reported it to Yan'an. In June the Nationalists attacked the Liberation Army in the Central Plains, and local civil war broke out; Zhou Enlai had made arrangements in advance, and the Central Plains Field Army got away.

At a closed-door secret meeting of the Nationalist center, the elder statesman Zhang Ji pointed at Chiang Kai-shek and denounced him: "The Communists are sitting right at your side and you still haven't found them out!"

At the Generalissimo's side, at that very moment, sat Shen Anna.

For intelligence work around the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, the most important task was to guard the safety of the Party Center. That called not only for obtaining intelligence in advance but for passing it on swiftly and in time.

The fastest means of passing it was wireless. The organs of Yan'an all hurried to equip themselves with stations; the Military Commission's Second and Third Bureaus had a great number, and the Intelligence Department deployed secret stations in the strategic regions across the country. To the Shanghai secret station it sent a Red Army cadre, Li Bai. By the time the third civil war broke out, in 1946, wireless links had become general among the Communist base areas and among the units, a sign that the Liberation Army and its intelligence system alike were moving toward the modern.

Setting up a station in Xi'an, the hardest problem was safety. Wang Chaobei designed an elaborate great work: two cave-rooms dug five meters underground, the tunnel with three entrances and two exits. Should agents come to search, a bell in the front court would sound the alarm, and one could slip into the tunnel from the back court and hide.

The tunnel station reported Hu Zongnan's movements to Yan'an many times: on January 1, 1946, that Hu was mobilizing to strike Yan'an; before dawn on May 2, that Chiang Kai-shek had come to Xi'an to direct the fighting; on June 21, of Liu Kan's return from Nanjing.

The Nationalist secret-service organs in Xi'an were fitted with advanced American radio direction-finding gear and had long since detected a secret station's signal in the district. Yet several times they raided this compound, and found not a trace.

One summer day, agents had searched the compound that morning, and Wang Chaobei, sure no one would come in the afternoon, came out of the tunnel to bathe. He was in the middle of it when shadows flickered outside. His wife, Li Tianchou, cried out, "You can't come in! I'm bathing!" As those outside hesitated, Wang Chaobei snatched the moment to hide, and by the time the police came in they saw only Li Tianchou getting dressed and the child still soaking in the tub.

### 5. China Also Had a "Democratic Allied Army"

What had been activated was not only the Communists' secret operatives, but the heart of the whole nation.

The War of Resistance had been fought for fifteen years, and the people longed for peace and the building of the nation. Yet at just this moment old Chiang was set on civil war. He had lost the people's heart.

Old Chiang cared nothing for the heart of the people; an army in hand was enough. The Nationalists were far stronger in military strength than the Communists, and Chiang judged he could wipe out the Communists in three months to half a year.

But the heart of the army, as it turned out, follows the heart of the people, and Gao Shuxun's forces rose up.

Gao Shuxun's troops were old Northwest Army men of Yang Hucheng's, long squeezed by Chiang's Central Army. When victory came Chiang forbade Gao Shuxun to take any surrender, so Gao advanced on his own; when civil war broke out Chiang made Gao his vanguard, and Gao naturally would not serve as cannon fodder. The Northwest Army had always kept secret contact with the Communists; Gao Shuxun had worked with Peng Dehuai during the resistance, and in the Shangdang campaign he even held a front-line parley with the Communist general Chen Xianrui. Unreconciled to his defeat at Shangdang, Chiang gathered three armies to push north, to reinforce the Beiping garrison in seizing the Northeast. And Gao Shuxun's forces at Handan were just then well placed to block the Nationalist army's northward march. Liu Bocheng and Deng Xiaoping weighed Gao Shuxun's situation, judged he could be won over to revolt, and sent Wang Dingnan with a letter from Liu Bocheng to make contact.

On October 30, 1945, Gao Shuxun, deputy commander of the Nationalist 11th War Zone and commander of the New 8th Army, declared his revolt at Handan.

A whole army breaking from the Nationalist camp shook the Nationalist forces across the country.

Mao Zedong rated the revolt highly and called on the whole army to launch a "Gao Shuxun Movement."

The "Gao Shuxun Movement" advanced on every front. On the northwestern front the 11th Brigade at Anbian revolted in October 1945; on the eastern front Hao Pengju's forces revolted in January 1946; on the northeastern front Pan Shuoduan's division revolted at Haicheng in May 1946; on the Central Plains front Kong Congzhou's 38th Army revolted; and in October 1946 Hu Jingduo's 6th Cavalry Division revolted at Hengshan.

Curiously, these revolted units had not yet raised the Communist banner.

Gao Shuxun's forces, the first to revolt, were called the "Democratic National-Reconstruction Army." The name suited the heart of the whole nation, which hoped to check old Chiang's dictatorship and build China in peace. Hu Jingduo's 6th Cavalry Division and Kong Congzhou's 38th Army were called the "Northwest Democratic Allied Army"; these two units had once belonged to the Northwest Army, and now fought alongside the Communists under the banner of democracy. Hao Pengju, who revolted in the east with a strong force, went so far as to style himself commander-in-chief of the "Chinese Democratic Allied Army." Pan Shuoduan's division, which revolted in the Northeast, was raised to the "Chinese Democratic League Army"; this crack unit came from the Yunnan Army and had deep ties to the Democratic League of the democratic parties.

Nor was it only the revolted units: even the Communist forces seizing the Northeast were called the "Northeast Democratic Allied Army."

A fine thing, the banner of democracy.

The democrats who opposed Chiang Kai-shek's dictatorship had taken a middle stand during the resistance, urging Nationalist-Communist collaboration; after victory they answered the Communists' call for a coalition government and opposed old Chiang's civil war. Fighting and talking by turns, with the Nationalist-Communist talks not yet broken off, the fighting of this stage was not yet called by the Party Center a "war of liberation" but a "war of self-defense."

Self-defense stood upright and just, civil war had lost the people's heart, and a revolt against civil war was to raise high the great banner of democracy.

Talking and fighting by turns, in the very course of the Nationalist-Communist talks the Nationalists in April 1946 launched a fierce assault on the Communists' Central Plains Military Region.

The Central Plains forces were in peril, and the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, where the Center sat, was beset on every side. To the south stood Hu Zongnan's 200,000-strong army; to the west, Ma Hongkui's two crack cavalry divisions; to the north, Deng Baoshan's 22nd Army. Should Chiang mount a pincer attack from north and south, Mao Zedong would have nowhere to go but into the Yellow River to the east.

Mao Zedong summoned Xi Zhongxun, secretary of the Northwest Bureau, to learn how the Border Region's war preparations stood, and directed him to seize the moment and open the northern-line campaign first, inciting the Hengshan revolt to win the Center greater room to maneuver in the rear.

Xi Zhongxun knew the northern-line garrisons intimately. Their three commanders, Hu Jingtong, Hu Jingduo, and Hu Xizhong, were all his middle-school classmates, and the united-front tie had held throughout the resistance. The three, uncle and nephews, were all kin of the patriotic general Hu Jingyi; Xi Zhongxun had already secretly brought Hu Jingduo into the Communist Party, built the Party up within that force, and sent in more than forty cadres besides.

Xi Zhongxun dispatched Fan Ming with his own secret letter to Hu Jingduo to arrange the revolt. On October 13 the northern-line campaign opened, and Hu Jingduo led 2,100 men in armed revolt.

The Hengshan revolt widened the Border Region northward by five thousand square kilometers and added 120,000 to its people, and moreover it settled the northern border, freeing troops to defend the southern line.

Mao Zedong took Hu Jingduo's hand and laughed: "In a situation where the enemy was strong and we were weak, you got off Deng Baoshan's boat and onto Xi Zhongxun's. The road you chose was the right one."

A choice?

In peacetime such a choice is perhaps only a chance; get it wrong, and it is wrong.

In wartime the choice is a matter of life and death, and never must one board a sinking ship.

### 6. The "Latter Three Heroes" of CCP Intelligence

The Nationalists' civil war had lost the people's heart, and under pressure at home and abroad the Nationalists, Communists, and Americans formed a three-party military mediation group, with its executive headquarters in Beiping. The Communist delegation's chief representative was Lieutenant General Ye Jianying, its secretary-general Major General Li Kenong. While the two generals carried on the diplomatic work, they carried on intelligence work in secret.

Chiang Kai-shek was plotting a surprise strike on Zhangjiakou, the Communists' largest city, and Xie Shiyan, operations chief of the 11th War Zone who drafted the battle plan, brought the intelligence to Ye Jianying of his own accord.

This Xie Shiyan was a trusted general of Chen Cheng's, dropped into the 11th War Zone to take charge of operations as a guard against the reliability of commander Sun Lianzhong, who came out of the Northwest Army. Chen Cheng had perhaps forgotten that this Xie Shiyan was also a general of the anti-Japanese war. Xie Shiyan admired the Eighth Route Army's firmness in the resistance and most detested the Nationalists' civil war.

The battle plan Xie Shiyan drew up with his own hand was already before Nie Rongzhen, commander of the Jin-Cha-Ji Military Region, before Fu Zuoyi, commander of the 12th War Zone, had even seen it, and with it came the counter-plan Xie Shiyan had sketched. The Jin-Cha-Ji forces pulled out of Zhangjiakou ahead of time and suffered no loss.

Under close watch by agents, Ye Jianying and Ma Ciqing slipped by night to Xie Shiyan's home to conduct his admission to the Party.

If some cadres within the Communist Party had harbored a soft, peacetime illusion, the intelligence and security system never had that illusion at all. Inside sources reported the Nationalists' internal intelligence without letup, fully confirming that Chiang would surely launch an all-out civil war.

On the eve of the civil war, Wang Yu got word from Yan Youwen that Fu Zuoyi's forces were about to attack the liberated area of eastern Suiyuan. The situation was urgent, and Wang Yu had no time to return to Yan'an; he reported straight to the field headquarters of the 120th Division. As it happened, division commander He Long was away, and the caretaker cadres would not believe Wang Yu, and before long paid the price for disbelieving the intelligence. In October 1946, Lu Nan, who had worked his way into the secretariat of the Suiyuan joint Party-government-army conference, sent Cui Jisheng back to Yan'an with the vital intelligence of Fu Zuoyi's coming great offensive. He Long, receiving the intelligence relayed from Yan'an, took it very seriously and asked that Lu Nan's group be transferred to Jin-Sui for closer use.

The Center's own seat was all the more the heart of the intelligence struggle. In April and May 1946, the Central Intelligence Department issued directive after directive to strengthen wartime military intelligence work, and the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region Security Office held an intelligence work conference too, calling for intelligence work to be joined to the Party's political tasks and for the shift from defensive to all-out offense.

Chiang Kai-shek always overrated his own military strength. In November 1946 he simply flung the Communists and the Democratic League aside and convened a "National Assembly" of his own. The Nationalist-Communist talks broke down, Zhou Enlai led the delegation back to Yan'an, and all-out civil war began.

Chiang summoned the "King of the Northwest," Hu Zongnan, and designed a masterly strategy to cut out the heart: first a surprise strike on the Guanzhong salient, then a surprise strike on Yan'an, seizing the Communists' supreme headquarters at a stroke.

Hu Zongnan was all eagerness. In eight years of resistance he had won no brilliant victories; now, in striking Yan'an, he meant to show his hand.

In February 1947, Hu Zongnan secretly ordered the Reorganized 36th Division and Ma Bufang's cavalry brigade to attack by surprise, only to strike empty air: the Communists' two brigades at Guanzhong and the sub-district organs had moved out in advance. A few days later the Communists struck back, surprising the Reorganized 48th Brigade at Longdong and inflicting heavy losses, and brigade commander He Qi was killed.

In a surprise attack, everything hinges on secrecy. Yet this surprise of Hu Zongnan's had been detected in advance by several Communist intelligence systems.

In the Nationalist 165th Division stationed opposite the salient lay hidden the Border Security's intelligence men Chen Rujie and Liu Liangji, directed on the spot by Yu Sang, chief of the Border Security's 1st Section, from the front-line intelligence group at Fu county. When Hu Zongnan struck at the salient, the 165th Division was ordered to cooperate, and Chen Rujie and Liu Liangji, getting wind of it, reported the intelligence to the Border Security in advance.

Mao Peichun, at this time head of the special-high group at the Nationalist military police headquarters in Luochuan, likewise obtained Hu Zongnan's plan to strike the salient, through the Juntong system, and reported it to the Border Security in advance.

And there was besides a Xi'an military intelligence group directly under the Central Social Affairs Department: Yang Yindong, who had worked his way into Hu Zongnan's supply-area headquarters, obtained the plan too.

Before the troops moved, the spies moved first. To have obtained strategic military intelligence of this kind in advance showed that the Communist intelligence system had already seized the initiative in the Nationalist-Communist intelligence war.

Intelligence from a single channel is at times bound to raise doubt. When Stalin got the intelligence of Hitler's coming attack on the Soviet Union, when Roosevelt got the intelligence of Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor, both found it hard to be sure it was true. Yet the intelligence Mao Zedong got of the surprise strike on Yan'an came from at least three channels.

The master who can play an exquisite move sees clear through his opponent's inner scheming and is skilled at masking his own true design. This is the battle of wits; this is the intelligence struggle. Intelligence war has laws all its own, different from ordinary war. Communist intelligence, having lived through the errors of the Rescue Campaign, no longer relied on mass campaigns to do intelligence work. The new experience was to draw men widely, cultivate the key few, and keep a lean, well-hidden force: to pan gold from sand, painstakingly nurturing a small number of key people; to work them for the long term, patiently placing them in strategic, confidential posts; and to bring them out at the crucial moment, when they would work wonders.

Communist intelligence work had opened up its ground, spread its net, and raised its class.

Hu Zongnan too was drawing the lesson: had his own battle plan leaked?

On February 28, 1947, Chiang Kai-shek summoned Hu Zongnan in Nanjing, and the two conferred in secret on the battle plan, resolved on a surprise strike at Yan'an to knock out the Communists' headquarters at a stroke.

Having learned from the leaks in his two earlier surprise strikes at the salient, Hu Zongnan this time resolved on strict secrecy, keeping even his most trusted generals in the dark. But secrecy or no, someone had to do the work, and Hu Zongnan could only lean on the able and reliable Xiong Xianghui.

Xiong Xianghui was an "idle chessman" whom Zhou Enlai had placed at Hu Zongnan's side at the start of the resistance, not to be lightly brought out save at the crucial moment.

On May 18, 1946, when Hu Zongnan put to Chiang Kai-shek his *Plan for Operations to Take Northern Shaanxi*, asking to strike Yan'an by surprise, Xiong Xianghui at once reported it secretly to Yan'an through Wang Shijian. Just then Zhou Enlai carelessly left a small notebook aboard the aircraft of the American special envoy Marshall, and in it was Xiong Xianghui's Nanjing address. By the practice of intelligence work, Xiong Xianghui should have been told to move at once. But Mao Zedong had said that Xiong Xianghui at Hu Zongnan's side was worth several divisions. Zhou Enlai thought it over and over, judged that Marshall would not report the material to the Nationalists, and had Xiong Xianghui lie low for half a month to watch how things went. In January 1947 Xiong Xianghui married in Nanjing; commander Hu sent a representative to offer congratulations, and Chiang's own eldest son, Chiang Ching-kuo, served in person as witness to the marriage. By Hu Zongnan's arrangement, Xiong Xianghui was to study in the United States.

On March 1, someone from the Bureau of Investigation and Statistics suddenly came to Shanghai looking for Xiong Xianghui, and Xiong Xianghui, judging himself exposed, hurried home to bid a last farewell to his wife, Chen Xiaohua. But in Nanjing he found Hu Zongnan roaring with laughter: put off going abroad three months, take Yan'an first and then go!

Chiang Kai-shek's secret order was for Hu Zongnan to strike Yan'an on March 10, the day the foreign ministers of the four powers, America, the Soviet Union, Britain, and France, would meet and raise the China question anew; Chiang meant to present the world with a fait accompli.

Given this chance to win a feat for the ages, Hu Zongnan was elated. Hu Zongnan was no mere man of arms; Hu Zongnan was a man of ideas and of views, with the political ambition to succeed Chiang Kai-shek. On taking the Communist capital of Yan'an, Hu Zongnan meant to issue a *Program for the Government of Yan'an and Northern Shaanxi Liberated by the National Army*, and this program was to be written more revolutionary than the Communists' own.

For a document so grand, Xiong Xianghui, that great pen, had to be called on. On the evening of March 2, Xiong Xianghui, with background material Hu Zongnan supplied, shut himself in a secret room to draft it. In this *Plan to Take Yan'an*, the force committed to the attack came to 15 brigades and 140,000 men; the time of the attack was dawn on March 10; the day before, 94 aircraft would be gathered from Shanghai and Xuzhou to bomb Yan'an; the Longdong corps would feint, while the two corps, left and right, seized Yan'an by lightning action, guaranteeing the destruction of the Communist main force. With intelligence this important before him, Xiong Xianghui of course copied it all down faithfully.

On the morning of March 3, Xiong Xianghui flew with Hu Zongnan to Xi'an and that night passed the intelligence to Wang Shijian. By the time Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai saw this plan of attack, the Nationalist army's own commanders, division commanders, and brigade commanders still did not know it, for their commander Hu Zongnan set such store by secrecy.

The idle chessman was not idle, the cold piece not cold: Xiong Xianghui, this piece, was Zhou Enlai's tesuji indeed, and served, sure enough, to the greatest effect in guarding the Communist headquarters.

Communist intelligence had long spoken of the "Three Heroes of the Dragon's Pool." Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di, and Li Kenong, planted in the Nationalists' earliest secret-service organs, had intercepted in time the intelligence of Gu Shunzhang's defection and saved the Party Center.

Now Communist intelligence had another three, Xiong Xianghui, Chen Zhongjing, and Shen Jian, planted at the side of the Nationalist general Hu Zongnan who ringed the Border Region, who intercepted in time the intelligence of the Nationalist attack on Yan'an and once more guarded the safety of the Party Center. Zhou Enlai hailed them as the "Latter Three Heroes."

### 7. The Great Withdrawal from Yan'an

The deployment of Communist intelligence grew more thorough by the day. Besides the "Latter Three Heroes," many other channels sent in military intelligence without letup. Mao Peichun reported Hu Zongnan's battle plan, and Chen Rujie and Liu Liangji sent in besides the *Ground-to-Air Contact Signals* issued within the Nationalist army. This confirmed that Hu Zongnan planned not only to bomb Yan'an but possibly to drop paratroops on the Yan'an airfield in a direct strike at Liberation Army headquarters.

Having obtained the strategic intelligence in advance, Yan'an at once made its war preparations. Mao Zedong had always used troops boldly. The Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region had only 7 brigades, some 30,000 men, and even so Mao had let Yan'an garrison commander Wang Zhen take two main brigades across the Yellow River eastward to reinforce the fighting in Jin-Sui, the joint-defense headquarters' own security regiment going along with them. To order Wang Zhen back now was distant water that could not quench a near fire, and should Hu Zongnan land by air he would enter Yan'an straight away.

Chief of the general staff Ye Jianying urgently ordered the garrison district to send troops to guard the airfield and set obstacles, but the duty cadre did not grasp it and sent only a single company. The commander had just finished breakfast when Ye Jianying's jeep arrived: "What's going on? Still no obstacles? If something goes wrong, it's on you!" Only then did the garrison district learn the enemy meant to land by air, and it hurried to set obstacles and tear up the airfield runway.

Yet though the Communists had obtained the intelligence of Hu Zongnan's strike in advance, and had publicly announced across the country that the Nationalists had betrayed the peace agreement, this time it was no longer possible, as in 1943, to stop the attack by loudly singing the Empty Fort. The great enemy Japan was gone, the Nationalists had no more scruples, and Chiang's edge in strength was now 3.37 to 1, just the moment to move against the Communists.

The enemy strong, we weak, Mao Zedong decided to pull out of Yan'an, lure the enemy in deep, and look for the chance to destroy his effective strength.

On March 11 the American observer group in Yan'an withdrew. Seven hours later Nationalist aircraft came over to bomb. Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai moved from Zaoyuan to Liberation Army headquarters at Wangjiaping to direct the fighting. The commander of the Northwest Field Army brought up a regiment to guard the airfield and prevent an air landing.

The organs of Yan'an moved in a tense scramble, scattering as they withdrew. The Security Office's work was especially hard: on the one hand it had to guard Yan'an's safety by the Center's arrangement, and on the other to move its own organs and their families. Chief Zhou Xing was down below doing land reform at the time, deputy chief Li Qiming was ordered to lead more than a hundred cadres to reinforce the front, and the Border Security organ was left with deputy chief Zhao Cangbi alone in charge.

On the 17th, the Nationalists' 140,000-strong army attacked northward; a bomb fell in Mao Zedong's courtyard, and the blast toppled the thermos on his table.

The organs of Yan'an were busy withdrawing, and even the common people had all left the town, and only then did Mao Zedong pull out of Yan'an. Border Security chief Zhou Xing hurried back through the night, leading a platoon of cavalry to guard the rear. When the building of the Central General Office caught fire, Border Security regiment chief of staff Xia Fei ascertained it was not set on purpose. Yan'an police chief Hao Su made a last inspection of the town, and saw every organ, every shop, every house, every cave stripped bare of people, with a bombed crow lying in the middle of the main street. Some houses in the town were still burning, and Hao Su would not let them be put out: never mind these pots and jars, we go up the hills to fight as guerrillas!

Xia Fei took men to lay mines in the town, Hao Su led the guerrillas out, and no sooner were they clear of the town than Nationalist troops descended, the two great columns closing like a giant pincer, and the last man of the guerrillas was taken. Hu Zongnan, entering Yan'an, had a catch at least.

The Great Withdrawal from Yan'an. For all that it had been prepared in advance, there was no lack of confusion.

The Shaanbei people call such a withdrawal *paofan*, "fleeing the raid." From organs to common folk all had to flee, all had their families and children, all their pots and jars, and so all had their troubles. As the Border Security organ pulled out, deputy chief Zhao Cangbi went about inspecting with the clerk Ma Fu and the secretary Cai Cheng. The important archives were all carried off, but what of the papers there was no time to load? The three gathered all the papers into one cave, then dug earth down from the cliff and buried the whole cave over.

When Chairman Mao's car passed the airfield, the road was already torn up and impassable. The Border Security got the order to mend the road, and Zhao Cangbi hurried to lead men to mend it, and once the convoy was through, had to tear it up again.

Confusion was hard to avoid. From the end of 1936 to the start of 1947, Yan'an had been ten years at peace; people had grown used to peacetime life, and could not make the turn all at once.

And the trouble grew worse than anyone imagined, for a traitor turned up: Han Ji'en.

Having taken the Communist capital at a stroke, Chiang Kai-shek was in high spirits and organized a group of Chinese and foreign reporters to visit Yan'an. Hu Zongnan, holding an empty town, knew perfectly well he had too few prisoners in hand, yet had to fake it to cope: he trained a few men to play Communist cadres, but sadly they all acted badly. Just then along came Han Ji'en, who had come over of his own accord, calling himself a section chief of the Security Office. Han Ji'en held forth glibly before the crowd of reporters and even wrote a book, *Yan'an Then and Now*, to smear the Communists. A one-time Border Security section chief, Han Ji'en had used torture to extort confessions, was corrupt, took drugs, was of vile character and slept around, and had been dismissed and investigated by the Border Security. But in the flight the Border Security had never thought to deal with the man, and so left a bane behind. Han Ji'en not only blackened the Communists in the press but led men to Ansai and dug up the archives the Border Security had buried.

The flight from Yan'an had in it both the willing and the forced; the Communist withdrawal had in it both heroes and traitors. This is real and complete life, and real life includes war.

In wartime the most imposing thing is the army: tightly organized, and relatively well supplied. The greatest trouble is the families, a great crowd of women and children, a burden wherever they go.

The Border Security's organs and their families moved to the rear, grown-ups carrying children on their backs, mules laden with baggage and archives. In the black of night the family column took the wrong direction, the river roaring as if to swallow them. The young cadre Gao Qida, a child on his back, lay flat on the ground and crawled feeling along the planks of a bridge, and in a whole night covered only thirty li. Three days' walk brought them to Wayaobao, where they met the Border Region government's camel train, which drew an air raid down on them. Hui Yuxiu's two-year-old son fell off the kang, his head red and swollen and festering with a high fever, and was cut open without anesthetic, until the child cried itself breathless.

The hardest hit in the flight were the women cadres: the men were off to the front with a flick of the hand, and the children were left wholly to the wives. Su Ping took to the road with her child on her back barely two months after giving birth, falling behind day after day on the march. The child was fat, the mother thin, and the people urged Su Ping to leave the child and take a horse to ride, but she would not do it, not for the life of her. When her husband Hao Su sent a guard with a mule, Su Ping refused it: your mule goes to fight, and if the rest walk, I walk too! Marching toward Suide, Su Ping, fallen behind, grew anxious at losing sight of the column's tail, and it was lucky a cadre appeared ahead to point the way. Crossing the Yellow River, she chanced on the Border Security's family column, where the capable Hui Yuxiu was raising pigs to trade for grain for the Border Security's families and children. Hou Liang's new bride, Luo Fei, came along too, and they leaned on one another and held out for more than half a year. The Guanzhong sub-district was the most dangerous front; in the battle of Yetai Mountain, when Qin Ping was ordered to stay behind with the organ, Wang Yingqiang carried her four-month-old daughter to the rear, and in the dark and the rain missed her footing and fell off a cliff into the Malan River. By the time she reached the campground, the child's skin was soaked white with rain.

Speaking of war, the saying abroad is: war, keep the women out of it. For China's Communists, war kept no one out. The men charged in war, the women bore up in war, and the children grew up in war.

The Great Withdrawal from Yan'an should have been a huge, systematic undertaking, but the cadres of the various units differed in quality and in preparation, and so it came out all uneven: orderly here, hurried and confused there.

Before the war Yan'an had foreseen that the Nationalists would surely fight. On March 5, Border Security deputy chief Li Qiming led men south to prepare to campaign with the army. Zhao Cangbi sent three work-groups ahead to plant hidden strength for after the enemy took the ground. On April 5, Zhou Xing's Yangjiagou land-reform team joined up with Zhao Cangbi's Wayaobao work-group. On April 7, the Border Security general office was organized into the 7th Detachment: 1st company under Su Zhenyun with deputy Zhang Jizu; 2nd company under Wang Ping with deputy Ma Fu; 3rd company under Su Mingde with deputy Wang Baoxian. The security regiment summed up its work since the war began: in this period it had fully carried out its task of escorting the Border Region government, but there had also been 38 deserters. In early August the Border Security general office moved to Sanshilipu in Suide, and in mid-month crossed the Yellow River.

With the constant moving and the ceaseless fighting, the prisoners held in jail became a problem. The Border Security's screening was long since basically done, the great majority of cadres had been assigned work, and only some forty-odd remained under the Third Rear Section (the pre-trial section). Most were political prisoners; there were a few White Russians, a hardcore bandit from the Laoshan ambush of Zhou Enlai, merchants who had dealings with Nationalist agents, and a few cadres known to be clean whom higher-up had not yet ordered released. These remaining cases were fairly complicated, and pre-trial section chief Yang Gang sent Liang Ji to escort them. It took two or three weeks to find the Border Security's rear organ at Zhenwudong. On the march, the soldiers of the security regiment's 6th company had to carry the prisoners on stretchers, shoulder their bundles, boil water, and cook for them, and through the whole move not one prisoner escaped.

The Social Affairs Department held very few, but among them was a famous man, Wang Shiwei. The Department's screening verdict on Wang Shiwei was "counter-revolutionary Trotskyist agent element," but Wang Shiwei would not sign, and so it was shelved. In the flight the Department's rear organ moved to Shanxi and took Wang Shiwei along. Call him a prisoner, and no one escorted him on the march; call him a cadre, and once at the campground someone had to be set to guard him. The Department's cadres were used to this Wang Shiwei, and all got on peaceably. On reaching Xing county, capital of the Jin-Sui Border Region, the Department handed Wang Shiwei over to the Jin-Sui Public Security General Bureau to hold. The General Bureau, receiving this world-famous prisoner, kept him under close guard, which Wang Shiwei took very ill, repeatedly demanding better living conditions. But Xing county's security was far worse than Yan'an's, enemy agents often raiding by night. The safety of the Border Region organs was itself in question, and to guard on top of it a grave prisoner transferred from Yan'an felt an unbearable load. Someone proposed that, given the wartime setting, a batch of prisoners of vile conduct ought to be dealt with, and Wang Shiwei was judged to have "carried on sabotage such as sowing discord on the march," "showing no remorse and growing worse." On July 1, 1947, Nationalist aircraft bombed Xing county, the General Bureau's interrogation section was hit too, the detention house had to move at once, and that very night Wang Shiwei was put to death.

It was not only the Border Security that dealt with some; certain sub-district Security Offices, and even some guerrilla units, dealt with prisoners too. Before Hu Zongnan's attack, the Guanzhong sub-district hastily dealt with a batch of prisoners, among them Yu Chao, who had robbed Liulin town, and some armed plainclothesmen and bandits. A sub-unit of the Yanxi guerrillas had seized two prisoners from the Nationalist army and carried them along, and when the enemy pressed the pursuit hard the two seemed a nuisance. Release them, and they had passed through every place the guerrillas hid, and one word of theirs would leave the guerrillas nowhere to hide. Kill them, and higher-up would surely not approve; one guerrilla sub-unit's political commissar had defected and the Yanxi Work Committee had not even killed him. So this sub-unit took it on itself to bury the two prisoners. The Yanxi Work Committee, learning of it, did not pursue the matter either.

In the peril of war, when one's own life and death were themselves in grave question, who could manage to prize a prisoner's life?

The Border Security had always laid down that to put a counter-revolutionary prisoner to death required approval three levels up; sub-district, county, and district, the three lower levels, had no direct power to deal with such cases. But in wartime a temporary rule was made besides: for those who openly defected to the enemy, their crimes should be published before the masses and repentance called for, and if they were resolute anti-Communists, the people had the right to arrest them and execute them on the spot. Execution on the spot was, in a word, "killing."

On the matter of killing, the Communist policy had always been perfectly clear: "neither wrong the innocent nor let the guilty slip." Not to kill the innocent wrongly, and not to indulge escapes, was a sound and comprehensive line. But in the wartime setting it was very hard to hold to both "nots" at once. Zhang Yong'an of the Sanbian sub-office had confessed in 1943 to being a Zhongtong agent, was later screened as having no political problem, and even became police chief of Dingbian. When war came, this man tipped off the Nationalist agent station the Border Security already controlled to pull out ahead of time, and defected the moment the enemy arrived. Lei Minggang and Wei Ming, whom the Border Security had turned to its own use, fled back to the enemy again, and Wei Ming even wrote *Five Years in Yan'an* to serve the enemy's propaganda. The Hanzhong-class agent Hu Siyuan, planted in the Military Commission's Second Bureau, whom the Border Security failed to keep in hand while turning him at Longdong, went over to the enemy in the war and supplied the inner workings of the Second Bureau.

All this shows how to regard and use suspect persons is a hard problem indeed. Fear "wronging" and you may "let slip," letting a bad man escape to kill you; fear "letting slip" and you may "wrong," killing wrongly one who should not have been killed.

The grassroots cadres and masses, who felt the danger of the struggle keenly, often held to a fixed cast of mind: better to kill wrongly than let one slip. In truth it was "rather wrong than let slip."

Mao Zedong saw it otherwise. The cadre-vetting's "Nine-Point Directive" said plainly: in certain circumstances, rather let them escape than kill too many, for to let them escape is more advantageous than to kill them. That is, "rather let slip than wrong."

The sense of this is hard to savor for any but a man of great gifts and vision.

Killing, on China's political stage, was a common thing. When the Nationalists put the Communist leaders Xiang Zhongfa and Qu Qiubai to death, they went through no legal procedure, still less for killing ordinary members. In wartime Chiang Kai-shek killed all the more freely: assassinating Li Furen in Xi'an on May 1, 1946, and openly shooting the Xi'an Democratic League chairman Du Bincheng on October 7, 1947. Killing is simple: with a man's head gone, how can he oppose you still? Yet Chiang's enemies only multiplied as he killed. Mao Zedong did not do it so. From the Red Army days it was strictly forbidden to kill prisoners, and the cadre-vetting campaign laid down "not one to be killed." For enemy spies too, Yan'an's policy was not to kill but to "turn the enemy to serve us."

When Mao Zedong learned in 1948 that Wang Shiwei had been killed, he flew into a rage. The Nationalists had once, in 1944, made Wang Shiwei "die," a great loss of face. And now Wang Shiwei had truly died at Communist hands?

In August 1948, Social Affairs Department head Li Kenong sent Mao Zedong and the Central Secretariat a written report taking responsibility on himself. After the founding of the state, at a break in a meeting, Mao Zedong said to Lin Boqu, "Give me back my White Russians!" and to Li Kenong, "Give me back my Wang Shiwei!" In the 1960s, more than a dozen years after Wang Shiwei's death, Mao Zedong at one meeting still called Li Kenong out by name on the spot: "Give me back my Wang Shiwei!"

Some said it was anger talking, some said it was a joke, but anger or joke, the words came out heavy enough. Mao Zedong not only opposed killing Wang Shiwei; he also opposed killing the former Qing emperor Puyi, opposed killing the Japanese war criminals, opposed killing the Nationalist secret-service chiefs. Many men whose crimes seemed to leave no room for pardon were reformed while serving their sentences, and later even granted special amnesty.

Better to remake a man than to kill him.

In truth, who in the end approved Wang Shiwei's execution is not the most important question. When the Jin-Sui Public Security Bureau asked up the line, there were three possibilities for who approved: Social Affairs Department head Kang Sheng, then doing land reform nearby; deputy head Li Kenong, in charge of the Department's work; or He Long, head of the Jin-Sui sub-bureau. The rough situation of the time is fairly clear: Mao Zedong surely held to "not one to be killed," those below surely leaned toward killing, and the middle layer surely did approve killing some. Cadres at different levels of work do indeed judge from different angles.

On June 21, 1947, the Party Center's Northwest Bureau called for "correcting the past one-sided tendency to leniency," and for "those who openly defect to the enemy, their crimes should be published before the masses and repentance called for, and if they are resolute anti-Communists, the people have the right to arrest them and execute them on the spot." This was the early war, the opening of the killing.

On February 24, 1948, the Party Center's Northwest Bureau issued another directive: "After correcting the numb, hand-tied paralysis of the early war's purge struggle, the Party in the various parts of the Border Region has repeatedly produced the phenomenon of 'Left,' rash killing," and "if these grave, mistaken phenomena continue, they will surely stir a climate of terror among the masses inside and outside the Border Region, lower our Party's prestige, give the enemy a pretext to consolidate his ranks and drive a wedge between us and the masses, and even gravely harm the opening of work in the new liberated areas; the consequences do not bear thinking of." This was the later war, a strict ban on wanton killing.

Both directives, the earlier and the later, came from the same organ: first guarding against the Right, then against the Left, and both were about killing. And the day Wang Shiwei was executed fell precisely between these two documents.

On November 5, 1948, the Border Security once more summed up the killings in the 1947 war. In the whole Border Region 2,296 people had been killed, more than half of them killed wrongly. The wrongful killings occurred mostly in the early war: with war's cruelty, grassroots cadres and masses generally gave way to hatred of the enemy and the urge to revenge, some were panic-stricken, a few used the chance to avenge private grudges, and bad men used the chance to make trouble. The chief ideological roots were "Left is better than Right," "rather kill than release," and "rather kill a suspect than let one bad man slip." The old, sound killing procedure was not carried out, the deed done before the report, sometimes done and never reported. The leading organs bore part of the blame for this. The Border Security had directives on killing, but they had their vague spots. The high-court cadres in charge of executions were busy with wartime mobilization and paid the killing question no mind. The Northwest Bureau's directives too had a few unsound points, as in laying down "execution on the spot" without setting concrete standards. In sum, these problems, already settled in peacetime, grew grave in war.

Such is war.

If the struggle in peacetime is only a question of who lives better, then the struggle in wartime is a question of who lives and who dies. If a work error in peacetime leaves only an erroneous written verdict from struggling and persecuting a man, then a work error in wartime may mean wounding, killing, or even the loss of one's head.

The most precious thing a man has is his life. The security organs that wield the Knife bear, by their very nature, on the great matter of heads falling. In wartime this ceaselessly swinging Knife is more chilling still.

In the war of self-defense, the faults and errors of security work too were laid starkly bare.

Against Hu Zongnan's attack, the Border Security, though it had the intelligence in advance, was still somewhat thrown in the early war: the archives were not fully dealt with, and a few traitors did much harm. The intelligence forces had not arranged contingency measures in time, so that when the enemy came most contact was lost and the sources of intelligence broke off. The intelligence groups were generally turned into armed work teams, which strengthened the harassment of the enemy but slackened the gathering of intelligence. Short of radios, once cut off by the enemy they could not for a time restore contact up and down the line. The Liulin intelligence group's Yang Feng rashly took men into Xi'an to lie low, was quickly exposed, and dragged in with it Liu Wu's group, which had gone in earlier, so that many were killed. Looking into the matter, it emerged that the very sending of Liu Wu's group had been an error, for in this hidden group was Zhang Bingnan, a famous performer of the Border Security's yangge troupe: how many people knew that face!

In the end all the problems came back to the leadership. In early December 1947, the Northwest Bureau held a conference at Yihe town on the bank of the Yellow River to sum up the early war's work, review the Border Security's failings, and above all criticize Zhou Xing, head of the Northwest Bureau's Social Affairs Department and chief of the Security Office. The Border Security's work errors were of course not few, and Zhou Xing, who had led it for more than ten years, had made all the more enemies in the Rescue Campaign; throughout the conference he criticized himself in earnest. Yet the pitch of the conference climbed higher and higher, and new scores and old were reckoned together. From lax anti-smuggling it pursued him to trafficking his own contraband; from the confusion of the early self-defense war it pursued him to why he had trusted men who had confessed; from a review of the security system's not-close-enough ties with the Party and government organs it rose to the charge that the security system played at independence from the Party committee; from chief Zhou Xing's bureaucratism it stretched to his improper promotion of young cadres.

The Yihe conference put the Border Security in its place. Before the war the Northwest Bureau had wanted to bring the Border Security's intelligence stations under unified control, and Zhou Xing had held out against it; now at last they were merged. After the conference the intelligence cadres of the various sub-districts and counties were handed to the Urban Work Department, and even the intelligence force lying low in enemy areas was worked over on class composition, thinned from 113 people to 2. This campaign-style conference once more disrupted the work, and for two full quarters the Border Security's intelligence work basically ground to a halt.

At year's end, in his essay *The Present Situation and Our Tasks*, Mao Zedong stressed that the main problem now was to oppose the Left, not the Right, and only then did the conference cool. The Northwest Bureau encouraged the Security Office not to lose heart over the criticism but to take charge with a free hand under the Party's leadership. On March 27, 1949, Zhou Xing put his views to the Center in writing, holding that the Yihe conference had been one-sided, that its achievements should be affirmed as the basic and principal side of the Security Office's work. That the work had grave failings and even errors was fact, but it was not "a complete mess," and the ordinary failings and errors the leadership had all corrected, and was still correcting the exceptional ones. The Center wired back and affirmed Zhou Xing's views.

### 8. The Yan'an Guerrillas

When war came, the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region's existing organs of government lost their governing standing in a single day, and Party, government, and mass bodies all threw themselves into guerrilla war against the enemy. Everywhere in the Border Region guerrilla and armed work teams were formed; the heads of the public-security departments at each level mostly served as guerrilla leaders, and the Party committee secretaries at each level as political commissars. The guerrilla and armed work teams took the Border Security's cadres and its security sub-units as their backbone, drawing in militia and the masses.

Where in all the world do merchants fight as guerrillas? A merchant is always a middleman of the economy, and his trade breeds in him a neutral code that keeps him from dying for one side in a war. Yet the traders of Yan'an threw themselves willingly into the war of self-defense.

Chen Jiafu kept a seal-carver's shop in the new market of Yan'an, and had saved up a sum to go home to Henan and bring back a bride, when he was seized on the road as a conscript by Nationalist troops. This merchant actually became an underground worker, secretly inciting his fellows to flee, and brought out a machine gun besides. His deeds were made into a play by the Border Security's yangge troupe, and the customers and merchants of the new market all loved to watch this *Chen Jiafu Comes Home*. After the pullout from Yan'an, Chen Jiafu went back into the town many times to scout the enemy, and by cruel chance ran into the very company he had once fled with the gun. Even the Nationalist troops knew this celebrity of the yangge play, and so Chen Jiafu was shot before the crowd.

Nationalist troops broke into the Communists' heartland and naturally dared not slacken: wherever they took ground they set up district and county local government, and at the village level built *baojia* bodies too, joint-security offices and security posts. In the one town of Yan'an there were more than seventy Nationalist organs, the "commander's field headquarters," the "garrison command," the "Northern Shaanxi administrative office," and the like, of which more than ten were secret-service bodies: the commander's intelligence section, the news bureau's military press agency, the Northwest news agency, Zhongtong's Shaanxi Yan'an sub-office, and others.

The Communists, for their part, harassed from outside the town. Commander Peng Dehuai and political commissar Xi Zhongxun led the Northwest Field Army in mobile operations, while Border Security chief Zhou Xing took charge of organizing the Border Region's guerrilla war. On June 1, 1947, Zhou Xing published on the Northwest Bureau's behalf an essay, *Widely Develop Guerrilla War*. In the mere three months after the enemy's invasion, the Border Region guerrillas had grown to 7,663 men, with 4,592 long and short arms and 15 light machine guns, had fought 114 engagements, killed or wounded 614 of the enemy, and taken 1,281 prisoners.

The Communist Yan'an municipal committee was divided into an eastern-district and a western-district work committee, each leading the guerrilla fighting in its district. Bai Shoukang was commander of the Yan-shu headquarters; Yao Anji, political commissar of the Yanxi detachment, was also mayor of Yan'an; Yan'an police chief Hao Su served as member of the western-district work committee and security-section chief of the Yan-shu headquarters, and deputy chief Kang Shichang as leader of the Yanxi detachment. The Yan'an guerrillas fought hard and invented the "Five-Strike, Five-Don't-Strike" tactic: strike the tail, not the head; the scattered, not the massed; the hidden, not the exposed; the soft, not the hard; the few, not the many. The Border Region government promptly issued citations to the militia who distinguished themselves in battle.

The Border Security's confidential secretary Hang Shangzeng and interrogation-section cadre Liang Ji were Yan'an natives; each picked seven or eight men and formed a lean armed work team, Hang Shangzeng operating northeast of Yan'an and Liang Ji to the southeast. The armed work teams wore plainclothes and carried pistols, lay up by day and moved by night, slipping into the town of Yan'an to scout and to root out the traitors who had gone over to the enemy.

Liang Ji wrote to the turncoat Han Ji'en: you're an old Party member and should know the policy; to leave yourself no way back is plain foolishness! Han Ji'en, keeping a foot in both boats, sent Liang Ji intelligence on the rifts within the enemy.

The Nationalists too set great store by the espionage struggle. The deputy commander of the Yan'an garrison command, Han Zhipei, was a graduate of Juntong's Hanzhong class and organized secret-service activity with great zeal.

An espionage man of the 142nd Regiment stationed at Guaimao often disguised himself as a "country crone" to tell fortunes in the villages, prying out intelligence and seducing women besides. The armed work team caught this agent in his woman's disguise, to great effect among the masses. A certain assistant Wang, of the Nationalist Yan'an commissioner's inspection office, was doing an opium deal with Hang Shangzeng, the two meeting with Mauser pistols at their belts in a geomancer's house. Assistant Wang gave only some soon-to-be-worthless intelligence to fob him off, and Hang Shangzeng hauled him back to the Border Security for interrogation.

As the Northwest Field Army looked for a chance to attack Yan'an, wireless monitoring learned of a move by Liu Kan's First Army, yet ground reconnaissance showed it was Dong Zhao's 29th Army moving. Headquarters ordered the Yan'an armed work team to verify the intelligence, and Liang Ji took four men by night into Ershilipu to lie in wait for prisoners, waiting a night and a day in a bitter winter more than ten below zero, until they caught two officers out chasing women. It turned out Hu Zongnan had meant to move the 29th Army, but did not trust army commander Dong Zhao, a Shaanxi man, and had Liu Kan go in his stead. Liang Ji delivered the accurate intelligence to headquarters, and the commander, pleased, rewarded the armed work team with two submachine guns and two Canadian pistols.

The little mouse mastered the elephant. Where once the Nationalists had chased the Communists into the mountain gullies to lie sleepless, now it was the Nationalists who could not sleep a whole night in the town, while the Communists slept sound in the gullies.

In the early war the Nationalist army was fierce and the Communists were on the defensive. The "Iron 8th Regiment," holding Dingbian to the death, fought the Ma clique army a hundred days and died to a man. With Yan'an abandoned, heaven and earth changed color: the landlords and politicians who had been driven out came flocking home, and great numbers of the common folk had to bow their heads and submit.

Some Communists too staked their bets on a Nationalist victory. The Border Security system produced great traitors like Han Ji'en and Wu Shengyuan. Wang Aixian of Chishui county drove his own ox to clear mines for the Nationalist army; Zhang Shumao scouted for the 123rd Brigade and twice guided raids on Baimiao village; the 1st company of the Ganquan guerrillas saw two cases in a row of men defecting with their guns; the agent Bai Chengzhi trussed up a squad leader and forced more than ten men of the Yan'an southern-district guerrillas to defect; and Zhu Zhifeng of Yanchang county shot the Party cadre Li Qinglian.

Facing the tense situation, the Yan-shu military sub-district headquarters and the Yan-shu Security sub-office issued directives to strengthen the guerrillas' security work: guerrilla detachments were to have the security work held concurrently by the political commissar and his deputy, sub-units by the instructor and his deputy, or a second deputy post set up for full-time security. Sub-units set up a security-group leader. Security cadres at every level, directly under the security section, consolidated the units and strengthened education in moral firmness. When the leader of one sub-unit of the Yanxi guerrillas meant to defect, Yan-shu security-section chief Hao Su gathered the whole unit, took away their arms, and on announcing the truth the men denounced the traitor one after another. Song Zhenjiang, deputy chief of the Fu county security section, held out in the struggle behind enemy lines, and the enemy pressed his father to talk him into surrender. Song Zhenjiang warned his father: come again and I'll shoot you!

The struggle in the Longdong region was especially cruel. The Yimaguan checkpoint stood only forty li from the enemy's stronghold town of Xifeng: the first station chief, Yu Tingji, was seized by the home-returning corps and tortured to death at Xifeng; the second, Chen Bin, was sold by a layabout to the Nationalist garrison and bayoneted to death; and the third, Du Dinghua, carried on the struggle. The masses held a memorial meeting of their own for the martyrs Chen Bin and the others, and stoned to death the man who had welcomed the enemy army into the town.

War is the hour that gives birth to heroes, and a hero's future is often to be a martyr. The Liu Wu intelligence group that the Border Security sent out had slipped into Xi'an in advance before the war, and had gone beyond the bounds of intelligence work to build a secret military organization. On February 10, 1948, the Nationalists took Liu Wu and 32 others to the foot of Yaowang Mountain in Yao county and buried them alive together. To this day the martyrs' monument here bears the names of nineteen of them, with "Zhang Bingnan" written by mistake for "Zhang Bingnan."

Mao Peichun, planted in the Nationalist army, had all along secretly passed intelligence to the Border Security. On April 25, 1949, when a Nationalist aircraft bombed its own troops by mistake, Mao Peichun was killed. A grievous loss of an intelligence talent. The Communist Border Security and the Nationalist Juntong, the two hostile sides both held memorial services for Mao Peichun.

Not everyone dares to be a martyr. Wu Manyou, a Border Region model laborer, took the lead in joining up, and the moment he enlisted was given the exceptional appointment of mass-mobilization head of the 2nd Column. This fellow, a high cadre now, still kept his peasant nature, and in the withdrawal could not bear to throw away the spoils on his horse's back and so was captured. Hu Zongnan at once flew this Shaanbei celebrity to Nanjing, for reporters' interviews and radio speeches. When the Liberation Army took Nanjing it sent Wu Manyou back to Yan'an. Though Wu Manyou had been a cadre, he was in the end only a peasant, and the Party organization did not pursue the matter. Wu Manyou fell into gloom and died of illness.

The cruel reality showed that not everyone can see the direction clearly, and not everyone can hold on to the end. At every turning-point of history there is always someone who boards the wrong car, and one false step brings eternal regret.

For the side at a disadvantage, everyone had to face the cruel reality.

Over the men bound up with Juntong's Hanzhong class, the Nationalist and Communist intelligence and security organs had struggled fiercely all along. By the time of the civil war, when the Nationalist army entered the Border Region, each of these men faced a choice of life and death.

Juntong prided itself on being a closed "fraternity" with its own "house law": "enter alive, leave only dead." When the fighting started, Juntong at once put out warrants for the turncoats Qi Sanyi and Li Chunmao. Qi Sanyi's eldest brother was beaten to death, his third brother beaten into a cripple, his father jailed, and no one dared harvest the ripened Qi family wheat. The Nationalists treated Qi Sanyi as a mortal enemy, and Qi Sanyi treated the Nationalists as a mortal enemy in turn. Qi Sanyi served as leader of the Longdong armed work team, turned the demolition skills Juntong had trained him in against the Nationalists, led the militia in a grand array of mines, and *Liberation Daily* ran a feature on his deeds. Li Chunmao served as deputy leader of the armed work team at Fu county, and Juntong's attempt to assassinate him failed. Wang Xingwen had all along done intelligence work around the Border Region, and later came to Longdong too. Of the five formally taken on by the Border Security as field personnel, only two, Zhao Xiu and Zhang Zhigang, fled; the other three grew keener in the struggle. There were a few more former Hanzhong-class men in Guanzhong, and Feng Pingbo, Zhu Langzhou, Jin Guang, and others all took part in the Border Security's field work.

There were some others, who though turned by the Border Security were in fact only humoring the Communists, and who turned coat the instant the Nationalist army attacked the Border Region. Chen Ming, the very first man Wu Nanshan had detected at the Longdong commissioner's office, not only defected but lured Hanzhong-class schoolmates into a trap, causing the death of Ma Ming. Liu Zhicheng, once head of the Heshui county theater troupe, stayed on in the troupe after confessing and volunteered military intelligence to the enemy, causing the Liberation Army heavy losses in the battle of Xihuachi, and was arrested and executed in 1947. There were some too who, disheartened in the struggle, slipped back home to live as commoners.

That most deeply buried radio group had been much valued by the Social Affairs Department, and after training its three members were all sent to Longdong for field work. When war came, the three went their separate ways. Hu Shiyuan, denounced and secretly arrested by his Hanzhong-class schoolmate An Yonglu and threatened with Juntong discipline, agreed to gather intelligence for the Nationalists, and was later dismissed for poor work. When the Liberation Army counter-attacked in 1949 and the Border Security sent someone to make contact, Hu Shiyuan turned back to Border Security work. Merit did not cancel guilt, and in October 1949 he was again put under supervision. His co-defendant Xia Zhenqing was sent by the Border Security back to his home at Qingyang for field work, but on returning home hid himself and farmed, and volunteered intelligence to Juntong besides, until the plainclothesmen of the 385th Brigade arrested and re-educated him. From then on the man went home to farm, was even elected head of the peasant association in the early years of the state, and in 1953 was arrested and served five years. Of the three co-defendants, Yang Zicai came off best: he worked at the Xihuachi local-products company during the war, and after the founding of the state became deputy manager of the Xiahe trading company in Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture.

The most crucial figure of all was still Wu Nanshan.

Wu Nanshan, the first to confess of his own accord, was the last Juntong discovered; several times Juntong sent men to test him, and each time he fobbed them off. In early 1947, with war about to break out, the organization resolved, to educate the masses, to make public the truth of the Hanzhong class. Arranged by Zhao Cangbi, Wu Nanshan wrote a piece, *My Way Out: A Young Man Once Framed by Chiang's Agents Tells His Own Story*, published on February 7 in *Liberation Daily*. Wu Nanshan calmly announced that he had joined the Communist Party, and called on other young people framed by the Nationalists: "Only standing together with the people is the true way out for a young person!"

Only now did Juntong learn the truth of the Hanzhong class's collapse, and of course it hated Wu Nanshan to the bone. In April 1947, when the Nationalist army attacked Qingyang, it swore to seize Wu Nanshan, Qi Sanyi, Li Chunmao, and Wang Xingwen and shoot them.

Qingyang county Party secretary Lu Weigong, together with Wu Nanshan and Tian Shaoxi, the three of them formed the Qingyang Work Committee, and led the armed work team in guerrilla war behind enemy lines. Wu Nanshan's whole family went up the hills: his father old, his mother bound-footed, his son in the guerrillas, his wife dead of illness in the hills. Once, surrounded by the enemy, Wu Nanshan wrapped himself in his greatcoat and leapt off a cliff more than ten zhang high, and was knocked senseless on the spot. The next day he made it back to his own people, who were then arguing over whether he had surrendered.

Facing the test of life and death, Wu Nanshan held firm to his purpose, and moreover found every means to carry on the work. The Nationalist officials of Qingyang were in dread, and many were called home by their kin; those who stayed reined in their swagger and sought out the armed work team to keep on good terms. When the Liberation Army counter-attacked, Wu Nanshan obtained Qingyang's military dispositions through his intelligence ties, drew a map, and sent it to Northwest Field Army headquarters. He even directed the Nationalist county magistrate Zhang Guozhen to move the armed self-defense corps out of the way in advance, seal up the granaries and public property, and await the Communist takeover.

Wu Nanshan, tested and proven, won the trust of the Communist organization, and after the founding of the state became public-order section chief of the Gansu provincial public security department and materials-bureau chief of the Pingliang prefecture. In the Cultural Revolution Wu Nanshan too came under attack, but the public-security department still affirmed his contribution, and Zhao Cangbi even wrote out attesting testimony in person. After the Cultural Revolution Wu Nanshan served as industry-bureau chief of the Pingliang region and adviser to its economic commission, and after retiring spent his last years in peace at Lanzhou. Qi Sanyi, after the founding of the state, became a section chief of the First Bureau of the Gansu provincial public security department and religion-section chief of the provincial nationalities commission; branded a "historical agent" in the Cultural Revolution and expelled from the Party, he was rehabilitated afterward and became head of the Kangnan forestry general station. Li Chunmao, after the founding of the state, became secretariat-section chief of the First Bureau of the Gansu provincial public security department and pastoral-district chief of the animal-husbandry department; sent down to a cadre school for review in the Cultural Revolution, he was rehabilitated afterward and became a deputy-department-grade inspector of the provincial forestry department. Wang Xingwen, in the early years of the state, became deputy chief of the public-order division of the Xinjiang public security department. Zhao Xiu, Zhang Zhigang, Yang Chao, and others were all arrested and sentenced after the founding of the state in 1949.

The armed strength of the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia Border Region, besides these plainclothes guerrillas and armed work teams, had the uniformed security regiments too.

The security regiment, in good order and full strength, held off Hu Zongnan's great army seven days on the southern line, covering the withdrawal of the Field Army's main force, and kept its eye on the enemy right into Yan'an town. After this the security regiment became the Yan-shu military sub-district's Yan'an guerrilla detachment, fighting guerrilla actions around Yan'an. The more the guerrillas fought the more they grew, and later they were organized into the 4th Garrison Brigade: brigade commander Guo Baoshan, political commissar Li Zonggui, deputy commander Bai Shoukang, deputy commissar Liu Zhen, chief of staff Xia Fei, and former Yan'an police chief Hao Su as security-section chief. The Border Region Security Command was reorganized into the 3rd Garrison Brigade, with commander He Jinnian and commissar Wang Shitai.

Once formed, the 4th Garrison Brigade's first task was to sweep the "stockades" of the Huanglong Mountains. The Shaanbei region had fought without cease since ancient times, and the local strongmen built rammed-earth walls to protect themselves. These "mud stockades" were not large, yet they were highly odious. Mao Zedong, criticizing cadres who played at independence, called the big ones "independent kingdoms" and the small ones just "mud stockades." In ten years of ruling the Border Region the Communists had never rid themselves of these thorns in the back; now, the mask torn off in civil war, they would pull them out with a free hand. The first assault, on "Taotao Stockade," lacked cannon and had to use the fire-attack on the gate out of the *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*. The second, on Lieshi Stockade, dug a tunnel to blow the stockade wall. The third, on Yangquan Stockade, went up the high wall on a scaling ladder five and a half zhang tall. Three battles opened three mud stockades; the Northwest Bureau issued a citation, and the 4th Garrison Brigade's heroic deeds went into the primary-school textbooks.

The "dirt Eighth-Routers" won great fame, took part in the Fufeng campaign near Xi'an, and were raised to main-force troops. The Shaan-Gan-Ning-Jin-Sui Joint Defense Army's 1st Garrison Brigade was reorganized into the 10th Division, the 3rd Garrison Brigade into the 11th, and the 4th Garrison Brigade into the 12th, the three divisions forming the 4th Army of the 2nd Column of the Northwest Field Army. As the great army pushed west, the 2nd Column took part in the Fumin campaign and the Lanzhou campaign, and the 12th Division led the assault on the works of Gouwa Mountain. The 12th Division's security-section chief Hao Su was promoted to deputy head of the 2nd Column's security department, and after entering Lanzhou helped organization head Zhu Peiping and others build the Gansu military region, later serving as its security-department head as well. Hao Su, who had come up from local public-security work, thus became a soldier.

Among the main-force units of the Chinese People's Liberation Army, this unit born in the war of liberation could not count as an old hand, yet the stock it started from was the glorious Yan'an guerrillas.

On April 22, 1948, the Communists recovered Yan'an. Zhou Xing, returning to his old post, found the place a ruin before his eyes. At once he put the whole town under military control, gathered up and managed the agents, police, and stragglers left in it, and sent the town's refugees back home to be resettled. The one man formally arrested was the Border Security's own traitor, Han Ji'en.

The Yan'an police bureau quickly resumed work: chief Hao Su had joined the army, so deputy chief Kang Shichang rose to chief, and Liang Ji became deputy chief. The Yan'an bureau had always had a great many outside cadres, yet the new team was made up of local ones. This was because, with the Communists' territory beginning to expand outward, most of the outside cadres were being transferred away to take over their own home places, and the new territory too needed local cadres who knew the ground to take charge.

Mao Zedong, for his part, was too busy to spare the time to return to Yan'an and celebrate: he crossed the Yellow River eastward and went out to North China to direct the counter-offensive across the country. Under the great marshal Mao, how many guerrilla bands there were like Yan'an's, how many garrison brigades like the 4th, and how many "dirt Eighth-Routers" like the Shaan-Gan-Ning-Jin-Sui Military Region.

If they could beat the strong from weakness while still "dirt," would they not be invincible once they went "modern"?

By the time it turned to the strategic counter-offensive, the great Communist army was already a tiger with wings. The American gear seized from the Nationalist forces gave this fierce tiger one wing; the advanced, effective intelligence work it had created for itself gave the tiger its other wing.

The decisive battle. A great war that would settle the fate of China was about to begin, and China's secret war too would enter a new realm.
