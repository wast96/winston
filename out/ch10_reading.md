## A Strange Accident
Among the tangled ninja of the capital a vacuum had opened. The movements of Tsuzura Jūzō's band—the thing every one of them, from his own angle of interest, had kept his eyes fixed on—had vanished from the Kyoto night like mist.
It was Jūzō's very existence that had let both sides, friend and foe alike, earn their bread; and so for the first few months they felt oddly deflated, and then, as half a year wore on, a nameless unease began to spread among them.
While they waited for Jūzō to surface, that unease surfaced instead as a run of strange accidents among the ninja. One accident called up the next, and one calamity after another broke out, none of them in any scenario a single ninja had plotted.
First, there was the death of Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, who fell to the spear of Mari Dōgen of Kōga.
He died, of course.
The root of it, again, lay in the unease of Jūzō's disappearance; but the immediate cause was that Kazama Gohei had goaded Jirōzaemon into it.
"Gohei—when are you going to do something about Kisaru?"
Marry the girl properly, Jirōzaemon meant, and he pressed the point the moment Gohei showed up at the annex of the Chinnō-in.
"That very matter, sir."
Gohei took it up with a show of contrition.
"I'm impatient too. But until I've taken Tsuzura Jūzō's band, dragged whoever stands behind them into the light, earned a great commendation, and been raised in stipend and promoted to the upper ranks, I've felt a marriage would be best held off—that is why I've been slow. All the more so when the bride is one of our own Iga. While this affair is running its course my lord Tokuzen'in would never grant it; and having sworn openly that I've cut all ties with the Iga, I can't afford some needless suspicion. Won't you wait just a little longer, until the thing is settled?"
"I see."
Jirōzaemon rubbed his thin knees, fretting.
"So it all comes back to Jūzō."
"No. As I told you the other day, there's one more, a fearsome enemy—Mari Dōgen of Kōga. By what connection I can't say, but my lord Tokuzen'in trusts him far more deeply than he does me. Let that man snatch the credit, and all my pains go to water."
"Then cut him down. He's still passing himself off as the shrine-keeper at the Maeda mansion, is he?"
"Just so."
"A mere Kōga man—what of him? Leave his punishment to me."
"But he's no ordinary Kōga man. Your opponent is Mari himself."
"And I'm the man they once called Jirōzaemon of Iga."
Jirōzaemon laid his scheme: he would pass himself off as one of Kuroami's band of thieves, break into the Maeda mansion, ransack it thoroughly, cut down the shrine-keeper, and withdraw.
"There's the honor of the Iga to think of, besides."
The old man was worked up. Gohei fanned the flames for all he was worth and watched the old man's excitement with a cold eye.
For ten days from then Jirōzaemon scouted the mansion thoroughly, and on the night the capital sent off its dead at the Bon festival, a little past the Hour of the Ox, he stepped out onto the road, wrapped himself for the first time in years in a ninja's black, and breached the high wall of the Maeda mansion.
Jirōzaemon hid in the shrubbery of the grounds a small half-hour, patiently working his breath-control to bring his own aura into tune with the house's; then he ran, low and swift, along the foot of the wall toward the storehouses.
On the way he passed under the eaves of the row-house. Gohei had told him that Mari Dōgen of Kōga slept in the last room at the eastern end. The lamp was already out. When he had passed the eaves of that dark, three-bay building, sweat broke in a rush from Jirōzaemon's lean body.
(This has never happened to me before. …)
The old man looked at himself and shuddered afresh.
(Have I grown old?)
He tried to reason himself calm, but the truth was that from the instant he entered these grounds Jirōzaemon had lost his empty mind. Where there is no empty mind, they say, there is no Iga art of hiding. In the brain of this old ninja, which should have been void, the image of Mari Dōgen of Kōga had been swelling larger and larger. From about the moment he passed Dōgen's quarters the image ballooned; his composure suffocated beneath it; and the instant he was past the row-house—released, perhaps, from the image—his whole body went slack and the sweat burst out of him all at once.
Flat on the ground, Jirōzaemon turned only his eyes into the darkness ahead. Two storehouses stood there in the dark.
One was the armory. The other, Jirōzaemon already knew perfectly well, was the store of household goods.
What he had planned in advance was to break into that household store first. Then he would fall on Mari Dōgen's quarters.
Not by stealing into Dōgen's bedchamber: he meant to throw open the shutters, kick down the sliding screens, and, fully exposed, bring his blade down on the man in plain sight. When one ninja attacks another's house, that open way was the usual method. To slip in and kill by stealth was, on the contrary, to risk being outfoxed.
But lying there watching the storehouses, Jirōzaemon suddenly changed his mind. He would cut down the image in his head first. Cut it down, then go for the household store. And for that, an attack by the open way on Mari Dōgen's quarters, which would rouse the neighborhood with its noise, would not do. No—unfavorable as it was, he must take the shadow way and steal into the man's bedchamber after all, Jirōzaemon decided.
Flat on the ground, his empty mind gone, the old ninja shifted his heading, scuttling like a black spider. This was what tipped him over the brink into his own undoing.
He crawled in beneath the floor, directly under the bedchamber, and, holding his breath, listened for Dōgen's breathing overhead.
Up on his bedding, Mari Dōgen of Kōga had already sensed the presence. At the moment he sensed it his breathing shifted, just slightly; and Jirōzaemon, beneath the floor, caught the change and went white.
(It may be my imagination.)
Jirōzaemon comforted himself. He had, from the start, held the Kōga men's skill in contempt. Breath stilled, he counted Dōgen's sleeping breaths once more. But he never noticed that the breaths he had begun to count anew were already a counterfeit of sleep.
Still feigning the breath of sleep, Dōgen quietly reached out, took up the hand-spear at his pillow, and lifted it into the air.
Bracing the hand-spear in his fist, Dōgen felt out with his whole body for the presence beneath the floor, and at the same time ran his eyes over the matting, counting the rows of the weave.
The instant Dōgen's eyes, there on the pillow, fastened on a single point of the matting, he flung his body up and drove the spearhead down into that spot of the weave—both in the same motion. The whole weight of his body, sprung into the air, dropped in that instant onto the point of the spear.
Beneath the floor the luckless old ninja, who had gone on counting that counterfeit breath to the last, lay skewered on the dark, damp earth. Yet even now he would not let his own breathing break, not even through the throes of death. His shoulder run clean through, a ghastly, bitter smile rose on his pain-twisted face.
(…So I've failed.)
At last the empty calm of the seasoned ninja came back to Jirōzaemon's mind.
Slowly he slid his left hand into the breast of his robe.
It closed on a small black lump, and he set the lump down beneath his face.
Jirōzaemon's work went on. He drew out a tinder-pouch, worked the flint deftly with one hand, and took into his mouth the short fuse that trailed from the tip of the lump.
Presently a small explosion boomed, muffled, under the floor, and white smoke drifted quietly out into the open.
Mari Dōgen of Kōga, who had been waiting with bared blade at the spot he'd judged the outside escape-hole, saw the smoke and quietly let the strength go out of his sword-hand. A shadow of pity for the man rose about his eyes.
(An end worthy of an Iga man. He's burned his own face past knowing, most likely.)
Footsteps sounded here and there across the grounds. Several of the men came running up to Dōgen.
"Oh—it's the shrine-master."
Dōgen sheathed his sword with a lazy air.
"The noise came from your quarters, didn't it? What's happened?—Ah. Smoke."
"No cause for alarm. A thief. But he's already dead."
Waving back the men who made to rush in,
Dōgen was wary: the Iga man who'd stolen in was dead, true, but there was no telling what devices he might have left behind.
He stood still a while, then jerked his chin at the man beside him.
"It should be safe now. Go in, by all means. You'll see something rare under the floor. A ninja's carcass—like the carcass of a wagtail, a thing one seldom gets to see."
With something sour in his face, he strode off slowly, in long strides, into the dark.
Mingled in among the men was one who watched his retreating back with a fixed, cold eye.
It was Kazama Gohei.
His was no gaze mourning a master's death. If anything, seeing at first hand how powerless that death had been, he felt let down. That this Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, who from Gohei's boyhood had weighed on him at every turn, should die so tamely—he could not have believed it. And at the same time Jirōzaemon's death gave his heart a small, warm rush of relief. Now, in name and in fact both, he was free of the ways of the Iga; his life, it seemed to him, had become wholly his own.
(—And yet.)
Turning sharply on his heel, drawing away from the house where Jirōzaemon had died, Gohei thought:
(To enter the enemy's ground and die there is the most shameful blunder a ninja can make. Wasn't that the very thing the man drummed into Jūzō and me, first to last? He died a good-for-nothing, the like of which even Iga has seldom produced.)
The more he laid the whip to Jirōzaemon's corpse in his mind, the more a strange, tingling pleasure of release welled up in Gohei's chest.
It seemed a waste to go to bed as he was. He would run into the town, he thought, and hold Kisaru's body.
Once he had entered the bamboo path of the Chinnō-in, an odd feeling came over Gohei. The grove was dark. The blackness swayed; wind poured ceaselessly through it. Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon, who had lived in this grove day after day to fix upon the world the impression of an eccentric monk called the Bamboo Saint, seemed to him even now to be somewhere in this wind, guarding Kisaru.
(Foolishness.)
Gohei sneered at himself, drew all at once, and flicked the point of his sword up aslant toward the sky, as though slashing the image of Jirōzaemon before him. A stalk of green bamboo at his feet rang with a hard crack.
The bamboo's crown swept a great arc across the sky and toppled into the grove. For the first time Gohei opened a clean, bright smile. Kisaru, hearing the noise perhaps, opened the wicket gate and came out in her nightclothes.
"Who's there?"
"It's me."
The words were hardly out before Gohei had his arm around her waist. The face bent over hers burned with lust.
Kisaru twisted her hips, trying to pull away. Gohei caught the moving hips and pressed them to his own thighs.
"Don't move."
"The dew—I'll get wet."
"Change your clothes after. That's all."
The two of them fell silent. Fallen bamboo leaves lay strewn about. Dew had settled everywhere, and it wet Kisaru's calves.
Gohei caressed her fiercely, as though meting out a punishment. He ground his back teeth. Perhaps a joy of revenge on Jirōzaemon—who now had no power left to check him in anything—was throbbing in his body.
Each time Kisaru writhed, the bamboo leaves rustled slowly. And under those brows of hers, curved like a bow-moon, her two eyes narrowed like a heavenly maiden's.
Gohei let her body go. On his face was the emptiness that follows a completed revenge. His eyes, gone cold again, looked down at her body, fallen like a corpse.
Kisaru had turned her fine-cut chin upward, the sky mirrored in her lightly closed eyes. The emptiness that comes after desire was in her too, though of a different kind. As always, a gnawing remorse welled up in her.
(Is this, in the end, all there is between this man and me?)
Gohei came, for the most part, in the dead of night. And when the act was done he would go back to the town of Kyoto as if in dread of the light of dawn. Each time, Kisaru turned over the emptiness of her tie to this man. Yet the print he had first pressed into her body would not fade, and whenever his hand touched her again, she trembled to her very heart, as though under a spell.
(Over and over.)
By the time she could think that, her mind had begun to grow as her body had grown. The deepest sorrow a human knows, perhaps, begins the moment one grasps this single thing. How many times over a lifetime, Kisaru wondered, does a person die through the chain of desire—born, burning, then perishing? A girl grows quickly. She was no longer a mere child.
But Gohei did not notice. As always, he spoke in a tone that toyed with her heart the way one toys with a thing under the tip of one's toe.
"Listen. Your father's dead."
"What?"
Whatever Gohei had said, Kisaru seemed not to have heard it. He stood still, looking down at her. She lay on her back, her right knee raised a little.
"Your father was killed by Mari Dōgen of Kōga."
She caught her breath and sprang upright. She did not ask whether it was true; she only held Gohei's face in a hard, glittering stare. But like a true child of Iga her mind spun quickly, and she had grasped the whole of the situation. Lips parted a little, her gaze fixed on Gohei and nothing else, Kisaru was silent for a long time. Then, in a voice that came rasping from the depth of her throat:
"…And you watched it happen."
"By the time I got there, it was over and done."
"Why didn't you avenge him?"
"Avenge? —Avenge what?"
"As an Iga man."
"I'm no Iga man. —The dead Jirōzaemon acknowledged that, and you yourself have already granted it, haven't you?"
"Then avenge him as a son-in-law would."
"There's a limit to what you can foist on me. I've not yet made the marriage with you—and you know the reason for that as well."
"Then what are you to my father?"
"No more than a stranger, I'd say."
Kisaru went white and rose to her feet in one motion, trembling in small shudders with rage. Gohei smirked. This must have been exactly what he was after. He had provoked her anger on purpose. For him, no doubt, it was the perfect chance to be rid of this Iga woman.
"No need to fly into such a rage. Wasn't this understood between us from the very start?"
"Very well."
As she rose, Kisaru dashed the clod of earth she'd snatched up in her hand down at her feet with all her strength.
"Then it's you I'll take my revenge on!"
"Don't lose your head. The one who ran Jirōzaemon through was Mari Dōgen of Kōga."
"I've not lost my head. I'll kill Dōgen. And I'll kill Kazama Gohei."
"Wait."
Gohei twisted aside on the instant and pinned her sword-arm under his right armpit. A dagger glinted blue in her hand.
"Let go!"
"Don't be mad."
With that, he flung her body off like a ball. She flew some five ken and lit on her feet in the grove. But in the same instant her face twisted with fierce pain: the sole of her right foot had come down on a sharpened bamboo stump, and it had pierced clean through.
Gohei walked off at his ease. Crouched where she was, Kisaru lost all will to go after him. That everything was over, she knew now to the bone.
As she crouched on the dead leaves, the seed Gohei had left in her a moment before ran warm and heedless down the inside of her thigh. Feeling it creep across her skin, Kisaru bent double and crumpled limply to the earth. And at that same moment, as if only now waking to the sorrow of it all, she began to sob, hard, choking down the sound. Her long intimacy with Gohei had cost her her father and, in the end, cost her Gohei himself. Only that fluid ran on, heedless, going back to the soil, as if to tell the emptiness of the bond between man and woman.
The man who dropped lightly down inside the earthen wall of Kohagi's villa at Komatsudani was Mari Dōgen of Kōga. As he crossed the thatch-grassed garden at a brisk walk, the seventeenth-night moon, just risen, threw his figure up in black silhouette. Strolling at his ease through the moonlight, the man had every look of one long used to coming to this house.
Prying the shutters open with a picklock, Dōgen stepped up into the dark corridor and went on inward with that footless gait, soft as treading on cloud. At every turn of the corridor a stout barred gate had been fitted, and Dōgen lowered each lock behind him. Presently it became a covered passageway, and across it a study-style building came into view. Of late it was being used as Kohagi's sleeping quarters.
Merchant folk though they were, the household aped a daimyo's style and even kept a guard-room for the night watch; but Kohagi made no use of it. Boldly, she always went to sleep alone under this roof, on layered quilts of silk.
He slid the screen smoothly open, and the sweet, womanly scent that flowed out pleased Dōgen's nostrils.
"I've come breaking in again, Lady Kohagi."
Lit in a round pool by the dark-lantern Dōgen held out, Kohagi—no bedding even laid beside her at this dead hour—sat prim and upright before a woman's writing-desk.
"Well, this is a surprise."
"What is, pray?"
"Such fine readiness—as if you'd known I would be calling tonight."
Dōgen plumped himself down cross-legged.
In the darkness Kohagi let a faint half-smile rise to her lips.
"That said, I don't mean it for a welcome, mind."
"Don't say such cutting things."
"What is your business?"
"Come now. I left it unfinished the other night, if you recall."
"Mochizuki Gyōbuzaemon's—"
"Just so. Gyōbuzaemon of Kōga. Your foster-father. The matter of whether that Gyōbuzaemon, at the point of death, left some word for you, Lady Kohagi."
"My answer is as I gave it the other night: I heard nothing."
"By the Kōga code, when Kōga folk—though they work in distant provinces and are split into enemy camps—when one of them comes asking, there's a rule that they must share their secrets with one another. Without it, the whole province's rappa would go to ruin. Best be honest and tell me."
"Kohagi was raised by the hand of a Kōga warrior, yes. But, as I have said time and again, Kohagi is no Kōga shinobi."
"A woman who keeps up her concealment with rare persistence. Take the master's certificate of the art from a ninja like Gyōbuzaemon, and you're a fine shinobi indeed. You may fool other men's eyes, but you'll not blind the eyes of this Mari Dōgen of Kōga. —Hear me. Once you've become a Kōga hand, then whether you were once the love-child of the lord Sasaki Hakkansai, and are now, through the good offices of Ishida Jibu-no-shō Mitsunari, the adopted daughter of the Ōkurakyō Hōin, Imai Sōkyū of Sakai, living in wealth—all such stations are no more than a hollow gilding to dress up the floating world. What you are, at bottom, is a Kōga hand. And so, when I, your fellow of Kōga, tell you to reveal it, reveal it you must."
"What a grand way of putting it—"
and pressing her sleeve to her mouth, Kohagi laughed, letting her body sway as if in mockery of Dōgen.
"Call whom you like, by all means. If you won't open your mouth, I'll give you my own guess instead."
"Dōgen, or whatever you are—how many times must I say it? If you don't behave yourself, I'll call for someone."
Dōgen leaned his black-robed knees forward and, fixing his glittering eyes full on Kohagi, held her transfixed and said nothing for a while; then, in a hard voice, he spoke.
"You're a rappa of Jibu-no-shō Mitsunari."
"………"
"What orders have you had from Mitsunari? —Will you not answer?"
Dōgen sprang all at once, seized Kohagi by the collar in an eagle's grip, and threw her down in a flash.
"Speak. —Or by the Kōga code I'll deal out your punishment."
Pinning both Kohagi's legs with his knee, tightening his left hand at her throat, he held his short-sword inverted in his right and lowered the point until it brushed the lashes of her right eye.
"I'll pierce the eye. —Understood?"
Dōgen tickled her beautifully even lashes with the point of the short-sword. Without so much as a blink, Kohagi spoke, almost whispering, into his eyes.
"Next will be the left eye. And after that, you'll shave off my ears and pull out my front teeth. I heard of those dreadful Kōga punishments from Gyōbuzaemon. Do your worst. What I don't know, I can only say I don't know."
"A stubborn one, aren't you."
With a look of mild astonishment, Dōgen said:
"So—it seems I've been questioning you too head-on. Women, by nature, stand up well to torment. There's a way to question a woman. What do you say—won't you become my kunoichi?"
Dōgen snapped the short-sword back into its sheath, but did not slacken the force with which he pinned her.
"Let's take our ease, and I'll hear about Mitsunari and the rest as pillow-talk. Eh, daughter of the Ōkurakyō Hōin?"
Dōgen's right hand, free of the sword now, was already moving toward the skirt of Kohagi's robe.
"Hold."
Kohagi, not even struggling, let Dōgen keep her body pinned as he pleased, and in a clear, still voice said:
"I'll not allow you to force me."
"Ravishing a woman is a sort of perquisite of the rappa's trade."
"For that perquisite, you may not leave this Komatsudani alive."
"You'll call for help, you mean? Don't trouble. In Kōga they called me Mari."
"Then let me put it as a plea. There is one to whom I must keep my chastity."
"Chastity? Ho—and who might that be? Some townsman? Or is it a lord's young heir you've set your heart on? Either way, it's no concern of Dōgen's."
"Ah—"
Even Kohagi's face went white. Dōgen's hand moved quickly and threw her skirts wide open.
"………?"
But against Kohagi's worst expectation—she had closed her eyes in resignation—Dōgen's hand made no further move. His breath, as he stared at something in the darkness, had gone quiet and cold. Presently he took up the dark-lantern he'd set down at his feet and, saying nothing, shone it on the base of her right inner thigh.
In the lantern-light Kohagi's leg stirred a little in shame. Dōgen's eyes coldly ignored it. Presently he spoke.
"You know an Iga man."
At the words, Kohagi gave a voiceless cry in the dim light.
"This scar. Made by the slender blade an Iga man uses only in the work of stealth. To stab a woman in such a place, there must be some deep obsession behind it. Tell me the man's name."
As if echoing Dōgen's voice, there came just then a voice calling his name, sounding for all the world like an old friend's.
"Mari Dōgen of Kōga—"
Dōgen whipped around. In the same motion his hand flew out and snuffed the lantern's flame. The room fell back into its earlier darkness, with only a thread of moonlight slanting in through a gap in the shutters.
"Who's there?"
Dōgen said it in a strangled voice. From the darkness there was no answer for a while. Then a low voice came, from unexpectedly close by.
"Meeting in so strange a place as this, it seems odd to give one's name. I've an arrow here. It's trained on your chest, Dōgen. Get off the woman, and be quick about it."
At the word "arrow" Dōgen sprang up like a black bird and crouched in a corner of the room with his back to the shutters, his sword already drawn.
"Very prudent."
As a precaution, perhaps, the voice had shifted from where it had been a moment before.
"While you're at it, open the shutter by your hand, won't you? I'd like to cut you down—but blade-play in a woman's room is a crude business."
"Heh. As if you could cut me down."
Dōgen had rallied, sure enough, and recovered the composure that was his.
"Open it."
"I'll refuse. To be nailed to the door-board like a pilloried man the moment I open it—that's no pretty picture. Why don't you open it?"
"Kohagi."
At her name Kohagi started to her feet. She had realized it some while ago: the owner of that voice could be none other than Tsuzura Jūzō. But how had the man come to be here? Why, and from where?—the questions flickered through her mind. Since Jūzō had stabbed her and gone, long ago, she had never once set eyes on him. That he had been working in the capital through Kuroami she of course knew perfectly well; but where he had gone after that, however she searched, she had never been able to learn.
"Take the crossbar off the shutters for him. Don't stir until then, Dōgen. Throw them wide, Kohagi."
Kohagi opened the paper screen, stepped out into the corridor, and threw the shutters all the way open. Moonlight poured in like a flood, and the room filled with pale blue light.
As though born that very instant out of the darkness, Jūzō stood planted heavily in the middle of the room, bathed in the light. It seemed to have been a mere bluff—there was no sign of a bow or arrow in his hands.
"Ahh…"
Dōgen let out a full, delighted sound, almost absurdly out of place in such a crisis—the innocent joy of a ninja whose riddle had just been solved. The man before him was, by any reckoning, an Iga man. And if an Iga man, then this was Tsuzura Jūzō.
"Jūzō. Well come indeed. I've been long in the hunting of you. My thanks to the Kohagi I used as bait, too."
"It's too soon to be giving thanks."
Keeping his eyes on Dōgen, Jūzō slowly closed the distance between them. As he did, Dōgen too, sword hanging from his right hand, edged sidelong toward the open shutters at the same pace and breath. The moment he passed through the shutters would be his moment of danger. Jūzō's own sword was still undrawn. Yet toward the crest of that danger for Dōgen, Jūzō's stride seemed to be climbing with exact measure.
For his part, Dōgen had no intention of stopping Jūzō's breath here and now. Better, he reckoned, to cut an arm or a leg and halt him, take him alive, and get out of him the background to this whole affair.
Jūzō, on the other hand, meant to cut Dōgen clean in two. The one great tumor of this whole business, when all was said, was Dōgen. And, seizing the chance, he no doubt meant as well to avenge his master, Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon. How this man, who ought to have been lying low at Otogi Pass in Iga, came to know of Jirōzaemon's death and how it happened—that will be told later.
At last Dōgen had inched his way to the open gap of the shutters. In the streaming moonlight the shadow of his left side stretched long. He broke into a broad grin. But with the light behind him it only widened Jūzō's pupils, and his easy smile never reached the man.
The instant Dōgen's whole body stood fully revealed in the moonlight, Jūzō, still in his thrusting stance, drove straight in.
It was a plain, ninja's own stroke, bold to the point of recklessness; yet there was an eeriness to it—try to parry it by working the blade as in ordinary swordsmanship, and there was no telling how, at the last, it might transform.
Dōgen knew that much of the Iga ninja's swordwork. Meeting the wind-pressure of Jūzō's rush, he did not cross blades but leapt sideways clear onto the wet veranda outside the shutters.
But Jūzō was quicker still: he swept past in front of Dōgen, came out to the front edge of the veranda, and spun round to face him—to cut off the man's escape, no doubt.
As he spun, he launched another reckless thrust. Dōgen held his guard at middle height. For an instant it looked as if Jūzō's thrust extended. But that was an unlucky illusion for Dōgen. The long sword alone shot from Jūzō's hand like a thing alive and flew at Dōgen's throat like a lightning-flash. Dōgen struck it away in his desperation. Jūzō's sword rang out and went spinning high into the empty air. In the same instant Jūzō had sunk low and drawn his short-sword; and as he drew, Dōgen's left hand was lopped clean off at the wrist.
The hand fell to the veranda. But the hand was all that lay there. Dōgen's body had long since vanished from sight.
"We'll settle it another day."
Dōgen's bright voice came down from above. There was no seeing him. The moment he was cut, he must have sprung up to the eaves of the roof, trailing blood as he went. Up overhead, footfalls whispered along the cypress-bark shingles and faded into the distance.
"Is there any shōchū?"
"What for?"
Kohagi had come out and asked.
"I'd like to pickle the hand in a liquor-cask and send it back to Dōgen. When day breaks, will you have someone carry it to the magistrate's mansion?"
Sheathing his sword, Jūzō went on,
"And while you're at it, I'd like a horse."
"A horse—?"
"I came all this long night-road to see you, and a meddler I didn't want spoiled the tryst. Dōgen's no ordinary man. Either he's still lurking in these grounds, or he'll whistle up his genin at once and come back at us. I've no wish to be run through with you in my arms. I'll go back the way I came."
"Is it true? That you came for the sake of seeing me?"
"It's no lie. Being apart from you a while, I came to know my own mind. I'm ashamed to say it, but in all truth, it seems I've grown fond of you."
"That makes me happy."
Kohagi clung to Jūzō. Presently, tilting her head a little,
"And where is it you're going back to?"
"You troublesome woman. No sooner do you say you're happy than you show the rappa in you, right there in the tail of the words."
"No."
Kohagi shook her head.
"I only want to live with you a while, Master Jūzō—even for a little."
"I'll refuse. There's no knowing when you'd cut my throat in my sleep. —Where's the stable?"
"I'll have it tied outside the brushwood gate at the back. Wait a moment, if you please."
Kohagi crossed the garden and was gone. Jūzō made his way slowly toward the back door. When he had waited a while outside the brushwood gate, Kohagi came leading two horses, holding their bridles herself.
"One will do."
"No. The chestnut is my horse."
"Where are you going?"
"I don't know—wherever you go, Master Jūzō."
"Do as you please."
With that, Jūzō set off at a gallop like a gust of wind. Well behind, Kohagi came after.
A pine-lined road runs north and south along the foot of Higashiyama. Jūzō's horse came out onto it, then turned south and galloped hard—making, no doubt, to cross the hills at Shibutani and come out at Daigo. Just as Kohagi's horse was about to turn south along the pines, a small shadow came gliding down from a pine branch like a flying squirrel through the air. It dropped onto Kohagi's saddle, snatched the reins in an instant, and, scooping up her thigh, tried to throw her off.
"Get off."
"What are you doing?"
"Can't you see this?"
The shadow held a dagger up and pressed it to Kohagi's breast.
All the while the horse ran on after Jūzō. Presently one rider dropped away, and the shadow, flattened against the saddle, sent the horse flying with a splendid handling of the reins.
Somewhere past Gō-no-kuchi, riding south down the Uji River, the horse must have been ridden to death. Jūzō threw down the reins and got out of the saddle. Away to the east, over the hazy hills of Kōga, the night was already beginning to whiten into dawn.
Presently the horse behind caught up. It came right up to Jūzō's chest and stopped, and from its back a human shadow leapt down.
"What—is that you, Kisaru?"
"Sorry it's not your Lady Kohagi?"
Jūzō said nothing and started briskly up the little path that cut into the hills.
"Wait."
For all the wheedling in her voice, it was Kisaru who came up the slope after him the more nimbly, and she said,
"I saw the whole business at Komatsudani."
"I know it. I stopped by your house at the Chinnō-in, and went straight on from there to Komatsudani—and you dogged my heels all the way, stubborn as a young raccoon-dog."
"How cruel. —Then if that Kōga man hadn't been there, you'd have been holding Kohagi right before my eyes, Master Jūzō."
"Well—does it come to that, now?"
Jūzō gave a wry smile and, refusing to be drawn by the Kisaru at his heels, climbed briskly on up the slope.
Even after he had gone to ground at Otogi Pass in Iga, Jūzō would run the night-roads and come out often to the capital. It was just yesterday: appearing in Kyoto after a long absence, Jūzō had gone from Kuroami's hideout and stopped by the Chinnō-in to look in on Jirōzaemon and his daughter—but neither in the grove nor in the hermitage was there any sign of the two of them. Going round to the annex, he found Kisaru alone, gazing from the window at the sky above the grove, and asked:
"Where's your father?"
"Killed. Yesterday."
Kisaru had her eyes open, vacant, gazing dully at Jūzō's face.
"By whom?"
"Mari Dōgen of Kōga."
"What…"
Jūzō lost his words, and his two fists trembled a little. His master Shimotsuge Jirōzaemon had, in the past, given him more than a few bitter cups to swallow. Yet, oddly, he had never been able to hate the man from the bottom of his heart—because to Jūzō, who loved the traditions of the Iga ninja, Jirōzaemon's very character, steeped in intrigue and deceit, had seemed the incarnation of the Iga man himself. He had the margin to regard the old man's nature with something closer to affection than to respect. And it was a bond of twenty years, counting from his early childhood; such a death could not but be a blow to him.
And by Dōgen, at that. Whatever the circumstances, it could be read as an open challenge from the Kōga ninja to the Iga.
"Why don't you take vengeance?"
Jūzō laid a hand on Kisaru's shoulder as he said it. Startled, she looked up at him.
"Me?"
"If you go at it meaning to kill, you can. If your heart fails you, get Gohei to second you."
"I'm done with Gohei. I can't ask that man for anything now."
Kisaru poured out, all in a jumble, Jirōzaemon's end and Gohei's behavior of late, and then:
"Let me ask you, Master Jūzō. Please."
"That I refuse. My own work would never get done if I stopped to deal with every Dōgen who came along. This is for you to do alone, Kisaru. Luckily the Kōga men don't know your face, so you'll find some way or other to get close."
He pushed her off with deliberate coldness, to drive her toward revenge. But even so, the reason he went straight on to call on Kohagi was that he thought the movements of the Kōga men in the capital might be learned from what Kohagi let slip. As Jūzō climbed from the bamboo thickets of Matsubara up toward Komatsudani on Amidagamine, Kisaru followed softly after, keeping out of sight. There was suspicion of his conduct in her following; but more than that, no doubt, a woman's instinct had caught the scent of a man on his way to a woman.
The path came at last to the ridge, and the view of the small hours opened out. The trees had already fallen away. Before them a sweep of bamboo grass spread wide and ran on to the morning haze of the far mountains. As the two of them parted the grass with a soft rustle, the faint light of the pre-dawn dark spilled over its tips. Jūzō turned back toward Kisaru, who followed with a small whisper of straw sandals behind him, and said:
"So it was then. When I was fighting Dōgen, why didn't you help me—land even a single stroke on him? I'm not blaming you. But if you'd acted, the Kōga man would most likely never have got out of Komatsudani alive. A pity. Why was it?"
"I don't know—not even myself. —I just stared, and stared, at the face of that woman they call Kohagi, and nothing else."
"Is that all?"
"Yes. —My body went hot. And my fingers and toes, I think, had gone numb."
"Numb? A strange creature you are."
"Why should it be? —If you call this strange, then a man like you knows nothing of a woman's heart, Master Jūzō."
"No, I don't understand it. So a woman's heart means abandoning a comrade in his moment of danger, does it?"
"No. It means Kisaru is in love with you, Master Jūzō."
"I'd like to thank you for it—but if you love me, why didn't the wish rise in you to stab Dōgen and save me?"
"Still on about that. Dōgen means nothing to Kisaru, one way or another."
"That's a hard thing to say."
"You'd never lose to the likes of Dōgen, Master Jūzō. —Never mind Dōgen: when I saw Kohagi and thought, so this is the one he loves, it was as if fire ran through my whole body. It's no lie. …I could hardly keep on my feet."
"And that's what you call a woman's heart?"
"Yes."
Jūzō burst out laughing.
"Your father Jirōzaemon used to boast of you, but at this rate you're worse than any plain fool of a girl. A woman can't be made into a ninja after all, it seems."
"I needn't be any ninja. Kisaru will just be your bride, Master Jūzō."
"That would put me in a fix."
"So you do like that woman Kohagi better, after all?"
"I can't say I'm fond of her—but there's a bond of the flesh between us."
"Then make a bond of the flesh with Kisaru too."
"I'll not pledge my heart in it, mind. Will that do?"
"With Kohagi as well?"
"Of course. If a ninja gave his heart to every other soul, he'd not have bodies enough to spare."
"Then Kisaru will do with just the body too."
"One day, we'll do that."
"'One day' won't do."
"And what would you have me do?"
"Here. Now."
Kisaru stopped short, and, for all her boldness, reddened and said it in a small voice—though her eyes alone, strangely tense, held Jūzō's face in a hard stare. Jūzō gave a wry smile and said:
"First you go back to the Shimotsuge house in Iga. Then set about finding a way to kill Dōgen. Once you've done that, I'll grant you any wish you name."
"The bride business too?"
"Mm."
Once the words were out, a sudden, uncertain smile crossed Jūzō's face, and he added,
"Don't count on it, mind. If I'm still alive, that is."
It was a bad habit of Jūzō's—he still thought of Kisaru too much as a child. He had certainly not meant those words in earnest; but Kisaru, for her part, all but danced for joy as she answered:
"Then I'll go home to Shimotsuge, good as gold. A promise from you, Master Jūzō, is a sure thing."
