## Author's Preface: The Full Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi

The Full Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi is the second book of my whole work, Nameless Heroes.

The Full Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi has, with the first book, Rooting Out Traitors in the North, an unbreakable continuity and a single, unbroken spirit running through them both. Beyond that, there is something in the writing of it I have sought to better: it is my hope to write more carefully, more soberly, and to avoid so far as I can any needless aftertroubles arising out of the words themselves.

The main substance of The Full Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi centers, to be sure, upon the whole working course of the failed sanction of Wang Jingwei, taking in many an intricate episode: the flying transfer to Vietnam, the passing on of the mission, the shifting of personnel, the gathering of intelligence, the disposition of reconnaissance, the sudden receipt of orders, three strikes in one day, the wrong carriage struck by mistake, the recall to Chongqing, the special dispatch to Shanghai, renewed effort upon effort, and sacrifice four times over; but it is matched, too, with precious materials bearing upon every side, set down as corroboration. Set side by side, this said and that answered, there is much here of the fineness of a three-way confrontation of testimony; and you, my readers, may of your own accord ponder out the boundless turns of possibility stored up within.

As the saying has it, without coincidence there is no story; and the writer himself is one who was there on the ground, who directed the affair, who tasted defeat at first hand, who ate the bitter fruit of his own doing—all four together—and who yet remains among the living, a survivor of the calamity, and may be called, one might say, the most powerful witness of all. This truthful record, so rich in the stuff of legend, if the reader will bear with my clumsy pen, must surely have its readableness, and a certain historical value besides.

In the book I have also given a brief introduction to the man himself—Mr. Wang Zhaoming (Jingwei), a founding hero of the Republic and a forerunner of the revolution, who in his own day had likewise "sanctioned" the Prince Regent of the Manchu Qing, and of whom those lines were written: "With a full heart he sang in the market of Yan; / calmly he played the captive of Chu; / one clean stroke of the blade, and gladly— / he would not fail the head of his youth."—so that the reader may know who he was. To the older generation he is of course known to all; but to the young of the present day he has grown somewhat unfamiliar. And Wang Jingwei being the foremost key figure, it is as well that he be clearly accounted for.

To this end I specially asked the learned and gifted Mr. Liu Yuanshen to analyze, from an objective standpoint and drawing upon Mr. Wang's own poems and writings, his native character and his cast of mind, and thereby to trace the thread and track by which he lost his self-knowledge, failed in the end to keep his integrity, and came to endless remorse. This passage amounts to a confession in his own words, and reveals him far more aptly than any other man's appraisal could.

The sanction of Wang Jingwei at Hanoi took place on the twentieth and twenty-first of March in the twenty-eighth year of the Republic, now more than forty years ago; and in all that time there have never ceased to be those who took it up as matter for their writing, appraising the people and events within the case and without—true and false alike, every manner of telling—and who, by forced and fanciful connection, bred no few side-branches and offshoots besides. This Full Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi is aimed precisely at these questions, and offers the correct answer to them, an answer consonant with reason and in accord with the facts. To be sure, the Hanoi case is long since a thing of the past; there is nothing now to shrink from, and nothing that needs clearing up—I speak to the matter as it stands, doing no more than affirming a single fact.

Having come this far, let me disclose a little more of what is in my heart. I have thought all along that, although men like us have spent the better part of our lives in secret-service work—work of a nature so mysterious and so peculiar, and shown above all in the cast of the mind—so that to all appearance every one of us seems to have a heart of forged iron, to acknowledge neither kith nor kin, and to be markedly unlike ordinary men: yet the truth is that whatever the calling a man follows, he is a man for all that, and a man must needs have human nature and human feeling. And so, in the words with which I set these stories down, I have held fast to just this point, to press home that human nature and feeling are present in every part.

The course of my writing about the sanction of Wang at Hanoi had many a twist to it. In fixing upon a title I spent no little deliberation. At first I used "A Record of the Mission to Hanoi," which I judged not quite fitting; later I changed it to "Disgrace at Hanoi," yet still felt it wanting in ring. A friend pointed out that it might as well be called "The Stabbing of Wang at Hanoi" or "Stabbing Wang, Orders Dishonored"; and I thought these two titles good enough, and striking too—only that word "stab" in them I truly could not accept. For by its very definition, to "stab" is to kill a man off his guard: which not only runs counter to all that is open and aboveboard, but is muddled in its meaning besides, and stands quite at odds with us who assert justice and execute the law of the nation. And so I would not have it.

Afterward, for all our further turning it over, we still had not one that satisfied; and so, having no choice, we set down for the time being the title "Disgrace at Hanoi." It was only when this preface had been set in type and I was reading the proofs that I at last decided to change it to "The Full Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi."

As to whether I was willing to write or unwilling, able to write or unable, and how it should be written, and the like—it is a thing that, to speak of it, stirs deep feeling in me.

Back in November of the thirtieth year of the Republic, once the news was confirmed that I had come to grief in Shanghai and fallen into the hands of the enemy and their puppets, our old adversary Chen Bijun—Wang's wife—came post-haste through the night from Nanjing to Shanghai, together with her clansman-cousin Chen Chunpu, bent on interrogating me in person. Full of vengefulness, overbearing and haughty, she stamped her feet and howled, pointing at my nose and cursing me roundly, and forcing me to write out a confession then and there. I glared back at her, half minded to bite her to ease my hatred; and finding nothing else for it, I resolved to turn my head away and take no notice, and let her do with me as she would. In the end it was Ye Jiqing, the wife of Li Shiqun, who by cajoling and coaxing on the side broke the impasse for the moment; and the upshot was that I gave her not a scrap of paper, not a single word.

The next year—some months, that is, after my capture—one Nakajima Shin'ichi, who served as a "shokutaku" (the equivalent of an adviser) in the Ume Kikan, one of the three great Japanese special-service organs in China, and who acted on behalf of the Kikan's deputy chief, Colonel Haruke Yoshitane (successor to Kagesa Sadaaki), tried by every wile to inveigle me into writing an account of the "Hanoi operation." I put him off with words, but never once set pen to paper.

In the years that followed, articles about me and the Hanoi affair appeared off and on in the newspapers and magazines; and though some of them were wide of the mark, I kept my silence still, and said not a word.

Not until the fifty-fifth year of the Republic, after I had retired from the army, did I mean, of my own accord, to write this story down; but, unlooked-for, a friend's well-meant counsel had it that I had still better not, and so I laid it aside. And with that pause the slow years slipped by, another twelve or thirteen of them.

In October of the sixty-eighth year, the Intelligence Bureau of the Ministry of National Defense brought out A Life of Mr. Dai Yunong and The Collected Works of Mr. Dai Yunong (not for sale, and not distributed to the public). Among these was a set piece of five and a half pages under the heading "Discerning Loyalty from Treason: Rooting Out the Traitor at Hanoi"; and with that the affair was, in an unofficial way, made public at last.

Having read it, I could not help the old hunter's thrill at the sight of game; and after some thought I said to myself: better set it all down, from first to last, just as it was! In truth the thing had long been an open secret, and ours was a cause with just warrant, upright and aboveboard—what was there in it that could not be told to the world?

Last of all, I cherish a private wish—that I may be granted to write this book well: first, that it may furnish the historian with trustworthy material; second, that it may give the curious a story to enjoy; and beyond that, holding still to my first intent, that I may, in a spirit of penitence, honor the memory of our nameless heroes, who for the War of Resistance gave themselves in sacrifice, and for the rooting out of traitors laid down their lives to keep their virtue whole!

The Japanese army raised the "July 7 Incident" at the Marco Polo Bridge. Before that, such provocative "incidents" on the Japanese part had often enough occurred in the parts about Beiping and Tianjin, so that at the time it never once entered our minds that this was the prelude to a long eight-year War of Resistance.

In fact, it was only in a telegram from Mr. Dai on the thirteenth of July in the twenty-sixth year that we were informed: "It is judged here that the Japanese side has no sincerity toward a peaceful settlement, and that we should, of course, make war upon them in resistance."

By the nineteenth of July, the Generalissimo, Chiang, delivered an address formally proclaiming China's resolve to resist, saying: "We being the weaker, should we come to the 'last extremity,' there is nothing for it but to stake the life of the whole nation, that the country may live! … If ever we truly reach that unavoidable 'last extremity,' then of course there is nothing for us but sacrifice, nothing but resistance. …"

And it is no idle boasting on our part: the comrades of the Juntong had already, as early as the twenty-first and twenty-second years of the Republic, thrown themselves into active anti-Japanese work. By the early years of the war, the twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth, we had done not merely a few operations to sanction traitors, but were bending all our strength, in silence, to fall in with the government's whole strategy! If the words "bloodshed and sacrifice" be used to describe what we actually did, we should be worthy of them without shame.

At the beginning of the twenty-eighth year, I was ordered south, and by way of Shanghai and Hong Kong reached Hanoi, there to take up once more a great charge whose nature not even I could foresee.

The cause of it was this: Wang Zhaoming (Jingwei)—Vice-Director-General of the Chinese Nationalist Party and chairman of the People's Political Council—openly betrayed party and country, forsook the ranks of the resistance, slipped away from Chongqing on the eighteenth of December in the twenty-seventh year, and by way of Yunnan made for Hanoi; and then, on the twenty-ninth of December, in answer to the statement of the Japanese premier Konoe, actually issued the "Yan Telegram," brazenly suing for peace on terms of every humiliation. With the very life-thread of the nation at stake, how could such a thing be borne!

After Mr. Dai had come to Hanoi together with me and Wang Luqiao, the task given us at the outset was this: "to keep strict watch on Wang's movements, and by every means to reconnoiter the doings of the Wang faction."

Two days later Mr. Dai returned to Chongqing, laying this heavy burden upon me.
