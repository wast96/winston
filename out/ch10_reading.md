## Chapter 10

Only Mei Yingzi was gone, and yet I felt an emptiness past telling. My eyes travelled from Bai Ping's face to where Mei Yingzi's should have been, and still I could see that smile of hers—proud, and yet not proud; pleased, and yet not pleased.

"Well, Xu, won't you come and sit a while at my place too?"

I became aware that the car was slowing; Bai Ping was making ready to get out.

"No," I said. "Tomorrow at three o'clock I shall be at the Liti Café."

What I meant was that, if Bai Ping had anything to say to me, I hoped she too would come at three. Then the car stopped before an apartment house, and Bai Ping opened the door to take her leave of Mrs. Stephen and Stephen.

I watched her get out. As the car started again I was still watching her, but she did not, after all, go in through the apartment gate; she paused only a moment at the door, and then seemed to walk on. The moving of the car made it impossible for me to see her any longer. I began to be anxious for her; several times an impulse rose in me to get out, but—whether from weariness, or from a dread of trouble, or from a fear that Mrs. Stephen would think it strange—I did not act on it, nor did I tell them that Bai Ping had not gone home. What rose in my mind was the talk Bai Ping and I had had, while dancing, about Mei Yingzi: had I wounded her heart by it? Could it be true, as Stephen and his wife said, that Bai Ping was in love with me?

The street-lamps were dim; only one or two bars still burned their lights, with music and singing sounding from them; not a soul was on the road, and the car raced past. Within it the silence was desolate. At last I broke the stillness, saying:

"Mrs. Stephen, I have really put you to far too much trouble today."

"Once in a while I find it quite diverting too," she said with composure.

"You must ordinarily go to bed very early."

"Always by about eleven o'clock, at least," she said.

"Then I have wronged you indeed."

"Don't say so," she said. "Do you love amusing yourself like this so very much?"

"Not at all," I said. "It is only that my present circumstances and state of mind leave me no way to go on living an orderly life."

"I hope that, through all the turmoil of circumstance and of mind, you may still do well the work you love to do."

"Thank you."

"Marry," she said. "I often say to Stephen that marriage does him harm, but that it would do you good."

"You think so?"

"Because he loves adventure, loves novelty, loves motion; while you—your nature needs a calm and quiet setting."

"Perhaps that is so," said Stephen. "But my marriage gives my love and my faith a fixed point, and gives my too-lopsided nature a balance."

"Yet with that balance, you will achieve nothing much in your career."

"Why must a man demand achievement in a career?" I said with feeling. "To be able to order one's life into harmony is happiness enough."

"If I had developed along the line of my love of adventure," said Stephen, "I might have become an explorer—or might long since have lost my life by it."

"But as for him—" Mrs. Stephen turned to me again: "if you were to marry well, it would help your career and your work, and would surely add to the happiness of your life."

"I am a confirmed bachelor."

"You have no ideal partner in view?" said Mrs. Stephen.

"By now I rather think Mei Yingzi has quite swept you off your feet," said Stephen.

"I do not love a life lived out under the sun," I said.

"To my mind Bai Ping is starlight at the bottom of the sea," said Stephen.

"But," said Mrs. Stephen, laughing, "what he needs is lamplight."

"I am a confirmed bachelor still."

"That is merely a reaction," Stephen said, contradicting me.

"I have not denied that," I said. "The fancies women rouse in me are ludicrous. Some are like a piece of cream cake—one feels one wants a little of it only when one is hungry; some are like chewing-gum, to be chewed idly when one has nothing to do and the hour hangs dull; and some are like a fresh flower, at which I only wish to take one look, and linger over a moment—no more."

"What you want—is it a cat, then? One to keep you company, placid and true of heart, understanding your speech and gentle. That is no hard partner to find, either," said Stephen.

"Perhaps what I need is a god, a religion—something I may worship, something I may believe in. She is beautiful, she is true, she is good, she is loving, she is serene, she is clever, she—"

"She has every virtue," Stephen cut in.

"She lives only in your imagination," said Mrs. Stephen.

"And so his history of love is a history of his faith: disappointed in one, he trades her for another."

"And so I am a confirmed bachelor now."

"But still loves the company of girls," said Stephen.

Looking about me, I noticed that the car had driven a long way past my turning, and so I told the driver to turn back. The moment I was in my room, I thought again of Mei Yingzi and her doings with the Japanese, and then of where Bai Ping had gone; and carrying these two disquiets I went to bed.

I took a book to my pillow, but before I had read two pages I turned off the lamp and settled to sleep—yet do what I might, sleep would not come. Suddenly I seemed to hear a sound beyond the window; listening closely, I found it was indeed the sound of tapping at the pane. I switched on the light, and now it was plain that someone was knocking. So I threw on a robe and rose; it was black outside, with a flickering gleam, and I could not make out who it was. Running towards the window, I asked as I went:

"Who is there?"

"Me."

"Who?"

"Me."

—Bai Ping?

"Bai Ping?"

"Are you asleep?"

I went out and opened the door. She had changed her clothes: all in black, in soft shoes, with no overcoat, and not an ornament on her.

"What is it? Has something happened?"

"What—must something have happened, for me to come?" She smiled serenely and came in with an easy grace.

She looked round my room, looked at my writing-desk, looked at my bed, and without a word sat down on the sofa. I began to feel a little impatient, and said:

"How did you come to know my address?"

"Do you remember never having told me?"

"I don't think I did," I said; "for I remember you never asked."

"Did I really never ask you?" she said. "Not even tonight, at the Arcadia?"

"No."

"Then I must have asked Stephen—while we were dancing."

"Did you mean all along to come and see me tonight?"

"Yes," she said. "To settle the talk we left unfinished."

"About Mei Yingzi?" I said.

"Of course," she said. "If you love her, I am willing to spend all my strength plucking her down out of the nebula and setting her on your writing-desk to be your lamp."

"I do not want the sun for my desk-lamp; my lamp is bright enough already," I said, strolling idly about the room, in a bantering tone. But Bai Ping seemed to pay my words no heed, and went on:

"If you do not love her, then do not go too near her—I warn you."

"For fear of being scorched by the sun?"

"So you do not care for my good counsel?"

I took out my cigarettes and said:

"Have one, just for something to do?"

She took one from my hand, and I touched her hand—ah, how cold it was! I saw that her cheeks were somewhat flushed and dry, and feared she was ill. I crouched down, gripped both her hands, and said:

"What is it, Bai Ping—are you feeling unwell?"

"No." At once she put off her former gravity, and showed the smile of a lily just opening.

A strange sensation came at me from the palms of my hands, and I could not tell what it was. Suddenly she drew her hands back, and I too felt, of a sudden, a kind of shyness. I stood up, struck a match to light her cigarette, and said, low and lightly and in banter:

"Bai Ping, tell me truly—are you in love with Mei Yingzi too?"

"Yes." Then I said: "And might that be the very reason you are jealous of me?"

"Jealous of you—what a joke!" she laughed. "Why should I not be jealous of those two lucky Japanese instead?"

"Do you mean me, then?"

"So you are jealous too?"

"Yes."

"Jealous only that Mei Yingzi rode away in their car?" she asked.

"What else should there be?"

"That, for my part, I am not jealous of; I take it merely for one of the ordinary devices of good society. But who could have known…" She blew out smoke and did not go on.

"Known what?"

She watched the smoke scatter in the air, and said slowly:

"I ran into them again, as it happened."

"You mean…?"

"I did not go home; I meant to have a drink at a bar nearby, and I saw the four of them there."

"Did they see you?"

"Of course, and they hailed me; they asked me to join them a while, but I said I felt a little unwell and went home. Yet once I was in bed my mind would not rest, and so I made up my mind to get up and come to you."

"To come to me, so we might go to the bar together and watch them?"

"No; I only wished to tell you that, unless you truly love her, to chase after Mei Yingzi out of a wish to win, or out of vanity, would be a sacrifice quite without value for you," she said earnestly.

"Thank you; I shall certainly do no such thing. I do not love her, it is true, nor would I sacrifice anything for the sake of winning or of vanity; if now and then I do pursue her, it is no more than a game of chess in my idlest hours—a football match against our enemy, more amusing even than gambling with our friends."

"Are you not afraid the enemy will do you some harm in secret?"

"Not in the least—if the victory is to be mine."

"Trading your life for a few of Mei Yingzi's tears?"

"Do you not believe Mei Yingzi to be a woman who would avenge the one she loves?"

"Perhaps," she said; "but what she loves is her own radiance."

"So do I," I said.

"If your radiance is now to be spent in this way," she said, "then I would not have you deny any longer that you are in love with her."

"No," I said. "When I love someone, I shall always have the fullest courage to own it."

"But you already incline towards loving her—that is a fact," she said. "I will not discuss the matter further now; my purpose was of two kinds only. The first, that I hope you will value yourself; the second, that in this international Shanghai, where all things are tinged with politics, I hope you will not let yourself be made a stage-property within it."

"…" I fell silent. After a moment she said:

"Have you something I might eat?"

I set the electric stove going, to make coffee and toast bread. Bai Ping sat there without a word. I spread a white cloth on the table and laid out the cups and plates, setting in the middle a vase of roses the household had arranged for me that day; I drew down the green-shaded lamp so that its white light fell just over the round table-top; and last I chose a record of Schumann's Reverie and set it on the gramophone. I poured the coffee, and put rather more milk in Bai Ping's cup. I said:

"Eat a little; and then, I think, you ought to rest."

Without a word she rose and came to the table; I set the chair for her, and she sat down in thought; I started the music, and quietly took my place across from her.

We sat in silence, listening to the music, drinking our coffee, eating a slice of bread, not a word between us, and let the music run through the night, and the night run through our hearts; deeply we tasted the beauty of the night.

After four serenades, I lit a cigarette, took up a book, and said low, at her side:

"Rest now, Bai Ping; I shall wake you at one in the afternoon."

"Thank you," she said.

I took her to my bedroom at the back, and went myself up to the little garret over the stair-landing; I got to bed quickly and quickly fell asleep, and a calm of mind kept me wholly at peace within my dreams.
