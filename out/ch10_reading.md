## Chapter Ten. Opening a Shop, Doing Trade

### 1. No Ordinary Man

"It was a cloudy morning, the air cool and damp after the rain. Driving along the airport expressway, a thought rose in me all at once: maybe it was time I looked into the stories of the older generation. Family is shared blood, after all, and my ignorance of my forebears had long been a quiet guilt. In what I could now find out, in the energy I had for it, even in my own grasp of things, the moment had come when I might make a little sense of them." (Qin Hong, 2015)

The Party Center spent thirteen years in Shanghai. If Chen Duxiu's seven years were years of summoning the wind and the rain, and Qu Qiubai's not-quite-one a sudden cloudburst, and Xiang Zhongfa's three-and-a-bit a wind of blood and a rain of gore, then Bo Gu's two years were a cold wind and a bitter rain.

Bo Gu, courtesy name Zemin, pen name Shanglin, was born in 1907, a native of Wuxi in Jiangsu. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in 1925, and in September 1931, in a small hotel, he took up the baton at the age of twenty-four, the man now "in overall charge."

Wang Ming left for Moscow in October 1931; Zhou Enlai reached Ruijin, in the Central base area, at the end of December. In the second half of that September, before the two of them left Shanghai, fewer than half the Central Committee and Politburo members remained in the city, and on the proposal of the Comintern's Far Eastern Bureau a Provisional Central Politburo was formed there, made up of six men: Bo Gu, Zhang Wentian (Luo Fu), Kang Sheng, Chen Yun, Lu Futan (who later turned traitor), and Li Zhusheng (who later turned traitor). Bo Gu, Zhang Wentian, and Lu Futan served as its three Standing Committee members, with Bo Gu bearing overall responsibility. (Party History Research Office of the CCP Central Committee, 2002)

Bo Gu's real name was Qin Bangxian. He was a thirty-first-generation descendant of the Northern Song lyric poet Qin Guan (Shaoyou), and the fourteenth-generation descendant of Qin Jin (Fengshan) — first master of the Jichang Garden in Wuxi, and, in the Jiajing reign of the Ming, a "president of five ministries across the two capitals, Grand Guardian through three reigns and nine promotions."

Qin Jin was a man of honor, a scholar who could take the field at the head of troops when he had to. But his interests ran beyond the glory of official life; he was also a man devoted to his family, proud of its long history. (Qin Jiacong, 2016)

Bo Gu, too, was "a man devoted to his family." He loathed the "man-eating rites" that "treat a person not as a person at all, but as the family's captive, the father's slave" (Shanglin, 1925), and yet he dearly "loved" and "cherished" his own father and mother. In 1916 one of them died of consumption, and he was left "seized in his heart by a grief beyond words, gripped by a cold anguish, all but senseless" (Zemin, 1924). In 1932 there came both a reunion, on his return from the Soviet Union, and a leave-taking, before he set out for the far Soviet areas: tenderness for kin and a hero's failing nerve, all of it bleak and forlorn and steeped in grief.

"My father was the eldest son of the house. My grandmother raised him alone, a widow, and he loved her dearly. As a schoolboy in Wuxi he did all he could to ease her cares, and thought of his younger brother and sister at every turn. In those days my grandmother could not keep three children in school at once, and she meant to take my aunt — her own daughter — out of school and save the money so my father could go on studying. But my father would not hear of it; he understood his mother, and he cared about his sister's future, and he would sooner find another way for himself than see his sister leave school. After much effort all around, he won a place at the tuition-free Second Provincial Industrial School of Suzhou. After he and my grandmother parted, he thought of her often; but to his lasting sorrow, in fourteen years he never once set eyes on his old mother again. After my father died in the line of duty, my uncle Yang Lin, fearing it would break her, kept the news of his death from my grandmother, and she went on watching the road, waiting for my father to come and see her, to fetch her home. Right up to her death in 1950 she was still calling out: 'Where is Changlin? Why doesn't he come to see me?'" (Qin Moya, 2015)

Qin Moya is Bo Gu's eldest daughter. The "uncle Yang Lin" she writes of is Bo Gu's own younger brother, Qin Bangli, the founder of Hong Kong's Huarun — China Resources.

Qin Bangli was born in 1908. At fourteen he became an apprentice at the Fuyuan money house in Wuxi. In 1924 he served as an executive member of the Wuxi money-trade workers' union led by Qin Qi. In 1927 he followed Yan Pu into a peasant uprising in Wuxi; when it failed he went to Shanghai to find the Party organization, clerking in a small shop, where he learned to reckon and remember in his head and to forget nothing he had seen. At about the same time Bo Gu and Liu Qunxian were studying at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow, and there they married. In 1930 the two of them came home together, taking up posts as a propaganda officer of the All-China Federation of Trade Unions and head of its women workers' department, respectively. In 1931, when Gu Shunzhang betrayed the revolution, Chen Yun "spent a few thousand dollars and, through reliable people sympathetic to the revolution, set up a dozen or twenty small shops scattered across the districts of Shanghai, using the cover of trade to screen the comings and goings and the contacts of Special Branch personnel" (Party Literature Research Office of the CCP Central Committee, 2005). Badly short of reliable hands, he turned to Bo Gu. Bo Gu did not shrink from promoting his own kin, and put Qin Bangli forward; and with Yan Pu to sponsor him into the Party, Chen Yun then "handed Qin Bangli two gold bars and told him to open a shop. Qin Bangli used them to open a rice shop, shipping Wuxi rice to Shanghai to sell. With that shop, whenever our Party rented a house, or set out to ransom someone under arrest, he could step forward and 'stand the surety.'"

The rice shop soon turned a profit. Qin Bangli then opened a furniture-and-woodware shop, kept expressly to fit out safe houses and to serve as meeting places for the Party's organs. The furniture was specially made, with false compartments; when the Party Center met, if anything suspicious was noticed, or the enemy came searching, documents were hidden in the compartments, and Qin Bangli, as the sub-landlord, could retrieve them once it was safe. The Party was then at a turning point and met constantly, as is plain enough from the Chronicle of Zhou Enlai. Bo Gu was then the Party's man in overall charge, and his brother Qin Bangli was one of those he trusted most. The rice shop and the furniture shop served as cover on the one hand, and on the other supplied a living and logistical support. (Editorial Committee of Red Huarun, 2010)

On the "furniture-and-woodware shop," the Biography of Chen Yun (2005) says that Chen Yun once "sent people to open a woodware-and-furniture shop at Hongmiao in Shanghai, made to look like a secondhand store." With a shop like that, "whenever an organ moved house, everything was carted over there; when an organ was to be set up, furniture could be carted back — very convenient; it was warehouse, store, and contact point all at once." But it was not "kept expressly to fit out safe houses and to serve as meeting places for the Party's organs"; still less was it a matter of "specially made furniture with false compartments, so that when the Party Center met and something suspicious was noticed, or the enemy came searching, documents were hidden in the compartments and Qin Bangli, as sub-landlord, could retrieve them when it was safe." On this point, at least, Qi Yuande — wife of Wu Defeng, then head of the Central Communications Bureau — sets things out in her late memoir, and makes the whole tangle very clear.

"In those days, apart from the dressing-up and socializing that secret work required, the life of us comrades could only be called hard and bitterly poor. Often we shut the door and ate brown rice and coarse grain, even scavenged vegetable leaves; what greens we had were mostly pickles and chili peppers, and for months on end we saw no meat. Many comrades' homes and belongings were bare. At first, like everyone, we rented a fairly cheap place. The landlord saw the three of us — a simple little household, modestly comfortable, honest out-of-town business folk — and was delighted; he agreed to rent to us on the spot, without another word. But the moment we moved our luggage and furniture in, his face changed, and he insisted we find three shop-guarantors before he would let us stay. Quite puzzled, we asked him why. The landlord's wife pulled us over to look at a room stacked with two sets of furniture exactly like ours, and said the owners of both sets were suspected Communist bandits who had been hauled off by the Public Security Bureau to stand trial, and that it had dragged the landlords into no end of trouble. 'So the sight of your furniture frightens me. We can't take another upheaval. You must find three shop-guarantors to rent, or we won't rent at all.' Defeng smiled at once and said: 'Set your mind at ease. We're honest businesspeople, with a family to feed; we'll be up to no petty thievery. We've only just come to Shanghai to trade and haven't settled in yet, so we bought a cheap set of furniture off the street on the spur of the moment. I'll go find three guarantors right away.' Once we were settled, Defeng reported the matter to the Center at once and asked that note be taken of it." (Qi Yuande, 2007)

Wu Defeng's "report" drew the Center's close "attention." Qin Bangli was to step forward and open a further "furniture-and-woodware shop," so that our comrades, when "buying furniture," could readily obtain "furnishings suited to each different station and trade." Only by working hard to "avoid" the "stopgap mindset" that would "give the game away and stir up needless questions and trouble" could the latent danger under the White Terror be cut down a step further.

The single most important move in the Communists' underground work was the setting-up of secret command posts at every level and for every kind of task. To shoulder this task the Red agents would, in several of China's great cities — the important command posts of Communist underground work being mostly set up in great cities — commonly open commercial fronts dealing in daily necessities: furniture shops, real-estate companies, rice shops, sauce-and-pickle shops, department stores, and the like. These served, first, to hide underground workers who had no ostensible profession, and second, for the convenience of setting up the secret command posts. The method was extraordinarily roundabout: even the people placed inside these businesses, or running them, did not know the secret. Suppose the Communists needed to set up a secret command post. First the Party leadership would notify the head of the Red agents' team; on receiving the order, the team head would send A to arrange it. A, on his orders, would go to B, who ran a real-estate company, to rent a house, and then to C, who ran a furniture shop, to rent furniture. These three — A, B, and C — were all Communist underground workers, but they could not meet face to face to handle the matter, so a fourth man, D, had to be brought in to connect the three sides through ordinary business dealings, so that A, B, and C knew one another only as ordinary commercial contacts and not as comrades. Once A had finished the arrangements, he reported the task done to his superiors; as for who in the end would live in the house, A did not know. (Xu Enzeng, 1992)

In the first months after Gu Shunzhang's defection Xu Enzeng was very pleased with himself, for Gu Shunzhang's "history and standing inside the Communist Party gave him an extremely wide knowledge of its upper and middle personnel; in the Party's command organs across the country, no small number were his old subordinates. He was like a living dictionary," and any "knotty point, once put to him, was resolved at a stroke." So Xu Enzeng's "record in the underground fight against the Communists across the country suddenly grew brilliant, and cases no longer went as awkwardly as before, especially in cracking the Communist underground organizations of Nanjing, Shanghai, Hangzhou, Suzhou, Tianjin, Beiping, Hankou, and other great cities," where he "dealt the Communists blow after unprecedented blow."

But Xu Enzeng was soon downcast again.

Chen Yun's "new strategy of concealment" had once more "severed" the "leads he had built up," so that his "ears went deaf again, his eyes went blind again." He "knew only that the Communist underground had changed — but how had it changed? Who was in charge? Where were the organs? Of all the particulars he was utterly in the dark." Moscow, for its part, was full of praise, and said, "This man is no ordinary man." The man who "opened a shop and did trade" — "no ordinary man."

### 2. The First Pot of Gold

After Chen Yun was transferred to the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, Qin Bangli came under Yan Pu's leadership instead.

Yan Pu was the man who had brought Qin Bangli into the Party. Under Yan Pu, who knew him inside and out, Qin Bangli naturally worked with ever more drive, and to ever greater effect.

In 1931, under Chen Yun and Yan Pu, Qin Bangli opened six small shops in Shanghai to serve as the Party's underground communications stations. There were the furniture-and-woodware shop, a sugar mill, a southern-goods store, a rice shop, and a stationery-and-tobacco shop — after Xiang Zhongfa's defection, Zhou Enlai and Deng Yingchao once lodged for a time in the little loft above the stationery-and-tobacco shop — and the largest of them was the "Shantou Branch of the Shanghai Sino-French Drugstore," opened in Shantou, a communications station that anyone traveling from Shanghai to the Central Soviet had to pass through. Chen Geng, Zhang Hanfu, Zhang Wentian, and others all stayed there. (Qin Fuquan, 2007)

At that time, traveling "from Shanghai to the Central Soviet" meant passing through Hong Kong or Guangdong and then taking the line Shantou–Sanheba–Dabu–Yongding–Shanghang–Tingzhou. This line ran clear across White areas, guerrilla areas, and Soviet areas; it had water routes and land routes both, open plains and towering ranges alike. Lu Weiliang, once head of the Dabu communications station, and later deputy chief procurator of the Guangdong Provincial People's Procuratorate, recalled it in detail: "The Dabu station's job was to run the escort of traffic along Qingxi–Dabu town–Chao'an–Shantou–Hong Kong–Shanghai, and along Qingxi–Duobaokeng–Bogongkeng–Tiekeng–Taokeng–the Yongding Soviet. On this route, and especially on the stretch through Duobaokeng, Bogongkeng, Tiekeng, and Taokeng to the Yongding Soviet, the roads were overgrown goat-tracks, choked with trees and brush, and very hard going. Our couriers could only move like an army; the point men took great care over safe passage, and in the wooded hills they often met wild boar, but to avoid drawing the enemy's notice our comrades never once fired a shot." (Lu Weiliang, 1980)

By Lu Weiliang's account, from the winter of 1930 to the spring of 1932 the Dabu station "escorted several hundred comrades in all through enemy-held territory." Those whose "names he still remembered" included: Zhou Enlai, Ye Jianying, Deng Xiaoping and his wife, Liu Shaoqi, Xiang Ying, Ren Bishi, He Shuheng, Liu Bocheng, Zuo Quan, Xiao Jinguang, Zhu Rui, Chen Geng, Li Fuchun, Li Kenong, Qian Zhuangfei, Hu Di, Nie Rongzhen, Zhang Aiping, Hu Yaobang, Lin Boqu, Dong Biwu, Xie Juezai, Li Liuru, Wang Guanlan, Yang Shangkun, Li Bozhao, Liu Ying, Zhang Wentian, Wang Jiaxiang, Lu Dingyi, Wu Xiuquan, Zhang Jingwu, Feng Wenbin, Li Yimang, Gong Weizhi, Guo Huaruo, Cheng Zihua, Jia Tuofu, Liao Siguang, Kai Feng, Zhang Ruxin, Yan Zhong, Cai Shufan, Chen Youmei, Yue Shaohua, He Chang, Wu Yunfu, Huang Su, Wu Liangping, Li Zhuoran, Cheng Fangwu, Zeng Xisheng, Zeng San, Liu Shaowen, Wang Bingzhang, Ouyang Qin and his wife, Zhang Ranhe and his wife, Liu Bojian and his wife, He Cheng and his wife, Liang Guang, and others.

A Short Life of Yang Lin (2010) says that from the spring of 1932 to the summer of 1933 the Shantou–Sanheba–Dabu–Yongding–Shanghang–Tingzhou line also "moved" Bo Gu and Chen Yun, and "escorted" Otto Braun, who went under the alias Li De.

On January 17, 1933, Qin Bangli accompanied Bo Gu and Chen Yun out of Shanghai; disguised as merchants, they reached Shantou by steamer. They spent a night in Shantou, and then Bo Gu and Chen Yun, made up as local country folk, were escorted onward by the next courier, Zhuo Xiong. Reaching Yongding County, they ran into a thousand-strong Kuomintang "mopping-up" force; Zhuo Xiong, with two other couriers, drew the enemy off, and only so did Bo Gu and Chen Yun come safely through. (Editorial Committee of Red Huarun, 2010)

The account in A Short Life of Yang Lin squares with that of Wu Baopu and Li Zhiying, authors of A Biography of Qin Bangxian (Bo Gu) (2007): "At the time the Central Special Branch secretary, Chen Yun, personally sent Qin Bangxian's younger brother Qin Bangli to set up a station in Shantou, in charge of the secret passage from Shanghai to western Fujian. In January 1933 Qin Bangxian and Chen Yun took ship from Shanghai to Shantou and stayed inside this secret liaison station, the Shantou Branch of the Shanghai Sino-French Drugstore. In the manager's office Qin Bangxian and Chen Yun held a secret talk with Qin Bangli, and Chen Yun urged Qin Bangli again and again to mind the station's security, and to escort in person every confidential document sent from the Central Soviet to the Party Center in Shanghai." But the record in the Biography of Chen Yun (2005) runs otherwise: "On January 17, Chen Yun and Bo Gu left Shanghai in secret under Central Special Branch escort, taking ship for Shantou in Guangdong. Along the way they wore long gowns and passed as merchants. The next day they reached Shantou and made contact with Chen Pannian, a Central Special Branch worker waiting there. Under Chen Pannian's cover they 'stayed a day in Shantou, took the train on to Shaoyang, went on by boat to Sanyangkan, and then by small boat to Dabu,' near 'the border of the Soviet area.'"

For the original source on this "Chen Pannian," see Zhuo Xiong's recollections. Zhuo Xiong was not the "next courier" but "chief of the executive section of the Central Political Security Bureau." (Party Literature Research Office of the CCP Central Committee, 2005) In an interview with a reporter from the Oriental 21st Century website, Zhuo Xiong said the "sea line from Shanghai by way of Hong Kong to Shantou" was "built by the communications section of the Central Political Security Bureau. The man in direct charge was named Chen Pannian, nicknamed Fat Chen; he was the communications-section chief." (Sun Junting and Li Shuting, 2003) A Short Life of Yang Lin, however, simply lifts the "sea line's" description wholesale — "three stretches, across Soviet, semi-guerrilla, and White areas; below Dabu and Sanheba it is water, above it mountain country" — while turning a blind eye to "Chen Pannian" alone. Since the notes to A Short Life of Yang Lin already record an "interview with Zhuo Xiong," why still not admit that a "chief of the communications section of the Central Political Security Bureau" existed? The likeliest thing is that the "Editorial Committee of Red Huarun" mistook "Chen Pannian" for "Pan Hannian." And so the more Zhuo Xiong says he "led an armed squad of a dozen-odd men straight for Chaozhou in Guangdong" and handed over to "Chen Pannian" the Otto Braun (Li De) who entered the Soviet area by the "sea line," the more A Short Life of Yang Lin insists on dragging the utterly unrelated "Pan Hannian" into the ranks of the "escorts."

Li De was a German, his looks conspicuous, and he was stopped for questioning again and again along the way. In August 1933 Qin Bangli got Li De up as a tourist and took him to Shantou aboard a British steamer. In Shantou he first lodged at the Sino-French Drugstore, then was remade as an archaeologist, the next stretch escorted by Pan Hannian. At Dabu he was disguised again, this time as a missionary, a cross on his chest, in a black cassock and lined coat, and escorted onward by Zhuo Xiong, reaching the Soviet area in September. (Editorial Committee of Red Huarun, 2010)

Looking across Qin Bangli's whole life, no achievement outshines his founding of "Huarun."

Qin Hong, in Huarun: A Miniature of Modern History (2014), says the forerunner of "Huarun" was the "Lianhehang." "Lianhe" — union — is, in the Wuxi dialect, a homophone of "Lian'an," and "Yang Lian'an" was precisely the name Qin Bangli used when he first came to Hong Kong.

In the autumn of 1946, when the Nationalist–Communist "peace talks" broke down, Yang Lin — Qin Bangli — was summoned to Shanghai and given a new charge by Zhou Enlai: to open sea transport, build up foreign trade, and exchange goods between China and abroad; to meet the Party's financial targets; and to train foreign-trade cadres. Back in Hong Kong, Yang Lin renamed the "Lianhehang" the "Lianhe Import and Export Company" — the "Lianhe Company" for short — rented an office in the Hongkong Telephone Company Building (Prince's Building) on Des Voeux Road, and also registered the "Tianlonghang," setting up a "Tianlonghang" branch in Guangzhou, so as to run trade between Hong Kong and the mainland through companies in both places.

At Christmas 1947, at a "Lianhe Company" gathering, Yang Lin spoke with deep feeling: "'Lianhe Company' was named, back then, after my own name, 'Lian'an.' Now the company has grown, and we should give it a resounding new name." After much discussion, the "Lianhehang" was renamed "Huarun." The English name, "China Resources," was translated by the Huarun company's accountant, Huang Meixian, and was approved by Commander-in-Chief Zhu De.

Qin Hong was born in 1967 and graduated from the School of Economics at Peking University in 1990.

Qin Hong is the daughter of Qin Gang. Qin Gang was Bo Gu's second son.

Qin Hong offered heartfelt homage to the spirit of her granduncle. Huarun, she said, is "the sole surviving fruit among the companies founded before the Communists took power." "'Huarun's' history is nothing less than a miniature of modern Chinese history," and "'Huarun's' origins can be traced back to 1931."

In 1931 the Central Special Branch was reorganized, and Chen Yun, taking command in the crisis, turned the tide.

In 1931 Qin Bangli entered the Party, opened a shop and did trade, and dug the Party its first pot of gold.

From Shanghai to Shantou and on to Hong Kong: this was at once an unseen political-security front and, more than that, a visible foreign-trade front.

From an underground communications station, to the first overseas company under the Party Center's banner, to a key state-owned enterprise ranked among the Fortune Global 500 — this was both a growth and a rising to new ground. The first broke the imperialists' economic blockade with "collarless shirts"; the second "seeks more than profit," furthering the civilization and progress of society as a whole. The first said, "Let them blockade us! Blockade us for decades, and we will have everything we need"; the second, "We are not only good at destroying an old world, we shall also be good at building a new one."
