## Chapter 9. The Flying Swallow Comes and Goes

The carriage was not large; it seated just four, and no more. The horses that drew it were long and well trained, and on the yellow clay road the carriage ran very smooth. Ma Xiuzhen and Shi Xiuxue sat in one row, Sun Xiuqing and Ye Xiuzhu on the bench opposite.

The carriage had gone a long while when Shi Xiuxue noticed of a sudden that every one of them was staring at her. She meant to pretend she did not see it, but could not keep from pouting. "What are you all forever staring at me for? Has a flower grown on my face?"

Sun Xiuqing laughed. "Even if a flower had grown on your face, someone's just plucked it off." She had great eyes and thin lips, and anyone could see this was a girl who spared no one in talk.

She would not let Shi Xiuxue get a word in, and went on: "The strange thing is, this girl's forever saying no flower's half so good to look at as a green vegetable; so why is it that now every other word out of her is 'flower, flower'?"

Shi Xiuxue did not blush; she only said easily, "There's nothing strange in it. It's just because his surname is Hua, and so every other word out of me is 'flower, flower.'"

Sun Xiuqing tittered. "His? And who's he?"

"His surname is Hua," said Shi Xiuxue; "his name Hua Manlou."

"How do you come to know even his name?" said Sun Xiuqing.

"Because he told me just now," said Shi Xiuxue.

"How was it I didn't hear?" said Sun Xiuqing.

"We were talking our own talk," said Shi Xiuxue. "Why must you hear it? Besides, your mind just then was surely still on Lu Xiaofeng."

Sun Xiuqing cried out, "I was thinking of Lu Xiaofeng! Who says I was thinking of Lu Xiaofeng?"

"I say so," said Shi Xiuxue. "While he sat in the tub your eyes were on him the whole time; I marked it long ago, and you'll not wriggle out of it."

Sun Xiuqing, half angry and half laughing, scolded through her laughter, "Look at this girl, is she mad? Her mouth's full of nonsense."

Ma Xiuzhen said easily, "The girl is a bit mad; only your eyes truly were on Lu Xiaofeng the whole time."

Shi Xiuxue clapped her hands and laughed. "There, senior sister's spoken fair for once."

Sun Xiuqing rolled her eyes, then sighed of a sudden. "Fair she's spoken, right enough; only there's a sour smell to it."

Ma Xiuzhen glared too. "Sour? What sour?"

"A sour smell," said Sun Xiuqing, "much of a kind with vinegar."

Ma Xiuzhen cried out as well. "You mean to say I'm jealous?"

"I never said so," said Sun Xiuqing. "You said it yourself."

Holding in her laughter, she hurried on: "They all say what a gallant, dashing man Lu Xiaofeng is; but today I saw him sitting there in his tub like a gourd, a stupid gourd, and far the worse of the two beside Ximen Chuixue."

Shi Xiuxue was startled. "What did you say?"

"I said," said Sun Xiuqing, "that if I were to choose a man, I'd choose Ximen Chuixue. There's a man with a real man's mettle; ten Lu Xiaofengs couldn't match him."

Shi Xiuxue sighed. "Now you're the one gone truly mad. Were every man on earth to die off, I'd never take a fancy to that self-important walking corpse."

"You don't fancy him; I do," said Sun Xiuqing. "That's radishes and greens: each to her own taste."

Ma Xiuzhen could not help laughing. "The way you two go on, it's as though you'd shared out the radishes and the greens already."

Sun Xiuqing tittered. "We've allotted you that great radish, Lu Xiaofeng."

Shi Xiuxue blinked. "Then wouldn't Miss Ye the Third be left with nothing at all?"

Ye Xiuzhu's face had reddened. "Listen to you," she said, red-faced. "You've met them the once, and you're lovesick as if you'd caught a fever. Have you never in your lives seen a man?"

Sun Xiuqing sighed. "We've simply never seen men like these before."

She cut her eyes at Ye Xiuzhu, and went on: "In honesty, of the three men we saw today, any one of them would do; and you may say nothing, but I daresay you like all three."

Ye Xiuzhu, flustered, went redder still. "You... you... you truly are mad!"

"That's the one bad thing about Sun the Second," said Ma Xiuzhen. "She loves to pick on the honest ones."

Sun Xiuqing curled her lip. "She, honest? Honest on the face of it, maybe; but of us four, she'll be the first wed, I'll wager anything."

"You... on what grounds do you say so?" said Ye Xiuzhu.

Shi Xiuxue cut in: "Because she knows well she'll never get herself a husband. Not a man with four eyebrows, no, nor a man with four helpings of nerve, would dare take her to wife!"

"True enough," said Ma Xiuzhen. "Marry a sharp-tongued, slippery-lipped girl like her, and it'd be a wonder if she didn't nag you into your grave!"

Shi Xiuxue held in her laughter. "Perhaps only a deaf man could still..."

Sun Xiuqing had already sprung up. "Fine!" she cried. "The three of you gang up to bully me; at the most I'll hand all three men over to the lot of you, and then you'll be satisfied, I suppose?"

"Hand them to us?" said Shi Xiuxue. "Are those three men yours to give?"

Ma Xiuzhen sighed. "It seems this girl knows everything, except how to feel shame."

Sun Xiuqing glared at them, then cried out of a sudden, "I'm starving to death."

Ma Xiuzhen stared at her, for all the world as though watching one who had truly gone mad.

Sun Xiuqing could not help laughing at herself. "The moment I'm angry my belly goes hungry; well, I'm angry now, so I'll find somewhere for a late supper."

Set four girls together, and to tell them not to talk of men is as hard a thing as to tell four men together not to talk of women.

But Hua Manlou and Lu Xiaofeng were not talking of women now; they had no heart for it. They were talking of Ximen Chuixue.

"I only hope he hasn't found Dugu Yihe yet," said Lu Xiaofeng.

"You think he's no match at all for Dugu Yihe?" said Hua Manlou.

"His swordplay is keen and cutting, and merciless in the striking, like the man himself," said Lu Xiaofeng; "he never leaves another an inch of room."

Hua Manlou nodded slowly. "A man who will never leave another room leaves none for himself either."

"So the moment his sword clears the sheath," said Lu Xiaofeng, "if it cannot wound the other man, he himself is surely dead!"

"He isn't dead yet," said Hua Manlou.

"Only because he has never yet met an adversary like Dugu Yihe!" said Lu Xiaofeng.

He went on slowly, "Dugu Yihe's swordsmanship is steady and mighty and his inner force deep; fierce in attack, and tighter still in guard. In the sheer wealth of his fighting years he is more than Ximen Chuixue can match; so that if Ximen cannot take him within thirty strokes, he must surely die under Dugu's sword."

"You think he can't take him within thirty strokes?" said Hua Manlou.

"No man can bring Dugu to his death inside thirty strokes," said Lu Xiaofeng, "and no more can Ximen Chuixue!"

Hua Manlou was long silent, and he too sighed. "It was you who called him out."

Lu Xiaofeng smiled wryly. "Which is why I hope only that he hasn't found Dugu Yihe yet."

They had crossed the silent high road and come to the little river outside the Pavilion of Pearls and Splendour.

The running water glinted with fine silver scales under the pale light of the first-quarter moon, and a man stood quiet by the little river, robed all in snow-white.

When Lu Xiaofeng saw him, he had seen Lu Xiaofeng too, and said of a sudden, "I'm not dead yet."

Lu Xiaofeng laughed. "You certainly don't look like a dead man."

"It's Dugu Yihe who is dead," said Ximen Chuixue.

Lu Xiaofeng stopped laughing.

"You didn't look for that?" said Ximen Chuixue.

Lu Xiaofeng owned it, though he had not wished to.

But Ximen Chuixue smiled, a very strange smile. "I didn't look for it myself."

"Oh?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"When Su Shaoying brought out those twenty-one strokes," said Ximen Chuixue, "I marked three flaws in them."

"So you reckoned you had at least three chances to kill Dugu Yihe?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

Ximen Chuixue nodded. "As a rule one chance is enough for me; but crossing hands with him just now, I could not seize even one."

"Why?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"His swordplay had flaws," said Ximen Chuixue; "but the instant my sword thrust out, he had mended the flaw. I have never seen a man who knew where the flaws in his own swordplay lay; yet he knew."

"All the swordplay under heaven has its flaws," said Lu Xiaofeng; "but the men who know the flaws in their own are few indeed."

"Three times I struck, three times I was sealed off dead," said Ximen Chuixue, "and I knew then I could not kill him. And a killing swordplay that cannot kill means the swordsman's own certain death!"

Lu Xiaofeng sighed. "Proud as you are, you know your own measure too; and that is why you're still alive!"

"I am not dead," said Ximen Chuixue, "only because after thirty strokes his swordplay of a sudden fell into disorder."

"For a master like him," said Lu Xiaofeng, "swordplay that falls suddenly into disorder can have but two causes."

Ximen Chuixue was listening.

"If the heart is in disorder," said Lu Xiaofeng, "the swordplay must be too."

"His heart was not in disorder," said Ximen Chuixue.

"Then was his inner force failing him?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

If the force fails, the swordplay too falls into disorder.

"With inner force as deep as his," Lu Xiaofeng went on, "how should he be spent after only thirty strokes?"

"As I said, I cannot account for it either," said Ximen Chuixue.

Lu Xiaofeng pondered. "Could it be that, before he crossed hands with you, someone had already drained much of his force? That some man had fought him first?"

Ximen Chuixue said coldly, "When you force a man to draw, when did you ever give him the chance to talk?"

Ximen Chuixue's face was without expression as ever, but a shadow seemed to have come into his eyes; and after a long while he said slowly, "Yet before he died he said a very strange thing."

"What did he say?" said Lu Xiaofeng.

"He said he..." said Ximen Chuixue.

When the sword was drawn out, there was blood on its point still.

Dugu Yihe watched another man's sword-point carry his own blood, watched his blood blown from it drop by drop, and there was neither pain nor fear on his face; instead he cried out of a sudden, "I understand! I understand..."

"He said he understood!" said Ximen Chuixue.

Lu Xiaofeng frowned. "Understood what?"

The shadow in Ximen Chuixue's eyes grew heavier, and he let out a long, long sigh. "Perhaps he had understood that life is short, no more than the morning dew; perhaps he had understood that the fame and rank he had bought at any cost were, in the end, no more than an emptiness..."

Lu Xiaofeng mused, then said slowly, "It is just because life is short that it must not be squandered. Did he truly understand, then, or not? What was it he really meant to say?"

Ximen Chuixue's gaze rested on the distance; and after another long while he too said a thing that no one could have looked for.

He said of a sudden, "I'm hungry."

Lu Xiaofeng looked at him in surprise. "You're hungry?"

"I'm always hungry after a killing," said Ximen Chuixue coldly.

It was a little wine-shop that should by rights have shut for the night, outside a mulberry wood thick with leaves.

There were a few households within the wood, and a few without, most of them small families that raised silkworms. This family's house stood nearer the road, and so they had put up a little wooden room, windowed on all four sides, to sell plain wine and food to travellers passing by. When the Four Beauties of Emei found their way here, the host was near enough asleep; but who could turn away four such lovely girls?

There were but three wooden tables in the wine-shop, yet all was clean and neat; the cold dishes to go with the wine were plain and fresh, and the mild wine suited the girls' taste, and they ate with great content. And girls, when they are content, have always a great deal to say; they chattered and laughed like a flock of merry hens.

Sun Xiuqing said of a sudden, "That Hua fellow of yours speaks with a touch of a Jiangnan accent. I wonder if he's one of that Hua family."

"Which Hua family?" said Shi Xiuxue.

"The Hua family of Jiangnan," said Sun Xiuqing. "They say you might gallop a fast horse a whole day and still be within their lands."

"I know of that family too," said Ma Xiuzhen; "but I don't think Hua Manlou is one of them."

"Why?" said Sun Xiuqing.

"They say that family lives in the greatest luxury," said Ma Xiuzhen, "most particular in their food and dress; even their grooms come out looking like young lords. This Hua Manlou seems very plain; and besides, I never heard they had a blind man among their sons."

Shi Xiuxue gave a cold laugh at once. "And what if he is blind? Blind he may be, but he can see more than all of us with our eyes put together."

Ma Xiuzhen knew she should not have said it, and changed her tune with a smile. "His skill is good, no doubt of it; even I could never have thought that with one idle reach of his hand he could pinch your sword and hold it."

Sun Xiuqing laughed. "That's perhaps only because this girl is already bewitched by him."

Shi Xiuxue shot her a glare. "If you're not convinced, go and try him yourself next time. I'm not boasting for him: on that one stroke alone, there's no one under heaven to match him."

"And Ximen Chuixue?" said Sun Xiuqing. "Was that stroke of his any the less?"

Shi Xiuxue said nothing; she too could not but own that Ximen Chuixue's stroke had been fearsome.

"They say Ximen Chuixue's swordplay is peerless," said Ma Xiuzhen, "and his family great besides; the wealth and splendour of the Ten Thousand Plum Manor stand no lower than the Hua family of Jiangnan."

Sun Xiuqing's eyes shone. "I like him, and not for his birth either; were he a penniless pauper, I'd like him just the same."

Shi Xiuxue said lightly, "For my part I can't find one lovable thing about him, from his head to his heels."

"Whether there's a lovable thing about him," said Sun Xiuqing, "why must you be the one to find it? So long as I..."

Her voice broke off of a sudden, and her face flushed red to the very roots of her ears. For at that moment a man was coming in from outside, robed all in snow-white: it was Ximen Chuixue. Shi Xiuxue too was struck dumb; the four chattering girls closed their mouths all at once. They had seen not only Ximen Chuixue, but Hua Manlou and Lu Xiaofeng as well.

Ximen Chuixue's eyes, keen as a blade's edge, had been fixed on them; of a sudden he came over and said coldly, "I have killed not only Su Shaoying, but now Dugu Yihe as well."

The faces of all four girls changed; Sun Xiuqing's above all had gone white, without a drop of blood left in it.

In a young girl's heart, hatred is easily driven out by love; and the more so here, for Su Shaoying, vain of his own dash, had always fancied these four martial-sisters ought to be vying for his favour, so that in truth not one of them liked him. But the hatred owed for a slain master was another matter altogether.

Sun Xiuqing's voice failed her. "You... what did you say?"

"I have killed Dugu Yihe," said Ximen Chuixue.

Shi Xiuxue sprang up of a sudden and cried aloud, "My second sister likes you so; how... how could you do such a thing?"

No one had dreamed she would say such a thing; even Ximen Chuixue seemed struck still.

Sun Xiuqing's face went red and green by turns; then of a sudden she set her teeth, and her twin swords were out of the sheath, their light flickering, stabbing in hatred at Ximen Chuixue's breast.

Ximen Chuixue, for a wonder, did not strike; a light flick of his sleeve, and his body had slid backward, seven or eight chi to the rear.

Sun Xiuqing's eyes had reddened round the rims. "You killed my master," she said hoarsely; "I'll fight you to the death."

She worked her twin swords and flung herself, teeth clenched, at Ximen Chuixue. The sword-form runs above all to lightness and change; and in the flicker of the blades, like a flurry of falling blossom, she loosed seven strokes in a breath.

Seeing her sister's swords already drawn, Shi Xiuxue cried aloud, "This is a matter between us and Ximen Chuixue; others had best not meddle in it." She said it, of course, for Hua Manlou's ears; and indeed Hua Manlou could not put in a hand.

Yet how could he let these four innocent girls die under Ximen Chuixue's sword?

Just then came a ringing "ting"; Ximen Chuixue's hand shot out and pressed on Sun Xiuqing's elbow, so that the sword in her left hand struck the sword in her right.

At the clash of the two blades her elbow went numb, and of a sudden both swords were in Ximen Chuixue's hands.

"Stand down," said Ximen Chuixue coldly. "Do not force me to draw!"

His voice was cold, but his eyes were not; and so Sun Xiuqing was still alive.

He was a man, when all was said; a man. How could he bring himself to a murderer's stroke against a lovely girl who loved him?

Sun Xiuqing, paler still, tears standing in her eyes, said through her teeth, "I told you: today we'll all fight you to the death; and if we cannot kill you, then... then we'll die before your face!"

Ximen Chuixue gave a cold laugh. "Dying will serve no purpose. If you want your revenge, you'd do better to hurry back and call out every man of the one hundred and eight Blue-Robe Towers."

But Sun Xiuqing seemed astonished, and her voice failed her. "What are you saying?"

"Since Dugu Yihe was the grand-master of the Blue-Robe Tower," said Ximen Chuixue, "the Blue-Robe Tower..."

But Sun Xiuqing cut him short of a sudden, her eyes blazing in anger. "You say my master was of the Blue-Robe Tower? Are you out of your mind? He came to Guanzhong this time for one reason only: because he had had word, had learned that the First Blue-Robe Tower is at..."

Of a sudden, beyond the window at her back, there came a "zheng"; a thread of black light, fine as an ox-hair, broke through the window and struck Sun Xiuqing between the shoulders.

Sun Xiuqing's face twisted all at once, and she toppled toward Ximen Chuixue. Shi Xiuxue, nearest the back window, spun with an angry cry and rushed at it; but just then another shaft of black light flashed in at the window, so swift in its coming that she could not dodge it at all.

She cried out, the sword flying from her hand, and toppled to the ground.

By now Sun Xiuqing had fallen against Ximen Chuixue; and Ximen Chuixue caught her round the waist with one hand, and with a back-turn of the other drew his sword. In one flash of sword-light man and blade seemed to have become one thing, and of a sudden he was gone out through the window.

But Lu Xiaofeng had already darted out at another window; and he heard Ma Xiuzhen and Ye Xiuzhu come rushing after, crying out in their anger.

The night was deep, the evening wind stirring the vegetable plot behind the window; where was there a single figure to be seen?

Further off, out of the dense mulberry wood, came the barking of a dog. Ximen Chuixue's sword-light had passed into the wood.

Ma Xiuzhen and Ye Xiuzhu plunged in after it, heedless of everything. The few households in the mulberry wood were all asleep; not a lamp showed, and Ximen Chuixue's sword-light too was lost to sight. A yellow dog barked wildly down the little path behind the wood.

"After them," said Ma Xiuzhen. "Whatever comes of it, we've got to bring Second Sister back." The words were not out before the two of them had rushed on.

But Lu Xiaofeng did not follow further. He stopped of a sudden beneath a tree, bent, and picked up a thing from the ground...

The keeper of the wine-shop cowered in a corner, the colour gone clean from his face.

Hua Manlou bent down and gently gathered Shi Xiuxue up. Her heart was beating still, but beating very faintly now.

A dreadful ashen grey had come over her lovely face. She opened her eyes slowly, gazed at Hua Manlou, and said softly, "You... you haven't gone?"

"I'm not going," said Hua Manlou gently. "I'll stay with you."

A very strange look came into Shi Xiuxue's eyes, half solace and half grief; and she forced a smile. "I didn't think you'd still know me."

"I'll know you always," said Hua Manlou.

Shi Xiuxue smiled again, more forlorn still. "I never became a mute; and yet I'm dying now, and the dead can't speak either, can they?"

"You... you won't die," said Hua Manlou. "You never will."

"You needn't comfort me," said Shi Xiuxue. "I know it myself; it's a poisoned needle I've taken."

Hua Manlou was moved. "A poisoned needle?"

"My whole body seems gone numb already," said Shi Xiuxue; "the poison must be near its working. You... you may feel my wound; it's surely burning hot."

Of a sudden she took Hua Manlou's hand and laid it on her wound. The wound was over her heart, and her breast was soft, and smooth, and warm. As she laid Hua Manlou's cold hand against her soft breast, her heart of a sudden beat fast again.

Hua Manlou's heart too was beating hard; and just then he heard Lu Xiaofeng's voice at the back window, asking, "What hidden weapon did she take?"

"A poisoned needle," said Hua Manlou.

Lu Xiaofeng was silent a while, then said of a sudden, "Stay here with her; I'll go and find someone."

As he spoke the last word, his voice was already far off.

Shi Xiuxue said, breathing hard, "You truly haven't gone? You truly are still here with me?"

"Close your eyes," said Hua Manlou. "I... I'll draw the poison out of the needle-wound for you."

Shi Xiuxue's pale face seemed to flush again, and a light came into her eyes. "You truly would do that?"

Hua Manlou said, low and grieving, "If only you're willing..."

"I'm willing for anything," said Shi Xiuxue; "only I won't close my eyes, for I want to look at you."

Her voice grew fainter and fainter; and then the smile on her face stiffened all at once, and the light in her eyes went suddenly out.

Death, of a sudden, without a sound, had taken her from Hua Manlou's arms.

Yet her eyes seemed to gaze at Hua Manlou still, to gaze on and on for ever...

Darkness; before Hua Manlou's eyes was only darkness.

He hated of a sudden that he was blind, that he could not look on her one last time.

She was so young still; yet her body, that had been so full of the sap of youth, had of a sudden gone cold and stiff.

Hua Manlou drew his hand gently away, and tears ran down from his empty eyes.

He did not stir, and did not go; and for the first time he felt the pitilessness and cruelty of life.

The wind blew in at the window, in at the door; the wind of the fourth month blew upon him, and was for all the world like the cold of deep winter.

Of a sudden he caught a breath of fragrance on the wind, and heard the back window creak. At once he turned his head, ready to spring.

But by then, beyond the back window, a soft and honeyed voice had risen, saying to him gently, "Don't be startled; it's me!"

The voice was one he knew well: the very one he had been longing for.

He could not help crying out, "Feiyan?"

"Yes, it's me. I didn't think you'd still know my voice."

A figure came drifting lightly in at the back window; and there was, it seemed, a note of jealous mockery in her voice as she said, low and plaintive, "I thought you'd forgotten me!"

Hua Manlou stood as though turned to stone, and only after a long while said, "You... how do you come to be here, all at once?"

"Do you mean I ought not to have come?" said Shangguan Feiyan.

Hua Manlou shook his head, and sighed. "Only that I never thought; I thought you were already..."

"You thought I was dead?" said Shangguan Feiyan.

Hua Manlou no longer knew what to say.

Shangguan Feiyan sighed again, low and plaintive. "If I am to die, I would die as she did: in your arms."

She came slowly over and stood before Hua Manlou, and said, "I saw you both just now, and... and it hurt me so; had she not been dead already, I might well have killed her myself."

Hua Manlou was long silent, then said of a sudden, "One day I heard you singing."

Shangguan Feiyan mused. "Was it outside the Ten Thousand Plum Manor, in that ruined old shrine of the hill-god?"

"It was," said Hua Manlou.

Shangguan Feiyan too was long silent, then said softly, "But by the time you came seeking, I was already gone."

"Why did you go?" said Hua Manlou.

Shangguan Feiyan's voice grew fainter still. "You must know that I did not want to go."

"Someone forced you to go?" said Hua Manlou.

"That song too I was forced to sing," said Shangguan Feiyan. "At first I did not even know what for; only later did I learn they meant to lure you into that shrine."

"They?" said Hua Manlou. "Who are they?"

Shangguan Feiyan did not answer; her voice began of a sudden to tremble, as though in great fear.

"Have you fallen, then, into those people's hands?" said Hua Manlou.

Shangguan Feiyan said in a trembling voice, "You had best not know too much, or else... or else..."

Hua Manlou could not help asking, "Or else what?"

Shangguan Feiyan was long silent again, then said, "That day they lured you there to warn you: to make you leave this matter alone; they wanted you to know that I had fallen into their hands." She would not let Hua Manlou speak, but went on, "And they have had me come today for the same end, to persuade you to leave the matter alone; or else... or else they'll have me kill you!"

Hua Manlou was moved. "They sent you to kill me?"

"Yes," said Shangguan Feiyan; "for they know you'd never dream that I would harm you, never be on your guard against me. But this they never thought of: how could I ever bring myself to lift a hand against you?"

Of a sudden she flung herself forward and held Hua Manlou tight, and said in a trembling voice, "By now you must surely have guessed who they are; but you'll never guess how dreadful their power is..."

Now that Yan Tieshan and Dugu Yihe were both dead, there was only one man left who would stop this business: Huo Xiu.

Hua Manlou said in a low voice, "However dreadful their power, you have no need to be afraid..."

"But I am afraid, truly," said Shangguan Feiyan; "not for myself, but for you. Were it not for me, you'd never have been drawn into this at all; and if any harm should come to you, how could I go on living?"

She held him tight, trembling in every limb, her breath fragrant and sweet.

Hua Manlou could not help opening his arms to take her in; but Shi Xiuxue's body was still there at his side, this loving girl who had died, only a moment since, in these very arms. How could he now put those same arms around another?

His heart was full of pain and division; he tried to master his feelings, and could not, for the life of him, master them.

When he made to embrace her again, she of a sudden pushed him away. "My meaning you must surely understand by now."

"I don't understand," said Hua Manlou.

"Whether you understand or not," said Shangguan Feiyan, "I... I must go now."

Hua Manlou cried out, "You're going? Why must you go?"

"I don't want to go either," said Shangguan Feiyan, "but go I must!"

Her voice was full of pain and dread, and she went on, "If you've any kindness left for me, don't ask me why, and don't hold me back; or you'll ruin not only yourself, but me as well!"

"But I..." said Hua Manlou.

"Let me go," said Shangguan Feiyan. "Only to know that you are still alive and well is enough to content me; and if you keep me, you do me wrong..."

Her voice grew further and further off, and of a sudden was gone.

Darkness: Hua Manlou found of a sudden that he had sunk into a boundless darkness and solitude. He knew she must have some hard necessity upon her, some bitter grief, and that this was why she went.

But he could only stand there like a fool, able neither to help her out of her trouble nor to soothe her pain, just as, a moment since, he could only stand and let Shi Xiuxue die in his arms.

"What manner of man am I, after all? What am I worth?" There seemed a voice at his ear, laughing coldly: "You're nothing but a blind man; a useless blind man!"

In a blind man's life there is only darkness, a darkness without hope.

He clenched his fists, standing in the evening wind of the fourth month, and felt of a sudden that life was not always so fair a thing as he had fancied it, and that in it there is, of its nature, much helpless sorrow and pain.

He truly did not know how a man might win free of it.

The fourth month is the very season when the swallows fly home; but his own swallow had flown away, and, like the youth of men, once gone would never turn back again.

He walked slowly across the grass beyond the door, and the grass was already soaked through with dew.
