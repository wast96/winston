## Chapter Ten. The Hour of the Dog, First Half (7 p.m.)

{v} Before the blaze had even taken shape, the blackest smoke was the first to rise; sparks swirled all around, like a black dragon of spilled ink leaping up into the night sky.

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fourteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Dog, first half.

{d} Chang’an; Chang’an County; Yining Ward.

“No contact? How can that be?”

Tanqi stared at the signal-runner, unable to believe it. The watchtower system had been planned and designed by the young master himself; it did not pass messages along a single line, for any watchtower within a constable’s line of sight could speak straight to any other. That way, even if one watchtower was slow to respond, there were other lines to carry the word.

Unless every one of the several hundred watchtowers in all of Chang’an had gone down at once, there was no way contact could be lost.

The signal-runner said: “The one that’s gone silent is the great watchtower.”

That puzzled Tanqi all the more. The great watchtower? That was the Jing’an Bureau’s master relay-tower, set up in the garden behind the great hall. It held two offices at once: to receive, at every moment, word from all across the city, and to send, at every moment, orders to any point in it. If it fell silent, the Jing’an Bureau would become a blind man struck down on one side.

A place so vital—how could the young master let it fail? Tanqi craned her long neck again and looked off toward Guangde Ward, but the night was deep and the lantern-glare dazzling, and there was no seeing so far.

“It should come back soon; the young master hates nothing more than word arriving late,” she told herself.

Meanwhile, Zhang Xiaojing was taking stock of the spoils in the alley. When he had knocked the infirmary apprentice senseless just now, he had pried a poison pellet out of his mouth. Zhang Xiaojing held it under his nose and sniffed: a mixture of wild-kudzu and aconite, he judged, though it gave off no particular smell.

A pellet like this was not something an ordinary man could compound; it showed that the power standing behind these people was a fearsome thing indeed.

Just then Tanqi hurried over and told Zhang Xiaojing that the great watchtower had gone silent. His brows knit too—this was strange indeed. Tanqi said: “Since we can’t reach them, why not just take the assassin straight back to Guangde Ward ourselves?”

“No.” Zhang Xiaojing refused flatly. “It’s already the hour of the Dog; the streets are packed solid. To haul them all the way over there—who knows how many double-hours it would take on the road. We haven’t that to spare.”

“Then what do we do?”

“Take him to the Persian Temple and question him on the spot,” Zhang Xiaojing decided. Tanqi was about to press the point, but his single eye swept over her and he said mildly: “What you do, mistress, you need not settle with me; but here it is I who decide.”

Tanqi pursed her lips and had to hold her tongue. Still uneasy, though, she sent a man back to Guangde Ward to report.

The Lüben Guards loaded the infirmary apprentice and the ox-cart driver back into the carriage and, under the astonished gaze of the sightseers along the street, drove them once more into the Persian Temple. So great a commotion stirred even the temple’s bishop, who sent a deacon out to ask what was afoot.

“Heathen villains are now hatching some wicked plot, and the court has need of the Lord’s awful radiance to overawe the powers of evil; and so it turns to my humble self, to conduct the deposition and the fine adjudication here within the temple,” the deacon Yisi told his fellows. They could make nothing of what “deposition” or “fine adjudication” might mean, but they understood that the court was thereby taking the true God to its bosom, and one after another they declared themselves honored to share in it.

The place where they held the infirmary apprentice happened to be the very confession room in which Zhang Xiaojing and Tanqi had earlier been shut up. Yisi explained that it was the quietest spot in the temple and could not be better suited to an interrogation. He was all eager helpfulness now, for fear that Zhang Xiaojing might vent his anger on the Nestorian temple.

The infirmary apprentice, trussed hand and foot, was crammed into the cramped little room and then roused with a bucket of ice-water flung over him.

“You had best make yourself scarce for what comes next,” Zhang Xiaojing said to Yisi, a cruel light glinting in his single eye. Yisi hesitated, but did not stir a step: “He did his killing in our humble temple; the temple should rightly take part in the questioning, to show that justice is done.”

“Suit yourself.”

Zhang Xiaojing slid open the little window and looked in. The man hung his head and did not move, water dripping from his hair in strings; but the faint trembling of his shoulders showed that he had come to.

The fellow was a man of the Central Plains, thin-faced and short-bearded, not heavily muscled but evenly built, with two faint blue veins running from below his ears down into his neck—plainly a killer honed by years of training. Zhang Xiaojing said nothing, only watched him coldly.

“Kill me,” the assassin said weakly.

“Let me tell you what happens next.” Zhang Xiaojing’s voice carried into the confession room. “In the Shenlong reign there was a censor named Zhou Lizhen who, at Wu Sansi’s command, went to kill Huan Yanfan. Zhou Lizhen had a whole grove of bamboo cut down on purpose, leaving the sharp stumps jutting up, and then dragged Huan Yanfan back and forth across the ground over them. Strip by strip his flesh was scraped open, torn, and ground raw, until the sinew and bone showed through. He was dragged for a full day before the breath left him, and by the time he died his flesh had all but parted from his bones, and the bamboo stumps were red—this they called ‘sunset-glow on the bamboo.’”

Zhang Xiaojing spoke with relish, dwelling on each detail as though he had seen it with his own eyes. Yisi, at his side, had begun to shake; he could not help picturing that bloody scene of ‘sunset-glow on the bamboo,’ and at once his stomach heaved. What the prisoner in the confession room felt, hearing all this, there was no telling.

Zhang Xiaojing went on: “But I haven’t a whole day just now, so I’ll try another method. This was the one Zhou Xing used on Hao Xiangxian in his day—it’s called ‘the flying stone that summons the immortal.’” That he could cite chapter and verse even for atrocities like these left Yisi not knowing whether to laugh or weep.

“I’ll push an iron hook up your fundament and catch it in the end of your bowel. One end of the hook is tied to a crossbar, and from the other end of the bar a stone is hung. Hang the crossbar on a wooden frame, you and the stone on either side of it, and it’s just like a balance-scale—you’ve used a scale, haven’t you—then I pull the stone down on my side, the bar tips up, and the hook draws your guts slowly out of your backside, inch by inch, and every inch of the way you’ll feel it clear as clear. If I pull a little faster, your guts come out all at once and are flung up into the air.

“Of course, one could just as well swap the iron hook for a sharpened bamboo point, and let the spring of the pole toss the whole man up into the air and skewer him coming down—that does nicely too.”

Then Zhang Xiaojing gave a low, chuckling laugh, and a smug one at that. Had the prisoner lifted his head and caught the single eye that flickered at the little window, he would have known the man meant every word.

Tanqi, listening at one side, knew perfectly well that Zhang Xiaojing was pressing the prisoner, and yet a chill ran through her all the same. The aura that came off him was such that she could scarcely breathe, and she had to shift her feet and stand a few paces further off.

All this while she had taken Zhang Xiaojing for a lecher, for a dutiful commander of the Jing’an Bureau, and for a reliable companion. Now, at last, she remembered what this man truly was: the Five-Faced Yama of Wannian County.

Which five? Vicious, venomous, ruthless, stubborn, pitiless.

Nine years a buliang chief in Chang’an—there was no knowing how many times he had used this method, how many men he had tormented with it.

She fought to shake the thought out of her head and traded a glance with Yisi; each read regret in the other’s eyes. Had they known, they would never have come in to listen—far better to have waited in the corridor for the result. Yisi scratched his head, at a loss: if Zhang Xiaojing really did set about the torture, was he to stop it or not? This was, after all, a holy place…

“Kill me,” the assassin repeated, low.

Zhang Xiaojing bared his teeth, his voice gone grim and cold: “You need not doubt that it works. I’ll tell you: Zhou Lizhen, Zhou Xing, all the various arts of torment we jailers use—they come down from a single lineage: Lai Junchen. The eight methods of the Lai clan are famous indeed.”

The moment the three words “Lai Junchen” were spoken, the temperature in the room dropped at once. That was the eternal nightmare of the people of Chang’an; dead these many years though the man was, his name could still hush a child crying in the night. Sometimes the name worked better than any of the cruel tortures he had devised.

“Pah!” The prisoner meant to spit, but found no spittle came, for his lips had been trembling all the while.

All of it, Zhang Xiaojing marked.

Had they been Türk Wolf Guards, he would have had no confidence of prying words out of them; but these men were different. That they carried poison pellets on their persons showed that, though they did not fear death, they feared severe torture all the same. He was trembling now—a good sign.

Zhang Xiaojing snapped the little window shut with a whoosh, letting the terror ferment a while. In the black, sealed space the prisoner would picture those scenes over and over in his own mind, unable to stop. Any sound from without—a footstep, a wooden stool dragged across the floor—would be taken for the signal that his torture was about to begin. Some men had been frightened clean to death this way.

Zhang Xiaojing deliberately asked no questions at all, so as to plant in the prisoner’s mind the false notion that his interrogators wanted nothing from him. That was the way to make him ever more afraid, ever more anxious to prove his own worth.

The art of interrogation, like the art of the bedchamber, has its essence in the foreplay.

Having set it all up, Zhang Xiaojing turned and left the confession room. Tanqi and Yisi stood well back by the door, a touch of dread in the way they looked at him. Zhang Xiaojing brushed at the hollow of his eye and offered no explanation. The world these two lived in was too fair a place; they had no idea what the world at the very bottom really looked like.

Yisi wavered a long while, then sidled up all the same: “Commander Zhang must have pored over a good many old books; that assassin looks truly frightened, I’d say.”

“I didn’t learn it from books,” said Zhang Xiaojing, with a slight smile. Yisi felt a chill rise from the soles of his feet to the crown of his head, and his already-fair skin lost what color it had.

“You keep watch here; the moment the prisoner speaks, tell me at once. I’ll go out and look over the ground.”

“The ground?” Yisi did not understand.

“‘The flying stone that summons the immortal’ is best done on level ground, so the frame stands firm.”

“Now—this—surely this is against all humanity…” This time Yisi was truly appalled: the man actually meant to torture someone on the spot, here inside the Nestorian temple! How were the Nestorian monks to hold up their heads afterward?

Zhang Xiaojing paid him no heed but walked out of the confession room and began surveying the yard, now and then holding up two fingers to take a measurement, or stamping at the earth with his foot to test how soft it was, for all the world like the most conscientious of builders.

Before long Yisi came stumbling out of the hall, robe hitched up: “Commander Zhang! Don’t set up your frame! He’s confessed, he’s confessed!” In his haste he dropped his fine words altogether and spoke plainly.

“Oh? He’s told all?”

“Yes, all of it!”

The prisoner’s confession was owed, as it happened, to Yisi. After Zhang Xiaojing had gone, Yisi turned it over this way and that, growing more and more uneasy, until he slipped round to the other side of the confession room and, just as he would when hearing a believer’s confession, began earnestly, patiently, to talk the assassin round.

Whether there was truly some power of persuasion in Yisi’s words, or whether the atmosphere Zhang Xiaojing had conjured beforehand was simply too terrifying, the prisoner at last gave up all resistance. Yisi came running to head Zhang Xiaojing off.

From the standpoint of interrogation, one soft and one hard, one to beat and one to draw, can indeed make a man talk the sooner.

As they neared the confession room, Yisi caught hold of Zhang Xiaojing: “He has promised to hold nothing back—but you must pardon his crimes. This man has agreed to take refuge in my Lord, and henceforth to cultivate himself in quiet and never set foot beyond the temple gate.”

“That you can take up with the Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau; I’m only here to ask the questions.” Zhang Xiaojing shook off his hand. This deacon was overstepping his place indeed, meddling now in the court’s affairs.

The prisoner was still bound within the confession room, but the wooden door stood open now, so that he could see the light. Tanqi sat across from him to lead the questioning, while Zhang Xiaojing stood at one side and never took his eyes from the man’s face—first to bring an unseen pressure to bear, and second to watch the assassin’s smallest movements, so that the least half-word of a lie would be caught on the instant.

The assassin slowly began to speak, calling himself a Shouzhuolang. At that name, Zhang Xiaojing found himself knitting his thick brows.

The word “shouzhuo” had first meant the small garrison-towns on the frontiers of the Great Tang. These little towns did not stand at any strategic pass, were very small in scale, and the court took next to no notice of them. In peace they governed themselves, in war they defended themselves; and over time each garrison-town became a lawless patch that neither the Tang code nor the emperor’s grace could reach, where dragons and serpents mingled together.

From the Kaiyuan years on, the Tang militia fell ever more into decay, and the garrison headquarters had almost no troops left to send up on their tours of duty. At just this time an organization called the Shouzhuolang quietly appeared, providing mercenary service to the local governments, the military commissioners, and the great merchants of every region. Its membership was of the most mixed sort: fugitive criminals, old discharged garrison-soldiers, the sons and daughters of farmers driven into exile on the frontier, and a great many Hu of the Western Regions of unknown origin. They had but one thing in common: all of them came out of the garrison-towns of one region or another.

The Shouzhuolang’s fighting men were hard and capable, and their work was clean; in a dozen-odd years they had grown into a force of decisive weight within the bounds of the Great Tang.

That these two assassins should come from the Shouzhuolang made the whole business stranger still.

Zhang Xiaojing had had dealings with the Shouzhuolang a time or two; at bottom they were men of business, quiet and careful in what they did. Their chief clientele was the Tang itself—how should they be in league with the Türks to bring ruin on Chang’an? Had they tired of living?

Then a second thought struck him: quite possibly the Shouzhuolang had merely taken a commission for a killing, and knew nothing of what lay behind the man they were to kill. So he quietly told Tanqi to ask along those lines.

Sure enough, as Tanqi questioned further, the assassin admitted that he did not know this Elder Puzhe at all. He had only had his orders: to lie hidden in the Persian Temple and keep watch on the elder’s every move, and the moment a signal came, to strike at once, kill, and withdraw.

Zhang Xiaojing pressed him as to who gave the signal, and the assassin said no one did: the signal was a crow’s nest at the top of a scholar-tree in the Persian Temple. Whenever the crow’s nest was gone, that meant they could make their move.

That way the two sides never had to meet, which lowered the chance of anything leaking. It was a common enough arrangement—only hard luck on that nestful of crows.

“Then who issued you your orders?” Zhang Xiaojing asked again. The assassin might know nothing of the true nature of the client, but he was bound to know his own superior.

The assassin fell silent; this touched their gravest taboo. These Shouzhuolang all had wives and children living back in the garrison-towns. If a man died, the organization would see his family cared for and pensioned; but if he turned traitor, there was no telling what fate his kin at home might meet.

Zhang Xiaojing said coldly: “Since you’ve already opened your mouth and confessed, you’ve already betrayed the Shouzhuolang—better to tell the whole of it; perhaps then the court will show you some favor.” The assassin caught the threat in Zhang Xiaojing’s words and, a look of despair crossing his face, gazed imploringly at Tanqi and Yisi.

Yisi could not bear the sight and spoke up: “Since his heart turns toward the Lord, it is not right to press him too—” Zhang Xiaojing suddenly stabbed a finger at the doorway and roared:

“Out!”

This sudden thunderclap made everyone in the room start. Yisi was struck dumb, hardly able to believe it. Since coming to Chang’an, no one had ever spoken to him so harshly, in voice and face alike.

Zhang Xiaojing cursed him roundly: “Do you take yourself for the Minister of Justice, or the Chief of the Court of Judicial Review? Standing here jabbering away, waving your hands and pointing!”

“I only meant—”

“This Persian Temple of yours has harbored a wanted criminal and brought harm on Chang’an; and you have obstructed the Jing’an Bureau in its work and all but let an assassin escape. On these two charges alone there is enough to tear your temple up by the roots! And you still think you’ve done a service?”

“But—”

“Get out!”

Yisi, cursed white as ash, took a long moment to summon his courage; then he made the sign of the cross and said: “I am a servant of the Lord, and I have care only for His grace.” He bowed deeply, turned, and left, his steps stumbling, as though hard hit.

Tanqi watched his retreating back and gave a soft sigh. She felt a little pity for this vain, guileless Nestorian monk; but the matter was grave, and left no room for the mercy of a bodhisattva—only the glare of a wrathful guardian-king.

Seeing Zhang Xiaojing vent himself so on Yisi, the assassin too was a little cowed. Zhang Xiaojing slapped the table: “Let me tell you—the man you killed was the Türk Right Shad. He was laying plans for a band of villains to destroy the whole of Chang’an this very night. The commission you took was to silence a witness for those villains.”

The assassin’s pupils shrank sharply at that. He did not know what a Right Shad was, nor could he well make out the tangled connections among all this; but he knew well enough what it would mean for the whole of Chang’an to be destroyed.

“The Shouzhuolang playing the tiger’s jackal, setting itself against the court—when that day comes, never mind your organization: every last garrison-town on the frontier will be wiped out to a man.”

The assassin held his silence, but the corners of his brows twitched faintly. “Wiped out”—two words only, yet they meant well over a hundred thousand garrison women and children cast adrift and sunk into base slavery. The court of the Great Tang was capable of such a thing.

“Name your superior—it is your own Shouzhuolang you’ll be saving.” Zhang Xiaojing struck his last blow.

The assassin broke down at last, utterly. He covered his face and, stammering, gave out an address: “Ping… Pingkang Ward. Our lodging and our commissions are all handed over there, at Liu’s Bookshop inside it.”

Pingkang Ward?

Zhang Xiaojing was startled for a moment; then, thinking it over, he found it could not be more reasonable.

Pingkang Ward held more than its green-tower brothels: it also held the resident-agent courtyards of the ten military commissioners—of Fanyang, Hedong, Pinglu, Shuofang, Hexi, Anxi, Beiting, Longyou, Jiannan, and the Lingnan Five Prefectures.

These ten resident-agent courtyards handled every affair of the ten military commissioners in the capital—from the great, such as the movement of money and grain, the comings and goings of officials, and the presenting of memorials, down to the small, such as outings for the commissioners’ households and the buying of gifts, all fell to them. They had another task too, not to be spoken of aloud: to serve as each region’s intelligence-post in the capital, gathering local reports to pass up to the court while at the same time being the commissioner’s eyes and ears in the city.

The whole matter of the Türk Wolf Guards’ assault on the capital had first been discovered by the Shuofang resident-agent courtyard, which then reported it to the court; the Jing’an Bureau’s taking it over came only afterward.

The military commissioners were the Shouzhuolang’s great clients, and it was usually the resident-agent courtyard that fronted the commissions. For the Shouzhuolang to set their lodging inside Pingkang Ward made the dealings between them as convenient as could be.

It seemed that today he was fated to enter Pingkang Ward a second time.

As he thought it over, Zhang Xiaojing worked his fingers. The wound on the little finger of his left hand had begun to ache dully again. He was about to set off when he suddenly heard a Lüben soldier come running up outside in alarm. Tanqi recognized him as the very man who had been sent to the Jing’an Bureau in Guangde Ward, and hurried to stop him and ask why he had come back.

“The Jing’an Bureau’s been attacked!” The soldier’s voice broke, and he could scarcely catch his breath. “The whole great hall’s gone up in flames!”

The great hall of the Jing’an Bureau in Guangde Ward was growing brighter than it had ever been. Countless points of flame leapt out from within the walls and between the tiles, devouring the building in a frenzy with a crackling and popping, spreading wildly with every snap of a finger. Before long these flames would gather into one and turn the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau into a torch no less grand than any of the colored lanterns of the West Market. At the same time, fire rose in the left and right side-halls as well.

Before the blaze had even taken shape, the blackest smoke was the first to rise; sparks swirled all around, like a black dragon of spilled ink leaping up into the night sky. The smoke was black past blackness and thick past thickness, and it carried an acrid, stinging smell; the night sky, that the lantern-fires of all the wards had already lit bright, was forcibly blotted black again by this pall of smoke.

The watchtowers, near and far, were all waving their purple lanterns toward headquarters in vain, waiting for a reply that was fated never to come again.

Many of the Jing’an Bureau’s clerks came pouring out of the main door and the side doors, every one of them in wretched disarray. Some fell to the ground, some cried aloud for help, and some ran with the backs of their robes still alight, shrieking piteously as they went.

It was fortunate that Chang’an had always taken the fire-hazard of the Lantern Festival very seriously, and every year, when the lantern-fair came round, posted great numbers of constables on constant standby. The moment fire showed in Guangde Ward, the fire-fighting constables of the neighboring wards reacted at once and hastened over. But there were simply too many people out to see the lanterns, and it cost them twice the time to make their way there.

The relief that arrived first was too few in number and could only tend to the survivors and then see that the fire did not spread to the surrounding buildings. For the great hall itself they were wholly helpless.

Many officials, once they had fled to safety, plumped down on the ground and wept bitterly before the blaze. The great hall and the two side-halls had held a vast store of vital documents and records, and all of it, in a stroke, was burning away to nothing. Without those, there could be no working of the art of the Great Archive, and the Jing’an Bureau would lose its most vital power of insight.

In the hearts of these survivors there was one image of horror past all telling. Before they had fled the great hall, they had seen the enormous sand-table of Chang’an inside it engulfed in fire: the surface of the Vermilion Bird Avenue split into great cracks, the Leyou Plateau melting under the licking tongues of flame, smoke rising from Qujiang Pool, the one hundred and eight wards toppling and collapsing patch by patch—it was a scene very like hell itself. Everyone who saw it was pressed so hard by this vast and ill-boding omen that they could not draw breath.

The great fire stirred every government office round about. From the constables’ posts on the ward corners to the buliang men of the Jingzhao Prefecture, from the Lüben Guards to the Right Xiao Guard, all sent men to try to draw near and find out just what had happened. And many sightseers and idlers out for the lanterns, taking it for some new spectacle, crowded up in curiosity to watch.

The Jing’an Bureau’s standing was too sensitive; for it to catch fire at such a moment was bound to draw attention from every quarter.

By rights, at a moment like this, one should first contrive to rescue the documents in the hall, then work to restore the great watchtower’s communications and dispatch the various forces to a defensive posture. But of the two chiefs, He Zhizhang and Li Bi, one lay gravely ill and the other was a captive; the Jing’an Bureau’s commander and the head of the Lüben Guards were both far off in Yining Ward; and the recorder Xu Bin had vanished no one knew where. The whole scene was a dragon-host without a head, all confusion.

The Jing’an Bureau was like a giant struck by a poisoned arrow, collapsing to the ground in an instant, all sense gone from it.

A troop of cavalry came charging up at speed, leopard-skin trimming the lower rims of their shoulder-guards—plainly the elite Leopard Cavalry of the Right Xiao Guard. Whirling their whips, the Leopard riders roughly drove back the crowd of onlookers and soon cleared a safe patch of open ground near the scene of the fire. Gan Shoucheng, in full armor and hemmed round by a dozen-odd household guards, hurried up.

Beyond the imperial city was not, properly, the Right Xiao Guard’s charge. But Gan Shoucheng had happened to be on patrol nearby, and so had come over.

Gan Shoucheng lifted his head and watched the fire in the great hall without a word, no expression at all on his taut face. A household guard beside him laughed: “The Jing’an Bureau burned us, and not a few double-hours later here’s the retribution. Quick work, this reckoning in the here and now…” He had not finished when—crack!—the whip lashed savagely across his thigh, so that he leapt high with the pain.

Gan Shoucheng snarled under his breath: “Shut your dog’s mouth!” In his heart just now there was not the least gloating of revenge—only fear.

His men had just now found several surviving clerks. By the survivors’ account, a band of masked men calling themselves the “Pifu” had stormed the Jing’an Bureau, worked a spell of slaughter and destruction, and then, before those outside were aware of it, swiftly carried off Deputy Director Li. Before they left, they had also sprayed a great quantity of rock-oil about and given the whole great hall and the side-halls to the flames.

A layman hearing this would only be shocked at the raiders’ cruelty; but Gan Shoucheng, with his decades of soldiering, felt instead a chill that went to the bone. What nerve, what confidence must the man pulling the strings possess, to conceive a plan that struck straight at the nerve-center like this.

This raid—in its command of intelligence beforehand, in the making of the plan, and in the decisive cleanness of its execution—showed the very highest standard. It was as though some nameless minor officer had ridden a single horse through the passes and, in the midst of ten thousand troops, taken the head of the enemy’s supreme general clean off. Gan Shoucheng did not believe any of the capital’s palace armies had such a capacity; even the frontier armies might not be its match.

Set against this, the awkwardness of being forced into that wager by Li Bi and He Dong a while ago counted for nothing at all.

“Pifu… Pifu…” Gan Shoucheng murmured the two words under his breath; he could not recall any organization by that name.

A troop as formidable as this—what if its target had not been the Jing’an Bureau, but the imperial city, or the three great palaces?

At the thought, the wrist that gripped the whip trembled despite him, and his heart went cold. Just then a rider galloped up to report: “We’ve found Commander Cui.” Gan Shoucheng said: “Have him come and report at once.” Cui Qi had stayed behind to hold the great hall of the Jing’an Bureau all along, and would know things there in more detail. But the rider looked troubled: “That’s just… best you go to him, sir.”

Gan Shoucheng knit his brows, shook the reins, and followed the rider over.

At the door of an apothecary near the Jing’an Bureau that dealt in raw and prepared medicines, a dozen-odd wounded lay on hastily spread matting, their groans unbroken. The proprietor and his man were busy working sesame oil in a great stone mortar—the quickest burn-remedy to prepare just now—while a few kind-hearted neighbors bustled about carrying clean water. At the shop door, several Right Xiao Guard riders had already taken their stand to left and right, letting no one draw near.

Gan Shoucheng lifted the door-curtain and stepped inside. There were four people within: besides Cui Qi, there were two men and a woman, all of them grimed with soot and ash, and of them Gan Shoucheng knew only Yao Runeng.

Seeing Gan Shoucheng come in, Yao Runeng only rolled his eyes toward him, his face dark as dead ash. He had never imagined the great hall in front would be ten times more terrible than the prison. When he had seen that raging fire, he had all but gone mad. His faith, his confidence, and the very object of his loyalty had turned, just so, to flying ash.

Gan Shoucheng’s gaze passed over Yao Runeng and went to Cui Qi beside him.

His state was worse than Yao Runeng’s. He lay stiff and straight on a door-plank taken off its hinges, his lower belly one mass of blood smeared over with a paste of styptic powder. One look told Gan Shoucheng that the styptic powder had done no good at all, washed away by the blood; the man was surely past saving. Hearing the footsteps, Cui Qi suddenly opened his eyes and looked toward him weakly, his mouth opening and closing.

Gan Shoucheng had little liking for this traitor, but seeing him in so pitiful a state now, he did not for a moment know what to say. So he simply bent forward and put the question straight:

“Commander Cui—who do you think the attackers were?”

After a long moment a very feeble voice came: “Soldiers… they were all soldiers…”

Gan Shoucheng’s heart sank. He had suspected all along that so precise and vicious a manner of attack could come from no body but professional soldiers. Now, it seemed, the whole military world of the Great Tang was in for an upheaval.

“Can you tell what soldiers, from where?” Gan Shoucheng pressed.

Cui Qi closed his eyes and gave a faint shake of the head. Seeing his state, Gan Shoucheng had to give up the questioning and offered a few absent-minded words of comfort. Just then Cui Qi spoke again:

“General Gan… I should never have come to Chang’an.”

“Mm?” Gan Shoucheng was taken aback.

“I came to the capital thinking I could win merit and make my name, but I should never have come. Chang’an has turned me into the very kind of coward I once despised most. Liulang… I want to go back to the Long Mountains, I want to go back to the Long Mountains…”

Cui Qi gazed up at the ceiling, muttering, two trails of tears running down his cheeks. Those around him were silent. All at once he mustered the last of his strength and gave two great roars: “Cui Qi of the Long Mountains! Cui Qi of the Long Mountains!” Then the cries broke off short, and his breathing stilled along with them.

Wen Ran quietly crouched down and wiped Cui Qi’s dead face with a sweat-cloth. She did not know what this man had done before, but the figure that had fought so bravely against the enemy before the prison she had seen clearly with her own eyes. Yao Runeng turned his head aside, a deep grief in his gaze, and Zhang Xiaojing’s words came back into his mind: “In Chang’an, if you don’t become a monster like it, it will devour you.”

Gan Shoucheng rose to his feet, laid his left arm across his chest, and struck his chest three times. It was the soldiers’ salute to a fallen comrade, and the household guards beside him performed it in unison after their general.

A voice rose in the room: “Have you not heard how the note of the nomad reed-pipe is saddest of all, blown by the purple-bearded, green-eyed men of the Hu? A single tune of it is not yet ended, and already it grieves to death the garrison boys of Loulan… The reed-pipe wails as it sees you off; from the hills of Qin you gaze afar at the clouds of the Long Mountains. Night upon night in the frontier town the sad dreams throng—who, turning to the moon, is glad to hear the nomad reed-pipe?”

The poem sang of garrison duty on the frontier, and through its lines wound a mournful longing to go home. All turned their heads to look: a square-faced, straight-nosed young man leaned against the corner of the wall, arms folded, and it was from his mouth that the poem had just come.

“Did you write that?” Gan Shoucheng asked. Cen Shen cupped his hands: “Only something struck out of feeling, a few scattered lines, not yet a finished piece—I am Cen Shen of Xianzhou.”

“A fine poem, only ill-suited to the times. The age of splendor is at its height—why give voice to such grief and complaint?” Gan Shoucheng passed a few offhand remarks and then turned to go out. Cen Shen called loudly after his back: “General, do you truly think this age of splendor needs nothing but words of flattery and praise? The five colors blind the eye, and a man gone blind cannot see the crisis lying hidden in the dark.”

Gan Shoucheng’s steps halted.

It was not that Cen Shen’s words had startled him—there was nothing new in that scholar’s kind of complaint—but that from his last sentence he had leapt to a terrible conjecture.

Those men had attacked the Jing’an Bureau carrying oil on their persons; plainly they had come to destroy, and had withdrawn the moment their aim was met. Such an act was not like revenge; it was more like a precaution: the Jing’an Bureau was the eye of Chang’an. Gouge out the eye, and the city became a blind man, and the enemy could do as they pleased.

That was to say: the raid on the Jing’an Bureau was only one necessary link in a plan, and the attackers must have some larger target still.

Once Gan Shoucheng had thought this through, a film of cold sweat at once came out on the lining of his armor. Targets larger than the Jing’an Bureau were not many in Chang’an.

At the thought, he had no mind at all to linger here longer, and strode quickly out the door. Outside it was still all uproar. The fire went on, with not the least sign of dying down. Men from seven or eight different offices were mixed together, shouting and bawling, each going his own way, with no one at all directing from the center, so that the relief and the fire-fighting went woefully ill.

“If there is no new chief, the Jing’an Bureau is likely finished,” Gan Shoucheng thought.

He did not like the Jing’an Bureau, but he had to admit that its part in hunting down enemies was one no other office could replace. If it were finished, it would be a very great blow to the safety of all Chang’an.

A great burning chunk of eave-bracketing came down and struck an ox-cart that was carrying the wounded, raising a chorus of cries. The carter had made a mistake and stopped his cart too close to the scene of the fire.

Several post-soldiers were binding up stretchers, ready to carry people off. But their position happened to block the passage before the ward, so that the water-skins behind could not be brought through, forcing the soldiers fighting the fire in front to fall back, and in doing so they carelessly trod several stretchers to pieces. A quarrel broke out between the two sides.

Such things happened again and again at the scene, gravely slowing the progress of the relief.

Watching these blundering errors one after another, Gan Shoucheng could scarcely bear it, and stepped forward and raised his right hand. He was the highest-ranking official on the scene just now; he had only to lift his arm and call out, and things would take a turn for the better. But Gan Shoucheng hesitated again and again, and lowered his hand.

A palace-army general taking over command of the city’s defenses? No, that was far too great a breach of taboo; it could by no means be done. The backer behind the Jing’an Bureau was the heir apparent, and whoever came to clear up the wreckage must be of the Eastern Palace’s faction.

Mm? Wait—that need not be so.

A fine idea suddenly rose up in Gan Shoucheng’s mind. He called over a rider, wrote out a letter on the spot, and had him carry it straight to the Secretariat at once. The letter’s content was simple: the Jing’an Bureau had suffered a calamity of arms, its leadership was broken, and there was danger to the governance of the city; he therefore requested that the central authority choose worthy men anew and reorganize the Bureau’s affairs.

He knew that Li Linfu had long coveted control of the Jing’an Bureau, and had only been at a loss for a way in. This letter could hand the Right Minister a high-sounding pretext and a very great favor.

And the act was, on its official face, beyond all reproach. I, a general of the Right Xiao Guard, out of concern for security, advise the Secretariat Director to choose a new official to take over the Jing’an Bureau—all upright and aboveboard, springing from a public-spirited heart; no one could say there was any suspicion of overreach or of meddling in what was not his charge.

He would both do a favor and hold the high moral ground, and drive forward the Jing’an Bureau’s rebuilding besides—one stone bringing down three birds.

As for the confusion before his eyes, it would just have to stay confused a while longer. With a look of regret, Gan Shoucheng cast another glance at the torch-like great hall of the Jing’an Bureau, wheeled his horse about, and hurried away. He had to get back quickly and go over the Right Xiao Guard’s security once again.

The black smoke and the flames went on dancing in the night sky, while the other quarters of Chang’an still sang and danced in peace, the sightseers thick as woven threads, wholly unaware of all that was happening here, still less of what it all meant.

***

When she heard the news that the Jing’an Bureau had been attacked, Tanqi was struck utterly dumb.

She felt it could only be a rumor—how could such a thing possibly happen? This was the Jing’an Bureau! Casting reserve aside, she seized the soldier by his armor and all but shouted at him, demanding to know exactly what had happened.

But the soldier had had no chance to get near the great hall and knew none of the particulars. He had only learned that someone seemed to have attacked the Jing’an Bureau and set it ablaze, and had then hurried back to bring word.

“Then the young master? Where is Deputy Director Li?”

“I—I don’t know,” the soldier stammered.

Tanqi drew a deep breath, shoved the soldier aside, ran to her mount, and, without even troubling to step on the mounting-stone, swung herself up onto the horse in her haste and shook the reins to be off. Just then a man suddenly blocked the way before the horse and, with one great hand, seized the bridle and held it fast.

“Where do you think you’re going?” Zhang Xiaojing barked, his face dark.

“Back to Guangde Ward! Didn’t you hear—the Jing’an Bureau’s been attacked!” Tanqi’s voice was shrill, and edged with a sob.

Zhang Xiaojing’s face was grim: “Going back now serves no purpose at all.” Tanqi cried: “I’m not under your command! Out of my way!” She shook the reins again and urged the horse forward to knock Zhang Xiaojing aside. Zhang Xiaojing squared his chest and stood in the road without stirring a hair: “We have more important things to do.”

Tanqi was beside herself. That the man could be so heartless and unfeeling, without a scrap of a heart in him: “You’re a condemned prisoner—the Jing’an Bureau is nothing to you! But I can’t abandon the young master!” She spurred the horse on, meaning to leap past.

Zhang Xiaojing did not let her go forward. A fierce light flashed in his single eye, and with both hands he struck hard at the horse’s ears on either side. The horse, stung with sudden pain, took fright at once and began to buck and kick; unable to keep her seat, Tanqi was thrown clean from its back.

The fall left Tanqi dizzy, and she lay sprawled on the ground unable to rise. Zhang Xiaojing paced over but did not reach out to help her up, only looked coldly down at her: “Deputy Director Li is at the Jing’an Bureau. If even he cannot deal with it, then even if you did rush back, what could you do?”

Tanqi propped herself half up and turned her face away—how could this cruel butcher who tortured prisoners understand human feeling? Zhang Xiaojing saw straight through her thoughts and said without a shred of mercy: “Yes, you care, you’re furious, you’re full of human feeling—but these shit emotions are of no use whatever to the situation! Read my lips: of no damned use at all.”

The sudden coarse words flushed Tanqi’s face red. She was about to fire back when Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye glared and, in a still louder voice, bore her down: “Do you take this for some rich young lady’s game among the flowers—go when you please, leave when you please? Wrong! This is war! War allows no acting on feeling! Every last person must obey orders, to the letter!”

Tanqi had never seen the man so ferocious; the tirade left her unable to lift her head.

“I have good friends in the Bureau too—do you think I’m not worried? Do you think I don’t want to rush straight back? But our task is not to defend the Jing’an Bureau! It is to track down the Que-le Huo-duo and save this city of Chang’an! Until that is settled, the task goes on.”

“First—first go back and take one look, then go after the Shouzhuolang…” Tanqi still tried to argue.

“There’s no time for it! Two places—you can choose only one. Make your choice, and you must bear the cost of it.” Zhang Xiaojing shot her a glare, turned, took a few steps, and flung back one cold sentence:

“Your young master agreed to let you come with me because he believes you can do something worth more than waiting on people.”

With that he left Tanqi weeping aloud and walked to the gate of the Persian Temple. The Shouzhuolang, held fast by two soldiers, stood off to one side. His face was haggard and anxious, not knowing whether good or ill awaited him next.

There was no water-clock nearby, and no telling the exact hour, but the hour of the Rooster was likely well past its half already. Outside, the lantern-market was working slowly toward its height, wave upon wave of noise rising higher, the brightness only growing. Zhang Xiaojing pressed down his unease and said shortly: “Bring this man along; we set out.”

So the soldiers bundled the assassin into a covered cart, and several soldiers climbed in as well. Drawing the cloth curtain to from outside, they hid the inside from view.

Zhang Xiaojing led over his own mount and was just settling into the saddle to leave the temple when a hand suddenly caught the bridle at his side; the startled horse gave a whinny and reared its forehooves high. Zhang Xiaojing hastily gripped with his legs and drew in his belly, clinging fast to the horse’s back, and so kept from falling.

He turned his head and saw Tanqi standing before the horse. Traces of tears not wiped clean still lingered at the corners of her eyes, and her clear, lovely face had taken on a touch more of weariness, and a touch more of resolve. She let go the bridle and lifted her chin: “Now we’re even. Let’s go.”

Without waiting for Zhang Xiaojing to answer, she had already turned and mounted, binding her long hair back with a length of thin cord and then coiling it up on the crown of her head. That way, when she moved, her hair would not come loose and fall; and Tanqi’s neck being especially long and slender, the high-bound hair made her whole bearing the more brisk and capable.

Zhang Xiaojing made no remark at all, but waved a hand and gave the order to set out.

The party swiftly left the Persian Temple, squeezing a path through the tide of lantern-viewers and making for the Pingkang Quarter at their utmost speed. After they had gone a little way, the party suddenly scattered before a crossroads, splitting into two groups that went off in two directions. Very soon another rider came hurrying up from behind, torn a long while which way to go, until at last he settled on the right-hand branch and galloped after it.

He galloped in one breath to the gate of Yining Ward, where the party ahead suddenly vanished. Just as he was craning his head to search for them, several riders closed in around him left and right. Zhang Xiaojing stepped out of the shadows, looked hard, and his expression showed more astonishment even than the ambushed man’s: “Deacon Yisi?”

“Commander Zhang—I trust you’ve kept well.” Yisi straightened his chest and made the sign of the cross on his mule. Roundly cursed by Zhang Xiaojing a little while before, he now showed not the least awkwardness, but seemed rather like an old friend met again.

No sooner had they left the Persian Temple than Zhang Xiaojing had noticed a tail behind them. They had laid a snare thinking to catch a member of the Shouzhuolang, and never dreamed it would turn out to be the vain, guileless deacon of the Persian Temple.

“What are you following us for?”

Yisi, working to keep his balance on the mule, spoke up: “The Commander’s stern lesson just now was truly like clarified butter poured over my head to enlighten me. I have searched myself in fear and reverence, and indeed our temple has doted on doctrine and neglected to examine itself. And so I have resolved to come and share the Commander’s burdens. If I can be of the smallest help, then the Nestorian temple will not have failed the court’s kind regard.”

Zhang Xiaojing understood the whole speech. The Persian Temple had hidden a Türk Right Shad and two Shouzhuo assassins; if the matter were really brought to light, the whole temple was likely in for trouble. For the sake of the Nestorian faith’s survival in Chang’an, Yisi could only thicken his skin and press in to help, and so at least try to strike off his temple’s faults against some merit.

On his horse, Zhang Xiaojing narrowed his single eye and simply said nothing. Yisi waited in fear and trembling, his throat bobbing as he swallowed, not knowing whether these words would move this god of fury.

Seeing no response from him after a long while, Yisi cupped his hands, his voice turning to entreaty: “We Nestorian monks spread our faith in the Middle Kingdom only with difficulty; I beg the Commander to show mercy beyond the letter of the law. I am willing to hold your reins and steady your stirrup, glad to go before you as vanguard—and besides, in investigating a case, does not the Commander just have need of a nimble-handed, keen-eyed, sharp-witted helper?”

“…” This time even Zhang Xiaojing had nothing to say.

Tanqi held herself in a long while before she could master the urge to kick this self-adoring man off his mule. Yisi too felt he had put it none too aptly, and hastened to change his words:

“In dealing with the Hu, my standing as a Persian prince is sure to be of some help.”

The Hu for the most part believed in the Three Foreign Religions, of which the Nestorian faith was one great branch, so in saying this Yisi was not merely boasting. As for the “Persian prince” and the rest of it, one might set that down to his own trumpet-blowing. Zhang Xiaojing was moved at last: “Do as you like—but I don’t guarantee your safety.”

Yisi was overjoyed and hurriedly whipped his mule to keep close behind the party. He had come out after them in too great a rush to ready a horse, and had simply grabbed a mule to hand. It was as well that the street was so crowded now that mule and horse moved at much the same pace. Not daring to draw too near Zhang Xiaojing, Yisi went to make up to Tanqi; but Tanqi’s mind was on the young master, and she could not be bothered with him, so Yisi had to trail along alone at the rear.

Stopping and starting, they made their way with great difficulty through the crowds of lantern-viewers and came to Guanglu Ward. Ahead lay the Vermilion Bird Avenue, and beyond it the jurisdiction of Wannian County. But at this point the carts truly could go no further.

The broad Vermilion Bird Avenue now was one dense mass of common folk, shoulder against shoulder, past all counting, a great black press so thick that not even the wind could get through.

They were all waiting to see the lantern-floats.

The lantern-floats were not lanterns at all, but troupes of performers singing and dancing on great specially-built carts. These lantern-float carts were selected by the local governments of every region and sent into the capital to add luster to the Lantern Festival. After the lamps were lit, they entered the city from three directions—east, west, and south—and moved slowly along the streets, each troupe showing off its arts, until at the fourth watch, that is the hour of the Ox, second half, they gathered before the Xingqing Palace. The performer who won the most praise and made the most dazzling show was called the “Lantern-Crown Red Tally.”

There the Son of Heaven would graciously permit the “Lantern-Crown Red Tally” to mount the Qinzheng Wuben Tower and, together with him, light the greatest lantern-tower in all Chang’an, carrying the festival mood to its very peak—and this was the origin of the lantern-floats.

Besides viewing the lanterns, another great pleasure of the people of Chang’an was to chase after these lantern-float carts. Wherever a cart went, they followed; and some especially outstanding performers had, year after year, a fixed following that trailed them the whole way.

Now, in the middle of the Vermilion Bird Avenue, two hugely popular lantern-float trains were vying in their arts: on one side a scarlet-robed dancing-girl playing the pipa behind her back, on the other a half-naked big man beating a four-faced jie-drum. Each had a band of musicians playing alongside. Countless devotees pressed in all around, holding silk batons high and shouting in unison, streaming with sweat.

One look at this told Zhang Xiaojing that the crowd here was not likely to break up within half a double-hour, and the wide carts could surely never get through. He conferred briefly with the others and decided to have the soldiers escort the carts round by a southern detour, slowly, while he himself went on ahead. A single rider crossing the Vermilion Bird Way was much faster than several riders with a cart besides.

Zhang Xiaojing had meant Tanqi to go with the carts, but she glared: “Don’t you always say each person must answer for their own choices? A moment ago you insisted I come along, and now you’d shake me off?” Stubbornly she turned her horse’s head.

Zhang Xiaojing could only agree with a wry smile. So he and Tanqi, two riders on two horses, went on ahead, while the rest took the roundabout way.

As for that deacon Yisi trailing along at their heels, Zhang Xiaojing’s notion was to pay him no mind: follow if he liked, and if he lost them, it served him right.

The plan settled, the carters turned the carts about and made off southward. Zhang Xiaojing and Tanqi dismounted and looped the reins several times around their wrists. These two horses had no tortoiseshell brow-plates, and so could not gallop on the Vermilion Bird Way. Besides, with so many people on the great way now, riding was slower than leading a horse on foot.

So the two of them, side by side, led their two horses and worked hard to squeeze through the crowds pressing all around. On every side were candle-shadows and colored lanterns, the din of zither and drum, and now and then shredded spring-streamers and flower-coins tossed up into the air, drifting slowly down again to raise gusts of delighted cries. The whole great way was steeped in the smell of powder and paint, of sour sweat, and of the cloying sweetness of several thousand candles, rich enough to drip, until the onlookers were quite intoxicated with it.

These two people and two horses went on in silence, quite out of keeping with the excited crowd. Threading through the throng, Zhang Xiaojing had drawn in his killing air and his ferocity, keeping so low it was as though he did not exist. Several times excited sightseers bumped into him before they realized there was a man there at all. More than once Tanqi turned her face aside, wanting to say something to Zhang Xiaojing, yet not knowing what to say.

Lecher, condemned prisoner, fiend-Yama, gentleman who would not let a woman die in his place, cruel official, capable agent, knight-errant… in the space of a few short double-hours Tanqi had already met many faces of Zhang Xiaojing, and yet the man remained hard for her to grasp. Now this jumbled tide of people, oddly enough, was like a purling stream that washed the gaudy paint from Zhang Xiaojing and laid bare the grain beneath.

In Tanqi’s mind, two words distilled themselves: alone.

Zhang Xiaojing’s figure was deeply solitary. The livelier it was around him, the stronger that solitude grew. He moved through the most flourishing, most teeming place in all the human world, and yet it was as though he and his surroundings were set within two separate paintings, a hair’s breadth apart and never to be blended.

In a certain sense, he stood further from this dusty world than even the young master did.

Thinking so, without knowing it she let her head droop, and, leading the reins with her hands behind her, softly began to hum the Muhu song. The singing wound and lingered, never leaving the two of them. Low as it was, it was never once drowned out by the clamor without.

It was a tune the country folk of the Qishan region used to make merry as they drank the blessing-wine after a sacrifice to the gods; near to the vulgar though it was, it held a truth all its own. The young master had once said that the melody had been handed down from high antiquity: it could mirror the moon above and reach through to the first, original heart below—the truly elegant seeming vulgar, only the men of today did not know it.

Just now the bright moon hung high in mid-heaven, full and round and pure, shedding a cool, clear light. Tanqi believed the moon had already stirred in answer—only she did not know into whose first, original hearts it might reach.

Singing as she went, Tanqi suddenly noticed that as Zhang Xiaojing walked leading his reins, his thick fingers were lightly tapping the bronze ring on the bridle—exactly in time with the Muhu song. His movement was very furtive, as though he were ashamed to have it seen.

Tanqi gave a soft smile and did not let on, but went on humming. In quiet accord the two of them, one singing and one keeping the beat, passed so through the clamorous crowd. Zhang Xiaojing’s gait seemed to have eased a little.

It took the two of them a full quarter-hour to squeeze clear of the crowd. When Tanqi caught sight of the ward wall of Xingdao Ward, she felt a great weight lift, and could not help sighing: “If only the watchtowers were still working—at least they could tell us in advance where the way was clear.”

Ever since the Jing’an Bureau had been attacked, the whole watchtower system had ceased to work. In truth the great majority of the watchtowers were still in operation; it was only that, with no great watchtower to coordinate them from the center, they were no more than a scattering of separate towers, not to be kneaded into one.

Without the moment-by-moment updating of news from across Chang’an, the Jing’an Bureau’s people felt the inconvenience keenly.

At the thought, Tanqi looked back over her shoulder toward Guangde Ward, and a heavy worry welled up again in her eyes. She had chosen to go on to the Pingkang Quarter—she believed that the young master, in her place, would have chosen the same—but worry was a feeling that could not be controlled.

Zhang Xiaojing suddenly reined in his mount and turned to Tanqi with a grin: “You’ve reminded me—let me do a little conjuring trick for you.” Tanqi was startled, not knowing why he said it.

Zhang Xiaojing took a folded purple lantern out of the saddlebag beside his horse. He drew the lantern open again, lit it, then unfolded a long bamboo pole that had been folded in three and hoisted the lantern high. Tanqi was quite mystified. This gear was what the Jing’an Bureau’s field agents used to signal the watchtowers by night; but the great watchtower was dark now—what point was there in passing word with it?

Zhang Xiaojing raised the purple lantern and swung it up and down in a regular rhythm, now shrouding it, now lifting it high. Tanqi was not well versed in this lantern-language and did not know what he meant to say. But Zhang Xiaojing put a forefinger to his lips, hushed her, and told her to wait and watch.

Before long the watchtower of Xingdao Ward lit up a purple lantern that flashed several times, seeming to have received Zhang Xiaojing’s message. At once the watchtower of Kaihua Ward to the south lit a purple lantern too, flickering at much the same rate as Xingdao Ward’s.

Zhang Xiaojing went on swinging the lantern, and away in the distance the watchtowers of Guanglu Ward and Zhiye Ward answered too. Before long Anren, Fengle, Wuben, Chongyi… the watchtowers of the wards near and far all around woke one after another, their purple lamps flashing bright and dark, and soon linked into a single sheet, all echoing Zhang Xiaojing’s motions. The sight was for all the world like a Celestial Master conjuring away the baleful stars.

Zhang Xiaojing fixed the bamboo pole with its purple lantern into a strap at the back of his saddle, and only then said to Tanqi: “The watchtower system is working again now. It’s only that their center is no longer the great watchtower in Guangde Ward—it’s me.” So saying, he cocked the thumb of his left hand and tapped it against his own chest.

“I am, right now, the nerve-center of the Jing’an Bureau.”

Tanqi’s eyes went wide—this was more marvelous than any conjuring trick indeed. Why had he taken over the watchtowers so easily and become their highest-ranking commander?

Zhang Xiaojing mounted again; the horse’s body gave a shiver, setting the purple lantern hoisted high at its hindquarters trembling a few times.

“Don’t forget—at the hour of the Monkey, first half, Deputy Director Li granted me the authority of commissioning the watchtowers, and that order has never once been rescinded.”

***

Yao Runeng passed over a cup of water; Wen Ran took it and drank a shallow sip, finding the water too full of the smell of smoke and fire. Yao Runeng said apologetically: “Sorry—the few wells nearby are all mobbed with people; we’ll just have to wait a bit longer.” Wen Ran smiled ruefully: “It’s enough to be alive; how could I be choosy?”

After Gan Shoucheng had gone, they had nowhere to go and had to stay on in the apothecary. Outside it was still all bustle and disorder; even Cui Qi’s corpse there had been no time to lay out, and it rested for now on the door-plank beside them.

“Can I go home?” Wen Ran asked pitifully. From noon today onward not one good thing had befallen her; seized and hauled about with never a moment’s rest, she was worn out in spirit indeed. Yao Runeng made an apologetic gesture: “Sorry—no. Deputy Director Li had me shut you up, and there’s been no order to release you.” Then, afraid Wen Ran might take it amiss, he hastened to explain: “It’s not safe out there just now; you’re safest staying here.”

“Because it’s already burned here?” Wen Ran shot back.

“Er…” Yao Runeng, caught off guard, was stopped short. Wen Ran gave a snort of laughter, then suddenly noticed that the wound on Yao Runeng’s shoulder was only carelessly bound with a scrap of ragged cloth, all crooked, and she called him over to sit down. Bowing her head, she tore a strip of cloth from the lower hem of her skirt and bound the wound up again, carefully this time.

Wen Ran’s slender, onion-white fingers worked the cloth deftly, and Yao Runeng caught wafts of a faint fragrance rising to his nose, and quickly bowed his head. So it was by such a scent, he thought, that Commander Zhang had tracked this girl down. The fragrance seemed slight at first, yet it lingered long and would not fade—handy indeed for the government’s thief-catching later on.

Ah—how would Commander Zhang and Mistress Tanqi take the news that the Jing’an Bureau had been attacked? And how went the search for the Que-le Huo-duo?

As he thought this, it suddenly struck him that here was a fine opportunity, and he asked as if in passing: “You and Commander Zha—er, Commander Zhang Xiaojing—how are you to each other?”

Wen Ran, attending closely to the wound, answered softly: “He is my benefactor.”

“He saved you?”

A look of deep grief came over Wen Ran’s face: “Saved me—and more than that… For our Wen family, he threw his very life into the scale.” Yao Runeng started—so this was the reason for his death sentence? Had not Tanqi said it was for killing the county commandant?

With nothing to do just now, Wen Ran told the tale at leisure.

It turned out that Zhang Xiaojing and Wen Ran’s father, Wen Wuji, had been comrades-in-arms while soldiering in the Western Regions. Among the three soldiers who had survived the desperate defense of the beacon-fort in those days, Wen Wuji was one. He had once saved Zhang Xiaojing’s life, and lost a leg for it.

After the siege of the beacon-fort was lifted, Wen Wuji could no longer soldier and chose to leave the ranks. With his daughter and the Protectorate’s reward, he came to Chang’an and opened an incense shop, and lived well enough. Later, when Zhang Xiaojing became the buliang chief of Wannian County, the two old comrades, bound by a friendship sealed in life and death, looked out for each other all the more constantly.

Last October, just when Zhang Xiaojing happened to be away on business, the Wen Incense Shop suddenly received notice from the Forestry and Crafts Bureau that the court meant to build a guesthouse for the envoys from Little Balur, on a site chosen in Dunyi Ward. The price the Bureau named was extremely low; Wen Wuji naturally would not have it and stood firm against moving. Then, unlooked for, one night a gang of masked idle young toughs burst into the shop with heavy cudgels in hand, smashing and beating everything; when Wen Wuji came out to reason with them, he was beaten to death on the spot. Wen Ran too was nearly ravished, but thanks to her quick wits and stubborn resolve she spied a gap and fled.

Wen Ran had meant to go and report to the authorities, but ran straight into the county commandant leading a night patrol in person, who insisted flatly that she had broken the curfew and had her arrested. She wept and pleaded every way she could, but no one paid her any heed, and she was kept shut in a deep cell. Before long a deposition was passed in to her from outside, requiring her to confess that her father had colluded with bandits and been beaten to death in a falling-out over the spoils, and that the incense shop had been bought with stolen goods. If she would not put her mark to it, she was to be sold off into slavery.

When Wen Ran heard this she refused outright, whereupon several jailers came and pinned her down and forced her fingerprint onto the deposition. Utterly despairing in her heart, she had more than once thought of killing herself.

After a few days she was suddenly let out. Once free, Wen Ran made inquiries and learned that the world outside had been turned upside down. Zhang Xiaojing had come back to the capital, and on learning what had befallen the Wen Incense Shop had first all but torn the Bear Fire Gang up by the roots, and then—for what reason no one knew—killed the county commandant of Wannian, throwing the whole Wannian county yamen into an uproar. In the end he had actually seized Prince Yong and all but poked the trouble clear up into the heavens.

Just how Zhang Xiaojing had come to be tangled up with Prince Yong, and how he had been taken and sentenced to death, the twists and turns of it Wen Ran did not clearly know. She knew only that from then on the Wen Incense Shop was safe and sound, and no one came to trouble her. A frail woman, with no power to reach her benefactor, she could only keep a living-shrine to him at home and offer incense to it each day.

As she talked on, Wen Ran leaned against his arm and, of all things, fell asleep.

Yao Runeng did not move, but within him raged a tempest of waves. It was not only Zhang Xiaojing’s deeds that astonished him, but the black-hearted greed of those other men.

For one must understand that a county commandant did not lightly go out on a night patrol in person. That he should appear that very night showed plainly that he had long since been in league with the Forestry and Crafts Bureau and the Bear Fire Gang: the underworld’s cudgels and the government’s penal brush, plied together, to nail Wen Wuji down dead and swallow up his land. Zhang Xiaojing, he was sure, had seen it too, and that was why he had killed in his fury.

Yao Runeng had heard much of the dark places of official governance, but so ruthless and venomous a thing as this was the first he had known. A modestly well-off household broken and its people dead in a moment—and that was with Zhang Xiaojing throwing himself in to shield them; had it been some other family, the end would surely have been more wretched still. When Zhang Xiaojing said Chang’an was a beast that devoured men, it was no exaggeration at all.

At last he understood why, whenever Zhang Xiaojing so much as mentioned the court, his rancor ran so deep.

“Long do I sigh and wipe away my tears, grieving that the people’s life is beset with so many hardships.” A sigh of feeling came from one side; Yao Runeng turned his head to find Cen Shen leaning against a corridor-pillar, listening rapt as well.

The two lines he recited, Yao Runeng knew, meant sorrow and heartache. Cen Shen praised on: “This young lady’s account, with a little touching-up, would make a first-rate yuefu ballad—one that takes its theme from a real event and holds up the politics of the day to reproof.” He bowed his head, wanting to find a brush and set it down, only to discover that his poem-bag had long since burned away, and so had to rummage through the wooden pigeonholes of the apothecary to see whether there were paper and brush to be had.

Yao Runeng was a little bemused: “Can such a thing go into a poem too?”

Cen Shen waved a hand in indignation: “Why should it not? Most who write poems these days are all lovely diction, all flowery show, forever setting their poems off in some far country, and unwilling to look squarely at the shabby wrongs before their eyes. There ought to be someone to urge a new style—to write for the sake of the matter, not for the sake of the words.” Then he buried his head and went on rummaging.

Yao Runeng urged him, at a loss: “Sir, you were only held at the Jing’an Bureau on a temporary detention; if you wish to leave now, you may leave at any time.”

Cen Shen had first been held because he had obstructed Zhang Xiaojing’s investigation and was suspected of a connection to the Türk Wolf Guards. Now that his identity was cleared up, he could be released; and besides, even to keep him there was nowhere left to shut him up…

Cen Shen lifted his head from behind the counter, his tone aggrieved: “Leave? I can’t leave now. My horse and my poems are both gone; you have to make it good to me.”

“A mount can at least be reckoned in money… but how is one to repay a poem?”

“Mm, very simple—just let me come along with you.” Cen Shen wore the smug look of a man whose clever scheme had carried. “I’ve been watching all along: Mistress Wen’s affair, Cui Qi’s affair, your affair, that Zhang Xiaojing’s affair, and your Jing’an Bureau’s hunting of the Türks… You know a little poetry yourself, don’t you? Do you know what splendid material this is for a poet?”

Yao Runeng was rather taken aback—in this fellow’s eyes, were all these things merely stuff for poems? He shook his head: “Sorry, I know nothing of poetry; I only know a little of the rhymes.”

Hearing that he knew the rhymes, Cen Shen grew excited at once and cried that this was quite enough, they could have a bit of a talk. Yao Runeng smiled wryly again and again: he knew the rhymes of the characters only because the watchtowers’ passing of messages was founded on the Tang Rhymes, which had nothing whatever to do with composing poetry.

Cen Shen, far from losing interest, grew only more curious, and pestered him to explain just how one passed messages using the Tang Rhymes. Yao Runeng put a hand to his brow, regretting his loose tongue. He had Cen Shen push the window open; in the distance one could see the lanterns hung before the gate of the Cibei Temple. Facing this lantern, Yao Runeng gave a brief explanation of the principle by which the watchtowers signaled in rhyme-form, by drumbeat in the daytime and by lantern at night.

Cen Shen beat time in admiration: “To pass the rhymes by lantern and drum, and by the rhyme-classes to pass the words—superb! Who thought of this? A great talent indeed! It seems that hereafter I need not go about offering my verses everywhere; I have only to sound a single drum from on high, and my poems will spread to the eight directions and be known to the whole city!”

The corner of Yao Runeng’s mouth twitched; he barely mastered the urge to contradict, thinking, well, so long as you’re happy… Cen Shen faced the window and the lantern and began to gesture, muttering to himself—he was trying to translate his own verses into lantern-language.

Just then the main door was flung open with a bang, and in came a petty clerk in bright leather clothes. The clerk looked around and called loudly: “Any of the Jing’an Bureau’s people still here?”

Seeing the man’s face unfamiliar, Yao Runeng hesitantly raised his hand to show that he was one. The clerk said: “The Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau orders that all subordinate officials still able to move gather before the Cibei Temple; there is to be an address.” Yao Runeng was startled—had not Li Bi been taken captive? Could he have been rescued? The clerk glanced at him: “It’s the newly appointed Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau.” Then he hurried out of the shop to go and notify others.

Someone had taken over already, so soon? Yao Runeng felt a little uneasy about it. And yet, with Deputy Director Li carried off, his whereabouts unknown, there did indeed have to be someone to restore order as quickly as possible—how good it would be if that someone were Zhang Xiaojing; but that, alas, was quite out of the question.

He gently laid the sleeping Wen Ran flat on the matting and took his leave of Cen Shen. Cen Shen waved a hand and said, off you go, I’ll keep an eye on the girl for now, and then went on absorbed in his hunt for paper and brush.

The gate of the Cibei Temple was not far from the Jing’an Bureau, and before it lay a broad open square. The lantern-viewing sightseers had all been cleared away, and the monks had shut their gate fast; now several dozen people stood in the square, all of them survivors of the Jing’an Bureau still able to move, every face marked with grief.

Yao Runeng counted the number: only a third of what it had been before the thing happened. In other words, fully a hundred of his colleagues had died in this raid, and his heart ached at the thought. Acquaintances in the square, on seeing one another, wept before they could even clasp hands. Beyond rejoicing to have survived the disaster, there was nothing else to be said.

Before long a gong sounded, and from all four sides some twenty-odd soldiers came crowding in, each bearing a torch, lighting the square bright as day. An official paced up to the gate of the Cibei Temple and stood on the steps, looking down over the square. He was about forty, tall of build, his cheekbones high on both sides, so that they squeezed the bridge of the nose between them out to the front, as though it might leap from his face at any moment. On his chin was a fine beard that glistened with oil under the torchlight—plainly one he took pains to tend.

Yao Runeng noticed that the man wore a pale-green official robe, its silver belt set with nine gleaming bronze belt-plaques. This was the belt of the seventh official rank, one grade lower than Li Bi’s.

The gong sounded again, calling for the crowd’s attention. Holding a bronze seal, the official declared aloud to those below: “Be it known to you all, gentlemen: I am Ji Wen, Palace Censor and Commissioner of the Left Patrol. I now, by order of the Secretariat, reorganize the Jing’an Bureau. Each return to his post, and let there be no delay.”

This standing set the people in the square to murmuring among themselves. They all knew that the Jing’an Bureau’s backer was the Eastern Palace, and now the Secretariat Director had appointed a censor to take it over—an affair that sounded strange however one heard it.

Ji Wen had plainly come prepared. He nodded, and at once another official came forward carrying a thick scroll of documents in his hands. Unrolling it, the official read aloud, his voice ringing across the whole square:

“The Tang Liudian, scroll thirteen, ‘The Censorate: Palace Censors,’ records: Within the walls of the two capitals the duty is divided into the Left Patrol and the Right Patrol, each inspecting for unlawful matters within the circuit it patrols. That is to say: those demoted or sent into exile who tarry and hide and will not depart; and sorcery and false rumor, prowling by night, dice and gambling, and theft; wrongful and excessive litigation and imprisonment; and, among the various prefectures, malfeasance by the transport-officers, illicit trading, concealment and theft, and the levying of taxes contrary to the statutes—all matters of this kind they shall investigate and memorialize to the throne.

“And further! The Statutes on Officials: The Left Patrol has charge within the capital city, the Right Patrol without, to the bounds of the two prefectures of Yong and Luo. They change once a month; as the month draws to its close, they make their rounds of the Ministry of Justice, the Court of Judicial Review, the eastern and western convict-workshops, the Jinwu Guard, and the county jails.”

As the crabbed, tongue-twisting articles of the official code were read out one by one before them all, the Jing’an Bureau’s people gradually came to understand.

A Palace Censor held two commissions: Commissioner of the Left Patrol and Commissioner of the Right Patrol, with the power to oversee unlawful matters within the two capitals; and what the Jing’an Bureau administered was the threat-prevention of the western capital. The duties of the two overlapped—one might say they did the same work under different offices.

Whether by the letter of the law or in practice, there was nothing improper in a Commissioner of the Left Patrol taking over the Jing’an Bureau.

This Censor Ji neither leaned on the weight of office to force them down, nor borrowed the Secretariat Director’s great power to coerce them, but read out the official code before them all—showing him a man who scrupulously observed the statutes. Now that the host was a dragon-host without a head, and men’s hearts full of dread, there was need indeed of someone to clear up the wreckage. Besides, the censor held the Secretariat Director’s warrant—why set oneself against him?

The crowd’s hostility eased somewhat, and the murmuring gradually died away. Ji Wen stroked his beard and spoke again: “That the Jing’an Bureau should have been overtaken by bandits grieves me doubly. But the chief culprits are not yet bound, and stubborn foes remain; I ask that you all rein in your grief and hatred for now, keep the Son of Heaven in mind, first slay the bandit-chiefs, and only then make offering to the spirits of the fallen.”

The speech was handsomely made, at once pointing out the urgency of the case and hinting that the court would surely reward them richly. The surviving officials of the Jing’an Bureau, high and low, all cupped their hands and bowed, performing the salute of obeisance. This was the courtesy of a subordinate greeting his superior, an acknowledgment of him as the new Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau.

Seeing most of them won over, Ji Wen was hugely pleased and turned his head to murmur to the official who had just read out the code: “Gongfu, that stroke of yours looked clumsy but was cunning indeed—it really worked.” The official smiled: “Would I ever deceive you, Duangong? Strike while the iron is hot; say it as we agreed.”

An Attendant Censor was addressed off duty as “Duangong”; a Palace Censor, as “Vice-Duan.” The official had deliberately styled him a rank higher, and Ji Wen, hearing it, was greatly gratified. He took up the bronze seal at once: “Hear my order, all of you!”

This was the first order he gave upon assuming the office of Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau, and everyone fell quiet.

Ji Wen declared aloud: “The Jing’an Bureau was stormed by bandits; there must be a traitor within in league with them. To repel the foe without, one must first settle things within; the foremost task from here is to dig out this malignant tumor. As for his identity, I have already found it out—” He swept his gaze over the whole square, saw everyone staring fixedly at him, was well pleased with the effect, and spat out a name: “The Jing’an Bureau’s commander, Zhang Xiaojing! He is the traitor within, in league with the Pifu.”

This conclusion set those below in an uproar.

The smile on Ji Wen’s face turned cold: “Perhaps you do not know that Zhang Xiaojing was earlier sentenced to hang for killing his own immediate superior. As the saying goes, a bandit’s nature is hard to change; having done it once, he will hardly fail to do it a second time. The abduction of Wang Zhongsi’s daughter earlier—he had a hand in that too. And now the Jing’an Bureau has been attacked; it must again be he who led the wolf in at the door—pass my order to every ward-post and street-watch: take this man throughout the city, alive or dead, no matter which!”

Yuan Zai stood to one side, unhurriedly rolling the official code back up, a faint smile showing at the corner of his lips.

The bandits who had attacked the Jing’an Bureau, it was said, called themselves the “Pifu”—and did that not suit the standing of a low creature like Zhang Xiaojing perfectly?
