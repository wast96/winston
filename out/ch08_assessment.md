# Chapter Eight — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 153–176. Section 27 ("The Explosives Detonate in Water Far From
the Warship") and following. Chapter title: "The Plot to Assassinate the
Japanese Chieftain Shirakawa."*

**The chapter's central claim.** That the Hongkou Park bombing of 29 April 1932
— the single most consequential act of resistance violence in Shanghai in this
period, which killed a Japanese general and cost the Japanese minister to China
a leg — was **Wang Yaqiao's operation**, conceived by him, and executed through
the Korean independence movement because his own men could not get into the
park. Everything else in the chapter is arranged to make that claim land.

*Verdicts below are held pending the scholarship check now running; the
synopsis is complete and the questions it raises are marked.*

---

## What happens

### The attempt on the Izumo

Hu Amao's death has made the Iron and Blood corps famous; Shanghai sends
condolence money for his family, and the public response sharpens Wang's
appetite for something larger. He fixes on General Shirakawa Yoshinori,
commander of the Japanese forces.

He goes down to the 19th Route Army lines at Gaochangmiao on the Huangpu and
finds Yu Likui there, who after the Soong shooting returned to soldiering and
now commands a regiment under Cai Tingkai. Wang's opening proposal is
characteristically theatrical — take Shirakawa's head and hang it on the big
clock on the Bund. Yu Likui points out the difficulty: Shirakawa directs the
battle from the water, aboard the flagship *Izumo*, and never comes ashore.

So Wang proposes going under the ship. Yu Likui can supply the explosives and
waterproof them; what worries him is finding men who can swim several
kilometres at night under the guns of a fleet, carrying tens of catties of
charge, without making a sound. Wang is unbothered — half the Hatchet Gang grew
up on the Huangpu.

Three swimmers are chosen after trials: Li Dasheng, Tao Bailing and Ma Zhenguo.
On the night of **29 February 1932** they make repeated trips, placing more
than a dozen charges under the flagship, timed for eleven o'clock. With minutes
left, they have not come back: the wind and current have pulled several charges
loose and are carrying them downriver, and the three are swimming after them,
trying to push them back into place. The explosion comes in open water, some
distance from the ship.

The press account has Shirakawa addressing a conference of his commanders when
the blast knocks him off his feet, with no real damage to the fleet. Two of the
three swimmers do not come back.

### Nanjing is angry at the wrong people

Chiang's reaction is fury at the disruption to his negotiations — T.V. Soong is
in Nanjing talking to the Japanese minister alongside the foreign minister,
Eugene Chen. Soong's answer is drier and shrewder: an attack of this kind
tells Tokyo that taking Shanghai will mean fighting the population, which
strengthens rather than weakens the Chinese hand at the table.

Then the withdrawal, and the Songhu ceasefire. At a villa by Xuanwu Lake in
Nanjing a group of generals — Feng Yuxiang, Ji Hongchang, Fang Zhenwu, Chen
Mingshu — denounce Chiang for pulling Cai Tingkai and Jiang Guangnai out of
Shanghai, and read in the papers that Shirakawa intends a grand victory
celebration in the city on the Emperor's birthday.

### The generals commission a killing

In March 1932 Fang Zhenwu comes secretly to Shanghai carrying a letter from
Chen Mingshu, meets Wang at Xiaotaoyuan and at Broadway Mansions, and tells him
that killing Shirakawa is the united wish of the patriotic officers. Wang weeps
over the two men lost in the river and says the thing worth fearing is not
Japanese ferocity but Chiang's appeasement.

The obstacle is access: the Tenchōsetsu celebration will be held in Hongkou
Park, admission effectively limited to Japanese and Korean residents, and no
Hatchet Gang man can get through the gate. Which is why Wang turns to the
Koreans.

### The Korean partnership

Wang meets Kim Ku, and through him An Changho, chairman of the Korean
Independence Party. An Changho is wary — what he has heard of Wang Yaqiao is
that he is a gangster — and comes round when he understands that Wang wants
Shirakawa dead for taking his country rather than out of any private grievance.
Japan, as he puts it, is the enemy both sides hold in common.

Wang lays out the method he has been turning over: a timed bomb built into a
thermos flask, carried into the park amid the crowd of Japanese families and
Korean residents, and placed as near the platform as possible. The Koreans
adopt it, and add the cover — the flask to be carried in by a couple posing as
young marrieds, Yun Bong-gil among them.

### 29 April 1932

The reports Wang reads: at least eight badly wounded and carried to the army
hospital in Hongkou; General Shirakawa blown off the platform; the minister to
China, Shigemitsu Mamoru, one leg destroyed but conscious and expected to live;
and the head of the Japanese commercial association dead of his wounds. Wang
picks his wife up off the floor and spins her round.

Kim Ku comes to him afterward with blood dried on his white sleeve, and is
almost apologetic about it, saying the Koreans should be thanking Wang for the
chance rather than the other way round. Wang, alarmed, presses him to leave
Shanghai at once, since a Japanese general and a Japanese minister down in one
morning will bring a reckoning on every Korean in the city. Kim Ku refuses to
save himself ahead of a party decision, and Wang — struck by a discipline his
own loose organization has never had — walks him to An Changho instead.

The chapter closes on Kim Ku's public declaration of responsibility, with its
account of handing Yun Bong-gil the bomb, the two of them holding hands and
agreeing to meet in the next world, and his own refusal to give up the fight
until Korea is free.

## The questions this raises

1. **Did Wang Yaqiao originate or direct the Hongkou operation?** This is the
   whole chapter. The documented account credits Kim Ku and the Korean
   Patriotic Corps, and the bombs are usually traced to a Korean officer
   serving in the Chinese army with arsenal help — not to Wang Yaqiao.
2. **Was Shirakawa aboard the *Izumo*?** He commanded the Shanghai Expeditionary
   Army — an army officer. The *Izumo* was the navy's flagship. The premise of
   the entire underwater attack may rest on putting the wrong service's
   commander on the wrong ship.
3. **The 29 February 1932 underwater explosion** — is there any record of it?
4. **Was Yu Likui a regimental commander in the 19th Route Army?** Earlier
   research had him raising a separate volunteer death-squad force that fought
   alongside it, which is not the same thing.
5. **The dead Japanese official.** The book names the head of the Japanese
   commercial association; the man who died at Hongkou was Kawabata Sadatsugu,
   head of the Japanese Residents' Association. The name as printed does not
   match.
6. **Shirakawa "blown off the platform"** — he was mortally wounded and died
   some weeks later, which the chapter's triumphant newspaper account does not
   yet know.
