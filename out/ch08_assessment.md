# Chapter Eight — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 153–176. Section 27 ("The Explosives Detonate in Water Far From
the Warship") and following. Chapter title: "The Plot to Assassinate the
Japanese Chieftain Shirakawa."*

---

## Verdict first

The chapter's central claim — that the **Hongkou Park bombing of 29 April
1932** was Wang Yaqiao's operation, run through the Korean independence
movement — **is not documented history, and its provenance can be traced.**

It originates in a **1983 article by Wang Yaqiao's younger brother, Wang
Shuqiao**, which was the first text to assert that the 19th Route Army, Wang
Yaqiao and the Koreans jointly planned the bombing. Everything downstream,
including Baidu Baike and this book, descends from that memoir.

It is rejected where it can be checked:

- Wang Yaqiao appears in **no Korean source** — not the national encyclopedia
  entries for Yun Bong-gil or Kim Ku, not the Korean Patriotic Corps
  literature, not the Kim Ku Museum's scholarly materials. For a claim that he
  conceived and financed the operation, silence across the whole of Korean
  historiography is close to dispositive.
- He appears in no Japanese or English account.
- **Chinese Wikipedia's own article carries the claim with a caveat** that no
  solid historical source supports it, and notes that scholars differ, some
  holding that Wang was not involved at all. The Chinese scholar Mu Tao is
  cited for that view.

**The diagnostic marker is the thermos flask.** The book's method is a *timed*
bomb concealed in a vacuum flask and left near the platform. The documented
weapon was a water-flask-shaped bomb **thrown** by Yun Bong-gil after the
gun salute, during the anthem. Korean sources are unanimous. Any version
featuring a timed flask bomb is downstream of the rejected memoir — which
makes this chapter's own set piece the tell.

**Who really made the bombs.** Not Wang Yaqiao. **Kim Hong-il**, a Korean
serving as a lieutenant colonel in the Chinese Nationalist army — he appears in
Kim Ku's memoir under his Chinese alias Wang Xiong — arranged manufacture at
the **Shanghai Arsenal**, authorized by its chief Song Shibiao and built by a
Chinese technician, **Wang Boxiu**. Two devices: a lunchbox and a water flask.
Yun threw the flask; the lunchbox was for his own suicide and was never
detonated. So there is a real Chinese state connection — it simply runs through
the Nationalist arsenal, not the Hatchet Gang.

**An Changho is contradicted outright.** The book has him deliberating with
Wang before the operation. He was **arrested by the Japanese in the French
Concession round-up on 29 April 1932, after the bombing**, tried in Seoul and
given four years.

### But there is a real Wang Yaqiao–Korean connection, and it is more interesting

In late 1931, in the French Concession, Korean, Chinese and Japanese anarchists
founded the **Anti-Japanese National Salvation League**. On the Korean side, Yi
Hoe-yeong, Jeong Hwa-am and Baek Jeong-gi; on the Chinese side, **Wang Yaqiao**
and Hua Junshi. Korean academic sources state plainly that **Wang was
responsible for procuring weapons and finance.** Its action arm carried out the
attempt on the Japanese minister Ariyoshi Akira on **17 March 1933**.

So Wang did work with Korean revolutionaries, did supply them with money and
guns, and did help mount an assassination — just a *different* faction, a
*different* target and a *later* date. Kim Ku's Patriotic Corps and these
anarchists were rivals. Jeong Hwa-am's memoirs describe Wang as close to the
southwestern politicians, hostile to Chiang's "pacify internally first" line,
and cultivating the Korean anarchists partly for use **against Chiang** — very
nearly the reverse of the patriotic-alliance story this chapter tells.

The book has taken a real relationship with one Korean faction and transplanted
it onto the far more famous operation of another. That is the same move as
chapter seven's relocated monk murder, executed on people instead of events.

## What happens

### The attempt on the Izumo

Hu Amao's death has made the Iron and Blood corps famous. Wang fixes on General
Shirakawa Yoshinori and goes down to the 19th Route Army lines at Gaochangmiao,
where he finds Yu Likui, now commanding a regiment. His opening idea is to take
Shirakawa's head and hang it on the Bund clock; Yu Likui points out that
Shirakawa never comes ashore, directing from the flagship *Izumo*.

So Wang proposes going under the ship. Three swimmers are chosen after trials —
Li Dasheng, Tao Bailing, Ma Zhenguo — and on the night the charges are placed
and timed for eleven. With minutes to go the wind and current have pulled
several charges loose and the three are swimming after them, trying to push
them back. The explosion comes in open water. Two of the three do not return.

Nanjing's reaction is fury at the disruption to negotiations; T.V. Soong, drier,
observes that it tells Tokyo that taking Shanghai means fighting the
population, which helps rather than hurts the Chinese position.

### The generals commission a killing

After the withdrawal and the ceasefire, generals gather by Xuanwu Lake — Feng
Yuxiang, Ji Hongchang, Fang Zhenwu, Chen Mingshu — and denounce Chiang. In
March 1932 Fang Zhenwu comes to Shanghai with a letter from Chen Mingshu and
tells Wang that killing Shirakawa is the united wish of the patriotic officers.

The obstacle is access to the Tenchōsetsu celebration in Hongkou Park, so Wang
turns to the Koreans: Kim Ku, then An Changho. Wang proposes the timed flask
bomb; the Koreans adopt it and add the cover of a couple posing as young
marrieds.

### 29 April 1932

The reports: eight badly wounded, Shirakawa blown off the platform, Shigemitsu
one leg gone, the head of the Japanese commercial association dead. Kim Ku
comes to Wang afterward with blood on his sleeve, refuses to flee ahead of a
party decision, and Wang — struck by a discipline his own organization lacks —
walks him to An Changho. The chapter closes on Kim Ku's public declaration of
responsibility.

## Verdicts in detail

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| The Hongkou Park bombing itself | **CORROBORATED** in full |
| Wang Yaqiao behind it | **UNCORROBORATED**, traceable to a 1983 family memoir, disputed within Chinese scholarship |
| The timed thermos-flask bomb | **CONTRADICTED** — the real device was thrown |
| An Changho planning it with Wang | **CONTRADICTED** — arrested the day of the bombing |
| Wang's Korean connection generally | **CORROBORATED** — but to the anarchists, not Kim Ku |
| The underwater attack on the *Izumo* | **CORROBORATED as an event**, attribution unverified — see below |
| Shirakawa aboard the *Izumo* | **CONTRADICTED** — see below |
| The dead Japanese official's name | **CONTRADICTED** |
| 19th Route Army command titles | **check** — see below |

### The Izumo attack really happened

This is where the book earns credit. Japanese naval sources record that **on the
night of 1 March 1932 two men disguised as fishermen brought a boat alongside
the *Izumo* and tried to fix a mine to her hull, and that the strong current at
the Yangtze mouth defeated them.** Event, date and failure all match the
chapter's set piece almost exactly — the book's 29 February is a day or so out,
or is counting the night that runs into 1 March.

What Japanese sources do not do is name Wang Yaqiao. And the Chinese
embellishments — forty sealed oil drums, a naval escort, shock damage to the
ship — are not in them.

**But the premise is wrong.** Shirakawa commanded the **Shanghai Expeditionary
Army**; he was a soldier, and the *Izumo* was the **navy's** flagship, flying
Vice Admiral Nomura's flag. The chapter's whole rationale — that Shirakawa must
be reached underwater because he lives aboard the flagship — puts the wrong
service's commander on the wrong ship.

### The casualties

- **Kawabata Sadatsugu**, chairman of the Japanese Residents' Association, was
  the **only fatality on the day** — the book calls him head of the commercial
  association, which is not his post.
- **Shirakawa** was mortally wounded and died on **26 May 1932**, nearly a month
  later. The chapter's triumphant newspaper does not know this yet, which is
  fair enough, but the book never returns to it.
- **Nomura** lost his right eye; **Ueda Kenkichi** lost his left leg;
  **Shigemitsu** lost a leg and signed the ceasefire six days later.
- Wound counts conflict: Japanese sources give Shirakawa 108 wounds, Chinese
  sources uniformly 204 fragments. The higher figure appears to be Chinese-side
  only.

### A correction the book could have used

There is no documented Japanese plot against Shirakawa — but there is a real
and better story in the same place. **Shirakawa's ceasefire order of 3 March
1932 was itself an act against his own army's hardliners**: he issued it on the
strength of the Emperor's expressed trust, refused the General Staff's pursuit
orders, and the Army was enraged. A book looking for tension around Shirakawa
had a documented one available and used an invented one instead.

### Command titles

Jiang Guangnai was **commander-in-chief** of the 19th Route Army; Cai Tingkai
was **army commander** under him; Dai Ji was Songhu Garrison Commander. Cai only
became commander-in-chief in **June 1932**, when Jiang moved to Fujian. Where
the chapter makes Cai the overall commander during the winter fighting, it is
describing the later arrangement.

## For the final map

Chapter eight is the book's characteristic operation performed on people rather
than events. Chapter seven relocated a real conspiracy in space and time;
chapter eight relocates a real relationship from one Korean faction to another,
so that Wang Yaqiao stands behind the most famous act of anti-Japanese violence
of the period instead of the less famous one he actually helped arm.

And it is worth saying what the checking also shows: **the book is not
uniformly unreliable.** The Izumo mine attempt is real and correctly described.
Chang Yuqing's collaboration is real. The 19th Route Army material and the
ceasefire are sound. The failures cluster with precision around one thing —
claims that make Wang Yaqiao the hidden author of other people's operations.
