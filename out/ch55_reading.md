## Ōtori Keisuke

Now to another matter.

It was the tenth of the fourth month of Keiō 4 (the first year of Meiji).

In the small hours, at the hour they reckoned the second, so that by rights it was already the eleventh.

From the gate of a hatamoto mansion in Surugadai, black shapes were disgorged.

Three of them.

One a manservant, shouldering a wicker trunk. One a stalwart in cotton clothes.

One the master of the hatamoto house, thirty-six or thirty-seven, in a crested coat of black habutae silk, hakama of Sendai-hira, a Nirayama war-hat on his head, holding an umbrella high by its shaft.

The rain that had set in the evening before would not let up.

"Kimura (Ryūkichi), a poor day to take the field,"

{j} said the hatamoto, with a rueful smile.

After that he said nothing for a time, and crossed the Shōheibashi. Out through Asakusa Fukiya-machi, past the Ōkawabashi, and so at last to the Ogura-an in Koume village, Mukōjima.

"This was to be about where the assembly-point is, they said. Kimura, go ask at that tofu-shop over there,"

A tofu-maker would be up already.

Kimura, who seemed to be a pupil of his, ran off. But he was back at once.

"They say they don't know,"

"Strange. Four or five hundred men in Western dress are to gather here. The neighborhood can hardly not know of it. Go wake the watch-house,"

The hatamoto waited in the rain.

Fair-skinned, broad of brow, the line of his nose clean and straight, a dashing handsome man.

In the watch-house, four or five fellows in shogunal infantry dress lay sleeping higgledy-piggledy.

Roused by Kimura, they sprang up with a start.

"Ah, we were waiting for you. We only just dozed off,"

"The teacher is waiting outside,"

"Is that so,"

The infantrymen came out and gave the man they called "teacher" a French-style salute.

"Hm,"

The teacher drew in his chin.

"I'll show you the way. It's the Hōon-ji, just there,"

They set off walking through the rain.

The "teacher" was Ōtori Keisuke, the shogunate's infantry commander (later he served the new government as head of the College of Engineering, head of the Peers' School, envoy extraordinary and minister plenipotentiary to the Qing empire, privy councillor, baron; died Meiji 44, aged eighty).

Like many of the shogunal men who fought against the government at the Restoration, he was no hereditary hatamoto.

He was the son of a village doctor of Akō in Banshū.

He studied Dutch learning at the Ogata Kōan academy in Osaka, took a particular interest in the Dutch-style army, and while turning out translations on military organization, tactics, drill, and fortification came to the shogunate's notice; two years before, in Keiō 2, he was raised to direct shogunal vassalage. When Napoleon III, emperor of the French and the shogunate's backer, sent over a military mission of some twenty-odd officers of foot, horse, artillery, and engineers, Ōtori took their training.

In time Ōtori was made the shogunate's infantry commander, and came to command the French-drilled foot.

In time the shogunate crumbled.

"Absurd,"

{j} thought Ōtori and the other officers of the French-modeled shogunal army, more keenly than anyone. They knew, better than anyone, that in point of equipment the shogunate's modern army and navy could stand fully against Satsuma and Chōshū.

It was only natural that Matsudaira Tarō of the army and Enomoto Takeaki of the navy opposed the surrender of Edo to the last.

These commanders of land and sea secretly planned to make a stand within Edo; but the former shogun Yoshinobu, then in penitent seclusion at Ueno, heard of it, summoned Matsudaira and the rest, and,

──Your resort to arms is the same as laying a naked blade to my own head.

{j} so he admonished them, and the plan for a stand, at least, was given up.

So they resolved instead to break out of Edo just before the surrender, and did so.

It was for this that Ōtori Keisuke had left his Surugadai mansion and come to the secret mustering-place at Mukōjima.

To give the date once more: this day was the eleventh of the fourth month.

The sun was not yet up.

Come morning, come noon, Edo Castle was to be handed over to the Imperial army's receiving officers. It was just before this that the shogunal infantry corps was to break out of Edo in force.

At the Hōon-ji were gathered thirty or forty officers (the drill-masters) and four or five hundred infantry.

Ōtori, as infantry commander, naturally became their commanding officer. The numbers under him would swell as they marched.

At dawn they set out from Mukōjima.

Marching over the miry roads, they made for Ichikawa (now the city of Ichikawa, Chiba). Other former shogunal men, and retainers of Aizu and Kuwana, were to be mustered there, and the plan was to join with them.

When they reached the Ichikawa ferry, the former shogunal man Ogasawara Shintarō had come to meet them with boats made ready.

On the boat, Ogasawara, all worked up, said close to Ōtori's ear,

"Lord Hijikata Toshizō of the Shinsengumi is here too,"

{j} he said.

"Oh?"

{j} said Ōtori, but with the air of a man taking pains to keep his face blank.

Ogasawara did not notice.

"That man — I've only seen him from a distance, but he's come through the Kyoto troubles, Toba-Fushimi, no end of flashing blades and hails of shot, and it shows: the way his eyes move, his bearing, there seems to be something in him beyond the common run,"

"………"

Ōtori bore Toshizō no goodwill.

It was a trifle that had soured his feeling. When Toshizō came back from Nagareyama to Edo, the truth is that the former shogunal men in the castle, one and all,

──So he's come back again.

{j} thought.

Among them, Katsu Kaishū and Ōkubo Ichiō above all, being of the submit-and-surrender party, least liked having the Shinsengumi inside Edo Castle, holding it an obstacle to the peace talks with the Imperial army. It was for just this reason that they had handed over such large war-chests for the Kōshū sortie and the Nagareyama muster.

The break-out-and-fight party too, being mostly officers of the Western-style shogunal army, were of a different grain from this band of swordsmen. In Kyoto the Shinsengumi had cut down far too many men. There was an eeriness about them, as of men with a taste for killing.

When Toshizō came back into the castle, Ōtori, quite formally,

──I hear Kondō has been taken. A sorry business.

{j} he said to Toshizō.

Toshizō only fixed Ōtori with a hard stare and said nothing.

Ōtori was nettled. Nettled, and yet he felt a strange pressure from the man.

Even in the castle Toshizō was loath to speak of Kondō. To think of the too-cruel end of the sworn friend with whom, for so long, of one body and one mind, he had literally cut his way through wind and storm left him no wish to make it a topic for strangers.

Of such things Ōtori knew nothing.

(A disagreeable fellow.)

{j} he thought.

On the boat —

Ogasawara Shintarō, for his part, understood nothing of Ōtori's feeling.

"The infantry and such, they say, 'So that's the Demon Hijikata of the Shinsengumi,' and he's wildly popular. His joining us has raised the men's spirits. A hero of the hour, surely, is what one must call him,"

"He's a fencing-tradesman,"

{j} Ōtori spat out. At his tone Ogasawara Shintarō looked at him in astonishment and fell silent.

The truth was that among the shogunal men mustered at Ichikawa there had been some talk of whether Ōtori or Hijikata should be their general. Either way, if Ōtori bore Hijikata no goodwill, this might in time make trouble, Ogasawara thought.

***

Entering the post-town of Ichikawa, he found shogunal men broken out of Edo, samurai of the various domains, infantry, over a thousand in all, occupying the inns and temples in enormous uproar.

Ōtori Keisuke ordered the noon meal for the unit he had led, then left it and, guided by Ogasawara Shintarō, went into a temple.

"This is the headquarters,"

{j} said Ogasawara.

Stepping into the main hall, he met a stifling fug of bodies. The chief men sat ranged in a row, backs to the altar-dais, in order of rank.

The highest in rank of the assembly was Hijikata Toshizō, head of the Ōgobangumi.

In crested dress, he sat sullen in the seat of honor, making an atmosphere apart from the rest. He did not join in the talk and laughter.

The faces of the assembly were these:

Shogunal retainers:

Hijikata Toshizō, Yoshizawa Yūshirō, Kosuge Tatsunosuke, Yamase Shiba, Amano Denshirō, Suzuki Bannosuke;

Aizu retainers:

Kakizawa Yūki, Amazawa Seinoshin, Akizuki Tonosuke, a certain Matsui, a certain Kudō;

Kuwana retainers:

Tatsumi Kanzaburō, Sugiura Hideto, Baba Sankurō.

Such were they.

The shogunal retainer Amano Denshirō was an old acquaintance of Ōtori's, and,

"Ah, we've been waiting. Please,"

{j} he made to seat Ōtori above Hijikata Toshizō. His rank being somewhat the higher, it was natural enough; but Ōtori, out of courtesy, made a show of hanging back.

Toshizō looked at Ōtori.

"Please,"

{j} he said, low. As if drawn in, Ōtori sat down in the place shown him. Once seated, he felt the displeasure of a man who had been directed to it by Toshizō.

A council of war began.

The march on Utsunomiya was already fixed as settled policy.

"Ōtori,"

{j} said Amano Denshirō.

"What's gathered now at Ichikawa is the Ōtemae battalion, seven hundred men; the 7th Regiment, 350; the Kuwana retainers, two hundred; the pioneer troops, two hundred; and counting in the men you brought, it comes to over two thousand. And two field-guns besides,"

"You have guns?"

Ōtori showed interest because it was chiefly the French artillery branch that he had studied.

"At all events, in strength and numbers we are not far short of the force under the Imperial army's Tōsandō commander. Only, there is no one to lead it,"

"There is Hijikata,"

{j} said Ōtori, meaning nothing of it. But at his side Hijikata himself sat curtly silent.

"That was proposed, too. He's the only one among us who has commanded in a real fight. But Hijikata firmly declines,"

"On what grounds?"

"I,"

{j} said Toshizō sourly,

"was beaten at Fushimi,"

"No, there the whole shogunal army was beaten. It was not you alone who lost,"

"I say we were beaten by Western guns. It is you, Ōtori, who have studied them, who should command this army,"

"With respect,"

{j} Ōtori said, looking round the assembly,

"I have never taken the field: that is the first thing I lack. Next, the Ōtemae battalion I know well, having trained it myself; but of the other units and men I know nothing at all. So I decline the supreme command,"

"No — before ever you arrived, we had settled here to name you. Even as we talk the hour slips by. I must ask you to consent,"

{j} said Amano Denshirō.

With the air of a man who had no choice, Ōtori Keisuke accepted.

Toshizō became second in command, and took charge of the sword-and-spear men outside the Western-style corps. Each of them, to be sure, had been issued a rifle apiece.

The order of march was fixed, and at once they began the advance on Utsunomiya.

Toshizō, in a French officer's uniform, on horseback.

He rode at the head of the main body, his horse abreast of Ōtori Keisuke, who was dressed the same.

On the twelfth, at Matsudo, about fifty country-samurai and peasant-soldiers, led by a warrior in full armor, joined them.

On the fifteenth, at Morokawa post-station, the shogunal retainers Katō Heinai, Miyake Daigaku, Makino Kazue, and Amano Kaga joined, leading demesne troops, and the array swelled larger still.

On the sixteenth the first battalion of the vanguard (with two guns attached) engaged a small Imperial detachment at Oyama (now the city of Oyama, Tochigi), routed it, and took one cannon.

On the seventeenth, again toward Oyama, the main body clashed with about two hundred Imperial troops, and the spoils were two guns, two horses, old-model Gewehr muskets, and the rest.

The enemy of those two days was not the modern-equipped troops of the three domains of Satsuma, Chōshū, and Tosa, but, Imperial all the same, the old-equipped and moreover half-hearted men of such domains as Hikone and Kasama.

It was, at all events, a force sweeping all before it, one might say.

That day's noon meal they took at Oyama post-station.

A town of three thousand, the finest post-station in Shimotsuke.

Ōtori, Toshizō, and the officers under them were resting at the honjin when all at once there was a bustle at the gate: villagers came thronging up, hauling in sake-casks one after another and cooking red rice to celebrate the victory.

Ōtori, hugely pleased, had the assembly-bugle blown to gather near the honjin the units scattered on every side, knocked in the heads of the sake-casks, and,

"Today is the festival day of the Tōshōgū. That we should win a victory this very day, and unlooked-for, must be a sign from the god that the house of Tokugawa will surely be restored,"

{j} and mightily fired their spirits. In no time every inn in Oyama was full of the victorious soldiery, the serving-women turned out in force to wait on them, and the post-town rang from broad noon with such music and song it seemed fit to split.

(So this is the French way.)

So thought Toshizō, listening hard to the music and song that carried even to the inner rooms of the honjin.

"Ōtori, do you mean to stay the night in this town?"

"That is my intention,"

Ōtori was well pleased with himself. He had not yet passed through gunfire, but it seemed he had come to think war as easy a thing as this.

"I want to rest the men here, raise their spirits high, and push on to Utsunomiya,"

"Bad idea,"

{j} Toshizō began to laugh.

"Carry on this giddy and the Imperial troops on every side have long since got wind of it. If they mount a night attack tonight, we'll be running off with our shamisen under our arms,"

"………"

"Besides, this town is paddy on all four sides and hard to hold. Two ri north of here up the Mibu highway is a little village, Iizuka. The Sanpai River and the Sugata River enclose it and make a natural moat, so I'd say the whole army had best camp there,"

"Well, now,"

Ōtori, being from Banshū, was in the dark about the military lay of the Kantō. And for all that he was the general, the man left every scouting of the ground to others and would go to see nothing himself.

"That's one plan, true enough; but we may take it the enemy has already reached the Iizuka country,"

"If they have, then this Oyama is in all the more danger. Well, no matter. I'll go and scout while I see to the billeting,"

Toshizō went down into the honjin garden, gathered two hundred of the Western-equipped Denshūtai, put a single gun at their head, and set out. With every quarter to be reckoned enemy ground, the scouting perforce became a reconnaissance in force.

But just as Toshizō's scouting party was leaving Oyama, gunfire broke out suddenly to the east, and some three hundred Imperial troops (Hikone men) came attacking from the direction of Yūki.

(Here they come.)

{j} and Toshizō wheeled his horse's head about,

"Follow me,"

{j} and set off at a gallop up the town's central road. In a mass the Denshūtai ran after him.

Several shells fell within the town.

Just as Toshizō had foreseen, within the town was an uproar past all looking at: serving-women dashing out into the road in nothing but their long under-shifts and running about lost, drunken soldiers bolting into the mulberry fields with their rifles forgotten — a scene to make one think the rout of the Heike in the war-tales must have looked much like this.

Coming out at the edge of the town, Toshizō leapt from his horse and, just as he had learned from the Western drill-books, ordered his riflemen to deploy in open order and had them fire hard on the old-style Hikone force coming straight up the highway.

Presently the gun was brought forward.

With each round it fired he advanced his skirmishers, and before long he ran up beside a half-company under a man named Sakuma Teiji,

"That oak wood,"

{j} he said, pointing to a wood about a chō to the southeast,

"get round behind that wood and close on the enemy from the far side,"

{j} and with that order flung out, he himself took six men of the old Shinsengumi headed by Saitō Hajime, went into the mulberry field on the left, and, threading through the mulberries, came out on the enemy's flank.

Saitō and the rest, too, carried rifles.

Firing and running, running and firing, when at last they had come within ten ken of the enemy, Toshizō raised his naked blade,

"Charge in,"

{j} and sprang in among the enemy on the road.

He cut the first man down with a diagonal stroke from the right, raised the point a little to run through the man behind him, and, drawing the blade back to himself, in the same motion swept it across the belly of the man beside him.

Toshizō cut down three, Saitō as many, and Nomura Risaburō two.

Without losing the moment the Denshūtai charged, and the Hikone men, thrown into confusion, broke and fled.

The corpses the enemy left behind numbered twenty-four or twenty-five; the weapons, three French mountain-guns and nine Mito-made Japanese guns.
