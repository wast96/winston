## Chapter 55

Half past six in the morning.

Mei Yingzi went first to make a telephone call. Coming back, she told me she would go out to reconnoiter first, and would telephone later, when she had a fixed place, to call me. She divided with me the little money she had, to pay the reckoning when I left, and then hurried off.

Now I was left alone. The room was utterly still, while outside it was noisy beyond compare; a flower-girl and a newspaper boy called at the door, and I had them called in and bought a good many papers.

Every paper carried news of Bai Ping, much of a muchness, roughly the same as yesterday's evening edition; but today a few of the papers had something on the search of Bai Ping's lodgings.

"— Bai Ping lodged on Route Prosper Paris. The Japanese military, together with the police authorities, made a search of it at eleven yesterday morning, but found nothing; the maidservant too was taken for questioning and is still held in custody."

Not detailed, but at least it told me something of Ah Mei's whereabouts. On the one hand I thought Ah Mei surely was no confederate and had nothing she could tell; on the other I felt that perhaps Ah Mei knew a little something, and that once she was taken for a confederate, she surely could not come back alive. A newer unease rose again in my heart.

Carrying this unease, I read idly through the papers I had bought. By now the weather seemed to have cleared, and sunlight came slanting in at the window. I wanted to see the view outside, so I pushed open the little window: below was a small court, and opposite a tall building, and the sunlight that had just slanted in was thrown off from that tall building. This little court was damp and dark, as though it could never in its life get the full shining of the sun; someone was making water down in it. Next door was a small court too, but a wall shut it off, and one could not see into it; besides these there was only a small patch of sky, where blue-white clouds, flashing golden light, drove across, one after another. Such an outward scene could of course lift nothing in me, and in an instant I had a pressing longing to wander in the great open country — how broad the sky over there, how bountiful the sun! But I could not enjoy it; I had to keep to this cell of a room. And so I lay down on the bed again. I read the papers once more; I read through every telegram, every item of news, read through the supplements and the advertisements too. Among the advertisements were many marriage announcements, and I seemed to want, on purpose, to see whether anyone I knew had lately married; reading one after another, suddenly a line of words leapt to my eye and startled me:

MARRIAGE ANNOUNCEMENT OF STEPHEN AND BAI PING

We two respectfully appoint ten o'clock in the morning of the tenth of April for our wedding at the Catholic cathedral of Xujiahui, Shanghai. Relatives and friends will not receive separate cards. Gifts respectfully declined.

I supposed all along that I had read it wrong; I rubbed my eyes and read it five or six times over — black on white, plain before me. I thought today must be the tenth of April, and that I ought to hurry to the wedding and offer them my congratulations. But suddenly I thought — hadn't Stephen a wife? And how noble and refined his wife was. How could Stephen be so absurd? And Bai Ping was strange too: she plainly knew Mrs. Stephen, and yet, without consulting me beforehand, married thus by announcement in the papers. But I ought certainly to go to the wedding. I was just rising when suddenly there came a burst of laughter; I started, turned to look, and there on the sofa sat Mrs. Stephen. Amazed, I sprang down from the bed and said:

"It's you — when did you come?"

"I've only just come."

I saw her in a black overcoat, a snow-white scarf showing at the collar, a brooch on the scarf, a round thing in its middle, like... like the earring Cishan gave Mei Yingzi. Just so — perhaps it had even been reset from that one; but if it had been reset, the faded gilt ought to have been regilded, and it was as before, the character for "long life" on it still quite clear. I wanted to ask, but dared not. Somehow, all at once, I felt that perhaps she did not yet know of Stephen and Bai Ping's marriage; I ought not — at least not now — to let her know, and the paper on the bed — I feared she would see it, and, as if tidying the papers, I folded it up; but...

"Is that today's paper?" she asked.

"I think — I think it is."

"Have you seen the news of their marriage?"

"They? Who?"

"Stephen and Bai Ping."

"Truly?" I said. "They're to be married?"

"Isn't it fine?" she said, smiling. "That day at my house I saw how much Stephen liked Bai Ping."

I saw in her not the least jealousy or distress, and it seemed very strange to me. I said:

"Married! Oh — how can it be?"

"How so?"

"Isn't he still your husband?"

"We — we were only ever play-acting," she said, her smile a little far-off, as if she felt it all very empty. "Just playing our parts for the time of the war."

"But…"

"Now the war is over, and of course we've come off the stage."

"The war is over?"

"The enemy have surrendered unconditionally — didn't you know?"

"These papers, look," I said, "full of nothing but marriage announcements, and not even so great a piece of news!"

"How many days have you been asleep? I won't hide it from you — this is no longer stuff for the newspapers. Perhaps it's in the history textbooks by now."

"I don't understand!" I said, thinking to myself: could a single day in Cishan's boat have cut me off from the world so utterly as this?

"You don't understand?" she laughed. "The war is over, the world at peace, everyone marrying who will marry and going home who will go home. And you? Still holding to your single life?"

"Single — but with no 'ism' about it," I said. "Ah, are you going to their wedding too?"

"Too late," she said. "The bride and groom, I think, will soon be back."

"The bride and groom are here!" someone suddenly cried outside, and then pipes and flutes, drums and reeds, all sounded together.

"The bride and groom are here!" someone was crying outside.

I woke; outside there was still someone calling:

"The bride and groom are here!"

Outside the door were the sounds of music, of footsteps, of voices... And within the room, where was Mrs. Stephen? Where was the sofa? The paper, at my side — where was the marriage announcement of Stephen and Bai Ping?

"Telephone for Number 203," someone was calling.

Then someone knocked at the door:

"Telephone for Number 203."

I knew this was Mei Yingzi's call, and hurried down and picked up the receiver, and said:

"Who is it?"

"It's Third Sister," came Mei Yingzi's voice. "I've registered you with Dr. Philip — come at once."

The music was loud, the voices a jumble, but happily I need not say much; I hung up the receiver, and there was still someone calling:

"The bride and groom are here."

The doorway and the hall beside it were packed with people, and I went over too; through the crowd I saw the bride and groom come in.

The groom was a very tall, thin young man, a little stoop-shouldered, in a blue gown and black jacket, of no vulgar countenance. The bride was a plump young woman, her face round, her eyes round, of middling height, but too fat about the waist, and a wedding gown that was not becoming only made her look the more bloated.

"Suppose that were Stephen and Bai Ping... suppose that were Stephen and Bai Ping..." Thinking so, I left the crowd, called the waiter to make up my bill, and hurried upstairs alone. I sat down where, in my dream, Mrs. Stephen had sat (it was no sofa there, but a hard-backed chair), and an unspeakable sorrow welled up in me. I wished the soul immortal, wished the world below to be just as the world above; I wanted to be superstitious, wanted to know that all the tidings of my dream were true — to let my vision see the free, lively, healthy Stephen joined with the slender, lovely, winsome Bai Ping in the clouds, and let us pray for them. —

The waiter came in, I paid the bill, and, like one fleeing calamity, hurried downstairs, pushed through the wedding scene below, and, not raising my head, walked out the door. Out on the road I saw the sunlight, saw the trams coming and going and the people within them, saw the shops and the goods in the shops — the bustling world going on as before, while I seemed to have come loose from it somewhere, as though a lifetime lay between, so that everything felt new. I sprang into a rickshaw, glancing left and right, and could not help asking myself: had Bai Ping's death, then, no effect at all on this world?

I hired a rickshaw to the New World, changed there to a third-class tram to Gordon Road, and so walked to Dr. Philip's clinic — my third visit there.

I went upstairs, and saw by the clock over the lift that it was just ten past eleven. I knew the mornings were Dr. Philip's hours for house calls, and that consultations began at two in the afternoon, so there would surely be no outsiders. I knocked softly at his door; the door opened, and it was Mei Yingzi.

"Mei Yingzi!" I cried out in unwitting amazement, as if I had met her in another world.

This was because she had wholly changed her dress — a cheongsam of silver-grey, with a hidden blue-and-red check in it, throwing off little points of light; gossamer silk stockings, and grey buckskin rubber-soled shoes; her hair permed into a spiral, a fringe curled over her brow — but at her ears she still wore Cishan's earrings, and these gilt-worn silver rings had been set off now into something most rich and harmonious. A once-familiar scent assailed me. After I had come in she shut the door, and then, showing a smile that seemed long grown strange to me, said:

"Another passage of life!"

She took my arm and led me in. Dr. Philip was within, and he came to meet me and shook my hand gravely and sincerely; Mei Yingzi said:

"You'd better change your clothes too — they're all laid ready for you inside."

"But don't shave," said Philip.

I went in, through the consulting room and the operating room, and saw on a chair several suits of Chinese clothes folded very neatly. There was a bathroom beside the operating room, and I washed my face in it of my own accord, but did not dare to shave. Then I began to take off the black jacket and blue trousers, and my shirt too, though I kept my own suit trousers; then I changed into the clothes on the chair — first putting on a grey flannel shirt or two, then the woolen waistcoat I had been wearing, and last that lined gown of evergreen silk, which fitted well but for the sleeves being a little long. When I had dressed and come out, Philip pointed, in the consulting room, to two shoe-boxes on the writing-desk still unopened, and said:

"If they don't fit, I'll telephone and have him send more."

I opened the boxes, looked at the sizes, and said:

"This pair is just my size."

And so I changed into the black leather shoes there. Last I took my odds and ends from the clothes I had taken off.

Mei Yingzi came in too, and we sat down in the consulting room; Philip handed us each a glass of wine and blessed us. But he went off at once to the waiting room, and I was eager to ask Mei Yingzi:

"Is everything quite all right?"

"It's you who may have a difficulty."

"I?"

"You're too deeply tied to Bai Ping."

"And you?" I asked.

"I'm quite safe," she said, with a smile that seemed at once ashamed and triumphant; "otherwise I could no longer appear in society in the character of Mei Yingzi, nor change into these clothes."

"I think you too should be a little careful," I said.

"I'm better off than before, in fact," she said, smiling. "Because they suppose... ah, all their suspicion of me is settled on Bai Ping — Bai Ping has taken on the robe of guilt for me."

Mei Yingzi's manner was handsome and light, but I found it very cold; she had not the sympathy I had imagined for Bai Ping's death. I fell silent, my eyes on my own hands.

"Which is to say," said Mei Yingzi, "that I have all the greater freedom now to work."

"Fine," I said, with a mocking smile, "so that in the end it is our Bai Ping who bears off the cross while the crown is set on your head."

"But," said Mei Yingzi, suddenly grave, "you cannot show your face now, and Bai Ping's blood-debt is to be claimed by me alone."

"Mei Yingzi!" I was somewhat startled.

"Don't insult me," she said. "I tell you, I love Bai Ping even more than you do!"

She rose, filled the wine-cups before us, and said:

"You should now go to the Chinese interior; but — believe me! Drain this cup with me."

She raised her glass and touched mine, and I drained it with a reverent shudder. I said:

"Can I really work with you no longer? I think I should at least do something to comfort Bai Ping's soul."

"You can't; you can't show your face again, nor go home. I've wound up your lodgings too," she said, pointing to the grip beside us. "These are your things from there. You'd best go to Helen's and stay a few days, and make haste to get to the interior — there's no world for you here now."

"Then we shall not be able to meet."

"Later, perhaps..." Mei Yingzi bowed her head, her long soft lashes veiling her gaze. "But believe me — Mei Yingzi will not fail a friend she thinks well of."

"A parting in life! A parting in death!" I sighed to myself, and then, as if in sudden understanding, said: "Believe you — yes, Mei Yingzi, I ought to believe you!" I rose and gave her my hand. She shook it with the deepest sincerity, then suddenly glanced at her watch and said:

"It's time you let Philip make you up."

And so she slipped away to the waiting room, and Dr. Philip came in gravely. He sat in his usual place for examining patients, and had me sit where the patient sits; then he pressed his two hands to my temples, turning my head gently this way and that, gazing at me with his glittering eyes, then looked at my eyes, and tested my sight with the eye-chart in the mirror opposite, then took from a drawer an instrument to look at my eyeballs; he drew out a little case of lenses too, and, changing them in a frame, had me read the letters on the chart, until at last he chose two. Then he took spectacle-frames from the drawer and tried several on me, and at last chose a black one, thick in the temples and thin in the rim, and fitted the lenses into it and put it on me; but he looked, and took it off again. Thereupon he put these things away, rose, and fetched from the medicine cabinet two bottles of liquid and some cotton wool, and a dish as well with several small tweezers in it, and came back and sat down again facing me. He dipped cotton wool in the liquid in the bottle and wiped it on my eyebrows, then plucked at my brows with the tweezers, plucked a while, looked, adjusted them once, looked and adjusted them once more, and at the last dipped cotton wool in the liquid of another bottle and wiped it on my brows. Then he gave me a mirror, and just as I was noticing how much thinner, how much thinner my brows had grown, he said:

"Now go and shave — you may keep the moustache like this." And with a pencil he marked out my face for me.

From first to last I did not say a word, my heart tasting only its hidden gloom and faint sorrow and the savor of parting in life and death. I let Philip do with me altogether as he would. Now I rose and went inside to shave as he had directed, and found indeed that I was no longer myself. When I came out, Mei Yingzi was inside too, and on the writing-desk were my spectacles and an elegant chromium spectacle-case. Just as I meant to put the glasses in it, Mei Yingzi said:

"From today, you should wear the glasses always."

I did not answer, but obediently put them on. Dr. Philip looked at me and said:

"Good, good." And with that he went out again; I put away the spectacle-case, and Mei Yingzi handed me two banker's orders and two checks, and said:

"This is one hundred thousand yuan. When you get to Helen's, go and provide yourself for the journey, and get to the interior soon."

I did not answer.

"Don't go to fetch anything from home," she said again. "You can write a letter, and I'll find a way to send it for you."

She found me some blank paper and an envelope in the middle drawer, and I wrote a simple letter to my uncle, telling him I was setting out at once for the interior.

Mei Yingzi sat in the room all the while; only when I had written it, sealed it, and addressed it did she come and take it up. Then she said:

"We can't even have a meal together."

"Do you mean I should go now?"

"Yes," she said. "Go to Helen's, but don't come out together with her, and don't be with any of the old acquaintances, and don't go to the dance halls, the restaurants, the cafés, or any of the places you used to frequent; and on the road, if you see anyone you know, don't greet a single one — all these things are dangerous for you."

"Then we shall not be able to see each other often?"

"Perhaps, at night, when I have time, I'll come to Helen's to see you," she said. "Goodbye, my friend; I bless you."

I gathered up the notes listlessly. Mei Yingzi's narcissus-like hand was already stretched out before me; I took her fingers and bent to kiss the back of her hand; but as I raised my head, my eyes already blurred, I saw Mei Yingzi's lovely form leaning against the table, her left hand propped on the corner of it, her eyes closed. I said:

"Goodbye, Mei Yingzi; I shall pray for you always."

She did not stir, nor make a sound. I took up the grip beside me and went quietly out.

Dr. Philip saw me to the waiting room, and I bowed my head and shook his hand in farewell, then hurried out. At the door I clumsily shut it behind me. I could not hold myself up; I set the grip on the ground, leaned against the door, wiped my tears with my handkerchief, and for a moment lost all consciousness.
