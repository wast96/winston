## Chapter 34. The Guest Turns Host in a Rash Presumption

"Good," said Muci.

Muci set the bowl down hard. He caught sight of A-Chu's back; A-Chu seemed to let out a long breath, and in spite of himself Muci felt like laughing.

"You need only answer the questions we want answered." A-Chu turned steadily around. Seeing Muci on the verge of laughter, he shot him a stern glare.

"It is a long story... My name is Momokawa Keiko. In Edo I was a fine kabuki performer." Keiko spoke haltingly.

"It was 1909, the early spring of the second month.

"I danced and performed at the 'Cherry Blossom Grand Stage' in Tokyo. My accomplished skill and graceful dancing drew great crowds, and among them was Koyama Chino, of the Land Survey Department of the Army General Staff of the Japanese military.

"Chino asked to meet me alone.

"I was told that I possessed every quality of a seduction agent, and was asked to enlist; that for a geisha to serve the Empire would be my highest honor. Without a moment's hesitation or second thought, I agreed wholeheartedly. I was given a brief training, and in the middle of May I was placed in a small tavern on Mount Fuji. My task was plain: I was to use my beauty to seduce a Chinese exchange student, Yang Yuhua.

"I did it. Not only did I do it, I did it very well.

"I obtained photographs of his family, and the Land Survey Department of the General Staff rewarded me promptly. I understood that Yang Yuhua was only a pretext; we meant to use his close resemblance to his elder brother to carry off a substitution, one plum standing in the place of the peach.

"I volunteered of my own accord, willing to sacrifice my face to impersonate 'Xu Yuzhen'; I would be a true and worthy 'Imperial Flower.'

"But Koyama Chino went back on it.

"Wanting a younger sister of the family to become the 'Imperial Flower,' Chino schemed and pulled strings, and pushed that sister up over my body. That shameless woman, Koyama Eiko, snatched the mission from my very hands, snatched away the duty and the honor the Empire had granted me. She underwent the face-changing surgery, and I was forgotten, cast aside without mercy by the military.

"So from beginning to end I had been nothing but an insignificant bit player.

"My volunteering was treated as a 'laughingstock' inside the Land Survey Department; they mocked my folly and arrogance. Only then did I see clearly that a kabuki performer with no proper agent's training could never carry out a specialized surveying mission; even had I reached Shanghai, I would have been utterly at a loss.

"But I would not accept it. I would not accept defeat. I would not..."

Momokawa Keiko actually began to weep.

"So you slipped into Shanghai as well?" said Muci.

"Yes. Through connections I came to Shanghai. My task was to help Koyama Eiko kill Xu Yuzhen... that is, your mother. I lay hidden at Ciyun Temple as a false nun, and when your mother came to the temple to take her vegetarian meal, I lured her into a hidden room..." She broke off her account.

"You killed her?" A-Chu asked.

"No! She took her own life!"

"Suicide?" Muci and A-Chu cried out in astonishment almost in the same instant; plainly neither of them had foreseen the true cause of their mother's death.

"Why?" Muci pressed.

"A woman... to guard a woman's dignity." Keiko lowered her head.

There was no need to ask: a man had been hidden in that room.

"Can you tell me the details?" Muci asked suddenly, in Japanese.

"I can, if you are willing to bear... the pain." Keiko answered in Japanese. "She died a terrible death; she was most unfortunate. Our plan had been to abduct her first, to learn from her the Yang household's habits and daily routines, and even, from her body, some private details of what she was like... in bed. But we bungled it."

"Speak Chinese!" A-Chu barked, past all endurance.

Yang Muci said, in Japanese, "Go on... or you will die a very ugly death."

"She would sooner die than yield. When we were off our guard, she flung herself against the wall. She had fixed her mind on death, and so her skull shattered. Her blood was everywhere, her brains, her hatred; by that extreme she made us know, for the first time, the fierce mettle of a Chinese woman."

Muci lowered his head, sick at heart.

"What did she say?" A-Chu demanded.

Yang Muci raised his head and looked at A-Chu. "She says our mother died bravely; that after she was seized she killed herself in defiance. She was not violated, because her fierce spirit won even her captors' respect."

"Where are my mother's remains?" A-Chu asked Keiko.

"Beneath the withered tree at Ciyun Temple, buried shallow; they should still be there." Keiko had gone back to answering in Chinese.

A-Chu drove his fist down on the tea table. The table did not split, only shuddered under the blow. But blood came welling from between his fingers, spattering drop by drop across the wood.

"That sound of the spade, that dreadful sound of the spade: it was that night beneath Ciyun Temple that seared the terror into your memory. You were very small then; you had come with your mother to offer incense. In the depth of the night you could not sleep, and you heard the sound that has never left you to this day. You ran out alone, and it was well that Koyama Eiko caught you and led you back to your room by the hand. That night we were terrified; we did not know what you had heard. We even discussed killing you along with the rest. But if you had died the affair might have blown up, and so we gave it up and let Eiko take you home."

"And how did you come to pose as Amah A-Yue?" Muci asked.

"Because of Koyama Eiko," Keiko said. "Once she had the upper hand she ordered me about at will, and demanded I return to Japan at once; she wanted the glory of the Imperial Flower all to herself. I could not swallow such an insult, so I complied to her face and defied her behind her back, and dragged my feet about leaving. As luck would have it, Amah A-Yue, fleeing the killers on her trail, brought the young Yang Muchu to Ciyun Temple for refuge. She came there to die; I am not to blame."

"You dosed me with something?" A-Chu said.

"Yes. At set times I gave you sleeping draughts and sedatives. You were very small, very docile, very obedient."

"Enough." A-Chu's head felt as though it would split with pain.

"You killed Amah A-Yue, and then you disfigured yourself?" Muci went on.

"Yes. For the future, it was worth one gamble. I scalded my own face to ruin with boiling oil, which kept the flame from my eyes, and wrapped my ugliness in a veil, and lay hidden at Ciyun Temple for years. Even Koyama Eiko never knew." At this a look of pride crossed her face.

"My sister never suspected you?" A-Chu said.

"No. She was the Fourth Madam in the Rong house, I was a nun at Ciyun Temple; we did not meet two or three times in a year. And my ruined face had become a natural screen to hide behind."

"But in doing all this, were you not setting yourself against Koyama Eiko?" Muci was somewhat puzzled. "Why?"

"Because every honor of hers had been seized from my hands, and I meant to seize back what was mine," Keiko said. "I wanted her dead! I believed that if Yang Mulian's plan succeeded, I would remain in the Yang house forever in the guise of its loyal servant; I too would work for the military, and I would do it better than that vile creature!"

Hearing all this, Yang Muci felt that Momokawa Keiko was indeed a madwoman.

"And did you manage it?" A-Chu said with cold sarcasm.

"I very nearly did."

"You had made contact with the Japanese military?" What concerned Muci now was more than the family's feud.

"Only recently. Because of Yang Muchu's forceful return, the Japanese Army's Land Survey Department decided to drop Koyama Eiko and throw all its weight behind raising me up."

"You had meant to blow us up?" Muci said.

"Yes, and not only you two; I wanted Koyama Eiko dead as well. I judged Rong Chu the easier to control, for I had been his wet nurse and knew his nature inside and out."

"Did that not strike you as a gamble?" A-Chu said. "If I did not die, the first one suspected would be you, because that night I telephoned you."

"I know. Perhaps I was too sure of myself. I hypnotized you three times, and the hypnosis took beautifully; I believed you could never suspect me, because I had poured into your mind, countless times, an absolute trust in me."

"Nothing in this world is absolute," A-Chu said.

"I have told you everything I can tell you. What will you do with me?"

"I think we will hand you over to the Detective Division of the Central Shanghai Garrison Command; they may take an interest in your other affairs," said Muci.

"No need," A-Chu said. "I have already notified the police bureau. She will be charged with the abduction and murder of twenty years ago; I would far rather see her publicly executed."

"Sir, may I come in?" Liu A-Si was knocking at the door.

"Come in." A-Chu sat down.

Liu A-Si pushed the door open. "Sir, Deputy Bureau Chief Han has come to the gate with his men."

"Show him in."

"Yes, sir."

In a little while Han Zhengqi arrived with his subordinates. Following procedure, they questioned Momokawa Keiko briefly, then took her out under guard.

As Keiko drew level with A-Chu she suddenly halted and said, "I should like to see Rong'er."

A-Chu's eyes went dark. "There is no need. I will tell him that the nurse dearest to him in all his life was murdered by the Japanese spy Momokawa Keiko. I do not wish to see my family grieve and weep for an enemy again." The words were spoken for Keiko to hear, but his eyes were on Muci.

Muci, for his part, picked up the half-bowl of leftover broth. "Are you still eating this? I am going to the kitchen." He turned and walked inward.

Keiko was led away by the police.

Only then did Han Zhengqi murmur a few words in A-Chu's ear.

"Good, I understand. In any case, today the net comes in, and it will come in clean and neat," A-Chu said.

"Yes, sir."

"Keep Yashu safe. I am counting on you."

"Rest easy, sir." With that Han Zhengqi hurried away.

A-Chu told Liu A-Si to tidy the drawing room a little. He noticed that something was missing from the tea table, though for the moment he could not think what; then, remembering that Muci was still in the kitchen, he went through by the side door.

The Yang kitchen lay close to the drawing room, handy for the master to make a late-night supper. Since A-Chu kept no servants, the work of the kitchen fell, in the main, to "Amah A-Yue" and to A-Chu himself.

The kitchen was full of steam. Muci was warming a "lotus-seed and longan congee"; he ladled it into a snow-white porcelain bowl and lifted a porcelain spoonful to smell its fragrance.

"Are you so hungry?" A-Chu asked, leaning against the kitchen door.

"No, I only wanted to taste her handiwork. It is not bad; will you have a bowl?" Muci filled a bowl unbidden and held it out to A-Chu in both hands.

A-Chu was faintly taken aback; he was not, perhaps, used to such attentiveness from Muci.

"Forgive me. Have I turned the guest into the host?" Muci said with a slight smile.

"No, please yourself." A-Chu took the bowl and drank. "Do you know? Something has happened to Yang Yuhua."

Muci's hand lowered a little.

"We may have one more charge to bring against Yang Yuhua," A-Chu said.

"What charge?"

"Killing his wife."

Yang Muci had truly not foreseen this turn; his expression was a complicated one.

"Your present 'mother' has been murdered by your present 'father.' None of us foresaw it; call it an unexpected 'happy surprise.' The prosecutor can add a charge of homicide, though I do not intend to let him live to see tomorrow."

Muci was "struck" still, seized by a distress he could not name.

"What is the matter with you?"

Muci knew that A-Chu was asking what he already knew.

"Do you not want to know what Yang Yuhua truly thinks? I mean, when a man comes to his end, perhaps he will repent." Muci began to cough.

"Then let him repent to Father and Mother down in the Nine Springs," A-Chu said coldly. "In any case I have no wish to look on his honored face again, no, his face in death."

"My uncle raised me once; that is a fact there is no going around or avoiding."

"Compassion cannot filter out evil, and by the same token the debt of one's rearing cannot cancel the blood-debt of a murdered father." A-Chu set down the bowl.

"Mr. Yang." Muci suddenly drew something from his pocket. "Have you seen this wooden charm before?"

A wooden charm?

"Do you know, the first time I went to Ciyun Temple I found the place strange, because I noticed charms for 'driving out demons' hung in the main hall, the kind of wooden charms one commonly sees in Japanese temples. Look closely at this one: it is exquisitely made, the carving first-rate. Sometimes such a thing can play a decisive part, for instance, in making a person see visions."

A-Chu's eyes fixed on the gleaming wooden charm in Muci's grip. They stood very close together. The charm swung in a steady rhythm, like the hand of a clock, left and right, utterly quiet; A-Chu's senses began to swim, his eyes grew unfocused, his awareness blurred by degrees...

As fast as he could, Yang Muci laid the sleeping A-Chu flat on the kitchen floor. He unbuttoned A-Chu's clothes, put his own military uniform and leather shoes on him, and in the same motion drew the clothes he had taken off over A-Chu's body. Then, unhurried, he went out to the drawing room. It was very quiet; Liu A-Si had already gone out to stand ready. Muci warily picked up the telephone.

"Connect me with the director's office at Chunhe Hospital, please."

"Hello, this is Xia Yuechun. Who is speaking?"

"This is Yang Muci."

Yuechun paused a moment. "Is something the matter, Adjutant Yang?"

"I am at No. 18 Changle Street. You must come, without fail."

"No. 18 Changle Street? How do you come to be there? What has happened to A-Chu?" There was a note of alarm in Yuechun's voice.

"I have given him a little barbital. It is nothing, only a deep sleep. If you come to look after him, I shall be easier in my mind."

"What are you meaning to do?"

"I do not want Yang Yuhua to die too ugly a death."

"Adjutant Yang, you must on no account act rashly."

"I am sorry; this is my family's affair." Muci made to hang up.

"A-Ci! You really do not understand A-Chu. Do this to him, and do you think he will let you off lightly?"

"And what then? A taste of the family rod?" Muci laughed, not caring in the least. "In any case, I leave him in your hands. Thank you."

"A-Ci!"

The line went dead.

Inside the main building of the Yang mansion on Yuyuan Road, agents of the Detective Division came and went. Yu Xiaojiang followed the winding passage of the main building to Yang Muci's room on the right of the second floor. She pushed the door open and saw Du Luning.

Du Luning had gotten there first.

He wore a pair of snow-white gloves, and brushed the desktop lightly; it was clean.

"A-Ci has said he does not often stay at home, but his room is cleaned by the servants every day," Yu Xiaojiang said.

"He lives frugally," Du Luning concluded, after surveying Muci's room.

Muci's room was simply and comfortably arranged, and full of light: a bed, a bookcase, a writing desk, and a German-made table lamp.

"He lives casually, and romantically too." With her gloved hand Yu Xiaojiang picked up a crystal iceberg that stood on Muci's nightstand. "It is 90 percent pure crystal; it would not have come cheap." The crystal iceberg seemed at a stroke to overturn that four-character appraisal, 'he lives frugally.'

"Chief, beneath the shrine hall in the Yang garden we have found the secret radio set and the codebook, along with some blueprints that were not destroyed in time."

"Maps of Shanghai?"

"Yes. Street maps of Shanghai, with the road markings set out in full detail."

"Can you tell me whether Yang Muci knew that his father, or his mother, was a Japanese spy?"

"He did not know," Yu Xiaojiang answered with confidence.

"Why?"

"Intuition."

"A woman's intuition again?" Du Luning laughed. "If he did not know his parents were Japanese spies, then faced with this sudden ruin of his family, this utter overturning of his parents' image, tell me: would he still be willing to own that family as his own?"

"A-Ci's manner toward feeling looks carefree on the surface, but in truth he is a man of deep feeling."

"If both his parents were Japanese spies, might he not also be..."

"No!"

"Why?"

"If A-Ci were a Japanese spy, he would not have telephoned of his own accord to expose the secret of Ciyun Temple, nor gone exploring it himself, still less told us there was a secret radio beneath his family's shrine hall."

"As a son, how could he have the heart to drive his own parents into a corner with his own hands?"

"And if they were not his own parents?" Yu Xiaojiang said.

"Now that... is an interesting remark." Du Luning found this more and more diverting. "But let us think it through for him another way. As the saying goes, father and son are of one heart. Even if they are not father and son by blood, can twenty years' debt of upbringing be cut off at a word?"

"You mean, Chief, that A-Ci wants to save his father and get him away?"

"Not necessarily. A-Ci may want to find his father first." Something else occurred to Du Luning. "How is his sister's state of mind?"

"Very unsteady; I have had a servant stay at her side every moment," Yu Xiaojiang said. "By her account her father killed her mother, and she is close to breaking down."

"And where is A-Ci now?"

"He has just taken sick leave; he said he was going to the hospital for a follow-up."

A faint, contemptuous smile hung at the corner of Du Luning's mouth. "And Gao Lei? Where is he?"

"Standing by at headquarters."

"Have him come at once." Speaking, Du Luning walked out of Muci's room.

"Yes." Yu Xiaojiang followed close behind, pulling the door to as she went.

"Reporting, Chief: in the garden pond of the Yang house we have found a discarded pistol. It may well be the murder weapon, thrown away on purpose by the killer." Adjutant Liu held out the pistol they had recovered.

Du Luning took the pistol and examined it closely; it was plainly a type of weapon women commonly carried.

"The master of the house may have acted in self-defense," Yu Xiaojiang said.

Du Luning shrugged, committing himself to nothing. They went down the corridor of the main building to the drawing room. Yu Xiaojiang used the drawing-room telephone to reach Gao Lei, while Du Luning paced with his hands behind his back.

"Chief." Yu Xiaojiang set down the telephone, a troubled look on her face.

Du Luning waved a hand. "You need not say it; I have already guessed. A-Ci has surely pulled Gao Lei away."

"More than that. A-Ci has borrowed Squad Leader Gao's whole team."

Du Luning gave a "hmph" of a laugh, with the look of a man to whom all this was entirely expected.

"We wait," Du Luning said.

"Wait? Wait for what?"

"For A-Ci's telephone call."

"At so delicate a moment, he would telephone back?" Yu Xiaojiang did not follow.

"Did you not say he was a man of deep feeling? Would he not care whether his sister lives or dies?" The words were scarcely out when the drawing-room telephone shrilled.

Du Luning and Yu Xiaojiang exchanged a glance.

The telephone went on ringing...

Du Luning lifted the receiver. He did not speak first; and the other, remarkably, did not speak either. Plainly the caller was deliberately waiting for him to open his mouth.

"A-Ci, where are you..."

Yu Xiaojiang watched Du Luning's face intently, and saw on it a smile at once self-mocking and abashed.

"No small nerve!" Du Luning said.

"What is it?"

"He dares to hang up on me."

Yu Xiaojiang laughed. "Then it is downright mutiny."

Yang Muci hung up and ran out of a hardware shop. Gao Lei was leaning against the car door, a cigarette between his lips, waiting for him.

"Well?" Yang Muci ran up and asked him.

"The men are all out helping you look. Right now both the underworld and the law across Shanghai are hunting your father. Wanted notices for Yang Yuhua have gone up at every wharf, hotel, and railway station; there is no way out for him now."

"Wait. You are saying the police bureau's wanted notices went out long ago? Had they reckoned in advance that he would 'kill his wife'?"

"The notices name him not for murder but for theft. Your father is suspected of stealing fifty million in cash from the Xianghe Cotton Mill and the Mingfeng Mine. The notes all run in a numbered series, and the owners reported it to the police beforehand, so the moment he tries to spend any of them he will be found out at once. In effect he has not one cent on him."

"For someone to turn a multimillionaire into a pauper in an instant, that is impressive indeed." Muci sighed with genuine feeling. "Squad Leader Gao, tell me: if you were him, what would you do?"

"Take revenge on the man who ruined me, of course. Think of it: no money, no way to run; it would be a wonder if I did not fight it out to the death, fish dead or net broken. Tell me, who is it that has such a grudge against your father?"

"My brother."

"What? That one, the doctor at the hospital? This is a palace coup, start to finish."

"Get in," Muci said.

"What?" Gao Lei's mouth hung open, not yet closed, even as his body slid of its own accord into the passenger seat.

"I know where he is!" Muci said.

As he drove, Yang Muci could not stop his loud coughing. Two days of ceaseless running about, of trials through water and through fire, had left his body worn out. He fished a cigarette from his bag; Gao Lei drew out a lighter and lit it for him, and the smoke in his mouth served to rouse him a little.

He drove at a furious speed, all but "charging straight in" to Plum Blossom Lane.

Plum Blossom Lane was deeply quiet, the scent of blossom winding through it, fresh and easeful; the plum trees were in full flower, their little red hearts drifting and swaying, a beauty beyond taking in.

At the gate of No. 7 Plum Blossom Lane, plainclothes police were everywhere.

"He is already here," Muci said.

"Yes; it is too quiet, unnaturally quiet," Gao Lei agreed. "This lot from the police bureau have little field experience, though they will do well enough to catch a thief."

"Get out and draw off the watchdogs for me; I am going in," Muci said.

Gao Lei caught hold of him. "A-Ci, your father is armed."

"He and I have been father and son for more than twenty years," Muci said quietly. "It is only right that I see him part of the way."

Gao Lei looked into Muci's faded eyes and grim face, and let go. "Take care of yourself."

"Thank you."

Gao Lei got out and strode toward two of the plainclothesmen; showing his credentials, he gathered the police together to question them. In that interval Muci, silent as a snake, slipped into the little courtyard of No. 7 Plum Blossom Lane.

It was the second time Yang Muci had entered this elegant little courtyard, and he knew his way well enough. The courtyard ran three layers deep, front to back; the front yard was laid out like a small garden, its flower paths and green grass giving off a natural fragrance. In the middle lay a room that served as both study and bedroom. Keeping himself hidden, Muci looked in through the carved window...

He saw a pair of woman's feet hanging in midair.

A pair of jade feet, filled by their very beauty with a sensual charge, swayed in the haze. Fragrant wind curled, cloud and mist spread wide, auspicious clouds gathered in drifts, and A-Chu floated through a sky-wide sea of cloud. A pair of red shoes embroidered with golden lotuses appeared abruptly among the drifting vapors. A-Chu knew the owner of those shoes to be the Fourth Madam; he missed her as he might miss a loving mother, and he ran along the stairway of cloud toward her, crying, "Fourth Madam! Fourth Madam!" The shoes did not stop their drifting, and A-Chu could never quite touch them. Then he remembered all at once that the Fourth Madam had in fact been his own elder sister, and that he had called her wrongly; and up among the clouds he cried, "Sister..."

And the shoes did indeed grow still. The golden lotus blossoms opened in a boundless halo, springing up into the air from the surface of the shoes, and a fashionable young woman in Manchu dress stood before A-Chu.

A-Chu looked closely at her face: it seemed a little like Rongrong, and a little like Ronghua. Not daring to be rash, he studied her from head to foot, and found on her feet a pair of high-heeled shoes. They were Yashu's shoes. How could they be Yashu's?

A-Chu grew more and more uneasy and afraid. He remembered suddenly that the Fourth Madam, Rongrong, and Ronghua were all now among the dead, and in great terror he muttered aloud, "Guanyin, Bodhisattva, save me! Guanyin, can it be that some disaster has befallen my Yashu?"

He gave a great shout: "Yashu, run!"

His eyes flew open. He saw the streamlined lotus lamp on the drawing-room ceiling, its light bright; he was lying on a soft sofa, his limbs weak, waking all at once from the nightmare, drenched in sweat.

"You are awake?"

A-Chu saw Doctor Xia's gentle, faintly mischievous face.

"You must forgive the spectacle." A-Chu sat up.

Liu A-Si came over and gave A-Chu a hand.

"A-Ci has gone?"

"Yes." Liu A-Si lowered his head. "I am sorry, sir."

"It is nothing; I have not slept so deeply in a long while," A-Chu said. "Go and ready the car; we are going out."

Liu A-Si went off at his word.

"Deep sleep is good for your health," Xia Yuechun said.

"Are you making his excuses for him?" A-Chu shot back.

"What business is it of mine?" Yuechun was preparing an injection.

"None of your business, and yet you got here faster than a rabbit!"

"You worthy brothers turn your spears on each other, and it was you whose skill fell short, unhorsed and caught in the jar; I came rushing to the rescue. You will not thank me, and blame me instead?" Xia Yuechun spoke half in jest and half in earnest.

"Have I not thanked you enough?" There was more than one meaning in A-Chu's words.

As they spoke, Yuechun gave him the injection, pressing the plunger a shade too fast, and A-Chu cried "Ah!" with the pain.

"You are settling private scores under public cover," A-Chu said. "No wonder the ancients said: with a friend, give generously and look for little in return."

"Were you schooled in the Classics?"

"This is the spirit of Confucian learning."

"Ah, I forgot you are a blend of East and West." Xia Yuechun smiled. "Just now in your nightmare it was all 'Guanyin, Bodhisattva' and 'Yashu, run.' What did you dream?"

"I dreamed..." A-Chu grew tense. "I think I had better make a telephone call first."

A-Chu finished dressing and picked up the telephone. "Connect me with No. 7 Plum Blossom Lane, please."

The line at No. 7 Plum Blossom Lane was busy without cease... A foreboding came over A-Chu, and he set the telephone down.

"What is it?" Yuechun asked.

"I laid a trap, and Yang Yuhua has walked into it."

"The thing is as good as done. What is there to worry about?"

"The one who breaks the trap has changed."

Yuechun understood that he meant Muci's forcing his way into the game, and sought to reassure him. "The same problem, only worked a different way."

"Because of his special tie to Yang Yuhua, I think he will find the whole board hard to hold," A-Chu said. "It seems I must admit my reckoning had its limits."

A-Chu took up his overcoat and started out.

Yuechun followed. "Do you need my help?"

"Take your medicine chest back to the hospital; that will be help enough." A-Chu reached the car, where Liu A-Si came to meet him. "Sir, the owner of the Huamei Bookstore has just sent a book round for you. He says Miss Yashu has already paid for it."

A-Chu took it. It was a thin book wrapped in a gaudy, overbright butterfly-patterned paper. He unwrapped it himself: a beautifully bound volume of the Yuefu.

He was brought up short for a moment, and turned the flyleaf with great care. On it, printed in a graceful imitation-Song typeface, stood: "O Heaven! I long to know you truly, to love you long and without end. Only when the hills have lost their peaks, and the river's waters have run dry, when winter thunder rolls and snow falls in summer, when heaven and earth are joined as one, only then would I dare to part from you!"

This was a passage from "Shang Ye," among the Eighteen Nao-Bell Songs of the Yuefu. Yashu's purpose in buying the book was, beyond doubt, to tell A-Chu that she went knowing the danger ahead and yet followed his order without flinching; with her very life Yashu was declaring her love for him.

Unless heaven should fall and earth split apart, Yashu's love for A-Chu would never change.

A-Chu felt his nose sting all at once, his heart and bowels torn to pieces, that he had failed so deep a devotion. He had thought Yashu "shallow in feeling and fond of gold," a woman through whose soul he could come and go as he pleased; who could have known she held her feelings so dear, and that he himself had become the one drowning in the "river of love"?

To save Yashu was, in the end, to save himself.
