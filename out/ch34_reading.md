## Fish Congee

The light and the sound vanished all at once, and darkness closed in on every side. After a while Wei Dafu shifted to the left, put out his arm, and touched a wall. He shifted a little to the right, and touched a wall there too. Then he remembered: this was an exceedingly narrow cell, small as a box, small as a coffin.

He rapped it with his knuckles: the wall was cement. He did not know how long it had been since the interrogation — even that he had been interrogated came back to him only slowly. Time mattered. He had heard Chen Qianli say that to keep a clear head one must hold fast to a sense of time; with a sense of time the body settles into a kind of order. But he had lost track of it, and even how long ago the stabbing light had suddenly gone out was growing hazy to him now.

He remembered only one thing — he meant to turn traitor. There was important intelligence in his head; let him only speak it, and everything would change. But he must open his mouth at the right moment, so that what he said would be worth something, would tell. Otherwise he would harm others and himself alike: not only would it do no good, but he would have played the traitor for nothing.

Someone opened the door; a flashlight wavered, and its ring of light fell on his face. Two men came in, gripped him under the arms from either side, hauled him up, and pushed him out. Down an unlit corridor he was pulled around several turnings and down a flight of stairs, and at last shoved into a room. In the room were a sofa, a tea table, and a small round table, on which stood several dishes of wine-cured chicken and cured pork, and an earthenware pot besides.

The man sitting on the sofa he did not know, but the moment the man spoke he knew him by his voice — it was the one who had just been sitting in the shadows. In the dairy shed at Fahua town, Chen Qianli had told him that there was a spymaster named Ye Qinian who looked like a grave professor, and of whom he must be very wary. The man asked Wei Dafu to sit at the little round table and lifted the lid of the pot: a steaming potful of fish congee.

“The late-night eating-houses near the railway station — this is about the best they run to,” Ye Qinian said politely. But he did not sit down himself; he left the room. Wei Dafu had not been through many such scenes; uncertain what lay behind it, he sat there and dared not stir.

Someone came in: it was Lu Zhongde. To see him appear here startled Wei Dafu greatly, and the shock was not wholly feigned. After a blank moment he cried out: “Old Yi — how do you come to be here too?”

Lu Zhongde sat down across the little round table, opened his cigarette case, lit one for himself, and said to Wei Dafu: “Eat something first; have your congee, then smoke.”

In the days when Lu Zhongde was still, to Wei Dafu's mind, that same Old Yi, his manner had always been a thing Wei Dafu admired. Of the petty swindlers who knocked about the city Wei Dafu had seen no few, but it had never crossed his mind that Old Yi too was a counterfeit. He longed to ask him to his face: what in the end have you done with Ling Wen? He clung to a thread of hope that she was still alive at this moment, even were she shut up in some prison. He remembered still her lovely profile; only later had it come to him that he had seen her once before, at an unlooked-for rendezvous. Had it not been for the revolution, he would never have come to know a woman writer. There had been a time when he thought her relations with Old Yi were out of the ordinary. If it were truly as Chen Qianli guessed, and Ling Wen had been murdered by this very man before him, then the fellow was no better than a beast.

But Wei Dafu did not dare ask him so.

“Was it Chen Qianli who sent you to the newspaper office?” Lu Zhongde asked it offhand, just as the Yi Junnian of old had always questioned Wei Dafu offhand, knowing in his heart that Wei Dafu would never keep a thing from him.

Wei Dafu thought a moment and said: “This I can tell you — yes.”

“And what is there you cannot tell me?”

Wei Dafu shook his head and said nothing. Lu Zhongde began to lead him on by patient degrees, as in the old days, playing “Old Yi,” he had so often coaxed a Wei Dafu who was now devil-may-care, now downcast. He began with the state of things on the two sides, Nationalist and Communist, and mentioned how many underground Party organs the Special Operations Headquarters had cracked in recent years, how many people it had taken. He told over the various ends the arrested had come to, letting fall, as if by chance, the words shooting and death. From there, by way of the hoary old tag that our bodies, to the very hair and skin, are a gift from our parents, he turned the talk about and brought it round to Wei Dafu's father and mother.

Lu Zhongde knew Wei Dafu all too well. He knew that Wei Dafu's parents were dead. The year the Yangtze flooded, their fields destroyed in a single night, the two of them, husband and wife, had come by oar-boat from Anhui to Shanghai and put up a shack on the north bank of Suzhou Creek. After his parents died, there had been a time when Wei Dafu often told “Old Yi” that, taking people round to view houses, each time he opened a door and stepped into a dim room he would seem, in a flash, to catch his mother's and father's faces passing before his eyes. Wei Dafu had told him that the place where his parents were now was as dark as the place they had lived in while alive, and windowless. He hoped that one day he might take over a house of his own, and so fulfill the old couple's wish.

“And will you go to join the two old people with your wish still unfulfilled?”

Wei Dafu gave a soft sigh. He asked himself in his heart: is it time? He said nothing, drank off a bowl of the fish congee in two or three swallows, then picked up a piece of the wine-cured chicken and chewed it a long while.

“Intelligence is like this raw fish — once it's sliced it must go into the pot at once; past its time, it's no longer good to eat,” said Lu Zhongde.

“But it can't go into the pot till the cook has come,” Wei Dafu put in. “The finest raw fish must still be given to a chef who knows its worth.”

Lu Zhongde laughed; he knew what this self-styled sharp street-runner before him was thinking. Through his laughter he said: “You don't trust me — afraid I'll pocket your reward money?”

Wei Dafu had made his decision, and, smiling too, said to him: “Nonsense, Old Yi — you know me well enough. Wei Dafu has always played a straight hand; when it came to laying down the stake, when did I ever let my hand shake? This hand of cards is a very big one. To put it grandly, play it well and your whole ‘Communist-suppression’ enterprise is half accomplished. What I fear, rather, is that you, Old Yi, cannot play this hand, and I'll have turned traitor for nothing.”

Lu Zhongde nodded. Unhurriedly he finished the cigarette in his hand, and, seeing that Wei Dafu had stopped eating, tossed the silver cigarette case onto the table, said, “Smoke as you please,” and turned and went out.

“Set two men to stand guard at the far end of the corridor, and you keep watch in the room. Not one of the Shanghai-station men is to come over here; whatever Wei Dafu asks for, telephone up and have them see to it.” Having shut the door, out in the corridor Ye Qinian gave his orders to Secretary Ma. He and Lu Zhongde, come out from Wei Dafu, slipped back into the room where Lu Zhongde had been before.

It was now the small hours of the eighteenth of the first month. Ye Qinian had interrogated Wei Dafu through the night and gained astonishing results. When Wei Dafu heard that the man standing before him was Deputy Director Ye of the Special Operations Headquarters, he had told out everything he knew. Lu Zhongde was in a rare high excitement, yet, seeing that Ye Qinian still seemed somewhat distracted, he was a little puzzled: “Teacher, are you too tired? Would you not go and rest a while?”

Lu Zhongde did not know that his teacher, Deputy Director Ye of the Special Operations Headquarters, had for two days now been dead in heart as cold ash. At certain moments he even felt that he ought, like Old Meng, to retreat into Xiaotaoyuan and live out the remnant of his days. He drifted a while in a daze, then made an effort and pulled himself together.

Had Chen Qianli not laid bare that old affair of years ago, Ye Qinian's heart at this moment should have been wild with joy. Was this luck? He thought not; it was a harvest won by years of racking his brains and paying a great price. Very likely, tomorrow night he would seize a whole company of the Communists' leading figures. Such a victory would be without parallel even in the annals of the world's secret services; in Nanjing he would stand first among men of merit.

To hire a freighter — he could not but admire Chen Qianli's sheer audacity. The freighter would be laden with rice, timber and cotton. Once the loading, the checking of the manifests, and the customs clearance were done, only in the evening, before she sailed, would they use a steam-launch to carry the people out to her over the dark water, giving out that a last lot of cargo had only just reached the wharf.

No one would care who those people were. Most of the crew would not be on deck at that hour, and would see nothing of what passed at the ship's side. A few of the crew might think it a little odd, but the captain would have given word beforehand that this was the shipowner's arrangement, and there was no cause for alarm.

The Municipal Police's anti-smuggling patrol boats out on the river would not care either; such things happened all the time. So long as a steamer had berths to spare, she sold as many as she could, right up to the last moment before sailing — the shipowners would gladly have sold the captain's own cabin for cash. Let the right man have a word with the police, and they turned a blind eye and let it pass.

Once the freighter was out of port, no one had any hold over them; the one thing to fear was the disembarking and clearing outward. That too was easy. A freighter commonly carried a few passengers besides, and one need only get ready some papers of identity — and at such matters as surety bonds and guarantee documents the Communists had always been very adept.

The most important thing, of course, was to get hold of a ship. The ship, Chen Qianli said, he had got; but he was close as the grave about it, and would not tell the whole of the matter to comrades who had no need to know. Yet that quick-witted rascal Wei Dafu had caught a hint of it. He was a slippery one, not to be trusted too far — but this once his quick wits had lit on the right spot.

When Chen Qianli had drawn the drafted advertisement out of his pocket, he had not noticed a small scrap of paper tucked in with it — a punched tram ticket. Wei Dafu, on the sly, had slipped the ticket into his own pocket. Ye Qinian, once he had the ticket, at once had Secretary Ma telephone the Chinese Tramway Company; from the ticket's color and number, together with the pattern of the punched holes, the man who answered told Secretary Ma directly that it was a No. 1 tram, and the place of boarding was Dongjiadu.

That made things easy. He told You Tianxiao, who had been waiting all this while in the station chief's office, to take men to the wharves through the night and make inquiries. Whatever happened on the wharves, someone always knew. Whether it were the Six-Gang, the Eight-Gang, the Sixteen-Gang, the Thirty-Two-Gang or the Seventy-Two-Gang, he wanted You Tianxiao, in the name of the Judge Advocate's office of the Songhu Garrison Command, to lean on these wharf gangs good and hard, and to have word before daybreak.

“As things stand now, we have the whole of it clear,” said Lu Zhongde in high glee; he could hardly wait to go over Ye Qinian's head, straight to Nanjing, and report to the Generalissimo.

“Li Han has gone to meet someone. Wei Dafu placed the advertisement, and so made contact with yet another. Teacher, to have the Shen Bao offices run the advertisement — it was masterly.”

Secretary Ma checked the telephone number given in the advertisement; the telephone company said the instrument was installed in a house at Fahua town.

“We may now suppose that at least three leading Communists mean to escape from Shanghai aboard this freighter. Considering that Chen Qianli means to hire the ten-odd remaining passenger cabins of the ship, we may even imagine the number could be greater.”

Ye Qinian sat with his eyes closed and paid his pupil no heed. He was waiting for the news You Tianxiao would bring back from the wharves.

Toward four in the morning You Tianxiao drove back. The moment he reached the station-chief's room he telephoned Ye Qinian on the internal line. Ye Qinian judged that by now the ‘Xi Shi’ plan had been brought to perfect completion. He had You Tianxiao come downstairs, straight to this hidden room on the ground floor of the Zhengyuan Hotel — a place the Special Operations Headquarters' Shanghai station had kept for directing its secret operations; on this visit, the moment Ye Qinian reached Shanghai, he had had them set it aside for his own sole use, and no one else from the station might come there.

You Tianxiao, seeing Yi Junnian seated in the room, was struck dumb. In the next instant it came to him: so it was this man who was the ‘Xi Shi.’ You Tianxiao had once put him to the electric torture, and to recall it now brought a sheepish look to his face. But no one wanted to hear his civilities —

“What news from the wharves?” Ye Qinian asked at once.

“Wangjia Wharf Street, the Lintai Shipping Company. Early on the morning of the sixteenth of the first month, a man of thirty-odd, got up like a wealthy merchant, came to them saying he wanted to book the whole passenger cabin of a freighter, and paid a deposit on the spot. A single bar of gold.

“They happened to have a ship bound for Xiamen and Shantou, carrying a consignment of foreign goods, the shipper being the Aquarius Company. On the return voyage she was to load timber and ore.

“The sailing time is the evening of the eighteenth of the first month. The man booked the cabins in the name of a travel agency, but he said the passengers would come to Shanghai by train at five in the afternoon, so they could not board until after eleven at night. What is more, he wished the freighter to leave the wharf early and wait near the mouth of the Wusong River for them to come alongside by lighter. After this the man went also to the Gongmao Transport firm, beside the Sand-Boat Guild hall, and hired a steam-launch for use on the evening of the eighteenth, requiring it to put in at some small wharf in Pudong to take people aboard, and then carry them out to the big ship at the mouth of the Wusong River.

“As to just which wharf in Pudong the people were to be picked up from, the man said that on the afternoon of the day he would send someone to the transport firm to board the launch ahead, and the exact pickup point would be given at the time. Here too he paid a deposit.”

“This Chen Qianli — he is too cunning by far,” Lu Zhongde swore. This being so, it was hard for them to fix on the place of arrest. The small wharves of Pudong were so many, the launch would ply up and down the Huangpu, and by night at that: heaven knew where the people would go aboard. Perhaps they might lay an ambush aboard the freighter — but to speak plainly, Ye Qinian mistrusted those wharf-gang fellows deeply. If You Tianxiao could get word by the merest inquiry, then were they to take control of the big freighter beforehand and send soldiers and police aboard from the wharf jetty, the news might well leak out.

To make a great freighter afloat on the Huangpu into a trap held far too many uncertainties. Where were the soldiers and police to go secretly aboard? Would the other side set men to watch the freighter? How many were they? He judged that they must first get word of the wharf in Pudong where these leading Communists would assemble; only so could the arrest be carried through without a hitch.

Lu Zhongde had kept turning over, and could not let go of, that first thing Wei Dafu had said on seeing him earlier. Now, as the two of them sat smoking, he told Wei Dafu his true identity.

“It doesn't come easy,” said Wei Dafu. “Having called you Old Yi these several years, to change now doesn't come easy at all.”

“No matter — you may still call me Old Yi; I reckon you'll be calling me Old Yi for one more day yet.”

Wei Dafu turned it over a moment: “What do you mean — you mean to go back?”

“We must know their assembly point before they board.”

“Why not just go to Fahua town and take Chen Qianli, and be done with it?” A whole night without sleep, Wei Dafu yawned, thought a moment, and added: “Though, true enough — take them all in, and there's no one to keep the rendezvous, and the line is cut. Or else — throw a cordon round the whole of Dongjiadu?”

“That won't do. Chen Qianli is a very devil; he's sure to have set lookouts near Dongjiadu, and at the least stir they'll draw back and not move.”

“And the ship? Finding the ship would serve as well.”

Lu Zhongde shook his head, and asked again: “Since you came out of the Shen Bao offices you've not gone back all this while — do you think Chen Qianli might suspect something has befallen you?”

“Yesterday, at the newspaper office, I telephoned him. He told me not to go back to Fahua town, but to meet him at eight this morning at Gujiazhai Park. He's to set me a new task then.”

On this point Wei Dafu had not been talking wildly. After they seized him at the door of the Shen Bao offices, they had gone to the paper's advertising section and questioned that Miss Wu. Miss Wu said that he had made a telephone call afterward, and, hanging up, had said cheerfully that the boss had given him a day off, and that he meant to ask Miss Wu out to lunch.
