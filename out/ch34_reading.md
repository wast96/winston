## 4

The execution ground of the Chongqing local court lay at the foot of Mount Gele. Before every execution the nearest police station sent men to clear the site and seal off the intersections, and there they waited for the vehicles carrying the prisoners and the bailiffs to come rolling in through the dust. This time, though, was a little different. The newly appointed court president had come up through the party apparatus, and to serve the ends of propaganda and of making an example, he had, for the execution of that dozen-odd traitors who had sold out the country and gone over to the enemy, expressly invited newspapermen and observers from every quarter of society to watch.

Tang Ya was posted to protocol duty. In her black uniform, her hair coiled up inside her cap, her hands behind her back, she stood the whole time at the guard's stance, legs apart. One reporter, struck by the woman bailiff's bearing, raised his camera at her and had just pressed the shutter when two plainclothesmen hauled him aside, searched him head to foot, opened the back of the camera, and confiscated the film.

While the reporter was still shouting his protest, the execution began. As a volley of shots rang out, a few faint cries of alarm went up from the viewing stand, then it fell utterly silent. It was only when the medical examiner bent over a body and worked a length of iron wire into the bullet hole, twisting it back and forth inside a head with half its face blown away, that someone on the viewing stand clapped a hand over his mouth and began to retch.

The whole way back from the execution ground, Old Jin kept glancing at Tang Ya's face. When the car reached a corner in Shapingba he pulled over to the curb. "Go home and rest." Before Tang Ya could speak, Old Jin glanced at the rearview mirror. "I know that car behind us."

Tang Ya knew the car too. She also knew that the two men sitting in it were the plainclothesmen who had searched the reporter earlier. In sending men to protect her, Yang Qun had also made her his bait. Tang Ya gave a cold laugh inside, took the police cap from the center console, got out without a word, and slammed the door hard.

The two plainclothesmen were quick to follow, trailing the young woman bailiff down the street as though it were nothing to them.

Since her mother's death Tang Ya had moved into a Chongqing hostel for government staff. The little two-story building was lost among the jumbled houses of the street, shops open on the ground floor and clamoring all day long, the stairs and passages heaped with clutter and laundry of every color hung out to dry.

The plainclothesmen opened the door with Tang Ya's key, and only when they were sure the room was safe did they withdraw outside and, with a courteous gesture of invitation, remind her, "Miss Tang, we'll be just downstairs."

Tang Ya took back the key, shut the door, and threw herself down on the narrow single bed. She was half asleep when her eyes snapped open: Jiang Yongnan was already standing at the foot of the bed, looking at her with the same still, dim gaze as that day years ago on the Hankou wharf.

Once she was sure it was not a dream, Tang Ya felt an ease she had never known before. She lay stretched out flat. "I know your rules. You've come to silence me."

In all the long time he had been hiding in the hostel room across the hall, Jiang Yongnan had thought of many things to say, yet now he found there was no way to begin. He stood at the bedside, and it was a good while before he found one line: "Is Mrs. Tang well?"

Tang Ya said calmly, "Kill me, and I'll be able to find out whether she is."

Mrs. Tang had died the year before in the air-raid tunnel disaster at Jiaochangkou. That day, tens of thousands of Chongqing civilians suffocated to death trying to escape the bombing. Three days later, when Yang Qun sent men to find Mrs. Tang among the heaped corpses, her body, from the rot, had swollen to twice its size. This frail and sickly woman had come thousands of hard miles to Chongqing to be reunited with her husband. Standing at the reception desk of the Ordnance Department, looking at the envelope that held her husband's death benefit, Mrs. Tang had opened her mouth and collapsed all at once into her daughter's arms.

Mr. Tang had been an engineer at the Hanyang Arsenal; on the road west with the relocating factory, the boat he was traveling on was sunk by the Japanese in the Yangtze. No living man was ever seen, no dead body ever found.

Mrs. Tang began to lose her mind after she came to. Wetting her fingers with spittle, she counted the death benefit in that envelope over and over, glaring wide-eyed at her daughter and saying again and again, "This is your father's blood money—every cent we spend is his life."

In fact the money was not even enough for two months' rent. Prices in Chongqing were soaring day and night. At first Tang Ya washed other people's clothes by the Jialing River in the daytime and sold flowers at night in the dance halls on Duyou Street; later she simply went into the trade and became a dance-hall girl, so as to free her days to care for a mother growing sicker by the week.

But the government soon issued its ban on entertainment. It was in a raid to shut down a dance hall that Yang Qun first took a fancy to Tang Ya. In those days he was still at the police department, overseeing public order in Chongqing; unlike the coarse and greedy peace-keeping police, he was more like a gentleman in uniform. One day Yang Qun put a key into Tang Ya's hand and looked at her intently. "Your mother needs you, but you need me." When Tang Ya gave no reaction at all, he smiled and pointed at the sky beyond the window. "The Japanese planes come whenever they please. If a bomb dropped this very moment, you'd be part of me and I of you."

Turning the key over in her fingers, as if facing a favored patron in the dance hall, Tang Ya said in a soft, coaxing voice, "I'd thought Officer Yang was different from those others out there."

"Different or not, a man's still a man." As he spoke Yang Qun handed over, with a genial laugh, a sheet of paper—his letter of recommendation to the special-training class at the Central Police Academy. Yang Qun smiled. "But I find you're not like the rest of them. You're an educated young woman of the new kind, and the new youth should have a new life." There was much of the past that could only be buried in her heart; Tang Ya would never speak of it to anyone. She sat on the edge of the bed, and when Jiang Yongnan had said what he had come to say, she answered lightly, "Why go to all this trouble? Kill me now, shut the door, and leave—isn't that the end of it, once and for all?"

"If you were anyone else, I would." Even as he said it Jiang Yongnan surprised himself. He looked away from Tang Ya's eyes. "Since you know our rules, you must understand that even if I leave today, others will come . . . The Police Administration Department could send any number of men, and still not protect you."

"Then go." Tang Ya rose and went to the window, gazing down at the street in the setting sun. "They're lying in wait like the man watching the stump for a hare—all to take you."

Jiang Yongnan nodded. He took the felt hat from the table and rose to go to the door, then suddenly stopped. "These past years, I've often thought back on the old days . . . How good they were. All I wanted was to be a decent doctor and put down roots in this country . . . I even thought once of becoming a pastor in a church."

With that he turned his head, and saw that Tang Ya had already turned and was facing him. In the backlit shadow her face was a blur. Jiang Yongnan said, "You have to believe me. I am not your enemy."

"There's no believing or not believing about it," said Tang Ya. "I have no family, and I have no enemies."

"Then what is left here worth clinging to?" With that Jiang Yongnan put on the felt hat, opened the door, and left.

By Jiang Yongnan's plan, Tang Ya was to slip away partway through the bailiffs' gathering that night and go to a Korean noodle shop at the corner of Lianhuachi, where someone would be waiting for her and would take her out of Chongqing the next day. But Tang Ya behaved as if she had long forgotten the arrangement.

When the bailiffs back from the execution ground had washed the reek of blood from their bodies in cup after cup and were leaving the White Night one by one, liquor on every breath, Old Jin took particular note of the two plainclothesmen seated not far off and said to her in the tone of an elder, "That's about enough. You ought to be getting home too."

Tang Ya only pressed her lips into a smile, drew a cigarette from the case he had left on the table, held it between her fingers, and drifted off toward the bar. Sometimes, watching this woman under his charge from the shadows, Old Jin's eyes held something like the look one gives a worn shoe on another man's foot—an unspeakable regret, and a small ache of the heart besides.

Lighting the cigarette off the American bartender's lighter, Tang Ya ordered a double of the nameless liquor.

It was a long while before Jiang Yongnan came into the bar. He chose an inconspicuous spot and sat, and nursed a whisky until Tang Ya was slumped over the bar, half asleep. He went over and, like a man deceived by his own fond hopes, leaned to her ear. "How much longer are you going to make me wait?"

Tang Ya languidly propped herself up and stared at him blankly a moment. "Sir, do we know each other?"

"I'll deal with those two. Go now, out the back door." When he had said it and saw her unmoved, Jiang Yongnan added with a smile, "Time won't wait. Curfew's nearly here."

"Then let's drink." As if she had just remembered the man before her, Tang Ya made a sign to the bartender. "Liquor will make you forget a great many things." And then, laughing gaily, she launched into an aimless account of this nameless cocktail—the provenance and vintage of the base spirits, right down to the proportion of the two liquors. Then all at once Tang Ya said, "There are two more standing guard outside. You can't handle four men."

"That's my affair." As he spoke Jiang Yongnan reached out of habit for the silver dollar in his pocket. When Guo Bingyan had first put the thing in his hand, he had said gravely that it was the last gift a killer keeps for himself, and that the potassium cyanide inside was enough to kill an elephant. That had been the first time Jiang Yongnan carried out an assassination—at the Japanese Naval Club in Hongkou, Shanghai. Jiang Yongnan drew out the silver dollar and spun it on the bar. "You need only do as I say."

"Why should I do as you say? What am I to you?" Laughing, Tang Ya took the drink the bartender had set on the bar and raised it before her, looking at the milky liquid in the shot glass. She smiled more seductively still. "Take a sip. It's like a ball of fire."

Jiang Yongnan took the glass, slowly tipped back his head, and swallowed the whole of it in one; he held it in his mouth and it cost him great effort to get it down, and then he looked, for an instant, as if he were suffocating. He clapped a hand over the spinning silver dollar. "It's not fire. It's a glass of potassium cyanide."

"Only the dead know the taste of poison." Tang Ya broke into a giggling laugh, looking so gleeful and unrestrained that she drew not a few drunken eyes in the bar. When she had laughed her fill, Tang Ya said, her glance sliding sidelong, "Has it occurred to you—what if I gave you up right now?"

The smile stayed on Jiang Yongnan's face, but no smile however gentle could hide the desolation in his eyes. He said, offhandedly, "That too would be a way to end it once and for all."

Just then the blind Black man came downstairs and began to play his saxophone. The mournful melody rose like water and stopped up every breast. All at once Tang Ya felt a misery she could not name, a burning in her. She reached out to call the bartender for more, then pointed at the mixing tin and, playing the fool in her drink, said loudly in English, "You've got a drink that kills a man—why don't you call it potassium cyanide?"

But every sound was in an instant drowned by the air-raid siren splitting the air. All at once the bar door became a breach in a dike, and only the Black man played on, as though seeing off each of the people scrambling for their lives; the music he drew out even changed key and turned merry.

Jiang Yongnan pulled Tang Ya out into the street. The lamps had gone dark, the whole city black, and yet there was nowhere for them to flee. It was almost swept along by the flood of people that they came into the air-raid shelter, pressed among all manner of smells and sounds.

Just then a hanging storm lantern was lit. Jiang Yongnan gathered his courage and lifted with his hand the hair that had fallen over Tang Ya's face, and saw the teardrop hanging on her lashes. As the roar of the planes came nearer, in an explosion that shook the earth and the hills, the teardrop slid down all at once, yet Tang Ya seemed to have fallen asleep. Her eyes were shut, and she rested her head lightly against Jiang Yongnan's chest.

It came to Jiang Yongnan all at once: this was the most beautiful moment of his life. In the dust sifting down, in the swaying light and the frightened or despairing eyes around him, he would even have been willing to let life stop right there.

The Japanese bombing lasted half an hour, and when it ended the city of Chongqing was ablaze on every side.

The moment she came out of the shelter Tang Ya saw Yang Qun's car through the flying ash. She turned to Jiang Yongnan. "Get away, quickly."

But it was already too late. A crowd of men rushed in together, some in plainclothes, some in the uniforms of the fire brigade. As they threw Jiang Yongnan down they cuffed his hands behind him.

Without a second thought Tang Ya ran to the sedan and pulled the door open. "Let him go, and I'll go back with you."

Yang Qun looked at her with keen interest. "What did you say?"

"Let him go." Tang Ya said, "And I'll be with you the rest of my life."
