## Chapter 34

The electric lamps were dazzling white; the trolley tables rolled in tea and refreshments of every kind. I was with Miko, Mrs. Manfield with Philip, Mei Yingzi among the group of Japanese naval officers; Bai Ping was diagonally across from me, always at a good distance, with people passing to and fro between us. I much wanted to move nearer, but always felt it would be a little forced; luckily I could still see all that she did. I saw that she seemed a little weary—from too much dancing, I supposed—and I saw Takeshima bring her refreshments, though she took little. At last she set her own cup on a trolley table against the wall, waving her handkerchief lightly like a fan, and, looking utterly worn out, sat down quietly on a sofa.

I much wanted to go over and see her, but I felt that to cross such a distance would surely draw others' notice and set many people going to her, which seemed, on the contrary, a disturbance to her. Bai Ping was weary of life, weary of the men who partnered her; I had always felt sympathy for her, and so now I hoped she might have a quieter rest.

Lit with candle-glow, glittering with color, a very large Christmas cake was rolled in on a trolley; the candle-flames tilted with the motion, but righted themselves once the cake was set in the middle of the room. The cake was beautifully adorned, with what looked like the Japanese and Chinese flags for a background, and, added to them, Japanese characters reading "Christmas Sino-Japanese Friendship Soirée." We all gathered round to look, and just as I was about to make it out in detail, I suddenly heard Mei Yingzi cry excitedly:

"Where is our beautiful president? Do call her to come and cut this beautiful cake."

Only then did I take notice of Bai Ping. Bai Ping wore a weary, fretful air, and, languid as that Persian cat of hers at home, was just about to go out through a door. Where that door led I did not know; but Mei Yingzi's call made me suddenly realize that Bai Ping's weariness might be a pretense, and made me see, besides, the reason Mei Yingzi had named Bai Ping president.

"This is, of course, the duty of our goddess of peace." Bai Ping said unhurriedly, and turned and sailed back over with the stateliness of a sailing ship.

Bai Ping did not firmly refuse to cut the cake; she first cut apart the Chinese and Japanese flags, then cut it daintily into small pieces. Mei Yingzi egged on the officer beside her to hold out a plate and receive one for her, and thereupon, one after another, they all went up holding out plates, and Bai Ping serenely dealt out the pieces one by one, showing a silent smile. At last Bai Ping laid down the knife and came over affectionately to stand before Mei Yingzi; I was just then behind Mei Yingzi, and Bai Ping glanced at me with a smile, then leaned close to Mei Yingzi and said affectionately:

"I am really too tired, Mei Yingzi," she said with a very sweet smile, in a coaxing tone: "Forgive me—don't tease me any more."

Mei Yingzi said nothing, but took her hand affectionately and went to a sofa at the back; I saw them sit down together, seeming to talk intimately, though I could not hear what they said.

The refreshments were cleared away, and Umetake announced several performances of Japanese folk dance. The music struck up, and several Japanese women dressed in old-style costume came out to perform in the dancing space; at this Miko told me she had a part in the third item, and went in through a side door like a bird taking wing. Now, of course, I understood that those who took part in the performance were the very gorgeous Japanese women I had seen earlier, and that Miko was one of them.

I had seen very little Japanese dance, and knew nothing of its history; but from the two dances performed I felt by intuition an art gentle and refined, carrying a sorrowful, poetic quality—the very opposite of the proud, complacent bearing of the soldiers present. In one of the dances she took part in, Miko played the leading role; after one movement of the dance came a passage of song. I could not understand the meaning of the song, but the tune expressed nothing other than a sorrowful grieving for the passing of spring. Miko's beauty shone the more in the dance, so that when the performance was over and the social dancing began, many came to ask her to dance.

A burst of wild revelry: colored paper streamed through the air, balloons floated up, "Merry Christmas," "Merry Christmas." The trolley tables were covered all over with wineglasses, which people snatched up and raised; and then they clinked glasses, drank deep, danced wildly—this was midnight, twelve o'clock. But I did not know how many were truly mad with revelry, how many feigned it, and how many, following custom, learned to feign it!

After this, the dancing did not seem to go on much longer; I noticed people going in by twos and threes through the door that Bai Ping had earlier meant to leave by. When the dance music paused briefly, I noticed at last that Bai Ping was no longer there, and Mei Yingzi was gone too; they must, I thought, have gone in through that door. So when the music struck up again, Miko and I had danced but halfway when I followed the Japanese officers who were going in, and took Miko in with me.

It proved to be a very spacious rear gallery, set all along with tables and chairs to sit on, though no one was sitting; beyond the windows was a stretch of blackness, and a few beams of light told me there was another house out in the garden. The two officers ahead turned a corner, and I followed them; the corner gave onto a broad staircase, and they climbed the steps, and I climbed after.

Upstairs was the brilliantly lit gaming table, with many people crowded round; as if looking for someone, I made my way in through the press of people and saw Bai Ping seated beside Arita, and Mei Yingzi seated diagonally opposite. Bai Ping saw me and called me over. In the record of our open social acquaintance, I was of course closer to Bai Ping than to Mei Yingzi: I was entitled to stand behind Bai Ping, but not entitled to stand beside Mei Yingzi; Bai Ping was entitled to order me about, while Mei Yingzi, on the surface, still had to keep up a certain reserve. I saw Mei Yingzi glance at me, but I did not know her meaning, and I had already gone toward the seat behind Bai Ping, so I did not stop. I went behind Bai Ping and asked:

"Winning?"

"Not badly," said Bai Ping.

They were playing at poker, and those round the table were all laying bets. I did not understand this game, so Bai Ping explained it to me, and said besides that it was a thing of pure chance, not skill; at last she said:

"Take a turn for me," her words like a command, and as she spoke she stood up herself: "I'll be back in a moment; thank you." She had already squeezed out, and of course I could only sit down; but I realized at once that this was Bai Ping's device for slipping away. I glanced at Mei Yingzi; she was looking at me, with a mocking smile. She seemed to have lost a good deal; this time she bet all she had, and those around seemed to be looking for someone to take her place too, but the people to her left and right and behind were all intent on their own stakes—there was, I thought, not one connection of hers who could gamble in her stead. For the moment she seemed anxious to get away.

Luckily, at this hand, Mei Yingzi lost again, losing all the money on the table before her, and she stood up and said:

"It's too stuffy; I'll take a breather and come back."

Everyone knew these words for a pretext, but I believed all would take her for the usual sort of gambler who, having lost, says a face-saving word and leaves the seat, so that no one held any other suspicion of her, and no one detained her. After she went, someone sat down behind, and I went on gambling. I gambled very small, and though my mind was on other things, I kept winning; about twenty minutes later I saw Mrs. Manfield, who squeezed over and said:

"You've won a great deal."

"It isn't my own," I said. "Haven't you seen Bai Ping? Why hasn't she come back?"

"No," she said.

I looked about on every side, pretending to search for Bai Ping, and said:

"Will you take a turn for her? I'll go and find her. She must, I think, be dancing."

So I gave up my seat to Mrs. Manfield and went alone down toward the ballroom.

I felt sure Bai Ping would not be there, nor did I know where I might find her; I was reckoning up in my mind the places I might look and the things I might do, worrying over Mei Yingzi's work—could she be with Bai Ping—

But Bai Ping was, after all, dancing in the ballroom, and astonishment broke off my thoughts. The music was a hot jazz; the Chinese puppet officials had, I suppose, all drifted off by now, and the whole hall was Japanese girls and young Japanese officers, the air very romantic, without a trace of the formal, solemn air of before. Bai Ping was dancing with a very young officer, a smile on her face like a lily just opening, a glad light shining in her eyes. I wondered greatly, and it made me think at once of Mei Yingzi—could it have failed already? A dreadful foreboding: had Bai Ping already brought Mei Yingzi to grief? My heart leapt, and I longed to catch hold of Bai Ping and ask her—but the music went on and on.

"How is it you've come down?"

It was Miko, who had also come in from the back. So I danced with her. I asked:

"Where did you go?"

"I've been here all along; I just stepped out for a moment."

"Have you seen Mei Yingzi?"

"Wasn't she at the gaming table?"

"No," I said, and just then I had the chance to dance near Bai Ping. She saw me: "What? You've come down too?"

"Why do you never come up?" I said; and with just these two questions, we had each danced away. It was not until the music stopped that we resumed our talk. I went over and said:

"You're very comfortable, dancing down here." I fixed my eyes on her shining eyes.

"I got too bored with gambling," she said naturally. "And now?"

"Mrs. Manfield is gambling in your place," I said.

As the music started up, I danced with Miko again, and I watched Bai Ping lead a young officer through the door that gave onto the gallery.

This was my test of her, and I believed that this time she was surely going upstairs. I meant to go up after the dance and spy on her; but I was anxious for Mei Yingzi too. In those few exchanges just now with Bai Ping, I had watched her eyes closely. Though I had not asked her about Mei Yingzi, if she had done anything to bring Mei Yingzi to grief, then under my gaze she would surely have shown some unease and awkwardness; and in fact there was none at all—she seemed glad and open, with perhaps the faintest excitement and unease, but that was a thing common with her. For an instant I turned suddenly very soft, and felt that my suspecting Bai Ping of harming Mei Yingzi was a thing that greatly wronged her, and a very shameful thing besides. But the more pressing question was that I must know at once where Mei Yingzi had got to; and, apart from the few rooms I had been in, I had no way to find out. So I bethought me of Miko at my side, and said:

"It's odd about Mei Yingzi—I don't know where she's gone off to."

"Do you want her for something?"

"Yes." I said with a smile. "Could you go and find her for me by and by?"

"Of course I can." She smiled innocently. "And how will you repay me?"

"When you've found her, I'll stand you a dinner," I said.

As the music was ending, Miko said:

"Shall I go and find her now?"

"Thank you—but don't let anyone know I'm looking for her," I said. "I'll wait for you by the gaming table; you can tell her to come and see me."

So Miko hurried out like a bird. I came out at the back myself, meaning to go upstairs. The rear gallery was broad, and beyond the windows was pitch-blackness; earlier I had seen only a few lamps massed into the shape of a house. Now, from the heat and stuffiness of my body and the curiosity of my mind, I went to the window, lit a cigarette, and opened a casement to let in the cold air from outside. I drew a deep breath and began to take note of the garden scene beyond the window: a few trees, a few clumps of flowers, arranged with great taste, a small stone pagoda of Japanese style, beside it a dense grove of bamboo, and beyond the bamboo the boundary wall; on the far side stood a little three-story Western house, seemingly built later, at the same time as this garden. I put my head out to look at the whole structure of the little house, and made out, on that side, two low lamps; they lit a path paved with pebbles, one end of which led straight to the door of the little house, the other end undoubtedly to the house on this side, with a branch path in the middle leading into the garden here. The windows of that house were all shut, and within it was still and quiet, no figure, no sound; and just then I saw a woman go from the house on this side onto the pebbled path. I shrank back—and by the well-placed lamp I recognized her: it was Miko. She looked neither right nor left, but went straight to the little house on the far side, pushed the door and went in, and with a bang the door slammed loudly behind her.

"Is Mei Yingzi in there?" I wondered.

There was no moon in the sky, only scattered starlight. From the door that had just shut I looked to the pebbled path, and looking on past it, I saw again the ornament of cypress and other trees. I realized that this little house stood at the very center of this garden, and so in my mind I let my gaze travel round the little house: I saw the stone steps of the little house—one, two, three, four, five, six—then a cinder path, then cypress again; a few spring flowering shrubs had now withered, and beyond them three hollies were still very green; over there seemed to be a little pool mirroring the starlight; passing a pitch-black corner, my gaze sprang to the little white-stone pagoda and the bamboo grove. Just then I found that beside the stone pagoda there seemed to be a broad path leading to the bamboo; I followed the path in with my eyes—and I started! A woman was coming out of the grove. I shrank back a little, and looking closely again—ah, it was Mei Yingzi! I did not alarm her; I thought there might be someone behind her, but there was none. She dragged her steps dully, head bowed, seeming to brood hard on something; she came to the stone pagoda, then to the little pool, and stood by the pool perhaps three minutes, when suddenly, as if struck by some realization, as if she had found something, she crossed the hollies onto the pebbled path and walked resolutely along it. This path was the branch of the pebbled path connecting the two sets of buildings; before she had stepped onto the main path, I saw the door of the little house open. I gave a start, and Mei Yingzi too seemed to start; but the one who came out was Miko, who went gladly up to meet her. I did not hear clearly, but she seemed to be saying:

"So here you are—I've run right into you."

Mei Yingzi caught hold of her, and what came after I could not make out at all; the two of them came over toward the house on this side.

I closed the casement, and, thinking it better to go up and wait for them, I climbed the stairs.

Mei Yingzi's anxious brooding was a sign that the work had failed; but her safety gave me much comfort, and with a calmer, steadier bearing I went up to await the unfolding of the story.
