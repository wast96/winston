### Appearances Must Be Kept Up

The Chinese are famous lovers of a show, and those who live in Shanghai dote on it the more. Let a household be so poor it has eaten and pawned itself bare, the wife weeping and the children wailing, nothing at all left in the place—still they will go out of doors as trimly turned out as ever, swaggering along with a fine roll. The Shanghai proverb, "Silk draped down the back, and no rice for supper at home," is the very portrait of such people. And when it comes to a wedding or a funeral, all the more must they follow precedent and outdo it, lay on a great display, and make a show of wealth. "It is a matter of face," they say; "one cannot but do the thing up so." Now if a rich man puts on a show and squanders a little money, there is no great harm in it; but where the means fall short—where a man is of the down-and-out sort—the show must be kept up all the same, so that for the sake of a single wedding or funeral no small number of them are saddled with debts piled so high they cannot clear them to the end of their days!

When I think it over on their behalf, it is a wretched folly; and yet, with people who dote on vanity, to raise a towering debt for the keeping-up of appearances is a thing done willingly and without a word of complaint.

There are, besides, many lovers of a show who go to eat at a snack-shop, and when the reckoning comes at the end—the whole bill only a dollar and some jiao—though they have a good many one-dollar notes tucked about them, will as often as not fish out a ten- or a five-dollar note to get change; which is rather puzzling. Once the author asked these friends: "You have spent but a dollar-odd; why keep your one-dollar notes hidden away and pull out tens and fives?" And they answered: "Because a one-dollar note makes no show of wealth, and the waiter would look down on us; only if you spend in big notes will they not dare think small of you." And this too, I suppose, is a way of keeping up appearances.
