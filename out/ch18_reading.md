## How Du Yuesheng Broke into the Dada Steamship Company

{d} By Huang Yongyan

In the society of old Shanghai, Du Yuesheng was a figure of dazzling eminence, all but unrivaled. Politically, Chiang Kai-shek made him an army major-general; economically, he held the chairmanship, directorships, and trusteeships of a great many large and medium enterprises, and was styled a magnate of industry. By trickery and force, moreover, he broke his way into the steamship trade.

In old Shanghai the manner of Du's rise was summed up in the phrase "three suns usher in prosperity" — a play on the three men surnamed Yang, Yang Du, Yang Zhixiong, and Yang Guanbei, who served him as counselors and strategists and helped him extend his ground. As early as 1931, Yang Guanbei opened the road, breaking Du into the Dada Steamship Company; and from then on Du figured as a capitalist of the shipping trade, his power in the industry widening by the day.

Before this, gang men had long since made their way among the crews and dockworkers of the steamship lines and the wharves. The business chiefs — also called stewards, and commonly compradors — of such lines as the China Merchants, the Sanbei, and the Ningshao — Shen Huating, Zhang Yanling, Shen Rongsan, Xie Baosan, and the rest — had all taken an "old man," and a China Merchants captain, Huang Muzong, had even become a "mountain lord" in the brotherhood. The gang-foremen on the wharves needed a gang standing all the more, to hold the workers down and squeeze them: the chairman of the China Merchants' Five-Wharf staff union during the First Revolutionary Civil War — which had charge of the five wharves of Jinliyuan, Yangjiadu, the Central Godown, the North Godown, and the China Godown — one Yu Xianting, was a pupil of Du Yuesheng's. But at that time Du had still not gained standing as a capitalist of the shipping trade, and so he and Yang Guanbei made use of each other and by every means contrived to break in.

### (1) How Yang Guanbei Came to Know Du Yuesheng

Yang Guanbei, a native of Zhenjiang, had studied at Zhijiang University. During the First Revolutionary Civil War, when the Second Route Army of the Northern Expedition under Bai Chongxi reached Shanghai, Chen Qun was director of the political department of that army's headquarters and Yang Hu was commander of the Songhu garrison. The head of the political department's liaison section, Li Gongpu, was Yang Guanbei's fellow townsman and schoolmate, and drew him into the liaison section as a political worker. In May 1927 the May Third Incident occurred at Jinan, and a movement to boycott Japanese goods swept the country; Shanghai too organized a "Grand Alliance for Economic Severance," with its office in the Tianhou Temple on Henan Road, and Chen Qun, Yang Hu, and Du Yuesheng all took part in it. Yang Guanbei was Chen Qun's delegate to the Alliance, young and elegant and a ready speaker, and Du took a liking to him, so that he gradually became an honored guest at Du's.

### (2) Yang Guanbei Squeezes into Dada

Shipping was a great industry, and the steamship companies were mostly joint-stock limited companies. At that time neither Du Yuesheng nor Yang Guanbei held a single share in any steamship company, and to break into one bare-handed was no easy matter. What was more, to break into the trade — given Du's standing at the time — the target had to be chosen with care: it had to be a large company already of some scale, one that could be counted on for solid profits. On these terms they fixed upon the Dada Steamship Company.

The Dada Steamship Company was an enterprise founded by the two brothers Zhang Jian and Zhang Cha of Nantong. Zhang Jian, the top graduate — the zhuangyuan — of the last years of the Qing, held a special place in politics and in society; at Nantong he had set up the Dasheng Cotton Mill, salt-reclamation companies, and other enterprises, and between 1903 and 1908 he founded, one after another, the Dada Inland-River Steamship Company, the Dada Steamship Company, and the Tianshenggang-Tongjing wharf (lunbu, "steamer wharf," being a term of Zhang Jian's own coining). He declared Nantong a non-treaty port and monopolized the northern Jiangsu shipping route between Shanghai and Yangzhou (commonly called the Little Yangtze Line). When the Dada Steamship Company opened for business at the beginning of 1908, its capital was a mere 23,537 taels of standard silver; by 1920 this had grown to 527,500 taels; and over the twenty years from 1908 to 1927, profit and loss balancing out, it cleared a net profit of 1,655,734 taels in all. Nantong and Yangzhou were the two great gateways of northern Jiangsu, taking in and sending out the goods that flowed between the two banks of the Yangtze; there were besides many ports in Rugao, Jingjiang, and other counties, with a brisk traffic in passengers and freight. To hold a monopoly on this northern Jiangsu route was to have great prospects. The Dada Company began with steamers of only a hundred tons and grew to four river steamers — the Daji, the Dade, the Daqing, and the Dahe — of more than 4,000 tons all told, and had charge besides of such tugs and wooden barges as the Chuyuan and the Chuxiang. People of the day spoke of the Dada Company as the giant of the inland-river steamship trade.

Yang Guanbei had picked out this target for Du Yuesheng, and it happened that the Dada Company just then offered an opening to be seized. Real power at Dada lay in the hands of the chairman, Zhang Cha, with Bao Xinzhai as manager. In 1925 Zhang Jian died, and the Zhang family's influence began to wane. In 1927 Zhang Cha backed the Northern warlord Sun Chuanfang in his counterattack at Longtan; the Nationalists branded him a reactionary, and at the same time the people of Nantong denounced him as a local tyrant and evil gentleman, so that the Nationalist government put out a warrant for his arrest. Zhang Cha fled to Dalian, and the Zhang family's power sank lower still. In Zhang Cha's day of power the Dada Company's profits had been very rich, yet each year the shareholders could draw only their guaranteed fixed dividend and never a bonus. Once the Zhang family's fortunes fell, the shareholders grumbled aloud. The manager, Bao Xinzhai, ran things in the old conservative way, insisting on putting money out at interest, and had deposited 200,000 taels of the company's profits at the Deji native bank in Nantong. Around 1930 the Deji failed, Dada was caught by the bad debt, and the shareholders' feelings ran especially high. In 1931 the two steamers Daji and Dade were wrecked and burned while under way, with the loss of more than a thousand crew and passengers, and the company became the target of attack from every side, from shareholders and public opinion alike. In these straits Bao Xinzhai was forced to resign, and the board named Jiang Jingtang, as a standing director, to act as chairman, and Zhang Cha's grandson Zhang Weici, as a director, to act as manager. Both men had a taste for opium and both belonged to Zhang Cha's line, and so were not to the shareholders' liking. Of all these frictions, within and without, Yang Guanbei had a very clear understanding, for his uncle by marriage, Xu Tao'an, was a director of the Dada Company, a right-hand man of the Zhang brothers in their enterprises, and the manager of the Nantong Flour Mill.

Seizing this chance, Yang Guanbei set about buying up Dada shares for Du Yuesheng and for himself. At the Zifeng native bank in Shanghai he bought 3,000 yuan of Dada shares (by one account these shares were a gift from the Zifeng to Du Yuesheng) and had them transferred into Du's name. He himself received a quantity of shares as a gift from Xu Tao'an, and through the friends of Xu Jiehe (Xu Tao'an's son, just back from studying in America) bought up a further portion in Nantong, transferring them into his own name and those of several of his young friends. So Du, Yang, and a few others became shareholders of the Dada Company. In 1932 Yang Guanbei and several other young shareholders demanded that the board reorganize the Dada Company. The board met, and the upshot was that Zhang Jian's son Zhang Xiaoruo was made chairman, Jiang Jingtang kept his standing directorship, Shen Yanmou was engaged as general manager, and Xu Jiehe was asked to serve as a director. Shen Yanmou was a close relative of the Zhang family (his grandfather Shen Jingfu and Zhang Cha were bound by a marriage between their children); he was already general manager of three mills at once — Dasheng No. 1, the branch mill, and No. 3 — and now took on the Dada general managership too, holding this plum post in absentia at Nantong, while in fact it was still Zhang Weici who filled it.

Du Yuesheng, Yang Guanbei, and the rest had come away with nothing this time and were far from resigned to it, so, acting in concert and pressing on again, they mounted a second assault. Several young shareholders led by Yang Guanbei took the van and lodged a complaint against the Dada Company with the Shanghai Municipal Bureau of Social Affairs, charging it with an illegal reorganization. The bureau chief, Wu Xingya, at Du Yuesheng's instigation, ordered the Dada Company to convene a general meeting of shareholders, reorganize the board, and elect a new chairman and general manager. Du Yuesheng's and Yang Guanbei's shares in the Dada Company were, in truth, of no account; but with Du drawing on the power of officialdom and the young shareholders charging into the breach, some of the old shareholders and directors found the whole business such a headache that in the end they had no choice but to elect Du and Yang as directors. When the new board met in 1933, the plan proposed was to make Zhang Xiaoruo chairman, Du general manager, Yang manager, and Xu Jiehe deputy manager. This arrangement Zhang Xiaoruo would not agree to. In the end, through the mediation of Shi Liangcai, who was on good terms with both the Zhang and the Du families, the posts of Zhang Xiaoruo and Du Yuesheng were exchanged — Du taking the chairmanship and Zhang the general managership, while Yang Guanbei and Xu Jiehe kept the posts first proposed — and only then did Zhang Xiaoruo reluctantly consent. From that time Du Yuesheng and Yang Guanbei had seized their place in the shipping trade.

While the new Dada board was still deliberating over the choice of chairman and general manager, Du and his party, fearing they might yet fail to break into Dada, staged a "martial play" besides. They set a gang man, Dai Buxiang, to lead a band of followers in seizing the foreman's post at the Dada wharf, driving the men of the original foreman, Zhang Jinyan, off the wharf. An armed brawl broke out between the two sides; the police chief, Cai Jinjun, came in person with a great body of plainclothes and armed police to put it down, sealed off the Dada wharf, cut off the traffic, and left Dada's steamers unable to load or unload, so that they had to lie at anchor beside the wharf. In fact the situation was not so very serious, and had no one meant to make an issue of it, it need never have come to this. Dai Buxiang's men slit a layer of their own skin with a sharp knife, drew a little blood, then claimed grievous injury, and for days on end gathered menacingly, refusing to let the matter drop, deliberately keeping Dada's ships from sailing. In the end Zhang Xiaoruo had to ask Xu Yuan (a former Shanghai commissioner of foreign affairs) to come and smooth it over — Dai Buxiang was proprietor of the Great China Hotel, of which Xu was chairman — and the Dada Company paid out 3,000 yuan to compensate Dai Buxiang for his "losses," settling the wharf affair first of all. Having suffered this grave threat, the company found the question of who should run it taking a sudden turn, and with Shi Liangcai's mediation coming just then, the thing fell into place of itself: Du Yuesheng, Yang Guanbei, and the others took up their posts, and had broken into the Dada Company. Zhang Jinyan, though a gang man, was no disciple of Du Yuesheng's and had to give way; and Dai Buxiang, once the dispute was settled, was led by Yang Guanbei to kowtow and take Du Yuesheng as his master.

### (3) The Company After Du Broke In

After Zhang Xiaoruo became general manager he took no part in the company's affairs and let Yang Guanbei do as he pleased in everything. When Du first entered Dada he declared that he would draw no salary, only petrol money and carriage money; how the truth of that stood, no outsider could know. From then on Dada never once held a shareholders' meeting and rarely held a board meeting, and year after year it stopped paying even a dividend, and not a shareholder dared say a word against it. Du Yuesheng had at first declared, too, that he would turn no one out and take no new man on; but his very first shot on taking office was to engage Yang Zhixiong as supervisor of Dada, in reward for Yang's services in the planning. Before long he brought in many who had served him before — the business chief Xuan Chunshu, the warehouse chief Yan Ruiwu, the chief inspector Chen Runqing, and so on — all of them Du's own men. After Du Yuesheng entered Dada, the enterprise did develop somewhat: for after the two steamers Daji and Dade were burned, Du used his power to borrow from the banks and had a new steamer, the Xin Dada, built to order, of 1,600 tons, making good and more than making good the tonnage lost; and with further bank loans he built at the Shiliupu wharf a huge godown of steel-reinforced concrete.

In Zhang Jian's day at the helm, the Dada Company had monopolized the northern Jiangsu route, and the other steamship companies looked on with watering mouths but could not compete with it. Once the Zhang family's power waned and Dada was reorganized, the shipping world thought its chance had come, and from then on a fierce competition began.

### (1) The Rivalry with the Datong Renji Navigation Company

The earliest forerunner of the Datong Renji Navigation Company was the Yongji Navigation Company, founded by the salt-trader Yang Zaitian, and later reorganized as the Datong Xieji Company. Yang went by the nickname "Sea-Churning Golden Dragon" and stood higher in the brotherhood's ranks than Du Yuesheng. In 1924 Yang, together with Lu Bohong and Zhu Zhiyao, set up the Datong Renji Navigation Company. Lu and Zhu were both Catholics; Lu had been made a "Knight of the Cape and Sword" by the Pope in Rome, and Zhu was the bishop of Haimen. They had gathered the capital of the "Catholic Action" society, held a considerable place in the Catholic Church, and commanded a social force of another kind. The Datong Company's ships ran the northern Jiangsu line under a special license granted by Zhang Jian in his day, but the open and hidden struggle between the two companies never ceased. After Du Yuesheng and Yang Guanbei entered Dada, the two companies were evenly matched in tonnage, competing openly for cargo and secretly cutting rates, and the struggle grew fiercer than before. The Dada Company had the Tianshenggang-Tongjing wharf and warehouses, where Datong's ships were not allowed to berth, so that Datong was plainly at a disadvantage. Yang Zaitian, since Du Yuesheng was likewise a man of the brotherhood, went in person to Du's door to ask for an alliance, and made Du a present of 3,000 yuan of Datong shares; and just then, with the Ping'an, the Daxing, the Sanbei, and other companies all coveting the northern Jiangsu route, the conditions were ripe for the Dada and Datong companies to join against the field. By agreement between them a joint Dada-Datong office was set up, which pooled the freight receipts by an agreed percentage, and from then on the two companies made common cause to shut out the other ships that plied against them.

### (2) The Rivalry with the Ping'an Steamship Company

The proprietor of the Ping'an Steamship Company, Zheng Xitang, was comprador of the British firm Gongmao; besides keeping steamers of his own, he had also taken over the British Old Gongmao shipyard, so that he combined two waterborne businesses in one. After Dada's steamer Dade was burned, Gongmao bought up the wreck of the Dade, repaired it, renamed it the Datong, set up the Yixiang Shipping Bureau, made Li Yaoting — deputy manager of the Ping'an Company — concurrently manager of the Yixiang, and, on the pretext that the Dade had originally run the northern Jiangsu line, demanded a share in the northern Jiangsu traffic. Zheng Xitang, taking advantage of the personnel turmoil while Du Yuesheng and Yang Guanbei were newly into Dada, contrived to win over Yang Qingbang, who then had charge of Dada's customs-clearance business, meaning to give him the title of deputy manager of the Yixiang and turn him to his own use, and hoping, through Yang Qingbang's connections, to draw over the men at the various northern Jiangsu ports as well, to gather cargo and passengers for the Yixiang Shipping Bureau. When Yang Guanbei learned of this, he at once promoted Yang Qingbang to be director of general affairs of the Dada Company, to set his mind at rest; and, at the same time, paying the man back in his own coin, he secretly bought Li Yaoting over. Li then turned about and urged Zheng Xitang to sell the Datong. With no footing left, within or without, Zheng had to agree to sell the Datong for 120,000 yuan. Yet Yang Guanbei did not buy it in under the Dada name, but registered it with the Shipping Administration under the name of Huan Zhongfu, still under the sign of the Yixiang Shipping Bureau, made Li Yaoting manager of the Yixiang, and subleased the ship to the Dada-Datong office. This Yixiang Shipping Bureau was a "shell company," with a sign but no office and no staff, its one seal kept in Li Yaoting's coat pocket. The purchase money had been borrowed, through Du Yuesheng's connections, from the Bank of Communications; and repayment did not begin until the currency had fallen in value after the victory in the war.

### (3) The Rivalry with the Daxing and Sanbei Steamship Companies

Long before Du Yuesheng broke in, the shipping business of the British firm Xiangmao had been crushed by the Dada Company, and its riverside agents (where a steamship company kept no branch office at a port of call, local men would set up an "agency" to handle its cargo and passenger business for a commission) had been out of work for years and were forever restless for a move. After the Dada Company was reorganized, they grew lively again and got the Daxing Commercial Steamship Company and the Sanbei Steamship Company to send ships onto the northern Jiangsu route. The Sanbei's proprietor, Yu Qiaqing, was a figure of great note in his day, who treated the Shanghai Shipping Guild as his personal instrument; and the Daxing's proprietor, Xu Zhongxin, a fellow Ningbo man close to Yu Qiaqing, was intimate with him. The out-of-work agents, having found these two backers, felt sure of carrying the day at a stroke, and raised funds to help Xu Zhongxin buy from Jardine Matheson the steamer Changhe, which he renamed the Daxing; and this ship, together with Yu Qiaqing's Yongjia, applied at the same time to run the northern Jiangsu line. The agents waved the flag and beat the drum to lend the two companies their support, and got gentry and merchants everywhere to petition and clamor for the northern Jiangsu route to be opened, raising a considerable din. But the chief of the Shanghai Shipping Administration, Wu Yong, refused to issue certificates for the two ships to ply the northern Jiangsu line. Yu Qiaqing flew into a rage and went up to the Minister of Communications, Yu Feipeng, to demand redress. But the Ministry's ruling came down: "Until the national plan for shipping cooperation is published, all routes are to be kept as they stand and are not to be changed." Yu Qiaqing had run against a hard nail. Yet the Ministry's order did not square with the actual facts of the time: there were plenty of cases of steamers changing routes, and only the northern Jiangsu route was under this restriction — and even on the northern Jiangsu route the restriction fell only on the other companies, the Dada Company being the exception. When the Dada-Datong office chartered the Yixiang Shipping Bureau's Datong, for instance, and the Dada Company chartered the Hanping and other steamers, and other tugs and iron barges, from the Hanyeping Coal and Iron Company, the Shipping Administration issued certificates for them all — which Yu took for a case of "the magistrate may burn down houses, but the common folk may not so much as light a lamp." At this the friction between him and Du Yuesheng grew all the sharper, and, with his riverside agents and their underlings, he kept up the attack and would not give over. The Shipping Administration and the Ministry of Communications, unable to reconcile their own words, were driven to it at last, and in 1934 convened a Committee for the Regulation of Shipping Routes, hinting that the shipping world should thrash the matter out for itself. Under the mediation of the shipping world it was fixed that the Daxing Company's Daxing should run the Beisha line (from Shanghai to Haimen and Qidong), and that the Sanbei Company's Yongjia should be chartered by the Dada-Datong office to run the northern Jiangsu line. Only then did the struggle between Du and Yu over the northern Jiangsu route come to an end.

Writing this far, I am put in mind of the great squeeze Du Yuesheng put on Huang Zhendong, proprietor of the Huaxin Steamship Company. As I have said, Yang Guanbei, to make himself agreeable to Du Yuesheng, had made a great show of himself in the boycott of Japanese goods after the May Third Incident; and this squeeze of Du's, too, was worked through Yang Guanbei.

There was in the shipping trade at that time a Huaxin Company, the sole proprietorship of Huang Zhendong, which owned two steamers, the Huagou and the Huada, of seven or eight thousand tons each, and had all along been in collusion with the Japanese steamship company Yamashita Kaisha. Huang Zhendong's father, Huang Jingquan, kept the Yuanhe Sugar House in Shanghai and had made his start dealing in Java sugar; and by this family origin Huang Zhendong's own Huaxin Company also traded in sugar. They had at first shipped their sugar on the steamers of the Dutch firm, the Java-China Line; but, seeing the profit flow out to foreigners, Huang Zhendong later kept steamers of his own to carry it. The sugar the Huaxin Company dealt in was Java sugar, and Japanese sugar too. Of all this — because Yang Guanbei and Huang Zhendong's younger brother were sworn brothers — Yang Guanbei had a very clear knowledge, and it gave him his chance to lay a snare.

The boycott of Japanese goods was, on the face of it, carried out with the utmost rigor: the proprietor of the Yuelai provisions shop on Guangdong Road, Bi Fanglai, for selling Japanese goods, was crowned with a tall paper hat and led through the streets, and every merchant without a backer was frightened out of his wits. But the more rigorous the boycott, the more chances there were for Du Yuesheng, Chen Qun, Yang Hu, and the rest to band together and put on the squeeze. Chen Qun and Yang Hu played the black-faced villains and Du Yuesheng the white-faced one: once they had marked a target, it was always Du Yuesheng who came out to mediate and settle it, turning the great into the small and the small into nothing, and leaving the victim of the squeeze endlessly grateful. The affair of Huang Zhendong was a case in point.

Yang Guanbei spread a net over Huang Zhendong on every side. Once, when the "Grand Alliance for Economic Severance" (commonly called the Boycott Association) summoned the merchants to a meeting at the Tianhou Temple on Henan Road, Huang Zhendong was among those summoned. All those called had to declare their hearts at the meeting, and Huang Zhendong declared: "I have never trafficked in Japanese sugar, and I will never traffic in it hereafter." No sooner were the words out of his mouth than the staff of the Huaxin Company produced account-books and receipts on the spot, denouncing Huang for trafficking in Japanese sugar and pointing out that some of it was in the warehouse and some already in transit, to the amount of several thousand tons. The evidence being conclusive, Huang Zhendong was seized then and there and, early the next morning, was handed over to the Shanghai County government in Nanshi.

The tenser the situation was made, the more Huang Zhendong's family took fright; and since Huang Zhendong's younger brother and Yang Guanbei were sworn brothers, the family inevitably came to Yang Guanbei's door. Yang Guanbei, playing now the good man and now the bad, asked "Mr. Du" to come to the rescue; and in the end, on Huang Zhendong's laying out 400,000 yuan, Du Yuesheng bailed him out. With that 400,000 yuan in hand, Chen Qun and Yang Hu naturally held their peace as well.

In Huang Zhendong's eyes Du Yuesheng was a benefactor high as heaven, who had saved his life and preserved his fortune. And since the Huaxin Company was bound up inseparably with the Japanese firm Yamashita Kaisha, Huang Zhendong needed Du Yuesheng's protection all the more urgently, and so he kowtowed and took Du as his master. When Du Yuesheng's family shrine in Pudong was dedicated, Huang Zhendong went so far as to have a splendid pleasure-yacht built to order, at a cost of 100,000 yuan, at the Jiangnan Shipyard, naming it the Yuebao and setting it aside for Du Yuesheng's outings on the Huangpu.

The Boycott Association, making a name of resisting Japanese goods while in fact practicing extortion, drew the censure of Shanghai opinion. Du Yuesheng, Chen Qun, and Yang Hu had to find a scapegoat and gloss themselves clean. It happened that one Yin Zhiheng of the Boycott Association had put a squeeze on Ye Hongying; and so Yang Hu, in his capacity as garrison commander, had him shot, to throw dust in people's eyes.
