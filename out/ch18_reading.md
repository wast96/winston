## The Ishida Mansion

A little before that hour, Kohagi sat in a room of the Komatsudani villa, facing the wandering monk Dokutan across an inner dry-landscape garden where only a banana plant put out its sparse leaves.

"And so, what did Lord Jūzō say?"

"No, it was wasted breath. They say the Vairocana Buddha brought even the heathen to the Law, but a heathen like that one, who dreams of the beauty of hell, is more than my powers can master."

"But then, surely, that leaves Kohagi in difficulty?"

"That is no affair of mine. It was you who set your heart on such a man to begin with. Pine after a man possessed by the beauty of hell, and you'll surely fall into hell for his company, Lady Kohagi."

"How heartless. ...I'd never have thought it of you, Master Dokutan."

"Ha-ha-ha. Now there's a fine feeling. To be resented so by a beautiful woman melts old Dokutan, body and soul, I do assure you."

Dokutan turned his eyes to the garden and laughed, broad and open, the white muscle of his cheek standing out thick and hard in profile.

Now and then a wind crossed the garden and stirred the little shade the banana leaves cast among the green stones.

"A good wind's blowing."

So said Dokutan.

"It was not the wind we were speaking of."

"Ah, but it is the wind. A human life is born out of aeons beyond counting and scatters away into aeons beyond counting. The wind is no different. From where it comes, to where it blows off, no one knows. Only in the moment it brushes your cheek does a man call it wind. And yet, has anyone ever seen the wind?"

"The wind is hardly a thing one sees."

Kohagi said it with a touch of vexation.

"Just so. The wind cannot be seen. All that is there is the show of it, the banana leaves swaying. A human life, too, is a phantom like the wind, and cannot be seen with the eye. The one thing that stands there as fact is the deeds of a man's whole life. That, surely, any eye can see, as the eye sees the banana bend when the wind stirs it."

"As always, Master Dokutan, you've gone and made the talk difficult."

"No, no, I'm speaking of Jūzō. All I meant to say is that there are a great many ways to live out a life. If the way a man raises up for himself is beautiful to him, then it is good enough. Every life is free to be lived just as it pleases. And every one of them, in the end, is blown to dust and scattered. I saw the whole absurdity of it: that I, who will be scattered to the same dust, should go and lodge a complaint against another man's way of living, and tell him, that way of yours is not beautiful. And it was Jūzō, of all people, who without a word made me see it."

"What a faint-hearted Master Dokutan you are."

Kohagi smiled, and:

"I'll ask you no longer. Kohagi will change Lord Jūzō's way of living herself, with a woman's wit and a woman's truth. You'll see."

"A woman's truth?"

Dokutan let his mouth fall a little open, as though startled, looked hard at Kohagi's face, and then quietly slapped his knee.

"It may be so, at that. Yes. A woman's truth. Well, well."

"Are you making sport of me?"

"Not at all. I'm honestly astonished, that is all. It was like a thunderclap out of a clear sky. A woman's truth. I am, as you see me, a wandering monk; I had clean forgotten that any such strange thing existed in the world. It is a truly fearsome thing."

"There you go, teasing again."

"No, I'm not mocking you. And so, when do you mean to set this truth of yours to work?"

"Oh. The way you speak of it, Master Dokutan. I'd rather not say."

"I'm in dead earnest. There's no man in the world a woman's love won't soften, and above all no man a woman's wit won't lead astray. But once the talk turns to that sort of thing, a monk like me has no choice but to withdraw."

When he had said it, Dokutan drew his knees a little forward, and:

"Still, the sooner you set that power of yours to work, the better. Leave it too late, and the great chance may slip away. When I looked Jūzō over the other day..."

"What? You saw Lord Jūzō? ..."

"This is a hunch. Only a hunch, but I doubt it's wrong. Behind that man's manner, his mood, his every movement, there's the settled calm of one whose preparations are all complete. He'll have scouted the castle he means to slip into, most likely. And Hideyoshi's comings and goings he'll have worked out to the day, down to the last detail, no question of it. The look of him now is the look of a man with nothing left to do but wait for his hour."

"And the time?"

"There is none."

Catching up the tail of her words, Dokutan nodded with relish, for all the world like the sole spectator at a play.

"Then, Master Dokutan. I am truly loath to ask it of you, but..."

"What is it, then? You've still some request to make of me?"

"No. Only that, for today, I must beg you to take your leave."

"Well, you have me there. So the favor is that I go home? What a mercenary soul you are."

Dokutan got to his feet, and:

"Still, if a man that even a monk, sworn to ferry all living beings across, could make nothing of, is to be saved by this so-called truth of a woman, why, that would be a rare irony for holy men and laymen alike."

"But the world would go on well enough without holy men in it, Master; and without women it could not stand at all."

"Beaten, beaten. She's routed me utterly. Ah-ha-ha!"

Clowning, as if in flight, Dokutan sprang down into the garden. There was something so comic in the mischievous-boy air of him that Kohagi laughed for the first time in a long while, like a young girl.

Ishida, the Jibu-no-shō, kept his Fushimi mansion on ground within the outer works of the castle: thirty ken across the front, sixty deep, a building only just brought to completion in the early summer of Bunroku 3. Like his castle-seat at Sawayama in Ōmi, the place bore the stamp of Mitsunari's peculiar care. Its frame was raised on huge baulks of dark timber barely dressed with the adze; the walls were left for the most part as rough daub, and save on the side that faced the front they were not even given a finish coat. Dried cuttlefish had been worked into the wall-clay against a day of siege, to be eaten in the last extremity; and in the same spirit arrow-bamboo had been planted all through the grounds, so that the whole mansion gave concrete form to a battlefield-mindedness carried to excess. The corridors went deliberately unpolished, floored only with thick, close-grained cedar planking. Besides the samurai barracks, the chief buildings within the compound were two: a White Study, which Mitsunari used for official business while he was in Fushimi, and a Black Study, which his general Shima Sakon Katsutake used for his own duties.

Shima Sakon, at this time a small-built commander just over the crest of middle age, had begun as a retainer of the Tsutsui house of Yamato, and his valor and cunning were known across the realm early on. Later, for reasons of his own, he quit his lord's service and lived in retirement at Takamiya, on the banks of the Inukami River in his native Ōmi. In Tenshō 13, when Ishida Mitsunari was first raised to the junior fifth rank, lower grade, as Jibu-no-shō and made a daimyō with a domain of forty thousand koku, the very first thing Mitsunari did was to cut away nearly half of that stipend, fifteen thousand koku, to take Shima Sakon on as his chief retainer. Today, with Mitsunari one of the five commissioners, lord of one hundred and ninety-four thousand koku at Sawayama in Ōmi and wielding power and favor under the Toyotomi government, it is still said that most of his policies, at home and abroad, come out of Sakon.

After sunset that day Sakon was in his private room in the Black Study, going over the Ishida house's provisioning ledgers for the Korea campaign by the light of a candlestand, when all at once he became aware of a presence at his back.

A man of Sakon's caliber knew that at such a moment a careless word or the least movement would only panic the intruder into driving home a desperate blade. He held his posture exactly as it was, turning the pages of the ledger, and tried to draw in the intruder's presence through his back. Oddly, the presence behind him showed no sign of moving further. Stranger still, there was none of what one would call bloodlust in it at all.

Even Shima Sakon, who years later would fight the most savage single combat of the whole Warring-States age and die a death worthy of a warrior of that time, on the field of Sekigahara, had one weakness. He was, a little, a man for women. Just possibly, he thought, it was some serving-girl he had lately favored, come stealing to him; and unguarded relief showed in him for an instant. It showed at his back. The one behind him caught it in a flash, and, as if to strike at the opening:

"Sir Shima."

came the voice. Sakon started, as though his shadow had been trodden on. There was a slyness in whoever it was that one could almost hate for its skill.

"Who's there?"

"It is Kohagi."

And so it was. Kohagi, whom he had not seen in a long while, knelt now with the knees of her black costume drawn neatly together, her white hands set on the matting, her lashes lowered.

"What brings you? At this hour."

Sakon turned slowly on his knees toward Kohagi and spoke. That the girl should come calling by night, and in such a guise, did not surprise him. When there was need to meet, she would present herself in this very dress even at the Jibu-no-shō's own bedchamber. It was the work that required it, of course; and yet, because she came of a line that carried the blood of the Sasaki, the great house of Ōmi, neither Mitsunari nor Sakon treated her as a mere spy. To both she was, first and last, a daughter of the Sasaki, and they dealt with her courteously.

Kohagi kept silent. Sakon smiled, and:

"If you say nothing, how am I to know? Tell Sakon whatever it is. His lordship has gone off to Nagoya on the Korea business, but as for you, Kohagi, he left the strictest word that I was to look after you in his absence. Come, is it the Kiwano matter?"

"No. That I have already reported, in writing."

"So you did. Then—"

He began, and checked himself. He had noticed that Kohagi's eyes, looking up at him, were strung unusually tight, not their ordinary selves. Seeing some feeling she did not usually show hidden in her eyes, Sakon judged that the kinder course here was to spare his words until she brought the matter out herself.

The dark before the garden stirred, and a quiet cool wind came blowing through. Sakon narrowed his eyes as if to savor it a while, and let his body rest against the armrest.

"The thing is—"

Kohagi said it as though she had made up her mind. Slowly, Sakon's gaze came round from the garden. In the eyes he now bent on her upturned face there was a gentle light.

"What is it?"

"You once told Kohagi, Sir Sakon, an orphan girl with no one of her own, that if ever anything should befall her she was to rely on you as an uncle. If you have forgotten it, I beg you to call it to mind."

"No, I've not forgotten. It was three years ago, I think, when the Jibu-no-shō presented you, Kohagi, to me as the last of the Sasaki line. At that meeting, pitying your circumstances and your work, I made bold to say it. So, then. This 'if ever' of yours, has it come to pass?"

"Yes."

"Speak it, then."

"It is love."

"Oho. If it's love, why, a man like Sakon has done it many times over half a lifetime, and can see nothing so very grave in it—"

"For a man, perhaps. But for a woman, love comes once in a lifetime. Kohagi is staking her life on it now."

At that, it was Sakon rather than Kohagi whose face quietly stiffened.

"Who is he?"

He had asked it as a matter of form, but Sakon's quick judgment had already given Kohagi up. Whoever the man might be, a woman spy who had once come to know love was of next to no use now, her value close to zero; and there was no use, he knew, in trying to argue her round. But that was not all of it. Behind Sakon's grave face a warm feeling was beginning to rise, like a spring tide, in pity for the girl. As has been said, Shima Sakon was a man who, some years on, would meet his end on the field of Sekigahara. In brightness of stratagem his lord Mitsunari was no match for him. He had known the recklessness of his lord's rising in arms, and had remonstrated with him on both its timing and its method; but once he saw how fixed Mitsunari's resolve was, he threw himself, with all his passion, into the planning and the carrying-out of that war, until death should take him from it. Sakon's whole life was run through by that one passion: to die for a man who truly knew him. It was for that pure, clear rapture that his heart stood always ready to be moved. And in the depths of Sakon's heart now, faced with Kohagi, that same string must have begun to sound its high note. The Kohagi within his breast showed as the image of a pitiable woman, born to noble blood and made wretched for the very fact of it. She would live out a woman's whole life, and grow old, inside a monstrous fate that had robbed her of herself. And that Kohagi had now come to love, and was ready to die for that love. The pity of it dissolved, scattering a clear, transparent pattern through Sakon's unknowing heart.

"Who is he?"

Sakon said it once more. His voice was quiet, drowsy almost.

"...He is—"

Kohagi faltered, and it took her the better part of another small half-hour of silence before she could bring out the name: Tsuzura Jūzō, the Iga man. Through all that time Sakon waited, unhurried, his eyes closed in the cool of the wind.

Even when Kohagi finally spoke the name, he took care to show no surprise. He said only, lightly:

"You mean to make your life with him."

Kohagi looked down and said nothing. She wanted to nod. But surely it was a thing with far too little hope of ever coming true.

"A promise is a promise. I'll lend you my strength."

Sakon said it as though it were nothing. Kohagi's eyes flew up.

"But..."

"You needn't say it. That the man is the one who, put up to it by Sōkyū, means to cut short the days of His Highness the Taikō, that much I already know, from your own reports. So tell me: is he a man fine enough to be worth your falling for?"

"Y-yes."

"I see. Now this is a rare feast indeed."

Sakon smiled, the skin creasing beneath his eyes.

"But by what means...?"

That was the whole question. To Kohagi, who edged toward him on her knees as if clinging, Sakon answered:

"I don't know. Lady Kohagi, you've brought me a riddle that neither the gods nor the buddhas could unravel. But Katsutake is not a man to decide yes or no by whether there's a means to hand. Having once said yes, Katsutake has said yes. The working of it out I'll turn over later, after a smoke."

……

"Sōkyū, the Ōkurakyō Hōin of Sakai, and his scheme of treason: that much is plain now. But think a moment. No act of treason has actually been committed, and there is no proof of the plot. The one piece of evidence, if it can be called that, is the Iga man Tsuzura Jūzō, and he alone."

……

"First, we take Jūzō and cut him down in secret. That is the essential thing. As for Sōkyū at the heart of it, we don't drag the conspiracy into the open, we lay no finger on Sōkyū himself, we let the whole matter pass unquestioned. Sōkyū will carry on his trade as before, appraising his tea things, holding forth on the way of tea, and end his days in peace. The realm, if you want a figure for it, is like a single great tiger. A tiger sleeps quietly only under two conditions: when it has a keeper strong enough to crush it, and when it is gorged full with feeding. Just now the keeper of the realm of Japan has grown old and feeble. And with troops sent across to Korea, the land is at last beginning to go hungry. The tiger may wake one day and run mad; but for the moment it lies sleeping. To seize Sōkyū now and bring the whole background of his plot into the light would be to go out of our way to tread on the tiger's tail. Far better to keep everything quiet around us until the season comes."

"Then you mean to cut down Lord Jūzō, no, Tsuzura Jūzō, and him alone?"

"That was my reckoning. Mind you, that was last night's thinking. Well, how shall it be? Won't you pour me a cup?"

Sakon took up a gourd that lay beside him and held it out to Kohagi. She edged closer on her knees and received it in both hands. It was old, with a sheen on it like lacquer. Sakon's gourd was famous in the world. Time and again it had gone with him and his spear to the battlefield and back, and under that strange black gloss, no doubt, the blood and sweat of the field had soaked deep.

Sakon brought the cup to his lips and said:

"You ask me to let this Jūzō live. Is that not a hard riddle indeed? For my own part, I'd gladly mow down not only Sōkyū but the Tokugawa Naifu and his whole line, and cut the root of the trouble clean away. But do that, and the land falls back into the disorders of the Genki and Tenshō years. His Highness's work of unification comes to nothing. Whereas kill Jūzō alone, here within the Ishida house, then wipe our mouths and put on an innocent face, and everything passes off without a hitch. Is it not so?"

"Jūzō is not a man to be cut down so tamely."

"Not even with the whole of the Ishida soldiery?"

"Not even with every soldier in the realm."

"You overrate the man."

Sakon did not rise to it, and went on:

"The dagger hidden in Sōkyū's breast is, you might say, this Iga man Jūzō. To cut Jūzō down is to draw that hidden dagger out of Sōkyū's bosom and throw it away. Though in truth he need not even be killed; one can simply draw the blade. Render him useless as a dagger, and the thing is done. And that— But it seems you have no confidence, Lady Kohagi, that you can draw the very core out of Jūzō. For a woman, that may be a somewhat shameful thing to own."

"A man has his own way of living, surely. Would you, Sir Sakon, change your way of life because a woman's feeling had drawn you to it?"

"There you have me."

"This man Jūzō, I think, is much like you, Sir Sakon. He means to paint the finished picture of his life, with his own life for the pigment. Even were the plot laid bare and the Hōin Sōkyū of Sakai put to death, Jūzō alone might carry the thing through, clear of all gain and loss, for the sake of his passion and nothing else. Could a heart like that be melted by any tenderness of a woman's?"

"That is a type one meets often enough among assassins. But even if you ask me, I cannot tell without standing in Jūzō's own skin. A man, as I hold it, is like a hunter racing across the wild country with the arrow of his own passion set to the string. Every man lives with some vision of a wilderness painted in his dreams. And a woman, I'd say, is the officer heaven sends to cage that hunter. How cunningly she can get him into the cage, there, surely, is a kind of contest between the wits of the woman and the man."

"One like Kohagi has no such wit."

"If you haven't it, there's no chance a man like me would."

"Oh. But you said only a moment ago that you would lend me your strength. Please, teach me how."

"This is a hard one. That Jūzō of yours, above all, seems a hunter possessed by the wilderness, so much so that even I could half envy him. As for a scheme to catch him, nothing comes to me at all. Still, let us do this. Sakon will lend you as many of his own men as you like, Lady Kohagi. Use them freely, as beaters, to drive the hunter out of the field. But the caging of him is yours to do, a woman's task, first and last. And yet, if the hunter should break through the beaters' net and come near His Highness's residence, then I cut him down. The safety of the realm is not to be weighed against anything. That much you understand, I take it."

"Yes. It cannot be helped. If it comes to that, Kohagi will cut his life short with her own hand."

"Good, good. With spirit like that in you, there's no saying you can't get him into the cage after all. Most likely— here—"

and Sakon, tapping Kohagi's knee lightly with the base of the emptied gourd, went on:

"Now here's an exquisite cage, I'd say. This Jūzō, for all that he excels in the arts of war, looks to be a man who has never known the good things of life."

and for the first time he gave the rich, lecherous laugh that was so like Sakon.
