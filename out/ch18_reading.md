## 4

The Battle of Shanghai broke out the day after the Japanese Naval Landing Force came ashore.

The battle lasted three months, and for those three whole months the mail routes in the concession were cut. Zhongliang, though, was busy. Day and night he carried the things Zhou San gave him to their appointed places, and sometimes carried things back: half a pack of cigarettes, an old fountain pen, a few expired lottery tickets.

This day, as Zhou San put a box of Jintan pastilles into his hand, Zhongliang said suddenly, "You had so many people. You could have saved him."

Zhou San started and asked, "Saved whom?"

Zhongliang said nothing, only looked at him.

It was a while before Zhou San said, "We tried. But the Japanese moved too fast."

Zhongliang dropped his eyes, took the Jintan, and turned and walked out of the gatehouse.

Zhou San called after him through the window, "Remember: it isn't you people. It's we."

Zhongliang gave no sign of hearing. He got on his bicycle and rode off without once looking back.

The streets were full of refugees and wounded. Planes swept low over people's heads and dived toward the thickest of the gunfire, and the blasts carried from the banks of Suzhou Creek set every pane of glass rattling.

After Zhongliang handed the Jintan to a shop-hand at a silk-and-cloth store, he went out of his way to the Siming Apartments on Rue Ratard, crept up the stairs, and slipped an envelope through the crack of the door of No. 203. There was no name on the letter and no address; inside was only a poem by Shelley, or sometimes by Byron. These were Zhongliang's two favorite poets. He always felt his own love ought to be like their poems, splendid and sorrowful.

Like a thief, Zhongliang slipped a love poem through the crack of Su Lina's door every day. Then he would draw back to the street and steal a glance up through the bare branches of the plane trees. On the balcony hung a kingfisher-green cheongsam and some women's underthings. Yesterday it had been a printed bedsheet; the day before, two silk slips; but never once on that balcony did he see Su Lina.

One day, over a game of chess with Zhou San, Zhongliang hesitated a long time and said, "I passed the Siming Apartments today."

Zhou San pushed his chariot forward and said, "Check."

"What's her name?" said Zhongliang.

Zhou San's head came up at once, and a light flared in his eyes and died, past all telling. Zhou San said, "There's no road left for you to live now."

Zhongliang looked down at the board. He knew there was much he ought not to ask, and that no one would tell him, but still he wanted to say it: "You have me do your work. Surely you can let me know who you people are."

Zhou San pressed his lips tight, and only when he had set the pieces back on the board did he say slowly, "When it's time for you to know, you'll be told."

"When is that?" Zhongliang kept his eyes stubbornly on the pieces.

"Play," said Zhou San.

But Zhongliang came to know all the same that the woman he thought of every day was called Su Lina.

A few days after Shanghai fell, the mail routes opened, and letters by the countless sackful were carried into the concession. The postmaster seemed to breathe again; he bowed low to all the postmen and said, "These next days will ask a great deal of you."

It was on his round that Zhongliang saw those letters, in kraft-paper envelopes, seven in all, every one addressed to No. 203, Siming Apartments, Rue Ratard, to a Miss Su Lina. Zhongliang stood with the letters at the door of the Siming Apartments, wavered a good while, did not go in, but turned and rode off fast on his bicycle.

That night Zhongliang came home with no thought for cooking. He boiled a kettle of water and, over the steam, carefully worked open the seals of the letters.

The water boiled on the stove, but Zhongliang's heart went cold by degrees. So she was married. So her husband was an officer, who had fallen back with his unit from Shanghai to Nanjing, and from Nanjing to Wuhan. All this while he had been fighting the Japanese. He loved his country so, and missed his wife so.

The water in the kettle boiled dry and the fire in the stove went out. Zhongliang sat on in the dark, as though he sat in a bottomless abyss.

The next day he knocked at the door of No. 203, Siming Apartments, and as he put the letters into Su Lina's hands, she said, "Wait a moment." Su Lina turned back into the room and came out with a sheaf of envelopes, held them out to him, said nothing, only looked at him. Her gaze was as indifferent as ever, languid. Zhongliang felt he could sink through the floor for shame; he turned and fled down the stairs, rushing out into the street in a single breath. A crowd of Japanese soldiers came surging along Rue Ratard just then, marching; in the biting wind they seemed to feel no cold at all, in nothing but white shirts, white bands bound round their foreheads, fists raised like demonstrators, shouting slogans no one could understand. Flanking them on both sides were the concession's police and soldiers of every nation, fully armed, eyes wide, fixed hard on these unarmed Japanese troops. Zhongliang halted at the roadside and, without thinking, raised his head. He saw Su Lina leaning on the balcony rail, a shawl wrapped round her, a cigarette in one hand and the letters in the other, gazing down at the street with a look of deep thought.
