## Chapter Eighteen. The Hour of the Tiger, First Half (3 a.m.)

{d} The third year of Tianbao, the fifteenth day of the first month, the hour of the Tiger, first half.

{d} Chang’an; Wannian County; the Anyi–Changle crossing.

From the moment the Lantern-Crown Red Tally had, a little while before, flung out the lit candle, Li Bi had kept close behind that four-windowed carriage belonging to the Eastern Palace. He had not, however, hurried up to declare himself, but held back a stretch of distance and followed quietly.

Li Bi gripped the reins, his body leaning forward, his legs lightly clamped to the horse’s belly, holding a posture from which he could give chase at any instant. But he dared not press too close, for a dreadful surmise was even now taking shape in him. This thought was what the Daoists call a ‘demon of the heart’: the more one resists it, the stronger it grows, seizing on any opening to break in, twining about the heart like a creeping vine, until it choked his breath and left him cold within.

Once this four-windowed carriage had left the Xingqing Palace, it had passed through the Anyi–Changle crossing and gone straight on toward the south. This was a strange direction to take, for the heir apparent’s residence lay in Changle Ward, within the Ten Kings’ Residence in the annexed park-city east of the Anguo Temple; to be heading south now was to go plainly the wrong way.

Neither attending the spring banquet nor returning to his own mansion—on so fine a night as this, where in the world could the heir apparent be bound?

The streets hereabouts were thronged with common folk out to watch the lamps, all of them lost in rapt delight before the splendor of the distant lantern-tower; they were hardly likely to bow their heads and yield the way in deference merely because a crimson pennon-banner rose from a four-windowed carriage. The carriage pressed on impatiently, shouldering roughly through the packed crowd and raising a wake of curses and shouts—less the arrogance of rank than the headlong flight of a man who has lost his way in panic.

Only a few guardsmen rode in escort on either side of the four-windowed carriage; of ceremonial train there was none at all. That hand resting on the window-lattice tapped on and on in agitation, and did not still for a single moment.

Li Bi, crouched low over his horse’s back, glanced round now and then and saw the lantern-chambers of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower lighting up one after another. The common folk about him cried out again and again in glad astonishment, but the more he heard, the more his own heart filled with dread. Once all twenty-four lantern-chambers were lit, the Que-le Huo-duo would come back to life, and then, he feared, a great calamity would fall upon the whole city of Chang’an.

Before ever he set off after the carriage, he had had a word with General Chen Xuanli, warning him that fierce-fire thunder lay hidden within the lantern-tower and bidding him evacuate the Qinzheng Wuben Tower at once. Whether Chen Xuanli would heed him was past Li Bi’s power to control—and in any case, to begin the evacuation even now was already too late. There were several hundred guests on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, and tens of thousands of common people on the Xingqing Palace plaza; in such haste there was simply no way to get them clear of the blast’s reach.

The one hope was that Zhang Xiaojing might stop the lantern-tower from starting up in time. That was the sole hope the city of Chang’an had left.

At the very thought, Li Bi’s brows drew faintly together, and he strove to master that demon of the heart. But this time no method of the Dao availed him; the demon swelled swiftly, until it had all but tainted the whole of Li Bi’s spirit-terrace, forcing him along a train of thought he was most loath to follow.

At this delicate juncture in time, anyone who had left the Qinzheng Wuben Tower was worthy of suspicion.

So then—why should the heir apparent leave the Xingqing Palace at just this moment? Could it be that, knowing already that fierce-fire thunder lay within the lantern-tower, he had withdrawn ahead of time?

Once the thought was framed, it was like a sluice-gate flung open on a flood, past all gathering back in: the instant the fierce-fire thunder blew, the whole Qinzheng Wuben Tower would be ground to powder in a breath, and from the Son of Heaven down to the Right Minister not one soul would be spared—the whole upper tier of the court would be swept clean away.

All save the heir apparent. No—by that time, he would already be Emperor.

Li Bi’s heart clenched all at once, and his nails dug deep into the oxhide reins, leaving marks pressed hard into them. He could carry the reasoning no further; the further down it ran, the more it appalled him. Li Bi had known the heir apparent many years, and he could not believe that that honest, timid prince would ever do such a thing.

And yet... Li Heng was, when all was said, of the line of Li. In the blood of that house there had always lain buried a strain of kin-slaying ferocity. The bloodstains before the Xuanwu Gate could never quite be wiped away. At the thought, Li Bi’s body swayed in the saddle, and his confidence wavered.

Ahead, the carriage had by now drawn clear of the crowd-choked quarter, and its speed picked up. Li Bi bit the tip of his tongue, forcing himself to grow calm. He shook the reins and urged his own mount faster too, so as not to be shaken off.

The four-windowed carriage passed the wards of Changle, Jinggong, Xinchang, and Shengdao, its head set the whole way toward the south. Li Bi noted that the shafts pointed with unwavering resolve, and that the driver never hesitated for an instant at the crossings—which meant the carriage had a fixed destination in mind.

The lamps along the streets still burned bright, but this was, after all, the southern city now, and its bustle was not to be spoken of in the same breath as that of the north. To the east of this quarter ran the eastern wall of Chang’an; to the west rose the high slope of the Leyou Plateau—so that the two flanks stood up steep and the middle sank low, forming a valley-road within the city. The people of Chang’an all called this stretch the ‘Screened Gully.’ By day it was a fine place for an outing, but by night the high dark shadows of the walls stood on either hand, and the air of it was grim and forbidding.

As the four-windowed carriage entered the Screened Gully, its pace slowly slackened. When it reached the crossing of the Xiuxing–Shengping road, it turned suddenly to the right, grazing along the very edge of the southern foot of the Leyou Plateau as it went.

Li Bi, keeping hidden behind, turned the matter over swiftly in his mind, wondering what there could be hereabouts to rouse suspicion. Before he could hit upon it, the four-windowed carriage had already come to a stop, far off ahead.

There were not many households in this neighborhood, and no great bulk of lantern-frames; only at the more needful spots had a few thick leather wind-lanterns been hung, so that the light was none too good. At the place where the carriage had halted, one could see to the south a tall pagoda spire—the pagoda of the Tongfa Temple in Xiuxing Ward; and to the north stood a high blue-gray ward wall, in which was set a small toppled-stele gate. Such a gate, when opened, does not swing apart to left and right; instead the whole leaf of it topples forward and lies flat upon the ground, drawn by iron chains on either side so that it can be hauled back up again. Because it looks like a stone stele laid low, it takes its name from that.

In Chang’an, a ward wall was opened with a gate onto the street in only two cases: either by imperial grace, a gate permitted in commendation of some minister’s merit; or for some pressing practical use—such as the brick-kiln in Changming Ward where the Türk Wolf Guards had hidden themselves, which, because the volume of goods passing in and out was so great, had of necessity a second gate cut.

So then—what kind of place was this, that a toppled-stele gate had been opened in its ward wall? Li Bi’s gaze swept across it and marked that above the gate ran an arched band of openwork tracery, its pattern most intricate: honeysuckle, calamus, costus, shicaozi, and the like, leaf-and-flower motifs, all of them plants used in medicine.

Li Bi remembered at once: this was Shengping Ward, and within it lay a physic garden, kept expressly to raise all manner of medicinal herbs for the Eastern Palace. A physic garden needs great quantities of manure, soil, and cut greenery, and it was for the heir apparent’s use besides, so that a gate onto the street was natural enough. Li Bi recalled that Li Heng had once bestowed on him some herbal salves, and had boasted, not without pride, that he had grown, dried, and blended them himself; so it was from here, then, that the stuff had come.

But what could the heir apparent have come all this long way to a physic garden to do?

Doubts sprang up thick within Li Bi, and, wholly taken up with his thinking, he forgot to hold in the reins. The horse, seeing a light ahead and its master offering no check, took the matter into its own hooves and edged over toward it.

There were few passersby nearby, and the guards about the carriage, hearing the sound of hoofbeats, at once discovered Li Bi’s presence. Greatly on edge, they gave a warning shout and bared their weapons. The hand resting on the window-lattice of the four-windowed carriage shrank back all at once, like a startled hare.

Hearing the shout, and knowing his presence was already exposed, Li Bi simply swung down from his horse and called out loudly: ‘I am Li Bi, Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau!’ The guards all knew Li Bi well, and hearing that it was he, they lowered their weapons one and all. What the guards did not notice was that the four-windowed carriage gave the faintest of shudders.

‘I must see the heir apparent.’ Li Bi called out as he walked forward. The guards looked at one another, somewhat at a loss. The heir apparent was inside the four-windowed carriage, and must have heard the exchange outside perfectly clearly; yet from within the carriage came only unbroken silence, and no command was handed down.

‘I, Li Bi, Deputy Director of the Jing’an Bureau, beg audience of the heir apparent!’ Li Bi’s voice rose a few degrees louder, his feet never stopping, and the distance to the four-windowed carriage closed by a few more paces. His feelings were growing heated now; he had to get to the bottom of this business, whatever the bitterest cost it might exact.

Still no response came from within the four-windowed carriage. Li Bi’s steps halted all at once, and he looked to the north with knitted brows. The horses beside the carriage all turned their ears at the same moment, snorting uneasily through their nostrils. The guards, with no time to gentle their mounts, turned their own necks as one toward the north.

Man and horse alike had sensed it: a faint rumbling coming from far off, and, hard upon it, an uneasy tremor underfoot. Even though from this spot the whole northward view was blocked by the Leyou Plateau, Li Bi knew that something must have gone wrong at the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower.

***

The twenty-four lantern-chambers of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower fell mainly into three sections: the candle section, the lantern-figure section, and the mechanism section. The mechanism section lay buried deep in the lowest tier of the lantern-chamber, screened without by wood-veneer and silk; and within were the hook-rods that worked the lantern-figures—a hundred teeth meshing together, the secret art of Master Mao Shun that he passed on to no one.

When Yuchang had pushed home the scarlet long lever upon the wooden stand, the force was passed on through layer upon layer, and in an instant reached the mechanism sections within all twenty-four lantern-chambers. A bronze ratchet-wheel gave a sudden click and slipped a tooth out of true against the qilin-arm beside it. This small displacement let a lit candle slide directly beneath the qilin-arm, and its blazing flame licked just against the oil-soaked wick left bare outside.

The oil-wick caught with a whoosh. It was but a few inches long, and the sparks soon bored their way into the inside of the qilin-arm, burning on toward the inner sac.

The great wheels of the lantern-tower still turned rumbling round, their radiance solemn, glittering and resplendent; in all the city of Chang’an at that hour there was no more dazzling structure than this. The onlookers, lost in rapt delight, were sunk past all self-recall in its wondrous, mystic air.

A few dozen finger-snaps later, at the lower part of the ‘Martial Might’ lantern-chamber there burst out a point of exceedingly brilliant sparks. Amid a crack like thunder, the spark first turned into a crimson flower-heart, then swiftly gathered into a cluster of stamens; and then the stamens spread swiftly outward on every side, opening into petal upon petal of leaping, streaming fire. Seen from afar, it was as though a peony’s furious blooming had been sped up some dozens of times over, swallowing the whole tableau of lantern-figures in an instant.

Not one spectator took it for a mishap; they all thought it part of the show, and cheered with all their might, so wrought up they were near to madness.

The Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower did not disappoint them. Before long the fire-colored peonies of the other lantern-chambers bloomed in their turn, one after another—flower on brilliant flower, resplendent to the utmost, and the whole night sky lit up for it. The deafening explosions came one upon another, like the Thunder God beating out a quick measure on a jie-drum.

This string of violent explosions whipped up a hurricane all around. The music-band’s playing broke off short; a run of cries of alarm went up on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, and many officials and servants who had stood too near the railing were bowled off their feet, the whole scene thrown into disorder. Not a few of the common folk on the Xingqing Palace plaza were knocked down as well, setting off a small patch of confusion. Yet even this stirred no wariness in the multitude; more of them laughed aloud, awaiting with lively interest the next bit of spectacle.

When the first bursting was done, the cluster of lantern-chambers had turned into twenty-four huge torches, blazing fiercely up, so that the ground before the Xingqing Palace was bright as day. Several dozen lantern-figures stood wrapped in the raging flames, the colored lacquer of their faces peeling swiftly away, their limbs charred and withered, tongues of fire spurting from the seams of their bodies—yet still they moved, stiff and precise as ever, in a spectacle bewitching and uncanny. Had Chao Fen been there, he would likely have relished this hell-like scene.

Within the lantern-tower, Yuchang gazed in triumph toward Zhang Xiaojing, savoring the sight of that pitiful wretch all but toppled into the abyss. He had already set the mechanism working; the rite was complete; only a few dozen finger-snaps now stood between this moment and the full resurrection of the Que-le Huo-duo.

The rounds of fierce-fire thunder hidden in the lantern-chambers had all been carefully tuned: their bursting was the lesser matter—their chief purpose was to feed the flames. Now twenty-four seething columns of heat wrapped the Tianshu about from every side. The Tianshu still turned, like a lamb roasting slowly on a spit. Once the temperature rose high enough, the great round of fierce-fire thunder concealed within the body of the Tianshu would burst with terrible violence. And then, for miles around, all would be reduced to scorched earth.

And that pitiable wretch could only watch it all happen with his own eyes, powerless to stop it.

Yuchang was well pleased. Very seldom did he let his feelings show so nakedly; he had half a mind now even to spare Zhang Xiaojing’s life. The despair that had surfaced on that fellow’s face was truly too lovely a thing—like a jar of rich new Xinfeng wine poured into the mouth; he would have liked to relish it a while longer.

But this wish was fated to go unfulfilled. Once the mechanism was set going, he and Xiao Gui were quits, the one owing the other nothing. Next, he had to get swiftly clear of the lantern-tower before the blast—and there was still a score to settle with that swine Xiao Gui.

As for Zhang Xiaojing—let him be devoured by the Que-le Huo-duo together with the lantern-tower.

Even as Yuchang laid these plans, he stepped forward to set his foot down from the wooden stand. Before the sole of his foot had left the ground, he felt of a sudden a searing heat in the arch of it. Yuchang lowered his head to look—and first a gorgeous burst of light struck full into his eyes, and then flame exploded upward from below and wrapped his whole body in an instant.

Zhang Xiaojing, clinging to the rim of the wooden wheel, watched Yuchang turn into a man-shaped torch, flung high into the air by the violent shock, and then, tracing a bright arc, drop away into the darkness at the foot of the tower.

Xiao Gui had said he would not suffer this killer to live. Zhang Xiaojing had supposed he would tamper with the line of retreat; he had never thought it would be so crude and simple as this. Beneath the wooden stand, it seemed, a round of fierce-fire thunder had likewise been buried. The mechanism Yuchang set off had not only roused the twenty-four lantern-chambers—it had touched off this round of fierce-fire thunder beneath his own feet. With his own hands he had sent himself to the dead end of the road.

Zhang Xiaojing, his whole body hanging beneath the wooden wheel, had by luck dodged the greater part of the shock-wave. He had no leisure to sigh over it; gritting his teeth, he barely managed, before the muscles of his arms could cramp, to haul himself back up onto the wooden wheel.

By now twenty-four fiercely burning fireballs stood ringed about on every side, like twenty-four suns risen at once, filling the lantern-tower with a fearful brightness. Zhang Xiaojing could make out clearly every last detail within the tower. Flames blue and crimson spread along the turning-arms into the interior of the lantern-tower, like a company of couriers holding banners aloft; wherever they passed—outer skin, framework, hanging bridge, binding-rope, or wooden wheel—all answered the summons in turn and hoisted the Vermilion Bird’s standards.

Before long the whole lantern-tower, within and without, was abloom with crimson-red peonies, clustered thick about the Tianshu, their flames leaping, dancing the steps of the Hutuo to the crackle and snap of the fire, awaiting the final blossoming.

Zhang Xiaojing slumped down against the square stand, gazing at the ever-swelling hell of fire on every side, his heart sunk into a despair and anguish beyond all measure.

He had hacked his way through bramble and thorn, weathered troubles past all counting, and reached at last the very side of the Que-le Huo-duo. And yet here was the utmost limit; not one step nearer could he come. All his striving could not, in the end, prevent this one disaster from befalling; he had fallen at the spot nearest to success. Only a little short—but that little was a gulf as wide as heaven’s own moat.

The Tianshu turned solemnly on, standing unshaken amid the great fire, its pillar-top pointing toward the north pole of the sky—that of which it is said, ‘The heavens revolve without end; sun, moon, and stars shine each in turn; yet the pole-star does not move.’ But Zhang Xiaojing knew that, seared by the great fire, the fierce-fire thunder hidden within the pillar had already woken, and might burst at any moment, dealing the city of Chang’an a blow past all mending.

How cruel a thing this was—to make a rescuer who had lost all hope watch, wide-eyed, as it all went striding toward a bottomless abyss. Zhang Xiaojing was not a man to give up lightly, but even he could think, by now, of no way whatever to undo this calamity.

This time, he had truly come to the end of the road.

***

Just as the twenty-four lantern-chambers went up one after another in flame, Yuan Zai happened to be leading his people out past the cordon of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower, hurrying away outward.

The shock-wave the explosion loosed was like an unseen scythe sweeping across a meadow. Yuan Zai felt only a great force shove him in the back, and with a crash he was pitched to the ground and fell so hard that stars swam before his eyes. The soldiers of the Longwu Army and the Lüben Guards round about him fell sprawling too, and some luckless wretches near the lantern-tower let out screams and rolled on the ground clutching their legs.

Yuan Zai scrambled up from the ground in disarray, his ears ringing and buzzing from the blast. Half-crawling, half-running, he pressed on another few dozen paces, until he had dashed behind a low wall and set his back against it—only then did he feel safe enough. Yuan Zai gasped for breath, cold sweat oozing in beads from his broad forehead.

A wave of terror-after-the-fact swept through him: had he not, a moment ago, made up his mind on the instant and ordered every man to fall back at once, he might now have been blown or burned to death within the lantern-tower.

Those foolish lamp-watching commoners, knowing nothing of the danger, were still cheering off in the distance. Yuan Zai raised his head once more and saw the whole lantern-tower grown dazzling in the flames, twenty-four masses of seething, furious fire scorching the sky into a sheet of crimson. This was assuredly no rigged-up spectacle: no craftsman, however ingenious, would set his own main structure ablaze with a single torch. The flames had spread even to the turning-arms; it was beyond doubt an accident—and an accident done on purpose!

So this was the fierce-fire thunder Zhang Xiaojing had spoken of?

At the mere thought of the name, Yuan Zai’s head began to ache again. He had seen with his own eyes Zhang Xiaojing stuff a round of fierce-fire thunder into the turning-gear—was that not plainly the doing of a man bent on some outrage? Now the plot had succeeded at last, the lantern-tower was blown up at last, and look at it how one might, the whole affair was Zhang Xiaojing’s work. Yet Yuan Zai could never make sense of it: too many of Zhang Xiaojing’s acts were shot through with contradiction. When he had rushed into the lantern-tower from the crown-loft at the end, he had gone out of his way to charge Yuan Zai and the rest to raise the alarm—and what villain was ever so kindly as that?

Yuan Zai shook his head, trying to fling these doubts out of his mind. Had those blasts a moment ago knocked him witless? What was Zhang Xiaojing to him, one way or another? Now that the evidence was ironclad and there was someone to bear all the blame, why put himself to further trouble? There were more important things to be done.

Yuan Zai had a strong presentiment that this business was not yet over, that a greater peril still lay behind. And the plan of the moment was to get a warning out as fast as could be. This warning could not be left to another to give; it had to be Yuan Zai himself who gave it, so as to show forth the loyalty of one who ‘risks his own body in the service of his sovereign.’

Yuan Zai stretched out both hands and rubbed his face, to bring himself to his senses as quickly as he could.

By this time the Longwu Army’s cordon near the lantern-tower had fallen into utter disorder: more than half the soldiers had been caught by the blast just now and lay strewn about the ground, while the few that remained, not knowing what to do, brandished their weapons to keep anyone from drawing near, and would let no one come to tend the wounded either.

Yuan Zai paid this shambles no heed; tucking the skirts of his gown into his belt, he raced at top speed along the emergency link-passage the Longwu Army had opened up, dashing toward the Jinming Gate. As he ran, Yuan Zai saw that the Qinzheng Wuben Tower too was in a shambles—candlelight scattered, footsteps in confusion—and that even the endless, unbroken music had stopped.

Yuan Zai knew the rules of the palace well. This was the most important spring banquet of the whole year, and for the playing to break off of a sudden with a tune half-finished would be reckoned a great ill omen; the musicians of the band must play a piece through to the end though their very hands were broken. Now that even the music was gone, it could only mean some great disaster had struck.

He ran in a single breath up to the foot of the Jinming Gate, and saw Chen Xuanli standing atop the gate-tower. Gone was his wonted air of awe-inspiring composure; he was conferring in low, hurried words with a few aides about him, while soldiers ran up without cease to make their reports.

The whole scene of the lantern-tower’s blazing, a moment ago, Chen Xuanli had already seen. The disorder at the spring banquet, too, had reached the Jinming Gate at the first instant. But Chen Xuanli was a cautious man, and he had not sent the Longwu Army into action at once. Even after receiving Li Bi’s warning, he had not stirred.

The Longwu Army was of the imperial guards, its station a delicate one, and not to be moved save by command. In the palace strife of the earlier reigns of Great Tang, the imperial guards had figured every time. To take but the nearest cases: in the Tanglong and the Xiantian—the two coups the reigning sovereign had himself set in motion—it was by first securing the advantage of the imperial guards that Empress Wei and Princess Taiping had been put to death. Both of these Chen Xuanli had lived through in person, and he understood full well what it was the Son of Heaven most dreaded.

Think of it: were he, Chen Xuanli, to lead troops bursting into the spring banquet without the Son of Heaven’s order to move, what would come of it? Even were it done to guard the sovereign’s person, the Son of Heaven could hardly help but think: this time you force your way in unbidden; next time you may force your way in unbidden as well; and then... then there might well be no ‘and then’ at all.

So Chen Xuanli had first of all to get clear just what had happened at the lantern-tower a moment ago. Was it a rigged-up spectacle, or an accident? Or was it truly as Li Bi had said—that someone had deliberately packed the thing full of fierce-fire thunder? Only as the case might be could the Longwu Army make the most fitting response.

Chen Xuanli was at his wits’ end when he suddenly noticed a man below the wall running toward the Jinming Gate, shouting and bawling as though he had some urgent matter to report. By the blue of the man’s robe he was a low-ranking official; but he was all over dirty gray dust, and even his headcloth was knocked askew.

‘Yuan Zai of the Jing’an Bureau begs audience.’ A soldier soon came up to announce him.

Chen Xuanli felt a flicker of surprise. The Jing’an Bureau? Li Bi had only just gone—how was it another had now come? Yuan Zai clambered panting up onto the gate-tower, and the moment he saw Chen Xuanli, heedless of any salute, cried out: ‘General Chen, I beg you, evacuate the Lantern Festival spring banquet with all speed!’

Chen Xuanli started. Li Bi, a moment ago, had said the very same—how came this one to speak in the very same vein? He asked in turn: ‘Do you mean to tell me that within that Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower there is a great charge of fierce-fire thunder?’

‘I cannot say for certain; but by my bureau’s intelligence, the lantern-tower has already been infiltrated by the Pifu, and there is surely some device meant to work harm against the sovereign!’ Yuan Zai was not so well acquainted with the inner truth of it as Li Bi, and could only make his words as smooth as he might.

Chen Xuanli pressed him: ‘Has it happened already, or is it yet to happen?’

If the former, there was after all no need for haste: the spring banquet had suffered only a spell of confusion, not yet gone so far as loss of life. If the latter, the trouble would be great indeed.

Yuan Zai answered: ‘I have but now returned from the lantern-tower, and saw with my own eyes Mao Shun flung down from the high tower, and the villain climb up with fierce-fire thunder in his hand. I fear the Pifu’s devices are not so simple as the mere burning of the lantern-chambers.’ Chen Xuanli lightly stroked his whiskers, still hesitating and undecided. Yuan Zai took a step forward and said in a low voice: ‘There is no need for a heavy guard to escort His Majesty; you need only convey the Sage secretly out—the rest may withdraw at their leisure.’

He understood well Chen Xuanli’s dread of overstepping and courting suspicion, and so counseled that there be no great show made of it, but only two or three men sent to move the Son of Heaven quietly to some place of safety. Thus the sovereign would be kept whole and entire, without rousing mistrust. Chen Xuanli fixed his eyes on Yuan Zai. What monstrous nerve this fellow had—was he not hinting, in all he said and left unsaid, that so long as the Son of Heaven were safe, the rest might die and be done? There were the princes of the imperial clan, the great ministers of the fifth rank and above who were the very limbs of the state, the envoys come to pay homage from ten thousand lands—and in Yuan Zai’s mouth, these might die and be done? Yet turn it over as he might, Chen Xuanli could think of no more fitting course.

After a moment’s silence, Chen Xuanli at last made up his mind. Two men of the Jing’an Bureau, one after the other, had given the very same warning; whether or not the threat of fierce-fire thunder truly lay within the lantern-tower, the Son of Heaven was no longer fit to remain on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower.

He at once summoned his subordinates and ordered the gates of the Xingqing Palace closed against a possible assault; then he took off his helmet: ‘I shall go to the Son of Heaven myself.’ While on duty a man ought not to doff his armor; but were he to burst into the spring banquet in a general’s helm, it would be far too conspicuous.

Yuan Zai clasped his hands and said: ‘Then this humble officer takes his leave...’

‘You will come with me.’ Chen Xuanli said coldly. For some reason he did not in the least like this fellow with his very reasonable talk. Yuan Zai’s face changed several times over: ‘No—no—this humble officer is of far too low a rank; to mount the tower rashly would offend against court ceremony.’

‘You need not go up the tower, but you must stay at my side.’ Chen Xuanli insisted. He had no time to verify Yuan Zai’s identity or his intelligence, and so chose simply to keep him near; should anything go amiss, it could be dealt with on the spot.

Outwardly Yuan Zai was all reluctance, but within he was blossoming with joy. He had reckoned rightly on Chen Xuanli’s cautious nature and played the stratagem of ‘yielding a step to gain the advance.’ Only let him keep close to Chen Xuanli, and there would surely come a chance to see the Sage, and to leave in the old gentleman’s mind an impression of himself—an opportunity sent from Heaven that no amount of money could buy.

To be sure, there was great risk in going too: there was no telling when that lantern-tower might blow. But Yuan Zai resolved to hazard it this once—were not riches and honor always to be sought in the midst of danger?

Chen Xuanli had no interest in what passed in Yuan Zai’s mind; he stood on the gate-tower and looked off toward the plaza. That lantern-tower had turned into one enormous torch, giving off heat and light; even here at the Jinming Gate one could feel the menace of it. That sky-scorching blaze seemed already very near some limit or other. By this time everyone had begun to feel that something was wrong.

A Lantern Festival tower, be it never so grand and costly, would surely not burn to such a pitch as this.

Chen Xuanli, his brows locked tight, gave a great shout—‘Move!’—and hurried down from the gate-tower with Yuan Zai and several guards.

***

Zhang Xiaojing leaned half against the wooden stand, staring blankly as the walls of fire on every side crept slowly in toward him.

All that could be done, he had done; the passage by which he had hoped to escape had been swallowed up by the tongues of fire, and to go down the tower was no longer possible. Having spent his every choice, he could only sit and wait for the coming of the last moment.

They say that in the instant before death a man may see his whole life pass in review. But what flickered before Zhang Xiaojing’s eyes was face upon face: Xiao Gui’s, Wen Wuji’s, the faces of his brothers of the Eighth Company, Li Bi’s, Xu Bin’s, Yao Runeng’s, Yisi’s, Tanqi’s, Wen Ran’s... Every one of them seemed to have something to say to him, yet none could hold for long, but broke apart in the firelight soon enough.

Zhang Xiaojing fixed his gaze on them, straining hard for a long while, before he could barely make out the words they meant to speak—which came, in truth, to but a single line: Do you regret it? Do you regret it? Do you regret it?

It was a most piercing question. Zhang Xiaojing closed his eyes, and there rose in his mind the scene of the morning before, at the hour of the Snake, second half, when he had walked out of the death cells. Were it all to be lived over once more, would he make the same choice again?

Zhang Xiaojing smiled. He worked his cracked, parched lips and slowly let out two words: ‘No regret.’

He did not regret the choice he had made this day—made not for the sake of any one emperor, any one court, but for this city of Chang’an and the multitude of ordinary folk who lived within it.

Zhang Xiaojing felt only that there was too much still left undone: he had not stopped this plot, and had failed the trust Deputy Director Li had placed in him; he had not seen Wen Ran come safe through; he had had no chance to make those who had bullied the old veterans of the Eighth Company pay as they deserved; and he had dragged Xu Bin, Yao Runeng, and Yisi down into ruin along with him... and yes—he had wronged Tanqi too. He had promised, in his own grand words, to settle this affair, and had come instead to such a pass as this; how did she fare now, he wondered?

At the thought, a graceful, hazy figure floated up before his eyes. Zhang Xiaojing sighed helplessly and shook his head, and the figure at once dispersed.

Looking back over all he had done this day, Zhang Xiaojing felt that he had, in truth, made a great many low blunders. Given the chance once more, things might have gone quite differently. Had he reached Changming Ward earlier, the fierce-fire oil would never have had the chance to be carried out; had he laid Yuchang by the heels in Pingkang Ward, the Pifu’s plan might have been exposed the sooner; had the round of fierce-fire thunder mounted on the turning-gear not been damaged and lost its force, but gone off cleanly, all the troubles that followed need never have come to be...

Zhang Xiaojing, half-dazed, was musing thus amid the fire, when of a sudden his eyelid twitched. He thought it a little strange—what was the matter with him? Had he been roasted witless by the great heat? So he rolled his thoughts back and ran through them once more; and sure enough, his eyelid twitched again.

So it went a third time; and then all at once he snapped his eyes open and, bracing himself against the wooden stand, rose to his feet. The vital spirit that had been ebbing away from him gathered, in that instant, back again.

That was it! If the seal of a round of fierce-fire thunder were damaged and its force leaked away, it would not explode! Great or small, the principle held good all the same!

Mao Shun had meant to blast the turning-gear askew precisely so as to use the slanted angle to grind apart the base of the Tianshu and let the rock-oil run out. Now, though there was no turning-gear to use, the Tianshu stood turning ceaselessly close at hand—and it was made of bamboo; by human strength, even if he could not grind it apart, he could still cut a few gashes into its outer wall and let the rock-oil leak away.

Zhang Xiaojing had never worked out just how many gashes he would have to hack, how much rock-oil he would have to bleed off, before this one huge round of fierce-fire thunder lost its inner force altogether. He had only grasped that the possibility was there, and had no wish to die with regret; and so he came to make his last desperate throw.

The moment he thought of hope, vigor burst afresh through Zhang Xiaojing’s whole body. He cast his eyes about and saw, in a wicker basket near the wooden stand, a scatter of tools flung down—left carelessly here by the Pifu’s craftsmen when they had finished mounting the qilin-arms. Zhang Xiaojing took several axes from the basket; the helves had been baked so hot he could scarcely keep hold of them.

Gripping these axes, Zhang Xiaojing turned and rushed to the foot of the Tianshu. The Tianshu still turned with a grinding creak, as though nothing in this world were worth its stopping for. The searing firelight all around lit up clear as clear its pitted, greenish surface.

The Tianshu stood as tall as the lantern-tower itself; no bamboo in the world could be so high. In designing it, Mao Shun had strung together lengths of hard bamboo, joint upon joint, fixing the junctures with cast-iron sleeves. If it had any weak point, it should be there, near the iron sleeves.

Zhang Xiaojing made no ceremony of it, but swung up a great axe and hacked down with all his might. Unluckily the surface of the Tianshu had been treated to a hardening, and the axe-blade left but a shallow white scar. Zhang Xiaojing hacked again, and this time barely opened a small crack, from which black rock-oil seeped out, like blood welling from a wound. A third time Zhang Xiaojing swung the axe, striking the selfsame spot with all the strength he had, and only then chopped open a great gash.

Rich, sticky black rock-oil spurted from the narrow crack, like a fountain spilling over the wooden wheel. The heat outside was by now exceedingly high, and no sooner had the rock-oil spurted onto the surface of the wooden wheel than it caught with a whoosh into a sheet of flame. In a little while the floor of the wooden wheel was wholly ablaze, and had become a wheel of fire.

Zhang Xiaojing knew this was not yet enough. For a Tianshu that stood near as tall as the lantern-tower, so slight a wound was a mere hair plucked from nine oxen, not enough to bleed off its force. He would have to cut more gashes still, and bleed off more rock-oil.

But by now the wooden wheel had been set alight by the spurting rock-oil, and there was no footing left on it. Zhang Xiaojing could only take up his axe and climb on upward along the remnants of the scaffolding. At every stretch he climbed, he swung the axe and hacked like a madman, until he had cut open a great gash gushing rock-oil, and only then went on up.

This out-gushing rock-oil would make the interior of the lantern-tower burn the more wildly, which in turn would drive the Tianshu to burst the sooner. Zhang Xiaojing was racing not only against time; he was, as he ran, helping his own adversary along. And so, within this blazing hell of the burning lantern-tower, a resolute figure threaded its way through flame and thick smoke. Again and again he closed in upon the great pillar of the Tianshu, on the very verge of bursting, to strive with all his strength for a chance so small it was all but not worth the reckoning.

The great fire waxed ever fiercer, the crimson flames sprouting up everywhere like wild grass after spring, and the heat within the tower grew scorching enough to rival the oven that bakes mutton flatbread. Zhang Xiaojing’s eyebrows were soon singed away, and the skin of his scalp scorched near to catching fire; the clothes upon him, powerless to fend off the heat, turned one by one into charred and gaping holes, and his whole body was seared by the flames—his back above all, which, having just been burned once already in the Jing’an Bureau, now met this fierce heat afresh, in a torment past all bearing.

Yet Zhang Xiaojing’s movements did not slacken in the least. Nimbly he leapt among the bamboo and timber frames, now and again pouncing to the Tianshu’s side to hack with his axe. Wherever he passed, he left black fountains spurting behind him, that set the flames below to seething the more.

Bang, bang! Crack! Whoosh—

Another gash was opened in the Tianshu, and black oil sprayed forth.

Zhang Xiaojing did not know which number of gash this was that he had broken open, still less could he reckon how many jin of rock-oil had spurted out; he only trusted to his last breath, hoping, before he died outright, to lessen so far as he could the harm of the lantern-tower’s blast. He flung away the axe, its edge already curled and blunted, and drew from his waist the last one.

He raised his head and strove to make out the path upward. At this height, near the top of the lantern-tower, the flames had not yet spread; but the smoke was already thick past all measure. The whole tower’s smoke had gathered here and was drifting up toward the sky. Zhang Xiaojing’s single eye was reddened raw by the smoke, and he could scarcely breathe; he could only cough loudly as he climbed on upward.

A push of his foot, and he had swiftly clambered up another level. This level was narrower than those below, no bigger than the courtyard-well of an ordinary household; within it, besides the Tianshu, were but a sparse few timber frames crossed together, with no hanging ropes and no suspended bridges. Zhang Xiaojing strained to look about him: nothing but rolling smoke, and not a thing to be seen.

To climb higher, it seemed, there was no way out left. Zhang Xiaojing could feel his body swaying faintly. No—not his body: it was the whole space that was swaying, and by no small span. He stretched out his left hand and groped—touched the Tianshu—and found that he had, in fact, groped his way to the very top.

So Zhang Xiaojing had climbed to the highest point of the lantern-tower. Here the Tianshu reached up no further, its top inlaid with a ring of raised bronze cinnabar seal-script. Above it rested a wooden cross-frame wrought in the form of a suanni, and from the frame there hung aslant a tongue-shaped trip-plate. When the Tianshu started up, the moving lantern-chambers would pass beneath the suanni cross-frame, and the plate would flick open the oil-vessel at the chamber’s roof and kindle the candle of itself.

Zhang Xiaojing swung his axe and hacked a few times at the top of the Tianshu, first prizing the bronze cinnabar seal-script bodily off, then chiseling out a gash. At this height, even were there rock-oil left in the Tianshu, there was no way for it to run out. Zhang Xiaojing did this chiefly to set his own heart at ease, as one completes some needful rite.

When all this was done, Zhang Xiaojing flung the axe far down the tower, feeling his whole body near cooked through with the heat. With the last of his strength he climbed up onto the suanni cross-frame, set his back against the trip-plate, and collapsed in a heap.

This time it was truly, wholly over. He had done everything that could be done; what came next lay with the will of Heaven.

The Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower stood more than 150 chi high, and from its summit one might look down over the whole of Chang’an. But it was night now, more’s the pity, and smoke wreathed all about, so that nothing could be seen. Zhang Xiaojing felt it a great shame: seldom did a man climb so high, and yet he could not, in the end, take one last look at this city he had striven with all his might to protect.

Smoke and fire wreathed all about, the thick smoke close-packed; the main body of the lantern-tower below had by now become an utter sea of fire, its scorching breath surging without cease. The summit of the tower at this hour was the one patch of pure ground not yet wholly taken by the flames. Zhang Xiaojing laid his body limply against the trip-plate beneath the cross-frame, his head lolling to one side; yet within, a strange calm had come over him.

Nineteen years ago, he had leaned just so against the flagstaff of the beacon-fort, waiting quietly for the end that was soon to come. Nineteen years on, fate had come round full circle. Only this time, there would be no relief force at all.

As Zhang Xiaojing mused thus, half-dazed, he suddenly felt the lantern-tower beneath him give the faintest shudder, and then let out a low, deep rumble.

***

The Longchi of the Xingqing Palace was a most singular sight in all the city of Chang’an.

Back in the years when Empress Wu held court, this had been but an ordinary ward of Wannian County, called Longqing Ward. In Longqing Ward there was a well that of a sudden, and for no cause, gushed forth; the clear water welled up unchecked, and in a single night drowned some acres of land round about, so that the place became one great expanse of marsh. At sunrise a mist would often rise steaming from it, and the scene was exceedingly fair. The diviners of vapors in Chang’an held it a place of excellent geomantic fortune, and among the wards it was whispered besides that the pool bred a dragon-vapor. And so the members of the Li imperial house moved one after another to dwell beside this marsh—among them the reigning Son of Heaven, Li Longji.

Later, when the Son of Heaven ascended the throne, he renamed the Longqing Pool the Longchi, the ‘Dragon Pool,’ as a token of the dragon’s auspicious rising. At this the imperial kin who dwelt beside the Longchi dared no longer stay long there, but offered up their mansions one after another. The Son of Heaven then took the Longchi for his center, absorbed several wards, and raised up the Xingqing Palace. And the Longchi, having been touched by the imperial grace, was widened again and again, till it formed a stretch of water broad exceedingly, its misty expanse able to bear long boats and painted barges, with pavilions and galleries beyond counting along its banks, planted all about with peonies, lotus, and drooping willow, and stocked besides with no small number of birds and fowl.

On the shore of the Longchi stood the Qinzheng Wuben Tower and the Hua’e Xianghui Tower, no more than a hundred-odd paces apart. At this hour the Qinzheng Wuben Tower blazed with lights, alive with revelry, the banquet at its very height. The Longchi, by contrast, had but a few lanterns hung here and there along its banks—at the Chenxiang Pavilion, the Dragon Pavilion, and the like—for a sprinkling of ornament; the greater part of its surface was one dark and silent stillness.

A red-crowned crane stood upon a rockery in the midst of the lake, its head tucked beneath its wing, sunk deep in sleep. All at once it snapped up its long neck and looked warily about on every side. All around lay darkness, with nothing amiss. Yet the crane shivered its plumage uneasily and, with a beat of its wings, flew off across the water and away.

Click.

At the very spot where the crane had but now been standing, a stone on the rockery worked loose. These stones were all rare and fantastic rocks fetched from deep in the Zhongnan Mountains, each of a different shape, piled together by the craftsmen at cunning angles, their joins none too firm one with another. In a little while the stone stirred again—and then was thrust bodily aside.

A black hole opened in the rockery, and out from it, crouching low, crept Xiao Gui, soaked through from head to foot, an excited light in the eyes on either side of his hooked nose. This, after all, was the Xingqing Palace—the heart of Great Tang, the pivot of Chang’an; the men fortunate enough to enter here were exceeding few, and now here he stood, within it.

The rockery stood very near the bank. Xiao Gui crouched warily at its edge and looked all around. There were no imperial guards in this quarter; the Longwu Army’s whole attention was fixed on the outer cordon of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, the southern plaza, and the halls of the Xingqing Palace; no one would pay any special heed to a place at once so broad and so unimportant as the Longchi.

Once Xiao Gui had made sure it was safe, he mimicked toward the black hole the low chirp of a cricket. Soon there filed out of the black hole a good twenty-odd hardy soldiers, each in a close-fitting fish-skin water-suit, each bearing an oilcloth bundle upon his head, and every one of them steeped in a chill and murderous air.

To keep the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower’s works in easy motion, Mao Shun had led a water-source from Daozheng Ward down beneath the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower; but so great a volume of water had of necessity to be given somewhere to drain. To dig a separate drainage channel would have been too much trouble; to let it run straight off into the Longchi was the best choice of all. The Longchi was both deep and broad, and to take in so trifling a quantity of water was no matter to it at all.

For the Son of Heaven’s part, he was glad to see the Longchi’s waters swell, and so this work was passed without demur. The Longwu Army, veteran guardsmen though they were, had settled into a fixed cast of mind: their eyes were on the gateways and the dry ground alone, and it never once occurred to them that this drainage channel running deep into the palace precincts might be turned to use by the Pifu.

Xiao Gui led the twenty-odd men into the lake, and holding their oilcloth bundles high, they swam some ten-odd steps and then stepped up onto a bank laid with cobblestones. These cobbles were all of a single size—no small labor must have gone into the picking of them. Xiao Gui gave a couple of clicks of his tongue and found a hidden patch of open ground among a few willows and a thicket of shrubs.

The twenty-odd men stripped off their water-suits one after another, opened their oilcloth bundles, and took out the crossbow parts and keen blades within. Through the silent willow grove rose the clicking and clacking of assembly—yet from first to last not one man spoke.

Xiao Gui was the first to finish assembling his. He raised the crossbow and loosed a trial shot at a willow ahead; the bolt drove straight into the trunk, only the fletching left showing without. Xiao Gui nodded in satisfaction—the spring-lock, it seemed, had not been fouled by the water. They would be before the Son of Heaven presently, and were a crossbow to fail then, it would be a grave discourtesy indeed.

His preparations made, he stepped to the edge of the thicket, lifted a willow-branch, and looked off toward the south. His line of sight passed over the city wall, and he could see that towering lantern-tower turned now into a huge torch, raging flames leaping up from its every part. Those twenty-four fireballs still turned of themselves. The handiwork of Master Mao Shun—it was durable and long-lasting, and out of the common run indeed.

The plan was going very smoothly; Yuchang, he trusted, had by now been blown to death as well. A pity there was no knowing where Zhang Xiaojing might be at present, whether he had already got safely away to the Hydraulic Hall. But this thought lingered only an instant in Xiao Gui’s mind. He was within the Xingqing Palace now, and about to do a great deed such as no man had ever done before; he must keep his mind fixed, and thrust every misgiving behind him.

‘Big-Head, ah—let me show you how it is I take my vengeance for Wen Wuji.’ Xiao Gui murmured to himself under his breath.

Just then the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower gave a low, deep rumble, as though something had burst apart within it. ‘It begins!’ Xiao Gui’s eyes went wide, and he gazed off full of expectation. His men about him, too, clustered at the edge of the clearing, holding their breath as they looked off into the distance.

A few finger-snaps later, a ball of light ten times more dazzling than the flames around it burst out from the middle section of the lantern-tower. The Que-le Huo-duo in its fury stretched forth its limbs from within, thrust out a giant hand, and in an instant the whole lantern-tower was torn in two across the middle; its huge body twisted in midair into a shape ghastly to behold, its skeleton dimly seen to crack and shatter. Above the Xingqing Palace, on the sudden, wind rose and clouds gathered. A crash of thunder swept out on every side; over the surface of the Longchi rose at once the cries of countless startled birds, and countless roosting fowl sprang up into the air.

But at this moment no one would cast their eyes upon them. At the tower’s broken rent, roiling crimson flame and smoke shot out madly on every side, gorgeous as a peony new-opened, dazzling as the Vermilion Bird come into the world. In one instant it had swallowed up the neighboring Qinzheng Wuben Tower, the Hua’e Xianghui Tower, and the southern plaza.

In that instant the city of Chang’an passed from clamor to a dead hush. Whether the lamp-watching folk of Yanshou Ward, the nobles feasting on the Leyou Plateau, the monks and Daoists and lay believers at their rites in the several shrines, the Hu merchants drinking and dancing merrily in the East Market, or the officials of the Jing’an Bureau busy at their work in Guangde Ward—all, in one instant, raised their heads. The pitch-black night sky had been pierced by a sudden burst of light. And then the whole city, as though some fiend had snatched away its very soul, saw every one of its lamps dim in the selfsame moment.

Xiao Gui gripped the willow-tips tight, trembling all over with excitement. After so long and bitter a labor, the Pifu had at last shaken the towering tree. Those sufferings he had borne in years past—it was time now those others had a taste of them too.

But of a sudden he found something was not right! The Tianshu of the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower had truly and verily blown apart—yet the force of the explosion was far smaller than Xiao Gui had looked for.

For it must be understood that the Que-le Huo-duo’s chief means of destruction was not the fire, but the shock-force that burst forth in an instant—formless and without substance, yet enough to shatter the stoutest city wall. By the earlier reckoning, the charge of rock-oil should have split the lantern-tower from top to bottom, loosing a shock enough to level the neighboring Qinzheng Wuben Tower to the ground. But now the Taishang Xuanyuan Lantern-Tower had merely been blasted through across the middle. For all its rolling smoke and fire, its imposing show of sound and fury, its killing power had been greatly discounted.

A blast of this manner showed that the Tianshu’s explosion had been incomplete—that only the middle section had been touched off. Xiao Gui’s eyes went wide, and he saw, amid the wreathing smoke, that the tall, upright form of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower yet stood. It had been hard hit, but the main structure stood unshaken.

‘Damn it—can the reckoning have been wrong?’ Xiao Gui ground his teeth and snapped the willow-branch in his hand clean in two.

Before long, the upper half of the lantern-tower’s structure let out a wail as of something pressed past its utmost limit, tore wholly free of its warped base, and came toppling slantwise down into the Xingqing Palace. This blazing upper half of the high tower stood better than seventy chi tall, and bore down with a pressure beyond all compare, whistling as it toppled from on high—no less a thing than Mount Tai itself crashing down upon one’s head.

The spot it faced was none other than the Qinzheng Wuben Tower. That broad, up-curling hip-and-gable roof-ridge stood proud and erect, meeting the greatest trial since the day it was raised. This was a contest between two giants, that mortal men could only look on at, powerless to stay the great edifice in its fall.

The upper half of the lantern-tower crashed without the least hesitation down upon the straight roof-ridge of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower, with a huge sound of collision; in that moment wood-splinters flew and black tiles came crashing down. The lantern-tower was, after all, made of bamboo and timber, and had been baked soft besides by the great fire; the instant it struck a structure of brick and stone, it broke apart on the spot. And the main body of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower still stood erect—yet the lantern-tower had not wholly failed: its shattered fragments, together with fires past all counting, scattered and flew on every side, settling upon beams and pillars, sifting in among the rafters, spattering into the gaps of every last tile-end.

If no move were made to fight the fire, the Qinzheng Wuben Tower too would likely, before long, become a hell of Zhurong, the fire-god.

‘Now!’

Xiao Gui flung away the willow-branch and stepped out of the clearing, a fierce light blazing in his eyes. Though the hoped-for effect had not been won, still, with this one blast and this one crushing fall, the Qinzheng Wuben Tower must be in a turmoil within. The Longwu Army had likely not yet grasped what had happened—this was the moment when the defenses of the Xingqing Palace were at their weakest.

He raised his hand, thrust out his forefinger and pointed that way, then clenched it into a fist. The soldiers behind him rose up all together, leveled their crossbows, and followed close upon his heels.

The last and fiercest assault of the Pifu began.

***

Even across the high barrier of the Leyou Plateau, the great roar from the direction of the Xingqing Palace could be heard even in the Eastern Palace physic garden. Li Bi’s face went white; his body swayed, and he could scarcely keep his feet.

This sound meant that Zhang Xiaojing had failed in the end after all—which was to say that the Qinzheng Wuben Tower had likely been devoured by the Que-le Huo-duo, and the fate of those within it needed no asking. If Chen Xuanli had not got the Son of Heaven away in time, the fearful train of consequences that would follow made Li Bi’s head near burst with blood.

The curtain of the four-windowed carriage was slowly drawn aside, revealing a face touched with alarm. He looked off toward the source of the explosion, seemingly at a loss what to do.

‘Heir apparent!’ Li Bi took a step forward and called out with the utmost breach of courtesy.

‘Changyuan?’ Li Heng’s first reaction was, of all things, glad surprise. He sprang down from the carriage at a bound and threw his arms about Li Bi, crying in excitement: ‘So you truly are alive after all!!!’

Li Bi was greatly taken aback by this reaction of the heir apparent’s. He had expected that Li Heng, seeing him, would show either guilt, or coldness, or the smugness of a scheme brought off; he had never dreamed it would be a reaction such as this. On the strength of their friendship of so many years, he could feel that the heir apparent’s joy sprang from the heart, without the least particle of pretense.

This was not at all the mood that ought to move a crown prince who had just now connived at villains blowing his own father to death. For in theory, remember, he was already at this moment the Son of Heaven.

Li Bi pushed Li Heng away, stepped back a pace, and knelt on one knee: ‘Your Highness, there is a thing I do not understand.’ Li Heng, all smiles, reached out both hands to raise him, but Li Bi stubbornly held to his former posture.

‘Why did Your Highness leave the banquet in such haste?’ Li Bi lifted his head and demanded.

Hearing the question, Li Heng’s face filled with bewilderment: ‘To come and find you, Changyuan, of course!’

‘Eh?’

Yet another unlooked-for answer. Li Bi’s brows knit tight, and he glared hard at Li Heng. Li Heng knew that whenever Li Bi had some misgiving, this was the very look he wore. Growing uneasy, he could only begin to explain.

Earlier, Tanqi had told Li Heng that the Jing’an Bureau had been attacked and Li Bi carried off, which had left him sitting on thorns at the spring banquet. Afterwards Tanqi had even brought the matter up before the Son of Heaven, so that Li Heng had been given a dressing-down by his imperial father. Not long after, he had received a secret letter—one brought by no messenger, but pressed, after a performance of the Rainbow-Feather Dance, beneath a glass cup by some unknown hand.

The letter said that they were the Pifu, and that they now held Li Bi’s life in their hands; and that if the heir apparent did not believe it, he might lean on the railing and look.

Hearing this, Li Bi understood in a flash. So that was why Xiao Gui had earlier hauled him up into a lantern-chamber and made him stand there a while—it had been to show him to the heir apparent. He recalled that at the time the lantern-chambers on either side had both been lit; it had not, then, been for a test, but to let the heir apparent make out his features the more easily.

‘And then what?’

‘Once I had made sure you had fallen into their hands, I had no more heart to stay on at the banquet, but thought only of going to save you. Yet I feared to throw the rat and break the vase, dreading that if I pressed the chase too hard, you might come to some cruel harm. Just then, a second letter appeared out of nowhere.’ Li Heng went on. ‘It said I must go to the Eastern Palace physic garden, and brook no delay. There I would be told what I was to do, in exchange for your life. And it warned that if I told anyone, you were as good as dead.’

‘That is to say—Your Highness left the spring banquet in such haste for the sake of this servant’s life, and for no other cause?’

‘Of course!’ Li Heng answered without a moment’s hesitation. ‘Changyuan, your very life was at stake—the spring banquet is of no account at all. However my imperial father may reproach me, it is no matter.’

His look was not that of a man dissembling; and from his tone one could hear that he did not even yet know what that noise a moment ago had meant.

A faint warmth stirred in Li Bi’s heart: this playmate of his childhood was not, after all, a man of ruthless and unfeeling stamp. But more doubts came crowding one upon another. If what Li Heng said was true, then what was Xiao Gui about in doing this? To what end go to such lengths to kidnap Li Bi, save only to draw Li Heng away from the Qinzheng Wuben Tower? And by Li Heng’s account, at least one traitor of the Pifu had got in among the guests on the Qinzheng Wuben Tower—who, then, was he, or she?

Was there yet some further scheme of the Pifu still to come?

The mood that had just now eased in Li Bi’s breast wrung tight once more. Li Heng, fixing his eyes on Li Bi, saw his face shift through light and shade, and pressed him to say what all this was about. Li Bi opened his mouth, and knew not how best to answer.

How was he to say it? The lantern-tower has blown up, the Qinzheng Wuben Tower is destroyed, your imperial father has already been blasted to death, and you are now the Son of Heaven of Great Tang?

Matters had already come to the worst possible pass; the whole city was now a shambles, perilous past all telling. Before he had the situation clear, Li Bi dared draw no rash conclusion. This heir apparent was of too soft a temper, and too easily swayed by feeling; what his reaction might be, hearing news so shattering, could not be foretold at all.

At such a moment out of the common, one false step might plunge all into ruin past redemption.

Faced with a disaster without precedent, another man might have wailed aloud, or lost all mastery of himself; but not Li Bi. Since the Que-le Huo-duo had already come to pass, then however he might regret and be aghast, the hour could not be turned back; and what mattered most now was what was to be done next.

Li Bi strove to drive the alarm and fury from his mind, and to make himself calm.

‘Do you still have the letters?’

‘I do.’ Li Heng handed over the two letters. Li Bi took them and looked them over briefly: the writing was a fly’s-head fine regular script, such as any petty clerk might set down.

Li Bi tucked the letters into his bosom and said to Li Heng: ‘Your Highness, do you know what it is the Pifu would have you do at the Eastern Palace physic garden?’

Li Heng shook his head: ‘I do not yet know. I had but just come here when you arrived—ah, but seeing that you, Changyuan, are already out of danger, does that not mean I need no longer submit to their threats and do their bidding?’

Li Bi gave a faint, bitter smile: ‘I fear they never looked to have Your Highness do anything at all.’

‘Eh?’

‘To draw Your Highness out of the Qinzheng Wuben Tower—that was their whole and entire aim.’ At these words Li Bi suddenly stood stock-still a moment, as though something had struck him, and then asked in haste: ‘Besides Your Highness, who else left the Lantern Festival spring banquet?’

Li Heng pondered a long while, then shook his head. There had been far too many people at the spring banquet, and he had left in such haste that he had had no leisure at all to take a tally of just who was already absent. Li Bi knit his brows in disappointment, and turned a cold, keen gaze toward the Leyou Plateau, as though to pierce through that stretch of hills and see clear to the Xingqing Palace on its other side.

Just then the driver of the four-windowed carriage timidly poked out his head: ‘This—this humble servant thinks he may know.’ Li Heng shot him a displeased glare: ‘None below the fifth rank are so much as fit to attend the Lantern Festival spring banquet—by what right would you know?’ But Li Bi checked Li Heng: ‘Let us hear what he has to say.’ The driver stood with his hands tucked in his sleeves, shrinking and cringing: ‘It is but a guess of this humble servant’s, only a guess.’

‘Say on, and no harm in it; the heir apparent will not lay it to your charge.’ said Li Bi. The driver looked at Li Heng; Li Heng gave a cold snort, which passed for assent to Li Bi’s words. Only then did the driver begin, stammering, to speak.

Within the Xingqing Palace none might ride, whether on horse or in carriage; so all who attended the banquet went in on foot from the Jinming Gate. The oxen, horses, and conveyances they had ridden were all left standing at a mustering-ground on a patch of open ground not far from the Xingqing Palace. Throughout the banquet the drivers would wait there upon call.

The four-windowed carriage was of especially high standing, and had its own particular area to stand in, thick-clustered all about with the horses and carriages of the princes and of the meritorious ranks of the third grade and above. Somewhere about the hour of the Tiger, first half, the driver had received the command that the heir apparent was about to leave, and had hurried to harness up and be off. He remembered that a sumptuous seven-fragrance carriage had stood blocking the passage in front, which must be moved aside before he could get out. When he raised his head, he found the carriage had vanished at some point he had not marked—and he had been rather pleased, for it saved him a deal of bother.

‘Whose was that seven-fragrance carriage?’ Li Bi pressed.

‘It belonged to the Right Minister; his household is fondest of such lavish playthings.’ The drivers had a circle of their own; whose household kept what sort of carriage, harnessed to what sort of horse, favored what style of ornament—of all this they were perfectly well-informed.

The driver had not yet done speaking when Li Bi had already leapt back onto his horse, and said to Li Heng, spacing each word out: ‘I beg Your Highness to rest here a while, and remember—from this moment on, go nowhere, and heed no man’s word, unless it be my own.’

Hearing how exceedingly grave his tone was, Li Heng could not help a start, and hastily asked where he was going. Li Bi sat his horse, his gaze deep and far-off:

‘The Jing’an Bureau.’
