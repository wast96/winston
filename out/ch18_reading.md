### The Red Airwaves That Never Die

Though the wireless communications branch was set up rather late, under Zhou Enlai's own planning and leadership, and through two years' effort overcoming obstacle after obstacle, it built a fighting body rich in talent and of enormous power. Around the branch head, Li Qiang, gathered a whole host of the Party's first generation of radio workers. Among them were those trained in Shanghai under Zhou Enlai's direct leadership — Zhang Shenchuan, Huang Shangying, Wang Zigang, Wu Yunfu, Zeng San, Zeng Hualun, Chen Shouchang, Wang Youcai, and the rest, along with three women fighters, Liu Guanghui, Zhao Yinxiang, and Pu Qiuchao; and there were those who had returned from study in Moscow — Mao Qihua, Tu Zuochao, Shen Kanfu (Chen Baoli), Cheng Zuyi, Fang Tingzhen (Fang Zhongru), Li Yuanjie, Song Ru, and others (note: this list is incomplete). These comrades Zhou Enlai had arranged to study before he returned from the Party's Sixth Congress in Moscow, and he had spoken with Mao Qihua himself, telling him: "You must study hard; the country urgently needs radio communications." There was besides Cai Shuhou, who had studied electrical engineering in Japan. Holding fast to their posts on the radio cause, they wrought many admirable feats of heroism. Through them, in turn, came the tens of thousands of radio workers spread across the whole country, and the great and imposing communications enterprise of New China. For want of space, only the deeds of a few outstanding figures can be set down here.

### Cai Shuhou, Electrical Engineer Trained in Japan

Cai Shuhou (1898–1971), originally named Cai Suhou and also known as Cai Shaodun, was of Zhuji county, Zhejiang, by ancestry, but was born in Tianjin. When he graduated from the Zhejiang First Industrial School in 1916, his family was poor and could not afford further schooling, and he worked in turn at the Xiaofeng paper mill and at the Japanese-run Naigai Cotton Mill in Shanghai. In the great strike by which the Shanghai workers answered the May Fourth patriotic movement, he indignantly quit the Naigai mill. After the strike ended he served in turn as an electrical engineer at the Baocheng, Hunan, and Shenxin No. 2 cotton mills. In the autumn of 1921 he won a government scholarship to study in Japan, where he specialized in electrical engineering, then went on to enter the graduate school of the Tokyo Institute of Technology, researching the design and manufacture of piezoelectric devices, and graduated and returned home in 1924.

After returning from Japan to Shanghai, Cai Shuhou declined an appointment from the Zhejiang provincial construction bureau and resolved to support himself; on the strength of the technique he had learned, he founded the Shaodun Electrical Company at No. 1 Youheng Road in Shanghai, hoping to make his country strong and rich by reviving industry. Harsh reality shattered his dream of saving the nation through industry. In his search for a road to save the country, Cai Shuhou came into contact with all the new currents of thought.

After the Great Revolution failed, his middle-school classmate Shen Duanxian (Xia Yan) returned from Japan to Shanghai and stayed at his house. At the time Yang Xianjiang, head of the Party's Zhejiang provincial committee, and his wife, and the Communists Zhang Qiuren and Chen Dehui, also stayed one after another at the Shaodun Electrical Company to escape the enemy's pursuit. Some Party comrades often held meetings there, and some had to lodge there as well. Under the influence and help of these revolutionaries, Cai Shuhou came to see that only socialism could make his country strong. In the winter of 1927, at the height of the White Terror, filled with hatred for the Kuomintang reactionaries who had betrayed the revolution, he resolutely asked to join the Chinese Communist Party. Before long he was gloriously admitted. When Li Qiang was developing the wireless transceiver in 1929, Cai Shuhou was transferred to the Central Special Section to assist him. Together with Zhang Shenchuan and Tu Zuochao, they built the first transceiver, which became the Party's first secret radio station.

Using his standing as boss of the Shaodun Electrical Company, Cai Shuhou built up a range of social connections and grew quite well known in Shanghai's social circles. Not long after he joined the Party, the electrical company became a liaison organ for it. With people coming and going, it soon drew the notice of the garrison command and the foreign police. "Once, the Party received intelligence that the enemy meant to search the Shaodun Electrical Company. The Party organization told Cai Shuhou to keep away for a time, but he reflected that if he did so, the enemy might instead plant themselves in the company and lure comrades who had come to Shanghai from elsewhere to find the Party into a trap. He proposed to the organization that he stay and prepare to deal with the enemy. In the dead of night the enemy searched the company thoroughly and got nothing. As they were leaving, Cai Shuhou even had them write out a statement certifying that nothing had been found on his premises. The enemy searched several more times afterward, and each time Cai Shuhou dealt with them shrewdly and warily. On another occasion, one of our comrades was unfortunately arrested and held in the police station. Learning of this, Cai Shuhou and another comrade worked out a plan of rescue. Cai Shuhou's Japanese was very fluent, and he passed himself off as a Japanese and went to the station to demand the man. He told the police that the man was one of his own, and asked them to hand him over for him to deal with. The men at the station, seeing his manner, took it for true and let him take the comrade away."

After Gu Shunzhang was arrested and turned traitor, Cai Shuhou moved out of the company for a while and rented a room across the street. Later, seeing nothing suspicious, he moved back. In 1932, with the approval of the Central Committee and the Central Special Section, Cai Shuhou was transferred out of the Section to work in the Comintern's China group, and as the rules required, he broke off all ties with the Chinese Party and its subordinate organs. During this period he used his social connections to gather Kuomintang military intelligence, performing feat after remarkable feat and drawing repeated praise from his superiors.

Though he was a "boss," he never set store by money. His income was modest, and he lived frugally year in and year out. But when the organization and his comrades met economic difficulties, he always helped with all his strength. As early as the end of 1927, he received and housed at the company several dozen people who had returned to Shanghai after the failure of the Guangzhou Uprising and the party purge in Zhejiang — Ye Jianying, Zeng Xianzhi, Xing Xiping (Xu Bing), Liao Chengzhi, Feng Xuefeng, Li Qiushi, Zhang Jibu, Kuang Yaming, and others. Many more comrades came to him for refuge afterward, and he received them all warmly, doing his utmost to lodge and feed them well and, when they left, giving them what travel money he could. And so the comrades jokingly called his company the "relief society."

"After Cai Shuhou was transferred to the Central Special Section, he moved the Shaodun Electrical Company to No. 403 Fuxi Road (now No. 369 Yan'an Central Road). As the new nature of the work required, he broke off dealings with ordinary Party comrades. Sometimes, to cover his identity, he even put on an air of negligence and dissipation. He acted it so well that some comrades reported to the Party organization on Cai Shuhou's 'passivity and backsliding.' Cai Shuhou knew well that Shanghai's social scene was extremely complex, and that to do the Special Section's work well one had first to gain a firm footing in Shanghai. To that end he used his open identity as boss of the Shaodun Electrical Company to move constantly in high-society circles. Playing on the reactionary officials' love of money and good food, Cai Shuhou often treated them to meals, and sometimes did them financial favors. In this way he slowly made the acquaintance of certain officials and notables. He grew known in Shanghai's social circles, and Party comrades too affectionately called him 'Boss Cai.' By these means he successfully concealed his true identity and created favorable conditions for carrying on the Special Section's work…"

"After Cai Shuhou was transferred to the Comintern's China group, the tasks his superiors set him were: one, to secure as far as possible an open way of life, arrange a good social standing, and see well to the cover of his identity; two, to open up as wide a range of social contacts as possible and reach the confidential figures within the reactionary regime; three, to learn of Kuomintang plots to attack the Red Army and oppose the Soviet Union, and of Kuomintang dealings with the German, Italian, and Japanese fascists and other imperialists. In this work Cai Shuhou was quick and brave; time and again he penetrated the inner Kuomintang government, developed working personnel, and obtained secret intelligence, performing feat after remarkable feat. For instance: the plan drawn up by the Kuomintang's chief military adviser Seeckt (a German) for a thorough reform of the army, and the plan for the plum-blossom blockhouse tactics of the fifth 'encirclement and suppression' campaign against the Red Army, and so on — all of these Cai Shuhou, on the organization's instructions, contrived by many means, through comrades working within the enemy, to obtain in good time and deliver to the organization. On one occasion above all, Lou Zhendan, who worked in the Ordnance Department of the Kuomintang government, discovered a top-secret document on the army's order of battle that the Kuomintang's Military Administration Department had compiled for submission to Chiang Kai-shek, the Ordnance Monthly. When Lou Zhendan told Cai Shuhou in Shanghai of this over the Ordnance Department's long-distance line, Cai Shuhou decided on the spot, at once took his own Leica camera, and caught the night train that same day to Nanjing. Braving the danger, together with Lou Zhendan he photographed the document through the night, rushed back to Shanghai the next day, developed the film, and handed it to the organization. Afterward, through Lou Zhendan, he secretly developed Yang Yuqing, secretary of the Ordnance Department, and each month took out the Ordnance Monthly, secretly photographed it, and delivered it to the organization — from 1932 to 1935, without a break and without a single slip. The China group's leadership praised him in admiration: an achievement so brilliant ought to be awarded the Order of Lenin."

In 1933, as Japan pressed harder in its seizure of China, the focus of the China group's work began to shift toward Japan. Richard Sorge (a German, formerly of the German Communist Party), a member of the Soviet Communist Party who had led the China group, was ordered to Japan, and Lawrence (a Lithuanian, an old Bolshevik and a Red Army colonel) took over his leadership of the group. Under Lawrence's leadership, Cai Shuhou made great contributions in obtaining intelligence on Japanese-German relations as well.

In April 1934, Lawrence was arrested through a traitor's betrayal, becoming the "mysterious foreigner case" that stirred Shanghai for a time. Cai Shuhou himself was also wanted by the enemy; he first made careful arrangements for the materials in his charge before turning to his own removal. To evade the enemy's searches, he moved the company to No. 417 Fuxi Road (now No. 379 Yan'an Central Road) and renamed it the China Electrical Engineering Enterprise Company. In his student days in Japan he had come to know the senior Kuomintang general Tang Enbo, and after returning home the two had kept up a good private friendship. He now went to Nanjing to seek Tang Enbo's help, and Tang Enbo at once had a word with Wang Xinheng, the Juntong agent in charge of the case, and forced Wang Xinheng to call off the pursuit of Cai Shuhou.

After this, dangerous as his situation was, Cai Shuhou still held to his work for the Party and helped a number of comrades re-establish their ties with the Party organization. In the autumn of 1935, when Liu Yi escaped from an enemy prisoner camp at Jiujiang to Shanghai, it was through Cai Shuhou that he got back in touch with the Central Committee. But in early 1936 the comrade responsible for the Comintern's contact with him suddenly left Shanghai for the Soviet Union for reasons of his own. This unforeseen event broke Cai Shuhou's ties with the higher organization, and his work was suspended for the time being.

This comrade, who in his work had always put the revolutionary cause first, never weighed personal gain or loss, and served the Party with utter loyalty, later met with unjust treatment. In his article "Remembering Comrade Cai Shuhou," Li Qiang set down in detail this unhappy passage of his life: after that unforeseen event of early 1936 broke his ties with the Party, he sought the Party organization everywhere while going on actively working for the Party. In 1938 he met Liu Shaowen, head of the Shanghai Party organization, at a small gathering, and explained his situation to him. Afterward he also found Pan Hannian, but this too failed to put him back in touch, and his wish to restore his ties with the old organization went unfulfilled.

"Under the leadership of Liu Shaowen and Sha Wenhan, Cai Shuhou worked on until the liberation of the whole country, chiefly taking part in the left-wing cultural movement. After Shanghai fell, Comrade Yu Ling ran a drama troupe in the city, staging patriotic plays as anti-Japanese propaganda. For the troupe to operate openly, it had to have the help and cover of a man of some social standing. Cai Shuhou was invited to serve as a director and did much for the troupe: introducing theaters, negotiating performance contracts, advancing performance costs, standing surety for the troupe, and so on. When the police came to make trouble, he would step forward on the troupe's behalf. After the victory in the War of Resistance, Yang Hansheng and other comrades, following Zhou Enlai's instructions, opened the Kunlun Film Company, and on the Party's instructions Cai Shuhou joined it as a shareholder. Working among the capitalist shareholders, he did much useful work. Yu Ling later praised him: that a great many of our film workers were able to take part, and that the Kunlun Film Company could turn out a whole run of fine films still beloved by audiences today, owed a great deal to Cai Shuhou."

"Cai Shuhou always held that, having explained his situation to Liu Shaowen and gone on doing some work for the Party, he had re-established his ties with the organization — until, in 1943 in Chongqing, when he mentioned to a comrade that he had not seen Party documents in a long while, he learned that he was actually being treated as a 'non-Party figure.' He at once found the relevant comrades and asked to have his Party ties restored, but the circumstances of the time would not allow it. Even so, Cai Shuhou still counted himself a Communist and strove to complete every task the Party gave him. In Chongqing he arranged and shielded a secret radio station the Party had set up on the roof of his firm's office building. In 1946, when Liu Shaowen was hurt while evading arrest, Cai Shuhou took him into his home to nurse him back to health. On the eve of liberation, on the Party's instructions, he used his social connections to work among certain senior Kuomintang generals."

"After the liberation of the whole country, Cai Shuhou again raised with the Party, several times, the restoration of his Party membership, but for historical reasons the matter was never resolved. For the time being he worked in the standing of a democratic personage, serving in turn as head of the Shanghai film administration office and as deputy director of the city's public-utilities bureau, planning and construction bureau, machinery bureau, and machinery and electrical bureau. In 1954, implicated in the so-called 'Pan-Yang counterrevolutionary spy-ring case,' the question of restoring his membership was shelved indefinitely. As soon as the 'Cultural Revolution' began, he was slandered as a 'suspected spy,' his home ransacked, and he interrogated, detained, and held, until on May 6, 1971, he died wronged in a Beijing prison. A veteran comrade who had worked for the Party in utter loyalty for many years left us thus."

"After the Party's Third Plenum of the Eleventh Central Committee, the relevant departments in Shanghai made a full investigation of Cai Shuhou's history and, in the light of his services, overturned the slanders the 'Gang of Four' had heaped upon him. In September 1980 the Shanghai Party committee cleared Cai Shuhou's name; on March 19, 1983, the Organization Department of the Central Committee approved the restoration of his Party membership." At the last, Li Qiang said with deep feeling: "Comrade Cai Shuhou has left us, but that spirit — utter loyalty to the Party's cause, fearlessness before hardship and danger, self-sacrifice; and that noble character — warm help to comrades, and all-out rescue of a comrade in peril — will live forever."

### Zhang Shenchuan, the Party's First Radio Operator

Zhang Shenchuan, the Party's first radio operator, had been a student at Qingdao University, had served as chairman of the Qingdao Student Union, and had taken part in leading dockworkers' struggles. He joined the Chinese Communist Party in November 1926. In October 1928, not long after Zhou Enlai returned to Shanghai from the Sixth Congress in Moscow, he sought out Zhang Shenchuan and urged him to study radio technique.

At the time Zhang Shenchuan was serving as secretary of a Chinese Communist local Party branch in the French Concession in Shanghai, and Zhou Enlai was head of the Central Committee's Organization Department. Zhou Enlai asked him to meet at the Huizhong Hotel on Third Avenue. On seeing Zhang Shenchuan, Zhou Enlai questioned him very fully about his circumstances: where he had studied, what political activities he had taken part in, when he had joined the Party and on whose introduction, and so on. When Zhang Shenchuan had answered each in turn, Zhou Enlai said: the organization has decided that you are to study radio-communications technique. At the same time, pointing to Gu Shunzhang, who sat beside him, he said: from tomorrow you come under his leadership. The next day Gu Shunzhang had Li Qiang find him, and they discussed the study of radio technique.

Just then a Shanghai paper carried a recruitment notice for the "Shanghai Radio School," at Menglai Road by the Old West Gate. Zhang Shenchuan went there and found the "Shanghai Radio School" signboard hung together with that of the "National Revolutionary Army General Headquarters Sixth Military Radio Station." Under the cover name Zhang Yanming, he went in, registered, and passed the examination into the school. As it turned out, the school had been set up by the head of the Sixth Military Radio Station and several of its operators to make a bit of money on the side. The first intake took fifty students, all day pupils, most of the study done in the evenings; the course ran half a year, opening in January 1928 and finishing in May 1929.

After enrolling, Zhang Shenchuan found that the head of the station, who was also the school's principal, was Liu Henian, formerly a radio operator at the Qingdao meteorological observatory; in 1925 Zhang Shenchuan and he had together taken part in the "Qingdao–Shanghai Atrocity Relief Committee" supporting the striking workers of the Japanese cotton mills, and so they knew each other. Zhang Shenchuan told him that because things were hard at home he could no longer stay at university, and had come to Shanghai to learn radio technique so as to support his family.

The school engaged two professors from Jiaotong University to lecture on theory, while the station's operators taught the sending-and-receiving technique. Zhang Shenchuan lived in a garret on the second floor of the Peide Primary School on Peimeili, Caishi Road, in the French Concession, not far from the school. To keep on good terms with this primary school, by day he taught its pupils an hour of lessons for free. When the Shanghai Radio School opened, the organization bought him a Morse key, a buzzer, dry cells, and other tools for self-study, and for twelve silver dollars a crystal set and headphones; Li Qiang helped him rig an antenna and ground wire at his lodging, with which he could then copy only the French-language weather forecasts broadcast on long wave by the Xujiahui observatory.

After finishing in May 1929, with the consent of Liu Henian, head of the Sixth Military Radio Station, Zhang Shenchuan moved into the station to intern. Seeing that Zhang Shenchuan worked earnestly and could handle the sending and receiving, Liu Henian often had him take the night shift, so that their crowd could carouse outside. At first the operating chief at the station, a man named Li, still helped him practice receiving — having Zhang Shenchuan receive while he watched and listened alongside, and taking down what Zhang Shenchuan sent. After a few tests with no mistakes, he confidently let Zhang Shenchuan stand in. In the course of his "internship" Zhang Shenchuan gradually made out the pattern of their activities, and on the nights he was on duty alone he secretly copied out two volumes of the military station's ciphers and handed them to the Party organization. He also learned from a fellow lodger, a technician, how to fit and change the transmitting antenna and refill wet cells, and built a single-tube radio receiver.

During his internship at this station, one night while standing in, Zhang Shenchuan received a plain-code telegram sent to him by the Fourth Military Radio Station of the National Revolutionary Army General Headquarters at Liuzhou, Guangxi, saying that one of the heads of the Guangxi provincial government, Division Commander Li Mingrui, had wired the finance minister of the National Government to disburse 3,000 yuan to Zhang Shenchuan to buy books on his behalf. (Note: Li Mingrui afterward took part in the Bose Uprising that Deng Xiaoping led; the political director of his division, Jiang Gengcheng, knew Zhang Shenchuan, and so entrusted the buying to him.) Zhang Shenchuan went to the Central Bank in Shanghai to get T.V. Soong's authorization; T.V. Soong questioned him closely about his relation to Li Mingrui before granting it. Zhang Shenchuan took the cash and gave it to the Central Committee's Propaganda Department, which bought a large batch of progressive social-science books to send to Guangxi, preparing public opinion for the Li Mingrui force's part in the Bose Uprising in the winter of 1929.

This also brought trouble. Even before Zhang Shenchuan finished at the school, at an evening party held there in the spring of 1929, someone had spoken up urging everyone to watch out for Communist activity in the school. Zhang Shenchuan had found no Party activity within the school, and from then on he raised his guard. Not long after buying the books for Guangxi, in July of that year, Zhang Jian and two others, graduates of the Kuomintang Whampoa Academy's radio-training class, were assigned to intern at the Sixth Military Radio Station. These men had had agent training; flaunting Chiang Kai-shek's name, looking all around, overbearing, they unsettled the station. One operator said to Zhang Shenchuan: with those three here, we'll be pushed out. They took great notice of Zhang Shenchuan, the "intern," too, and one of the three agents, a man named Wu, came one day out of the blue to "chat about home" with Zhang Shenchuan and feel out his "background." The next day, when Zhang Shenchuan went to the sending-and-receiving room to fetch something, he suddenly saw a note stuck on the transceiver: "Whoever was on the night shift yesterday made errors in receiving." It was precisely Zhang Shenchuan who had stood in the night before, and the duty log was written up plainly; this was clearly meant to make trouble for Zhang Shenchuan, and he decided on the spot to find Liu Henian and say: with so many interns at the station, chances to practice are few, and I've decided to look for other work. Having gotten Liu Henian's consent, he left the station at once. Before long Zhang Shenchuan heard that Zhang Jian, checking over the past sending-and-receiving drafts, had turned up the message slip on Li Mingrui's telegram and T.V. Soong's disbursement to Zhang Shenchuan for books, and had secretly denounced Liu Henian to the general headquarters for using a military station to "traffic privately with a commercial paper"; Liu Henian was dismissed.

Zhang Shenchuan left the Sixth Military Radio Station and rented a second-floor front room on Hede Road (now Changde Road) in the British Concession; the organization sent He Guo, who had once gone to France on the work-study program, to live and study with him. At this time they bought a good deal of radio gear, and Li Qiang drew up circuit diagrams for a transceiver and had them build one, copying by deep night the foreign-language political and economic news of San Francisco, Khabarovsk, and other places to supply the leadership for reference.

On August 24, 1929, through the betrayal of the traitor Bai Xin, Peng Pai and others were arrested in Shanghai. Yan Changyi, arrested in this affair, had lived in the garret of the same front-room building where Zhang Shenchuan lodged. The next morning, Zhang Shenchuan saw through a chink in the partition four detectives climbing in the window to search the garret Yan Changyi had lived in. Then Li Qiang came to give warning about the matter, and they moved house afterward. Zhang Shenchuan at this time went on taking part in Li Qiang's development of the transceiver, and at the same time began training radio technicians.

In the winter of 1929, Li Qiang's development of the transceiver succeeded, and he and Zhang Shenchuan began setting up the Party's first secret radio station. Zhang Shenchuan said: "In the autumn of 1929, the organization rented a three-story shikumen house at No. 9 Fukangli on Route Ghisi (now Yan'an West Road) in western Shanghai as the site of the secret station. One day Zhou Enlai asked me for a talk and introduced Pu Qiuchao (a woman, a Sichuanese just back from study in the Soviet Union) to live at the organ and do cover work, and to study sending and receiving too. To suit the surroundings, the house was furnished like a well-off family's, and our clothes were fairly fine. Once the organ was in order, Gu Shunzhang came to look it over as well. The transceiver was built to the circuit diagram Li Qiang drew, the parts bought bit by bit; once made, at night we called under an amateur station's callsign, other amateur stations answered, and after a few nights' trials we could exchange traffic. And so the Party's first underground radio station was set up."

At the time the White Terror in Shanghai was severe, and for a secret station to stay hidden and unbroken for long was very hard. The Kuomintang government, in league with the Municipal Council of the British Concession, often mounted a direction-finding station on a car and cruised the streets each night to locate secret stations. When the Central Plains War broke out in 1930, in which Yan Xishan and Feng Yuxiang opposed Chiang Kai-shek, the secret stations that Feng and Yan had set up in Shanghai were soon located, one after another, by Chiang Kai-shek's direction-finding station. That the Fourth Branch's station could be preserved was owed chiefly to the Party's security education and its extremely strict organizational discipline. All the operators kept to themselves and stayed indoors, cut off almost entirely from social ties, writing home once in a year or two. Their working hours were all in the small hours of the night. Their lives were extraordinarily hard. Zhang Shenchuan said: "For two years I often worked deep into the night, short of sleep, my body growing weaker, with severe anemia. In August 1930 there was much traffic back and forth. The weather was very hot; each night after working deep into it, with no hot water to bathe, I rinsed off with tap water, and before long fell ill with typhoid. The organization asked the noted physician Zhou Junchang to treat me, paying seven dollars fifty for medicine and car fare each visit. The doctor determined I had typhoid A and must rest lying quiet when the fever was high. But because I had to send traffic deep in the night, I could not rest. When I grew worse, the Yu Kun couple still propped me up to work. So the fever ran on and the illness worsened by the day. The doctor said several times: 'Others with the same illness as yours recover after a week's medicine — why does it not take with you?' I could not tell him about my work. Later, Comrade Li Qiang told me that when I was gravely ill, raving and unconscious, Comrade Nie Rongzhen and Comrade Chen Geng came to see me and even prepared for my funeral. I lay abed more than forty days; the skin of my spine was rubbed raw joint by joint, I was worn to a skeleton and coughing constantly. The organization sent Comrade Wu Kejian to look after me, rented a room on Route Ghisi, and had me leave the station and live there alone. Whether Comrade Wu Yunfu or Comrade Zeng Hualun took over the station work, I cannot recall. When I served as branch secretary in the French Concession, my living allowance was two or three dollars a month, sometimes not even that, and I mainly had to make shift myself. At the station, the living allowance was six dollars a month; while convalescing, twenty-one dollars a month. Comrade Wu Kejian told me that our Central Committee members' living allowance came only to that much a month. I was deeply moved, and could only work hard to repay the Party's care and concern for me."

On December 17, 1930, the second radio training class that the Fourth Branch had set up at No. 12 Sichengli on Rue Ratard in the French Concession in Shanghai was broken up. Zhang Shenchuan and the other teachers and students present there, twenty in all, were arrested together and held in prison for six years, until Zhou Enlai's rescue efforts secured their release on bail in August 1937. Zhang Shenchuan said: "After our batch of comrades was arrested, we were held in a Kuomintang prison. Though the enemy interrogated us again and again, using every kind of torture and every kind of softening tactic, they could never shake our will to struggle. Everyone was loyal to the Party, loyal to the revolution, kept our Party's secrets locked tight, and deceived the enemy with a confession prepared beforehand. At the same time the enemy got no real evidence against us; in the end, some comrades were tortured to death in prison, and some were held for long years. Later, they got out through the Party's rescue or by their own contrivance and struggle. Whether the enemy tailed and broke us up or arrested and held us, it was all to no avail. The Party's Red communications fighters, with the revolutionary spirit that fears neither hardship nor death, mastered the art of struggle against the enemy; the Party's underground communications were never broken, and the Red airwaves were never snuffed out."

### Mao Qihua, Trained in the Soviet Union

Mao Qihua, one of the Party's early secret-station workers, was an expert returned from study in the Soviet Union. One day in the summer of 1928, while studying at the Communist University in Moscow, Mao Qihua was handed a list by the university's secretary-general, Abramov, who told him to notify the people on it to come and see him. There were six people on the list in all: Fang Tingzhen (Fang Zhongru), Chen Changhao, Shen Kanfu (Chen Baoli), Li Yuanjie, Cheng Zuyi, and Mao Qihua. In his office Abramov told them: on the needs of the Chinese revolution, and with the support of the Comintern, it had been decided to send them to study radio-communications technique. This was an absolutely secret matter, to be spoken of to no one. They would attend classes at the university by day and go to study in the evening. And so the six of them went to the "International Radio Training Class" to study radio-communications technique. They went there twice a week, "always in the evening, about two hours each time. Study and work at 'Sun Yat-sen University' went on as usual."

The training of radio-communications personnel among the Chinese students in Moscow had been proposed by Zhou Enlai, who all along took a close interest in it. One day, when Mao Qihua and another student were taken by a Soviet military instructor to an encampment at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East to observe and study, they happened upon Zhou Enlai, who had come to the university to convey the spirit of the Sixth Congress. Mao Qihua had met Zhou Enlai back in China, and after Zhou Enlai finished conveying the report he went with the students to the dining hall to eat. When he saw Mao Qihua, he said to him: "You must study hard; the country urgently needs radio communications." There were many students around at the time, and Zhou Enlai spoke only these two sentences to him. Mao Qihua said: "But those two sentences left a deep impression on me."

At the end of 1929, Mao Qihua and four others moved to live at the "International Radio Training Class" (Chen Changhao had already been transferred at the end of 1928 to Youth League work), returning to Sun Yat-sen University to hear its major reports. In late autumn of 1930 they graduated from the International Radio Training Class. Mao Qihua and three others — Fang Tingzhen, Shen Kanfu, and Li Yuanjie — were ordered home. Secretly they went from Moscow to Vladivostok, and through the Northeast back to Shanghai.

After reaching Shanghai they stayed at a hotel on Third Avenue that the organization had designated, and the first man they saw was Gu Shunzhang. Mao Qihua knew Li Qiang, and asked Gu Shunzhang: "Do you happen to know a man called Zeng Peihong? He did intelligence work in Wuhan in 1927 — where is he now?" Gu Shunzhang said: "He's your leader, the engineer; he'll come to meet you tomorrow." The next day Li Qiang came to see them and told them to arrange lodging first. Mao Qihua said: "As required, I rented a small garret and brought my wife, Pan Linzhen, out from the countryside, and settled a home in Shanghai. About two weeks later, Li Qiang told me to move the household to No. 17 Xingqingli on Rue Moliere (now No. 17, Lane 111, North Maoming Road). This was the site of a newly built underground station of the Party. The sub-landlord of the house was Wu Kejian, who lived downstairs, and whom we called the 'bookkeeper.' Zeng San (Comrade Old Kang) was the operator and lived in a little garret. My family were third-tier tenants and lived upstairs. The transceiver was kept in my wardrobe, and each night I worked in my room. After Wu Kejian went to the Soviet Union, my family moved down from upstairs to take his place as sub-landlord."

Once he had settled his lodging, Mao Qihua went, on Li Qiang's word, to make contact with the second radio training class the Central Special Section ran at Sichengli on Rue Ratard. On December 7 the class was broken up; Mao Qihua and Li Qiang were not on the scene at the time and were spared. After this affair, Li Qiang went into hiding for a while, and Chen Shouchang took over the leadership of the Fourth Branch. After Gu Shunzhang's betrayal, the station was placed under the direct leadership of the Central Committee's Secretariat, separate from the Central Special Section. The radio training class was still to go on, but taking the lesson of the second class's destruction, it had to hold the more firmly to the principles of secret work, restoring the scattered-housing, single-line teaching method Zhang Shenchuan had used before. Mao Qihua said: "After we moved out of Rue Moliere, the task Chen Shouchang gave me, on the Central's instructions, was to train technical strength — operators and generator-repair mechanics. I was to train three operators, in two batches, teaching in two places at once. One batch was two people, in Hongkou; the other was three people (two of them a married couple), near Tongfu Road. We taught by home tutoring. I bought some parts and rigged an oscillator with a triode, one headset per person, so that those living in the same house could not hear the buzzing. At first I went every day; after a while, once every two days, mainly to press them to practice on their own. Training generator-repair mechanics was more troublesome — it needed a place, machinery, and a cover name. At the time the base areas urgently needed such people. So we found a small generator-repair works in Hongkou; the works was small, only a single shopfront room, but it had more than ten worn-out little generators. This boss looked to be of worker stock too, took part in the labor himself, and employed one worker and one apprentice. We talked it over with the boss, saying that a small town needed people who could fit and repair generators, and hoped he would help us train a little skilled strength in dismantling, assembling, and simple repair of generators, the training costs to be paid. With the boss's agreement, we ran two classes there in succession, two months to a class, two or three people a class. These trained people were all sent by the organization to the Soviet areas. It proved that training by this method was safer and more effective."

As the several Soviet areas grew apace, radio-communications equipment was urgently needed. Mao Qihua again took on the task the organization gave him of assembling sets. He now took over a fairly suitable house, and the organization specially transferred a Sichuanese woman comrade, He Chengying, to live in the garret as a third-tier tenant.

Shanghai's surroundings grew daily more perilous, and the struggle harder. The enemy often sent out black-canopied cars fitted with direction-finding gear to cruise about and detect the bearings of our Party's stations. Mao Qihua said: "In an underground setting, one cannot be the least careless about safety. Qianxinfang on Dalianwan Road had only one lane running straight through, and the place was fairly safe. The parlor downstairs in our house was furnished much like the neighbors' — a long table, a side table with an incense stand and candlesticks, a square table in the middle, chairs on either side. The front doors of the households thereabouts were usually left open, and ours was not shut either; everything was done upstairs. Old Pan and the aunt, two women, lived downstairs, and the cover held well enough. I alone bought a workbench and various tools, metal parts, and meter gear, and set up a works upstairs. That summer the heat was especially high, but with such a safe setting, and as the developing revolutionary situation and the organization required, I worked day and night, making one set and improving the next by plan, and the more I worked the more heart I had for it. In making new sets, one had to keep in mind the reality of the secret setting: they had to be small and light, to be carried easily and to shorten operating time, while the sound had to be small and clear, both easy to receive and safe from the enemy's notice. Each set made had to be tested over and over, and only when it met the standard did I send it off with an easy mind. That summer I assembled five or six transceivers in a row, the most productive year. Once a set was made, some were fetched by single-line leaders; some, by prearranged time, place, and recognition signal, I delivered myself. In 1932, I delivered two sets in turn to the liaison post on Route Magy, where the man in charge was Chen Gang…"

Perilous as the surroundings and hard as the material conditions were, the transceivers they assembled were of very good quality. Through traitors' betrayals, some stations were lost. When the enemy got hold of this station equipment, they said in amazement: "To think the Communist 'bandits' have no factory equipment and can make such small, exquisite, high-efficiency transceivers by hand!"

Besides making transceivers, Mao Qihua had to work at the station too. To keep the station from exposure, they used an indoor antenna, hung when in use and taken down when done. But in sending, at the press of the key, the light bulbs in the neighbors' homes, day or deep night alike, would flash red, and if a neighbor noticed it, trouble was sure to follow. So each time a station was set up, one had first to press the key and adjust the wavelength while contriving to peep at the neighbors' bulbs to see whether they flashed red, tuning until they no longer did. Choosing a house to set a station in was thus a matter of the greatest difficulty at the time. And so they had to move house often. In the run-up to the 1933 Spring Festival, for instance, they spent more than five hundred silver dollars to rent a house at No. 20, Lane 437, Dagu Road, well placed and of good quality too. Mao Qihua's family lived on the ground floor as sub-landlord, and he had Cheng Zuyi's family, three of them, come as third-tier tenants, living upstairs to send traffic; the cipher was carried to and fro by Shen Gulan, wife of Li Zhusheng, then secretary of the Chinese Communist Shanghai Bureau. They passed the Spring Festival there in peace. But after only a month, Shen Gulan came suddenly to tell Mao Qihua that something had gone wrong with the guarantor's papers on the house, and for safety they should move at once. Mao Qihua first found a broken-down room at Laozhaqiao to be a third-tier tenant in, and so as to send traffic quickly, he rushed about in the rain until he found a row of new houses at Gaolangqiao Square, and Cheng Zuyi's family at once brought the station there and began work.

In a situation bristling with dangers, there was peril at every turn, just as Mao Qihua said: "In the work, running into danger was a common thing. Once I was carrying a transmitter, riding a rickshaw, and passing along Beijing Road near Nicheng Bridge, when I suddenly ran into several armed police and plainclothesmen checking the passersby one by one on the road. Just then I thought of having the rickshaw turn and go around these 'friskers,' but on second thought that would only make them suspicious, so I kept going. Those few 'friskers' fixed their eyes on me; seeing me in a long gown, holding in my arms a large kraft-paper parcel printed with the trademark of the 'China Department Store,' come up to them without a change of color, looking very much the man of standing, they could not make out my background, and dared not be rash. They waved a hand, meaning let him pass. A moment of peril was past, but I had broken out in a great sweat. Once, when Shen Gulan came to the station, she too often met 'friskers' on the way. She carried a basket of eggs, with the cipher laid at the bottom, got up as a visitor bearing gifts. Reaching Huade Road, she suddenly ran into several 'friskers,' men and women; every passerby was searched, and Shen Gulan was no exception. The danger was that the policeman wanted to check Shen's basket of eggs; slowly she set the eggs out one by one from the basket onto the ground, and they rolled about. Then, quick-witted, calm yet very hot-tempered, Shen said: 'These are gifts I'm giving people — if they break, you'll have to pay for them!' At that, the searchers put the eggs back in the basket and let her pass."

Facing so grim a situation, Mao Qihua and his comrades held to their work in Shanghai all along. In September 1935, the organization decided they should withdraw. By then the Central Red Army, having come through the Long March, was speeding toward northern Shaanxi. There were two routes for withdrawing from Shanghai: to the Soviet Union, or to northern Shaanxi. They went first to Tianjin, then through all manner of ordeals, and when at last they made their way by Xi'an into northern Shaanxi, the Central Committee had already settled at Wayaobao. Mao Qihua said: "When I reached the Party Central at Wayaobao safely, the first to receive me was Vice-Chairman Zhou, to whom I made a full report. I also saw other responsible comrades of the Central, and Zeng San, the comrade who had worked the station with me in Shanghai — he was still at the same line of work… Before long Liu Changsheng came home, bringing the Comintern's telegraph cipher, and so in the spring of 1936 we restored radio contact with the Comintern."

By Mao Qihua's account: in the spring of 1936, on the day after the Central meeting held at Wayaobao, Comrade Mao Zedong had a talk with him at his quarters about the Party's work in the White areas. Having heard a full report on that work, Comrade Mao Zedong said: in the cities, where the enemy holds absolute superiority, rash action will not do! He then asked Mao Qihua, in a consultative tone, whether — the enemy holding the advantage over the city as a whole — in a single factory or a single workshop, after painstaking mass work, we could build up a hidden advantage of strength. Comrade Mao Qihua answered: from past experience in Shanghai's factories, if we develop four or five capable Party members, we can perfectly well bring a whole factory's workers under our Party's leadership and influence. Comrade Mao Zedong went on: we must keep our forces hidden and lean, husband our strength, not act rashly, lie low for the long term, and wait our chance! The workers' struggle exists objectively; what matters is how we organize and lead them to wage it. The struggle must be waged with strategy — "with reason, to advantage, and with restraint" — with care to husband strength, and brought to a stop at the right point. In his recollection Comrade Mao Qihua said: "Afterward, we used this thinking all along to guide and educate the underground workers of the enemy-occupied and Kuomintang-ruled areas, so that the Party's underground organization grew and strengthened without cease, going from victory to victory."

### Tu Zuochao, the "Carpenter"

Tu Zuochao (1903–1984) was a native of Changsha, Hunan. Poor from childhood, at nearly fourteen he went to a second sister's home to learn carpentry from his brother-in-law. Only because he could not get the knack of the work, he was given a beating and went off home in a rage. About a year later he learned carpentry again with his eldest brother in Changsha. In 1920 he joined the "Hunan Labor Association" in Changsha, attended a building workers' night school, and took part in the general strike of Changsha's masons and carpenters led by the Chinese Communist Party. It was from such a background that he later got the well-known nickname "Carpenter." In June or July 1928, while the Party's Sixth Congress met in Moscow, Tu Zuochao was studying at the Communist University of the Toilers of the East and attended the congress as a nonvoting delegate. When Zhou Enlai, talking with him, learned his history, he clapped him warmly on the shoulder and called him "Carpenter." From then on "Carpenter" became Tu Zuochao's code name in the Party's hidden-front work, and his lifelong nickname.

Afterward, "long out of work, with no living and wandering everywhere," Tu Zuochao reached Shanghai in 1924 to make a living, entering the center of the Chinese workers' movement. At first he did odd jobs at the Hengfeng cotton mill and enrolled at a workers' night school. At the night school he came to know Cai Linzheng, elder half-brother of Cai Hesen, and through their frequent talks his thinking gradually changed, and the wish to join the Chinese Communist Party took root. That spring, Li Lisan, learning that he wished to join, talked with him, explained the nature of the Communist Party and other basics of Party knowledge, and examined his history and family circumstances. Afterward, on the introduction of Cai Linzheng and Lin Zhongdan (Lin Yuying), Tu Zuochao joined the Chinese Communist Party.

At the start of the May Thirtieth Movement, when he heard that the Japanese had killed Gu Zhenghong, a Communist worker at the Japanese Naigai Cotton Mill in Shanghai, he was seized with fury. He tore off two feet of white cloth, wrote on it "A Speech in Support of the Gu Zhenghong Affair," raised it on a bamboo pole, went to an open lot on Longjiang Road, and made a speech to several hundred workers coming off shift, denouncing the Japanese outrage. He was hauled off to jail by the British police and put to harsh punishment. The Party organization worked hard to rescue him, and with the power of the May Thirtieth Movement striking fear into the imperialists, he won his freedom a month later.

In the later part of the May Thirtieth Movement, in October 1925, Tu Zuochao and a batch of already-exposed comrades were sent by the Party organization to study in the Soviet Union. On reaching Moscow, Tu Zuochao reported to the Communist University of the Toilers of the East, and in the class he was first put into, besides political courses, he studied marksmanship, demolition, invisible writing, and the like. In the second half of 1927 he transferred into the international class, graduating in May 1928. Before graduating from the university, Tu Zuochao had asked to stay on in Moscow to study demolition. During the Party's Sixth Congress, Zhang Guotao, on the organization's behalf, sought out Tu Zuochao for a talk and told him: what the Red Army at home urgently needs now is radio-communications personnel. In battle, a unit as large as a regiment, unable to make communications contact, often cannot carry out its task well, and even suffers losses it ought not to. The organization does not agree to your studying demolition; switch to radio communications! Tu Zuochao agreed to the organization's decision. Zhou Enlai also sought out Tu Zuochao and said: go study communications, put your heart into it; the forces are waiting for people, and we have none, no grasp of the technique, so the forces have trouble keeping in touch.

In January 1929, Tu Zuochao entered the Frunze Communications School in Leningrad. Here he studied the use of dogs and pigeons, wire and wireless communications, drawing military maps, repairing motorcars, carpentry, and forging. In studying radio communications, because he had once had meningitis and his head did not serve him well, he had trouble with sending and receiving. He took his instructor's advice and decided to concentrate on the equipment side, studying station repair. In October 1929 he and Song Lian were transferred to serve as operators in the Chinese-worker guerrilla detachment of the "Comintern Far Eastern Detachment" that Liu Bocheng had organized at Khabarovsk. Afterward he also studied for a short while at the "Cheka" station in Vladivostok, then was ordered secretly home.

In March 1930, Tu Zuochao and Song Lian returned to Shanghai and stayed at the New Luming Hotel on Fourth Avenue. The next afternoon Li Qiang came to the hotel and took them to the third floor of a tailor's shop west of the Jiuxing Theater near the Great World, which was a radio-repair shop under the Fourth Branch. Once Li Qiang learned of his study in the Soviet Union, he had him stay and live there.

The Central Special Section's station at the time had an output of 100 watts, and in working it the induced current often lit up the neighbors' lamps, so that it was far from hidden. Drawing on what he had learned in the Soviet Union, Tu Zuochao proposed reducing the transmitting power by the method of raising the receiver's sensitivity, which worked very well and showed off his superb equipment skill.

In the autumn of 1930, Tu Zuochao took part in the teaching of the radio training class the Fourth Branch ran on Rue Ratard in Shanghai. In December the class was broken up; not knowing, Tu Zuochao blundered in and nearly came to grief, but by his wit and composure got clear of the danger (this has been told earlier).

At the end of December 1930, the Central Soviet won the first "encirclement and suppression" campaign and captured alive the Kuomintang Eighteenth Division's lieutenant general and front-line commander-in-chief, Zhang Huizan. The Kuomintang was shaken from top to bottom. Zhang's wife, Zhu Xingfang, hurried to Nanchang on the news to beg the Kuomintang's Jiangxi provincial chairman Lu Diping to "think of a way," and wired Cheng Qian, Tang Shengzhi, and others for help. Zhang Huizan was a favorite general of Chiang Kai-shek's, stubbornly anti-Communist all along; in 1929 he had served as Nanchang garrison commander, slaughtering more than a thousand Communists, and was known as "Butcher Zhang." Before long Chiang Kai-shek conferred on him the rank of lieutenant general and made him "commander-in-chief for encirclement and suppression throughout Jiangxi." Now, at these Kuomintang generals' urging, Chiang Kai-shek agreed to ransom Zhang with guns and a great sum of money. Lu Diping and the others weighed it over and over and fixed the ransom terms: the Fifty-fourth Brigade of the Eighteenth Division, which had not gone to the front, more than four thousand men with all their arms and ammunition, to surrender to the Red Army; twenty thousand silver dollars and twenty loads of Western medicine to be delivered to the Red Army under the guarantee of three Shanghai banks, plus enough guns and ammunition to arm two regiments; and the release of more than a hundred Communist "political prisoners" held in the Xiashawo prison in Nanchang. Once these very generous ransom terms were drawn up, they were reported for the approval of the Nanjing Military Administration Department, and then the provincial government secretary-general Wang Xin was sent by seaplane to Shanghai to ask the Central Committee to send someone to Nanchang to negotiate Zhang's release. Zhang's wife had come to Shanghai first and had made contact with the Central Committee through Gong Yinbing. Zhou Enlai and others studied the "ransom-Zhang" terms Wang Xin brought and judged that, could they be realized, they would be very much to the advantage of the Central Soviet's Red Army. The Central decided to send Li Xiangwu, deputy secretary-general of the Central Military Commission, as representative of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Red Army, and Tu Zuochao as representative of the Central Committee, to Nanchang to negotiate with Lu Diping. Zhou Enlai also wrote, through the underground courier line, to Mao Zedong and Zhu De, telling them to prepare for Zhang's release.

On February 4, after arrangements by Gong Yinbing, the two sides talked in Shanghai and reported to the Central Committee for approval; then Tu Zuochao and Li Xiangwu changed into the uniforms Wang Xin supplied and, with Wang Xin, flew by seaplane from Shanghai to Jiujiang on February 6, to take the train to Nanchang the next morning.

Unexpectedly, at this moment things took a sharp turn. Radio contact between the Central Committee and the Central Soviet was not yet established, and news traveled slowly along the underground courier line; the decision to release Zhang that the Central Committee had made in Shanghai had not yet reached the Soviet. Zhang Huizan had already been executed: on January 28, 1931, in the Donggu district of Ji'an county in the Central Soviet, a victory rally with more than twenty thousand taking part was held for the Red Army's triumph in the anti-"encirclement" campaign, and someone proposed a public trial of Zhang Huizan. The enemy's Eighteenth Division had this time burned, killed, and plundered, and popular fury ran high.

Zhang Huizan had said at his oath-taking rally: "Our party and the Communist bandits cannot stand together under the same sky; if this time I do not level the Red district and wipe out the Red bandits, I vow not to come back alive! May you all strive with me to that end." He had also incited the soldiers: "The main forces of Zhu and Mao are massed at Donggu, where the Red bandits have their bank; brothers, break into Donggu, the gold and silver are yours to carry off — fight your way into Donggu, and what you make is your own!" After Zhang's division entered the Soviet, it burned, killed, and plundered, stopping at no evil. So the moment they saw Zhang Huizan, the masses of Donggu turned all their hatred of the Kuomintang forces upon him. Many peasants, fired with feeling, sprang onto the platform and set upon Zhang Huizan. The single platoon of Red Army men charged with protecting Zhang could not control the scene at all. Above all, the meeting's chairman, yielding to the will of the masses, and without the Red Army headquarters' approval, declared Zhang Huizan sentenced to death. After Zhang was executed, they cut off his head, packed it with lime in a bamboo cage, fixed it to a large wooden board, and floated it into the Gan River. The board drifted to the city of Ji'an, where the enemy fished it out, and on February 7 the Jiangxi Minguo Ribao in Nanchang published the news. Of all this, Tu Zuochao, Li Xiangwu, and Wang Xin knew nothing.

On the morning of February 7, arriving at Nanchang, Wang Xin settled the two of them at a hotel and said he would first go home for a bit. After Wang Xin left, Tu Zuochao and Li Xiangwu went out for a look around the streets, and not far off they bought a copy of the Jiangxi Minguo Ribao, which carried the news that Zhang Huizan was dead and that a "Zhang public-mourning affairs office" had been set up. At one sight of it they were aghast, hurried back to the hotel, and at once fled the Nanchang district. Luckily they were in Kuomintang uniforms and met with no obstruction. After crossing the Gan River, they hurried more than forty li and hid at the home of a merchant, Wei Chaopeng, at Weicun in Chenggang. This man often knocked about in the world and set great store by loyalty; taking them for Kuomintang officers who had deserted, he helped them with all his might and even swore brotherhood with them. When they left, Wei Chaopeng had them pose as business partners and saw them all the way to Shanghai. Tu Zuochao and Li Xiangwu reported this experience, and their wit and courage drew praise from the comrades of the Central. The Central Special Section even sent someone posing as a friend of Tu Zuochao's to give a banquet for Wei Chaopeng, and presented him with a fur gown, silks, and other things to show their thanks.

In March 1931, the Central decided that Tu Zuochao should go with Zeng San and Wu Yunfu to strengthen the work of the Red Army's radio battalion in the Central Soviet. Following the courier sent by the Hong Kong liaison post, they took a French mail steamer to Hong Kong, then went by way of Shantou and Sanheba in Fujian into the Central Soviet. On reaching the Red Army General Headquarters, Tu Zuochao "washed" the cipher he had brought from Shanghai, the "Hao cipher," faultlessly off his white undershirt.

Afterward Tu Zuochao returned to Shanghai again in April 1935, and then, on Cai Shuhou's introduction, went to Xi'an, where after making contact with Liu Ding he stayed on at his work. After the "December 12 Incident" of 1936, Tu Zuochao, in Zhang Xueliang's headquarters, had an unforgettable meeting with Zhou Enlai. Recalling it half a century later, Tu Zuochao's memory was still fresh:

There is one thing I shall remember forever. On the fourth day after the "December 12 Incident," in Zhang Xueliang's mansion, I saw Zhou Enlai. He wore a clean, neat black Sun Yat-sen suit, and the moment he caught sight of me, a look of surprise came over his gaunt face, and he warmly gripped both my hands. Under his heavy brows, a pair of bright, keen eyes fixed on my face, as if seeking something in it, and his mouth kept calling out my nickname, warmly: "Carpenter! Carpenter! We meet again. You've had a hard time these years." Zhou Enlai's words were like a warm current that in an instant ran through my whole body, making me feel the Party's boundless warmth and care. So moved I did not know what to say, my eyes brimming with tears of happiness, I stood there dumbly to one side. Of course I knew, too, that Zhou Enlai was then charged by the Party with the busy work of settling the "December 12 Incident," a matter bearing on the survival of the whole nation, and I could not take up too much of his precious time; so I only said, simply but again with deep feeling, over and over: "How are you! You've had a hard time too! … You've had a hard time too!" After a moment, Comrade Zhou Enlai suddenly stroked his beard and said to me in a most cheerful tone: "Say, Carpenter, look how long my beard's grown — have you got a razor?" I said: "I have. Only it's none too sharp." He said: "Never mind." When Comrade Zhou Enlai had finished shaving, glowing and full of spirit, he left at once with Liu Ding.

Before long Liu Ding came back and told me to pack my things, saying we were to move. After a while, Liu Ding sent someone to take me by truck to the Red Army's Xi'an office at Jixianzhuang, where I saw Li Kenong, Zeng San, Wu Yunfu, and the wife of Deng Zhongxia.
