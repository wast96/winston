## Chapter 18

Three days later I gathered up a few everyday books, some writing things, my clothes and bedding, and moved into the apartment on Route Prosper Paris; Bai Ping had moved in a day ahead of me, and she welcomed me. My room had already been arranged by Bai Ping; she had fitted it out with a whole set of brand-new apricot-yellow furniture, set off against newly hung pale-yellow wallpaper, so that it looked fresh and striking. The four walls were bookshelves; the furniture stood free about the room; behind a white gauze screen was the bed, and behind the bed a dressing-closet with its door slightly ajar. I looked in: Bai Ping's two trunks were already set inside it. At the head of the bed was a standing floor lamp; the bed was already made, with a yellow rug laid over it. The writing-desk stood before the window, in front of a low bookshelf, and beside it was an apricot-yellow wastepaper basket of a very quaint shape; just inside the door were a suite of sofas large and small and a low table, and here the bookshelf had become a cabinet, fitted with sliding panelled doors, and inside it were kept the teapot, the thermos and the cups and saucers, all of ivory-coloured heavy porcelain without any pattern.

Bai Ping looked at my bedding and said:

"You really do take me for a shrewd sublandlady."

The room was very large; the bookshelves ran all round it, and I thought that even were I to bring every book I owned at home, they would at most fill half of it; and now I had come only to lodge a few months, and had brought a bare twenty-odd volumes. Bai Ping set my books in one corner of the shelves, and laughed, and said, as if mocking me:

"I never took you for so accomplished a traveller. A pity my place here is no hotel."

"My home is not far, I think; when I need a thing, can I not go and fetch it at any time?"

"If you truly cannot set your mind at rest like this," she said, sinking down onto the sofa, "then I would rather you did not lodge here at all."

"Bai Ping, in a world like this, how should I not be at rest?" I said. "Only you are far too good to me."

"Did I not tell you long ago that I have often wished to make a kind of experiment—to see whether I too have the power to make a man do, at my side, the work he ought to do?"

"Of course," I said; "I shall certainly not fail your hopes."

"Then will you carry your bedding home again, and bring your books here?"

I assented entirely; my heart had already yielded wholly to her will, and that afternoon I brought over my books and my more particular gear. Bai Ping stayed in all day, setting everything in order for me. From then on I lived there in her place. Yet though Bai Ping was my neighbour, we met, if anything, far less often than before. Ah Mei waited on me most attentively, and my long companion was her Persian cat, Jimi. Bai Ping rose very late; of a morning she never came to my room, and nine times in ten she went out to lunch; on the rare occasions she lunched at home I would come into the dining-room and see her, abruptly, already seated there, and she would show that smile like a lily just opening and say:

"It is a rare thing to be able to lunch with you."

After the meal there might be a few idle words; but once I had smoked a cigarette I always went to take my nap, and when I woke she had surely long since gone out. As for the chance of dining together in the evening, it was rarer still; ordinarily we met only after two in the night, and then, if my lamp was lit, she would surely knock at my door. Afterwards I fell into the habit of waiting for her, and when she came she always brought sweets and cakes, or a book, or a basket of flowers someone had given her; then, in high spirits, she would make me coffee and arrange the flowers in a vase; and at last she would change her clothes and come, her powder and rouge gone, to drink tea and talk with me—to tell of what had befallen her that day, of how Mei Yingzi did lately, of how things stood with Helen, of word from Stephen, and of all the shifting business of society; and often too we spoke of love, of dreams, of the impermanence of life and the loneliness of living, until we both fell silent and listened to the ticking of the clock; and at last she, or perhaps I, would say:

"It's late; go and sleep."

So the days passed; my spirits were very good, and the work of thought went forward smoothly; now and then I wanted a book, and would jot it down of a morning on the newspaper as I read. Ah Mei always gave the paper to Bai Ping when she woke, and having seen the note she would bring the book back for me that night. My mood was very tranquil, my life very happy; I gave myself up to the pure quiet of the single life. Sometimes I thought: were Bai Ping my wife, I could no longer let her be a dancing-girl; I should naturally want to know of her comings and goings; I might be jealous, I might meddle in her life; and she would no longer take rent from me, no longer refrain from troubling me with the household's petty affairs. Between us the distance would be lost, and with it the beauty, and life would sink into the mire of the commonplace; whereas now I had beauty, and this beauty was a thing distilled and saved out of the precious feeling between us.

In this tranquil life I seemed wholly cut off from the world; the one thing that did not cut me off was my correspondence with Mei Yingzi, with the Stephens, with Helen, and even with Bai Ping. My letters went to my occupied native district, where friends and kin posted them on again from there, and their replies too came to me enclosed by those same friends and kin at home. This correspondence had its own charm: I wrote of the pleasures of the countryside, of my longing for Shanghai, of the people of the village. These were the people of my memory, and I painted their loveliness, their honesty, their greatness; I even imagined several country girls, wrote them very charming, and said half in jest that I might perhaps give up the single life for one of them. Looking back now, I feel that the writing of those letters was as it were the foreshadowing that destined me to write this very book. Their replies too were most amusing: Mrs. Stephen wrote the longest and the best; Mei Yingzi seemed a little disordered, but had her own sharp turns of phrase; Helen's were not bad either, though they no longer had the length and delicacy of our talk over books; she too now and then touched on thought and belief, but mostly on the matters of practical life. She always mentioned her life of singing-practice, and mentioned too the fine times she had outings with Bai Ping, Mei Yingzi and Stephen; and always bade me come out soon, and bring Cishan with me to her concert when I came. Cishan was a country girl I had invented in my letters, and she it was who particularly caught Stephen's fancy; at first he only added a few lines on others' letters, but afterwards, for Cishan's sake, he wrote with real relish about her, saying that had he known earlier I had so charming a girl in my native place he would surely have gone back with me, and that next time he would not miss the chance, but would make friends with her.

Bai Ping told me that my letters to them were always passed round for reading at the Liti Café or the DD's Café, so that I must be sure to keep enclosing letters to her too, and she in turn must enclose theirs to me from them; the thing was done with great amusement, and though it cost much time in the writing, it made a fine seasoning to my life, and served besides as the stuff of our late-night talk and laughter.

Of Bai Ping's social life I never inquired, and she never told me; when now and then she spoke of her daytime doings, it was mostly droll and amusing matter. Though she came home very late every night, it was always about two or three o'clock; on the rare occasions she was later than three, a telephone call would come without fail beforehand. Only twice did she not come home at all, but the night before she had told me she would stay at Mei Yingzi's the next day, and sure enough the next day she telephoned to say she could not return until the following afternoon. As a rule I fell into the habit of expecting her at two or three o'clock; I would set my manuscript in order, lay out the tea-table and the tea-things, make the coffee, sometimes even prepare a little cake, and sit on the sofa with some lighter book, smoking, waiting for her to come home. She never once disappointed me, for if she had some special engagement she was sure to telephone about one o'clock.

But one day an exception came.

It was a late-autumn night; the wind blew outside, by the radiator came Jimi's snoring, and at two o'clock I brought my work to a resting-point, set the manuscripts on my desk in order, and rested a while. It was by now about half past two; I was too lazy to stir, a little tired and a little hungry, and much wished Bai Ping would come home and make me a cup of lemon tea and a little cake; but Bai Ping had not yet come. So I got up myself, laid out the tea-table and the tea-things, steeped the black tea, made the coffee—it was already past four—but still Bai Ping did not come, and there was no telephone call; so I first drank two cups of tea myself and ate two slices of bread. When I had first moved in there had been such nights, and I had then set my mind at ease and gone first to sleep. But that night I was, for some reason, unusually anxious; tired as I was, I had no wish to sleep, and kept listening for the sound of the lock turning and the door opening, kept waiting for the ring of the telephone; yet from Bai Ping there was no word at all. I went to the window and opened it: outside was a desolate night, the street-trees with only a few dead leaves left trembling in the wind, the street-lamps lonely to the point of terror, two or three autumn stars shivering in the sky and letting through a wan, deathly colour, the shapes of the houses across the way rimmed against the sky in stiff, broken lines. I looked down on the road: not a passer-by, not a vehicle; there were black, turbid fragments creeping, and dirty white scraps drifting down, and under the dim yellow lamplight I made them out to be withered fallen leaves and discarded newspapers. I thought how thick the trees had been when I moved in, so that from the fourth floor I could not see the green of the street below—and now it lay wasted on the ground! I thought how that scrap of newspaper had perhaps last night still been a clean white sheet, printed off the rotary press with the civilization and culture of mankind, and now in this terrible night was crumpled and befouled and drifting down in the wind! I know not what feeling it was that soaked through my heart; I was a little cold, a little afraid; but Bai Ping still had not come! From which quarter would she come? Coming home through such a streetscape, springing from her car, ought she not, at one glance and one moment's thought, to feel how shallow and turbid is this life of green wine and red lamps, this life of luxury and dissipation? Yet to sell one's youth in the market for a living—this too is life! To spend one's life in the wasting of illusion—this too is life! Mine and hers were no different, philosophy and singing were no different, Helen before and Helen after were no different; the wave behind drives on the wave ahead, rolling on through boundless time and space—

But still Bai Ping had not come, and there was no telephone call; I shut the window, drew the heavy woollen curtains, lit every lamp in my room. I had no more drowsiness in me; I paced back and forth in the room, in my expectation of Bai Ping—such misgiving and dread as I had never known!

Five o'clock; six o'clock; half past six; … at seven Ah Mei rose; I told her Bai Ping had not come home and had not telephoned. She too thought it strange, and began to telephone the Paramount; but by then everyone had scattered, and no one knew where she was.

I could think of no reason, unless the telephone here had broken last night and left her unable to send word; but now it was proved the telephone was not broken—then where had she gone? To Mei Yingzi's? To the gambling-house? To the church? But wherever it was, there should have been a telephone.

Ordinarily I made light of it; today it was proved how I cared for Bai Ping. I had not slept; I washed my face, went to take some breakfast, and Ah Mei gave me the paper. I had no heart to read it, but idly turned it over and glanced at the headlines, and saw a startling piece of news:

"Paramount Star Bai Ping Shot and Wounded, Assassin Flees, Manhunt Under Way."

I started, but at once controlled myself and read on:

"By our special report: at two o'clock last night the red dancing-girl of the Paramount, Bai Ping, left the Paramount with two Japanese dance-patrons, and just as she was about to get into a car, two shots came flying suddenly from behind it; one missed, the other struck Bai Ping in the right arm, and the two Japanese guests fled in confusion and vanished; several American soldiers before the Yuyuan Road post office heard the shots and hurried up, but the assassin had long since made off. Bai Ping, once wounded, was carried by ambulance to Renji Hospital, and the wound is said not to be serious. As to the cause of the shooting, some say a political connection, some a scandal of the heart, and some that the assassin's true aim was to kill the Japanese and that he struck Bai Ping by mistake."

I read it several times over, then looked in the other papers, but none of them had the item. I stood dazed a long while before I told Ah Mei, and Ah Mei was startled. I said I would go at once to Renji Hospital to see Bai Ping; Ah Mei wished to go too. I said, very well—but on second thoughts I felt Ah Mei ought first to buy a little fruit and the like, and get some clothes together, to take to Bai Ping. So I threw on my overcoat and hurried out, bought a bunch of white roses at the flower-shop across the way, and meant to go to the garage and hire a car; it was but a matter of twenty-odd paces, but it set me thinking of the many awkwardnesses of going to see her: first, there would surely be at the hospital the Japanese who had been with her last night, and the friends she had made at the dance-hall; second, Mei Yingzi and Stephen, once they saw the paper, would surely tell each other and go to the hospital to see her, and then my lie about going to the country would be exposed. Weighing it, I carried the flowers back; Ah Mei told me the hospital had telephoned. I handed her the flowers and asked:

"Was it Bai Ping who called?"

"No." said Ah Mei. "It was a nurse; she said to come at once."

"Very well; go at once, then." I said, taking off my overcoat as I spoke.

"You are not going, then?"

"I am not going," I said. "Take these flowers; and if you meet anyone, do not speak of me."

"I understand." said Ah Mei, and went into Bai Ping's room; I followed her in and said: "Buy a little chocolate and fruit on the way."

Ah Mei nodded very solemnly, set the flowers on the copper tray, and began to open the cabinet and get out Bai Ping's clothes. My mind was in great disorder; I fingered the flowers, with a feeling past telling. This bunch was not yet in full bloom, white and most lovely, and in the silver air looked over-innocent; it came to me suddenly that the bunch wanted some touch of silver, that I wished for a silver band to tie it with; and thinking of a silver-grey necktie of mine, I went back to my room, found the silver-grey tie, and went over and bound the flowers.

"Will you not write a few words?" Ah Mei asked me.

"No." I said.

Ah Mei took up the clothes and the flowers, said a word to me, and hurried out. But it came to me suddenly that there was still a word I ought to have her carry; I ran out after her and stopped her, and said:

"If her wound is not serious, tell her to telephone me when no one is by."

So Ah Mei hurried away, and I came back alone and shut the door. Ordinarily I too often had the chance to be alone in these few rooms; but today, in the instant of shutting the door, I first grew aware of this peculiar air. I walked from this room to that, and from that room to this. I sat on the sofa, idly took up a book and lit a cigarette, and a weariness came over me; only then did I grow aware that from last night to today I had not yet slept; and so I began to draw the curtains, undress, and go to bed.

When I rose it was already five in the afternoon; there were voices outside the door, and I said:

"Is it Ah Mei?"

"No." A lively, mischievous laugh: "It is Mei Yingzi."

"Mei Yingzi?" I asked composedly, springing down from the bed.

"Yes," she said. "Get up quickly. I am going to cook something for you to eat."

I heard footsteps go off to the kitchen. Mei Yingzi? How had she come? And what had she come to do? In my alarm and doubt I hurriedly dressed, thought out many forms of words, and calmly opened the door and went out; I met Ah Mei, and asked:

"Mei Yingzi—how is it?…"

"Ah, it is a long time since we met." Mei Yingzi came out of the kitchen, girt in one of Ah Mei's snow-white aprons, showing her almond-tinted front teeth, smiling intimately, and came lightly over to shake my hand. Ah Mei hurried into the kitchen; I grasped Mei Yingzi's narcissus-like hand and said:

"It is a long time since we met; you are forever as lovely as I see you in my dreams."

"Can one who dwells in the Palace of the Moon dream of the sun?"

"In the dark earth I dream of all things bright."

"Tonight I may have a good long night's talk with you," she said. "Bai Ping's wound is very slight; set your mind at rest; now I mean to cook a little in Bai Ping's stead." So saying, she left a thin film of a smile, and with a rich strange fragrance walked off to the kitchen, and I went to the washroom.

In the washroom I saw the meaning of Mei Yingzi's words, and felt that she meant to stay here tonight; but what after all was her purpose? I could by no means make it out. I felt only that I must find an occasion to ask Ah Mei whether Mei Yingzi's coming here was Bai Ping's idea or her own, and whether she had had the address from Bai Ping or from Ah Mei. So when I came out of the washroom I called Ah Mei in the tone of command I never ordinarily used. I called:

"Ah Mei!"

But it was Mei Yingzi who came out of the kitchen; I made as if I had not seen her, and, with an air of anger, strode toward the kitchen.

"Ah Mei!" Signalling to Ah Mei on the one hand and feigning temper on the other, I said: "What is the matter with you—I call you and you do not even come out!"

"Mr. Xu, what is it?"

"I want you to go and buy some fruit, and some chocolate." And I took out my wallet.

"Ah! It is the first time I have seen you lose your temper. It does not become you." Mei Yingzi came over laughing: "Fruit, chocolate—I have bought them all; they are in Bai Ping's room."

"Then go and buy me some cigarettes."

"Era?" Mei Yingzi asked.

"Era, then," I said.

"I have bought four tins," said Mei Yingzi.

"Ah." I could only laugh: "Thank you." But still in a grave tone I said to Ah Mei: "Have the evening papers come?"

"They are in the sitting-room," said Ah Mei.

"Every evening paper with news of Bai Ping I have already bought; they are on the rack by the hat-stand," said Mei Yingzi, smiling, with a mischievous, mocking air.

I said no more, went to the rack and took the papers, and went into the sitting-room.

I turned the papers over: the news of Bai Ping was all set in the first column of the society page, and most of the papers printed her photograph too; the accounts of the affair were much of a muchness—the assassin not yet caught, the cause much guessed at, none of it confirmed.

I laid down the papers and listened to the ticking of the clock, with an unspeakable turmoil in my heart; what most held my thoughts and most baffled me was Mei Yingzi's descent upon us and her strangely gentle manner; and short of talking with her carefully tonight to sound her out, I could see no second course.

At last I saw Mei Yingzi laying out the knives and forks in the dining-room, and I went over composedly and said:

"Is it to be a Western dinner?"

"Yes." she said, laughing happily: "Ah Mei tells me Bai Ping has laid in very fine knives, forks, plates and dishes, and has never yet used them here."

"I always took you for a pretty girl; I never thought you were a lovely housewife besides."

"Only a pretty girl makes a lovely housewife."

I was just meaning to slip out and speak with Ah Mei, but she had already set out the knives, forks, cups and dishes, and gone out ahead of me. She seemed the whole time to give me no chance of a word apart with Ah Mei. At last the time for dinner came; Mei Yingzi sat facing me, and had now taken off Ah Mei's apron; she wore a blue-grey cheongsam, with no excess of powder on her face, and had a plain beauty and grace I had never seen in her before. Mei Yingzi's every different toilette was to me a new and lovely realm—and what a fearful thing that was! Under the red lamplight her eyes flashed with a boundless charm, and a brilliant smile broke through. I was smothered, unable to draw breath; I did not look her full in the face, nor speak; and to escape the fearful air I said, when Ah Mei came in:

"Ah Mei, will you give me a little whisky?" And as Ah Mei went to fetch it, I said: "And you, Mei Yingzi?"

"Ah Mei, I should like a Kolisa," said Mei Yingzi to Ah Mei, and smiled down at me from above.

Ah Mei poured our drinks, set the bottle on the table, and went out; I raised my glass and said:

"Here's that our Mei Yingzi may shine forever." But she raised her glass and said in a low voice:

"Let us first drink the health of our lovely hostess, Bai Ping."

"Yes." I said. "Here's that Bai Ping may be forever lively and lovely."

We drained our glasses to each other, and I filled them again for us both, and said:

"Now let me drink that our Mei Yingzi may shine."

"No." She smiled and said: "Let us first drink to the success of Helen's concert."

"Yes," I said; "here's to Helen's success."

We drained the glasses, and Mei Yingzi poured, and I said:

"Now I must drink that our Mei Yingzi may shine."

"Thank you." She drained her glass, but at once filled it again, rose to her feet, held the glass high, and said in a ringing voice:

"I bless you and Bai Ping." And she drained the glass.

"What do you mean by that?" I said.

"Will you go on deceiving us even today?"

"You mean…"

"Bai Ping has told me," she said. "There is no need to keep it secret from me."

"You mean that I did not tell you I had moved here?"

"I mean that you have lived together a long while and never let me bless you." "It is an insult to us!" I said.

"It is loving you," she said.

"I would rather you did not…"

"I would rather you did not lack the courage," she said gravely: "to possess Bai Ping, and use deceit to feed your vanity."

"Vanity?"

"Is it not for fear the name 'dance-hostess' should sully your standing?"

"What nonsense." I said. "In my eyes Bai Ping is as honourable as you."

"Then drink, my friend." Mei Yingzi laughed: "I hope you are the man that we women hold noble."

"Nobility is my own character."

"Then I bless you." She drained the glass; Ah Mei brought the dishes, and we fell silent. Amid this excellent fare, the places in Mei Yingzi I did not understand seemed to grow only the more. When the pie was brought in, Mei Yingzi said, tender and softly sweet:

"It is the first time I have asked anyone to eat a pie of my own making!"

"Is it true?"

"To lie is never my glory." She smiled a mocking, faint smile.

"Then this pie shall be my greatest glory," I said; and I was indeed astonished at Mei Yingzi's handiwork—it was a flavour rarely to be tasted—and I said:

"More."

Afterwards I ate a little fruit as well, and we drank coffee in my room. Mei Yingzi sat down very much at her ease, and said, calm and composed and slow:

"Xu, now is the time for our talk." She paused a while, then changed to a very grave tone: "Have you made any study of the cause of Bai Ping's shooting?"

"I think it was because she made herself too conspicuous."

"More conspicuous than I?"

"Society indulges you, but does not allow it her," I said with feeling.

"Do you not think it was for a scandal of the heart?"

"If it were a scandal of the heart, do you believe I should not be caught up in it?"

"That is for you to say." Her gaze sank, and she said very low: "Have you heard the talk that is going about?"

"No."

"Could it not be a political connection?"

"Because she has dealings with the Japanese?"

"Naturally."

"But is there any more intimate with the Japanese than you?"

She made no answer, laughed, drew herself up to smoke a cigarette, and went to the window; then turned suddenly and, leaning against it, said:

"Naturally the talk in the papers is not to be trusted; still, I think you understand her better than most."

"I understand her nothing near so well as you," I said. "But my lodging here is itself the counter-proof that she has neither scandal of the heart nor political connection."

"I hope it is so," she said.

"And besides, whenever she came home late at night, she always spoke, when she talked with me, of being weary of the dancing-girl's life."

"And so you lodge here very comfortably." She came closer.

"I am no more than her temporary lodger."

"Temporary lodger?" She laughed: "It puzzles me greatly that you will never own to your connection with her."

"What connection?"

"A single man and a dancing-girl living together—what connection should one call it?"

"I would rather such insulting words did not come from lips so lovely."

"I can leave them unsaid," she said. "But how will you stop others from saying them?"

"I only hope they will not come from the lips of my friends."

She walked off smiling. After a good while she came close again and asked:

"Is it to flee the lure of the lamps that you have withdrawn into the Palace of the Moon?"

"No," I said. "I want only a garden of my own."

"And this is your own garden?" she said mockingly.

"These books, this stillness, this night—these are my own garden."

"So the moment you left us you came here."

"Yes."

"Ah Mei tells me you came out from the country only a week or two ago," she said. "Then you and Bai Ping have deceived us far too long!"

"But it did you no harm."

"You gave us all those false letters?"

"Was it not an amusing friendship even so?"

"Yet it was not right," she said. "Do you know how Helen thinks of you?" "Helen?" I said. "What she thinks of now is only her singing."

"And so you are displeased."

"I long ago gave up my ideal of her," I said. "Her gift for singing has become the slave of her vanity."

"Is that to be blamed on my luring her?"

"It is to be blamed on the coarseness of her soul."

The telephone rang; I ran out, and Mei Yingzi followed; I took up the telephone and said:

"Is it Bai Ping?"

"Yes."

"Is all well?"

"Thank you."

"When may I come and see you?"

"Tomorrow morning at nine."

"Mei Yingzi is here," I said, and pressing the receiver hard to my ear: "right beside me."

"Mei Yingzi?" She seemed startled: "How did she come?"

"Do you wish her to hear?"

"Very well, let me speak with her."

Mei Yingzi took the telephone, and said:

"No more pain?"

"…"

"It is beyond your expectation, is it not?" Mei Yingzi laughed: "Will you let me sleep in your bed tonight?"

"…"

"Thank you."

"Set your mind wholly at rest," Mei Yingzi said, laughing: "sleep early, then."

Mei Yingzi hung up the telephone and said:

"Bai Ping pleases me too much."

So saying she went into my room, and I followed her, and said:

"Will you do a thing for me, or will you not?"

"What is it?"

"I hope you will not tell anyone that I did not go back to the country, but am lodging here."

"Whom?"

"Anyone," I said; "even Helen and Stephen."

"Why?"

"I fear they may take it amiss in some other way, above all as regards Bai Ping."

"Very well," she said; "but on one condition."

"Say it."

"That you move out of here before long."

"What do you mean by that?" I said.

"Nothing," she said calmly. "It is only—believe me, Xu—it is only concern for you."

"Because of the horror of Bai Ping's shooting, I am to move away out of cowardice?"

"No," she said earnestly. "Because the cause of Bai Ping's shooting is unknown."

"…" I could say no more. I felt I had no ground to believe Bai Ping had any scandal of the heart or political connection; but I had still less ground to say my life should be entangled with hers, and that if the word of my lodging here got about, who would still believe that my connection with Bai Ping was one of friendship only? Harmful as this was to me, what good was it to Bai Ping? So I said:

"Very well. But it must wait till Bai Ping is out of hospital."

"Naturally," she said. "Then to Helen and Stephen you had best say afterwards that you got my telegram, learned the news of Bai Ping's shooting, and hurried here."

"Thank you."

My heart began to grow calm; I felt great gratitude toward Mei Yingzi, my secret wariness long since dispelled, and I understood deeply her magnanimity and gentleness. The night thickened slowly; her talk grew more tranquil and lovely, giving off, like a fragrance, the mood of her cigarette-smoke, at once diluted and profound. Tonight's Mei Yingzi was indeed altogether changed; she spoke of herself, and again of Helen. She said:

"You always take life too solemnly. Is it, then, everyone's duty to live for philosophy and for art?" She said:

"The life of mankind's childhood belongs to society, and the life of mankind past middle age belongs to society too; only youth belongs to oneself, and it wins from society its brilliant praise and song." She said again:

"Life is but a few tens of years—what is so great about it? A woman's life is her youth, and vanity is the brocade flower with which mankind adorns its youth. Then why not let Helen enjoy her youth to the full?" She said again:

"I have enjoyed my youth to the full, and I hope everyone younger than I may understand this philosophy. How many have, for some superstition, spent a whole life in sacrifice, and worked upon themselves an irretrievable regret." She said again:

"To give one's life over to a single study and a single art—that is the ideal of monks and ascetics; of all who hold forth so in the universities, how many are there that do it?" She said again:

"Mrs. Manfield's ideal for her daughter's singing is just the road she is now on—not the superstition of a book-fool like you. So what I lead her to is a normal life, whereas your hopes for Helen are but a chain to bind her for life."

Like the night-song of a brook, like the low crooning of a nightingale, she used a boundless disenchantment and pathos to tint the lamplight into an incurable fate, glittering everywhere with a brilliant radiance; that in the heart of so lovely a woman should lie buried so fearful and pessimistic a view of things! I could no more work any change upon this translucent soul; I had no more heart to argue the contrary with her; I had no more spirit to lay before her the theories of the many philosophers on the meaning of life.

I kept silent.

Then she spoke of Bai Ping:

"Desire has no end; a woman who does not give full rein to her radiance in her youth will, past middle age, be either greedy for money or grasping after power—so it was with Wu Zetian, so with the Western Empress Dowager. As for Bai Ping, in her own sphere she has already reached the very summit of conspicuousness. Naturally her talents and her looks do not stop there; but in such a sphere, what is there above it? Not the conquest of men, not the envy of women, but the making of adventure into a diversion and of politics into a toy."

Then she spoke of Mrs. Stephen:

"Hers is the most tranquil of lives; from the enjoyments of society to the enjoyments of home, she is the forerunner leading from Helen to me, the most normal and fated of paths. All she needs now is a child."

I had nothing to say, but listened in silence as this lovely life gazed off toward its fated future—a flower in full bloom that has already seen the shadow of its own withering; and with never a flicker of expression she went quietly out, leaving me sitting there alone, sunk in bewildered thought.

Five minutes later she came in bearing hot tea and the pie left from dinner, and said: "Are you hungry?"

I did not answer, but helped her set out and share it. I drank the warm tea and ate the delicious pie, and felt a rare ease. Mei Yingzi opposite me seemed in that instant no longer merely a scarlet rose but a pure white water-lily too; her eyes flashed with a tender, disenchanted light, and the fine tips of her brows were wrapped in a silent haze of smoke; I was wholly melted in the air she had created.

"It is deep in the night." At last she rose and said: "Good night!"

"Good night." I watched the ethereal curve of her glide past the threshold; with her narcissus-like hand she drew my door softly to; and whether it was enlightenment, or penitence, or gratitude, or love, I know not, but I fell back witless against the soft chair-back, and found that tears had crept itching down my cheeks.
