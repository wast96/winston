## Chapter 8. Renewed Effort, Wave upon Wave

### (1) Always a Step Behind, the Initiative Already Lost

From the twelfth of the eighth month of the twenty-eighth year of the Republic, the work of the Shanghai District was in my charge. To describe it as "taking up office and assuming duties" would not square with the facts; for in truth it amounted to no more than a single meeting with Zheng Xiuyuan, secretary of the Shanghai District, who gave me, by word of mouth, a brief account of how the Shanghai District then stood. To our way of thinking, that discharged the formalities of the handing-over. To put it by way of comparison: had Brother Xiuyuan and I, once we parted, never met again, I truly should not have known in what corner of the earth to go looking for the Shanghai District!

At this juncture the Shanghai District, having suffered such wrecking, had lost the working power of its organization; every tie, inward and outward, depended wholly upon Brother Zheng Xiuyuan, who upheld it single-handed in a perilous environment. The course of it he has set down in detail in his own True Record of Three Perils on the Shanghai Shore, and I shall not say much of it here. In a word, it was thanks to Brother Xiuyuan's mighty propping and bridging that things held; else I, this "District Chief," might well have gone a good long while a chief in name with nothing under my command.

Of the glorious deeds wrought by the thousands of comrades of the Shanghai District—which won for the eight years of the War of Resistance the highest renown in slaying the foe to good effect—in a span of little more than two years, by the blood and sweat, the very liver and gall of the comrades, more than two hundred sanction-actions against Chinese traitors were carried through, such as those of Fu Xiao'an and Zhang Xiaolin; more than fifty works of sabotage in concert with military operations were carried out, such as the blowing up of airfields and the demolition of munitions depots; and thirty or forty Japanese officers on active service, in full uniform, were cut down, among them several of the rank of major-general and colonel, each one named and known. And there was more:

Beyond the gathering and handling of intelligence proper to its station, which ranked as routine work, there was, at a higher level, the political fomenting of defections back to the fold, and, on the broadest scale, mass movements and the like; all of which may be reckoned intelligence work, or special-service work, of the very first order.

What this account is to set down is still the matter of how the sanction of Wang Jingwei was carried on; as for the many episodes lying outside the Wang case, those I shall leave to be written in the Third Part of Nameless Heroes.

The carrying-on of the Wang case was a settled charge of the Bureau; the more so as Wang Jingwei had, from the eighth of the sixth month, become a wanted man to be seized and brought to justice. By this time those ordered to carry out the sanction of Wang were no longer confined to the single unit of the Shanghai District, and not all who had a hand in the case were under the Shanghai District's liaison; so that what is set down here is only what I have seen in the records, and what I can still call to mind. Yet I believe there will be no great many omissions—and if there are, they will be the particular connections that Mr. Dai Yunong worked directly and in person.

Before any activity could be set afoot, one had first to gain some understanding of Wang Jingwei's movements and of the arrangements guarding him. It was learned that Wang left Japan on the eighteenth of the sixth month of the twenty-eighth year, went to Tianjin to draw over Wu Peifu and was rebuffed; and, after exchanging a round of hollow courtesies over the telephone with the number-one head of the puppet regime of North China—that same Wang Kemin who had survived our guns—returned again to Shanghai on the twenty-eighth of the sixth month. Writing to this point, the writer is stirred by no little feeling. In the third month of the twenty-seventh year, I had led the men under comrade Wang Wen and, before the gate of No. 30 Meizha Lane in Beiping, braved death to sanction Wang Kemin; the thing was all but done, and failed at the last—had there not, at that moment, been that Japanese adviser Yamamoto Eiji who threw away his own life to shield him, that work might very well have been brought off. And the blow at Hanoi likewise met with mishap and failed; else—one might even say that, had the circumstances been but a little altered—where would there have been this whole play of the Wang-and-Wang meeting at all?

On the twenty-eighth of the eighth month, at No. 76 Jessfield Road in Shanghai, Wang convened what was styled the "Sixth National Congress of the Chinese Kuomintang." Two months had gone to preparing it; it played for two or three days; and Wang Jingwei thereupon named himself its "Chairman." Rather than call this "banding together for private gain," it were fitter to call it "passing off fish-eyes for pearls." Yet of the place where the congress met—No. 76 Jessfield Road—there is more to be said than of the congress itself. Jessfield Road lay in the western part of Shanghai and belonged to that portion of roads built by the International Settlement beyond its bounds; which is to say that it was not, at the outset, within the Settlement's limits, but that the administration of the International Settlement, half openly and half in the dark, built up many a road there and took it under its jurisdiction. Put grandly, this was "aggression"; looked at more plainly, it may be reckoned "encroachment"; in a word, it was unlawful. "No. 76" was the property of Chen Diaoyuan, former chairman of Anhui Province; whether Wang and his fellows had the owner's consent to use the place there is now no way to look into, nor does it matter. It was, they say, at that time no more than an empty house. But once that great mansion had been used for the congress, it became the cradle of the Wang puppet regime—the "thieves' den" where the high heads of the enemy and the puppets forever gathered and dispersed. And after the traitor Wang had gone to Nanjing to play the monkey in a hat, "No. 76" was transformed into the "General Headquarters of Special Services." In that sink of iniquity there were courtrooms, there were prisons, there were execution-grounds, every shape and kind of thing, brimming with mystery, cruelty, and terror. In those days a Shanghainese had but to mention "No. 76," and for the most part would blanch as at the name of a tiger, and shudder, with a touch of nausea besides.

Of all that concerns "No. 76," the writer means to give a most minute account, in the Third and Fourth Parts of Nameless Heroes respectively: the Third Part to tell how the Shanghai District under my lead grappled with "No. 76"; the Fourth Part to tell the true tale of how I myself, once fallen into the demons' clutches, dealt and fenced with such fellows. As for how it stood with me after my arrest, I have described it myself as turning about upon my heel in the very "Courts of Hell" and coming back to the light of the living, reborn a man—though there are some who reproach me for not having gone calmly to my death, for a failing in the great point of honor; and others still who ask, doubting, why did they not kill you? To all this the writer will in due course render the reader true answers; only wait and watch. To return to Wang's movements and the puppet regime he was contriving:

On the twelfth of the ninth month, the Japanese military, to unify the command of all their army forces in China, set up the "General Headquarters of the China Expeditionary Army" at Nanjing, naming the Inspector-General of Military Education, General Nishio Toshizō, as Commander-in-Chief, and transferring in the former War Minister, Lieutenant-General Itagaki Seishirō—he who had once held negotiations with Wang Jingwei at Tokyo, there asking a high price and giving a low estimate—as Chief of the General Staff. This was a deliberate arrangement; for this Itagaki Seishirō, from the time Japan seized the Northeast and threw North China into disorder, had all along been at work manufacturing China's division, and was the man among Japanese soldiers most adept at the use of "political stratagem." What the Japanese called "political stratagem," our own ancient China described as "the arts of the Vertical and Horizontal Alliances"; evolved to the present day, grown ever more tangled and manifold in its devices, it might well be brought under the heads of "psychological warfare" and "political warfare"—a four-character idiom can no longer contain it. Wang, at this moment, was all afire to set up his puppet regime; and though he knew well he had boarded a pirate ship, he meant to stake all upon a single throw, and would take no heed of the rest.

On the twenty-fourth of the tenth month, the Japanese government put before Wang the "Guiding Principles for the Central Political Conference." This was, in effect, a general reply to the "Hopes Concerning Respect for China's Sovereignty" that Wang had submitted to Japan during his visit there; and it was, at the same time, a set of guiding principles for the puppet regime that Wang purposed to organize. Its content: that, over and above the "Central Government," Japanese advisers should be installed throughout the departments in charge of finance, economy, science and technology; and that in the "Military Advisory Corps" soon to be formed, no persons of "third countries"—German, Italian, and the like—might be engaged. That is: save for the Japanese, no person of any other nationality whatever was to be allowed within the "Military Advisory Corps."

The "consultation" meetings between the two sides were held at Wang Jingwei's residence on Yuyuan Road in Shanghai.

By this time I had taken over as chief of the Shanghai District for a little more than two months; and among its many tasks, the sanction of Wang Jingwei was of course one of the weighty ones. For that task alone, the first thing we had to reconnoiter was where Wang lodged. It was learned that Wang lived on Yuyuan Road—a very, very long street. Whether the stretch near the center of the International Settlement belonged to the Settlement, or was road built beyond its bounds, I still could not make out; one would have to inquire of an old Shanghai hand. Going west, the stretch that grew ever quieter was likely the "western Shanghai" belt of the extra-boundary roads. In other words, "western Shanghai" was the zone that lay within the reach of Japanese power. Wang Jingwei's temporary residence was in Lane 1136, Yuyuan Road (another source gives it as No. 1132)—a narrow little lane; to enter it from the street one passed along a crooked alleyway, wide enough for a motorcar to pass but with no place for two to meet, its breadth thereby easily imagined. Within the lane were only private dwellings, and those coming and going were no mixed or crowded throng. It was a "dead-end lane," allowing passage only one way in and out, with no side turning to be taken. Wang lived in the two households at the very end; what numbers those two doors bore I cannot now recall. One of them opened to the east, the other faced the front; between the two ran a brick wall, with no passage through. The two houses were much of a plan—three storeys high, broad in their bays, covering an area of somewhere about a hundred ping—only the courtyard was not large enough, and not even a motorcar could be driven in.

Now for their defenses: Wang's residence, as it now stood, was no longer at all what the Gao Lang Street house at Hanoi had been. On the main street at the mouth of Lane 1136 one could already see plain-clothes bodyguards loitering to and fro; they wore no uniform, but were all in short jackets, a box-cannon slung at the hip—covered, to be sure, beneath the lapel, yet careless of the sidelong glances of passersby. From the lane's mouth inward, every few dozen paces there stood one or two of them; and further in, there passersby were stopped altogether. In such a case, we had no opening. It was, again, that I let a fine chance slip.

The two houses at the end of Lane 1136, Yuyuan Road, of which I spoke just now, were said to be the property of Wang Boqun, former Minister of Communications, and were, in all likelihood, requisitioned by the Japanese army. While Wang Jingwei lived there I could not possibly go in to look; I never so much as passed along Yuyuan Road and turned my head to steal a glance at the lane's mouth. But afterward Wang moved away, and the two households of Li Shiqun and Wu Sibao moved in; and I myself was arrested at the end of the tenth month of the thirtieth year, and Li Shiqun's first interrogation of me was in this very place. And so my impression of this house is particularly deep. There is more: by the thirty-first year, when Li Shiqun and I had "come to an understanding," we conferred there yet more secretly on how to seize Wang Jingwei, making ready for a great stroke—this too was on the third floor of this same house, with Li's wife Ye Jiqing putting in her word from the side at the time. It sounds more and more far-fetched; but this is no idle chatter, and the Fourth Part of the book will make all clear.

Again as to where Wang lodged. Besides the two houses at the end of Lane 1136, Yuyuan Road, mentioned above, there was yet another place, at No. 20 Yuyuan Road. This was the property of Chen Qun, who was wont to use the house as a means of drawing in men of political weight. And so, in the Wang puppet regime, he fished up for himself a "Chairmanship of Jiangsu Province."

There is a hidden bit of history bound up with No. 20 Yuyuan Road, which I may as well bring in here. Back in the twenty-first year of the Republic, when Deng Yanda, leader of the Third Party, was carrying on political activity in Shanghai, Chen Qun entertained him too as a guest lodging in this house—and then the mischief befell: Deng Yanda was arrested here and taken off to Nanjing. Now Chen offered the same house up again to curry favor with Wang Jingwei; and if the Wang household set any store by old wives' tales, this was no good omen.

What this house looked like I cannot set down, because I never went there. Wang Jingwei did once make it his office, but only for a very short while.

On the Japanese side, the organ that kept up regular liaison with Wang and his crew was a queer and curious body called the "Ume Kikan," whose first chief was that same Kagesa Sadaaki who had gone to Hanoi to fetch Wang Jingwei to Shanghai. As for why it was called the "Ume Kikan"—the Plum Agency—the story goes that the organ was housed in a mansion in the Hongkou district of Shanghai called the "Hall of the Plum Blossom," whence the name was taken; though some say it was ranged after the three words "pine, bamboo, plum." In those days, throughout the Japanese-occupied areas and across Southeast Asia, there were still "special-service organs" of every sort of name.

I bring in this "Ume Kikan" on purpose, because two years further on I had direct dealings with this "special-service organ"—one might call it a whole "war of spies." By that time the first chief, Kagesa, had been posted elsewhere, and the duties were being filled in an acting capacity by Colonel Haruke Yoshitane, with, besides, a chief planner named Nakajima Shin'ichi. Nakajima held no formal soldier's rank, but was a university professor; and the most subtle thing of all was that, though Nakajima had discovered that I was still in contact with Chongqing and kept a secret wireless station in regular communication with Yanshan in Jiangxi, he yet held his peace and did not expose it. Nor was that all: when the Japanese gendarmerie in Shanghai, tracking down the site of the station, turned up the transmitter-receiver, I myself was fortunate enough to get clear, but the operator Zhang Yamin was unwarily taken—while the other comrades still hung in deadly peril. At that point Nakajima, far from working with the gendarmerie, safely shielded my subordinates, and had them escorted to Hangzhou and hidden away, right up until the victory in the War of Resistance and Japan's surrender. This affair, in the ordinary reason of things, is simply past all belief; had it not been a fact I lived through myself, I fear I should not credit it even now. How did the particulars run? Wherein lay the cause? For that, too, one must wait until the Fourth Part of Nameless Heroes is published before the answer can be seen.

Let me go on, taking up the thread again from the twelfth month of the twenty-eighth year, when Wang Jingwei set his hand to the terms that sold out the country.

On the thirtieth of the twelfth month Wang Jingwei signed a document styled the "Outline for Adjusting the New Relations between Japan and China," conceding to Japan a great many, many special privileges; and he even pleaded in his own defense: "A country as vast as China is not one I could sell; even had I signed, it would be no more than the indenture of my own person." The contents of this "Outline" were made public on the twenty-first of the first month of the twenty-ninth year.

On the twentieth of the first month, Wang Jingwei went from Shanghai to Qingdao to treat of the merging and reorganization with Wang Kemin, head of the Beiping puppet "Provisional Government," and Liang Hongzhi, head of the Nanjing puppet "Reformed Government"; and Itagaki Seishirō, Chief of Staff of the Japanese Expeditionary Army General Headquarters, went along too. The upshot was that the Beiping "Provisional Government" was taken into the Wang puppet regime and renamed the "North China Political Affairs Council," while yet retaining a high degree of "autonomy"—which needs explaining: the so-called "autonomy" belonged not to the "North China Political Affairs Council" but to the master behind it, the Japanese Expeditionary Army stationed in North China. The Nanjing "Reformed Government" was dissolved, and its members absorbed by the Wang puppet regime.

We got wind of Wang Jingwei's going from Shanghai to Qingdao; but this was only a fleeting scrap of intelligence, gone in a moment. Though the head office of the Juntong wired orders to the "Qingdao Station" to gather, close at hand, intelligence on the movements of "Wang, Wang, and Liang," and, should the chance offer, to carry out a sanction, yet the "Qingdao Station" was an intelligence unit, with no equipment for action; how could it discharge so formidable a task?

From the time Wang left Shanghai for the conference at Qingdao, the Shanghai District under my lead lost its grip upon Wang Jingwei's movements; as to exact times, we knew neither when he returned to Shanghai nor when he removed to Nanjing. This showed that the work of sanctioning Wang Jingwei grew harder the further it went on—and that it would bring our comrades, too, to face grievous sacrifice.

On the twenty-second of the third month of the twenty-ninth year, Wang gathered a number of people at Nanjing to hold what was styled a "Central Government Conference," at which the name of the puppet establishment was passed as the "National Government"; and it was resolved to hold its "Ceremony of the Return of the Capital" on the twenty-sixth of the third month. As I have said above, this way of proceeding was, in the truest sense, "passing off fish-eyes for pearls." On the twenty-third Wang went further and issued a public statement, falsely claiming that this stage-set of his was the one "orthodox and legitimate government."

The "return of the capital" farce staged at Nanjing was put off four days from the appointed twenty-sixth of the third month, and was played on the thirtieth. Why the hindrance? Here too there are many tales told; and the Japanese government's "formal recognition" of the Wang puppet regime was likewise held in abeyance a full eight months, its date being the thirtieth of the eleventh month of the twenty-ninth year. And why was that? There are not a few inside stories in all this; but as they have no bearing on the Wang case that I carried out, there is no need to let the branches grow wild. There is, however, one thing I must particularly mention to guard against misunderstanding: the Juntong did once assign working comrades to take part, in disguise, in the "peace talks" with Japan. Understand, please, that this was "intelligence work" and by no means "political activity"; and if it could not be wholly cut off from the political sphere, that was only a political application of the work. The Juntong had a traditional rule that forbade any taking part in "political activity." So far as the writer knows, one of the Bureau's Hong Kong chiefs during the War of Resistance, the senior comrade Mr. Liu Fangxiong, once had charge of the matter. On the Japanese side the representative was Imai Takeo, once head of the Second Section of the Staff Office of the "China Expeditionary Army General Headquarters," afterward promoted Deputy Chief of Staff. The full particulars of it Mr. Liu Fangxiong set forth some years ago in a book he brought out, entitled The Highest Warfare Attacks Strategy—though it has not been widely circulated; it is a first-rate source of real historical value.

### (2) Grief Recollected: Every Word a Lament for Those Who Fell in the Wang Case

Though it was hard past all telling, we still, wave upon wave—one falling and the next stepping forward—carried on the work of sanctioning Wang Jingwei.

From the spring of the twenty-ninth year, when Wang settled for a long stay at Nanjing, there were two large units fixed with the task of sanctioning Wang Jingwei: one, the Shanghai District in my charge; the other, the Nanjing District in the charge of Qian Xinmin. Besides these, there were many individuals or small units "sent out singly"; and these small units, needing support and guidance, were assigned severally to the liaison of the Shanghai District or the Nanjing District, which—according to their differing needs—forwarded letters and telegrams for them, allotted funds, conveyed equipment, and furnished technical advisers and the like. To put it plainer still: over the lines of connection that the Shanghai District or the Nanjing District had themselves developed and set going, they held the power of command; but over those merely handed to them for liaison by higher authority, they did not.

It was precisely because higher authority handed such working connections to the Shanghai District for liaison that the writer, as the man in charge of the Shanghai District, came to know the general outline of them, or at least some odd scale and half-claw of what was linked to them. In what follows I set down these deeds of valor.

Item One: the Martyr Wu Gengshu and the Martyr Dai Jingyuan.—Mr. Wu Gengshu, in the twenty-fourth and twenty-fifth years of the Republic, had served as chief of this Bureau's "Tianjin Station"; he was both my successor and my predecessor. This circumstance I mentioned in the fifth section of the First Part of my Nameless Heroes, Rooting Out Traitors in the North. That is to say: at the end of the twenty-third year, after I left the post, Mr. Wu Gengshu took over the "Tianjin Station"; and in the summer of the twenty-fifth year, after he was transferred away, I was again ordered back to the "Tianjin Station." Twice thus our terms joined end to end, yet we had never met. After that, each went about his own affairs.

Mr. Gengshu, a native of Changsha in Hunan, was a graduate of Lingnan University in Canton. Before joining this Bureau he had worked in the "Investigation Section of the Nanchang Field Headquarters," a unit originally led by Mr. Deng Wenyi; and after it was reorganized and merged into the Juntong, Mr. Gengshu became a capable cadre of the Bureau, holding in turn such high posts as chief of the "Henan Station," chief of the "Canton Station," and director of the "Hengyang Office." In the eleventh month of the twenty-eighth year of the Republic he was ordered in to train in the Party-Government Training Class of the Central Training Corps.

When I trained in the third session of the Party-Government Class of the Central Training Corps, it was the seventh month of the twenty-eighth year—four months apart from him. By this time I had already been posted to Shanghai; and so, to my regret, there was again no chance for us to come to know one another.

During his training Mr. Gengshu met an old schoolmate, Mr. Dai Xingbing—Dai's original name was Xingbing, later changed to Jingyuan—who served as a major-general adviser to the Military Affairs Commission. Old friends met again, they would often, in the rest hours after class, find some quiet spot to talk over old times; and one thing led to another until they came to speak of the matter of Wang Jingwei. This was a great affair; anyone with the least care for the affairs of the state would of course speak of it.

Whether they lamented the failure of the blow at Hanoi I do not know; but they did give vent to the sigh, "Jing Ke and Nie Zheng—where are they now?" At this point Mr. Dai Jingyuan then laid the whole matter, plainly and fully, before Mr. Wu Gengshu: that he had received a letter from one of Wang's trusted intimates, inviting him to go over and join the puppets.

At this Mr. Gengshu, struck by a sudden inspiration, fell straightway to planning, and the two went together to ask an audience of Mr. Dai Yunong. Mr. Dai accepted the two men's proposal to root out a traitor for the country's sake, and bade them at once draw up a plan of work.

The plan ran roughly thus: Mr. Dai Jingyuan should come forward, under the name of accepting the invitation to join the Wang puppet regime, and go to Shanghai and Nanjing, there first to meet the contact-man Chen Shisheng, and then to ask Chen Shisheng to present him to Wang Jingwei, and thereafter, according to the actual circumstances, to contrive the further design. All the activity Dai Jingyuan would carry on in the open, while Wu Gengshu supported him in the dark.

The two, Wu Gengshu and Dai Jingyuan, reached Shanghai in the first month of the twenty-ninth year. By this time the Shanghai District had received Mr. Dai's telegraphed instructions to give them proper support and every needful aid. It was then that the writer first met Mr. Wu Gengshu. My first impression of him was that at a glance he looked the scholar, and his refined speech bespoke his cultivation. Yet in point of tradecraft he kept something back—which was that he never once let fall a word about Dai Jingyuan.

Writing to this point, I must put in a word of supplement: at the time I knew only that Mr. Wu Gengshu's task was to lay out the work of sanctioning Wang Jingwei, and had no clear idea to whom his line of connection ran; while what I now write has of course been blended with material I came to know later. This explanation, I think, also makes plain that I was no seer who knows beforehand.

At the end of the first month of the twenty-ninth year Wang left Shanghai for Qingdao; and after returning from Qingdao to Shanghai, within a few days he went on again to Nanjing. And so Dai Jingyuan, together with Wu Gengshu, followed after him in pursuit. From this time the Shanghai District lost contact with Mr. Wu Gengshu. The hard thing to fathom is that in all those years no further word was heard of Wu Gengshu and the rest. Whether the Wang puppet side ever made the matter public I never saw; and on the Bureau's side, I suppose, few knew of it, and so no one ever spoke of it. I know not how many years passed before, reading the Bureau's records, I learned that the two, Mr. Wu Gengshu and Mr. Dai Jingyuan, had already laid down their lives for the country.

It is recorded that, after the two, Wu and Dai, reached Nanjing, Dai Jingyuan alone met with Chen Shisheng, and by Chen Shisheng was presented to Wang Jingwei. Wang thereupon appointed Dai Jingyuan a member of the "Special Committee on Social Enterprises." This committee was subordinate to the puppet "Central Political Committee," and though it was an idle sinecure, it had nonetheless gained a footing.

Who exactly this "Chen Shisheng" recorded in the material may have been, there is no explanation; by my own guess, perhaps he too was of Wang's wife's kindred—else how should he have come by such a foothold?

The affair had been developing normally enough; but because Mr. Dai Jingyuan pressed too hard, and kept probing Chen Shisheng for Wang Jingwei's daily comings and risings, he awoke Chen Shisheng's suspicion. Had he but taken warning betimes and drawn in a little, he might perhaps have passed safely through the crisis, and then, going slowly, still have hoped to advance. But after Dai had brought his family over, in the course of exchanging courtesies with Chen's wife he once more let slip an unwary word; and so all that had been gained was thrown away—the affair failed, and he was seized; nor did Mr. Wu Gengshu, who oversaw from the side, escape, but was drawn in with him.

After the two, Mr. Dai Jingyuan and Mr. Wu Gengshu, were arrested, they contrived, while in custody, to get a letter sent out to Mr. Dai Yunong, in which they bitterly set forth their sorrow and shame at having failed to fulfill their charge.

What most wrings my heart with pain and shame is that the traitor Wang took to means of revenge, choosing of set purpose that particular day—the twenty-first of the third month, the very day of the blow struck at him at Hanoi—to march Dai and Wu out to Yuhuatai for execution; and so the two martyrs, Dai and Wu, gave up their lives at one and the same moment.

Item Two: the Martyr Chen Sancai.

Mr. Chen Sancai, too, was a volunteer worker whom Mr. Dai Yunong handed to the Shanghai District for liaison. It was more than a single telegram; because there were many turns and complications in the business, Mr. Dai wired instructions more than once. I never once met Mr. Chen Sancai face to face; the keeping-up of regular contact with Mr. Sancai was assigned to the "New Group One" under the Shanghai District.

The "New Group One" was a unit of mixed establishment, both intelligence and action, of high quality and superior technical standard; and withal able to give Mr. Sancai strong support and aid.

Mr. Chen Sancai, a native of Wu County in Jiangsu, after graduating from Tsinghua University in Beiping went to study in America, read electrical engineering, and took his degree. He then entered the famous American firm of Westinghouse for practical training, and returned home in the fourteenth year of the Republic. He founded the Arctic Electric Company at Shanghai and made himself its manager, and had a part in launching the "Chinese Institute of Engineers." In his leisure he gave himself to social-welfare work, and at ordinary times was especially mindful of the great affairs of the nation. In the twenty-first year of the Republic, when in the "January 28" affair the Japanese army fell upon Shanghai, Mr. Sancai at once drew together many patriotic men of spirit and, with his technical skill, helped our forces to throw up defensive works, giving both money and effort, and contributing much.

How Mr. Sancai came to be acquainted with Mr. Yunong I do not quite know—whether it was through the introduction of a certain general's consort, a lady named Tian, or a lady named Chen, I truly dare not say for certain; in any case, the relation between them was that of friends, and by no means of superior and subordinate.

Mr. Sancai's helping the Bureau to carry on the sanction of Wang Jingwei was wholly a deed of righteousness—one might call it a patriotic loyalty sublimed out of high indignation. Only because Mr. Sancai could get no chance to cut down the traitor with his own hand did he spare no money or silk in casting a wide net for leads, that he might not fail Mr. Dai's trust, and might go on to fulfill his heart's wish of rooting out traitors to requite the country.

Mr. Chen Sancai had dealings with not a few who called themselves men who "had ways and means"; but in the end it was either a tiger's head trailing off to a snake's tail, or they went off and were never heard of again—and among them, of course, there were bound to be some who meant all along only to cheat a bit of money to spend. The Shanghai District and the "New Group One" received, at one time and another, several letters from Chen Sancai marked "for the record," and nothing ever came of any of them. This did not mean that Mr. Sancai's efforts fell short, but that it truly was too hard a thing.

In the course of this Mr. Sancai made use of a man of uncertain nationality; the fellow gave out that he was Italian, but this was mere talk—by all judgment he was either a Jew or a White Russian. The terms he touched on had, beyond money, no political factor at all. In truth, Mr. Sancai was taken in by this man's honeyed words; and most detestable of all, the man, once he had cheated the money into his hands, then, with all conscience blotted out, sold Mr. Sancai out into the bargain.

It was learned afterward that Mr. Sancai, taken in the summer of the twenty-ninth year at Suzhou, was sent under guard to Nanjing. It is told that the traitor Wang in person conducted the examination and questioning, and that Mr. Sancai answered, "A traitor to the state is one whom every man may put to death"; and so, on the second of the tenth month of the twenty-ninth year, at Nanjing, he went calmly to his death.

In the seventh and eighth months Mr. Dai wired the Shanghai District several times, bidding it do all in its power to rescue Mr. Chen Sancai; but what could be done where nothing could avail! After Mr. Sancai's martyrdom, Mr. Dai, at Chongqing and in the name of the Tsinghua University Alumni Association, held a memorial service, and petitioned Generalissimo Chiang to bestow a funeral tablet inscribed "A Valor to Rank with Changshan," to set forth the martyr Chen's righteous heroism.

Item Three: the Martyr Huang Yiguang.

Mr. Huang Yiguang was a stout airman; because he had a certain past connection with Wang, he was recommended by air-force commanders into the Juntong, given a short course of training, and sent to Nanjing, charged with the task of sanctioning Wang Jingwei.

Between the Shanghai District and Mr. Huang Yiguang there was no working tie; it was, as I understand, the Nanjing District that was charged with keeping contact with him.

The material records: Comrade Huang Yiguang, a native of Chixi in Guangdong, an overseas Chinese of Mexico. In the twenty-fourth year of the Republic he joined with friends to make a journey round the world on foot, and reaching Paris, at a welcoming gathering came to know Wang Jingwei. In the twenty-seventh year, when Comrade Huang Yiguang was traveling through Africa, he heard that his homeland's capital had already fallen into the enemy's hands, and thereupon broke off his round-the-world journey, took ship back to China, and went straight to Hankou, where he met Wang Jingwei once more. Wang kept him lodging in his private residence, and wrote a letter introducing him to the Kunming Aviation School for training; and at his departure gave him money for the road besides.

When Huang was assigned his class at the aviation school, being unfit for combat flying he was put to ground crew, which Comrade Huang keenly regretted. And when the traitor Wang, having betrayed the country, was contriving his puppet establishment at Nanjing, Wang's brother-in-law Chen Yaozu sent a letter to Comrade Huang Yiguang, holding that Wang had shown him kindness, and inciting Comrade Huang to answer within the air force to the "Peace Movement." Huang's reporting to his superiors the matter of the letter he had received from Chen Yaozu was, indeed, the chief reason he was recommended to enlist in the service of the Juntong.

After Comrade Huang Yiguang reached Nanjing, though he was appointed to such posts as puppet "Special Commissioner of the Ministry of Education" and "compiler-reviser of the Central Propaganda Department," yet, his position being no high one, he can have had but few chances, one supposes, of drawing near to Wang.

Of late I have seen an essay by Mr. Liu Shoufa, "Remembering an Old Friend, Recalling Bygone Days at the Aviation School: Huang Yiguang, the Stout Fellow Who Would Have Struck Down Wang," which gives a somewhat more minute account of Comrade Huang Yiguang; and I here excerpt a few passages, in the hope of deepening the impression of him in men's minds, that they may add a few gleams of light to his valor.

"On our way from Liuzhou toward Kunming, the Central Aviation School was ordered to be changed into the Air Force Officers' School, the principal's post still held concurrently by Generalissimo Chiang; the dean of studies was General Zhou Zhirou, and General Wang Shuming was promoted to chief of the education section. The branch schools were abolished and a three-grade system—advanced, intermediate, and elementary—was set up instead: the advanced class at the main school in Kunming, the intermediate class at Mengzi, the elementary class at Yunnanyi in Xiangyun County. In the days just before our flying began, a motorcar brought in a dozen or more young men to report to the corps and join the flight training. Among these dozen or more young men there were military civil officials, university graduates then serving on the Aviation Affairs Commission; there were serving officers already graduated from the Army Officers' School; there were some who had learned flying in Japan or in the South Seas; and there was one most singular friend of all—Huang Yiguang, who had studied aviation in Mexico and traveled the whole world on foot. We held a welcoming feast, with extra dishes, to greet this batch of new comrades. From then on we led the soldier's life, sharing sweet and bitter alike, standing together through hardship and peril. Huang Yiguang was not very tall, about 165 centimeters only, but his build was uncommonly sturdy—his skin dark with a ruddy glow, thick brows and great eyes, thick arms, broad shoulders, a round waist, and mighty strength; whether at trials of strength or at wrestling, none of us was his match, for he had besides the experience of having traveled the whole world on foot.

"He would say: 'My native place is Chixi County in Guangdong Province; from childhood I lived abroad in Mexico. Feeling how poor and weak my homeland was, forever bullied and abused by foreigners and called by them the Sick Man of East Asia, I took it deeply to shame, and so bent all my will to grow strong: on the one hand training my body for self-defense, on the other studying to serve society and to save my homeland. First I studied education, graduating from the Mexican National Normal School, that I might rear men of talent; then I studied aviation, graduating from the civil aviation school, awaiting the chance to requite my homeland. When the war of resistance against Japan broke out, I had thought to offer what I had learned to my homeland—to fly a warplane in defense of my homeland's skies and beat back the Japanese bandits' incursions; but who could have known that civil aviation and military aviation differ in so many points, and that what I had learned could not be put to use? It is a bitter disappointment indeed.' He would say: 'A true man, born into a time of his country's peril, ought to slay the foe and requite his country....'"

The conduct and character of the martyr Huang Yiguang, as set down by Mr. Liu Shoufa above, may give us a fuller knowledge of him.

As to Comrade Huang Yiguang's later mischance and arrest, there is one account: that it too was owing to his pressing too hard, and so waking men's doubts, until in the end his working identity was laid bare. But I have in my hands a document sufficient to prove that he was implicated through others.

This document is a telegram Mr. Dai Yunong sent me from Chongqing on the eighteenth of the twelfth month of the twenty-ninth year, at which time my post was that of chief of the Shanghai District. The wording ran thus: "Deliver at once. Shanghai. Cipher grade O. For Brother Guzhong to decipher in person. After the trouble in the Nanjing District, the puppet General Headquarters of Special Services sent men to Nanjing some days ago and carried off Comrade Huang Yiguang, whom I had dispatched to Nanjing to sanction the traitor Wang. All personnel of the Shanghai District who have had dealings with, or are known to, the Nanjing District should at once be severally moved away and put under cover. Pray, Brother, look into it and act accordingly without delay! Let there be no miscarriage, above all! From your younger brother Jinshui, respectfully—sent from Chongqing in person, the day Hao, hour Hai."

(Note: "Guzhong" was the writer's cover name at that time; "Jinshui" was one of Mr. Dai's cover names.)

From this telegram it may plainly be seen, beyond all doubt, that Comrade Huang Yiguang's arrest was a thing spread to him by the arrest of certain comrades of the "Nanjing District." This shows not only how perilous the working environment then was; it also makes clear that in our work the least little leak or gap could brew a great calamity.

Comrade Huang Yiguang was arrested on the nineteenth of the eleventh month of the twenty-ninth year, and gave up his life on the seventeenth of the twelfth month; before his death he asked for paper and brush and wrote these lines: "Beloved China, I would sing for you, I would die for you!"

Writing to this point, the writer cannot but be dimmed and wounded in spirit. Beyond offering this martyr my most reverent mourning, I feel too the more keenly our heartfelt guilt: true, there is no lack of earlier instances of our working comrades falling in such circumstances; yet those who stand in the leading rank, the men in charge of the units, can never be free of the blame of oversight, of dereliction, of miscalculation—and searching my heart in the still of the night, how should I not be inwardly ashamed before the gods?

Item Four: the Martyr Shao Mingxian.—

Comrade Shao Mingxian, a graduate of the Hangzhou Police Officers' School, had held such posts as chief of the police bureau of Jiangning County. The magistrate of Jiangning County at that time was none other than Mei Siping, now a leading figure of the Wang puppet regime. It was precisely because there was this bond of past connection between Shao and Mei that, once Mei Siping had stepped forth robed and tabletted upon the stage, he wrote a special letter inviting Comrade Shao Mingxian—then conducting police training at Guiyang—to come and assist him. Shao at once reported to Mr. Dai, and of his own will offered to seize this chance and go at the invitation: first to gain a puppet office as cover, and then, watching his moment, to sanction Wang Jingwei.

Shao Mingxian, in the winter of the twenty-eighth year, reached Shanghai with his family; and by Mei Siping's arrangement he first served as secretary-general in the puppet "Zhejiang Provincial Party Headquarters," and in the thirtieth year was transferred to be inspector-general of the puppet "Capital Police Department."

Comrade Shao Mingxian had no tie of contact with the Shanghai District; by inference, he was likely in liaison with the Nanjing District. Whether his mischance and arrest, like Comrade Huang Yiguang's, came of being implicated through others, cannot be known. On what day he was arrested the material likewise does not record; only that, together with Comrade Huang Yiguang, on the same seventeenth of the twelfth month of the twenty-ninth year, he gave up his life at Nanjing.

So far as I know, these were the fourth group to lay down their lives for the country, after the "Hanoi work," through the failure of the sanction of Wang Jingwei. Of course these were not the only ones sacrificed; of those not entered in the records there must surely be others still.

These four cases above fully show forth our great and dauntless spirit—pressing ever valiantly forward, wave upon wave, one falling and the next stepping into his place. Yet to speak of each on its own merits, and weigh in earnest every case, the working environment and the timing of the attempts were in each too little ripe, the conditions too thin and weak. Leaning solely on a scrap of undependable personal connection, how could the thing be brought off? Even had one waited for some chance turn that let him come near Wang, though he threw away his life to seek to perish together with his foe, the mission was not thereby sure to be fulfilled. Then why, in such a case, did we still do so?—and again and yet again go to the sacrifice? For no other reason but this: our will was set on ridding the nation of this arch-traitor, and while there remained but a hair's breadth of hope, we would never lightly let it pass!

In the matter of sanctioning Wang Jingwei, though the Juntong, never relenting, sought out and followed the trail without cease—from Hanoi to Shanghai, from Shanghai to Nanjing—and though it sacrificed, one after another, the lives of many working comrades and patriotic men of spirit, yet it still could not fulfill the mission.

By about the eighth month of the thirty-second year, Wang Jingwei, feeling pain again from the bullet still lodged in his chest and back (the result of the shooting he had suffered on the first of the eleventh month of the twenty-fourth year at the Central Party Headquarters in Nanjing) which had not yet been taken out, underwent, on the nineteenth of the twelfth month, an operation by Japanese physicians at the Japanese Army Hospital in Nanjing to remove the bullet, and for a time felt tolerably well. At the beginning of the thirty-third year he felt unwell again—not only with repeated high fevers, but even paralysis of the lower limbs; and, having no choice, on the third of the third month of the thirty-third year he crossed eastward with Chen Bijun and the rest, and entered the hospital attached to the Imperial University at Nagoya for treatment. Medicine availing nothing, eight months later, at twenty minutes past sixteen o'clock on the tenth of the eleventh month, Wang died at Nagoya in Japan.

He is dead and gone; there is no more to be said.

### (3) In Life and Death, Honor and Disgrace, Fortune and Misfortune Alike

The failure of the Wang case ought rightly to be laid to my own dereliction, while the comrades who took part in the actual work all gave the utmost of their heart and strength. And so in this Nameless Heroes that I write, every one of them is a person worthy to be remembered; and, only so far as the writer knows, I here render an account of their life and death, their whereabouts, and the fortunes they met with in this world. Yet what I myself know is not wholly complete, nor of a certainty a hundred percent correct; should there be any omission, or any discrepancy with the facts, I beg the readers on every hand to instruct me freely, that it may be supplemented and set right.

I mean to write it in three parts: first those who have already passed away; then those whose whereabouts are unknown—among whom, of course, there must be some who have perished, only that no sure news of them can be had; and last of all come the few of us still in the land of the living. As for Mr. Dai Yunong, the leader of our work, he was of course the one who brought it all to a head; but he is already a famed and mighty hero, and several biographies of him have come out, so that there is no need for me to add my own busy touches to set him off.

Those who took direct part in the Hanoi work, besides Mr. Dai, numbered nineteen in all; and now, according to the three parts marked out above, I set them forth severally below.

Tan Tianqian: By word from higher authority, he had been sent to Hanoi to assist me in my work. Because he and I had not known one another before, after reaching Hanoi we had but a few limited contacts, and never discussed in any depth any concrete question bound up with the work; so that my knowledge of him is, one may say, very shallow. As I said earlier, from his outward look, in my impression, he was a deep and brooding man, forever with a careworn face and the air of one weighed down by cares; but for what cause, or whether it was simply his nature, I do not know to this very day. Afterward I heard from others that he had another task at Hanoi; but again I do not know whether this task too had to do with the sanction of Wang Jingwei. Only half a year ago—that is, about the second month of the seventy-first year—a friend in the know told me that Brother Tan Tianqian, after the Hanoi affair, had stayed on for a short while and been active in some way; and before long, on the ground of "handling the work ineffectively," was recalled to the great rear. For what reason exactly? It is past fathoming. Recalled—was it to Chongqing in Sichuan? or to Xifeng in Guizhou? This friend did not say plainly, and I did not think it fitting to press. But from that time on, what became of Brother Tan Tianqian was not known. Why do I not place him in the column of the "whereabouts unknown"? Because no one has said he was ever seen again—and no one has ever so much as spoken of him again.

This is a thing little known to men; and though the affair is long past and the scene long changed, still one cannot speak of it carelessly and at random—which is the chief reason that I would speak and then stop short.

Cen Jiazhuo: a senior schoolmate of the third class of the Military Academy. In the twenty-first year, when I trained at Honggong Ci in Nanjing, he was already assistant to Mr. Zheng Jiemin of the education group. When he was sent to Hanoi, he came of course full of hope; and it was my fault alone that I found him no chance to display his talents to the full—that I gave him not even a place among the confidential staff. It was too great a wrong. This small worldly wisdom I now understand; but at the time I was careless and heedless, and knew nothing of it. Between us there was no struggle for power whatever; it was truly that I was too poor in the experience of the world, and not yet ripe enough. When I think of it, I am filled with regret.

About ten years ago, I think it was, my classmate of the same session, Brother Han Jiwen (styled Shangying), told me that Brother Jiazhuo had died of illness many years before—but where he died, and in what year, he could not say clearly. Not a few years after Brother Shangying gave me this news, he too departed this life. And here I may as well bring in this: Shangying and I have a piece of unforgettable shared history, when at the risk of his own life he once saved mine; had anything gone the least awry at that moment, he and I together might very well long ago have gone hand in hand to the Western Heaven. This story is dramatic enough, and deeply moving too, and must wait to be written in the Fourth Part of Nameless Heroes.

Yu Lexing: he appeared many times in this case, and after I was recalled to Chongqing he still made his mark—he may be called a man of ability. Thereafter it was not until about the third month of the thirty-eighth year, on the eve of the Communist armies' crossing the river and sweeping south, that we managed a hurried meeting in Shanghai. By that time Mr. Yu seemed already to have left intelligence work and turned to public office. He told me he was buried deep in writing a book; but that his wife had fallen into schizophrenia, raging most violently, needing tending every hour and moment, so that each day he had to find three or four people to take turns keeping her company at mahjong before the days could be got through. It had worn him ragged, running himself off his feet, sunk in bitter distress, and truly at his wits' end what to do. His look as he spoke was dejected, listless, and without spirit—no longer the man of dash and grace he had been in those years. When the government removed to Taiwan, he stayed behind on the mainland; the reason is unknown. If it were a matter of ideology, that did not seem to fit either; more likely it was still the burden of his family that undid him! Earlier the report was that he was in Shanghai; later others said he had gone to Beiping. What exactly his standing on the mainland was, and what he did there, no true news of it ever could be got.

Thinking back to our days at Hanoi, I had marked even then that he was a man of the most nervous temper, exceedingly sensitive to anything and everything that happened around him, so that often the least small shock would keep him wakeful the whole night through. With a nature such as this, and his own peculiar background besides, how could he ever have fitted himself to the Communist zone on the mainland, and how could he have gone on existing there? And sure enough, in the end his mind gave way, past all recovery, and before long word of his death came through. Truly, the dead are gone beyond recall! And what is one to say of it?

Yu Jiansheng: he was taken on the spot at Hanoi, sat six years in a French prison, and only at the victory in the War of Resistance was set free and reached Shanghai. I grope after his shadow—it seems I did meet him once while in Shanghai, but of how the scene went I have not the least impression left.

By what Brother Liu Yuanshen tells me, after the victory Brother Yu Jiansheng came from the rear and served in the Inspection Office of the Songhu Garrison General Headquarters. At that time Brother Jiansheng was chief of the inspection detachment for the Lanni Ferry district of Pudong, while Brother Yuanshen was chief of the inspection detachment for the Gaohang district of Chuansha; the two came and went between Shanghai and Pudong, often crossing the Huangpu River at the same time, and moreover, at every memorial-week assembly or work conference, were frequently together; and so from comrades and colleagues they became good friends besides. Brother Yuanshen says further that Jiansheng was honest and simple by nature, richest in a sense of responsibility and of justice, only that he seemed to lack the common run of administrative experience, and was not much used to this sort of open political-security work, so that at times he took great pains for little thanks and was left not knowing whether to laugh or cry. Yet in a man long accustomed to secret work, this is a thing from which even the worthy are not exempt; and so his superiors could commonly make allowance for it.

To speak again of the time Brother Jiansheng and I spent together at Hanoi: his bearing and cultivation, and his way with people and affairs, did indeed leave upon me a most deep impression and remembrance. After his arrest, those several years of prison life tempered him into a man of yet more outstanding parts. In the thirty-eighth year he came to Taiwan and once held the post of chief of the Criminal Police Corps. At that time I was in Taipei too, but to my regret there was no chance for us to meet. And by year's end I was sent abroad to work with a certain nation in laying out operations against the mainland, whereupon mountains and rivers cut us off, and word grew ever scanter between us.

A year or two, or two or three years, later, I suddenly heard that Brother Jiansheng had, most wrongfully and for nothing, lost his life in a motor accident. This is one of life's great misfortunes, and grievous indeed! Had he not died so young, he might very likely have risen high and fast long since. Yet the good name he left behind—whether men knew him or not, none but praised him; and so his one life in this world was not lived in vain.

Mr. Xu: he was, to begin with, an "outsider"—by which "outsider" should be understood one not belonging to the "Juntong." And yet the place he held in the Wang case was truly too important; as may be seen from the many facts written above. Most precious of all, he acted purely as a volunteer, making his offering for the country's sake, with no seeking after name or gain. In truth we never paid him a single cash, nor did he win any office thereby; while we, for our part, brought him no end of trouble, at which he never uttered a word of complaint—a rare man indeed. And when it came to clearing up that whole tangled mess at the last, it was still upon him that we had to lean.

As for Mr. Xu's taking part in the work, at first I always supposed it was a connection handed down from on high, and that this was why he threw himself into it so keenly; only later did I come to realize that very likely it was Brother Fang Bingxi who had recommended him to Mr. Dai, and that above that there was no root at all. If it were indeed so, then he is the more to be admired. That throughout the book it has not been convenient to give his name is truly a thing to regret; and I believe the typical "nameless hero" is, for the most part, shaped just so.

Mr. Xu has been withered and gone these many years; the news reports said he died in Iran, but of the other circumstances I have no clear knowledge.

Last of all, the writer has one small wish: I do not know whether Mr. Xu's part in the Hanoi work has been entered into the records of "this Bureau"? If by any chance it has not, let this account be taken for reference.

Wei Chunfeng: Chunfeng and Miss Nguyen had been joined in wedlock and had children in plenty; I had meant to write of them together, but Brother Chunfeng has been dead now nearly twenty years, while Madame Wei, since returning from Taipei to Vietnam, is to this day of unknown life or death; and so it seems fitter, after all, to speak of them apart. In the fiftieth year of the Republic, before I had yet retired from the Intelligence Bureau, a comrade posted to Saigon brought back word that Brother Wei Chunfeng was now serving as counselor in our embassy in Vietnam, and that, hearing I worked in the Intelligence Bureau, he was greatly pleased. He presently sent, by a passing hand, a name-card; and though it bore but a few words of greeting, it was enough to gladden the heart of an old friend. Parted more than twenty years, having lived through war and turmoil, to get news of him at last—naturally I could not contain my joy, and so wrote a reply, and I recall I bought a Dajia reed mat besides to send him, and set forth too my ardent wish to meet. But the ways of the world are hard to foretell; not long after, he departed this life forever. In the papers seeking a pension it was set down that he died of illness brought on by overwork; yet there were those who said, with every appearance of certainty, that he died of melancholy—and what was the truth of it? That I have no means of judging; I only grieve that my friend lived but fifty-odd years, which is, when all is said, a bitter thing.

While we were at Hanoi he had spoken of it—how he greatly hoped to get into the Consulate-General at Hanoi as a temporary hire. How he afterward rose to be a counselor must have cost a whole course of striving. But Heaven was unkind, and would not grant us even the chance to talk over old times.

Wang Luqiao: the two of us were in the first party to reach Hanoi with Mr. Dai; we lived together for more than two months, and shared many tastes in common—our tempers, though not wholly alike, were on the whole near enough—so that a private friendship grew up between us. After the victory in the War of Resistance, when he came out from prison at Hanoi, we met in Shanghai; and in the thirty-seventh year, when I came out of prison, we met again in Tianjin. At that time Luqiao was inspector-general of the Inspection Office of the Tianjin Garrison Command, and I was commander of a unit directly under the Ministry of National Defense, leading a body of special troops just finished with their training and fitted with the newest equipment, dispatched to the Hebei-Chahar-Suiyuan region to fight the Communist armies. Because we were both in such haste, we had not even time for a cup together and a sitting, and so parted. In the thirty-eighth year we came to Taiwan, one after the other; whether we ever met I cannot recall. After that I was out of the country ten years, and it was only after I came back from Japan in the forty-seventh year that we gathered again. What I cannot forget—and it is a little shaming to tell—is this: I was forever hard up, and over a dozen years and more borrowed of him many times to "tide over"; each time I would write a short note and send an aide-de-camp to "fetch" the money from him, and when it was not convenient for him he would still get it from somewhere else so as to see me through. And to say no more of the rest, I never once repaid him—which, when I speak of it, fills me with shame. There is this too: when he was chief of the Taipei City Police Bureau, people were forever coming to me to put in a word with him for them—all of them, of course, matters that put him in a hard place. And the wonder of it was that every time he would, frank and open-handed, agree with a whole heart to see to it—and yet in the end not one of them came off. From these two things, one may say he was a man who kept public and private clearly apart.

He worked many years in Taiwan's police world, and his name stood very well—only that he died a somewhat wretched death. Yet he may close his eyes in peace in this: that to this very day there are still many who remember this "Elder Wang" who held faith and honor so dear.

After Luqiao's death, at the bidding of old friends and of several journalists, I was asked to write a "record of his conduct" for him; and just as I was turning over in my mind how to set brush to paper, word suddenly came again that it was not needed. For what reason I do not know. And so here too I can only sketch him lightly, as above.

Fang Bingxi: in the early days of writing the Wang case, I always supposed he was still living—and indeed, two years back, someone had seen him with his own eyes in Brussels. Early this year, chatting one time with the younger folk, I mentioned Brother Fang Bingxi by chance, and only then heard that he had already passed away. He was born in the fourth year before the Republic; if he died two years ago, he too was above seventy.

Some years ago I heard he had opened a little bookshop near National Taiwan University on Roosevelt Road in Taipei, and I went on purpose, with a friend, from door to door to seek him out, but everyone said they did not know the man. Then I heard he was a clerk in a private middle school, but could not make out which school, and had no way to begin the search. In what year he afterward went abroad to Belgium, I knew still less. A relative of his, still at his work, mentioned Mr. Fang Bingxi again by chance, and only then did I come to understand a little of his true circumstances: I had always supposed him to be in trade abroad, and some indeed said so, but in fact it was not quite that—that is, besides doing a little business, he bore too the mission of the struggle against the bandits overseas. At the age of the "rare threescore and ten," still not shrinking from hardship—well may one cry out a word of praise: "Remarkable!"

I have talked at length and perhaps still not made it plain; the one thing that matters most to say is this—that Brother Bingxi and I, after our parting at Hanoi, never met again; yet that generous kindness with which he treated me is a thing I can never forget.

These eight above have died, one after another; comrades once in a common cause, I here gaze into the empty air and mourn them! In the life of each of them there were many doings, and greater achievements it may be; and their part in the "Hanoi work" was perhaps but one small passage among them.

Next, to speak of those whose whereabouts are unknown:

Tang Yingjie: through all these many years, I have never heard any word of him. What can now be said for certain is that he stayed on the mainland and did not come out. As for what he stayed on the mainland to do, there is no sequel to tell.

This man was a little odd; he never liked to talk with others about the affairs of his household, and the moment "home" was so much as mentioned, he would either look this way and that and speak of something else, or slip evasively away. There was an old rumor, that in his youth he had beaten his own wife to death and fled into exile, living by performing feats for a crowd. It is but idle talk—where should one go to verify it? But that he had once been a street-performer was true enough.

To speak further of his skill and his gongfu, he did indeed have something in hand; but to describe him as able to "walk up eaves and scale walls," and I know not what marvels, would be to overshoot the mark. I once tried every means to get him to show a few of his tricks for me to see, but he always found a pretext to put me off—whether it was that he hid it and would not show, or that he feared to give his bottom away, that I cannot tell.

This man followed me in the work on two occasions, and truly could not be called of much use; his greatest defect was that he carried no political cell in him at all. Reckoning by the years, were he still alive, he too would be above seventy.

Zhang Fengyi: when he came out of prison at Hanoi, he could already speak fluent French; and with the grounding of a graduate of the seventh class of the Military Academy besides, had he but had another year or half a year of higher training, and then had the good fortune to be posted to the military-administration or military-command systems, then even without battle merit, might he not long since have risen to full general? But such a chance he never met. I heard he was sent to be chief of the track-guard detachment on the Beiping-to-Shijiazhuang stretch of the Ping-Han Railway; and when Shijiazhuang fell, he was recalled to Beiping, and after that nothing at all is known. Were he in Taiwan, he would surely come to see me; and now, no news these many years, he must, I suppose, be trapped on the mainland and never come out. Yet—can he have lived to this day?

Why do I always think the worst? Because, as a rule, anyone who has been a Kuomintang special agent will, on the mainland, sooner or later one day be found out by the Communists; and once fallen into the bandits' hands, all is over—alive or dead, it comes to the same thing.

Chen Bangguo: earlier I had it wrong, and so wrote Chen Bangguo as Zheng Bangguo. In the Complete Works of Mr. Dai Yunong put out by the Intelligence Bureau it was Chen Bangguo from the first, and I had taken this for a slip of the brush and deliberately changed it to Zheng Bangguo. Later, thanks to a letter of instruction from Mr. Tang Junqi of Kaohsiung, I corrected it.

At the victory in the War of Resistance, after Bangguo was released from Hanoi, it seems I met him once more in Shanghai; but searching my memory closely, I truly cannot recall it clearly. I have now received two letters from Mr. Tang of Kaohsiung, of the twenty-third and the twenty-ninth of the fourth month, telling me: "Chen Bangguo was a native of Hefei in Anhui; he and I, after the victory, were colleagues for a full three years in the inspectors' office of the Police Affairs Department of the Two Railways in Shanghai. Chen was reluctant to speak much of his years of imprisonment at Hanoi." And further: "Mr. Chen Bangguo, in the thirty-fifth year, brought his wife and a little daughter to Shanghai, and lived in the Railway Bureau dormitory in Senxun Lane on Qiujiang Road in Zhabei. At the withdrawal in the thirty-eighth year, being burdened with family and without means of transport, he could not get out, and I hear he soon returned to Hefei—I fear he has perished." Mr. Tang's thinking is the same as mine: for one in intelligence work to stay on the mainland is always more ill-boding than good.

Chen Buyun: Bangguo and Buyun had been colleagues to begin with, both serving before the War of Resistance in the reconnaissance detachment of the Songhu Garrison Command. Transferred to Hanoi after the war began, they came together too, and went together to the sanction of the traitor Wang at Gao Lang Street; and afterward, when Bangguo had the ill luck to be taken, Buyun came safely back. As Mr. Tang Junqi mentions in a letter: "Mr. Chen Buyun, in the winter of the thirty-seventh year, came from Nanjing to Shanghai, wishing to transfer into the Police Affairs Department of the Two Railways (Nanjing-Shanghai and Shanghai-Hangzhou-Ningbo) for service, but the transfer did not come off in the end. At that time tickets on the Nanjing-Shanghai line were hard to buy, and I bought a ticket for him in his stead and saw him onto the train; and after that there was no more news. I recall that at the time he seemed to be serving in a river-defense inspection unit."

The word above of the two comrades, Bangguo and Buyun, though it be but three words and two phrases, was yet like "footfalls in an empty valley"; and for it I have chiefly to thank Mr. Tang's telling—else who would have known, and where should one have gone to ask?

What stirs a certain sigh is this: Bangguo, burdened with family, could not get away; Buyun, wishing to change to a very ordinary post, could not get it. Their fated road was full of stumbles, and what they sought went ever awry. This is the grief that little people find so hard to utter—and one may say, too, a thing common enough in an age of war and turmoil.

Mr. Zeng: Mr. Zeng's surname was truly Zeng, but his given name I really cannot recall. He was a Fujian man, tall of stature and broad of stride; and though it be tens of years past, even now, if I close my eyes, I can still sketch out his figure. Strictly speaking, Mr. Zeng did not take part in our work; yet our work had the benefit of Mr. Zeng's help—and so, whatever Mr. Zeng's own thoughts may have been, in the reckoning of what we gained we must always set down this account to his credit.

Mr. Zeng, at Mr. Xu's entrusting, helped in our activity at Hanoi; he gave of his effort, but never took any recompense whatever. The Wei Chunfeng mentioned above was the very man Mr. Zeng introduced to us.

After we left Hanoi, I heard that Mr. Zeng too had been drawn into work at the local Consulate-General, and rose in time to be a director of affairs, and thereafter came to an unknown end. Which is to say, more properly, that I do not know what became of Mr. Zeng afterward. Judging by Wei Chunfeng's rising to counselor, Mr. Zeng, had he not left the diplomatic world, must surely have risen a good deal higher than director of affairs. Indeed, one has but to ask someone to make inquiry at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and his whereabouts might well be found out.

Miss Nguyen: Miss Nguyen is Wei Chunfeng's wife. She was a Vietnamese; having married a Chinese, she must by now have taken Republic of China nationality. In those years, when we first knew Wei and Nguyen at Hanoi, the one was a golden lad of just past twenty, the other a jade maiden of eighteen or nineteen—a very well-matched pair, a match made in heaven; and sure enough they afterward became man and wife.

About the fifty-first or fifty-second year, when I was still serving in the Intelligence Bureau and living in the fourth section of Xinyi Road, I had by that time already had the bad news of Wei Chunfeng's death. One day I suddenly received a limited-time special-delivery postcard, sent from Muzha. Looking closely, I found it was Madame Wei, asking me to meet her near National Chengchi University at Muzha. This was strange indeed—not seen for years, could she have learned to write Chinese? I went after work, taking a taxi; but hunt as I might for a good while, I could not find the place named in the letter, and had to come away with nothing done. Two days later another postcard came, written out plain and clear, and only then did I meet, after so long a parting, that once-familiar face.

More than twenty years had gone by, and Madame Wei, now a widow, had lost the bloom of her earlier years. Still she could not speak Mandarin, and the words on the letter were not in her own hand. But her children were mostly grown, and several of them were studying at Chengchi University; and the one who spoke and interpreted for us was the eldest Miss Wei. I had thought Madame Wei would settle in Taipei, but she had not lived there long before, unable to grow used to it, she moved back again to Saigon; and once gone, from then on all word of her was lost. Now that Vietnam has fallen under Communist rule, who can tell how it is with Madame Wei's safety? I can but pray Heaven keep her.

Comrade Zhang: First I must offer a word of apology. The "Comrade Zhang" set down here ought to stand for two men; of whom one I still dimly recall was surnamed Zhang, while of the other I cannot even remember the surname. The two of them were transferred in from the southwest; the time of their reaching Hanoi was likely already the first ten days of the third month, and because the place where I lodged could no longer hold them, they were quartered in another spot—and it was for this very reason that the chances of contact were fewer, and my impression of them the fainter. Where the two were afterward transferred, I have to this day heard nothing. Those listed above as of unknown whereabouts are, again, eight; and I believe that of them at least half will from this time forth never appear again.

Besides these, of those still living in the world, there remain but three:

Cao Shi'ang: the one who studied aviation in France, of whom the text above has already given a full account. One day in the course of the seventieth year, Brother Shi'ang telephoned me; he first gave a word of introduction of himself, and the moment I heard his voice it called up the memories I had buried away for forty years. We both longed to meet; he left his address and telephone number, which I jotted offhand on a small slip of paper and laid on the desk. But as luck would have it, the very day he telephoned my gout was flaring, my right foot aching and sore, and I could not walk well. By the time, two days later, I could go about, hunt as I would I could not find that little slip of paper. And so, with this stumbling delay, half a year slipped by; and when I asked old friends after this Mr. Cao, none of them knew him. By the spring of this year I wrote a letter to inquire of the former Director of the Intelligence Bureau, Mr. Zhang Bing, and was favored with a reply, which not only told me his address but showed me his recent state besides; and from the letter it was already plain that Brother Shi'ang's health was not good.

Climbing to the fourth floor is, for me, a hard business; but to call on an old friend one must always take the trouble. The one who answered the door was a woman of fifty-odd years with grizzled hair, whom I did not know; she seated me in the parlor and at the same time called out for Mr. Cao to come. Brother Shi'ang seemed to be in the lavatory; he called my name aloud, bade me wait a moment, and said he would be right out. I waited some seven or eight minutes, and still there was no stir. Dressing, I thought, need surely not take so long? A few minutes more, and there indeed came Brother Shi'ang out of the lavatory—but he did not come walking; he came crawling out on his hands and feet!

Parted forty-two years, I could never have thought to meet him again in so desolate a sight. He told me that last winter he had suffered a stroke; at first he could not stir at all, and now that he could crawl was already progress; and that in time, with rehabilitation, he would surely be able to stand and walk again.

We talked over many bygone things, but what he could not put out of his mind was a single medal he had never got into his hands.

I have been to see him three times, and by now he can already walk, swaying and tottering. I asked him what he would like to eat; he told me that when I came again, a piece of cake would be enough. His circumstances are not good; he spent great effort trying to get into a Veterans' Home for a long convalescence, and could not! What most troubles my heart is that he entrusted me with a thing to do, and I have not done it. For we are both men who have been through all the vicissitudes of the world; what could be given we have given, and what can be got is just this much and no more—beyond this, why ask for anything further? I would like to hear Brother Shi'ang's strong voice again—did you not say: "When I can get about freely, let us find some place to go and drink a couple of cups!" Good! I am looking forward to that very day.

Wang Zhongyue: had it not been for the letter that Mr. Wang Zhongyue sent from America, forwarded on by Biographical Literature, I might very well have written this old colleague of ours down among those of unknown whereabouts. You mention that the cover name you used at the time was "Wang Le"; and when my eye fell on those two words, "Wang Le," I truly did remember. We must have met, and shaken hands too—am I right or not? What I recall the more clearly is that the chances of contact between us were all taken up by Brother Fang Bingxi. And now Brother Bingxi meddles no more in the idle affairs of men, while ten thousand li of mountain passes hold us far apart.

Brother Wang Zhongyue was the main channel by which our "Hanoi work" reached through to Chongqing; and though his time was short, he held a most important place. If this be not plain enough, then let me say: he was the chief of our wireless station.

As I said in an earlier passage, not long after the several advance comrades who carried out our "Hanoi work" arrived, they set up the wireless station; and the one in charge of setting it up, and of keeping up the regular signaling, was this same Brother Wang Zhongyue. At the time, rack my brains as I might, I truly could not even call his name to mind, and so had no choice but to pass over it in a single stroke—which went against the very purpose of writing Nameless Heroes, and for which I ought to offer some apology to our Brother Zhongyue.

In his letter he mentions that Brother Zhongyue would at times help Brother Bingxi to "render the cables"—that is, the work of turning text into cipher, or cipher back into text. The reader may not wholly understand that in doing so he was already giving over and above his due; for his duty went no further than keeping the wireless clear and unclogged, and losing no time in sending and receiving—that counted as doing his part—and ordinarily he had no charge of translating the telegrams at all. Writing to this point, a sudden feeling comes over me: Brother Zhongyue, if one day the natural distance between us can be shortened, what say you we talk a while again?

The Author: the author is none other than I, and I am the one who has set down this book, The Whole Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi. In the "Hanoi work" of the twenty-eighth year, from Mr. Dai Yunong on down, I was the highest field commander on the spot; and now, among all the personnel who carried out the Wang case, those confirmed to be still alive in the world are but three, myself among them. This is Heaven leaving one side of the net open—as though it had kept me back on purpose to be the recorder and the witness of the Wang case; and to report, for the nameless heroes of Nameless Heroes, their deeds of loyalty and valor besides.

Of late, from what the readers, one and all, have expressed, they very much hope to learn a little sooner of the fortunes that befell me thereafter—above all of the various things that passed from the time of my arrest, and my not being put to death, in the tenth month of the thirtieth year of the Republic. Of the strange turns and changes in all this I shall of course write; not only am I long prepared in mind to do so, but I shall frankly lay bare my own failings, never hiding the facts, and heeding not at all what judgment others may pass on me. I reckon my life a half-baked thing, of no substantial contribution to society or to the nation; and so, while memory yet remains, to write a little of what truly happened and speak a few words of truth may perhaps furnish some material for the history of the War of Resistance. When The Whole Story of the Wang Case at Hanoi is finished, then in order of time it will be the turn of the Third Part of Nameless Heroes. The name first fixed for this Part was Renown Won in a Hundred Battles, though it may perhaps be changed; its content will be the several hundred action-cases that shook the great city of Shanghai in those years, which set an unprecedented record since ever there was action work at all—and, with the changing of the times, this record of deeds may as well be called one that will never be matched again. So long as I am not lazy, the story itself will carry a readableness of its own.

Only after that will it be the turn of the "Fourth Part," whose title is not yet fixed, which will tell the whole course of my arrest and my not being put to death. The time of its publication is set for the seventy-third year of the Republic. I have told my own fortune by the mystic art of guessing odd-or-even, and shall not die within these two or three years yet.
