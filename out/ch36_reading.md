## Chapter 4. Seizing the Initiative, Spread Too Thin

The “use of intelligence” is a professional body of knowledge. As to its efficacy: on the subjective side, one must have a clear understanding of how to dissolve what is unfavorable and clear it away, and of how to bring matters around to what is favorable; on the objective side, beyond the methods and techniques of putting intelligence to use, one must further weigh gain against loss, and so decide what to take up and what to set aside.

The writer, not shrinking from his own ignorance, will venture one blunt assertion: even now, in the nineteen-eighties, the administrative chiefs of the several departments who set store by “putting intelligence to use,” let alone who understand “putting intelligence to use,” are still few; for in their basic conception they do not reckon this too among the affairs of “government.” Another reason is that between “the gathering of intelligence” and “the use of intelligence” there is no track along which the thing may run.

As a rule the “use of intelligence” leans heavily toward the military and toward diplomacy; yet down to the present day any government department, or indeed any private body, has both the need and the necessity of “putting intelligence to use.” And whoever is charged with the business, whatever his original post may be, none is fitter for it than the unit’s own “spokesman.” The reason is plain in itself, and needs no laboring.

What the writer wishes to convey lies not in fault-finding; the point on which his eye is fixed is this: “the gathering of intelligence” is no easy matter—at times it exhausts the mind and the wits, at times it braves danger and hardship—and if no effective use be made of it, then not only is a heaven-sent thing wantonly squandered, but the people’s labor and the state’s wealth are spent in vain: is not the whole original purpose thereby lost!

The distinctive quality of “intelligence” lies chiefly in its “time-boundedness.” Intelligence that has passed its hour, whether it has been verified or not, and however high its worth, has already become mere archival material, and possesses the value of intelligence no longer.

In the spring of 1948 we came by several pieces of important intelligence, of considerable strategic value, and for them we received a commendation from the Ministry of National Defense. But should one now turn those same reports up and look them over, they have long since become the stale affairs of bygone years; beyond the sighs they may stir, they will never again carry that savor of a treasure newly won.

A directly subordinate section of this brigade, stationed at Anci, took part at the close of 1947 in a “heart-extraction tactic.” The operation was directed by the “Provisional Third Army,” which sent a cavalry battalion ahead as its vanguard, with a great body of men and horses following after, in a forced march straight for the Temple of the Medicine King at Anguo County. Whether word had leaked out is not known; the upshot was that we came a step too late, and the very man who was our object slipped away. And who was that man? Mao Zedong!

Anguo County had already fallen into bandit hands for a good eight or nine years. Lying off in the western end of the “Beiping-Tianjin-Baoding triangle,” hard by Shanxi, it was extremely backward in its communications.

Setting out from the Anci county seat, with “Tiaohetou township” as the way-station, and so on to the Anguo county seat, meant pushing 200 li deep into bandit territory.

This was of course a great matter. In the whole operation there was much that deserved review; besides the imperfections of the planning, we suspected throughout that some traitor within had passed the word along.

I have received from my fellow-student Xiao Runyu, who took part in this operation at the time, an essay, a “Record of the Work,” which, besides setting down the course of the action, gives a searching account of the state of mind of the people under Chinese Communist rule once they saw the light of day again. I here reproduce his text word for word, with my thanks.

Before the Shimen work-group set out, the district was already under Communist siege. By that time the two railways, the “Ping-Han” and the “Zheng-Tai,” had both been cut, and only by negotiating an airlift could one get there at all.

After the national army, in March 1947, drove straight to the dragon’s lair and at a single stroke recovered Yan’an, the old nest of the Chinese Communists, and destroyed the Communist center of command, the great pack of bandit chieftains headed by Mao Zedong fled in a body to the “Wutai Mountains district.” From April that same year they began their fierce assault on Shimen, precisely in order to build a new nest that should take Yan’an’s place.

The main strength of the Shimen garrison was the “32nd Division of the Third Army”; after seven months of stubborn holding, and seven days and nights of bloody fighting, Shimen fell at last. This brigade’s Shimen work-group, in the thick of an all-out “traitor-rooting” action, likewise suffered grievous losses.

I have received from my fellow-student Niu Guangjin—who came back bearing a gunshot wound, having passed through every peril—an essay, “A Brief Record of the Shijiazhuang Work,” setting down the true state of things before and after the fall. For his frankness and sincerity I feel the prouder.

### 1. From the Value of Intelligence to Its Use

The very word “intelligence” already carries within it the sense of “time.” Set down a piece of intelligence, once verified, that belongs to the past: whatever worth it may have held at the time, its time-boundedness is gone, and so it becomes archival material and can no longer be reckoned “intelligence.” Turn the file up and read it through again, and it will have wholly lost that flavor of the unresolved, the still-in-doubt, that it had before it was confirmed.

In the old days, when the Emperor looked at intelligence, he would set down in the vermilion brush the rescript “Noted”; later, when the Republican era came in, the great mandarins, on reading intelligence, needed only one sweep of the great brush to write the character “Seen,” and that counted as having dealt with it. In truth he had not necessarily so much as read it. The reason the man who “gathers intelligence” was ever no more than a pawn running before the horse, and won no regard, is just this: it shows that though men knew the thing called “intelligence” to be useful, they did not know how it was to be used.

The last two sections of the foregoing Chapter Three told how, on the strength of friendship, we prevailed upon Li Mingqiu to make the journey to the Northeast and seek out the two men Lin Biao and Tao Zhu, the aim being first to gather intelligence and then, as the situation might develop, to plan some further advance. I had supposed the whole of that account already set down and done, but on later reflection there were still things left out, and I therefore add a little here by way of supplement.

First, of Lin Biao. In August 1945, upon the victory in the War of Resistance, Lin Biao led more than a hundred thousand Chinese Communist troops, mustered together from Jiangsu, Shandong, Hebei, Shanxi, and Shaanxi, and, with the Russian army covering his passage, infiltrated the Northeast along the Bei-Ning railway. At once he seized the arms of the Japanese forces and took the puppet troops into his ranks, forming the “Northeast Democratic Allied Army,” in which Lin Biao made himself “Commander and Commissar both”; everywhere he wrecked the communications and obstructed the national army’s takeover. In 1946 the “Northeast Democratic Allied Army” threw off its mask and renamed itself the Chinese Communist “Northeast People’s Liberation Army,” with Lin Biao still “Commander and Commissar both,” and also “Secretary of the Northeast Bureau of the Chinese Communist (Party) Central” and president of the “Chinese Communist Northeast Military and Political University.” By this time he was already high in place and heavy in power, to all appearance the king of the Northeast.

In the spring of 1947 Lin Biao’s forces four times fell upon Changchun and Jilin, and were beaten back each time. In June he threw in the whole of his main strength against Siping, but in three weeks could not take it, losing above thirty thousand officers and men. The next year, through the defection of Zeng Zesheng and his troops, he stormed and took Changchun.

In October 1948 the Chinese Communist “Northeast People’s Liberation Army” was reorganized once more into the “Northeast Field Army,” Lin remaining commander, while the post of commissar passed now to Luo Ronghuan. At that time his forces went on to take Shenyang, and the whole of the Northeast fell into Communist hands.

In the winter of 1948 Lin led his forces in several columns in through the passes of the Great Wall—Shanhaiguan, Lengkou, Xifengkou—and was for the time being made the Chinese Communist “Commander of the Beiping-Tianjin Front,” with troops that were given out as a host of a million.

At the beginning of 1949 Tianjin and Beiping fell one after the other, and Lin Biao’s forces, renamed for the third time as the “Fourth Field Army,” had him still for commander.

In April, without waiting to refit and make good his losses, Lin Biao led his forces south down the Ping-Han railway, serving as the Chinese Communist “Commander of the Central China Army” and “Secretary of the Central China Bureau of the Chinese Communist (Party).”

In May that year he took Wuhan and Yueyang, and, joining with Liu Bocheng (“Commander of the Second Field Army”), who was pushing west along the Zhe-Gan railway, spied out Changsha. In October that same year the sham Chinese Communist régime was set up at Beiping, and Lin Biao was made a member of its sham “Central People’s Government Council” and its sham “Central People’s Revolutionary Military Commission,” and was chosen a member of the sham “First National Committee of the Political Consultative Conference.” At that moment Lin Biao was throwing his whole force into the storming of Hengyang and Guangzhou, then turning to fall upon Guilin and Nanning, and to harry the Leizhou Peninsula.

In June 1950, at the Chinese Communists’ “Third Plenary Session” of the Seventh Central Committee, he was raised to be a member of the “Political Bureau of the Chinese Communist (Party) Central.”

From this point Lin Biao climbed ceaselessly higher, climbing at last to a place below Mao Zedong alone and above the swarm of ten million lackeys, and became Mao Zedong’s “successor.”

Next, of Tao Zhu. He followed Lin Biao to the Northeast, and from the “Northeast People’s Liberation Army” through the “Northeast Field Army” to the “Fourth Field Army” was throughout the “Director of the Political Department.” When he reached Guangzhou, he vaulted at a bound into the sham post of “Governor of Guangdong.” Thereafter he passed further from the “locality” toward the “Center,” worming his way in to become a member of the “Political Bureau of the Chinese Communist (Party) Central,” and taking the office of “Director of Propaganda.”

But fair days do not last: Lin Biao died a violent, unnatural death, and Tao Zhu perished under “reform through labor”; neither of the two came to a good end.

But let that pass; the thing to stress is the importance of these two men, Lin and Tao. Since we had once established contact, then even if the go-between Li Mingqiu inclined toward the Communist Party, or was indeed a true Communist, he could still be turned to use as occasion served—one might even call it a “contest of wits.”

The abler party could gain the upper hand.

On this journey Li Mingqiu brought back, besides a “verbal message,” only what he had seen and heard along the road, and a summary of his talks with the two men Lin and Tao. For Li Mingqiu, having gone was itself the discharge of his errand; for us, out of the whole of what he told we sifted many pieces of important intelligence, of which the following three can still be recalled today. First: of the Chinese Communist “North China People’s Liberation Army” under Nie Rongzhen, a part of the eight columns—a force of some seventy thousand men—was, in order to support the offensive of Lin Biao’s “Northeast People’s Liberation Army” in the Northeast, to keep up an unceasing harassment of the Ping-Bao line (the stretch of the Ping-Han railway from Beiping to Baoding), and so pin down our army and keep it from marching out beyond the passes to the relief.

Second: the “guiding points” of the Chinese Communist “Northeast People’s Liberation Army” for its operations at that time were, broadly, to complete the blockade of the sea approaches with all speed.

Two, to sever every line of transport and supply within the region.

Three, to isolate the three strong-points of Changchun, Shenyang, and Jinzhou.

Four, once the national army’s fighting strength had been worn down, to pick out the weakest and take it at a single blow.

Five, to break up the unity within our ranks.

Six, to strike at the will to fight and sap our morale.

Third: the setting-up of a joint operational command organ for the “Northeast People’s Liberation Army” and the “North China People’s Liberation Army” was even then being planned by Luo Ronghuan (commissar of the “Northeast People’s Liberation Army”).

Besides the several items set out above there were many other scraps of material. Today these look plain and unremarkable—one might even have guessed them—but at the time, in April and May 1948, they were well worth the seeing.

This line of approach was well worth keeping up and working further, but the one connection who could serve as go-between, Li Mingqiu, unhappily fell ill and died; and to find a second such man—how could that be easy? We did make the attempt, but without result.

After careful thought, the only man who could take Li Mingqiu’s place was Jiang Tian; but that would not do. The reasons I have already given above: chiefly, that Jiang Tian was a commanding officer in our unit, with duties of his own, so that for him to go and “deal” with the other side would be unseemly; and further, that once his identity was discovered by the enemy, it would surely produce the opposite of the effect intended. In such matters one may harbor no least thought of taking a chance; the more so with a thing of this kind, which asks for the greatest care.

This project we were unwilling to give up; but as no fit person could be found to go, it came to a halt for sheer want of any way forward. In the meantime the thought did occur: might we not, in the guise of a “double game,” let Jiang Tian play the part after all, and hazard the attempt? Jiang Tian’s own view was that go he could; but by his own reckoning, once gone he would never come back. And in the confusion of war, with liaison a thing of the utmost difficulty, it could serve no purpose whatsoever. At the same time, one would have had first to obtain, hard and fast, a guarantee from above—a point on which there could be no carelessness at all. I could not be sure of giving him a satisfactory answer, and I could not force him; so the matter had to be dropped.

As was mentioned earlier in Chapter Three, when Li Mingqiu came back from the Northeast he brought a word of Tao Zhu’s, the sense of which was that they would send someone to Beiping to “convey” their “views.” But right down to the close of 1948, when Beiping was already under siege, there was still no word. After that I let the matter rest.

Not until 1986, one day, when the old comrades of the First Brigade now in Taiwan were gathered at a dinner talking over old times, did Brother Wu—who to this day still holds public office—call up the past and say: “That winter (of the thirty-seventh year) the brigade commander” (meaning myself) “was talking in the office with a man who had come in. It seemed there was some dispute, but one could not make out what they were disputing; or else the brigade commander was asking something and the man would not answer. I” (Wu speaking of himself) “took a look from outside the door: the man had changed color and looked somehow not right, and lest he should turn rough and the brigade commander come off the worse then and there, I darted over and seized him round the waist—and, to my surprise, felt out a pistol on his body. It was, as I recall, a little Browning, loaded with cartridges; but for the moment there was no telling whether he carried it to guard himself or with some other design.” What was said that day went no further, for there were many people about, and it was not pursued.

Some days later, the writer, turning over this old story of Brother Wu’s, wondered how it was that he had not the least shadow of it in his own memory—and, above all, how the man had been dealt with after a pistol was found on him.

I then sent word through another to ask, and Brother Wu replied: “We never did get to the bottom of what it was all about. As near as I recall, we took the man into another room and questioned him; he hemmed and hawed and would give no straight answer, and in the end we could only keep the pistol and let him go.” At the time of the “siege” of Beiping all was calm on the surface, but the order of society was no longer what it had been; and one of the strange sights of it was that the left-leaning sorts and the Communist Party men were already stirring, restless and unabashed. So a thing like a man with a pistol tucked on him was all too common, and would leave no impression. Besides, from the moment one opened one’s eyes each day there was more business than could be got through; and unless a thing was of some peculiarly sharp and stirring kind, it was as good as all forgotten.

Thinking it carefully over now: was that pistol-carrying man of unclear intent perhaps one sent by Tao Zhu’s people to make contact? At the time the thing came so suddenly, and I myself was careless and inattentive; and it may be, too, that the man never clearly declared his errand, so that the chance was lost. Even now, thought over, it remains a riddle.

### 2. A Freak Mischance, Nearly Drawn into an Abuse of Power

There is a certain affair which to this day remains past explaining, and no one knew the inside of it better than our deputy brigade commander, Mr. Li Yulin. He has now been dead these several years, and even to seek a clear account of it there is no longer anyone to ask.

There was another man too, one of those concerned in the affair; but back in 1948 he was implicated in another case, sentenced to death, and executed. With his death, of course, a part of the inner story vanished as well.

And as for myself, there was much that stayed dark and unclear; but with affairs so many and time so wasted, I never set about clearing it up. Before writing this book I did ask my comrades of the unit what they knew, but none of them knew anything.

Now let me first give a simple account of the background of the work at that time. Among the many items of pacification work, “helping the people to build up armed self-defense” was one. But to build up an armed force one must have arms; and at that time, just after the eight years of the War of Resistance, what the common people urgently wanted was peace, and their will to take up arms and stand against the Communist Party was none too high—the less so as they had not the means to procure arms of their own. Even where, in a few districts, some great household wished to buy a few guns to guard itself, it was hard put to it to know how the buying was done, or where to find the way in. As for the government, its cares of governance were many, a hundred things lay in ruins awaiting revival, and it seemed to have no leisure as yet for this.

The deputy brigade commander had a friend, a Mr. Liu Yuzhu—the name sounds like a woman’s, but he was in fact a great strapping man. The two were fellow-sufferers from the same school of hardship: during the War of Resistance they had been in jail together in the “Paojuzi” at Beiping. What trade Mr. Liu Yuzhu had followed before I do not know; at the time, from the end of 1947 into the beginning of 1948, he was already a working member of some unit or other. Yet I could never make out whether he had long lain hidden in the fallen zone or had joined only lately. I had worked at Beiping for many years, and did not know the man, nor knew that there was any such worker at all.

Through the deputy brigade commander’s introduction we came to know each other, and this Mr. Liu declared to my face that he was very willing to help us do a little more in our work—by which he meant that he hoped we might all become friends and work together.

Afterward, on inquiry, I learned that the man was a deft manager of affairs, with a great gift for “operating”; and though he had once been a penniless wretch, he was now a person of some “consequence.”

I am not a man of close and watertight calculation, still less one who knows how to scheme and reckon against others; we had met, we had talked, and there it ended. It never so much as entered my head to make any design upon him—or, one might say, I had never looked to him for help of any kind.

One day the deputy brigade commander told me: “Liu Yuzhu has in hand a lot of guns, left behind by the Japanese soldiers when they surrendered, now stored at Tianjin; he is willing to make us a present of them, no strings attached—all the unit need do is send an official letter to the Railway Bureau asking that two forty-ton freight wagons be allotted, to carry them from Tianjin back to Beiping, and they are ours.” For someone to make us a present of arms was, for me, no first occasion (see Chapter Five of the third part of my humble work), and so I agreed to it without a second thought. Moreover I took it for an ordinary matter, a thing to be expected as of course; little did I know that there were many questions of procedure bound up in it.

And if to a simple matter one then adds certain complicating factors, it becomes a grave case of “breaking the law.”

So the brigade headquarters sent off an official letter to the Railway Bureau. Just who was sent to see to the business I can no longer recall. In sum, the process was simple enough, and the guns were shipped back without the least trouble.

What left the deeper impression: the guns numbered more than two thousand, with a small quantity of cartridges besides; the greater part were “Type 38” rifles, a smaller part “Type 30” rifles, and they were indeed every one of Japanese make.

In truth we ourselves had no need of such weapons, which counted already as out of date; for all our own equipment was, for that time, of the newest light kind—even the national army’s combat units could not match ours for being modern, fine, and powerful. What I mean is that our taking these arms was wholly for the sake of strengthening, in the carrying-out of pacification work, the self-defense of the people in the townships and villages. This had been planned beforehand.

As to where the guns went—that is, how they were distributed and used—I recall that the greater part were made over to the “Fourth Command Room,” which used them to arm the self-defense corps of Shunyi County (very near Beiping, on the Ping-Gu line). Where the smaller part went I cannot recall; most likely it was still “piled up” at the unit headquarters.

This was but one small matter, and I gave it no place in my thoughts at all.

In 1948, Liu Yuzhu’s superior in charge—that is, his highest superior of all—came from Nanjing to Beiping to inspect the work; we met him at the airfield with due form, feasted him in welcome, and the air of harmony was kept up as usual. Then, roused from sleep by a ringing bell, I had word over the telephone that Liu Yuzhu and his leaders and the rest had suddenly been “shut up.” “Shut up” is the spoken phrase; written out, it is “taken into custody.” What for? No one dared ask much—it was a thing to keep clear of.

When I first heard the news, since I had met Liu Yuzhu only two or three times and had, one may say, no close acquaintance with him, I was indeed quite unmoved. And of affairs of this sort I had truly heard all too many.

The next day, having come to the unit headquarters as always, deputy brigade commander Li Yulin told me of this matter with a most composed air. At first he was waiting for my reaction; but as I had never liked to talk of such things, I did not take it up. After a little while the deputy brigade commander pressed me once more, saying: “Look now—Liu’s affair could hardly have anything to do with that lot of guns, could it?” It was these words of his that woke me up: all at once I saw the peril in it, and felt the gravity of the case. To have “no concern in a matter” and to have “the wildfire scorch one’s own body” are, after all, two different states of things.

The deputy brigade commander and I exchanged views on the matter, deeply and at length; taken together, they came to something like this. When the Japanese army surrendered, its weapons ought to have been handed over to our government; the taking-over of the weapons ought to have been the business of a government organ. How, then, had they come to fall into private hands?

Two or three of them one might well call an oversight; but two thousand of them, enough to fill two railway cars, was a large number.

Though Liu Yuzhu had a working connection with the Bureau, his ostensible trade was still commerce; why should he have had the “power of disposal” over this not-inconsiderable lot of arms, and be able to give them to whomever he pleased? We knew that after the victory in the War of Resistance there had been a great uproar of “taking over,” with every sort of freak and marvel and trick under the sun—a wild gale of it—so that men mocked it as “looting over.” Yet the objects of the “looting over” were mostly gold and silver and treasure; one seldom heard of guns and shells and cartridges, unless it were the “treasured samurai sword” that a high Japanese general wore at his side. On these two points we ought a thousand times, ten thousand times over, to have obtained a satisfactory answer before accepting his “gift”—that would have been the right way of it; how could we have taken them in so muddle-headed a fashion? One may forgive oneself by pleading mere want of seasoning; but others will not necessarily grant so much!

There was yet another point: since it was all for the work, then why had it not been reported and placed on record? Once the trouble had arisen, to try then to patch it up—would that not look like a guilty conscience, and expose one’s own weakness into the bargain?

The upshot of our discussion was that only to watch quietly how things turned was the best course. The deputy brigade commander declared firmly: “Brigade commander, set your mind at rest; if they press the question from above, I will bear all the consequences myself.” So he said; but I, being the man in charge, how could I shift the responsibility onto another?

That officer who had come to inspect the work stayed several days at Beiping. One night, in the small hours—somewhere, I think, between two and three o’clock—he sent a man to ask me to come to his temporary lodging for a talk. That at dead of night he could actually summon a man to talk shows well enough how this person presumed upon his authority. Had it been the calling of an “emergency meeting,” that of course would have been another matter.

I was, in truth, a little flustered, yet had to keep my composure. By the look of things there was still some courtesy in it, unlike the “arrests” I had met with or seen before. Though this gave me a slight comfort, I was uneasy all the same. It was as well that I knew the “error” I had committed to be of the “benign” kind, giving rise to no “pang of conscience.”

The man drove me to No. 18 Nanchizi, a private residence; that the officer had taken the place for his lodging was a thing of friendship.

The master of the house, one Wu by surname, was a high-class rogue; everyone said he was a gambling-den card-sharp—but who could say for sure? The man’s acquaintance was very wide; he had struck up a friendship even with Liu Yuzhu’s superior, which shows how great his reach was. It is said that some years afterward he was killed by another idler-about-town, in the dark of night, in a blind alley; but that is a long story, and to tell much of it would stray too far from the point, so I had best break off here.

Getting down from the car and going in, I found two acquaintances—good friends, too—already seated there before me. We three looked at one another without a word, none of us able to guess what it could all be about.

By the time that long-drawn talk was over, day had already broken.

The first thing to be said is that what was discussed had nothing to do with Liu Yuzhu’s gift of guns, and why Liu Yuzhu had been taken into custody was not so much as touched on in the talk. What, then, was it all about? Broadly put, it was a far graver thing than the private giving and taking of guns!

This officer plainly declared that with the greater number of persons he could deal leniently, but that a few were caught up in a case of “corruption,” and these must be proceeded against without fail. And so the matter was decided.

In truth there was nothing in the whole of it that could not be told, and it would even be well worth the setting-down; but it touched a good many people, and for their sake, living and dead alike, the secret ought to be kept. So, upon reflection, I can do no more than touch upon it and stop.

Let me put it thus: it was an affair of the abuse of power, near akin to a “strife of factions,” a “making a pretext to work one’s will,” a “striking at rivals,” a “taking revenge under cover of a grudge,” a “killing the chicken to warn the monkeys”! Seen at the time, it was startling; thought over afterward, it was fearful; and judged by the meanness of its methods, it was shameful.

The chief figure of the whole case was that Mr. Ma, mentioned in the first chapter of this book, who lived in Meizha Hutong. His charge was “corruption”; just what unlawful gains he had coveted no one knew. What no one could forget, though, was that in his capacity as Director of Civil Affairs he had presided over an election of the people’s representatives. Who could have known that to turn away entreaties and refuse solicitations would also give offense to the powerful?

The one tried in the same case was Liu Yuzhu. Why the two of them should have been tied together I truly cannot say; but that Liu Yuzhu became, in the end, one “buried alongside” the other—that is a fact.

After the case was closed, Mr. Liu Yuzhu’s “gift” of guns was never, from first to last, brought into it. The dread had been borne through; yet the way things tended took many a turn, for the wielder of power still nursed a lingering wrath, and had it not been for my old friend Brother Mao Wanli, who put in a good word on my behalf, it would very nearly have reached to me.

### 3. The ‘Heart-Extraction’ Tactic Mistimed; the Bandit Chief Escapes

As to the field activities of this brigade—that is, the real state of things in which its several working units, in the various counties, cities, townships, and villages, worked in concert with the local military and civil organs and matched wits with the Chinese Communists—I set down here, selectively, and after further ordering, what has already been gathered together, whether by word of mouth or in written material.

My first intent was to write it out systematically, unit by unit, following the order of this brigade’s establishment; but things fell out against my wish, for what with dispersals, deaths, and partings, that is now very hard to do. Still, I shall keep it as far as possible from disorder and confusion, always taking a single affair as my unit; as for the order of time, I can no longer see to setting them in their sequence.

The First Brigade’s “First Directly Subordinate Section,” when first formed, was at the outset stationed at Langfang, on the Bei-Ning railway line between Beiping and Tianjin. There was a railway station there, a large town belonging to Anci County. Afterward, at the invitation of Zhu Zhankui, magistrate of Anci County, it moved its station to the Anci county seat.

In its relation of attachment, the “First Directly Subordinate Section” worked in the main together with Anci County, and, as a matter of course, had also to keep in concert with the troops garrisoned there.

The magistracy of Anci County was held concurrently by Zhu Zhankui, commissioner of the “Third District Administrative Commissioner’s Office of Hebei Province.” Zhu Zhankui had originally been commander of the “Fifth Military Sub-district” under the Chinese Communist “Central Hebei Military District” (whose commander was Lü Zhengcao); after the victory in the War of Resistance, when he was harrying about the East Hebei region, our army dealt him a hard blow and took him prisoner, whereupon he came over and stayed on to work for the Ministry of National Defense. In August 1947 he came to the “Central Training Corps” and the “Lizhi Class” to set forth the course of his joining the bandit army and his coming over after capture. In September that year he returned to Hebei with the “First Brigade of the Pacification Corps” and took up his present post. By 1948, while Zhu Zhankui was “cooperating” with us in operations against the bandits, he actually turned traitor and went over to the Communists once more; and at the time, had our side not been so keenly on the alert, he would very nearly have fitted our “Second Command Room” into the “pocket-shaped gap” he had laid ready, and made it a thing caught in his bag. The particulars of that action will be set down in detail elsewhere, in the material furnished by my fellow-students Chang Shaozeng, Wang Hongzhu, Wu Chunxiang, and Chen Junxiang.

The combat force garrisoning Anci County was the “Provisional Third Army” under Fu Zuoyi’s command. (The writer’s note: On December 2, 1947, the “Zhangyuan Pacification Office” and the “Baoding Pacification Office” were abolished together. On December 3 the “North China Bandit-Suppression Headquarters” was established: Fu Zuoyi was deputy director of the “Beiping Field Headquarters” and concurrently commander-in-chief of the North China Bandit-Suppression forces, and the garrisons of Shanxi, Hebei, Chahar, and Suiyuan all came under the command of the “North China Bandit-Suppression HQ.” On December 11 the “North China Bandit-Suppression HQ” moved from Zhangjiakou to Fengtai, and on December 28 moved again to the western suburbs of Beiping. The time at which this brigade established its relation of attachment to the North China Bandit-Suppression HQ was probably in January 1948, and the “North China Bandit-Suppression HQ” issued to the “First Brigade of the Pacification Corps” its “code-designation” as the “0760” headquarters.) The commander of the “Provisional Third Army,” An Chunshan, was a doughty and battle-hardened fighter, a fierce general, and for that reason he enjoyed in full measure Fu Zuoyi’s support and trust. This brigade’s “First Directly Subordinate Section” had twelve members: the section chief Chen Xiutong, the deputy chief Zheng Jingting, and the members Xiao Runyu, Feng Zhijun, Jiang Bingchen, Bai Yongling, Zhao Wanli, Wang Dexin, Zhang Guowei, Ma Huizhang, Li ——, and Guo Qingyu. Of these, five are now in Taiwan; Guo Qingyu died of illness in Taiwan; and six are of unknown fate. I have received from my fellow-student Xiao Runyu (now serving in the South-Link Railway Engineering Office) a “Record of the Work,” and have also consulted my fellow-student Feng Zhijun (now retired as a major general, serving in the “Servicemen’s Friends’ Association” of Taipei County) for what he knew, by way of reference; and I here reproduce, with slight additions and emendations, the text of my fellow-student Xiao Runyu’s “Record of the First Directly Subordinate Section’s Battlefield Work.”

In it there is a story of real interest: had the intelligence been true, and had we caught our man in the act, then many of the “chronicles of great events” after 1948 would all have had to be rewritten.

What follows is the original text of the “Record of the Work” furnished by my fellow-student Xiao Runyu: “When the First Directly Subordinate Section was first formed, it was at the outset stationed at Langfang, a town of importance on the Bei-Ning line between Beiping and Tianjin; afterward, in order to work in concert with Zhu Zhankui, commander of the ‘Demobilization Army’ and concurrently magistrate of Anci County, it moved into the Anci county town. It is said that our Directly Subordinate Section was formed, from the first, at Zhu Zhankui’s own request.

“In the eleventh month of the thirty-sixth year (the exact day I no longer clearly recall), on instructions from above, our section was to send out a small ‘task-detachment’ to work together with the Anci county government and in concert with the Provisional Third Army, on the march with the troops. But as to what the task was, no further instruction was vouchsafed us from above.

“As for the Provisional Third Army’s combat mission and its objective, these too were not clearly declared. This was of course a military secret, into which it was naturally not for us to inquire. Happily there was a principle to go by: we had only to join with the county government and move as the command post moved, and all would be right.

“The First Directly Subordinate Section had twelve members, and was equipped with one wireless set in direct communication with the brigade headquarters at Beiping. For this task-detachment six comrades were named, led by the deputy section chief Zheng Jingting, with the members Jiang Bingchen (concurrently head of the Anci county town), Zhang Guowei (concurrently confidential secretary to magistrate Zhu Zhankui), Wang Dexin, Feng Zhijun, and Xiao Runyu. The arms allotted came to one Canadian-made pistol, one M1 automatic rifle, two MB submachine guns, two carbines, and a number of American-made grenades (the writer’s note: this light equipment counted, at that time in the thirty-sixth year, as of the very newest kind; the other units of the national army, seeing it, were one and all filled with envy).

“Since the order from above required us to set out at once, we could only work out the plan for carrying out our task as we went along. The tasks our little group set for itself, of its own accord, were, broadly: one, to clean out the bandit cadres and Communist spies lying hidden in the recovered areas; two, to gather intelligence; three, to work up defections to our side; four, to comfort and reassure the people and to build up local grass-roots organization.

“The strength the national army put into the field this time came to: one cavalry battalion as the fast-moving spearhead—a battalion in which each man had a horse and a light machine gun, and which, in past bandit-suppression actions, charging into the fray, had again and again won distinction by surprise attack. The main body was the Provisional Third Army, its commander An Chunshan taking personal command. An Chunshan was a favorite general of Fu Zuoyi’s, brave and skilled in battle, and was reckoned the nemesis of the Communist army.

“The code-name of this action was the ‘heart-extraction tactic,’ and the force pushed forward straight toward the ‘Beiping-Tianjin-Baoding triangle.’

“The so-called ‘Beiping-Tianjin-Baoding triangle’ means the dozen or more counties of central Hebei Province. Not long after the outbreak of the War of Resistance on the Double Seventh, it was occupied by the Chinese Communist forces of Lü Zhengcao. In the first month of the twenty-seventh year the Chinese Communists convened at Fuping (near Shanxi) a ‘Congress of Military, Political, and Civil Representatives of the Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region,’ and usurpingly set up a ‘Provisional Administrative Committee of the Border Region,’ with thirteen men—Song Shaowen, Hu Renkui, Nie Rongzhen, Peng Zhen, Lü Zhengcao, and the rest—as its members and Song Shaowen as chairman: this was the embryo of the bandit-puppet régime. Under the ‘Jin-Cha-Ji Border Region’ was set a ‘Central Hebei Administrative Office,’ with Luo Yuchuan as its director, having under it the Seventh through Eleventh Commissioner’s Offices. The counties and towns of Anci, Yongqing, Gu’an, Jinghai, and the like were governed by the sham ‘Seventh Commissioner’s Office’; Qingyuan (Baoding), Anguo, and the like by the sham ‘Ninth Commissioner’s Office.’ As for military affairs, there was set up besides a ‘Central Hebei Military District,’ with Lü Zhengcao as its commander, Meng Qingshan as deputy commander, and Cheng Zihua as commissar. Under the ‘Central Hebei Military District’ were five military sub-districts, which severally usurped the whole or a part of twenty-odd counties. After the victory in the War of Resistance, though the national army struck at it many times, it was never wholly recovered, and might well be reckoned a deep-rooted old bandit region.

“Our forces led off with the cavalry battalion’s powerful charge, sweeping all before it, the main body advancing in search behind. In the course of it, save for brushes with small bands of the bandits at the county and district level, we met no resistance of any size, and the advance may be called a smooth one.

“We set out toward dusk one day, and by dawn the next had reached ‘Tiaohetou township’ on the border of Anci County, a place already some seventy or eighty li from the Anci county seat. Our command post pitched here; and though there was still scattered gunfire, the bandit forces and bandit cadres had all fled far away.

“Our section held that, the fighting having paused for the time, whether we pushed on or not we ought to open our work then and there; so at once we split into two working teams and went into the streets of the township to observe how things stood. Lifting our eyes and looking about us, we found broken walls and ruined ramparts everywhere, houses wrecked and tiles shattered, a scene most desolate; and though our army had met no resistance in recovering the township, the place still wore the look of ashes left after the fire—one lifted one’s eyes on utter waste, nine houses in ten standing empty, not so much as the shadow of a man in the streets! All this, surely, was the fruit of the Communist Party’s settling-accounts struggles, its persecutions and killings, its pulling-down of houses and tearing-out of walls, its sweeping of people out of doors—every kind of cruel and evil deed. Why, then, did the common folk not set things to rights? Destitute and stripped bare, with what were they to set anything to rights, with what to mend?

“During the War of Resistance, the Japanese army in the fallen zone could hold only the points (county seats and great market towns) and the lines (the arteries of rail and road); at times it would send troops down into the villages to clear and sweep away the ‘guerrilla forces,’ which was called ‘village-clearing.’ To dodge the brunt of it, the Communist bandits carried out in every village a policy of ‘three connections’; and after the victory in the War of Resistance, when the Communist bandits broadened their rebellion and feared the national army’s advance to suppress them, they kept to the old way still. By the so-called ‘three connections’ was meant that in all the villages they had usurped they would dig through the walls and tunnels of the houses and courtyards and open them all into one another—the roofs of adjoining courtyards, for instance, were to be joined up, and the party-walls between neighbors bored through and opened. Within each household an underground passage was dug, connecting with the neighboring houses, running all the way out to an exit beyond the village. And village was joined to village by communication trenches (open passages).

“As we advanced with the troops we had already seen how, beside almost every high road, a communication trench ran parallel to the road. The trench was about two meters deep and about three meters wide, wide enough within for three men to march abreast, and from a little way off, at the level of the horizon, it was not easily made out. All this the Communist bandits had set up—wasting the people’s labor on purpose—to ease the flight of small detachments or of bandit cadres when they fled.

“The steps of the work our section had drawn up required that, before we could set to, we first find inhabitants, so that there might be someone to work upon; and so the first step was to go from house to house making visits. After we had visited a few houses, we came upon a thing we had never looked for: all we saw were the old, the weak, women and children, with scarcely any of the young and hale; and every one of them played deaf and dumb, answering beside the question, as though under some constraint. Was it that they dared not, or that they would not, come near us? For the moment there was no telling. Meeting with such a state of things, and the work being no longer able to go forward, we could only pause for the present, and set about at once a review and a discussion of what to do.

“We judged that the reason they dared not, or would not, come near us was most likely that in some corner we had not yet marked there still lurked Communist spies keeping secret watch; and that we must first rid them of their fear and of their anxiety for what might follow, or else they would never dare to have dealings with us, let alone, of course, to work together with us. So from the second day on, we were first to get the traitor-rooting work well done, and speak of the rest afterward.

“It cannot be denied that we—a band of fellow-students just come back from the training class and posted to the work—were full of courage, with a certain rashness in us, ready to act the moment we spoke of acting; but as for skill in the work and forethought, there was much in us that had yet to be tempered. What we leaned upon was our revolutionary faith and our anti-Communist conviction, both not only firm but unyielding. When we had made up our minds that the underground passages within the houses might be the hiding-places of bandit cadres and spies, we set about, without the least hesitation, to find the passages’ entrances and go down into them to search and seize—an act, with the enemy in the dark and ourselves in the light, that was truly a reckless venture, ill-considered. For the passage-mouths were all within the houses, one to be found in every two or three households; the opening was narrow, admitting but one man crawling in; those outside, looking down, saw only pitch darkness, while from within, looking out, all was perfectly clear. Had the enemy lain in wait there, he could have struck down each man as he came, without our having so much as a chance to strike back. Such a thing did indeed happen: one soldier of the force was, at an entrance, shot and grievously wounded by a bandit hidden within (they had also dug hollows in the passage walls, to hide in, to escape the search, and the better to make their attacks), and the bandit afterward slipped away and got clean off, without a trace.

“The passage ran down some ten-odd feet, where one could stand upright; a few paces on there was a widened space, about five or six feet square, with earth built up in the middle into a platform, high and low like a low stool, that might serve for rest, or for sitting cross-legged on the ground and using as a table, to write on and handle documents. In the passage there was a small water jar, holding clean water laid by for drinking, and a little dry food besides. Whenever the bandit cadres, meeting a clearing-out, could not flee, they hid themselves within, and might pass three or four days there without coming to harm.

“We went down into the passages several times, but afterward, taking that risk no more, we changed to an ‘attack by fire’: that is, we would light dry grass and fine kindling and stuff it in at the passage-mouth—which might, on the one hand, smoke out any bandits in hiding, and, on the other, let us find where the smoke rose and so discover another exit. Smoking them thus, we did indeed smoke out no small number of bandit cadres.

“From the mouths of the captured bandit cadres we learned that most of the grain they had wrung from the common people they had buried on the spot, or stored in cellars against removal; and when, together with men of the county government, we dug it up, we brought out several hundred piculs of it—by which one may see how little they cared whether the people lived or died. Once the bandit cadres and spies had been cleared away, the inhabitants at last put off their doubts and fears, and little by little more of them returned to their dwellings and showed themselves friendly toward our people, their one anxiety being the fear that the national army, having garrisoned the place but a short while, would go marching grandly off again. Our section went on visiting the inhabitants; as our contacts grew, we made ready to draw up a household register, to arrange for confessions and surrenders, and to loosen the old, ingrained notion of wrong requiting wrong—it being understood that grudges and debts should await a reasonable settlement by the government. Just as the pacification work was on the point of being brought onto its proper track, the vanguard cavalry force, without our looking for it, turned back from the Anguo county seat; and presently there came word from the Provisional Third Army’s command post that all the county-government and section personnel were to return at once to their garrison.

“We had just been saying to the local inhabitants, ‘Set your minds at rest; now that the national army has recovered this place, it will not abandon it again!’—and they, hearing it, were all overjoyed, and declared they would support the government with all their hearts, in the hope of living and working in peace from then on. Who could have known that this turn of things would so cruelly disappoint them, and lose us their trust? Though it was not a thing that one small group of ours could have set right, still we could not but feel some pang of conscience; and one could picture it—once that pack of Communist ruffians came back, a great many people were sure to be made to suffer for it.

“In our minds we all took this action of the national army’s to be for the sweeping-away of the bandit remnants and the recovery of the bandit region; little did we think that, first and last, in a mere four or five days, we should be returning to garrison—which not only disappointed the local people but filled us too with deep regret! Only after we had returned to garrison at Anci County did we learn the true purpose of this action of the national army’s, that it had had its own particular mission and objective. As we came to hear it: after our First War Zone forces under Hu Zongnan recovered Yan’an in Shaanxi at ten in the morning of the nineteenth of March in the thirty-sixth year, Mao Zedong and many bandit chieftains slipped through the net and fled, roving into the border region of Shanxi and Hebei, and lingering about the country from the Wutai Mountains of Shanxi to Fuping, Yangqu, and Anguo in Hebei. In the early part of the eleventh month of the thirty-sixth year, Fu Zuoyi’s forces received intelligence establishing that Mao Zedong and sundry bandit chieftains were then staying at the Temple of the Medicine King within the Anguo county seat, directing the fighting in the attack on Shimen (Shijiazhuang). To guard against a leak, Fu Zuoyi summoned An Chunshan, commander of the Provisional Third Army, in person, and instructed him face to face: he was to appoint one cavalry battalion as the forward search force, and it was laid upon commander An Chunshan to lead the main body in person behind it, the objective being the seat of Anguo County (long since usurped by the Chinese Communists), and the sole task being to take the bandit Mao Zedong alive. This, in the main, was how things stood before that army set out on its orders. (The writer’s note: On the twenty-ninth of June in the thirty-sixth year, the Procuratorate of the Supreme Court issued a directive—Ping No. 1906 of the twenty-fifth of June, thirty-sixth year—to the several High Courts throughout the country, charging that Mao Zedong had raised troops in rebellion and brought ruin on the state and calamity on the people, and duly ordering the subordinate courts to arrest and deal with him with all severity.) When the forward force reached the Temple of the Medicine King within the Anguo county seat, they found only that on a table in the rear hall the food was set out, the cups untouched and still warm—he must have fled headlong, with no time so much as to eat. In the upshot they struck at emptiness, and had to withdraw with nothing to show for it.

“Plainly the news had leaked out, and it may well be that someone went and passed the word along. At that time the three men closest to Fu at his headquarters—the deputy commander-in-chief Deng Baoshan, the deputy chief of staff —— Shuzai, and Fu’s eldest daughter Fu Dongju—were, if not Communists, then fellow-travelers of the Communist Party. Besides these there were, of course, others unknown to us, and any of them might have been an inside line for the bandit gang.

“It is rumored that the bandit chief Mao and the rest, on getting word, fled in panic to a small hill-village outside the Anguo county seat. After the Chinese Communists usurped the mainland, there was a commemorative building put up on the spot; but whether this be true there is now no way to look into.” The above is the account of a single tour of duty as set down by my fellow-student Xiao Runyu.

At the end of it my fellow-student Xiao, with weighty and heartfelt words, said: “We came away with one conception too: that the Chinese Communists’ tyranny, working its cruel will upon the people, can never win the people’s hearts in truth. Take only this ‘Tiaohetou township’ that we came to: it had been held down by the Communist bandits for close on ten years, governed and worked over, remade time and again; yet the national army, in a mere three to five days, could win back the people’s hearts, so that they broke through the bandit-puppet control and gave us their allegiance. This shows one thing besides: the nearer a man is to the Chinese Communists, the more clearly he understands them, and the firmer grows his will to oppose them.” There is a regret in this affair: the most grievous pity was that our “task-detachment” did not go along together with the forward force; and next, that afterward they would not disclose to us so much as the true facts—so what, then, was the part that we played?

As to whence Fu’s headquarters had come by the intelligence that disclosed Mao Zedong’s whereabouts—that is, what the “source of the intelligence” was—we never knew a thing from first to last.

The man Fu was one careful and cautious in his affairs, and given, moreover, to changing his mind often; he must certainly have weighed and studied this intelligence with care, for had he had the least doubt of it, he would never have sent his own trusted troops, made up his mind, and taken direct action.

Referring, besides, to other related material: at that time the bandit army was in the midst of a large-scale military action to besiege and storm Shijiazhuang (Shimen), and was seeking to seize Shijiazhuang for a “strategic strong-point”; that Mao Zedong and a body of chief men should be directing it from close by, at Anguo County, was of course altogether likely.

Seen thus, this piece of intelligence carried a high degree of truth.

To seize Mao Zedong was, of course—on the principle that to catch the bandits one catches their king—a far more important thing than to strengthen the forces and somewhat ease the strait of the besieged Shijiazhuang.

But to move the whole strength of an entire army, and then, on finding that the man Mao had already fled, to turn straight about and go—there was much in this that called for weighing. Can it be that, in issuing the order, they had thought only one move ahead and made no second or third provision? Had they not been hemmed in by the notion of “preserving their strength,” then for so great an object as this, even to see the whole army wiped out would still have been worth the price!

Suppose our “task-detachment” had set out along with them—then, I am persuaded, the way they went about it would surely have been different. At the very least they would have, in the shortest time and without dawdling, gone at it severally: seen how many sets of bowls and chopsticks were on the table.

Felt them with the hand to see whether they had gone cold, and might even have given them a taste. Were the dishes all vegetarian, or was there meat and fish among them?

Looked into how the fittings stood in the main hall, the rear hall, and the two side-chambers.

Was there anyone still in the temple? Whether monk or layman—had the fire in the kitchen-stove already gone out, or was there yet some warmth left in it?

Kept an eye out everywhere: was there not a single thing left behind—above all, any documents!

All these, more or less, would have helped toward a judgment, or even proved certain facts, in determining whether the man Mao or the high chief men had “stopped” here, or had “lodged” here; whether they had been gone a long while, or not so long, or scarcely any time at all—and then a decision should have been made at once.

Had it been judged that a body of bandit chieftains had indeed just left the place, then even if they had “gone far off,” the force should have divided in two and pursued upon their tracks, never willing to stop until it had got some result; and if, in the search, one met with resistance from the bandit army, one might even take a few prisoners and sound them out, and so, on the contrary, come by a guide—would that not have been better still? All this our own units would have done, and very likely with more care than the things here set out. As to whether the Provisional Third Army’s cavalry battalion did likewise, and how much of it they did—since they kept their mouths shut tight as a bottle, we truly do not know.

By any reckoning, cavalry move fast to begin with; and this cavalry battalion being both well equipped and strong in firepower, even to push ten or twenty li deeper, and to linger thirty to fifty minutes more, they could still have managed with ease. Even had they not caught Mao Zedong, and come back empty-handed, they would at least not have felt the journey made in vain!

### 4. Our Fighting and Sacrifice at the Battle of Shijiazhuang

From the middle of April 1947, the fighting on the outskirts of Shimen (Shijiazhuang) grew fiercer.

A few days later Zhengding, reckoned the screen that shielded Shimen, was given up, and Shimen fell into isolation. The national army began to fly in reinforcements, making ready to hold fast and, awaiting its chance, to break out and strike back.

Before this, the national army under Hu Zongnan, with a strength of about seven divisions, had on March 19—that is, about twenty days earlier—in the face of stubborn resistance, stormed and taken at a single blow Yan’an, the old nest of the Chinese Communists, and so carried out the settled strategic aim of “destroying the seat of the Chinese Communist leading center.” In this action, of 100,000 Communist troops 16,000 were killed and wounded and above 20,000 taken prisoner, the rest all routed and scattered. By another account, several thousand corpses were left on the field and above 10,000 taken prisoner; but say it how one will, besides the grievous casualties, that the “Chinese Communist Central” headed by Mao Zedong was broken up—this must be reckoned a fact.

At the moment of Yan’an’s fall, Mao Zedong himself first fled by motorcar, and then, when the car broke down, changed to horseback—and, past all expectation, this was the very thing that let him slip past his pursuers, so that he made his escape after all: a thing truly not to be borne thinking of.

Mao’s whereabouts became for a time a riddle; by common conjecture he had probably fled into the Taihang Mountains district of Shanxi. Whether at that moment there was any intelligence to guide the judgment, I do not know, and dare not speak at random.

At that time the Chinese Communist “leadership stratum,” Mao Zedong among them, was anxious to build up anew a “central strong-point” to take Yan’an’s place, and so fixed upon Shimen, than which none could be fitter.

Shimen (Shijiazhuang) lies at the meeting-point of the Ping-Han railway (Beiping to Hankou) and the Zheng-Tai railway (Zhengding to Taiyuan), on the border between Shanxi and Hebei, joined alike to the “Taihang Mountains district” and the “Beiping-Tianjin triangle” that the Chinese Communists had worked up, and to the “South Hebei region” that had long since fallen into Communist hands—one might say it had ground to advance from and to fall back on, and in its conditions suited most exactly their urgent need.

To turn now to our own side. After the national army had been penned in holding Shimen more than forty days, in June a trouble arose: unlooked-for, the population of the city of Shimen swelled sharply, from the original 190,000 inhabitants up to 300,000, so that the price of grain and rice soared and trade fell utterly slack. The chief cause was that people from the neighboring counties were all running to Shimen to flee the Red scourge. This, of course, told unfavorably on the fighting.

In the first ten days of June that year, Qu Linghan, head of the Social Affairs Department of the Hebei Provincial Government, Luo Wenhao, deputy head of the Peace-Preservation Department, and Li He, a member of the Hebei Provincial Provisional Council, flew to Shimen on behalf of Governor Sun Lianzhong and Speaker Liu Yaozhang to bring comfort to the troops and the people. Judging by this, our military and civil authorities still cherished the intent of holding Shimen fast and awaiting a chance to counterattack.

From the strategic point of view, was it better in the end to hold fast, or better to give up? The writer has not the insight to answer such a question; but from the standpoint of his own post (one tiny station) and its work, then of course not one inch of ground could be let go for nothing.

On November 12, 1947 the fighting at Shimen reached its height: in the forenoon a fierce “trench” battle opened within the city, and thereafter all telegraphic communication with the outside was cut. On November 15 the “Beiping Field Headquarters” at last officially announced: “The Shimen garrison, after seven days and nights of bloody fighting, at length, through the vast disparity of numbers, lost contact at six in the afternoon of the eleventh.” The phrase “vast disparity of numbers” above calls for explanation: the “many” meant the Chinese Communist army, the “few” the national army. This was local, not general; that is to say, the garrison of Shimen was few, while the Communist army attacking Shimen was many, and the difference between them very great.

Taken as a whole: at the end of December 1946 the total strengths of the two sides, enemy and ourselves, were roughly reckoned at 800,000 for the Communist army and 3,200,000 for the national army—just four to one—and in equipment too the national army was the better of the Communist army. After the space of a year, by the time Shimen fell, the total strengths were roughly reckoned at 1,150,000 for the Communist army, now increased, and 2,700,000 for the national army, now diminished: ours dwindling and the enemy’s growing, the situation had changed.

In strategy and tactics, the national army, to safeguard the lives and property of the mass of the people, had no choice but to disperse its garrisons through the combat zones and the recovered areas, and thereby lost a measure of the initiative; while the Communist army, aiming at just this weakness of a scattered and isolated national army, would mass what force it could bring together and fall upon it, and time and again, when the national army drew off troops to go to the relief, would seize the opening and drive in, forcing the national army into a state where head and tail could not look to each other. These few sentences are of course not enough to set forth the tangled circumstances of the time and the many inward factors; they give only the general shape of it. This unit had a working post stationed at Shimen, the very first sent out after we reached Beiping from Nanjing. There was also a unit working in concert with the Second Section of the “Beiping Field Headquarters.”

When the Shimen work-group set out from Beiping, Shimen was already under siege, its land communications severed, and so the whole group went by aircraft.

I have received from my fellow-student Niu Guangjin an essay, “A Brief Record of the Shijiazhuang Work,” which I here reproduce word for word, adding some notes of my own at the end.

The original text runs as follows: “After finishing our training in the Lizhi Class of the Central Training Corps, the whole company was assigned to Beiping, and were straightway posted to work in the Shijiazhuang region. On the ——th of October in the thirty-sixth year, we went by aircraft from Beiping, together with the section chief Zhang Tielin and all the comrades of the group, and reached Shijiazhuang.

“Off the plane, we lodged for the time near the West Garden; after a few days the whole group moved to the former seat of the ‘Third Army’ headquarters (the commander was Luo Lirong; his headquarters had already been shifted elsewhere). The place was at No. 19 South Main Street, a two-story building with a cellar, and in its fittings and surroundings it was, for us, tolerably suitable.

“By the time we set out for Shijiazhuang, the national army in that region was everywhere holding to its strong-points in the towns; the two railways, the Ping-Han and the Zheng-Tai, had long since been cut, and the rails and the communications installations were forever being wrecked by the Communist army—which we would repair by day, only for the Communist army to play the same old trick again by night, so that the defending troops were harassed past bearing and the situation was thoroughly unsettled. Though the great mass of the townspeople were turned toward us and away from the enemy, in their hearts they were in truth alarmed and uneasy the whole day long.

“This state of things bore none too favorably on the opening of our work. By the general signs of the enemy, the Communist army was massing to the west of Shijiazhuang, along the skirts of the hills, and in the neighboring townships was pressing the people into forced labor and driving its own militia to and fro in constant haulage—very much with the look of one about to lay siege to Shijiazhuang.

“By this time the situation at Shijiazhuang grew tighter by the day. Liu Ying, commander of the ‘32nd Division of the Third Army,’ called together the several intelligence and security units and formed a ‘Joint Traitor-Rooting Group,’ in whose actual work our section took part. In the early part of the eleventh month (of the thirty-sixth year) the Communist army began its assault on the Shijiazhuang airfield and on the outskirts of the city and other points; and whenever the fighting was at its fiercest, the spies lurking within the city, together with the local rogues and hooligans and the like, would seize the chance to stir by night—doing such things as signaling, wrecking the electric lines, setting fires, robbing, and so on. Though the ‘Joint Traitor-Rooting Group’ went out night after night and arrested many suspects, we could not slaughter the innocent wholesale; without interrogation we might not deal with them out of hand, and even those caught in the very act were all brought back and shut up first, to await the handling of the Judge Advocate’s office. But the situation grew ever tighter, and the makeshift traitor-rooting was already no longer equal to the need!

“After seven days and seven nights of bloody fighting in this wise, the Shijiazhuang airfield was the first to be stormed and taken by the bandit army; hard upon it the outer line of defense was breached in many places, and the city fell at last on the night of the eleventh of the eleventh month.

“When the situation grew critical, our section’s activity and response to it—the section chief, comrade Zhang Tielin, even in the utmost extremity still held his post in the office, directing the several working comrades as they went out severally, and keeping in touch with the headquarters of the 32nd Division, with the work-group of the Field Headquarters’ Second Section stationed at Shimen, and with the other units concerned. The member, comrade Zhang Jiansan, had proposed that it were better to shift our position, hide our identities, and so meet the emergency, since, could we but get through the one moment of confusion, we might yet lie low for a time and seek some fresh opening later. Section chief Zhang, though he allowed that he would consider it, found that time was already not on our side, and there was no getting to any single piece of preparation in time. What became of section chief Zhang Tielin thereafter is to this day unknown.

“The three fellow-students Niu Qingchuan, Li Mingguang, and Yang ——fang, in the midst of the confusion, were still at their traitor-rooting work at the several key points; after the fall, their whereabouts became unknown.

“The three fellow-students Zhang Jiansan, Zhang Tongfu, and Yang Zhiyi broke out and made their way back to Beiping one after another to rejoin the unit, and were posted to other work. Afterward they came with the unit to Taiwan: fellow-student Zhang Jiansan is now in business, fellow-student Zhang Tongfu still holds public office, and fellow-student Yang Zhiyi died of illness while teaching at Jianguo High School.

“Fellow-student Yang Qing, withdrawing with the unit from Beiping, left the unit of his own accord while passing through Tianjin, and from that time was seen no more.

“Fellow-student Zhu Zhizhang, in the thick of the melee within the city of Shijiazhuang, grappled with the Communist army at Great Bridge Street, beneath the pier of the Zheng-Tai railway’s great bridge (the great bridge passes through the city), and there, alas, died a hero’s death!

“On the night of the eleventh of the eleventh month the Communist army rained a fierce fire of shells upon the city and the command post and the like. I (so my fellow-student Niu Guangjin speaks of himself) was at the time, together with Section-head Zhou of the 32nd Division’s Political Department and several of his section men, in the locomotive works of the Zheng-Tai railway, awaiting a chance to break out; and in the course of it, my right leg being wounded by a shell-fragment (three of us were wounded at that same moment), it pained me greatly and bled without ceasing, so that I could no longer move. As there were no medical orderlies to give first aid, we could only bind one another up to staunch the blood, and bear the pain till daybreak. Just as I was making ready to crawl and grope out a way of escape, I fell in with the Communist army clearing the battlefield, and was straightway taken prisoner.

“The Communist army swiftly conveyed the great body of captured military and civil personnel to a concentration camp some thirty li to the west, and rather to the northwest, of Shijiazhuang—what the place was called I do not know. Under strict control, they set about at once upon us the ‘washing of the brain and the changing of the heart,’ compelling every man to write a ‘confession’ and an ‘autobiography,’ and holding ‘confession-struggle meetings’ and ‘grievance-airing assemblies.’ Under torments of every such kind, some wrote and wrote at great length, some talked wild nonsense; but there were also those who played deaf and dumb and said not a word, and others who, roused and impassioned, cursed roundly and railed—a thousand freaks and marvels; the dignity of man was no more.

“My own way was to write as little as could be, and to leave unsaid whatever might be left unsaid; and if speak I must, then I would only say a few words to get by for the moment and see. I had thought it over: fallen now into the enemy’s hands, I resigned myself to Heaven’s will and put life and death out of my reckoning. If my life were to end here, then so be it—to kill me or to hack me, let it all be as they would; but could I get a chance to flee the tiger’s mouth, then I should still have the means to fight it out with them once more!

“With great difficulty I bore through every kind of torment and ravage of spirit and body, and at last, in the course of one long removal, seized my chance and got clear of the danger. It was as well that the weather had grown gradually warmer, so that, enduring hunger and cold, I made my roundabout way back to the brigade headquarters at Beiping. This is already a thing of forty years ago; yet each time it comes to mind, there is a residue of bitterness in me still.” The section above is a passage of recollection written by my fellow-student Niu Guangjin, of this unit’s Shijiazhuang work-group.

The writer will offer, from what he himself saw, a brief explanation as follows. The designation of the garrison at Shijiazhuang was the “Third Army,” its commander Luo Lirong. The designation of the garrison at Anci County was the “Provisional Third,” that is, the “Provisional Third Army,” its commander An Chunshan; each had its own chain of command, and they were not one and the same force. Designations of this sort not only breed confusion easily but lay bare the want of unity in the command.

Shijiazhuang, for the Chinese mainland, was but a small town, its population no more than 190,000; being a communications hub, it was comparatively prosperous. That, in the strait of war, its population should leap suddenly to 300,000 was a bad sign: in the large, it bred in the economy the troubles of a supply thrown out of balance and a distribution grown unequal; in the small, the Chinese Communists had mixed into the crowds of refugees flowing into Shijiazhuang a good many “advance parties” and “wreckers,” which worked great harm to the military and civil order and to every side of society.

In the military matter, to speak a frank word, we never could make it out: that on the one side relief was awaited in all urgency, while on the other the troops were held back and did not stir.

Was this a trouble in the chain of command, one wonders, or was there some selfish design of preserving one’s strength and each lording it over his own patch?

Only think of it: at the very time the Communist army was pressing its fierce assault on the city with a heavy fire of shells, there appeared within the city a host of “Communist spies” and “local rogues and hooligans” making trouble everywhere—what a fearful state of things that was! To unsettle the peace of the place would of necessity shake the hearts of the people; and truly there was no choice but to subject them beforehand to a rigorous purge.

But let us not judge the great by the small; let me speak further of some of the actual experiences of this little unit of our own. This force of ours could not, on the “battlefield,” play any very great part—such is the fact, and there is no denying it. Had the “moment” of its commitment been a little earlier, things might perhaps have gone better. Yet our comrades were not only of the finest, but had in truth a fervor for the struggle, what is called drive. That the sacrifices they made in the fighting at Shijiazhuang (counting the killed and wounded, the missing, and the captured) should have come about in a case where they could not fully use their powers—this truly moves one to grief and regret!

There is another point, which can count only as an old matter and a new reflection: namely, that the leadership of the work laid down no working conditions beforehand, yet at the last moment put forward its demands upon the work. To give an instance: when the members set out, they were all in military uniform; and yet they were instructed to “go into hiding at once should it prove necessary.” I ask you—they had not so much as a single suit of plain clothes, nor the knack of the seventy-two transformations; how were they to go into hiding? Was this not to ask of men what men cannot do? In the old days the superior officers would forever use slogans like “overcoming hardship” and “revolution” for a spur to the spirit—think of it, how unreasonable that was! I, and thousands upon thousands of my comrades-in-arms, came through just so. And therefore, for my own part, I hold this to have been a clumsy manner of leadership.

There is more: the chance-taking cast of mind that looked to the front but not to the rear, that could win but could not afford to lose, may be called a clumsy way of doing things—one that held only to the stiff and straight, that made no turn, that knew nothing of the roundabout. When the trainees were under instruction, there was simply no course on how to bear oneself after being “taken prisoner.” In truth, since such a thing might well happen, there ought to have been some preparation for it; one could not just shun the subject and leave it unspoken, or, when the time came, leave every man to do as he pleased!

Then, again, there is the question of the recovered areas being given up a second time—another matter overlooked beforehand. Since in the pacification work we had said this and that to the people of the recovered areas, and had, on the government’s behalf, offered them certain guarantees, and yet before long would pat our backsides and “withdraw”—this disappointed the common people, and left them feeling deceived. Just such a case as that of the Provisional Third Army at Anci County, working with our work-group at “Tiaohetou township,” is the very best example of it; in the end one could only comfort oneself, poorly enough, with the plea of “having no choice.”

All this is matter of technique and method, and in the field of “political warfare,” among the questions that will still be with us in days to come, there is real need of deeper study—which is to say that a day will surely come, hereafter, when it will be of use.
