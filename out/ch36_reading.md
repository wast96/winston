## The Chrysanthemum Banner

That day—

That is to say, it was the morning of the twenty-sixth of the ninth month of this year (the second of Keiō); as Toshizō was walking the corridor of the Kashō-chō barracks, he passed the staff officer Itō Kashitarō.

"Ah, there."

Itō was uncommonly affable, more so than usual.

It was some days now since his return from Nagoya.

"Quite—"

{j} Itō looked up at the sky beyond the eaves,

"cleared up, hasn't it."

{j} he said.

"Just so."

Toshizō's face was sour. That it was this Itō who had sent Shichiri Kennosuke and his assassins against him at the Nijō riverbed — the proof of it was in hand already.

But Toshizō had kept it secret from all but Kondō.

He feared the unsettling of the corps.

"Master Hōgyoku,"

{j} Itō called him by his poet's name. He had heard it from someone, no doubt.

"a fine season for verse. And how does it go with you, of late?"

"Ah — nothing but poor verses."

"With me it is the tanka; last night, seated before a single lamp-flame, my feelings gathered on me, and one poem came. Will you hear it?"

"Please."

Itō Kashitarō leaned on the railing and turned half his face to the garden. He was somewhat travel-browned, but his features were as finely handsome as ever.

{p} Breaking my body,

{p} spending all my heart — this world

{p} gone tangled as black hair, what am I to do with it?

"How do you find it?"

"I see."

Toshizō did not change his expression. Itō's loyalist anguish — how to gather up a world gone tangled as black hair — was plain enough; but within that "what am I to do with it," among the schemes, would surely be the killing of Toshizō himself. It was not a poem he could take much joy in.

"By now, Hijikata-san, Takao" — a place famed for its autumn leaves — "and the Arashiyama gorge will be turning color, don't you think?"

"No doubt they will."

"What do you say to it, some time — slipping free of corps duties for a poem-journey outside the city? I'll come along myself."

"That would be pleasant."

"Master Kondō, too, ought to take his ease now and then. When shall we make it?"

"Well, that would be well enough, but—"

That would be well enough. Takao would be well, the Arashiyama gorge would be well; but go there, and one might find some outrageous ambush lying in wait, and it would turn out no leaf-viewing party at all.

"I'll think it over."

As he made to go,

"Ah — that reminds me, Hijikata-san."

{j} Itō called after his retreating back, as though he had just remembered.

"Are you free this evening?"

"A poem-journey?"

"No — nothing so elegant as that. There is a matter I should like, most earnestly, to consult you upon."

(Here it comes.)

{j} Toshizō thought.

"Consult me on what?"

"That I'll say at the time. I am going now to put it to Master Kondō as well. As for the place — better, if it can be managed, not the pleasure quarter."

"The Kōshō-ji lower mansion, shall we say?"

It was Kondō's resting-place. He had redeemed the Ōsaka Shinmachi courtesan Miyuki-dayū from her house and kept her there.

"Very good. As for the hour — what o'clock would suit?"

"Let me see."

Toshizō drew out his pocket watch. A French piece he had lately come by; on Toshizō's big palm its hands moved along, sure and proper.

"Five o'clock would do."

He smiled a little. Not that Itō's proposal pleased him; it was looking at the watch that pleased him, no doubt.

Itō made a face of displeasure.

This too was less that Toshizō displeased him than that Itō, an extreme advocate of expelling the barbarian, thought the Western barbarians' watch a defilement even to look upon.

***

Toshizō went to Kondō's house one hour before the set time.

Kondō had come down from Nijō Castle and, without stopping at the barracks, gone straight home.

"Toshi — what's it about, do you think?"

"Breaking the league. He means to come out with it at last."

{j} and he sat down.

Kondō's mistress brought tea.

She was of a cast common enough in the Kamigata: fair-skinned, thin-browed, full in the lower cheek, with big front teeth. That, no doubt, was what an easterner like Kondō liked in her; but such women were not to Toshizō's taste.

(Edo women run darker, thick-necked, freckled maybe, but they've a crisper set to them.)

He thought, of a sudden, of that Oyuki.

"Do come in."

{j} She lowered her head slowly. In a clinging, cloying woman's voice — a voice of this sort, too, he could hardly bear.

The mistress withdrew.

"I can't believe it. Itō and I exchanged a promise as samurai. There'll be no breaking away."

"I don't know about that, but I'm the one who was nearly cut down."

"So I heard."

Kondō's face was clouded. That Itō should be tied to the affair of Shichiri Kennosuke — even now he could scarcely bring himself to believe it.

Presently the drum of the Hongan-ji was heard. Five o'clock.

At the entrance came the sound of voices.

"Here — a whole crowd of them."

{j} said Kondō, catching the feel of it.

"So it seems."

"Surely, Toshi, they don't mean to cut us down here and now."

"Are you a man to be cut down?"

"Ha, ha, ha — just so. Kondō and Hijikata are not the sort to let themselves be cut down for nothing."

—By your leave.

{j} and Itō Kashitarō slid open the fusuma.

The one who followed was Shinohara Tainoshin.

One might think that was all, but there came Itō's own younger brother Suzuki Mikisaburō, captain of the Ninth Unit, and the inspector Arai Tadao — a man who, sword in hand, was among the finest blades of the Shinsengumi.

After them the corporal Kanō Michinosuke, the inspector Mōnai Arinosuke (Kenmotsu), and the corporal Tomiyama Yahei.

"Is this all of you?"

{j} said Kondō; and as he did, one last, unlooked-for figure came in.

It was Tōdō Heisuke, captain of the Eighth Unit.

(Ah — this one too.)

The same shadow ran, at one instant, across the faces of both Kondō and Toshizō.

Tōdō was a fine fellow such as a painter might draw, and both Kondō and Toshizō had loved him like one of their own.

Indeed he was a comrade from the very forming of the league in Edo. Tōdō's style, true, was the Hokushin Ittō-ryū of the Chiba school, but he had come to play at Kondō's dōjō from early days.

At the very outset — that is, it was the dead Yamanami Keisuke and this Tōdō Heisuke who had gotten wind that the shogunate was recruiting rōnin, and urged Kondō and Toshizō to put themselves forward.

Come to think of it, both were of the same school, the Hokushin Ittō-ryū.

And Itō Kashitarō, too, for that matter.

(I see — so the bond of a shared school runs as deep as this.)

{j} thought Toshizō.

Tōdō Heisuke, of course, would have had his own thoughts, this long while. The core of the Shinsengumi — Kondō, Hijikata, Okita, Inoue Genzaburō, men of the same Tennen Rishin-ryū and the same home country — kept a common understanding among themselves, and toward the rest were, somewhere, outsiders. Could such men be called comrades?

(They make fools of us.)

To Heisuke, an Edo townsman with the legend about him that he was a stray by-blow of Lord Tōdō, that boorishness was more than he could stomach.

From early on he had grumbled of it to his senior in the school, Yamanami Keisuke. Yamanami had felt the same.

(In the end, we cannot share life and death with them.)

{j} so Yamanami, among others, had said. Yamanami had a strong loyalist feeling to begin with, and was in good part critical of the shogunate.

This was the temper of the Chiba school, and Tōdō Heisuke had it in him too. Strengthened the more by Yamanami's influence, it was Tōdō Heisuke, again, who in Edo had courted his schoolhouse senior Itō Kashitarō and brought him into the league.

Already at that time the secret compact of the present day existed. Only, midway, Yamanami's desertion and seppuku had checked it for a while, that was all. Had Yamanami then made his way safe back to Edo, he would have gathered comrades there, and, east and west answering each other, they would have built under Itō a powerful new body.

Kondō and Toshizō had misjudged the young man Tōdō Heisuke.

There was a jaunty dash to Heisuke that suited him less to a samurai than to the singing of a hauling-chant in the timber-yards of Fukagawa in Edo.

And so he was liked by everyone.

That he was thinker enough to sit here in this room at Itō's side — that they had never dreamed. Or rather, it was their carelessness not to have thought him a man capable of scheming on such a scale.

(Well, this is a surprise.)

Toshizō, however, kept his usual drowsy expression.

(It's the times. Even those who don't look it are drawn toward the drift of the age.)

The shogunate's prestige was waning by the day. It had come to such a pass that not one of the realm's patriots but preached, each after his kind, contempt for the shogunate and its overthrow. Even a man like Tōdō Heisuke, with a May wind blowing clean through his belly, would come to this.

"Heisuke, you—"

{j} said Kondō, smiling and smiling.

"so you're here on the same business as Itō-san, after all?"

"I am."

{j} Tōdō scratched the nape of his neck. He had that trick of his, a man who looked, at first glance, all innocence.

"Gentlemen, be at your ease."

Kondō, of late, was smooth and adroit. From drinking teahouse wine in Gion and the like with the domains' liaison officers, no doubt.

"Well then, Itō-san — let us hear you."

"I will speak. Today I mean to debate the great matters of the realm without reserve, and to examine the corps' way forward hereafter; at times my words may run to the intemperate. I beg you both to take that into account beforehand."

"Go on."

{j} Kondō's smile stiffened.

"Hijikata-san too — you have no objection?"

"None. Go ahead."

{j} said Toshizō.

After that Itō expounded the state of the realm, and taking China further for his example set forth the designs of the barbarian nations, and—

"A weak-kneed shogunate can no longer bear Japan on its back. Unless we return the government to the Court, unify Japan into one, and so face the outer barbarians, before long we shall meet the same wretched fate as the Qing empire. The Shinsengumi's founding purpose, at the outset, lay in expelling the barbarian. And yet the talk of the world has it that the corps has sunk to being the shogunate's claw and fang. Claw and fang — nay, there is a rumor it is to be raised into the shogun's own retainers. It is likely not the fact, I believe; but, Master Kondō — what say you?"

"—"

"What say you?"

"I too have heard the rumor."

{j} Kondō said, as though it pained him.

This very day, at Nijō Castle, the matter had been raised, and Kondō had held back a firm answer, saying he would first hear the mind of Itō's faction.

"Is it merely a rumor?"

"Who can say."

"No matter, let it be. The question is the Shinsengumi's course hereafter — whether it will serve as the Imperial Court's own guard, and as the vanguard of expelling the barbarian."

Kondō, stubbornly, took the side of the shogunate.

"I hold the Imperial Throne in the deepest reverence,"

{j} he said. A matter of course, and it did not on that account make Kondō an absolute imperial-loyalist. Reverence for the Throne was, in those days, a wholly common notion — not among the reading-class samurai only, but down to physicians, monks, village headmen, and the great farmers — and no political ideology.

"And, moreover, I mean to carry the expulsion of the barbarian through to the very end."

This too was a matter of course. In those days, one who preached opening the country was a rare man ahead of his time, and was treated as a crank, or as a traitor to the nation.

"But, Itō-san—"

From here on was the meat of Kondō's argument.

"the military power lies in the Kantō."

"That—"

"No — that military power, too, since the Tōshō Daigongen, Ieyasu, has held the office of Sei-i Taishōgun, a charge laid on it by command of the Court. And in plain fact, it is the Tokugawa shogunate, standing at the head of three hundred lords, that ought to be the core of expelling the barbarian; and by what I hear, even the Emperor of France acknowledges as much."

"Aha — even the Emperor of France."

Itō was startled by Kondō's leap. To begin with, the very bringing in of the Emperor of France was not the talk of an expel-the-barbarian man, but proof, surely, that Kondō had fallen in with the shogunate's creeping, open-the-country diplomacy.

"Hijikata-san."

{j} Itō turned his gaze slowly round.

"what are your thoughts?"

"The same."

{j} he said, as though it were a bother.

"The same as what?"

"As Kondō Isami here, that's what."

"Pro-shogunate, then."

"Well — I don't know what word it comes to. I'm of peasant stock, but such as I am, I mean to live and die as a samurai, in a samurai's way. I seem to be a man with little to do with the turning of the world."

"That is to say — you'll keep faith and give your all for the shogunate. That's it."

"That."

{j} he said, in a single word.

After that he said nothing. This kind of talk of the times, this arguing of ideas, was not much in his line.

The night grew late.

The two arguments standing opposed, they parted.

***

The next morning Itō Kashitarō and Shinohara Tainoshin, to make their last parley, met once more with Kondō and Toshizō at the Kōshō-ji lower mansion.

"You two gentlemen."

{j} The single-minded Shinohara fixed his eyes on them.

"will you not, at last, open your eyes? Today, if you two do not, we are all of us come resolved to split the corps and go our own way."

—A memoir of that night by Shinohara Tainoshin (after the Restoration, Hata Shigechika) survives.

"Again, on the night of the twenty-seventh following, we of our party went over, resolved that if this evening they did not submit, their heads and their feet should lie in separate places."

On the spot they had meant to cut down Kondō and Hijikata, but the two left no opening, and it never came to the drawing of a blade.

"[I,] arguing with a vehemence that made the very hair of my head bristle, could yet by no means — [they] balked at separation and would not submit. They [Kondō and Hijikata] know nothing of the Tokugawa's <i>seibai</i> [here the sense is perhaps its misgovernment], understand nothing of the loyalist purpose, and think only, always, to master men by force of the martial way."

{j} So it strikes to the very nature of Kondō and Toshizō; and further, Itō, wielding his gift for argument freely in every direction, pressed the two hard, and—

"in the end they fell into the toils of our party [our faction] and submitted to the argument for separation."

Whether they truly submitted is another question.

At all events, the two factions came to part ways.

That said, Itō and the rest did not leave the Shinsengumi at once, but for a while went on living in the barracks.

In this interval the corps was much unsettled, and sympathizers with Itō's faction came forward one after another.

Late one night Toshizō quietly asked Kondō his true mind. Kondō, in silence, tapped the hilt of his beloved Nagasone Kotetsu.

Toshizō nodded and gave a dry, open laugh.

Put in the style of Shinohara's memoir, this would be—

—No need of chatter. There is only the sword.

{j} that, no doubt, is how it would run.

The Shinsengumi's fighting units ran to ten. It came to this, that the commanders of the Eighth and Ninth Units, Tōdō and Suzuki, had broken away; and two days after this declaration of secession by Itō and Shinohara, the unsettling showed itself early, and, unexpectedly, Takeda Kanryūsai, who had not been especially close to Itō's faction, broke away on his own.

Itō was close to the Satsuma domain. It was rather that, a connection with Satsuma being made, he had declared his secession. Takeda, for his part, had approached Satsuma separately, on his own account.

"Takeda-kun, they tell me you've been going in and out of the Satsuma mansion a good deal of late; a fine thing, in times like these. Why not go over to them altogether?"

{j} and Kondō, gathering the corps' officers, held a farewell feast, and by night saw Takeda off from the barracks gate.

Seen off by two corps men, Takeda Kanryūsai left Kashō-chō.

One of the two corps men was Saitō Hajime.

When they had come as far as the Zenitori bridge on the Takeda road, Saitō, without so much as showing his hand to the draw, cut up into Takeda's flank and killed him outright with a single stroke.

—To leave the corps is death.

The corps law was alive.

The cut-down corpse of Takeda Kanryūsai might be called the silent answer of Kondō and Toshizō to Itō's faction, and might be called, too, a declaration of war.

At the close of that year, the Emperor Kōmei died.

On the tenth of the third month of the next year, the third of Keiō, Itō's faction received the appointment of Guards of the Imperial Tomb — the Goryō-eji — and, raising above the height of Kōdai-ji a corps banner with the chrysanthemum crest, made it their headquarters.

The name of their corps was a gentle one, but in truth it came to something like a loyalist Shinsengumi.

(It's war, then.)

That day Toshizō sent his Izumi-no-kami Kanesada blade out to be ground.
