## Chapter 36

The moment I met the fresh, cool air, a thousand kinds of loathing for tonight's gathering rose up in me at once. I had vexation, I had hatred, I had shame, and an unspeakable grief and repentance besides.

In the sky were sparse, faint flecks of stars; on the cypress and the holly trees were red and green electric lights, and everything seemed a scale-wound on my heart. The far, dim sky was full of loneliness, emptiness and pain, setting me shivering and trembling again and again. I wanted to weep bitterly, to kneel, to confess my sin faithfully to Bai Ping, and so ease the knot of reproach in my conscience. But still I supported her to the car.

But just as the page opened the car door for us, and Mrs. Manfield was helping Bai Ping up into it, Bai Ping suddenly seized my arm and retched, vomiting.

This vomiting proved that what Mei Yingzi had given me was no poison, and that my hand was no murderer's hand; my heart felt an unspeakable gladness and ease. I noticed all at once that in the instant of vomiting Bai Ping had already handed her handbag to Mrs. Manfield; and just while Mrs. Manfield was busy tending her retching, I took it. After I had helped them into the car, I shut the door and sent off the boy who had fetched the car for us. I climbed into the front seat and drove off along the little road, through the holly strung with red and green lights, past the sentry post. We reached the highway.

The open fields spread out all around me; the locust trees lining the road had long since shed their leaves; the crisscrossing asphalt roads, agleam with brilliant street lamps, wound and coiled and twisted, stretching away into the distance, with not a pedestrian on the road, and not a single car. I slowed the car to twenty-five li and with one hand opened Bai Ping's handbag beside me; but inside was nothing but a jumble of banknotes. I felt in past the notes, and underneath were odds and ends—a lipstick and a powder-compact; suddenly, at the side, I touched a stiff envelope, and my heart leapt violently—but I found at once it was a celluloid pass-holder, holding, I supposed, something like a park pass, and nothing more could I feel. So I opened the other compartment; inside were a few handkerchiefs, a fountain pen, a pencil, a notebook no bigger than an envelope, in which many small things seemed to be tucked—but none of them was the thing I sought.

The bag was built in just these two compartments, and I seemed to have reached a world of despair; but just then, by chance, in the second compartment I touched a mirror. This mirror was fairly large, set in a pocket on the wall of the bag. My original thought had been a suspicion that the document might be tucked behind the mirror, so I drew the mirror out; the back of it seemed to be leather, and at one corner it carried a slender, tough chain, joined to the wall of the bag. When I pulled it to its fullest extent, I peeped at it curiously; by the light within the car and along the road, I found it was a fine chain threaded with gold, one end joined to a delicate zipper in the wall of the bag. Driving with one hand, I took the chance to pull the zipper open. The zipper was very short; I felt in with four fingers and found, hidden inside, two stiff-paper envelopes lying flat against it—but the envelopes were nearly three times the width of the zipper, and had to be folded small before they could be got out. At last I touched the sealing wax on the flaps, and, thinking of the document of the time before, I drew them out without hesitation, my heart leaping violently. From the car's mirror I spied on Bai Ping in the back seat; she leaned against the side of the car, seeming very weary, and I believed she had not noticed my movements.

I tucked the documents I had taken out under me, drew the zipper shut, put the mirror back in place, and then closed the bag; I raised the car's speed to thirty-eight, and then to forty.

But my heart was still tense; I looked back in the mirror on the windscreen—Bai Ping leaned serene and weary in the corner of the car, and Mrs. Manfield too seemed to be showing signs of fatigue. Now I was anxious to get back early, just as one hopes to be past any hard pass early, and I raised the car to forty-four.

Silence, silence—no sound of wind, no sound of voices, no sound of car or horse, only the sound of our car sliding along the smooth road. I watched the road ahead of the headlamps, kept clear of my tangled thoughts, and drove on with my whole mind.

As we were nearing Hongkou, a quick thought came suddenly and made me, by reflex, stop the car; I turned my head and asked:

"Which hospital shall we go to?"

"No," said Bai Ping, opening her eyes wide. "I'll go home; when day breaks I'll send for a doctor."

"Do you feel a little better now?"

"Quite well—only tired."

"Dizzy?"

"No."

"Do you feel like vomiting?" asked Mrs. Manfield.

"No." Bai Ping wore a serene little smile. "I only want to sleep."

So I drove the car on again, through North Sichuan Road. Though the streets were dressed out with the sights and sounds of Christmas, the small hours had by now grown very cold and still. When a person is worn out in spirit, he easily responds to his surroundings and the air; but now this desolation-after-revelry did not stir any response in me, for my spent spirit had passed from keen feeling into numbness. I relaxed from the tensest state of mind—and yet was still held fast by my act of theft, and by the documents I had stolen.

The car crossed the Sichuan Road Bridge and drove straight on. I was anxious to see Bai Ping home early, take the documents to meet Mei Yingzi, and then bring them back to return to Bai Ping, so I raised the speed again. In the natural order of the route, I ought of course to see Mrs. Manfield home first. But whether to see Bai Ping home first, or to have Mrs. Manfield stay the night with her, would be of more advantage to my work—that was a question; and though I thought of it, I had no spirit to consider it in detail. By instinct I slowed the car, and asked:

"Mrs. Manfield, would you be willing to go over to Bai Ping's and look after her?"

"Of course, of course," said Mrs. Manfield warmly.

"No," said Bai Ping. "I'm quite all right now. Better see Mrs. Manfield home first—she must be very tired, I think."

Whether this was ordinary politeness, or whether she had some other purpose, I did not consider logically; but by instinct I felt that to leave Mrs. Manfield at Bai Ping's would at least delay the discovery that the packet of documents was missing.

"It's no trouble to me," said Mrs. Manfield. "Going home alone is very lonely too."

I paid no heed to what they said after this, nor did I hear any special insistence from Bai Ping; I drove the car straight to Bai Ping's lodgings on Route Prosper Paris.

I put the two packets of documents into my pocket and got out to open the door for them. I helped Mrs. Manfield down and, as if in passing, handed her Bai Ping's handbag; my movement was very natural, and I took the greatest care that Bai Ping should not see it, hoping she would believe her handbag had been at Mrs. Manfield's side all along. I slipped aside and went to help Bai Ping down. Bai Ping came down leaning on my hand; her hand was warm now, and I looked up at her face—this beautiful face was very calm, the ghastly pallor of a while ago seemingly gone. I was just rejoicing that Mei Yingzi had not broken faith with me, when Bai Ping's frail, lovely gaze met mine for an instant—like a punishment for my faithless act—and a feeling of shame made me bow my head and avoid her eyes. I hurriedly shut the car door and went with them into the desolate apartment building. Just then I noticed that the handbag was already in Bai Ping's hand. My heart began to pound again, and I longed to flee at once. Beside the elevator I said:

"Mrs. Manfield, do stay the night with Bai Ping."

"If you don't mind the discomfort." Bai Ping did not insist.

I saw that Mrs. Manfield had already given her assent, and so I said:

"Then I won't go up with you—I'll be off." And I said to Bai Ping: "Is anything else troubling you?"

"Only tired," she said. "I really made a great fool of myself today."

"Then get to sleep early." I patted her shoulder with a smile. "Goodbye." And with that I had already turned to Mrs. Manfield: "Good night, Mrs. Manfield."

I left them like a fugitive, sprang into the car, and drove straight to the Standford.

The glittering, splendid Christmas tree, the brilliant lights, the warm steam heat, the exciting music—these seemed no different from Umetake's gathering just now, and yet here I felt a kind of freedom and release. I saw the crowd, a crowd that had all made me feel loathing and disgust, but in this instant it made me feel affection; the reason was as when a fish leaps from the land into the water—however filthy the water, it is freedom all the same. It was as though I had come from hell to the human world, and the human world was already heaven, and everyone with pupils in their eyes seemed a god.

I ought to have been very tired, but at this moment I grew excited again. The stimulus of the shallow, tedious, dissolute bustle of the city I had long since wearied of, and yet at this moment I felt an unspeakable need of it. I squeezed in through the heat, squeezed in through the din, squeezed in through the soft hangings, squeezed in through the press of people. At last I found a seat, felt for the documents in my trouser pocket, and sat down. I called for a glass of iced beer, lit a cigarette, and felt the ease of release.

A rich, splendid, brilliant décor adorned the ballroom's Christmas night. Balloons, masks, paper hats of every color, whistles of every sound rippled here and there. Here were White Russian, Japanese, Korean and Chinese dance-hostesses; I went down and danced wildly, saying not a word, only squeezing into the crowd to flee my reality. Under strain, a person has just such need to escape reality; today, for the first time, I felt—it was I who discovered it—that all amusements are, in the spirit, of the same use as sleep, the only difference being that sleep, besides slackening the nerves, rests the body too, while amusement only slackens the nerves, or slackens them in some one respect, and to the body it brings, on the contrary, a fatigue of another kind.

Miko and the rest were all at Umetake's residence, so there was no stage performance today, and this left my dancing steps almost unbroken. I had already washed the stains and wounds from my mind and shaken off the pressure and heavy load on my heart; I heard all things without hearing, saw all things without seeing, used not a shred of feeling or thought, but only let the whole of the time—not slackening for even a second—the tedious music, the tedious scent of powder, the tedious light and color, work upon me. At last, on the dance floor, I heard one of the hostesses say:

"Miss Mei Yingzi has come."

As if by agreement everyone took notice, and only then did I come awake.

Mei Yingzi was dressed just as when she had walked into Umetake's drawing room a while before—simply a shaft of white light. She looked about her, seeming to search for me; I danced lightly over and pointed out my seat to her. Though I went on dancing, my heart had already come back to reality, and I was first of all conscious of the documents in my trouser pocket, so that tangled thoughts rose in my heart. When at last the music ended and the lights came up, I went back to my seat to meet Mei Yingzi. She had already ordered champagne, and without so much as a glance at me she told the waiter to pour; then, smiling and radiant, she raised her glass, gazed at me with the most gorgeous, gentle eyes, and said:

"Here's to the triumphant return of our hero."

"You think so?"

"What I think won't be wrong."

"On what do you base it?"

"On your having reached here before me."

I asked her nothing more, but clinked glasses and drank with her; at last, as the music struck up and the lights dimmed, I said in a low voice:

"I don't know if it's right—two in all, and I've brought them both."

"I don't think it will be wrong," she said with assurance.

"Are they to be returned to her?"

"Of course," she said. "Everything had best be as it was last time." With her own hand she poured me a glass of wine, then said: "Give them to me now; come to the Benner Inn for lunch, and I hope I can return them to you."

I handed her the two documents from my trouser pocket, and found they were a little creased now. She took them, glanced quickly at the sealing-wax stamp, and said:

"Quite right." She tucked them at once into her handbag, and then her eyes let out a light of victory, the wings of her nose lifted in a proud swell, a glad smile came to the corners of her mouth, and, raising her glass, she gazed at me in silence. I clinked glasses and drank with her, and said:

"Thank you."

"For what?"

"It was only vomiting after all."

"Trust me always, child," she said. "Now goodbye—you ought to go and rest too."

"And you?"

"Not until you wake, and we've had lunch together at the Benner, will it be my time to rest." She stood up with a smile, and added: "We are like two who take the tiller by turns, bringing this boat safely on through wind and wave."

I came out together with her; beyond the deep curtain she said goodbye to me, and vanished again, like a shaft of white light, in the crack between the curtains.

In an instant emptiness, loneliness, weariness all closed round me. Was it the sorrow that comes after victory? The desolation after a great feast breaks up? …I did not know. I had no heart to inquire; I only felt disappointment.

I put on my hat and coat and stepped out the main door. Outside, the sky had already grown light; a gust of cold made me shiver in spite of myself, and I turned up my collar, put on my gloves, and passed beneath the frost-covered Christmas tree, where the little red and green lights now looked truly like will-o'-the-wisps. I bowed my head and saw my own footprints on the frosty road, then hurried and sprang into the car and drove straight to Weihaiwei Road. The winter-morning air was thick with frost-mist, and my heart was as void as that air; I thought of nothing, only longed for the soft bed in my lodgings.

The Christmas Eve of 1941! This was the Christmas Eve of 1941!
