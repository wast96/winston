## 6

The Hankou docks were as loud and disordered as ever, a churn of traffic on every side. But for the few Rising-Sun flags flapping overhead, there was almost nothing to show the city had ever been occupied. Jiang Yongnan had gotten himself up as a Japanese resident home from his studies abroad, in a khaki youth-suit, his medical case on his back and a Japanese-made suitcase in one hand. He followed the crowd toward the exit, and only there did he catch sight of the Japanese soldiers on duty, short and dark, the bayonets on their Type 38 rifles already jutting higher than the tops of their heads.

The one who had come to meet him was a woman with graying hair, in a kimono and wooden clogs, speaking fluent Japanese. Before Jiang Yongnan could ask a thing, she switched to Chinese to set his mind at ease: she had been born in the Northeast, she said, and had lived more than twenty years in Jiamusi.

"What is my assignment?" Jiang Yongnan asked, still in Japanese, as they left the docks.

"You've come from Chongqing, and you don't even know your own assignment?" the woman said, in Japanese, throwing it back at him.

Jiang Yongnan's assignment was to go to Gannan, in southern Jiangxi, and take up a post as a military instructor at the cadre training class of the Youth Corps's Jiangxi branch. After announcing the appointment, Fang Bingzhong had added, as though it had only just come to him, that when Jiang passed through Wuhan he ought to linger a few days; someone would come for him.

With that he had drawn out an envelope marked burn after reading, and inside it the recognition signals, written by hand in Japanese.

"Your task is to find a way to be rid of him." Not until they were about to step into the hotel room did the woman pull from beneath a pillowcase a picture of a Japanese officer, clipped from an illustrated magazine. "This is Colonel Yamazaki, chief of staff of the Japanese Army's Third Air Group. It was he who planned the raid on the Huangshan residence last year, on the thirtieth of August."

Jiang Yongnan gave a soundless laugh. "You want me to charge into their Third Air Group and throttle the man?"

"He suffers from a bad stomach ulcer," the woman said. "Just now he's convalescing at the rear hospital for the wounded in Wuchang."

Jiang Yongnan understood at once: no one was better suited to the task than he was. He took the photograph up again and studied it a while. "I know the ground at that hospital. I'll need a concrete plan and a time to move."

The woman shook her head. There was no plan, no weapon, no one to cover him; ever since Wuhan fell, every field agent had long since withdrawn. As she spoke she fished a steamer ticket from inside her jacket. "The ticket has no expiry. When it's done, you can take a boat out whenever you please."

"If they all withdrew long ago, then how are you still here?" Jiang Yongnan said. "Whose orders do you answer to?"

"I'm only a wireless operator who has kept watch over a silent set for four years. This is my first time in the field." With that the woman rose to take her leave, but a few steps on she stopped, and when she turned back she had become another person altogether. She stared, glassy-eyed, at the ticket on the table, and her voice had gone hoarse. "That ticket was bought with my son's betrothal money. If he hadn't fallen at Changsha, you wouldn't have even that."

All that afternoon Jiang Yongnan sat at the table, staring into his own two hands. As night came on he gave up the room, took his luggage, and went to the little church. The priest betrayed not a flicker of surprise at the sight of him; he only crossed himself and went into the back room to open a bottle of grain liquor.

The two of them drank by candlelight until the priest stood and said he had to go and say his evening office. Only then did Jiang Yongnan say, in Korean, "I need a Japanese subaltern's uniform. The insignia had best be from Eleventh Army headquarters."

"You have your own organization," the priest said. "This is not something you should be bringing to me at all."

"If not for you, I'd never have taken this road." Jiang Yongnan tipped his head back and drained the last drop in his cup.

The priest looked at him and sat down again. When Jiang Yongnan had finished laying out the task he meant to carry through, the priest shook his head. "After that business of yours, the army hospital for the wounded doubled its guard. All these years it's been slack without and taut within. Whoever goes in finds only one road, and it ends in death."

"Even if it kills me, I have to go," Jiang Yongnan said. "It is my assignment."

"This is your death sentence." The priest got up and opened another bottle of liquor, filling both cups. "Your superior only wants you to die a little more handsomely."

"He gave me the chance to choose." Jiang Yongnan again drank his cup dry. "I can't turn deserter merely to go on living."

"It seems you've truly taken yourself for a Chinese." The priest sat down once more and filled the cup again. "Don't forget, your own homeland is also waiting for you to lay down your life for it."

Jiang Yongnan smiled and narrowed his eyes at the priest. "But I have only the one life."

"I could recommend you be sent to the Korean Liberation Army, under General Yi Cheong-cheon," the priest said. "If you mean to die, then die among your own people." The army's rear hospital for the wounded had not only raised its walls; it had strung a high-voltage fence along the top. From a distance it looked like nothing so much as a heavily guarded prison.

Jiang Yongnan had made thorough preparations for the operation. In the uniform of a Japanese Army subaltern, a briefcase in hand, he walked in at the main gate during the busiest hour of the morning clinic, passed the two sentry boxes without a glance to either side, and made straight for the doctors' changing room in the emergency department. There he pulled a white coat on over the uniform, tied on a surgical mask, and set his ear to the crack of the door, waiting until he heard several nurses wheel a patient past on a surgical trolley before he opened it and came out.

Jiang Yongnan shoved the briefcase into a nurse's hands. "The patient's blood pressure?" he said, in Japanese. "Pulse?"

Walking, he drew the case history from the nurse and leafed through the chart, and having passed without hitch the armed guard at the door of the surgical wing, Jiang Yongnan took back the briefcase, pushed through the operating-theater doors without a backward look, and went straight in. When he came out along the recovery corridor, the mask was off his face and the white coat gone from his back.

Two military policemen with pistols on their hips stood at the foot of the inpatient stairs. Jiang Yongnan looked clean through them. He stopped a nurse and gave the order in a domineering voice: "Take me to Colonel Yamazaki's ward. Now. Be quick!"

Colonel Yamazaki's private ward was on the second floor, his orderly at the door and, beside him, a sentry in full kit.

From the briefcase Jiang Yongnan took a document, its flap stamped top secret, and held it up before his chest. "A secret dispatch from headquarters. It must be read by Colonel Yamazaki himself."

The orderly put out a hand to take it, then, seeing the look on Jiang Yongnan's face, drew it back and hesitated, murmured a request that the officer wait a moment, and turned to knock and enter the ward.

Before long the ward door opened. The orderly followed Jiang Yongnan in and stationed himself by the shut door, his eyes wary, one hand pressed to the holster at his waist.

Colonel Yamazaki was a lean, pale, middle-aged man. Propped against the bed, he looked over the saluting Jiang Yongnan. "Who are you? I've never seen you at headquarters."

The orderly drew his pistol and worked the bolt with a clack.

"This humble officer is Yamada Kōichi, cipher staff at Eleventh Army headquarters of the Expeditionary Army," Jiang Yongnan said. "I came up to Wuhan this July with Commander Tsukada, transferred from the Southern Army."

"If it is a secret dispatch, then it has a secret channel of transmission. It should have gone to Third Air Group headquarters, not here."

"It was brought here because it touches on General Endō." Jiang Yongnan glanced at the orderly by the door. "Commander Tsukada hopes I can bring back Colonel Yamazaki's clear answer."

Even then he did not lay the dispatch in the hand Colonel Yamazaki held out, but looked once more at the orderly by the door, and only when the colonel waved the man out did he present it, respectfully, in both hands.

It was while he was opening the dispatch that Colonel Yamazaki's neck was snapped. Jiang Yongnan drew the quilt up over the body, took out his scalpel, and crossed to the door without a sound. His back flat to the wall, he gazed quietly at the sky full of light beyond the barred window, as if bidding the world farewell.

Jiang Yongnan discovered, at last, that among all the many people who rose in his mind in this moment of waiting for death there was, after all, Tang Ya. Those eyes of hers, round as a cat's, would not leave him.

At that very moment came a knock at the ward door. The orderly had barely put his head in when Jiang Yongnan opened his throat with one stroke, pulled the pistol from the man's belt, shot the sentry dead, and rushed with the gun toward the head of the inpatient stairs. Two more military policemen were waiting there. Jiang Yongnan could all but feel the scalding heat of bullets going through his chest.

Then, without warning, a great roar shook the ground and rocked the hills. All down the ward the windowpanes cracked at the sound.

A gap had been blown in the hospital's outer wall. The priest took a last draw on the cigarette clamped in his lips and came charging in through the breach, two Mausers, one in either hand.

In an instant gunfire broke on every side, and as the hospital's guards came swarming, the priest began to fall back. He ran toward the main road, firing to hold them off, and was soon hit and went down in the street. The priest heaved himself half upright and, waiting for the soldiers and police who were closing the ring to draw nearer, looked about him blankly under their muzzles. Then a light came into his eyes. Among the countless Japanese caps he had found Jiang Yongnan's face, still smeared with blood not yet dry.

"Lord, forgive me." The priest lifted his face to the sky, and when he had spoken he let the gun fall from his hand, made the sign of the cross over his breast, and drew a grenade from inside his coat.

The guns that had gone still roared out all at once. Countless bullets tore through the priest's body in the same breath, and every one of them landed as though on Jiang Yongnan.
