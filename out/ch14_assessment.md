# Chapter Fourteen — synopsis and reliability

*Printed pages 281–298. Sections 47 ("Threads in Cui Zhengyao's Last Letter"),
48 ("Hua Kezhi Says Something Startling"), 49 ("The Handsome-Man Stratagem and
the Swindler Gang"). Chapter title: "Shadows Crowding, Trapped by the Fragrant
River" — i.e. besieged in Hong Kong.*

---

## What happens

**Two rooms, far apart.** In Hong Kong, Wang is drinking to the memory of Sun
Fengming and his wife. In Nanjing, Chiang is worn down by Chen Bijun's
unrelenting recriminations over the shooting, and asks Soong Mei-ling across a
table of newspapers whether Wang Jingwei is really a man anyone would die for.
She finds the whole thing a mystery: the assassin can have had no personal
grudge against Wang Jingwei at all.

**Dai Li reads the letter.** He has Cui Zhengyao's last testament, and treats a
line in it — that distant relatives should not come to the funeral — as a coded
instruction to Wang Yaqiao's people in Hong Kong to stay away from Shanghai and
Nanjing rather than walk into arrest. From that single inference he concludes
the planner was not Li Zongren or Li Jishen but Wang Yaqiao. Chiang gets
gooseflesh listening to it, and Soong Mei-ling takes it as confirmation of her
own reading — that the real target had been Chiang and Wang Jingwei was a
substitute.

**Then the counter-operation.** The last section names it plainly: a handsome
man, and the *chaibai* gang — the Shanghai term for the professional gigolo
swindlers who worked women for money. This is the machinery being assembled that
will reach Yu Wanjun and, in the next chapter, kill Wang Yaqiao.

---

## How it holds up

**Soong Mei-ling's puzzlement is the book being accidentally right.** Sun
Fengming had no grudge against Wang Jingwei; Chiang was the target and did not
appear for the photograph. That is the documented account, and the book lets a
character state it plainly — which sits oddly beside its own insistence
elsewhere that Wang Yaqiao ran the operation, since the man who actually
organized it publicly said the same thing in 1936.

**Cui Zhengyao's last letter is almost certainly invented.** That she was
arrested and killed is stated everywhere; **no source gives a date, a place, a
manner of death, or any document.** A surviving testament with a coded line in
it is precisely the sort of specific that the record does not support, and the
inference Dai Li builds on it — the chapter's whole hinge — is a detective-story
device.

It is worth noticing what the device is *for*. The historical problem is that
nobody can show Wang Yaqiao organized the shooting. The book solves it by having
Dai Li deduce it from a clue. A deduction inside the fiction stands in for the
evidence that is missing outside it.

**Chen Bijun's confrontations with Chiang are attested** in outline, though the
wording and even the setting vary between accounts — some put it at the scene,
some in his office the next day. Standard in the literature, fixed in none of it.

**The *chaibai* gang is a real thing** — Shanghai slang for organized
seducer-swindlers — and using such a man against Yu Wanjun is plausible tradecraft.
Whether it happened is unattested; the entire seduction sequence rests on the
popular corpus, and the corpus is silent on how Dai Li's people actually reached
her beyond the money and the promise about her husband.

## Verdict

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Chen Bijun's recriminations against Chiang | **PARTLY** — attested, unfixed in detail |
| That Sun Fengming's real target was Chiang | **CORROBORATED** |
| Cui Zhengyao's last letter, and its coded line | **UNCORROBORATED** — almost certainly invented |
| Dai Li deducing Wang's authorship from it | **the fiction supplying the missing evidence** |
| A *chaibai* seducer deployed against Yu Wanjun | **UNCORROBORATED** |

---

## A note on this chapter's function

Chapters twelve and fourteen are where the book does its quietest and most
consequential work. Neither contains a famous event, so neither invites
checking — and between them they install the two things chapter fifteen needs:
a romance between Wang and Yu Wanjun, and a mechanism for turning her. Both are
unattested. By the time the reader reaches the killing at Wuzhou, the betrayal
has been given an emotional logic that the documented record does not have,
where the record has only a woman, a sum of money, and a promise about a man in
prison.
